Abstract
. Field studies are irreplaceable but costly, time-consuming, and error-prone, which need careful preparation. Inspired by rapid-prototyping in manufacturing, we propose a fast, low-cost evaluation method using Vision-Language Model (VLM) personas to simulate outcomes comparable to field results. While LLMs show human-like reasoning and language capabilities, autonomous vehicle (AV)-pedestrian interaction requires spatial awareness, emotional empathy, and behavioral generation. This raises our research question: To what extent can VLM personas mimic human responses in field studies? We conducted parallel studies: 1) one real-world study with 20 participants, and 2) one video-study using 20 VLM personas, both on a street-crossing task. We compared their responses and interviewed five HCI researchers on potential applications. Results show that VLM personas mimic human response patterns (e.g., average crossing times of 5.25 s vs. 5.07 s) lack the behavioral variability and depth. They show promise for formative studies, field study preparation, and human data augmentation.

1.
Introduction

Evaluating the interaction between autonomous vehicles (AVs) and pedestrians is essential for the safe and effective deployment of AVs. Understanding how pedestrians perceive and react to AVs informs both algorithm development and human-centric interaction design
(
Mahadevan et al. 2019
)
. In the context of studying AV-pedestrians’ interaction, field studies are important
(
Clarke and Dietz 2024
)
because they capture natural human responses (e.g., spontaneous behavior and emotion), which are difficult to replicate in lab environments
(
Sirkin et al. 2016
;
Gerig et al. 2018
)
. However, conducting field studies presents methodological challenges. Controlled environments limit behavioral authenticity, while real-world testing raises concerns about safety and ethics
(
Geelhoed et al. 2024
;
Reig et al. 2018
)
. Participants may misjudge situations, which can lead to accidents or increased stress. In addition, field studies require AV hardware, dedicated space, multiple experimenters, and extensive pilot testing
(
Rothenbücher et al. 2015
)
, which highlights the importance of careful study design beforehand.

To address these challenges, researchers explored alternative approaches that approximate real-world responses. One direction uses mixed reality (MR) and tangible interfaces to enhance immersion without physical risk
(
Hoggenmüller et al. 2021
;
Gao and Spence 2025
;
Dufresne et al. 2024
)
. However, controlled settings restrict natural movement, which is problematic for observational studies requiring spontaneous behavior
(
Gerig et al. 2018
)
. Extended Reality (XR) methods also lack a strong sense of presence due to technical limitations, especially the trust judgments
(
Abraham et al. 2022
)
. Another direction is to use AI agents to simulate human-like reasoning and responses
(
Liu et al. 2024a
;
Wang et al. 2025a
)
. We enhance this approach by proposing to use Vision-Language Model (VLM) personas to simulate subjective and behavioral reactions to approaching AVs. In this work, we explore how VLM personas can support field studies. For example, VLMs can help inform pilot tests to reduce costly errors or augment human participant data to balance cost and analytical depth. These may provide a low-cost, human-like preview of real field study results, helping to reduce errors and allowing researchers to make early adjustments to prevent additional costs.

While VLMs show human-level performance in language tasks (e.g., product reviews or consultations)
(
Hung et al. 2024
;
Meng et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
)
, performing field study tasks needs geospatial awareness
(
Gui et al. 2024a
)
, emotional synthesis
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, and behavior generation
(
Rasouli et al. 2017
)
. Although previous works (e.g., VLMs
(
Bordes et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Shao et al. 2025
)
) suggest that AI personas can now interpret spatial contexts and generate corresponding actions. However, it remains unclear how well their responses align with human results, both quantitatively and qualitatively. That is,
rather than relying solely on performance benchmarks, validating their utility requires a deeper comparison between AI-generated and human responses
. It also remains unclear how HCI researchers assess the value of using VLM personas in research, and what roles they envision for this tool. Therefore, our research questions were:

1) To what extent can VLM personas mimic human responses in field studies?

2) How do HCI researchers whose work requires field studies envision their use in embodied research?

To answer these questions, we conducted a three-step process (see
Figure 1
). First, we ran two parallel experiments: 1) a field study with 20 real participants, and 2) a video-based simulation using VLM personas modeled from the same participants. We rented a real AV, reserved a large testing area, and installed a custom external human-machine interface (eHMI)
(
Carmona et al. 2021
;
Bazilinskyy et al. 2021
)
to enable pedestrian-AV interaction. Both the real participants and their VLM counterparts performed the same task (i.e., crossing the street in the presence of an AV). We applied the same metrics to both testing groups (real humans and VLM personas), analyzing behavioral data (e.g., discretized trajectories, crossing times) and subjective data (e.g., 5-point Likert ratings on confidence, trust, and textual responses from post-study interviews).

Results indicate that the behavioral patterns generated by VLM personas fall within the distribution of human responses. Trust ratings were comparable, though VLMs consistently reported overconfidence. Thematic analysis found that VLMs captured key human insights but lacked nuance in nonverbal cues and complex social behaviors. To address the second question, we interviewed five HCI researchers experienced in field studies. After presenting our methods and findings, we discussed the applicability of VLM personas in the researchers’ workflows. They recognized the benefits of using VLMs for: 1) conducting exploratory studies on unfamiliar topics to establish a research direction, 2) pilot testing and refining experimental designs, and 3) augmenting human data by simulating responses from hard-to-reach or vulnerable populations (e.g., children, the elderly, or individuals with disabilities). These can generate human-like data at low cost throughout the research process, reducing the risk of errors in subsequent formal studies.
We extend our findings to broader embodied interaction, defining “embodied” as simulated personas rather than body-integrated technologies. An input–output analysis of embodiment literature shows how VLM persona simulations can generalize to other domains.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Pedestrian analysis in human-AV interaction

The shift from human-driven to self-driving vehicles introduces communication challenges
(
Rad and Hagenzieker 2020
)
. Pedestrians lose direct cues from drivers, such as eye contact and hand gestures, leading to increased uncertainty during interactions. That is, the AVs become “black boxes,” unable to clearly convey their perceptions or intentions
(
Carmona et al. 2021
)
. As a result, understanding and designing effective communication between pedestrians and autonomous vehicles has become a critical research topic
(
Colley et al. 2024
)
.
The evaluation of AV-pedestrian interaction typically contains objective and subjective metrics.

Objective metrics track whether participants perform actions that ensure their safety and achieve their goals (e.g., “crossing the street,” “avoiding collision”). Research
(
Rasouli et al. 2017
)
on crossing behavior shows that decisions and timing depend on numerous factors, including social factors (social norms, pedestrian flows), physical contexts (pedestrian state, traffic characteristics)
(
Colley et al. 2025
)
, and demographics (age, gender)
(
Holländer et al. 2021
;
Colley et al. 2021
;
Gehrke et al. 2023
)
. Subjective metrics capture a participant’s internal state and perception. This is commonly measured using metrics like Trust in Automation (TiA)
(
Körber 2018
)
, confidence
(
Wright et al. 2019
)
, attitudes
(
Nomura et al. 2006
)
, or mental workload (e.g., NASA-TLX)
(
Hart and Staveland 1988
)
.

A comprehensive analysis often combines both types of feedback. Sometimes, the subjective answer is coherent with and supplements the objective answer
(
Chauhan et al. 2023
)
, but sometimes it is contradictory, showing the complexity of human perception and performance. For example, a participant might make a wrong decision (e.g., colliding with the vehicle in a video-based study) but report high confidence
(
Frank et al. 2019
;
De Vries et al. 2003
)
. Alternatively, a participant may perform a safe behavior when facing the AV but still feel a low sense of safety
(
Fink et al. 2025
;
Rasouli and Tsotsos 2019
)
.

These evaluation results show that the absence of a human driver creates a communication gap between the AV and the pedestrian
(
Rasouli and Tsotsos 2019
)
. To enhance mutual understanding, researchers introduced external human-machine interfaces (eHMIs)
(
Tran et al. 2024
)
, which place the communicative interface on the exterior of the vehicle. Various eHMI modalities have been tested, including windshield text (e.g., “Stop”)
(
Eisma et al. 2021
)
, flashing light strips
(
Dey et al. 2020b
;
Bazilinskyy et al. 2020
)
, and projected virtual crosswalks
(
Bazilinskyy et al. 2019
;
Ochiai and Toyoshima 2011
)
. Results show eHMIs can lead to more correct or faster crossing decisions, with text-based eHMIs often being the most straightforward
(
Bazilinskyy et al. 2019
;
Chang et al. 2018
)
. In addition, some researchers explore anthropomorphic
(
Lim et al. 2023
;
Bazilinskyy et al. 2019
)
and bionic eHMIs
(
Oudshoorn et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
to improve the noticeability and perception of devices and design intuitive interaction compared to standard eHMIs perceived as cold and neutral
(
Weidel et al. 2025
)
.

Anthropomorphic eHMIs mimic human-like cues, such as robotic eyes to simulate gaze
(
Chang et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
, animated hands for gestures
(
Gui et al. 2024b
;
Mahadevan et al. 2018
;
Epke et al. 2021
)
, or facial expressions (e.g., smiling face)
(
Large et al. 2025
;
Semcon 2016
)
. Past research reveals significant individual differences in their reception. Some works report that these eHMIs improve the sense of safety
(
Bonneviot et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2025b
;
Jansen et al. 2023
)
, providing a warmer atmosphere
(
Gui et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
. Conversely, other works report feelings of discomfort or even being spied on
(
Gui et al. 2022
)
.
Several studies focusing on trust in anthropomorphic eHMIs have reported that anthropomorphic designs do not significantly increase trust in autonomous vehicles
(
Arabian et al. 2025
)
. In addition, a field study found that eye-based eHMIs were not considered a primary factor influencing pedestrians’ trust
(
Gui et al. 2023
)
.
As anthropomorphic cues rely on “common sense” norms, which differ between individuals
(
de Winter and Dodou 2022
)
, their communicative efficiency can be highly polarized, with people tending to either strongly favor or strongly dislike it
(
Lim et al. 2023
;
Gui et al. 2024a
;
Large et al. 2023
)
.

Therefore, comparing how anthropomorphic and non-anthropomorphic eHMIs influence pedestrians’ objective behavior (e.g., crossing) and subjective state (e.g., trust, psychological comfort) remains a central and unresolved topic
(
Bazilinskyy et al. 2019
)
. Thus, in our study, we selected one anthropomorphic and one non-anthropomorphic eHMI to explore whether humans and VLM personas exhibit similar behavioral tendencies, and whether the VLM personas can capture the complexity and individual differences observed in human responses.

2.2.
Evaluation Environment in AV-pedestrian Interaction

Field studies are widely used for their ability to capture pedestrians’ natural reactions to real vehicles. That is something video or virtual reality (VR)-based environments cannot fully replicate
(
Colley et al. 2024
;
Voit et al. 2019
)
.
Sirkin et al. 2016
identified three main types of field studies: Wizard-of-Oz setups
(
Habibovic et al. 2016
)
, real-road AV simulators
(
Matthews et al. 2017
)
, and public deployment prototypes (e.g., the
Ghost driver
platform)
(
Rothenbücher et al. 2016
)
. However, real-world evaluations require a highly controlled, labor-intensive setup
(
Currano et al. 2018
;
Palmeiro et al. 2018
;
Mahadevan et al. 2018
)
. These studies often involve multiple on-site observers, extended testing periods, and tightly managed conditions
(
Gui et al. 2023
;
Yu et al. 2025
)
. In some cases, research teams rely on support from automotive companies, fabrication specialists, media crews, and collaborators across institutions
(
Moore et al. 2019
;
Rothenbücher et al. 2015
)
.

The challenges lie not only in the execution of the formal study but also in the extensive and costly preparation needed, such as pre-tests, rehearsals, and iterative refinements. Because a full-scale run is both time-consuming and costly, any issues that occur during the formal experiment have a disproportionately large impact, potentially undermining substantial portions of the collected data. Given these difficulties, it becomes essential to explore supportive tools. In our research, we tested VLM personas simulation, which can help reduce risk, improve preparation, and make field studies more efficient and robust.

2.3.
AI-generated Persona as an Evaluator in HCI Research

A persona is defined as a fictional, detailed user model that represents archetypal users
(
Blomkvist 2002
)
. Later developments in the persona lifecycle
(
Miaskiewicz and Kozar 2011
;
Adlin and Pruitt 2010
;
Pruitt and Adlin 2010
)
showed that personas enhance the design process by giving designers a clear and evocative representation of the target user group. With the rise of LLMs, persona creation has become more accessible
(
McGinn and Kotamraju 2008
;
Salminen et al. 2021
)
. Users can now generate virtual agents, such as personas defined by behavioral profiles that simulate human-like interaction, simply by writing prompts or integrating personality traits
(
Anvari et al. 2015
)
, reducing the cost and effort of personification. This shift has led to a boost in the use of AI-generated personas in HCI research
(
Liu et al. 2024c
;
Prpa et al. 2024
)
.

In HCI, LLM personas currently serve two main roles, each offering promise but also raising critical concerns. First is enabling AI agents to adopt specific roles when collaborating with users. For example, interacting as a user’s “future self” to promote personal growth
(
Jeon et al. 2025
;
Khot et al. 2025
)
, or acting as co-creative partners in educational and productivity contexts
(
Chu et al. 2025
;
Qin et al. 2025
)
. These agents can simulate inner dialogues
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
and personalize interaction to boost engagement. The second role involves using LLM personas as evaluation tools to simulate user feedback. For example, reviewing videos
(
Hung et al. 2024
)
, analyzing the impact of demographics
(
Ilkka et al. 2025
)
, or participating in synthetic focus groups
(
Olawade et al. 2025
)
. Previous work has demonstrated that LLM personas can generate outputs that resemble human language and reasoning. However, relying too heavily on simulated feedback may give a false sense of validity. There is a risk to masking real human diversity and introducing biases from the LLM itself
(
Ye et al. 2024
)
. Therefore, the results from LLM simulation always need to be critically examined.

These improvements show that the LLMs are acquiring the abilities of environmental perception and behavioral response. This in turn can provide field study researchers with a significant opportunity: to explore using VLM personas as proxy participants (i.e., evaluators) in complex real-world tasks. For human-AV interaction, a VLM persona could take a video feed with an approaching AV as input, situate itself in the scenario (e.g., pretend to be a "pedestrian"), and generate a comprehensive response. This could include not only a crossing decision (behavior) but also its subjective perceptions (e.g., trust in the AV) and its interpretation of the AV’s eHMI.

However, while the technical functionality for such simulations is rapidly emerging, a critical research gap remains: no existing work has systematically compared AI-generated responses to human responses within high-fidelity field study tasks. This gap limits our understanding of how well VLM-generated results align with human data and which aspects of field studies VLMs can (or cannot) reliably support. To address this, we conducted a comparative experiment where both human participants and VLM-based agents performed the same tasks. Our goal was not to test performance, but to assess the similarity of responses across metrics. This allows us to evaluate whether AI and human participants exhibit consistent behavioral patterns (e.g., have the same strengths and limitations as humans) when engaged in embodied field study tasks.

3.
Scope and Experimental Setup

Before we conducted the studies to address RQ1, i.e., “to what extent can VLM personas
mimic
human responses in field studies?”, it is important to 1) formally define “mimicry” specialized in AV-pedestrian interaction (Sec
3.1
) and 2) build a unified prototype (Sec
3.2
) that can be applied consistently to both human field studies and VLM simulations. The following section explains on these critical preparations for the subsequent comparison studies.

3.1.
Definition of the “mimicry” metrics in AV-pedestrian interaction

Social psychology literature
(
Chartrand and Bargh 1999
;
Hess and Fischer 2013
;
Hale and Hamilton 2016
)
distinguishes several forms of mimicry, including behavioral, affective, communicative, and cognitive forms. In our research context, where
safe
interaction with AVs is the core objective, we do not aim to claim that VLMs replicate the entire human cognition. Instead, we narrow the scope of
defining “mimicry” as the VLM’s ability to reproduce “the sequence of decisions” in representative locomotion behaviors when facing an AV
, rather than social (communicative) or manipulative (task-oriented) behaviors, because locomotion behavior directly affects safety during AV-pedestrian interaction. At the same time, affective behavior, which is the expression of feelings during the interaction, is also considered, as it raises important psychosocial concerns when introducing AVs into real-world contexts. After introducing the eHMI on the AV, it can convey information to surrounding pedestrians, which involves only a part of cognitive behavior, specifically, the information-processing aspect of receiving cues from the AV and its eHMI.

That is, our use of “mimicry” here refers to capturing representative locomotion behavioral and subjective feeling tendencies rather than reproducing human responses in their full cognitive or social complexity.

3.2.
Target scenario and eHMI interaction modality

There are six individual conditions (3 eHMI types
\times
2 AV behaviors: stop vs. non-stop) in the evaluation. We focus on a widely examined and practically significant scenario:
street crossing
. In this context, the AV faces two behavioral options: 1) stop and yield to the pedestrian, or 2) continue driving.

We tested three eHMI conditions. The first was the
light strip
condition, in which the AV displayed red or green light signals. A green light instructed the pedestrian to cross (

(d)), while a red light indicated that the pedestrian should wait (

(a))
(
Dey et al. 2020b
;
Faas and Baumann 2019
;

)
. The second was the
animated eyes
condition, featuring a digital display mounted on the AV. When the eyes looked toward the pedestrian, it signaled that the AV had noticed them and it was safe to cross (
Figure 2
(e)). When the eyes looked away, it suggested the AV’s attention was elsewhere, implying that the pedestrian should wait (

(b))
(
Chang et al. 2022

Chang et al. 2017

. The third was a
baseline

3.3.
Apparatus and Autonomous mode setup

We reserved an open area and marked it using cones and signage labeled “study is ongoing.” The AV operated in autonomous mode using the open-source
Autoware 2.0

(
Kato et al. 2018
)
software that supports real-time localization and destination assignment. A
Lidar Ouster

(
Males et al. 2025
)
sensor was mounted on top of AV to detect surrounding and route planning. To simulate the AV’s behavioral decisions, we defined two destination points in Autoware. For the
non-stop
condition, the AV was assigned a destination 60 meters ahead, allowing it to drive past the pedestrian (
Figure 3
(c, d)). For the
stop
condition, the destination was set at 45 meters, which is
\approx
3 meters before the pedestrian’s location (
Figure 3
(a, b)). The AV’s speed was limited to 20 km/h to ensure consistency and safety across trials. This setup provided a highly standardization in terms of route, vehicle timing, and decision-making cues. Both human participants and VLM personas experienced nearly the same decision time windows (
\approx
8 seconds), creating a solid basis for comparing behavioral responses between real humans and VLMs in the subsequent analysis.

4.
Study 1: Field Study With Real Participants

4.1.
Participants

We distributed invitations across various online communities and recruited 20 participants (16 males and 4 females). The participants have an average age of 28.1 years (SD = 6.46). Participants are from six countries across Asia, Europe, and Africa. All participants were residing in Japan at the time of the field study. Length of residence ranged from 2 months to 7 years (mean = 3.71 years, SD = 1.48). This study is not viable for people with vision impairment.

4.2.
Birds-eye view of the schematic setup and timeline

The user study was conducted over three consecutive days from 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. each day, under consistently sunny weather. Three experimenters conducted the study (
Figure 4
(a)). An
instructor
guided participants and coordinated transitions between indoor and outdoor environments. An
observer
recorded (video record) participant behavior during the street-crossing task, with camera angles adjusted to avoid capturing facial features and protect privacy. The
controller
managed the AV, operated the eHMI interface, and monitored safety from the roadside. In case of unsafe crossing behavior or AV malfunction (neither of which occurred), the controller could trigger a
wireless emergency stop
via a red button on a remote, covering the entire test area.

Key position points of the field study are illustrated in
Figure 4
(b). Each trial began with the participant standing 3.2 meters away from the roadside. From this position, the AV was not visible to the participant, but became visible with a step forward. The AV took exactly
8 seconds
to drive from the starting point (Point A) to the crossing point (Point D). This timing (8 seconds) and the distance (3.2 meters) were validated by reference to the averaged pedestrians’ walking speeds
(
Montufar et al. 2007
)
(generally 1.2 m/s) and our pilot tests (minimum observed: 0.5 m/s). After initializing the AV and eHMI, the controller returned to the roadside and signaled the trial to begin. The observer then started recording. As the AV moved, the participant was notified by the observer to walk straight when the AV reached Point B. At Point C, the eHMI was activated, followed by the pre-defined action specified in
Figure 2
. Participants made their crossing decisions based on this cue, along with the AV’s motion, position, and their own judgment. The AV stopped either at Point D or Point E, depending on its assigned option (stop to yielding or keep driving).

4.3.
Procedure and Participant task

The study consisted of three phases: oral introduction, experiment, and post-interview. After arrival, each participant was guided to a room two minutes on foot from the field site. The instructor explained the study. Participants then reviewed and signed a consent form. They were then guided to the outdoor field area. In the experimental phase, participants were instructed to imagine themselves as pedestrians assessing when it would be safe to cross a road. They observed an AV, clearly identifiable as driverless, and had to decide whether to cross based on its behavior. The AV communicated its intent to stop or continue through an eHMI. Participants were instructed to
physically cross the road
if they judged it safe.

Each session included seven trials. The first was a practice trial to familiarize participants with the setup, with no data recorded. This was followed by six formal trials, representing all combinations of two AV behaviors (stop or go) and three eHMI conditions (eye display, light strip, or no eHMI). Trial order was counterbalanced using a Latin square design to control for order effects. After each trial, the AV returned to its starting position, and the destination was reset. After completing all trials, participants returned to the indoor room for a post-interview.

Participants were clearly informed that the AV operated in autonomous mode throughout the experiment and that safety protocols were in place (i.e., a remote emergency stop mechanism). They were encouraged to behave as naturally as they would in a real-world street-crossing scenario to ensure authentic responses. The field study is approved by the university’s ethical review committee.

4.4.
Measurement

Based on the narrowed scope, we then drew on established AV-pedestrian literature to identify the most commonly used metrics for evaluating human responses. These metrics cover three core aspects: 1) locomotion behavior (e.g., crossing decisions and time)
(
Rasouli et al. 2017
;
Rasouli and Tsotsos 2019
;
Rasouli et al. 2019
)
, 2) subjective feeling (e.g., trust, confidence, perceived safety)
(
Dirsehan and Can 2020
;
Niu et al. 2018
;
Gui et al. 2022
)
, and 3) communicative efficiency (e.g., eHMI interpretation correctness and acceptability)
(
Mahadevan et al. 2019
;
Carmona et al. 2021
;
Bazilinskyy et al. 2019
)
. These dimensions serve as the operational criteria for assessing VLM mimicry in our study.

During this reset period, participants answered two 5-point Likert-scale questions:
Q1) How confident are you in your decision?
, and
Q2) How much do you trust the autonomous vehicle?
Then, the next trial began.

This interview consisted of seven questions. The first four, rated on a 5-point Likert scale, asked participants
Q1) How similar do you think this field study is compared to real life?
,
Q2) How genuine do you think your behavior in this study was compared to real life?
,
Q3) After this interaction with the autonomous vehicle, how accepting are you toward it?
, and
Q4) How much do you think the vehicle’s interface helped you make your decision?
The final three questions were open-ended and explored participants’ understanding of the eHMI cues:
Q5) What do you think the “eye” display means?
,
Q6) What do you think the “light strip” display means?
, and
Q7) When there is no interface on the car, how do you make your decision?
While participants completed the post-interview, the instructor began briefing the next participant, following the same initial procedure.

These metrics were adapted from three related research domains. The two in the reset period are commonly used in road-crossing scenario evaluations to assess pedestrian subjective feeling when facing AVs
(
Rasouli et al. 2017
;
Rad and Hagenzieker 2020
)
. The next in the post-study questionnaire assesses the ecological validity of field studies
(
Hoggenmüller et al. 2021
;
Hock et al. 2022
;
Holleman et al. 2020
)
. The final four questions are derived from eHMI research to evaluate communicational efficiency and user acceptance
(
Gui et al. 2022
;
Dey et al. 2020a
)
.

Three types of data were collected from each participant. First, behavioral data were recorded via video, capturing participants’ actions, decisions, and relevant timestamps during the crossing process, processed by our developed UI (
Figure 7
). Second, subjective responses were gathered through Likert-scale questions assessing decision confidence, trust in the AV, perceived realism of the scenario, and the usefulness of the interface. Third, qualitative data were obtained through open-ended questions exploring participants’ interpretations of the eHMI cues and their reasoning behind crossing decisions.

5.
Study 2: Video Study with VLM Persona

In the second study, we introduced AI agents to “attend the user study”.
Two critical challenges are: 1) how to construct the agent to have a similar persona as a human participant that affects their decision-making processes, and 2) how to establish a fair comparative study that lets the agent have “the same” experience as its corresponding real participant.
In the following content, we will first explain on how we solve these two major challenges in this “study” and lastly show the results of our VLM studies.

5.1.
VLM Persona Construction

Our goal here is to create an persona to simulate its corresponding user’s decision-making process without letting the participant experience the full study.
To achieve this, we design a human-persona protocol so that the participant only needs to answer a few questionnaires, and then we can craft a persona representative of that participant in the decision-making processes of our target use scenarios.

This intuition is grounded in psychological studies showing that experts can reasonably infer individuals’ decision-making tendencies from questionnaire-based participant data
(
Raoust et al. 2023
;
Aczel et al. 2015
)
.

The construction of a VLM persona in our study had two major procedures:
1) Questionnaire for data collection
to gather information of human participants through a questionnaire, and
2) Crafting persona prompts
to process the collected data into a persona.

5.1.1.
Questionnaire: theoretical grounding and selected factors

Two weeks prior to the field study, we conducted a survey in which the same 20 participants who later took part in the field study completed the questionnaire (Sec.
4.1
).

We first explored what information should be collected from the questionnaire.
We referred to

from

and

The remaining factors, such as social factors (i.e., represented by group size), are not applicable in this context because this study focuses on interactions between a single pedestrian and a vehicle.
In the demographics, this includes the subbranch such as
age

gender
, and so on. In the characteristics, we explored
personality
and
past experience

5.1.2.
Crafting Persona Prompts

With the questionnaire responses from the 20 participants, we crafted persona prompts to build AI agent that serve as proxies for their corresponding participants. Unlike statistical modeling approaches
(
Tinsley and Brown 2000
;
Gelman 2007
)
, which mathematically assign weights based on the extracted parameters from questionnaire data, Our method uses
prompt engineering
to construct descriptive prompts that assign each AI agent its corresponding persona.

One naive approach to crafting the system prompt of an AI agent is to simply list each question and follow it with the human answer. We found that this direct-feed approach is not effective because it results in “frozen behaviors” (e.g., personas that consistently stop). We examined each persona’s self-generated “decision-making reasons” and found that the reasoning was often dominated by terminology:
“I saw the green light indicate safe to cross. But my
neuroticism score
is low, so I chose not to cross.”
This indicated that the model over-indexed on trait descriptors and incorporated them into decision rules in unrealistic ways. This finding is aligned with
(
Li et al. 2025a
;
Huang and Hadfi 2025
)
, which show that table- or rule-based data is not reliable for direct usage in persona construction.

To solve this issue, we first surveyed common practices in LLM/VLM-based persona construction
(
Bommasani 2021
;
Shorinwa et al. 2025
)
, and found that existing persona-design pipelines rely on ad hoc methods
(
Samuel et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
)
and typically follow a
posterior
design process
(
Li et al. 2025a
;
Amin et al. 2025
)
. Building on these
posterior
methods and following the instructions from the OpenAI official site
(
OpenAI 2025a
)
,
we developed an instruction prompt (Listing
1
) that enables the LLM to automatically use the collected questionnaire data to generate a “workable” persona.

To operationalize “workable,” we established three criteria (i.e., minimal requirements):

1) Variance.
A persona should exhibit both intra-persona and inter-persona variability, and its printed decision reasoning should be interpretable.

2) interpretable.
Its printed decision reasoning for each decision should be interpretable.

2) Generalizability.
The prompt should not be tied to a single scenario (e.g., only street-crossing) with over-specializing personas. Instead, they should allow straightforward replication and adaptation by future researchers in other AV-related or pedestrian-interaction contexts.
As for the generalizability, in Item 3 of the following prompt, other researchers can replace the street-crossing scenario with their own research scenarios. The LLM can then reason over the collected data and extract factors relevant to the new scenario.
To achieve these goals, we wrote the prompt with two processing steps, including
filtering
and
transferring
(items 3 and 4 in the dashed list below). The simulation results show that the iterated VLM personas were able to fulfill the above three criteria, demonstrating that such construction is feasible. We acknowledge that this is not an “optimal” construction method, and that future research combining prompt engineering with parameter-weighted approaches to construct more impersonable personas would be a valuable extension. 5.2.
Video Simulator and VLM persona task

The goal here is to establish a fair comparative study that allows the agent to have the same experience as its corresponding real participant.

This is challenging because a persona may decide to stop midway, then such mid-action branching cannot be simulated using the typical pre-shooting method
(
Hoggenmüller et al. 2021
;
Chang et al. 2022
)
, in which experimenters film real-world scenarios with handheld or 360-degree cameras and present them to participants through monitors or VR headsets. Because our focus is on modeling pedestrians’
interaction behavior
, which is a continuous process in which each second’s decision influences subsequent outcomes, using pre-recorded videos with experimenter-driven camera movements risks constraining the available decision space and unintentionally biasing the VLM personas’ choices.

To address this, we drew inspiration from choice-based video games
(
Booth 2024
)
, where multiple narrative paths emerge based on user decisions. We applied a similar logic to build an interactive video simulator tailored for VLM personas. However, expecting a VLM persona to reproduce
all possible pedestrian interaction behaviors
and enact every scenario using only pre-recorded videos is both impractical. To address this limitation, we used a
discretization
approach.

The first step was to discretize space and time. Five positions were marked on the ground at 0.8-meter intervals, covering a distance of 3.2 meters, which was the same length used in the field study. The AV’s approach time was the same, fixed at 8 seconds. At one-second intervals, the VLM was prompted to make a decision, resulting in eight discrete time points and five spatial points. After finalizing this spatial-temporal grid, we recorded the videos. For each of the six experimental conditions, we filmed five videos from each of the five spatial positions (see
Figure 5
, top), yielding 30 videos (six conditions * five spatial positions). All key parameters, such as driving distance, speed, and timing were held constant across conditions to ensure comparability.

These videos were then segmented into one-second clips. The simulation system was designed so that, at every one-second interval, it queried the VLM persona: “What is your next plan?” The VLM chose either to move forward one spatial interval or to stop. If it decided to move forward from point A, the system transitioned to the video clip recorded at point B after one second; otherwise, it continued displaying the video at point A. The simulation output included a structured record of decision sequences, spatial positions, and time steps (
Figure 5
). Finally, we generated 120 simulated trajectories, matching the number of videos recorded in the field study. A complete example of a fully simulated crossing task is provided in Appendix
B
, detailing every decision and its rationale (printed reason).

Analysis of Simulated questionnaire results

To generate answers to the questionnaire and interview, we simulated “memory” for our VLM persona. After each simulated street-crossing trial, the system built a complete “memory" of that interaction (See “All Position Status Summary” and “Combined video saved to …” in the end of Appendix
B
). This memory was a timeline of combined videos merged by all key video segments paired with the VLM persona’s decisions at each step, which offers a thorough retrospective on their past decisions and experiences.

Once the simulation was complete, the VLM persona was asked to answer the same questionnaire that our human participants had answered. We show an exemplary thinking procedure of one specific VLM persona (ID = G3x03g,

6.
Behavior Analysis: the comparison of trajectory

6.1.
Metric 1: Crossing time

In traditional evaluations of the “crossing the street” task, two primary behavioral metrics are typically analyzed: the final decision (i.e., whether the participant chooses to cross or not)
(
Chang et al. 2022
)
and the decision time (i.e., the time taken to reach that decision)
(
Chang et al. 2017
)
. Our study adopts these same metrics. However, due to the real-world setting of our field study, participants are required to eventually cross the street before or after the AV passes, resulting in all “final decision” results being “yes”. Given this constraint, we focus on crossing time as the behavioral metric. This is defined as the specific timing at which the participant initiates their crossing behavior on the road edge.

Figure 7
.
Left: Custom-built UI for annotating human trajectories in field study videos. Right: The generated trajectory shows timestamps of when participants passed each of the five critical points (corresponding to the video recording points used in the VLM simulation).
Left: Custom-built UI for annotating human trajectories in field study videos. Right: The generated trajectory shows timestamps of when participants passed each of the five critical points (corresponding to the video recording points used in the VLM simulation).

The raw data from our field study contains 120 videos of varying lengths, with each of the 20 participants completing six crossing tasks. To enable a valid comparison between human behavior and VLM personas, we aligned the format and temporal structure of the human data with that of the VLM simulation, which is based on discrete one-second intervals and fixed video durations.

To extract precise behavioral information, we developed a custom UI for annotating the human participants’ movement in the videos (
Figure 7
, left).
The extraction consists of five steps:
1) Pose Detection & Tracking: We use YOLOv8 models
(
Jocher et al. 2023
)
to detect human poses and track multiple people with unique IDs across video frames; 2) Trajectory Visualization: We calculate and display movement paths using hip center points as references, featuring a configurable trajectory history with color-coded lines and a real-time position overlay on videos; 3) Manual Trajectory Merging: We provide tools to merge selected trajectory IDs when tracking gets disrupted by occlusion or people blocking each other, creating consolidated movement paths; 4) Cross Mark Annotation: We provide manual marking on video frames to label green cross marks. In the videos, five physical green crosses are placed on the ground and are clearly visible. These markers correspond to the same five reference points used in the VLM video recordings; 5) Keyframe Annotation: We enable start and end keyframe annotations to define crossing time windows, allowing us to extract detailed timestamps of when participants pass each critical point, including the exact crossing time. Each video is annotated by one author and then validated by a second author. The annotation process for each video takes approximately one minute, not including the time required for Pose Detection & Tracking processing.

In the graph (
Figure 7
, right), the blue line maps position over time (x-axis is time), and the purple line maps position over video frames. The y-axis on the right represents the distance between each of the five reference points. Variations in the slope of the curve reflect acceleration or deceleration in the participant’s walking speed. Through this process, each raw video was transformed into a “timeframe + position” data format that mirrors the structure of the VLM simulation output.

We compared the average crossing times between real human participants and VLM personas across all experimental conditions. As the data did not follow a normal distribution, we employed a non-parametric three-factor ANOVA for the analysis. The human group has an average crossing time of 5.07 seconds (SD = 1.67 seconds), while the VLM group averaged 5.25 seconds (SD = 0.72 seconds).
This difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.8465,
Figure 8
). That is, quantitatively, VLM can generate high similarity crossing behavior as a real human.
The multi-factor ANOVA also examined the main effects and interactions among the experimental variables. The other two main effects of eHMI (p = 0.5175, F = 0.6607) and AV type (p = 0.0502, F = 3.8741) were not statistically significant. Similarly, the two-way interactions between eHMI and AV (p = 0.1922, F = 1.6610), and between eHMI and Group (p = 0.2632, F = 1.3428), did not reach significance. However, a significant interaction was found between AV type and Group (p = 0.0032, F = 8.9002), as well as a significant three-way interaction among eHMI, AV type, and Group (p = 0.0168, F = 4.1588).

6.3.
Subset Analysis: Effect of group size on similarity

We clarify that all our comparisons are between-subjects. From a theoretical standpoint, an LLM is an aggregate model trained on population-level data
(
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Wang et al. 2025b
)
, indicating that a VLM persona is unlikely to capture the high-variance behavior of a single individual. Conversely, we hypothesize it should be highly effective at mimicking the central tendency of a group, where individual noise is averaged out.

.

Our data empirically supports this. We conducted subset analyses by comparing the VLM persona cohort to human subsets of N=5 (
Figure 11
(first two rows)), N=10 (
Figure 11
(the third row)), and N=20 (
Figure 8
). We found that with small, high-variance samples (e.g., N=5), statistically significant differences between the human and VLM groups sometimes appeared. However, as the sample size increased, these differences diminished. The p-value increased, moving from statistical significance (p < 0.05 when N = 5 (top right and middle left group in
Figure 11

This leads to our conclusion that the VLM’s reliability to mimic human tendencies strengthens as the comparison group size increases. The VLM effectively captures the "average" human response, which only becomes statistically apparent when compared against a sufficiently large and stable human cohort. These can serve as the guidelines for other researchers using VLM personas for their simulation, that
1) VLM personas might be poor proxies for individuals or small groups. 2) VLM personas are better proxies for groups or populations for human-AV interaction.

7.2.
Thematic analysis for both human and VLM

Previous research has conducted various textual analyses to compare humans and LLMs, aspects such as response length, word usage, tone, writing style
(

)
, and human-likeness (e.g., through Turing tests
(

)
), showing that LLMs can generate human-comparable text. In contrast, our study investigates whether VLMs are capable of reasoning with a similar depth and breadth as humans. That is, we explore whether VLMs focus on similar conceptual aspects, enabling us to extract key insights to support further AV-pedestrian interaction. To analyze the open-ended responses (from Q5 to Q7), we used thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2021
)
and compared the themes (key findings) for the all three questions from both VLM-generated and human-generated responses.

VLM responses:

Comparative Insights:
The themes identified from the VLM responses (Findings 1-4) closely align with several core themes observed in the human data (Findings 1, 2, 3, and 5). However, the VLM responses lacked certain subtleties present in the human interpretations. They did not capture divergent interpretations expressed by a minority of participants (Finding 4), nor did they reflect user preferences related to eHMI choices (Finding 6). Finding 4 shows that while VLMs can replicate the consensus-level reasoning found in human analysis, they struggle to surface minority viewpoints. Finding 6 shows that VLMs provide direct answers to a given query, while human responses often incorporate wider contexts and associative thoughts that extend beyond the initial question. That is, although VLM-generated insights are directionally consistent with human interpretations, they offer narrower and shallower results.

8.
Interview with HCI researcher

To answer the RQ2, we conducted interviews with five human-automation researchers whose work requires field studies. We chose the sample size N=5 to prioritize quality over quantity. We decided to conduct interviews after the experiments and data comparison to establish a necessary performance baseline for VLM persona simulation. Each interview lasted about one hour and included a concept introduction, a VLM simulation demonstration, a review of the comparison results, and a structured discussion with senior researchers. This aligns with qualitative principles
(
Nielsen and Landauer 1993
)
, where a small expert sample with deep engagement is sufficient to reach thematic saturation.

Among the five interviewee, two are assistant professors focused on robotics, two are associate professors researching remote tele-operation and social interaction, and one is a senior researcher with over 10 years of experience in human-robot interaction. Our interview contains three parts. We first asked about the role of field studies in their research and the difficulties they encountered. Second, we introduced our concept, demonstrated the simulation, and showed comparison results. We then asked for their general impressions and whether they would consider using VLM personas to support their field studies. Finally, we asked how they might integrate VLM personas into their own research.

8.1.
Field study importance and difficulties

All five researchers emphasized the necessity of field studies. They mentioned two key reasons:
1) Understanding real-world problems
: Three interviewees mentioned that field studies help them better understand human behavior and test interactions in real-world settings.
2) Limitations of lab studies
: Two noted that lab-based experiments often lack authenticity, making it harder to evaluate natural human-robot interactions.

Regarding challenges they faced in their past research, we summarized four main categories:
1) Environmental constraints:
Finding suitable controllable locations is difficult. Unpredictable human behavior also adds complexity.
2) Participant recruitment:
It’s often hard to recruit the target population. Moreover, unexpected behavior by participants can pose safety risks.
3) Hardware and technical issues:
Their field studies frequently suffer from unreliable hardware, such as poorly integrated systems, connectivity problems or technical bottlenecks.
4) Resource demands:
Field studies demand more time, stricter safety standards, and stronger coordination than lab studies, requiring detailed planning and effective knowledge sharing.

8.2.
Willingness to use VLMs and key concerns

All interviewees expressed interest in the potential of VLMs to simulate human behavior, noting that VLMs reflect how humans process information. We asked about their willingness to adopt VLM personas to support their field study. The result is 2 researchers said "yes", 2 said "no", and 1 remained undecided (see
Figure 1
, red part on the right). They raised two major concerns. The first concern is
stability of VLM
. Several researchers worried about the consistency of VLM outputs. If VLM behavior is unreliable, it undermines the credibility of the simulation. Human behavior also contains randomness. Further research to explore how randomness in both real human and VLM personas (i.e., temperature
(
Pavlovic and Poesio 2024
)
) influences the similarity in comparison is important. The second concern is
applicability to special populations
. While VLM personas may reasonably simulate the average person, they may not represent specific groups (e.g., children) accurately. Because children’s stride speed and perception of the world are different. Meanwhile, proving their usability in these contexts is difficult since it is hard to pass the ethical review to call children in a field study for comparison.

8.3.
Potential Use Cases

Even the researchers who previously answered "no" in a field study expressed openness to using VLMs as supplementary tools in other steps within the overall research process.

1) Formative or Pilot study tool
: Three interviewees mentioned that using VLM personas as a preliminary study tool could be beneficial, as long as the results are
not over-interpreted
. As a formative study tool, “
this approach can provide an initial understanding of interactions and assist in designing future experiments with real human participants
”. As a pilot study tool, “
it helps improve simulations before conducting field studies, allowing researchers to be better prepared
”.

2) Application for under-represented groups
: One interviewee suggested that the approach could be useful for studying hard-to-reach groups (e.g., individuals of extreme age
(
Hensch et al. 2021
)
or with disabilities). However, before applying the method in such contexts, the interviewees emphasize that “
it must be demonstrated that VLM personas can provide reliable simulations in the specific situation
”. At a minimum, a
clear user guideline
should outline all known limitations.

3) Large-scale simulation
: Other interviewees emphasized the potential of using VLM personas to simulate a
large and diverse user base
. This could be valuable for preparing field studies by exploring a wide range of possible users. It could also support pretraining parts of systems that require behavioral data. However, both interviewees warned “
never blindly increasing the sample size in field studies by mixing real and synthetic data, as this can be difficult to justify
”.

9.
Discussion

In this discussion, we first conclude our results into three actionable guidelines (Sec.
9.1
) for researchers utilizing VLM personas in evaluation simulations, highlighting critical measures and potential pitfalls. We then integrate these guidelines with expert insights from five field researchers to propose a research process diagram (Sec.
9.2
). This diagram (
Figure 13
) describes how VLM personas can be effectively incorporated at various stages of the research workflow. Finally, we expand our findings from the Human-AV domain to broader embodied interaction (Sec.
9.3
). Although “embodied” in HCI usually refers to technologies physically integrated with the human body, in this project we use “embodied” specifically to describe the context of simulated personas. By analyzing the input-output modalities within embodiment literature, we identify the necessary technical steps to expand VLM persona simulations to broader domains.

9.1.
Usage guidelines from the comparison result

Our behavioral analysis indicates that while VLM personas successfully replicate the central tendency of human actions (showing no statistically significant differences in mean and median), they fail to capture the full spectrum of human variability. The VLM data exhibits significantly lower standard deviations, effectively representing only a “safe” subset of the broader human behavioral distribution. Furthermore, our subset analysis demonstrates that small sample sizes exacerbate sampling bias.
Guideline 1: VLMs are reliable for predicting general behavioral trends, but not for modeling extreme or highly variable human actions. A sufficient volume of personas is required to mitigate sampling bias and stabilize the distribution.

A distinct pattern emerged in the questionnaire data depending on the reference point. When VLM personas assessed intrinsic states (e.g., self-confidence, genuineness), they exhibited the “hyper-confidence” phenomenon often observed in LLMs
(
Sun et al. 2025
;
Davidson et al. 2024
)
, leading to inflated ratings. Conversely, when evaluations were directed outward at external objects (e.g., trust in the AV, eHMI utility), the VLM ratings aligned closely with human ground truth.
Guideline 2: Avoid asking VLM personas to rate their own internal states. To minimize calibration errors, framing should focus on evaluating external objects or system features.

In the interview simulation by VLM persona shows that the VLM acts as a “consensus engine,” producing logical but homogeneous text responses. It failed to capture the minority perspectives and unexpected interpretations found in our human dataset.
Guideline 3: Use VLM personas to summarize standard user expectations, but do not rely on them to uncover overlooked usability issues. Human participants remain essential for capturing diverse, non-standard, or creative interpretations.

9.2.
Cross discussion between comparison result and HCI researcher interview

The typical research process is generally divided into four stages
(
Lazar et al. 2017
)
: initial exploratory research, iterative system design, pilot study, and formal field study. We integrate guidelines from our comparative analysis with use cases from follow-up interviews to illustrate how VLM personas can support each stage (
Figure 13
).

In the exploration stage (
Figure 13
, column 1), VLMs have shown the capacity to generate general insights, allowing researchers to quickly form a preliminary direction in unfamiliar domains. VLM’s ability to synthesize widely accepted knowledge can help accelerate early-stage ideation.

During the formative study stage (
Figure 13
, column 2), VLM personas show potential but have key limitations. They struggle to capture ambiguous or minority perspectives (Guideline 3) and to reflect first-person perceptions (Guideline 2). Thus, while feasible at this stage
(
O’Leary et al. 2018
;
Miaskiewicz and Kozar 2011
)
, real human feedback remains necessary to ensure adequate coverage and depth.

In the pilot study stage (
Figure 13
, column 3), VLM personas are useful for preliminary testing. They can simulate general behavior to identify procedural flaws, assess clarity of instructions, and ensure the study setup aligns with user expectations. This pre-testing improves efficiency before involving human participants.

In the field study stage (
Figure 13
, column 4), real human data remains irreplaceable. However, VLM personas can supplement studies (
Figure 13
, column 5) by expanding participant diversity, provided their alignment with real data is carefully validated. They may also support preliminary hypothesis testing or scenario exploration to inform subsequent empirical research.

9.3.
Extension to Broader Embodied Interaction

Our method can be extended beyond the specific case of AV-human interaction to the wider domain of embodied interaction
(
Fung et al. 2025
;
Duan et al. 2022
)
. Our primary focus on AVs was driven by necessity (the high costs and safety risks associated with field studies in traffic environments). The proposed evaluation framework with VLM personas offers significant value for other domains where distinct constraints exist. For example, even in scenarios with lower physical risk, such as indoor human-robot interaction (e.g., household robot
(
Shiokawa et al. 2025
)
), evaluating with real human participants presents challenges. These include recruitment difficulties for underrepresented populations (e.g., individuals with disabilities)
(
Banas et al. 2019
)
, designing a user study to overcome VR headsets’ uncomfortableness and motion sickness
(
Koohestani et al. 2019
;
Chattha et al. 2020
)
, and the logistical complexity of analyzing cognitive states in multi-party coordination tasks (e.g., disaster response)
(
Chew and Jayarathne 2024
;
Kantharaju and Pelachaud 2021
)
.

To generalize the VLM persona method for these broader embodied tasks, we believe the future research needs to address two main technical categories
(
Wilson 2003
)
: input modalities
(
Vatavu 2024
)
and output fidelity
(
Grubert et al. 2015
)
. For example, in our field study of AV-pedestrian interaction, the LLM takes vision input and generates human mimicry behaviors.
Regarding the input modalities, we call the multimodal input research to explore pipelines that enable VLMs to acquire information through diverse senses, mimicking human perception.
In this study, we processed visual inputs by segmenting 30 videos into 150 clips, allowing the VLM to build a unique “interaction memory” based on step-by-step decisions. However, broader embodiment requires extending this beyond vision. Future simulators may integrate audio
(
Tao et al. 2025
)
, haptic
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
, olfactory
(
Maggioni et al. 2020
)
, or even direct VR visual feeds
(
Zhong et al. 2025
)
, effectively giving the VLM the sensory capacity to perceive specific physical environments just as a human participant would.
Regarding the output fidelity, we call for research to validate the human-likeness of the VLM’s generated data across different dimensions.
For example, in our study, we compared generated behavior (i.e., locomotion
(
Wang and Tsagarakis 2024
)
) and subjective perceptions toward AV. However, embodied interaction involves complex physiological and psychological states. Future work should investigate the VLM’s ability to simulate: emotional responses (e.g., Ekman’s model
(
Ekman et al. 1999
)
), cognitive load (e.g., NASA-TLX scores
(
Hart and Staveland 1988
)
), and physiological signals (e.g., heart rate, EEG, ECG patterns)
(
Chiossi et al. 2024b
;
Chiossi et al. 2023
;
Chiossi et al. 2024a
)
. Validating these outputs would require new comparative studies between human physiological data and VLM persona generated results.

Besides technical feasibility, we need the application-oriented exploration to identify the specific domains where VLM personas could uniquely benefit embodied interaction research. Here are three example applications. The first is smart city robotics. Unlike AVs on roadways, urban service robots often navigate sidewalks and shared public spaces. VLM simulations could safely test these frequent and socially complex interactions. The second is augmented reality gaming. Applications like
Pokémon Go
require physical movement and spatial interaction that are difficult to replicate in static lab settings. VLM personas could simulate user behavior in these unbounded real-world environments. The third is a high-risk cognitive investigation. In scenarios such as disaster preparedness, real-world testing is both dangerous and ethically fraught. VLM personas offer a method to study embodied cognition and adaptive decision-making under pressure without risking human lives. These studies require participants to engage in real-time spatial sensing and behavioral and emotional responses. They are typically large-scale, focus on societal rather than individual systems, and rely on extensive physical infrastructure, real-time sensing technologies, and a significant number of participants. The demonstrated similarity between VLM and human responses in certain aspects validates the use of VLM personas for supporting the research. For example, it streamlines formative and pilot studies by minimizing reliance on human participants without compromising the study quality.

9.4.
Limitation and Future work

This study used GPT-4.1. That is, the findings and guidelines may evolve as model capabilities improve. For example, unlike humans, who rely on binocular vision for depth perception, the current VLM lacks this ability. Future advancements in binocular or multi-view perception could enhance performance on tasks requiring spatial reasoning. Opportunities remain to improve simulated evaluation. While this study used real-world video stimuli, it is unclear whether alternative visual formats (e.g., VR models or animations) would yield comparable results. If validated, VLM personas could further simplify the pipeline by reducing the need for on-site filming.

One important limitation is the prompt of the construction VLM persona.
Our extraction process relies entirely on LLM reasoning driven by the designed prompts, making the modeling result highly rely on the model performance. While feasibility can be examined through simulation reasoning traces, the overall interpretability remains limited. An important direction for future work is to develop more principled and transparent modeling approaches that improve both variability and interpretability. Building on our findings, future work could analyze reasoning traces (Appendix B) to inform parameter-weighted model structures or to refine prompt engineering (e.g., by modifying Listing 1).

Constructing robust AI personas remains an unresolved challenge in NLP due to two main issues. First, there is no standardized design framework or consensus on an “optimal” methodology
(
Tseng et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
;
Luz de Araujo and Roth 2025
)
. Second, robust benchmarks are lacking to validate whether a persona faithfully replicates a target profile across dimensions
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
. Prior work shows that persona-shaping prompts cannot fully override models’ internal preferences
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
. Although alignment with general human values and social norms is essential for safety, it may reduce output diversity and constrain behaviors that conflict with safety policies
(
Editors 2024
;
OpenAI 2025b
)
. This limitation can affect the diversity and fidelity of LLMs in role-playing tasks such as those in our study.

10.
Conclusion

VLM personas can partially simulate human behavior in embodied tasks. Our study extends this line of work by exploring how this capability can support field studies in HCI research. To figure out the quality of using VLM personas as a tool (either in pilot studies or to augment field studies), we
conducted two parallel experiments
: 1) a field study with human participants and 2) a video study using VLM personas, enabling both quantitative and qualitative comparison. The findings reveal no significant overall differences, although there is reduced variability and diversity.

We identified
three key guidelines
that outline the aspects under which VLM persona responses align with those of real humans.
1) Use VLM-generated data cautiously
, as it can reflect human responses but only captures a subset due to reduced variability.
2) Frame evaluations in third-person
, since VLMs tend to exaggerate when responding from a first-person perspective.
3) Match VLM use to research goals
as they seem suitable for broad exploration of new topics, but less effective for uncovering nuanced or overlooked issues in familiar domains. To explore broader applicability, we interviewed HCI researchers and developed a
research process diagram
showing how VLM personas can be integrated into the HCI workflow.
