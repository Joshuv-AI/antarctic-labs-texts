Percutaneous Ultrasonography-guided Release of the Transverse Carpal Ligament versus Open Surgery for Carpal Tunnel Syndrome: A Prospective Comparative Study
Authors: Manuel Castro-Menéndez, Patricia Balvís-Balvís, Elena Perez-Alfonso, Ines Oiartzabal, Miguel Cela-Lopez, Diego M. Dominguez Prado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To compare clinical outcomes between ultrasonography-guided percutaneous and open transverse carpal ligament (TCL) release for carpal tunnel syndrome at short-term follow-up.Methods:A prospective cohort study was conducted in 200 patients with CTS (100 per group) treated with either ultrasonography-guided percutaneous or open TCL release. Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire (BCTQ) scores, grip strength (JAMAR dynamometer), postoperative symptoms, complications, and satisfaction were evaluated preoperatively, at 6 weeks, and at 3 months. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was assessed. A Joinpoint model analyzed the effect of requiring technically demanding skills for the percutaneous group.Results:Both groups showed significant improvement in BCTQ scores (P < 0.001). The percutaneous group, despite worse preoperative scores, demonstrated significantly greater improvement in total BCTQ and its subscales (P < 0.001), even after relative MCID adjustment. Grip strength recovery was superior in the percutaneous group at 3 months (P < 0.001). Postoperative scar tenderness, pillar pain, and residual paresthesias were significantly less frequent in this group (P < 0.05). Complication rates were low and comparable. One case of transient neuritis occurred in the percutaneous group. Patient satisfaction exceeded 97% in both groups.Conclusion:Ultrasonography-guided percutaneous flexor retinaculum release offers superior early clinical outcomes and faster functional recovery compared with open surgery, with a comparable safety profile. These findings support its adoption in experienced hand surgery centers with ultrasonography capabilities.

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most common compressive neuropathy of the upper extremity.1 Conventional open surgery involving a short palmar incision to section the carpal ligament (TCL) is effective but may result in scar pain, pillar pain, residual paresthesias, and delayed functional recovery.2-5 Minimally invasive approaches such as ultrasonography-guided percutaneous release of the TCL have been developed to mitigate these sequelae by enabling real-time visualization of neurovascular structures with high-resolution ultrasonography. Although efficacy has been demonstrated, concerns persist regarding safety and reproducibility.3-10 This study aims to determine whether the percutaneous ultrasonography-guided technique offers clinical and functional advantages over open release.

The aim of this study was to evaluate whether ultrasonography-guided percutaneous release of the transverse carpal ligament provides superior clinical and functional outcomes compared with open surgery at short-term follow-up.

Methods

We conducted a prospective observational cohort study including 200 patients with CTS treated between January 2021 and January 2024. Allocation was done alternately (nonrandomized), open surgery (n = 100), or ultrasonography-guided percutaneous release (n = 100), in consecutive order of surgical scheduling. Follow-up data were collected by a research team member independent of the operating surgeons to minimize observer bias. Inclusion criteria were patients aged 18 years or older, diagnosed with CTS on clinical and neurophysiologic grounds, and who underwent surgical treatment between January 2021 and January 2024. All patients gave written informed consent to participate and for their anonymized data to be published. Exclusion criteria were patients who had previously undergone surgery for the same pathology in the same hand, patients with another associated pathology in the same hand, and patients who were incapacitated or unable to follow the study protocol (Figure 1).

Flow diagram illustrating the design and progression of the study.

The primary outcome measure was functional status and symptom severity as assessed by the Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire (BCTQ).11 Secondary outcomes included recovery of grip strength, postoperative symptom profile, and complication rates.

BCTQ scores (total, symptom severity scale [SSS], and functional status scale [FSS])11 and grip strength (measured with a JAMAR dynamometer) were recorded preoperatively, at 6 weeks, and at 3 months. Postoperative symptoms—including scar pain, paresthesias, and pillar pain—were registered along with complications.

Follow-up assessments were conducted by a research team member independent of the operating surgeons to minimize observer bias.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size was calculated based on the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the BCTQ,12 requiring at least 96 patients per group (α = 0.05, β < 0.2). This sample size was also validated for our target population of 450,000 individuals, assuming a 10% margin of error and a 95% confidence level. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR). Normality was assessed with Kolmogorov-Smirnov or Shapiro-Wilk tests. Group comparisons were done using chi-square for categorical variables and t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. Repeated measures were analyzed with Friedman test and Bonferroni correction. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen d or Hedge correction. Analyses were done with SPSS v29.

Surgical Techniques

All procedures were done by the same surgeons (open by P.B.B., percutaneous by M.C.M.). Both had level 3 expertise in hand surgery13; Surgeon II additionally had extensive experience in ultrasonography-guided hand procedures, with prior cadaveric training. All procedures were done in the operating room under standard aseptic conditions. No prophylactic antibiotics were administered. Patients were instructed to shower preoperatively using chlorhexidine soap, and no hair removal was done. Local anesthesia was used in all cases, consisting of 8 to 10 mL of 2% mepivacaine with 1 mL of bicarbonate infiltrated around the median nerve. Ulnar nerve block was occasionally added, particularly in the open surgery group. A pneumatic tourniquet was used only in open surgery cases, inflated immediately before the incision.

In both groups, primary skin closure was achieved using 3-0 Monocryl interrupted sutures without drain placement. A compressive dressing (Möndall technique) was applied and maintained for 3 to 4 days. All patients received the same postoperative analgesic regimen: paracetamol 1 g every 8 hours and ibuprofen 600 mg every 8 hours, prescribed as a fixed regimen for the first 5 postoperative days, unless contraindicated.

Patients initiated finger mobilization within 24 to 48 hours. Sutures were removed at the 2-week follow-up visit, after which patients were instructed to gradually resume daily activities and progressively increase load-bearing until they felt comfortable returning to full function and work. No immobilization splints were used, and physical therapy was not routinely prescribed. The initial dressing was replaced by a light protective covering to facilitate unrestricted active hand motion.-Open release: A short palmar incision (<4 cm) was made along the radial axis of the fourth finger, distal wrist flexion crease extending to the Kaplan line (Figure 2). Dissection proceeded to the palmar fascia and TCL, which was divided under direct vision. Hemostasis was done before skin closure with monocryl 3/0 sutures.-Ultrasonography-guided percutaneous release: A Kemis H3VR knife (Newclip, France) (Figure 3A) and a Sonosite II ultrasonography machine with a 12 MHz probe were used. Before the procedure, a systematic preoperative ultrasonography examination was done to identify the course of the median nerve, its palmar cutaneous sensory branch, and the thenar motor branch, as well as to detect any relevant anatomic variants. The ulnar artery, ulnar nerve, superficial palmar arch, and deep palmar arch were also identified and traced sonographically (Figure 3B). The distal edge of the trapezium and the hook of hamate were marked to determine the distal flexor retinaculum edge.14 An 8 to 10 mm incision was made on the ulnar side of the palmaris longus tendon proximal to the wrist crease. An ultrasonography-guided curved mosquito forceps is initially introduced above and below the flexor retinaculum to aid section. The Kemis H3 knife (Newclip, France) was introduced, with ultrasonography visualization ensuring identification and protection of the median nerve and ulnar artery. The flexor retinaculum was sectioned under longitudinal and transverse ultrasonography guidance (Figure 4). Color Doppler ultrasonography was used to avoid vascular injury. Complete flexor retinaculum sectioning was confirmed with ultrasonography-guided forceps (Figure 5).

Results

The percutaneous group, despite having higher preoperative BCTQ scores, demonstrated significantly greater improvement in total score and subscales (SSS and FSS) at both follow-up points (P < 0.001), including after adjustment with relative MCID (Figure 6, Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

This prospective comparative study demonstrates that ultrasonography-guided percutaneous release of the flexor retinaculum is a safe and effective alternative to open surgery for the treatment of carpal tunnel syndrome. Despite the percutaneous group starting from a more functionally and symptomatically impaired baseline—as evidenced by higher preoperative Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire11 (BCTQ) scores—patients undergoing this technique experienced greater improvement in both symptom severity and functional status. This suggests a strong therapeutic capacity for the percutaneous approach, particularly in more severely affected cases.

Although both groups surpassed the thresholds for the minimal clinically important difference (MCID),12 the absolute and relative MCID analyses favored the percutaneous technique, confirming not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful superiority. The relative MCID,16 in particular, adjusted for baseline differences and strengthened the argument that the percutaneous approach offers more substantial benefits, especially in function. This may be of particular importance in clinical practice, where patients with more severe presentations often require greater gains to achieve acceptable outcomes.

De La Fuente et al17 studied a cohort of 89 patients 47 of whom underwent percutaneous surgery and 42 standard open surgery. They concluded that although both techniques provided comparable symptomatic relief, patients treated with the percutaneous approach experienced less pain at 3 months of follow-up and improved hand function, assessed using the BCTQ functional subscale (BCTQ-FSS), with statistically significant differences. However, this advantage was no longer evident when applying the MCID criterion. This study was the first to compare BCTQ MCID between the two techniques using the approach described by Kleermaeker et al16

Although both techniques resulted in notable clinical improvement from baseline, residual paresthesias were more frequently observed in the open surgery group. This finding is most likely explained by the markedly higher proportion of patients with very severe electrophysiologic compromise at baseline in this cohort (37% vs. 3% in the percutaneous group; Table 1) because advanced neurophysiologic impairment is known to be associated with slower and less complete symptom resolution after decompression. In addition, greater intraoperative manipulation and traction of the median nerve inherent to open dissection may have further contributed because direct nerve handling can produce a transient neurapraxic response even in the absence of frank structural injury.

Grip strength recovery was also more rapid and robust in the percutaneous group. At the 3-month mark, these patients had markedly better dynamometric performance, which is likely to translate into earlier return to daily activities and work. Predictive factors associated with better strength outcomes included male sex, absence of hypertension or fibromyalgia, and engagement in physically demanding occupations—findings that are consistent with existing literature and may assist surgeons in tailoring postoperative expectations.18

Postoperative morbidity was lower with the percutaneous technique. Patients reported less scar tenderness and a markedly lower incidence of pillar pain. Although pillar pain is often considered a consequence of TCL transection rather than the surgical approach itself, our data suggest that the minimally invasive nature of the percutaneous technique—combined with a more proximal incision and reduced soft-tissue trauma—may mitigate this symptom. The reduction in scar-related complaints further supports this interpretation, aligning with previous reports that favor minimally invasive techniques in reducing early postoperative discomfort.17,19

The overall complication rate was low in both groups. No major neurovascular injuries, revision surgeries, or technique conversions occurred. One case of transient median neuritis was observed in the percutaneous group, potentially related to limited working space in the presence of very severe compression. Importantly, this case resolved without sequelae. A slightly higher number of superficial wound infections were observed in the percutaneous group, which may have been related to the use of nonsterile ultrasonography gel. For subsequent cases, we changed to 2% aqueous chlorhexidine in this procedure, with no additional infections.

Sociodemographic and clinical variables also influenced outcomes. Male patients achieved better results in the open surgery group; however, this sex-based disparity was less evident in the percutaneous group, suggesting a more uniform functional recovery. Of particular note, diabetic patients experienced superior functional improvement after percutaneous release, in contrast to concerns about impaired healing or neuropathic progression in this population.20 These findings support the hypothesis that reduced tissue trauma and shorter incision length may benefit patients with systemic comorbidities. Similarly, patients with fibromyalgia showed poorer outcomes after open release but not with percutaneous surgery, a result that aligns with the known hypersensitivity and pain amplification seen in this condition.21 Thus, ultrasonography-guided percutaneous release may be especially suitable for patients with central sensitization syndromes.

Patient satisfaction was uniformly high in both groups, exceeding 97%, and no notable differences were found. However, the absence of formal evaluation of variables such as cosmetic satisfaction, time to return to work, or perceived recovery speed may have limited the detection of subtler differences. In practice, the quicker resolution of symptoms, faster strength recovery, and reduced scar pain in the percutaneous group are likely to contribute to improved patient-perceived outcomes, even when standard satisfaction metrics appear similar.

The adoption of this technique, however, requires adequate preparation. In our unit, the structured learning pathway comprised three sequential stages: (1) attendance at dedicated musculoskeletal ultrasonography training courses, (2) performance of ultrasonography-guided injections and peripheral nerve blocks to develop real-time instrument-tracking skills, and (3) a focused cadaveric simulation workshop before the first supervised clinical case. Based on this experience, and although no neurovascular complications were encountered in our series, we estimate the effect of requiring technically demanding skills for this technique to be approximately 30 cases.10 We therefore strongly recommend that surgeons complete a formal structured training program—including cadaveric workshops and proctored clinical cases—before independent practice. Furthermore, it is imperative to perform a systematic preoperative ultrasonography examination of the neurovascular structures within and around the carpal tunnel to minimize the risk of iatrogenic injury. In our series, preoperative ultrasonography examination of the percutaneous group identified one patient with a bifid median nerve associated with a persistent median artery occupying virtually the entire carpal canal. Given the high risk of neurovascular injury inherent to this anatomic configuration, a decision was made to perform open surgery instead; this patient was consequently excluded from the study. In none of the 100 percutaneous cases was a Lanz type II thenar motor branch (transligamentary variant)22 identified on preoperative ultrasonography. This systematic anatomic assessment before skin incision is a fundamental component of the technique and should be considered mandatory.

This study is not without limitations. First, follow-up was limited to 3 months, which precludes definitive conclusions about the long-term durability of the observed differences. Although early recovery is critical, particularly in working-age populations, additional studies with longer follow-up are needed to determine whether functional and symptomatic advantages persist over time. Second, despite the use of MCID-relative analysis,17 baseline differences between groups—especially the worse initial status in the percutaneous group—may have influenced the magnitude of improvement observed. Third, the study did not assess time to return to work, cosmetic outcomes, patient-reported pain intensity, or the need for postoperative analgesia, all of which are relevant factors in evaluating minimally invasive procedures. Furthermore, the study did not assess procedural cost or surgical duration, both of which are critical factors in the decision-making process when adopting a new technology. The percutaneous technique requires specific disposable instrumentation (the H3 knife system) and dedicated ultrasonography equipment, which entail additional costs compared with open surgery. Future cost-effectiveness analyses and time-motion studies are needed to fully characterize the economic effect of adopting this technique. Finally, ultrasonography measurements of TCL thickness were not done. Given that TCL hypertrophy has been associated with compression severity and technical difficulty in percutaneous procedures, its inclusion could have provided valuable anatomic correlations with outcomes.

Despite these limitations, this study provides robust prospective evidence supporting the use of ultrasonography-guided percutaneous carpal tunnel release in routine practice. The technique offers notable clinical advantages—greater symptom relief, faster strength recovery, and fewer postoperative complaints—without increased complication risk. Its safety profile and favorable results across diverse patient subgroups make it a compelling option, particularly in centers with ultrasonographic expertise.

Future research should focus on long-term outcome persistence, health economics, patient-centered metrics, and anatomic predictors of technical success to further define the role of this technique in modern hand surgery.
