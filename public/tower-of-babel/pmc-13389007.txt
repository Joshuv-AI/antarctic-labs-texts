Selective lignin conversion via flow photocatalysis for vanillin and bioplasticizers production
Authors: Xavier Marset, Salvador Montilla-Verdú, Francisco J. Pastor, Elio Rico, Jaume Gomez-Caturla, Mario Miranda-Pinzon, Carlos Lazaro-Hdez, Stefania Bertella, Manuel A. Ortuño, Rafael Balart, Ulrich Aschauer, Néstor Guijarro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lignin valorization remains a roadblock in the development of green biorefineries. Currently, depolymerization strategies predominantly depend on energy- and resource-intensive thermochemical methods, while potential applications of the resulting bio-based products are often overlooked. Here, we show a scalable photocatalytic flow reactor for lignin deconstruction that operates at ambient conditions, along with a downstream process that yields commercially viable products at gram scale. An anthraquinone-based photocatalytic platform, when packed in a flow bed reactor, is demonstrated to selectively and nearly quantitatively cleave lignin’s β-O-4 moieties, achieving a benchmark yield of 7.1 wt% for vanillin. The utilization of residual oligomers as plasticizers for biobased polylactic acid resulted in improved mechanical properties and shape memory effects, while maintaining its suitability for conventional 3D printing. This aldehyde-oriented platform represents a key advancement that complements existing ketone-oriented photocatalytic approaches targeting valuable chemicals from lignin conversion, and it ultimately contributes to the holistic utilization of lignin.

Introduction

Establishing reliable and scalable methodologies to source petrochemical analogues from biomass feedstocks is essential to shift to a bioeconomy1–3. Lignin, one of the major constituents of lignocellulose, has been anticipated to play a crucial role in this transition since it is the largest renewable reserve of aromatic molecules in nature4–10. However, current lignocellulosic biorefineries lack economically competitive strategies to valorize lignin, i.e., to extract the high-value aromatics locked inside its structure11,12. Lignin is constituted by a sequence of oxygenated aromatic units, namely p-hydroxyphenyl (H), guaiacyl (G) and syringyl (S), that are inter-connected via various types of C–C or C–O bonds (Fig. 1a), amongst which the so-called β-O-4 linkages are the most abundant. As a result, the scission of the β-O-4 moieties has become the target of lignin’s valorization to release highly functionalized aromatics13. Reductive treatments (e.g., hydrogenolysis) demonstrated quantitative cleavage of β-O-4 ether bonds5, but the often deoxygenated and dearomatized products find limited application as platform chemicals14. Alternatively, the two-step oxidative activation and reductive depolymerization route has shown to retain the functionalization in the monomeric structures, yielding primarily benzylic ketones due to the chemoselective fragmentation of the Cβ-O ether bonds5. On the other hand, oxidative depolymerization has been reported to yield highly functionalized products, primarily benzylic aldehydes, because of the preferential cleavage of the Cα–Cβ bond of the β-O-4 motifs15,16. Photocatalysis has recently emerged with the promise of deconstructing lignin at mild conditions, relying solely on light to activate and drive the valorization12,19. Several photocatalysts were shown to drive lignin valorization via the two-step pathway starting with the hydrogen atom abstraction from the Cα position11,20,21, but only few reports have demonstrated Cα–Cβ bond fragmentation. On the one hand, leading studies by Wang22,23, Son24, Zhang25, Moody26, and Knowles27,28 reported the selective Cα–Cβ bond cleavage of lignin model dimers using various photocatalysts. These studies portrayed a reaction mechanism highly dependent on the catalyst nature and conditions. Indeed, it has been argued that the Cα–Cβ bond scission could be initiated via single electron transfer (SET) process, as well as via hydrogen abstraction from the β position and from the hydroxyl group in the α position19. However, little is known about the parameters that dictate the specific mechanism. On the other hand, when treating lignin samples, Zhang’s group reported obtaining a mixture of aromatic aldehydes (up to 5.0 wt%) using an Ir-based photocatalyst25, while Knowles’s group achieved a comparable total monomer yield, and a 2.3 wt% for vanillin with the same photocatalyst (Fig. 1b)28. Arguably, vanillin is the most attractive product given the established industrial application and demand29,30. Note that very recently, a promising low-cost anthraquinone derivative was explored as a photocatalyst but releasing only about 0.5 wt% of vanillin within a complex monomer mixture31. Scaling current photocatalytic methods presents significant difficulties arising primarily from the cost of the chemicals (such as catalysts, proton and electron shuttles, inert atmosphere), and especially from the homogeneous type of catalysis carried out in batch reactors, which complicates catalyst recycling and products isolation. In this context, continuous-flow photoreactors with immobilized photocatalysts present several advantages for scaling photoredox processes, including enhanced light harvesting and the elimination of catalyst recycling requirements32. Flow reactors employing homogeneous photocatalysts have been reported, and although they offer improved performance with respect batch operation, challenges associated with catalyst recovery persist33.

Despite the encouraging progress on the photocatalytic routes, the yields of vanillin still fall short compared to those achieved with thermocatalytic systems, where vanillin yields of approximately 10 wt% were reported to be extracted from technical lignins34,35. It is worth noting that oxidative catalytic fractionation processes employing whole biomass, rather than technical lignins, generally deliver higher aromatic monomer yields. However, challenges remain, particularly regarding processing of solids and the comparatively low carbohydrate extraction efficiencies relative to conventional methods36–38. A common challenge present in photocatalytic and oxidative catalytic fractionation routes is the lack of product selectivity. The poor control over the mechanism and the harsh reaction environments promote undesired Cβ-O bond scission, product overoxidation, and additional side reactions that complicate downstream separation5,36. Consequently, the development of novel strategies capable of operating at ambient conditions while affording precise control over the reactivity is essential to facilitate efficient lignin valorization.

While the production of specific high-value aromatic monomers remains a central objective, recent studies have drawn attention to the relevance of the oligomeric fraction generated alongside the monomers during the lignin depolymerization. Interestingly, these fragments have been successfully repurposed in several applications, for instance, as building blocks for the construction of functional materials14,39. Given the chemical structure and relatively low molecular weight, these oligomers could potentially find application as bio-based plasticizers40. These additives are increasing in demand due to growth of bioplastic industry41, yet the benefits of lignin oligomers for this application remain largely overlooked. More generally, to the best of our knowledge, there are no reports on photocatalytic systems demonstrating the holistic utilization of lignin, i.e., the isolation of the monomer fraction and the upcycling of the oligomer fraction.

In this work, we demonstrated a photocatalytic platform for selective Cα–Cβ bond fragmentation based on anthraquinone (AQ) that operates under ambient conditions to achieve an unprecedented selectivity towards vanillin. The presence of oxygen was found to control the selectivity and to guarantee the regeneration of the photocatalyst under operation. Our detailed study combining experimental and computational approaches revealed that the steric hindrance in the AQ-lignin interactions dictates the initiation of the reaction via oxidation of the hydroxyl group in the Cα position. Furthermore, to explore the scalability of the process, AQ was immobilized in various supports, and validated in a packed-bed flow photoreactor. Indeed, the improved response of the heterogenized systems with was found to originate from the anchoring group grafted onto AQ. Vanillin was isolated with a yield of ca. 7.1 wt%, which accounts for about 94% of the extractable monomer (Fig. 1c). The oligomer fraction was repurposed as plasticizer for polylactic acid (PLA) inducing a noticeable improvement of the mechanical properties and shape-memory characteristics outperforming those obtained with unfragmented lignin.

Results

Optimization of the reaction conditions

AQ has been widely used in synthetic photocatalysis, both as an effective hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) catalyst to trigger photo-oxidation reactions or to perform single electron-transfer processes42. This, combined with its low cost, makes it an ideal choice for exploring lignin deconstruction. To demonstrate the prospects of AQ to undertake the photoinduced Cα–Cβ bond cleavage and optimize the reaction conditions, a β–O–4-type lignin dimer model compound (2-phenoxy-1-phenylethanol, PPol, (1a) was employed as substrate under homogeneous conditions. Supplementary Table 1 displays the conversion as well as the yield of the corresponding products as a function of the reaction conditions. Illumination was performed at 365 nm, based on the absorption spectrum (see Supplementary Fig. 1). At first glance, the β–O–4 linking motif was selectively cleaved via the Cα–Cβ bond, yielding benzaldehyde or benzoic acid (2a, 2b) that resulted from the aromatic ring on the left side of the molecule (ring A), and phenol, anisole or phenyl formate, (2c, 2 d, 2e) that originated from the right side (ring B) of the model dimer. The highest conversion and product yields were achieved when acetonitrile (MeCN) was used as solvent in the presence of air (see Supplementary Table 1, entry 7). Experiments performed in other solvents (such as dichloromethane (DCM) or HFIP)) were unable to trigger the Cα–Cβ bond cleavage, while experiments in the absence of air displayed lower conversion yields and produced anisole instead of phenol (see Supplementary Table 1). Other state-of-the-art photocatalysts, such as 9-fluorenone or 5,7,12,14-pentacenetetrone, were tested but the conversion values were demonstrated to be lower (see Supplementary Note 1).

Mechanism for β-O-4 bond cleavage

To gain insights into the photogenerated charge carriers dynamics during the Cα–Cβ bond cleavage, transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy measurements were carried out. TA spectra recorded for AQ displayed a distinct band at ca. 450 nm (see Supplementary Fig. 2). This has been attributed to the long-lived excited triplet state (3AQ)43, which is responsible for the strong electron- and hydrogen-atom-abstracting properties of AQ under photochemical conditions42. Figure 2a displays the TA signal recorded at 450 nm to track the exited state. As observed, the presence of PPol causes a rapid deactivation of the excited species, with an estimated lifetime ~20 ns, likely related to the photochemical reaction with PPol. Next, TEMPO was introduced in the reaction medium to trap radical intermediates45,46. As expected for a radical-mediated photochemical process, Fig. 2b shows that the conversion drastically decreased. In addition, an adduct containing TEMPO and the anisyl radical was identified and isolated. The detection of the anisyl radical, together with the formation of benzaldehyde, provides compelling evidence that the photocatalytic fragmentation of the β–O–4 motif takes proceeds via the Cα–Cβ cleavage, rather than through the scission of the Cβ–O ether bond (see Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3)11,47. Additionally, deuterium labeling at the Cα position and the adjacent alcohol group revealed that the Cα–H bond was preserved upon fragmentation, while no kinetic isotope effect (KIE) was detected (see Supplementary Note 3). The latter suggests that the scission of the O–H bond was not involved in the rate determining step.

The photocatalytic fragmentation of the β–O–4 bond was reported to be initiated via hydrogen atom abstraction from the Cα, from the secondary alcohol or from the Cβ, or via single-electron transfer (SET)11,23,25,28. To elucidate the reaction mechanism, a series of control experiments were carried out using methylated PPol models to selectively block the different reaction sites (Fig. 2c). Results revealed that methylation of the alcohol inhibited the photoinduced Cα–Cβ bond cleavage, therefore supporting the hypothesis that the fragmentation process started at this specific site. It is worth noting that the bond dissociation energy (BDE), a common proxy of the reactivity of the different sites, is higher for the O–H bond (99.08 kcal·mol−1) than for the Cα–H (80.64 kcal·mol−1) and Cβ–H bond (92.61 kcal·mol−1). Likewise, the oxidation of the ring via SET is estimated to require 174.6 kcal·mol−1 (see Supplementary Note 4). The lower BDE value associated with the Cα–H scission supports the observation that most photocatalysts trigger the Cβ–O bond fragmentation via hydrogen atom abstraction from Cα11, and therefore, strongly suggests that other factors, such as kinetic barriers, govern the selectivity in this particular case.

To explore this idea, the energetics of the system, i.e., excited AQ (AQ*) in the presence of PPol and O2, were computed for two different reaction mechanisms, wherein either the hydrogen atom was abstracted from Cα or from the Cα–OH group (see Supplementary Fig. 4). As observed, the hydrogen abstraction was virtually barrierless when involving the OH group, unlike in the case of the Cα–H. Overall, the higher Gibbs energy barrier associated to the Cα–H could originate from the steric hindrance that prevents the approach of AQ, while access to the O–H site benefits from hydrogen bond formation. Similarly, the spatial impediment could be invoked to rationalize the lack of reactivity towards Cβ–H. A similar conclusion was drawn by Li and co-workers when exploring the reactivity of an AQ derivative for PPol conversion31. Note that unlike this previous study, the photocatalytic route proposed here involves the active participation of O2 in the reaction. The very low Gibbs energy barrier for the hydrogen abstraction aligns well with the KIE analysis, whereby the O–H bond scission was found not to be involved in the rate-determining step. In fact, all major elementary steps identified in the computed reaction mechanism exhibit low activation barriers that are readily accessible at room temperature. To elucidate the rate determining step, we hypothesized that the photocatalyst excitation might limit the reaction rate. Experimental studies revealed a linear correlation between the initial reaction rate and the incident photon flux, whereas the apparent quantum yield (AQY) displayed only a weak dependence on light intensity. Taken together, these observations confirm that photocatalyst excitation is indeed the rate-determining step under the reaction conditions employed (see Supplementary Fig. 5).

With the most plausible photocatalytic pathway in hand, it remains to be clarified whether the initial oxidation of the Cα–OH proceeds via a hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) or a proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET). Zheng et al. previously proposed that oxidation proceeds via a PCET process31, rather than the more commonly invoked HAT route42, based on the distribution of the molecular orbitals in the computed transition state31,48,49, but no experimental evidence supporting the PCET mechanism was provided. To shed further light on this question, we examined how the nature of the solvent, as well as the addition of acids or bases, influenced the yield of benzaldehyde during the conversion of PPol (Fig. 2d). Firstly, considering that the PCET involves charged transition states, polar protic solvents are expected to accelerate this pathway by stabilizing these intermediates, whereas apolar aprotic solvents should disfavor it. In contrast, a HAT mechanism should exhibit a much weaker dependence on solvent polarity50,51. As shown in Fig. 2d, the reaction proceeds with moderate to good yields in apolar solvents and reaches the highest yield in MeCN, but it sharply decreases in the presence of protic solvents. This behavior supports the occurrence of a HAT mechanism. Secondly, because the PCET pathway is sensitive to the presence of proton donors or acceptors, the addition of a base would be expected to lower the activation barrier for proton removal, thereby stabilizing the transition state and ultimately increasing the product yield50. In contrast, a HAT mechanism should be largely insensitive to these additives. As shown in Fig. 2d, the reaction performance remained essentially unchanged as the pKa of the additive increased, which is consistent with a HAT pathway. A few exceptions were observed. On the one hand, N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) and 1,4-diazabicyclooctane (DABCO) suppressed photocatalytic activity, likely due to quenching of the excited state. On the other hand, strong inorganic bases led to partial inhibition, which may arise from substrate deprotonation, an event that would preclude the HAT entirely. Finally, to provide further confirmation of the negligible effect of the base, the reaction rate was monitored as a function of the concentration of 2,6-lutidine, revealing no significant variation (see Supplementary Fig. 6). Notably, if the reaction proceeds via PCET, the rate is reported to increase with the concentration of base52.

Based on all this data, a tentative mechanism for the Cα–Cβ bond fragmentation was proposed (Fig. 2e). Upon photoexcitation, AQ triggered the HAT from the O–H bond, which then underwent a radical rearrangement that triggered the Cα–Cβ scission (BDE = −2.8 Kcal·mol−1, see Supplementary Note 4) yielding benzaldehyde and the corresponding anisyl radical (Fig. 2b). Subsequently, O2 was trapped by this molecule generating the peroxyradical (a) which then reacted with AQH• via HAT to produce the hydroperoxide (b) and regenerate the AQ. Subsequently, a second AQ* abstracts a hydrogen atom from b (via HAT), releasing H2O and giving rise to phenyl formate (2e). This can then undergo hydrolysis, yielding formic acid and phenol as final products. The presence of excess O2 could account for a partial oxidation of benzaldehyde into the corresponding benzoic acid.

Modified and immobilized AQ as photocatalyst

With the optimum reaction conditions in hand, the scope of substrates was expanded to include the substitutions present in native lignin’s β–O–4 bonds (Table 1). In all cases, a final work-up with water was performed to convert the phenyl formate product into phenol. As observed, the incorporation of methoxy groups in the aromatic rings or of the Cγ–OH did not significantly alter the conversion nor the yield of the products. However, it was found that the most substituted model (Table 1, entry 6) required a longer reaction time to achieve over 90% conversion. While the batch reaction successfully demonstrated lignin model fragmentation, the fact that both photocatalyst and lignin are dissolved in the same medium poses a severe challenge for product isolation and catalyst recycling. To address this issue, AQ was covalently grafted on various supports to facilitate recovery after reaction (see Supplementary Methods). Supports such as polystyrene-based resins, glass wool, SiO2 and Al2O3 nanoparticles were selected based on their chemical stability and poor light absorption at the operating wavelength (see Supplementary Fig. 1), although SiO2 yielded the best photocatalytic results (see Supplementary Table 1). The coverage of AQ onto SiO2 was found to be 26.7 % (see Supplementary Note 6 and Supplementary Fig. 8), and the loading of immobilized catalyst in the experiment was adjusted so that the amount of AQ is similar to that used when employing free AQ. Interestingly, anchoring AQ to SiO2 (AQ/SiO2) did not significantly alter the product selectivity. Notably, the yields of the carbonyl-bearing fragments, which ultimately formed an aryl aldehyde or acid, generally differed from those of the complementary phenolic fragment. Likely, the low oxidation potential of the phenolic fragment makes it more vulnerable to be attacked by photogenerated radicals causing the polymerization or the over-oxidation towards ring-opened acids53. Control experiments performed with guaiacol as substrate supported this hypothesis. It is worth noting that attempts to cleave other structural motifs such as the β–1 did not succeed leading primarily to the oxidation of the CαOH to the corresponding ketone (see Supplementary Table 2).

Figure 3a depicts the AQY values for AQ and immobilized AQ on SiO2 (AQ/SiO2) showing a drastic improvement from 17%–67% (see Supplementary Note 7). We note that the AQY for AQ/SiO2 is relatively high when compared to that reported for other photocatalytic systems (AQY = 4-39%)54. At this point, we hypothesized that the improvement could arise from either (i) the support or (ii) the modification of the photoredox properties of AQ upon grafting a trimethoxysilylpropyl amide group to anchor it to SiO2. Infrared spectroscopy in combination with computational studies revealed interactions consistent with hydrogen bonding between the substrate and SiO2 surface that could account for a weakening of the CαO–H bond that facilitated the substrate activation (see Supplementary Note 8 and Supplementary Fig. 9). To assess the influence of the grafting group on photoredox activity, AQ was functionalized with a butylamide group (AQ-2-CONHBu), to resemble the immobilized counterpart. To rationalize the improved photocatalytic response upon modification, Stern-Volmer quenching plots were collected (Fig. 3b). The higher slope observed for AQ-2-CONHBu is consistent with an increased effective excited-state lifetime, assuming comparable bimolecular quenching rates (see Supplementary Fig. 10). Figure 4 shows the kinetic traces for the conversion of PPol using AQ, AQ-2-CONHBu and AQ/SiO2. As expected, the conversion rate increased in the order AQ < AQ-2-CONHBu <AQ/SiO2 in agreement with the trend observed for the AQY. Encouraged by the excellent performance of AQ/SiO₂ under batch conditions, we sought to translate this system into a flow packed‑bed reactor to enable continuous lignin depolymerization (see Supplementary Methods). In this setup, the lignin solution flows into the reactor and passes through the catalytic phase, where irradiation triggers the depolymerization process (see Supplementary Fig. 11). An optimization of the particle size, AQ loading and flow rate was performed (see Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Note 10). While the conversion and yield of products did not change drastically (Table 1), the use of the flow reactor impacted two key features. Firstly, the aldehyde:acid (H:OH) ratio increased drastically in the flow reactor, leading to higher product selectivity. We hypothesize that this result originates from the different availability of oxygen in the reaction medium. While in the batch reactor, the reaction medium is open to air affording a continuous supply of oxygen that will dissolve according to its solubility at ambient conditions, the concentration of oxygen within the flow reactor will be below this threshold. Although the oxygen content in the solution entering the flow reactor is governed by its solubility, its consumption during the photocatalytic process results in a lower oxygen concentration within the reactor, thereby limiting over-oxidation towards the acid. Secondly, the reaction in flow-conditions proceeded at a much faster rate, as confirmed by the quantitative conversion of 1 f compound (Table 1, entry 6) in less than half the time required for the batch systems. Lignin fragmentation using a photocatalytic flow reactor

We next investigated the fragmentation of oligomeric lignin samples using the photocatalytic flow reactor. We started with a polymeric model compound (Polymer G, 1 g), which is based on G units connected through β–O–4 linkages (Fig. 5a)55. Two-dimensional 1H-13C heteronuclear single quantum coherence nuclear magnetic resonance (2D-HSQC NMR) spectra were recorded before and after photocatalytic treatment to unveil the impact on the linking and terminal structural motifs of 1 g (Fig. 5b). As shown, the characteristic aliphatic and aromatic signals related to the β–O–4 linkage, i.e., PG-α,-β and -γ, and PG-2, −5 and −6, respectively, disappeared upon treatment55–59. Note that signals corresponding to terminal aldehydes (X) and oxidized guaiacyl units (G’) were distinctly noticeable, supporting the oxidative fragmentation of the polymeric compound. To gain some insight into the degree of fragmentation of 1 g, gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analyses were performed (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Note 7). The average molecular weight (Mw) decreased from 3024 g·mol−1 to 623 g·mol−1 confirming the effective fragmentation of 1 g. Comparison of the GPC profile with that of a control sample of vanillin, confirmed the release of vanillin monomers after the photocatalytic treatment accounting for about 16.1% of the products, as well as of an oligomeric fraction (Mw = 714 g·mol−1) consisting mostly of 2–4 G-units (see Supplementary Table 3). We note that unlike with model dimers, the effectiveness of the β–O–4 cleavage was not quantitative. This was also found to occur when operating in batch conditions (see Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 14). The fragmentation of organosolv lignin samples extracted from pine (softwood) and bamboo (hardwood) was carried out using the previously optimized conditions but adjusting the reaction time accordingly (Table 2). A noticeable color change is even observable to the naked eye, further indicating disruption of the polyaromatic structure (see Supplementary Fig. 19). This limited yield was attributed to the poor solubility of the bamboo lignin. In the case of pine lignin, only 2ab was detected as the major monomer given the absence of S units in its structure (Table 2). Benchmark vanillin yields of 7.1 wt% and 6.4 wt% were obtained from unmodified and acetylated lignin, respectively, with β–O–4 cleavage yields reaching 94% and 90%. These yields represent a significant improvement over those previously achieved via photocatalytic routes. To date, the best performing system produced vanillin yields of 3 wt% while the yields of β–O–4 bond cleavage were estimated to be around 40%, using Iridium-based photoredox catalysts28. Note as well, that the work of Zheng et al. using 2-Br-AQ reported a maximum vanillin yield of 1.09 wt%31. In this case, there are substantial amounts of additional monomers that are inconsistent with the proposed C-C bond cleavage. This lack of selectivity likely arises from the elevated reaction temperatures and competitive reactions promoted by the halogenated solvents used in this study. In addition, in this work the reaction was carried out under inert atmosphere which, according to the mechanism, cannot release vanillin, but methyl vanillin from lignin. The superior performance of our approach can be tentatively attributed to three key factors: (i) the reaction conditions employed, specifically, operation at ambient temperature and, under air which actively participates in the mechanism; (ii) the use of a tailored AQ derivative with enhanced photocatalytic activity; and (iii) the implementation of a flow‑reactor configuration, which improves mass transfer and accelerates the overall reaction. It is worth noting that the presence of oxygen, while essential for completing C–C bond cleavage and enabling vanillin release, can also promote partial over‑oxidation of the resulting vanillin into vanillic acid when the reaction is allowed to proceed for extended periods of time, longer than those employed here. Importantly, no additional products, such as dimers or small molecules typically formed in the presence of reactive oxygen species, were detected under these conditions, based on GPC and GC–MS analysis of the crude reaction mixture (see Supplementary Fig. 25).

To gain some insights into the structural and chemical changes that lignin underwent after the treatment and, to unveil the reactivity of the photocatalyst, 2D HSQC NMR spectra were collected. Qualitative assessment of the aliphatic region revealed that the intensity of the signal attributed to the β-O-4 linkages is significantly reduced upon the photocatalytic treatment (Fig. 6a)57–59. This aligns well with the hypothesis that AQ/SiO2 selectively targets the cleavage of these bonds. We note that the signals corresponding to the β−5 and β-β linkages also decreased. A recent study on the photocatalytic fragmentation of β−5 model compounds reported the formation of aromatic aldehydes (e.g., vanillin) together with benzofuran derivatives62. The absence of any benzofuran-type products in our system strongly suggests that AQ-based photocatalytic platforms do not promote β‑5 bond cleavage. Given the structural similarities between β‑5 and β‑β motifs, a comparable reactivity would be expected. Careful inspection of the aromatic region revealed a substantial decrease in the intensity of the characteristic signals of the guaiacyl units (G2, G5, and G6) following the treatment. Simultaneously, signals corresponding to their oxidation products (G’) and the aldehyde (X) emerged, corroborating the occurrence of oxidative depolymerization. Finally, GPC profiles of both the initial and depolymerized lignin were analyzed to assess the degree of fragmentation. The noticeable delay in the GPC profile and the detection of vanillin after treatment further corroborate the efficiency of the photocatalytic fragmentation. Indeed, the Mw value decreased from about 3186 g mol−1 (pine lignin) to 1654 g·mol−1 after the treatment (see Supplementary Table 3), and a similar decrease was detected for the acetylated counterpart (Table 2). Batch reactors with free and immobilized AQ were also tested for lignin fragmentation (see Supplementary Table 6). As expected, the use of immobilized catalysts led to higher yields. For instance, β‑O‑4 cleavage yields in pine lignin were 47% with free AQ, rising to 75% with the AQ/SiO₂ system.

To assess the flow-reactor performance, the space-time yield (STY) was determined (see Supplementary Note 1). STY was first evaluated using 1a model compound, providing a direct measure of the reactor configuration’s effectiveness toward β-O-4 bond cleavage. Under these conditions, an STY of 2.4 g benzaldehyde·L⁻¹·h⁻¹ was obtained. When lignin was used as substrate, the corresponding STY for vanillin was 0.1 g·L⁻¹·h⁻¹. This lower value likely arises from parasitic light absorption by lignin, the requirement to cleave two adjacent β‑O‑4 linkages to release a single vanillin molecule, and the presence of competing photocatalytic pathways. Although no directly comparable photocatalytic flow‑reactor studies have been reported, it is informative to contrast our results with STY values from thermocatalytic flow systems (see Supplementary Table 7). The vanillin monomer yield achieved here (7.1 wt%) is comparable to those obtained via thermocatalysis using kraft or organosolv lignin. However, in most cases the corresponding STY values reported for thermocatalytic systems are approximately one order of magnitude higher, due to the longer residence times employed in our reactor. The presence of preferential circulation channels within the packed reactor likely limits the effective active volume, resulting in the extended residence times observed. We also note that the use of CuAHP lignin affords substantially higher monomer yields, and thus higher STY values, owing to the greater preservation of β‑O‑4 linkages during lignin extraction. We note that the use of CuAHP lignin in our photocatalytic system did not lead to higher yields. This can be likely attributed to the fact that most of the Cα-OH in the β-O-4 motifs have been oxidized to the corresponding ketones, thereby preventing initiation of the photocatalytic depolymerization pathway.

Scaling up lignin fragmentation and downstream steps

The scalability of the flow reactor was assessed by setting a target for gram-scale vanillin production (Fig. 7a). A 20 g·L−1 solution of organosolv pine lignin (20 g of lignin in MeCN) was fed to the reactor, maintaining the optimized conditions but increasing the reaction time up to 48 h, when the vanillin production was detected to reach a constant value. Separation and isolation of lignin depolymerization products was initiated via flash chromatography. Fraction A mostly contained monomers and a small fraction of oligomers (Mw,A = 517 g·mol−1), whereas fraction B contained oligomers that exhibit a wider molecular weight distribution (Mw,B = 1535 g·mol−1) (see Supplementary Fig. 26). Note that vanillin is the most abundant monomer albeit small quantities of benzaldehyde derivatives were also detected (see Supplementary Fig. 27). Note that the narrow selectivity towards aromatic aldehydes, and primarily vanillin, simplifies subsequent isolation compared to state-of-the-art oxidative catalytic fractionation processes or photocatalytic routes, which typically generated a broad distribution of aromatic monomers5,28,31,36. Fraction A was subsequently separated using semipreparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), yielding 1.2 g of vanillin with a purity exceeding 99.0% (see Supplementary Fig. 28 and 29). The oligomers contained in fraction B were collected after solvent evaporation in the form of a powder. Prior to their utilization as plasticizers, oligomers were esterified to improve the interaction with the host polymer63 (see Supplementary Methods). Naturally occurring fatty acids, namely, oleic acid (OA) and caprylic acid (CA) were selected for this application. It is worth noting that the oligomers, originally obtained in solid form, turned into an oil at room temperature after esterification. This feature, common amongst plasticizers, facilitates subsequent blending with the host polymer. Note that unfragmented lignin was used as control sample in the process yielding a solid after esterification. Plasticized materials based on PLA were successfully processed by extrusion and inject molding processes (see Supplementary Methods). Thermal analysis via differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) demonstrated that the glass transition temperature (Tg) as well as the peak maximum of cold crystallization of PLA dropped after blending, and especially when incorporating OA-esterified oligomers (see Supplementary Fig. 31 and Supplementary Table 8) In all cases, 20 wt% loading led to the larger decrease with respect to PLA. Bearing in mind that the lower the Tg and the peak maximum the higher the flexibility41, the use of oligomers led to higher plasticization efficiency than unfragmented lignin.

The impact of plasticization on the mechanical properties was explored by measuring the stress-strain curves (see Supplementary Fig. 32 and Supplementary Table 9). Representative values of the elongation at break as a function of the plasticizer are displayed in Fig. 7b. Upon blending with the plasticizer, the elongation at break values increased, suggesting an improved ductility and mitigation of the original brittleness of PLA64. Note that the use of CA-esterified oligomers as plasticizing agent led to a two-fold increase of the elongation at break, whereas employing OA resulted in a benchmarking five-fold enhancement. Substituting the oligomers with lignin led to a 50% reduction in the elongation at break. The maximum tensile strength (MTS) displayed a similar trend (Fig. 7c). The MTS value decreased with the addition of plasticizers because of the improved ductility, showing the largest decrease when OA-esterified oligomers were incorporated. Notably, parameters like impact strength and work of fracture, linked to the ability of the plasticized polymer to absorb energy and its toughness, respectively, showed significant improvement when oligomer-based plasticizers were used instead of lignin-based ones. Finally, it was observed that only the formulation containing OA-esterified oligomers led to a noticeable decrease in Shore D value, confirming the enhanced flexibility of this blend65. Overall, the improved performance of OA- over CA-based plasticizer could be attributed to the longer aliphatic chain of the former66, whereas the increased rigidity of lignin and its less effective blending with PLA could account for the lower performance as plasticizer. Compared to the state-of-the-art, while the degree of plasticization is significantly lower than that reported for bio-based monomeric esters67,68, it is comparable to those achieved from waste streams such as functionalized vegetable oils69,70.

The versatility of the plasticized PLA formulation based on OA-esterified oligomers was investigated for 3D printing applications. The biocomposite demonstrated to be suitable for filament extrusion and subsequent printing (see Supplementary Methods, Supplementary Fig. 33, Supplementary Tables 10 and 11). As shown in Fig. 7a, a phone case was 3D printed, displaying the characteristic brown color induced by oligomers and enough flexibility to fix the phone in place (see Supplementary Fig. 34 and 35). On the other hand, we found that this biocomposite displayed shape memory effects (SMEs), that is, it recovers its original shape after deformation and subsequent exposition to a proper external stimulus71, such as heat (see Supplementary Movie 1). Interestingly, this feature opens exciting possibilities for applications in soft robotics or adaptative surfaces amongst others72. Indeed, several authors have reported SMEs for PLA72, but its inherent brittleness at room temperature limits the scope of use. Blending with plasticizers, like polyethylene glycol (PEG)73 or functionalized oligomeric lactic acid74, which lower PLA’s Tg and improve flexibility, was demonstrated to be a viable strategy to exploit PLA’s SMEs. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has demonstrated a PLA plasticized exclusively with lignin‑derived waste streams exhibiting SMEs.

Discussion

In this study, we present a comprehensive approach to holistic lignin valorization. First, we demonstrated a photocatalytic flow reactor capable of selectively cleaving the Cα–Cβ bonds of β–O–4 linkages in lignin with benchmarking efficiency. Second, we successfully isolated vanillin, the primary monomeric product, in high purity, and introduced an application for the resulting oligomers as plasticizers for PLA. Combined experimental and theoretical studies using lignin model compounds confirmed that AQ operates as a selective photocatalyst for lignin depolymerization in ambient conditions. AQ photocatalyst was heterogenized by immobilizing it onto SiO2 particles, resulting in a pronounced enhancement of the photocatalytic response. AQY measurements and Stern-Volmer quenching experiments revealed that the alkylamide linker used for covalently graft AQ to SiO2 improves the photoredox properties of parent AQ. In‑flow depolymerization was demonstrated by circulating lignin through a packed‑bed reactor composed of AQ‑modified SiO2 particles under illumination. Vanillin was obtained in yields exceeding 7 wt% on the gram scale, and esterification of the resulting oligomers with OA generated a PLA plasticizer that outperforms its unfragmented lignin‑based analogue. The suitability for 3D printing and the demonstration of shape-memory behavior in this biocomposite holds potential for future applications. In a more general vein, this work advances the field of lignin valorization by establishing a benchmarking photocatalytic platform for depolymerization, fractionation and conversion into sustainable chemicals and materials.

Methods

Evaluation of the catalytic performance

For the conversion of lignin model compounds, typically 1 mL MeCN, either AQ (0.04 mmol, 8.3 mg) or AQ/SiO2 (100 mg) and 0.2 mmol lignin model compound were added into a quartz reactor (5 mL) for the batch reaction. The reactor was sealed with a rubber septum in ambient conditions to keep air inside and the reaction mixture was stirred at 1000 r.p.m. In the case of the flow reactor, the immobilized catalyst was packed into a Diba Omnifit column, and the solution containing the model compound (0.2 mmol) in MeCN 10 mL was circulated using a peristaltic pump. The reaction was typically carried out for 16 h under UV light (365 nm) irradiation (LED 50 W). In the case of using immobilized AQ in the batch reactor, the catalyst was recovered by filtration prior product characterization, whereas the effluent of the flow reactor was collected as the crude product.

For the photocatalytic conversion of lignin, typically 100 mg organosolv lignin (pine or bamboo) was dissolved in MeCN (2 mL) and either AQ (0.08 mmol, 16.6 mg) or AQ/SiO2 (200 mg) was added into the quartz reactor (5 mL) for the batch reaction. In the case of the flow reaction, 100 mg of organosolv lignin in MeCN (20 mL) was fed into the column using a peristaltic pump (15 mL·min−1) and recirculated. The reaction conditions were essentially the same as those for the conversion of lignin models. For the experiment at gram scale, a solution prepared by dissolving 20 g dioxanosolv lignin in 1 L MeCN was recirculated 15 mL·min−1) for 48 h under irradiation.

Quantification of fragmentation

In the case of lignin models, the products were identified and quantified using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), GC with flame ionization detector (GC-FID), and by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) using 1,2,4,5-tetramethylbenzene as an internal standard. For lignin samples, the crude mixture was accurately weighed, ethylvanillin was added as an internal standard, dissolved in pyridine (3 × 0.2 mL), transferred to a GC vial and treated with 0.2 mL of N,O-bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide. The sample was heated to 50 °C for 30 min and analyzed by GC-MS and GC-FID to determine monomer yields using calibration curves (see Supplementary Methods). In the case of the polymer lignin model and organosolv lignin, the average molecular weight (Mw) distribution of lignin and the fragments resulting from the photocatalytic conversion were estimated by means of gel permeation chromatography (GPC). Typically, 2–3 mg were dissolved in 1.5 mL of THF and filtered through a 0.22 μm HPLC filter. Samples were analyzed using a PLgel 5 µm MIXED-D 300 × 7.5 mm column with THF as mobile phase in a 1 mL/min flow rate and polystyrene standards ranging from Mp = 51,950-162 Da. To evaluate lignin’s chemical structure and track the changes induced by the photocatalytic conversion Heteronuclear Single Quantum Coherence (HSQC) spectroscopy was used. Typically, lignin sample (50 mg) was dissolved in 0.6 mL of DMSO-d6 and inserted in an NMR tube. Spectra were acquired on a Bruker AC-400 NMR spectrometer.

Downstream processing

The effluent of the photocatalytic reactor was first separated by flash chromatography. The column was packed with silica gel (0.04–0.06 mm) using n-hexane as the packing solvent. The crude of the mixture was loaded on the column, and the elution was carried out with a gradient of n-hexane to 3:7 ethyl acetate:n-hexane. The composition of the eluate was monitored using thin-layer chromatography to separate fraction A and B. Fraction A was mainly constituted by vanillin (81% purity). Further purification of vanillin was carried out using preparative HPLC, using a mixture of methanol and an acetic acid aqueous solution (0.1%) in a 7:3 ratio as a mobile phase at a flow rate of 2 mL·min−1. Vanillin was obtained after solvent removal under reduced pressure, as confirmed by GC-MS and 1H NMR analyses. Fraction B, mainly constituted by low-molecular-weight oligomers, was first treated under reduced pressure to remove the solvent, and subsequently, it was dissolved in dry DCM (100 mL), adding triethylamine (23 mL, 165 mmol) under argon atmosphere. The mixture was cooled to 0 °C and the corresponding acid chloride (60 mmol) was added dropwise. Once the addition was completed, the reaction mixture was allowed to reach room temperature and stirred overnight. Saturated aqueous NaHCO3 solution was added, and the organic phase was separated and washed with brine, HCl (1 M) and deionized water. Finally, the organic phase was dried under vacuum to obtain the esters. A similar protocol was utilized to undertake the esterification of lignin. Preparation of biocomposites constituted by polylactic acid (PLA) and esterified lignin or oligomers, as well as their mechanical characterization are described in the Supplementary Methods.
