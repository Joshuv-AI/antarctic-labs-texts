Muscle loss and pentraxin-3 as predictors of immunotherapy outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer: a prospective study
Authors: Takaaki Masuda, Satoshi Watanabe, Ryo Suzuki, Kensuke Yanai, Ryo Yamazaki, Yumi Ando, Tomoya Wakabayashi, Susumu Tanaka, Kunihiro Shono, Naohiro Yanagimura, Miyuki Sato, Tomohiro Tanaka, Koichiro Nozaki, Yu Saida, Kenjiro Shima, Yosuke Kimura, Nobumasa Aoki, Yasuyoshi Ohshima, Toshiyuki Koya, Kazuko Sakai, Kazuto Nishio, Motohiko Yamazaki, Hiroyuki Ishikawa, Toshiaki Kikuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenia has been reported that as a prognostic factor in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), mainly based on retrospective, imaging-based analyses. However, its prognostic value has not been prospectively evaluated. In this prospective study we investigated the clinical impact of sarcopenia and explored circulating proteins associated with muscle loss and ICI outcomes. Patients with advanced NSCLC who received ICIs as first-line therapy were enrolled. Before treatment initiation, muscle mass was assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis, together with grip strength, walking speed, and physical activity. Pretreatment plasma samples were subjected to comprehensive protein profiling. Between October 2019 and August 2022, 41 patients were enrolled. Bothe overall survival and progression-free survival were significantly shorter in patients with sarcopenia. Notably, patients with pre-sarcopenia, defined as reduced muscle mass without impairment of muscle strength or physical performance, also exhibited poor clinical outcomes. Protein analysis identified penraxin-3 as being significantly associated with muscle loss and overall survival. These findings demonstrate, for the first time in a prospective setting, that even pre-sarcopenia is associated with poor prognosis in NSCLC patients treated with ICIs, and suggest that pentraxin-3 may serve as a biomarker linking sarcopenia to unfavorable outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51933-w.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are now an essential part of therapy for patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). ICIs target programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) 1, PD ligand 1 (PD-L1)2, and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) 3. By inhibiting the interaction of these molecules with their target proteins, ICIs activate cytotoxic immune programs, thereby overcoming immune escape mechanisms of tumor cells and exerting antitumor effects. These agents are increasingly used to treat many tumor types, including lung cancer, renal cancer, and melanoma4,5. One notable finding of ICIs compared to existing chemotherapy is that some patients treated with ICIs show markedly prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) compared to those treated with existing chemotherapy, sometimes for long periods of time without progression, and in some cases, patients are cured without worsening of the disease6,7. While some patients may experience long-term clinical benefits from ICIs, others may not benefit at all, even when diagnosed with the same carcinoma. Predictive biomarkers for the efficacy of ICIs, such as PD-L1 and tumor mutational burden, have been developed8,9. However, even in patients with cancer and high levels of these biomarkers, there are cases in which ICIs have shown limited efficacy. Identifying the background characteristics of patients who are less responsive to ICIs and elucidating the mechanisms underlying reduced effectiveness remain important research challenges.

Sarcopenia, as defined by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People is “a syndrome characterized by a progressive and generalized loss of muscle mass and strength and is associated with physical dysfunction, reduced quality of life, and death” 10. Sarcopenia has been shown to have a negative prognostic effect on various cancer therapies11–13. Retrospective studies have shown that sarcopenia is a poor prognostic factor for various types of cancers, including NSCLC14–18. The diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia comprise three components: loss of muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance. Grip strength is commonly used to assess muscle strength, while functional performance can be evaluated using the 6-min walk test or stair climbing test. Several methods are available to assess skeletal muscle mass. Typical methods include body imaging techniques and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). Imaging-based techniques estimate muscle mass or slow fat mass, whereas BIA quantitatively measures the components of the human body based on the principle that water-rich muscle tissue conducts electricity easily, whereas fat tissue, which contains little water, does not. However, these instruments are generally expensive and require considerable skill. In contrast, BIA has the advantage of being less expensive and less invasive to the patient. A simplified method for estimating muscle mass involves the use of cross-sectional imaging at the lumbar level via computed tomography (CT), which is frequently employed. Notably, most analyses on the relationship between sarcopenia and the outcomes of ICI therapies have been retrospective in nature and rely on CT-derived cross-sectional muscle mass estimations26,28–31. However, owing to the retrospective nature of these studies, parameters such as muscle strength and physical activity levels were not assessed.

In this study, we prospectively evaluated whether sarcopenia, diagnosed using BIA, serves as a negative predictor of ICI efficacy in patients with NSCLC. Furthermore, we conducted comprehensive proteomic analyses to identify factors involved in both sarcopenia and the attenuation of therapeutic efficacy of ICIs.

Materials and methods

Patients

We prospectively enrolled treatment-naïve patients with stage III, IV, or recurrent lung cancer who underwent systemic therapy at Niigata University Medical and Dental Hospital (UMIN Clinical Trials Registry: UMIN000050295). In the current study, only patients who received ICI regimens were analyzed. Patients who were unable to undergo BIA or those deemed unsuitable for participation by the investigators were excluded. The clinical medical background was documented prior to the initiation of ICI therapy, and anthropometric measurements, including weight, height, and body mass index (BMI), were recorded. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (revised in 2013) and approved by the Ethics Committee of Niigata University (registration number: 2019 − 0233). All patients who participated in this study provided written informed consent.

Assessment of sarcopenia

Body composition was assessed using BIA with the InBody system (InBody, Seoul, Korea) before cancer therapy. InBody is a body composition analyzer that uses the BIA method to quantitatively measure the components of the human body (body water, proteins, minerals, and body fat) from the impedance generated when an electric current is applied to the body19. The diagnosis of sarcopenia according to the 2014 of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia, was based on low muscle mass, low muscle strength, and/or low physical performance (appendicular skeletal muscle mass). Cutoff values used to define sarcopenia were as follows: skeletal muscle index (SMI) (limb muscle mass/height2) of < 7.0 kg/m2 for men and < 5.7 kg/m2 for women, handgrip strength (< 26 kg for men and < 18 kg for women), and usual gait speed (< 0.8 m/s) 20. Patients with reduced skeletal muscle mass but without a decline in handgrip strength or gait speed were classified as having pre-sarcopenia.

Analysis of plasma proteins

Blood samples were obtained before the initiation of the first-line therapy. We analyzed the expression levels of plasma proteins and their related proteins associated with sarcopenia (including APRIL/TNFSF13, BAFF/TNFSF13B, sCD30/TNFRSF8, sCD163, chitinase3-like1, gp130/sIL-6Rβ, IFN-β, sIL-6Rα, IL-11, IL-19, IL-20, IL-22, IL-26, IL-27 (p28), IL-28 A/IFN-λ2, IL-29/IFN-λ1, IL-32, IL-34, IL-35, LIGHT/TNFSF14, MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-3, osteocalcin, osteopontin, pentraxin-3 (PTX3), sTNF-R1, sTNF-R2, TSLP, TWEAK/TNFSF12, basic FGF, exotoxin, G-CSF, GM-CSF, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-1ra, IL-1α, IL-2Rα, IL-3, IL-12 (p40), IL-16, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-9, GRO-α, HGF, IFN-α2, LIF, MCP-3, IL-10, IL-12 (p70), IL-13, IL-15, IL-17 A, IP-10, MCP-1 (MCAF), MIG, β-NGF, SCF, SCGF-β, SDF-1α, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, PDGF-BB, RANTES, TNF-α, VEGF, CTACK, MIF, TRAIL, IL-18, M-CSF, TNF-β, ACT-A, C-reactive protein (CRP), ghrelin, GIP, GLP-1, glucagon, insulin, leptin, PAI-1, resistin, visfatin, adiponectin, adipsin, myostatin, IGF-1, TIMP-1). Serum cytokine levels were evaluated using a Bio-Plex Pro Human Inflammation 1 Assay (37-plex, Cat. #171AL001M), Bio-Plex Pro human Cytokine Screening assay (48-Plex, Cat. #12007283), Bio-Plex Pro Human Diabetes assay (10-Plex, Cat. #171A001M), and Bio-Plex Pro human Diabetes Adipsin/Adiponectin assays (2-Plex, Ca. #171A002M). Data were obtained using the Bio-Plex suspension array system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum samples were diluted with the appropriate diluents prior to the assay. The samples were tested in duplicate, and data were analyzed using a five-parameter (5-PL) logistic-log curve fitting model with the Bio-Plex Manager software. Serum IGF-I, TIMP-1, GDF-8, and activin A concentrations were evaluated using a human activin-A Quantikine ELISA Kit (Cat. #DAC00B), GDF-8/myostatin quantitative ELISA kit (Cat. #DGDF80), human IGF-I/IGF-1 Quantikine ELISA Kit (Cat. #DG100B), and human TIMP-1 Quantikine ELISA Kit (Cat. #DTM100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Serum samples were diluted with the appropriate diluents prior to the assay. Sample absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a VERSAmax (Molecular Devices, San Jose, VA, USA). The samples were tested in duplicate, and the data were analyzed using a four-parameter (4-PL) logistic-log curve fitting model with SoftMax Pro Software (Molecular Devices, San Jose, VA, USA).

Statistical analysis

PFS and OS curves were constructed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the groups were compared using the log-rank test. Treatment response was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. Welch’s two-sample t-test, Pearson’s chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, and Mann–Whitney U test were used to test for differences between groups. The correlation between two variables was examined using regression analysis. Cox proportional hazard regression analyses were used to assess independent risk factors for PFS and OS. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the Cox proportional hazards model. P values were calculated using the likelihood ratio test. All statistical analyses were performed using the JMP software (JMP Statistical Discovery, Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Study population

Between October 2019 and August 2022, 127 patients diagnosed with stage III, IV, or recurrent lung cancer at Niigata University Medical and Dental Hospital were enrolled. Among them, 44 had stage IV or recurrent NSCLC and were receiving ICIs or chemo-ICI therapy as first-line treatment. However, three patients were excluded due to the lack of sarcopenia testing, resulting in a final study cohort of 41 patients (Fig. 1).

Association between pre-sarcopenia and sarcopenia with PFS and OS

To evaluate the impact of sarcopenia on prognosis, Kaplan-Meier curves for PFS and OS were constructed by stratifying the groups based on the presence of sarcopenia and pre-sarcopenia. During the follow-up period (median: 15.9 months [95% CI: 13.2–21.6]), 12 (29.3%) patients died. Patients with sarcopenia, compared to those without sarcopenia or pre-sarcopenia, showed a significantly shorter median PFS of 4.6 months vs. 16.7 months (p = 0.011), and also exhibited a significant reduction in OS (p = 0.0172) (Fig. 2). Even among patients with pre-sarcopenia, there was a significant deterioration in PFS (median, 6.7 months vs. 16.7 months, p = 0.0028) and OS (median, 15.7 months vs. not reached, p = 0.0048) compared to those without sarcopenia. However, no significant differences in PFS and OS were observed between the sarcopenic and pre-sarcopenic patients.

Loss of muscle mass is also associated with poor PFS and OS

Given the observed worse prognosis at the stage characterized solely by reduced muscle mass without a decline in handgrip strength or gait speed (pre-sarcopenia), the role of muscle mass as a crucial prognostic factor was questioned, prompting the generation of survival curves stratified by the presence or absence of muscle mass loss. The group with muscle mass loss showed a significant deterioration in both PFS (median, 5.5 months vs. 16.7 months, p = 0.0012) and OS (median, 15.7 months vs. not reached, p = 0.0036) compared to the group without muscle mass loss (Fig. 3). This novel finding suggests that a decline in ICI efficacy and a worsening prognosis can occur as early as the pre-sarcopenia stage, where only muscle mass is reduced. To explore this further, we analyzed patient backgrounds related to metabolism and physical parameters stratified by the presence or absence of muscle mass loss. The group with muscle mass loss showed a tendency toward lower BMI than the non-loss group, but no decrease in body fat percentage was observed (Supplementary Table S1). This suggests that the weight loss stems from muscle mass reduction rather than from fat loss. Additionally, the survey conducted during sarcopenia diagnosis included activity levels. In the pre-sarcopenia group, a decrease in activity levels was not observed compared to the non-sarcopenia group.

Previous studies assessing the relationship between sarcopenia and the efficacy of ICIs have primarily used CT scans to estimate SMI. To validate the use of BIA in our study, we examined the correlation between CT-derived SMI—based on cross-sectional skeletal muscle area at the L3 level on pretreatment CT—and SMI estimated by BIA26,28–31. A strong correlation was observed, with an R2 value of 0.54441 (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure S1).

Correlation between comprehensive proteomic analysis and muscle loss

Next, we selected 10 plasma core proteins previously reported to be associated with sarcopenia and muscle mass reduction and measured all proteins that could be assessed on the same plate using multiplex analysis. The core proteins were IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TWEAK, TNF-α, IFN-γ, Activin-A, myostatin, LIF, and IGF-1 21,22. To identify proteins associated with muscle mass loss, the Mann-Whitney U test was performed to compare protein levels between the muscle mass loss and normal muscle mass groups. Proteins that exhibited significant differences between two groups included PTX3 (p = 0.0033), leptin (p = 0.0225), IL-1β (p = 0.0495), and IL-12 (p70) (p = 0.0344) (Fig. 4).

Univariable and multivariable analyses revealed that PTX3 is a significant factor for survival

Univariable analysis using the Cox proportional hazards model was performed for various factors that could potentially influence the treatment efficacy of ICIs, including sex, age, smoking history, number of metastatic organs, tumor PD-L1 expression, peripheral blood neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), peripheral blood eosinophil count, lactate dehydrogenase, CRP, PS, BMI, immune-related adverse events, level of physical activity (measures in Mets: less than 600/600 or more), walking speed, grip strength, and muscle mass (measured by BIA). Factors significantly associated with OS included the NLR, BMI, and muscle mass (Fig. 5A). In contrast, only muscle mass loss significantly correlated with PFS (Supplementary Figure S2). To identify the proteins associated with OS after ICI therapy, the protein levels were categorized into two groups, low and high, based on their respective median values. The proteins that exhibited significant differences were PTX3, leptin, IGF-1, TIMP-1, GDF-8, activin-A, insulin, IL-2Rα, IL-8, IL-13, G-CSF, IP-10, MMP-3, and osteopontin (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Figure S3). In contrast, plasma proteins that showed significant differences in PFS were insulin, leptin, IL-8, MMP-3, and osteopontin (Supplementary Figure S4). After accounting for multiple testing using false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment, no significant associations were observed for PFS, whereas only PTX3, leptin, IGF-1, Activin-A, IL-2Rα, IL-8, IP-10, and osteopontin remained significantly associated with OS. Notably, PTX3 and leptin were the only proteins significantly associated with both muscle mass loss and OS (Supplementary Table S2). However, because leptin is primarily regulated by adiposity and weight loss, and is not directly linked to ICI efficacy, we excluded it from further analysis and focused on PTX3.

Next, we performed a multivariable analysis for OS by adding PS to the factors identified as significantly associated with OS in the univariable analysis (peripheral blood NLR and PTX3). Because the number of death events were 12 in this study, we restricted the number of covariates included in the multivariable model to minimize the risk of overfitting, in accordance with the principle of events per variable. The analysis revealed that PTX3 was an independent predictor of poor OS, with a hazard ratio of 9.4 (95% CI: 1.06–83.29; p = 0.0141) (Supplementary Figure S5A). Finally, PTX3 was divided into two groups, high and low, based on the median value, and analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves to assess the differences in OS and PFS. OS was significantly worse in the high PTX3 group (median, 16.1 months vs. non-reached, p = 0.0006) (Supplementary Figure S5B). Although no significant difference was observed in PFS, there was a tendency toward deterioration in the high PTX3 group (median, 5.8 months vs. 12.9 months, p = 0.065) (Supplementary Figure S5C).

Discussion

This study is the first to demonstrate that similar to sarcopenia, pre-sarcopenia is also associated with a worse prognosis in patients with NSCLC treated with ICIs. Additionally, the novelty of this study lies in the prospective evaluation of the correlation between sarcopenia, pre-sarcopenia, and the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs as well as the use of BIA to assess whole-body muscle mass. Previous retrospective studies have shown that sarcopenia worsens the prognosis of lung cancer treated with ICIs26,29. However, in these studies, the diagnosis of sarcopenia was based solely on past images showing muscle mass reduction and the extent to which other elements constituting sarcopenia, such as reduced physical function and muscle strength10, contributed to the attenuation of ICI effectiveness. Furthermore, by analyzing pretreatment plasma proteins, PTX3 was found to be associated with both muscle mass reduction and poor prognosis of either ICI monotherapy or chemo-immunotherapy. This establishes that soluble molecules associated with sarcopenia contribute to the attenuation of ICI therapy, potentially serving as predictive markers for the efficacy of ICIs, alongside factors such as PD-L1 expression rate and TMB.

Mechanisms underlying sarcopenia involve multiple factors. Persistent chronic inflammation can lead to adipose tissue inflammation which spreads to neighboring muscle cells, resulting in muscle cell loss and functional impairment23,24. Mitochondrial dysfunction disrupts muscle cell homeostasis and induces fibrosis25. Declines in hormones such as insulin-like growth factors also contribute to sarcopenia26. Satellite cell DNA damage and degeneration of neuromuscular junctions impair muscle regeneration capacity26. Furthermore, factors such as malnutrition and physical inactivity exacerbate the development of sarcopenia27. In patients with cancer, chronic inflammation is perpetuated by various pro-inflammatory cytokines secreted by tumor cells, tumor-induced immune cells, and stromal cells, which may contribute to muscle wasting28. Recent studies have revealed that skeletal muscle cells engage in tissue repair by crosstalk with immune cells, such as macrophages and monocytes and by secreting cytokines like IL-6 29. Additionally, skeletal muscle cells regulate innate and adaptive immunity by presenting antigens and secreting cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 30. Disruption of the crosstalk between skeletal muscle cells and immune cells has been reported to cause chronic inflammation and tissue destruction, such as in myositis31, suggesting that the skeletal muscle cells of patients with sarcopenia are involved not only in musculoskeletal impairments, such as muscle loss and dysfunction, but also in systemic immune dysregulation. However, the mechanisms underlying the relationship between sarcopenia and the efficacy of ICIs remain unclear. We hypothesized that soluble molecules produced by tumor cells or other cells induced by tumor cells contribute to muscle loss, directly or indirectly, via muscle cells, and impair antitumor immune responses. Based on this hypothesis, we conducted the present study and identified a correlation between PTX3, muscle mass loss, and poor prognosis after ICI therapy in patients with NSCLC.

PTX3 is a member of the PTX family and is characterized by an early increase during inflammation, making it a highly sensitive factor that reflects systemic inflammation32. PTX3 is produced by mesenchymal cells, smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, and mononuclear phagocytes in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β and TNF-α, microbial components, and tissue damage32. The functions of PTX3 include host defense against microbial infections, regulation of inflammatory responses, clearance of dead cells, and tissue repair. PTX3 has been reported to act as both an immune stimulator and a regulator. PTX3 enhances inflammation through complement activation33 in contrast, PTX3 reduces neutrophil recruitment to the infection sites, resulting in the regulation of inflammation34 and high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in patients with cancer and sarcopenia. Also, PTX3 may serve as a link between pro-inflammatory cytokines, skeletal muscle loss, and the suppression of antitumor immunity35. Indeed, we identified an association between PTX3, muscle mass loss, and poor prognosis following ICI treatment. Similarly, a previous study demonstrated the relationship between elevated serum PTX3 levels and reduced muscle mass in patients with cachexia36. When both PTX3 and muscle mass loss were included in the multivariable analysis for OS, PTX3 remained significantly associated with OS and appeared to be an independent prognostic factor (data not shown). In contrast, muscle mass loss was no longer statistically significant in the multivariable analysis. One possible explanation for this finding is the strong correlation between PTX3 and muscle mass loss, leading to collinearity in the model. We hypothesize that PTX3 may be associated with muscle mass loss; thus, muscle mass loss may reflect PTX3-related inflammatory processes. We are currently investigating, using murine models, the impact of PTX3 on antitumor immune responses, with a particular focus on its effects on the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibodies and its role in T-cell exhaustion.

In additional analyses, leptin levels were positively correlated with BMI, consistent with its role as an adipokine derived from adipose tissue (Supplementary Figure S6). These findings suggest that lower leptin levels in our cohort may, at least in part, reflect reduced adiposity associated with sarcopenia or cancer-related weight loss. Given its established immunomodulatory effects, including the regulation of T-cell function and PD-1 expression, leptin may serve as a key link between metabolic status and antitumor immunity. In this context, the inverse relationship between leptin and PTX3 may reflect distinct yet interconnected axes-namely, a metabolic axis and inflammatory axis-that collectively shape the tumor immune microenvironment and influence responsiveness to ICIs. Further investigation into their combinational effects is warranted.

One limitation of this study was its single-center design and relatively small sample size. BIA may be influenced by factors such as food intake and hydration status. Although BIA measurements were primarily performed in the morning, conditions such as fasting status, timing of measurements, and fluid balance were not standardized across patients. The collection of plasma proteins was designed only for pretreatment. However, a more detailed analysis comparing post-treatment plasma proteins could provide insights into the differences between immunotherapy responders and non-responders. In addition, another limitation is the issue of multiple testing in the proteomic analysis, and therefore this comprehensive protein analysis should be considered exploratory in nature. PTX3 can be secreted by various cell types; however, it remains unclear whether it is produced by tumor, stromal, or inflamed muscle cells in patients with sarcopenia. It is also unknown whether PTX3 directly induces muscle mass loss owing to skeletal muscle inflammation triggered by other mechanisms. Furthermore, the mechanism by which PTX3 inhibits the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs has yet to be elucidated. The suppressive effect of PTX3 on antitumor immunity is currently being investigated in a mouse model.

Conclusion

Unlike previous retrospective studies using CT-based assessments of sarcopenia, the present study prospectively evaluated whole-body muscle mass using BIA in combination with physical performance. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to prospectively demonstrate that the prognosis of patients with NSCLC following ICI therapy is significantly worse even in a state of pre-sarcopenia, which has not yet progressed to sarcopenia. These results suggest that therapeutic intervention should be considered at least from the pre-sarcopenia stage. Among the plasma proteins associated with sarcopenia, PTX3 is associated with the prognosis of ICI therapy. Further analyses are warranted to elucidate how PTX3 is associated with the effectiveness of ICIs, including the signaling pathways involved.
