Computing weighted sheaf cohomology using noncommutative differential modules Thanks: The authors were supported by NSF grants DMS-2200469 and DMS-2302373, respectively Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. 14Q20, 13D02
Michael K. Brown, Daniel Erman

Abstract
. We describe a novel method for computing sheaf cohomology over weighted projective stacks using exterior algebra and differential module techniques, generalizing an algorithm due to Eisenbud-Fløystad-Schreyer over projective space.



1.
Introduction

We develop a novel algorithm for computing sheaf cohomology over weighted projective stacks (and weighted projective spaces) using exterior algebra and differential module techniques. This generalizes work of Eisenbud-Fløystad-Schreyer
[
20
]
, who utilized Koszul duality and Tate resolutions to transform sheaf cohomology calculations on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
from a computation over the polynomial ring
S
to a computation over the dual exterior algebra
E
.

While our method is also based upon Tate resolutions, it requires a number of fundamentally new methods.
As a quick review of the Eisenbud-Fløystad-Schreyer method: they start with a graded module
M=\bigoplus_{e\in\mathbb{Z}}M_{e}
over a standard graded polynomial ring
S=k[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
. They truncate it to
M_{\geq d}
where
d>\operatorname{reg}(M)
; then they apply the Koszul duality functor to obtain a free complex of modules
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d})
over the exterior algebra
E
of the form

\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d})=\left[\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{k}}(E,M_{d})\to\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{k}}(E,M_{d+1})\to\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{k}}(E,M_{d+2})\to\cdots\right].

Using facts about the truncation
M_{\geq d}
, they prove that
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d})
is a coresolution of an
E
-module
P
. They then compute a minimal free resolution of
P
, and utilize the theory of Tate resolutions on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
to relate the Betti numbers of
P
to sheaf cohomology groups of
\widetilde{M}
.

Adapting this method to the weighted projective setting requires several innovations. First, when we utilize Koszul duality in this setting, we must pass from working with free complexes to working with differential modules. For instance, if
M
is a graded module over a nonstandard graded polynomial ring
S={\bf k}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]
with degrees
1,2,3
then the terms of
\mathbf{R}(M)
will still have the form
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{k}}(E,M_{e})
, but the differential will inherit a nonstandard homological grading, i.e. the differential can “jump” by
1,2
or
3
steps:

\textstyle{\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d})=\Bigg[\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{k}}(E,M_{d})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{k}}(E,M_{d+1})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{k}}(E,M_{d+2})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{k}}(E,M_{d+3})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\cdots\Bigg]}

Working in the categorical framework of differential modules requires new foundational computational methods, including a method for computing a minimal free resolution. Second, in the classical case, the fact that
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d})
was a coresolution boiled down to well-known facts about linear resolutions of
M_{\geq d}
; but in the weighted case, there are many open questions about the resolutions of such truncations. Third, in the weighted setting it is simply not the case that
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d})
is quasi-isomorphic to its homology even for
d\gg 0
; since
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d})
generally has infinite rank, we thus need to develop a method for producing a finitely generated subquotient
P
which is quasi-isomorphic to
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d})
. Finally, if we overcome all of these, then we can compute the minimal free resolution of the differential module
P
in the category of differential modules, and we can apply the theory of Tate resolutions on weighted projective spaces to relate the Betti numbers of
P
to sheaf cohomology groups of
\widetilde{M}
.

One reason to pursue this approach is that there are computational advantages to working over an exterior algebra
E
as opposed to a symmetric algebra
S
, as the number of monomials of degree
d
is generally much smaller in
E
than in
S
. This streamlines the complexity of symbolic computations such as producing minimal free resolutions, as it avoids the doubly exponential explosion that can occur with symbolic computation over a polynomial ring
[
22
,
26
,
5
]
. In reference to their Tate resolution method for
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, Decker-Eisenbud
[
15
]
write: “In many cases this is the fastest known method for computing cohomology.” As our paper is about the theoretical foundation for this novel computational approach, and not about the efficiency of a specific implementation, we will not attempt to give specific speed comparisons. In fact, an efficient implementation of our algorithm would rely upon fast algorithms for performing computations with differential modules, including for computing minimal free resolutions. This theory remains nascent—especially in comparison to the tremendous work on computing minimal free resolutions of modules—but we hope that this paper will provide motivation for further improvements in that direction.

Let us go into a bit more detail on the main idea behind our algorithm. Let
S
denote the
\mathbb{Z}
-graded ring
\mathbf{k}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
with
\deg(x_{i})=a_{i}
, where
\deg(x_{i})=a_{i}\geq 1
, i.e. the coordinate ring of the weighted projective stack
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a}):=\mathbb{P}(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})

(see Section
3
for background on weighted projective stacks and the relationship with weighted projective spaces). The Koszul dual of
S
is the exterior algebra
E=\bigwedge_{\mathbf{k}}(e_{0},\dots,e_{n})
, and Koszul duality furnishes an equivalence between coherent sheaves on
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})
and certain
differential
E
-modules
(see Section
2
for the definition of a differential
E
-module). We show in our previous work
[
7
]
that this equivalence is described explicitly via the theory of toric Tate resolutions
[
7
, Theorem 6.1]
, and we prove that Tate resolutions transform ranks of sheaf cohomology over weighted projective stacks into Betti numbers of free resolutions of differential
E
-modules
[
7
, Theorem 3.3]
. Our algorithm is an application of this result.

To give an indication of how this works in practice, and the technical issues that arise, let us consider an example. Let
\mathbb{P}(1,1,2)
denote the weighted projective stack with
\mathbb{Z}
-graded coordinate ring
S=\mathbf{k}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]
, and let us compute certain sheaf cohomology groups of the coordinate ring
M\coloneqq S/(x_{0}^{4}+x_{1}^{4}+x_{2}^{2})
of an elliptic curve embedded in
X
. Tate resolutions have infinite rank, and so our goal is to compute a finite rank piece.

By Serre vanishing, there are no higher cohomology groups for degrees
\geq d_{0}
for some
d_{0}
, and so in this case,
H^{0}(E,{\mathcal{O}}_{E}(d))=M_{d}
. Since the differential
E
-module
\mathbf{R}(M)
, constructed via Koszul duality, has the form
\bigoplus_{d}M_{d}\otimes\omega_{E}(-d;0)
(see Section
3
for a detailed description of the functor
\mathbf{R}
, and see the Notation section below for the meaning of
\omega_{E}
), the Betti numbers of
\mathbf{R}(M)
—or equivalently the Betti numbers of
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d_{0}})
—compute the ranks of the
H^{0}
groups in degrees
\geq d_{0}
.

For the higher cohomology groups, we need to compute a portion of the Tate resolution, which can be done by taking a minimal free resolution
F
of
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d_{0}})
for any
d_{0}\gg 0
. We thus need: (i) an algorithm to compute the minimal free resolution of a finitely generated, differential
E
-module (see Algorithm
2.2
), and (ii) a finitely generated
E
-module
P
that is quasi-isomorphic to
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d_{0}})
, so that we can apply this algorithm to produce the desired resolution
F
. Finally, the specific recipe for matching Betti numbers of
F
with sheaf cohomology groups is proven in Corollary
3.13
, generalizing a result of Eisenbud-Fløystad-Schreyer in the standard graded case
[
20
, Theorem 4.1]
and applying the authors’ previous work in
[
7
]
.
We return to the example of the elliptic curve from above throughout the paper, e.g. in Examples
3.3
,
3.7
, and
3.14
.

To summarize, the key technical issues involved are the following:

(1)

What is the smallest
d_{0}
that we can use to truncate?

(2)

Since
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d_{0}})
is infinite rank, how small of a piece can we utilize without losing information about its homology?

(3)

In the standard graded case, for any finitely generated
S
-module
N
that is
r
-regular, the complex
\mathbf{R}(N_{\geq r})
is formal, i.e. quasi-isomorphic to its homology
[
20
, Corollary 2.4]
. But this is simply not the case in the weighted setting (Example
4.3
). How do we (efficiently) produce an appropriate finitely generated subquotient of
\mathbf{R}(N_{\geq r})
that is quasi-isomorphic to the infinite rank differential module
\mathbf{R}(N_{\geq r})
?

(4)

How many steps of the minimal free resolution algorithm for differential modules do we need to run to obtain all sheaf cohomology groups in a desired range of degrees?

Resolving these issues is the fundamental contribution of this paper, as it transforms the theory of Tate resolutions into a workable algorithm.

An overview of our answers to the technical issues are as follows. For (1): if
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}M=0
, then we can choose
d_{0}
to be the (weighted) regularity of
M
, as defined by Benson
[
6
]
(see Definition
3.4
). If
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}M\neq 0
, then we need the regularity plus
1
; see Lemma 4.7 for details. For (2): we can work with
\mathbf{R}(M)
in degrees ranging from
d_{0}
to
d_{0}+\sigma+1
, where
\sigma=\sum(\deg(x_{i})-1)
is the Symonds constant (see Definition
3.6
); this follows from Lemma
4.4
, which provides a sharp bound on the longest “jumps” in the differentials of the Tate resolution, for a particular flag structure. For (3): our answer, which is a subquotient involving
\sigma+2
distinct degrees of the initial module
M
, appears in Lemma
4.9
. For (4): to go from
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq d_{0}})
to the sheaf cohomology groups
H^{i}
in degree
\alpha
, we need to iterate the resolution algorithm
d_{0}-\alpha-i
steps; see Algorithm
4.11
.

In addition to the aforementioned algorithms for projective space, the theory of Tate resolutions on products of projective spaces was introduced in
[
18
]
and implemented in Macaulay2
[
25
]
in the TateOnProducts package
[
19
]
. For an arbitrary toric variety, the question of effectively computing Tate resolutions remains open
[
7
, Question 7.3]
.

Overview of the paper.

In Section
2
, we recall some background on differential
E
-modules, and we describe an algorithm for computing their minimal free resolutions (Algorithm
2.2
). In Section
3
, we recall some needed facts about weighted projective stacks, weighted Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity, and weighted Tate resolutions. For instance, we prove in Section
3
a key result relating the weighted Tate resolution of a sheaf to a sufficiently large truncation of a module representing it. With these technical details in hand, we describe our algorithm for computing sheaf cohomology over weighted projective stacks in Section
4
.

Notation

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field,
S
the
\mathbb{Z}
-graded polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
with
a_{i}\coloneqq\deg(x_{i})
satisfying
1\leq a_{0}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}
, and
\mathfrak{m}
the homogeneous maximal ideal of
S
. Set
a\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}
. Let
E
be the exterior algebra
\bigwedge_{\mathbf{k}}(e_{0},\dots,e_{n})
, equipped with the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-grading given by
\deg(e_{i})=(-a_{i};-1)
. Given
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-graded
E
-modules
N
and
N^{\prime}
, we let
\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}_{E}(N,N^{\prime})
denote the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-graded
E
-module of
E
-linear maps from
N
to
N^{\prime}
. Let
\omega_{E}
be the canonical module
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{k}}(E,\mathbf{k})\cong E(-a;-n-1)
of
E
.

2.
Differential
E
-modules

A
differential
E
-module

D
is a
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-graded
E
-module
D
equipped with a degree
(0;-1)

E
-linear endomorphism
\partial
such that
\partial^{2}=0
. A
morphism
of differential
E
-modules is a degree
(0;0)

E
-linear map that is compatible with differentials. Let
\operatorname{DM}(E)
denote the category of differential
E
-modules. Differential modules appear in Cartan-Eilenberg’s textbook
[
13
]
, and they played an important role in the history of rank conjectures related to the Carlsson Conjecture and the Buchsbuam-Eisenbud-Horrocks Conjecture
[
12
,
1
,
16
,
24
]
; they have also since
been applied in many works on
commutative algebra
[
8
,
10
,
3
,
4
]
, algebraic geometry
[
9
,
7
]
, representation theory
[
28
,
33
,
34
,
27
,
29
]
, and beyond
[
30
,
32
,
23
]
.

The
homology
of
D\in\operatorname{DM}(E)
is
H(D)\coloneqq\ker(\partial\colon D\to D(0;-1))/\operatorname{im}(\partial\colon D(0;1)\to D)
. A morphism of differential
E
-modules is a
quasi-isomorphism
if it induces an isomorphism on homology. The
mapping cone
of a morphism

f\colon D\to D^{\prime}
of differential
E
-modules is the differential
E
-module
\operatorname{cone}(f)\coloneqq D^{\prime}\oplus D(0,-1)
equipped with the differential

\begin{pmatrix}\partial^{\prime}&f\\
0&-\partial\end{pmatrix}
. A morphism of differential
E
-modules is a quasi-isomorphism if and only if its mapping cone is exact. A differential
E
-module
D
is called
minimal
if the induced differential on
D\otimes_{E}\mathbf{k}
is the zero map.

Remark 2.1
.

One may alternately think of
E
as a differential bigraded algebra with homological grading given by
|e_{i}|=-1
, “internal grading” given by
\deg(e_{i})=-a_{i}
, and trivial differential. From this perspective, the category
\operatorname{DM}(E)
is isomorphic to the category of differential bigraded
E
-modules; see
[
7
, Remark 2.3]
. As in
[
7
]
, we work with differential modules rather than dg-modules because the former are more amenable to computation in
Macaulay2
.

A differential
E
-module
F
with differential
\partial_{F}
is called a
free flag
if it may be equipped with a decomposition
F=\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}F_{i}
such that each
F_{i}
is free,
F_{i}=0
for
i\ll 0
, and
\partial_{F}(F_{i})\subseteq\bigoplus_{j<i}F_{j}
for all
i\geq 0
. A
minimal free flag resolution
of a differential
E
-module
D
is a quasi-isomorphism
F\xrightarrow{\simeq}D
, where
F
is a minimal free flag. Minimal free flag resolutions of differential
E
-modules with finitely generated homology are unique up to isomorphism
[
7
, Theorem B.2]
. We introduce the following algorithm for constructing them in our setting. We remark that flag structures are not unique, and we refer to the choice in this algorithm as the “twisted flag” because it interweaves the bidegree on
E
.

Algorithm 2.2

(Twisted flag)
.

Let
D
be a differential
E
-module with finitely generated and nonzero homology. Write
H(D)_{(i)}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{j-d=i}H(D)_{(d;j)}
, and
set
n\coloneqq\min\{i\text{ : }H(D)_{(i)}\neq 0\}
.

(1)

Let
y_{1},\dots,y_{t}\in D
be homogeneous cycles that descend to a
\mathbf{k}
-basis of
H(D)_{(n)}
, and let
F_{(n)}
be a graded free
E
-module of rank
t
equipped with a basis whose
i^{\operatorname{th}}
element has the same bidegree as
y_{i}
. Define a morphism
\varepsilon_{n}\colon F_{(n)}\to D
of differential
E
-modules (where the differential on
F_{(n)}
is trivial) that sends the
i^{\operatorname{th}}
basis element of
F_{(n)}
to
y_{i}
.

(2)

The map
\varepsilon_{n}
determines an isomorphism on homology in bidegrees
(d;j)
with
j-d=n
. If
\operatorname{cone}(\varepsilon_{n})
is exact, we’re done;
\varepsilon_{n}
gives a minimal free flag resolution of
D
. Otherwise, apply Step (1) to
\operatorname{cone}(\varepsilon_{n})
, observing that
H(\operatorname{cone}(\varepsilon_{n}))_{(i)}=0
for
i<n-1
.

Iterating this procedure gives a free flag resolution
F\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i\geq 0}F_{(i)}\xrightarrow{\simeq}D
, where each
F_{(i)}
is finitely generated in degrees of the form
(d;j)
such that
j-d=i
. Moreover, the differential on
F
is minimal, since if
i^{\prime}<i
, any map
F_{(i)}\to F_{(i^{\prime})}
is necessarily minimal.

3.
Tate resolutions on weighted projective stacks

3.1.
The nonstandard
\mathbb{Z}
-graded BGG correspondence

Let
\operatorname{mod}(S)
denote the category of finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}
-graded
S
-modules. The nonstandard
\mathbb{Z}
-graded version of the BGG correspondence gives a functor

\mathbf{R}\colon\operatorname{mod}(S)\to\operatorname{DM}(E)

given by

\mathbf{R}(M)=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb{Z}}M_{d}\otimes_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_{E}(-d;0),\quad\text{with differential}\quad\partial_{\mathbf{R}}(m\otimes f)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{i}m\otimes e_{i}f.

The functor
\mathbf{R}
induces an equivalence on bounded derived categories; see
[
7
, §2.2]
for additional background on the nonstandard

[
21
]
).

Remark 3.1
.

Suppose

[

Remark 3.2
.

We will be interested in applying the functor

is simply the differential

Definition 3.4

If

implies:

\operatorname{reg}(M)=\max\{j-i\text{ : }\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(M,\mathbf{k})_{j}\neq 0\}+n+1-a.

In particular,

. Recalling that

, this also leads to the following definition:

.

We define the

for

.

Example 3.7

We have

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
M
be a graded
S
-module, and suppose

is
r
-regular.

(1)

The module

is also
r
-regular. In particular,
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M_{\geq r+1})=0
.

(2)

If

, then

We have

for

3.3.
Tate resolutions on weighted projective stacks

Let
\mathbf{a}
be the ordered list
a_{0},\dots,a_{n}
of the degrees of
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\in S
, and let
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})
be the weighted projective stack
[\mathbb{A}^{n+1}-\{0\}/\mathbf{k}^{*}]
associated to the action of the multiplicative group
\mathbf{k}^{*}
on
\mathbb{A}^{n+1}
given by

\lambda\cdot(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=(\lambda^{a_{0}}x_{0},\dots,\lambda^{a_{n}}x_{n}).

Remarks 3.9
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\text{var}}(\mathbf{a})
denote the associated weighted projective
variety
. We work over the stack
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})
rather than the scheme
\mathbb{P}_{\text{var}}(\mathbf{a})
for two reasons: first, the stack is better behaved than the variety, in many senses; and second, sheaf cohomology computations over
\mathbb{P}_{\text{var}}(\mathbf{a})
can be reduced to computations over the stack. In more detail, we record the following observations concerning weighted projective stacks:

(1)

The category
\operatorname{coh}(\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a}))
of coherent sheaves has a simple algebraic description. It is the abelian quotient of the category
\operatorname{mod}(S)
of graded
S
-modules by the subcategory of objects with finite dimension over
\mathbf{k}
. Given an object
M
in
\operatorname{mod}(S)
, we let
\widetilde{M}
denote the associated sheaf on
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})
. By contrast, the correspondence between sheaves on the variety
\mathbb{P}_{\text{var}}(\mathbf{a})
and
S
-modules is more complicated, as one must differentiate between degrees that correspond to elements of the Picard group of
\mathbb{P}_{\text{var}}(\mathbf{a})
and those that correspond to elements of the divisor class group of
\mathbb{P}_{\text{var}}(\mathbf{a})
.

(2)

The stack
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})
is a toric Deligne-Mumford stack, and so the sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})}(i)
on
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})
is a line bundle for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. By contrast, the variety
\mathbb{P}_{\text{var}}(\mathbf{a})
is generally singular, and the sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}_{\text{var}}(\mathbf{a})}(i)
is not a line bundle unless the least common multiple of
a_{0},\dots,a_{n}
divides
i
. This creates various subtleties in working with the variety. For instance, while the relation
\mathcal{O}(i)\otimes\mathcal{O}(j)\cong\mathcal{O}(i+j)
holds over
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})
, the analogous relation does not necessarily hold over
\mathbb{P}_{\text{var}}(\mathbf{a})
. See
[
11
, Remark 3.1]
for a more detailed discussion of these pathologies of weighted projective spaces, and others.

(3)

Sheaf cohomology computations on the variety can be reduced to computations on the stack. This is because pushforward along the coarse moduli space map
\pi\colon\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})\to\mathbb{P}_{\text{var}}(\mathbf{a})
is exact on coherent sheaves
[
17
, Proposition 3.5]
, and so computing the cohomology of a coherent sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})
is the same as computing the cohomology of
\pi_{*}(\mathcal{F})
over
\mathbb{P}_{\text{var}}(\mathbf{a})
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a coherent sheaf on
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})
,
M
a finitely generated graded
S
-module such that
\widetilde{M}=\mathcal{F}
, and
r>\operatorname{reg}(M)
. It follows from
[
7
, Proposition 2.11(a)]
that, given any finitely generated graded
S
-module
N
, the homology of
\mathbf{R}(N)
is finitely generated. We may therefore apply Algorithm
2.2
to construct a minimal free flag resolution
\varepsilon\colon F\xrightarrow{\simeq}\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
. We let
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})
denote the exact, free differential
E
-module
\operatorname{cone}(\varepsilon)
. The following theorem shows that
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})
coincides with the
Tate resolution of
\mathcal{F}
, as defined in
[
7
, §3]
; in particular,
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})
is independent of the choice of
r
, and its Betti numbers encode the sheaf cohomology of the twists of
\mathcal{F}
. The Tate resolution functor
\mathcal{F}\mapsto{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})
is a geometric analogue of the weighted BGG functor

; see
[
7
, §3]
for details.

Let

(1)

The differential

We have an exact sequence

. Suppose

for

.

\displaystyle h^{i}(\mathcal{F}(j))=\dim_{\mathbf{k}}\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}_{E}(\mathbf{k},{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F}))_{(j,-i)}=\dim_{\mathbf{k}}\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}_{E}(\mathbf{k},F(0,-1)\oplus\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r}))_{(j,-i)}.

The algorithm

Given a graded
S

, which is typically infinite. Thus, for computational purposes, we can only work with subquotients of

. To transform Theorem
3.13
into a workable algorithm for computing sheaf cohomology over

, we therefore need a method for building the Tate resolution of

out of a finitely generated portion of

Given a coherent sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})
, we define:

(4.1)

{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{\ell}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{-i-j=\ell}H^{i}(\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a}),\mathcal{F}(j))\otimes_{\bf k}\omega_{E}(-j;i).

By Theorem
3.10
(4), the Tate resolution
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})
is isomorphic, as a graded

-module, to

. For the rest of the paper, we identify the underlying

-module of
\mathbf{T}(\mathcal{F})
with this direct sum.

Remark 4.2
.

In the standard graded case, it follows from
[
20
, Theorem 4.1]
and Remark
3.16
that
\mathbf{T}(\mathcal{F})_{\ell}
may be identified, via the unfolding functor
[
7
, Definition 2.6]
, with the

homological degree term of the Tate resolution as defined in
[
20
, Section 4]
.

Since the differential
E
-module
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
has infinite rank as an
E
-module, we cannot view it in
Macaulay2
in its entirety. In order to allow for explicit computation, we therefore need a finitely generated differential

-module to which
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
is quasi-isomorphic. In the standard graded case, this is achieved as follows. First, we have that the Tate resolution is a free complex, and thus the differential always shifts by one in homological degree. Second, in the standard graded case,
if

then
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
is formal, i.e. it is quasi-isomorphic to its homology
[
20
, Corollary 2.4]
.
Taken together, these imply that one can compute the Tate resolution by working with just two graded pieces of

and resolving the kernel of

M_{r}\otimes\omega_{E}(-r;0)\to M_{r+1}\otimes\omega_{E}(-r-1;0).

In the weighted case, we have two obstacles. Instead of a complex, the best we can hope for is that
\mathbf{R}(M)
has a flag structure. So to bound the portion of
\mathbf{R}(M)
we need to consider, we need an upper bound on how much the differential can shift the flag index. This is the content of Lemma

. Second, in the weighted case,
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
generally fails to be formal, even if

.

When
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
fails to be formal, as in the above example, we thus need an alternate method for producing a finitely generated differential module that is quasi-isomoprhic to
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
: this is given by Lemma
4.9
. Let us now begin establishing these technical facts.

Lemma 4.4
.

The differential on
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})
sends
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{\ell}
to
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{\ell-1}\oplus\cdots\oplus{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{\ell-\sigma-1}

Proof.

Choose a
\mathbf{k}
-basis of each cohomology group
H^{i}(\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a}),\mathcal{F}(j))
. This yields a basis of
\mathbf{T}(\mathcal{F})
as an
E
-module, and we view the differential on
\mathbf{T}(\mathcal{F})
as a matrix with respect to this basis. We fix the following notation: for any
0\leq i\leq n
, let
w^{i}
(resp.
w_{i}
) denote the maximum (resp. minimum) degree of a square-free monomial in
S
involving
i
variables.

Let
\omega_{E}(c;s)
and
\omega_{E}(d;t)
be summands in
\mathbf{T}(\mathcal{F})
such that the corresponding entry
f\in E
of this matrix is nonzero. Since the differential on
\mathbf{T}(\mathcal{F})
is minimal of degree
(0;-1)
, the element
f\in E
has bidegree
(-(c-d);-(s-t+1))
, where
0\leq s-t\leq n
. The element
f
is a sum of squarefree products of
s-t+1
of the variables in
E
, and so
s-t+1\leq c-d\leq w^{s-t+1}
.

The terms
\omega_{E}(c;s)
and
\omega_{E}(d;t)
lie in
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{c-s}
and
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{d-t}
, respectively. We must show:

1\leq c-d-(s-t)\leq\sigma+1.

We have
s-t+1\leq c-d
, which implies

(4.5)

1\leq c-d-(s-t).

The inequalities
c-d\leq w^{s-t+1}
and
n-(s-t)\leq w_{n-(s-t)}=w^{n+1}-w^{s-t+1}
imply

(4.6)

c-d-(s-t)\leq w^{s-t+1}-(s-t)\leq\sigma+1.

Combining (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) gives the desired inequalities.
∎

Given
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{\leq j}
denote the differential submodule
\bigoplus_{\ell\leq j}{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{\ell}
of
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})
arising from the decomposition (
4.1
), and set
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{>j}\coloneqq{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})/{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{\leq j}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated, graded
S
-module and let
\mathcal{F}=\widetilde{M}
be the corresponding sheaf on
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a})
. If
r>\operatorname{reg}(M)
, then there is an isomorphism
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})\xrightarrow{\cong}{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{\leq-r}
of differential
E
-modules. If
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
, then the same statement holds for
r=\operatorname{reg}(M)
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon\colon F\xrightarrow{\simeq}\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
be the minimal free resolution of
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
. By Theorem
3.10
, we have
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})\cong\operatorname{cone}(\varepsilon)
. In particular, there is an inclusion
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})\hookrightarrow\mathbf{T}(\mathcal{F})
, and this inclusion clearly takes values in
\mathbf{T}(\mathcal{F})_{\leq-r}
. We must show that no summand of
F(0,-1)
lies in
\mathbf{T}(\mathcal{F})_{\leq-r}
. Let
\omega_{E}(-d;i)
be such a summand. If
i=0
, then Corollary
3.13
implies that
d<r
, and so
\omega_{E}(-d;i)
is not contained in
\mathbf{T}(\mathcal{F})_{\leq-r}
. If
i>0
, then
\omega_{E}(-d;i)
corresponds to a dimension 1 subspace of
H^{i}(\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{a}),\mathcal{F}(j))\cong H^{i+1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M_{\geq r})_{j}
. Since
M_{\geq r}
is
r
-regular (Lemma
3.8
), we must have
-i-j>-r.

∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated graded
S
-module, and let
r>\operatorname{reg}(M)
.
Denote by
N
the following
E
-submodule of
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
(recall the notation
\sigma
from Definition
3.6
):

N\coloneqq\bigoplus_{d=r}^{r+\sigma+1}\omega_{E}(-d;0).

Write
\partial|_{N}\colon N\to\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
for the restriction of the differential on
\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
to
N
. The inclusion of differential
E
-modules
N+\operatorname{im}(\partial|_{N})\hookrightarrow\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
is a quasi-isomorphism. If
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
, then this statement also holds when
r=\operatorname{reg}(M)
.

Proof of Lemma
4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\widetilde{M}
, and let
\varepsilon\colon{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{>-r}\to{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{\leq-r}
be the map induced by the differential on
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})
. Since
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})
is exact, the map
\varepsilon
is a quasi-isomorphism. By Lemma
4.7
, there is an isomorphism
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{\leq-r}\cong\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
of differential
E
-modules, and so we have a quasi-isomorphism
\varepsilon\colon{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{>-r}\xrightarrow{\simeq}\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
. By Lemma
4.4
, the image of
\varepsilon
is contained in
N
. The isomorphism induced by
\varepsilon
on homology therefore factors as
H({\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{>-r})\to H(N+\operatorname{im}(\partial|_{N}))\hookrightarrow H(\mathbf{R}(M_{\geq r})
, where the second map is the inclusion. Thus, both maps in this factorization are isomorphisms.
∎

Corollary 4.10
.

Adopting the notation of Lemma
4.9
: if
r>\operatorname{reg}(M)
, then the minimal free resolution of the finitely generated differential
E
-module
N+\operatorname{im}(\partial|_{N})
is isomorphic to
{\mathbf{T}}(\mathcal{F})_{>-r}
. If
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
, then the same is true when
r=\operatorname{reg}(M)
.

3.13
and
4.10
