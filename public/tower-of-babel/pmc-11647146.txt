Fishing with skis, digging with noodles: Resolving task-and-tool mismatches in efforts to advance health equity
Authors: Katrina Plamondon, Sana Z Shahram
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhen it comes to advancing equity, across the health sciences, efforts repeatedly target interventions on those most burdened by inequities rather than the systems or structures that give rise to inequities. This mismatch, in and of itself, is an important determinant of equity. While many conceptual models draw collective attention to deeper, structural causes (e.g., social, political, and commercial determinants of health) as the 'what', inattention to questions of 'how'–or the collective practices that serve to connect what is known with what is done–are like a wedge holding this gap in place. In this article, we use an exaggerated metaphor of mismatched task-and-tool to provoke critically reflective dialogue about collective attachment to scholarship and practices incoherent with our own body of knowledge. We offer a set of five practices easily integrated in any aspect of work related to advancing equity, through everyday actions anyone (anywhere) can take to purposefully act from an evidence and equity-informed position.Keywords: Practice, Social determinants of health, Equity, Population health, Knowledge-to-action

Whether a vocation or hobby, fishing is an ancient human practice. So is digging. Humans know a lot about both. We have access to advanced tools and information to support our efforts to fish or dig. We know how to optimize our chances of achieving a desired outcome (fish, or a hole), taking care to adapt our gear, approach, and locations to season and weather. We know who to talk to and how to plan for fishing or digging. We match what we do with what we know, and act accordingly. Winter mountain gear is not the best choice for fishing, and—whether cooked or uncooked—noodles are unlikely to serve well to shovel earth. Even people who do not fish or dig (or ski or eat noodles) would not find this pairing of task-and-tool well matched: fishing with skis or digging with noodles is ridiculous! Yet, when it comes to advancing health equity, humans seem stuck, pulling skis out to fish and scratching our heads in bewilderment when noodles fail us in our digging.

As keen as the health sciences are on using evidence to guide actions, when evidence shows that our task and tools are mismatched, we seem to double down instead of course- correct. We knowingly load a fishing boat with an assortment of skis instead of nets, rods, line or tackle—sit in our boats with a ski balanced over the edge—and wonder why, after all these years, we still have no fish to show for all our efforts. We reject the seemingly obvious and logical limitations of our mismatched task-and-tools.

Research shows that, across the health sciences, we tend to target efforts on the people most burdened by inequities, instead of those whose actions are instrumental in shaping conditions generative of inequities [1,2]. We examine individual behaviours or knowledge limitations, instead of intervening on the (well-documented, well-known) systemic and predictable determinants of equity [3]. We fail to recognize the ways in which our own normative practices belie both our own scholarly standards and the evidence we produce [4]. We respond to global outcry for equity with empty rhetoric, asserting a false logic for transforming tenaciously unfair systems; justifying interventions aimed at changing the behaviours of people suffering the harms of systemic disadvantage, rather than addressing systems of harm; and convincing ourselves that sincerity, intention, benevolence, and an unwavering commitment to documenting inequities will surely result in change.

Agreement about the existence and consequences of inequities is widespread, and evidence of their distributional causes (i.e., of resources and power) well established [5,6]. Despite scientific advances, technology, and global commitments that should equip humanity with the know-how to ensure a basic standard of living and dignity for all, geographic, economic, and other social location in society remains deterministic of peoples' quality of life, health trajectories, and life expectancy [5]. Rather than targeting interventions at what we know gives rise to untenable conditions of daily life, most work focuses on convincing those people most affected by inequities to behave differently or cope better [7]. In recent waves of equity, diversity, and inclusion strategies, systematically disadvantaged folks are often targeted to be ‘more included’ in the harmful systems imagined to be improved simply by more visibly diverse peoples' willingness to participate in and endure them (and, implicitly, fix systems of disadvantage) [7]. Instead of investing in action to address causes, we pour energy and resources in reacting to differential outcomes. Perhaps worst of our perplexing habits are the many ways outlier success stories are offered as a means to satisfy actual hunger or suffering, or leveraged to ingratiate recipients of ‘aid’. We criticize vague groups of ‘decision makers’ and ‘power holders’ in ways that externalize causal conditions, creating an inaccurate and distracting belief that someone else (e.g., health systems leaders, governments, elected officials, international leaders) is responsible for change [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. We often do so with exasperation, overwhelmed and preoccupied with quick fixes to systems failures, as health-systems collapse and progress toward global targets unravel [12]. While people in decision making or power-holding roles are also responsible for how their everyday actions serve to redress (or not) health inequities, placing all responsibility on vague notions of ‘others’ abdicates our own complicity. Our everyday practices, everywhere, all together are actually the things that give life to distributional ideas, values, and norms.

While metaphors are useful tools for understanding how and where inequities exist [13,14], they can also serve as interrupters. Appreciating that many important contributions in this field have illuminated the role of systemic and structural determinants of health, the use of a ridiculous metaphor here is both explanatory and interventional. It draws attention to the relationship between our own normative practices—which we believe are often mundane, everyday sorts of choices and actions—and their impacts, across our spheres of influence, in ways that undermine our work and perpetuate the very inequities we say we are committed to resolving.

Matching task with tool is a choice. Interrupting this mismatch is a choice. The tenaciousness of inequities is not at all surprising. Millennia of ancient (largely other-than-western) wisdom about the finely balanced, interconnected, and relational nature of human existence on this Earth, remarkably, survive today, edifying what more recent science and scholarship also confirms: the ways in which humans think about others and the Earth manifest in societal problems that harm all of us [15]. Responding to their shared causes is infinitely possible and promising for the well-being of humanity, if we match task and tool. Drawing on our research and scholarship on overcoming inertia in health sciences' contributions to advancing equity, we offer below some ubiquitously beneficial everyday practices to interrupt long-standing habits of fishing with skis or digging with noodles. Each of these everyday practices illuminate powerful and mundane moments in our daily work, as situated within intersecting social systems, which are points of intervention on the distributional causes of inequities within each and every person's spheres of influence.

Five well-matched task-with-tool strategies anyone, anywhere, can do

Everyday practices open possibilities for advancing equity by illuminating feasible and impactful choices that anyone involved in health-related work can take to meaningfully respond to inequities. These practices do not replace efforts to intervene on macroeconomic policies, or on other structural or commercial determinants of health at play at various layers of the socioecological contexts that influence health, nor are they an exhaustive list; but they are likely to spur other day-to-day actions that can extend and compound our collective capacity for accelerating equity. Importantly, these everyday practices bring us into better coherence across our intentions, evidence, proclamations and actions.

1.Recognize your own role, agency, and responsibility. Decisions and actions made by people whose work affects health, across multiple intersecting spheres of influence, give rise to our collective conditions of daily living. Regardless of where we are situated in society, particularly those of us who are health professionals, economic leaders, influencers, or policy leaders (among others), our actions affect others [9]. Efforts to advance equity are unlikely to result in change if those of us actively leading research, policy or practice aimed at redressing inequities become distracted by vague, distant factors shaping the distribution of resources and power. Resources and power are not only distributed in grand, global, distal ways. They are distributed across all layers of socioecological systems, from micro-levels that encompass day-to-day interactions between people, ideas, organizations, and institutions through to meso-levels, such as within the work of municipalities or regions, and macro- and inherently global levels.Regardless of where we are situated, it is possible to integrate equity-advancing practices within our own everyday work. When we begin any effort with careful understanding of what our own spheres of influence are, and how they intersect with others, as members of an interconnected world, we demonstrate respect for the potential that rests within our actions. We open our imagination to possibility, simply by refusing to minimize the collective and cumulative impact of our intersecting spheres of influence and embracing the agency each of us holds within those spheres. Among tools that can support everyday reflection on role and responsibility include equity-responsive context mapping (see, for example, the How to Equity series) [16]. Take time every day to reflect on how you are exerting power through your own sphere of influence.

2.Unpack influential worldviews. Worldviews are sets of ideas and assumptions about how the world works, and what matters within it. As many brilliant scholars have argued, worldviews have a tricky way of reinforcing flawed lines of logic [15]. Assumptions buried within worldviews can reinforce streams of logic that do not serve us well, like under-resourcing upstream, population-level interventions for health promotion and illness prevention (including action on the structural determinants of health) in the context of overburdened health systems. In this case, a worldview narrowly focused on reacting to individual disease loses sight of the important benefits of also investing in collective thriving. Unpacking the worldviews we are invited to buy into and uphold is simple, and difficult. It invites us to ask questions about the everyday assumptions that shape how we make sense of the world, what we see as relevant within it, and how we understand the ways in which our contributions reinforce dominant worldviews, with deep curiosity.When western individualism, biobehaviorism, capitalism, or neoliberalism dominate our worldview, for example, we can convince ourselves that the current distribution of resources and power in society is the result of hard work, rather than the direct outcome of colonial policies that channel natural resources, wealth, and human cargo from the global south to north. Or, perhaps more insidious, as Labonté [17] argues, we can find ourselves in a complacent or even apathetic “retreat into technical proficiency” (p. 702) that may be both scientifically sound and dangerously passive. Extending from his call to declare ourselves, as people working in public health, as “partners with the revolutionary radicalism of those who follow in Virchow's footsteps,” unpacking worldviews is, again, not only about identifying dominant discourses of others. It is about becoming highly attuned to the lenses through which we, ourselves, see the world (see, for example, a the systematic equity action analysis framework) [8]. Every day, critically reflect on how you are enacting dominant ideas and assumptions.

3.Get clear about equity and its determinants. Equity, as something people are committed to advancing or promoting, is an increasingly ubiquitous word across the health sciences and beyond. Its ubiquitous use is almost universally vague, left undefined as though it carries a universal meaning. As is true with any normative ideal, clearly defining equity and its determinants is a necessary step for planning action [15]. When definitions are available, they often blur over the core essence of equity as an idea of justice or fairness—Perhaps leaning on an assumption that everyone, everywhere will understand justice in the same ways. We can strengthen our collective capacity to advance equity by dedicating time and energy in critically reflective dialogue about the philosophies of justice underpinning our notions of equity.We can extend shared reflection on our understandings of equity to set clear benchmarks and goals, interrogate how it is experienced, and identify the points of connection in our everyday work where equity choices come to life [8,15,16]. Metaphors can help us with this readying, as can a range of incredible resources available to support equity work. Often, we offer a metaphor from the ‘Water of Systems Change’ [14] to draw awareness to the systems we navigate. It offers a simple story: one fish comes up to another, and asks, “how's the water?” and the other fish responds, “what's water?!” A forest metaphor in our discussion of the determinants of equity, also draws attention to how we might understand the relationship between social and structural determinants of health, in the context of determinants of equity [15]. 4.Align intentions with actions. When it comes to advancing equity, our collective tolerance for empty rhetoric is remarkable. We warmly embrace empty promises, celebrating the declaration with little expectation or anticipation of anything more or, more often, tolerating interventions that target symptoms, rather than causes [8,15]. Even if we make mistakes or do not see the progress we might like to, our efforts to do anything about inequities are guaranteed to fail if we do nothing to align intentions with actions. This practice can be easily integrated any time you or your teams are facing a decision, simply by pausing to ask yourselves how the action proposed sits in relationship to what is known about the root causes of inequities in that context (see, for example, Plamondon's tool to assess alignment between intention and action [18]). Use this pause to hold each other to account for matching rhetoric with action.

5.Align actions with evidence. Akin to hollow declarations, equity actions misaligned with evidence about their causes are unlikely to nudge humanity toward different outcomes. Rather than taking up well-established directives on how best to do this work, we often see folks dive into developing their own, largely opinion-driven and crowd-sourced, strategies and actions that are easy to promote (they sound great!) and easy to support (they require very little substantive change to power holders!), but are predictably ineffective. Not all equity efforts are equity-promoting, or benevolent (even if well-intentioned). For example, offering encouraging words, positive peer reviews, or other scholarly endorsements of equity efforts that mismatch task with tool all risk entrenching inequities. Dialogue or action about inequities that does not intervene on the distribution of resources or power (e.g., on systemic advantage) [7,8,15] takes attention, resources, time and energy away from actually advancing equity. Any time we choose to act in greater alignment with evidence, we amplify the possibility of outcomes that actually advance equity [8]. Again, we recommend using equity-centred and evidence-informed tools mentioned above as antidotes for becoming entangled in everyday practices that undermine our capacity to promote equity in meaningful ways.

We can, indeed, fish with nets and rods, instead of skis. Digging with diggers, like shovels or tractors, is infinitely more productive than digging with noodles. When our tasks and tools are mismatched in the health sciences, we participate in entrenching power imbalances that rob all of us of our humanity. If even a small majority of us committed to bringing these five everyday practices into whatever work we do—whether in direct practice with patients or in negotiating a new treaty to guide our global efforts to respond to looming threats to our collective health—our choices to match task and tool can literally make a world of difference.
