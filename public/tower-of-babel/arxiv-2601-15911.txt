Abstract
. Our main objective in this work is to show how Sobolev orthogonal polynomials emerge as a useful tool within the framework of spectral methods for boundary-value problems. The solution of a boundary-value problem for a stationary Schrödinger equation on the unit ball can be studied from a variational perspective. In this variational formulation, a Sobolev inner product naturally arises. As test functions, we consider the linear space of the polynomials satisfying the boundary conditions on the sphere, and a basis of mutually orthogonal polynomials with respect to the Sobolev inner product is provided. The basis of the proposed method is given in terms of spherical harmonics and univariate Sobolev orthogonal polynomials. The connection formula between these Sobolev orthogonal polynomials and the classical orthogonal polynomials on the ball is established. Consequently, the Sobolev Fourier coefficients of a function satisfying the boundary value problem are recursively derived. Finally, one numerical experiment is presented.

1.
Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to numerically solve a Dirichlet boundary value problem on the
unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{d}=\{x:\|x\|^{2}\leq 1\}
, where
\|x\|^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{d}^{2}
defines the usual Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, we will consider the solution of the elliptic partial differential equation

(1.1)

-\Delta u+\lambda(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\kappa}u=f(x)\qquad x\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d},

with the Dirichlet boundary condition

(1.2)

u(x)=0\qquad x\in\partial\mathbb{B}^{d}={\mathbb{S}}^{d-1},

where
\lambda>0
,
\kappa
is a nonnegative integer, and
f(x)
is a several times continuously differentiable function on
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
.

For the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) the standard variational formulation reads as follows: Find
u\in H^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{B}}^{d})
such that

(1.3)

\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}\left[\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\lambda\,u(x)\,v(x)\,(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\kappa}\right]\,dx=\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}f(x)\,v(x)\,dx,\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{B}}^{d})

Introduce the bilinear form

(1.4)

\mathcal{A}(v,w)=\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}\left[\nabla v\cdot\nabla w+\lambda\,v(x)\,w(x)\,(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\kappa}\right]\,dx\quad\forall v,w\in H^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{B}}^{d}),

and the bounded linear functional

\mathcal{L}(v)=\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}f(x)\,v(x)\,dx,\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{B}}^{d}).

Therefore, the variational problem (
1.3
) can be expressed as follows: find
u\in H^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{B}}^{d})
for which
\mathcal{A}(u,v)=\mathcal{L}(v)\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{B}}^{d}).

It is straightforward to show
\mathcal{A}
is coercive and bounded. Assuming some mild conditions on
f(x)
the Lax-Milgram Theorem implies the existence of a unique solution
u
to (
1.3
).

Let
\Pi_{n}^{d}
be the space of polynomials of total degree at most
n
in
d
variables.
To implement a Galerkin method for obtaining an approximate solution to (
1.3
) we consider as our test functions the linear space of polynomials satisfying the boundary conditions at the sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}

(1.5)

\mathcal{R}_{n}^{d}=\left\{(1-\|x\|^{2})p(x):p(x)\in\Pi_{n}^{d}\right\}

and the problem can be stated as follows: find
u_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}^{d}
for which
\mathcal{A}(u,v)=\mathcal{L}(v)\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{R}_{n}^{d}
. Again, the Lax-Milgram Theorem implies the existence of a unique
u_{n}
for all
n
.

There exist many different methods to construct the approximate solution
u_{n}
. When the approximation in
L^{2}
norm is most relevant, orthogonal functions appear naturally. In this context, classical orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball have been used in spectral methods for solving partial differential equations
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
7
,
12
]
. One usual limitation in this technique is that the discretization of partial differential
equations by spectral methods generally leads to the solution of large systems of linear equations involving full matrices, which are usually badly conditioned. In some cases, for
d=2
and
d=3
, classical orthogonal polynomials have been used to devise sparse spectral methods (involving banded matrices, as opposed to full matrices) on the unit ball, see
[
14
,
15
]
.

Our approach to this problem differs from the usual one. Realizing that
the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}
defines an inner product in
H^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{B}}^{d})
, we can benefit from the variational formulation by constructing a basis of
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{d}
constituted by polynomials that are orthogonal with respect to
\mathcal{A}
.

Orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball with respect to an inner product that involves the integral of
\nabla f\cdot\nabla g
are usually called
multivariate Sobolev orthogonal polynomials
and have been studied in a few cases
[
10
,
11
,
16
]
. The study in this direction was
initiated in
[
16
]
, where the author considered the inner product

(1.6)

\langle f,g\rangle_{I}:={\frac{\lambda}{\omega_{d}}}\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}\nabla f(x)\cdot\nabla g(x)dx+{\frac{1}{\omega_{d}}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}}f(x)g(x)d\sigma,\quad\lambda>0,

and another one that has the last term on the right-hand side of
\langle f,g\rangle_{I}
replaced by
{f(0)g(0)}
. The orthogonal polynomials with respect to the inner product
\langle f,g\rangle_{I}
have the distinction that they are eigenfunctions of a second-order partial differential operator (see
[
11
]
). The case
\kappa=0
in (
1.4
) was considered in
[
10
]
.

However, to guarantee that our approximate solution satisfies the boundary condition (
1.2
) in this work we consider the Sobolev inner product defined in
\Pi^{d}
by means of the following expression:

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{S}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{A}\left((1-\|x\|^{2})u(x),(1-\|x\|^{2})v(x)\right)=\lambda\,\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\kappa+2}u(x)v(x)dx

(1.7)

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}\nabla((1-\|x\|^{2})u(x))\,\cdot\nabla((1-\|x\|^{2})v(x))dx.

It is easy to see that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{S}
defines an inner product in
\Pi^{d}
.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}(S)
the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree exactly
n
with respect to the inner product (
1.7
). Our first objective is to construct a basis for this space. To take full advantage of the central symmetry of (
1.7
) we will reproduce the structure of the classical orthogonal polynomials on the ball, writing the polynomials in spherical polar coordinates as the product of spherical harmonics and a radial part connected to the Jacobi polynomials.
Next, we will show how these Sobolev orthogonal polynomials appear as a useful tool in the framework of spectral methods for boundary value problems, since they will allow us to obtain easily the coefficients in the Fourier–Sobolev expansion of the solution to the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
),
avoiding the need of solving large linear systems of equations.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we recall the background materials on spherical harmonics and orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball. In Section 3, Sobolev orthogonal polynomials with respect to the inner product (
1.7
) are obtained in spherical polar coordinates. The connection formulas for the radial parts of these polynomials with the Jacobi polynomials are stated and, as a consequence, in Section 4, the Fourier–Sobolev coefficients of the solution to the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) are deduced in a recursive way. Finally, a numerical experiment is shown.

2.
Spherical harmonics and orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball

In this section, following
[
8
]
, we describe the background materials about orthogonal polynomials and spherical harmonics. The first subsection collects the properties of the Jacobi polynomials that are needed later. The second subsection presents the basic results on spherical harmonics and classical orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball.

2.1.
Classical Jacobi polynomials

We present the properties of the classical Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)
that we need subsequently. Most of these properties can be found in
[
13
]
. The Jacobi polynomial
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)
is normalized as follows:

(2.1)

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1)=\binom{n+\alpha}{n}=\frac{(\alpha+1)_{n}}{n!},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

For
\alpha,\beta>-1
, these
polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the Jacobi weight function

w_{\alpha,\beta}(t):=(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta},\qquad-1<x<1,

and their
L^{2}
norms in
L^{2}(w_{\alpha,\beta},[-1,1])
are given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle h_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}:=

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)^{2}\,w_{\alpha,\beta}(t)\,dt=\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}}{2n+\alpha+\beta+1}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\,\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{n!\,\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)},

in particular

h_{0}^{(\alpha,\beta)}=2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\,\Gamma(\beta+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+2)}.

The polynomial
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)
is of degree
n
, and its leading coefficient
k_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}

is given by

(2.3)

k_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\,\binom{2n+\alpha+\beta}{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Jacobi polynomials satisfy a three-term recurrence relation

\displaystyle 2(n+1)

\displaystyle(n+\alpha+\beta+1)(2n+\alpha+\beta)P_{n+1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)=

(2.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)\left\{(2n+\alpha+\beta+2)(2n+\alpha+\beta)t+\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\right\}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)

\displaystyle-2(n+\alpha)(n+\beta)(2n+\alpha+\beta+2)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t).

The derivative of a Jacobi polynomial is again a Jacobi polynomial,

(2.5)

\frac{d}{dt}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)=\frac{n+\alpha+\beta+1}{2}P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}(t).

The following relations between different families of the Jacobi polynomials can be found in
(
[
1
, Chapt. 22]
):

(2.6)

\displaystyle(1-t)P_{n}^{(\alpha+1,\beta)}(t)=\frac{2(n+\alpha+1)}{2n+\alpha+\beta+2}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)-\frac{2(n+1)}{2n+\alpha+\beta+2}P_{n+1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t),

(2.7)

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)=a_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}P_{n}^{(\alpha+1,\beta)}(t)-b_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta)}(t),

(2.8)

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)=a_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1)}(t)+b_{n}^{(\beta,\alpha)}P_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta+1)}(t),

where

(2.9)

a_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}=\frac{n+\alpha+\beta+1}{2\,n+\alpha+\beta+1},\quad b_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}=\frac{n+\beta}{2\,n+\alpha+\beta+1}.

They are useful to prove the following two relations that we need later.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
\alpha>-1
and
\beta>0
,

(2.10)

\displaystyle(1-t)\,\frac{d}{dt}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)=\alpha\,P_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta+1)}(t)-n\,P_{n}^{(\alpha-1,\beta+1)}(t),

(2.11)

\displaystyle-\alpha P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)+(1-t)\,\frac{d}{dt}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)=-(n+\alpha)\,P_{n}^{(\alpha-1,\beta+1)}(t).

Proof.

From (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), we deduce

\displaystyle(1-t)\,\frac{d}{dt}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)=\

\displaystyle(1-t)\,\frac{n+\alpha+\beta+1}{2}P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}(t)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{n+\alpha+\beta+1}{2n+\alpha+\beta+1}\left[(n+\alpha)\,P_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta+1)}(t)-n\,P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1)}(t)\right].

Replacing the last term by

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1)}(t)=\frac{2n+\alpha+\beta+1}{n+\alpha+\beta+1}P_{n}^{(\alpha-1,\beta+1)}(t)+\frac{n+\beta+1}{n+\alpha+\beta+1}P_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta+1)}(t),

according to (
2.7
), and simplifying the result, we obtain (
2.10
). The relation (
2.11
) is deduced from
(
2.10
) upon using
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(t)-P_{n}^{(\alpha-1,\beta)}(t)=P_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)
,
which follows from (
2.7
) and (
2.8
).
∎

2.2.
Orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball

For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we use the usual notation of
\|x\|
and
\langle x,y\rangle
to denote the Euclidean
norm of
x
and the dot product of
x,y
. The unit ball and the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
denoted, respectively, by

\mathbb{B}^{d}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|\leq 1\}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mathbb{S}^{d-1}:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\xi\|=1\}.

For
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, let
W_{\mu}
be the weight function defined by

W_{\mu}(x)=(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu},\qquad\|x\|<1.

The function
W_{\mu}
is integrable on the unit ball if
\mu>-1
, for which we denote the normalization
constant of
W_{\mu}
by
{b_{\mu}}
,

b_{\mu}:=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{B}^{d}}}\,W_{\mu}(x)\,dx\right)^{-1}=\frac{\Gamma(\mu+d/2+1)}{\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(\mu+1)}.

The classical orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball are orthogonal with respect to the inner product

(2.12)

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mu}=b_{\mu}\int_{{\mathbb{B}^{d}}}\,f(x)\,g(x)\,W_{\mu}(x)\,dx,

which is normalized in such a way that
\langle 1,1\rangle_{\mu}=1
.

Let
\Pi^{d}
denote the space of polynomials in
d
real variables. For
n=0,1,2,\ldots,

let
\Pi_{n}^{d}
denote the linear space of polynomials in several variables of (total) degree
at most
n
and let
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{d}
denote the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree

n
. It is well known that

\dim\Pi_{n}^{d}=\binom{n+d}{n}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\dim\mathcal{P}_{n}^{d}=\binom{n+d-1}{n}:=r_{n}^{d}.

A polynomial
P\in\Pi_{n}^{d}
is called orthogonal with respect to
W_{\mu}
on the ball if

\langle P,Q\rangle_{\mu}=0
for all
Q\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
, that is, if it is orthogonal to all polynomials of lower degree. Let
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}(W_{\mu})
denote the space of orthogonal polynomials of total degree
n
with respect to
W_{\mu}
. Then
\dim\,\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}(W_{\mu})=r_{n}^{d}.

For
n\geq 0
, let
\{P^{n}_{\alpha}(x):|\alpha|=n\}
denote a basis of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}(W_{\mu})
.
Then, every element of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}(W_{\mu})
is orthogonal to the polynomials of lower degree. If the
elements of the basis are also orthogonal to each other, that is,
\langle P_{\alpha}^{n},P_{\beta}^{n}\rangle_{\mu}=0
whenever
\alpha\neq\beta
, we call the basis mutually orthogonal. If, in addition,
\langle P_{\alpha}^{n},P_{\alpha}^{n}\rangle_{\mu}=1
, we call the basis orthonormal.

Harmonic polynomials of
d
-variables are homogeneous polynomials in
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{d}
that satisfy the Laplace equation
\Delta Y=0
, where

\Delta=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}+\ldots+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{d}^{2}}
is the usual Laplace operator. Let
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{d}
denote the space of harmonic polynomials of degree
n
. It is well known that

a_{n}^{d}:=\dim\mathcal{H}_{n}^{d}=\binom{n+d-1}{n}-\binom{n+d-3}{d-1}.

Spherical harmonics are the restriction of harmonic polynomials on the unit sphere. If
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{d}
, then
Y(x)=r^{n}Y(\xi)
in spherical–polar coordinates
x=r\xi
, so that

Y
is uniquely determined by its restriction on the sphere. We shall also use
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{d}
to denote the space of spherical harmonics of degree
n
. Let
d\sigma
denote the surface measure
and
\omega_{d}
denote the surface area,

\omega_{d}:=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}d\sigma=\frac{2\,\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}.

Spherical harmonics of different degrees are orthogonal with respect to the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}:=\frac{1}{\omega_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(\xi)g(\xi)d\sigma(\xi).

Moreover, by Euler’s equation for homogeneous polynomials,

x\cdot\nabla Y_{\nu}^{n}(x)=nY_{\nu}^{n}(x).

In spherical-polar coordinates
x=r\xi
,
r>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, a mutually orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}(W_{\mu})
can be given in terms of the Jacobi polynomials stated in Sec.2.1 and spherical harmonics. Let
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)
denote the usual Jacobi polynomial of degree
n
.
The following well-known result provides such an orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}(W_{\mu})
(see, for example,
[
6
]
).

Theorem 2.2
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
0\leq j\leq n/2
, let
\{Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}:1\leq\nu\leq a_{n-2j}^{d}\}
denote an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}_{n-2j}^{d}
. Define

(2.13)

P_{j,\nu}^{n,\mu}(x)=P_{j}^{(\mu,n-2j+\frac{d-2}{2})}(2\,\|x\|^{2}-1)\,Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x).

Then, the set
\{P_{j,\nu}^{n,\mu}(x):0\leq j\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\,1\leq\nu\leq a_{n-2j}^{d}\}
is a mutually
orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}(W_{\mu})
. More precisely,

\langle P_{j,\nu}^{n,\mu}(x),P_{k,\eta}^{m,\mu}(x)\rangle_{\mu}=H_{j,n}^{\mu}\delta_{n,m}\,\delta_{j,k}\,\delta_{\nu,\eta},

where
H_{j,n}^{\mu}
is given by

(2.14)

H_{j,n}^{\mu}:=\frac{(\mu+1)_{j}(\frac{d}{2})_{n-j}(n-j+\mu+\frac{d}{2})}{j!(\mu+\frac{d+2}{2})_{n-j}(n+\mu+\frac{d}{2})},

where
(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\ldots(a+n-1)
denotes the Pochhammer symbol.

3.
Sobolev orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball

Let us consider the
Sobolev
inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\nabla,\mathbb{B}^{d}}

\displaystyle:=\lambda\,\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}f(x)g(x)(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\kappa}dx+\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}\nabla f(x)\,\cdot\nabla g(x)dx,

where
\lambda>0
. And let
\{Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}:1\leq\nu\leq a_{n-2j}^{d}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{n-2j}^{d}
.
To construct an orthogonal basis for the linear space
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{d}
defined in (
1.5
), we can try polynomials of the form

R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x):=q_{j}(2\|x\|^{2}-1)Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x),

where
q_{j}
is a polynomial of degree j in one variable satisfying the orthogonality condition

\langle(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x),(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{k,\eta}^{m}(x)\rangle_{\nabla,\mathbb{B}^{d}}=K_{j,n}\delta_{n,m}\,\delta_{j,k}\,\delta_{\nu,\eta}.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\{Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}:1\leq\nu\leq a_{n-2j}^{d}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{n-2j}^{d}
and let

R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x):=q_{j}(2\|x\|^{2}-1)Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x)

be a basis of Sobolev mutually orthogonal polynomials then
q_{j}=q_{j}^{(\beta)}
is orthogonal with respect to the univariate
Sobolev inner product

\displaystyle(f,g)_{\beta}:=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}\int_{-1}^{1}q_{j}(t)q_{k}(t)(1-t)^{\kappa+2}(1+t)^{\beta}dt

(3.1)

\displaystyle+\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)q_{j}(t)\right)\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)q_{k}(t)\right)(1+t)^{\beta+1}dt,

where
\beta=n-2j+\frac{d-2}{2}
.

Proof.

From (
1.7
) we get

\displaystyle\langle R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x),R_{k,\eta}^{m}(x)\rangle_{S}=

\displaystyle\lambda\,\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)R_{k,\eta}^{m}(x)(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\kappa+2}dx

(3.2)

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}\nabla((1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x))\,\cdot\nabla((1-\|x\|^{2})R_{k,\eta}^{m}(x))dx.

The proof uses the following identity:

(3.3)

\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}f(x)dx=\int_{0}^{1}r^{d-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(r\,\xi)\,d\sigma(\xi)\,dr

that arises from the spherical-polar coordinates
x=r\,\xi
,
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. In this way, the first integral in (
3.2
) gives

\displaystyle I_{1}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)R_{k,\eta}^{m}(x)(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\kappa+2}dx=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}q_{j}(2r^{2}-1)q_{k}(2r^{2}-1)(1-r^{2})^{\kappa+2}r^{n-2j+m-2k+d-1}dr\times

\displaystyle\times\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(\xi)Y_{\nu}^{m-2k}(\xi)d\sigma(\xi)\quad\text{ (with }n=m,j=k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}q_{j}(2r^{2}-1)q_{k}(2r^{2}-1)(1-r^{2})^{\kappa+2}r^{2n-4j+d-1}dr

\displaystyle\times\omega_{d}\delta_{n-2j,m-2k}\delta_{\nu,\eta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}q_{j}(t)q_{k}(t)\left(\frac{1-t}{2}\right)^{\kappa+2}\left(\frac{1+t}{2}\right)^{\frac{2n-4j+d-2}{2}}\frac{1}{4}dt

\displaystyle\times\omega_{d}\delta_{n-2j,m-2k}\delta_{\nu,\eta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{\kappa+3+n-2j+d/2}}\int_{-1}^{1}q_{j}(t)q_{k}(t)(1-t)^{\kappa+2}(1+t)^{n-2j+\frac{d}{2}-1}dt

\displaystyle\times\omega_{d}\delta_{n-2j,m-2k}\delta_{\nu,\eta},

after the change of variable
t=2r^{2}-1
.

For the second integral, integration by parts gives

\displaystyle I_{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}\nabla((1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x))\,\cdot\nabla((1-\|x\|^{2})R_{k,\eta}^{m}(x))dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}\Delta((1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x))(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{k,\eta}^{m}(x)dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}\operatorname{div}\left(\nabla((1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x))(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{k,\eta}^{m}(x)\right)dx=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}\Delta((1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x))(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{k,\eta}^{m}(x)dx,

which follows from the divergence theorem and the fact that
1-\|x\|^{2}
vanishes on the sphere.

Now, let us write

\displaystyle f_{j}(\|x\|^{2})

\displaystyle:=(1-\|x\|^{2})q_{j}(2r^{2}-1)

in this way

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle((1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x))=\Delta\left(f_{j}(\|x\|^{2})Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x)\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}^{2}\left(f_{j}(\|x\|^{2})Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta(f_{j}(\|x\|^{2}))Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x)+4f_{j}^{\prime}(\|x\|^{2})\langle x,\nabla\rangle Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x)+f_{j}(\|x\|^{2})\Delta Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x)

\displaystyle=\left[4\|x\|^{2}f_{j}^{\prime\prime}(\|x\|^{2}))+2(2n-4j+d)f_{j}^{\prime}(\|x\|^{2})\right]Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x),

since

\displaystyle\langle x,\nabla\rangle Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x)

\displaystyle=(n-2j)Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x),

\displaystyle\Delta Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\Delta(f_{j}(\|x\|^{2}))

\displaystyle=2df_{j}^{\prime}(\|x\|^{2})+4\|x\|^{2}f_{j}^{\prime\prime}(\|x\|^{2}).

Thus, using polar coordinates
x=r\xi
, for the second integral we obtain

\displaystyle I_{2}=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}\left[4\|x\|^{2}f_{j}^{\prime\prime}(\|x\|^{2}))+2(2n-4j+d)f_{j}^{\prime}(\|x\|^{2})\right]f_{k}(\|x\|^{2})Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(x)Y_{\eta}^{m-2k}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}\left[4r^{2}f_{j}^{\prime\prime}(r^{2}))+2(n-2j+d)f_{j}^{\prime}(r^{2})\right]f_{k}(r^{2})r^{2n-4j+d-1}dr

\displaystyle\times\omega_{d}\delta_{n-2j,m-2k}\delta_{\nu,\eta}.

Next, since
f_{k}(r^{2})r^{2n-4j+d}
vanishes at the ends of the interval
[0,1]
, integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}4rf_{j}^{\prime\prime}(r^{2})f_{k}(r^{2})r^{2n-4j+d}dr=-2\int_{0}^{1}f_{j}^{\prime}(r^{2})\frac{d}{dr}\left(f_{k}(r^{2})r^{2n-4j+d}\right)dr

\displaystyle=-4\int_{0}^{1}f_{j}^{\prime}(r^{2})f_{k}^{\prime}(r^{2})r^{2n-4j+d+1}dr-2(2n-4j+d)\int_{0}^{1}f_{j}^{\prime}(r^{2})f_{k}(r^{2})r^{2n-4j+d-1}dr,

and we get

I_{2}=4\int_{0}^{1}f_{j}^{\prime}(r^{2})f_{k}^{\prime}(r^{2})r^{2n-4j+d+1}dr\omega_{d}\delta_{n-2j,m-2k}\delta_{\nu,\eta}

Finally, using the change of variables
t=2r^{2}-1
, we deduce

\displaystyle 4\int_{0}^{1}f_{j}^{\prime}(r^{2})

\displaystyle f_{k}^{\prime}(r^{2})r^{2n-4j+d+1}dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dr}f_{j}(r^{2})\frac{d}{dr}f_{k}(r^{2})r^{2n-4j+d-1}dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n-2j+d/2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(-q_{j}(t)+(1-t)q_{j}^{\prime}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(-q_{k}(t)+(1-t)q_{k}^{\prime}(t)\right)(1+t)^{n-2j+d/2}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n-2j+d/2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)q_{j}(t)\right)\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)q_{k}(t)\right)(1+t)^{n-2j+d/2}dt,

and the result follows.
∎

4.
The radial parts

From now on, we will denote by
q_{k}^{(\beta)}
the polynomial of degree
k

orthogonal with respect to the Sobolev inner product (
3.1
),
and normalized in such a way that it has the same leading coefficient as the Jacobi polynomial
P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)
, that is,

(4.1)

\displaystyle q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)=k^{(1,\beta)}_{k}\,t^{k}+\ldots,\quad k_{k}^{(1,\beta)}:=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\binom{2k+\beta+1}{k}.

With this normalization, we have
q_{0}^{(\beta)}(t)=P_{0}^{(1,\beta)}(t)=1
.
Our next result relates
q_{k}^{(\beta)}
with the Jacobi polynomials.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\beta>-1
. Then, for
k\geq 0
,

(4.2)

P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)=q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)+\sum_{j=k-(\kappa+1)}^{k-1}a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}q_{j}^{(\beta)}(t)\quad\text{with}\quad a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}=\frac{(P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},q_{j}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}}{(q_{j}^{(\beta)},q_{j}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}}.

Proof.

Let us expand
P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)
in terms of the sequence of polynomials

\{q_{j}^{(\beta)}(t)\}_{j\geqslant 0}

which are orthogonal with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\beta}

(4.3)

P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}q_{j}^{(\beta)}(t)\quad\text{with}\quad a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}=\frac{(P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},q_{j}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}}{(q_{j}^{(\beta)},q_{j}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}}.

Since
q_{k}^{(\beta)}
is normalized so that its leading coefficient is the same as the one of
P_{k}^{(1,{\beta})}
, it follows that
a^{(\beta)}_{k,k}=1.

We now calculate
a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}
for
0\leq j<k
. We need to compute

\displaystyle(P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},q_{j}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,q_{j}^{(\beta)}(t)(1-t)^{\kappa+1}\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt+

(4.4)

\displaystyle+\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\right)\,\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)q_{j}^{(\beta)}(t)\right)\omega_{0,\beta+1}(t)dt.

From (
2.11
) we have

(4.5)

\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\right)=-(k+1)P_{k}^{(0,\beta+1)}(t).

Therefore the second summand in (
4.4
) is null for
0\leqslant j<k
, whereas the first integral also vanishes for
0\leqslant j<k-(\kappa+1)
. In this way expression (
4.3
) contains only
\kappa+2
terms.
∎

Of course, the connection formula (
4.2
) can be identified as the Gram–Schmidt algorithm applied to the sequence
\{P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\}_{k\geqslant 0}
to obtain an orthogonal basis with respect to the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\beta}
, but here, the algorithm uses only the
\kappa+1
previous polynomials.

4.1.
An alternative algorithm

We show that the coefficients in (
4.2
) can be obtained recursively. The key for this algorithm is a
2\kappa+3
term recurrence formula for the Jacobi polynomials
\{P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\}_{k\geqslant 0}
.
With
\alpha=1
, the three-term recurrence relation (
2.4
) can be written as

(4.6)

(1-t)P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)=a_{k}P_{k+1}^{(1,\beta)}(t)+b_{k}P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)+c_{k}P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}(t),

where

\displaystyle a_{k}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2(k+1)(k+\beta+2)}{(2k+\beta+2)(2k+\beta+3)},

\displaystyle b_{k}=

\displaystyle\frac{4(k+1)(k+\beta+1)}{(2k+\beta+1)(2k+\beta+3)},

\displaystyle c_{k}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2(k+1)(k+\beta)}{(2k+\beta+1)(2k+\beta+2)}.

Iterating (
4.6
) we can obtain a representation for
(1-t)^{\kappa+1}P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)
in terms of the Jacobi polynomials as follows:

(4.7)

(1-t)^{\kappa+1}P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)=\sum_{j=k-\kappa-1}^{k+\kappa+1}\gamma_{k,j}^{(\kappa+1)}P_{j}^{(1,\beta)}(t),

where the coefficients
\gamma_{k,j}^{(\kappa)}
satisfy the recurrence

\displaystyle\gamma_{k,j}^{(h+1)}=

\displaystyle c_{j+1}\gamma_{k,j+1}^{(h)}+b_{j}\gamma_{k,j}^{(h)}+a_{j-1}\gamma_{k,j-1}^{(h)},

for
h=0,\ldots\kappa
, assuming
\gamma_{k,j}^{(h)}=0
for
j>k+h+1
or
j<k-h-1
, and the initial conditions

\displaystyle\gamma_{k,k+1}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=a_{k},\quad\gamma_{k,k}^{(0)}=b_{k},\quad\gamma_{k,k-1}^{(0)}=c_{k}.

Let us assume
k\geqslant\kappa+1
. Next, we compute the numerator of
a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}
for
k-\kappa-1\leqslant j<k
in (
4.3
).

Proposition 4.2
.

Let us denote

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=((P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},q_{k-\kappa-1}^{(\beta)})_{\beta},\ldots,(P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},q_{k-1}^{(\beta)})_{\beta})^{t},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{k,k-\kappa-1}^{(\kappa+1)},\ldots,\gamma_{k,k-1}^{(\kappa+1)})^{t},

then

A_{k}=C_{k}\Gamma_{k}

where
C_{k}
is a lower banded triangular matrix with entries

c_{i,j}=\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}\int_{-1}^{1}q_{i}^{(\beta)}(t)\,P_{j}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt,

for
i=k-\kappa-1,\ldots,k-1
and
j=k-\kappa-1,\ldots,i
.

Proof.

From (
4.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle(P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},q_{i}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,q_{i}^{(\beta)}(t)(1-t)^{\kappa+1}\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt

(4.8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k-\kappa-1}^{k+\kappa+1}\gamma_{k,j}^{(\kappa+1)}\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}\int_{-1}^{1}q_{i}^{(\beta)}(t)\,P_{j}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k-\kappa-1}^{i}\gamma_{k,j}^{(\kappa+1)}c_{i,j},

where

c_{i,j}=\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}\int_{-1}^{1}q_{i}^{(\beta)}(t)\,P_{j}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt.

Observe that
c_{i,j}=0
if
i<j
.
∎

To compute recursively
A_{k}
we don’t need to compute all the integrals in
C_{k}
since the first
\kappa
rows in the matrix are known from the previous iterations and the last row
can be obtained from (
4.2
) with
k
replaced by
k-1
. In fact, for
k-\kappa-1\leqslant j<k-1
we have

\displaystyle c_{k-1,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}\int_{-1}^{1}q_{k-1}^{(\beta)}(t)\,P_{j}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\left(P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}(t)-\sum_{h=k-\kappa-2}^{k-2}a^{(\beta)}_{k-1,h}q_{h}^{(\beta)}(t)\right)\,\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt

\displaystyle=-\sum_{h=k-\kappa-2}^{k-2}a^{(\beta)}_{k-1,h}c_{h,j}.

The diagonal entries in
C_{k}
satisfy

c_{i,i}=\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}\int_{-1}^{1}q_{i}^{(\beta)}(t)\,P_{i}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt=\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}h_{i}^{(1,\beta)}.

Finally, the denominator of
a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}
for
k-\kappa-1\leqslant j<k
in (
4.3
) can be obtained recursively. For every value
k
we have

\displaystyle(q_{k}^{(\beta)},q_{k}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}

\displaystyle=(P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},q_{k}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}=\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)(1-t)^{\kappa+1}\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt+

(4.9)

\displaystyle+\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\right)\,\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)\right)\omega_{0,\beta+1}(t)dt

Since
P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)
and
q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)
have the same leading coefficient, from (
2.11
) we deduce

\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\right)\,\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)\right)\omega_{0,\beta+1}(t)dt=(k+1)^{2}h_{k}^{(0,\beta+1)}.

Now, for the first integral in (
4.9
) it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)(1-t)^{\kappa+1}\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt

(4.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2^{\kappa+3}}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\sum_{j=k-\kappa-1}^{k+\kappa+1}\gamma_{k,j}^{(\kappa+1)}P_{j}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\right)\,q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k-\kappa-1}^{k}\gamma_{k,j}^{(\kappa+1)}c_{k,j},

and therefore we conclude

\displaystyle(q_{k}^{(\beta)},q_{k}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k-\kappa-1}^{k}\gamma_{k,j}^{(\kappa+1)}c_{k,j}+(k+1)^{2}h_{k}^{(0,\beta+1)}.

The case
0<k<\kappa+1
follows exactly in the same way, just taking into account that
P_{j}^{(1,\beta)}(t)=q_{j}^{(\beta)}(t)=0
for
j<0
and all the sums in the previous reasoning starts at
j=0
.

4.2.
The case
\kappa=0

In the case
\kappa=0
relation (
4.2
) contains only two terms and we can give a closed representation for the coefficients.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\kappa=0
and
\beta>-1
. Then, for
k\geq 0
,

(4.11)

P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)=q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)+d_{k}^{(\beta)}q_{k-1}^{(\beta)}(t),

where
d_{k}^{(\beta)}
is defined by means

(4.12)

d_{0}^{(\beta)}=0,\quad d_{k}^{(\beta)}=-\frac{\lambda k(k+\beta+1)}{4(2k+\beta)(2k+\beta+1)}\frac{h_{k}^{(1,\beta)}}{\widetilde{h}_{k-1}^{(\beta)}},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,

with
\widetilde{h}_{k-1}^{(\beta)}=(q_{k-1}^{(\beta)},q_{k-1}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}
.

Proof.

In this case relation (
4.2
) contains only two terms and reduces to

P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)=\sum_{j=k-1}^{k}a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}q_{j}^{(\beta)}(t)\quad\text{with}\quad a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}=\frac{(P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},q_{j}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}}{(q_{j}^{(\beta)},q_{j}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}}.

Again, since
q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)=k_{k}^{(1,{\beta})}t^{k}+\ldots
, it follows that
a^{(\beta)}_{k,k}=1.

To compute
a^{(\beta)}_{k,k-1}
we proceed as before

\displaystyle(P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},q_{k-1}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{8}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,q_{k-1}^{(\beta)}(t)(1-t)\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt+

\displaystyle+\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\right)\,\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)q_{k-1}^{(\beta)}(t)\right)\omega_{0,\beta+1}(t)dt,

where the second integral vanishes by virtue of (
4.5
). Let us denote
d_{k}^{(\beta)}:=a^{(\beta)}_{k,k-1}
. Then expression (
4.12
) follows by

\displaystyle(P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},q_{k-1}^{(\beta)})_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{8}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,q_{k-1}^{(\beta)}(t)(1-t)\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt=-\frac{\lambda}{8}\frac{k_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}}{k_{k}^{(1,\beta)}}h_{k}^{(1,\beta)}

∎

Our next proposition shows that the coefficients
d_{k}^{(\beta)}
satisfy a nonlinear recursive relation.

Proposition 4.4
.

The coefficients
d_{k}^{(\beta)}
satisfy the following recurrence relation:

(4.13)

\displaystyle d_{1}^{(\beta)}=\frac{(P_{1}^{(1,\beta)},P_{0}^{(1,\beta)})_{\beta}}{(P_{0}^{(0,\beta)},P_{0}^{(1,\beta)})_{\beta}},

(4.14)

\displaystyle d_{k}^{(\beta)}=\frac{(P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)})_{\beta}}{(P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)},P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)})_{\beta}-d_{k-1}^{(\beta)}\,(P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)},P_{k-2}^{(1,\beta)})_{\beta}},\quad k\geq 2,

Proof.

Relation (
4.13
) follows from (
4.11
) for
k=1
, and

q_{0}^{(\beta)}(t)=P_{0}^{(1,\beta)}(t)=1
.
Next, iterate identity (
4.11
) to obtain

\displaystyle q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)

\displaystyle=P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)-d_{k}^{(\beta)}q_{k-1}^{(\beta)}(t)

\displaystyle=P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)-d_{k}^{(\beta)}P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}(t)+d_{k}^{(\beta)}d_{k-1}^{(\beta)}q_{k-2}^{(\beta)}(t),

and (
4.14
) follows from
(q_{k}^{(\beta)},P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)})_{\beta}=0
and considering that

(q_{k-2}^{(1,\beta)},P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)})_{\beta}=(P_{k-2}^{(1,\beta)},P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)})_{\beta}.

∎

The inner products in (
4.14
) can be explicitly computed in terms of the parameters
k_{k}^{(1,\beta)}
and
h_{k}^{(1,\beta)}
. In fact, using (
4.5
) we have

\displaystyle(P_{k}^{(1,\beta)},P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)})_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{8}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\,P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}(t)(1-t)\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{8}\frac{k_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}}{k_{k}^{(1,\beta)}}h_{k}^{(1,\beta)}=-\frac{\lambda}{8}\frac{k(k+1)2^{\beta+3}}{(2k+\beta)(2k+\beta+1)(2k+\beta+2)}.

On the other hand

\displaystyle(P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)},P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)})_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{8}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\right)^{2}(1-t)\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt

\displaystyle+\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\right)\right)^{2}\omega_{0,\beta+1}(t)dt.

For the first integral in this expression, using (
2.6
) we get

(1-t)P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}(t)=\frac{2k}{2k+\beta}\left(P_{k-1}^{(0,\beta)}(t)-P_{k}^{(0,\beta)}(t)\right)

and therefore

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\left(P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle(1-t)\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt=\frac{2k}{2k+\beta}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\left(P_{k-1}^{(0,\beta)}(t)-P_{k}^{(0,\beta)}(t)\right)\omega_{1,\beta}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2k}{2k+\beta}\left(\frac{k_{k-1}^{(0,\beta)}}{k_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}}h_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}+\frac{k_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}}{k_{k}^{(0,\beta)}}h_{k}^{(0,\beta)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2k^{2}2^{\beta+3}}{(2k+\beta-1)(2k+\beta)(2k+\beta+1)}

For the second integral, again in view of (
4.12
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)P_{k-1}^{(1,\beta)}(t)\right)\right)^{2}\omega_{0,\beta+1}(t)dt

\displaystyle=k^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(P_{k-1}^{(0,\beta+1)}(t)\right)^{2}\omega_{0,\beta+1}(t)dt

\displaystyle=k^{2}h_{k-1}^{(0,\beta+1)}=\frac{k^{2}2^{\beta+3}}{2(2k+\beta)}

Therefore, after some obvious cancelations (
4.14
) can be written as

\displaystyle d_{k}^{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{k+1}{(2k+\beta+1)(2k+\beta+2)}}{-\left(\frac{2k}{(2k+\beta-1)(2k+\beta+1)}+\frac{4k}{\lambda}\right)+d_{k-1}^{\beta}\frac{k-1}{(2k+\beta-1)(2k+\beta-2)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{t_{k+1}}{(2k+\beta+1)}}{-\left(\frac{1}{(2k+\beta-1)}+\frac{1}{(2k+\beta+1)}+\frac{4(2k+\beta)}{\lambda}\right)t_{k}+d_{k-1}^{\beta}\frac{t_{k-1}}{(2k+\beta-1)}}

where
t_{k}=\frac{k}{2k+\beta}
.

The recurrence relation (
4.14
) shows that
d_{k}^{\beta}
can be expressed as a continuous fraction. In this way, we can express
d_{k}^{\beta}
in terms of a rational function of
\lambda
whose numerator and denominator are, respectively, the
k
th and
k+1
th elements of a sequence of orthogonal polynomials. Consequently, we have

Lemma 4.5
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, define the polynomials
r_{k}^{(\beta)}(s)
by
r_{-1}^{(\beta)}(s)=0,r_{0}^{(\beta)}(s)=1,
and the recurrence relation

\displaystyle\frac{t_{k+1}}{2k+\beta+1}r_{k+1}^{(\beta)}(s)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{1}{(2k+\beta-1)}+\frac{1}{(2k+\beta+1)}+(2k+\beta)\,s\right)t_{k}r_{k}^{(\beta)}(s)

(4.15)

\displaystyle+\frac{t_{k-1}}{(2k+\beta-1)}r_{k-1}^{(\beta)}(s).

Then

d_{k}^{\beta}=\frac{r_{k}^{(\beta)}(\frac{4}{\lambda})}{r_{k+1}^{(\beta)}(\frac{4}{\lambda})}

Therefore, the three-term relation in (
4.5
) offers an effective way to generate
r_{k}^{(\beta)}
.

5.
The approximate solution of the BVP

Let us consider the Fourier expansion of the solution of the BVP (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) in terms of the orthogonal system of polynomials
\{(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)\}
, with
R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)
as defined in Prop.
3.1

(5.1)

u(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\sum_{\nu}\widehat{u}_{j,\nu}^{n}(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x),

with

\widehat{u}_{j,\nu}^{n}=\frac{\langle u(x),(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)\rangle_{\nabla,\mathbb{B}^{d}}}{\|(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)\|_{\nabla,\mathbb{B}^{d}}^{2}}.

In this way, the coefficients
\widehat{u}_{j,\nu}^{n}
satisfy

\displaystyle\widehat{u}_{j,\nu}^{n}

\displaystyle\|(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)\|_{\nabla,\mathbb{B}^{d}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}u(x)(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\kappa+1}R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)dx+\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}\nabla u(x)\,\cdot\nabla\left[(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)\right]dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}\left[-\Delta u(x)+\lambda(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\kappa}u(x)\right](1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}f(x)(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)dx.

This identity is where the
fully diagonalized spectral method for the BVP
cames from.

Using spherical polar coordinates, relation (
4.2
) provides a connection formula for the classical and Sobolev orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball. In fact, with
\mu=1,\beta=n-2k+\frac{d-2}{2}
in (
2.13
), we have

\displaystyle P_{k,\nu}^{n,1}(x)

\displaystyle=P_{k}^{(1,\beta)}(2\,\|x\|^{2}-1)\,Y_{\nu}^{n-2k}(x)

\displaystyle=\left[q_{k}^{(\beta)}(t)+\sum_{j=k-(\kappa+1)}^{k-1}a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}q_{j}^{(\beta)}(t)\right]Y_{\nu}^{n-2k}(x)

(5.2)

\displaystyle=R_{k,\nu}^{n}(x)+\sum_{j=k-(\kappa+1)}^{k-1}a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}R_{j,\nu}^{n-2(k-j)}(x)

Let us denote

\widetilde{f}_{j,\nu}^{n}:=\widehat{u}_{j,\nu}^{n}\|(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)\|_{\nabla,\mathbb{B}^{d}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}f(x)(1-\|x\|^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x)dx.

If we denote by
\widehat{f}_{j,\nu}^{n,1}
the Fourier coefficient of
f
in the basis of classical
ball polynomials,
P_{j,\nu}^{n,1}
, expression (
5.2
) can be written as follows

(5.3)

\widehat{f}_{k,\nu}^{n,1}H_{k,n}^{1}=\widetilde{f}_{k,\nu}^{n}+\sum_{j=k-(\kappa+1)}^{k-1}a^{(\beta)}_{k,j}\widetilde{f}_{j,\nu}^{n-2(k-j)}.

Therefore, we obtain a recursive method to obtain the coefficients
\widetilde{f}_{k,\nu}^{n}
.
The computation of the Fourier coefficients of
f(x)
in the
L^{2}
basis
\widehat{f}_{j,\nu}^{n,1}
could be accomplished with
fast transforms for orthogonal polynomials in the unit ball, as described in
[
9
]
. Observe that the algorithm devised in Section 4.1 to compute the coefficients

in (
5.2
) can be used simultaneously to implement (

).

6.
A numerical experiment

In this section, we analyze a numerical experiment to explore the reliability and accuracy of the Sobolev spectral method for solving Dirichlet boundary problems on the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{d}
.

We consider a second-order Dirichlet boundary value problem associated with the non-homogeneous Schrödinger equation with potential
V(x)=1
on the unit circle, i.e. the case
\kappa=0,\lambda=8,d=2
of problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2

To illustrate the convergence of the Sobolev spectral method we have computed the Fourier-Sobolev partial sums

u_{N}(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\sum_{\nu}\widehat{u}_{j,\nu}^{n}(1-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})R_{j,\nu}^{n}(x_{1},x_{2})

for
N=0,1,\ldots,7
. Observe that the computation of
u_{N}(x_{1},x_{2})
implies
(N+1)(N+2)/2
orthogonal polynomials. Whereas the Sobolev orthogonal polynomials were computed symbolically, the Fourier coefficients were computed numerically using an adaptative cubature procedure on the unit disk.
