Persuasion at Play: Understanding Misinformation Dynamics in Demographic-Aware Human-LLM Interactions
Angana Borah, Rada Mihalcea, Verónica Pérez-Rosas

Abstract
Existing challenges in misinformation exposure and susceptibility vary across demographics, as some populations are more vulnerable to misinformation than others. Large language models (LLMs) introduce new dimensions to these challenges through their ability to generate persuasive content at scale and reinforcing existing biases. This study investigates the bidirectional persuasion dynamics between LLMs and humans when exposed to misinformative content. We analyze human-to-LLM influence using human-stance datasets and assess LLM-to-human influence by generating LLM-based persuasive arguments. Additionally, we use a multi-agent LLM framework to analyze the spread of misinformation under persuasion among demographic-oriented LLM agents. Our findings show that demographic factors influence LLM susceptibility, with up to 15 percentage point differences in correctness across groups. Multi-agent LLMs also exhibit echo chamber behavior, aligning with human-like group polarization patterns. Therefore, this work highlights demographic divides in misinformation dynamics and offers insights for future interventions.

2
Related Work

LLM-generated Persuasion.
Recent research has examined the persuasive capabilities of LLMs, including their ability to influence others
Gabriel et al. (2024)
;
Matz et al. (2024)
and their susceptibility to being influenced
Griffin et al. (2023)
;
Chen et al. (2024)
. Studies have shown that LLMs can play a dual role in persuasion: they can be beneficial, as shown by
Gabriel et al. (2024)
, and potentially harmful, as highlighted by
Danry et al. (2022)
. Understanding these dynamics is critical, as LLMs are increasingly integrated into human-facing applications, from education and customer service to social media and healthcare tools
Andrew (2024)
;
Nair et al. (2024)
. Their persuasive power can shape opinions, behaviors, and beliefs, making it essential to assess how they influence human thought processes and decision-making
Breum et al. (2024)
;
Salvi et al. (2024)
.

Human-LLM Misinformation Dynamics.
Several studies have examined the impact of LLM-generated misinformation on humans.
Chen and Shu (2023)
found that such misinformation is more difficult to detect for both humans and automated systems. Moreover, recent studies have shown that LLM-generated misinformation can significantly degrade the performance of Open-Domain Question Answering (ODQA) systems, highlighting the cascading effects of synthetic misinformation
Pan et al. (2023)
. Furthermore, LLMs have been shown to be vulnerable to misinformation attacks that can alter their internal knowledge representations
Han et al. (2024)
. Despite these findings, limited research has explored the mutual influence of persuasion in humans and LLMs
Salvi et al. (2024)
. To our knowledge, our study is the first to systematically compare and contrast the quality and impact of human and LLM-driven persuasion in the context of misinformation, offering novel insights into their bidirectional interactions.

Multi-Agent LLM Simulations.
LLMs have been increasingly studied as tools to simulate social systems
Park et al. (2023)
;
Zhou et al. (2024)
. Previous work has shown that LLM agents produce plausible individual and emergent social behaviors, collaborating or competing to achieve complex social goals. Although computational models have been developed to simulate human susceptibility to misinformation in various demographics
Liu et al. (2024)
, multi-agent LLM simulations remain underexplored in this domain. For instance,
Li et al. (2024)
examined how LLM personality traits, such as extraversion, influence the dissemination of information within groups. However, their work did not address demographic-oriented LLM simulations, leaving open questions on how demographic factors shape misinformation dynamics in multi-agent LLM systems. Our study addresses this gap by introducing, to our knowledge, the first use of demographic-aware multi-agent LLM interactions in the context of misinformation.

3
PANDORA Framework

The PANDORA framework comprises three components to explore persuasion dynamics on misinformation under single-agent (LLM-to-human and human-to-LLM) and multi-agent LLM settings.

3.1
LLM-to-Human Persuasion

Our LLM-to-Human persuasion component examines how persuasive content generated by LLMs influences human beliefs. Fig.
1
(1) shows an overview of our setup, where LLMs produce both refuting and supporting persuasive arguments for a given claim, and their impact on human judgment is assessed through a two-stage process:

(1) LLM Persuasion
:
To generate persuasive texts, we create prompts grounded in a misinformation and persuasion taxonomy
Enestrom et al. (2024)
(see sample prompts in Appendix
C.1
). For each claim, the LLM produces arguments in favor and against the statement. Fig.
3
shows an example, and additional examples are provided in Table
4
.

(2) Human Response Evaluation
: We conduct a human study to assess how individuals from diverse demographic groups (rural, urban, female, male, young, and old) respond to persuasive texts generated by LLMs. As shown in Fig.
3
, participants review the claim and associated arguments (produced by LLMs) and then indicate their belief. A control group views only the original claim -without persuading content- to help isolate the effect of the LLM-generated argument.

3.2
Human-to-LLM Persuasion

This component evaluates how persuasive arguments written by humans influence LLMs responses. Fig.
1
(2) shows an overview of the setup, where LLMs are shown human-authored arguments supporting or refuting the given claim, and we assess how these arguments influence their beliefs. As before, the process consists of two stages:

(1) Human Persuasion
: We use existing human-stance datasets (details in Sec
4.1
) that contain arguments written by humans expressing support or opposition towards specific claims.

(2) LLM Response Evaluation
: We assess the influence of human persuasion on demographic-aware LLM personas. First, the LLM is instructed to adopt a demographic persona (rural, urban, female, male, young, old) using the prompt
‘‘Assume you belong to <group>’’
, next it is presented with human-authored arguments and asked to state their belief in the claim. Full prompt details are illustrated in the Appendix
D.2
.

3.3
Multi-agent LLM Persuasion

Misinformation affects demographic groups differently, leading to varying levels of belief and skepticism toward claims
Verma et al. (2022)
;
Knuutila et al. (2022)
;
Chandrasekaran et al. (2024)
. Individuals are more likely to trust information from their own demographic (in-group) more than from others (out-group), perceiving it as more credible
Ma et al. (2019)
;
Hinsley (2021)
. This tendency reinforces echo chamber behavior, where individuals primarily engage with in-group peers, amplifying shared beliefs and filtering opposing views
Nikolov et al. (2020)
.

We investigate whether similar dynamics emerge from multi-agent LLM interactions. Building upon our single-agent component, we explore how both human- and LLM generated persuasive arguments affect belief formation in multi-agent LLMs across demographics.

Multi-agent LLM architecture
: In line with our previous components, we use the same set of demographic groups to initialize personas for LLM interactions. To better understand the beliefs these personas reflect, we conduct a qualitative analysis of persona-specific beliefs, detailed in Appendix
F.2
. Our findings show that demographic LLM personas often produce stereotypical associations - for example, portraying men as achievement-oriented, women as vulnerable/empathetic, rural individuals as traditional, urban as progressive or innovation-oriented, the young as activist and the old as conservative. Although these patterns align with certain social science constructs
Hofstede (2011)
;
Villa and Moya (2020)
;
Kunkel and Settersten (2021)
;
Bussoletti (2023)
;
Cheshmehzangi et al. (2024)
, they also underscore the potential for stereotype reinforcement. This highlights the need for caution when designing persona-based prompts, as they can both mirror real-world dynamics and unintentionally reinforce stereotypes, particularly in the context of misinformation. We leave a deeper exploration of these effects to future work.

Figure 3:
Human annotation guidelines. Stances are generated by LLMs.

The multi-agent setup involves two agents interacting to explore how in-group (homogeneous) vs. out-group (heterogeneous) dynamics affect the acceptance or rejection of information. In the homogeneous setting, both agents share the same demographic identity (e.g., female-female, rural-rural), while in a heterogeneous setting, agents represent different groups (e.g., female-male, rural-urban). Fig.
2
shows an overview of this setup. The interaction unfolds over four rounds. In the first round, agents independently assess the claim and make an initial judgment. In the second round, they are shown persuasive arguments supporting and refuting the claim. This is followed by two rounds of agent-to-agent interactions, where they attempt to persuade each other while remaining open to opposing perspectives. In the final round, agents make their final judgments, deciding whether they believe the claim is true or false. Our setup draws inspiration from social science studies on group behavior
Lord (2015)
;
Rania et al. (2021)
and multi-agent societal simulations
Borah and Mihalcea (2024)
. (See Appendix
F.1
for prompt details).

4.3
Evaluating Persuasion

For LLM-to-Human persuasion, we compute the
correctness rate
of humans, a very straightforward approach to evaluate the impact of LLM persuasion. Let
N
be the total number of data points,
h(x_{i})
represent the human annotation for the i
{{}^{\textrm{th}}}
datapoint,
x_{i}
, where
h(x_{i})\in\{-1,+1\}
. Here,
h(x_{i})=+1
indicates the human believes
x_{i}
and
h(x_{i})=-1
indicates the human does not believe
x_{i}
. Similarly, let
y_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
represent the veracity of the
x_{i}
where
+1
and
-1
signify the data point is factually correct and incorrect, respectively.

\textrm{CR}_{human}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}(h(x_{i})=y_{i})}{N}

(1)

This metric measures the accuracy of humans in identifying true or false information based on the claim and the persuasive texts provided. Similarly, for Human-to-LLM persuasion, we adopt the correctness rate metric but replaced
h(x_{i})
with
l(x_{i})
, where
l(x_{i})\in\{-1,+1\}
denotes the LLM’s belief.

\textrm{CR}_{LLM}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}(l(x_{i})=y_{i})}{N}

(2)

Finally, for the multi-agent LLM setup, we compute the differences in correctness rates for the first responses (before persuasion) and final responses (after persuasion and interaction), respectively. Let the correctness rate of the initial response be
CR_{i}
and the final response be
CR_{f}
. Therefore,

\Delta\textrm{CR}=\textrm{CR}_{f}-\textrm{CR}_{i}

(3)

Here,
\Delta\textrm{CR}>0
indicates increased correctness after persuasion, and
\Delta\textrm{CR}<0
indicates decline, and
\Delta\textrm{CR}=0
suggests no change in correctness. We conduct additional linguistic analyses, along with other quantitative evaluations of LLM responses, detailed in Sections
5.3
and
5.4
. Statistical significance tests are reported in Appendix
G
.

Connecting Our Findings to Prior Research.

Several studies have examined demographic differences in susceptibility to misinformation. Regarding

, prior research presents mixed findings. Some show that women are more likely to believe and share misinformation
Pan et al. (2021)
;
Peter et al. (2024)
;
Kyrychenko et al. (2025)

Almenar et al. (2021)
;
Enock et al. (2024)
. While other studies show that women are less likely than men to endorse conspiracy theories
Cassese et al. (2020)
. In our analyses, we find that both female human participants and LLM-based female personas show lower correctness rates than their male counterparts, aligning with some of the studies above.

Taking into account
rural/urban differences
, the findings of our study also align with patterns of previous research showing that rural communities are more vulnerable to misinformation
Lister and Joudrey (2022)

.

Finally, in terms of

, our experiments reveal trends similar to previous research that show that older adults are more susceptible to false news and have a greater tendency to share them

Connecting Our Findings to Prior Research.

Röchert et al. (2021)
shows that misinformation spreads more rapidly within hom networks, where false information is often perceived as “normal”. Such networks tend to form like-minded cocoons, referred to as “echo chambers”. Additionally
Tanwar et al. (2024)
show that diverse community networks show better performance in maintaining accurate information. This aligns with our findings.

6
Lessons Learned

Our findings revealed the bidirectional impacts of persuasion on humans and LLMs, alongside demographic-aware misinformation-based multi-agent simulations. We demonstrate how demographic factors shape susceptibility to persuasion and highlight the potential of demographic LLMs. These findings offer actionable information for designing targeted demographic interventions.

LLMs for Exploring Demographic Susceptibility to Misinformation.
LLMs offer a preliminary but useful way to study demographic differences in misinformation susceptibility. With simple persona prompts, their responses show positive alignment with human demographic trends (corr
=0.58
), aligning with previous studies. While not fully replicating human behavior, refining prompts or sophisticated training could further improve demographic simulation, making them a practical tool, especially when human data is scarce.

Human- and LLM-persuasions can have varied effects.
Our results reveal a key asymmetry: in multi-agent LLMs, while LLM-generated persuasion improves correctness, human persuasion reduces it. This suggests that LLMs may offer unique advantages in countering LLM misinformation by generating reliable arguments
(
Gabriel et al., 2024
;
Costello et al., 2024
)
. Future research should investigate these mechanisms, and optimize LLM persuasion to complement human reasoning.

LLMs show performance variations in homogeneous versus heterogeneous multi-agent settings.
Homogeneous agent groups exhibit lower correctness rates (demonstrating echo chamber effects), while heterogeneous groups show improved performance. This aligns with the Contact Hypothesis Theory
Allport (1954)
, suggesting diverse interactions enhance perspective-taking. Our findings indicate that structured exposure to varied viewpoints could serve as an effective mitigation strategy.

7
Conclusion

This paper investigated the bidirectional persuasion dynamics between LLMs and humans, and explored their susceptibility to misinformation across diverse demographics. Using our preliminary analyses, we show that LLMs show potential to simulate demographic differences and trends in the context of misinformation. We showed that multi-agent LLMs exhibit echo chamber behavior when exposed to misinformation, a phenomenon that can be mitigated in a heterogeneous setting, consistent with established psychological theories. Based on our findings, we share ideas for future research and open-source our framework, PANDORA.

Limitations and Ethical Considerations

Simplified Representations of Human Demographics.

Our approach to simulating human demographics using LLMs may oversimplify and not capture the complexity and diversity of human demographics in the real world. Therefore, caution is needed when extrapolating large-scale simulations to draw conclusions about human behavior. Our study shows that while LLMs can simulate trends similar to those observed in humans with a moderately strong point-wise correlation (
0.58
), a considerable amount of research is still needed before they can fully replicate human thought processes, particularly in the context of misinformation. Additionally, our demographic granularity remain coarse, focusing only on gender. age and geographic region, and is limited by Prolific pre-screens, and these categories only provide a starting point for analyzing demographic differences. In the future, incorporating a validated rurality index
Chen et al. (2025)
for screening individuals and/or including socio-economic context would offer deeper insights.

Greater caution is needed when utilizing LLMs for persuasion.

Linguistic analysis shows that LLM-generated persuasion is often more effective, as supported by existing studies. Given its potential for both positive
Gabriel et al. (2024)
and negative
Danry et al. (2022)
outcomes, it is crucial to approach the use of persuasion with caution and thoroughly analyze the context before application.

Generalizability across cultures.

Most prior research referenced in our paper is based in the US. Hence, the generalizability of the findings across different cultural and geographical contexts remains unclear and requires further investigation. In addition, we only include participants from the US for each demographic group in our study. While their responses align with earlier trends, this limits the findings and highlights the need for a larger cross-cultural pool of annotators
Mihalcea et al. (2025)
. We encourage future work to analyze demographic differences across cross-cultural contexts.

Stereotypes, Risks and Biases.

We observe that simulating misinformation in homogeneous groups leads to an increased spread of misinformation within multi-agent LLM environments. Therefore, our simulation may reinforce existing biases or stereotypes, particularly if the training data includes harmful assumptions about specific demographic groups. There is also the risk that LLMs could be used maliciously to generate persuasive content that manipulates vulnerable populations. Furthermore, it is essential to consider the implications of using LLMs in sensitive areas such as political discourse or public health, where misinformation could have serious real-world consequences. Ensuring that these ethical considerations are addressed is crucial to make a responsible contribution to both AI and society.

E.4
LIWC dimensions

We analyze linguistic markers of persuasion using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC-22
Boyd et al. (2022)
) to compare human- and LLM-generated texts across five dimensions: (1) emotional appeal (
Affect, emo_pos, emo_neg, emo_anx, emo_anger
), where emotions may enhance manipulative persuasion; (2) credibility (
certainty, tentative, insight, cause
), with high certainty signaling authoritative claims; (3) logical structure (
causation, insight, discrepancy
), where imbalanced causation-to-insight ratios may indicate fallacious reasoning; (4) social dynamics (
social, family
), reflecting in-group appeals that reinforce echo chambers; and (5) cognitive complexity (
cognitive processes, insight, discrepancy
), where lower scores suggest simplistic arguments. This approach builds on established links between linguistic features and persuasion in misinformation contexts. We find that LLM-generated persuasive texts show higher emotional appeal, logical structure, and cognitive complexity, whereas human-generated texts have higher scores for credibility and social dimensions. The observed differences likely arise from LLMs’ training on large-scale, engagement-optimized corpora, which emphasize emotional resonance (e.g., heightened positive/negative affect), explicit logical markers (e.g., causation terms), and lexical diversity
Mieleszczenko-Kowszewicz et al. (2025)
;
Juzek and Ward (2025)
, inflating their scores in emotional appeal, logical structure, and cognitive complexity
Breum et al. (2024)
. In contrast, human writers prioritize credibility through nuanced certainty/hedging patterns
Halomoan (2024)
;
Yao et al. (2023)
and leverage socially grounded persuasion (e.g., in-group appeals)
Fleming (2025)
, which LLMs may not be able to replicate very authentically. These differences reflect LLMs’ strength in formulaic linguistics and humans’ reliance on pragmatic and relational persuasion strategies.

Persona Prompting

We use the same demographic groups - female/male, rural/urban, and old/young. We initialize personas such as -
‘‘Assume you are a person from <demographic group>’’
.

To better understand beliefs associated with these personas initializations, we prompt the LLMs to output their beliefs associated with these personas. We use this prompt to achieve the above:
‘‘Assume you are a <demographic group>. Which beliefs do you think define people like you the most, and how important is it for you to share them with others? Give me the top 10 beliefs.’’
Table
10
shows per model beliefs given a certain demographic persona. We find that persona beliefs across demographics reveal stereotypical associations that resonate with long-standing social science constructs. Male personas emphasize integrity, hard work, and individual achievement, aligning with Hofstede’s “masculinity” dimension
Hofstede (2011)
and the psychological framing of men as achievement- or agency-oriented. Alternatively, female personas emphasize empathy, kindness, feminism, and community, mapping onto relational and communal orientations documented in gender role theory. Rural personas stress tradition, self-reliance, family, and faith – values tied to conservatism and collectivism in sociological studies
Villa and Moya (2020)
, while urban personas emphasize diversity, sustainability, innovation, and accessibility, showing cities as multicultural, progressive hubs
Cheshmehzangi et al. (2024)
. Age-related stereotypes are also evident: younger personas emphasize inclusivity, climate change, and self-expression (consistent with social science research on youth activism and identity politics
Bussoletti (2023)
;
Millhouse (2024)
), whereas older personas focus on tradition, family, saving, and patience, aligning with generational theories of conservatism and life-course perspectives
Kunkel and Settersten (2021)
. These alignments suggest that the LLMs are not inventing new constructs but reproducing socially grounded stereotypes that may be embedded in training data.

Across models, the outputs are mostly overlapping for LLaMA and Qwen, which indicates a convergence of learned associations from similar training distributions rather than model-specific beliefs. There are a few differences: for example, Qwen highlights innovation and sustainability for men, and intersectionality and economic independence for women, suggesting more progressive framings. Additionally, opposing demographics also share overlapping beliefs: both male and female personas highlight education and mental health; rural and urban personas highlight community; young and old agree on family and integrity. Such overlaps suggest that while LLMs amplify group differences, they also construct shared cultural anchors. In misinformation contexts, these associations may become critical: stereotypes may exaggerate demographic divides (e.g., rural = traditional, urban = progressive), while shared values (e.g., family, community) could be leveraged to spread narratives across groups. Thus, persona-based prompting showcases both faults and bridges through which misinformation can spread, underscoring the need for cautious framing of findings and demographic-aware LLM design.

Building on the framework established by
Tessler et al. (2024)
, we operationalize persuasion effects through three key dimensions:
(1) emotional shift, (2) coverage, and (3) specificity
.
Emotional shift
is quantified using JS-divergence of sentiment
Elahimanesh et al. (2025)
between initial and final responses, capturing how persuasive interactions alter affective tone. This measure reveals whether arguments succeed through emotional appeals versus rational discourse.
Coverage
evaluates content retention by comparing information preserved in final responses, serving as an indicator of evidence integration versus echo-chamber behavior
Tessler et al. (2024)
. Higher coverage values suggest engagement with opposing evidence, while lower values may indicate ideological entrenchment.
Specificity
is measured through average Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) of response content, where lower scores reflect reliance on generic language that may signal manipulative vagueness
Sparck Jones (1972)
, while higher scores indicate concrete, substantive arguments.

demonstrate

urban/older groups
exhibit
stronger evidence integration (high coverage)
, indicating more analytical processing that helps counter misinformation but may also lead to
dismissal of emotionally compelling truths
.
Female group

, suggesting a balanced deliberative style that integrates both affective and evidentiary appeals.
Male/older demographics
display resistance to vague claims (
high specificity and low emotional shift
), though this potential strength may come at the cost of reduced flexibility when updating beliefs in light of new evidence.
