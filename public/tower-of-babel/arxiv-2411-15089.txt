Bayesian Analysis for Remote Biosignature Identification on exoEarths (BARBIE) III: Introducing the KEN
Natasha Latouf, Michael D. Himes, Avi M. Mandell, Michael Dane Moore, Vincent Kofman, Geronimo L. Villanueva, Chris Stark

Abstract
We deploy a newly-generated set of geometric albedo spectral grids to examine the detectability of methane ( CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} ) in the reflected-light spectrum of an Earth-like exoplanet at visible and near-infrared wavelengths with a future exoplanet imaging mission. By quantifying the detectability as a function of signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and molecular abundance, we can constrain the best methods of detection with the high-contrast space-based coronagraphy slated for the next generation telescopes such as the Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO). We used 25 bandpasses between 0.8 and 1.5 � ​ m \umu\textrm{m} . The abundances range from a modern-Earth level to an Archean-Earth level, driven by abundances found in available literature. We constrain the optimal 20%, 30%, and 40% bandpasses based on the effective SNR of the data, and investigate the impact of spectral confusion between CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} and H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} on the detectability of each one. We find that a modern-Earth level of CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} is not detectable, while an Archean Earth level of CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} would be detectable at all SNRs and bandpass widths. Crucially, we find that CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} detectability is inversely correlated with H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} abundance, with required SNR increasing as H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} abundance increases, while H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} detectability depends on CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} abundance and selected observational wavelength, implying that any science requirements for the characterization of Earth-like planet atmospheres in the VIS/NIR should consider the abundances of both species in tandem.



I
Introduction

The Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO), recommended by the
National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine [26]
, is slated to launch in the 2040s with the foundational goal of constraining the properties of Earth-like exoplanets using high-contrast imaging. This type of imaging is one of the first steps on the path to determining planet habitability for potentially Earth-like worlds and providing context for the development of our own Earth through time. The
National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine [26]
identified a primary science driver to detect and characterize 25 exoEarth candidates (EECs) in the habitable zones (HZ) of nearby stars. Characterization of an exoplanet’s atmosphere can provide vital information on the formation, history, and physical composition of the planet; for potentially habitable planets, we can also search their atmospheres for biosignatures that can hint at the likelihood of clement conditions and biological activity
[
33
]
. A holistic understanding of robust biological indicators and necessary signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for detection is crucial to establishing an efficient observing procedure and driving instrument development for HWO.

With the advances in high-contrast imaging instrumentation planned for HWO
[
37
]
, the ability to detect flux from a habitable Earth-twin is becoming a realistic possibility, which can unlock new molecular detections. Previous works, such as
Feng et al. [10]
,
Damiano et al. [8]
, and
Young et al. [41]
, have investigated biosignature detectability at varying wavelengths, such as
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
in the UV, and found that detectability is drastically effected by the chosen observational wavelength.
There are many molecules that peak in their absorption outside of the optical region, and would thus require a more thorough investigation of required resolving power and abundance for detection.

Methane (
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
) shows absorption in the visible and NIR wavelength regimes, with a range of spectral features varying in optical depth. Other molecules such as
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
can have abiotic production mechanisms and thus may not necessarily indicate a biosignature
[
32
,
9
]
. Even if from biotic sources, many eras of early Earth had extremely low levels of
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

[
28
,
22
]
. However, in low-
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
eras of Earth, it is possible that the atmosphere was instead
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
-rich, such as in the Archean era, and thus
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
would be readily detectable
[
1
,
40
]

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
abundance would strongly indicate biogenic sources as well as abiogenic
[
20
,
40
]

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}

In this work, we use the spectral grid-based Bayesian inference method first described in
Susemiehl et al. [36, hereafter S23]

\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}

II
Methodology

II.1
A New Grid-Building Scheme

Although spectral retrieval studies have been used extensively to explore the detectability of atmospheric compositions for the direct imaging of exoplanets for multiple telescopes both current and future
[
24
,
27
,
34
,
7
,
41
, e.g.,]
, these retrievals are extremely computationally expensive. Most Bayesian retrievals use real-time radiative transfer calculations, combined with exploring large parameter spaces, mission capabilities, and constant model improvement that increases computation time to a rate that is not easily usable. Different methods to accelerate retrievals have been explored through efficient radiative transfer schemes or machine learning
[
30
,
42
,
25
,
6
,
12
,
16
, e.g.,]
.

In order to facilitate the efficient production of new optimized spectral grids for our grid-based retrievals, we developed a Python package called Gridder, which is a generalized grid-building scheme based on the methodology of S23
[
15
]
. Gridder produces arbitrary spectral grids using the Planetary Spectrum Generator, a publicly available radiative transfer model for creating planetary spectra
[
39
,
38
, PSG,]
. PSG can be used to calculate spectra over an ultra-broad wavelength range (50 nm to 100 mm) and includes planetary atmospheres, surfaces, and bulk properties such as aerosols, atomic, continuum, and molecular scattering/radiative processes implemented layer-by-layer. The Gridder parameter structure is highly customizable, allowing any input parameter to PSG to be used as a parameter in the optimized grid, including parameterized thermal profiles (e.g., isothermal, adiabatic,
Line [23]
) and atmospheric chemistry (e.g., constant-with-altitude, thermochemical equilibrium). In addition to the error metric of S23, Gridder offers other common metrics, such as the maximum absolute percent error and mean squared error, for greater control over the resulting grid’s accuracy. Gridder features various optimizations (e.g., parallelization, checkpoints) to ensure efficient production of optimized spectral grids in a consistent, reproducible manner. Using Gridder, we have built a new set of spectral grids, each with six parameters to decrease grid-building computational time, but over a far wider wavelength range from the UV to NIR (0.2–2.0
\umu\textrm{m}
), and we have added the additional molecular atmospheric constituents
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
CO
,
\text{SO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
.

Our newly built grid set, hereafter the KEN grids (no acronym; they’re just Ken), allow for a direct comparison between grids since they cover the same wavelength range and three base parameters of
\mathrm{P_{0}}
,
\mathrm{A_{s}}
, and g with
\mathrm{R_{p}}
fixed to 1
R_{\earth}

Any molecules that have overlapping spectral features (for the range of atmospheric pressures examined) are housed within the same grid, while any that are orthogonal to each other do not need to be simultaneously retrieved within one grid, such as
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
and
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
. The background gas in each grid is set to
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
= 1 -
P_{1}
-
P_{2}
-
P_{3}
where
P_{1}
–
P_{3}
are the molecules per grid. In this way, we can also investigate the effects of false positive or negative molecular detection due to overlapping features. We developed four different spectral grids at a resolving power of 500 (R = 500) which can then be binned to lower resolving powers, with the ability to vary the cloud fraction
\mathrm{C_{f}}
at any value between 0% cloudy to 100% cloudy. We describe the molecular parameters in each named grid in the KEN grid set, along with a modern Earth-like spectrum per grid and the highlighted wavelength regimes for use per grid, in Figure
2

The KEN grids will allow us to investigate varying atmospheric compositions beyond
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-dominated atmospheres, such as
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
or
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
dominated, to develop observational procedures for multiple planetary archetypes through wavelength.

Results

III.1
Grid Validation

In S23 Figure 8, they investigate the interpolation error in the developed grid to ensure that the grid is not introducing large error past any known interpolation error. We have replicated this in Figure
3
using the same technique as in S23 using our M-KEN grid, as that has the same parameters as in the S23 grid. To summarize, we ran 6D retrievals for each value of each parameter in the test grid. The test grid in use consists of the midpoint values of the M-KEN grid as interpolation error is typically maximized near the midpoints, and thus to test the error we want to investigate the area of highest error. For each parameter set, a “true” spectrum was created (i.e., pulling the data spectrum directly from the midpoints grid) at an SNR of 20. The retrievals were then repeated, but the data spectrum was created by interpolating from the main M-KEN grid (i.e., the way that all retrievals will work when using KEN grids with PSGnest). We use the offset between the values retrieved for the “true” and interpolated spectra to assess the impact of interpolation error on these retrievals.

Although the M-KEN grid covers a wider wavelength range than the S23 grid, Figure
3
covers the wavelength range of 0.4–1
\umu\textrm{m}
in order to directly replicate the range in S23. The error from retrieving on interpolated data is shown in pink dots, while the error from retrieving on“true” data is shown in purple stars. We also calculate the 68% credible region and portray those as error bars. The left column shows the full extent of the offset for every point, while the right column zooms in to the regions shown in the black dotted lines. We select these areas based on where there is a high concentration of overlapping points. Any differences between the results are due to interpolation error.

We find that the retrieval error is generally very similar for both the “true” and interpolated data retrievals, with the derived retrieval error for both always within
1\sigma
of the input value. The outlier errors, as in S23, are at small

This confirms that the Gridder selection of grid points has been successful, with interpolation error not serving as a significant inhibitor for performing retrievals in this region.

IV
Discussion

Understanding the potential for dual detection of atmospheric constituents is an important factor in optimizing the required telescope observing time. Figure
5
a shows how crucial this investigation is in the NIR. At the lowest detectable values of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
in our initial investigation, in order to detect both
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
an SNR of 9 is required at 1.1
\umu\textrm{m}
, and this remains possible out to 1.2
\umu\textrm{m}
. In order to detect both
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and low
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
at shorter wavelengths, such as at 0.9
\umu\textrm{m}
, an SNR of
\geq
13 is required. This is a significant difference in SNR and would result in a drastic increase in required exposure time. However, the optimal wavelength for a detection of both molecules reverses at higher
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
abundances - the detectability of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
is impacted at longer wavelengths as shown in Figure
5
c. In order to detect both
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
at 1.1
\umu\textrm{m}
, an SNR of
\geq
15 is required; however, at 0.9
\umu\textrm{m}
the combined detection is accessible at an SNR of 5. We begin to see that there is a relationship between
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
, which is further confirmed by the degeneracy between
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
shown in Figure
4
.

The overlap of molecular absorption features as shown in Figure
7
drives this degeneracy. The bottom most panel shows both a modern Earth
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance (3
\times 10^{-3}
VMR) and a late Archean Earth
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
abundance (7.07
\times 10^{-3}
VMR). At 0.9
\umu\textrm{m}
, we can see the absorption features are orthogonal to each other and thus more easily disentangled. At 1.1
\umu\textrm{m}
and longer, the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
feature absorbs much deeper and wider than the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
feature, thus completely preventing the possibility of detection without a high SNR. The opposite effect is seen at lower
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
abundance, such as the Phanerozoic abundance (4.15
\times 10^{-4}
VMR) shown in the top panel of Figure
7
. Here, the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
features at 0.9
\umu\textrm{m}
are too shallow to be detected without high SNR, and at longer wavelengths (such as 1.4
\umu\textrm{m}
), the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
feature is completely contained within a much larger
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
feature and is thus not detectable. At 1.1
\umu\textrm{m}
, both
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
are detectable due to the slight offset between the absorption features. We can clearly see that the detectability is a function of the abundance of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
, and the abundances of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
are degenerate in certain regimes.

To further investigate this degeneracy, we first looked at comparing a null versus modern
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
value in Figure
6
, specifically at 1.1
\umu\textrm{m}
where the overlapping features compete. In Figure
6
a with modern Earth
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance, we achieve a strong
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
detection at 4.15
\times 10^{-4}
VMR at SNR = 9, while still achieving at most a weak detection at 1
\times 10^{-4}
. Comparatively, in Figure
6
b 4.15
\times 10^{-4}
VMR requires an SNR of 6 for strong detection with no
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
present, compared to the previous SNR of 9. This confirms thatd
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
are degenerate, with detectability reliant on the abundances, and thus we explored a range of abundance values of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
to finely understand the relationship we found.

In Figures
8
and
9

V
Conclusions & Future Work

To Summarize:

Modern levels of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
(1.65
\times 10^{-6}
VMR) are not detectable at any SNR
\leq 20
with any bandpass width. Archean levels of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
(7.07
\times 10^{-3}
VMR) are easily detectable at all SNRs and bandpass widths.

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
detectability is a function of the abundances of both
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
, due to the close overlap of spectral features for both species. This overlap between
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
absorption features becomes most acute at low to moderate
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
abundances, and as the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance increases, the required SNR for
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
detection increases.

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
detectability depends on the abundance of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
; as
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
abundance increases, the required SNR to detect low to moderate
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
increases.

\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
detectability clearly relies heavily on the bandpass selected for observation. At longer wavelengths, detectability drops for both molecules, and instrument efficiency decreases as a result of moving further into the NIR. Shorter wavelengths remain preferable for detection, namely at 1.1
\umu\textrm{m}
for both
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
, and 0.9
\umu\textrm{m}
for
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
. However, when searching for moderate to low
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
in an
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
-rich atmosphere, or searching for moderate to low
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
in a
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
-rich atmosphere, a higher SNR will be required to disentangle the molecular absorption signals. This may impact the science goals for HWO depending on whether an Archean Earth atmosphere or Modern Earth atmosphere drives the SNR requirement.

In future works, the BARBIE project will culminate with an investigation of molecular detection across the full expected wavelength range for the Habitable Worlds Observatory. We will explore the requirements for molecular detection across the UV, optical, and NIR to determine the optimal wavelength range and SNR per molecule for strong detection. These metrics will be coupled with also varying the bandpass width across 20%, 30%, and 40% bandpasses, with the new addition of a 10% bandpass width. By using the KEN grids, we will explore molecular relationships through wavelength to investigate the most efficient observing strategy to detect multiple molecules with the most optimal bandpass wavelength, width, and SNR.

We also note that many of the critical wavelength bandpasses for optimal observation of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
fall at the intersection of the high-sensitivity regions of different detector technologies. This wavelength dependency could significantly impact the requirements for biosignature detection and result in significant differences to characterization yields, as shown by
Stark et al. [35]
. In future works, we will investigate how coronagraph detectors and their wavelength-dependent sensitivity can vary the detectability of different molecular species.
