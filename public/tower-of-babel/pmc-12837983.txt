Exploring dental implant placement by dental students using freehand, static guide, and dynamic navigation: an in vitro study
Authors: Yitian Liu, Zonghe Xu, Lin Zhou, Haoqing Huang, Kaijin Lin, Sihui Zhang, Yanjun Xiao, Dong Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the surgical performance, learning curves, and subjective perceptions of undergraduate dental students using freehand, static, and dynamic computer‐assisted implant surgery (freehand-CAIS, s-CAIS, and d-CAIS) to place implants.MethodsTwelve undergraduate dental students were enrolled in this study. All of the students completed implant procedures in mandibular models utilizing freehand-CAIS, s-CAIS, and d-CAIS. Each surgical approach was repeated five times, with two implants (left central incisor and left first molar, 31 and 36) inserted each time. Surgical time and implant trueness were used to analyse the students' surgical performance and learning curves for the three surgical approaches. Furthermore, following each surgical approach, students were required to complete the relevant self-assessment surveys.ResultsThe 12 students successfully placed 360 implants. The surgical time significantly decreased over the first two and first three trials for freehand-CAIS and d-CAIS, and both significantly decreased again in the fifth trial. While the surgical time of s-CAIS significantly decreased over the first three trials and then plateaued. However, the implant trueness of the three surgical approaches did not improve across trials. The surgical time for d-CAIS was significantly longer than that for s-CAIS and freehand-CAIS (P < 0.001). The implant trueness of d-CAIS and s-CAIS was significantly better than that of freehand-CAIS. The global apical deviation (P < 0.01) and angular deviation (P < 0.001) for d-CAIS were significantly lower than those for s-CAIS. Students reported significantly increased self-confidence with implant placement using s-CAIS (P < 0.001) and d-CAIS (P = 0.020).ConclusionBoth dynamic navigation and static guides can improve students' implant trueness and self-confidence in implant placement. The surgical speed of students improved significantly over the first two attempts using freehand-CAIS, and within the first three attempts using d-CAIS and s-CAIS. Moreover, the surgical speed of s-CAIS reached a plateau after the third attempt. In terms of implant trueness, dynamic navigation exhibited superior performance in angular deviation control. However, due to the complex workflow of the dynamic navigation system and the consideration of students’ acceptance of this technique, we recommend scheduling training in the d-CAIS approach after training in the freehand-CAIS and s-CAIS in the teaching curriculum.Trial registrationClinical trial number: not applicable.

Background

Optimal implant placement in three dimensions (3D) is critical for long-term stability and excellent aesthetic outcomes in implant restorations [1]. Improper implant positioning frequently causes issues that impair functional or aesthetic performance, and may even necessitate implant removal [2, 3]. Furthermore, the growing use of therapeutic approaches like immediate restoration and immediate loading demands excellent implant accuracy, as any error could jeopardize the entire treatment plan. As a result, dental implant surgeons must be able to precisely install implants [4]. How to best prepare dentistry students for this expertise before they become dental implant surgeons remains a challenge in contemporary dental implant education. In this regard, dental students must attend implant placement training prior to performing implant surgery in order to increase implant accuracy while reducing procedural risks and complications [5].

Freehand placement has traditionally been used during training, but it is very difficult for new students to correctly control the position and direction of implant placement [6]. In contrast, computer-assisted implant surgery (CAIS) involves the use of computed tomography (CT) combined with planning software to position a virtual implant in the optimal 3D position on a multiplane reconstruction image of the orofacial tissue [3], which is then accurately transferred to the surgical site via a static guide or dynamic navigation system [1]. In vivo and in vitro studies have demonstrated that the accuracy of static and dynamic computer-assisted implant surgery (s-CAIS and d-CAIS) is comparable in terms of coronal, apical, and angular deviations, and both are superior to freehand computer-assisted implant surgery (freehand-CAIS) [3, 7–9]. As a result, s-CAIS and d-CAIS have become routine treatment protocols in many institutions. In recent years, some researchers have also used dynamic navigation and static guides for dental implant placement training, so that dental students are exposed to cutting-edge technologies in the field of dental implantology before starting their clinical careers. The goal is to improve surgical performance, shorten the learning curve for implant placement, and develop surgeons who are more competent in their future clinical work [6, 10, 11]. Learning curve theory has been widely used in various fields, reflecting the relationship between the number of exercises and skill proficiency [12, 13]. Both non-guided (freehand) and guided CAIS (s-CAIS and d-CAIS) must be practiced extensively before being used clinically by students and novice dentists [14]. Using a learning curve, we can determine the minimal number of exercises required for students to master implant placements using non-guided and guided CAIS.

However, few studies have compared the learning curves and surgical performance of dental students undergoing training in implant placement with freehand-CAIS, s-CAIS, and d-CAIS. Therefore, this study investigated the learning processes of the above three surgical approaches among undergraduate dental students, in order to provide some guidance for the use of dynamic navigation and static guides in dental implant training and to inform the development of relevant training plans. The null hypothesis was that there would be no statistical difference in outcome measures between the three surgical approaches.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This study was an in vitro model experiment, so the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Stomatology Hospital of Fujian Medical University waived the ethical approval requirements. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

Study design

Twelve final-year undergraduate dental students were recruited. All students had no previous experience with implant placement and had not undergone training with static guides or dynamic navigation systems. At enrolment in the study, all students had finished the theoretical course (a five-year undergraduate stomatology course) required by the School of Stomatology at Fujian Medical University.

One hundred and eighty standard mandibular models were printed by a 3D printer (ProJet 3500 HD Max 3D printer, 3D Systems, USA). The models had the left first molar and left central incisor removed (31 and 36, International Federation of Dental Systems), but there was still adequate edentulous space and residual bone volume. After fixing the registration device (U-tube) with markers in the corresponding edentulous area using silicone rubber impression material (3 M, USA), a preoperative CBCT (cone beam computed tomography) scan was taken. The CBCT system (i-CAT FLX V10, Kava, Germany) was set with the following parameters: 120 kV, 5.0 mA, voxel size of 0.2 mm, and exposure time of 26.9 s. The mandibular model was also scanned using an intraoral scanner (IOS) Aoralscan L (SHINING 3D DENTAL, China). The resulting data were saved in the standard tessellation language (STL) format. Then, both the preoperative CBCT and IOS data sets were imported into the implant planning software of the dynamic navigation system (YiZhimei, Dcarer Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China), and the positions of the two implants (31: Nobel PMC 3.5 × 11.5 mm; 36: Nobel PMC 4.3 × 10 mm, Nobel biocare service sag, Gothenburg, Sweden) were designed using a prosthetically driven principle under the guidance of the instructors. Two dental implantologists (W.D. and Z.L.) with 10 years of clinical experience participated in designing the preoperative plan (Fig. 1). Subsequently, the preoperative plan was imported in STL format into another planning software (3shape Dental System, 3shape, Denmark) to design the surgical and positioning guides according to the planned implant positions. The thickness of the surgical guide was 3 mm. All sleeves were manufactured by a third party and had an outer diameter of 6.0 mm, a height of 3.5 mm, a distance from the top of the sleeve to the implant platform of 9 mm, and a tolerance of 0.075 mm between the guide and the teeth. The positioning guide had a thickness of 3 mm and a positioning hole width of 2.5 mm. The surgical guide was used for the s-CAIS approach, and the positioning guide was used for the freehand-CAIS approach. All guides were fabricated by a 3D printer (AccuFab-D1s dental 3D printer, Shining 3D Dental, China) (Fig. 2). The cavity preparation sequences for the three surgical approaches were designed according to the implant manufacturer's protocols.Fig. 1Implant plan designingFig. 2Positioning guide (A) and surgical guide (B)

Implant placement training

Prior to implant placement, each student was trained by a dental implant surgeon (Z.L.) to place implants using the freehand-CAIS, s-CAIS, and d-CAIS approaches. To ensure that every student understood the surgical procedures, the principles of implant placement, the use of the dynamic navigation system, and the corresponding tool kits, each student received a learning file that included information about the implant type, osteotomy preparation sequences, and surgical cautions. Additionally, students watched a video of the related surgery and were given an interpretation of the scene. Once the students were acquainted with the 3D positions of implants 31 and 36, they started practicing implant placement on a phantom. A completely randomized order crossover design was used to randomly arrange the three surgical approaches for each student; randomisation was performed using Microsoft Excel software 2021 (Microsoft corporation, Washinton, USA), with an interval between each training for each surgical approach of 7 to 10 days.

d-CAIS approach: The preoperative planning was imported into the dynamic navigation system's navigation software. A reference frame was then connected to the handpiece, the reference array was fixed to the contralateral residual teeth, and the infrared optical tracker of the dynamic navigation was adjusted to be 1.25–1.5 m above the phantom. After the registration and calibration, the students randomly selected three points to test the accuracy of the position before preparing the osteotomy with the use of the dynamic navigation system. This osteotomy preparation involved pilot drilling, sequential drilling, tapping, and implant placement. The implant position, axis, and depth were continuously adjusted according to the system's prompts during the operation. Red denotes a deviation from the desired implant position, while green indicates that the current site and axis are good. The site and angle warning values of the dynamic navigation system were 0.60 mm and 1.20°, respectively (Fig. 3).Fig. 3The training of dynamic computer-assisted implant surgery

s-CAIS approach: Before commencing implant placement, the students had to make sure that the guide was of high quality, the sleeves were securely embedded in it, the guide could be seated stably on the model, and the drill handles, drills, and metal sleeves all matched. Then, the students performed the surgery, including pilot drilling, sequential drilling, tapping, and implant placement according to the protocols under the guidance of the surgical guides (Fig. 4).Fig. 4The training of static computer-assisted implant surgery

The training of static computer-assisted implant surgery

Freehand-CAIS approach: Based on the implant position specified in the protocol, the student had to determine the implant site using the following guidelines: the implant should be located at the midpoint of the line connecting the contour's high points in the mesial-distal direction when viewed from the occlusal surface and on the line connecting the lingual fossa or the central fossa of the adjacent teeth in the buccolingual direction. The implant platform was level with the bone surface. Subsequently, the positioning guide was placed on the model to verify if the goal position was precise. If not, the students changed the implant site until they thought it was near the ideal implant site, at which point they began the cavity preparation procedure. The preparation steps were the same as for the d-CAIS approach (Fig. 5).Fig. 5The training of freehand computer-assisted implant surgery

The training of freehand computer-assisted implant surgery

All drilling was performed at 800 rpm. Tapping and the implant placement were performed at 25 rpm. After five trials, the surgical guide was replaced with a new one.

Outcome assessment

Surgical time: A stopwatch (Casio, Japan) was used to record the surgical time. The surgical time for freehand-CAIS was recorded from the point at which the students located the 31 and 36 implant sites with the positioning guide to the point at which the two implants (31 and 36) were placed in that trial. The surgical time for s-CAIS was recoded from the point at which the surgical guide was seated on the model to the point at which the two implants (31 and 36) were placed in that trial. The surgical time for the d-CAIS started with the registration and calibration and ended with the placement of the two implants (31 and 36) in that trial.

Implant trueness: A postoperative CBCT was taken using the same parameters as the preoperative CBCT to obtain the actual implant positions, and the preoperative and postoperative CBCT were imported into the accuracy verification software (RemeDent, Baihui Weikang Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) to calculate the implant deviations. The following parameters were measured (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Schematic representation of measurement of implant deviations

Statistical analysis

Measurement data were statistically analysed using SPSS 29.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA), and the results are presented as means and standard deviations. The normality of the data distributions was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the equality of variance was checked using the Levene's test. To test for differences in implant trueness and surgical time among the five trials for the three surgical approaches, one-way repeated measures ANOVA was used when the data distribution was normal, and post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the LSD (Least significant difference) method; the Friedman test was used when the data were non-normally distributed, and post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni method. To compare the implant trueness and surgical time among the three surgical approaches for each trial and for the five trials, one-way ANOVA was used when the data were normally distributed and the variance was equal, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was used when the data were non-normally distributed or had uneven variances. To compare the total scores and the scores for each question of the self-confidence measure among the three surgical approaches, one-way ANOVA was used when the data were normally distributed and the variance was equal, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was used when the data were non-normally distributed or had uneven variances. P < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference. The statistical analyses were performed by an investigator (L.Y.T.).

Sample size calculation

Sample size calculations were based on the entry, apex, and angle deviations of the three surgical approaches as reported in a previous study (the entry deviations for the freehand-CAIS, s-CAIS, and d-CAIS approaches were 1.40 ± 1.01 mm, 0.92 ± 0.38 mm, and 1.14 ± 0.46 mm, respectively; the apex deviations for the freehand-CAIS, s-CAIS, and d-CAIS approaches were 2.54 ± 1.58 mm, 1.66 ± 0.64 mm, and 1.76 ± 0.71 mm, respectively; the angle deviations for the freehand-CAIS, s-CAIS, and d-CAIS approaches were 8.15° ± 4.73°, 7.07° ± 4.38°, and 3.19° ± 1.89°, respectively) [16]. Minimum sample size calculations were performed using PASS 15.0 (Power Analysis and Sample Size Software version 15.0, NCSS, LLC) for one-way ANOVA with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power (1 − β) of 0.9 [14]. The results indicated that the minimum numbers of total samples needed in each attempt for the three approaches for the entry, apex, and angle deviations were 51, 39, and 15 implants, respectively. Therefore, a sample size of 72 implants (2 implant sites × 12 students × 3 approaches) was considered sufficient to compare the three surgical approaches in each attempt.

Discussion

Since a consensus was established at the First European Workshop on Implant Dentistry University Education in 2008 that implant dentistry should be an integral part of the undergraduate curriculum [17], many universities around the world have integrated theoretical and surgical training in dental implantology [18]. Although the training appears to be beneficial in strengthening students' grasp of dental implantology, further advances in teaching techniques and training materials are required [19, 20]. With the development of clinical treatments, teaching devices, and teaching concepts, training methods have evolved, and computer-assisted simulations are now being employed in the classroom [21]. This study explored the learning curves and surgical performance of the freehand-CAIS, s-CAIS, and d-CAIS approaches during implant training among undergraduate dental students, in order to provide guidance for skill training in dental implant placement for dental students in the future.

Surgical time

In this study, students had significantly longer surgical times with the d-CAIS approach compared to freehand-CAIS and s-CAIS. This is due to the registration and calibration of the dynamic navigation system, the switching of the surgeon's sight between the surgical area and the computer interface, and the pausing for observation and adjustment of the drill's position and axis, all of which result in an extended surgical time, as previously reported [14, 15, 22]. Therefore, it has been suggested that the dynamic navigation system be further simplified to improve surgical efficiency [23, 24]. In the present study, there was no statistically significant difference in surgical time between s-CAIS and freehand-CAIS. However, studies by Wu et al. [6] and Abduo et al. [25] reported that surgical time was shorter for s-CAIS compared to freehand-CAIS. The difference in findings may be because the the preparation procedure for s-CAIS in this study was more complex than that for freehand-CAIS, resulting in no significant difference in surgical time between the two approaches. Another reason may be that the use of a positioning guide reduced the time required for positioning in freehand-CAIS, consistent with the findings of Schulz et al. [5].

Previous studies have shown that the learning curve for guided CAIS plateaus after five attempts, providing a reference for determining the number of attempts for students in this study [6, 14, 26]. The surgical time for the three surgical approaches decreased across the trials, indicating that students were growing more proficient at the surgical techniques. Agha et al. [27] reported that surgical time decreased when students mastered a new medical technique. The surgical times for freehand-CAIS and d-CAIS improved significantly over the first two and first three trials, respectively, and decreased significantly again in the fifth trial, although the extent of improvement was smaller. This suggests that the surgical times for both freehand-CAIS and d-CAIS may be further reduced with more practice. The surgical time for s-CAIS decreased significantly by the third trials and then gradually plateaued. This indicates that students can quickly become proficient in the surgical procedure of s-CAIS for implant placement after training. Wu et al. [6] reported that the surgical time for s-CAIS decreased significantly at the third attempt, which is similar to the results of our study, while the surgical time for freehand-CAIS decreased significantly at the fifth attempt. This may be because the use of positioning guides helped students master the implant placement position quicker, resulting in a faster decrease in surgical time for freehand-CAIS in this study than in Wu et al. [6] 's study. Wang et al. [14] and Golob et al. [26] reported that the surgical time for d-CAIS improved significantly after the first two trials, which is similar to the results of the current study. However, Wang et al. [14] also found that the surgical time for s-CAIS did not improve significantly across attempts. The above research results indicate that static guides and dynamic navigation are not difficult for students to master. Xu et al. [28] 's study further indicated that dynamic navigation system can help to shorten the learning process of performing implant placement surgeries for novices.

Implant trueness

According to the Glossary of Computer‐Assisted Implant Surgery and Related Terms, First Edition, 2025, “accuracy” is composed of two parameters: trueness and precision. Precision relates to the the consistency or repeatability of measurements, showing how closely grouped the results are to each other. In contrast, trueness relates to the the closeness of the actual implant position to the planned one. Since this study did not assess the closeness of more than one consecutive measurement of the actual implant position to each other, the term “trueness” is used to describe the results [29]. The implant trueness of the three surgical approaches in this study was higher than that of previous studies [22, 30, 31]. This suggests that after training, the implant trueness of students reached a baseline. The present study also indicates that both dynamic navigation and static guides can significantly increase students' implant trueness. However, as freehand implant placement depends on the operator’s theoretical knowledge and skills, which are often acquired over a long period of extensive surgical training, it is difficult for novices to place implants in the ideal position [2]. Furthermore, dynamic navigation appears to be superior in improving apical deviation and angular deviation. According to Zhong et al. [22], implant insertion by students using the freehand-CAIS approach resulted in an angular deviation that was almost three times greater than that using d-CAIS. Further, two additional studies showed that dynamic navigation significantly decreased angular deviation as compared to freehand and static guides [15, 31]. The large angular deviation of s-CAIS may be due to undesirable mechanical tolerances between the drill and the sleeve or cumulative errors in data collection, software processing, and guide manufacturing, as well as displacement of the static guide during surgery [32, 33].

For s-CAIS, there was no significant improvement in the global coronal, global apical, and angular deviations after five attempts. One possible reason for this finding is that there is no typical learning curve effect for the accuracy of s-CAIS [14]. Another possible reason is that each static guide in this study was reused five times in order to reduce experimental costs. During practice, friction between the drill, the drill handle and the sleeve caused wear on the inner surfaces of the drill handle and sleeve, and increased the tolerances between the drill, drill handle, and sleeve, leading to cumulative error [32, 34]. This also resulted in a significant increase in the angular deviation of s-CAIS as the training progressed. For d-CAIS, the fact that there was no significant improvement in global coronal deviation may be because the global coronal deviation of d-CAIS was already very low at the beginning, leaving little room for further improvement. Therefore, there was no significant decrease with subsequent practice. This may also indicate that students were able to attain a good control of the global coronal deviation from the start when using dynamic navigation. The changes in the global apical deviation of d-CAIS were very similar to those of the global coronal deviation, likely also due to the aforementioned reasons. In the study by Golob et al. [26], there were no significant improvements in the global coronal and apical deviations after five attempts among students. Xu et al. [28] also found that even students without implant experience could effectively control the global coronal and apical deviation during their first attempt using dynamic navigation. Studies by Spille et al. [10] and Zhan et al. [11] found that after training in the d-CAIS approach, the global coronal deviation showed no significant improvement, but the global apical and angular deviations both improved significantly. Wang et al. [14] also found that there was a learning curve effect in the angular deviation of d-CAIS for undergraduate students during five attempts. Golob et al. [26] found that the angular deviation of d-CAIS improved significantly in the first two trials and then plateaued. However, there was no significant improvement in the angular deviation of d-CAIS across attempts in the present study. This is most likely because the students who performed implant placement procedures utilizing dynamic navigation were more cautious [15, 24] and precisely prepared the cavities in accordance with the system's guidance to avoid any unnecessary change in angulation. In addition, the dynamic navigation systems vary across studies, which may also contribute to varying learning curves for dynamic navigation [35]. Currently, there is a lack of studies on the learning curve of implant trueness for freehand-CAIS. The study by Wu et al. [6] showed that the global coronal deviation of freehand-CAIS for students significantly decreased after the fourth attempt, while the global apical and angular deviations did not significantly decrease until the sixth attempt. The study by Zhan et al. [11] found no significant improvement in the implant trueness of freehand-CAIS between the first five attempts and the subsequent five attempts. Another study also found that the learning curve for freehand-CAIS was rather steep [36]. Future studies need to investigate the minimal number of trials for students to improve the implant trueness of the freehand-CAIS approach.

Self-assessment

Self-assessment is the foundation of surgical training and can enhance learning in the absence of instructor feedback [37]. Self-confidence is defined as "a feeling or belief of trust in one's ability", and perceptions of self-confidence may have a direct impact on clinical performance [38]. In line with previous findings, the current results revealed that dynamic navigation and static guides were both associated with improved implant placement self-confidence among students [15, 25, 31]. Because higher self-confidence has a beneficial effect on dentistry practice [39], students' learning of implant placement procedures may be enhanced by static guides and dynamic navigation, perhaps leading to improved surgical outcomes. However, for d-CAIS, the complex preoperative preparation work and high demands on hand–eye coordination may impair students' enthusiasm for learning, leading to lower evaluations of their skill levels in d-CAIS compared to s-CAIS [24]. Additionally, as the handpiece used in d-CAIS is equipped with a reference frame, which is heavier than traditional implant handpieces, students often complain of hand fatigue and have difficulty gripping during practice, resulting in a poor experience of comfort when using a dynamic navigation system to place implants [10, 36]. It is worth noting that there is no unified questionnaire for evaluating students' levels of self-confidence in dental implant surgery, and the methods used to gauge participants' levels of confidence differ in the published literature. As a result, it is not possible to compare the quantitative outcomes of students' self-confidence assessments in various studies.

To sum up, the null hypothesis, that there would be no differences in the learning curves and surgical performance of the three surgical approaches, was rejected. Although static guides and dynamic navigation contributed to enhanced performance of implant placement, this does not mean that there is no need for students to have solid theoretical knowledge and practical abilities. Conversely, guided CAIS is more technically demanding and can be associated with specific surgical risks such as guide template fracture or navigation systems malfunction. During this experiment, we also encountered situations where the drill handle was embedded in the sleeve and difficult to remove. These problems were also reported in the study by Abduo et al.[25]. In these situations, the surgeon must be able to switch to a conventional implant approach, or the surgery must be cancelled [40, 41]. Furthermore, guided CAIS is not always an optimal choice because time–cost effectiveness must be taken into account in clinical practice [2, 42]. Thus, traditional freehand implant placement is still a crucial surgical skill that is unlikely to be entirely replaced. Furthermore, based on the results of question 5 of the survey, students did not show a preference for one of the three surgical approaches, and their scores for the three training approaches were comparable. Therefore, in the future, it may be best to train students in implant placement by combining dynamic navigation and static guides with the traditional freehand approach. In this way, students will be prepared to perform more appropriate surgical protocols for their patients once they start working [10]. However, further research is still needed to effectively combine dynamic navigation and static guides with traditional surgical training approaches [22, 36]. In the current study, we observed that students appeared more relaxed and confident when practicing the s-CAIS approach and had fewer complications, such as buccal or lingual bone perforations or dehiscence, as compared to the other two approaches. This may be attributed to the physical constraints of the guide, which helped students maintain more stable hand movements and drill more evenly, avoiding excessive cutting on one side. The results of the self-confidence questionnaire also showed higher scores for the s-CAIS approach compared to the d-CAIS approach. Although the trueness of the d-CAIS approach was the highest, it took students some time to master its complex workflow, which might impact students' confidence. Therefore, we recommend scheduling training in the d-CAIS approach after the freehand-CAIS and s-CAIS approaches, in order to facilitate students' progressive learning. For example, the s-CAIS approach can be taught first to teach the protocol for drilling, tapping, and implant placement. Next, the freehand-CAIS approach can be taught to develop hand–eye coordination, practice positioning according to the adjacent teeth, and inspect the drill position from different viewpoints to control the angle. Finally, we suggest introducing the d-CAIS approach. Students should first master the use of the dynamic navigation system. Then, students can use the dynamic navigation system to visually monitor the position and angular deviations of dental implants in real-time to improve their positioning and angular control outcomes. Based on the maximum time required for each surgical approach and the minimum number of trials needed to improve the surgical time, at least one session should be allocated for each of the freehand-CAIS and s-CAIS approaches in the curriculum, while the d-CAIS approach requires at least two sessions (each session lasting 40 min).

There are several limitations of this study that should be acknowledged. First, there was a gap in the implant surgery training between the actual teaching of simulated implant surgery in the laboratory and the implementation of the technique in clinical practice, and only a small number of students received this training. Some researchers have proposed that because classrooms have relatively set hours and learning durations, studies on implant placement training ought to be conducted in the classroom [14]. Further, the dynamic navigation devices used in this research may not be suitable for teaching. In the future, it will be necessary to develop dynamic navigation devices more suitable for implantation education to reduce teaching costs and alleviate the workload of teaching. Additionally, although the one-week interval was similar to previous studies, it might not be enough to eliminate the effect of the previous training approach on the later approach [15]. Individual students may still have been affected by the order effect. In addition, this study did not consider each student's gender, their experiences with previous phantom exercises (such as tooth preparation and dental caries removal), video games, or playing instruments. Moreover, the small sample size of students in this study may have biased the results. Future multi-centre studies might be useful for increasing the sample size of comparable populations [4]. Further, implant trueness varies with different static guides [25, 43, 44], however, this study only assessed the half-guided protocol. Finally, this study may have underestimated the trueness of s-CAIS due to repeated use.

Conclusion

Based on the results of this study, both dynamic navigation and static guides can improve students' implant trueness and self-confidence in implant placement. The surgical speed of students improved significantly over the first two attempts using freehand-CAIS, and within the first three attempts using d-CAIS and s-CAIS. Moreover, the surgical speed of s-CAIS reached a plateau after the third attempt. In terms of implant trueness, dynamic navigation exhibited superior performance in angular deviation control. However, due to the complex workflow of the dynamic navigation system and the consideration of students’ acceptance of this technique, we recommend scheduling training in the d-CAIS approach after training in the freehand-CAIS and s-CAIS in the teaching curriculum.
