Activation of Anergic B Cells During Development of Autoimmunity
Authors: Mohammad Mansoor, Brandon K. Hilliard, Mia J. Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn healthy individuals, autoreactive B cells are normally silenced via central and peripheral tolerance mechanisms. In the periphery, when autoreactive B cells recognize self‐antigen via their B cell receptor (BCR) (signal 1) but do not receive T cell help (signal 2), they undergo the tolerance mechanism called anergy. Human anergic B cells are termed BND (Naïve IgD+ B cells). Previous studies have shown that BND cells are decreased in the peripheral blood of individuals with autoimmunity, suggesting they may have become activated and contributed to the development of disease. In line with this, recently it has been found that a portion of the remaining anergic B cells present in the blood of autoimmune individuals exhibit an activated phenotype. In this review, we will summarize what is known and unknown about these cells, including their relation to other recognized autoreactive B cell subsets, how they become activated in the first place, and what their pathogenic functions are. Understanding how BND cells break tolerance and contribute to disease will advance the development of new therapeutic approaches and highlight novel cell populations amenable to depletion.

Silencing of Autoreactive B Cells via Anergy

A key feature of the adaptive immune system is its ability to differentiate self and foreign antigens. To this end, self‐reactive B cells are regulated at two specific checkpoints, known as central and peripheral tolerance. These mechanisms of regulation are essential, as it is estimated that 70% of B cells produced in the bone marrow are autoreactive [1]. Central tolerance operates through two key mechanisms that will be outlined briefly here. Immature B cells that express an autoreactive BCR will first undergo receptor editing, in which the B cell will rearrange its light chain genes to remove self‐reactivity. If this process is successful, the B cell will enter the periphery as a transitional B cell. However, if autoreactivity persists, the B cell undergoes apoptosis through clonal deletion [2]. It is generally believed that autoreactive B cells with high affinity for self‐antigen are removed through mechanisms of central tolerance. B cells with low to moderate affinity for self, on the other hand, can enter peripheral circulation where they will undergo peripheral tolerance.

Studies have estimated that approximately 20%–40% of B cells that exit the bone marrow retain some level of autoreactivity [1]. In healthy individuals, these autoreactive B cells are maintained in a state of anergy, a reversible form of functional unresponsiveness. Due to chronic antigen exposure and lack of T cell help, these autoreactive B cells exhibit impaired B cell receptor (BCR) signaling, characterized by reduced calcium mobilization and diminished tyrosine phosphorylation following cross‐linking of the BCR [3]. As a result, anergic B cells have a decreased half‐life compared to non‐autoreactive B cells and are passively eliminated over time. Importantly, this state of functional unresponsiveness has been shown to be reversible [3, 4], and therefore, these cells have been proposed to serve as precursors to the pathogenic B cells observed in autoimmune diseases. Consistent with this possibility, it has been shown that there is an increased frequency of autoreactive B cells in the periphery of individuals with various autoimmune diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), Sjogren's disease (SjD), Type 1 diabetes (T1D), myasthenia gravis (MG), and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [5]. These results imply a connection between the inability to maintain peripheral B cell tolerance and the development of autoimmunity.

Phenotypically, anergic B cells are characterized as mature “naïve‐like” B cells that have downregulated surface IgM but maintain normal levels of IgD. In humans, functional anergy has been identified in a population of B cells coined BND (Naïve IgD+) B cells [3]. In addition to low levels of IgM on the cell surface, anergic B cells exhibit reduced expression of co‐receptors such as CD19 and CD21, increased expression of the inhibitory receptor CD22, and lack of expression of activation markers, such as CD86 and CD69 [3]. Functionally, these cells demonstrate diminished proliferative capacity, impaired differentiation, and reduced antibody secretion following stimulation [4, 6]. The state of unresponsiveness has been shown to be mediated, at least in part, by increased inhibitory signaling mechanisms, such as increased activity of two inositol phosphatases, SHIP‐1 and PTEN [7, 8, 9].

To characterize the antigen specificity of BCRs within the BND compartment, BCRs were sequenced from these cells, cloned, expressed as monoclonal antibodies, and tested against various autoantigens and human epithelioma cell line 2 (HEp‐2) cells [3]. These studies confirmed that BND‐derived antibodies are autoreactive. Interestingly, however, they also demonstrated that many of these antibodies are polyreactive, recognizing more than one antigen, including insulin, LPS, and DNA [10]. These observations raise the possibility that the BND compartment is not simply a silenced autoreactive population but may also fulfill physiological functions. Consistent with this hypothesis, polyreactive antibodies have been shown to contribute to broad neutralization of pathogens, such as influenza and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [11, 12], suggesting that BND cells may serve as a reservoir for broadly protective humoral immune responses. Further evidence comes from SARS‐CoV‐2 infection where BND cells from severely infected individuals were activated and exhibited reduced inhibitory receptor expression [13]. Together, these studies highlight the reversibility of the anergic BND state and its potential to serve a physiological function.

However, excessive accumulation of these auto‐ and polyreactive B cells in the periphery could tip the scale towards development of autoimmunity. In line with this, individuals with autoimmunity have an increased frequency of polyreactive mature naive B cells in their periphery compared to controls [5]. Furthermore, we recently found that clonally expanded islet‐reactive B cells from the blood of autoantibody positive prediabetic and new‐onset T1D donors are enriched in polyreactivity [14]. While not directly shown, these clonally expanded polyreactive B cells may have arisen from BND cells.

Perturbation of the Anergic BND Compartment in Autoimmune Patients

Following the identification of the human anergic BND compartment in peripheral blood, we and others sought to determine whether the frequency or responsiveness of anergic B cells was altered in individuals with autoimmunity. Studies have shown that the frequency of self‐reactive anergic B cells is decreased in autoantibody positive and new‐onset T1D patients [10], as well as individuals with autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD) [15], and SLE [16]. Functionally, BND cells from patients with rheumatoid arthritis have been shown to harbor alterations in BCR signaling molecules leading to enhanced responsiveness [17] and BND cells from patients with T1D and AITD exhibit decreased expression of PTEN, the negative regulator of the PI3K signaling pathway [7]. Taken together, while anergy is a bona‐fide mechanism that prevents activation of autoreactive B cells in non‐autoimmune individuals, this state of unresponsiveness is fragile and has been shown to be disrupted in patients with various forms of autoimmunity.

Using mass cytometry and a magnetic enrichment scheme to enrich insulin‐binding B cells from the peripheral blood [18], we more deeply phenotyped the BND compartment of new‐onset T1D and control donors. We found that the insulin‐binding BND compartment in T1D donors expresses increased markers of activation, including CD69, CD86, CD95, CXCR3, HLA‐DR, and CD11c compared to healthy controls [4], suggesting these cells have broken anergy and become activated. These findings are in line with a previous study that identified that BND cells from SLE patients exhibit increased expression of CD95 [19]. Upon further evaluation of the BND compartment, we found that BND cells can be divided into two distinct subsets based on differing surface marker expression of CD21 and CXCR5. We termed these subsets BND1 (CD21+ CXCR5+) and BND2 (CD21‐ CXCR5‐). BND2 cells were found to express the markers of B cell activation, while BND1 cells reflect the more classic anergic B cells that lack these markers of activation. In addition, we found that in young‐onset rapidly progressing T1D donors the ratio of insulin‐binding BND1 (anergic) to BND2 (activated) cells had shifted more heavily towards BND2 cells compared to controls and older‐onset (> 18 years of age) T1D donors demonstrating the instability of the anergic B cell compartment in young autoimmune individuals [4]. Since their initial description, increases in BND2 cells have also been identified in autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD) (unpublished) and childhood‐onset SLE [20], establishing this population as a conserved subset across multiple autoimmune disorders. Taken together, these findings suggest that BND cells represent a therapeutically targetable population that may directly contribute to disease pathogenesis via mechanisms including, but not limited to, autoantibody secretion, antigen presentation to self‐reactive T cells, and cytokine secretion.

Activation of Anergic B Cells and Differentiation into BND2 Cells

Given that autoreactive B cells appear to break anergy in individuals with autoimmunity, it is important to understand the various mechanisms by which this might occur. Studies from mouse models of B cell anergy have shown that anergy can be breached in multiple ways [21]. Interestingly, in mice ssDNA‐ and dsDNA‐specific B cells have been shown to differ in signals required to break anergy, highlighting the heterogeneity that exists in B cell anergy. Whereas ssDNA‐specific B cells in mouse models can break anergy via receipt of anti‐IgM, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and CD40L/IL‐4 + anti‐IgM, dsDNA‐specific B cells are more resistant to T‐independent stimuli [22]. However, the anergic state of the dsDNA‐specific B cells is partially reversible via CD40L and IL‐4 stimulation [5]. This data indicates that anergy in B cells is reversible and can be overcome either via T‐independent or T‐dependent mechanisms.

Similarly, we have shown in humans that T cell derived factors such as CD40L and IL‐21 are sufficient to convert human anergic BND1 cells into activated BND2 cells in vitro [4]. In accordance with this, it has been shown that individuals with T1D have an increased frequency of insulin‐reactive T cells in their periphery, which correlates with anti‐insulin antibody titers [23]. Thus, improper removal of self‐reactive T cells from the thymus that allows their escape into the periphery likely enables cognate B‐T cell interactions that can break B cell anergy in T1D individuals. Previously we have demonstrated that loss of anergic insulin‐binding B cells in T1D donors was associated with the high‐risk HLA DR4‐DQ8 haplotype, supporting this notion that receipt of T cell help may be a major mechanism by which anergy is broken [24].

Large‐scale genetic association studies have identified numerous single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with autoimmune diseases, including SLE, T1D, and RA. These analyses have identified mutations in various pathways implicated in B cell regulation and activation. These include TLR/STAT signaling (e.g., IRF5, PTPN2), BCR signaling (e.g., PTPN22, BLK) and B cell development (e.g., IKZF3, IL7R) [25, 26]. Indeed, decreased frequencies of anergic B cells in non‐autoimmune but high‐risk first‐degree relatives has been shown to be associated with SNPs in IKZF3, PTPN2, and PTPN22 [24], and mice that lack Ptpn2 in their B cells exhibit a decrease in the anergic T3 compartment [27]. Supporting the idea that aberrant signaling may contribute to activation of anergic B cells, chronic BCR signaling has been shown to convert mouse anergic B cells into age‐associated B cells (ABCs) [28]. Stimulation of human anergic BND1 cells with the TLR7 agonist, R848, and IFN‐γ can also break anergy, but stimulation with TLR7 or TLR9 agonists alone do not, nor do IFN‐γ or IFN‐α alone (data not shown).

B cell survival factors are similarly associated with autoimmunity. Given that anergic B cells have a relatively short half‐life, and therefore are in competition with other non‐autoreactive B cells for survival, they rely heavily on the survival factor, BAFF [29]. Indeed, in both mice and humans, elevated levels of BAFF have been associated with autoimmunity [29, 30]. In addition, following chronic stimulation with elevated levels of BAFF, anergic B cells have been shown to mount an antibody response [31, 32], supporting the idea that increased survival factors may also contribute to the breakdown of peripheral tolerance.

As mentioned previously, polyreactive B cells that bind multiple antigens, such as self and foreign antigens, are expanded in multiple autoimmune diseases. There is significant evidence showing that B cells expressing polyreactive BCRs contribute to the breakdown of tolerance. For instance, when recombinant antibody sequences that were cloned from BCRs specific to La antigen, a self‐antigen implicated in SLE, were reverted to their germline form, they exhibited polyreactivity which was lost post somatic hypermutation [33]. These findings indicate that polyreactive immunoglobulin genes can somatically mutate away from polyreactivity towards specific autoreactivity. Similar evidence has been shown for BCRs against aquaporin‐4 from patients with neuromyelitis optica where polyreactive germline clones somatically mutate to become specific for self‐antigen [34]. It is not well understood what leads to activation of these polyreactive B cells. Correlative evidence with viral infections, such as EBV [35], and mechanistic studies using model particulate antigens mimicking virus epitope display [36, 37], have shown induction of strong B cell signaling, which could push polyreactive cells out of anergy: turning BND1 cells into BND2.

Together, these results indicate that receipt of T cell help, TLR signaling, altered BCR signaling, B cell survival signals, and polyreactivity can drive the breakdown of peripheral tolerance and loss of B cell anergy. These pathways are not mutually exclusive, and therefore, when an anergic B cell encounters one or, more likely, a combination of these factors, anergy can be disrupted and lead to the participation of self‐reactive B cells in the development of autoimmunity.

Pathogenic Function of BND2 Cells in Development of Autoimmunity

Autoreactive B cells participate in the pathogenesis of autoimmunity through autoantibody production, antigen presentation, and cytokine production. While autoantibodies are hallmarks of many autoimmune disorders, some play more of a pathogenic role, such as in SLE, MG, and Graves' disease, whereas antigen presentation to self‐reactive T cells is a primary pathogenic function of B cells in diseases such as T1D, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, and Celiac disease. B cells can also produce pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IFN‐γ, TNF‐α, and IL‐6, that can help shape the immune response and contribute to the local inflammatory environment [4].

Once anergy is broken, these autoreactive B cells likely contribute to all three of these main effector functions. Previously, it was shown that the frequency of insulin‐binding BND2 cells positively correlates with anti‐insulin autoantibody titers in T1D patients. These findings would suggest that BND2 cells, or their differentiated progeny, may become plasmablasts/plasma cells. However, when sorted and stimulated with a combination of factors to induce differentiation into antibody secreting cells (ASCs), BND2 cells were found to differentiate slightly better than mature naive and BND1 cells, but not as well as memory B cells or DN2 cells [4]. In contrast to BND2 cells, DN2 cells rapidly differentiate into ASCs, which has been reported by others [38, 39]. DN2 cells phenotypically resemble BND2 cells, in that they both have lost expression of CD21 and CXCR5 and express CD11c. However, the majority of DN2 cells have class‐switched to IgG and are therefore considered a memory subset, whereas BND2 cells have not class‐switched and fall within the previously recognized anergic BND compartment. Due to increased expression of Tbet in both the BND2 and DN2 compartments [4], both subsets would be among the broader age‐associated B cell subset (ABCs) [40]. Since their discovery, ABCs have been shown to represent a heterogenous subpopulation that is enriched in patients with autoimmunity, including SLE, RA, and MS [41]. Therefore, it is possible that BND2 cells are precursors to DN2 cells. However, preliminary studies in our laboratory indicate BND2 and DN2 cells share a similar but also distinct BCR repertoire, including different J segment usage, and have not yet been found within the same clonal trees (data not shown).

Interestingly, when human B cell subsets were assayed for their ability to produce various cytokines immediately ex vivo without stimulation, we found that BND2, DN2, and memory B cells produced similar elevated levels of IL‐10, TNF‐α, and IL‐6 compared to mature naive or BND1 cells, but BND2 cells produced significantly more IFN‐γ compared to all other B cell subsets [4]. To further elucidate whether BND2 cells could act as antigen presenting cells, we performed single‐cell RNA sequencing on sorted B cell subsets, including BND2 and DN2 cells. We found that BND2 cells express significantly more genes associated with antigen processing and presentation compared to all other B cell subsets, including DN2 cells (data not shown). Functional studies to validate these findings are currently ongoing in our laboratory. Nevertheless, the current data thus far suggest that BND2 cells may represent a distinct B cell subset from DN2 cells, and that while DN2 cells are primed to differentiate into ASCs, BND2 cells may be more apt to become potent antigen‐presenting cells. Hence, after receipt of T cell help, TLR stimulation, cross‐reactivity with another antigen, and/or increased survival signals, BND1 cells may break anergy and become BND2 cells, where they secrete various cytokines and likely act as APCs. Unanswered Questions in the Field Regarding BND2 Cells

Despite these advances on how human anergic B cells may break anergy and their role in the pathogenesis of autoimmunity, several fundamental questions regarding the ontogeny and biology of BND2 cells remain unanswered. While we have shown that BND1 cells can differentiate into BND2 cells in vitro [4], it is unclear whether BND1 cells are the only cell subset from which BND2 cells can originate. Likewise, their relationship to the DN2 B cell population remains to be determined. Finally, the molecular and cellular signals responsible for BND2 differentiation, including the roles of inflammatory stimuli and CD4+ T cell help, have yet to be fully elucidated and confirmed. Further studies to understand how autoreactive B cells break anergy, what they become once anergy is broken, and how they participate in disease will inform future therapies to predict, delay, or prevent development of autoimmunity.
