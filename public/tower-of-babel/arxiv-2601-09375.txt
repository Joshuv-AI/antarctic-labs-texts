1.
Introduction

The study of Toeplitz and Hankel operators on Hardy spaces is a central theme in operator theory and complex function theory. Classical foundational works such as Duren
[
13
]
, Garnett
[
17
]
, Bercovici
[
3
]
, and Peller
[
26
]
established a deep interplay between bounded analytic functions, invariant subspaces, and operator-theoretic factorization phenomena. In this framework, Sarason’s introduction of truncated Toeplitz operators (TTOs)
[
28
]
marked a significant development: for a nonconstant inner function
u
, the operator acts on the model space

\mathcal{K}_{u}=H^{2}\ominus uH^{2},

revealing new algebraic, geometric, and spectral structures (see also
[
16
]
). Subsequent works explored compactness, commutation, invariant subspaces, and structural decompositions for TTOs and related multiplication compressions; see, for example, Gorkin–Zheng
[
18
]
and Câmara–Ptak–Klís–Łanucha
[
7
]
.

A parallel but comparatively newer direction concerns the “dual” version of truncated Toeplitz operators.
Ding and Sang
[
11
]
introduced dual truncated Toeplitz operators (DTTOs), acting on the orthogonal complement

\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}=uH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-},

and established their basic structural properties.
Their work demonstrated that DTTOs admit a representation as a
2\times 2
Toeplitz–Hankel operator matrix, a viewpoint refined further by Sang, Qin, and Ding
[
29
]
, who proved Brown–Halmos type theorems and factorization results for DTTOs.
Subsequent contributions deepened the structural theory:
Li, Sang, and Ding
[
24
]
described the commutant and invariant subspaces of DTTOs;
Gu
[
15
]
obtained characterizations and structural criteria;
and Wang, Zhao, and Zheng
[
31
]
analyzed essential commutation and related spectral questions.
The dual of the compressed shift, studied by Câmara and Ross
[
6
]
, further highlighted the natural role of DTTOs in harmonic analysis and operator models.

The norm attaining property of bounded linear operators is deeply connected to several fundamental structures in functional analysis and Banach space geometry. One prominent link is with the Radon–Nikodym property, introduced into operator theory by Choi
[
10
]
, which provides powerful duality tools for detecting norm attainment. Another important connection is with
reflexivity
: in many classical settings, reflexive Banach spaces ensure weak compactness of the unit ball, which plays a decisive role in guaranteeing that operators attain their norm.

The study of norm attaining operators has developed along multiple active directions. A quantitative refinement is given by the Bishop–Phelps–Bollobás theorem and its various extensions
[
1
,
2
,
9
]
, which not only assert density of norm attaining functionals or operators, but also provide
explicit stability estimates
for approximating almost norm attaining operators by genuinely norm attaining
ones.

From a structural viewpoint, norm attainment has been used to analyze invariant subspaces of non-normal operators

[
20
,
23
]
, where classical spectral methods fail to apply directly. Moreover, norm attaining techniques are useful in spectral analysis
[
21
,
22
]
. In fact, we have the class of norm attaining operators is dense in
\mathcal{B}(H)
(see
[
14
,
25
]
).

Toeplitz operators that attain their norm were first systematically studied in the foundational work of Brown and Douglas
[
5
]
. Their celebrated rigidity theorem shows that a nonzero Toeplitz operator on the Hardy space over unit disc is a partial isometry if and only if it is of the form
T_{u}
or
T_{u}^{*}
for an inner function
u
, highlighting how norm attainment interacts with inner function structure.

Yoshino
[
30
]
later gave a complete characterization of norm attaining symbols for Toeplitz and Hankel operators. His results establish that these operators attain their norm precisely when the symbol admits an analytic and coanalytic factorization.

In classical Toeplitz operator (TO) theory on
H^{2}
, the norm attainment of
T_{\varphi}
with
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=1

forces global unimodularity of the symbol
\varphi
. In contrast, for a dual truncated Toeplitz operator

D_{\varphi}=(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})M_{\varphi}|_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}},

extremal vectors in
\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
may vanish on sets of positive measure, so norm attainment alone can not guarantee
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
without a separate
non–vanishing argument. The examples given later highlight this difference and motivate our standing assumption that the symbol
\varphi
is unimodular when studying norm–attaining DTTOs and their relation with TOs.

Analytic case.

We prove that
D_{\varphi}
attains its norm on
uH^{2}
if and only if there exist inner functions
\psi_{+}
and
\chi_{+}
such that

\varphi=\bar{u}\,\bar{\psi}_{+}\chi_{+}.

Moreover, the full extremal set is explicitly described by a Beurling-type form

\mathcal{E}^{(+)}_{\varphi}=u\psi_{+}u_{1}H^{2},

where
u_{1}
is an inner divisor of
u
.

Coanalytic case.

Using the natural conjugation
C_{u}
on
L^{2}
(which exchanges
uH^{2}
and
H^{2}_{-}
), we obtain the coanalytic analogue:

D_{\varphi}
attains its norm on
H^{2}_{-}
if and only if there exist inner functions
\psi_{-}
and
\chi_{-}
such that

\varphi=\,u\,\psi_{-}\overline{\chi}_{-}.

The corresponding extremal set is

\mathcal{E}^{(-)}_{\varphi}=\bar{\psi}_{-}\bar{u}_{1}H^{2}_{-},

where
u_{1}
is an inner divisor of
u
.

Structure of the paper.

Section 2 collects preliminaries on Hardy spaces, Toeplitz and Hankel operators, and the block model for DTTOs.
Section 3 develops the core extremal vector analysis and establishes the analytic and coanalytic factorization theorems for norm attaining DTTOs. Subsequently, the section contains some illustrative examples to demonstrate these results. Section 4 relates the norm attaining property of dual truncated Toeplitz operators to that of classical Toeplitz operators via factorization of the inducing symbol.

2.
Preliminaries

We first fix some standard notation and recall basic facts about Hardy spaces, model spaces, and (truncated) Toeplitz-type operators; see
[
16
]
for a general reference. Let
\mathbb{D}
be the open unit disk and
\mathbb{T}
be the unit circle in the complex plane. Let
L^{2}:=L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
denotes the Hilbert space of square-integrable complex-valued measurable functions on
\mathbb{T}
with respect to normalized arc-length measure
m(d\theta)=\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}
.

2.1.
Hardy space

The Hardy space
H^{2}:=H^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is the norm-closed subspace of
L^{2}
consisting of functions whose Fourier series contain only non-negative frequencies. More precisely, every
f\in L^{2}
admits a Fourier expansion

f(e^{it})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{n}e^{int},

where the Fourier coefficients are given by

c_{n}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(e^{it})\,e^{-int}\,dt.

Then

f\in H^{2}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad c_{n}=0\text{ for all }n<0.

Since
H^{2}
is a closed subspace of the Hilbert space
L^{2}
,
we have the orthogonal decomposition

L^{2}=H^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-},

where the orthogonal complement of
H^{2}
is

H^{2}_{-}:=L^{2}\ominus H^{2}.

The space
H^{2}_{-}
can be identified explicitly as

H^{2}_{-}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{e^{-int}:n\geq 1\},

that is, the closed linear span of the negative-frequency exponentials.

We denote by
L^{\infty}:=L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
, the Banach space of essentially bounded
m
-measurable functions on
\mathbb{T}
. The space
H^{\infty}
is defined as

H^{\infty}:=H^{2}\cap L^{\infty}.

For any
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
, the multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}:L^{2}\to L^{2}
is defined by

M_{\varphi}f=\varphi f,\qquad f\in L^{2}.

Its compression to
H^{2}
yields the
Toeplitz operator
(TO)
T_{\varphi}:H^{2}\to H^{2}
,

T_{\varphi}f=P_{+}(\varphi f),\qquad f\in H^{2}.

Likewise, the
Hankel operator

H_{\varphi}:H^{2}\to H^{2}_{-}
is defined by

H_{\varphi}f=(I-P_{+})(\varphi f),\qquad f\in H^{2}.

We will frequently use the well-known identity

T_{\varphi}^{*}=T_{\bar{\varphi}}.

The
dual Toeplitz operator

S_{\varphi}
on
(H^{2})^{\perp}=H^{2}_{-}=\overline{zH^{2}}
is defined by

S_{\varphi}f=(I-P_{+})(\varphi f),\qquad f\in H^{2}_{-}.

2.2.
Model spaces and truncated Toeplitz operators

Let
u
be a fixed nonconstant inner function, that is,
u\in H^{\infty}
with
|u(e^{it})|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. The associated
model space
is

\mathcal{K}_{u}:=H^{2}\ominus uH^{2}=H^{2}\cap(uH^{2})^{\perp}.

This is a closed subspace of
H^{2}
of finite codimension when
u
is a finite Blaschke product, but of infinite codimension in general (for example, when
u
is an infinite Blaschke product or a singular inner function).

Let
P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}
denote the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}
onto
\mathcal{K}_{u}
. Note that
P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}=P_{+}-P_{uH^{2}}
, where
P_{uH^{2}}=M_{u}P_{+}M_{\overline{u}}
. For
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
, the
truncated Toeplitz operator
(TTO) on
\mathcal{K}_{u}
is defined by

A_{\varphi}:=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}\left.M_{\varphi}\right|_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}.

When
\varphi(z)=z
, the corresponding operator is called
compressed shift
and is denoted by
S_{u}
.

For a nonconstant inner function
u
, the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{K}_{u}
in
L^{2}
is given by

\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}=uH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}.

Thus any
h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
can be written as
h=uf+\overline{zg}
for some
f,g\in H^{2}
. In particular, we have

\mathcal{K}_{u}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}=L^{2}=H^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}=\mathcal{K}_{u}\oplus uH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}.

The orthogonal projection of
L^{2}
onto
\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
can be written as

P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}:=I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}=I-P_{+}+P_{uH^{2}}=I-P_{+}+T_{u}T_{u}^{*}.

2.3.
Dual truncated Toeplitz and truncated Hankel operators

The
dual truncated Toeplitz operator
on
\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
with symbol
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
is defined by

D_{\varphi}:=(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})\left.M_{\varphi}\right|_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}.

Using the identity

P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}=P_{+}-M_{u}P_{+}M_{\bar{u}},

we obtain the concrete formula

(2.1)

D_{\varphi}(h)=(I-P_{+})(\varphi h)+u\,P_{+}(\bar{u}\varphi h),\qquad h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}.

In particular,
(I-P_{+})(\varphi h)\in H^{2}_{-}
and
M_{u}P_{+}M_{\bar{u}}\varphi h\in uH^{2}\subset H^{2}
, so these two terms are orthogonal.

In the special case
\varphi(z)=z
, the operator
D_{\varphi}
is referred to as the
dual of the compressed shift

S_{u}
, and is denoted by
D_{u}
.

The following elementary observations will be useful:

(i)

Let
u\in H^{\infty}
be inner and
g\in H^{2}
. Then
ug\in uH^{2}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
, and by (
2.1
),

\|D_{\varphi}(ug)\|^{2}=\big\|(I-P_{+})\varphi ug\big\|^{2}+\big\|M_{u}P_{+}M_{\bar{u}}\varphi ug\big\|^{2}.

(ii)

For
g\in H^{2}
, we have

\|T_{\varphi}g\|^{2}=\big\|M_{u}T_{\varphi}M_{\bar{u}}ug\big\|^{2}=\big\|M_{u}P_{+}M_{\bar{u}}\varphi ug\big\|^{2}\leq\|D_{\varphi}(ug)\|^{2},\;\text{ by }(i).

For
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
, the big
truncated Hankel
operator
B_{\varphi}:\mathcal{K}_{u}\to\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
is defined by

(2.2)

B_{\varphi}(f)=(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})\varphi f,\qquad f\in\mathcal{K}_{u}.

Its adjoint is given by

(2.3)

B^{*}_{\varphi}(f)=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\bar{\varphi}f),\qquad f\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}.

With respect to the decomposition
L^{2}=\mathcal{K}_{u}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
, the multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}
admits the block matrix representation

M_{\varphi}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{\varphi}&B^{*}_{\bar{\varphi}}\\
B_{\varphi}&D_{\varphi}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{on }\mathcal{K}_{u}\oplus\mathcal{K}^{\perp}_{u}.

From the relation
M_{\varphi}M_{\psi}=M_{\varphi\psi}
, we obtain

(2.4)

\displaystyle B^{*}_{\bar{\varphi}}B_{\psi}

\displaystyle=A_{\varphi\psi}-A_{\varphi}A_{\psi},

(2.5)

\displaystyle B_{\varphi}B^{*}_{\bar{\psi}}

\displaystyle=D_{\varphi\psi}-D_{\varphi}D_{\psi},

(2.6)

\displaystyle B_{\varphi}A_{\psi}

\displaystyle=B_{\varphi\psi}-D_{\varphi}B_{\psi}.

2.4.
Conjugation and basic properties of
D_{\varphi}

For a nonconstant inner function
u
, the canonical conjugation
C_{u}:L^{2}\longrightarrow L^{2}
is given by

C_{u}f(e^{it}):=u(e^{it})\,e^{-it}\,\overline{f(e^{it})},\quad f\in L^{2}.

It satisfies
C_{u}^{2}=I
,
\langle C_{u}f,C_{u}g\rangle=\langle g,f\rangle
, and
\|C_{u}f\|=\|f\|
for all
f,g\in L^{2}
. A key feature is that
C_{u}
exchanges the analytic and coanalytic components of the decomposition

\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}=uH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}.

More precisely,

(2.7)

C_{u}(uH^{2})=H^{2}_{-},\qquad C_{u}(H^{2}_{-})=uH^{2},

and
D_{\varphi}
is
C_{u}
–symmetric
[
15
, Theorem 2.7]
that is,

(2.8)

D_{\varphi}^{*}=C_{u}D_{\varphi}C_{u}.

This identity will later allow analytic norm attainment results for
D_{\varphi}
to be transferred directly to coanalytic norm attainment results for
D_{\bar{\varphi}}
.

Next, We recall some basic properties of DTTOs.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
11
]

The following are true:

(i)

D_{\varphi}
is bounded if and only if
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
. Moreover,
\|D_{\varphi}\|=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
.

(ii)

D_{\varphi}
is compact if and only if
\varphi=0
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.

(iii)

D^{*}_{\varphi}=D_{\bar{\varphi}}
.

2.5.
Block representation of
D_{\varphi}

Define a unitary operator
U:L^{2}=H^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}\to\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}=uH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}
by

(2.9)

U=\begin{bmatrix}M_{u}&0\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}.

Then we have the following block representation:

Lemma 2.2
.

[
29
]

Let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
. Then the dual truncated Toeplitz operator
D_{\varphi}

admits the following unitary equivalence

U^{*}D_{\varphi}U=\begin{bmatrix}T_{\varphi}&H^{*}_{u\bar{\varphi}}\\
H_{u\varphi}&S_{\varphi}\end{bmatrix}

on the space
L^{2}=H^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}
, where the unitary operator
U
is given by (
2.9
).

The anti-unitary operator
V
on
L^{2}
is given by

(2.10)

Vf(z)=\bar{z}\overline{f(z)},\quad f\in L^{2},z\in\mathbb{T}.

Note that
V=V^{-1}
,
VT_{\varphi}=S_{\bar{\varphi}}V
. In addition,
V(H^{2})=H^{2}_{-}
and
V(H^{2}_{-})=H^{2}
, see
[
19
]
for details.

2.6.
Norm attaining operators on Hilbert spaces

We now recall the basic framework of norm attaining operators on a Hilbert space. Let
(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a complex Hilbert space, and let
\mathcal{B}(H)
denote the algebra of all bounded linear operators on
H
. For
T\in\mathcal{B}(H)
, we write

\mathcal{N}(T):=\{x\in H:Tx=0\},\qquad\mathcal{R}(T):=\{Tx:x\in H\}.

An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(H)
is said to be
norm attaining
, or
T\in\mathcal{NA}
, if there exists a nonzero vector
x\in H
such that

\|Tx\|=\|T\|\,\|x\|.

By homogeneity of norm, this is equivalent to the existence of a unit vector
x\in H
with
\|Tx\|=\|T\|
. Any such vector is called an
extremal vector
(or
norm attaining vector
) for
T
. We denote the set of all extremal vectors by

\mathcal{E}_{T}:=\{\,x\in H:\|Tx\|=\|T\|\,\|x\|\,\},

which is a closed subspace
H
. In fact, by
[
27
, Lemma 3.1]
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{T}=\mathcal{N}(\|T\|^{2}I-T^{*}T)
.

We summarize the following phenomenon, which will be used in the study of the norm attaining properties of DTTOs (see
[
8
, Corollary 2.4; Proposition 2.5]
):

Theorem 2.3
.

For
T\in\mathcal{B}(H)
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

T\in\mathcal{NA}
;

(ii)

T^{*}\in\mathcal{NA}
;

(iii)

T^{*}T\in\mathcal{NA}
;

(iv)

TT^{*}\in\mathcal{NA}
;

(v)

\|T\|^{2}
is a common eigenvalue of
T^{*}T
and
TT^{*}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(H)
be such that
A
is unitarily equivalent to
B
. Then
A\in\mathcal{NA}
if and only if
B\in\mathcal{NA}
.

Remark 2.5
.

For
\varphi(z)=z
, by Lemma
2.2
, we obtain

U^{*}D_{u}U=\begin{bmatrix}T_{z}&H^{*}_{u\bar{z}}\\
0&S_{z}\end{bmatrix}:=X\quad\text{on }H^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}.

We claim that the operator
D_{u}
is norm attaining. Let
f\in H^{2}
be a nonzero vector with
\|f\|=1
. Then

\left\|X\begin{bmatrix}f\\[2.0pt]
0\end{bmatrix}\right\|=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}T_{z}f\\[2.0pt]
0\end{bmatrix}\right\|=\|f\|=1=\|X\|.

Thus
X\in\mathcal{NA}
, and by the lemma above, we conclude that
D_{u}
is also norm attaining.

2.7.
The block operator viewpoint

Via the unitary identification between
\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
and
H^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}
, Lemma
2.2
shows that the dual truncated Toeplitz operator
D_{\varphi}
is unitarily equivalent to the block operator matrix

\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}:=\begin{bmatrix}T_{\varphi}&H^{*}_{u\bar{\varphi}}\\
H_{u\varphi}&S_{\varphi}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining if and only if
\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}
is norm attaining, and hence there exists a unit vector
f\oplus g\in H^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}
such that

\|\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}(f\oplus g)\|=\|\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}\|.

Writing out the action,

(2.11)

\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}(f\oplus g)=\big(T_{\varphi}f+H^{*}_{u\bar{\varphi}}g\big)\oplus\big(H_{u\varphi}f+S_{\varphi}g\big),

we obtain coupled extremal relation involving all four block entries of
\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}
. However, in general, norm attainment of the block operator does
not
force any of the individual entries
T_{\varphi}
,
H_{u\varphi}
,
H^{*}_{u\bar{\varphi}}
,
S_{\varphi}
to have trivial kernel or to satisfy any simple kernel condition.

3.
Norm attainment of
D_{\varphi}
on
\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}

A key link between dual truncated Toeplitz operators and block Toeplitz models
arises when the inner function
u
satisfies
u(0)=0
.
By
[
6
, Corollary 4.3]
, we have the following unitary equivalence

D_{\varphi}\cong T_{\varphi}\oplus T_{\varphi}^{*},\qquad\varphi(z)=z.

This observation motivates us to first investigate the norm attaining property of block operators of the form
T_{\varphi}\oplus T^{*}_{\psi}
.

Let
\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}
and consider

T:=T_{\varphi}\oplus T_{\psi}^{*}:H^{2}\oplus H^{2}\longrightarrow H^{2}\oplus H^{2}.

We have
\|T_{\varphi}\|=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
,
\|T_{\psi}^{*}\|=\|T_{\psi}\|=\|\psi\|_{\infty}
, hence

\|T\|=\max\{\|\varphi\|_{\infty},\|\psi\|_{\infty}\}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(H_{1})
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(H_{2})
with
\alpha:=\|A\|
,
\beta:=\|B\|
, and
\gamma:=\max\{\alpha,\beta\}=\|A\oplus B\|
. Then

(i)

If
\alpha>\beta
, then
A\oplus B
is
\mathcal{NA}
if and only if
A
is
\mathcal{NA}
.

(ii)

If
\beta>\alpha
, then
A\oplus B
is
\mathcal{NA}
if and only if
B
is
\mathcal{NA}
.

(iii)

If
\alpha=\beta
, then
A\oplus B
is
\mathcal{NA}
if and only if at least one of
A,B
is
\mathcal{NA}
.

Moreover, whenever
A\oplus B
is
\mathcal{NA}
, there is an extremal vector supported entirely in a maximal–norm summand.

Proof.

Proof of (
i
):
Suppose
\alpha>\beta
.
If
A
is
\mathcal{NA}
at a unit vector
x
, then
x\oplus 0
is the extremal vector for
A\oplus B
.

Conversely, if
A\oplus B
is
\mathcal{NA}
at a unit vector
x\oplus y
, then

(3.1)

\|(A\oplus B)(x\oplus y)\|^{2}=\|A\oplus B\|^{2}=\alpha^{2}.

If
\|y\|\neq 0
, we would get

\|(A\oplus B)(x\oplus y)\|^{2}\leq\alpha^{2}\|x\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\|y\|^{2}<\alpha^{2}\big(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}\big)=\alpha^{2},

which contradicts to the above equality (
3.1
). Therefore,
\|y\|=0
, that forces
y=0
and
\|x\|=1
. From the equation (
3.1
), it follows that
\|Ax\|=\alpha
, that is,
A
is
\mathcal{NA}
.

Proof of (
ii
):
Proof is same as (
i
) by changing the role of
\alpha
by
\beta
.

Proof of (
iii
):
Assume that
\alpha=\beta
.
If
A
is
\mathcal{NA}
at some unit vector
x_{0}
, then
x_{0}\oplus 0
is the extremal vector for
A\oplus B
. On the other hand, if
B
is
\mathcal{NA}
at some unit vector
y_{0}
, then
0\oplus y_{0}
is the extremal vector for
A\oplus B
.

Conversely, if neither
A
nor
B
is
\mathcal{NA}
, then for every nonzero
x\in H_{1}
,
y\in H_{2}
, we have
\|Ax\|<\alpha\|x\|
and
\|By\|<\alpha\|y\|
. Hence for any unit vector
x\oplus y\in H_{1}\oplus H_{2}
,

\|(A\oplus B)(x,y)\|^{2}=\|Ax\|^{2}+\|By\|^{2}<\alpha^{2}.

Then
A\oplus B

\mathcal{NA}

Next, we show that norm attainment of
D_{\varphi}
on either analytic or coanalytic components forces unimodularity of the symbol.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
with
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=1
. If
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining on either
uH^{2}
or
H^{2}_{-}
, then
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Let
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining in
uH^{2}
. Then there exists a nonzero
h\in H^{2}
such that

\|D_{\varphi}uh\|=\|uh\|.

By Proposition
3.7
, it follows that
M_{\varphi}uh\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
. Therefore,

\|D_{\varphi}uh\|=\|(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})M_{\varphi}uh\|=\|\varphi uh\|=\|\varphi h\|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\,\|h\|=\|h\|.

Since
\|D_{\varphi}uh\|=\|uh\|=\|h\|
, equality holds in the above inequality. Thus
\|\varphi h\|=\|h\|
, and it gives us

\int_{\mathbb{T}}(1-|\varphi|^{2})|h|^{2}\,dm=0.

Since
(1-|\varphi|^{2})|h|^{2}\geq 0
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, we get

(1-|\varphi|^{2})\,|h|^{2}=0\qquad\text{a.e.\ on }\mathbb{T}.

Thus
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
S:=\{e^{it}\in\mathbb{T}:|h(e^{it})|>0\}
. Since
h\in H^{2}
is nonzero, by
[
12
, Theorem 6.13]
, we have

m(\{e^{it}\in\mathbb{T}:h(e^{it})=0\})=0,\quad\text{ that is, }\;m(\mathbb{T}\setminus S)=0.

Consequently,
S
has full measure, and hence
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.

Assume that
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining in
H^{2}_{-}
. Then by the definition of
C_{u}
,
D_{\bar{\varphi}}
is norm attaining in
uH^{2}
. Using the earlier argument, we conclude that
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.
∎

In classical Toeplitz operator theory on
H^{2}
, norm attainment of
T_{\varphi}
with
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=1
forces
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.
But for the dual truncated Toeplitz operator
D_{\varphi}
,
norm attainment on the mixed components
uH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}
does not ensure that an extremal vector is non-vanishing a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. Hence global unimodularity of
\varphi
can not be concluded without an additional non-vanishing argument, as shown by the example below.

Example 3.6
.

Let
u(z)=z
. Then the corresponding model space is

\mathcal{K}_{u}=H^{2}\ominus zH^{2}=\operatorname{span}\{1\}.

Consequently, the orthogonal complement is

\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}=\{\,h\in L^{2}:\langle h,1\rangle=0\,\}=\left\{h\in L^{2}:\int_{\mathbb{T}}h\,dm=0\right\}.

Now choose a measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{T}
satisfying
0<m(E)<1
, and define

\varphi=\chi_{E}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}),\qquad\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=1.

Since
\varphi(e^{it})=0
on
\mathbb{T}\setminus E
(which has positive measure),

|\varphi|\neq 1\quad\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{T},

so
\varphi
is not unimodular.

Next, choose disjoint measurable subsets
E_{1},E_{2}\subset E
with
m(E_{1}),m(E_{2})>0
,
and define

h:=\chi_{E_{1}}-\frac{m(E_{1})}{m(E_{2})}\,\chi_{E_{2}}.

Then
h\neq 0
, because
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
have positive measure and are disjoint.
Now

\widehat{h}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}h\,dm=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(\chi_{E_{1}}-\frac{m(E_{1})}{m(E_{2})}\chi_{E_{2}}\right)dm=m(E_{1})-\frac{m(E_{1})}{m(E_{2})}m(E_{2})=0.

From the definition of
\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
, it follows that

h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}.

Since the support of
h
lies inside
E
, multiplication by the symbol gives

\varphi h=\chi_{E}h=h.

Therefore,

D_{\varphi}h=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(\varphi h)=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}h=h

(because
h
already belongs to the range of
P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}
).
Thus

\|D_{\varphi}h\|=\|h\|,

so
D_{\varphi}
attains its norm at the nonzero vector
h
.

However, the symbol
\varphi=\chi_{E}
is not unimodular a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. Therefore, unlike the classical Brown–Douglas norm-attainment phenomenon for Toeplitz operators, norm attainment for a DTTO does
not
imply global
unimodularity of the symbol.

The following proposition establishes that for unimodular
\varphi
, the norm attaining set of
D_{\varphi}
is invariant for
M_{\varphi}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
with
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. Define

\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}=\{h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}:\,\|D_{\varphi}h\|=\|h\|\},

and

C=\Big\{\,h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}:M_{\varphi}h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}\Big\}.

Then
C=\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
, and
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining if and only if
C\neq\{0\}
.

Proof.

First, we show if
C\neq\{0\}
, then
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining.
Let
0\neq h\in C
. Then by definition
M_{\varphi}h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
. Since
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, we get

\|D_{\varphi}h\|=\|(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})M_{\varphi}h\|=\|M_{\varphi}h\|=\|h\|.

Moreover,
\|D_{\varphi}\|=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=1
. Hence
D_{\varphi}
attains its norm at
h
.

If
D_{\varphi}
attains its norm, then there exists a nonzero
h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
such that

\|D_{\varphi}h\|=\|h\|.

Now

\|h\|=\|D_{\varphi}h\|=\|(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})M_{\varphi}h\|\leq\|M_{\varphi}h\|=\|h\|.

Since the leftmost and rightmost quantities in the above inequality coincide, we get

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})M_{\varphi}h\|=\|M_{\varphi}h\|.

Now,
\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}M_{\varphi}h\|^{2}+\|(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})M_{\varphi}h\|^{2}=\|M_{\varphi}h\|^{2}
and (
3.2
) further imply that
M_{\varphi}h=(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})M_{\varphi}h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
.
Thus
h\in C
, and
C\neq\{0\}
.

This proves the equivalence.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
with
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
be the extremal set for
D_{\varphi}
. Then
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}\subseteq N(B^{*}_{\bar{\varphi}})
, where
B_{\varphi}
is the operator defined by (
2.2
).

Proof.

Let
h\in\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
. Then by Proposition
3.7
,
M_{\varphi}h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
. This gives
B^{*}_{\bar{\varphi}}h=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(M_{\varphi}h)=0
, that is,
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}\subseteq N(B^{*}_{\bar{\varphi}})
.
∎

Next, we show that for a unimodular symbol
\varphi
, extremal vectors of a norm-attaining
D_{\varphi}
satisfy the following Toeplitz–Hankel equations.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
with
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
and
g_{+}\in H^{2}
,
f_{-}\in H^{2}_{-}
be such that
D_{\varphi}D^{*}_{\varphi}(ug_{+}\oplus f_{-})=ug_{+}\oplus f_{-}
. Then
f_{-},g_{+}
satisfy the following Toeplitz-Hankel equations:

(3.3)

[T_{\varphi}T^{*}_{\varphi}-T_{\bar{u}\varphi}T^{*}_{\bar{u}\varphi}]g_{+}=0

(3.4)

[H_{\varphi}H^{*}_{\varphi}-H_{u\varphi}H^{*}_{u\varphi}]f_{-}=0.

Simplifying (
3.4
), further we obtain

(3.5)

[T^{*}_{\varphi}T_{\varphi}-T^{*}_{u\varphi}T_{u\varphi}]Vf_{-}=0.

Proof.

Let
h=ug_{+}\oplus f_{-}\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
. Then

\begin{split}D_{\varphi}D^{*}_{\varphi}h&=h\\
D_{\varphi}D_{\bar{\varphi}}h&=h\\
[(I-P_{+})\varphi+uP_{+}\bar{u}\varphi]D_{\bar{\varphi}}h&=h\\
uP_{+}\bar{u}\varphi D_{\bar{\varphi}}h&=uP_{+}\bar{u}h\\
uP_{+}\bar{u}\varphi[(I-P_{+})\bar{\varphi}h+uP_{+}\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}h]&=uP_{+}\bar{u}h\\
P_{+}\bar{u}\varphi(I-P_{+})\bar{\varphi}h+P_{+}\varphi P_{+}\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}h&=P_{+}\bar{u}h\\
P_{+}\bar{u}|\varphi|^{2}h-P_{+}\bar{u}\varphi P_{+}\bar{\varphi}h+P_{+}\varphi P_{+}\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}h&=P_{+}\bar{u}h\\
\end{split}

For
g_{+}\in H^{2}
, let
h=ug_{+}\in uH^{2}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{\perp}_{u}
. Then using the unimodularity of
\varphi
, the above equation becomes

(3.6)

\begin{split}P_{+}\bar{u}ug_{+}-P_{+}\bar{u}\varphi P_{+}\bar{\varphi}ug_{+}+P_{+}\varphi P_{+}\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}ug_{+}&=P_{+}\bar{u}ug_{+}\\
P_{+}\varphi P_{+}\bar{\varphi}g_{+}-P_{+}\bar{u}\varphi P_{+}\bar{\varphi}ug_{+}&=0\\
[T_{\varphi}T^{*}_{\varphi}-T_{\bar{u}\varphi}T^{*}_{\bar{u}\varphi}]g_{+}&=0.\end{split}

Again
D_{\varphi}D_{\varphi}^{*}h=h,\quad h\in K_{u}^{\perp}=uH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}
. Then

\begin{split}D_{\varphi}D_{\bar{\varphi}}h&=h\\
[(I-P_{+})\varphi+uP_{+}\bar{u}\varphi]D_{\bar{\varphi}}h&=h\\
(I-P_{+})\varphi D_{\bar{\varphi}}h&=(I-P_{+})h\\
(I-P_{+})\varphi[(I-P_{+})\bar{\varphi}h+uP_{+}\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}h]&=(I-P_{+})h\\
(I-P_{+})\varphi(I-P_{+})\bar{\varphi}h+(I-P_{+})\varphi uP_{+}\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}h&=(I-P_{+})h\\
(I-P_{+})|\varphi|^{2}h-(I-P_{+})\varphi P_{+}\bar{\varphi}h+(I-P_{+})\varphi uP_{+}\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}h&=(I-P_{+})h.\end{split}

Now let
h=f_{-}\in H^{2}_{-}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{\perp}_{u}
, then the above equation becomes

(3.7)

\begin{split}(I-P_{+})f_{-}-(I-P_{+})\varphi P_{+}\bar{\varphi}f_{-}+(I-P_{+})\varphi uP_{+}\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}f_{-}&=f_{-}\\
[H_{\varphi}H^{*}_{\varphi}-H_{u\varphi}H^{*}_{u\varphi}]f_{-}&=0.\end{split}

For
\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}
, we have the following relation (see
[
19
]
),

S_{\varphi\psi}=H_{\varphi}H^{*}_{\bar{\psi}}+S_{\varphi}S_{\psi}.

Thus from equation
3.7
, it follows that

\begin{split}[S_{\varphi\bar{\varphi}}-S_{\varphi}S_{\bar{\varphi}}-(S_{u\varphi\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}}-S_{u\varphi}S_{\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}})]f_{-}&=0\\
[S_{\varphi}S_{\bar{\varphi}}-S_{u\varphi}S_{\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}}]f_{-}&=0\\
[VT_{\bar{\varphi}}VVT_{\varphi}V-VT_{\bar{u}\bar{\varphi}}VVT_{u\varphi}V]f_{-}&=0\\
[T^{*}_{\varphi}T_{\varphi}-T^{*}_{u\varphi}T_{u\varphi}]Vf_{-}&=0.\end{split}

∎

Consider the orthogonal decomposition
L^{2}=\mathcal{K}_{u}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
. Then for any
f\in L^{2}
, we can write

(3.8)

\|f\|^{2}=\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}f\|^{2}+\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}f\|^{2}.

The following lemma provides a componentwise analysis for the study of norm-attaining DTTOs.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
with
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=1
. Then for
h\in H^{2}
and
g\in H^{2}_{-}
,

(i)

\|D_{\varphi}(uh)\|=\|uh\|\iff P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi uh)=0\iff P_{+}(\varphi uh)\in uH^{2};

(ii)

\|D_{\varphi}(g)\|=\|g\|\iff P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi g)=0\iff P_{+}(\varphi g)\in uH^{2}.

Proof.

Note that for
f\in L^{2}
,

\begin{split}P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(f)&=0\\
\iff P_{+}f-M_{u}P_{+}M_{\bar{u}}f&=0\iff P_{+}f\in uH^{2}.\end{split}

Since
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=1
, by Proposition
3.5
,
\varphi
is unimodular. Taking
f=\varphi uh
and
f=\varphi g
in the identity (
3.8
), we get (i) and (ii), respectively.
∎

A central difficulty in understanding extremal vectors for
D_{\varphi}
on the orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}=uH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}
is that the projection
P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}
destroys the natural orthogonality between
analytic and coanalytic components. Although
x\in uH^{2}
and
y\in H^{2}_{-}
satisfy
x\perp y
in
L^{2}
, their images under
D_{\varphi}
typically interact in a highly nontrivial way. The behaviour of the mixed term

\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle
becomes especially delicate when one studies the function

\mathbb{R}\ni t\ \longmapsto\ \|D_{\varphi}(tx+y)\|^{2}-\|tx+y\|^{2},

whose maximization at
t=1
encodes the extremality of
f=x\oplus y
. The next result (cf. Lemma
3.11
) isolates precisely how this dependence must collapse. It shows that extremality forces the three quantities
\|D_{\varphi}x\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2}
,
\|D_{\varphi}y\|^{2}-\|y\|^{2}
, and

\Re\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle
to satisfy rigid algebraic identities, causing the quadratic to degenerate into a perfect square. This structural constraint is fundamental for the geometric rigidity present in the extremal set of
D_{\varphi}
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
u
be inner and
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
with
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
,
and suppose
0\neq f\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
is extremal for
D_{\varphi}
, that is,
\|D_{\varphi}f\|=\|f\|
.
Write
f=x\oplus y
with
x\in uH^{2}
and
y\in H^{2}_{-}
. Define, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

F(t):=\|D_{\varphi}(tx+y)\|^{2}-\|tx+y\|^{2}.

Then we get the following:

(i)

F(t)=\alpha t^{2}+2\beta t+\gamma
, where

\alpha:=\|D_{\varphi}x\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2}\leq 0
,

\beta:=\Re\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle
,

\gamma:=\|D_{\varphi}y\|^{2}-\|y\|^{2}\leq 0
;

(ii)

\beta=-\alpha,\qquad\gamma=\alpha.
Equivalently,

\|D_{\varphi}x\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2}=\|D_{\varphi}y\|^{2}-\|y\|^{2}=-\,\Re\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle\,.

In particular,

F(t)\leq 0
for all
t
,
F(1)=0
, and
F(t)=\alpha\,(t-1)^{2}.

Proof.

Proof of (i) :
Set
a:=\varphi x,\quad b:=\varphi y.
Then we can write

F(t)=\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(ta+b)\|^{2}-\|ta+b\|^{2}.

P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}
being an orthogonal projection implies,

\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}v\|\leq\|v\|
for all
v\in L^{2}
. Hence
F(t)\leq 0
for all
t
.

Since
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
,
M_{\varphi}
is unitary on
L^{2}
. Write
f=x\oplus y
with
x\in uH^{2}
,
y\in H^{2}_{-}
. Then
x\perp y
in
L^{2}
, and unitarity of
M_{\varphi}
gives

\langle a,b\rangle=\langle\varphi x,\varphi y\rangle=0
. Therefore

\|ta+b\|^{2}=t^{2}\|a\|^{2}+\|b\|^{2}=t^{2}\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}.

Since
f
is extremal,
F(1)=0
, that is,

0=\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(a+b)\|^{2}-\|a+b\|^{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(a+b)\|=\|a+b\|.

Now,

\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(ta+b)\|^{2}=\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}a\|^{2}t^{2}+2\,\Re\langle P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}a,P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}b\rangle\,t+\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}b\|^{2},

so

\displaystyle F(t)

\displaystyle=(\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}a\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2})\,t^{2}+2\,\Re\langle P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}a,P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}b\rangle\,t+(\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}b\|^{2}-\|y\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\alpha t^{2}+2\beta t+\gamma.

Proof of (ii) :
We already know
F(t)\leq 0
for all
t
and
F(1)=0
. Thus
t=1
is a (global) maximum of the quadratic
F
, and hence
F^{\prime}(1)=0
. Consequently,

F^{\prime}(t)=2\alpha t+2\beta\quad\Rightarrow\quad 0=F^{\prime}(1)=2(\alpha+\beta)\ \Rightarrow\ \beta=-\alpha.

Using
F(1)=0
,

0=\alpha+2\beta+\gamma=\alpha+2(-\alpha)+\gamma\ \Rightarrow\ \gamma=\alpha.

Therefore,

F(t)=\alpha t^{2}+2(-\alpha)t+\alpha=\alpha(t-1)^{2}\leq 0,

forcing
\alpha\leq 0
. Rewriting
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
in terms of
D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y
gives the final equality.
∎

Example 3.12
.

Let
u(z)=z,\;z\in\mathbb{T}
and let
\varphi(z)=z^{k}
for some integer
k
with
|k|\geq 2
.
Then
|\varphi|=1
a.e on
\mathbb{T}
.
Since
\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}=zH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}
, choose

x=z\in zH^{2},\qquad y=\bar{z}=z^{-1}\in H^{2}_{-},

and set
f:=x\oplus y
, which we identify in
L^{2}
with
x+y=z+z^{-1}
. Then

\|f\|^{2}=\|z\|^{2}+\|z^{-1}\|^{2}=1+1=2.

We have

M_{\varphi}f=z^{k}(z+z^{-1})=z^{k+1}+z^{k-1}.

Thus

D_{\varphi}f=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(M_{\varphi}f).

Case
k\geq 2
.

Here
k+1\geq 3
and
k-1\geq 1
, so

z^{k+1},z^{k-1}\in zH^{2}\subset\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}.

Hence

D_{\varphi}x=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(z^{k+1})=z^{k+1},\qquad D_{\varphi}y=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(z^{k-1})=z^{k-1},

so that

D_{\varphi}f=D_{\varphi}x+D_{\varphi}y=z^{k+1}+z^{k-1}.

Since
z^{k+1}
and
z^{k-1}
are orthogonal in
L^{2}
, we obtain

\|D_{\varphi}f\|^{2}=\|z^{k+1}\|^{2}+\|z^{k-1}\|^{2}=1+1=2=\|f\|^{2},

and

\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle=\langle z^{k+1},z^{k-1}\rangle=0.

Thus
f=x\oplus y
is a norm attaining vector for
D_{\varphi}
, and at the same time the mixed term
\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle
vanishes.

Case
k\leq-2
.

In this case
k+1\leq-1
and
k-1\leq-3
, so
z^{k+1},z^{k-1}\in H^{2}_{-}\subset\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
.
The same computation gives

D_{\varphi}x=z^{k+1},\quad D_{\varphi}y=z^{k-1},\quad\|D_{\varphi}f\|^{2}=2=\|f\|^{2},\qquad\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle=0.

Again
f
is norm attaining and the mixed term vanishes.

In particular, for any integer
k
with
|k|\geq 2
, the vector
f=x\oplus y

is extremal for
D_{\varphi}
and satisfies

\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle=0,

so this furnishes a nontrivial family of examples with vanishing mixed term.

Example 3.13
.

Let
u
be a nonconstant inner function so that

\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}\;=\;uH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}\subset L^{2}.

Define a unimodular symbol

\varphi(e^{it}):=\overline{u(e^{it})},\qquad t\in[0,2\pi).

Now choose

and set

.

with

in

, we have

\|f\|^{2}=\|u^{2}\|^{2}+\|\bar{z}\|^{2}=1+1=2.

Now compute

M_{\varphi}f=\varphi(x+y)=\bar{u}\,(u^{2}+\bar{z})=u+\bar{u}\,\bar{z}.

Since
u\in uH^{2}
and
\bar{u}
is anti-analytic,

\bar{u}\,\bar{z}\in H^{2}_{-}.

Thus

M_{\varphi}f\in uH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}=\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp},

and hence

Since

in
L^{2}
, we have

\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle=\langle u,\bar{u}\,\bar{z}\rangle=0,

so the mixed term vanishes. Moreover,

\|D_{\varphi}f\|^{2}=\|u\|^{2}+\|\bar{u}\,\bar{z}\|^{2}=1+1=2=\|f\|^{2}.

Thus
f=x\oplus y
is a norm-attaining vector for
D_{\varphi}
, and at the same time the mixed term
\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle
vanishes. Therefore, for every nonconstant inner function
u
, this construction produces a unimodular symbol
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
and a nonzero
f\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
such that

\|D_{\varphi}f\|=\|f\|\quad\text{and}\quad\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle=0.

Remark 3.14
.

These examples illustrate the subtlety underlying the mixed term
\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle
. Even though
x\perp y
in
L^{2}
, the images
D_{\varphi}x
and
D_{\varphi}y
interact in a delicate way because multiplication by
\varphi
and the subsequent projection
P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}
can mix analytic and coanalytic components in a nontrivial manner, despite the unimodularity of
\varphi
. For this reason it is important to examine the extremality of
D_{\varphi}
separately on the analytic part
uH^{2}
and the coanalytic part
H^{2}_{-}
. Although each component is well understood individually, their interaction under
D_{\varphi}
can be highly nontrivial. The Lemma
3.11
shows that whenever
x\oplus y
is extremal, these interactions are not arbitrary but must satisfy the rigid balance

\|D_{\varphi}x\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2}=\|D_{\varphi}y\|^{2}-\|y\|^{2}=-\,\Re\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle,

forcing the associated quadratic to collapse to a perfect square. This structural rigidity explains precisely when the mixed term can vanish and how such vanishing fits naturally into the extremality behaviour of
D_{\varphi}
on the analytic and coanalytic components.

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
u
be inner,
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
with
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.
Suppose
0\neq f=x\oplus y\in K_{u}^{\perp}
is an extremal for
D_{\varphi}
, that is,
\|D_{\varphi}f\|=\|f\|
.
Set

\alpha:=\|D_{\varphi}x\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2},\qquad c:=\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle\in\mathbb{C}.

For
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, define the rotated vector
f_{\theta}:=x\oplus e^{i\theta}y
. Then

(i)

f_{\theta}
is extremal for
D_{\varphi}
if and only if

\Re\!\big(e^{-i\theta}c\big)=-\,\alpha.

(ii)

There always exists at least one
\theta
that preserves extremality.

(iii)

One can make
\Re(e^{-i\theta}c)=0

and
keep extremality
if and only if
\alpha=0
(equivalently,
\|D_{\varphi}x\|=\|x\|
and
\|D_{\varphi}y\|=\|y\|
).

Proof.

Proof of (i):

By Lemma
3.11
applied to
f=x\oplus y
, if we write

a:=\varphi x,\quad b:=\varphi y,

then the function

F(t):=\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(ta+b)\|^{2}-\|ta+b\|^{2}

is a real quadratic of the form

F(t)=\alpha t^{2}+2\beta t+\gamma,

where
\quad\alpha=\|D_{\varphi}x\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2}\leq 0,\ \beta=\Re\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle,\,\gamma=\|D_{\varphi}y\|^{2}-\|y\|^{2}\leq 0.

Moreover, extremality of
f
yields
F(t)\leq 0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
F(1)=0
, hence necessarily

\beta=-\alpha,\qquad\gamma=\alpha,\qquad F(t)=\alpha\,(t-1)^{2}\ \ (\leq 0).

Fix
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and set
f_{\theta}:=x\oplus e^{i\theta}y
.
Define

F_{\theta}(t):=\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}\big(\varphi(tx+e^{i\theta}y)\big)\|^{2}-\|tx+e^{i\theta}y\|^{2}=\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(ta+e^{i\theta}b)\|^{2}-\|ta+e^{i\theta}b\|^{2}.

Expanding,

\displaystyle\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(ta+e^{i\theta}b)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}a\|^{2}\,t^{2}+2\,\Re\langle P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}a,\,P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(e^{i\theta}b)\rangle\,t+\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(e^{i\theta}b)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|D_{\varphi}x\|^{2}\,t^{2}+2\,\Re\!\big(e^{-i\theta}\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle\big)\,t+\|D_{\varphi}y\|^{2},

and since
a\perp b
in
L^{2}
,
\|ta+e^{i\theta}b\|^{2}=t^{2}\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}
.
Therefore,

F_{\theta}(t)=\underbrace{\big(\|D_{\varphi}x\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2}\big)}_{=\alpha}t^{2}+2\,\underbrace{\Re\!\big(e^{-i\theta}\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle\big)}_{=\ \Re(e^{-i\theta}c)}\,t+\underbrace{\big(\|D_{\varphi}y\|^{2}-\|y\|^{2}\big)}_{=\ \gamma}.

Hence

(3.9)

F_{\theta}(t)=\alpha t^{2}+2\,\Re(e^{-i\theta}c)\,t+\gamma.

Evaluating at
t=1
gives

(3.10)

F_{\theta}(1)=\alpha+2\,\Re(e^{-i\theta}c)+\gamma.

Using
\gamma=\alpha
, we obtain the key formula

(3.11)

F_{\theta}(1)=2\big(\alpha+\Re(e^{-i\theta}c)\big).

By definition,
f_{\theta}
is extremal for
D_{\varphi}
if and only if

\|D_{\varphi}f_{\theta}\|=\|f_{\theta}\|\quad\text{if and only if}\quad F_{\theta}(1)=0.

Using (
3.11
), this is equivalent to

2\big(\alpha+\Re(e^{-i\theta}c)\big)=0\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\Re(e^{-i\theta}c)=-\alpha,

and this completes the proof.

Proof of (ii):

Write
c=|c|e^{i\psi}
. Then

\Re(e^{-i\theta}c)=|c|\,\cos(\psi-\theta).

Since
f
is extremal, by Lemma
3.11
, we get
-\alpha=\Re(c)=|c|\cos\psi
.
We need to solve

|c|\cos(\psi-\theta)=-\alpha.

Because
|-\alpha|=|\Re(c)|\leq|c|
, there always exists
\theta
with

\cos(\psi-\theta)=-\alpha/|c|
(indeed
\theta=0
already works, then

\Re(e^{i\theta}c)=\Re(c)=-\alpha
). Hence at least one
\theta
preserves extremality.

Proof of (iii):

Suppose there is
\theta
with

\Re(e^{-i\theta}c)=0\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\theta}\text{ is extremal}.

Then by (i),

0=\Re(e^{-i\theta}c)=-\alpha\quad\Rightarrow\quad\alpha=0.

Conversely, if
\alpha=0
then
F_{\theta}(1)=2\,\Re(e^{-i\theta}c)
by (
3.11
),
so choosing
\theta=\arg c+\frac{\pi}{2}
gives
\Re(e^{-i\theta}c)=0
and hence
F_{\theta}(1)=0
, that is,
f_{\theta}
remains extremal.
∎

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
u
be a nonconstant inner function and
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
with
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. Define

M_{+}=\{h\in H^{2}:P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi uh)=0\},\qquad M_{-}=\{g\in H^{2}_{-}:P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi g)=0\}.

If
D_{\varphi}
attains its norm on
\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
, then the following assertions hold:

(i)

Either
M_{+}\neq\{0\}
or
M_{-}\neq\{0\}
;

(ii)

If
h_{0}\in M_{+}
, then for any
h\in H^{2}
, we have

P_{+}(Fh)=T_{F}(h)\in\overline{\operatorname{span}}\Big(uH^{2}\cup\{P_{+}(z^{N}(Fk_{0})_{-})\}_{N\geq 0}\Big),

where
F:=\varphi\,u\,\psi_{+}\in L^{\infty}
and
\psi_{+},\,k_{0}
are the inner and outer factor of
h_{0}
, respectively.

Proof.

Proof of (i) :

Assume that
D_{\varphi}
attains its norm. Then

\|D_{\varphi}f\|=\|f\|
for some
0\neq f=x\oplus y\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
.

By Lemma
3.11
, we have

\|D_{\varphi}x\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2}=\|D_{\varphi}y\|^{2}-\|y\|^{2}=\alpha\leq 0,\qquad\Re\langle D_{\varphi}x,D_{\varphi}y\rangle=-\alpha.

Define the maps

T_{+}:H^{2}\to\mathcal{K}_{u},\text{ by }\quad T_{+}(h):=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi uh);

T_{-}:H^{2}_{-}\to\mathcal{K}_{u},\text{ by }\quad T_{-}(g):=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi g).

Then

M_{+}=N(T_{+})\quad\text{and}\quad M_{-}=N(T_{-}).

Since
T_{\pm}
are bounded, their kernels are closed; hence
M_{+}
and
M_{-}
are closed subspaces.

Claim:
It is impossible that
M_{+}=\{0\}=M_{-}
.

If
M_{+}=\{0\}=M_{-}
, then for every nonzero
x\in uH^{2}
and
y\in H^{2}_{-}
,

P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi x)\neq 0,\qquad P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi y)\neq 0.

Define

\delta_{x}=\frac{\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi x)\|}{\|x\|},\qquad\delta_{y}=\frac{\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi y)\|}{\|y\|}.

Then
\delta_{x},\delta_{y}>0
.
So we have

\|D_{\varphi}x\|^{2}=\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(\varphi x)\|=\|x\|^{2}-\|P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi x)\|^{2}=(1-\delta_{x}^{2})\|x\|^{2},

and similarly

\|D_{\varphi}y\|^{2}=(1-\delta_{y}^{2})\|y\|^{2}.

Set
\rho=\max\{\sqrt{1-\delta_{x}^{2}},\sqrt{1-\delta_{y}^{2}}\}<1
.
For
f=x\oplus y
,

\|D_{\varphi}f\|^{2}\leq\|D_{\varphi}x\|^{2}+\|D_{\varphi}y\|^{2}\leq\rho^{2}(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})\leq\rho^{2}\|f\|^{2}.

Thus
\|D_{\varphi}\|\leq\rho<1
, contradicting norm attainment.
Hence at least one of
M_{+}
,
M_{-}
is nontrivial.

Proof of (ii) :

Let
M_{+}\neq\{0\}
, and
0\neq h_{0}\in M_{+}
and
h_{0}=\psi_{+}k_{0}
be the inner–outer factorization, where
\psi_{+}
is inner and
k_{0}
is outer.
Since
h_{0}\in M_{+}
, using Lemma
3.10
, we have

P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}}(\varphi uh_{0})=0\quad\text{if and only if }\quad P_{+}(\varphi uh_{0})\in uH^{2}.

Define

F:=\varphi u\psi_{+}\in L^{\infty}.

Then

(3.12)

P_{+}(Fk_{0})\in uH^{2}.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the space of all analytic polynomials. Define the bounded operator

T_{F}:H^{2}\to H^{2}

by

T_{F}(h):=P_{+}(Fh).

From (
3.12
), we get

(3.13)

T_{F}(k_{0})\in uH^{2}.

Since
k_{0}
is outer, the set

\mathcal{D}:=\{\,k_{0}p:\ p\in\mathcal{P}\,\}

is dense in
H^{2}
.
For the monomial
p(z)=z^{N}
,
N\geq 0
,

T_{F}(k_{0}z^{N})=P_{+}(z^{N}\,Fk_{0}).

Write the Fourier decomposition
Fk_{0}=(Fk_{0})_{+}\;+\;(Fk_{0})_{-}
with

(Fk_{0})_{+}=P_{+}(Fk_{0})\in H^{2}
and
(Fk_{0})_{-}=(I-P_{+})(Fk_{0})\in H^{2}_{-}
. Then

(3.14)

T_{F}(k_{0}z^{N})=z^{N}(Fk_{0})_{+}+P_{+}(z^{N}(Fk_{0})_{-}).

Because of (
3.13
),

(Fk_{0})_{+}=T_{F}(k_{0})\in uH^{2},

and since
uH^{2}
is
M_{z}
–invariant,

z^{N}(Fk_{0})_{+}\in uH^{2}.

The second term
P_{+}(z^{N}(Fk_{0})_{-})
is analytic, but not necessarily in
uH^{2}
.
Thus each
T_{F}(k_{0}z^{N})
lies in the closed subspace

(3.15)

uH^{2}\cup\operatorname{span}\big\{\,P_{+}(z^{N}(Fk_{0})_{-}):N\geq 0\,\big\}.

By linearity the same holds for all
T_{F}(k_{0}p)
,
p\in\mathcal{P}
.

Since
T_{F}
is bounded and
\mathcal{D}=k_{0}\mathcal{P}
is dense in
H^{2}
, for any

h\in H^{2}
there exist a sequence of polynomials
(p_{m})
such that
k_{0}p_{m}\to h
in
H^{2}
. By the continuity of
T_{F}
, we get

T_{F}(k_{0}p_{m})\to T_{F}(h)=P_{+}(Fh).

Since each
T_{F}(k_{0}p_{m})
lies in the space (
3.15
),
we obtain

(3.16)

P_{+}(Fh)=T_{F}(h)\in\overline{\operatorname{span}}\Big(uH^{2}\cup\{P_{+}(z^{N}(Fk_{0})_{-})\}_{N\geq 0}\Big)\subset H^{2}.

This completes the proof.

∎

3.1.
Norm attainment on the analytic component

We begin with a fundamental lemma, which is crucial for the proof of the main result of this section.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
F\in L^{\infty}
satisfy
|F|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, and let
k_{0}\in H^{2}

be a nonzero outer function. If

Fk_{0}\in H^{2},

then
F
is inner.

Proof.

Let
G=Fk_{0}\in H^{2}
. Then
G
admits the inner-outer factorization

, where

is inner and

is outer. Since

a.e. on

But also

a.e., so

By

and nonzero boundary

Theorem 3.19
.

. Then the following are equivalent:

(ii)

\|D_{\varphi}f_{0}\|=\|f_{0}\|,

(

)
\Rightarrow
(
ii
):

\psi_{+}
and
\chi_{+}

) holds. Then we obtain

.

.

(

)
\Rightarrow
(
i
).

\psi_{+}
is inner and

, by Proposition

,

.

).

Theorem 3.21
.

Remark 3.22
.

By the first part of the proof, the analytic extremal space for
D_{\bar{\varphi}}

is

\mathcal{E}_{\bar{\varphi}}^{(+)}=\{\,u\psi_{-}u_{1}h\oplus 0:h\in H^{2}\,\},

and the corresponding coanalytic extremals for
D_{\varphi}
are precisely their

C_{u}
–images, that is,

\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{(-)}:=C_{u}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\bar{\varphi}}^{(+)}\big)=\{\,C_{u}(u\psi_{-}u_{1}h\oplus 0):h\in H^{2}\,\}\subset H^{2}_{-}.

Now for any nonzero
h\in H^{2}
,

C_{u}(u\psi_{-}u_{1}h)=u\bar{z}\overline{u\psi_{-}u_{1}h}=\bar{\psi}_{-}\bar{u}_{1}V(h)\in H^{2}_{-}.

Therefore, we get the following

\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{(-)}=\{0\oplus\bar{\psi}_{-}\bar{u}_{1}g:g\in H^{2}_{-}\}.

3.3.
Examples

In this subsection, we illustrate Theorem
3.19
with two fully
explicit examples.
The first one is genuinely nontrivial: the unimodular symbol
\varphi
is a
non-analytic quotient of Blaschke products, and the analytic extremal
subspace
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{(+)}
involves nontrivial inner factors.
The second example is the simplest possible case (
u(z)=z
,
\varphi=\bar{z}
),
which nevertheless illustrates the mechanism of the theorem very clearly. Subsequently, we discuss a few additional examples of norm attaining DTTOs.

Example 3.23
.

Choose distinct points
a,b\in\mathbb{D}
and let

B_{a}(z)=\frac{z-a}{1-\overline{a}z},\qquad B_{b}(z)=\frac{z-b}{1-\overline{b}z}

be the corresponding Blaschke factors.
Define the inner functions

u(z)=z,\qquad\psi_{+}(z)=B_{a}(z),\qquad\chi_{+}(z)=z\,B_{b}(z).

Set

\varphi(e^{it}):=\frac{\chi_{+}(e^{it})}{u(e^{it})\psi_{+}(e^{it})}=\frac{z\,B_{b}(z)}{z\,B_{a}(z)}=\frac{B_{b}(z)}{B_{a}(z)},\qquad z=e^{it}.

Since
B_{a}
and
B_{b}
are inner, their quotient is unimodular on
\mathbb{T}
. Thus

\varphi\in L^{\infty}
and
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=1
.

By construction,

\varphi\,u\,\psi_{+}=\frac{B_{b}}{B_{a}}zB_{a}=zB_{b}=\chi_{+}\in H^{\infty}.

Therefore the factorization of
\varphi
in Theorem
3.19
holds, that is

\varphi=\bar{u}\bar{\psi}_{+}\chi_{+},\qquad\text{where $\psi_{+}$, $\chi_{+}$ inner.}

We compute

\gcd(u,\chi_{+})=\gcd(z,\ zB_{b})=z.

Therefore, writing
u=du_{1}
and
\chi_{+}=d\chi_{1}
, we obtain

d(z)=z,\quad u_{1}(z)=1,\quad\chi_{1}(z)=B_{b}(z)\quad\text{with }\gcd(u_{1},\chi_{1})=1.

By Theorem
3.19
,

\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{(+)}=u\,\psi_{+}\,u_{1}\,H^{2}=z\,B_{a}\,H^{2}.

Thus every analytic extremal for
D_{\varphi}
has the form

f=zB_{a}h\oplus 0,\qquad h\in H^{2},\ h\neq 0.

This example exhibits norm attainment for a unimodular symbol

\varphi=\frac{B_{b}}{B_{a}}
, which is in general non-analytic.

Example 3.24
.

Let
u(z)=z\text{ and }\varphi(z)=\bar{z}.

Then
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, and

\mathcal{K}_{u}=H^{2}\ominus zH^{2}=\{\text{constants}\},\qquad\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}=zH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}.

Compute

\varphi\,u=\bar{z}z=1.

Take

\psi_{+}\equiv 1,\qquad\chi_{+}\equiv 1.

Thus

\varphi\,u\,\psi_{+}=1=\chi_{+}\in H^{\infty}.

Since
\gcd(u,\chi_{+})=\gcd(z,1)=1
, we have

d=1,\qquad u_{1}=u=z,\qquad\chi_{1}=\chi_{+}=1.

Hence

\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{(+)}=u\psi_{+}u_{1}H^{2}=z^{2}H^{2}.

Choose
h(z)=1\in H^{2}
. Then

f_{0}:=u\psi_{+}u_{1}h\oplus 0=z^{2}\oplus 0.

Now

D_{\varphi}f_{0}=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(\varphi z^{2}\oplus 0)=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(\bar{z}z^{2}\oplus 0)=P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}}(z\oplus 0)=z\oplus 0.

Hence

\|D_{\varphi}f_{0}\|=1=\|f_{0}\|.

Thus
f_{0}=z^{2}\oplus 0
is a norm attaining analytic extremal for
D_{\varphi}
.

This elementary example illustrates the analytic part of the theorem in the
simplest possible nontrivial case.

Example 3.25
.

Let
u(z)=z
. Then the corresponding model space is
\mathcal{K}_{u}=\mathrm{span}\{1\}
and
\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}=zH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}=\{f\in L^{2}:\widehat{f}(0)=0\}
.
Let
\varphi(e^{it})=e^{i\psi(t)},\,t\in[0,2\pi]
be any unimodular symbol (for example,
\psi
may be taken as continuous and nonconstant).
Since
|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, the multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}
is an isometry on
L^{2}
.
Choose
0\neq h\in L^{2}
with

\langle h,1\rangle=0\quad\text{and}\quad\langle h,\bar{\varphi}\rangle=0,

which is possible because
\operatorname{span}\{1,\bar{\varphi}\}
is at most two-dimensional while
L^{2}
is infinite-dimensional.
Then
h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
and

\langle M_{\varphi}h,1\rangle=\langle h,\bar{\varphi}\rangle=0,

so
M_{\varphi}h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
as well.
Hence

D_{\varphi}h=(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})M_{\varphi}h=M_{\varphi}h,

and therefore,

\|D_{\varphi}h\|=\|M_{\varphi}h\|=\|h\|.

Thus
D_{\varphi}
attains its norm.

Example 3.26
.

Let
u
be any nonconstant inner function and set

\varphi(e^{it})=\frac{1+e^{it}}{2}\,.

Then
\varphi\in H^{\infty}
with
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=1
, but

|\varphi(e^{it})|=\big|\tfrac{1}{2}(1+e^{it})\big|=\big|\cos(t/2)\big|<1\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,2\pi).

Thus
|\varphi|<1
on a subset of
\mathbb{T}
with positive measure.
We claim that
D_{\varphi}
is
not
norm attaining on
\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
.

Proof of the claim:

Fix
0\neq h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}=uH^{2}\oplus H^{2}_{-}
.
Since
|\varphi|<1
on a subset
E\subset\mathbb{T}
of positive measure, we have

\|M_{\varphi}h\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\varphi|^{2}\,|h|^{2}\,dm<\int_{\mathbb{T}}|h|^{2}\,dm=\|h\|^{2}.

Therefore
\|M_{\varphi}h\|<\|h\|
for every nonzero
h\in\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}
.
Since
D_{\varphi}=(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})M_{\varphi}
, we get

\|D_{\varphi}h\|\leq\|M_{\varphi}h\|<\|h\|\qquad(h\neq 0).

Hence there is no nonzero
h
with
\|D_{\varphi}h\|=\|h\|
, that is,
D_{\varphi}
does
not
attain its norm.

4.
A connection between norm attaining TO and DTTO

In this section, we examine how norm attainment for Toeplitz operators influences the norm-attaining property of dual truncated Toeplitz operators.

Theorem 4.1
.

[
30
]

For
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

T_{\varphi}
is norm attaining on
H^{2}
;

(ii)

there exist inner functions
\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2}
with no common inner factor such that

\varphi=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\Theta_{1}\,\overline{\Theta_{2}}\quad\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}.

In this case, there is a nonzero
f\in H^{2}
with
H_{\varphi}f=0
(that is,
0
is an eigenvalue of
H_{\varphi}
) such that
\|T_{\varphi}f\|=\|T_{\varphi}\|\|f\|
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
.
If
T_{\varphi}
is norm attaining, then
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining.

Proof.

Assume that
T_{\varphi}
is norm attaining.
By Theorem
4.1
, we have
\varphi=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\Theta_{1}\,\overline{\Theta_{2}}
for some inner
\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2}
.
Then for any
f\in H^{2}
,

M_{\varphi}(u\Theta_{2}f)=\varphi\,u\Theta_{2}f=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\Theta_{1}uf\in uH^{2}\subset\mathcal{K}_{u}^{\perp}.

This gives us

\displaystyle\|D_{\varphi}(u\Theta_{2}f)\|

\displaystyle=\|(I-P_{\mathcal{K}_{u}})M_{\varphi}(u\Theta_{2}f)\|

\displaystyle=\|M_{\varphi}(u\Theta_{2}f)\|

\displaystyle=\|\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\Theta_{1}uf\|

\displaystyle=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\|u\Theta_{2}f\|.

Choosing
f\neq 0
, we get an extremal vector. Therefore,
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Suppose
\varphi\in H^{\infty}
, and
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining in
uH^{2}
. Then we have
\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}=\begin{bmatrix}T_{\varphi}&H^{*}_{u\bar{\varphi}}\\
0&S_{\varphi}\end{bmatrix}
and there exits a nonzero
h\in H^{2}
such that
\|D_{\varphi}(uh\oplus 0)\|=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\|uh\|.
Using the unitary equivalence between
D_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|U\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}U^{*}(uh\oplus 0)\|

\displaystyle=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\|uh\|

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}(h\oplus 0)\|

\displaystyle=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\|h\|

\displaystyle\|T_{\varphi}h\|

\displaystyle=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\|h\|.

Thus
T_{\varphi}
is norm attaining.

Similarly, if
\bar{\varphi}\in H^{\infty}
and
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining in
H^{2}_{-}
, then
S_{\varphi}
is norm attaining. Consequently,
T_{\bar{\varphi}}
is norm attaining, and hence
T_{\varphi}
attains its norm.

Combining Proposition
4.2
and Remark
4.3
, we get the following corollary.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
. Then

(i)

If
\varphi\in H^{\infty}
, then
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining in
uH^{2}
if and only if
T_{\varphi}
is norm attaining;

(ii)

If
\bar{\varphi}\in H^{\infty}
, then
D_{\varphi}
is norm attaining in
H^{2}_{-}
if and only if
T_{\varphi}
is norm attaining.
