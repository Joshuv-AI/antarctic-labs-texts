A Study of Second-Order Linear Recurrence Sequences via Continuants
Hongshen Chua

Abstract




1
Introduction

Consider the
d
-periodic Fibonacci numbers, defined by the second-order linear recurrence

\displaystyle f_{\nu}=a_{r}f_{\nu-1}+b_{r}f_{\nu-2},\quad\text{ where }\nu\equiv r\pmod{d},

with initial values
f_{0}=0
and
f_{1}=1
. Such a sequence was studied by Carson
[
3
]
, who, using matrix manipulation, managed to rewrite the sequence in the form:

\displaystyle f_{\nu}=af_{\nu-d}+bf_{\nu-2d},

for some constants
a
and
b
. This extends an earlier partial result by Lehmer
[
11
]
. An alternative method via continuants was provided by Panario et al.
[
15
]
. In the same paper, they also derived the generating function and Binet formula for
d
-periodic Fibonacci numbers, supplementing earlier work by Edson et al.
[
6
]
. Our paper will follow this approach closely. Other related studies include the papers by Tan
[
19
]
and Yayenie
[
21
]
, which focus on the special case of
d=2
.

The concept of continuants was initially introduced by Euler during the development of a theory for continued fractions. In particular, continuants appear in the numerators and denominators of the convergents to a continued fraction. Subsequently, Muir
[
14
]
studied continuants as determinants of tridiagonal matrices. A major contribution by Muir is the notation that we employ in this paper. Another perspective on continuants is through a combinatorial approach, where they are viewed as a tiling problem, as discussed by Benjamin et al.
[
2
]
. However, the main inspiration for the content of this paper stems from Perron’s book
[
16
]
on continued fractions.

The goal of this paper is to highlight the similarities between continuants and second-order linear recurrence sequences, with a focus on
d
-periodic Fibonacci numbers. Additionally, we address a problem posed by Panario et al.
[
15
]
, seeking an application for generalized continuants. We hope that this will give continuants the attention they deserve.

While not a prerequisite to understand this paper, we assume here that readers are familiar with Lucas sequences, especially with the Fibonacci numbers, as most of our results are analogous to classical theorems on Fibonacci numbers. This familiarity may help the readers to grasp the motivation behind the results. Some selected articles that may be useful include
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
9
,
17
,
20
]
.

2
Generalized continuants

Suppose that
(a_{\nu})
and
(b_{\nu})
are sequences of positive integers. Consider the generalized continued fraction

\displaystyle\xi_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=b_{\lambda}+\frac{a_{\lambda+1}}{b_{\lambda+1}}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{a_{\lambda+2}}{b_{\lambda+2}}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+ \ldots}=b_{\lambda}+\cfrac{a_{\lambda+1}}{b_{\lambda+1}+\cfrac{a_{\lambda+2}}{b_{\lambda+2}+\ldots}}.

The convergents to the continued fraction are the numbers obtained by truncating the continued fraction. For example, the
\nu
-th convergent of
\xi_{\lambda}
is exactly

\displaystyle\xi_{\nu,\lambda}=b_{\lambda}+\frac{a_{\lambda+1}}{b_{\lambda+1}}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+ \ldots+}\frac{a_{\lambda+\nu}}{b_{\lambda+\nu}}.

Define the
\nu
-th generalized continuants

\displaystyle A_{\nu,\lambda}

\displaystyle=K\binom{a_{\lambda+1},\ldots,a_{\lambda+\nu}}{b_{\lambda},b_{\lambda+1},\ldots,b_{\lambda+\nu}},

\displaystyle B_{\nu,\lambda}

\displaystyle=K\binom{a_{\lambda+2},\ldots,a_{\lambda+\nu}}{b_{\lambda+1},b_{\lambda+2},\ldots,b_{\lambda+\nu}},

as the numerator and denominator, respectively, of the
\nu
-th convergent. In other words,

\displaystyle\frac{A_{\nu,\lambda}}{B_{\nu,\lambda}}=b_{\lambda}+\frac{a_{\lambda+1}}{b_{\lambda+1}}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+ \ldots+}\frac{a_{\lambda+\nu}}{b_{\lambda+\nu}}.

By the choice of sequences
(a_{\nu})
and
(b_{\nu})
, both
A_{\nu,\lambda}
and
B_{\nu,\lambda}
are integers. We can slightly extend
\nu
to negative numbers by setting the initial values

\displaystyle A_{-1,\lambda}=1,\quad A_{0,\lambda}=b_{\lambda},

\displaystyle B_{-1,\lambda}=0,\quad B_{0,\lambda}=1,\quad B_{1,\lambda}=b_{\lambda+1}.

The ordinary continuants correspond to the case when
a_{\nu}=1
is constant. To simplify our notation, we set
A_{\nu,0}=A_{\nu}
and
B_{\nu,0}=B_{\nu}
. Observe that
A_{\nu,\lambda}
and
B_{\nu,\lambda}
are related via the identity
B_{\nu,\lambda}=A_{\nu-1,\lambda+1}
. Furthermore, there are more general identities connecting
A_{\nu,\lambda}
and
B_{\nu,\lambda}
, notably an analogue of Cassini’s identity for Fibonacci numbers.

Theorem 1

(Cassini’s Identity)
.

For non-negative integers
\lambda,\nu
and
\mu
, we have

\displaystyle A_{\nu+\lambda+\mu-1}B_{\nu-1,\lambda}

\displaystyle=A_{\nu+\lambda-1}B_{\nu+\mu-1,\lambda}+(-1)^{\nu-1}a_{\lambda+1}\cdots a_{\lambda+\nu}A_{\lambda-1}B_{\mu-1,\nu+\lambda},

\displaystyle B_{\nu+\lambda+\mu-1}B_{\nu-1,\lambda}

\displaystyle=B_{\nu+\lambda-1}B_{\nu+\mu-1,\lambda}+(-1)^{\nu-1}a_{\lambda+1}\cdots a_{\lambda+\nu}B_{\lambda-1}B_{\mu-1,\nu+\lambda}.

Corollary 2

(Catalan’s Identity)
.

For non-negative integers
\lambda
and
\nu
, we have

\displaystyle A_{\nu+\lambda}

\displaystyle=A_{\lambda}B_{\nu,\lambda}+a_{\lambda+1}A_{\lambda-1}B_{\nu-1,\lambda+1},

\displaystyle B_{\nu+\lambda}

\displaystyle=B_{\lambda}B_{\nu,\lambda}+a_{\lambda+1}B_{\lambda-1}B_{\nu-1,\lambda+1}.

Corollary 3

(d’Ocagne’s Identity)
.

For non-negative integers
\lambda
and
\nu
, we have

\displaystyle A_{\lambda}B_{\nu-1,\lambda-\nu}

\displaystyle=A_{\lambda-1}B_{\nu,\lambda-\nu}+(-1)^{\nu-1}a_{\lambda}\cdots a_{\lambda-\nu+1}A_{\lambda-\nu-1},

\displaystyle B_{\lambda}B_{\nu-1,\lambda-\nu}

\displaystyle=B_{\lambda-1}B_{\nu,\lambda-\nu}+(-1)^{\nu-1}a_{\lambda}\cdots a_{\lambda-\nu+1}B_{\lambda-\nu-1}.

Corollary 4
.

For integer
\nu\geq-1
, we have

\displaystyle A_{\nu+2}

\displaystyle=b_{\nu+2}A_{\nu+1}+a_{\nu+2}A_{\nu},

\displaystyle B_{\nu+2}

\displaystyle=b_{\nu+2}B_{\nu+1}+a_{\nu+2}B_{\nu}.

Corollary 5
.

For non-negative integers
\lambda
and
\nu
, we have

\displaystyle A_{\nu,\lambda}

\displaystyle=b_{\lambda}A_{\nu-1,\lambda+1}+a_{\lambda+1}A_{\nu-2,\lambda+2},

\displaystyle B_{\nu,\lambda}

\displaystyle=b_{\lambda+1}B_{\nu-1,\lambda+1}+a_{\lambda+2}B_{\nu-2,\lambda+2}.

From Corollary
4
, we can express
A_{\nu}
and
B_{\nu}
as a determinant of tridiagonal matrices.

Corollary 6
.

For non-negative integer
\nu
, we have

\displaystyle A_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\begin{vmatrix}b_{0}&-1&0&\ldots&0&0\\
a_{1}&b_{1}&-1&\ldots&0&0\\
0&a_{2}&b_{2}&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&b_{\nu-1}&-1\\
0&0&0&\ldots&a_{\nu}&b_{\nu}\end{vmatrix},\quad B_{\nu}=\begin{vmatrix}b_{1}&-1&0&\ldots&0&0\\
a_{2}&b_{2}&-1&\ldots&0&0\\
0&a_{3}&b_{3}&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&b_{\nu-1}&-1\\
0&0&0&\ldots&a_{\nu}&b_{\nu}\end{vmatrix}.

Alternatively, we can write
A_{\nu}
and
B_{\nu}
as a product of matrices.

Corollary 7
.

For non-negative integer
\nu
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A_{\nu}&A_{\nu-1}\\
B_{\nu}&B_{\nu-1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\prod_{j=1}^{\nu}\begin{pmatrix}b_{j}&1\\
a_{j}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

The proofs of all the results above can be found in Perron’s book
[
16
]
, so we omit them here. It is worth noting that it is easier to prove the corollaries before proving Theorem
1
.
∎

Remark 8
.

The results presented here exhibit similarities to those of Fibonacci numbers. This connection is not surprising since choosing
(a_{\nu})=(b_{\nu})=(1)
leads to
(B_{\nu})=(F_{\nu+1})
, which is just the usual Fibonacci sequence.

3
Periodic continuants

In this section and the remainder of this paper, we consider the case when
(a_{\nu})
and
(b_{\nu})
are both periodic with the same period
d
for
\nu\geq 1
, i.e.,
a_{\nu+d}=a_{\nu}
and
b_{\nu+d}=b_{\nu}
for all
\nu\geq 1
. This type of continued fraction arises frequently in various contexts. For instance, when
N
is a positive integer, the continued fraction expansion of
\sqrt{N}
is periodic. Under this assumption,

\displaystyle K\binom{a_{d+2},\ldots,a_{d+\nu}}{b_{d+1},b_{d+2},\ldots,b_{d+\nu}}=K\binom{a_{2},\ldots,a_{\nu}}{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{\nu}},

so
B_{\nu,d}=B_{\nu}
(in fact,
A_{\nu,\lambda}
and
B_{\nu,\lambda}
are periodic with period
d
in the second argument). Using this fact and Corollary
3
, we can derive an identity that will be useful later on.

Corollary 9
.

For non-negative integers
\lambda
and
\nu
with
d\mid(\lambda-\nu)
, we have

\displaystyle A_{\lambda-1}B_{\nu}-A_{\lambda}B_{\nu-1}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\nu}a_{\lambda}\cdots a_{\lambda-\nu+1}A_{\lambda-\nu-1},

\displaystyle B_{\lambda-1}B_{\nu}-B_{\lambda}B_{\nu-1}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\nu}a_{\lambda}\cdots a_{\lambda-\nu+1}B_{\lambda-\nu-1}.

3.1
An equivalent recurrence relation

When
(a_{\nu})
and
(b_{\nu})
are periodic with period
d
, we can write
(B_{\nu})
as a second-order linear recurrence relation,

\displaystyle B_{\nu}=b_{r}B_{\nu-1}+a_{r}B_{\nu-2},\quad\text{where }\nu\equiv r\pmod{d},

with the appropriate initial values. Motivated by Carson’s result
[
3
]
, we seek a recurrence relation of the form

\displaystyle B_{\nu+2d}=C_{d}B_{\nu+d}+D_{d}B_{\nu},

for some integers
C_{d}
and
D_{d}
. The next theorem provides the desired values.

Theorem 10
.

If
(a_{\nu})
and
(b_{\nu})
are periodic with period
d
, then

\displaystyle B_{\nu+2d}=\frac{B_{2d-1}}{B_{d-1}}B_{\nu+d}+(-1)^{d-1}a_{1}\cdots a_{d}B_{\nu}.

Proof.

Using Corollary
2
with
\lambda=d
and
2d
, we get

\displaystyle B_{\nu+d}

\displaystyle=B_{d}B_{\nu}+a_{1}B_{d-1}B_{\nu-1,1},

\displaystyle B_{\nu+2d}

\displaystyle=B_{2d}B_{\nu}+a_{1}B_{2d-1}B_{\nu-1,1}.

By canceling out
a_{1}B_{\nu-1,1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle B_{\nu+2d}

\displaystyle=B_{2d}B_{\nu}+B_{2d-1}\bigg(\frac{B_{\nu+d}-B_{d}B_{\nu}}{B_{d-1}}\bigg).

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{2d-1}}{B_{d-1}}B_{\nu+d}+\bigg(\frac{B_{d-1}B_{2d}-B_{2d-1}B_{d}}{B_{d-1}}\bigg)B_{\nu}.

Again using Corollary
2
with
\lambda=d
and
\nu=d-1
, we get

\displaystyle B_{2d-1}

\displaystyle=B_{d}B_{d-1}+a_{1}B_{d-1}B_{d-2,1},

so
C_{d}=B_{2d-1}/B_{d-1}=B_{d}+a_{1}B_{d-2,1}
is a non-negative integer. On the other hand, using Corollary
9
with
\lambda=2d
and
\nu=d
, we get

\displaystyle B_{2d}B_{d-1}-B_{2d-1}B_{d}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d-1}a_{1}\cdots a_{d}B_{d-1}.

Thus,
D_{d}=(-1)^{d-1}a_{1}\cdots a_{d}
is an integer.
∎

One can check that the constants are indeed the same as those found by Carson
[
3
]
. From Theorem
10
, it is immediate to deduce the next corollary.

Corollary 11
.

Let
\Delta=C_{d}^{2}+4D_{d}
. For
n\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle B_{(n+1)d-1}=\frac{C_{d}B_{nd-1}+\sqrt{\Delta B_{nd-1}^{2}+4(-D_{d})^{n}B_{d-1}^{2}}}{2}.

Proof.

If we choose
\lambda=\mu=d
and
\nu=nd
with
n\geq 1
in Theorem
1
, then

\displaystyle B_{(n+1)d-1}^{2}-B_{nd-1}B_{(n+2)d-1}=B_{(n+1)d-1}^{2}-C_{d}B_{nd-1}B_{(n+1)d-1}-D_{d}B_{nd-1}^{2}=(-D_{d})^{n}B_{d-1}^{2}.

Solving this quadratic equation for
B_{(n+1)d-1}
gives the desired equation.
∎

Remark 12
.

For the corresponding identities for Lucas sequences, refer to the book by Andrica and Bagdasar
[
1
]
.

3.2
Generating function and Binet’s Formula

A benefit of having a single recurrence relation for
(B_{\nu})
is that we can compute its generating function and Binet formula easily. We follow the proof by Panario et al.
[
15
]
for the next two theorems. Firstly, let

\displaystyle G(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}x^{n},

be the required generating function. Then

\displaystyle(1-C_{d}x^{d}-D_{d}x^{2d})G(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}x^{n}-C_{d}\sum_{n=d}^{\infty}B_{n-d}x^{n}-D_{d}\sum_{n=2d}^{\infty}D_{d}B_{n-2d}x^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{2d-1}B_{n}x^{n}-C_{d}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}B_{n}x^{n+d}.

Therefore, we arrive at our generating function.

Theorem 13
.

The generating function of
B_{n}
is

\displaystyle G(x)=\frac{\sum\limits_{n=0}^{2d-1}B_{n}x^{n}-C_{d}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{d-1}B_{n}x^{n+d}}{1-C_{d}x^{d}-D_{d}x^{2d}}.

Using this generating function, we can then calculate the Binet formula for
B_{\nu}
. Since the continued fraction has period
d
, it is perhaps not surprising that the Binet formula depends on the residue modulo
d
. Let
\Delta=C_{d}^{2}+4D_{d}
be the discriminant of the equation
D_{d}z^{2}+C_{d}z-1=0
. We assume throughout this paper that
\Delta\neq 0
. The next theorem gives the Binet formula for
(B_{\nu})
.

Theorem 14

(Binet’s Formula)
.

Suppose that
\Delta\neq 0
. Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be the positive and negative roots of
D_{d}z^{2}+C_{d}z-1=0
, respectively. For integers
n
and
r
such that
n\geq 0
and
r\geq-1
, we have

\displaystyle B_{nd+r}=(-D_{d})^{n-1}\bigg(\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}B_{d+r}-\frac{\alpha^{n-1}-\beta^{n-1}}{\alpha-\beta}B_{r}\bigg).

Proof.

Consider the generating function of the subsequence
(B_{nd+r})
given by

\displaystyle G_{r}(x)=\frac{B_{r}x^{r}+B_{d+r}x^{d+r}-C_{d}B_{r}x^{d+r}}{1-C_{d}x^{d}-D_{d}x^{2d}}.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be the positive and negative roots of
D_{d}z^{2}+C_{d}z-1=0
, respectively, or equivalently,

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{-C_{d}+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2D_{d}},\quad\beta=\frac{-C_{d}-\sqrt{\Delta}}{2D_{d}}.

Note that,
\alpha+\beta=-C_{d}/D_{d}
and
\alpha\beta=-1/D_{d}
. By partial fraction decomposition, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-C_{d}x^{d}-D_{d}x^{2d}}=\frac{1}{\alpha-\beta}\bigg(\frac{\alpha}{1+D_{d}\alpha x^{d}}-\frac{\beta}{1+D_{d}\beta x^{d}}\bigg).

Substituting this into
G_{r}(x)
, we obtain

\displaystyle G_{r}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{r}x^{r}+B_{d+r}x^{d+r}-C_{d}B_{r}x^{d+r}}{\alpha-\beta}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}D_{d}^{n}(\alpha^{n+1}-\beta^{n+1})x^{nd}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{D_{d}^{n}(\alpha^{n+1}-\beta^{n+1})B_{r}}{\alpha-\beta}x^{nd+r}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{D_{d}^{n}(\alpha^{n+1}-\beta^{n+1})(B_{d+r}-C_{d}B_{r})}{\alpha-\beta}x^{(n+1)d+r}.

By replacing
n
with
n-1
in the second sum, it becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{D_{d}^{n-1}(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})(B_{d+r}-C_{d}B_{r})}{\alpha-\beta}x^{nd+r}.

This combined with the first sum gives the expression

\displaystyle G_{r}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\bigg(\frac{D_{d}^{n}(\alpha^{n+1}-\beta^{n+1})B_{r}-D_{d}^{n-1}(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})(B_{d+r}-C_{d}B_{r})}{\alpha-\beta}\bigg)x^{nd+r}.

The terms containing
B_{r}
simplifies to

\displaystyle D_{d}^{n}(\alpha^{n+1}-\beta^{n+1})+C_{d}D_{d}^{n-1}(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})=D_{d}^{n-1}(\alpha^{n-1}-\beta^{n-1}).

Therefore, we arrive at

\displaystyle G_{r}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-D_{d})^{n-1}\bigg(\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}B_{d+r}-\frac{\alpha^{n-1}-\beta^{n-1}}{\alpha-\beta}B_{r}\bigg)x^{nd+r}.

The theorem holds by comparing the coefficient of
x^{nd+r}
.
∎

In particular, when
r=-1
, one observes that
B_{nd-1}/B_{d-1}
and
B_{2nd-1}/B_{nd-1}
behave like the Lucas sequence of the first kind and second kind, respectively. Hence, new identities can be discovered by utilizing known identities involving Fibonacci and Lucas numbers. For example, the identity
L_{n}^{2}-5F_{n}^{2}=4(-1)^{n}
yields the following identity, which can be verified directly:

\displaystyle\frac{B_{2nd-1}^{2}}{B_{nd-1}^{2}}-\frac{\Delta B_{nd-1}^{2}}{B_{d-1}^{2}}-

\displaystyle=4(-D_{d})^{n}.

Remark 15
.

We also have another related identity

\displaystyle\frac{B_{2nd-1}^{2}}{B_{nd-1}^{2}}+\frac{\Delta B_{nd-1}^{2}}{B_{d-1}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2B_{4nd-1}^{2}}{B_{2nd-1}^{2}}.

3.2.1
Example: bi-periodic Fibonacci numbers

When
d=2
, the recurrence relation for
(B_{\nu})
can be written as

\displaystyle B_{\nu}=\begin{cases}b_{1}B_{\nu-1}+a_{1}B_{\nu-2},\quad\text{ if }\nu\text{ is odd};\\
b_{2}B_{\nu-1}+a_{2}B_{\nu-2},\quad\text{ if }\nu\text{ is even},\end{cases}

with initial values
B_{-1}=0
and
B_{0}=1
. The first few terms of
B_{\nu}
are

\displaystyle B_{1}=b_{1},\quad B_{2}=b_{1}b_{2}+a_{2},\quad B_{3}=b_{1}^{2}b_{2}+a_{1}b_{1}+a_{2}b_{1},

\displaystyle B_{4}=b_{1}^{2}b_{2}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+2a_{2}b_{1}b_{2}+a_{1}b_{1}b_{2}.

We calculate

\displaystyle C_{2}=\frac{B_{3}}{B_{1}}

\displaystyle=b_{1}b_{2}+a_{1}+a_{2},\quad D_{2}=-a_{1}a_{2}.

Hence,
(B_{\nu})
can be defined using a single recurrence relation

\displaystyle B_{\nu}=(b_{1}b_{2}+a_{1}+a_{2})B_{\nu-2}-a_{1}a_{2}B_{\nu-4},

with initial values as stated above. Its generating function is

\displaystyle G(x)=\frac{1+b_{1}x-a_{2}x^{2}}{1-(b_{1}b_{2}+a_{1}+a_{2})x^{2}+a_{1}a_{2}x^{4}},

and its Binet formula is

\displaystyle B_{2n+r}=(a_{1}a_{2})^{n-1}\bigg(\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}B_{r+2}-\frac{\alpha^{n-1}-\beta^{n-1}}{\alpha-\beta}B_{r}\bigg).

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are the positive and negative roots of
a_{1}a_{2}z^{2}-(b_{1}b_{2}+a_{1}+a_{2})z+1=0
, respectively.

Consider the convergents to the continued fraction of
\sqrt{8}=[2,\overline{1,4}]
. The denominators of these convergents are given by the sequence
1,1,5,6,29,35,169,204,985,\ldots
(see
A041011
on OEIS). This sequence is generated by the following second-order linear recurrence relation:

\displaystyle B_{\nu}=6B_{\nu-2}-B_{\nu-4}.

Its generating function is

\displaystyle G(x)=\frac{1+x-x^{2}}{1-6x^{2}+x^{4}}.

The roots of
1-6x+x^{2}=0
are
3\pm 2\sqrt{2}
. Thus, the Binet formula is given by

\displaystyle B_{2n+r}=\frac{(3+2\sqrt{2})^{n}-(3-2\sqrt{2})^{n}}{4\sqrt{2}}B_{r+2}-\frac{(3+2\sqrt{2})^{n-1}-(3-2\sqrt{2})^{n-1}}{4\sqrt{2}}B_{r}.

We will return to this example a few more times throughout this paper.

3.3
Limits

We use Theorem
14
to compute the limit of consecutive term, which we need for later. Observe that,
|\alpha|<|\beta|
. Hence, we deduce the next two theorems.

Theorem 16
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be as defined in Theorem
14
. For integer
r\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{B_{nd+r}}{B_{nd+r-1}}=\frac{\beta B_{d+r}-B_{r}}{\beta B_{d+r-1}-B_{r-1}}.

Proof.

Take
n\longrightarrow\infty
in the expression below

\displaystyle\frac{B_{nd+r}}{B_{nd+r-1}}=\frac{(\alpha(\alpha/\beta)^{n-1}-\beta)B_{d+r}-((\alpha/\beta)^{n-1}-1)B_{r}}{(\alpha(\alpha/\beta)^{n-1}-\beta)B_{d+r-1}-((\alpha/\beta)^{n-1}-1)B_{r-1}},

and use the fact that
(\alpha/\beta)^{n}\longrightarrow 0
.
∎

Theorem 17
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be as defined in Theorem
14
. For integer
r\geq-1
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{B_{(n+1)d+r}}{B_{nd+r}}=-D_{d}\beta.

Proof.

Take
n\longrightarrow\infty
in the expression below

\displaystyle\frac{B_{(n+1)d+r}}{B_{nd+r}}=\frac{-D_{d}(\alpha^{2}(\alpha/\beta)^{n-1}-\beta^{2})B_{d+r}-(\alpha(\alpha/\beta)^{n-1}-\beta)B_{r}}{(\alpha(\alpha/\beta)^{n-1}-\beta)B_{d+r}-((\alpha/\beta)^{n-1}-1)B_{r}},

and use the fact that
(\alpha/\beta)^{n}\longrightarrow 0
.
∎

3.4
Extending to negative indices

Replacing
n
with
-n
in Binet’s formula (Theorem
14
) yields the next theorem.

Theorem 18
.

For integers
n
and
r
such that
n\geq 0
and
r\geq-1
, we have

\displaystyle B_{-nd+r}=-(-D_{d})^{n-1}\bigg(\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{(-D_{d})^{n}(\alpha-\beta)}B_{d+r}-\frac{\alpha^{n+1}-\beta^{n+1}}{(-D_{d})^{n-1}(\alpha-\beta)}B_{r}\bigg).

In particular, when
r=-1
,
(-D_{d})^{n}B_{-nd-1}=-B_{nd-1}
.

3.5
Some curious telescoping sums

Many interesting sums involving Fibonacci numbers arise from telescoping sums. We demonstrate here some of the analogous sums based on continuants.

3.5.1
Telescoping sum 1

When
d=2
, we have encountered this sequence before. It has the form

\displaystyle B_{\nu}=\begin{cases}b_{1}B_{\nu-1}+a_{1}B_{\nu-2},\quad\text{ if }\nu\text{ is odd};\\
b_{2}B_{\nu-1}+a_{2}B_{\nu-2},\quad\text{ if }\nu\text{ is even},\end{cases}

with initial values
B_{-1}=0
and
B_{0}=1
. The next theorem is a continuant version of the Millin series
[
13
]
for Fibonacci numbers.

Theorem 19
.

If
d=2
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{n-1}}}{B_{2^{n+1}-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b_{1}\beta}.

Proof.

By choosing
\lambda=2^{n+1}
and
\nu=2^{n}
with
n\geq 1
in Corollary
9
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{B_{2^{n}}}{B_{2^{n}-1}}-\frac{B_{2^{n+1}}}{B_{2^{n+1}-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{n-1}}}{B_{2^{n+1}-1}}.

The LHS is telescoping, hence summing from
1
to
N
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{B_{2}}{B_{1}}-\frac{B_{2^{N+1}}}{B_{2^{N+1}-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{n-1}}}{B_{2^{n+1}-1}}.

When
N\longrightarrow\infty
, the second term on the LHS approaches a finite limit

\displaystyle\frac{\beta B_{2}-1}{\beta B_{1}},

by Theorem
16
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{n-1}}}{B_{2^{n+1}-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{2}}{B_{1}}-\frac{\beta B_{2}-1}{\beta B_{1}}=\frac{1}{b_{1}\beta}.

∎

In the case of
\sqrt{8}
, this sum becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{B_{2^{n+1}-1}}=\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{204}+\frac{1}{235416}+\ldots=\frac{1}{3+2\sqrt{2}}=3-2\sqrt{2}.

3.5.2
Telescoping sum 2

For general
d
,
(B_{\nu})
has the form

\displaystyle B_{\nu}=b_{r}B_{\nu-1}+a_{r}B_{\nu-2},\quad\text{where }\nu\equiv r\pmod{d},

for the appropriate initial values.

Theorem 20
.

If the period is
d
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-D_{d})^{n-1}}{B_{nd-1}B_{(n+1)d-1}}=\frac{\alpha}{B_{d-1}^{2}}.

Proof.

By choosing
\lambda=\mu=d
and
\nu=nd
with
n\geq 1
, Theorem
1
becomes

\displaystyle\frac{B_{(n+1)d-1}}{B_{nd-1}}-\frac{B_{(n+2)d-1}}{B_{(n+1)d-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{nd}(a_{1}\cdots a_{d})^{n}B_{d-1}^{2}}{B_{nd-1}B_{(n+1)d-1}}.

The LHS is telescoping, so summing from
1
to
N
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{B_{2d-1}}{B_{d-1}}-\frac{B_{(N+2)d-1}}{B_{(N+1)d-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{nd}(a_{1}\cdots a_{d})^{n}B_{d-1}^{2}}{B_{nd-1}B_{(n+1)d-1}}.

When
N\longrightarrow\infty
, the second term on the LHS approaches
\beta
by Theorem
17
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{nd}(a_{1}\cdots a_{d})^{n}B_{d-1}^{2}}{B_{nd-1}B_{(n+1)d-1}}=C_{d}+D_{d}\beta=-D_{d}\alpha.

This proves the theorem after simplifications.
∎

It is a known fact that, for a positive integer
N
, the continued fraction of
\sqrt{N}
is periodic (refer to Khrushchev’s book
[
10
]
for a proof), say with period
d
. When
(B_{\nu})
represents the denominators of the convergents to
\sqrt{N}
, we have
C_{d}=B_{2d-1}/B_{d-1}=2A_{d-1}
and
D_{d}=(-1)^{d-1}
. This implies that
\alpha=(-1)^{d}A_{d-1}-(-1)^{d}\sqrt{A_{d-1}^{2}-(-1)^{d}}
. Furthermore, there is an interesting interpretation of the number
B_{nd-1}
as a solution to the Pell’s equation
x^{2}-Ny^{2}=1
, as stated in the next two theorems.

Theorem 21
.

The fundamental (minimal) solution to
x^{2}-Ny^{2}=1
is

\displaystyle(x_{1},y_{1})=\begin{cases}(A_{d-1},B_{d-1}),&\text{ if }d\text{ is even};\\
(A_{2d-1},B_{2d-1}),&\text{ if }d\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

Theorem 22
.

All the solutions to
x^{2}-Ny^{2}=1
are given by

\displaystyle(x_{n},y_{n})=\begin{cases}(A_{nd-1},B_{nd-1}),&\text{ if }d\text{ is even};\\
(A_{2nd-1},B_{2nd-1}),&\text{ if }d\text{ is odd},\end{cases}

where
n
is a positive integer.

Proof.

See Khrushchev’s book
[
10
]
for proofs.
∎

Hence, our telescoping sum can be reinterpreted as the next corollary.

Corollary 23
.

If
d
is even and
(x_{n},y_{n})
is the
n
-th solution to
x^{2}-Ny^{2}=1
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y_{n}y_{n+1}}=\frac{x_{1}-\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}-1}}{y_{1}^{2}}.

In the case when
N=8
, the sum becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y_{n}y_{n+1}}=\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{210}+\frac{1}{7140}+\ldots=3-\sqrt{8}.

3.5.3
Telescoping sum 3

We assume for this part that
(B_{\nu})
is the sequence of denominators of the convergents to
\sqrt{N}
. In this case,
a_{n}=1
is constant and
D_{d}=(-1)^{d-1}
. The next corollary is similar to the previous one.

Corollary 24
.

If
d
is even and
(x_{n},y_{n})
is the
n
-th solution to
x^{2}-Ny^{2}=1
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x_{n}x_{n+1}}=\frac{x_{1}-\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}-1}}{x_{1}\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}-1}}.

Proof.

If we choose
\lambda=\mu=d
and
\nu=(n-1)d
with
n\geq 1
, then Theorem
1
becomes

\displaystyle\frac{B_{nd-1}}{A_{(n+1)d-1}}-\frac{B_{(n-1)d-1}}{A_{nd-1}}=\frac{(-1)^{(n-1)d}(a_{1}\cdots a_{d})^{n-1}A_{d-1}B_{d-1}}{A_{nd-1}A_{(n+1)d-1}}.

The LHS is telescoping, so summing from
1
to
N
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{B_{Nd-1}}{A_{(N+1)d-1}}-\frac{B_{-1}}{A_{d-1}}=\frac{B_{Nd-1}}{A_{(N+1)d-1}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{(n-1)d}(a_{1}\cdots a_{d})^{n-1}A_{d-1}B_{d-1}}{A_{nd-1}A_{(n+1)d-1}}.

When
N\longrightarrow\infty
, using the same procedure as in Theorem
17
, one checks that

\displaystyle\frac{B_{Nd-1}}{A_{(N+1)d-1}}\rightarrow-\frac{2B_{d-1}}{D_{d}^{2}\beta(\alpha-\beta)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{(n-1)d}(a_{1}\cdots a_{d})^{n-1}A_{d-1}B_{d-1}}{A_{nd-1}A_{(n+1)d-1}}=-\frac{2B_{d-1}}{D_{d}^{2}\beta(\alpha-\beta)},

which can be simplified to

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-D_{d})^{n}}{A_{nd-1}A_{(n+1)d-1}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\alpha}{A_{d-1}(\alpha-\beta)}.

We get our conclusion by noting that
D_{d}=-1
and
\alpha-\beta=-2\sqrt{A_{d-1}^{2}-1}
.
∎

In the case when
N=8
, we have the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x_{n}x_{n+1}}=\frac{1}{51}+\frac{1}{1683}+\frac{1}{57123}+\ldots=\frac{3\sqrt{2}-4}{12}.

3.5.4
Telescoping sum 4

Let
(x_{n})
and
(y_{n})
be any sequences . Consider the identity, which can be verified directly,

\displaystyle\frac{4(x_{n+1}y_{n}-x_{n}y_{n+1})(x_{n}x_{n+1}-y_{n}y_{n+1})}{(x_{n}-y_{n})^{2}(x_{n+1}-y_{n+1})^{2}}=\frac{(x_{n}+y_{n})^{2}}{(x_{n}-y_{n})^{2}}-\frac{(x_{n+1}+y_{n+1})^{2}}{(x_{n+1}-y_{n+1})^{2}}.

We use this identity to prove the next theorem.

Theorem 25
.

If the period is
d
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-D_{d})^{n-1}B_{(2n+1)d-1}}{B_{nd-1}^{2}B_{(n+1)d-1}^{2}}=\frac{1}{B_{d-1}^{3}}.

Proof.

Choose
x_{n}=\alpha^{n}
and
y_{n}=\beta^{n}
in the identity above to get

\displaystyle\frac{4(\alpha-\beta)(\alpha^{2n+1}-\beta^{2n+1})}{(-D_{d})^{n}(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})^{2}(\alpha^{n+1}-\beta^{n+1})^{2}}=\frac{(\alpha^{n}+\beta^{n})^{2}}{(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})^{2}}-\frac{(\alpha^{n+1}+\beta^{n+1})^{2}}{(\alpha^{n+1}-\beta^{n+1})^{2}}.

The RHS is telescoping, so summing from
n=1
to
N
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{4(\alpha-\beta)(\alpha^{2n+1}-\beta^{2n+1})}{(-D_{d})^{n}(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})^{2}(\alpha^{n+1}-\beta^{n+1})^{2}}=\frac{(\alpha+\beta)^{2}}{(\alpha-\beta)^{2}}-\frac{(\alpha^{N+1}+\beta^{N+1})^{2}}{(\alpha^{N+1}-\beta^{N+1})^{2}}.

Taking
N\longrightarrow\infty
, the second term on RHS approaches
1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{4(\alpha-\beta)(\alpha^{2n+1}-\beta^{2n+1})}{(-D_{d})^{n}(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})^{2}(\alpha^{n+1}-\beta^{n+1})^{2}}=\frac{(\alpha+\beta)^{2}}{(\alpha-\beta)^{2}}-1.

Rewriting this equation in terms of
B_{\nu}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{4(-D_{d})^{n-2}B_{(2n+1)d-1}B_{d-1}^{3}}{B_{nd-1}^{2}B_{(n+1)d-1}^{2}}=\frac{C_{d}^{2}}{D_{d}^{2}}-\frac{\Delta}{D_{d}^{2}}.

We conclude our theorem upon simplification of this last equation.
∎

Suppose that
(B_{\nu})
are the denominators of the convergents to
\sqrt{N}
for some positive integer
N
. The next corollary is immediate.

Corollary 26
.

If
d
is even and
(x_{n},y_{n})
are the
n
-th solution to
x^{2}-Ny^{2}=1
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{y_{2n+1}}{y_{n}^{2}y_{n+1}^{2}}=\frac{1}{y_{1}^{3}}.

When
N=8
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{y_{2n+1}}{y_{n}^{2}y_{n+1}^{2}}=\frac{35}{1^{2}\cdot 6^{2}}+\frac{1189}{6^{2}\cdot 35^{2}}+\frac{40391}{35^{2}\cdot 204^{2}}+\ldots=1.

3.5.5
Telescoping sum 5

Suppose that
D_{d}=1
, so

\displaystyle B_{(n+1)d-1}-B_{(n-1)d-1}=C_{d}B_{nd-1}.

Motivated by the identity
F_{n}^{2}-F_{n+1}F_{n-1}=(-1)^{n-1}
for Fibonacci numbers, we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{B_{nd-1}^{2}}{B_{d-1}^{2}}-\frac{B_{(n+1)d-1}}{B_{d-1}}\cdot\frac{B_{(n-1)d-1}}{B_{d-1}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-1},

where
n\geq 1
. The application of this identity yields the next result.

Theorem 27
.

If
D_{d}=1
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tan^{-1}\frac{B_{2d-1}}{B_{(2n+1)d-1}}=\tan^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}}{B_{2d-1}}.

Proof.

Using the addition formula for
\tan^{-1}
(see §
3.6.1
),

\displaystyle\tan^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}}{B_{2nd-1}}-\tan^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}}{B_{2(n+1)d-1}}

\displaystyle=\tan^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}(B_{2(n+1)d-1}-B_{2nd-1})}{B_{d-1}^{2}+B_{2(n+1)d-1}B_{2nd-1}}

\displaystyle=\tan^{-1}\frac{B_{2d-1}}{B_{(2n+1)d-1}}.

The LHS is telescoping, so summing from
1
to
N
, we obtain

\displaystyle\tan^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}}{B_{2d-1}}-\tan^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}}{B_{2(N+1)d-1}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tan^{-1}\frac{B_{2d-1}}{B_{(2n+1)d-1}}.

Taking
N\longrightarrow\infty
, the second term on the LHS vanishes, so

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tan^{-1}\frac{B_{2d-1}}{B_{(2n+1)d-1}}=\tan^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}}{B_{2d-1}}.

∎

Remark 28
.

This theorem generalizes an earlier theorem by Hoggatt and Bruggles
[
9
, Theorem 4]
.

Using a similar construction, we can get a corresponding sum for
\tanh^{-1}
.

Theorem 29
.

If
D_{d}=1
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\tanh^{-1}\frac{B_{2d-1}}{B_{2nd-1}}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{B_{3d-1}+B_{d-1}}{B_{3d-1}-B_{d-1}}.

Proof.

Using the addition formula for
\tanh^{-1}
(see §
3.6.2
),

\displaystyle\tanh^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}}{B_{(2n-1)d-1}}-\tanh^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}}{B_{(2n+1)d-1}}

\displaystyle=\tanh^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}(B_{(2n+1)d-1}-B_{(2n-1)d-1})}{B_{(2n+1)d-1}B_{(2n-1)d-1}-B_{d-1}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\tanh^{-1}\frac{B_{2d-1}}{B_{2nd-1}}.

The LHS is telescoping, so summing from
2
to
N
(
\tanh^{-1}1
is undefined), we obtain

\displaystyle\tanh^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}}{B_{3d-1}}-\tanh^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}}{B_{(2N+1)d-1}}=\sum_{n=2}^{N}\tanh^{-1}\frac{B_{2d-1}}{B_{2nd-1}}.

Taking
N\longrightarrow\infty
, the second term on the LHS vanishes, so

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\tanh^{-1}\frac{B_{2d-1}}{B_{2nd-1}}=\tanh^{-1}\frac{B_{d-1}}{B_{3d-1}}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{B_{3d-1}+B_{d-1}}{B_{3d-1}-B_{d-1}},

where we used the fact that

\displaystyle\tanh^{-1}x=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+x}{1-x}.

∎

Let us apply these theorems to the continued fraction of
\sqrt{2}=[1,\overline{2}]
. The denominators,
(B_{\nu})
, of the convergents to
\sqrt{2}
are
1,2,5,12,29,70,169,408,985,\ldots
. Each
B_{\nu}
corresponds to the
(\nu+1)
-th term of the Pell number (see
A000129
on OEIS). The theorems above imply that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tan^{-1}\frac{2}{B_{2n}}=\tan^{-1}\frac{2}{5}+\tan^{-1}\frac{2}{29}+\tan^{-1}\frac{2}{169}+\ldots=\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{2},

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\tanh^{-1}\frac{2}{B_{2n-1}}=\tanh^{-1}\frac{1}{6}+\tanh^{-1}\frac{1}{35}+\tanh^{-1}\frac{1}{204}+\ldots=\tanh^{-1}\frac{1}{5}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{3}{2}.

3.6
More series involving
\tan^{-1}
and
\tanh^{-1}

Besides the telescoping sums in the last section, another set of series can be generated using
\tan^{-1}
and
\tanh^{-1}
. The results in this section are inspired by the papers of Castellanos
[
4
,
5
]
. However, Castellanos’ original method using Chebyshev polynomials is cumbersome. Therefore, we provide a direct method that is applicable to his results, while generating new ones.

3.6.1
Series involving
\tan^{-1}

Let
\zeta
be a number to be determined later. Using the addition formula for
\tan^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\tan^{-1}(x-y)=\frac{\tan^{-1}x-\tan^{-1}y}{1+\tan^{-1}x\tan^{-1}y},

and the Gregory series,

\displaystyle\tan^{-1}z=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}z^{2k+1}}{2k+1},

we get

\displaystyle\tan^{-1}\frac{(\alpha-\beta)\zeta}{\alpha\beta+\zeta^{2}}=\tan^{-1}\frac{\zeta}{\beta}-\tan^{-1}\frac{\zeta}{\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}(\alpha^{2k+1}-\beta^{2k+1})}{(2k+1)(\alpha\beta)^{2k+1}}\zeta^{2k+1}.

Since the series expansion for
\tan^{-1}z
is only valid for
|z|<1
, we require that
|\zeta|<\min(|\alpha|,|\beta|)=|\alpha|
. The next two theorems are direct consequences of the last equation.

Theorem 30
.

Let
\zeta
be a root of
D_{d}z^{2}-\sqrt{3\Delta}z-1=0
that satisfies
|\zeta|<\alpha
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\pi B_{d-1}}{6\sqrt{\Delta}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}B_{(2k+1)d-1}}{2k+1}\zeta^{2k+1}.

Proof.

We choose
\zeta
such that

\displaystyle\frac{(\alpha-\beta)\zeta}{\alpha\beta+\zeta^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}.

We obtain our theorem upon simplification and using the fact
\tan^{-1}(1/\sqrt{3})=\pi/6
.
∎

Theorem 31
.

Let
\zeta
be a root of
D_{d}z^{2}-(\sqrt{2}-1)\sqrt{\Delta}z-1=0
that satisfies
|\zeta|<\alpha
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\pi B_{d-1}}{8\sqrt{\Delta}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}B_{(2k+1)d-1}}{2k+1}\zeta^{2k+1}.

Proof.

We choose
\zeta
such that

\displaystyle\frac{(\alpha-\beta)\zeta}{\alpha\beta+\zeta^{2}}=\sqrt{2}-1.

We obtain our theorem upon simplification and using the fact
\tan^{-1}(\sqrt{2}-1)=\pi/8
.
∎

3.6.2
Series involving
\tanh^{-1}

We also have similar addition formula for
\tanh^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\tanh^{-1}(x-y)=\frac{\tanh^{-1}x-\tanh^{-1}y}{1-\tanh^{-1}x\tanh^{-1}y},

and Taylor series expansion,

\displaystyle\tanh^{-1}z=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2k+1}}{2k+1}.

So one expects similar series using
\tanh^{-1}
. Again, take
\zeta
as a number to be determined later, then

\displaystyle\tanh^{-1}\frac{(\alpha-\beta)\zeta}{\alpha\beta-\zeta^{2}}=\tanh^{-1}\frac{\zeta}{\beta}-\tanh^{-1}\frac{\zeta}{\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{2k+1}-\beta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)(\alpha\beta)^{2k+1}}\zeta^{2k+1}.

The series expansion for
\tanh^{-1}z
is also valid for
|z|<1
, so we have the same inequality condition
|\zeta|<|\alpha|
. The previous equation gives the next two theorems.

Theorem 32
.

Let
\zeta
be a root of
D_{d}z^{2}+2\sqrt{\Delta}z+1=0
that satisfies
|\zeta|<\alpha
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{B_{d-1}}{2\sqrt{\Delta}}\ln 3=-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{(2k+1)d-1}}{2k+1}\zeta^{2k+1}.

Proof.

We choose
\zeta
such that

\displaystyle\frac{(\alpha-\beta)\zeta}{\alpha\beta-\zeta^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}.

We obtain our theorem upon simplification and using the fact
2\tanh^{-1}(1/2)=\ln 3
.
∎

Theorem 33
.

Let
\zeta
be a root of
D_{d}z^{2}+3\sqrt{\Delta}z+1=0
that satisfies
|\zeta|<\alpha
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{B_{d-1}}{2\sqrt{\Delta}}\ln 2=-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{(2k+1)d-1}}{2k+1}\zeta^{2k+1}.

Proof.

We choose
\zeta
such that

\displaystyle\frac{(\alpha-\beta)\zeta}{\alpha\beta-\zeta^{2}}=\frac{1}{3}.

We obtain our theorem upon simplification and using the fact
2\tanh^{-1}(1/3)=\ln 2
.
∎

3.6.3
Example (continued)

For
\sqrt{8}
, the first two theorems give

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{24\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}B_{4k+1}}{2k+1}\bigg(2\sqrt{6}-5\bigg)^{2k+1},

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{32\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}B_{4k+1}}{2k+1}\bigg(\sqrt{23+16\sqrt{2}}-2\sqrt{2}-4\bigg)^{2k+1}.

On the other hand, the latter two theorems give

\displaystyle\frac{\ln 3}{8\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{4k+1}}{2k+1}\bigg(\sqrt{33}-4\sqrt{2}\bigg)^{2k+1},

\displaystyle\frac{\ln 2}{8\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{4k+1}}{2k+1}\bigg(\sqrt{73}-6\sqrt{2}\bigg)^{2k+1}.

3.7
Sums involving continuants

The Binet formula enables us to calculate an intriguing sum of involving
B_{\nu}
, as stated in the next theorem.

Theorem 34
.

Let
x\neq\alpha,\beta
. For integer
r\geq-1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}x^{n}B_{nd+r}=\frac{x^{N+1}(B_{(N+1)d+r}/D_{d}+xB_{Nd+r})-x(B_{d+r}/D_{d}+xB_{r})}{(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)}.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}(xD_{d})^{n}\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{x(xD_{d})^{N+1}(\alpha^{N}-\beta^{N})+(xD_{d})^{N+1}(\alpha^{N+1}-\beta^{N+1})-xD_{d}(\alpha-\beta)}{(\alpha-\beta)(x^{2}D_{d}-xC_{d}-1)},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(xD_{d})^{n}\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{x(xD_{d})^{N+1}(\alpha^{N-1}-\beta^{N-1})+(xD_{d})^{N}(\alpha^{N}-\beta^{N})-xD_{d}(\alpha-\beta)}{(\alpha-\beta)(x^{2}D_{d}-xC_{d}-1)}.

Hence, by applying Binet’s formula (Theorem
14
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}x^{n}B_{nd+r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigg((-D_{d})^{-1}(-xD_{d})^{n}\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}B_{d+r}-x(-xD_{d})^{n-1}\frac{\alpha^{n-1}-\beta^{n-1}}{\alpha-\beta}B_{r}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{-x^{N+1}B_{(N+1)d+r}-x^{N+2}D_{d}B_{Nd+r}+xB_{d+r}+x^{2}D_{d}B_{r}}{1+xC_{d}-x^{2}D_{d}}

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{N+1}(B_{(N+1)d+r}/D_{d}+xB_{Nd+r})-x(B_{d+r}/D_{d}+xB_{r})}{(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)}.

∎

Remark 35
.

One could also apply telescoping sum directly to the identity

\displaystyle(x-\alpha)(x-\alpha)B_{kd+r}=x^{k+1}(B_{(k+1)d+r}/D_{d}+xB_{kd+r})-x^{k}(B_{kd+r}/D_{d}+xB_{(k-1)d+r}),

although our derivation gives an explanation for this identity.

For the next series, we take
r=-1
.

Theorem 36
.

It holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\binom{N}{n}(-1)^{n}(\alpha+\beta)^{n}B_{nd-1}=\frac{B_{2Nd-1}}{D_{d}^{N}}.

Proof.

Note that
1-C_{d}z=Dz^{2}
for
z=\alpha
and
\beta
. Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\binom{N}{n}(-C_{d})^{n}\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}=\frac{(1-C_{d}\alpha)^{N}-(1-C_{d}\beta)^{N}}{\alpha-\beta}=D_{d}^{N}\frac{\alpha^{2N}-\beta^{2N}}{\alpha-\beta}.

The sum in question is then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\binom{N}{n}(-1)^{n}(\alpha+\beta)^{n}B_{nd-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\binom{N}{n}\bigg(\frac{C_{d}}{D_{d}}\bigg)^{n}B_{nd-1}

\displaystyle=-D_{d}^{N-1}\frac{\alpha^{2N}-\beta^{2N}}{\alpha-\beta}B_{d-1}=\frac{B_{2Nd-1}}{D_{d}^{N}}.

∎

3.7.1
Example (continued)

Returning to the example of
\sqrt{8}
, we get, for
x\neq 3\pm 2\sqrt{2}
, the sums

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}x^{n}B_{2n+r}=\frac{x^{N+1}(-B_{2(N+1)+r}+xB_{2N+r})-x(-B_{2+r}+xB_{r})}{(x-3+2\sqrt{2})(x-3-2\sqrt{2})},

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\binom{N}{n}(-6)^{n}B_{2n-1}=(-1)^{N}B_{4N-1}.

3.8
Divisibility properties

In this section, we explore some of the divisibility behaviors of the continuants. For similar results associated with Lucas sequences, refer to Ribenboim’s book
[
17
]
.

3.8.1
Divisibility within the sequence

We saw in the proof of Theorem
10
that
B_{d-1}\mid B_{2d-1}
. This can be further extended to the following theorem.

Theorem 37
.

If
m\mid n
, then
B_{md-1}\mid B_{nd-1}
.

Proof.

Using Corollary
2
, when
\nu=nd-1
,
\lambda=md
, then

\displaystyle B_{(m+n)d-1}

\displaystyle=B_{d}B_{nd-1}+a_{1}B_{md-1}B_{md-2,1}.

We get our conclusion by an inductive argument, which is omitted here.
∎

Stated differently, this theorem implies that the sequence
(B_{n}^{\prime})=(B_{nd-1})
is a divisibility sequence. In fact, it is a strong divisibility sequence, i.e.,
B_{\gcd(m,n)}^{\prime}=\gcd(B_{m}^{\prime},B_{n}^{\prime})
. One can prove this using the same technique for Fibonacci sequence, so we leave the proof as an exercise.

3.8.2
Divisibility by primes

Let
p
be an odd prime. If
p\mid C_{d}
and
p\mid D_{d}
, then
p\mid B_{nd+r}
(this also holds for
2
). For the case of
p\mid C_{d}
and
p\nmid D_{d}
, the result is given in the next theorem.

Theorem 38
.

If
p\mid C_{d}
,
p\nmid D_{d}
, and
n\geq 2
, then

\displaystyle B_{nd+r}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(-1/2)^{n-2}D_{d}\Delta^{(n-2)/2}B_{r},\quad&\text{ if }n\text{ is even};\\
(-1/2)^{n-1}\Delta^{(n-1)/2}B_{d+r},\quad&\text{ if }n\text{ is odd},\end{cases}\pmod{p}.

In particular,
B_{nd-1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
when
n
is even.

Proof.

We expand the term
(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})/(\alpha-\beta)
in Binet’s formula (Theorem
14
),

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2D_{d})^{n-1}}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\binom{n}{2k+1}(-C_{d})^{n-2k-1}\Delta^{k}.

If
p\mid C_{d}
and
p\nmid D_{d}
, then

\displaystyle(-D_{d})^{n-1}\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,\quad&\text{ if }n\text{ is even};\\
\Delta^{(n-1)/2}/2^{n-1},\quad&\text{ if }n\text{ is odd},\end{cases}\pmod{p}.

Hence, the theorem follows.
∎

If
p\nmid C_{d}
and
p\mid D_{d}
, then
\Delta\equiv C_{d}^{2}
(mod
p
). Using this fact, we conclude the following theorem.

Theorem 39
.

If
p\nmid C_{d}
and
p\mid D_{d}
, then

\displaystyle B_{(p-1)d+r}\equiv B_{r},\quad B_{(p-1)d-1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}

Proof.

When
p\mid D_{d}
, the term involving
B_{r}
vanishes modulo
p
. Consider again the expansion of
(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})/(\alpha-\beta)
,

\displaystyle 2^{p-1}B_{pd+r}\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{(p-1)/2}\binom{p}{2k+1}(-C_{d})^{p-1}B_{d+r}\pmod{p}.

Since
\binom{p}{k}\equiv 0
(mod
p
) for
1\leq k\leq p-1
, this simplifies to

\displaystyle B_{pd+r}\equiv B_{d+r}\pmod{p},

and the theorem follows.
∎

Suppose now that
p\nmid C_{d}D_{d}
. By setting
n=p+1
in the expansion of
(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})/(\alpha-\beta)
, we get

\displaystyle 2^{p}B_{(p+1)d+r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{(p-1)/2}\binom{p+1}{2k+1}C_{d}^{p-2k}\Delta^{k}B_{d+r}+2D_{d}\sum_{k=0}^{(p-1)/2}\binom{p}{2k+1}C_{d}^{p-2k-1}\Delta^{k}B_{r}.

Taking modulo
p
on both sides,

\displaystyle 2B_{(p+1)d+r}\equiv C_{d}B_{d+r}+C_{d}\Delta^{(p-1)/2}B_{d+r}+2D_{d}\Delta^{(p-1)/2}B_{r}\pmod{p}.

If
p\mid\Delta
, then

\displaystyle 2B_{(p+1)d+r}\equiv C_{d}B_{d+r}\pmod{p}.

This is equivalent to our next theorem.

Theorem 40
.

If
p\mid\Delta
and
p\nmid C_{d}D_{d}
, then

\displaystyle 2B_{pd+r}\equiv C_{d}B_{r},\quad B_{pd-1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

Otherwise,
\Delta^{(p-1)/2}\equiv(\Delta\mid p)
(mod
p
), where
(\cdot\mid p)
denotes the Legendre symbol modulo
p
. Hence, we have our next result.

Theorem 41
.

For an odd prime
p\nmid C_{d}D_{d}
, if
\Delta
is a quadratic residue modulo
p
, then

\displaystyle B_{(p+1)d+r}\equiv C_{d}B_{d+r}+D_{d}B_{r},\quad B_{(p+1)d-1}\equiv C_{d}B_{d-1}\pmod{p}.

If
\Delta
is a quadratic non-residue modulo
p
, then

\displaystyle B_{(p+1)d+r}\equiv-D_{d}B_{r},\quad B_{(p+1)d-1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

On the other hand, if we take
n=p
with
p\nmid C_{d}D_{d}
, then

\displaystyle 2^{p-1}B_{pd+r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{(p-1)/2}\binom{p}{2k+1}C_{d}^{p-2k-1}\Delta^{k}B_{d+r}+2D_{d}\sum_{k=0}^{(p-3)/2}\binom{p-1}{2k+1}C_{d}^{p-2k-2}\Delta^{k}B_{r}.

Taking modulo
p
on both sides and using the fact that
\binom{p-1}{k}\equiv(-1)^{k}
(mod
p
), we get

\displaystyle B_{pd+r}

\displaystyle\equiv\Delta^{(p-1)/2}B_{d+r}-\frac{2D_{d}}{C_{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{(p-3)/2}\bigg(1+\frac{4D_{d}}{C_{d}^{2}}\bigg)^{k}B_{r}\pmod{p}

\displaystyle\equiv\Delta^{(p-1)/2}B_{d+r}+\frac{C_{d}}{2}(1-\Delta^{(p-1)/2})B_{r}\pmod{p}.

Thus, we can deduce the following theorem.

Theorem 42
.

For an odd prime
p\nmid C_{d}D_{d}
, if
\Delta
is a quadratic residue modulo
p
, then

\displaystyle B_{(p-1)d+r}

\displaystyle\equiv B_{r},\quad B_{(p-1)d-1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

If
\Delta
is a quadratic non-residue modulo
p
, then

\displaystyle B_{pd+r}

\displaystyle\equiv C_{d}B_{r}-B_{d+r},\quad B_{pd-1}\equiv-B_{d-1}\pmod{p}.

Remark 43
.

In particular, if
d=1
,
C_{d}=(a+1)
and
D_{d}=-a
for some integer
a
, then
\Delta=(a+1)^{2}-4a=(a-1)^{2}
. For any prime
p\nmid C_{d}D_{d}\Delta
, then
(\Delta\mid p)=1
and

\displaystyle p\mid B_{p-2}=a^{p-2}\frac{a^{-(p-1)}-1}{a^{-1}-1}=\frac{a^{p-1}-1}{a-1}.

Therefore,
p\mid a^{p-1}-1
(this is trivial for
p\mid C_{d}D_{d}\Delta
), which is Fermat’s little theorem.

Theorems
40
to
42
suggest a certain periodicity of
B_{\nu}
modulo
p
. This period is commonly referred to as the Pisano period modulo
p
. While we do not delve into the full theory here, we will demonstrate a minor consequence of these theorems. Set
\sigma_{p}(n)
as the order of
n
modulo
p
, i.e., the smallest positive integer
k
such that
n^{k}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
. The next corollary gives the Pisano period modulo
p
.

Corollary 44
.

The Pisano period of
(B_{\nu})
modulo
p
, where
p\nmid C_{d}D_{d}
, divides

\displaystyle\begin{cases}pd\sigma_{p}(C_{d}/2),&\quad\text{ if }p\mid\Delta;\\
(p-1)d,&\quad\text{ if }\Delta\text{ is a quadratic residue modulo }p;\\
(p+1)d\sigma_{p}(-D_{d}),&\quad\text{ if }\Delta\text{ is a quadratic non-residue modulo }p.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
p\mid\Delta
, then Theorem
40
implies that

\displaystyle B_{pd\sigma_{p}(C_{d}/2)+r}\equiv(C_{d}/2)^{\sigma_{p}(C_{d}/2)}B_{r}\equiv B_{r}\pmod{p}.

Thus, the Pisano period divides
pd\sigma_{p}(C_{d}/2)
. The other cases are similar.
∎

Remark 45
.

For a more detailed account of Pisano period involving Fibonacci numbers, refer to the papers by Falcón
[
7
]
and Wall
[
20
]
.

3.8.3
Example (continued)

Using the example of
\sqrt{8}
as before, we calculate
\Delta=32
. We choose
p=3
and
7
, since
32
is a quadratic non-residue modulo
3
and a quadratic residue modulo
7
. Theorems
41
and
42
give us the following results, respectively:

\displaystyle B_{r+8}\equiv B_{r}\pmod{3},\quad B_{r+16}\equiv 6B_{r+2}-B_{r}\pmod{7},

and

\displaystyle B_{r+6}\equiv 6B_{r}-B_{r+2}\pmod{3},\quad B_{r+12}\equiv B_{r}\pmod{7}.

3.9
Law of apparition and repetition

3.9.1
Law of apparition

The results in §
3.8.2
resemble the law of apparition of Lucas sequences. For any prime
p
, define
\omega(p)
as the rank of apparition of
p
, which is the minimum positive integer
k
such that
B_{kd-1}\equiv 0
(mod
p
). The next theorem summarizes what we know about
\omega(p)
.

Theorem 46

(Law of apparition)
.

For a prime
p
, we have

(i)

If
p\mid C_{d}
and
p\mid D_{d}
, then
\omega(p)=1
.

(ii)

When
p=2
, the following results hold:

•

If
2\nmid C_{d}
and
2\mid D_{d}
, then

\displaystyle\omega(2)=\begin{cases}\text{does not exist},\quad&\text{ if }B_{d-1}\equiv 1\pmod{2};\\
1,\quad&\text{ if }B_{d-1}\equiv 0\pmod{2}.\end{cases}

•

If
2\mid C_{d}
(or
2\mid\Delta
) and
2\nmid D_{d}
, then
\omega(2)=1
or
2
.

•

If
2\nmid C_{d}
and
2\nmid D_{d}
, then
\omega(2)=1
or
3
.

(iii)

When
p
is an odd prime, the following results hold:

•

If
p\mid C_{d}
and
p\nmid D_{d}
, then
\omega(p)=1
or
2
.

•

If
p\nmid C_{d}
and
p\mid D_{d}
, then
\omega(p)\mid p-1
.

•

If
p\nmid C_{d}
,
p\nmid D_{d}
, and
p\mid\Delta
, then
\omega(p)=1
or
p
.

•

If
p\nmid C_{d}
,
p\nmid D_{d}
, and
p\nmid\Delta
, then
\omega(p)\mid p-(\Delta\mid p)
.

Proof.

We have already dealt with the case of odd primes, so we can suppose
p=2
. Using the fact
B_{2d-1}=C_{d}B_{d-1}
, we see that if
p\mid C_{d}
(or
p\mid\Delta
) and
p\nmid D_{d}
, then
B_{2d-1}=0
. Otherwise, if
p\nmid C_{d}
and
p\nmid D_{d}
, then

\displaystyle B_{3d-1}\equiv B_{2d-1}+B_{d-1}\equiv 2B_{d-1}\equiv 0\pmod{2}.

However, if
p\nmid C_{d}
, but
p\mid D_{d}
, then for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle B_{(n+1)d-1}\equiv B_{nd-1}\pmod{2}.

An inductive argument shows that
B_{nd-1}\equiv B_{d-1}
(mod
2
) for all
n\geq 1
. Thus, it is possible that
\omega(2)
does not exists if
B_{d-1}\equiv 1
(mod
2
).
∎

3.10
Lucas pseudoprime

Theorem
46
provides an extension to the notion of Lucas pseudoprime. For any positive integer
n
, by an abuse of notation, set
\epsilon(n)=(\Delta\mid n)
as the Jacobi symbol modulo
n
. We say that
n
is a Lucas pseudoprime with respect to
(B_{\nu})
, if
n\nmid C_{d}D_{d}\Delta
and

\displaystyle B_{(n-\epsilon(n))d-1}\equiv 0\pmod{n}.

If the congruence is false, we can conclude that
n
is composite. However, if the congruence holds, it does not necessarily imply that
n
is prime and hence the term ‘pseudoprime’. For example, we take
(B_{\nu})
as the sequence of denominators of the convergent to
\sqrt{8}
again. Choose
n=35
, then the Jacobi symbol is
(32\mid 35)=-1
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle B_{71}=641614773393652358999201580\equiv 0\pmod{35}.

Hence,
35
is a pseudoprime with respect to
(B_{\nu})
.

3.10.1
Law of repetition

For the next part, we require an identity that can be traced back to Lagrange. Given any odd integer
m
, it holds that

\displaystyle X^{m}-Y^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{(m-1)/2}\frac{m}{k}\binom{m-k-1}{k-1}(XY)^{k}\bigg(X-Y\bigg)^{m-2k},

This gives us an expansion for the term
B_{mnd-1}/B_{d-1}
as

\displaystyle\frac{B_{mnd-1}}{B_{d-1}}=\sum_{k=0}^{(m-1)/2}\frac{m}{k}\binom{m-k-1}{k-1}\Delta^{(m-2k-1)/2}(-D_{d})^{nk}\bigg((-D_{d})^{n-1}\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}\bigg)^{m-2k}.

We write
p^{e}\mid\mid N
if
e
is the highest power of
p
dividing
N
. The next theorem gives the law of repetition.

Theorem 47

(Law of repetition)
.

If
p^{e}\mid\mid B_{nd-1}/B_{d-1}
and
p\nmid m
, then
p^{e+f}\mid B_{p^{f}mnd-1}/B_{d-1}
. In addition, if
p\nmid D_{d}
, then the power is exact.

Proof.

By induction, it suffices to establish the theorem for
f=0
and
1
. The previous identity gives

\displaystyle\frac{B_{mnd-1}}{B_{d-1}}

\displaystyle\equiv m(-D_{d})^{n(m-1)/2}\frac{B_{nd-1}}{B_{d-1}}\pmod{(B_{nd-1}/B_{d-1})^{3}},

\displaystyle\frac{B_{pmnd-1}}{B_{d-1}}

\displaystyle\equiv pm(-D_{d})^{n(pm-1)/2}\frac{B_{nd-1}}{B_{d-1}}\pmod{(B_{nd-1}/B_{d-1})^{3}}.

Hence, if
p^{e}\mid\mid B_{nd-1}/B_{d-1}
, then
p^{e}\mid B_{mnd-1}/B_{d-1}
and
p^{e+1}\mid B_{pmnd-1}/B_{d-1}
. The second part is clear.
∎

3.11
Non-integer sequences

While all the theorems presented above assume that
(a_{\nu})
and
(b_{\nu})
are integer sequences, there is no reason not to extend them to real sequences as well. The only casualties are that
C_{d}
,
D_{d}
, and consequently
B_{\nu}
are not necessarily integers. However, most of our results still apply except those involving divisibility properties in §
3.8
. We demonstrate here an example. Consider the sequence

\displaystyle B_{\nu}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{3}B_{\nu-1}+B_{\nu-2},\quad&\text{ if }\nu\text{ is odd};\\
B_{\nu-1}-B_{\nu-2},\quad&\text{ if }\nu\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

The first few values of
B_{\nu}
are

\displaystyle B_{1}=\sqrt{3},\quad B_{2}=\sqrt{3}-1,\quad B_{3}=3,\quad B_{4}=4-2\sqrt{3}.

We compute
\alpha=-(\sqrt{3}-\sqrt{7})/2
and
\beta=-(\sqrt{3}+\sqrt{7})/2
, so using Binet’s formula (Theorem
14
), we have

\displaystyle B_{4k+1}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{7}}(\alpha^{2k-1}-\beta^{2k-1}-\sqrt{3}(\alpha^{2k}-\beta^{2k})).

Plugging this value into Theorem
27
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tan^{-1}\frac{3}{B_{4k+1}}=\tan^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}+\tan^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{19}+\tan^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{91}+\ldots=\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}=\frac{\pi}{6}.

4
Further discussion

The results presented in this paper primarily focus on the denominator,
B_{\nu,\lambda}
. However, it should be possible to give similar results involving the numerator,
A_{\nu,\lambda}
. We leave that to the readers.

Apart from that, the Fibonacci numbers have a companion sequence called the Lucas numbers. By examining the initial values, we observe that
(A_{\nu,\lambda})
is not the desired companion sequence. Therefore, it is still an open problem to construct the corresponding Lucas sequence for
(B_{\nu,\lambda})
.
