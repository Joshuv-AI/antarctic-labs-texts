Long-term straw incorporation improves nutrient accumulation in wheat seedlings by changing the soil’s hydrothermal environment to promote root growth
Authors: Hongmei Cai, Feifei Wang, Weixiang Huang, Li Yang, Mengmeng Zhang, Jiyuan Zhang, Baoqiang Zheng, Shan Jiang, Jincai Li, Xiang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrop straw incorporation plays an important role in improving soil environment, increasing crop yields and developing green and sustainable agriculture. However, the effects of long-term straw return on soil’s hydrothermal environment and the growth of wheat seedlings have not been well explored. Based on a 13-year long-term stationary experiment of straw returning to the field (2008–2021), this study investigated four straw management practices—no straw return (CK), wheat straw return (W1M0), maize straw return (W0M1), and the return of both wheat and maize straws (W1M1)—on the soil water content and temperature of lime concretion black soil, root structure, nitrogen uptake, and the quality of wheat seedlings. The results showed that straw returning treatment significantly increased soil water content at the three-leaf and four-leaf stages, but had no significant effect on soil temperature. Compared with the CK, soil water content at the three-leaf and four-leaf stages increased by 13.14%–24.67% and 7.42%–17.82%, respectively. Meanwhile, straw returning also promoted the increase of root activity at the three-leaf and six-leaf stages, at the three-leaf stage, W1M0, W1M1, and W0M1 were significantly higher than CK by 148.64%, 111.11% and 148.64%, respectively. In addition, long-term straw incorporation also promotes an increase in the nitrogen content and nitrogen accumulation of wheat seedling plants. Compared with CK, the phosphorus content of W1M0 and W1M1 treatments was higher than that of CK, while that of W0M1 treatment was lower than that of CK. The SEM model shows that soil moisture and temperature have a significant negative impact on wheat root physiology indicators (root activity and root active absorption area), while root physiology significantly indicators affects root morphology, which in turn exerts a significant negative influence on the nitrogen and phosphorus contents of above-ground plant parts. In conclusion, long-term straw incorporation is conducive to creating a soil hydrothermal environment that is suitable for the growth of wheat seedlings, thereby promoting the formation of strong wheat seedlings. Introduction

Efficient use of agricultural resources is an important measure to achieve green and sustainable agricultural development. It is of great significance to ensure global food security, promote the sustainable growth of agriculture, improve rural ecological environment and increase farmers’ income [1, 2]. Straw incorporation is currently the most widely used measure in agricultural resource utilization [3]. This measure has a wide range of ecosystem services and plays an important role in soil ecological environment, food production, carbon sequestration and emission reduction [4, 5]. A large number of studies have shown that straw incorporation can better regulate soil water and heat characteristics and maintain soil’s hydrothermal environment required for crop growth and development [6]. However, due to the influence of region and soil properties, the water and heat characteristics of the soil under different straw return methods do not always have a positive impact.

The ability of straw incorporation to better maintain soil moisture and temperature mainly depends on the type of straw, the method of straw returning, and the land management method of the preceding crop [7, 8]. Different straw types and their return methods have specific effects on soil’s hydrothermal conditions and crop residue input, which in turn affect the growth of subsequent crops. In the black soil region of Northeast China, a field trial study showed that when the temperature is low, straw incorporation can increase the average soil temperature of the 0–100 cm soil layer, while it maintains the lower soil temperature when the temperature is high [7]. On the contrary, the study by Yin et al. [6] reported that the difference in the soil’s hydrothermal environment was not significant between rotary tillage with straw returning and rotary tillage without straw return. These differences in results may be due to various reasons, including climatic conditions and soil fertility. However, the research results on the effects of different straw-returning methods on soil moisture and temperature in the lime concretion black soil areas with low soil fertility remain unclear.

The root system is an important organ for plants to absorb and transport soil water and minerals, and plays an important role in the growth and development of crops [9]. The growth of the root system can affect the synthesis of auxin, the storage of nutrients, the diversity of symbiotic microorganisms, etc. Therefore, a strong root system plays an important role in ensuring the growth and development of crops [10, 11]. For crop seedlings, after straw returning, the root growth is often restricted by uneven distribution of straw in the soil, failure to meet the requirements of crushing length or serious bacterial infection, and the inability to quickly form a strong root system, thus affecting the growth and development of the aboveground [12]. Previous studies have shown that the formation of a strong root system can be promoted by soaking seeds with chemicals, adding straw decomposing agents, and inoculating microorganisms to improve soil health [13]. However, these methods have geographical limitations in field production, and there are relatively few studies on whether long-term straw incorporation can improve the soil environment and promote the formation of strong wheat root systems.

Nitrogen and phosphorus are the two most needed nutrients in the growth and development of plants. Their demand runs through the entire growth process of plants and is of great significance to global food production [14–16]. For wheat, the early growth and development mainly relies on absorbing nitrogen for large-scale photosynthetic production, producing substances to meet the needs of nutritional growth. Previous studies have confirmed that there are two peaks of nitrogen demand during wheat growth, namely the tillering stage and the jointing stage [17, 18]. After straw returning, microbial decomposition also requires nitrogen supply. Therefore, in many areas, tillering fertilizer is applied during the tillering stage to balance the nitrogen demand of crops and the nitrogen demand of microbial decomposition [17, 19]. However, this leads to an increase in production costs during the actual production process. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the situation of nitrogen accumulation and distribution during the seedling stage of wheat under the condition of long-term straw incorporation for achieving efficient utilization of fertilizers and promoting the green and sustainable development of agriculture.

The Huang-Huai-Hai Plain is China’s largest cereal crop producing area. Winter wheat and summer maize are the main cropping systems in the region, with China accounting for 70% and 30% of wheat and maize production respectively [20, 21]. With the grain production increased in recent years, straw production has also increased significantly. This is a significant result of the application of chemical fertilizers. But, the large-scale application of chemical fertilizers has also seriously reduced soil quality. A large number of studies have reported that the carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and other elements contained in straw can improve soil fertility and increase crop yields after returning [22]. Therefore, straw return has become one of the main ways of straw treatment in the region. However, the problems of wheat germination and seedling establishment caused by short-term straw return to the field cannot be ignored. Therefore, this study is based on a 13-year long-term straw return positioning experiment to evaluate the effects of soil water and heat changes under long-term straw return conditions on the root growth and nutrient absorption of wheat seedlings. In this study, our objectives were to evaluate the effects of returning the previous crop straw and only the current crop straw on the soil’s hydrothermal environment, and to examine the effects of different straw incorporation on the root growth and nutrient absorption of wheat seedlings, so as to explore the relationship between soil ecological environmental factors and the root growth and nutrient absorption of wheat seedlings.

Materials and methods

Study sites

This experiment was conducted based on a long-term in situ positioning experiment, which was started in 2008 at the Scientific Observing and Experimental Station of Crop Cultivation in East China, Ministry of Agriculture, P. R. China in Mengcheng County, Bozhou City, Anhui Province during the winter season of 2021–2022 and 2023–2024 (33°9′N, 116°32′E, a.s.l 28 m). The area has a warm temperate semi-humid monsoon climate, featuring an average annual temperature of 14.8℃, annual precipitation of 732.63 mm (mainly concentrated in June-August), a 214-day frost-free period and annual sunshine of 2,410 h.The properties of 0-20cm soil layer at the beginning of the field experiment (2008) were as follows: 12.46 g kg‾1 organic matter, 0.99 g kg‾1 total N, 80.20 mg kg‾1 alkali-hydrolyzed N, 15.40 mg kg‾1 available P, and 100.30 mg kg‾1 available K, with a typical lime concretion black soil. Experimental design and crop management

Long-term maize (Zea mays L.; Zhengdan 958)–wheat (Triticum aestivum L.; Yannong 19)—a double-cropping field—was established for this study. The experiment included four treatments: i) no straw return (CK), ⅱ) wheat straw return(W1M0), ⅲ) both of wheat and maize straw return (W1M1), and iv) maize straw return (W0M1). Each treatment was applied with three replicates in field plots of area 43.2 m2 (8.0 m × 5.4 m). The wheat and maize straw were crushed into pieces less than 10 cm in length. Wheat straw was spread evenly over the soil surface as a mulch without tillage, whereas maize straw was returned to the 0–20 cm soil layer using a large rotary tiller (Lutong-1304, Luoyang, China). If straw was not returned, it was artificially removed from the experimental plots. In the experiment, agricultural potassium sulfate compound fertilizer (N: P2O5: K2O = 15: 15: 15, total nutrients > 45%) and urea (N ≥ 46%) were applied. The doses of N, P, and K in the chemical fertilizers were 240 kg N ha− 1, 90 kg P ha− 1, and 90 kg K ha− 1. Of the total amount, 55% of N was applied as a basal fertilizer and 45% was applied at the jointing stage as a topdressing, whereas all P and K were applied at sowing. Field management followed local conventional cultivation practices. Other management practices were identical to those of the high-yield field.

Plant and soil sampling and analysis

At the three-leaf stage, four-leaf stage, five-leaf stage, and six-leaf stage of wheat, at least three wheat plants were selected from each plot for the determination of root morphology, root activity, active root absorption area and dry matter. A 30 cm × 30 cm× 30 cm soil block was dug out with a shovel, and the wheat roots were slowly washed out in water and stored in a low-temperature box [23]. After being brought back to the laboratory, they were rinsed with pure water, and the root morphology was scanned using a root scanner. The root length, root surface area, root mean diameter and root volume parameters were obtained using the WINRHIZO PRO software (Regent Instruments Inc.). Root activity sampling was the same as root morphology. After obtaining the roots, they were washed with pure water and then measured according to the TTC (2,3,5-Triphenyl Tetrazolium Chloride) reduction method [24]. The active absorption area of the roots was measured using the methylene blue adsorption method [25].

Wheat plants were packed according to organs, withered in a constant temperature air drying oven, and weighed after drying to constant weight. The dried plants were ground according to organs using a grinder, passed through a 100-mesh sieve, and sealed for storage for plant nutrient determination. Next, the plants were pretreated with concentrated sulfuric acid-hydrogen peroxide (H2SO4-H2O2) digestion method. After pretreatment, the nitrogen and phosphorus content was determined using an Auto-Analyzer 3 (AA3, Bran Luebbe, Germany).

The 0–25 cm soil layer was divided into 5 cm layers, and the soil temperature of different soil layers was measured at 9:00 am and 2:00 pm every day using a right-angle geothermometer (Hengshui, China) [6]. Soil moisture was determined by the drying method. A soil drill was used to obtain the 0–20 cm soil layer according to the five-point sampling method. After mixing evenly, the soil was placed in an aluminum box and weighed for fresh weight. The soil was dried to constant weight in a constant temperature forced air drying oven and then weighed for dry weight. Then, the soil moisture content was calculated [26].

Statistical analyses

SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA) was used to conduct one-way analysis of variance on each data and subsequent Waller-Duncan test at the 5% significance level between each indicator of different treatments. All charts in this article were drawn using OriginPro2021 (Origin Lab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Redundancy Analysis (RDA) was performed using the vagan package of R language (R4.4.1, Auckland, New Zealand), plotting was performed using ggplot2, and graphics were spliced using patchwork. The Smart-PLS 3 (Bönningstedt, Germany) software was employed to construct the structural equation model using the partial least squares (PLS) method.

The straw return significantly affected the soil moisture content before the fourth-leaf stage of wheat (Fig. 2F). Compared with CK, the soil moisture content of W1M0, W1M1, and W0M1 treatments significantly increased by 13.14%, 24.67%, 13.47%, and 10.01%, 17.82%, and 7.42% at the three-leaf stage and the four-leaf stage, respectively. However, the changes between the five-leaf stage and the six-leaf stage were not significant. In summary, straw return had a positive effect on maintaining soil moisture.

Straw return on wheat seedling root morphology and physiological characteristics

Long-term straw return had a negative impact on the root morphology of wheat seedlings before the six-leaf stage (except for the root mean diameter of W1M1 and W0M1 at the four-leaf stage (Fig. 3). At six-leaf stage, the root length and root surface area of W1M0 and W0M1 treatments increased significantly by 29.11%, 22.48% and 27.86%, 18.68% compared with CK, respectively, while the root mean diameter of W1M1 increased significantly by 6.19% compared with CK. These results shows that different ways of returning straw promote root growth in different directions. Single-season straw return promotes the longitudinal growth of the root system, while annual straw return has a more obvious effect on promoting the lateral growth of the root system.

The influence of long-term straw return to the field on the root morphology of wheat seedlings 2023–2024

Long-term straw return had a significant effect on the physiological characteristics of wheat seedling roots (Fig. 4). The root vitality of all treatments reached its maximum at the four-leaf stage. The root vitality of different straw return treatments was higher than that of CK at the three-leaf stage. W1M0, W1M1, and W0M1 were significantly higher than CK by 148.64%, 111.11% and 148.64%, respectively. Besides, the root vitality of different straw return treatments was higher than that of CK at the six-leaf stage, but the difference were not significantly. The active absorption area of the W1M0 root system was significantly higher than that of CK at the four-leaf stage and the five-leaf stage, while the active absorption area of the W1M1 root system was significantly higher than that of CK only at the three-leaf stage, and was lower than that of CK at the four-leaf stage and the five-leaf stage. The active root absorption area of W1M0 and W1M1 treatments at the six-leaf stage was higher than that of CK, but the difference between treatments was not significant. The results showed that W1M0 treatment was more beneficial than W1M1 and W0M1in maintaining root vitality and active root absorption area of wheat seedling roots in 2023–2024.

Straw return on wheat seedling nutrient characteristics

Different straw return treatments had significant effects on the nitrogen content and nitrogen accumulation in different organs of wheat seedlings (Figs. 5 and 6). In 2021–2022, the nitrogen content in leaves and sheath organs of different straw-returning treatments showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing, reaching the highest value in the four-leaf stage, while the nitrogen accumulation showed an increasing trend. In 2023–2024, the nitrogen content of wheat leaves showed decreasing trend, while the nitrogen accumulation gradually increases. In summary, long-term straw return can promote nitrogen accumulation in various organs of wheat seedlings, mainly promoting nitrogen accumulation in the aboveground parts, among which W1M0 and W1M1 performed best.

Different straw-returning treatments had a significant effect on the phosphorus content and accumulation in different organs of wheat seedlings (Figs. 7 and 8). In 2021–2022 and 2023–2024, the phosphorus content of leaves and sheaths showed an overall downward trend, and the phosphorus accumulation showed an increasing trend. Compared with CK, the phosphorus content of W1M0 and W1M1 treatments was higher than that of CK, while that of W0M1 treatment was lower than that of CK. At the same time, the phosphorus content of the sheath was higher than that of the leaves, but the accumulation was lower than that of the leaves.

Straw return on proportion of nitrogen and phosphorus accumulation in different organs

As can be seen from Fig. 9, for different straw-returning treatments, the proportion of nitrogen accumulation in the leaves of wheat seedlings is the highest among the leaves, leaf sheaths, and roots, followed by that in the leaf sheaths and roots. The results show that the nitrogen accumulation in the roots of the single-season straw return treatment is higher than that of CK (except in the Four-LS in 2021), and it is also higher than that of the annual straw return treatment.

The effects of different long-term straw returning methods on the proportion of nitrogen accumulation in various organs of wheat seedlings at different growth stages

As an important element that promotes tillering, phosphorus shows a “preference” for leaf sheaths in its distribution among various organs of wheat seedlings (Fig. 10). During two important tillering periods, namely the Four-LS and the Six- LS, the proportion of phosphorus accumulation in leaf sheaths will significantly increase, and this variation range is even greater under different straw-returning treatments. The results show that different straw-returning treatments can regulate the distribution ratio of phosphorus among various organs, especially promoting the accumulation of phosphorus in leaf sheaths during the critical tillering periods (the Four-LS and the Six- LS).

Redundancy analysis and structural equation model

The redundancy analysis method was used to analyze the changes in soil moisture and the temperature in different soil layers on the root morphology and physiological characteristics of wheat seedlings and plant nutrients under different straw-returning methods. The results showed that the environmental factors had a significant impact on the root growth and nutrient content of wheat seedlings (Fig. 11). Among them, the soil temperature in 0–5 cm and 20–25 cm had the strongest impact on the growth of wheat seedlings, followed by soil moisture content. This shows that maintaining the soil bottom temperature in the early stage of wheat seedling growth is crucial, followed by ensuring appropriate soil moisture content.

Structural equation model (SEM) indicated that the impact of straw incorporation on the soil environment significantly affected the physiological characteristics of the roots (Fig. 12). In turn, this promoted the accumulation of nitrogen in the roots, thereby facilitating the growth and development of the roots. This indicated that long-term straw incorporation was conducive to promoting the absorption and utilization of nitrogen by the roots of wheat, thus promoting the growth and development of the aboveground parts, and providing a nutrient basis for the formation of strong wheat seedlings.

Discussion

Straw return can change soil temperature and moisture, but it is closely related to the way of straw-returning methods and the tillage methods, and other factors [27]. In this study, the soil temperature differences among the treatments were not significant (Fig. 2), which is consistent with the change trend of soil temperature in the 0–25 cm soil layer obtained by Yin et al. [6] in their study on conventional straw return and conventional tillage with straw return. This may be related to the destruction of the soil tillage layer and the increase in soil porosity after rotary tillage [28]. Under straw return, soil moisture content was significantly higher than CK at the three-leaf and four-leaf stages (Fig. 2), indicating that straw return has a positive effect on the maintenance of soil moisture. This might be the consequence of the elevation in soil porosity following the incorporation of straw return, coupled with the soil’s ability to capture water after rainfall [29]. Moreover, during the initial phase of straw incorporation into the field, the decomposition of the straw proceeds at a relatively sluggish pace, thereby guaranteeing a comparatively high soil moisture content during this period (Fig. 2). In addition, some studies have pointed out that burying straw and returning it to the field can increase the specific water capacity of the soil, thereby enhancing the soil’s water supply capacity [30]. This may be another reason why returning straw to the fields increases soil moisture.

Meanwhile, soil temperature and soil moisture are also among the important environmental factors affecting root growth and development. Root morphological characteristics and distribution in the soil directly affect the growth and development of the aboveground parts of crops, the formation of their quality, as well as the absorption and utilization of water and nutrients [31].Studies have shown that returning crop straw to the field can reduce soil bulk density and increase soil porosity, thus promoting the growth and development of crop roots [5, 32, 33]. In this study, single-season straw incorporation mainly promoted root length growth, while annual straw incorporation primarily enhanced root volume (Fig. 3). This also significantly improved root vitality and the effective root absorption area (Fig. 4). Meanwhile, the sound development of the root system aided wheat seedlings in absorbing, transporting, and accumulating nitrogen and phosphorus nutrients. Notably, the nitrogen accumulation in root increased significantly under both single-season wheat straw incorporation and annual straw incorporation (Fig. 9). This is likely due to the fact that in single-season wheat straw incorporation, there is scarcely any straw that needs microbial decomposition [34]. This eases the nitrogen competition among microorganisms and ultimately enhances the absorption and utilization of nitrogen by wheat seedlings [35]. After single-season corn straw incorporation, nitrogen competition between microbial straw decomposition and plant growth led to relatively low nitrogen accumulation in this treatment [34, 36]. However, in the annual straw incorporation, although microbial nitrogen competition still existed, the nitrogen stored in soil from the previous season’s wheat straw incorporation balanced part of the nitrogen demand [37]. Thus, there was no significant difference in nitrogen accumulation between single-season wheat straw incorporation and annual wheat and maize straw incorporation treatments.

Nevertheless, the increased root volume in the annual straw incorporation treatment indicated sufficient nitrogen supply. If this approach is continued, reducing nitrogen fertilizer application to encourage deeper root growth is advisable [38]. This will prevent premature senescence in the later stage and provide insights for reducing chemical fertilizer use, improving fertilizer efficiency, and promoting green, sustainable agriculture. Certainly, this study only investigated the effects of two soil environmental factors (soil temperature and soil moisture) on wheat seedlings, which has certain limitations. Therefore, future research should strengthen the exploration of the impacts of multiple factors—such as changes in soil available nutrients and soil physical structure under straw returning conditions—on nutrient accumulation and distribution in the roots and aboveground parts of wheat seedlings.

Conclusion

Long-term straw return to the field can maintain a stable soil water and temperature environment, promote root growth, nutrient absorption, and ultimately promote the accumulation of nutrients and the formation of strong seedlings in wheat seedlings. Compared with CK, there was no significant difference in soil temperature, but the soil moisture at the three-leaf and four-leaf stages was maintained. In addition, all straw-returning treatments promoted the growth of wheat seedling roots to varying degrees. Among them, single-season straw returning mainly promoted the longitudinal growth of the root system, while annual straw return promoted the lateral growth of the root system. It can be seen from the nitrogen accumulation between different straw-returning treatments that the wheat straw return in the previous season is more conducive to the nitrogen accumulation of wheat seedlings in the current season and promotes plant growth, but the maize straw return does not show such a trend. In summary, the implementation of straw return is beneficial to the growth of wheat seedlings in the lime concretion black soil area.
