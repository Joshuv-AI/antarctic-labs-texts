A Bayesian Perspective on the Maximum Score Problem
Christopher D. Walker

Abstract
This paper presents a Bayesian inference framework for a linear index threshold-crossing binary choice model that satisfies a median independence restriction. The key idea is that the model is observationally equivalent to a probit model with nonparametric heteroskedasticity. Consequently, Gibbs sampling techniques from Albert and Chib (1993) and Chib and Greenberg (2013) lead to a computationally attractive Bayesian inference procedure in which a Gaussian process forms a conditionally conjugate prior for the natural logarithm of the skedastic function.



1
Introduction

The threshold-crossing binary choice model is regularly encountered in economics and other social sciences. Under the model, an observable binary outcome
Y\in\{0,1\}
is determined by some observable covariates
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and an unobservable random variable
U\in\mathbb{R}
via the sign of a linear index
X^{\prime}\beta-U
, where
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
is an unknown vector of parameters. That is,

\displaystyle Y=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if $X^{\prime}\beta-U\geq 0$}\\
0&\quad\text{if $X^{\prime}\beta-U<0$}\end{cases}.

(1)

This is a semiparametric model as the conditional distribution for
Y
given
X
is determined by a finite-dimensional vector of index function coefficients
\beta
and an infinite-dimensional parameter representing the conditional distribution of
U
given
X
. It is relevant in economics because
Y
may represent an individual’s choice between two alternatives and
X^{\prime}\beta-U
is the difference in utility values.

This paper considers a setting where the goal is
structural analysis
(i.e., learning about the threshold-crossing process). Structural analysis is useful because it enables the researcher to learn about certain features of the decision-making problem, generate more precise predictions relative to simply estimating the conditional mean of
Y
given
X
using nonparametric methods, and extrapolate beyond the observed covariate values. A necessary ingredient for structural analysis is the objective identifiability of the index coefficients
\beta
, and, to that end, I will maintain that the conditional distribution of
U
given
X
satisfies the conditional median restriction,

\displaystyle Median(U|X)=0,

(2)

a constraint first imposed in
Manski (1975)
;
Manski (1985)
that is sometimes referred to as
median independence
in the econometrics literature.
1
1

1

See
Manski (1988)
and
Horowitz (2009)
for use of this terminology.
Frequentist estimators based on this location restriction are often called
maximum score methods
(e.g.,
Manski (1975)
;
Manski (1985)
;
Manski and Thompson (1986)
;
Horowitz (1992)
;
Khan (2013)
;
Komarova (2013)
;
Jun et al. (2015)
;
Jun et al. (2017)
;
Patra et al. (2018)
;
Gao and Xu (2020)
). For this reason, structural analysis under median independence will be called the
maximum score problem
.

This paper offers a Bayesian perspective on the maximum score problem. A Bayesian approach requires specifying a prior for the index coefficients
\beta
and the conditional distribution of
U
given
X
. The latter is challenging because any prior for the conditional distribution of the error term
U
given
X
must be supported on a set of conditional distributions that obey median independence. Without modification, standard priors for conditional distributions (e.g.,
Quintana et al. (2022)
and the references therein) do not impose the location normalization. Consequently, the resulting Bayes estimators do not have a structural interpretation (i.e., they can only be used for
reduced-form analysis
where the goal is to estimate conditional response probabilities). An exception is
Newton et al. (1996)
, a paper proposing Bayesian inference for a linear index threshold-crossing model using a median zero-constrained Dirichlet process prior. Their prior, however, assigns probability one to single index models for the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
(i.e., models in which the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
depends on the covariates through the linear index
X^{\prime}\beta

only
). This means their model is restrictive relative to the class of distributions accommodated by (
2
) because it does not allow for arbitrary heteroskedasticity. Unrestricted heteroskedasticity is important in economic applications due to the possibility of random coefficients in the utility function.

Rather than constraining a prior for the distribution of
U
given
X
to impose median independence, I work with a reparametrization of the threshold-crossing model that simultaneously enables structural analysis and unrestricted priors for the infinite-dimensional nuisance parameters.
Manski (1988)
and
Khan (2013)
show that the threshold-crossing model with median independence is observationally equivalent to a probit model with nonparametric heteroskedasticity. That is, conditional distributions for
Y
given
X
compatible with (
1
) and (
2
) can be matched to those implied by the threshold-crossing model

\displaystyle Y=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if $X^{\prime}\beta-\sigma(X)\varepsilon\geq 0$}\\
0&\quad\text{if $X^{\prime}\beta-\sigma(X)\varepsilon<0$}\end{cases},

where
\varepsilon
follows a standard normal distribution and
x\mapsto\sigma(x)
is an unknown positive function. This leads to a simple Bayesian inference procedure because the data augmentation strategy of
Albert and Chib (1993)
effectively reduces inference to that of a Gaussian linear regression model with unknown heteroskedasticity. Using this observation and writing
\sigma^{2}(X)=\exp(g(X))
, I employ techniques from
Chib and Greenberg (2013)
to derive a Gibbs sampler in which a Gaussian process forms a conditionally conjugate prior for
g
.

There are at least two benefits associated with a Bayesian approach in this setting. The first is that inference is based on a full probability model for the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
. Consequently,
any
inference question can be answered using the posterior. For example, a simulated sample from the posterior provides finite-sample Bayesian uncertainty quantification for the index function coefficients
\beta

and
the model-implied choice probabilities
\Phi(X^{\prime}\beta\exp(-g(X)/2))
, where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard normal cumulative distribution. Operationally, these questions are addressed with simple manipulations of an empirical distribution (computing sample means, empirical quantiles, etc.). The second benefit is computational. For the class of priors recommended in this paper, every step of the Gibbs sampler has a closed form. This means the researcher has to sample from standard parametric distributions during implementation. In contrast, maximum score methods typically require maximizing a nonconcave objective function with many local extrema, and, without smoothing, these optimization problems may require mixed integer programming
(
Florios and Skouras, 2008
)
. Quasi-Bayesian approaches to the maximum score problem (e.g.,
Jun et al. (2015)
;
Jun et al. (2017)
) are easier to compute through the use of Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods, however, they do not enable choice probability estimation. Beyond computation, asymptotic frequentist inference is challenging due to nonstandard limiting distributions associated with unsmoothed maximum score estimators
(
Kim and Pollard, 1990
)
, while inference based on smoothed maximum score estimators (e.g.,
Horowitz (1992)
) can be sensitive to choice of bandwidth parameters.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the Bayesian inference procedure. Section
3
presents a Gibbs sampling algorithm for the model. Section
4
discusses some extensions. Section
5
provides a small-scale simulation to illustrate some of the finite-sample properties of the method. Section
6
concludes. Notation is introduced when appropriate and proofs are in the Appendix.

2
Bayesian Inference

2.1
Sampling Model

I start with the specification of a conditional sampling model. Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a fixed set of covariates each taking values in
\mathcal{X}
, a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
with
d_{x}<\infty
.
2
2

2

Readers may view
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
as a fixed realization of a stochastic process
\{X_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
of covariates.
The outcome variables
(Y_{1},...,Y_{n})^{\prime}
are generated according to the linear index threshold-crossing model,

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if $x_{i}^{\prime}\beta-U_{i}\geq 0$}\\
0&\quad\text{if $x_{i}^{\prime}\beta-U_{i}<0$}\end{cases},\quad U_{i}|\beta,\{Q(\cdot|x):x\in\mathcal{X}\}\overset{ind}{\sim}Q(\cdot|x_{i}),\quad i=1,...,n,

where
\beta
is a parameter vector that belongs to a subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\{Q(\cdot|x):x\in\mathcal{X}\}
is a class of conditional cumulative distribution functions belonging to some space
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}
(i.e.,
Q(t|x)=\Pr(U\leq t|X=x)
for each
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{X}
). This specifies a conditional sampling model
\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}}
for
(Y_{1},...,Y_{n})^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}}=\left\{\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}Bin(1,Q(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta|x_{i})):(\beta,\{Q(\cdot|x):x\in\mathcal{X}\})\in\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}\right\},

where
Bin(k,p)
denotes a binomial distribution with
k
trials and success probability
p
.

Assumption 1
.

Elements
\{Q(\cdot|x):x\in\mathcal{X}\}
of
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}
satisfy

1.

t\mapsto Q(t|x)
is continuous for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

2.

t\mapsto Q(t|x)
is strictly increasing for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and

3.

\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:Q(t|x)\geq 0.5\}=0
for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Assumption 2
.

The parameter space
\mathcal{B}
satisifes
\mathcal{B}=\Theta\times\{1\}
, where
\Theta
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}-1}
.

Assumptions
1
and
2
restrict the joint parameter space
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}
. Assumption
1
formalizes median independence because Part 3 enforces that the conditional distribution of
U
given
X
has median zero.
3
3

3

Parts 1 and 2 are regularity conditions that are helpful for the proof of Theorem
1
below. Namely, they ensure that
Q(x^{\prime}\beta|x)=0.5
if and only if
x^{\prime}\beta=0
, a property used to establish observational equivalence with the heteroskedastic probit model.
Median independence is a minimal location normalization for the error distribution that ensures nontrivial identification of the index coefficients
\beta

(
Manski, 1975
;
Manski, 1985
)
.
4
4

4

In contrast, the mean independence restriction
E(U|X)=0
has no identifying power
(
Manski, 1988
)
.
For example, it accommodates heteroskedasticity of an unknown form. This is important in stochastic choice models, where
x_{i}^{\prime}\beta-U_{i}
represents utility differences between options (e.g., purchase a good or not), because heteroskedasticity may arise due to unobservable preference heterogeneity (i.e., random coefficients). Assumption
2
imposes that
\beta=(\theta^{\prime},1)^{\prime}
for some
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}-1}
. It is a scale normalization introduced because median independence places no restrictions on the scale of
\beta
. There are other approaches to scale normalization, for instance setting
\mathcal{B}=\{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}:||\beta||_{2}=1\}
a lá
Manski (1985)
, however, simply setting the last element of
\beta
equal to one is convenient for my paper.

Assumption
1
implies that the linear index threshold-crossing model is observationally equivalent to a probit model with nonparametric heteroskedasticity. Let
z\mapsto\Phi(z)
be the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of functions mapping from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}_{++}
. The heteroskedastic probit model is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{S}}=\left\{\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}Bin\left(1,\Phi\left(\frac{x_{i}^{\prime}\beta}{\sigma(x_{i})}\right)\right):(\beta,\sigma)\in\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{S}\right\}.

The next theorem is based on Theorem 2.1 of
Khan (2013)
, an extension of a lemma on p. 737 of
Manski (1988)
. It formalizes the observational equivalence of the two binary choice models.

Theorem 1
.

Assumption
1
implies
\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}}=\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{S}}
.

Theorem
1
justifies using the heteroskedastic probit model for inference about the threshold-crossing model. Indeed, it states that any vector
(Y_{1},...,Y_{n})^{\prime}
drawn from a distribution in
\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}}
can also be drawn from a distribution in
\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{S}}
(and vice versa).
Since elements of
\mathcal{S}
can be written as
\sigma=\exp(g/2)
for
g=\log\sigma^{2}
, I base inference on the sampling model

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{G}}=\left\{\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}Bin\left(1,\Phi\left(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}g(x_{i})\right)\right)\right):(\beta,g)\in\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{G}\right\}.

This leads to the likelihood function

\displaystyle L_{n}(\beta,g)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\Phi\left(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}g(x_{i})\right)\right)^{Y_{i}}\left(1-\Phi\left(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}g(x_{i})\right)\right)\right)^{1-Y_{i}}\right\}

for each
(\beta,g)\in\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{G}
. Notice that the nuisance parameter
g
is unrestricted in
\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{G}}
(i.e., it is just a mapping from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
). This contrasts with the original parametrization where the nuisance parameter is restricted to satisfy median independence. It is practically useful because the researcher can avoid semiparametric likelihood estimation over a constrained parameter space.
Khan (2013)
studies frequentist estimation of
\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{G}}
, approximating the nuisance parameter space
\mathcal{G}
using a finite-dimensional linear sieve.

2.2
Prior and Posterior

I adopt a Bayesian approach and equip the parameter space
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{G}
with a prior
\Pi
. Specifically, I assume
\beta\sim\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}
,
g\sim\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}
, and
\beta\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}g
so that the joint distribution
\Pi
of
(\beta,g)
is the product
\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}\times\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}
. The marginal distribution
\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}
can be virtually any (improper) probability distribution over
\mathcal{B}
, however, I specify
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}
as the probability law of a mean-zero Gaussian process
\{g(x):x\in\mathcal{X}\}
. That is, for any finite collection
x_{1},...,x_{k}
of inputs in
\mathcal{X}
with
k\geq 1
, the vector of function values
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{k}))^{\prime}
follows a mean-zero Gaussian distribution with covariance matrix
(\kappa(x_{j},x_{l}))_{j,l=1}^{k}
, where
\kappa:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a positive semidefinite kernel known as a
covariance function
. This choice primarily reflects computation (see Section
3
), however, it should be noted that Gaussian processes have become a default choice of nonparametric prior for unrestricted functions (see
Rasmussen and Williams (2006)
and the references therein). In Section
5
, I assume that
g
is drawn from a
Matérn Gaussian process
, a stationary Gaussian process with a covariance function hyperparameter that can accommodate any order of differentiability
(
Stein, 1999
)
.

The prior and the sampling model leads to a conditional distribution for
(\beta,g)
given
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, known as a
posterior
, that can be used to answer any inference question involving the pair
(\beta,g)
. Applying Bayes theorem, the posterior distribution
\Pi_{n}((\beta,g)\in\cdot|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
for
(\beta,g)
satisfies

\displaystyle d\Pi_{n}(\beta,g|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})\propto L_{n}(\beta,g)d\Pi(\beta,g).

Point estimates for the elements of
\beta
can be chosen as elementwise posterior medians, uncertainty quantification for functions
f(\beta,g)
of
(\beta,g)
can be obtained using a
credible set
, a set estimator
CS_{n,f}(1-\alpha)
that satisfies the coverage requirement

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}(f(\beta,g)\in CS_{n,f}(1-\alpha)|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})=1-\alpha

for some nominal level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and predictions can made using the posterior predictive distribution

\displaystyle P_{n,\Pi}(Y=1|X=x_{*})=\int\Phi\left(x_{*}^{\prime}\beta\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}g(x_{*})\right)\right)d\Pi(\beta,g|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})

for some covariate value
x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
. In practice, these integrals are replaced with sample averages based on a simulated posterior sample
\{(\beta_{s},g_{s})\}_{s=1}^{S}
. The takeaway is powerful: a researcher with a sample
\{(\beta_{s},g_{s})\}_{s=1}^{S}
can answer
any
inference question that arises in the structural analysis of the binary choice model. I now turn to the important issue of sampling from the posterior.

3
Sampling from the Posterior

3.1
Overview of the Gibbs Sampler

I propose Gibbs sampler for the parameters of the heteroskedastic probit model. The starting point is to expand the parameter from
(\beta,g)
to
(\beta,g,Z_{1},...,Z_{n})
using that
Y_{i}=\mathbf{1}\{Z_{i}\geq 0\}
,
i=1,...,n
, where
Z_{i}|\beta,g\overset{ind}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta,\exp(g(x_{i})))
for each
i=1,...,n
. This leads to the joint posterior

\displaystyle d\Pi_{n}(\beta,g,Z_{1},...,Z_{n}|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})

\displaystyle\quad\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\mathbf{1}\{Z_{i}\geq 0\}^{Y_{i}}\mathbf{1}\{Z_{i}<0\}^{1-Y_{i}}\phi(Z_{i}|x_{i}^{\prime}\beta,\exp(g(x_{i})))\right\}d\Pi(\beta,g),

(3)

where
z\mapsto\phi(z|\mu,\tau^{2})
is the probability density function of the normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\tau^{2})
. Gibbs sampling then proceeds by sequentially updating
\beta
,
g
, and
Z_{1},...,Z_{n}
. That is,

1.

Sample
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
from the conditional posterior
\Pi_{n}((Z_{1},...,Z_{n})\in\cdot|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta,g)
.

2.

Sample
\beta
from the conditional posterior
\Pi_{n}(\beta\in\cdot|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},g)
.

3.

Sample
g
from the conditional posterior
\Pi_{n}(g\in\cdot|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta)
.

This data augmentation strategy, due to
Albert and Chib (1993)
, is useful because, given
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the problem of learning
\beta
and
g
as a Gaussian linear model with nonparametric heteroskedasticity. Relative to
Albert and Chib (1993)
, the challenge is sampling from the conditional posterior of
g
given
\beta
and
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as the first two steps are identical to the parametric case except with transformed covariates
x_{i}\exp(-g(x_{i})/2)
.

Since data augmentation reduces Bayesian estimation of the heteroskedastic probit model to a Gaussian linear regression model with nonparametric heteroskedasticity, I adopt the approach from
Chib and Greenberg (2013)
to sample from the conditional posterior for
g
. Let

\displaystyle T(Z_{i},x_{i},\beta)=\log((Z_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime}\beta)^{2}),\quad i=1,...,n

for
i=1,...,n
. The latent Gaussian linear regression model can be transformed as

\displaystyle T(Z_{i},x_{i},\beta)=g(x_{i})+\nu_{i},\quad\nu_{i}|\beta,g\overset{ind}{\sim}\log\chi^{2}_{1},\quad i=1,...,n.

A Gaussian process is not a conjugate prior for a regression model with log chi-squared errors. Fortunately, the log chi-squared distribution is well-approximated by a
known
location-scale mixture of ten Gaussian distributions
(
Kim et al., 1998
;
Omori et al., 2007
)
. Consequently, I update the unknown function
g
using the approximating model

\displaystyle T(Z_{i},x_{i},\beta)=g(x_{i})+\nu_{i}^{*},\quad i=1,...,n,

where

\displaystyle\nu_{i}^{*}|\beta,g\overset{ind}{\sim}\sum_{j=1}^{10}\omega_{0,j}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0,j},\tau_{0,j}^{2}),\quad i=1,...,n

with
(\omega_{0,1},...,\omega_{0,10})^{\prime}
being a vector of probability weights,
(\mu_{0,1},...,\mu_{0,10})^{\prime}
being a vector of means, and
(\tau_{0,1}^{2},...,\tau_{0,10}^{2})^{\prime}
being a vector of variance parameters. The mixture parameters are
known
because the log chi-squared distribution does not depend on any unknown parameters.
5
5

5

See Table 1 of
Omori et al. (2007)
for the Gaussian mixture parameters.
Recognizing that the mixture model admits the hierarchical representation,

\displaystyle\nu_{i}^{*}|\beta,g,\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\overset{ind}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0,A_{i}},\tau_{0,A_{i}}^{2}),\quad A_{i}|\beta,g\overset{ind}{\sim}Categorical(\{\omega_{0,1},...,\omega_{0,10}\}),\quad i=1,...,n,

where
Categorical(\{p_{1},...,p_{10}\})
generically denotes a discrete probability distribution on
\{1,...,10\}
with probability weights
\{p_{1},...,p_{10}\}
, Bayesian updating of the heteroskedasticity parameter can be performed using the data-augmented conditional posterior

\displaystyle d\Pi_{n}(g,A_{1},...,A_{n}|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta,\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})

\displaystyle\quad\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{10}\left\{\omega_{0,j}^{\mathbf{1}\{A_{i}=j\}}\phi(T(Z_{i},x_{i},\beta)|\mu_{0,j}+g(x_{i}),\tau_{0,j}^{2})^{\mathbf{1}\{A_{i}=j\}}\right\}d\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(g).

(4)

This adds considerable tractability to the method because the mixture representation implies that the Gaussian process forms a conjugate prior, and, as a result, Bayesian updating of
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
requires sampling from a multivariate Gaussian distribution. Of course, this benefit follows from approximation. An inspection of the right panel of Figure 1 in
Omori et al. (2007)
shows that it is an excellent approximation, and, for that reason, I am not concerned about this discrepancy.

3.2
Steps of the Gibbs Sampler

This section describes a single iteration of a Gibbs sampler. That is, obtaining a new parameter vector
(\beta_{s+1},g_{s+1},\{Z_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n})
given a current parameter
(\beta_{s},g_{s},\{Z_{i,s}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i,s}\}_{i=1}^{n})
. Iterating this process until
s>S
for
S\geq 1
generates a sample
\{(\beta_{s},g_{s},\{Z_{i,s}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i,s}\}_{i=1}^{n})\}_{s=1}^{S}
from the posterior.

3.2.1
Step 1: Updating the Latent Variables.

The first stage is updating the latent variables
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
while holding
\beta,g
, and
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
fixed at their current value. From (
3
), the conditional posterior

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}\left((Z_{1},...,Z_{n})\in\cdot\middle|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta,g\right)

for the latent variables
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle d\Pi_{n}\left(Z_{1},...,Z_{n}\middle|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta,g\right)\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{Z_{i}>0\}^{Y_{i}}\mathbf{1}\{Z_{i}\leq 0\}^{(1-Y_{i})}\phi\left(Z_{i}|x_{i}^{\prime}\beta,\exp(g(x_{i}))\right),

which is the density kernel of a product of
n
independent truncated normal distributions. This implies that the vector of latent variables
(Z_{1,s+1},...,Z_{n,s+1})
is sampled according to the rule

\displaystyle Z_{i,s+1}|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta_{s},g_{s},\{A_{i,s}\}_{i=1}^{n}\overset{ind}{\sim}\begin{cases}\mathcal{N}_{[0,\infty)}\left(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta_{s},\exp(g_{s}(x_{i}))\right)\quad&\text{if $Y_{i}=1$}\\
\mathcal{N}_{(-\infty,0)}\left(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta_{s},\exp(g_{s}(x_{i})\right)\quad&\text{if $Y_{i}=0$}\end{cases},\quad i=1,...,n,

where
\mathcal{N}_{C}(\mu,\tau^{2})
denotes a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\tau^{2})
truncated to a set
C
.

3.2.2
Step 2: Updating the Index Coefficients.

The second stage is updating the index coefficients
\beta
while holding
g
,
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
fixed. From (
3
), the conditional posterior

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}(\beta\in\cdot|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},g,\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})

satisfies

\displaystyle d\Pi_{n}(\beta|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},g,\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi(Z_{i}|x_{i}^{\prime}\beta,\exp(g(x_{i})))d\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}(\beta).

This is Bayesian updating for a linear regression model with an unknown slope and known heteroskedasticity, a problem for which there exist priors in which it is simple to sample from the posterior. For example, an (improper) prior proportional to the product of the Lebesgue measure on
\Theta
and the point mass at
1
(due to scale normalization) means that samples from the conditional posterior of
\beta=(\theta^{\prime},1)^{\prime}
are drawn according to the rule

\displaystyle\theta_{s+1}|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{Z_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n},g_{s},\{A_{i,s}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\Theta}(\hat{\theta}_{n}(g_{s}),\hat{V}_{n}(g_{s})),

where

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{n}(g_{s})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp(-g_{s}(x_{i}))x_{i,-d_{x}}x_{i,-d_{x}}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp(-g_{s}(x_{i}))x_{i,-d_{x}}(Z_{i,s+1}-x_{i,d_{x}})

and

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{n}(g_{s})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp(-g_{s}(x_{i}))x_{i,-d_{x}}x_{i,-d_{x}}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}.

with
x_{i,-d_{x}}
being the subvector of
x_{i}
that excludes the last element
x_{i,d_{x}}
. This step and that of Section
3.2.1
are identical to the data-augmentation method for parametric probit models in
Albert and Chib (1993)
.

3.2.3
Step 3: Updating Mixture Assignments

The third stage is updating the mixture assignments
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
while holding
\beta
,
g
, and
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
fixed at their current values. The conditional posterior for the latent mixture assignments
(A_{1},...,A_{n})
implied by (
4
) is

\displaystyle d\Pi_{n}(A_{1},...,A_{n}|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta,\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},g)\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{10}\left\{\omega_{0,j}^{\mathbf{1}\{A_{i}=j\}}\phi(T(Z_{i},x_{i},\beta)|\mu_{0,j}+g(x_{i}),\tau_{0,j}^{2})^{\mathbf{1}\{A_{i}=j\}}\right\},

which implies that

\displaystyle A_{i,s+1}|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta_{s+1},\{Z_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n},g_{s}\overset{ind}{\sim}Categorical\left(\left\{\frac{\omega_{0,j}\phi(T(Z_{i,s+1},x_{i},\beta_{s+1})|\mu_{0,j}+g_{s}(x_{i}),\tau_{0,j}^{2})}{\sum_{k=1}^{10}\omega_{0,k}\phi(T(Z_{i,s+1},x_{i},\beta_{s+1})|\mu_{0,k}+g_{s}(x_{i}),\tau_{0,k}^{2})}\right\}_{j=1}^{10}\right)

for
i=1,...,n
, where
Categorical(\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{10})
denotes a discrete probability distribution on
\{1,...,10\}
with probability weights
\{p_{1},...,p_{10}\}
.

3.2.4
Step 4: Updating the Logarithm of the Conditional Variance

The final stage is updating the logarithm of the conditional variance
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
. From (
4
), the conditional posterior for
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
is given by

\displaystyle d\Pi_{n}((g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta,\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})

\displaystyle\quad\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\phi(T(Z_{i},x_{i},\beta)|\mu_{0,A_{i}}+g(x_{i}),\tau_{0,A_{i}}^{2})\right\}d\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(g).

Since
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}
is the law of a mean-zero Gaussian process, the conditional posterior for
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
implied by (
4
) is

\displaystyle(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta,\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{n}(\beta,\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}),V_{n}(\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})),

where

\displaystyle m_{n}(\beta,\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})=K_{n}(K_{n}+\Sigma_{n})^{-1}(T_{n}(\beta)-\mu_{0,n})

and

\displaystyle V_{n}(\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})=K_{n}-K_{n}(K_{n}+\Sigma_{n})^{-1}K_{n}

with
K_{n}=(\kappa(x_{i},x_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{n}
,
\Sigma_{n}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{0,A_{1}}^{2},...,\sigma_{0,A_{n}}^{2})
,
T_{n}(\beta)=(T(Z_{1},x_{1},\beta),...,T(Z_{n},x_{n},\beta))^{\prime}
, and
\mu_{0,n}=(\mu_{0,A_{1}},...,\mu_{0,A_{n}})^{\prime}
. These formulae arise from the conjugacy of Gaussian process priors regression models with normally distributed error terms
(
Rasmussen and Williams, 2006
)
. Consequently,

\displaystyle(g_{s+1}(x_{1}),...,g_{s+1}(x_{n}))^{\prime}|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta_{s+1},\{Z_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{n}(\beta_{s+1},\{A_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n}),V_{n}(\{A_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n})).

As such, the nuisance parameter updating only requires sampling from a multivariate Gaussian distribution. A caveat, however, is that the inversion of
K_{n}+\Sigma_{n}
typically scales at
O(n^{3})
. Fortunately, Gaussian process methods are regularly encountered in machine learning applications in which the sample
n
is very large, which in turn has led to the development of computational methods for large datasets (see Chapter 10 of
Rasmussen and Williams (2006)
for an introduction).

4
Extensions

4.1
Discrete Covariates

Some computational simplifications can arise when some of the elements of
X
are discrete. Suppose that
x=(x_{D}^{\prime},x_{C}^{\prime})^{\prime}
with
x_{D}\in\mathcal{X}_{D}
denoting the discrete variables and let
x_{C}\in\mathcal{X}_{C}
denoting the continuous variables. Moreover, let
\mathcal{X}_{D}=\{x_{1,D},....,x_{R,D}\}
with
R<\infty
. In such case,
g
can be written as

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{R}g(x_{r,D},x_{C})\mathbf{1}\{x_{D}=x_{r,D}\}.

Consequently, for any
i=1,...,n
, the log variance
g(x_{i})
admits the additive representation

\displaystyle g(x_{i})=\sum_{r=1}^{R}g_{r}(x_{i,C})\mathbf{1}\{x_{i,D}=x_{r,D}\},

meaning that the last step of the Gibbs sampler (i.e., that described in Section
3.2.4
) may be applied groupwise. This can be computationally advantageous. To see why, suppose that
g_{r}\overset{ind}{\sim}\Pi_{\mathcal{G}_{r}}
,
r=1,...,R
, where
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}_{r}}
is the law of a Gaussian process on
\mathcal{X}_{C}
and let
\mathcal{I}_{r}\subseteq\{1,...,n\}
be such that
x_{i,D}=x_{r,D}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}_{r}
. From (
4
), the conditional posterior of the log variance
g
is

\displaystyle d\Pi_{n}((g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta,\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})

\displaystyle\quad\propto\prod_{r=1}^{R}\left\{\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{r}}\phi(T(Z_{i},(x_{r,D}^{\prime},x_{i,C}^{\prime})^{\prime},\beta)|\mu_{0,A_{i}}+g_{r}(x_{i,C}),\tau_{0,A_{i}}^{2})d\Pi_{\mathcal{G}_{r}}(g_{r})\right\}.

Consequently, for
\beta
,
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
fixed, the functions
g_{1},...,g_{R}
are independent
under the posterior
. This is amenable to computation because 1. the inversion of the group level covariance matrices
K_{n_{r}}+\Sigma_{n_{r}}
is
O(n_{r}^{3})
, where
K_{n_{r}}=(\kappa_{r}(x_{i},x_{l}))_{i,l\in\mathcal{I}_{r}}
,
\Sigma_{n_{r}}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{0,A_{i}}^{2}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{r})
, and
n_{r}=|\mathcal{I}_{r}|
(as opposed to
O(n^{3})
when dealing with
K_{n}+\Sigma_{n}
), and 2. Step 4 (i.e., Section
3.2.4
) of the Gibbs sampler can be performed in parallel across
r
.

4.2
Predicition

Another reason for undertaking a structural analysis is
prediction
. That is, determine a future outcome
y^{*}
given a covariate value
x_{*}
. Following Section 3.3 of
Manski (1988)
, I cast prediction as a decision problem in which the utility from action
a\in[0,1]
in state
y^{*}\in\{0,1\}
is
u(a,y^{*})=-|y^{*}-a|
. The goal is selecting the action
a\in[0,1]
that maximizes utility. Since
y^{*}
is unknown, a Bayesian reports the action that maximizes expected utility under the
posterior predictive distribution
,

\displaystyle P_{n,\Pi}(Y=1|X=x_{*})=\int\Phi\left(x_{*}^{\prime}\beta\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}g(x_{*})\right)\right)d\Pi(\beta,g|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}).

For
u(a,y^{*})=-|y^{*}-a|
, it can be shown that the optimal action
a^{*}(\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
solves

\displaystyle\min_{a\in[0,1]}\left\{|1-a|P_{n,\Pi}(Y=1|X=x_{*})+|a|(1-P_{n,\Pi}(Y=1|X=x_{*}))\right\}

from which it can be seen that

\displaystyle a^{*}\left(\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{if $P_{n,\Pi}(Y=1|X=x_{*})\geq 1/2$}\\
0\quad&\text{if $P_{n,\Pi}(Y=1|X=x_{*})<1/2$}\end{cases}

In practice, a researcher with a simulated sample
\{(\beta_{s}^{\prime},g_{s}(x_{*}))^{\prime}\}_{s=1}^{S}
from the posterior computes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Phi\left(x_{*}^{\prime}\beta_{s}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}g_{s}(x_{*})\right)\right)

and reports
y_{S}^{*}=1
if this quantity is greater than or equal to
1/2
and reports
y_{S}^{*}=0
if this quantity is less than
1/2
. This differs from the frequentist decision rule from
Manski (1988)
in which
a=\mathbf{1}\{x_{*}^{\prime}\beta\geq 0\}
.

Since the Gibbs sampler in Section
3
only samples from the posterior for
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
, it is inadequate for out-of-sample predictions (i.e.,
x_{*}\notin\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
). For extrapolation, I expand the parameter to

\displaystyle\left(\beta^{\prime},\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}),g(x_{*})\right)^{\prime}

and update in blocks
\beta
,
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
\{g(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and
g(x_{*})
. This requires that the step of the Gibbs sampler from Section
3.2.4
be modified and that an additional step is introduced.

4.2.1
Modifying Section
3.2.4

Since
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
and
g(x_{*})
are correlated under the Gaussian process prior
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}
, Bayesian updating of
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
given
\beta
,
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and
g(x_{*})
uses the conditional distribution
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
given
g(x_{*})
under
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}
as the prior. Let
\kappa_{*}=\kappa(x_{*},x_{*})
,
\kappa_{n,*}=(\kappa(x_{1},x_{*}),...,\kappa(x_{n},x_{*}))^{\prime}
, and
K_{n}=(\kappa(x_{i},x_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{n}
. The conditional distribution of
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
given
g(x_{*})
under
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\kappa_{n,*}\kappa(x_{*},x_{*})^{-1}g(x_{*}),K_{n}-\kappa(x_{*},x_{*})^{-1}\kappa_{n,*}\kappa_{n,*}^{\prime}).

Letting

\displaystyle m_{n,*}=\kappa_{n,*}\kappa(x_{*},x_{*})^{-1}g(x_{*})

and

\displaystyle K_{n,*}=K_{n}-\kappa(x_{*},x_{*})^{-1}\kappa_{n,*}\kappa_{n,*}^{\prime},

it follows from standard properties of multivariate Gaussian distributions that

\displaystyle(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta,\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},g(x_{*})\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{n,*}(\beta,\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}),V_{n,*}(\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})),

where

\displaystyle m_{n,*}(\beta,\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})=\Sigma_{n}\left(K_{n,*}+\Sigma_{n}\right)^{-1}m_{n,*}+K_{n,*}\left(K_{n,*}+\Sigma_{n}\right)^{-1}(T_{n}(\beta)-\mu_{0,n})

and

\displaystyle V_{n,*}(\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})=K_{n,*}-K_{n,*}(K_{n,*}+\Sigma_{n})^{-1}K_{n,*}.

Consequently, the stage of the Gibbs sampler described in Section
3.2.4
is modified to

\displaystyle(g_{s+1}(x_{1}),...,g_{s+1}(x_{n}))^{\prime}|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta_{s+1},\{Z_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n},g_{s}(x_{*})

\displaystyle\quad\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{n,*}(\beta_{s+1},\{A_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n}),V_{n,*}(\{A_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n})).

This is similar to the step outlined in Section
3.2.4
, however, the mean
m_{n,*}(\beta,\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
and variance
V_{n,*}(\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
are modified to account for the fact
g(x_{*})
is correlated with
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
under
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}
.

4.2.2
New Step

The new step is updating
g(x_{*})
while holding
\beta
,
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and
\{g(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
fixed. Since
g(x_{*})
does not enter the likelihood function, it follows that

\displaystyle d\Pi(g(x_{*})|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta,\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{g(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n})\propto d\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(g(x_{*})|g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))

Consequently, standard properties of multivariate Gaussian distributions imply

\displaystyle g(x_{*})|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta,\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{g(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\kappa_{n,*}^{\prime}K_{n}^{-1}g_{n},\kappa(x_{*},x_{*})-\kappa_{n,*}^{\prime}K_{n}^{-1}\kappa_{n,*}),

where
g_{n}=(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
. This implies that sampling the out-of-sample log conditional variance
g_{s+1}(x_{*})
is performed using the following rule

\displaystyle g_{s+1}(x_{*})|\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\beta_{s+1},\{Z_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{i,s+1}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{g_{s+1}(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\quad\sim\mathcal{N}(\kappa_{n,*}^{\prime}K_{n}^{-1}g_{n,s+1},\kappa(x_{*},x_{*})-\kappa_{n,*}^{\prime}K_{n}^{-1}\kappa_{n,*}).

Intuitively, the sample path properties of the Gaussian process enable information from the collection
\{g(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
to inform
g(x_{*})
(i.e., there is an extrapolation via smoothness).

5
Simulation

I present a small-scale simulation study to illustrate some finite-sample properties of the posterior.

Data Generating Process.

The simulation data-generating process is based on a design from
Horowitz (1992)
. Specifically, data is generated according to the rule

Y_{i}=\mathbf{1}\{X_{i1}+\theta X_{i2}\geq U_{i}\},\quad i=1,...,n,

where
\theta=1
,
X_{i1}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
X_{i2}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(1,1)
, and

U_{i}=0.25(1+2(X_{i1}+X_{i2})^{2}+(X_{i1}+X_{i2})^{4})V_{i}

with
V_{i}|\{(X_{i1},X_{i2})\}_{i=1}^{n}\overset{ind}{\sim}logistic
with median zero and variance equal to one. I generate one thousand independent datasets of size
n
according to this data-generating process for
n\in\{250,500\}
.

Prior.

The prior for
\theta
is an improper prior proportional to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
,
g
is assumed to be drawn from a centered Matérn Gaussian process, and
\theta\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}g
under the prior. The Matérn Gaussian process has the defining property that, for any realized covariates
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the vector of function values
(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}
follows a Gaussian distribution

\displaystyle(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{n}))^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,K_{n}),

where the covariance matrix
K_{n}=(\kappa(x_{i},x_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{n}
has elements

\displaystyle\kappa(x_{i},x_{j})=\frac{2^{1-\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\alpha}||x_{i}-x_{j}||_{2}}{l}\right)K_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\alpha}||x_{i}-x_{j}||_{2}}{l}\right),

for
i,j\in\{1,...,n\}
. Here,
\alpha>0
is a smoothness parameter,
l>0
is a length-scale parameter,
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function, and
K_{\alpha}
is a modified Bessel function of the second kind of order
\alpha
. In the simulation, I set
l=1
and choose
\alpha\in\{1/2,3/2,5/2,7/2\}
to evaluate how prior smoothness affects the procedure. Figure
1
illustrates how
\alpha
governs the smoothness properties of the process by plotting sample paths of a Matérn process defined over the interval
[-3,3]
for regularity parameter
\alpha\in\{1/2,3/2,5/2,7/2\}
. More precisely, it can be shown that the sample paths
g
are
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
-differentiable in mean-square, the Matérn Gaussian process with
\alpha=1/2
and one-dimensional
\mathcal{X}
is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process, and the
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
limit is the Gaussian process with the squared exponential covariance function, a process with analytic sample paths.

6
Conclusion

This paper presents a Bayesian inference procedure for the maximum score problem. The key idea is that a semiparametric binary choice model subject to a median independence restriction is observationally equivalent to a probit model with nonparametric heteroskedasticity. Consequently, a computationally attractive Bayesian inference procedure can be derived from Gibbs sampling techniques developed in
Albert and Chib (1993)
and
Chib and Greenberg (2013)
. Establishing formal asymptotic properties of the posterior for the index coefficients
\beta
(i.e., posterior consistency, rates of convergence, and frequentist uncertainty quantification guarantees) is work in progress.

Appendix A
Proofs

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The proof has two steps. In Step 1, I show that
\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{S}}
. In Step 2, I show that
\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}}
. By double inclusion, these two steps establish the claim.

Step 1.

Let
P_{Y|X}^{(n)}
be an arbitrary element
\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}}
. Let
i\in\{1,...,n\}
be fixed arbitrarily. If
x_{i}^{\prime}\beta\neq 0
, then
Q(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta|x_{i})=\Phi\left(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta/\sigma(x_{i})\right)
with
\sigma(x_{i})>0
given by
\sigma(x_{i})=x_{i}^{\prime}\beta/\Phi^{-1}(Q(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta|x_{i}))
. The positivity claim holds because Assumption
1
implies
\text{sign}(\Phi^{-1}(Q(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta|x_{i})))=\text{sign}(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta)
. If
x_{i}^{\prime}\beta=0
, then
Q(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta|x_{i})=\Phi(x_{i}^{\prime}\beta/\sigma(x_{i}))
with
\sigma(x_{i})=1
. This follows from Assumption
1
and
\Phi(0)=0.5
. Repeating this argument for all
i\in\{1,...,n\}
, it follows that there exists
\tilde{P}_{Y|X}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{S}}
such that
P_{Y|X}^{(n)}=\tilde{P}_{Y|X}^{(n)}
. Since I fixed
P_{Y|X}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}}
arbitrarily, it follows that
\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{S}}
.

Step 2.

Let
\tilde{P}_{Y|X}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{S}}
be fixed arbitrarily. The random vector
(\tilde{U}_{1},...,\tilde{U}_{n})
with
\tilde{U}_{i}=\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}/\sigma(x_{i})
with
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}|\beta,\sigma\overset{ind}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for
i=1,...,n
and
\sigma:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{++}
satisfies Assumption
1
. Consequently, it follows that
\tilde{P}_{Y|X}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}}
. Since I fixed
\tilde{P}_{Y|X}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{S}}
arbitrarily, it follows that
\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n,\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{X}}}
.
∎
