Microplastics induce liver inflammation in cattle through the rumen microbiota-gut-liver axis
Authors: Zhiqing Li, Yu Ge, Xinwei Ma, Shuyu Jiang, Zan Liang, Lei Liu, Yongle Luo, Chunqiu Xia, Hu Liu, Qianglin Liu, Jishan An, Zuo Wang, Xinyi Lan, Anwei Cheng, Weijun Shen, Fachun Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicroplastics (MP) pollution is widespread in livestock farming environments. Exposure to MP can impair the gastrointestinal barrier, alter the structure and metabolism of the microbiota, and subsequently lead to organ damage. MP not only hinder cattle farming but also enter the food chain, posing a potential risk. Polyethylene (PE), a type of MP commonly detected in ruminant feed, has not yet been studied for its specific effects on cattle. Using calves as an animal model, this study investigates how exposure to MP induces toxicity via the rumen microbiota-gut-liver axis.ResultsExposure to MP impaired weight gain and liver development in cattle, altered liver tissue pathology, increased blood lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels, and triggered a systemic inflammatory response, identifying the liver as the primary target organ. Inflammation was closely associated with the dysbiosis of rumen microbiota and metabolites. MP exposure also damages the barrier integrity of the rumen, jejunum, and colon. The underlying mechanism involves MP altering the rumen microbial composition, which in turn triggers metabolic disorders, activates LPS synthesis pathways, and inhibits tight junction protein expression in the jejunum and colon. Although MP do not cause significant architectural damage to muscle tissue, they disrupt lipid homeostasis and nutrient composition, thereby promoting the deposition of pro-inflammatory LPS within muscle tissue. Rumen fluid metabolomics analysis revealed that differential metabolites were mainly enriched in the ATP-binding cassette transporter (ABC) pathway, with 4-fluoro-3-phenoxybenzoic acid and isovalerylglutamic acid being significantly correlated with levels of LPS, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β. Notably, the concurrent increase in TNF-α and LPS in both the bloodstream and liver, alongside altered blood metabolomics, indicates that MP induce hepatic damage by disrupting the rumen microbiota-gut-liver axis. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that liver inflammatory injury was closely associated with NF-κB activation. Further mechanistic analysis supported the central role of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway.ConclusionsMP impair liver function in cattle by disrupting the rumen microbiota-gut-liver axis. This process involves the perturbation of rumen flora and intestinal barriers, triggering LPS translocation into the bloodstream, and ultimately causing liver damage. Introduction

Microplastics (MP) pollution in agricultural environments has emerged as a significant global concern [1]. Due to their small size and chemical stability, MP can accumulate in plants and animals, entering the food chain and ultimately posing risks to human health [2, 3]. MP have been detected in organisms across nearly all trophic levels [4, 5]. During cattle feeding, the use of silage wrapping film, concentrate woven bags, and plastic equipment (such as plastic gloves, plastic buckets, plastic waterproof tarpaulins, plastic basins, and plastic water pipes) can release MP into the feed, with polyethylene (PE) being one of the most widely used plastics in agriculture and also prevalent in animal feed [6]. MP contamination of feed, combined with the use of plastic products, is a major source of MP exposure in dairy farming [7]. A recent pilot study in Italy reported detectable MP in 100% of ryegrass hay samples, with an average concentration of 39,300 ± 7020 particles per kg of dry matter [8]. Similarly, a study in India found high levels of microplastic contamination in all samples of dairy cow feed [9]. A Spanish study detected MP in 92% of sheep fecal samples, with an average of 997 ± 971 particles per kg of dry feces [10], while a US study found MP in 41% of cattle fecal samples [11]. These findings confirm that MP can be ingested by ruminants through contaminated feed and transported through the rumen-gastrointestinal tract. Ruminants such as cattle and dairy cows, characterized by longer production cycles and reliance on roughage like crop straw, are particularly vulnerable to MP exposure. Therefore, the potential risks of MP to cattle health warrant urgent attention. As a rapidly proliferating and persistent pollutant, MP have been shown to significantly impact gut microbiota across a spectrum of animal species [12]. Critically, the escalating MP pollution in the livestock industry raises serious concerns regarding the safety of livestock products and human health. The intestine is the primary organ defending against oral MP exposure, playing a crucial role in regulating immune, endocrine, and digestive functions [13]. Studies have shown that MP exposure impairs the intestinal barrier, exacerbates inflammation [14], and alters the composition of intestinal microbiota and metabolites, promoting the proliferation of pathogenic and opportunistic microbes [15, 16]. Intestinal microbes play a pivotal role in maintaining liver physiological functions, including bile acid metabolism and immune regulation [17–19]. The gut-liver axis is a bidirectional system conveying intestinal products to the liver via the portal vein, thereby regulating hepatic metabolism and immune responses [20]. Impairment of intestinal barrier can lead to the translocation of microbes and their products (such as LPS) along the gut-liver axis, resulting in liver injury [21]. MP are known to exert toxic effects in various organs, including the liver, kidneys, and lungs [22–24]. As the central metabolic and immune organ, the liver is a primary target of MP, which can disrupt lipid metabolism, trigger liver inflammation, promote tissue fibrosis, and induce cell apoptosis [25–27]. Although MP have been shown to alter gut microbiota and hepatic function, and gastrointestinal-derived LPS is implicated as a key factor of hepatic injury [28], direct evidence linking MP induced LPS translocation to liver damage remains lacking.

Cattle have a unique digestive system characterized by the rumen [29]. Consequently, their digestive processing of MP differs fundamentally from that of other mammals such as mice [22–24] and fish [16]. The dense microbial population in the rumen maintains a symbiotic relationship with the host and plays a crucial role in regulating metabolism and physiological functions [30, 31]. However, it remains unreported whether MP disrupt the rumen or intestinal barrier, leading to microbial dysbiosis and subsequent metabolite translocation to the liver. The gut-liver axis facilitates bidirectional communication via the portal circulation and bile ducts, involving complex endocrine and immune interactions [20]. Although MP exposure is known to induce hepatitis and disrupt lipid metabolism [25], the potential role of the rumen-gut-liver axis remains unclear. Given this close interconnection, ruminal microorganisms may represent critical mediators in MP induced liver injury.

As a major global source of meat and dairy products, cattle are fundamental to food security and social development [30]. However, MP pollution in feed and breeding environments presents a significant challenge to the cattle industry and the safety of meat products [6, 7]. It is worth noting that current research on MP has largely ignored their impact on cattle growth and beef quality safety. Furthermore, no research has explored the mechanism by which MP affect cattle damage through the rumen microbiota-gut-liver axis. This study integrated metagenomics, metabolomics, and transcriptomics to systematically investigate, for the first time, the effects of MP exposure on cattle growth performance, liver damage, and meat product safety, providing new insights into the toxic effects caused by MP pollution. We hypothesized that MP may cause harm to calves by disrupting rumen microbiota and metabolism. This study aims to provide theoretical support for sustainable global animal husbandry, MP pollution mitigation, and livestock product safety, while also offering a reference for assessing the potential human health risks associated with MP pollution in cattle production.

Materials and methods

Chemical reagents

Based on the test results of MP in feed samples, the MP used in this study were pure PE, with a particle size range of 20–200 μm and spherical or irregular shapes (Fig. 1a–b). MP were purchased from Dongguan Yujian Plastic Raw Materials Co., Ltd. (Dongguan, China). The following enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were provided from Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China): D-lactic acid (D-LA, MM-51329O2), diamine oxidase (DAO, MM-50891O2); β-glucosidase (β-Glu, MM-51466O2), cellulase (CE, MM-51391O2), hemicellulase (HCE, MM-51424O2), and pepsin (PP, MM-51120O2); interleukin-1β (IL-1β, MM-36949O2), interleukin-6 (IL-6, MM-34736O2), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, MM-1568O2), and lipopolysaccharide (LPS, MM-1114O2). Total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, ADS-W-KY005-48), superoxide dismutase (SOD, ADS-W-KY011-48), catalase (CAT, ADS-W-KY002-48), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, ADS-W-G003-48), and malondialdehyde (MDA, ADS-W-YH002-48) were provided by Jiangsu Adisheng Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Experimental procedure

The experiment was conducted at Aux Fresh Seed Technology Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). Fourteen healthy male Holstein calves of similar age (25 ± 5 days old) were selected and housed individually in a newly built spray-disinfected barn. After a 7-day acclimatization period, during which the calves received deworming, vaccination, and routine veterinary care, all calves (average weight 60 ± 1.4 kg) were randomly assigned to two groups: control (CON, n = 7) and MPs treatment (MPs, n = 7). Each group was placed in a separate new enclosure in the same barn, with each calf having its own individual cage. To prevent cross-contamination during the experiment, an empty enclosure was left between the two experimental enclosures. Calves in the CON group were fed whole milk and starter pellets. Calves in the MPs group received MP via whole milk at a concentration of 75 mg/L, with each calf consuming 14 L of milk per day, resulting in an exposure dose of 17.5 mg/kg body weight per day. The dosage was selected based on the previous studies [32–35]. Whole milk was sourced from Shandong Aux Fresh Seed Technology Co., Ltd., and starter pellets were supplied by Jiangsu Jiahui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Table S1 presents the nutritional composition of the calves’ diets. The calves were fed twice daily (07:00 and 17:00) with whole milk (7 L) and pelleted feed (ad libitum feeding). The remaining feed was collected and weighed daily. The calves’ body weight was recorded throughout the 40-day experimental period.

Sample collection

One day prior to the experiment, the body size and weight of each calf were recorded. On day 40, a 7-mL blood sample was collected from jugular venipuncture of each calf before morning feeding. A 2-mL aliquot was transferred into a blood collection tube, gently inverted to mix, and immediately snap-frozen on dry ice in an insulated container for hematological analysis. The remaining 5-mL blood sample was centrifuged (4000 rpm) for 15 min at 4 °C, and the separated plasma was stored at − 80 °C for subsequent testing. On day 41, calves were fasted for 12 h prior to euthanasia via jugular vein exsanguination. After dissection, the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were collected, rinsed with physiological saline, and weighed. Samples of the longest back muscle and liver tissue were also collected. Liver samples were obtained from the mid-parenchymal region of the right hepatic lobe in all animals. The ileum was excised from the abdominal cavity, and its midsection was harvested. The midsection of the colon was then dissected for contents collection. Finally, the rumen was opened to collect its contents. Epithelial tissue was then harvested from the rumen sac and immediately rinsed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline. One epithelial sample, each from the rumen, ileum, and colon, was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The remaining epithelial samples were cut into small pieces, aliquoted into 5-mL DNA-enzyme-free cryopreservation tubes, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis. In addition, at the beginning of the experimental design, approximately 1000 g of wrapped corn silage samples was collected from three locations (Youzhuo Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd., Hunan Institute of Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Science, and Shuntian Henghe Agro-Tech Development Co., Ltd.). To ensure sample representativeness for subsequent analysis of MP type and particle sizes, the four-part method was employed during collection, with samples placed in kraft paper bags.

Ruminal fermentation parameters analysis

Rumen volatile fatty acid (VFA) profile was analyzed using gas chromatograph (GC-8600, Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with a DB-WAX UI column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm); following the method of Wang et al. [36], ammonia nitrogen (NH₃-N) was quantified according to the protocol of Broderick et al. [37], primarily using a microplate spectrophotometer (Multiskan SkyHigh, Thermo Fisher Scientific, China).

Muscle biochemical characteristics

Following a 12-h fasting period, calves were humanely slaughtered at a commercial facility in accordance with standard procedures [38]. At 45 min postmortem, the pH of the longissimus dorsi muscle was measured at three distinct sites using a portable pH meter (Testo 205, Testo AG, Schwarzwald, Germany) equipped with a penetration probe. Muscle color was evaluated under the CIELAB system, capturing lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*) using a D65 standard light source, 5 cm aperture, and 10° standard observer angle. Cooking loss was determined according to Soglia et al. [39]. Standardized muscle blocks (C2 segment; 8 × 4 × 3 cm) were vacuum-sealed individually and immersed in an 80 °C water bath for 45 min until core temperature equilibrated, then cooled, gently blotted, and reweighed. Cooking loss was expressed as the percentage weight reduction relative to the initial weight.

Analysis of muscle nutrient and fatty acid composition

Following freeze-drying and removal of external connective tissues, muscle samples were analyzed for nutrient composition. Dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), ether extract (EE), and ash content were determined according to the standard methods of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 2000) [40]. EE was quantified using a Soxhlet apparatus with petroleum ether. CP was analyzed via the Dumas combustion method (D60; Shandong Hannong).

For fatty acid analysis, duplicate portions of freeze-dried muscle powder were subjected to direct methylation to obtain fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs)36. Briefly, 5.0 g of sample was homogenized in a chloroform–methanol (1:2, v/v) at 8000 rpm for 5 min. After 1 h of equilibration, the extract was filtered and mixed with 50 mL of 0.88% KCl solution. The lower phase was collected using a separatory funnel and vacuum-evaporated at 37 °C to yield total lipids. Lipid extracts (200 μL) were saponified with 2 mL of 0.5 mol/L NaOH in methanol at 65 °C for 30 min, followed by methylation with 2 mL of 14% BF3 (boron trifluoride) in methanol at 70 °C for 15 min. After cooling, 2 mL of cyclohexane was added, vortexed, and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min. A 1.5 mL aliquot of the upper phase was transferred to a vial for gas chromatography (GC) analysis. FAMEs were separated using a GC (8860C, Agilent Technologies) equipped with an HP-88 column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.20 μm). The injection volume was 1 μL, with nitrogen as carrier gas. The oven program was initial 100 °C for 13 min, ramp to 180 °C at 10 °C/min (hold 6 min), then to 200 °C at 1 °C/min, and finally to 240 °C at 20 °C/min. The detector temperature was maintained at 250 °C. FAMEs were identified by comparing retention times with authentic standards and quantified as a percentage of total FAMEs by mass, using methyl eicosanoate (C23:0) as an internal standard.

MP detection

MP in liver and feed samples were analyzed using the Agilent 8700 LDIR (Laser Direct Infrared Imaging) system. The liver is the organ most closely associated with toxicity induced by MP through gastrointestinal exposure. In this study, three liver tissue samples from the MPs group of calves were randomly selected, and a precisely weighed 1.00 g aliquot from each sample was placed into glass beakers for MP detection, aiming to provide an exploratory and qualitative assessment of MP accumulation in the liver. Lipids were removed by refluxing with ethanol at 60°C. After complete ethanol evaporation, concentrated nitric acid (68%, three times the sample volume) was added. The digestion was carried out at room temperature for 12 h, followed by heating at 60 °C on a graphite heating plate for an additional 3 h to fully degrade proteins. The resulting digest was vacuum-filtered, and the filter membrane was washed multiple times with ultrapure water and ethanol. The membrane was subjected to ultrasonic treatment in an ethanol solution to disperse residual particles. Finally, the filter membrane was rinsed again with ethanol, and the ethanol solution was collected and concentrated [41]. For silage feed samples, a precisely weighed aliquot (5 g) was dried at 60 °C, ground, and passed through a 500-μm sieve. A sodium hydroxide solution was added, and the sample was digested in a water bath to break down the organic matter. If needed, large particles were prefiltered before vacuum filtration. The filter membrane was washed multiple times with ultrapure water and ethanol and then treated in an ethanol solution under ultrasonic conditions to transfer retained substances into the ethanol solution. Finally, the membrane was removed, rinsed with ethanol, and all ethanol eluates were combined and concentrated.

The concentrated solution was dropped onto high-reflective Kavi glass slides (75 × 25 mm, Kavi Technology, OH, USA). After complete drying, chemical imaging analysis was conducted using the Agilent 8700 LDIR system, and the data were analyzed using Agilent Clarity software (version 1.3.9). The particle analysis mode of the MP reference library was applied, with infrared scanning set to 1799 cm−1 for particle localization. Automated detection was set with a spectral match threshold > 0.65 and a particle size range of 20–500 μm. Mid-infrared spectra (1800–975 cm−1) of each particle were collected and matched against a commercial MP database (Shanghai Weipu Detection Technology Group Co., Ltd.) for polymer identification, using a mapping ratio threshold > 0.65 [42]. Additionally, MP samples were vacuum-dried, mounted on sample holders for surface morphology, and microstructure observation using a scanning electron microscope (FENSEM8500, Agilent Technologies, Germany). To assess surface charge and dispersion stability, MP particles were dispersed in a 1 mM KCl electrolyte solution, and their electrophoretic mobility was measured after concentration adjustment.

Histopathological examination

Liver, muscle, rumen, ileum, and colon tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathological analysis. The fixed specimens were dehydrated by gradient ethanol, cleared with xylene, and embedded using an embedding machine (JB-P5, Wuhan Junjie Electronics Co., Ltd., China). Embedded tissues were sectioned into 4-μm slices using a paraffin sectioning machine (RM2016, Leica Instruments Co., Ltd., China). The resulting slices were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Subsequently, slices were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under an upright optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100, Nikon, Japan).

Hematological analysis

Complete blood parameters and liver function markers, including total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLOB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total bile acids (TBA), total bilirubin (TBIL), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), were analyzed using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (BK-280, Shandong Boke Bio-Industry Co., Ltd., Shandong, China). The concentrations of D-LA, DAO, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were measured by ELISA. LPS concentrations in serum, liver, and muscle samples were quantified using a commercially available ELISA kit. The assay is based on the specific binding of capture and detection antibodies, followed by signal generation using an enzyme-linked secondary antibody and a colorimetric substrate. Quantification was performed by comparison to a standard curve constructed with serial dilutions of purified LPS standards, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Rumen digestive enzyme activities, specifically β-Glu, CE, HCE, and PP, were also measured. Additionally, T-AOC, SOD, CAT, GSH-Px, and MDA were assessed to determine antioxidant activity and oxidative stress. All assays conducted with commercial kits were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA extraction and RT‑qPCR

Total RNA from rumen, intestinal (ileum and colon), and liver tissues was extracted using the Steady Pure RNA extraction kit (column membrane purification method; AG21024, Hunan Accurate Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Changsha, China). RNA concentration and purity were quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using 1.0% agarose-formaldehyde gel electrophoresis, and only samples showing distinct 28S and 18S rRNA bands with minimal smearing were used for further analyses. Primer sequences were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Primer sequences and expected amplicon sizes from published studies are provided in Tables S2–S5 with corresponding references. qRT-PCR was performed using a BioRad CFX-96 real-time PCR system with SYBR Green chemistry to quantify target genes and the reference gene (GAPDH). GAPDH was selected as the reference gene after confirming stable Ct values between CON and MPs groups across all tissues, with no significant inter-group difference (p > 0.05) and low intra-group variability (CV < 10%). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^ − ΔΔCT method.

Rumen fluid metagenomic sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from rumen fluid samples (0.2 g) using the OMEGA DNA kit (M5635–02; Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and its DNA concentration and quality were evaluated. Qualified DNA samples were subjected to whole-genome shotgun sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq X-ten platform (Illumina Inc., CA, USA) of Shanghai Personalbio Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Filtered reads were de novo assembled with Megahit (v1.0.6), and open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted from contigs > 300 bp using Metagene. Contigs were pooled and dereplicated with CD-HIT (95% sequence identity) to generate non-redundant gene sequences. Gene abundance in each sample was quantified and normalized to create a gene abundance table. The taxonomic composition was assessed through high-precision sequence alignment against the NCBI NR reference database, employing DIAMOND (v2.1.8) with optimized RefSeq parsing parameters. Multilevel taxonomic characterization spanned four biological hierarchies (domain, phylum, genus, and species) with subsequent computation of relative abundance indices. Beta-diversity patterns were visualized through principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity metrics at species-level distributions [43]. Functional profiling was performed against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (Release 110.0) through hierarchical homology searches, with rigorous normalization of gene counts [44].

Rumen fluid and blood metabolome sequencing

Metabolomic analysis of rumen fluid and blood samples was performed using ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry [45]. Sample aliquots (100 μL) were transferred into sterile microcentrifuge tubes using calibrated positive-displacement pipettes. Methanol (300 μL) and deuterated internal standard solution (DL-o-chlorophenylalanine, 10 μL of 2.8 mg/mL in 0.9% saline) were added under the controlled temperature condition (4 °C). Immediate vortex homogenization (30 s, 2500 rpm) was followed by a standardized incubation protocol: primary agitation (30 s orbital shaking at 1800 oscillations/min), static equilibration (60 min at − 20 °C), and phase separation through high-speed centrifugation (20 min, 12,000 rpm, 4 °C) using a refrigerated microcentrifuge. During mass spectrometry analysis, 100 μL of acetonitrile–water solution (1:1) was added, vortexed, and filtered (0.22 μm) after centrifugation for 15 min. The supernatant (300 μL) was aliquoted into tubes for detection. Data analysis was performed using the Genes Cloud platform (www.genescloud.cn). Orthogonal projections to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were employed for dimensionality reduction and group classification. Differential metabolites were identified based on statistical significance, with variable importance in projection (VIP) values > 1, |log2FC|≥ 1, and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p < 0.05. Enrichment analysis of metabolic pathways was subsequently performed based on the differential metabolites using the KEGG pathway database.

Liver transcriptomics analysis

A whole-genome shotgun strategy was employed to construct a library with a 400-bp insert size, which was subsequently sequenced using next-generation sequencing technology on the Illumina NovaseqPE150 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). To accurately quantify transcript expression, data were normalized according to transcript length, and gene expression levels were measured using fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped fragments. Data analysis was conducted using the Genes Cloud platform (www.genescloud.cn). Differentially expressed genes were identified using the criteria |log2FC|≥ 1 and FDR-adjusted p < 0.05. KEGG database annotation was used for gene enrichment and pathway analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SAS (version 9.2; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Independent samples t-test (Student’s t-test) was conducted to evaluate differences between the two treatment groups. GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used for graph generation. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships among metabolites, core microorganisms, and associated genes. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For high-dimensional omics data (metagenomics, metabolomics, transcriptomics), differentially abundant features (microbes, metabolites, genes) were identified using a threshold of |log2FC|≥ 1 and an adjusted p < 0.05 (false discovery rate, FDR).

Results

LDIR was employed to detect MP particles in corn silage samples, specifically evaluating the performance of PE-MP

Results indicated that PE accounting for 16% was the predominant polymer type of MP in wrapped corn silage samples. The particle sizes were primarily below 200 μm, with the highest abundance observed in the < 30 μm fraction. Therefore, PE particles with the 20–200 μm sizes range was selected as the experimental material (Fig. 1a–b, n = 3). Morphological characterization revealed that PE in corn silage samples have spherical and irregular shapes, closely resembling the physical forms of MP found in environmental samples (Fig. 1c). FTIR further confirmed the polymer type of PE as polyethylene (Fig. 1d). Additionally, the zeta potential of PE was measured to be +14.97 mV (Fig. 1e).

Effects of MPs on liver damage

This study examines the effects of feeding calves with MP-contaminated feed on livers, with a focus on LPS-induced injury. Histopathological analysis showed regular hepatocyte arrangement in the CON group, while the MPs group showed hepatocyte nuclear fragmentation or dissolution, punctate lymphocyte infiltration (red arrows), and a small amount of punctate necrosis (Fig. 2e, yellow arrow). To conduct an exploratory assessment of MP accumulation in the liver of calves, three liver tissue samples from the MPs group were randomly selected for MP detection. Under the conditions of this study, no PE-MP were detected in the liver tissue (Fig. 2f). LPS levels in liver and muscle tissue were significantly elevated in the MPs group (p = 0.03). The absence of detectable MP alongside elevated LPS suggests that the observed liver injury may not be due to direct MP accumulation but rather mediated by LPS-induced inflammation. To further assess liver damage, we analyzed blood biochemical markers. As shown in Fig. 2g–h, exposure to MP significantly increased LDH levels (p = 0.01) and decreased ALB levels (p = 0.036). Correspondingly, hepatic inflammatory markers including IL-6 and TNF-α were significantly upregulated (p < 0.05), consistent with LPS-mediated inflammation. These findings collectively indicate that MP exposure was associated with hepatic injury-related responses, coinciding with elevated LPS levels and inflammatory activation.

MP exposure compromises intestinal barrier integrity and induces inflammation

Given that hepatic and systemic LPS primarily originate from the gastrointestinal tract, this study examined pathological alterations in ruminal and intestinal tissues. H&E staining of tissue sections revealed varying degrees of intestinal injury in the MPs group (Fig. 3a–c). Histopathological examination revealed apparent damage in the ileum and colon of the MPs group, as characterized by significant inflammatory cell infiltration and congestion; in contrast, rumen damage was relatively mild. To assess intestinal permeability in cattle, serum concentrations of DAO and D-LA were measured. As shown in Fig. 3d, MP treatment significantly increased D-LA levels (p < 0.01). In addition, the mRNA expression levels related to intestinal barrier function were evaluated. The results showed that the MP exposure significantly downregulated mRNA expression of Occludin in the rumen, ZO-1 in the ileum (p < 0.01), and Claudin-1 in the colon (p < 0.05, Fig. 3e, g, and i). Notably, MP exposure elevated the levels of inflammatory markers of inflammatory markers IL-6 and TNF-α in the ileum and colon (p < 0.05; Fig. 3f, h). MP induced systemic inflammation while concurrently alter blood metabolism

This study concluded that MP disrupt the integrity of the intestinal barrier, facilitating the translocation of gut-derived LPS to the liver. However, it is unclear whether MP-induced intestinal injury leads to the accumulation of LPS in the blood and alters blood metabolism. Based on these observations, we hypothesize that MP-induced intestinal barrier injury allows LPS translocation into the bloodstream, thereby triggering systemic inflammation [46, 47]. As shown in Fig. 2g, MP exposure led to a decrease in ALB levels. Plasma samples were further subjected to ELISA and metabolomic analyses. Levels of inflammatory factors IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were measured by ELISA (Fig. 5a), indicating a significant increase in TNF-α levels in the MPs group (p = 0.04). Compared to the CON group, levels of CAT and MDA in plasma were significantly increased (p < 0.05) in the MPs group (Fig. 5b), whereas T-AOC and GSH-Px levels decreased (p = 0.04). Therefore, these findings support the hypothesis that intestinal barrier disruption permits LPS entry into the bloodstream, leading to liver injury via triggered metabolic disturbances. The changes in plasma metabolites were investigated using an untargeted metabolomics approach. The OPLS-DA score plot was visualized to assess the differences in metabolites between the two groups (Fig. 5c). Differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were identified using thresholds of |log₂FC|≥ 1 and an false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p < 0.05 (Fig. 5d). Compared with the CON group, a total of 65 DEMs were present in the MPs group, of which 39 were upregulated and 26 were downregulated. The heatmap of metabolite expression patterns showed a marked alteration in the MPs group relative to CON, indicating that MP induced changes in bovine serum metabolism (Fig. 5e). Further analysis of high-abundance DEMs revealed four metabolites that were significantly positively correlated with liver weight (Fig. 5f, h). Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the lysine degradation and cholesterol metabolism pathways were primarily enriched between the two groups (Fig. 5g). Next, correlation analysis was performed among the four DEMs, inflammatory factors, and blood-related indicators (Fig. 5i; table S6). The four DEMs showed a strong negative correlation with IL-6, and niacinamide was negatively correlated with IL-1β and TNF-α (p < 0.05), whereas some DEMs were positively correlated with blood-related indices (p < 0.05). This suggests that alterations in these metabolites may not be the primary drivers of the inflammatory response, a hypothesis that requires further functional validation. In summary, MP-induced blood inflammation and metabolic changes may be mediated by lysine degradation and cholesterol metabolism, potentially as downstream effects of LPS translocation, microbial dysbiosis, and altered metabolites. MP exposure induces alterations in the rumen microbiota composition

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying MP induced liver damage, this study examined alterations in the rumen microbiota and screened for potential microbial biomarkers. The impact of MPs on the composition of rumen microbiota in cattle was analyzed using metagenomic sequencing. The richness and diversity of rumen microbiota were assessed using the Alpha diversity index, with no difference between the two groups (Fig. 6a). PCoA revealed distinct differences in microbial community structure between the groups (Fig. 6b). Analysis at the phylum level identified dominant phyla, including Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, and Proteobacteria; however, there was no significant differences in their relative abundances (Fig. 6c). At the genus and species levels, the relative abundance of the Gram-negative genus Prevotella increased in the MPs group (Fig. 6d, e). Species-level analysis revealed significantly elevated abundances of Prevotella sp017627765, Prevotella sp900316565, and Ruminococcus sp900319195 flavefaciens (p < 0.05; Table S7). These findings suggest that MP exposure leads to distinct rumen microbial signatures, potentially contributing to elevated LPS production, supported by significantly increased LPS concentrations in the blood of calves in the MPs group (p = 0.02; Fig. 6j). LEfSe analysis showed that the MPs group harbored more differential species than CON group (17 vs. 7 species), with s_Prevotella sp017627765, p_Spirochaeta, o_Oscillospirales, and g_Treponema more abundant in MPs (Fig. 6f; Table S8). Changes in rumen microbial abundance and structure were accompanied by alterations in fermentation parameters and digestive enzyme activities. Compared to CON, ruminal acetic acid concentration significantly decreased in MPs (p = 0.02; Fig. 6g), while digestive enzyme activities showed no significant change (Fig. 6i). Previous studies show that certain Prevotella species associate with systemic inflammation [48, 49]. Consistent with this, the s_Prevotella sp900316565, s_Prevotella sp017627765, and s_Prevotella ruminicola_B of the Prevotella genus are positively correlated with inflammatory cytokine levels, and TNF-α is significantly associated with all three species. Moreover, s_Prevotella ruminicola_B and Prevotella sp017627765 are also positively correlated with LPS. Conversely, these species are negatively correlated with serum metabolites, suggesting that inflammation and liver injury may be caused by LPS increases due to rumen microbiota changes induced by MP (Fig. 6k). Discussion

Although MP contamination is prevalent in ruminant feed [8, 9], its potential impacts on the health and performance of large ruminants such as cattle remain incompletely understood. In the present study, multi-omics analyses were used to characterize biological responses associated with dietary MP exposure along the rumen microbiota-gut-liver axis. Our results showed that MP exposure was accompanied by reduced average daily feed intake and lower terminal body weight in cattle. Consistent with this observation, Kang et al. [50] reported that MP entering the animal body can interfere with nutrient utilization. In their study, growth was minimally affected during the early stages of exposure, whereas growth retardation and even weight loss were observed at later stages. These findings suggest that growth responses to MP exposure may vary with exposure duration and physiological adaptation. In this context, the lack of a significant difference in average daily gain observed in the present study may reflect a time-dependent and non-linear growth response during prolonged MP exposure, rather than a uniform inhibition of growth throughout the experimental period.

MP exposure was associated with alterations in the rumen microbiota and metabolism, coinciding with an increased relative abundance of Gram-negative bacteria and enrichment of the LPS biosynthesis pathway. These changes were accompanied by increased LPS production in rumen fluid, indicating a potential increase in the risk of LPS from the gastrointestinal tract transferring to the bloodstream, liver, and even muscles. The alterations in intramuscular fat content and fatty acid composition may be mechanistically linked to elevated LPS levels. LPS is known to activate TLR4 signaling, which can impair lipid metabolism by suppressing anabolic pathways while promoting catabolic responses [51].

Metabolomic analysis further revealed that differential metabolites in rumen fluid were closely associated with inflammatory responses, primarily enriched in the ABC transporter pathway. MP exposure was associated with elevated LPS levels and impaired hepatic development in cattle. Transcriptome sequencing indicated a strong association between hepatic inflammation and activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. The qPCR validation of differentially expressed genes confirmed that elevated LPS levels following MP exposure were accompanied by activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, characterized by upregulation of TLR4 expression, which in turn promoted downstream gene expression and induced hepatocyte apoptosis. Initially, this study hypothesized that the liver might be the primary site of MP accumulation. However, no PE-MP were detected in liver tissue under the detection conditions applied in this study, possibly due to their size and morphology [52].

The gastrointestinal tract microbiota and its metabolic activity in ruminants not only significantly influence host metabolism and growth, but also contribute to the pathogenesis of various organ diseases [53–56]. Studies have shown that once ingested, MP interact with the intestinal microbiota, altering its composition and functionality [57, 58]. Similarly, this study found that MP exposure altered the rumen microbiota, leading to an increase in the relative abundance of Gram-negative bacteria. Metagenomic analysis further revealed that MP exposure activated the LPS biosynthesis pathway in cattle, potentially representing a self-protective mechanism in Gram-negative bacteria. Previous studies have shown that MP exposure increases the relative abundance of Prevotella, induces amino acid metabolism disorders, and elevates circulating LPS levels [59–62]. Prevotella can produce LPS and activate the TLR4 receptor [63]. Gram-negative bacteria may exhibit stronger adaptability to MP. This adaptability may be attributed to the structural diversity of bacterial membrane interfaces, which has been linked to enhanced tolerance to MPs [64]. Studies have demonstrated that the unique LPS structure on the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria can mitigate the impact of MP by reducing membrane perturbations and shielding against toxic molecules, thereby offering bacterial protection [65–67]. After entering the rumen, MPs promote the proliferation of more adaptable Gram-negative bacteria, which are protected by the unique LPS structure in their outer membrane, leading to an increase in their relative abundance. The increased abundance of Gram-negative bacteria and elevation of LPS levels in the rumen exacerbate both ruminal and intestinal inflammation. This conclusion is supported by histopathological observations, changes in barrier-related gene expression, and elevated D-LA levels in blood. Moreover, correlation analysis revealed that these differential microbial taxa were significantly positively associated with LPS and pro-inflammatory markers. These findings suggest that MP-induced intestinal barrier disruption may be mediated by microbial dysbiosis and dysregulated metabolites. While these correlations support the hypothesis that MP-induced intestinal barrier disruption may be mediated by microbial perturbations, validation in larger cohorts is required due to the limited sample size and the inherent risk of overfitting in correlation networks with numerous variables.

Alterations in the structure and function of rumen microorganisms are often accompanied by changes in metabolite profiles. Identified 173 DEMs in the MPs group. The largest metabolite category comprised lipids and lipid-like molecules (24.1%). Spearman correlation analysis revealed that four high-abundance DEMs were significantly positively correlated with IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, as well as LPS and blood-related inflammatory markers. Isovalerylglutamic acid showed a significant positive correlation with LPS. Its production may reflect the enhanced metabolic activity in specific bacterial taxa (p_Spirochaetes, s_Prevotella), which may concurrently release LPS. In higher organisms, including mammals, ABC transporters are widely distributed in the intestine, blood–brain barrier, and placenta, mediating the transport of phospholipids, lipophilic drugs, cholesterol, and other small molecules. They play a crucial role in physiological substance transport as well as the elimination of exogenous toxins and metabolic waste [68]. Gram-negative bacteria protect themselves from MP induced stress through their outer membrane architecture. This adaptive response facilitates increased LPS production. However, MP exposure was associated with changes in intestinal barrier–related gene expression, which may permit increased LPS entry into systemic circulation. Metabolomic analysis revealed that MP exposure is closely associated with altered serum metabolism, particularly involving isovalerylglutamic acid as a potential biomarker significantly correlated with increased LPS levels, which may further exacerbate the inflammatory response.

Microbial metabolites have been shown to modulate the physiological functions of internal organs in the host, particularly in regulating metabolic and immune processes [69]. Compromise of the intestinal barrier can allow microbe-derived LPS and related molecules to translocate via the bloodstream to the liver, potentially causing hepatic inflammation. Elevated serum levels of leukocytes, TNF-α, and LPS indicate systemic inflammation. Blood metabolomic analysis revealed enrichment of amino acid metabolic pathways, with glycine anhydride identified as a key metabolite. Moreover, MP exposure reduced the serum levels of glycine anhydride and niacinamide in cattle. Amino acid metabolism plays a crucial role in regulating inflammatory responses by modulating the synthesis and release of cytokines [70, 71]. Inflammation increases metabolic demands in immune cells, including macrophages and T cells, necessitating higher amino acid levels for energy production and protein synthesis. Under such conditions, reduced amino acid metabolism may impair immune cell function, compromising their activity and immune response efficacy [72, 73]. Niacinamide, a precursor of NAD and NADP, is involved in energy and lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and the regulation of inflammation [74, 75]. Ruminants acquire niacinamide (NAM) through microbial synthesis in the rumen, with its yield influenced by the rumen environment and dietary composition [76]. NAM modulates immune cell activity and the expression of inflammatory mediators [77, 78]. In LPS-induced inflammatory models, NAM suppresses proinflammatory cytokine expression and enhances antioxidant enzyme activity [79]. Additionally, NAM inhibits the NF-κB signaling pathway to modulate cytokine expression, contributing to its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [80]. In our study, MP exposure reduced serum levels of glycine anhydride and niacinamide in cattle, impairing immune responses, as evidenced by elevated serum TNF-α levels. Overall, our findings suggest that MP elevate blood LPS levels, which mediates metabolite alterations underlying inflammation. Specifically, glycine anhydride and niacinamide appear to contribute to systemic inflammation and hepatic injury.

Multi-omics approaches are highly suitable for assessing tissue and organ function, as well as metabolic status [81]. Transcriptomic analysis was employed to evaluate MP induced liver injury in cattle comprehensively. Given that the liver is the first organ to receive blood from the portal vein, it is directly exposed to intestinal microbial metabolites [82]. In inflammation research, KEGG-GSEA is effective for identifying enriched signaling pathways and revealing key disease mechanisms. The results revealed significant enrichment of the NF-κB signaling pathway in the liver transcriptome. Integrated with metagenomic analysis of the rumen microbiota, MP exposure significantly altered microbial composition and function, activated the LPS biosynthesis pathway, and affected pathways related to infectious diseases and immune responses. These findings support the view that LPS is a central mediator of hepatic inflammation [83]. Although previous studies have reported the intestinal and hepatic toxicity of MP, it remains unclear whether liver damage is mediated by intestinal microbes or their metabolites. Given the gut-liver axis, intestinal microbiota and their metabolites may play a critical role in MP induced hepatic injury. Antibiotic depletion and fecal microbiota transplantation have supported that MP induce colonic inflammation and disrupt the intestinal barrier, facilitating translocation of LPS into circulation, which subsequently triggers systemic inflammation and hepatic injury [62]. Previous studies have documented that LPS produced by rumen-derived bacteria can induce hepatic injury in ruminants [84, 85]. Moreover, due to the unique gastrointestinal structure of cattle, the microbial mechanisms underlying MP induced liver injury remain largely unexplored. Compared to the multilayered squamous epithelium of the rumen, the simple columnar epithelium of the small and large intestines is more likely to transfer bacteria and their metabolites to the liver, promoting hepatic injury [86, 87]. Accordingly, the expression of tight junction proteins in the small and large intestines of cattle was assessed, showing a moderate reduction in their mRNA levels. Approximately 60–80% of somatic infections are associated with biofilm formation [88]. Disruption of the intestinal epithelium facilitates microbial translocation and the development of pathogenic biofilms. This study suggest that MP-induced dysbiosis of the rumen microbiota disrupts the integrity of intestinal barrier, potentially facilitating LPS translocation [89]. In circulation, LPS first binds to LPS-binding protein (LBP), then associates with CD14 on immune cells to form an LPS–LBP–CD14 complex. This complex subsequently activates the TLR4–MD2 signaling complex, initiating inflammatory responses such as rumenitis and hepatitis [90–93]. Consistent with this mechanism, calves exposed to MP showed upregulated hepatic expression of genes associated with the NF-κB pathway, including TLR4, TNF-α, IL-1β, and NFκB.

TLR4 is a key member of the Toll-like receptor (TLR) family, central to regulating the transcription of inflammatory cytokines and the expression of apoptotic proteins during inflammatory responses [94]. NF-κB, acts as a downstream effector of TLR4, translocate to the nucleus upon activation to modulates proinflammatory and immune responses [95]. Zhang et al. demonstrated in a carp MP exposure model that MP exposure significantly upregulates TLR4 pathway components, and that NF-κB activation and expression of inflammation-related genes positively correlate with exposure dose [96]. Fan et al. further supported that MP induce liver damage in mice via the NF-κB signaling pathway [97]. In the present study, MP exposure upregulated hepatic expression of TLR4, TNF-α, IL-1β, and NF-κB, indicating activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling cascade in the liver. Histopathological analysis together with increased hepatic IL-1β and TNF-α expression further support the involvement of NF-κB signaling in MP induced liver inflammation.

This study focused on exploring the mechanisms underlying liver injury in cattle caused by feed-derived MP. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, the results indicated that these effects were not directly caused by MP themselves. Instead, this study revealed that MPs synergistically enhanced hepatic IL-1β and TNF-α expression, altered the expression of Claudin1 and Occludin in the rumen and intestines, and elevated plasma IL-6 and TNF-α levels. These changes were associated with elevated LPS levels, and multi-omics integration analysis further supported this association. Conclusions

This study highlights a link between MP exposure and liver injury in cattle via the rumen microbiota-gut-liver axis. Our comprehensive study reveals a mechanism by which MPs disrupt rumen microbiota, activate the LPS synthesis pathway, and impair the gastrointestinal barrier, leading to the entry of rumen-derived LPS into the systemic circulation and inducing liver injury in cattle. The findings provide preliminary evidence for MPs toxicity in large ruminants, specifically cattle, and offer critical insights for mitigating the potential threat to livestock.
