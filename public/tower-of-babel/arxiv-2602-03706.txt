Universal Characterization of Quantum Vacuum Measurement Engines
Robert Czupryniak, Bibek Bhandari, Paolo Andrea Erdman, Andrew N Jordan

Abstract
Quantum measurements can inject energy into quantum systems, enabling engines whose operation is powered entirely by measurements. We develop a general theory of quantum vacuum measurement engines by introducing the quantum vacuum bending function (QVBF), a quantity that characterizes the lowering of the ground-state energy due to interactions. We show that all thermodynamic observables, including work and efficiency, are governed solely by the shape of the ground-state energy landscape encoded in the QVBF, regardless of microscopic details. We further demonstrate that work fluctuations are defined by the curvature of QVBF modulated by a model-dependent quantity, and are constrained by a generalized quantum fluctuation relation that involves the interplay between quantum Fisher information and the ground-state energy landscape. Exactly solvable models and numerical simulations of single and many-body systems confirm the theory and illustrate how the QVBF alone determines the performance of quantum vacuum measurement engines.

Many-body systems can exhibit complex ground-state energetics that are sensitive to interactions and correlations, making them powerful platforms for quantum thermodynamic tasks
14

31

However, a broadly applicable theoretical perspective that identifies and unifies the ground-state features relevant for thermodynamic performance across different physical platforms has yet to be fully developed. Establishing such a perspective would help clarify the fundamental thermodynamic role of many-body structure and provide guidance for designing quantum engines beyond model-specific analyses.

In this Letter, we demonstrate that the thermodynamic behavior of vacuum measurement engines is entirely governed by the
quantum vacuum bending function
(QVBF) and its derivative with respect to a parameter controlling the coupling strength.
The QVBF is defined as how the ground state energy changes as a function of a Hamiltonian parameter, in analogy to “band bending” in semiconductor physics
36
.
The QVBF generalizes the concept of
local entanglement gap
introduced in Ref.
31
, allowing the description of systems with arbitrary coupling types beyond pairwise interactions. Jordan and Büttiker pointed out that measurements in the local energy basis can yield excited energies, serving also as an entanglement witness
30
.
We further demonstrate that this geometric description applies universally to both single and many-body systems, revealing a common underlying structure. We find that work fluctuations are set by the curvature of the QVBF, establishing a direct connection between thermodynamic noise and the geometry of the ground-state energy landscape. Building on this framework, we derive an effective thermodynamic uncertainty relation that bounds work fluctuations in terms of the quantum Fisher information. By expressing the quantum Fisher information in terms of experimentally accessible quantities such as work and its fluctuations, our results also provide a practical route to making fundamental bounds on quantum metrological sensitivity.

Together, these findings establish a universal and experimentally relevant characterization of vacuum measurement engines. To illustrate the generality of our approach, we apply the framework to representative examples based on qubits and harmonic oscillators, demonstrating how the QVBF geometry alone determines the work, efficiency, and fluctuations of measurement-powered engines across distinct physical platforms.

Quantum Vacuum Measurement Engines.
—We consider a general quantum system serving as the working medium, described by a Hamiltonian decomposed into two parts

H(\lambda)=H_{\text{loc}}+\lambda H_{\text{int}}.

(1)

The
local part
,
H_{\text{loc}}
, defines the measurement basis and may correspond to either a single quantum system, such as a qubit or a harmonic oscillator, or a collection of noninteracting subsystems in a many-body setting. The
interaction part

H_{\text{int}}
captures processes that enable engine’s operation, e.g., transverse driving in single-system scenarios or entangling interactions in many-body systems.
We introduce a tunable parameter—the
coupling strength

\lambda
—which controls the strength of the interaction term and specifies the operating point of the engine when the interaction is switched on.
We note that our framework can be applied to single-body, non-interacting systems, and the naming convention for
H_{\text{loc}}
and
H_{\text{int}}
is chosen to keep the notation consistent with Ref.
31
.

Let
|n(\lambda)\rangle
and
E_{n}(\lambda)
denote the eigentstates of
H(\lambda)
. Setting
\lambda=0
recovers the eigenbasis of the uncoupled system. We impose the condition
\langle n(0)|H_{\text{int}}|n(0)\rangle=0
, so that the interactions can be abruptly switched on and off at no energetic cost if the system is in the state
|n(0)\rangle
. We assume
E_{0}(\lambda)\leq E_{0}(0)
.

As shown in Fig.
1
, the engine’s cycle consists of six steps: (i) with coupling on and Hamiltonian
H(\lambda)
, we start with the quantum system in ground state
|0(\lambda)\rangle
; (ii) perform projective measurement in the eigenbasis of
H_{\text{loc}}
; (iii) switch off the coupling,
H(\lambda)\rightarrow H(0)
; (iv) work extraction: coherently rotate the system to the ground state of the local part
H_{\text{loc}}
,
|0(0)\rangle
; (v) switch coupling back on,
H(0)\rightarrow H(\lambda)
; (vi) allow the system to relax to
|0(\lambda)\rangle
by contacting with a zero-temperature bath. The cycle is then repeated.

In this letter, we assume a non-degenerate ground state
|0(\lambda)\rangle
. The expected work per cycle is
W(\lambda)=\sum_{n}|\langle 0(\lambda)|n(0)\rangle|^{2}(E_{n}(0)-E_{0}(0))=\langle H_{\text{loc}}\rangle_{\lambda}-E_{0}(0),

where
\{E_{n}(0),|n(0)\rangle\}
are the energy eigenvalues and eigenstates of the local systems, and the expectation is defined as
\langle\bullet\rangle_{\lambda}=\langle 0(\lambda)|\bullet|0(\lambda)\rangle

31
. Quantum heat
2
is defined as the average energy injected into the system by the measurement apparatus per cycle,

Q(\lambda)=-\lambda\langle H_{\text{int}}\rangle_{\lambda}
.
The engine’s efficiency is
\eta(\lambda)=W(\lambda)/Q(\lambda),

and the fluctuations of the engines work per cycle are
\sigma^{2}(\lambda)=\langle H_{\text{loc}}^{2}\rangle_{\lambda}-\langle H_{\text{loc}}\rangle_{\lambda}^{2}.

Geometric framework.
—The above relations for work, quantum heat, efficiency, and fluctuations were derived in Ref.
31
for a specific choice of
H_{\text{loc}}
and
H_{\text{int}}
. We define the
quantum vacuum bending function

\Delta(\lambda)=E_{0}(0)-E_{0}(\lambda),

(2)

as the coupling-induced lowering of the ground-state energy. By treating
\lambda
as a tunable parameter, we show below that all thermodynamic quantities,
W(\lambda)
,
Q(\lambda)
, and
\eta(\lambda)
, depend only on the QVBF and its derivative, while the fluctuations depend on
its curvature up to a model-dependent function. This identifies
\Delta(\lambda)
as the central geometric quantity characterizing the performance of quantum vacuum measurement engines.

To connect the engine’s performance to the QVBF, we use the Hellmann-Feynman theorem
24
;
21
to write
E_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)=\langle H^{\prime}(\lambda)\rangle_{\lambda}=\langle H_{\text{int}}\rangle_{\lambda}
, where the prime symbol
(^{\prime})
denotes the derivative
d/d\lambda
. Since
\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)=-E_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)
, the quantum heat becomes

Q(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda),

(3)

demonstrating that the quantum heat is governed by the slope of the QVBF. Similarly, the average work and efficiency can be expressed as

W(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)-\Delta(\lambda),\quad\eta(\lambda)=1-\frac{\Delta(\lambda)}{\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)},

(4)

showing that
the thermodynamic properties of a quantum measurement engine depend only on
\Delta(\lambda)
and its slope
. We stress the nonperturbative nature of this result. For any operational engine
W(\lambda)>0\implies\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)>\Delta(\lambda)
, which enforces
\eta(\lambda)
to be strictly below
1
.

Work fluctuations are governed by the curvature of the QVBF. As shown in the Supplemental Material
1
,

\sigma^{2}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\bar{e}(\lambda),

(5)

where
\bar{e}(\lambda)
is a funtion determined by matrix elements of
H_{\text{int}}
between the ground and excited states of
H(\lambda)
. It is defined through

{1}/{\bar{e}}\equiv{\sum_{n>0}(c_{n}^{2}/e_{n})}/{\sum_{n>0}c_{n}^{2}}
,
with
c_{n}^{2}\equiv|\langle n(\lambda)|H_{\text{int}}|0(\lambda)\rangle|^{2}
and
e_{n}\equiv E_{n}(\lambda)-E_{0}(\lambda)
. Since
\bar{e}(\lambda)
is a harmonic mean weighted by the coefficients
c_{n}^{2}
, it is bounded between the minimum and maximum excitation energies accessible through
H_{\text{int}}
. Denoting these bounds by
\bar{e}_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\bar{e}_{\mathrm{max}}
, respectively, we obtain the fundamental bounds

\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\bar{e}_{\text{min}}}{2}\leq\sigma^{2}(\lambda)\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\bar{e}_{\text{max}}}{2}.

(6)

An upper bound exists only when the local subsystems possess a bounded energy spectrum, as in the case of qubits. For systems with unbounded spectra, such as harmonic oscillators,
\bar{e}_{\mathrm{max}}
does not exist, and the work fluctuations can, in principle, grow without limit.

Information-geometric considerations based on the quantum Fisher information (QFI) provide a complementary lower bound of the fluctuations. The QFI for a pure state
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
about the parameter
\lambda
is given by
\mathcal{I}(\lambda)=4\left[\langle\partial_{\lambda}\psi(\lambda)|\partial_{\lambda}\psi(\lambda)\rangle-|\langle\partial_{\lambda}\psi(\lambda)|\psi(\lambda)\rangle|^{2}\right]

38
. Applied to the ground state
|0({\lambda})\rangle
, this quantity can be used to prove a universal lower bound on work fluctuations through the inequality

\frac{\sigma^{2}(\lambda)}{[W(\lambda)]^{2}}\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}(\lambda)}{[Q(\lambda)]^{2}}\geq\frac{2}{\Sigma_{Q}},\ \Sigma_{Q}=\frac{2\mathcal{I}(\lambda)}{\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}\ln\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)\right)^{2}},

(7)

as derived in the Supplementary Material
1
. This bound has the characteristic structure of a thermodynamic uncertainty relation, linking the work to its variance. Here
\Sigma_{Q}
plays the role of an effective entropy production associated with heat extraction. In the absence of thermal reservoirs with
T>0
, this bound must be viewed as an extension of thermodynamic uncertainty relations
27
;
3
;
39
;
9
;
34
;
23
to quantum vacuum measurement engines. The bound reveals a clear decomposition into an information-theoretic contribution encoded in the QFI and a geometric contribution determined by the variation of the QVBF.

Eq. (
6
) can be used to obtain the relation
\text{Var}[\lambda]\geq 1/\mathcal{I}(\lambda)
derived in the Supplementary Material
1
.
We note a similarity to the Cramér-Rao bound of statistical estimation theory
11
and its quantum generalization
6
. In that context, the bound on statistical uncertainty can be seen as tied to the sensitivity of the quantum state with respect to that parameter; here thermodynamic twist is that the bound is expressed in terms of work fluctuations, rather than the statistical variance. Nevertheless, by interpreting the work as a parameter-dependent observable, these fluctuations determine the uncertainty of an estimator for
\lambda
.

Constraints on Engine Performance.
— We now exploit the qualitative features of ground state energy and expected properties of thermodynamic metrics to derive model-independent bounds on the engine’s performance.
Let us assume that QVBF is a well-behaved function between
0
and the value of
\lambda
at which the engine is operated.
Since the quantum heat satisfies
Q(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)\geq 0
,
we have
\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)>0
for
\lambda>0
and
\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)<0
for
\lambda<0
. Thus,
\Delta(\lambda)
increases monotonically away from the origin for
\lambda>0
and decreases monotonically for
\lambda<0
. Because
\Delta(\lambda)
is positive for
\lambda
near (but not equal to)
0
, these monotonicity properties imply that
\Delta(\lambda)>0
for all
\lambda\neq 0
.

The condition
W(\lambda)\geq 0
implies
\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)\geq\Delta(\lambda)
, constraining the behavior of both the QVBF and
E_{0}(\lambda)
. This illustrates how fundamental bounds on engine performance restrict the structure of the ground-state energy.

In the weak coupling regime,
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, perturbation theory yields
\Delta(\lambda)\propto\lambda^{2}
(see Supplemental Material for details), and we find to leading order in
\lambda

\displaystyle W(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(0),\quad\eta(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\lambda}{12}\frac{\Delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)}{\Delta^{\prime\prime}(0)},

(8)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\langle 0(0)|H_{\text{int}}^{2}|0(0)\rangle,

(9)

indicating that both work and its fluctuations vanish at zero coupling, while the efficiency is universally 1/2 for all quantum vacuum fluctuation engines.

In the strong coupling regime,
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, the
H_{\text{loc}}
acts as a perturbation to
\lambda H_{\text{int}}
. Provided that the spectrum of the off-diagonal Hamiltonian is bounded, and that possible degeneracies of the ground state of
\lambda H_{\text{int}}
are lifted by
H_{\text{loc}}
, we find that in the large
\lambda
limit,

\Delta(\lambda)\xrightarrow{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}a\lambda+b
, where
a,b
are real constants.
Using this asymptotic form in Eq. (
4
) yields

W(\lambda)\xrightarrow{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}-b,\quad\eta(\lambda)\xrightarrow{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}0.

(10)

Thus, a quantum measurement engine with a bounded spectrum of
H_{\text{int}}
saturates in work output and becomes asymptotically inefficient at strong coupling, a universal feature emerging from the geometry of
\Delta(\lambda)
, that is absent in systems with an unbounded spectrum of
H_{\text{int}}
. More generally, we note that the engine can be engineered to near perfect efficiency by ground state engineering.
If for a given, arbitrary
\lambda
outside the perturbation theory regime QVBF exhibits locally polynomial scaling,
\Delta\sim c_{1}\lambda^{p}
, then the work scales polynomially
W\sim c_{1}(p-1)\lambda^{p}
, and the efficiency is
\eta=1-1/p
. Consequently, for large order
p
the efficiency approaches 1. If we have locally exponential scaling,
\Delta\sim c_{2}\exp(\beta\lambda)
, then the work scales exponentially,
W\sim c_{2}(\lambda\beta-1)\exp(\beta\lambda)
, while the efficiency is
\eta=1-1/(\beta\lambda)
. The efficiency approaches 1 for large
\beta
or
\lambda
. We conclude that the system should be engineered such that the QVBF increases as rapidly as possible as a function of
\lambda
to have efficient engines, as implied also by Eq. (
4
).

For any
\lambda\neq 0
, the extracted work necessarily increases with
|\lambda|
. This follows from the relation

W^{\prime}(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda),

(11)

together with the result, shown in the Supplemental Material
1
, that
\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)>0
for

\lambda\neq 0
. This relation provides a direct physical interpretation of the curvature of the QVBF as the rate at which work changes with the coupling strength.

\Delta(\lambda)

Conclusion.
—We have developed a general geometric theory of quantum vacuum measurement engines, extending and unifying the framework introduced in Ref.
31
. Our central result is that the quantum vacuum bending function
\Delta(\lambda)
—encoding the ground-state energy landscape as a function of coupling strength—fully determines the engine’s thermodynamic behavior. In particular, the average work, quantum heat, and efficiency depend solely on
\Delta(\lambda)
and its first derivative, while work fluctuations are governed by its curvature weighted by an effective excitation energy. This establishes the ground-state energy landscape as the fundamental object controlling engine performance, independently of the microscopic details of the working medium.

We have demonstrated how the features characterizing the ground state energy landscape can be translated into performance metrics of the engine. Constraints on work fluctuations take the form of an extension of a thermodynamic uncertainty relation adapted to quantum vacuum measurement engines, where the role of entropy production is played by a combination of quantum Fisher information and the geometry of
\Delta(\lambda)
. Therefore, the fluctuations can be used to probe information-theoretic metrics. Our results suggest a novel way of designing quantum thermal machines, where manipulating the ground state energy landscape can enhance their performance. The quantum vacuum bending function emerges as a physically relevant quantity beyond engine operation, linking quantum thermodynamics, metrology, and many-body correlations within a single geometric framework.

I
Derivation of fluctuations

In this section, we derive the result

\sigma^{2}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\bar{e}(\lambda)

(S1)

describing the fluctuations of the engine. The calculations are divided into parts and presented in the following subsections.

I.1
Derivative of the ground state of the total Hamiltonian

We aim to derive the expression for
|0^{\prime}(\lambda)\rangle
. To keep the notation concise, we will omit the dependence on
\lambda
and write it as
|0^{\prime}\rangle
and similar for the other
\lambda
-dependent quantities. First, consider the norm

\langle 0|0\rangle=1,

(S2)

and differentiate it over
\lambda
to get

2\mathop{\text{Re}}\nolimits\left\{\langle 0|0^{\prime}\rangle\right\}=0.

(S3)

Here the prime indicates the derivative with respect to
\lambda
; the result implies that the overlap of the ground state and its derivative must be purely imaginary

\langle 0|0^{\prime}\rangle=if(\lambda),

(S4)

where
f(\lambda)
is a real function. We will use this property in future calculations.

Now consider the eigenproblem

H|0\rangle=E_{0}|0\rangle,

(S5)

and take the derivative of both sides over
\lambda
to get

H^{\prime}|0\rangle+H|0^{\prime}\rangle=E_{0}^{\prime}|0\rangle+E_{0}|0^{\prime}\rangle.

(S6)

Applying the eigenstate
\bra{n}
of the total Hamiltonian
H(\lambda)
from the left results in

\langle n|H^{\prime}|0\rangle+E_{n}\langle n|0^{\prime}\rangle=E_{0}\langle n|0^{\prime}\rangle,

(S7)

and manipulating this expression gives

\langle n|0^{\prime}\rangle=-\frac{\langle n|H^{\prime}|0\rangle}{E_{n}-E_{0}}=-\frac{\langle n|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle}{E_{n}-E_{0}}.

(S8)

Let us come back to the ground state derivative

\displaystyle|0^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}|n\rangle\langle n|0^{\prime}\rangle

(S9)

\displaystyle=|0\rangle\langle 0|0^{\prime}\rangle+\sum_{n>0}|n\rangle\langle n|0^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=if(\lambda)|0\rangle-\sum_{n>0}|n\rangle\frac{\langle n|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle}{E_{n}-E_{0}}.

Using this expression to evaluate
\langle 0^{\prime}|0^{\prime}\rangle
results in

\langle 0^{\prime}|0^{\prime}\rangle=f(\lambda)^{2}+\sum_{n>0}\frac{|\langle n|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle|^{2}}{(E_{n}-E_{0})^{2}}.

(S10)

I.2
Second derivative of the ground state energy

We begin with the result obtained through Hellmann-Feynman theorem

E_{0}^{\prime}=\langle 0|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle,

(S11)

and differentiate it over
\lambda
. It gives

E_{0}^{\prime\prime}=(\langle 0|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle)^{\prime}=2\mathop{\text{Re}}\nolimits\{\langle 0|H_{\text{int}}|0^{\prime}\rangle\}.

(S12)

Using (
S9
) results in

E_{0}^{\prime\prime}=-2\sum_{n>0}\frac{|\langle n|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle|^{2}}{E_{n}-E_{0}}.

(S13)

This expression must be non-positive, implying that
\Delta^{\prime\prime}=-E_{0}^{\prime\prime}
must be non-negative. Equality is reached only for
\lambda=0
.
\Delta^{\prime\prime}>0
implies that
\Delta
is a concave up function which grows indefinitely as a function of
\lambda
.

I.3
Variance of the interaction Hamiltonian

Consider the expected value of the square of the interaction Hamiltonian evaluated in the ground state

\expectationvalue{H_\text{int}^2}=\sum_{n}\langle 0|H_{\text{int}}|n\rangle\langle n|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle=\langle 0|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle^{2}+\sum_{n>0}\langle 0|H_{\text{int}}|n\rangle\langle n|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle,

(S14)

which implies

\expectationvalue{H_\text{int}^2}-\expectationvalue{H_\text{int}}^{2}=\sum_{n>0}|\langle n|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle|^{2},

(S15)

and results in the variance we seek.

I.4
Variance of the work output

We begin with the expression

\sigma^{2}=\expectationvalue{H_{\text{loc}}^2}_{\lambda}-\expectationvalue{H_{\text{loc}}}_{\lambda}^{2}=\lambda^{2}\left(\expectationvalue{H_\text{int}^2}_{\lambda}-\expectationvalue{H_\text{int}}_{\lambda}^{2}\right),

(S16)

which connected with result (

\sigma^{2}=\lambda^{2}\sum_{n>0}|\langle n|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle|^{2}.

(S17)

\Delta
and its derivatives-they are dependent on the second moment of
\expectationvalue{H_\text{int}^2}
, which couples to the excited states of the total Hamiltonian [see Eq. (
S14
)]. This coupling is explicit in
(
S17
)
.

We can re-express the fluctuations to find explicit dependence on QVBF
\Delta
, its derivatives, and corrections introduced by couplings to the excited states. Let us define

c_{n}\equiv|\langle n|H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle|,\quad e_{n}\equiv E_{n}-E_{0}.

(S18)

Then

\expectationvalue{H_\text{int}^2}-\expectationvalue{H_\text{int}}^{2}=\lambda^{2}\sum_{n>0}c_{n}^{2},

(S19)

\Delta^{\prime\prime}=-E_{0}^{\prime\prime}=2\sum_{n>0}\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{e_{n}},

(S20)

where the last expression was obtained from Eq. (
S13
).

Let us define
\bar{e}
as the weighted average of
1/e_{n}
weighted by the coefficients
c_{n}^{2}

\frac{1}{\bar{e}}\equiv\frac{\sum_{n>0}(c_{n}^{2}/e_{n})}{\sum_{n>0}c_{n}^{2}}=\frac{\Delta^{\prime\prime}/2}{\sigma^{2}/\lambda^{2}}.

(S21)

The energy
\bar{e}
is bounded by the maximum and minimum energy difference between the excited state and ground state.

e_{1}\leq\bar{e}\leq e_{\text{max}},

(S22)

where
e_{1}=E_{1}-E_{0}
,
e_{\text{max}}=\max_{n}(E_{n}-E_{0})
.
From (
S21
) we have

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}\bar{e}

(S23)

which is the expression provided in the manuscript.

II
Comments on perturbation theory

II.1
Small coupling regime

Let us consider the behavior of QVBF in small
|\lambda|
regime. We assume that the ground state of the total Hamiltonian is non-degenererate within the perturbation theory regime.
The important ingredient of our calculations is the requirement

\langle n(\lambda=0)|H_{\text{int}}|n(\lambda=0)\rangle=0,

(S24)

which was assumed for the engine. We can approach the small
|\lambda|
regime in two ways, either through series expansion or through perturbation theory, where both approaches are expected to return the same result. We emphasize that the results demonstrated in the main manuscript assume non-degenerate ground state
|0(\lambda)\rangle
, which justifies the use of non-degenerate perturbation theory in computing the ground state energy.

Consider expanding QVBF in Maclaurin series

\Delta(\lambda)=\Delta(0)+\Delta^{\prime}(0)\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(0)\lambda^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3}),

(S25)

where we will neglect
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3})
as the previous terms are expected to be dominant. The definition of QVBF implies
\Delta(0)=0
. The first derivative can be computed by employing Hellman-Feynman theorem

\Delta^{\prime}(0)=-E_{0}^{\prime}(0)=-\langle 0(\lambda=0)|H_{\text{int}}|0(\lambda=0)\rangle=0,

(S26)

where in the final line we used Eq (
S24
). The second derivative can be calculated by noticing
\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=-E_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)
and using Eq. (
S13
). This implies that near the origin QVBI behaves like a function quadratic in
\lambda

\Delta(\lambda)\xrightarrow{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(0)\lambda^{2}.

(S27)

The same conclusions can be obtained through non-degenerate perturbation theory, where
\lambda H_{\text{int}}
is a perturbation to
H_{\text{loc}}
. The ground state can be expanded as

E_{0}(\lambda)=E_{0}(0)+\lambda E_{0}^{(1)}+\lambda^{2}E_{0}^{(2)}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3}),

(S28)

where
E_{0}^{(n)}
is the
n
-th order correction to the ground state energy.
Using the definition
\Delta(\lambda)=E_{0}(0)-E_{0}(\lambda)
yields

\Delta(\lambda)=-\lambda E_{0}^{(1)}-\lambda^{2}E_{0}^{(2)}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3}).

(S29)

The first-order correction to the ground state energy
|0(0)\rangle=|0(\lambda=0)\rangle
is

E_{0}^{(1)}=\langle 0(0)|H_{\text{int}}|0(0)\rangle=0,

(S30)

which vanishes due to the assumed constraint (
S24
). The second order correction is

E_{0}^{(2)}=\sum_{n>0}\frac{|\langle n(0)|H_{\text{int}}|0(0)\rangle|^{2}}{E_{0}(0)-E_{n}(0)},

(S31)

resulting in

\Delta(\lambda)\xrightarrow{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\lambda^{2}\sum_{n>0}\frac{|\langle n(0)|H_{\text{int}}|0(0)\rangle|^{2}}{E_{n}(0)-E_{0}(0)}=-\frac{1}{2}E_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda=0)\lambda^{2},

(S32)

which agrees with Eq. (
S13
) evaluated at
\lambda=0
.

Expressed in terms of a Taylor series in
\lambda
, the small
\lambda
behavior of work
W
can be seen because
\Delta
has its first non-vanishing order at
\lambda^{2}
, thus

W=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)-\Delta(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(0)\lambda^{2}+{\cal O}(\lambda^{3}).

(S33)

The efficiency can be expanded to first order in
\lambda
as

\displaystyle\eta(\lambda)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\Delta(\lambda)}{\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{(1/2)\Delta^{\prime\prime}(0)\lambda^{2}+(1/6)\Delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\lambda^{3}}{\Delta^{\prime\prime}(0)\lambda^{2}+(1/2)\Delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\lambda^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{12}\frac{\lambda\Delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)}{\Delta^{\prime\prime}(0)}+{\cal O}(\lambda^{2}).

Here, the linear term may vanish if have
\Delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=0.

The fluctuations of work, (
S17
) begin at order
\lambda^{2}
, so this order of perturbation theory can be obtained by setting
\lambda=0
in the other factors, yielding

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{n>0}|\langle n(0)|H_{\text{int}}|0(0)\rangle|^{2}

(S34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{n>0}\langle 0(0)|H_{\text{int}}|n(0)\rangle\langle n(0)|H_{\text{int}}|0(0)\rangle

(S35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\langle 0(0)|H_{\text{int}}^{2}|0(0)\rangle+{\cal O}(\lambda^{3}),

(S36)

where we used the completeness of the zero coupling states and the fact that we assume
\langle 0(0)|H_{\text{int}}|0(0)\rangle=0
.

II.2
High coupling regime

Let us now consider the regime of large coupling strength
|\lambda|
, in which the local Hamiltonian
H_{\text{loc}}
can be treated as a perturbation to the interaction Hamiltonian
H_{\text{int}}
. The purpose of this subsection is to qualitatively characterize the behavior of QVBF in this regime.

We assume that
H_{\text{int}}
possesses a ground state, which implies that
\lambda H_{\text{int}}
also has a ground state for
\lambda>0
. Even if the ground state of
\lambda H_{\text{int}}
is degenerate, we assume that this degeneracy is lifted by the perturbation
H_{\text{loc}}
, resulting in a non-degenerate ground state of the full Hamiltonian.

We denote by
|0_{\text{int}}\rangle
the ground state of
\lambda H_{\text{int}}
, or, in the degenerate case, the particular superposition of degenerate ground states that minimizes the lowest-order energy correction due to
H_{\text{loc}}
. Since the total Hamiltonian is assumed to have a non-degenerate ground state, this superposition is taken to be unique.

By definition,
|0_{\text{int}}\rangle
satisfies

H_{\text{int}}|0_{\text{int}}\rangle=E_{0}^{\text{int}}|0_{\text{int}}\rangle,

(S37)

implying

\lambda H_{\text{int}}|0_{\text{int}}\rangle=\lambda E_{0}^{\text{int}}|0_{\text{int}}\rangle
.

We anticipate that the lowest-order correction to the ground-state energy arising from
H_{\text{loc}}
scales as
\lambda^{-p}
with
p\geq 0
, where the case
p=0
corresponds to a non-vanishing first-order correction. Accordingly, the ground-state energy at large
|\lambda|
is expected to behave as

E_{0}(\lambda)\approx\lambda E_{0}^{\text{int}}+\lambda^{-p}E_{p},

(S38)

where
E_{p}
is a correction term independent of
\lambda
. The precise form of this contribution does not affect the leading-order conclusions. QVBF is then

\Delta(\lambda)=E_{0}(0)-E_{0}(\lambda)\approx-\lambda E_{0}^{\text{int}}+E_{0}(0)-\lambda^{-p}E_{p}.

(S39)

The term
E_{0}(0)
cannot be inferred from the large-
|\lambda|
expansion, as it is defined in a different coupling regime. When the first-order degenerate correction is active, the remaining terms beyond the linear contribution scale as constants.

Since both
E_{0}(0)
and the possible correction in
p=0
case are independent of
\lambda
at leading order, we adopt the simplified ansatz

\Delta(\lambda)\approx-\lambda E_{0}^{\text{int}}+C,

(S40)

where
C
is a constant. The corresponding derivatives are

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)\approx-E_{0}^{\text{int}},\quad\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\approx 0.

(S41)

Using the relations
Q(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)
and
W(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)-\Delta(\lambda)
, we obtain the expected asymptotic scalings in the high-
|\lambda|
regime:

\displaystyle Q(\lambda)

\displaystyle\propto\lambda,

(S42)

\displaystyle W(\lambda)

\displaystyle\propto\text{const},

\displaystyle W^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\propto 0,

\displaystyle\eta(\lambda)

\displaystyle\propto 1/|\lambda|\xrightarrow{|\lambda|\rightarrow\infty}0.

These results imply that the work output saturates at large coupling, while the efficiency vanishes asymptotically. We emphasize that our considerations in the high-
|\lambda|
regime apply only if the ground state of
\lambda H_{\text{int}}
exists; these results are not applicable otherwise.

III
Work and engine’s operational regimes

Consider the behavior of
W(\lambda)
for
\lambda\geq 0
and bounded spectrum of
H_{\text{int}}
: it begins at
0
, starts with quadratic growth, and saturates for large
\lambda
. It implies that there must be a critical point
\lambda_{c}
below which it is beneficial to increase coupling as it results in super-linear growth of
W(\lambda)
. Above this point, sublinear growth is expected and increased coupling does not lead to significant increases in
W(\lambda)
. We will call these regimes
convex-growth regime
, and
concave-saturation regime
respectively.

Recall that
W^{\prime}(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)
, resulting in

W^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)+\lambda\Delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(\lambda).

(S43)

The boundary between the regimes can be found from

W^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{c})=\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{c})+\lambda_{c}\Delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(\lambda_{c})=0,

(S44)

and solving this equation results in the critical point
\lambda_{c}
.

A similar argument can be made for negative
\lambda
. However, the critical points on both negative and positive branches will not be located at symmetric locations, and their symmetry is expected only when
\Delta(\lambda)=\alpha\Delta(-\lambda)
, where
\alpha
is a positive constant independent of
\lambda
. However, this relation is in general not satisfied.

IV
Integral expressions for work and quantum heat

Suppose that
\Delta(\lambda)
is a well-behaved function between
0
and
\lambda
. The expression for work provided in the manuscript is

W(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)-\Delta(\lambda).

(S45)

This can be recovered from the integral

W(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{x}x\Delta^{\prime\prime}(x)dx,

(S46)

where integration by parts returns (
S45
).

Quantum heat satisfies
Q(\lambda)=W(\lambda)+\Delta(\lambda)
, and we can use fundamental theorem of calculus to write

\Delta(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\lambda}\Delta^{\prime}(x)dx.

(S47)

Adding this to (
S46
) yields

Q(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\lambda}\left[x\Delta^{\prime\prime}(x)+\Delta^{\prime}(x)\right]dx.

(S48)

Eqs. (
S46
) and (
S48
) represent an alternative, integral formulation of the results derived in the main paper.

V
Bounds on fluctuations

The quantum Fisher information (QFI) for a pure state
|0(\lambda)\rangle
concerning the parameter
\lambda
is given by

\mathcal{I}(\lambda)=4\left[\langle 0^{\prime}(\lambda)|0^{\prime}(\lambda)\rangle-|\langle 0^{\prime}(\lambda)|0(\lambda)\rangle|^{2}\right].

(S49)

Using (
S18
) and (
S9
), this expression can be written as

\mathcal{I}(\lambda)=4\sum_{n>0}\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{e_{n}^{2}}.

(S50)

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\left(\sum_{n>0}\frac{c_{n}}{e_{n}}c_{n}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{n>0}\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{e_{n}^{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{n>0}c_{n}^{2}\right).

(S51)

Eq. (
S50
) together with the results derived in Sec.
I.4
, implies

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n>0}\frac{c_{n}}{e_{n}}c_{n}\right)=\frac{\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{2},

(S52)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n>0}\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{e_{n}^{2}}\right)=\frac{\mathcal{I}(\lambda)}{4},

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n>0}c_{n}^{2}\right)=\frac{\sigma^{2}(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}}.

Substituting these relations into the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\sigma^{2}(\lambda)\geq\frac{\lambda^{2}\left[\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\right]^{2}}{\mathcal{I}(\lambda)}=\frac{[W^{\prime}(\lambda)]^{2}}{\mathcal{I}(\lambda)},

(S53)

demonstrating that the curvature of QVBF, together with the inverse of the QFI, provides a lower bound on the fluctuations.

We now consider fluctuations relative to the quantum heat, using
Q(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)
. Dividing the above inequality by
Q^{2}(\lambda)
yields

\frac{\sigma^{2}(\lambda)}{[Q(\lambda)]^{2}}\geq\frac{2}{\Sigma_{Q}},\quad\Sigma_{Q}=2\mathcal{I}(\lambda)\left(\frac{\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)}{\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}\right)^{2},

(S54)

This expression has the same structure as thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TURs), where
\Sigma_{Q}
plays the role of an effective entropy production associated with the quantum heat current. Finally,
\Sigma_{Q}
can be expressed in an equivalent form as

\Sigma_{Q}=\frac{2\mathcal{I}(\lambda)}{\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}\ln\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)\right)^{2}}.

(S55)

This representation makes explicit that
\Sigma_{Q}
consists of two contributions: an information-theoretic component encoded in the QFI, and a geometric component determined by the derivative of QVBF.

Finally, since
W(\lambda)=\eta(\lambda)Q(\lambda)
, and
0\leq\eta(\lambda)\leq 1
, we must have

\frac{\sigma^{2}(\lambda)}{[W(\lambda)]^{2}}\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}(\lambda)}{[Q(\lambda)]^{2}}\geq\frac{2}{\Sigma_{Q}}.

(S56)

We can view work
W(\lambda)
as a parameter-dependent observable, where its fluctuations determine the uncertainty of an estimator for
\lambda
.
To make the notion more precise, we make error propagation,
\delta W=W^{\prime}(\lambda)\delta\lambda
. Let us consider the variance of
\lambda
as a statistical uncertainty though the work fluctuations

\sigma^{2}=\langle\delta W^{2}\rangle=(W^{\prime}(\lambda))^{2}\langle\delta\lambda^{2}\rangle.

(S57)

From the previous result (
S45
) we have
W^{\prime}(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime\prime}
. Using the inequality (
S56
), we have

\text{Var}[\lambda]=\langle\delta\lambda^{2}\rangle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{Q^{2}}\frac{Q^{2}}{\lambda^{2}(\Delta^{\prime\prime})^{2}}\geq 1/\cal I,

(S58)

where we have used
Q=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}
.
This is the same as the quantum Cramér-Rao bound.

Examples

VI.1
Coupled qubits

Let us consider the engine formed by
N=2
qubits. The Hamiltonian is

H(\lambda)=\frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_{z}^{(1)}+\frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_{z}^{(2)}+\lambda\sigma_{x}^{(1)}\sigma_{x}^{(2)}.

(S59)

We have

H_{\text{loc}}=\frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_{z}^{(1)}+\frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_{z}^{(2)},\quad H_{\text{int}}=\sigma_{x}^{(1)}\sigma_{x}^{(2)}

(S60)

It represents one of the simplest non-trivial example of an interacting system we can use to test the theory developed in the main body of the manuscript. This realization of vacuum measurement engine was considered by Juissau
et al.
, it defines the local entanglement gap which is equivalent to QVBF in case of many-body quantum vacuum measurement engines.
The equations below represents their results adapted to the notation including the interaction tuning parameter
\lambda
. The energy eigenstates are

\displaystyle|\phi^{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sin\frac{\phi}{2}|00\rangle+\cos\frac{\phi}{2}|11\rangle,

(S61)

\displaystyle|\psi^{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=(|01\rangle+|10\rangle)/\sqrt{2},

\displaystyle|\psi^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=(|01\rangle-|10\rangle)/\sqrt{2},

\displaystyle|\phi^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=\cos\frac{\phi}{2}|00\rangle-\sin\frac{\phi}{2}|11\rangle,

where

\cos\phi=\frac{\omega}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}}},\quad\tan\phi=\frac{\lambda}{\omega}.

(S62)

The corresponding eigenenergies are

E_{\psi}^{\pm}=\pm\lambda,\quad E_{\phi}^{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}}.

(S63)

The ground state is

E_{0}(\lambda)=E_{\phi}^{-}.

(S64)

The difference between the ground state and the first excited state is

E_{\psi}^{-}-E_{\phi}^{-}=-\lambda+\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}},

(S65)

and its always positive by definition, implying that the theory developed in this paper can be applied. QVBF is

\Delta(\lambda)=E_{0}(0)-E_{0}(\lambda)=-\omega+\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}},

(S66)

and its positive for
\lambda\neq 0
. Adapting the expressions for engine performance metrics derived by Juissau
et al.
results in

W(\lambda)=\omega-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}}},

(S67)

Q(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}}},

(S68)

\eta(\lambda)=\frac{\omega}{\lambda^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-\omega\right),

(S69)

\sigma^{2}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}\omega^{2}}{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}}

(S70)

We will now verify if these expressions agree with the theory derived in this manuscript. The derivative of QVBF is

\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}}},

(S71)

and when multiplied by
\lambda

\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}}}=Q(\lambda),

(S72)

as expected. Subtracting
\Delta(\lambda)
from this expression yields after some algebra

\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)-\Delta(\lambda)=\omega-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}}}=W(\lambda).

(S73)

The efficiency can be obtained through division
\eta(\lambda)-W(\lambda)/Q(\lambda)
; therefore, since work and heat agree with the results derived by Juissau
et al.
, the efficiency must agree as well. The second derivative of QVBF is

\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\omega^{2}}{\left(\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}\right)^{3/2}}.

(S74)

Now consider the coupling coefficients
c_{n}
defined in Eq. (
S18
).
H_{\text{int}}
connects only states with the same parity, therefore the ground state can only couple to the state
|\psi^{+}\rangle
. Since there is only one active coefficient
c_{n}
, we have

\bar{e}=E_{\phi}^{+}-E_{\phi}^{-}=2\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}}.

(S75)

Combining this expression with (
S23
) and (
S74
) returns (
S70
), in agreement with the previous work.

Let us now examine the laws describing the connection between thermodynamic properties and the shape of
\Delta(\lambda)
. We claimed that since
Q(\lambda)\geq 0
, we must have
\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)>0
for
\lambda>0
, and
\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)<0
for

Now, let us consider the low

regime. Expanding the work in Taylor series yields

W(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\omega}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4}),

(S76)

i.e., work begins at
W(0)=0
and scales quadratically with

, as predicted. Expansion of the efficiency gives

\eta(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8\omega^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4}),

which goes to
1/2
as

in the limit

) since

VI.2
Transverse Field Ising Model

We consider a one-dimensional chain of qubits with nearest-neighbor coupling, described by the Hamiltonian

H(\lambda)=H_{\text{loc}}+\lambda H_{\text{int}},

(S79)

where the local and interaction parts are given by

H_{\text{loc}}=\omega\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{\dagger}\sigma_{j},\quad H_{\text{int}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{x}\sigma_{j+1}^{x}.

(S80)

This model was solved analytically by Juissiau
et al.
; here we verify that their results are recovered within the QVBF framework developed in this work. Juissiau
et al.
derived the following expression for QVBF

\Delta(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{p}\Omega_{p}-N\omega\right),

(S81)

where the sum runs over fermionic momentum modes

\Omega_{p}(\lambda)=\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}+2\omega\lambda\cos p}.

(S82)

Differentiating this expression yields

\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p}\Omega^{\prime}_{p}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p}\frac{\lambda+\omega\cos p}{\Omega_{p}(\lambda)},

(S83)

resulting in quantum heat

Q(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)=\lambda\sum_{p}\frac{\lambda+\omega\cos p}{2\Omega_{p}(\lambda)}.

(S84)

Using the definition
W(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)-\Delta(\lambda)
, we obtain

W(\lambda)=\frac{N\omega}{2}-\frac{\omega}{2}\sum_{p}\frac{\omega+\lambda\cos p}{\Omega_{p}},

(S85)

in agreement with the expression reported by Juissiau
et al.

In the thermodynamic limit,
N\rightarrow\infty
, we can evaluate this result for
\Delta(\lambda)
by approximating the sum over
p=2m\pi/N
where
m=-N/2,\ldots,N/2
with an integral,

\Delta(\lambda)=\frac{N}{4\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dx\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\lambda^{2}+2\omega\lambda\cos x}-\frac{\omega N}{2}.

(S86)

We note the even versus odd number of sites does not matter in the thermodynamic limit.
This integral can be evaluated to find

\Delta(\lambda)=\frac{N}{\pi}|\lambda-\omega|{\cal E}\left(-\frac{4\lambda\omega}{(\lambda-\omega)^{2}}\right)-\frac{\omega N}{2},

(S87)

where
{\cal E}[\cdot]
is the complete Elliptic integral of the second kind. The nonanalytic feature in QVBF is a signature of a ferromagnetic to anti-ferromagnetic phase transition, discussed by Juissau
et al.

From this function the work, quantum heat and efficiency can be immediately found as in the main text. They are plotted in Fig.
5
.

To compute the work fluctuations, both
\bar{e}(\lambda)
and
\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)
are required. While the latter can be obtained directly by differentiating
\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)

H_{\text{int}}
. To evaluate these couplings, we express the interaction Hamiltonian in momentum space and perform a Bogoliubov transformation, following the approach of Jussiau

1
.

We define the operators

\hat{c}_{j}=\exp\left(i\pi\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\sigma_{k}^{+}\sigma_{k}^{-}\right)\sigma_{j}^{-},

(S88)

followed by their transformation to momentum space

\hat{c}_{p}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{c}_{j}\exp(-ijp).

(S89)

The interaction Hamiltonian in terms of
\hat{c}_{p}
operators is

H_{\text{int}}=\sum_{p}(\cos p\ A_{p}+i\sin p\ B_{p})

(S90)

where

\displaystyle A_{p}

\displaystyle=\hat{c}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{p}-\hat{c}_{-p}\hat{c}_{-p}^{\dagger},

(S91)

\displaystyle B_{p}

\displaystyle=\hat{c}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{-p}^{\dagger}-\hat{c}_{-p}\hat{c}_{p}.

The total Hamiltonian
H(\lambda)
can be diagonalized by the following transformation

\zeta_{p}=u_{p}\hat{c}_{p}+iv_{p}\hat{c}_{-p}^{\dagger},

(S92)

where

\displaystyle u_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda|\sin p|}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{p}(\Omega_{p}-\omega-\lambda\cos p)}},

(S93)

\displaystyle v_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{sgn}\ p}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{1-\frac{\omega+\lambda\cos p}{\Omega_{p}}}.

satisfying
u_{p}^{2}+v_{p}^{2}=1
,
\{\zeta_{p}^{\dagger},\zeta_{q}\}=\delta_{p,q}
,
\{\zeta_{p},\zeta_{q}\}=0
. For clarity of the notation, we omit the dependence on
\lambda
in
\Omega_{p}(\lambda)
. It leads to the diagonal form of the total Hamiltonian

H(\lambda)=\sum_{p}\Omega_{p}\left(\zeta_{p}^{\dagger}\zeta_{p}-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{N\omega}{2},

(S94)

which depends on
\lambda
through
\Omega_{p}
and
\zeta_{p}
operators.

To compute
\bar{e}
, we require the interaction Hamiltonian
H_{\text{int}}
expressed in terms of the Bogoliubov quasiparticle operators
\zeta_{p}
. We therefore introduce the number-like operator

N_{p}=\zeta_{p}^{\dagger}\zeta_{p}+\zeta_{-p}^{\dagger}\zeta_{-p}-1,

(S95)

and pairwise creation-destruction operator

P_{p}=\zeta_{p}^{\dagger}\zeta_{-p}^{\dagger}-\zeta_{p}\zeta_{-p}.

(S96)

In terms of these operators, the quantities
A_{p}
and
B_{p}
can be written as

\displaystyle A_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(u_{p}^{2}-v_{p}^{2}\right)N_{p}-2iu_{p}v_{p}P_{p},

(S97)

\displaystyle B_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(u_{p}^{2}-v_{p}^{2}\right)P_{p}-2iu_{p}v_{p}N_{p}.

Substituting these into (
S90
) yields

H_{\text{int}}=\sum_{p}\big\{\left[\left(u_{p}^{2}-v_{p}^{2}\right)\cos p+2u_{p}v_{p}\sin p\right]N_{p}+i\left[\left(u_{p}^{2}-v_{p}^{2}\right)\sin p-2u_{p}v_{p}\cos p\right]P_{p}\big\}.

(S98)

Using the identities

u_{p}^{2}-v_{p}^{2}=\frac{\omega+\lambda\cos p}{\Omega_{p}},\quad 2u_{p}v_{p}=\frac{\lambda\sin p}{\Omega_{p}},

(S99)

the interaction Hamiltonian can be simplified to

H_{\text{int}}=\sum_{p}\left[\frac{\lambda+\omega\cos p}{\Omega_{p}}N_{p}+i\frac{\omega\sin p}{\Omega_{p}}P_{p}\right].

(S100)

Expressing this result explicitly in terms of creation and annihilation operators in momentum space gives

\displaystyle H_{\text{int}}=\sum_{p}

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{\lambda+\omega\cos p}{\Omega_{p}}\left(\zeta_{p}^{\dagger}\zeta_{p}+\zeta_{-p}^{\dagger}\zeta_{-p}-1\right)

(S101)

\displaystyle+i\frac{\omega\sin p}{\Omega_{p}}\left(\zeta_{p}^{\dagger}\zeta_{-p}^{\dagger}-\zeta_{p}\zeta_{-p}\right)\bigg]

This representation makes explicit which excited states are coupled to the ground state by
H_{\text{int}}
, thereby allowing us to evaluate the coefficients defined in (
S18
). We note a minor notational subtlety: following the notation of Juissiau
et al.
, the operators introduced in Eqs. (
S88
)-(
S89
) are denoted by
\hat{c}_{j(p)}
, whereas the coupling coefficients appearing in Eq. (
S18
) are denoted by
c_{n}
, are written as
c_{n}
without a hat. We emphasize this distinction to avoid confusion.

The ground state
|0(\lambda)\rangle
corresponds to all momentum-space modes occupying their respective ground states. The only term in
H_{\text{int}}
that couples the ground state to excited states is proportional to
\zeta_{p}^{\dagger}\zeta_{-p}^{\dagger}
, which creates a pair of excitations with opposite momenta. We denote the resulting state by
|p,-p;\lambda\rangle
, defined through

\zeta_{p}^{\dagger}\zeta_{-p}^{\dagger}|0(\lambda)\rangle=|p,-p;\lambda\rangle.

(S102)

The corresponding coupling coefficients entering (
S18
) are

c_{p}^{2}=|\langle-p,p;\lambda|H_{\text{int}}|0(\lambda)\rangle|^{2}=\frac{\omega^{2}\sin^{2}p}{\Omega_{p}^{2}}.

(S103)

Using these coefficients, the effective excitation energy defined in Eq. (
S21
) evaluates to

\bar{e}(\lambda)=2\cfrac{\sum_{p}\cfrac{\sin^{2}p}{\Omega_{p}^{2}}}{\sum_{p}\cfrac{\sin^{2}p}{\Omega_{p}^{3}}}.

(S104)

The final ingredient required for computing the work fluctuations is the second derivative of QVBF. Evaluating
\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)
directly from (
S83
) without invoking the thermodynamic limit, yields

\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}\sum_{p}\frac{\sin^{2}p}{\Omega_{p}^{3}}.

(S105)

Combining these results according to (
S1
), we obtain

\sigma^{2}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}\omega^{2}}{2}\sum_{p}\frac{\sin^{2}p}{\Omega_{p}^{2}},

(S106)

in agreement with the result derived by Juissiau
et al.
for the transverse-field Ising model. In the thermodynamic limit, the sum can be replaced by an integral as before to find

\sigma^{2}=\frac{N}{2}\begin{cases}\lambda^{2},&\lambda\leq\omega\\
\omega^{2},&\lambda>\omega.\end{cases}

(S107)

VI.3
Qubits with random couplings

To verify our results for a broad class of Hamiltonians beyond analytically solvable models, we implement a numerical framework that evaluates all relevant thermodynamic quantities both using the expressions proposed by Juissiau
et al.
and within the QVBF formalism developed in this manuscript. The engines are evaluated for Hamiltonians of the form

H(\lambda)=H_{\text{loc}}+\lambda H_{\text{int}},

(S108)

with

H_{\text{loc}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{z}^{(j)},H_{\text{int}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}g_{jk}\sigma_{x}^{(j)}\sigma_{x}^{(k)},

(S109)

g_{jk}
may either be specified explicitly or sampled randomly.

[-1,1]

l2

\sqrt{\sum_{j<k}g_{jk}^{2}}=1
. This normalization ensures that the overall interaction strength remains fixed as the number of qubits is varied.

VI.4
Two coupled oscillators - model 1

In this section, we consider a system of two coupled oscillators with the Hamiltonian

H=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}\right)+\tfrac{k_{0}}{2}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\right)+\frac{g}{2}\left(x_{1}-x_{2}\right)^{2},

(S110)

which can be divided into following parts

H_{\text{loc}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(k_{0}+g\right)\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\right),

(S111)

H_{\text{int}}=-gx_{1}x_{2}.

(S112)

This model was considered by Juissau
et al.
We aim to verify if the theory developed in this manuscript recovers their results.

We begin by noticing that the parameter
g
appears in both
H_{\text{loc}}
and
H_{\text{int}}
. Thus, we cannot identify
g
with
\lambda
. To make our theories agree, we introduce
\lambda
as an additional parameter and write

H(\lambda)=H_{\text{loc}}+\lambda H_{\text{int}},

(S113)

and taking
\lambda=1
should recover the results given by Juissau et. al. Just as in their paper, we introduce

x_{\pm}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x_{1}\pm x_{2}),\quad p_{\pm}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(p_{1}\pm p_{2}),

(S114)

and write the Hamiltonian in terms of these variables. This results in Hamiltonian describing two decoupled oscillators

H(\lambda)=\sum_{j=+,-}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}p_{j}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\omega_{j}(\lambda)^{2}x_{j}^{2}\right],

(S115)

where

\omega_{\pm}=\sqrt{k_{0}+(1\mp\lambda)g}.

(S116)

The ground state of this Hamiltonian is composed of the ground states of both oscillators

E_{0}(\lambda)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\omega_{+}(\lambda)+\omega_{-}(\lambda)\right],

(S117)

which implies the QVBF

\displaystyle\Delta(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k_{0}+g}

(S118)

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{k_{0}+g-\lambda g}-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{k_{0}+g+\lambda g}

\displaystyle=\omega-\frac{\omega_{+}(\lambda)+\omega_{-}(\lambda)}{2},

where we used

\omega\equiv\sqrt{k_{0}+g}.

(S119)

The derivative of QVBF is

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\omega_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda)-\omega_{-}(\lambda)\right]

(S120)

\displaystyle=-\frac{g}{4}\left[\frac{1}{\omega_{-}(\lambda)}-\frac{1}{\omega_{+}(\lambda)}\right].

This allows us to compute the quantum heat, which after some algebra yields

\displaystyle Q(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)

(S121)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{+}(\lambda)+\omega_{-}(\lambda)}{4}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{+}(\lambda)\ \omega_{-}(\lambda)}-1\right).

It agrees with the expression provided by Juissau
et al.
if we make the replacement
\omega_{\pm}\rightarrow\omega_{\pm}(\lambda)
. Taking
\lambda=1
recovers their result. Combining it with (
S118
) results in

W(\lambda)=\frac{\omega_{+}(\lambda)+\omega_{-}(\lambda)}{4}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{+}(\lambda)\ \omega_{-}(\lambda)}+1\right)-\omega,

(S122)

which agrees with the result of Juissau
et al.
if we take
\lambda=1
. Let us now compute the fluctuations. We will need the second derivative

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\omega_{+}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)+\omega_{-}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\right]

(S123)

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}}{8}\left[\omega_{+}(\lambda)^{-3}+\omega_{-}(\lambda)^{-3}\right]

The other ingredient needed is the effective energy
\bar{e}(\lambda)
. To compute it, we will work with the interaction Hamiltonian in terms of creation an annihilation operators describing the decoupled oscillators

x_{\pm}=\frac{a_{\pm}^{\dagger}+a_{\pm}}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\pm}(\lambda)}}.

(S124)

H_{\text{int}}
contains the squares of these operators, implying that it couples only the states differing by two excitations. It couples the ground state of both oscillators
|0_{+},0_{-}\rangle
only to two other states, and the only non-zero
c_{n}
coefficiencts defined in (
S18
) are

c_{+}=|\langle 2_{+},0_{-}|H_{\text{int}}|0_{+},0_{-}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{g^{2}}{8\omega_{+}^{2}(\lambda)},

(S125)

and

c_{-}=|\langle 0_{+},2_{-}|H_{\text{int}}|0_{+},0_{-}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{g^{2}}{8\omega_{-}^{2}(\lambda)},

(S126)

with corresponding energy differences

e_{+}=2\omega_{+}(\lambda),\quad e_{-}=2\omega_{-}(\lambda).

(S127)

The effective excitation energy is given by

\bar{e}(\lambda)=2\frac{\omega_{+}(\lambda)^{-2}+\omega_{-}(\lambda)^{-2}}{\omega_{+}(\lambda)^{-3}+\omega_{-}(\lambda)^{-3}}.

(S128)

Calculating the fluctuations from (
S1
) yields

\sigma^{2}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}g^{2}\omega^{2}}{4\omega_{+}(\lambda)^{2}\omega_{-}(\lambda)^{2}}.

(S129)

Let us not take the square root and evaluate it at
\lambda=1

\sigma(1)=\frac{g\omega}{2\omega_{+}(1)^{2}\omega_{-}(1)^{2}},

(S130)

which agrees with the expression provided by Juissau
et al.
This shows that our predictions are consistent with each other.

VI.5
Two coupled oscillators - model 2

In this section, we consider an alternative version of the engine consisting of two coupled harmonic oscillators, where the interaction strength is directly controlled by the parameter
\lambda
. The Hamiltonian is given by

H(\lambda)=\frac{p_{1}^{2}}{2}+\frac{kx_{1}^{2}}{2}+\frac{p_{2}^{2}}{2}+\frac{kx_{2}^{2}}{2}+\lambda x_{1}x_{2},

(S131)

where the interaction part is

H_{\text{int}}=x_{1}x_{2}.

(S132)

In contrast to the model discussed previously, varying
\lambda
here directly tunes the interaction strength, making it a natural physical control parameter of the engine.

To diagonalize the Hamiltonian, we introduce the normal-mode coordinates
x_{\pm}
defined in (
S114
), which yields

\displaystyle H(\lambda)=\sum_{j=+,-}\left[\frac{p_{j}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{j}^{2}x_{j}^{2}\right],

(S133)

with frequencies

\omega_{\pm}(\lambda)=\sqrt{k\pm\lambda}.

(S134)

We observe that one of these frequencies becomes imaginary when
|\lambda|>k
, indicating the instability of the system. Consequently, the analysis that follows is restricted to the stable regime
|\lambda|<k
.

The ground-state energy of the coupled oscillators is

\displaystyle E_{0}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}[\omega_{+}(\lambda)+\omega_{-}(\lambda)],

(S135)

which leads to QVBF

\displaystyle\Delta(\lambda)=\sqrt{k}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{k+\lambda}+\sqrt{k-\lambda}\right),

(S136)

with derivatives

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)&=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k-\lambda}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{k+\lambda}}\right),\\
\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)&=\frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{1}{(k+\lambda)^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{(k-\lambda)^{3/2}}\right).\end{aligned}

(S137)

As expected,
\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)>0
for
|\lambda|\leq k
within the stable regime.

We obtain the quantum heat from the first derivative of the QVBF

Q(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k-\lambda}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{k+\lambda}}\right),

(S138)

and work

\displaystyle W(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k-\lambda}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{k+\lambda}}\right)-\sqrt{k}+\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{k+\lambda}+\sqrt{k-\lambda}).

(S139)

We now turn to the computation of work fluctuations. The interaction hamiltonian can be expressed as

H_{\text{int}}=\frac{x_{+}^{2}-x_{-}^{2}}{2},

(S140)

which is quadratic in position operators. Together with Eq. (
S124
), this form implies that
H_{\text{int}}
couples the ground state
|0(\lambda)\rangle=|0_{+},0_{-}\rangle
only to two-excitation states
|2_{+},0_{-}\rangle
and
|0_{+},2_{-}\rangle
, in close analogy with the previous oscillator model.

The coupling coefficients defined in Eq. (
S18
) are

\displaystyle c_{+}

\displaystyle=|\langle 2_{+},0_{-}|H_{\text{int}}|0_{+},0_{-}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{8\omega_{+}^{2}(\lambda)},

(S141)

\displaystyle c_{-}

\displaystyle=|\langle 0_{+},2_{-}|H_{\text{int}}|0_{+},0_{-}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{8\omega_{-}^{2}(\lambda)},

with associated excitation energies
e_{\pm}=2\omega_{\pm}(\lambda)
. This leads to the effective coupling energy

\displaystyle\bar{e}(\lambda)=2\frac{\omega_{+}(\lambda)^{-2}+\omega_{-}(\lambda)^{-2}}{\omega_{+}(\lambda)^{-3}+\omega_{-}(\lambda)^{-3}}.

(S142)

Substituting these results into the general expression for the work fluctuations yields

\sigma^{2}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}k}{4(k^{2}-\lambda^{2})}.

(S143)

The fluctuations are positive throughout the stable regime
|\lambda|<k
, diverge at
|\lambda|=k
, This behavior provides a complementary perspective on the stability of the engine, reinforcing the conclusion that physically meaningful operation is restricted to
|\lambda|<k
.

VI.6
Oscillator Chain

It is also of interest to examine the oscillator chain. The Hamiltonian is of the form

H=\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}^{2}/2+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{jk}x_{j}K_{jk}x_{k}.

(S144)

We consider the oscillator coupling matrix to be of tridiagonal form

K=k_{0}(2I-\lambda T)

(S145)

where
I
is the identity matrix, and
T
has 1s on the first off-diagonals and 0s on the main diagonal. The quantum system can be solved exactly with a Gaussian form ground state wavefunction. Jussiau
et al.

1
found that the QVBF is given by

\Delta=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}[\sqrt{K_{jj}}-(\sqrt{K})_{jj}].

(S146)

The solution of the oscillator chain can be adapted to the parameter
\lambda
to find the eigenvalues of the matrix
K
are given by
k_{j}=2k_{0}(1-\lambda\cos(j \pi/(N+1))
. In this model of equal couplings, the QVBF is given by

\Delta(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}(\sqrt{2k_{0}}-\sqrt{2k_{0}\left(1-\lambda\cos(\frac{\pi j}{N+1})\right)}.

(S147)

In the large
N
thermodynamic limit, we can approximate the sum with an integral, to find

\Delta(\lambda)=\frac{N}{\pi}\sqrt{k_{0}/2}\int_{0}^{\pi}dx(1-\sqrt{1-\lambda\cos x}).

(S148)

The integral is given in analytic form by

\Delta(\lambda)=N\sqrt{k_{0}/2}\left(1-\frac{2\sqrt{1+\lambda}}{\pi}{\cal E}\left(\frac{2\lambda}{1+\lambda}\right)\right),

(S149)

where once again,
{\cal E}[\cdot]
is the complete Elliptic integral of the second kind. The scaled QVBF
\Delta(\lambda)
, work
W(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}-\Delta
and efficiency
\eta(\lambda)=1-\Delta/(\lambda\Delta^{\prime})
are plotted in Fig.
6
. There we see that at
\lambda=1
, a divergence of the work occurs, resulting in the efficiency to limit to 1. This feature originates in the behavior of the Elliptic integral of the second kind - while the function itself remains finite and continuous (as can be seen in the behavior of
\Delta
), its derivative has a logarithmic divergence, signaling the break down of the theory at this point. A more careful analysis at the point
\lambda=1
by Jussiau
et al.

1
shows that the work scales as
N\ln N
, while the quantum heat still scales linearly in
N
, reflecting this singularity in the theory. This behavior results in near perfect efficiency of the engine, in principle.

The fluctuation’s dependence on
\lambda
can be calculated in a similar manner. The fluctuations are given in Jussiau
et al.

1
as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{0}^{2}}{2}{\rm Tr}K^{-1}-Nk_{0}/4

(S150)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{0}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{(1-\lambda\cos(j \pi/(N+1))}-1

(S151)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{k_{0}N}{4}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{dx}{\pi}\frac{1}{1-\lambda\cos x}-1\right).

(S152)

Evaluating the integral, we find the result

\sigma^{2}=\frac{k_{0}N}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}}-1\right).

(S153)

We see that the fluctuation diverge at the point
\lambda=\pm 1
, coinciding at the point when the efficiency limits to 1. A more careful evaluation of the sum indicated the scaling law
\sigma^{2}\sim N^{2}
at this point, showing much larger fluctuations at this point.

Figure 6:
For the oscillator chain, we plot the scaled QVBF, work and efficiency of the engine versus the interaction strength
\lambda
. The logarithmic divergence at
\lambda=1
causes the efficiency to limit to unity and the work to diverge.

VI.7
Single qubit with external field

We consider a single qubit engine with an external field coupling to the
\sigma_{x}
operator

H(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{z}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sigma_{x}.

(S154)

The eigenvalues are

E_{0}(\lambda)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}},\quad E_{1}(\lambda)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}.

(S155)

QVBF is

\Delta(\lambda)=-\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}},

(S156)

with derivatives

\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}},\quad\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\left(1+\lambda^{2}\right)^{3/2}}.

(S157)

Quantum heat becomes

Q(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}

(S158)

and work evaluates to

W(\lambda)=Q(\lambda)-\Delta(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}},

(S159)

resulting in efficiency

\eta(\lambda)=\frac{W(\lambda)}{Q(\lambda)}=\frac{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}-1}{\lambda^{2}}.

(S160)

Since the engine has only one excited state, we have

\bar{e}(\lambda)=E_{1}(\lambda)-E_{0}(\lambda)=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}},

(S161)

allowing us to compute the fluctuations

\sigma^{2}(\lambda)=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\bar{e}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4\left(1+\lambda^{2}\right)}.

(S162)

VI.8
Harmonic oscillator with external field

We consider a single-body engine consisting of a harmonic oscillator in external field. The Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2}}{2}+\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\lambda x

(S163)

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2}}{2}+\frac{\left(x+\lambda\right)^{2}}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2},

which we can split into the local and interaction part

H_{\text{loc}}=\frac{p^{2}}{2}+\frac{x^{2}}{2},\quad H_{\text{int}}=x.

(S164)

The external field displaces the equilibrium position of the oscillator. The ground state energy is

E(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2},

(S165)

resulting in QVBF

\Delta(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2},

(S166)

with derivatives

\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)=\lambda,\quad\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=1.

(S167)

This implies quantum heat

Q(\lambda)=\lambda\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)=\lambda^{2},

(S168)

work

W(\lambda)=Q(\lambda)-\Delta(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2},

(S169)

and efficiency

\eta(\lambda)=\frac{W(\lambda)}{Q(\lambda)}=\frac{1}{2}.

(S170)

Let us check if this theory is consistent with the generalized version of expressions provided by Juissau
et al.
We need the entangled ground state which is the coherent state

|\alpha=\tfrac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2}}\rangle={}{}|0(\lambda)\rangle=D\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2}}\right)|0(0)\rangle,

(S171)

where a coherent state must satisfy
|\alpha\rangle=D(\alpha)|0\rangle
, and
a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle
.
The expected value of
H_{\text{int}}
in that state is

\langle 0(\lambda)|H_{\text{int}}|0(\lambda)\rangle=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}.

(S172)

Generalization of expression of work provided by Juissau
et al.
to a single-body problem is

W(\lambda)=\langle 0(\lambda)|H_{c}|0(\lambda)\rangle-E_{0}(0),

(S173)

and evaluating it returns Eq. (
S169
). Heat can be computed from
Q(\lambda)=W(\lambda)+\Delta(\lambda)
, and evaluating it returns Eq. (
S168
). Therefore, efficiency must agree as well.
The excited states of the oscillator can be obtained through the displacement operator

|n(\lambda)\rangle=D\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2}}\right)|n(0)\rangle.

(S174)

This helps us with computing the fluctuations. We need the coefficients

\displaystyle c_{n}

\displaystyle=|\langle n(\lambda)|x|0(\lambda)\rangle|^{2}

(S175)

\displaystyle=|\langle n(0)|D^{\dagger}\left(\lambda/\sqrt{2}\right)xD\left(\lambda/\sqrt{2}\right)|0(0)\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\langle n(0)|(x+\lambda)|0(0)\rangle|^{2}=|\langle n(0)|x|0(0)\rangle|^{2}.

The only non-zero coefficient contains coupling to the first excited state with the corresponding energy
\bar{e}=e_{1}=1
. This yields

\sigma^{2}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\bar{e}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}.

(S176)

This can be computed also from the expression provided by Juissau
et al.
adapted to single-body case

\sigma^{2}(\lambda)=\langle H_{\text{loc}}^{2}\rangle_{\lambda}-\langle H_{\text{loc}}\rangle_{\lambda}^{2},

(S177)

which yields the same result.
