What is the price of approximation? The saddlepoint approximation to a likelihood function
Godrick Oketch, Rachel M. Fewster, Jesse Goodman

Abstract
The saddlepoint approximation to the likelihood, and its corresponding maximum likelihood estimate (MLE), offer an alternative estimation method when the true likelihood is intractable or computationally expensive. However, maximizing this approximate likelihood instead of the true likelihood inevitably comes at a price: a discrepancy between the MLE derived from the saddlepoint approximation and the true MLE. In previous studies, the size of this discrepancy has been investigated via simulation, or by engaging with the true likelihood despite its computational difficulties. Here, we introduce an explicit and computable approximation for the discrepancy, through which the adequacy of the saddlepoint-based MLE can be directly assessed even when the true MLE is unavailable. We present examples demonstrating the approximation’s accuracy in simple cases where the true likelihood can be calculated. Additionally, we present asymptotic results that capture the behaviour of the discrepancy in a suitable limiting framework.



Key words
: Approximation error;
cumulant generating function;
maximum likelihood estimation;
saddlepoint approximation.

1
Introduction

The saddlepoint approximation continues to attract research interest due to its impressive accuracy in approximating probability density and mass functions.
The approximation was first introduced to statistical applications by
Daniels, (1954)
.
It is typically used in its first-order formulation, but the availability of higher-order formulations facilitates the study of approximation accuracy.
Some significant contributions to this development include works by
Lugannani and Rice, (1980)
,
McCullagh, (1987)
, and
Barndorff-Nielsen and Cox, (1989)
.
Additionally,
Barndorff-Nielsen and Cox, (1979)
and
Reid, (1988)
offer detailed reviews of the historical evolution and broad applications of the saddlepoint approximation.

Here, we focus on the use of the saddlepoint approximation as a method for estimation.
When the true likelihood function is unknown or computationally intractable, several authors have used likelihoods based on the saddlepoint approximation to obtain maximum likelihood estimates (MLEs). We refer to such estimates as
saddlepoint MLEs
.

Pedeli et al., (2015)
used saddlepoint MLEs to overcome inefficiencies in existing methods for fitting integer-valued autoregressive models.

Zhang et al., (2019)
;
Zhang et al., (2021)
;
Zhang et al., (2022)
used the approach for models in ecology and epidemiology involving linear transformations of multinomial random variables, and noted considerable gains in computation speed relative to alternative Bayesian approaches.

Davison et al., (2021)
explored the use of
classical and conditionally adjusted saddlepoint approximations for modelling linear birth-death branching processes, again highlighting the computational efficiencies achieved for large population sizes.

When using saddlepoint approximations for estimation, a key question is whether the resulting saddlepoint MLEs are acceptably close to the
true MLEs
—those that would be obtained from the true, but potentially uncomputable, likelihood.
We refer to the difference between the two sets of MLEs as the
discrepancy
.

In cases where the true likelihood can be computed, the discrepancy can be obtained by direct comparison.
For instance,
Zhang et al., (2019)
showed that the saddlepoint MLEs were virtually indistinguishable from the true MLEs for a misidentification model known as
M_{t,\alpha}
, a comparison made possible due to an exact likelihood formulation derived for this model by
Vale et al., (2014)
.

Davison et al., (2021)
also presented a direct comparison between the true and saddlepoint MLEs for their branching process model.

However, the saddlepoint approximation is often employed precisely because the true MLEs are uncomputable.
In such cases, we require an assessment of the size of the discrepancy in order to justify the use of the saddlepoint MLE instead of the unknown true MLE.
Here, we develop a general approximation to the discrepancy that can be computed explicitly even when the true likelihood is unavailable.
We show that it achieves high accuracy using models for which the true likelihood and MLE can be computed directly,
and we refer the reader to
Oketch et al., 2026a
for a range of examples whose true likelihoods are unavailable, including the two-source model (
13
, section 5.4
).
We also provide theoretical justification via the limiting framework considered in
Goodman, (2022)
.
An R code package
(
Oketch et al., 2026a,
)
allows the user to compute the discrepancy approximation automatically along with the saddlepoint MLE.
The discrepancy approximation therefore permits a straightforward assessment of the appropriateness of saddlepoint MLE techniques, without requiring a model-specific analysis.

2
The discrepancy approximation

2.1
Notation for generating functions and derivatives

Let
X
be an observable
d
-dimensional random variable.
We write
X=X_{\theta}
to indicate the dependence of
X
on a parameter vector
\theta
.
We treat both
X_{\theta}
and
\theta
as column vectors (i.e.,
X_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1}
).
Define the moment generating function (MGF) and the cumulant generating function (CGF) of
X
by

M_{X}(t;\theta)=E(e^{tX_{\theta}})
and
K_{X}(t;\theta)=\log M_{X}(t;\theta)
, respectively.
Here,
t\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
is a row vector of the same dimension as
X
, and we
assume that
M_{X}(t;\theta)
is defined in some open neighbourhood of
\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1}
containing some point
(t,\theta)=(0,\theta_{0})
, and is twice continuously differentiable in both
t
and
\theta
in this neighbourhood.

To specify derivatives, we adopt the following conventions.
Gradient vectors with respect to
\theta
are denoted
\nabla_{\theta}
and treated as vectors with the shape of
\theta^{T}
.
Thus, for scalar-valued
f(\theta,t)
, we interpret
\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p}
as a row vector and its transpose

\nabla_{\theta}^{T}f(\theta,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1}
as a column vector.
Likewise, for vector-valued
g(\theta,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
, we define

\nabla_{\!\theta}\,{g(\theta,t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
.
For scalar-valued
f(\theta,t)
, we write the Hessian with respect to
\theta
as
\nabla_{\theta}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta,t)
, with
i,j
entry

\partial^{2}f/\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}
.

The gradient and Hessian of the CGF with respect to
t
, denoted
K_{X}^{\prime}(t;\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
and
K_{X}^{\prime\prime}(t;\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
respectively, are directly related to the first and second moments of the random vector
X_{\theta}
via
E(X_{\theta})=K_{X}^{\prime}(0;\theta)
and
\mathrm{cov}(X_{\theta},X_{\theta})=K_{X}^{\prime\prime}(0;\theta).

We assume throughout that
\mathrm{cov}(X_{\theta},X_{\theta})
is non-singular for all
\theta
.
Since
K_{X}^{\prime\prime}(0;\theta)
is then positive definite for each
\theta
, and
K_{X}^{\prime\prime}(t;\theta)
depends continuously on
(t,\theta)
,
it follows by restricting to a suitably small open set around
(0,\theta)
that

K_{X}^{\prime\prime}(t;\theta)~\text{is positive definite for every $(t,\theta)$ in the specified open neighbourhood.}

(1)

2.2
True discrepancy and discrepancy approximation

Suppose
x
is an observation of the random variable
X=X_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
, and denote the true likelihood function by

L(\theta;x).

The saddlepoint approximation to this likelihood is defined
as

\hat{L}(\theta;x)=\exp(K_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)-\hat{t}x)\{\det(2\pi K_{X}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{t};\theta))\}^{-1/2},

(2)

where
\hat{t}=\hat{t}(\theta;x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
is the solution to the
saddlepoint equation
:

K_{X}^{\prime}(\hat{t};\theta)=x.

(3)

Since the CGF is twice continuously differentiable and convex, the saddlepoint equation has a solution

\hat{t}(\theta;x)
for values of
(\theta,x)
within an open neighbourhood about
(\theta_{0},E(X_{\theta_{0}}))
.
We refer to (
2
) as the
saddlepoint likelihood
.

We can equivalently write (
2
) as the saddlepoint log-likelihood function

\displaystyle\log\hat{L}(\theta;x)

\displaystyle=K_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)-\hat{t}x-\tfrac{d}{2}\log(2\pi)-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det{K_{X}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{t};\theta)},

(4)

where
\hat{t}=\hat{t}(\theta;x)
is the solution to the saddlepoint equation.

Definition 1

(True discrepancy)
.

Let

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{true}}={\mathrm{argmax}}_{\theta}\,\log L(\theta;x)

be the true MLE, and
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa}}={\mathrm{argmax}}_{\theta}\,\log\hat{L}(\theta;x)
the saddlepoint MLE, if they each exist.
The true discrepancy is

\delta=\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{true}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa}}.

The discrepancy expresses the approximation error in the scale of model parameter values.
That is, the discrepancy is the (unknown) difference in
\theta
resulting from maximizing
\log\hat{L}(\theta;x)
instead of
\log L(\theta;x)
.
To obtain a computable approximation to the true discrepancy, we consider the second-order saddlepoint log-likelihood,

\log\hat{L}(\theta;x)_{2}=\log\hat{L}(\theta;x)+T_{X}(\theta,x),

(5)

where the correction term
T_{X}(\theta,x)
is given by

\displaystyle T_{X}(\theta,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}^{d}\frac{\partial^{4}K_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)}{\partial t_{j_{1}}\partial t_{j_{2}}\partial t_{j_{3}}\partial t_{j_{4}}}\hat{Q}_{j_{1}j_{2}}\hat{Q}_{j_{3}j_{4}}

(6)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}^{d}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}^{d}\frac{\partial^{3}K_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)}{\partial t_{i_{1}}\partial t_{i_{2}}\partial t_{i_{3}}}\frac{\partial^{3}K_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)}{\partial t_{j_{1}}\partial t_{j_{2}}\partial t_{j_{3}}}\hat{Q}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\hat{Q}_{i_{3}j_{1}}\hat{Q}_{j_{2}j_{3}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}^{d}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}^{d}\frac{\partial^{3}K_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)}{\partial t_{i_{1}}\partial t_{i_{2}}\partial t_{i_{3}}}\frac{\partial^{3}K_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)}{\partial t_{j_{1}}\partial t_{j_{2}}\partial t_{j_{3}}}\hat{Q}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\hat{Q}_{i_{2}j_{2}}\hat{Q}_{i_{3}j_{3}}.

Here,

\hat{Q}_{ij}
denotes the
i,j
entry of the inverse of the matrix
K^{\prime\prime}_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)
, i.e.,
\hat{Q}_{ij}=(K^{\prime\prime}_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)^{-1})_{ij}
and
\hat{t}=\hat{t}(\theta;x)
is the solution of the saddlepoint equation.
Unless otherwise indicated, all indices

i_{*}
and
j_{*}
range over
1,\dots,d
.
For the derivation of (
5
) and (
6
), and further extensions to higher-order terms, see
McCullagh, (1987, Chapter 6)
.

Definition 2

(Discrepancy approximation)
.

The discrepancy approximation is

\hat{\delta}=-\{\nabla_{\theta}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\hat{L}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa}};x)\}^{-1}\nabla_{\!\theta}^{T}\,{T_{X}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa}},x)}.

(7)

This expression for
\hat{\delta}
provides an approximation to the unknown true discrepancy
\delta=\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{true}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa}}
, which is computable solely in terms of the saddlepoint MLE
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa}}
.
The intuition behind the formula (
7
) is to approximate the true discrepancy by a single Newton step for adjusting
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa}}
closer to the root of
\nabla_{\theta}\log\hat{L}(\theta;x)_{2}=0
.

2.3
Considerations for exponential families

If the parametric model reduces to an exponential family for which
X_{\theta}
is the sufficient statistic, then the saddlepoint likelihood is exact up to a constant factor
(
Goodman,, 2022
, Section 2.5.6)
.
Thus the saddlepoint MLE will coincide with the true MLE, and the true discrepancy
\delta
is identically zero.
We show in
Section
4.4
of the Supplementary Material
that the same is true for
\hat{\delta}
.
That is, when the underlying model is an exponential family with
X_{\theta}
as its sufficient statistic, the discrepancy approximation correctly signals that the saddlepoint MLE is exact by returning the value
\hat{\delta}=0
identically.

In practice, however, the presence of exponential family components in a model offers little assistance when assessing the discrepancy.
Commonly, the observed data vector
X_{\theta}
arises from unobserved variables drawn from exponential families, but does not itself form an exponential family.
This is because saddlepoint methods are chiefly used in settings where operations on random variables preserve tractable CGFs but render the likelihood intractable
(
Oketch et al., 2026a,
)
;
and such operations do not typically preserve the exponential-family property.
Most applications from the literature follow this pattern
(
Pedeli et al.,, 2015
;
Zhang et al.,, 2019
;
Zhang et al.,, 2021
;
Zhang et al.,, 2022
;
Davison et al.,, 2021
, e.g.,)
, as do all the examples in
Section
3
.
In such cases, the discrepancy
\delta
will not vanish identically and its size must be assessed.

The asymptotics we prove in Theorems
1
–
3
apply to general families of distributions in a suitable limit, regardless of whether these distributions arise from exponential families.
The examples in
Section
5
confirm that the asymptotic behaviour is unchanged in cases where the distributions are derived from exponential families.

3
Explicitly computable examples

3.1
Validating approximation accuracy

In this section we illustrate the use and accuracy of the discrepancy approximation
\hat{\delta}
.
The examples, including two taken from the literature, are selected because the true likelihood and true MLE can be obtained with sufficient computational effort, so the discrepancy approximation
\hat{\delta}
can be compared directly with the true discrepancy
\delta
.
Although saddlepoint methods are not needed to fit these models, they provide controlled settings in which the discrepancy approximation can be tested against the exact discrepancy.

All saddlepoint-based computations below are performed using the framework introduced by
Oketch et al., 2026a ()
and implemented in the R package
saddlepoint

(
Oketch et al., 2026b,
)
.
In each example, a CGF code object corresponding to the modelled random variable is created using the operations described in
Oketch et al., 2026a ()
; each code object implements the mathematical CGF, as well as numerous specialized functions for efficiently computing the discrepancy approximation.
The saddlepoint MLE is computed using
find.saddlepoint.MLE(...)
, and the user can call for the discrepancy approximation by setting the argument
discrepancy=TRUE
.

3.2
Gamma distribution with a fixed rate

Consider a single observation
x
of a gamma-distributed random variable
X
with an unknown shape parameter
\alpha
and a fixed rate parameter
r=1
.
For this simple model, the true log-likelihood is

\log L(\alpha;x)=(\alpha-1)\log x-x-\log\Gamma(\alpha).

The CGF of
X
is

K_{X}(t;\alpha)=-\alpha\log(1-t)~\text{for}~\alpha>0~\text{and}~t<1,

and the saddlepoint log-likelihood function (
4
) can be found explicitly as

\log\hat{L}(\alpha;x)=(1-\alpha)\log(1-\hat{t})-\hat{t}x-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi\alpha\right),

where
\hat{t}=\hat{t}(\alpha;x)=1-\alpha/x
.

We use the
saddlepoint
R package to compute the saddlepoint MLE
\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{spa}}
by maximizing
\log\hat{L}(\alpha;x)
, and the discrepancy approximation
\hat{\delta}
.
Additionally, we obtain the true MLE,
\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{true}}
, by maximizing
\log L(\alpha;x)
, and compute the true discrepancy:
\delta=\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{true}}-\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{spa}}
.
Figure
1
demonstrates a close correspondence between the saddlepoint and true MLEs from
100
simulated observations using a true shape parameter of
\alpha=5
, displaying an evident visual alignment of estimates in the left panel.
The saddlepoint MLE is consistently slightly
smaller than the true MLE, i.e.,
\delta>0
.
The right panel illustrates that the discrepancy approximation
\hat{\delta}

accurately captures the sign and magnitude of the true discrepancy
\delta
.
Among the 100 simulated observations, the largest discrepancy occurs
at the observed value
x=1.58177
, where the true MLE is

2.0564
and the saddlepoint MLE is
2.0248
. Even in this instance,
the approximate discrepancy
\hat{\delta}=0.033
closely aligns with the true discrepancy
\delta=0.032
.

3.3
Capture-recapture model with misidentification

Model
M_{t,\alpha}
is a capture-recapture model for estimating the size
N
of an animal population in scenarios where captured animals may be misidentified
(
Link et al.,, 2010
;
Vale et al.,, 2014
;
Zhang et al.,, 2019
)
. On each of several capture occasions,
each of the
N
animals in the population may be
captured and correctly identified, captured but misidentified, or not captured. These responses for a single animal across all capture occasions are called the latent history for that animal,
and the vector
Y
of latent history counts is multinomial with index
N
. However, misidentified animals are observed as single-capture ‘ghost’ individuals, so instead of
Y
we observe the vector
X=AY
, where
A
is a known deterministic matrix
which splits misidentifications from correct captures for each latent history count.

The model parameters are the population size
N
, the probability
\alpha
of correct identification, and the vector
\vec{p}
of capture probabilities for each capture occasion. The vector
\pi
of multinomial cell probabilities for
Y
is a function of the model parameters.
The CGF of the observed counts
X
is

K_{X}(t;N,\alpha,\vec{p})=K_{Y}(tA;N,\pi),

where
t
is a row vector having the same dimension as
X
.

The linear mapping operation of the
saddlepoint
R package constructs the CGF code object for
X
, and the
find.saddlepoint.MLE(...)
function computes the saddlepoint MLEs and discrepancy approximations.
The true MLEs for this model can be computed using the true likelihood function detailed in
Vale et al., (2014)
.

3.4
Linear birth-death population model

Davison et al., (2021)
used a continuous-time birth-death model to infer demographic parameters of a whooping crane population based on records of annual counts of female whooping cranes arriving in Texas from 1938 to 2007.
They modelled per-individual birth and death rates from observations

z_{0},\dots,z_{k}
of the population sizes
Z_{0},\dots,Z_{k}
at
years
j=0,\dots,k
using
a branching process. By the Markov property it is sufficient to consider the conditional distribution of
Z_{j}
given
Z_{j-1}=z_{j-1}
for
j=1,\dots,k
, which is

Z_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{z_{j-1}}U^{(i)}.

(8)

Here, the
U^{(i)}
are i.i.d. copies of the offspring distribution
U
, parametrized by
birth and death rates
\lambda
and
\mu
, which are the parameters to be inferred from the population trajectory.

The true likelihood function for this model is available, but

Davison et al., (2021)
highlighted the computational efficiency of the saddlepoint likelihood, especially for large population sizes. In their work, estimates from true and saddlepoint likelihoods using simulations revealed moderate discrepancies. Here, we investigate the performance of the discrepancy approximation.

Since each
Z_{j}\,|\,Z_{j-1}=z_{j-1}
is the sum of
z_{j-1}
i.i.d copies of
U
, and denoting the CGF of
U
by
K_{U}
, we have for each step
j=1,\ldots,k
that

K_{Z_{j}\,|\,Z_{j-1}=z_{j-1}}(t_{j}\,;\,\lambda,\mu)=z_{j-1}K_{U}(t_{j}\,;\,\lambda,\mu).

For a given sample path
z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
, we can define a vector of independent random variables

\tilde{Z}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{Z}_{k}
such that
\tilde{Z}_{j}\sim(Z_{j}\,|\,Z_{j-1}=z_{j-1})
.
Then by the Markov property,
the model likelihood for
(Z_{1}=z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k}=z_{k}|Z_{0}=z_{0})
is equivalent to that for

(\tilde{Z}_{1}=z_{1},\ldots,\tilde{Z}_{k}=z_{k})
.
We leverage this equivalence in the
saddlepoint
R package by crafting the CGFs of the independent variables
\tilde{Z}_{j}
rather than formulating a multivariate CGF for the serially dependent variables
Z_{j}
.

Using the whooping crane data, the saddlepoint MLE slightly overestimates both
\lambda
and
\mu
by approximately
0.0024
, compared to the true MLEs (0.1952 vs. 0.1928 for
\lambda
, and 0.1515 vs. 0.1492 for
\mu
).
These true discrepancies of
-0.0024
for both rates align with results reported by
Davison et al., (2021)
.
The discrepancy approximation gives
-0.0026
for both rates, again demonstrating a close match to the true discrepancy in both sign and magnitude.
These discrepancies correspond to approximately
0.08
standard errors for both estimates, so the approximation error introduced by using the saddlepoint likelihood is minor compared to the overall inferential uncertainty of the estimation process.
Moreover, even without access to the true MLE, the discrepancy approximation correctly signals the smallness of the approximation error.

4
Computational details

The computation of the discrepancy approximation begins by obtaining the saddlepoint MLE

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa}}
, defined as the maximizer of the saddlepoint log-likelihood

\log\hat{L}(\theta;x)
.
The discrepancy approximation
\hat{\delta}
in (
7
)
comprises two factors.
The first factor,

(\nabla_{\theta}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\hat{L}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa}};x))^{-1}
,
is the inverse Hessian of the objective function
\log\hat{L}(\theta;x)
, available from standard optimization output.
For the second factor, once a routine for evaluating
T_{X}(\theta,x)
is in place the gradient
\nabla_{\theta}T_{X}(\theta,x)
is obtained by automatic differentiation using the tools CppAD and TMBad
(
Bell,, 2022
;
Kristensen et al.,, 2016
)
.
Since
T_{X}
is a scalar-valued function, the Cheap Gradient Principle asserts that computing its gradient has approximately the same cost as evaluating the function itself.
The main challenge is evaluating
T_{X}(\theta,x)
, which does not benefit from the Cheap Gradient Principle even though it is built from derivatives of
K_{X}
.
Here we explain how the calculation of
T_{X}(\theta,x)
can be organized for computational efficiency, using the structure of the underlying CGFs.

A straightforward evaluation of
T_{X}(\theta,x)
in (
6
) requires six nested summations, which quickly becomes computationally expensive for moderate or high-dimensional
X
.
Even computing and storing the full set of third- and fourth-order partial derivatives of
K_{X}
can become expensive.
Moreover, for typical applications, the mixed partial derivatives do not vanish identically, so sparsity does not offer much relief.

For a general symmetric positive semidefinite matrix
Q
, we define

\displaystyle g_{X}(t,\theta,Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}\frac{\partial^{4}K_{X}(t;\theta)}{\partial t_{j_{1}}\partial t_{j_{2}}\partial t_{j_{3}}\partial t_{j_{4}}}Q_{j_{1}j_{2}}Q_{j_{3}j_{4}}

(9)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}\frac{\partial^{3}K_{X}(t;\theta)}{\partial t_{i_{1}}\partial t_{i_{2}}\partial t_{i_{3}}}\frac{\partial^{3}K_{X}(t;\theta)}{\partial t_{j_{1}}\partial t_{j_{2}}\partial t_{j_{3}}}Q_{i_{1}i_{2}}Q_{i_{3}j_{1}}Q_{j_{2}j_{3}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}\frac{\partial^{3}K_{X}(t;\theta)}{\partial t_{i_{1}}\partial t_{i_{2}}\partial t_{i_{3}}}\frac{\partial^{3}K_{X}(t;\theta)}{\partial t_{j_{1}}\partial t_{j_{2}}\partial t_{j_{3}}}Q_{i_{1}j_{1}}Q_{i_{2}j_{2}}Q_{i_{3}j_{3}},

such that
T_{X}
is the result of
g_{X}
evaluated at
t=\hat{t}(\theta,x)
and
Q=\hat{Q}=K^{\prime\prime}_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)^{-1}
.
If
X
is constructed from a simpler random vector
Y
, there may be useful relations between
g_{X}
and
g_{Y}
even when no corresponding relation exists between
T_{X}
and
T_{Y}
.
In particular, if
X=AY
for a fixed matrix
A
, then
K_{X}(t;\theta)=K_{Y}(tA;\theta)
and

g_{X}(t,\theta,Q)=g_{Y}(tA,\theta,A^{T}QA).

(10)

No corresponding relation holds for
T_{X}
and
T_{Y}
.

If
X
has independent entries, (
9
) reduces to

\displaystyle g_{X}(t,\theta,Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{j=1}^{d}K^{(4)}_{X_{j}}(t_{j};\theta)\,Q_{jj}^{2}-\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}K^{(3)}_{X_{i}}(t_{i};\theta)K^{(3)}_{X_{j}}(t_{j};\theta)Q_{ii}Q_{ij}Q_{jj}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}K^{(3)}_{X_{i}}(t_{i};\theta)K^{(3)}_{X_{j}}(t_{j};\theta)Q_{ij}^{3},

which requires only two vectors of scalar derivatives

K^{(r)}_{X_{j}}(t_{j};\theta)=\frac{\partial^{r}}{\partial t_{j}^{r}}\,K_{X_{j}}(t_{j};\theta)

for
r=3,4
.
However,
g_{X}
must still accept a general positive semidefinite matrix
Q
, even when
K_{X}^{\prime\prime}(t;\theta)^{-1}
is diagonal, because transformations such as (
10
) can yield non-diagonal
matrices.

Even when the derivatives of
K_{X}
do not exhibit sparsity, the function
g_{X}
may simplify considerably.
For example, when
X
is multinomial, the function
g_{X}
can be evaluated in terms of a small number of relatively simple quantities that can be computed from
Q
in
O(d^{2})
steps; see
Section
6
of the Supplementary Material
.

Alternatively, we may use special structure of the positive semidefinite matrix
Q
.
Suppose
Q
admits a factorization of the form

Q=BDB^{T}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}D_{kk}\,b_{k}b_{k}^{T},

(11)

where
D
is a diagonal
r\times r
matrix,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
, and
b_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the
k
th column of
B
.
Factorizations of this kind include the standard Cholesky, LDL
T
, and eigenvalue decompositions of
Q
, all of which have
r=d
.
In terms of this factorization, we define

g_{X,\mathrm{factored}}(t,\theta,B,D)=g_{X}(t,\theta,BDB^{T}).

(12)

The function
g_{X,\mathrm{factored}}
has the advantage that it can be evaluated in terms of simple scalar-valued functions based on higher-order derivatives, with a triple sum instead of the six-fold sum in (
9
) without explicitly storing all third- and fourth-order mixed partial derivatives; the corresponding
formulas are given in
Section
5
of the Supplementary Material
.

The function
g_{X,\text{factored}}
is particularly useful in the important special case in which
X=AY
, where
A
is a non-square matrix of size
d\times\ell
, typically with
d\ll\ell
.
In (
10
), computing the matrix
A^{T}QA
required
\ell^{2}
entries.
By contrast, the relation

g_{X,\mathrm{factored}}(t,\theta,B,D)=g_{Y,\mathrm{factored}}(tA,\theta,A^{T}B,D),

(13)

only requires an
\ell\times r
matrix
A^{T}B
, with a computational cost linear in
\ell
.
Intuitively, the computational gain arises because computing
A^{T}B
in (
13
) preserves the rank
r
of the decomposition (
11
), whereas computing
A^{T}QA
does not.

Overall, the preceding discussion sets out two equivalent pathways for computing
T_{X}(\theta,x)
:
we may evaluate
g_{X}(t,\theta,Q)
at
t=\hat{t}(\theta,x)
and
Q=\hat{Q}=K^{\prime\prime}_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)^{-1}
; or,
with a convenient factorization
\hat{Q}=BDB^{T}
, evaluate

g_{X,\mathrm{factored}}(t,\theta,B,D)
.
In our implementation, we structure the computation in a modular way, with each CGF code object using whichever pathway best exploits the available structure of the CGF and of
\hat{Q}
.

The computation of the discrepancy, including the implicit dependence of
\hat{t}
and
\hat{Q}
on
\theta
, the evaluation of
T_{X}
, and the computation of its gradient, can be invoked transparently through the
saddlepoint
R package
(
Oketch et al., 2026b,
)
by the option
discrepancy = TRUE
.

5
Theoretical analysis and asymptotics

5.1
A limiting framework for saddlepoint methods

Here we provide a theoretical basis for the discrepancy approximation
\hat{\delta}
by showing that, in suitable limiting regimes, it captures the leading behaviour of the true discrepancy.
We present asymptotic results for the sizes of discrepancies in models with two distinct classes of identifiability conditions.

We suppose that the observed data vector
X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
is obtained as a sum

X_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}U^{\left(i\right)},

(14)

where the
U^{(i)}
are unobservable i.i.d. copies of a random vector
U_{\theta}
.
Thus the observed data consist of totals aggregated across a population of size
n
.
The vector
U_{\theta}
represents the response of a single individual, but the individual responses are not observed separately; only their aggregate contribution
X_{n}
is observed.
Thus the model is built by specifying the individual response
U_{\theta}
, which we can typically model but not observe, whereas the population totals
X_{n}
are observed but typically have an intractable likelihood.

Almost all applications of the saddlepoint likelihood in the literature concern models that match the sum-of-i.i.d. structure in (
14
).
For example, the branching process model from
Davison et al., (2021)
represents each observed population size as an i.i.d. sum of offspring, see (
8
).
Similar remarks apply to the models in
Pedeli et al., (2015)
and
Zhang et al., (2019)
;
Zhang et al., (2021)
;
Zhang et al., (2022)
.
The same structure underlies the classical asymptotic theory of the saddlepoint approximation, including the error estimate of
Daniels, (1954)
and Edgeworth expansions, where the sum-of-i.i.d. form yields error bounds with simple and interpretable expressions in terms of
n
.

Our analysis of the discrepancy is formulated in a small-sample asymptotics viewpoint: the number of summands
n
is taken to be large, but not so large that we can afford to ignore error terms.
As
n\to\infty
, the distribution of
X_{n}
becomes asymptotically normal, so a likelihood based on the normal approximation to
X_{n}
provides a natural alternative to the saddlepoint likelihood.
The discrepancy due to the normal approximation is asymptotically small, but substantially larger than that of the saddlepoint approximation:
O(1/n)
instead of
O(1/n^{2})
under suitable hypotheses
(
Goodman,, 2022
, Theorem 7; supplement, Example 26)
.

Davison et al., (2021)
make this comparison directly and find that the normal approximation, which in their model is equivalent to a moment-based approach, is insufficiently accurate for practical use.

The MGF and CGF of
X_{n}
are
M_{X}(t;\theta)=M_{U}(t;\theta)^{n}
and
K_{X}(t;\theta)=nK_{U}(t;\theta)
.
We write
X_{n}=X_{\theta,n}
when necessary to emphasize
\theta
-dependence, and omit the
n
-dependence when we write
M_{X}
and
K_{X}
.
Given a sequence of observed values
x_{n}
for
X_{n}
, we define the corresponding sample means
u_{n}=x_{n}/n
. In accordance with the law of large numbers, we consider a limiting scenario where
u_{n}
approaches a limiting value,
u_{0}
, while
x_{n}
scales with
n
:

u_{n}=x_{n}/n,\;\;x_{n}=nu_{n},~\text{where}~u_{n}\to u_{0}\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Under this scaling, we can explicitly portray the
n
-dependence of the first- and second-order saddlepoint log-likelihoods, see for instance
McCullagh, (1987, Chapter 6)
:

\displaystyle\log\hat{L}_{n}(\theta;x_{n})

\displaystyle=n\log\hat{L}_{U}^{*}(\theta,u_{n})+c_{n}+\log\hat{P}_{U}(\theta,u_{n}),

(15)

\displaystyle\log\hat{L}_{n}(\theta;x_{n})_{2}

\displaystyle=n\log\hat{L}_{U}^{*}(\theta,u_{n})+c_{n}+\log\hat{P}_{U}(\theta,u_{n})+T_{U}(\theta,u_{n})/n,

(16)

where

\displaystyle\log\hat{L}_{U}^{*}(\theta,u)

\displaystyle=K_{U}(\hat{t}_{U}(\theta;u);\theta)-\hat{t}_{U}(\theta;u)u,

\displaystyle c_{n}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}d\log(2\pi n),

\displaystyle\log\hat{P}_{U}(\theta,u)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det(K^{\prime\prime}_{U}(\hat{t}_{U}(\theta;u);\theta)).

The final term of (
16
) follows because
the matrix
Q
from (
6
) is

Q=K^{\prime\prime}_{X}(\hat{t};\theta)^{-1}=n^{-1}K^{\prime\prime}_{U}(\hat{t};\theta)^{-1}
,
so from (
5
) and (
6
) we obtain the relation

T_{X}(\theta,x_{n})=T_{U}(\theta,u_{n})/n.

(17)

In this limiting framework, the scaling of the true log-likelihood is commensurate with that of the approximations in (
15
) and (
16
).
See, for instance,
McCullagh, (1987, Chapter 6)
, where:

\displaystyle\log L_{n}(\theta;x_{n})-\log\hat{L}_{n}(\theta;x_{n})

\displaystyle=O(1/n),

(18)

\displaystyle\log L_{n}(\theta;x_{n})-\log\hat{L}_{n}(\theta;x_{n})_{2}

\displaystyle=O(1/n^{2}).

(19)

To make this comparison more explicit, we can define a function
P_{n}(\theta,u)
such that

\log L_{n}(\theta;x_{n})=n\log\hat{L}_{U}^{*}(\theta,u_{n})+c_{n}+\log P_{n}(\theta,u_{n}),

(20)

so (
19
) is the assertion that

\log P_{n}(\theta,u_{n})=\log\hat{P}_{U}(\theta,u_{n})+n^{-1}T_{U}(\theta,u_{n})+O(1/n^{2})
.

The MLEs and discrepancies depend on
n
, which we indicate explicitly by writing

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{true},n}=\underset{\theta}{\mathrm{argmax}}\,\log L_{n}(\theta;x_{n}),\quad\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa},n}=\underset{\theta}{\mathrm{argmax}}\,\log\hat{L}_{n}(\theta;x_{n}),

(21)

\displaystyle\delta_{n}=\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{true},n}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa},n},\quad\hat{\delta}_{n}=-\{\nabla_{\theta}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\hat{L}_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa},n};x_{n})\}^{-1}\nabla_{\!\theta}^{T}\,{T_{X}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa},n},x_{n})}.

Although
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{true},n}
,
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa},n}
,
\delta_{n}
, and
\hat{\delta}_{n}
also implicitly depend on
x_{n}
, or equivalently on
u_{n}
, we suppress this in our notation.

5.2
Regularity conditions

Some regularity assumptions are needed to ensure that, for instance, the random variable
X_{n}
has a well-behaved density for large
n
.
Here we use a sufficient condition expressed in terms of the MGF.
We will require that, uniformly over
(t,\theta)
in a neighbourhood to be specified, the MGF
M_{U}(t,\theta)
is well-defined and satisfies

\left|\frac{M_{U}(t+\mathrm{i}\omega;\theta)}{M_{U}(t;\theta)}\right|\leq(1+\eta|\omega|^{2})^{-\eta}~\text{for all}~\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}~\text{and a constant}~\eta>0,

(22)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{k+l}M_{U}(t+\mathrm{i}\omega;\theta)}{\partial\theta_{i_{1}}\dots\partial\theta_{i_{k}}\partial t_{j_{1}}\dots\partial t_{j_{l}}}\right|\leq\gamma(1+|\omega|)^{\gamma}~\text{for some}~\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{+},

(23)

\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq 4~\text{and}~1\leq k+l\leq 9.

That is, the MGF must decay at least polynomially along the imaginary direction of the complex plane, with derivatives that grow at most polynomially.
These conditions are very mild: for instance,
Goodman, (2022, Example 32)
shows that they can hold even for ill-behaved distributions.

5.3
Asymptotics when the model is fully identifiable at the level of the sample mean

Here we address the asymptotic behaviour of the true discrepancy
\delta_{n}
and its approximation
\hat{\delta}_{n}

for models in our first identifiability class. In general, if we observe

x_{n}\sim X_{\theta_{0},n}
, then by the law of large numbers, the sample mean
u_{n}=x_{n}/n
converges to a limiting value
u_{0}=E(U_{\theta_{0}})
that is uniquely determined by the parameter vector
\theta_{0}
.
Our first identifiability class describes the situation where, conversely, the parameter vector
\theta_{0}
is uniquely determined by
u_{0}
.
We begin with the well-specified case.
The following theorem is proved in
Appendix 1
.

Theorem 1
.

Consider a well-specified model with observation
x_{n}
drawn from the distribution
X_{\theta_{0},n}
,
where

\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1}
,
and let
u_{0}=E(U_{\theta_{0}})
. Suppose the parametric model is such that

\nabla_{\theta}K^{\prime}_{U}(0;\theta_{0})~\text{has rank $p$},

(24)

and (
22
)–(
23
) hold for
(t,\theta)
in a neighbourhood of
(0,\theta_{0})
.
Then, with
\theta
restricted to a suitable neighbourhood of
\theta_{0}
, the MLEs

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{true},n}
,
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa},n}

exist uniquely with high probability, i.e., with probability that tends to 1 as
n\to\infty
, and

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{true},n}=\theta_{0}+O_{P}(n^{-1/2}),\quad\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa},n}=\theta_{0}+O_{P}(n^{-1/2})~\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

(25)

Moreover there exists a constant

k_{0}=-\{\nabla_{\theta}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\hat{L}_{U}^{*}(\theta_{0},u_{0})\}^{-1}\nabla_{\!\theta}^{T}\,{T_{U}(\theta_{0},u_{0})}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1},

(26)

such that

\displaystyle\delta_{n}=n^{-2}(k_{0}+O_{P}(n^{-1/2})),

(27)

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}_{n}=n^{-2}(k_{0}+O_{P}(n^{-1/2})),~\text{and}~

(28)

\displaystyle\delta_{n}=\hat{\delta}_{n}+O_{P}(n^{-3})~~~\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

(29)

In Theorem
1
, the assumption (
24
) implies that the function
\theta\mapsto E(U_{\theta})
is invertible in a neighbourhood of
\theta_{0}
.
Thus, observing the sample mean
u_{n}
is asymptotically sufficient to identify the parameter
\theta_{0}
, as confirmed by (
25
).
In the terminology of
Goodman, (2022)
, the model is fully identifiable at the level of the sample mean.
Indeed, as shown in
Goodman, (2022, Theorem 5)
,
n^{1/2}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{true},n}-\theta_{0})
and
n^{1/2}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa},n}-\theta_{0})
both converge in distribution to a normal random variable with a known covariance matrix.

The next theorem, also proved in
Appendix 1
, extends Theorem
1
to the case where the model is misspecified. Suppose
the observed
x_{n}
is drawn according to
x_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}
, where the
V_{i}
are i.i.d. random variables whose distribution need not coincide with any
U_{\theta}
. By the law of large numbers,
the sample mean still satisfies

u_{n}\to u_{0}=E(V_{i})
, but now
u_{0}
need not coincide with any
E(U_{\theta_{0}})
.
With an appropriate generalization of the identifiability condition (
24
), the same conclusions apply.

Theorem 2
.

Fix
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
and suppose the sample means
u_{n}=x_{n}/n
satisfy
u_{n}=u_{0}+O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
.
Suppose there exist
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
and
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1}
such that

\displaystyle K^{\prime}_{U}(t_{0};\theta_{0})=u_{0},

(30)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\log\hat{L}_{U}^{*}(\theta_{0},u_{0})=0,

(31)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\hat{L}_{U}^{*}(\theta_{0},u_{0})~\text{is negative definite},

(32)

and (
22
)–(
23
) hold for
(t,\theta)
in a neighbourhood of
(t_{0},\theta_{0})
.
Then, with
\theta
restricted to a suitable neighbourhood of
\theta_{0}
,
the MLEs
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{true},n}
,
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{spa},n}
exist uniquely
with high probability,
and (
25
), (
27
)–(
29
) still hold with the same constant
k_{0}
from (
26
).

If instead the sample means satisfy
u_{n}=u_{0}+o_{P}(1)
, then (
29
) holds without change, and (
25
), (
27
)–(
28
) hold with
O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
replaced by
o_{P}(1)
.

Here, assumption (
30
) ensures that the saddlepoint equation has a solution. Assumptions (
31
)–(
32
) are the conditions for the parameter
\theta_{0}
to be the non-degenerate local maximum of
\log\hat{L}_{U}^{*}(\theta,u_{0})
.
The negative definiteness of the Hessian in (
32
) generalizes (
24
) from Theorem
1
, as shown in
Section
2.1
of the Supplementary Material
.
Importantly, Theorem
2
shows that, even under potential model misspecification, the discrepancy results remain the same, showcasing the robustness of these findings.

Overall, for models whose parameters are identifiable from their sample mean, Theorems
1
and
2
indicate that
the approximate discrepancy
\hat{\delta}_{n}
closely matches the true discrepancy
\delta_{n}
. Further, these discrepancies become negligible as
n\to\infty
.
To illustrate these results, we use the model in
Section
3.2
.

x_{n}=nu_{0}
with
u_{0}=1.3045
.

Example 1
.

Suppose we observe an instance of a random variable
X_{\alpha,n}
, assumed to be the sum of
n
i.i.d. copies of
U_{\alpha}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,1)
.
It follows that
X_{\alpha,n}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(n\alpha,1)
, and the corresponding CGF is

K_{X}(t;\alpha)=nK_{U}(t;\alpha)=-n\alpha\log(1-t)~\text{for}~\alpha>0~\text{and}~t<1.

With
K_{U}^{\prime}(0;\alpha)=E(U_{\alpha})=\alpha
, condition (
24
) is satisfied.
For a specific instance
x_{n}=nu_{n}
of
X_{\alpha,n}
, the true and saddlepoint log-likelihood are respectively

\displaystyle\log L_{n}(\alpha;x_{n})

\displaystyle=(n\alpha-1)\log x_{n}-x_{n}-\log\Gamma(n\alpha),

\displaystyle\log\hat{L}_{n}(\alpha;x_{n})

\displaystyle=(n\alpha-1)\log x_{n}-x_{n}+n\alpha+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-n\alpha\right)\log(n\alpha)-\tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi),

and we define

\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{true},n}=\mathrm{argmax}_{\alpha}~\log L_{n}(\alpha;x_{n})
and
\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{spa},n}=\mathrm{argmax}_{\alpha}~\hat{L}_{n}(\alpha;x_{n})
.

illustrates the theoretical findings (
27
)–(
29
), showing the true discrepancies
\delta_{n}=\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{true},n}-\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{spa},n}
and their approximations
\hat{\delta}_{n}
obtained using (
21
).

u_{n}
is held fixed at
u_{n}=u_{0}=1.3045
for all

, so
x_{n}=nu_{0}
.

|\delta_{n}-\hat{\delta}_{n}|
change across a range of values for
n
, in accordance with the predictions of Theorem
1
.

n
. Both discrepancies exhibit approximately
n^{-2}
decline, reflected by the slope of approximately
-2
in the log-log plot. The difference between discrepancies, represented by the purple line, shows a steeper
n^{-3}
decline.

5.4
Asymptotics when the model is partially identifiable at the level of the sample mean

We now consider models in our second identifiability class, where only some of the parameters in
\theta
are uniquely identified by
E(U_{\theta})
.
The following theorem is proved in
Section
3
of the Supplementary Material
.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose the model parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1}
can be decomposed as

\theta=\begin{pmatrix}\omega\\
\tau\end{pmatrix},~\text{where}~\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times 1}~\text{and}~\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times 1},

(33)

in such a way that

K_{U}^{\prime}(0;\theta)=E(U_{\theta})~\text{depends only on}~\omega.

(34)

Fix
\theta_{0}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega_{0}\\
\tau_{0}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and set
u_{0}=K_{U}^{\prime}(0;\theta_{0})=E(U_{\theta_{0}})
.
Suppose that (
22
)–(
23
) hold for
(t,\theta)
in a neighbourhood of
(0,\theta_{0})
, and that the
d\times p_{1}
matrix

B=\nabla_{\omega}K^{\prime}_{U}\left(0;\theta_{0}\right)

has rank
p_{1}
.

Fix
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
and consider the deterministic sequence of observations
x_{n}
for
X_{n}
given by

x_{n}=nu_{0}+n^{1/2}z_{0}.

(35)

To express the asymptotic identifiability condition, introduce the parametric model
Z_{v,\tau}=Bv+e
, where
v\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times 1}
and
e\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
is normally distributed with a zero mean vector and a covariance matrix

K_{U}^{\prime\prime}(0;\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega_{0}\\
\tau\end{smallmatrix}\right))
.
We suppose that this model has non-degenerate local MLEs for
v
and
\tau
when
Z_{v,\tau}=z_{0}
.

Under these assumptions:

(a)
A constant

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}k_{0,v}\\
k_{0,\tau}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1}

exists such that

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{n,\omega}\\
\delta_{n,\tau}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}n^{-3/2}(k_{0,v}+o(1))\\
n^{-1}(k_{0,\tau}+o(1))\end{pmatrix},

(36)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\delta}_{n,\omega}\\
\hat{\delta}_{n,\tau}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}n^{-3/2}(k_{0,v}+o(1))\\

Proposition 4

(Exactness of

.

Suppose the observable statistic

has density

\{f(\,\cdot\mid\eta)\}
is an exponential family with natural parameter

\eta
and sufficient statistic

Let

denote the saddlepoint likelihood constructed from the CGF of

X_{\eta}
, and suppose that the true and saddlepoint MLEs exist.
Then the true and approximated discrepancies satisfy

\delta=\hat{\delta}=0.

In particular, in this exponential-family setting the diagnostic correctly returns that the saddlepoint MLE is exact.
We note that a sufficient condition guaranteeing the existence of both true and saddlepoint MLEs is for

to be non-zero and for the saddlepoint equation to have a solution.

of Proposition
4
.

The fact that
\delta=0
is already shown in

By construction of the exponential family, the CGF of

can be written as

Note first that

From this we see that the solution of the saddlepoint equation

\eta+\hat{t}(\eta;x)

Let
