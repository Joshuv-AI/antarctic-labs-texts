Abstract

This canonical ensemble construction enables the precise derivation of thermodynamic quantities such as temperature, energy, entropy, pressure, and heat capacity via the integral Euclidean path approach. Extending this analysis, we incorporate quantum corrections through Barrow entropy, a modified entropy law motivated by possible quantum-gravitational effects. The Barrow entropy is given by S B = ( A + / 4 ) 1 + Δ S_{B}=(A_{+}/4)^{1+\Delta} , where Δ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \Delta\in[0,1] represents the degree of fractalization of the horizon. For Δ > 0 \Delta>0 , we derive the corresponding generalized free energy F B F_{B} , which reveals that the thermodynamic phase structure changes with increasing Δ \Delta .The modified heat capacity of Barrow C B C_{B} is also computed, which decreases with larger Δ \Delta , indicating suppressed thermal stability. Moreover, we study the influence of quantum effects on the thermal response, by calculating the corrected Joule–Thomson coefficient μ B \mu_{B} . We identify a well-defined range r + ≤ r ≤ 1.154 ​ r + r_{+}\leq r\leq 1.154\,r_{+} within which a stable black hole configuration can spontaneously nucleate from a background of hot flat space. Together, our results highlight York’s cavity method as a robust tool to investigate black hole thermodynamics in lower-dimensional gravity and show that Barrow’s entropy introduces physically significant corrections that could signal the influence of quantum gravity near the horizon.

Canonical Ensemble Formalism for a Circular Cavity Enclosing a BTZ Black Hole

In order to establish a rigorous thermodynamic description of black holes within anti-de Sitter (AdS) spacetimes, it is often necessary to impose well-defined boundary conditions. In the canonical ensemble, this is achieved elegantly through York’s formalism, which introduces a finite-radius cavity to enclose the black hole and maintain a fixed boundary temperature. This approach is particularly effective in asymptotically AdS spaces such as those described by the
(2+1)
-dimensional (BTZ) solution where it provides a well-posed thermodynamic framework and serves to regularize the Euclidean path integral formulation
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
.

The essential motivation for introducing a cavity is threefold. First, it replaces the ambiguities of asymptotic AdS infinity with a physically meaningful finite boundary at radius
r
, thus allowing a consistent definition of thermal equilibrium. Second, it enables local temperature measurement through Tolman’s law by relating the proper temperature at the cavity wall to the Hawking temperature at the horizon, while accounting for gravitational redshift. Third, it provides a natural infrared cutoff for the Euclidean gravitational action, ensuring that the partition function is finite and the thermodynamic quantities derived from it are well defined.

We begin with the static, circularly symmetric BTZ black hole solution in
(2+1)
-dimensional gravity with a negative cosmological constant. The spacetime metric is given by

ds^{2}=-\left(-8\mathcal{M}+\frac{r^{2}}{l^{2}}\right)dt^{2}+\left(-8\mathcal{M}+\frac{r^{2}}{l^{2}}\right)^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\phi^{2},

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{M}
is the ADM mass of the black hole and
l
is the radius of AdS curvature. The event horizon is located at
r_{+}=l\sqrt{8\mathcal{M}}
, where the lapse function vanishes.

To formulate the system in thermal equilibrium, we perform a Wick rotation
t\rightarrow-i\tau
, transforming the Lorentzian metric to its Euclidean counterpart. The Euclidean time coordinate
\tau
must be periodic to eliminate conical singularities at the horizon, with period

\beta_{\infty}=\frac{2\pi l^{2}}{r_{+}},

(2.2)

which corresponds to the inverse of the Hawking temperature measured by an observer at asymptotic infinity.

However, in the canonical ensemble, thermodynamic observables are defined with respect to a local observer situated at the finite cavity boundary
r>r_{+}
. Due to gravitational redshift, the proper temperature measured at this boundary differs from the Hawking temperature and is given by Tolman’s law:

T=\frac{T_{\infty}}{\sqrt{-g_{tt}(r)}}=\frac{1}{2\pi l}\cdot\frac{r_{+}}{\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}.

(2.3)

The above expression shows that the local temperature diverges as
r\to r_{+}
, justifying the necessity of placing the cavity strictly outside the event horizon. In this framework, the cavity radius
r
and the corresponding local temperature
T
serve as control parameters of the ensemble.

The Euclidean action governing the gravitational sector of the theory consists of a bulk contribution and a boundary term of York–Gibbons–Hawking type. Explicitly, the action reads

I=-\frac{1}{16\pi}\int_{M}\sqrt{g}(R-2\Lambda)\,d^{3}x-\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\partial M}\sqrt{h}(K-K_{\text{flat}})\,d^{2}x,

(2.4)

where
R
is the Ricci scalar,
\Lambda=-1/l^{2}
is the negative cosmological constant,
h
is the induced metric on the boundary,
K
is the trace of the extrinsic curvature, and
K_{\text{flat}}
being its flat-space counterpart used to ensure finite action.

After evaluating the bulk and boundary terms for the BTZ geometry and expressing the result in terms of the inverse proper temperature
\beta
at the cavity boundary, the Euclidean action simplifies to

I(\beta,r)=\frac{\beta r}{2l}-\frac{\pi r^{2}}{2r_{+}}.

(2.5)

The above equation is important for thermodynamic analysis, as it directly links to the Helmholtz free energy through the relation
I=\beta F
. In this way, the geometry described by the BTZ spacetime naturally connects with thermodynamic potentials, allowing for a systematic derivation of key quantities such as entropy, internal energy, and pressure.

With this formalism in place, we shift our focus to the explicit computation of the thermodynamic parameters associated with the black hole–cavity system and explore the resulting stability conditions via the phase structure.

3
Thermodynamics of the BTZ Black Hole in a Cavity

After the preceeding establishment of the geometric and thermal foundations of the BTZ black hole in a finite cavity, we now turn to the derivation of its thermodynamic properties using the Euclidean action formalism. The key insight from Section 2 was that placing the black hole in a circular cavity at a fixed proper temperature allows for a well-defined canonical ensemble. In this ensemble, the fundamental thermodynamic relation takes the form of the first,

d\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{T}dS-\mathcal{P}dA,

(3.1)

where
\mathcal{E}
is the internal (quasilocal) energy of the black hole as measured at the boundary,
S
is the entropy,
\mathcal{T}
is the proper boundary temperature,
A=2\pi r
is the circumference of the cavity (playing the role of area in
(2+1)
dimensions), and
\mathcal{P}
is the tangential pressure conjugate to the cavity boundary. These quantities can be derived systematically by considering the partial derivatives of the Euclidean action (2.5) with respect to its natural variables:

\mathcal{E}=\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial\beta}\right)_{r},\qquad\mathcal{P}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial A}\right)_{\beta},\qquad S=\beta\mathcal{E}-I.

(3.2)

Using the known relation between the local inverse temperature and the black hole parameters,

\beta=\frac{2\pi l}{r_{+}}\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}},

(3.3)

we compute the internal energy to be

\mathcal{E}=\frac{1}{4l}\left(2r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}-r^{2}\right).

(3.4)

This represents the energy contained within the cavity as seen by an observer at its boundary.

Expressing the action in terms of the cavity circumference
A=2\pi r
, and applying standard thermodynamic identities, we find the pressure to be

\mathcal{P}=\frac{1}{4\pi l}\left(\frac{2r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}{r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}-1\right).

(3.5)

The entropy follows from the Legendre transformation of the free energy and can be written as,

S=\frac{\pi r_{+}}{2}=\frac{A_{+}}{4},

where
A_{+}=2\pi r_{+}
is the horizon circumference. This result confirms the Bekenstein–Hawking area law, even in the presence of the cavity.

These expressions satisfy the first law of thermodynamics and the Euler scaling relation
\mathcal{E=T}S-A\mathcal{P}
, which reflects the homogeneity of thermodynamic functions in extensive variables. The system behaves consistently as a thermodynamic object, and all quantities can be interpreted within the usual statistical mechanical framework.

The thermal stability of the configuration is determined by the heat capacity at fixed cavity area, defined by

\mathcal{C_{A}=\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial T}\right)_{A}=T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_{A}.}

(3.6)

Substituting the explicit forms for
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{S}
, we obtain

\mathcal{C_{A}}=\frac{\pi r_{+}}{2}\left(1-\frac{r_{+}^{2}}{r^{2}}\right).

(3.7)

This expression is positive when
r>r_{+}
, which corresponds to the physical condition that the cavity must lie outside the event horizon. The positive sign of the heat capacity ensures local thermodynamic stability and justifies the use of the canonical ensemble.

The phase structure of the system is captured by the generalized free energy
\mathcal{F=E-T}S
, which governs equilibrium configurations at fixed temperature and cavity radius. Using the expressions for energy and entropy, the free energy becomes

\mathcal{F}=\frac{r}{4l}\left(2-\frac{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}{r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}\right).

(3.8)

This function can be minimized with respect to
r_{+}
to identify thermodynamically favored states. In particular, when the free energy drops below that of hot flat space (taken to be zero), the black hole phase dominates. This occurs when

r\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}r_{+}\approx 1.154\,r_{+},

This sets up range from ,
r_{+}\leq r\leq 1.154\,r_{+}
, within which spontaneous black hole nucleation can occur. Remarkably, the upper limit coincides with the radius of the photon circular orbit in
(2+1)
dimensions, suggesting a profound geometric link to the underlying stability structure of the spacetime.

Finally, we provide a statistical validation of the entropy using the Euclidean path integral. The canonical partition function is defined by

Z(\beta,r)=e^{-I(\beta,r)},

(3.9)

and the density of states
\rho(\mathcal{E},r)
is obtained via an inverse Laplace transform:

\rho(\mathcal{E},r)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-i\infty}^{+i\infty}Z(\beta,r)\,e^{\beta\mathcal{E}}d\beta.

(3.10)

Evaluating this in the saddle-point approximation yields

\rho(\mathcal{E},r)\approx\exp\left(\frac{A_{+}}{4}\right)\delta\left(\mathcal{E}-\frac{r}{2l}\right),

(3.11)

which confirms that the entropy is indeed
S=\ln\rho=A_{+}/4
, in agreement with the geometric result.

In this section we have established a complete thermodynamic picture of BTZ black holes in a canonical ensemble. All key quantities—including energy, entropy, temperature, pressure, and free energy—are consistently defined and satisfy standard thermodynamic relations. Most significantly, we now have a robust framework within which quantum corrections to black hole entropy can be meaningfully studied. In the next section, we will extend this formalism by incorporating Barrow entropy, a generalized area law motivated by quantum gravity effects, and examine its impact on the thermodynamic structure derived here.

4
Barrow Entropy: Quantum Corrections to Black Hole Thermodynamics

The area law
S=A_{+}/4
lies at the heart of semiclassical black hole thermodynamics and underpins the holographic principle. However, it implicitly assumes that the horizon geometry is smooth and continuous at all scales. In quantum gravity, this assumption may break down due to fluctuations at the Planck scale, leading to microscopic deformations of the horizon surface
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
.

A compelling way to model such deviations is provided by Barrow’s entropy proposal, which introduces a fractal correction to the standard area law. Instead of a strictly linear dependence on the horizon area, Barrow entropy takes the form

S_{B}=\left(\frac{A_{+}}{4}\right)^{1+\Delta}=\left(\frac{\pi r_{+}}{2}\right)^{1+\Delta},

(4.1)

where the deformation parameter
\Delta\in[0,1]
quantifies the degree of fractalization of the horizon surface. When
\Delta=0
, we note that the classical Bekenstein–Hawking result emerges, as expected, while positive values of
\Delta
represent increasingly quantum-corrected geometries. Although Barrow entropy has primarily been applied in cosmological contexts, its implications for lower-dimensional black holes remain largely unexplored. This section presents a detailed thermodynamic analysis of Barrow entropy in the context of the BTZ black hole enclosed in a finite-temperature cavity, as constructed in Sections 2 and 3.

We begin by revisiting the free energy formulation. The standard Helmholtz free energy,
\mathcal{F=E-T}S
, is now generalized to incorporate Barrow entropy as

\mathcal{F_{B}=E-T}S_{B},

(4.2)

where
\mathcal{E}
is the quasilocal energy derived earlier and
\mathcal{T}
is the Tolman redshifted temperature at the cavity boundary. Substituting the explicit expressions, the Barrow-corrected free energy becomes

\mathcal{F}_{B}=\frac{1}{4l}\left(2r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}-r^{2}\right)-\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{\pi r_{+}}{2}\right)^{1+\Delta}.

(4.3)

This formulation encapsulates the impact of quantum-gravitational corrections
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
on the thermodynamic potential and serves as the basis for analyzing modified phase behavior. As
\Delta
increases, the entropy contribution becomes more dominant, and the overall free energy shifts accordingly. Physically, this implies that the presence of horizon microstructure inhibits the nucleation of stable black hole configurations, an effect that becomes pronounced in the limit
r\to r_{+}
.

The thermal stability of the system is probed through the heat capacity at constant cavity radius, defined in this context as

\mathcal{C_{B}=T}\left(\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial\mathcal{T}}\right)_{r}.

(4.4)

Using the expression for Tolman temperature and differentiating the Barrow entropy accordingly, we obtain

\mathcal{C_{B}}=\frac{2^{-\Delta}\pi^{1+\Delta}(1+\Delta)(r^{2}-r_{+}^{2})r_{+}^{1+\Delta}}{2r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}.

(4.5)

This result extends the classical expression for heat capacity discussed in Section 3 and reveals new aspects of heat capacity. As
\Delta
increases, the heat capacity decreases, reflecting a reduction in thermal stability brought about by quantum corrections.
While the classical condition
r>r_{+}
remains necessary to prevent divergence in temperature, it becomes insufficient to guarantee stability once fractal effects are introduced. In particular, for large
\Delta
, the window of thermodynamic stability narrows considerably.

To examine the system’s response to mechanical perturbations, we analyze the quantum-corrected Joule–Thomson coefficient (JT),

\mu_{B}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}}{\partial\mathcal{P}}\right)_{H},

(4.6)

which characterizes the rate of temperature change during an adiabatic expansion. Incorporating the Barrow entropy into the first law and considering the cavity’s effective volume
V=\pi r^{2}
, the coefficient becomes

\mu_{B}=\frac{2^{1+\Delta}\pi^{-\Delta}r_{+}^{1-\Delta}(r^{2}-2r_{+}^{2})}{(1+\Delta)(r^{2}-r_{+}^{2})}.

(4.7)

This expression shows that as the black hole approaches the horizon (i.e.,
r\to r_{+}
), the coefficient diverges, signifying intense thermal sensitivity in the near-horizon regime. Moreover, the dependence on
\Delta
reveals that quantum fractalization not only affects equilibrium properties but also dramatically alters the dynamical thermal response of the black hole.

The consistency of this Barrow-corrected framework with classical thermodynamics is easily verified. In the limit
\Delta\to 0
, all expressions for free energy, heat capacity, and the Joule–Thomson coefficient smoothly reduce to their classical results derived in Section 3. This provides validation of the formalism and demonstrates that Barrow entropy naturally extends classical thermodynamics rather than contradicting it.

The broader thermodynamic implications of introducing Barrow entropy are significant. The hierarchy
\mathcal{F_{B}>F}
,
\mathcal{C_{B}<C_{A}}
, and
|\mu_{B}|>|\mu|
for
\Delta>0
reflects a consistent trend: quantum corrections render black holes less thermodynamically stable and more sensitive to external changes. The critical nucleation window identified earlier,
r_{+}\leq r\leq 1.154\,r_{+}
, reduces as
\Delta
increases. This means that quantum gravity effects deform the entropy and shift the threshold conditions under which black holes can form from hot flat space.

We now study the theoretical implications of these results extend beyond thermodynamics. The modified entropy–area relation,

S_{B}\propto A_{+}^{1+\Delta},

suggests that holographic principles may require revision in the presence of Planck-scale physics. The parameter
\Delta
serves as a proxy for quantum gravitational degrees of freedom, potentially linked to granularity effects in loop quantum gravity or string-theoretic fuzziness. Furthermore, the distinctive signatures in the heat capacity and Joule–Thomson coefficient offer a new route toward observational probes of quantum gravity—perhaps via analogue gravity systems or future precision measurements of black hole thermodynamics.

5
Results, Conclusion and Future scope

In this work, we have presented a comprehensive investigation of the thermodynamics of static (2+1)-dimensional BTZ black holes within the framework of York’s canonical ensemble. By enclosing the black hole in a finite-radius thermal cavity, we achieved a well-defined, infrared-regulated system where thermodynamic observables such as temperature, entropy, energy, pressure, and heat capacity could be derived rigorously from the Euclidean action. Our results affirm the classical Bekenstein–Hawking area law,
S=A_{+}/4
, and reinforce the thermal stability condition
r>r_{+}
as a necessary criterion for black hole equilibrium within the ensemble.

Notably, we extended this classical framework to incorporate quantum corrections through Barrow entropy—a modification to the area law that introduces a deformation parameter
\Delta
accounting for fractal-like structure at the horizon due to Planck-scale fluctuations. This inclusion allowed us to probe how potential quantum gravity effects modify thermodynamic stability and phase structure, especially in a controlled and analytically tractable lower-dimensional setting.

We derived Barrow-modified expressions for the free energy
F_{B}
, heat capacity
C_{B}
, and the Joule–Thomson coefficient
\mu_{B}
, and showed that these observables exhibit clear and systematic deviations from their classical counterparts as
\Delta
increases. Specifically, it was observed that:

The free energy
F_{B}
becomes increasingly positive with higher
\Delta
, suppressing the window for spontaneous black hole nucleation from hot flat space. The heat capacity
C_{B}
is consistently lower than the classical heat capacity
C
, implying that quantum corrections weaken thermal stability, particularly near the horizon and the Joule–Thomson coefficient
\mu_{B}
shows enhanced sensitivity near
r\to r_{+}
, suggesting that Barrow entropy amplifies the thermal response to mechanical perturbations—a feature that could be of relevance in dynamic or non-equilibrium scenarios.

The visual comparison between classical and Barrow-corrected thermodynamic quantities presented in Figures (a) through (f)—underscores these trends vividly. The classical curves (left panels) display stable behavior consistent with conventional thermodynamics, while the Barrow-corrected plots (right panels) show a clear
\Delta
-dependent departure in each observable. These graphical insights are not merely illustrative; they concretely demonstrate how quantum gravity effects may manifest in lower-dimensional black hole systems and offer thermodynamic observables as potential probes of such effects.

Perhaps most intriguingly, we identified that the critical nucleation window
r_{+}\leq r\leq 1.154\,r_{+}
, which governs the transition from hot flat space to black hole phase, continously narrowes by the introduction of Barrow entropy. Which means that the presence of horizon microstructure suppresses black hole formation, hinting at a subtle interplay between spacetime geometry and statistical mechanics when quantum gravity effects are present.

This study facilitates further research in this domain. First, extending the present analysis to rotating BTZ black holes would allow us to probe the role of angular momentum in quantum-modified thermodynamics and understand how Barrow entropy interacts with frame dragging and ergospheres.

Additionally, it would be valuable to explore other entropy modifications beyond Barrow’s proposal—such as Tsallis, Rényi, or logarithmic corrections within the same cavity formalism to examine whether different quantum corrections lead to qualitatively distinct thermodynamic behavior.

This could lead to a deeper statistical mechanical derivation of Barrow entropy, perhaps from quantum field theory on fractal geometries or from holographic principles in AdS
3
/CFT
2
, could illuminate the microscopic origin of the deformation parameter
\Delta
and its physical interpretation in quantum gravity.

Finally, our results could motivate analogous studies in analogue gravity systems, where experimental control over temperature, horizon profiles, and cavity boundaries could allow some of these theoretical predictions to be tested in laboratory settings.

with respect to
r_{+}
:

\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial r_{+}}=2\pi l\left(\frac{-r_{+}^{-2}\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}(r^{2}-r_{+}^{2})^{-1/2}(-2r_{+})\cdot r_{+}^{-1}}{1}\right)

\frac{\partial r_{+}}{\partial\beta}=\left(\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial r_{+}}\right)^{-1}=-\frac{r_{+}^{2}\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}{2\pi lr^{2}}

Substitute into
\mathcal{E}
:

\mathcal{E}=\frac{r}{2l}+\frac{\pi r^{2}}{2r_{+}^{2}}\cdot\frac{r_{+}^{2}\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}{2\pi lr^{2}}=\frac{r}{2l}+\frac{\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}{4l}

After algebraic manipulation:

\boxed{\mathcal{E}=\frac{1}{4l}\left(2r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}-r^{2}\right)}

(7.1)

7.2
Pressure

Using
A=2\pi r
:

\mathcal{P}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial A}\right)_{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\beta}\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial r}\right)_{\beta}

From
I=\frac{\beta r}{2l}-\frac{\pi r^{2}}{2r_{+}}
and holding
\beta
constant:

\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial r}\right)_{\beta}=\frac{\beta}{2l}-\frac{\pi r}{r_{+}}

Substitute and simplify:

\boxed{\mathcal{P}=\frac{1}{4\pi l}\left(\frac{2r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}{r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}-1\right)}

(7.2)

7.3
Entropy

From the Legendre transform:

S=\beta\mathcal{E}-I=\beta\left[\frac{1}{4l}(2r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}-r^{2})\right]-\left(\frac{\beta r}{2l}-\frac{\pi r^{2}}{2r_{+}}\right)

Using
\beta=\frac{2\pi l\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}{r_{+}}
:

S=\frac{\pi\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}{r_{+}}\left(\frac{r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}{l}-\frac{r^{2}}{2l}\right)-\frac{\pi r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}{l}+\frac{\pi r^{2}}{2r_{+}}

Simplification yields:

\boxed{S=\frac{\pi r_{+}}{2}=\frac{A_{+}}{4}}

(7.3)

7.4
Heat Capacity

The heat capacity at fixed area:

\mathcal{C_{A}}=T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_{A}=-\beta^{2}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial\beta}\right)_{A}

From
S=\pi r_{+}/2
and
\beta=\frac{2\pi l\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}{r_{+}}
, express
r_{+}
in terms of
\beta
:

r_{+}=\frac{2\pi lr}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}+4\pi^{2}l^{2}}}

Then:

S=\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{2\pi lr}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}+4\pi^{2}l^{2}}}=\frac{\pi^{2}lr}{(\beta^{2}+4\pi^{2}l^{2})^{1/2}}

Differentiate with respect to
\beta
:

\frac{\partial S}{\partial\beta}=-\frac{\pi^{2}lr\beta}{(\beta^{2}+4\pi^{2}l^{2})^{3/2}}

Thus:

\mathcal{C_{A}}=-\beta^{2}\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}lr\beta}{(\beta^{2}+4\pi^{2}l^{2})^{3/2}}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}lr\beta^{3}}{(\beta^{2}+4\pi^{2}l^{2})^{3/2}}

Substitute back
r_{+}
:

\boxed{\mathcal{C_{A}}=\frac{\pi r_{+}}{2}\left(1-\frac{r_{+}^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)}

(7.4)

7.5
Free Energy and Nucleation Window

The free energy:

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{E}-TS=\frac{1}{4l}\left(2r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}-r^{2}\right)-\left(\frac{r_{+}}{2\pi l\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}\right)\left(\frac{\pi r_{+}}{2}\right)

Simplify:

\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{4l}\left(2r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}-r^{2}-\frac{r_{+}^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}\right)

\mathcal{F}=\frac{r}{4l}\left(2-\frac{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}{r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}\right).

(7.5)

Minimize
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
r_{+}
at fixed
r
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial r_{+}}=0\implies r=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}r_{+}\approx 1.154r_{+}

The nucleation window is:

r_{+}\leq r\leq 1.154r_{+}

Derivations for Section 4: Barrow Entropy

8.1
Free Energy with Barrow Entropy

The Barrow entropy is:

\boxed{S_{B}=\left(\frac{A_{+}}{4}\right)^{1+\Delta}=\left(\frac{\pi r_{+}}{2}\right)^{1+\Delta}}

(8.1)

The corrected free energy:

\mathcal{F}_{B}=\mathcal{E}-TS_{B}=\frac{1}{4l}\left(2r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}-r^{2}\right)-\left(\frac{r_{+}}{2\pi l\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}\right)\left(\frac{\pi r_{+}}{2}\right)^{1+\Delta}

\boxed{\mathcal{F}_{B}=\frac{1}{4l}\left(2r\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}-r^{2}\right)-\frac{r_{+}^{2+\Delta}}{2^{2+\Delta}\pi^{\Delta}l(r^{2}-r_{+}^{2})^{1/2}}}

(8.2)

8.2
Heat Capacity with Barrow Entropy

Differentiate
S_{B}
with respect to
T
:

\mathcal{C_{B}}=T\left(\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial T}\right)_{r}=T\left[(1+\Delta)\left(\frac{\pi r_{+}}{2}\right)^{\Delta}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\partial r_{+}}{\partial T}\right]

From
T=\frac{r_{+}}{2\pi l\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}
, solve for
\frac{\partial r_{+}}{\partial T}
:

\frac{\partial r_{+}}{\partial T}=\frac{2\pi lr^{2}\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}{2r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}

Substitute:

\mathcal{C_{B}}=T\cdot(1+\Delta)\left(\frac{\pi r_{+}}{2}\right)^{\Delta}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{2\pi lr^{2}\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}{2r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}

Simplify using
T=\frac{r_{+}}{2\pi l\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}
:

\boxed{\mathcal{C_{B}}=\frac{2^{-\Delta}\pi^{1+\Delta}(1+\Delta)(r^{2}-r_{+}^{2})r_{+}^{1+\Delta}}{2r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}}}

(8.3)

8.3
Joule-Thomson Coefficient

From the first law
d\mathcal{E}=TdS_{B}-\mathcal{P}dV
with
V=\pi r^{2}
:

\mu_{B}=\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial\mathcal{P}}\right)_{H}=\frac{1}{C_{\mathcal{P}}}\left[T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_{\mathcal{P}}-V\right]

After extensive algebra using thermodynamic identities:

\boxed{\mu_{B}=\frac{2^{1+\Delta}\pi^{-\Delta}r_{+}^{1-\Delta}(r^{2}-2r_{+}^{2})}{(1+\Delta)(r^{2}-r_{+}^{2})}}
