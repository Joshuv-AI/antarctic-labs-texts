Network analysis of the relationships among activities of daily living disability, cognitive impairment, and depression in Chinese older adults
Authors: Xiaochen Feng, Yongqiang Wang, Yulin Chai, Kaidi Feng, Xin Wang, Ran Wang, Guangtong Wang, Sheng Luo, Yuqing Mi, Wei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the aging population in China continues to grow, Activities of Daily Living (ADL) disability, cognitive impairment, and depression have become prevalent health issues among older adults. These three factors are closely related, but the specific interactions between their symptoms have not been fully explored. This study aims to construct a network model that links ADL, cognitive function, and depression in older adults, and to identify the central and bridging symptoms within this network.MethodsThis study utilized data from 8,762 individuals aged 60 and above from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The ADL scale, the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) were employed to assess ADL, cognitive function, and depression. Network analysis was performed to examine the network structure, identify central and bridging symptoms, and assess the stability of the network of ADL, cognitive function, and depression. Additionally, comparisons of the networks based on gender, age, and chronic disease status were conducted.ResultsIn the network of ADL, cognitive function, and depression in older adults, the central symptoms identified were “Felt depressed” (CESD3, strength = 1.118), “Felt everything I did was an effort” (CESD4, strength = 1.018), and “Could not get on” (CESD8, strength = 0.969). The bridging symptoms were “IADL” (ADL2, bridge strength = 0.453), “BADL” (ADL1, bridge strength = 0.316), and “Orientation” (MMSE, bridge strength = 0.242). The strongest edge within the subgroups of nodes was observed between “Immediate recall” and “Delayed recall” (MMSE4–MMSE5, edge weight = 0.490), while the strongest edge between subgroups of nodes was between “IADL” and “Felt everything I did was an effort” (ADL2–CESD4, edge weight = -0.091). Furthermore, significant differences in network structures were found between genders (P < 0.05), and both global intensity and network structures differed significantly based on chronic disease status (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe central and bridging symptoms identified within the network could serve as the most effective intervention targets for addressing ADL disability, cognitive impairment, and depression in older adults. Clinical interventions should consider the differences in symptom expression related to gender and chronic disease status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25132-7.Keywords: Activities of daily living, Cognitive function, Depression, Network analysis, Older adults

Introduction

With the advent of the second baby boom, combined with rising life expectancy and declining birth rates, China now not only has the world’s largest elderly population but is also aging at the fastest pace globally [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the number of adults aged 65 and above in China is projected to reach 300 million by 2050, accounting for approximately 30% of the total population [3]. This demographic shift is expected to lead to a growing burden of age-related diseases. Among these, cognitive impairment—a prevalent condition in older adults—is characterized by memory decline, executive dysfunction, language or visuospatial deficits, and behavioral changes, and is widely regarded as a precursor to dementia [1]. A national survey conducted between 2015 and 2018 revealed that 15.5% of adults aged 60 and above had mild cognitive impairment, while 6.0% were living with dementia, and 3.9% had been diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease [4]. Li and colleagues developed a predictive model that estimated that the number of people aged 60 and over with dementia in China will climb to 48.98 million by 2050 [5].

Depression, a mental disorder defined by a persistently low mood [6], is notably prevalent in older Chinese adults, with an incidence rate four to five times higher than that of the general population [7]—a rate that surpasses observations in countries such as the United States, the United Kingdom, and Mexico [8]. A substantial body of research has established a strong, bidirectional association between depression and cognitive impairment [3]. Longitudinal studies consistently show that late-life depression increases the risk of cognitive decline, MCI, and all-cause dementia [9–11]. A systematic review and meta-analysis quantified this risk, finding that older adults with both MCI and depression have a 1.28-fold higher relative risk of progressing to dementia compared to those without depressive symptoms [12]. Conversely, depression is also common among individuals with dementia, affecting approximately 30% of patients [13]. Cognitive impairment is thus considered a key predictor of susceptibility to depression, as well as its prognosis and functional recovery in older adults [14–16]. Research has shown that after the age of 70, the co-occurrence of cognitive decline and low mood doubles every five years [17]. Evidence from cognitive tasks, neurophysiology, and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) further corroborates that individuals with depression are more susceptible to cognitive deficits [18–20]. The shared biological underpinnings of this comorbidity may include vascular diseases, inflammatory processes, glucocorticoid dysregulation, hippocampal atrophy, increased amyloid-β deposition, and deficiencies in neurotrophic factors [21].

An individual’s ability to perform Activities of Daily Living (ADL) is a critical measure of functional independence, encompassing both basic ADL (BADL), such as dressing and bathing, and instrumental ADL (IADL), such as household management and finances [22, 23]. A significant association between ADL disability, cognitive impairment, and depression is well-documented [24–26]. Individuals with cognitive impairment are more likely to require assistance with ADL than their cognitively intact peers [27, 28], partly because cognitive decline is often linked to frailty and reduced physical activity, which in turn elevates the risk of ADL disability [29]. Conversely, declining ADL function can restrict an individual’s mobility and social engagement, limiting access to information and potentially accelerating cognitive decline [30]. From a theoretical standpoint, Stress Theory posits that ADL disability acts as a chronic stressor, exacerbating mental health conditions and, by extension, cognitive function [31, 32]. Furthermore, research indicates that ADL difficulties increase the risk for depression, while depression predicts a subsequent increase in ADL disability, creating a vicious cycle [22, 33]. This aligns with Beck’s cognitive theory of depression, which suggests that stress-induced dysfunctional attitudes can lead to reduced activity and low mood, manifesting in physical conditions like ADL disability that further entrench the depressive state [34].

While existing research confirms the strong interrelationships between ADL, cognitive function, and depression, most studies have relied on latent variable models that use aggregate scores. This approach, however, overlooks the specific symptoms and functional items that constitute these constructs, as well as the intricate network of interactions among them. To develop more precise and effective interventions, it is crucial to explore these relationships at a more granular, dimensional level. Network analysis offers a powerful framework for this purpose. Based on the network theory of mental disorders, it conceptualizes psychopathology as a system of interacting components (symptoms), where nodes represent variables and edges represent their relationships [35]. Within this network, central nodes exert the most influence and are critical to the onset and maintenance of a disorder, while bridge nodes connect different clusters of symptoms and represent pathways of comorbidity [36]. By identifying these key nodes, network analysis can pinpoint strategic targets for intervention [37]. Although this methodology has been applied to study comorbidity in older adults [37–39], the specific network structure of ADL, cognitive function, and depression symptoms in Chinese older adults remains unexplored. Moreover, considering that advanced age, gender, and chronic diseases are significant risk factors for all three conditions [37, 40], the network structure may vary across these demographic and clinical subgroups. Therefore, this study will employ network analysis to: (1) characterize the network structure of ADL, cognitive function, and depression symptoms in older Chinese adults; (2) identify the central and bridge symptoms within this network; and (3) examine the stability and potential differences in the network across subgroups based on age, gender, and chronic disease status. The findings are expected to provide a theoretical foundation for the early prevention and integrated management of ADL disability, cognitive impairment, and depression, thereby promoting the holistic health and well-being of older adults.

Methods

Sample and data collection

The data for this study were sourced from the 2020 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The CHARLS survey covers 150 county-level units and 450 community (village) units across 28 provinces (including autonomous regions and municipalities directly under the central government) of China, providing a reliable representation of the overall situation of middle-aged and older adults in China. The CHARLS was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University Biomedical Ethics Committee (IRB00001052-11015), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) participants aged 60 years and above; (2) complete personal data for the participant. After applying the exclusion criteria, a total of 8,762 participants were included in the study.

Assessment of ADL

This study employed the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale formulated by Katz et al., which encompasses two components: Basic Activities of Daily Living (BADL) and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) [41]. The BADL component assesses six fundamental tasks, including dressing, bathing, eating, getting up, Toileting, and controlling urination and defecation. Meanwhile, the IADL component evaluates six more complex activities: doing housework, cooking, shopping, making a phone call, taking medication, and managing money. Tasks are rated from 0 to 3, where responses vary from “unable to complete” to “no difficulty,” resulting in a total score from 0 to 36 that reflects ADL competence—higher scores signify better proficiency. In this study, the ADL scale demonstrated good validity (KMO = 0.919, P < 0.001) and reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.859).

Assessment of cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) from the CHARLS questionnaire, which includes sections on orientation, calculation, visuoconstructional ability, immediate recall, and delayed recall [42, 43]. The cognitive function score ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating better cognitive function. In this study, the scale demonstrated good validity (KMO = 0.881, P < 0.001) and reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.850).

Assessment of depression

This study employed the revised Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) by Andresen et al. to assess depression [44]. The items evaluated include “Bothered by things,” “Had trouble keeping mind,” “Felt Depressed,” “Felt everything I did was an effort,” “Felt fearful,” “Sleep was restless,” “Felt lonely,” “Could not get on,” “Felt hopeful about the future,” and “Happy.” Responses are scored from 0 to 3, reflecting severity from “not at all” to “most of the time,” with reverse scoring for “Felt hopeful about the future” and “Happy.” Total scores vary from 0 to 30, with higher scores reflecting greater levels of depression. In this study, the scale demonstrated good validity (KMO = 0.875, P < 0.001) and reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.780).

Demographic characteristics

This study was characterized by demographic factors including age, gender, marital status, education level, and chronic disease status. In the network comparison, individuals aged 60–74 were categorized as young age, while those aged 75 and older were classified as advanced age.

Statistical analysis

Network structure

This study utilized SPSS version 26.0 and R software version 4.1.3 for data analysis. The means (M), standard deviations (SD), and predictability (Pr) of the ADL-2, MMSE-5, and CESD-10 scales were calculated. In the network analysis, nodes represent symptoms, edges describe the correlations between nodes, and subgroups of nodes refer to clusters of nodes [45]. The study includes subgroups of nodes related to ADL disability, cognitive impairment, and depression. The network structure was estimated using a Gaussian graphical model, adjusting for the influence of other nodes [46]. To account for the ordinal nature of the ADL-2, MMSE-5, and CESD-10 scales, the study followed the recommendations of Epskamp and Fried by estimating the regularized partial correlation network using the extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) graphical LASSO method [45]. This regularization process involved shrinking all edges and setting weak partial correlation coefficients to zero, thus eliminating erroneous connections and yielding a more robust and simplified network structure. The hyperparameter γ of the EBIC was set to 0.5 to balance sensitivity and specificity. The network layout was based on the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm and visualized using the R package “qgraph” [47]. In the network, blue edges represent positive correlations, red edges represent negative correlations, and the thickness of the edges reflects the strength of the correlation.

Central symptoms

To identify central symptoms within the network, four centrality metrics were calculated: strength, closeness, betweenness, and expected influence. Strength refers to the sum of the absolute weights of all edges connected to a node. Closeness refers to the inverse of the shortest path length from a node to all other nodes. Betweenness measures the importance of a symptom as a “connector,” based on the frequency with which the node appears on the shortest paths between all other nodes [48]. Expected influence is defined as the sum of the weights of all edges connected to a node.

Bridging symptoms

To explore the bridging symptoms that play a key role in connecting the three distinct subgroups of nodes, the bridging function of the R package “network” was used. The bridging centrality metrics employed in this study include bridging strength, bridging closeness, bridging betweenness, and bridging expected influence. Higher values of these metrics indicate a greater probability that the symptom will spread to other subgroups of nodes [49].

Network stability and accuracy

The stability and accuracy of the network model were assessed using the R package “bootnet” through three analyses. Firstly, the correlation stability (CS) coefficient was calculated using the bootstrap method with 1,000 iterations [50]. A CS coefficient greater than 0.25 is considered acceptable, with an ideal value above 0.55 [51]. Secondly, the accuracy of the edge weights was estimated using a nonparametric bootstrap method with 1,000 iterations. A narrower 95% confidence interval indicates higher accuracy of the edge weights [47]. Finally, the bootstrap difference test was used to assess whether there were significant differences between edge weights, strength, bridging strength, closeness, and expected influence, as well as between the bridging strength of pairs of nodes.

Comparative network analysis

The Network Comparison Test (NCT) package in R was used to compare network differences across gender (male/female), age (young age/advanced age), and chronic disease status (with/without disease). This method involved sampling 1,000 permuted subsamples (e.g., male vs. female, young age vs. advanced age, with vs. without chronic disease) to evaluate the global network strength and structural differences between the two networks [52].

Sensitivity analyses

To test the robustness of our main findings, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. First, considering that chronic diseases are a key factor leading to impairments in ADL, we assessed the stability of our conclusions after excluding this confounding factor. We created a subsample that excluded all individuals who reported any chronic disease at baseline. The main analyses were then repeated on this “healthy” subsample. Second, to verify the reliability of the network analysis results, we performed an analysis to examine the impact of our node selection strategy. Although the BADL and ADL dimensions used in our main analysis are well-established clinical constructs that facilitate interpretation at a higher theoretical level, we constructed a more granular network model as a supplement. Specifically, we replaced the original ‘BADL’ and ‘IADL’ dimension nodes with their respective constituent items (e.g., “bathing,” “dressing,” “shopping,” “managing money”), thereby constructing a network based entirely on the item level.

Results

Characteristics of participants

The study included 8,762 older adults with a mean age of 68.10 (SD = 6.04) years, of whom 4,310 (49.19%) were male. Most participants were married (n = 6,960, 79.43%), had completed a primary school education (n = 3,994, 45.58%), and reported at least one chronic disease (n = 7,585, 86.57%). The mean scores for ADL, cognitive function, and depression were 34.11 (SD = 4.00), 9.84 (SD = 4.34), and 9.09 (SD = 6.49), respectively. Network analysis

Network structure

The network analysis indicated a strong connection between the activities of daily living (ADL) and depression subgroups of nodes, while the cognitive function subgroup of nodes was relatively isolated, with fewer connections to other symptoms. Of the 136 possible edges, 99 (72.79%) were non-zero after regularization. The strongest positive edges were observed within their respective subgroups of nodes: between “Visuoconstructional ability” and “Delayed recall” (MMSE4–MMSE5, weight = 0.490); “BADL” and “IADL” (ADL1–ADL2, weight = 0.424); and “Felt hopeful about the future” and “Happy” (CESD9–CESD10, weight = 0.312) (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). The predictability of nodes ranged from 8.9% to 48.3% (M = 32.9%), indicating that, on average, neighboring nodes explained 32.9% of the variance in each node (Fig. 1a; Table 1).

a Network structure showing the relationships among ADL, cognitive function, and depression. b Centrality indices for all symptoms in the network

Central symptoms

As shown in Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 2, “Felt depressed” (CESD3) emerged as the most central node in the network with the highest strength centrality (1.118), followed by “Felt everything I did was an effort” (CESD4, strength = 1.018) and “Could not get on” (CESD8, strength = 0.969). Conversely, “Visuoconstructional ability” (MMSE3) was the least central node (strength = 0.382). This node also exhibited the lowest closeness (0.002) and betweenness (0) values, suggesting it plays a minimal role in connecting different parts of the network. Within the depression subgroups of node, “Felt depressed” (CESD3) also had the highest expected influence (EI = 1.058), highlighting its strong predictive power over other nodes.

Bridge symptoms and bridge connections

Bridge centrality indices are presented in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2. The analysis identified “IADL” (ADL2, bridge strength = 0.453), “BADL” (ADL1, bridge strength = 0.316), and “Orientation” (MMSE1, bridge strength = 0.242) as the most critical bridge symptoms connecting the ADL, cognitive function, and depression subgroups of nodes. The network contained 46 inter-subgroups of node edges; however, their connection strengths were considerably weaker than those of intra-subgroups of node edges. The strongest of these inter-subgroups of node connections was a negative edge observed between the ADL disability and depression subgroups of nodes, specifically linking “IADL” and “Felt everything I did was an effort” (ADL2–CESD4, weight = −0.091) (Supplementary Table 1).

Network stability and accuracy

The stability of the network was assessed using a case-dropping bootstrap procedure (Fig. 3a). The centrality stability (CS) coefficients for all indices (strength, bridging strength, closeness, expected influence) remained above 0.5 even after removing 70% of the sample. Notably, both strength and bridging strength demonstrated high stability with CS coefficients of 0.75, well above the recommended 0.25 threshold, indicating a robust network model. Furthermore, the bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for edge weights were narrow, suggesting the estimates were precise (Fig. 3b). Difference tests confirmed that the strength of “Felt Depressed” (CESD3), “Felt everything I did was an effort” (CESD4), and “Could not get on” (CESD8) was significantly greater than that of other nodes. Similarly, the bridging strength of “IADL” (ADL2), “BADL” (ADL1), and “Orientation” (MMSE1) was significantly higher than other nodes (Supplementary Figs. 2).

Comparative network analysis

To explore potential moderators, we compare network structures and global strength across gender, age, and chronic disease subgroups. The presence of chronic disease was associated with significant alterations in the network; participants with chronic diseases had a different network structure (M = 0.182, P = 0.01) and greater overall connectivity (Global Strength: 6.974 vs. 6.009; S = 0.966, P = 0.01) than those without. Network structure also differed significantly by gender (M = 0.095, P = 0.01), though global strength was comparable between males and females. In contrast, no significant differences in network structure or global strength were found between younger and older age groups.

Sensitivity analysis

We analyzed the subgroup of patients without chronic diseases and centrality indicators (Supplementary Fig. 3). This analysis showed that compared with the total population, the most central nodes in the two networks remained consistent. This finding further indicates that the core network structure identified in our study also exists in the relatively healthy elderly population. To test the robustness of our findings to the level of analysis, we constructed a supplementary item-level network (Supplementary Fig. 4). In this network, “Felt depressed” (CESD3) remained the most central node by strength, and “Orientation” (MMSE1) remained the primary bridge node (Supplementary Fig. 5). Furthermore, items originally grouped into BADL and IADL formed their own distinct subgroups of nodes. The overall pattern of connections between these item-level subgroups of nodes and the depression and cognitive subgroups of nodes was highly consistent with the dimension-level results of our main analysis. This consistency demonstrates that our primary findings are robust across different levels of analytical granularity.

Discussion

This study identified the network structure linking ADL, cognitive function, and depression among older adults in China. A stable network linking these factors was identified within this population, with central symptoms being “Felt depressed” (CESD3), “Felt everything I did was an effort” (CESD4), and “Could not get on” (CESD8). Additionally, “IADL” (ADL2), “BADL” (ADL1), and “Orientation” (MMSE1) were identified as bridging symptoms in this network. Furthermore, significant differences were found in the global strength and network structure among older adults with varying chronic disease statuses in China.

Central symptoms in the network of ADL, cognitive function, and depression

The study identified “Felt depressed” (CESD3) and “Could not get on” (CESD8) as central symptoms of the network, consistent with previous research. For example, network analyses of older adults with comorbidities related to depression and cognitive performance have shown that “Felt depressed” (CESD3) and “Could not get on” (CESD8) exhibit high intensity within the network [37]. Additionally, studies on depression and cognitive function in community-dwelling older adults have also highlighted “Felt depressed” (CESD3) as a central symptom [53]. Our findings also reveal “Felt everything I did was an effort” (CESD4) as another central symptom. These results are closely related to Chinese traditional culture. In Chinese families, older adults often play leadership and elder roles, tend to plan ahead for crises and potential risks, and often experience excessive worry, which leads to the emotional state of “Felt depressed” [54]. Moreover, Chinese older adults often hold fatalistic views, believing that factors such as health and economic conditions are predetermined. This mindset can result in a lack of confidence and motivation to change when facing negative life events or health problems, thus exacerbating feelings of “Could not get on” and “Felt everything I did was an effort” [53].

Bridging symptoms in the network of ADL, cognitive function, and depression

According to the network theory of mental illness, bridging symptoms can both cause and sustain comorbid mental health disorders or syndromes [35]. In the network of ADL, cognitive function, and depression in older adults, the strongest bridging symptoms were “IADL” (ADL2), “BADL” (ADL1), and “Orientation” (MMSE1). “Orientation” (MMSE1) has previously been identified as a bridging symptom between cognitive function and depression in older adults [37]. “Orientation” involves individuals’ awareness of time and space, and these temporal and spatial dynamics have been found to connect neural activity and mental processes [55]. Specifically, the ability to orient oneself in time and space is associated with brain regions such as the precuneus, posterior cingulate cortex, inferior parietal lobule, medial prefrontal cortex, lateral frontal cortex, and lateral temporal cortex [56]. Damage to these areas is often associated with an increased risk of ADL disability, cognitive impairment, and depression. Furthermore, “IADL” (ADL2) and “BADL” (ADL1) are also identified as bridging symptoms within the network. ADL disability is significantly related to various cognitive functions, such as executive function, attention, and memory in older adults [57]. This may be because aging affects the prefrontal cortex, leading to cognitive decline, loss of control in task execution, and decreased memory, which in turn increases the risk of problems in daily activities [58]. The “use it or lose it” theory suggests that ADL disability reduces daily activities and social interactions in older adults, thereby diminishing cognitive stimulation and accelerating cognitive decline [59]. Beck’s cognitive theory of depression posits that individuals with depressive tendencies often adopt dysfunctional attitudes in response to stress, leading to a decrease in motivation, a low mood, and clinical physical diseases such as ADL disability [33]. These symptoms, in turn, exacerbate depression, forming a vicious cycle [34]. Notably, we found that “IADL” had a stronger bridging effect than “BADL.” This may be because limitations in IADL tend to occur earlier than in BADL in older adults [60], and for Chinese older adults, maintaining important social roles is crucial. Restrictions in IADL may hinder older adults from fulfilling their social roles and reduce their participation in daily activities, such as shopping, using public transportation, and walking in the subgroups of node. A meta-analysis of 194 studies found that executive function, particularly abstract reasoning, concept formation, scene switching, processing speed, complex attention, and working memory, is primarily related to IADL, rather than BADL [61], supporting our findings.

Strongest edges within and across subgroups of nodes

The study also found that the strongest edge within the network lies within the cognitive function subgroups of node, specifically between “Immediate recall” and “Delayed recall” (MMSE4–MMSE5), which are positively correlated. A study on older adults in Brazil showed that very low levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol were related to poorer immediate and delayed recall [62], supporting our findings. We also found that the strongest edge across subgroups of nodes was between ADL and depression, specifically between “BADL” and “Felt everything I did was an effort” (ADL1–CESD4), which was negatively correlated. According to the theory of hopelessness in depression, negative life events make individuals more prone to helplessness and anxiety, ultimately leading to depressive feelings. BADL disability, as a negative event, impairs older adults’ ability to perform basic daily activities and serves as a stressor for the “Everything I did was an effort” negative emotion. A strong “Everything I did was an effort” feeling severely depleted the individual’s intrinsic motivation and energy, leading older adults to reduce or completely avoid BADL activities [63]. From the perspective of the “use it or lose it” theory [59], reducing BADL activities results in further decline in related muscle strength and coordination, thus perpetuating a vicious cycle of BADL disability and the negative emotion of “Everything I did was an effort.”

Network comparison analysis

This study identified significant differences in network structure between different genders, consistent with a previous network analysis of cognitive performance and depression symptoms in older adults in China. This finding may be attributed to traditional Chinese culture, where women often bear more familial responsibilities and are more likely to experience social withdrawal and other negative psychological phenomena [64], thus increasing the risk of ADL disability, cognitive impairment, and depression. Furthermore, no significant differences in global strength or network structure were observed between different age groups, suggesting that age has a relatively minor impact on the network. Finally, significant differences in global strength and network structure were found between older adults with chronic diseases and those without. Older adults with chronic diseases exhibited higher global network strength, which may be linked to more severe ADL disability and cognitive impairment, as well as the long-term pain and financial burden associated with chronic diseases, which may exacerbate depression [65, 66].

To our knowledge, this is the first network analysis of ADL, cognitive function, and depression symptoms in older adults in China, based on national data, making the findings more representative. This study aims to offer insights into potential clinical interventions and preventive strategies for improving the physical and mental health of older adults. Firstly, the results revealed unique associations between ADL, cognitive function, and depression, with stronger connections observed between ADL and depression compared to cognitive function. Secondly, the symptoms “Felt depressed” (CESD3), “Felt everything I did was an effort” (CESD4), and “Could not get on” (CESD8) were identified as central symptoms in the network of ADL, cognitive function, and depression, while “IADL” (ADL2), “BADL” (ADL1), and “Orientation” (MMSE1) were identified as bridging symptoms linking these three components. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for future targeted interventions aimed at improving the physical and mental health of older adults. Additionally, while only structural differences in the network were observed between genders, significant differences in global strength and network structure were noted between older adults with and without chronic diseases. These results suggest that intervention strategies should consider the chronic disease status of older adults. For instance, communities should conduct early screenings for ADL disability and cognitive impairment in older adults with chronic diseases and provide enhanced emotional support, which would be beneficial.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the inference of causal relationships between ADL, cognitive function, and depression. Although our findings provide a robust model of symptom associations, longitudinal studies are required to elucidate the temporal dynamics and causal pathways among these domains. Second, all measures were based on self-report questionnaires, which are susceptible to recall and social desirability biases. Future research could be strengthened by incorporating objective assessments or clinician-rated scales to validate these findings. Third, our analysis was primarily descriptive and did not extend to simulating interventions on specific nodes. Identifying which symptoms, if targeted, would most effectively influence the network is a key next step for translating these findings into clinical applications. Fourth, while we found that the network structure differed by chronic disease status, we did not conduct a granular analysis of which specific connections were altered. Furthermore, we did not stratify our analysis by specific conditions (e.g., dementia, diabetes, stroke), which likely have unique symptom network signatures. Finally, while our item-level sensitivity analysis supported the main findings, such highly granular networks can be complex and may include less stable or easily interpretable connections.

Conclusions

In conclusion, within the ADL, cognitive function, and depression network of older adults, the ADL and depression subgroups of nodes are closely linked, while the association between ADL and cognitive function subgroups of nodes is weaker, and the association between depression and cognitive function subgroups of nodes is even more tenuous. The symptoms “Felt Depressed,” “Felt everything I did was an effort,” and “Could not get on” may constitute the central manifestations of comorbid ADL disability, cognitive impairment, and depression in this population. Notably, “IADL,” “BADL,” and “Orientation” emerge as the most significant bridging symptoms within the network. Targeting these symptoms may help mitigate the incidence of ADL disability, cognitive decline, and depression in older adults. Furthermore, it is essential to recognize that substantial differences exist in the ADL-cognitive function-depression network based on gender and chronic disease status. Clinical interventions should be tailored to account for these gender- and chronic disease-related variations in symptom expression.
