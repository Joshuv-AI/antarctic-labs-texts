Similarities of neocortical deep layer Oprk1-positive neurons and claustrum neurons in connectivity and activity
Authors: Chao Fang, Huiqin Zhou, Michael Brecht, Hong Wang, Robert K Naumann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe claustrum (CLA) is densely connected to the cortex and plays a critical role in synchronized brain states, including sleep, anesthesia, and epilepsy. Although claustrum gene expression patterns differ from those of the cortex, a subset of deep-layer cortical neurons share the claustrum’s transcriptomic profile and marker genes, such as Oprk1 and Nurr1. However, little is known about the connectivity and function of these claustrum-like cortical (CLC) neurons. Using Oprk1-cre transgenic rats and cre-dependent viral tracing, we systematically compared the whole-brain connectivity of CLC and CLA neurons. We found that both CLC and CLA neurons are primarily connected to the cortex. However, while the claustrum shows widespread projections to midline and limbic cortices, CLC neurons project more densely to the sensory cortex. Both cell types show increased c-Fos expression in novel environments and synchronized activity under anesthesia. These findings suggest that CLC neurons form a parallel and complementary cortical microcircuit alongside the claustrum.Subject areas: neuroscience, systems neuroscience, neuroanatomy

Introduction

The claustrum is a slender nucleus-like structure that extends rostrocaudally in the frontal part of the cerebral cortex. Notably, the claustrum complex is extensively interconnected with the cortex, suggesting a role in modulating various cognitive, physiological, and pathophysiological states, including consciousness,1,2,3 pain,4,5,6,7,8 epilepsy,9,10,11 and sleep.12,13,14 Historically, the complexity of understanding claustrum function has been attributed to its secluded location under the cortex, which has made it challenging to specifically record and manipulate the activity of claustrum neurons. However, some of these challenges have been overcome with the introduction of various transgenic mouse models.13,15,16 Consequently, more recent studies have relied primarily on transgenic mice,17,18,19 while earlier studies of claustrum connections had focused on rats, cats, monkeys, and other mammals with larger brains.20,21,22,23 In rodents, most studies on claustrum connectivity have focused on the main part of the claustrum complex, the ventral claustrum,17,18,24,25 while connectivity of other claustrum subregions has received less attention.26,27 Despite these advances, the precise function of the claustrum remains unclear.

Surprisingly, commonly used claustrum gene markers, such as Nuclear Receptor Subfamily 4 Group A Member 2 (Nurr1, Nr4a2), Opioid Receptor Kappa 1 (Oprk1), and Latexin (Lxn), are not exclusively expressed in the rodent claustrum complex but are also present in the deep layers of the lateral and posterior neocortex.28,29,30,31 While the co-expression of a few genes in neurons located in different areas does not necessarily indicate that they belong to the same cell type, recent single-cell sequencing data show that the neurons expressing Oprk1, Nurr1, and Lxn in the deep layers of the mouse neocortex indeed share the same transcriptomic profile as claustrum neurons. This suggests that they may have a common developmental origin.29,32,33 In contrast to excitatory cortical neurons, which generally migrate in an inside-out pattern,34,35 claustrum neurons first migrate toward the cortical surface and then reverse course toward the ventricular side of the insular cortex.36 While claustrum neurons exhibit a unique migratory pattern, the question of whether they also migrate tangentially and disperse into the posterior neocortex remains debated.37,38 Several different terms have been used to refer to cortical neurons expressing claustrum gene markers, such as deep layer neurons (dLn), “claustrum-like” neurons, or CLA-Epd-CTX Car3 neurons.31,32,33,39 Here, we will use the term claustrum-like cortical (CLC) neurons. Using ethynyl deoxyuridine (EdU) labeling and fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) detection of Nurr1, we found that both claustrum and CLC neurons are mainly born on embryonic days E14.5 to E15.5.31 Otherwise, very little is known about CLC neuron development. A few previous studies have started to explore the efferent projections of CLC neurons,28,32,40 but there has been no systematic exploration of CLC connectivity.

In this study, we delineate the connectivity patterns and functions of CLC neurons in the rat brain. Specifically, we ask whether claustrum and CLC neuron projections and functions overlap, complement each other, or are distinct. To selectively target CLC neurons, we generated and characterized an Oprk1-cre rat line. We use cell-type specific virus tracing methods to label Oprk1+ neurons and their projections. Our results demonstrate that Oprk1+ CLC and claustrum neurons show similar connectivity patterns, in that both primarily project to and receive input from the cortex. However, we also found that the CLC neurons are mainly interconnected with sensory regions of the neocortex, whereas the claustrum neurons are much more strongly connected to midline cortical regions such as the prefrontal, cingulate, and retrosplenial cortices. Finally, both Oprk1+ claustrum and Oprk1+ CLC neurons show c-Fos activation when exposed to a novel environment and exhibit highly correlated activity patterns under isoflurane anesthesia.

Results

Claustrum and claustrum-like cortical neurons share a highly similar transcriptomic profile

Located in the anterior part of the cortex, the claustrum complex consists of the dorsal claustrum (dCL), the ventral claustrum (vCL), and the dorsal endopiriform nucleus (DEn).41 In the posterior part of the cortex, the claustrum complex is absent based on standard brain atlases (Figure 1A). However, earlier studies hinted that neurons in the posterior cortical deep layers share certain properties with claustrum neurons.28,40 Hence, we label them as claustrum-like cortical (CLC) neurons (Figure 1A). In this study, we aim to uncover the similarities and differences between the claustrum neurons and CLC neurons, focusing mainly on their mesoscopic connectivity profile. Both claustrum complex neurons and CLC neurons express common marker genes, such as Opioid Receptor Kappa 1 (Oprk1) (Figure 1B) or Latexin (Lxn) (Figure 1C), which are largely absent in other glutamatergic cortical cell types.28,40 Coronal and tangential sections reveal a degree of anatomical continuity between the claustrum complex and CLC neurons, as both are located mostly in the lateral part of the cortex. The claustrum complex is organized into distinct, elongated nuclei, yet claustrum-like cortical neurons are much more dispersed (Figures 1B and 1C). Whether the claustrum complex and CLC neurons represent a single cell type derived from a common developmental origin,29 remains an open question. Recent evidence supporting this view comes from comprehensive transcriptomic profiles of cell types in the entire mouse cerebral cortex.32,33 Based on these studies, the major glutamatergic cell type of the claustrum complex is CLA-EPd-CTX Car3 glutamatergic neurons (Figure 1D). Interestingly, this cell type is not strictly confined to the nuclei of the claustrum complex but instead distributed throughout the lateral parts of the cortex (Figure 1E). Thus, the claustrum complex and CLC neurons share a highly similar transcriptomic profile, but much less is known about their similarity in terms of connectivity and function.

Retrosplenial cortex-projecting claustrum-like cortical and claustrum neurons express Nurr1 and Oprk1

We first asked whether claustrum neurons share similar connectivity with other cell types in the cortex by using retrograde tracing to compare their projection patterns. The retrosplenial cortex (RSP) is an extensively studied projection target of the claustrum.42 To test whether neurons in the deep layers of the cortex also project to the RSP, we injected the retrograde tracer Fluoro-Gold (FG) into the RSP (Figure 2A). As expected, we found dense labeling of FG-labeled neurons in the claustrum.12,27,43 Interestingly, we also observed FG signals in the deep layers of posterior cortical regions (Figure 2A). In the anterior cortex, FG signals show a high density only in the ventral claustrum (vCL) (Figure 2B). In the posterior cortex, FG-labeled neurons are present in several regions, including primary sensory cortex (S1), secondary sensory cortex (S2), and secondary visual cortex (V2) (Figure 2C). To test whether FG-labeled RSP-projecting neurons in the posterior cortex express claustrum gene markers, we combined fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) for Nurr1 and Oprk1 with FG tracing. As expected, FG-labeled RSP-projecting neurons in the vCL express both Nurr1 and Oprk1 (Figure 2D). Surprisingly, we found that FG-labeled RSP projecting neurons in the posterior cortex also express Nurr1 and Oprk1 (Figure 2E). Quantification showed that in the vCL and deep layers of cortex, a large proportion of FG-labeled neurons co-expressed Nurr1 and Oprk1 (Figures S1A–S1C). In addition, there was no significant difference in the fraction of Nurr1+ and Oprk1+ FG-labeled cells between the vCL and deep layers of cortex (Figures S1B and S1C). Because Nurr1 is broadly expressed in multiple cortical cell types, we focused on Oprk1 as the more specific genetic marker and used an Oprk1-cre line to investigate claustrum-like cortical (CLC) neurons in subsequent experiments. In summary, these results suggest that claustrum neurons share projections to the RSP with a subset of deep layer posterior cortex neurons. Since the latter also express typical claustrum gene markers, we identify them as claustrum-like cortical (CLC) neurons.

Characterization of cre expression in Oprk1-cre rats

To explore the connectivity pattern of the CLC neurons, we generated an Oprk1-cre rat line (Figure S2). To characterize cre expression, we crossed Oprk1-cre rats with Rosa26-tdTomato (R26-tdT) reporter rats to generate Oprk1-cre::R26-tdT rats and compared Oprk1 and tdT expression using in situ hybridization (Figure S3A) as described previously.44 We found that Oprk1 and tdT are both expressed in the claustrum complex, including the dorsal claustrum (dCL), ventral claustrum (vCL), and dorsal endopiriform nucleus (DEn) (Figure S3B). We also found that Oprk1 and tdT are expressed in claustrum-like cortical (CLC) neurons (Figure S3C). In other brain areas with Oprk1 expression, tdT is also expressed. These include the olfactory tubercle, layer 2 (Tu2) (Figure S3D), the mediodorsal thalamic nucleus (MD) (Figure S3E), and the basolateral amygdala (BLA) (Figure S3F). Overall, the similar expression patterns of Oprk1 and tdT suggest that Oprk1-cre mediated recombination faithfully mirrors Oprk1 expression.

Retrograde tracing in Oprk1-cre rats shows that Oprk1+ claustrum-like cortical neurons project to retrosplenial cortex, prelimbic cortex, and medial entorhinal cortex

We investigated whether CLC neurons project to known claustrum projection targets by focusing on a few specific regions, including the RSP, prelimbic cortex (PL), and medial entorhinal cortex (MEC). We injected cre-dependent retrograde tracing viruses (AAV-Retro-DIO-EGFP or AAV-Retro-DIO-tdTomato) encoding a fluorescent protein into these areas in Oprk1-cre rats. In sections from the RSP-injected group, we observed retrogradely labeled Oprk1+ neurons in the claustrum, while retrogradely labeled Oprk1+ CLC neurons were present in posterior cortical regions (Figure 3A). In anterior sections, retrogradely labeled Oprk1+ neurons were concentrated in the vCL (Figure 3B), as observed before with FG tracing (Figure 2B). Similar to FG tracing from RSP in the posterior cortex (Figure 2C), AAV-Retro-DIO labeled Oprk1+ CLC neurons were also present in the deep layers of several posterior cortical regions, including S1, S2, and V2 (Figure 3C). Similarly, we observed retrogradely labeled Oprk1+ neurons in the vCL after PL injections. Surprisingly, we observed only a few retrogradely labeled neurons in sensory regions after PL injections but instead found the highest density of retrogradely labeled cells in the ectorhinal cortex (Ect) (Figure 3D). Following MEC injections, we found retrogradely labeled Oprk1+ CLC neurons in the vCL. Similarly, MEC-projecting Oprk1+ CLC neurons were present in the Ect (Figure 3E).

To investigate the distribution of Oprk1+ CLC neurons labeled by AAV-Retro-DIO injections in the RSP, PL, and MEC, we counted 2886 retrogradely labeled neurons in sections of the posterior cortex. We found that RSP-projecting CLC neurons were primarily located in S1, with approximately 35% of the neurons found in this region. A moderate proportion of labeled neurons was observed in Au2. For PL-projecting CLC neurons, the majority were located in the Ect, with a mean value of 60%. Smaller numbers of labeled neurons were present in primary auditory cortex (Au1), secondary auditory cortex (Au2), and perirhinal cortex (PRh). Similarly, most MEC-projecting CLC neurons were also located in the Ect, with a mean value of 23%. Additionally, a substantial number of retrogradely labeled neurons were identified in V1, V2, and the temporal association area (TeA) (Figure 3F).

Topography of claustrum-like cortical neurons projecting to prelimbic cortex, retrosplenial cortex, and medial entorhinal cortex

Since CLC neurons project to PL, MEC, and RSP, we aimed to reveal the differences between PL-projecting CLC neurons (CLCPL), MEC-projecting CLC neurons (CLCMEC), and RSP-projecting CLC neurons (CLCRSP). We performed dual viral retrograde tracing by injecting AAV-Retro-DIO-EGFP into the RSP and AAV-Retro-DIO-tdTomato into either the PL or MEC in Oprk1-cre rats. We found that Oprk1+ RSP-projecting vCL neurons (vCLRSP) are present in the “core” area of the vCL as described previously.43,45,46 They are surrounded by PL-projecting vCL neurons (vCLPL), but the vCLPL are more concentrated in the ventral part of the vCL. We note a similarity in topography in that in the posterior cortex CLCPL are present in the Ect, a more ventral part of the cortex, compared to CLCRSP, which are mainly located in the more dorsal somatosensory cortex (Figure 4A). Similarly, MEC-projecting vCL neurons (vCLMEC) surround vCLRSP, but are mostly located in the more ventral part of vCLRSP. Again, we found CLCMEC in a more ventral part of the cortex compared to CLCRSP (Figure 4B).

To better visualize the topographic patterns of CLCRSP, CLCPL, and CLCMEC, we prepared flattened hemispheres from two-color retrograde tracing experiments with virus injections in RSP and PL. In the anterior part, the vCL is visible as an elongated structure along the anterior-posterior axis. In the posterior cortex, CLC neurons labeled by retrograde tracing occupy a relatively wide region of the cortex (Figure 4C). CLCRSP are present in more dorsal regions of the cortex, mainly in S1, whereas CLCPL are located in more ventral parts of the cortex, mainly in Ect (Figure 4D). Retrograde tracing from MEC also labels vCL as an elongated structure in the anterior cortex, whereas the CLCMEC are present in more dispersed regions in the posterior cortex (Figure 4E). In the ventral claustrum, vCLRSP neurons are located in the “core” area, surrounded by a “shell” of vCLPL and vCLMEC neurons. However, vCLPL and vCLMEC are generally located in the more ventral parts of the claustrum compared to vCLRSP. Similarly, our data suggest that CLCPL and CLCMEC are present in more peripheral or “shell” areas of the posterior cortex, surrounding the more central CLCRSP “core” (Figure 4F).

Efferents of Oprk1+ claustrum-like cortical neurons mainly target the cortex

Since CLC neurons project to several areas targeted by claustrum neurons, we investigated the efferent projection patterns of the Oprk1+ CLC neurons using anterograde tracing. We injected cre-dependent anterograde tracing virus (AAV-DIO-EGFP) into the Au1 in Oprk1-cre rats. In a series of sections along the anterior-posterior axis, we observed widespread axonal fluorescence originating from Oprk1+ CLC neurons in several cortical areas (Figure 5A). We show examples of the Oprk1+ CLC neuron efferent signals in the secondary motor cortex (M2), RSP, S1, and Au1. This confirms the RSP as a projection target for the Oprk1+ CLC neurons, consistent with our retrograde tracing results (Figures 3A and 5B). Quantitative analysis showed that the Oprk1+ CLC neurons project widely in cortical areas, including frontal, cingulate, parietal, temporal, and occipital cortex. We observed the strongest signal in the temporal cortex, including Au1, Au2, TeA, and Ect. In the parietal cortex, the parietal association cortex (PtA) also showed comparable signals. In anterior and midline cortical areas, the fluorescence from axons was much weaker (Figure 5C). We found that a significantly larger proportion of Oprk1+ CLC neuron projections targeted posterior cortical areas compared to those targeting anterior cortical areas (89% vs. 11%, N = 4, p < 0.0001) (Figure 5D). A significantly larger proportion of Oprk1+ CLC neuron projections targeted the neocortex compared to extracortical projection targets (94% vs. 6%, N = 4, p < 0.0001) (Figure 5E). To directly compare the projection patterns of claustrum and CLC neurons in the cortex, we used anterograde tracing by injecting cre-dependent virus (AAV-DIO-Ruby) in the claustrum and AAV-DIO-EGFP in CLC neurons in Oprk1-cre rats (Figure 5F). Quantitative analysis revealed that Oprk1+ claustrum neurons and Oprk1+ CLC neurons project to different cortical regions. Specifically, Oprk1+ claustrum neurons preferentially target midline and lateral cortical areas, including the RSP, insular cortex (Ins), and PRh. In contrast, Oprk1+ CLC neurons project to cortical regions largely complementary to the claustrum projections, including somatosensory and auditory cortices (Figure 5G).

Deep layer extracortical projection neurons and Oprk1+ claustrum-like cortical neurons have different projection patterns

Since Oprk1+ CLC neurons show some similarities in connectivity to claustrum neurons, we suspected that Oprk1+ CLC neurons and other deep layer cortical excitatory neurons would differ in their projection patterns. To test this, we injected a combination of AAV-CamkIIα-mCherry to label excitatory neurons and AAV-DIO-EGFP to label Oprk1+ CLC neurons into the deep layers of Au1 (Figure 6A). Neurons labeled with AAV-CamkIIα-mCherry had dendrites extending toward the brain surface, whereas Oprk1+ CLC neurons did not show this morphological pattern (Figure 6B). Neurons labeled with AAV-CamkIIα-mCherry were mainly located in layers V and VI, whereas Oprk1+ CLC neurons were mainly located in layer VI (Figure 6C). We found that Oprk1+ CLC neurons project to several ipsilateral cortical areas, such as the medial parietal association cortex (MPtA), while the neurons labeled with AAV-CamkIIα-mCherry had no obvious projections to ipsilateral cortical areas (Figure 6D). Oprk1+ CLC neurons projected strongly within layers IV and VI but less strongly in layers I, II/III, and V, whereas neurons labeled with AAV-CamkIIα-mCherry showed much less axonal signal in any layer of the MPtA (Figures 6E and 6F). We found that neurons labeled with AAV-CamkIIα-mCherry project to the medial geniculate nucleus (MG), but the Oprk1+ CLC neurons showed no axonal signal in the thalamus (Figures 6G and 6H). In summary, Oprk1+ CLC neurons are located mainly in layer VI and project widely in the cortex, mainly to cortical layer IV and layer VI. They show no projections to the thalamus. In contrast, deep layer cortical neurons labeled with AAV-CamkIIα-mCherry were most likely extracortical projection neurons. Their projections in the cortex do not show significant lateral spread but instead target extracortical regions such as the thalamus (Figure 6I).

Neurons providing inputs to Oprk1+ claustrum-like cortical neurons are located in the posterior cortical regions

Since the Oprk1+ CLC neurons project broadly to the cortex, we next asked whether presynaptic inputs to Oprk1+ CLC neurons also originate mainly within the cortex. To this end, we used rabies virus tracing and injected both the cre-dependent helper virus (FLEX-TVA-mCherry) and pseudotyped EGFP-expressing rabies virus (RV-EnvA-ΔG-EGFP) in Au1 of Oprk1-cre rats. In a series of sections along the anterior-posterior axis, we observed more RV-labeled neurons in posterior cortical areas (Figure 7A). Figure 7B shows examples of co-labeled starter cells in Au1 and RV-labeled monosynaptic inputs from S1, vCL, and Au2. Quantification of the number of EGFP-positive monosyptically labeled cells revealed that S1 provides the largest presynaptic input to the CLC neurons in Au1, accounting for approximately 29% of the total, followed by significant inputs from Au1 and Au2, contributing an average of 18% and 20%, respectively (Figure 7C). We found that 83% of the neurons providing inputs to the Oprk1+ CLC neurons were located in posterior cortical areas, compared to 17% located in anterior cortical areas (N = 5, p < 0.0001, Figure 7D). Generally, 84% of the neurons providing inputs to Oprk1+ CLC neurons were located in the neocortex, while 16% of the presynaptic inputs were located in other brain areas (N = 5, p < 0.0001, Figure 7E).

Claustrum and claustrum-like cortical neurons show strong novelty induced c-Fos staining and synchronised burst-like activity transients under anesthesia

Since both Oprk1+ claustrum and Oprk1+ CLC neurons exhibit similar connectivity profiles, we wondered whether they would also be activated by similar stimuli. Although the general function of the claustrum remains debated, previous research reported robust increases in c-Fos expression in the claustrum following exposure to a novel environment,47,48 in particular in claustrum neurons projecting to the medial entorhinal cortex (MEC). To determine if novel environment exposure also induces c-Fos expression in MEC-projecting Oprk1+ CLC neurons, we used Oprk1-cre rats and injected Retro-DIO-tdTomato into the MEC to label MEC-projecting Oprk1+ CLC neurons (CLCMEC) and MEC-projecting Oprk1+ claustrum neurons (vCLMEC). Rats were either exposed to an open field (OF) test in the novel environment group or remained in their home cages in the control group. In the control group, animals showed minimal c-Fos expression in the vCLMEC (Figure 8A). However, following exposure to a novel environment, we observed more co-labeling of vCLMEC with c-Fos (Figure 8B). In the novel environment group, there was a notable increase in the total number of vCLMEC neurons co-labeled with c-Fos (N = 4, p = 0.0011, Figure 8C). In addition, we observed a significant increase in the proportion of co-labeled vCLMEC neurons, from 2% in the control group to 10% in the novel environment group (N = 4, p = 0.0014, Figure 8D). Similarly, in the control group, animals showed minimal c-Fos expression in CLCMEC neurons (Figure 8E). However, following exposure to a novel environment, we observed more co-labeling of CLCMEC neurons with c-Fos (Figure 8F). The novel environment group showed a significant increase in the total number of CLCMEC neurons co-labeled with c-Fos (N = 4, p = 0.0305, Figure 8G), along with an increase in the proportion of co-labeled CLCMEC neurons, from 2% in the control group to 14% in the novel environment group (N = 4, p = 0.0003, Figure 8H).

Claustrum neurons show highly synchronous neural activity under anesthesia.49 Therefore, we investigated whether Oprk1+ claustrum neurons and Oprk1+ CLC neurons both show synchronous neural activity under different levels of anesthesia. We used multichannel fiber photometry to simultaneously record calcium signals in both Oprk1+ claustrum and Oprk1+ CLC neurons with injections of cre-dependent virus (AAV-DIO-jGCaMP7s) in Oprk1-cre rats. After 2 weeks, optical fibers were implanted and acutely connected to the recording system (Figure 8I). AAV-DIO-jGCaMP7s expression and fiber tracks were verified in brain sections after recording (Figure 8J). The calcium signals from Oprk1+ claustrum and Oprk1+ CLC neurons were recorded under different levels of isoflurane anesthesia, including 3%, 2%, 1.5%, 1%–0%. Calcium signals of Oprk1+ claustrum and Oprk1+ CLC neurons showed similar patterns under anesthesia levels from 3% to 1.5% isoflurane (Figure 8K). The signals in both channels showed irregular activity when the anesthesia level was changed to 1%. When we reduced isoflurane anesthesia to 0%, we rarely observed large isolated calcium transients (Figure 8K). We calculated the average correlation coefficient between the signals from Oprk1+ claustrum and Oprk1+ CLC neurons at each anesthesia level. As the level of anesthesia decreased, the average correlation decreased from 0.83 (3%), 0.86 (2%), 0.68 (1.5%) to 0.34 (1%), and 0.15 (0%), respectively (Figure 8L). Recordings were also performed two weeks after fiber implantation, and similar synchronization of signals between the claustrum and CLC neurons was observed, indicating that this phenomenon is not limited to acute preparations.

Discussion

Claustrum neurons exhibit high similarity in gene expression with a subset of cortical neurons (Figure 1). However, very little is known about these claustrum-like cortical (CLC) neurons in terms of their morphology, connections, or function. Here, we show that CLC neurons project to several major output regions of the claustrum, including the retrosplenial, prefrontal, and entorhinal cortex (Figures 2, 3, and 4). Investigating the efferent and afferent projections of CLC neurons revealed that they are mainly connected to nearby sensory areas of the neocortex. In contrast, such connections are sparse in the claustrum, suggesting that claustrum and CLC neurons project to complementary regions of the cerebral cortex (Figures 5, 6, and 7). We provide further evidence of similarities between claustrum and CLC neurons by demonstrating that both have elevated c-Fos expression when exposed to a novel environment and show synchronous activity under anesthesia (Figure 8).

The projection targets, inputs, and functional properties of CLC neurons were previously largely unexplored. Using retrograde tracing techniques, Arimatsu’s studies have shown that Lxn-positive neurons project to the motor and somatosensory cortex.28,40 These results were confirmed in a recent study by labeling small numbers of “claustrum-like” neurons in the cortex and reconstructing their morphology and axonal projection patterns.32 Our investigation shows that Oprk1+ CLC neurons are broadly distributed in posterior cortical areas, consistent with previous studies that used Lxn and Nurr1 as marker genes for CLC neurons.28,31,40 Using anterograde, retrograde, and transsynaptic tracing techniques, we characterize projection targets and cortical inputs to CLC neurons to expand our understanding of this unique cell type.

The claustrum is best known for its extensive connections with the neocortex.17,18,27 In rodents, the strongest projections of claustrum neurons target midline or parahippocampal cortical regions such as the prelimbic cortex (PL), retrosplenial cortex (RSP), and medial entorhinal cortex (MEC).12,18,47 In contrast, claustrum connections with subcortical structures are much less prominent.50,51 Our results show that CLC neurons also have bidirectional connections with multiple cortical areas. We used retrograde tracing techniques to demonstrate that CLC neurons also project to the RSP, PL, and MEC. Similarly, the CLC also shows primarily interconnections with the cortex and have much less connectivity with subcortical areas.

Although the claustrum and the CLC neurons have several similarities, our study revealed certain distinctions. One notable feature of the claustrum is its strong intrinsic connections.52,53 Our investigation revealed limited connections between the CLC neurons and the claustrum. However, CLC neurons may still have intrinsic projections similar to those of the claustrum. Notably, although CLC neurons project to PL and RSP, the number of axons targeting these regions is limited compared to claustrum projections to these areas.17,18,27 Instead, CLC neurons project much more strongly to sensory cortical areas than claustrum neurons do. Previous studies have shown that axons of the mouse claustrum neurons project most densely to layers V and II/III of the cortex while avoiding layer IV.18,54,55 In contrast, our findings indicate that CLC neurons predominantly project to layers IV and VI of the cortex. Interestingly, claustrum projections in cats predominantly target layers IV and VI in sensory regions.21 Layer IV of the cortex is one of the major cortical projection targets of the thalamus. Since the CLC neurons also project strongly to layer IV, they are well positioned to modulate the afferent signals from the thalamus. In summary, we compare claustrum and CLC neurons and found both similarities and complementary connectivity patterns. These results warrant further investigations into the development, evolution, and functional properties of CLC neurons.

Although the birthdates of claustrum neurons are well characterized in rodents and primates, the developmental origins of CLC neurons are not. In mice, most claustrum neurons are generated between E11.5 and E12.5,36,56 in rats between E14.5-E15.5,31,57 and in monkeys between E48 and E55.58 By comparing Nurr1-positive neurons in the claustrum and the cerebral cortex, we found that both ventral claustrum neurons and CLC neurons are mainly born on E14.5 to E15.5 in rats.31 Previously, Puelles’ study had suggested that both claustrum and CLC neurons are generated in the lateral pallium.29 From there, tangential migration could lead to the dispersion of neurons with claustrum-like transcriptomic identity in the cortical sheet. If so, it should be possible to observe the migration of CLC neurons from their presumed origin in the lateral pallium to their final locations throughout the cortex.29,37 Whether this indeed takes place remains debated,38 though a recent study has shown that claustrum neurons do follow a unique developmental migration pattern.36

The last two decades have seen explosive growth in studies of the claustrum, although arguably a comprehensive understanding of claustrum function remains as elusive as it was at the beginning of this period.1 In our view, significant advances point to a role for the claustrum in modulating synchronized brain states.59 Thus, claustrum neuron activity likely impacts diverse cognitive, physiological, and pathophysiological states, including consciousness, sleep, and epilepsy. Furthermore, claustrum neurons may serve as a critical switchboard for transitions between brain states. Supporting evidence comes from studies demonstrating robust increases in c-Fos expression in claustrum neurons when animals were exposed to novel environments.47,48 Interestingly, we observed comparable novelty-induced increases in c-Fos levels in both CLC and claustrum neurons, strengthening the view that they may perform similar functions within distinct cortical circuits.

Several studies indicate that the claustrum plays a role in regulating brain function during anesthesia.49,60,61 Under isoflurane anesthesia, the claustrum shows dynamic calcium activity that changes in a dose-dependent manner. Higher amplitude neural activity occurs under deep anesthesia, while lower amplitude neural activity occurs under low levels of anesthesia.49 Electrical stimulation of the claustrum during isoflurane anesthesia can induce a deeper level of anesthesia.60 Although the function of claustrum neurons under anesthesia remains unclear, a recent study revealed that high levels of anesthesia enhance the synchronization between the claustrum and cortex.61 In this study, we simultaneously recorded calcium activity from Oprk1+ claustrum and Oprk1+ CLC neurons under isoflurane anesthesia. Depending on the isoflurane concentration, we observed calcium transients of different frequencies and amplitudes in both claustrum and CLC neurons. This activity was highly synchronized between claustrum and CLC neurons, with the strongest correlation at higher concentrations of isoflurane. Thus, both claustrum and CLC neurons may contribute to the regulation of synchronous brain activity under anesthesia. Unlike novelty-induced c-Fos staining, which also reports increases in neural activity, fiber photometry recordings are most sensitive to the synchronous firing of large numbers of neurons, a phenomenon often observed under anesthesia and during sleep. In contrast, studies of CLC neuron activity during animal behavior would benefit from recording techniques with single-cell resolution. A challenge for such recordings, as well as for optogenetic or chemogenetic perturbations, is the broad distribution of CLC neurons throughout the cerebral cortex. However, a recent study showed that chemogenetically manipulating a small subset of claustrum neurons reduced stress-induced anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in rodents.62 Since NR4A2/NURR1-positive deep layer excitatory neurons are enriched for genetic variants associated with major depressive disorder in the human cerebral cortex,63 studying the circuit function of CLC neurons in animal models of depressive disorders would be interesting. In summary, our study supports the idea that claustrum and CLC neurons are fundamentally similar, albeit embedded in distinct, complementary cortical circuits.

Limitations of the study

Our study primarily relies on viral tracing methods, which have certain inherent technical limitations. For example, despite using relatively small injection volumes, it was usually impossible to investigate local axonal projections very close to the injection site. Studies using random labeling of single neurons are better suited to this task.32 Consequently, our tracing approaches did not allow us to determine whether CLC neurons show a comparable degree of intrinsic connectivity to that observed in claustrum neurons. Further, although the transcriptomic similarity between claustrum and CLC neurons forms the basis of our study, the relationship between the gene expression patterns of these neurons and their function remains largely unexplored. For instance, dynorphin is the main peptide that binds to Kappa opioid receptors; thus, it would be interesting to explore the origin and functional relevance of dynorphin inputs to CLC neurons. In summary, the functional characterization of CLC neurons remains preliminary and requires further studies, for example, by exploring the functional modulation of the sensory cortex by CLC neurons during sleep or in novel environments.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

All procedures were performed according to the institutional guidelines on animal welfare and approved by the local institution in charge of experiments using animals as detailed below. Female and male adult SD rats (obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) were used in the study (Animal Care and Use Committee at the Shenzhen Institute of Advanced Technology (SIAT), Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS), China; permit number SIAT-IACUC-20210607-NS-NBJZX-ROBERT NAUMANN-A1706-01).

The Oprk1-ires-cre (Oprk1-cre) knock in rat line was generated by Beijing Biocytogen by inserting an ires-Cre cassette in the 3’UTR region of the Oprk1 gene locus in order to mimic the endogenous expression of the Kappa opioid receptor gene using EGE™ technology (Figure S2). The founder was confirmed with long range PCRs and southern blots. It was backcrossed for more than three generations with wild type SD rats before use in the experiments. All Oprk1-cre rats were kept on a SD background and heterozygous animals were used for experiments.

The cre-dependent tdTomato reporter rat line was a generous gift of Beijing Biocytogen. This line was generated by inserting a pCAG-loxP-3X STOP-loxP-tdTomato-WPRE-bGHpA cassette in between the first and second exons of the Rosa26 site using EGE™ technology.

Method details

Genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from ear biopsies using mouse genotyping kit (AD501, Transgen Biotech, China). Genotyping PCR was performed using 2×TransDirect® Mouse Genotyping SuperMix from the genotyping kit.

Surgical procedures

The location of the injection sites was mapped through comparison with a rat brain atlas.64 All surgical injections were performed using a nanoliter microinjection Pump (R-480, RWD) with glass pipettes. Before surgical procedures, general anesthesia was induced with 5% isoflurane (RWD Cat#R510-22-10). During surgery, rats were anesthetized with a continuous flow of oxygen and isoflurane (1.5-3%). The bone surface of cranium was leveled by measuring vertical coordinates between bregma and lambda, and between the left and right hemisphere. A small craniotomy was made above the target region in the left hemisphere. Ventral-dorsal depth of each target region was measured from the surface of the pia. A glass pipette was lowered to target region and remained in place for 5 minutes before injection of 300 nl AAV. The injection rate was 0.5 nl/s for all experiments. After each injection, the pipette remained in place for 5 minutes before retracting the pipette.

Fluorogold (Fluorochrome Inc., Englewood, CO, USA) was dissolved in deionized distilled water to make up a 4% solution. After injection of 100 nl FG, animals were returned to their home cages for 5 days after the stereotaxic injection to allow retrograde transport of Fluorogold.

For DIO/FLEX virus expression, cre-dependent inversion of the coding region is required for expression to occur.65 Animals were maintained for 14 days after the stereotaxic injection to allow retrograde transport of virus. For rabies virus experiments, 300 nl helper virus FLEx-TVA-mCherry was injected in the primary auditory cortex (Au1). After 14 days, a second injection of 300 nl pseudotyped rabies virus (Rb-EnvA-ΔG-EGFP) was performed at the same location. For fiber photometry experiments, 200 nl AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-jGCaMP7s was injected in the claustrum and primary sensory cortex (S1). Detailed information about fluorogold and virus injection coordinates is provided in Table S1. A list of brain region abbreviations is provided in Table S2.

Tissue preparation

Animals were anesthetized using isoflurane, and then euthanized with an intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of pentobarbital. Animals were then perfused transcardially with first 0.1 M phosphate buffered saline solution (PBS), followed by 4% formaldehyde, from paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (4% PFA). For in situ hybridization (ISH), animals were perfused by 0.1 M DEPC (diethyl pyrocarbonate)-PBS, followed by 4% PFA. Brain tissues were dissected and fixed with 4% PFA overnight. For cryoprotection, brain tissues were put into a 30% sucrose solution in DEPC-PBS. Brain tissue was ready for sectioning when it sank to the bottom. Brains were embedded by OCT compound, and 50 μm thick coronal sections were prepared. For flattened cortical sections, brain tissue was dissected immediately after perfusion. Subcortical structures were removed, the cortex was gently flattened between two glass slides, and then fixed in 4% PFA overnight. After cryoprotection, flattened cortex was sectioned into 80 μm thick tangential sections.

In situ hybridization

In situ hybridization (ISH) was performed following established protocols.31,66 To prepare riboprobes, rat total brain RNA was used to synthesize cDNA using the EasyScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Transgen Biotech). The probe was amplified from the cDNA, subsequently cloned into a pEASY-Blunt Zero backbone (Transgen Biotech), and validated through sequencing. The antisense digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled riboprobes were synthesized following the protocol provided by the manufacturer (Roche). Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S3.

Tissue Sectioning and Hybridization: Cryosections of 50 μm thickness were prepared for in situ hybridization. DIG-labeled riboprobes were used for the hybridization step, which was conducted overnight at 65°C without proteinase K treatment. Following hybridization, the sections underwent the following sequential washing steps: Twice in 2xSSC/50% Formamide/0.1% N-lauroylsarcosine at 65°C for 20 min each. Twice in 2xSSC/0.1% N-lauroylsarcosine at 37°C for 20 min each. Twice in 0.2xSSC/0.1% N-lauroylsarcosine at 37°C for 20 min each.

Detection and Visualization: Sections were blocked in MABT/10% goat serum/1% Blocking reagent (Roche, Cat# 11096176001) and incubated overnight with sheep anti-DIG-AP (1:1000, Roche Cat# 11093274910). Staining was performed using NBT/BCIP in NTMT solution until satisfactory intensity was reached. The staining reaction was stopped with 10 mM EDTA.

Fluorescent In situ Hybridization (FISH): Following the hybridization and washing steps in FISH, the sections were incubated with sheep anti-DIG-POD (1:1000, Roche Cat# 11207733910) to detect the DIG-labeled probes. Signal amplification was then performed using tyramide signal amplification (TSA) with biotin-tyramide (ApexBIO, Cat#A8011). For the TSA staining procedure, the sections were equilibrated in TSA buffer (10 mM imidazole in PBS) and then incubated in the TSA staining solution containing 2 μg/ml biotin-tyramide, 0.001% H2O2, and TSA buffer in the dark for 30 minutes. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with Streptavidin-Cy2 (1:1000, Jackson ImmunoResearch) for detection of the biotin-labeled probes. After thorough washing to remove excess staining solution, the sections were mounted using Eukitt® Quick-hardening mounting medium (Sigma Aldrich) for preservation and subsequent visualization under microscopy or imaging analysis.

Antibody characterization

Sections were processed for immunohistochemistry by incubating sections in blocking buffer consisting of 0.1 M PBS and 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, free-floating sections were incubated overnight with the primary antibodies in 0.1 M PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.5% Triton-X (PBS-X). For Fluorogold antibody staining, we use primary antibodies against Fluorogold (Anti-Fluorogold, Fluorochrome Inc., Englewood, CO, USA; 1:500). Next, sections were visualized with fluorescent secondary antibodies, Goat anti-rabbit cy3 (Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc.), 1:1000 diluted in PBS-X and then mounted on gelatinized slides. For c-Fos antibody staining, we used the c-Fos antibody (Synaptic Systems, Cat. No. 226 008; 1:500) and a secondary antibody, Goat anti-rabbit Cy2 (Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc.), diluted 1:1000.

Novel environment test

Several studies have reported an increase in c-Fos expression in the claustrum following exposure to a novel environment.47,48 In our study, a total of 8 rats (4 male, 4 female) were used in the experiments after receiving Retro-DIO-tdTomato virus injections in the medial entorhinal cortex (MEC). Following a 2-week habituation period in their home cages, the rats were subjected to the open field (OF) test. Behavioral experiments were conducted between 9 A.M. and 3 P.M. To habituate the rats to the experimenter, they were handled in two 5-minute sessions, twice daily, over a 2-day period. These handling sessions were spaced 3 hours apart.48 The OF apparatus, a 30 cm × 50 cm open-top box, was sterilized with 50% ethanol and water prior to each test and between each rat. Rats (2 male, 2 female) were randomly selected for the OF test, gently placed in the center of the apparatus, and allowed to explore for 10 minutes before being returned to their home cages. The other 4 rats (2 male, 2 female), as a control group remained in their home cages throughout the OF experiment. Following the test, all rats were left undisturbed for 120 minutes and sacrificed for tissue collection.

Fiber photometry recordings

Population neuronal activity was recorded with a fiberphotometry recording system (SingleColorMultiChannel v2.0, ThinkerTech Nanjing Bioscience Inc). In total, 5 rats were used for fiber photometry recording during anesthesia. The dual-channel fiber photometry experiments were performed acutely, with optical fibers implanted and recordings conducted on the same day. The optical fibers were securely positioned and fixed in place with dental cement prior to the start of recordings. In brief, a 470 nm light was used for excitation and the optic power at the fiber tip was 50-100 μW. The tips of the optical fibers were targeted to the same coordinates as used for the injections of AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-jGCaMP7s. Recordings were performed under isoflurane anesthesia. Then the concentration of isoflurane was adjusted from 3%, 2%, 1.5%, 1%, 0% during a continuous recording, with each stage lasting 10 min. Throughout the recordings, body temperature was maintained at ∼37°C using a temperature control pad. While under anesthesia, the rats’ heads were fixed, and upon discontinuation of isoflurane (0%), the animals were gently removed from the fixation apparatus. Data analysis was performed using custom MATLAB R2022a scripts (Thinker Tech. Nanjing Bioscience Inc). To measure the fluorescence change, deltaF/F (dF/F) was calculated by the following formula: dF/F=Fsignal-F0/F0. Where the F0 is the mean basal signal value and Fsignal is the fluorescence signal during recording. Pearson correlation values were measured between two channels for each anesthesia stage in MATLAB R2022a, using the corrcoef function.

Light and fluorescence microscopy

All chromogenic and fluorescent stainings were imaged at 10x or 20x magnification on a slide scanner (BX61VS, Olympus, Japan). The fluorescent images were acquired in monochrome and color maps were applied to the images post acquisition. Post hoc linear brightness and contrast adjustment were applied uniformly to the image under analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The analysis of all data was performed using the unpaired t-test or the unpaired Welch’s t-test as specified in the figure legends using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM and p values <0.05 were considered significant (p < 0.05 (∗), p < 0.01 (∗∗), p < 0.001 (∗∗∗), and p < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗).

Image analysis

All images were analyzed using ImageJ software. For quantification of FG+ cells co-labeled with Nurr1 or Oprk1, cell counts were performed using the “multi-point tool” on 8 coronal sections (0.96 mm intervals) spanning from 1.8 mm to –4.92 mm relative to bregma. For retrograde tracing, cell counts of Retro-DIO EGFP/tdTomato-labeled CLC were performed on 6 coronal sections (0.96 mm intervals) spanning from -3.0 mm to -7.8 mm relative to bregma, from 4-5 brains. RV-EGFP-labeled cells were counted on 12 coronal sections (0.96 mm intervals) spanning from 2.76 mm to -7.8 mm relative to bregma, from 5 brains. For c-Fos cells co-labeled with MEC-projecting vCL neurons, counts were conducted on 4 coronal sections (0.96 mm intervals) spanning from of 2.76 mm to -0.12 mm relative to bregma. For c-Fos+ cells colocalized with MEC-projecting CLC, counts were made from 8 coronal sections (0.96 mm intervals) spanning from -1.08 mm to -7.8 mm relative to bregma, from 8 brains.

For anterograde tracing with AAV-DIO-EGFP injections, fluorescence density was normalized and measured on 12 coronal sections (0.96 mm intervals) spanning from 2.76 mm to -7.8 mm relative to bregma, from 4 brains. For anterograde tracing with AAV-DIO-EGFP and AAV-DIO-Ruby injections, fluorescence intensity was normalized and measured on 3 coronal sections (1.92 mm intervals), spanning from -1.08 mm to -4.92 mm relative to bregma, from 4 brains. For AAV-DIO-EGFP and CamkII-mCherry tracing, fluorescence intensity was quantified using the “Plot Profile” tool in ImageJ. Average fluorescence intensity was measured in three columns per section, from 3 brains, with the fluorescence profile smoothed by averaging every 18 μm.
