Fractional Ridge Regression
Sihyung Park, Department of Statistics, North Carolina State University

Abstract
Regularization methods such as the Lasso combine shrinkage and variable selection, but strong penalization drives their solutions toward the null model. We introduce Fractional Ridge (Fridge) regression, a penalized regression method that instead shrinks toward models of a prespecified target size. Its penalty vanishes whenever the number of nonzero coefficients does not exceed that target. This behavior connects continuous shrinkage with best subset selection. We develop an efficient recursive penalty-evaluation algorithm, coordinate descent updates, and a predictor-corrector algorithm for tracing solution paths. Under suitable conditions, we establish asymptotic normality, the oracle property, and convergence of global minimizers to best subset solutions under extreme regularization. Simulation studies demonstrate competitive prediction and variable selection across a range of signal strengths and predictor correlations. An application to riboflavin production data illustrates how Fridge selects a small set of predictors whose leading coefficients agree with signals prioritized by a Bayesian method developed specifically for genetic fine-mapping.



Keywords:
Adaptive penalization; Best subset selection; Nonconvex optimization; Oracle property; Solution paths; Variable selection.

1
Introduction

Regression with many predictors requires balancing the information retained in a model against the variability of its parameter estimates. Variable selection addresses this problem by excluding predictors, while shrinkage reduces the magnitude of their coefficients. Best subset selection, ridge regression, and the Lasso take different positions along this trade-off, motivating methods that combine sparse modeling with selective shrinkage
(
Hastie et al. 2009
, Chapter 3)
.

For a response vector
\mbox{\bf y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and design matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, best subset selection solves

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathrm{BSS}}\in{\mathop{\rm arg\,min}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|\mbox{\bf y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq k,

(1)

where
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}
counts nonzero coefficients. The selected coefficients are fitted by least squares without further shrinkage, but small changes in the data can change the selected subset and increase prediction variability
(
Breiman 1996
)
. Recent advances have expanded the range of problems for which subset selection is computationally practical
(
Bertsimas et al. 2016
,
Zhu et al. 2020
)
; nevertheless, the statistical trade-off between sparsity and stability remains.

Ridge regression addresses instability by adding the penalty
\lambda\sum_{j}\beta_{j}^{2}
to the residual sum of squares
(
Hoerl and Kennard 1970
)
. It stabilizes estimation with correlated predictors and gives a unique solution for
\lambda>0
, including when
p>n
, but generally retains all predictors. The Lasso uses
\lambda\sum_{j}|\beta_{j}|
to combine shrinkage and variable selection in a convex optimization problem
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
. Related approaches include bridge regression, with penalties
\sum_{j}|\beta_{j}|^{q}

(
Frank and Friedman 1993
)
, and the nonnegative garrote, which shrinks preliminary coefficient estimates through constrained scaling factors
(
Breiman 1995
)
. Together, these methods provide ways to move between sparse selection and continuous shrinkage.

The Lasso’s shrinkage introduces bias in large coefficients
(
Fan and Li 2001
)
. Nonconvex penalties such as SCAD
(
Fan and Li 2001
)
and MCP
(
Zhang 2010
)
reduce this bias by allowing the penalty derivative to vanish for sufficiently large coefficients. Under suitable conditions, these estimators attain the oracle property: consistent variable selection and the same limiting distribution for the active coefficients as if the true model were known
(
Fan and Li 2001
,
Zhang 2010
)
. The adaptive Lasso pursues a similar goal through data-dependent weights, penalizing coefficients with larger preliminary estimates less heavily
(
Zou 2006
)
. These approaches make penalization selective, either through the coefficient magnitude itself or through evidence from an initial fit.

This selectivity raises a question about the model toward which shrinkage is directed. With the usual fixed positive weights, ridge and Lasso penalties vanish only at the zero vector. Their solutions therefore approach the null model as regularization increases. SCAD and MCP protect large coefficients at a given tuning parameter, but do not prescribe a non-null target model under extreme regularization. A penalty that instead vanishes on all models of a specified maximum size would provide a different connection between shrinkage and subset selection.

Contribution.
We introduce
Fractional Ridge (Fridge)
regression, whose penalty vanishes whenever at most
m
coefficients are nonzero. The parameter
m
specifies a target model size, while
\lambda
controls the strength of regularization toward that target. A fit at finite
\lambda
may retain more than
m
predictors, allowing shrinkage to operate without imposing a hard size constraint. Under the conditions established below, global minimizers approach best subset solutions as
\lambda\to\infty
. Figure
1
illustrates the geometric distinction for
p=2
: the Fridge penalty region retains coordinate subspaces as regularization increases.

We develop algorithms for evaluating the penalty, fitting Fridge models, and computing their solution paths. Our theoretical analysis establishes limiting distributions, oracle properties under the stated tuning conditions, and the connection to best subset selection. Simulations examine how these properties translate into prediction and selection across signal-to-noise ratios. A riboflavin application illustrates the behavior of Fridge with highly-correlated, gene-expression predictors.

2
Fractional Ridge Penalty

2.1
Definition

The motivation for the Fridge penalty is most clearly described in low-dimensional settings. Consider a response variable
Y_{i}
and
p=3
predictors
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(X_{i1},X_{i2},X_{i3})
with corresponding coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3})^{\top}
. Assume that
(\mathbf{X}_{i},y_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,n
are independent and identically distributed, and that, without loss of generality, both the response and predictors are centered.

Define the sum of squared errors as
Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})={n}^{-1}\|\mbox{\bf y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}
and the ridge penalty as
P_{0}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\beta_{j}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}
. The ridge estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0}(\lambda)
is then given by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0}(\lambda)={\mathop{\rm arg\,min}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left\{Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\lambda P_{0}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right\}.

Under extreme regularization, the ridge estimator shrinks to the zero vector:
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0}(\lambda)=\mbox{\bf 0}_{3\times 1}
. This is because
P_{0}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=0
if and only if
\boldsymbol{\beta}=0
.

Now, consider the alternative estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}(\lambda)
defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}(\lambda)={\mathop{\rm arg\,min}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left\{Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\lambda P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right\},

(2)

where
P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\beta_{1}^{2}\beta_{2}^{2}+\beta_{1}^{2}\beta_{3}^{2}+\beta_{2}^{2}\beta_{3}^{2}
. Observe that
P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=0
if and only if at least two of the
\beta_{j}
are zero; notably, it does not require all
\beta_{j}
to be zero. The solution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}(\lambda)
that minimizes
Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})
subject to
P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=0
corresponds to the best single-variable model in terms of the smallest sum of squared errors. Since
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}(\lambda)
minimizes
Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})
subject to
P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=0
, the Fridge estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}(\lambda)
, under extreme regularization, shrinks towards this best one-variable model, not towards the null model.
Similarly, one can consider the penalty
P_{2}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\beta_{1}^{2}\beta_{2}^{2}\beta_{3}^{2}
to shrink towards the best two-variable model. In this case,
P_{2}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=0
if and only if at least one
\beta_{j}
is zero, allowing as many as two non-zero coefficient estimates.

In general, to induce shrinkage towards the best subset of size
m<p
, the Fridge penalty is

\displaystyle P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{m+1}\leq p}g_{j_{1}}(\beta_{j_{1}})g_{j_{2}}(\beta_{j_{2}})\cdots g_{j_{m+1}}(\beta_{j_{m+1}}),~~\text{for}~~0\leq m<p,

(3)

where
\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=(g_{1}(\beta_{1}),\dots,g_{p}(\beta_{p}))^{\top}
and
g_{j}(\beta_{j})
is such that
g_{j}(b)\geq 0
for all
b
and
g_{j}(b)=0
if and only if
b=0
, e.g.,
g_{j}(b)=b^{2}
or
|b|
.
By construction,
P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
is zero if and only if at least
(p-m)
of the
\beta_{j}
are zero. For any
\lambda>0
, the corresponding Fridge estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}(\lambda)
is defined as the solution to
{\mathop{\rm arg\,min}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\{Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\lambda P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))\}
. As
\lambda\to\infty
,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}(\lambda)
shrinks towards the best
m
-variable model.

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}(\lambda)
is termed an
m
-
Fractional Ridge
estimator because it is designed to retain a fraction (
m/p
) of coefficients as potentially non-zero, rather than shrinking all coefficients towards zero. We will refer to
m
as the
target model size (TMS)
, as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}(\lambda)
shrinks towards a model of at most size
m
under extreme regularization. With this definition, ridge, Lasso, and other methods that shrink coefficients towards zero (i.e., towards a null model) have a TMS of
0
.

The choice
g_{j}(b)=b^{2}
arises naturally as a direct generalization of the ridge regression. For the algorithms described in later sections and for better finite-sample properties, we use
g_{j}(b)=|b|
or
g_{j}(b)=|b|/|\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{\text{init}}|
for some initial estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{init}}
. In this sense, Fridge is complementary rather than directly competitive to existing methods, as
g_{j}(\cdot)
can be any existing penalty function, e.g., SCAD, MCP. For the remaining sections, we fix
g_{j}(b)=|b|/|\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{\text{init}}|
and
P_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta}):=P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
with slight abuse of notation unless
g_{j}
is otherwise stated.

Our primary interest lies not in achieving the target model size
m
itself, but rather in identifying models along the solution path
\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}(\lambda):\lambda>0\}
that exhibit optimal prediction-generalizability balance.

2.2
Geometry

As touched upon in its definition, a key distinction between Fridge and other Lasso-type penalties lies in their behavior under extreme regularization. To illustrate these characteristics, consider the constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}~Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})\quad\textrm{subject to}~P_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\leq\tau.

(4)

For easier visualization, we assume
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{init}}
to be a vector of ones, and consider the
1
-Fridge penalty
P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

For
p=2
case with
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})^{\top}
, the penalty regions depicted in Figure
1
demonstrate the distinct behavior of Fridge as the constraint parameter
\tau
approaches zero (or equivalently,
\lambda\to\infty
). Unlike the Lasso, which forces all coefficients towards zero, Fridge can selectively shrink a fraction of the coefficient estimates. This is evidenced by the small change in
\beta_{2}
values along Fridge’s penalty contour, even when
\beta_{1}
is heavily penalized (Figure
1
). In the most extreme regularization case where
\tau=0
, the Lasso estimate is invariably
(0,0)
, regardless of the underlying data. In contrast, the Fridge estimate can still possess a non-zero component. Specifically, the Fridge penalty region always includes the axes. This unique property allows Fridge to preserve truly active features even under strong regularization.

For
p=3
case with
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3})^{\top}
(Figure

), if
\beta_{3}
is fixed at one, the penalty constraint on remaining
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
becomes
\lambda(|\beta_{1}|+|\beta_{2}|+|\beta_{1}\beta_{2}|)\leq\tau
. This corresponds to a star shape on the
\beta_{1}\times\beta_{2}
plane, which now exhibits “corners,” similar to how the Lasso penalty behaves (Figure

(d)). Generally, for
g_{j}(b)=|b|/|\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{\text{init}}|
, if
m
coefficients are held constant at non-zero values, the region defined by
P_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\leq\tau
for the remaining
(p-m)
coefficients no longer includes the coordinate axes. Instead, this constrained region displays sharp corners where the optimization path naturally leads to a zero estimate for one of the remaining coefficients. This property suggests that a coordinate descent algorithm applied to Fridge will yield sparse solutions.

Finally, the Fridge penalty
encourages sparse solution when predictors are correlated. For an illustrative example, consider a data generating process
\mathbb{E}[y|\mathbf{X}]=X_{1}+X_{2}
where
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are perfectly correlated and on the same scale. The Lasso penalty, represented as
|\hat{\beta}_{1}|+|\hat{\beta}_{2}|
, yields infinitely many solutions; ridge penalty
\hat{\beta}_{1}^{2}+\hat{\beta}_{2}^{2}
prefers dense solution with equal magnitudes. In contrast, the
1
-Fridge penalty
P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=|\beta_{1}\beta_{2}|
achieves its minimum when one of the coefficients is zero.

2.3
Fractional Ridge as a Weighted Lasso Problem

The adaptive Lasso utilizes weights derived from an initial estimator to provide a more consistent variable selection process. These weights are typically defined as
w_{j}={1}/{|\hat{\beta}_{j}^{\text{init}}|^{\gamma}}
for positive
\gamma

(
Zou 2006
)
.
By this formulation, the model ensures that predictors exhibiting a small initial signal (low
|\hat{\beta}_{j}^{\text{init}}|
) are assigned larger weights, thereby increasing the probability of their coefficients being penalized to zero. Conversely, predictors with strong initial evidence are preserved with minimal bias. Notably, the adaptive Lasso weight
w_{j}^{\text{adaLasso}}
can approach infinity if the initial estimate is near zero, effectively forcing the exclusion of that variable from the final model.

In contrast, the Fridge penalty introduces a collaborative, or “all-against-all,” competition among predictors. Under this regime, the Fridge penalty can be viewed as an adaptively weighted Lasso with weights proportional to the magnitudes of other coefficients in the model, creating an interdependent penalty structure:

w_{j}^{\text{fridge}}\propto\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{m}\leq p\\
j_{1},\dots,j_{m}\neq j\end{subarray}}\frac{|\beta_{j_{1}}\dots\beta_{j_{m}}|}{|\widehat{\beta}_{j_{1}}^{\text{init}}\dots\widehat{\beta}_{j_{m}}^{\text{init}}|}\frac{1}{|\hat{\beta}_{j}^{\text{init}}|}.

(5)

This approach additionally punishes weak predictors based on their relative magnitude compared to the broader feature set.
A critical distinction between these two methods lies in the behavior of their weights at the limit. While the adaptive Lasso weight can reach infinity to exclude a variable, the Fridge weight
w_{j}^{\text{fridge}}
can be zero, leaving a variable weakly or not penalized.

3
Algorithm

There are two challenges in evaluating the Fridge penalty and solving the penalized optimization problem.
First, the penalty
P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
involves
p
-choose-
(m+1)
terms, which grows at least as fast as
O(p^{m})
. Without a proper approach, even evaluating a single penalty term would cost heavy computation.
Second, the Fridge objective function is nonconvex. This necessitates an algorithm for efficient computation and conditions under which the algorithm guarantees convergence to the solution.

3.1
Penalty Evaluation

We demonstrate that the
m
-Fridge penalty
P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
can be computed with
\text{O}(mp)
arithmetic operations via a
forward recursive algorithm
. This makes Fridge feasible for values of
m
and
p
where enumerating all
\binom{p}{m+1}
possible combinations of products of
(m+1)
coefficients would be computationally infeasible.

For
\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, define
\mbox{\bf V}_{0}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))=\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
. It is straightforward to see that for
m=0
,
P_{0}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))=\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mbox{\bf V}_{0}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
. For
k=1,\dots,m<p
, we define the recursive relations:

\displaystyle{\mbox{\bf V}}_{k}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))

\displaystyle=\{P_{k-1}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))-{\mbox{\bf V}}_{k-1}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))\}\circ\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}),

(6)

\displaystyle P_{k}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top}{\mbox{\bf V}}_{k}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))}{k+1}.

(7)

Here,
\circ
denotes the Hadamard (element-wise) product.

Essentially, for
k\geq 2
,
P_{k}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
represents the sum of all
\binom{p}{k+1}
unique products of
(k+1)
terms from the elements of
g
. The
j
-th element of
\mbox{\bf V}_{k}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
is
g_{j}(\beta_{j})P_{k-1}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta})_{-[j]})
, which is
g_{j}(\beta_{j})
times the sum of all
\binom{p-1}{k}
products of
k
terms from the remaining elements. The case
k=1
provides a familiar and informative illustration:

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mbox{\bf V}_{1}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))P_{0}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))-\mathbf{1}^{\top}\{\mbox{\bf V}_{0}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))\odot\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))^{2}-\mathbf{1}^{\top}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\odot\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))

\displaystyle=(g_{1}+\cdots+g_{p})^{2}-(g_{1}^{2}+\cdots+g_{p}^{2})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i<j}g_{i}g_{j},

Dividing by
2
yields
P_{1}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))=\sum_{i<j}g_{i}(\beta_{i})g_{j}(\beta_{j})
, which is consistent with the definition for
m=1
.

When counting arithmetic operations for the penalty
P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
in (
7
), but ignoring operations required to calculate
g(\cdot)
, computing the Fridge penalty with the target model size
m
requires
(3m+1)p-1
operations with our algorithm, thus demonstrating linearity in both
m
and
p
.

3.2
Fitting a Fractional Ridge Model

For convex
g(\cdot)
, the Fridge penalty is coordinate-wise convex. Specifically, the penalty
P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
can be expressed as
g_{j}(\beta_{j})
multiplied by a function of the remaining coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{-[j]}
. Here,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{-[j_{1},\dots,j_{k}]}
denotes the reduced-dimension coefficient vector excluding
\beta_{j_{1}},\dots,\beta_{j_{k}}
.
This structure suggests a coordinate descent algorithm for fitting a Fridge model. Lemma
1
further details this by presenting the partial derivatives of the Fridge penalty.

Lemma 1
.

Let
P_{k}(\mbox{\bf g})=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{k+1}\leq p}g_{j_{1}}g_{j_{2}}\cdots g_{j_{k+1}}
for
\mbox{\bf g}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Let
P_{-1}\equiv 1
. For
k=1,\dots,p-1
and
j=1,\dots,p
, the partial derivatives of
P_{k}(\mbox{\bf g})
are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial g_{j}}P_{k}(\mbox{\bf g})=P_{k-1}(\mbox{\bf g}_{-[j]}),\qquad\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial g_{j}\partial g_{r}}P_{k}(\mbox{\bf g})=\begin{cases}0,&j=r,\\
P_{k-2}(\mbox{\bf g}_{-[j,r]}),&j\neq r.\end{cases}

Calculating
P_{k-1}(\mbox{\bf g}_{[-j]})
and
P_{k-2}(\mbox{\bf g}_{[-j,r]})
each requires
O(kp)
arithmetic operations, respectively. For
m
, the naive full gradient calculation requires
O(mp^{2})
arithmetic operations, and the Hessian,
O(mp^{3})
arithmetic operations.

To derive the coordinate descent (CD) update rule, we rewrite the Fridge objective function for target model size
m
as
R_{\lambda,m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))=Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})/2+\lambda P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
.
The coordinate descent update rule is formally stated in Theorem
1
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
S(z,\gamma)=\text{sign}(z)(|z|-\gamma)_{+}
be the soft thresholding operator. The coordinate descent update rule for Fridge estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
with
g_{j}(b)=w_{j}|b|
,
w_{j}>0
is

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{j}^{(\textrm{new})}\leftarrow S\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)}),~\lambda w_{j}P_{m-1}(\hat{\mbox{\bf g}}_{-[j]})\right\}\bigg/\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}^{2}\right),\quad j=1,\dots,p,

(8)

where
\widehat{\mbox{\bf y}}^{(j)}=\mathbf{X}_{-[j]}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{-[j]}
represents the fitted value excluding the predictor
\mbox{\bf x}_{j}
and
\hat{\mbox{\bf g}}=\mbox{\bf g}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
S\subset\{1,\dots,p\}
has
|S|=m
,
X_{S}
has full column rank, and let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{S}^{OLS}=(\mathbf{X}_{S}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{S})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{S}^{\top}\mbox{\bf y}
. Assume every component of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{S}^{OLS}
is nonzero.
Define

\displaystyle{\lambda_{S}=\max_{j\notin S}\frac{|\mbox{\bf x}_{j}^{\top}(\mbox{\bf y}-\mathbf{X}_{S}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{S}^{OLS})|}{nw_{j}P_{m-1}(\mbox{\bf g}_{-[j]})}.}

(9)

For any
{\lambda\geq\lambda_{S},}

the vector of

\tilde{\beta}_{j}=\begin{cases}\hat{\beta}_{j}^{OLS},&j\in S,\\
0,&j\notin S\end{cases}

satisfies all coordinatewise KKT conditions.

Corollary
1
provides a support-dependent threshold above which the OLS fit on a given
m
-variable support is a fixed point of the coordinate-descent updates. This establishes the existence of an
m
-sparse coordinatewise solution, but does not guarantee convergence to that solution or its global optimality. Since the threshold depends on the unknown support
S
, it cannot directly specify an upper endpoint for the regularization search. We instead use the following heuristic value to guide the search:
we suggest centering and scaling the data, and then computing a crude value
\lambda_{m,\max}
:

\lambda_{m,\max}=\max_{j\notin S}\frac{|\mbox{\bf x}_{j}|^{\top}|\mbox{\bf y}-\bar{\mbox{\bf y}}|}{nw_{j}P_{m-1}(\mbox{\bf g}_{-[j]})}

by plugging in
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\epsilon\mathbf{1}_{p}
for a small
\epsilon
, such as
0.001
. Across the synthetic settings, with results presented in Section
5
, we have verified that this crude value typically results in good estimates.

Each exact coordinate update in (
8
) cannot increase the Fridge objective. Coordinatewise convexity alone, however, does not establish convergence of the iterates or global optimality. For the weighted absolute-value penalty, Lemma
2
gives a sufficient condition under which exact cyclic coordinate descent converges to the unique global minimizer.

Lemma 2
.

Fix
1\leq m<p
,
\lambda\geq 0
, and
g_{j}(\beta_{j})=w_{j}|\beta_{j}|
with
w_{j}>0
. Let
\mbox{\bf C}_{n}=n^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
be positive definite, and let
c_{*}(\mbox{\bf C}_{n})
denote its smallest eigenvalue. Write
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathrm{OLS}}=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\bf y}
.
Start exact cyclic coordinate descent (
8
) at
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)}
. Choose

r>\left\{\frac{2\bigl(R_{\lambda,m}(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)})-(2n)^{-1}\|\mbox{\bf y}-\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathrm{OLS}}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)}{c_{*}(\mbox{\bf C}_{n})}\right\}^{1/2}.

Set
b_{j}=w_{j}(|\hat{\beta}_{j}^{\mathrm{OLS}}|+r)
and, with
P_{-1}\equiv 1
, define

M_{r}=\max_{j}\sum_{k\neq j}w_{j}w_{k}P_{m-2}(\mbox{\bf b}_{-[j,k]}).

If
\lambda M_{r}<c_{*}(\mbox{\bf C}_{n})
, the CD iterates converge to the unique global minimizer of
R_{\lambda,m}
.

For
m=1
,
M_{r}=\max_{j}w_{j}\sum_{k\neq j}w_{k}
is independent of
r
. Hence
\lambda\max_{j}w_{j}\sum_{k\neq j}w_{k}<c_{*}(\mbox{\bf C}_{n})
guarantees convergence of cyclic coordinate descent to the unique global minimizer from any starting point.

.

3.3
Solution Path Evaluation

Because the Fridge penalty creates a non-linear and potentially multi-modal objective surface, direct optimization at a single, arbitrary
\lambda
is prone to becoming trapped in poor local minima.
Instead, by tracing the solution path from a known, well-behaved initial estimate, we can guide the estimator along the solution path.
A straightforward approach to constructing the solution path is to employ the model-fitting algorithms described previously via a warm-start strategy.
Null-shrinkage methods usually start with a large value of
\lambda
that yields a null model and proceed iteratively with smaller values of
\lambda

(
Friedman et al. 2010
, Section 2.5)
.
For Fridge, since the best subset model associated with large
\lambda
is unknown, we do the opposite.
By starting with a small penalty
\lambda
(which gives almost OLS estimator) and using the resulting coefficients as initial estimates for the subsequent, slightly larger
\lambda
, one can iteratively trace the path. For regularized generalized linear models, dedicated path algorithms can reduce the number of optimization steps needed to approximate a solution path. This motivates developing a path following approach for Fridge.

Predictor–corrector methods for regularization paths are developed by
Park and Hastie (2007)
, and differential equation path following by
Zhou and Wu (2014)
. An alternative approach treats the coefficient vector
\beta
as a differentiable function of
\lambda
for fixed
m
. Write
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
and take its gradient and Hessian with respect to
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
On a smooth stationary branch where the Hessian is nonsingular, the first-order condition is
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\bf y}+n\lambda\nabla\mathcal{P}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=0
,
thus implicit differentiation gives

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{\beta}}{d\lambda}=-(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+n\lambda\nabla^{2}\mathcal{P}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))^{-1}n\nabla\mathcal{P}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta}),

(10)

which we leverage the current estimate of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}(\lambda)
to project the state of the model at the next infinitesimal increment of
\lambda
.

A global minimizer may jump as
\lambda
varies, so the derivative above describes only a smooth stationary branch. For a numerical predictor direction, replace its penalty Hessian by the nonnegative diagonal matrix
\tilde{\mbox{\bf H}}=\operatorname{diag}(|\nabla_{j}\mathcal{P}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta})|/|\beta_{j}|)
at points with nonzero coordinates. At
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)}
, this corresponds to the local quadratic model

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{|\nabla_{j}\mathcal{P}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)})|}{|\beta_{j}^{(0)}|}\{\beta_{j}^{2}-(\beta_{j}^{(0)})^{2}\},

(11)

whose value and gradient match
\mathcal{P}_{m}
at
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)}
.
This construction follows the local quadratic approximation principle of
Fan and Li (2001)
, whose connection to MM algorithms and Newton-type updates is developed by
Hunter and Li (2005)
. Here the diagonal weights depend on the full coefficient vector through the nonseparable Fridge penalty, and we use this approximation within a predictor–corrector scheme to trace the solution path.
Replacing the exact Hessian with
\tilde{\mbox{\bf H}}
gives the predictor direction

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{\beta}}{d\lambda}=-(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+n\lambda\tilde{\mbox{\bf H}})^{-1}n\nabla\mathcal{P}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta}).

(12)

By applying a small step
\delta
in the direction of this approximate derivative, we obtain an initial estimate
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}(\lambda^{\textrm{new}})
for the subsequent regularization level
\lambda^{\textrm{new}}
.

Damped quasi-Newton corrections then seek to reduce the residual at the stationary condition, again using
\tilde{\mbox{\bf H}}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\beta}-\gamma_{m}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+n\lambda^{\textrm{new}}\tilde{\mbox{\bf H}})^{-1}R_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta};\lambda^{\textrm{new}}),

(13)

where
\gamma_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
is the adaptive damping factor, and
R_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta};\lambda)=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\bf y}+n\lambda\nabla\mathcal{P}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is the first-order Karush-Kuhn-Tucker condition.
Starting from
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}(\lambda^{\textrm{new}})
, apply this correction
k
times to reduce the solution’s departure from the stationary condition, recomputing
\tilde{\mbox{\bf H}}
and
R_{m}
at the current
\boldsymbol{\beta}
before each step.
We use the heuristic
\gamma_{m}=\{1\wedge 4/(m+1)\}\vee 0.1
to moderate the correction for large
m
, and
k=2
.
Algorithm
1
describes the predictor–corrector process with
g_{j}(b)=|b|/|\hat{\beta}_{j}^{\textrm{init}}|
.

We note that the diagonal quadratic model is not generally a majorizer, so the method has no MM descent guarantee and need not find a global minimizer. Fits should be checked against the original objective and its stationary conditions. However, this approach performs well empirically, as shown in the subsequent simulation studies and applications.

Limiting Distribution

Following the theory of

(Limiting distribution)
.

Let

4.2
Best Subset Selection Property

In Section
2
, we introduced Fridge as a model that bridges regularized regression and best subset selection. The following result shows that,
for a fixed sample, increasing the regularization parameter indeed drives global Fridge minimizers toward the set of best subset least-squares solutions with at most
m
nonzero coefficients. If the best subset solution is unique, the Fridge estimator converges to it as
\lambda\to\infty
.

Theorem 3
.

Fix
n
and
m<p
. Let
R_{\lambda,m}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\frac{1}{2n}\|\mbox{\bf y}_{n}-\mathbf{X}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}(\lambda)
be a global minimizer. Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}^{\mathrm{BSS}}\in\arg\min_{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq m}\frac{1}{2n}\|\mbox{\bf y}_{n}-\mathbf{X}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}.

Then every accumulation point of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}(\lambda)
as
\lambda\to\infty
is a best subset minimizer. If the best subset minimizer is unique, then
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}(\lambda)\to\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{m}^{\mathrm{BSS}}.

While technically it is possible to fit a Fridge model with true model size for best subset selection purpose, our primary interest is in identifying a model that balances prediction and generalizability, and not in the recovery of the best subset selection. We delegate further study of the optimal best subset selection via Fridge to Appendix
\thechapter.E
.

Discussion

Fridge provides a way to specify the model size toward which regularization is directed. The target model size
m
determines where the penalty vanishes, while
\lambda
controls the strength of shrinkage toward that set of models. This distinction allows selective shrinkage at finite regularization levels and gives a connection to best subset selection under extreme regularization. The target model size
m
therefore has a direct interpretation, although the number of predictors retained in a fitted model can exceed
m
. In applications focused on prediction, both parameters can be selected by cross-validation, as in the riboflavin analysis.

The simulations suggest that this flexibility is useful when the evidence for individual predictors varies with signal strength. Fridge produced competitive prediction and selection results across the settings studied. Under weak signals in high dimensions, it often retained many small coefficients; with stronger signals, its fitted models became sparse. In alignment with
Hastie et al. (2020)
, these results suggest that allowing small coefficients to persist can help preserve predictive information when exact variable selection is difficult.

Computation and tuning remain central limitations. The Fridge objective is generally nonconvex, and the solution path algorithm follows a stationary branch that may differ from the path of global minimizers. The sufficient condition in Lemma
2
gives a setting in which coordinate descent converges to the global solution, but the general algorithms do not carry that guarantee. Objective comparisons across starting values and checks of the stationary conditions are therefore useful in practice. Likewise, the oracle property concerns global minimizers under specified assumptions and tuning rates; it does not establish the oracle property for a cross-validated numerical fit. However, the oracle properties of the competing methods likewise require specific assumptions and tuning rates, and therefore do not automatically apply to their cross-validated numerical fits.

The riboflavin application illustrates the potential for parsimonious selection in biological studies. Fridge selected fewer genes outside SuSiE’s ten highest-PIP genes than SCAD or the adaptive Lasso, and all three of its leading coefficients belonged to that list. It also selected one representative from each of the two reported correlation groups. Agreement with SuSiE provides a useful comparison, but we note that this does not establish biological relevance or identify false discoveries. The bootstrap results further distinguish stability of a group from stability of an individual gene: selection of at least one credible set member was substantially more stable than selection of the particular gene chosen in the original fit. Independent data or experimental validation would be needed to assess the biological interpretation.

Further work on Fridge could develop more reliable optimization methods, characterize sensitivity to the initial weights, and establish prediction and selection guarantees for finite regularization when the number of predictors grows with the sample size. Extensions to generalized linear models and structured groups of predictors would broaden the scope of the method. In applications with strongly correlated features, reporting stability at the group level alongside coefficient estimates may also provide a more informative account of what Fridge has identified.

On

Iteratively Reweighted Regression

As discussed in Section
2.3

the Fridge penalty can be seen as an adaptively weighted penalty.
Here, we assume
g_{j}(\beta_{j})=|\beta_{j}|
and write
v_{j}(\mbox{\bf g})=P_{m-1}(\mbox{\bf g}_{-[j]})=P_{m-1}(\mbox{\bf g})-\mbox{\bf V}_{m-1}(\mbox{\bf g})[j]
. Counting each product once for each of its
m+1
factors gives the identity

\displaystyle\lambda P_{m}(\mbox{\bf g})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{m+1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}v_{j}(\mbox{\bf g})|\beta_{j}|.

(14)

This identity also holds when some coefficients are zero.

To construct an iteratively reweighted Lasso (IRL) step, use the first-order penalty approximation at the current iterate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}
, with
v_{j}^{(t)}=v_{j}(|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}|)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}\in\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left\{\frac{Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})}{2}+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}v_{j}^{(t)}|\beta_{j}|\right\}.

(15)

An additive constant,
\lambda\{P_{m}(|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}|)-\sum_{j}v_{j}^{(t)}|\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{(t)}|\}
, makes the approximation agree with the original objective at the current iterate but does not affect the minimizer. The factor
1/(m+1)
from (
14
) is absent from (
15
): at a fixed point, the weighted-Lasso KKT equations then match the stationary conditions of
Q/2+\lambda P_{m}(|\boldsymbol{\beta}|)
. This first-order approximation is not generally a majorizer, so the iteration has no global optimality guarantee.

The weight
v_{j}
can be exactly zero, thus ensuring that a portion of coefficients are not constrained, which happens when
P_{m-1}(\mbox{\bf g})=\mbox{\bf V}_{m-1}(\mbox{\bf g})[j]
and
g(x)=|x|
. For example, when the TMS
=1
,
v_{j}
is zero if
\sum_{l\neq j}|\beta_{l}|
is zero, and when TMS
=2
, if
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l<m,\\
l,m\neq j\end{subarray}}|\beta_{l}\beta_{m}|=0
. These scenarios imply
\beta_{j}
should be among the allowable non-zero coefficients as the other
(p-m)
coefficient estimates are already set to zero. Thus the Fridge will not actively penalize the remaining
\beta_{j}
in the subsequent process.

A local quadratic alternative is the weighted-ridge step

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}\in\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left\{\frac{Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})}{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{v_{j}^{(t)}}{|\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{(t)}|}\beta_{j}^{2}\right\}.

Its gradient matches the Fridge penalty gradient at the current iterate. This iteratively reweighted ridge (IRR) step requires a specified treatment of zero coefficients and likewise has no general majorization guarantee.

IRL uses a local linear penalty approximation, analogous to the approach of
Zou and Li (2008)
; IRR uses a local quadratic one as in
Fan and Li (2001)
.
The inner weighted Lasso and weighted ridge problems can use existing solvers, including suitable screening methods for the Lasso step
(
El Ghaoui et al. 2010
,
Tibshirani et al. 2012
)
. Similar inner problems can be formed for generalized linear models. Their solutions remain numerical candidates for the Fridge objective.

q+k
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm over})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{n}^{c})+2\sum_{k=1}^{m_{max}-q}\exp\{k\log p_{n}-c_{*}k\gamma_{n}\}

\displaystyle\leq C\exp(-cn)+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{n}^{-k\{c_{*}\log\log n-1\}}=O(p_{n}^{-\alpha})

for some
\alpha>0
.

Case 2:
S\not\supseteq\mathcal{A}
.

The model
S
omits at least one true variable and may also include noise variables. Write

U=\mathcal{A}\setminus S,\qquad V=S\setminus\mathcal{A},\qquad k=|U|\geq 1,\qquad v=|V|,\qquad r=k+v.

Thus
k
counts omitted true variables,
v
counts included noise variables, and
|S|-q=v-k
.
We first bound the signal lost by omitting
U
, then control the noise terms simultaneously over all candidate models.

The signal that cannot be explained by
S
is
\Delta_{S}=\|a_{S}\|_{2}^{2}
where
a_{S}=\Pi_{S}^{\perp}\mathbf{X}_{n,U}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0,U}
.
Projection onto
\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{X}_{n,S})
gives the least-squares approximation, so
\Delta_{S}=\min_{b}\|\mathbf{X}_{n,U}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0,U}-\mathbf{X}_{n,S}b\|_{2}^{2}
.
For every
b
, the vector inside this norm is a linear combination of the columns indexed by
U\cup S=S\cup\mathcal{A}
, with coefficient vector
(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0,U}^{\top},-b^{\top})^{\top}
.
Since
|S\cup\mathcal{A}|\leq m_{max}+q
, the lower eigenvalue bound in (C3) gives

\|\mathbf{X}_{n,U}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0,U}-\mathbf{X}_{n,S}b\|_{2}^{2}\geq nc_{min}\bigl(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0,U}\|_{2}^{2}+\|b\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\geq nc_{min}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0,U}\|_{2}^{2}.

Taking the minimum over
b
and using (C2), we obtain

\Delta_{S}\geq nc_{min}k\beta_{min,n}^{2}\geq c_{min}Mk\gamma_{n}.

(17)

To identify the noise terms, note that

\Pi_{S}^{\perp}\mbox{\bf y}_{n}=a_{S}+\Pi_{S}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}
and

\Pi_{S}^{\perp}a_{S}=a_{S}
. Expanding the squared norm and subtracting
R_{\mathcal{A}}
gives

R_{S}-R_{\mathcal{A}}=\Delta_{S}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}^{\top}a_{S}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}^{\top}B_{S}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n},~~\text{where }B_{S}=\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}-\Pi_{S}.

(18)

The first term is the loss from the omitted signals. The remaining two terms describe how noise changes the residual comparison.

For a fixed
S
, the vector
a_{S}
is deterministic and

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}^{\top}a_{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{S})_{i}\varepsilon_{i}
is a weighted sum of independent, centered sub-Gaussian errors.
The general Hoeffding inequality
(
Vershynin 2018
, Theorem 2.6.3)
therefore gives

\mathbb{P}\bigl(|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}^{\top}a_{S}|>t\bigr)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{ct^{2}}{\Delta_{S}}\right),\qquad t>0,

where
c>0
depends only on
K
.
Fix
L>2
and choose
A_{L}
sufficiently large. Substituting

t=A_{L}\sqrt{\Delta_{S}r\log p_{n}}
makes this probability at most
2p_{n}^{-Lr}
.
Moreover,

2A_{L}\sqrt{\Delta_{S}r\log p_{n}}\leq\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{S}+4A_{L}^{2}r\log p_{n}.

Thus, with
C_{4}=4A_{L}^{2}
, the inequality

2|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}^{\top}a_{S}|\leq\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{S}+C_{4}r\log p_{n}

(19)

fails for this
S
with probability at most
2p_{n}^{-Lr}
.

For the quadratic noise term, we first state the properties of
B_{S}
needed for concentration.
For every unit vector
h
,

h^{\top}B_{S}h=\|\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}h\|_{2}^{2}-\|\Pi_{S}h\|_{2}^{2}\in[-1,1].

Because
B_{S}
is symmetric, all its eigenvalues lie in
[-1,1]
, so
\|B_{S}\|_{2}\leq 1
.
Both projections act as the identity on
\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{X}_{n,S\cap\mathcal{A}})
, so their difference vanishes there.
By symmetry, the range of
B_{S}
is orthogonal to this common subspace. It also lies in
\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{X}_{n,S\cup\mathcal{A}})
.
(C3) makes the columns in the union linearly independent. These two subspaces have dimensions
q-k
and
q+v
, respectively, and hence

\operatorname{rank}(B_{S})\leq(q+v)-(q-k)=r.

Also,

\|B_{S}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}(B_{S})^{2}\leq\operatorname{rank}(B_{S})\leq r,
where
\lambda_{i}(\cdot)
denotes the
i
th eigenvalue.

The errors have covariance
\sigma^{2}I_{n}
and mean zero. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}^{\top}B_{S}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\bigr)=\sigma^{2}\operatorname{tr}(B_{S})=\sigma^{2}(q-|S|)=\sigma^{2}(k-v).

Using the operator and Frobenius norm bounds above, the Hanson–Wright inequality

(
Rudelson and Vershynin 2013
, Theorem 1.1)
gives

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}^{\top}B_{S}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}-\sigma^{2}(k-v)\right|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left\{-c\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{r},t\right)\right\},\qquad t>0,

where
c>0
depends only on
K
.
Choose
t
to be a sufficiently large constant multiple of
r\log p_{n}
.
Then for a sufficiently large
C_{5}
,

|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}^{\top}B_{S}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}|\leq C_{5}r\log p_{n}

(20)

fails for this
S
with probability at most
2p_{n}^{-Lr}
, for all sufficiently large
n
.

We now make these bounds simultaneous. Define
\mathcal{E}_{n}
to be the event that both (
19
) and (
20
) hold for every
S\not\supseteq\mathcal{A}
with
|S|\leq m_{max}
.
For given
k,v
, there are

\binom{q}{k}\binom{p_{n}-q}{v}\leq p_{n}^{k+v}
possible models.
For each such model, the combined failure probability is at most
4p_{n}^{-L(k+v)}
.
The union bound yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c})\leq 4\sum_{k=1}^{q}\sum_{v=0}^{m_{max}}p_{n}^{-(L-1)(k+v)}\leq\frac{4p_{n}^{-(L-1)}}{\{1-p_{n}^{-(L-1)}\}^{2}}=O\bigl(p_{n}^{-(L-1)}\bigr).

On
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, substitute the two noise bounds into
(
18
). With
C_{7}=C_{4}+C_{5}
, this gives

\displaystyle R_{S}-R_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\Delta_{S}-C_{7}r\log p_{n},

(21)

\displaystyle|R_{S}-R_{\mathcal{A}}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{5}{4}\Delta_{S}+C_{7}r\log p_{n}\leq C_{6}(\Delta_{S}+r\log p_{n}),

(22)

where
C_{6}=\max(5/4,C_{7})
.
These inequalities hold for all misspecified models at once.

Considering only
\mathcal{E}_{n}\cap\mathcal{G}_{n}
, the preceding bounds hold and

c_{R}n\leq R_{\mathcal{A}}\leq C_{R}n
.
Note that we have

\mathbb{P}\{(\mathcal{E}_{n}\cap\mathcal{G}_{n})^{c}\}\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{n}^{c})=O(p_{n}^{-\alpha})

for some
\alpha>0
, decreased if necessary to also apply in Case 1.
We will show that every misspecified model has larger SIC on this event.
The two cases below distinguish whether the loss from the omitted signals or the number of added noise variables provides the decisive comparison.

Case 2a:
C_{7}r\log p_{n}\leq(3/8)\Delta_{S}
.

Then
R_{S}-R_{\mathcal{A}}\geq(3/8)\Delta_{S}>0
. Since
R_{\mathcal{A}}\leq C_{R}n
and

\log(1+x)\geq(\log 2)\min(x,1)
for
x\geq 0
, there are constants

c_{8},c_{9}>0
, depending only on
\sigma^{2}
, such that

\displaystyle n\log\left(\frac{R_{S}}{R_{\mathcal{A}}}\right)=n\log\left(1+\frac{R_{S}-R_{\mathcal{A}}}{R_{\mathcal{A}}}\right)\geq c_{8}\min(R_{S}-R_{\mathcal{A}},n)\geq c_{9}\min(\Delta_{S},n).

(23)

It follows that

\operatorname{SIC}_{n}(S)-\operatorname{SIC}_{n}(\mathcal{A})\geq c_{9}\min(\Delta_{S},n)+(v-k)\gamma_{n}
.
If
v\geq k
, the right-hand side is strictly positive.
If
v<k
and
\Delta_{S}\leq n
, (
17
) gives

\operatorname{SIC}_{n}(S)-\operatorname{SIC}_{n}(\mathcal{A})\geq(c_{9}c_{min}M-1)k\gamma_{n}>0

when
M
is sufficiently large. If
v<k
and
\Delta_{S}>n
, the same
difference is at least
c_{9}n-k\gamma_{n}>0
for all sufficiently large
n
,
since
k\leq q
is fixed and
\gamma_{n}=o(n)
.

Case 2b:
C_{7}r\log p_{n}>(3/8)\Delta_{S}
.

Combining this condition with (
17
) gives

r>\frac{3c_{min}M}{8C_{7}}k\log\log n.

(24)

Thus
r/k\to\infty
uniformly over models in this case. For all
sufficiently large
n
,
r\geq 4k
and hence

v-k=r-2k\geq r/2.

(25)

For every
S
in Case 2b, its defining inequality gives

\Delta_{S}<(8/3)C_{7}r\log p_{n}
. Combining this with (
22
) yields

|R_{S}-R_{\mathcal{A}}|\leq C_{12}r\log p_{n}
,
where
C_{12}=C_{6}(1+(8/3)C_{7})
. Since
r\leq m_{max}+q
and

R_{\mathcal{A}}\geq c_{R}n
on
\mathcal{E}_{n}\cap\mathcal{G}_{n}
, (C1) gives the following
bound uniformly over all models in Case 2b:

\left|\frac{R_{S}-R_{\mathcal{A}}}{R_{\mathcal{A}}}\right|\leq\frac{C_{12}(m_{max}+q)\log p_{n}}{c_{R}n}=o(1).

For all sufficiently large
n
, the left-hand side is at most
1/2
. Using
\log(1+x)\geq-2|x|
for
|x|\leq 1/2
, we obtain

n\log\left(\frac{R_{S}}{R_{\mathcal{A}}}\right)\geq-\frac{2n}{R_{\mathcal{A}}}|R_{S}-R_{\mathcal{A}}|\geq-C_{13}r\log p_{n}.

Together with (
25
), this gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{SIC}_{n}(S)-\operatorname{SIC}_{n}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle\geq-C_{13}r\log p_{n}+(v-k)\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle\geq r\log p_{n}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\log\log n-C_{13}\right\}>0

for all sufficiently large
n
.

Thus every misspecified model has strictly larger SIC than
\mathcal{A}

on
\mathcal{E}_{n}\cap\mathcal{G}_{n}
, so

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm miss})\leq\mathbb{P}\{(\mathcal{E}_{n}\cap\mathcal{G}_{n})^{c}\}=O(p_{n}^{-\alpha})
.
Finally,

\mathbb{P}(\widehat{S}_{n}\neq\mathcal{A})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm over})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm miss})\leq Cp_{n}^{-\alpha}.

Since
\widehat{S}_{n}=\mathcal{A}
implies
\widehat{q}_{n}=q
, the model-size
conclusion follows as well.
∎

Limiting Distribution

First, we discuss how the limiting distribution changes with the choice of
g_{j}(b)=|b|
.

Theorem 5

(Limiting distribution)
.

Let
p
be fixed. Assume that
\mbox{\bf C}_{12}=\mbox{\bf 0}
and
{\sqrt{n}}{\lambda_{n}}\to\lambda_{0}\geq 0
as
n\to\infty
.
For a fixed
m
, let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}^{\top}~\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2}^{\top})^{\top}
be a global minimizer of
R_{n}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=(2n)^{-1}\|\mbox{\bf y}_{n}-\mathbf{X}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{n}P_{m}(|\boldsymbol{\beta}|)
. We have

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{10})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}_{q}\left(-{\lambda_{0}}\mbox{\bf C}_{11}^{-1}\mbox{\bf d}_{10},\;\sigma^{2}\mbox{\bf C}_{11}^{-1}\right),

where the
j
-th element of
\mbox{\bf d}_{10}
is given as

\sign(\beta_{0j})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathcal{A}\setminus\{j\}\\
|S|=m\end{subarray}}\prod_{k\in S}|\beta_{0k}|.

Corollary 3
.

Under the assumptions of the theorem,

(i)

If
m\geq q
, then
\mbox{\bf d}_{10}=\mbox{\bf 0}
, and the active set coefficient estimators are asymptotically unbiased.

(ii)

If
m\leq q
and
\lambda_{0}>0
, then
\mathbb{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2}=\mbox{\bf 0})
converges to a strictly positive value.

Proof.

Most of the arguments are the same as in Theorem
2
. Only the penalty contribution changes: the active derivative is now the unweighted
\mbox{\bf d}_{10}
, and, for
m\leq q
, the inactive contribution has a finite
\ell_{1}
limit instead of forcing all inactive coordinates to zero.

By consistency, local Lipschitz continuity of the unweighted penalty and global optimality, with probability tending to one,

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{c}\left(\|n^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\|_{2}+\lambda_{n}L\right)=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

where
L
is a local Lipschitz constant and
c>0
bounds the smallest eigenvalue of
\mbox{\bf C}_{n}
below. The restricted estimator
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1},0)
satisfies the same rate.

Write
\mbox{\bf W}_{n}=n^{-1/2}\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\mbox{\bf W}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\mbox{\bf 0},\sigma^{2}\mbox{\bf C})
and
D_{m}=P_{m-1}(|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{10}|)
, taking
D_{m}=0
for
m>q
and
P_{-1}\equiv 1
. Uniformly for bounded
\mbox{\bf u}=(\mbox{\bf u}_{1}^{\top},\mbox{\bf u}_{2}^{\top})^{\top}
,

\sqrt{n}\left\{P_{m}(|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}+\mbox{\bf u}/\sqrt{n}|)-P_{m}(|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}|)\right\}=\mbox{\bf d}_{10}^{\top}\mbox{\bf u}_{1}+D_{m}\|\mbox{\bf u}_{2}\|_{1}+o(1).

Products with zero or one inactive factor give the two displayed terms; products with at least two are
O(n^{-1})
. Combining this expansion with the quadratic loss expansion yields the limiting local objective

V(\mbox{\bf u})=\tfrac{1}{2}\mbox{\bf u}^{\top}\mbox{\bf C}\mbox{\bf u}-\mbox{\bf u}^{\top}\mbox{\bf W}+\lambda_{0}\{\mbox{\bf d}_{10}^{\top}\mbox{\bf u}_{1}+D_{m}\|\mbox{\bf u}_{2}\|_{1}\}.

The expansion holds uniformly on every fixed bounded set, and
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})=O_{p}(1)
. Since
C
is positive definite and
\lambda_{0}D_{m}\geq 0
,
V
is strongly convex and coercive, and hence has a unique minimizer. Applying the argmax theorem to the negatives of the local objectives
(
Van der Vaart 1998
, Section 5.9)
therefore gives
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\xrightarrow{d}\arg\min_{\mbox{\bf u}}V(\mbox{\bf u})
. Since
\mbox{\bf C}_{12}=0
, its active component is
\mbox{\bf C}_{11}^{-1}(\mbox{\bf W}_{1}-\lambda_{0}\mbox{\bf d}_{10})
, proving Part (i) of the theorem. For
m>q
, both penalty terms vanish.

For Part (ii), the threshold now has a finite limit. On a fixed bounded convex neighborhood of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
, the unweighted penalty Hessians, where defined, have operator norm bounded by a constant
K
. Since
\lambda_{\min}(\mbox{\bf C}_{n})\geq c>0
and
\lambda_{n}\to 0
, the smooth Hessian of
R_{n}
is bounded below by
(c-\lambda_{n}K)I\succeq(c/2)I
for all sufficiently large
n
. The nonnegative slope jumps at zero coordinates preserve this bound across orthants, similar to the proof of Lemma
2
. Thus
R_{n}
is strongly convex on this neighborhood. Together with consistency, this makes
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2}=0
equivalent, with probability tending to one, to

\left\|n^{-1/2}\mathbf{X}_{n,2}^{\top}(\mbox{\bf y}_{n}-\mathbf{X}_{n,1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{n}\lambda_{n}P_{m-1}(|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}|).

The score vector on the left is
\mbox{\bf W}_{n,2}-\mbox{\bf C}_{21}^{n}\sqrt{n}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{10})=\mbox{\bf W}_{n,2}+o_{p}(1)
, and the threshold tends to
\lambda_{0}D_{m}
. The Gaussian limit assigns zero probability to the boundary, so for
m\leq q,\ \lambda_{0}>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2}=\mbox{\bf 0})\to\mathbb{P}(\|\mbox{\bf W}_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq\lambda_{0}D_{m})>0.

∎

For
m\leq q
, if
\mbox{\bf C}_{22}
is diagonal, the individual inactive-coordinate KKT conditions yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2j}=0)\to 2\Phi\left(\frac{\lambda_{0}D_{m}}{\sigma\sqrt{(\mbox{\bf C}_{22})_{jj}}}\right)-1,

(26)

Here
D_{m}=\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal{A},\,|S|=m}\prod_{k\in S}|\beta_{0k}|
. If
\mbox{\bf C}_{22}=\mbox{\bf I}
and
|\beta_{0k}|=c>0
for all
k\in\mathcal{A}
, then
D_{m}={q\choose m}c^{m}
and the limiting probability of excluding all
p-q
inactive coefficients is
[2\Phi(\lambda_{0}D_{m}/\sigma)-1]^{p-q}
. This joint probability can be close to one in some cases, for example, with
q=m=3
,
p=13
,
\lambda_{0}=\sigma=1
, and
c=1.5
, the probability is
[2\Phi(3.375)-1]^{10}\approx 0.993
.

\thechapter
.F.2
Irrepresentable Condition

The next theorem states that Fridge is somewhat robust to predictor collinearity.

Theorem 6
.

Let
m<q
,
P_{-1}\equiv 1
, and suppose the active coefficients are bounded away from zero. Suppose
\lambda_{n}\to 0
and
\sqrt{n}\lambda_{n}\to\infty
. If, for some
\eta>0
,

\left\|\mbox{\bf C}_{21}^{n}(\mbox{\bf C}_{11}^{n})^{-1}\nabla_{\mathcal{A}}P_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\right\|_{\infty}\leq(1-\eta)P_{m-1}(|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{10}|),

then the oracle active-sign solution satisfies the inactive KKT inequalities with probability tending to one. Under local strict convexity of the active block, there is consequently no false-positive coordinate in the corresponding local minimizer with probability tending to one.

If
|\beta_{0j}|=c
for all
j\in\mathcal{A}
, then the condition reduces to

\left\|\mbox{\bf C}_{21}^{n}(\mbox{\bf C}_{11}^{n})^{-1}\sign(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{10})\right\|_{\infty}\leq(1-\eta)\frac{q}{q-m}.

(27)

This corresponds with the Lasso’s “irrepresentable condition”
(
Zhao and Yu 2006
)
. Lasso instead requires the right-hand side of (
27
) to be strictly less than
1-\eta
. This shows that whenever the target model size is chosen smaller than
q
, the model is somewhat robust against the correlation between signal and noise variables. If
m=q-1
, the right-hand side of (
27
) is the largest with
(1-\eta)q
.

Proof.

For simplicity, let
d=\nabla_{\mathcal{A}}P_{m}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})
and
D=P_{m-1}(|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{10}|)
. Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}
denote the minimizer of the objective restricted to the active coordinates with the inactive coordinates fixed at zero. Its active first-order condition implies

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{10}=(\mbox{\bf C}_{11}^{n})^{-1}\left\{n^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}-\lambda_{n}d\right\}+o_{p}(n^{-1/2}+\lambda_{n}).

Consequently,

n^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{2}^{\top}(\mbox{\bf y}_{n}-\mathbf{X}_{1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1})=\lambda_{n}\mbox{\bf C}_{21}^{n}(\mbox{\bf C}_{11}^{n})^{-1}d+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})+o_{p}(\lambda_{n}).

Since
\sqrt{n}\lambda_{n}\to\infty
, the stochastic score is
o_{p}(\lambda_{n})
. The inactive KKT condition at zero is

\left|n^{-1}\mbox{\bf x}_{j}^{\top}(\mbox{\bf y}_{n}-\mathbf{X}_{1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1})\right|\leq\lambda_{n}P_{m-1}(|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}|)=\lambda_{n}D+o_{p}(\lambda_{n}).

The strict margin
(1-\eta)D
therefore makes all inactive KKT inequalities hold with probability tending to one. Local strict convexity on the active block then gives the stated local-minimizer conclusion.
∎

\thechapter
.G.1
Data Generating Process

We generated synthetic datasets from a linear regression model,
y=X\beta+\epsilon
. The process is specifically designed to construct highly correlated feature blocks.

We simulate
n=n_{train}+n_{test}
observations with
p
predictors. The rows of the design matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
are drawn independently from a multivariate normal distribution
\mathcal{N}_{p}(0,\Sigma)
.
To induce multicollinearity, the covariance matrix
\Sigma
is structured as an AR(1) process, where the correlation between the
i
-th and
j
-th predictors decays exponentially with their positional distance. The entries of
\Sigma
are
\Sigma_{i,j}=\rho^{|i-j|},
where
\rho\in[0,1)
controls the baseline correlation strength. After generation, the entire design matrix
\mathbf{X}
is column-standardized so that each predictor has an empirical mean of
0
and a standard deviation of
1
.

The true coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is designed to be strictly sparse, containing exactly
q
non-zero entries (the active set).
To control the degree of correlation among the active predictors, their indices are spaced out by a constant integer, step. The indices of the active variables are defined by the sequence
\{0,\text{step},2\times\text{step},\dots,(q-1)\times\text{step}\}
. When
\text{step}=1
, the active variables form a contiguous block
\{0,1,\dots,q-1\}
that are highly correlated due to the AR(1) structure of
X
.
For each index in the active set, the coefficient magnitude is fixed at exactly
1
, while the sign is randomly drawn with equal probability from
\{-1,1\}
. That is, for
j
in the active set:

\beta_{j}=c_{j}\times 1.0,\quad c_{j}\sim\text{Uniform}\{-1,1\}.

All other coefficients not in the active set are set to
0
.

To strictly control the complexity of the task, we define the SNR based on the empirical variances of the generated data. The deterministic signal vector is calculated as
f(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
.
Given a target
SNR
, the required variance for the random error term,
\sigma^{2}
, is calculated analytically as:

\sigma^{2}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}/n}{\text{SNR}}.

The noise vector
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is then generated from independent and identically distributed normal draws,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}_{n}(\mbox{\bf 0},\sigma^{2}\mbox{\bf I})
.

The final response vector
y
is computed by adding the noise to the true signal:
\mbox{\bf y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}.

The response
y
is then mean-centered. Finally, the
n
observations are partitioned sequentially: the first
n_{train}
observations are allocated to the training set
(\mathbf{X}_{train},\mbox{\bf y}_{train})
for model fitting, and the remaining
n_{test}
observations are reserved for the testing set
(\mathbf{X}_{test},\mbox{\bf y}_{test})
to evaluate out-of-sample predictive performance.

\thechapter
.G.2
Evaluation Metrics

Following
Hastie et al. (2020)
, we considered the following evaluation metrics:

•

F-score:

\displaystyle\text{F-score}=\left(\frac{\text{recall}^{-1}+\text{precision}^{-1}}{2}\right)^{-1}.

A perfect score is
1
.

•

Proportion of variance explained (PVE):

\displaystyle\text{PVE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})^{\top}\Sigma(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})+\sigma^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\Sigma\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}+\sigma^{2}}.

A perfect score is
\text{SNR}/(1+\text{SNR})
and the null score is
0
. This metric can be negative if a model performs worse than the null model.

•

Relative test error (RTE): this metric measures the expected test error relative to the Bayes error rate.

\displaystyle\text{RTE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})^{\top}\Sigma(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})+\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

A perfect score is
1
and the null score is SNR
+1
.

•

Model size: the number of non-zero coefficient estimates.
