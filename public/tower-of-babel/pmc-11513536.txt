Protocol for oil red O staining of low-density lipoproteins for in vivo cell treatment
Authors: Simone Bini, Stella Covino, Ilenia Minicocci, Laura D’Erasmo, Daniele Tramontano, Alessia Di Costanzo, Marcello Arca, Valeria Pecce
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryLow-density lipoproteins (LDLs) are the most abundant circulating lipoproteins and the most critical factor in the development of atherosclerosis. This protocol allows the staining of LDLs with oil red O to monitor particle uptake in bright-field microscopy. Here, we describe how to stain isolated LDLs using oil red O and how to use them to monitor LDL uptake in time-lapse experiments or fixed cells.Subject areas: cell culture, cell-based assays, metabolism, microscopy

Before you begin

This protocol presents an alternative non-fluorescent method for staining low-density lipoproteins (LDLs) in an in vitro assay to monitor and quantify their uptake. The already described protocols use the fluorescent dye Dil (1,1′-Dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-Tetramethylindocarbocyanine Perchlorate) or BODIPY (4,4-difluoro-4-bora-3a,4a-diaza-s-indacene).1,2 Here, we described the alternative use of Oil Red O to stain the LDLs (Red LDL) intended to monitor LDL uptake in bright field microscopy. This method can monitor the uptake in specific in vitro assays at specific time points or in a time-lapse experiment. We tested the function of our Red LDLs in different cell types: HepG2, HEK293T, HeLa, and human PBMCs. Unlike standard Oil Red O staining for intracellular neutral lipids,3 this protocol allows the staining of lipoproteins and monitoring of their uptake and catabolism without affecting intracellular neutral lipids. In all the tested conditions, the particles appeared to maintain both the uptake function and could be metabolized. In contrast to the fluorescent dyes, this method enables the formalin fixation of treated cells, with color retention of the internalized Red LDLs allowing the monitoring of the uptake in bright field microscopy without further LDL staining. Moreover, the recognition of subcellular structures is possible when the uptake is performed at a magnification of more than 400×. The use of Red LDLs in bright field microscopy is particularly advantageous compared to fluorescent dyes if the researcher aims to study the dynamic uptake of lipoproteins in a time-lapse experiment. The cell membrane is recognizable in bright field microscopy, and nuclear staining is unnecessary. Conversely, the same experiment performed using fluorescent dyes would need a contemporary nucleus or cytoplasm staining necessary to localize cells that might fade during the LDL uptake experiment. Fluorescent-stained LDLs are required if the LDL uptake is studied using flow cytometry.

Before the staining of native LDLs, isolate these lipoproteins from human plasma using gradient-based ultracentrifugation4,5 (commercially available lipoproteins can also be purchased) and prepare the Oil Red O working solutions.

Blood draw and plasma storage were performed in accordance with the protocol approved by the Sapienza University of Rome and the Policlinico Umberto I of Rome Ethics Committees (Protocol 0061/2022). Expected outcomes

Using the proposed protocol, we were able to stain human-derived LDLs and use them for cell treatment, either in time-lapse experiments for uptake monitoring or in paraformaldehyde-fixed cells to monitor LDL binding and uptake without the use of a fluorescence microscope.

All the tested cell lines show Red LDL uptake, particularly if starved. Figure 1A shows LDL uptake in HepG2, HeLa, and HEK 293T cells. As expected, the uptake of Red LDL is much more evident in HepG2 cells where both small uptake vesicles and bigger late endosomes are evident. HEK293T shows little LDL uptake, but small uptake vesicles are still noticeable.

Limitations

Although we found a method that is easily reproducible for monitoring LDL uptake, it is designed for use with a high-magnification and high-resolution microscope and cannot be used for flow cytometry. Using fluorescent dyes such as Dil or BODIPY might outperform this method for different applications and low-resolution microscopes.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Collecting all floating LDLs after ultracentrifugation in 1 mL of supernatant is impossible.

Retrieving all lipoprotein fractions after ultracentrifugation might be tricky since the amount of lipoprotein differs from patient to patient, and they tend to form a monolayer that might diffuse in the solution if pipetting is not performed correctly.

Potential solution

•A glass Pasteur pipette with a small-pointed tip helps to pipette the LDLs easily without solubilizing them. The tip must be kept steadily in the air-water interface, and suction should be done very slowly.

A glass Pasteur pipette with a small-pointed tip helps to pipette the LDLs easily without solubilizing them. The tip must be kept steadily in the air-water interface, and suction should be done very slowly.

•It is recommended to rotate the polycarbonate ultracentrifuge tube to direct LDLs to the Pasteur’s Tip.

It is recommended to rotate the polycarbonate ultracentrifuge tube to direct LDLs to the Pasteur’s Tip.

Problem 2

LDL denaturates when the Red LDL staining solution is added to lipoproteins. Although DMSO is not a strong denaturant of lipoproteins, it might denaturate them if LDL concentration in the solution is high. We recommend a final LDL concentration of 1–2 μg/μL. A high risk of LDL denaturation is observed for concentrations >5 μg/μL.

Potential solution

•Check passages. If pure DMSO is added to undiluted concentrated LDLs, it might misfold them.

Check passages. If pure DMSO is added to undiluted concentrated LDLs, it might misfold them.

•Check the Oil Red O mother solution. Oil Red O is commonly dissolved in isopropanol for lipid stain in formalin-fixed tissues. Check the used Oil Red O working solution. A separate staining solution is strongly recommended to be freshly produced using DMSO as a solvent.

Check the Oil Red O mother solution. Oil Red O is commonly dissolved in isopropanol for lipid stain in formalin-fixed tissues. Check the used Oil Red O working solution. A separate staining solution is strongly recommended to be freshly produced using DMSO as a solvent.

Problem 3

After LDL staining, the excess Oil Red sediments in the water solution form a pellet at the bottom of the Eppendorf tube. The amount of formed pellet depends on relative quantities of dissolved Oil Red in DMSO and LDL concentration in the Red LDL solution. If the protocol is followed precisely, the resulting pellet is tiny and easily removed after centrifugation. Oil Red O crystals might not precipitate if excess Oil Red is used, making images challenging to analyze, as shown in Figure 4.

An example of excessive red LDL

Potential solution

•Reduce the amount of Red LDL staining solution or increase LDL concentration in the Red LDL solution.

Reduce the amount of Red LDL staining solution or increase LDL concentration in the Red LDL solution.

•Resuspend the pellet, let the solution rest further 10′, and centrifuge the solution, doubling the centrifuge time (2.000 g 10′).

Resuspend the pellet, let the solution rest further 10′, and centrifuge the solution, doubling the centrifuge time (2.000 g 10′).

Problem 4

Cells detach after adding red LDLs. Although a final concentration of 10–30 μg/mL is well tolerated by the tested cells. Some more sensitive cells might detach if exposed to concentrated LDLs.

Potential solution

•Reduce the final concentration of Red LDLs to 1–10 μg/mL and repeat the experiment.

Reduce the final concentration of Red LDLs to 1–10 μg/mL and repeat the experiment.

•In some cases, residual DMSO might be toxic for some cells. In this case, after staining, Red LDL at 2 μg/μL and 30% DMSO v/v. can be further diluted using PBS to 0.2 μg/μL and 3% DMSO v/v and used accordingly.

Problem 5

A low number of activated Lymphocytes are found in the final glass slide.

Potential solution

•Check the starting sample for cell availability after thawing. Cell viability might be very low if the sample storage temperature is unstable or if the sample is frozen longer.

Check the starting sample for cell availability after thawing. Cell viability might be very low if the sample storage temperature is unstable or if the sample is frozen longer.

•If cell viability is >70%, check the fixation phase. Longer formaldehyde fixation time might increase the final cell number. Wait until the sample is dehydrated before placing the coverslip.
