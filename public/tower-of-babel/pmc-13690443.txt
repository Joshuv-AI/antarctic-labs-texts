Muscular pain causes lower urinary tract symptoms
Authors: Peng-Hui Wang
Published: 2026

Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), encompassing a range of symptoms of the lower urinary tract (LUT), involved in storage, voiding, and post-micturition symptoms, reflecting the phases of the micturition cycle.1 LUTS can be separated into daytime and nighttime symptoms, including urinary urgency and urinary incontinence (UI) related to urgency (UUI) or stress (SUI) from raised intra-abdominal pressure, with nocturia being the most notable nighttime symptom. These are commonly experienced by both men and women and encountered by a wide range of healthcare practitioners.1 LUTS have diverse manifestations and multiple etiologies. Most importantly, they can substantially impair patients’ quality of life (QoL). Conventionally, the Lower Urinary Tract Research Network Symptom Index-10 (LURN-SI-10) is the most commonly accepted tool to evaluate the severity and classification of LUTS. It identifies urgency as the most prevalent LUTS, accounting for more than half of participants, while UUI was observed in nearly one-third of participants.2 The International Continence Society (ICS) initially defined LUTS, limited to conditions, syndromes, and urodynamic manifestations thereof, in 2002, but missed the “causation” of LUTS, resulting in the absence of pathophysiological causes, which are associated with considerable variation in LUTS.3 In 2002, pain was not included in the definition of LUTS.3 In 2022, ICS integrated an anatomical pathogenesis to define LUTS, and offered the more feasible and reproducible references to manage the subjects with LUTS, which includes (1) storage symptoms (overactive bladder [urgency, frequency, and nocturia] and nocturnal enuresis; stress incontinence, mixed urinary incontinence, bladder sensations); (2) voiding symptoms (slow stream, splitting or spraying, intermittent stream, and straining); (3) feeling of incomplete emptying (post-micturition dribble); (4) incontinence during sexual activity; (5) symptoms associated with pelvic organ prolapse; and (6) symptoms or syndromes suggestive of LUT disorders, such as genitourinary pain syndromes, the specific or nonspecific pain syndromes to measure the frequency, severity, and impact of LUTS.3 Recent evidence suggests that “pain” may be one of the main causes contributing to the occurrence of “LUTS”,4 although the causal relationship between pain and LUTS remains uncertain. A recent article in the Journal of the Chinese Medical Association (JCMA) from the Taipei Medical University Hospital further attempted to clarify the correlation between pain, particularly chronic pelvic pain (CPP), and LUTS.5

Tsai et al. used their institution’s electronic clinical database to identify 1026 women diagnosed with CPP between 2020 and 2022 to identify 307 women who had a simultaneous diagnosis of LUTS.5 The authors evaluated pain characteristics of the levator ani muscle (LAM) and classified the patients into three groups: pure entheseal pain (n = 32), pure muscle pain (n = 81), and both (n = 194). They found that entheseal pain alone or in combination with muscle pain not only contributed to a higher incidence of urinary symptoms, including urinary frequency, residual urinary sensation, and bladder pain symptoms, but was also associated with other pain-related comorbidities, such as bearing-down sensation, dysmenorrhea, irritable bowel syndrome, and systemic myofascial pain.5 No statistically significant differences were found between pure muscular pain (n=81) and entheseal pain (n=32) in either urinary symptoms or pain-related comorbidities, and no significant intergroup differences were observed in urodynamic parameters, suggesting that urodynamic assessment methods cannot provide a reliable differentiation between entheseal pain and myofascial pain.5 However, the combined pain group showed the highest symptom prevalence, indicating that LUTS are unlikely to arise from peripheral structural or functional abnormalities of the LUT.5 Moreover, the authors found that the specific association between entheseal pain and morning stiffness implies a divergent, potentially inflammatory, pathophysiological component.5 Based on these findings, the authors concluded that uniform dissociation observed between symptom severity and normal urodynamic findings across all pain subtypes supports the presence of a shared downstream mechanism, most probably central sensitization rather than peripheral structural dysfunction in patients with LUTS.5 The current study is interesting and worthy of further discussion.

First, CPP and LUTS are believed to be different categories in clinical practice,1,6 and patients are treated by different healthcare practitioners. CPP, defined as pain perceived to originate from pelvic organs or structures, typically lasts longer than 6 months and is often associated with negative cognitive, behavioral, sexual, and emotional consequences, affecting 15% to 26% of women worldwide.6–8 Although pain is a major and key component of CPP, CPP frequently coexists with other chronic overlapping pain conditions, which include fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, chronic migraine, chronic tension-type headache, temporomandibular joint disorder, chronic low back pain, irritable bowel syndrome, interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS), vulvodynia, and endometriosis. Most importantly, CPP varies considerably in frequency, pattern, and location, suggesting that CPP is not a single disease but rather a symptom with many potential causes.6 The chronic overlapping pain conditions of CPP are similar to the complexity and overlapping symptoms of LUTS. Additionally, overlapping diagnoses are found in both CPP and LUTS, and IC/BPS is a typical example.3,6 Therefore, the conclusion reached by Tsai and Chin in the current study is reasonable, proposing that LUTS may share a final pathway of central sensitization rather than peripheral structural dysfunction.5 This central sensitization is also a key pathophysiological mechanism underlying CPP since CPP has long been believed to share common risk factors and pathophysiologic mechanisms, most notably central nervous system sensitization (also describing nociplastic pain, centralized pain, and central sensitization) that promotes chronic pain at multiple levels, such as spinal, subcortical, and cortical areas.6

Second, Dr. Tsai further suggested that the diagnosis of LAM-related pain should primarily rely on targeted physical examination rather than on urodynamic testing.5 In fact, the same group has reported entheseal pain of LAM as a novel source of CPP.9 They divided LAM into three locations, including an area measuring approximately 2 to 3 cm rostral to the ischial spine before the great sciatic notch; the cranial part of the inferior border of the superior ramus of the pubic bone; and the caudal part of the inferior border of the superior ramus of the pubic bone. They found that LAM entheseal pain caused referred pain in at least 10 areas, primarily in the lower abdominal quadrants (40.2%-47.3%) followed by the inguinal area (8.9%-15.1%) and other specific regions, such as the labia majora (7.1%), labia minora (4.5%), urethra (2.9%), and anus (2.7%).9 Furthermore, Patients with LAM entheseal pain exhibited LUTS, including urinary frequency (72.3%), urgency (42.9%), nocturia (53.6%), residual urine sensation (64.3%), urinary incontinence (30.3%), and a weak urine stream (47.9%). The LAM entheseal pain-induced LUTS can be explained by the crucial role of the LAM in maintaining urethral tension, subsequently directly affecting proper urination function.9 It is not surprising to see the results from the current study, which further support CPP as the cause associated with the development of LUTS, regardless of whether the urodynamic examination is normal or abnormal in these patients presenting with simultaneous CPP and LUTS.5

Third, entheseal pain is pain of the entheses, which are specialized tissues that connect tendons, ligaments, or joint capsules with bones.10 Because of their biological function, entheses are prone to mechanical overuse with subsequent inflammation and tissue remodeling (“degeneration”) accumulating after repeated mechanical stress, resulting in pain even in the absence of major structural changes.10 With a better understanding of entheseal pain, it is easy to understand why Dr. Tsai said that symptoms associated with either CPP or LUTS may result from central sensitization rather than structural abnormality or dysfunction.5 Moreover, based on the aforementioned hypothesis, the authors further tested the effect of analgesics on LUTS in patients with LAM entheseal pain and found that the prescription of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) can be successful in relieving the severity and frequency of LUTS,4 because prostaglandin E2 is a mediator in entheseal pain10 and LAM entheseal pain is a cause of LUTS.9 Therefore, NSAIDs can effectively relieve reduce the frequency and severity of pain, and indirectly ameliorate the severity or frequency of LUTS in their study.4

In conclusion, based on Dr. Tsai’s study,5 careful and detailed physical examination may be important in women with LUTS, since the bimanual examination can detect the LAM entheseal pain. NSAIDs may be used as a therapeutic test for treating women with LUTS and concomitant CPP, particularly for LAM entheseal pain, which may avoid a more invasive urodynamic examination or expensive and time-consuming procedures. Of importance, similar to the treatment of many diseases,11–15 other important considerations include cost and adverse events, since treatment may vary greatly between medication classes, but truly and significantly affect our clinical practice not only for patients but also for healthcare providers. This issue is also important for treating women with LUTS, because LUTS are generally not life-threatening.
