Predictors of low birth weight: a hospital-based matched case-control study in the Ashanti region, Ghana
Authors: Muftawu Mohammed Saani, Julius Kwabena Karikari, Emmanuel Konadu, Bhavana Singh, Alhassan Yushawu, Samson Alhassan, Daniel Boateng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBirth weight is an important measure for determining the health and development of newborns. Epidemiological findings indicate that infants with low birth weight (LBW) face a 20-fold higher chance of dying than those with normal birth weight. This study aimed to examine the factors associated with LBW at KNUST Hospital.MethodsA facility-based matched case-control study design was used. The study included all babies born at KNUST Hospital between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2023. Infants born with birth defects and those born from multiple births were excluded from the study. A total of 67 cases (birth weight < 2.5 kg) were identified and matched with 134 controls (birth weight ≥ 2.5 kg) based on maternal age and sex of the baby. Conditional logistic regression was used to examine the association between independent variables and LBW.ResultsThe study found that women with eight or more antenatal care (ANC) visits had 80% lower odds of delivering an LBW infant than their counterparts (AOR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.09–0.45). Women with hemoglobin levels ≥11 g/dL at registration had 86% lower odds of delivering an LBW infant than those with hemoglobin levels <11 g/dL (AOR = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.06–0.32). Each additional week of gestation at delivery was associated with a 41% reduction in the odds of low birth weight (AOR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.45–0.78).ConclusionInadequate ANC, anemia during pregnancy, and low gestational age at delivery impacted LBW.

Introduction

Low birth weight (LBW), defined as a birth weight below 2.5 kg, is a critical global health indicator associated with neonatal mortality, childhood developmental challenges, and long-term health risks, such as cardiovascular diseases and cognitive impairments [1], [2]. Globally, 14.7% of newborns, approximately 19.8 million infants, were classified as LBW in 2020, with marked regional disparities. Southern Asia reports the highest prevalence (26.4%), whereas Northern Europe and the Americas document lower rates of 4.7% and 7.0%, respectively [3]. In sub-Saharan Africa, including Ghana, LBW contributes significantly to neonatal mortality, accounting for 40–60% of newborn deaths and reflecting underlying maternal health challenges and socioeconomic inequities [2], [4]. Infants with LBW face a 20-fold higher risk of death than those with normal birth weight (2.5–3.9 kg), underscoring the urgency of addressing its determinants [5], [6].

In Ghana, despite improvements in institutional delivery rates (88% in 2022), approximately 12% of births still occur outside health facilities, limiting opportunities for routine birth weight measurement and documentation [3]. National household surveys have reported a low birth weight incidence of approximately 10%, whereas facility-based studies, including one conducted at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, have reported a higher incidence of 16.5% [7], [8]. These differences may reflect variations in birth weight documentation, place of delivery, referral patterns, clinical risk profiles of women delivering at health facilities, and differences in the populations included in household surveys and hospital-based studies. This is critical for achieving the World Health Organization (WHO) 2025 target of a 30% reduction in LBW incidence, which requires tailored interventions in high-burden regions [2].

Maternal and obstetric factors strongly influence the incidence of LBW. Sociodemographic determinants, such as low education, malnutrition, and poverty, intersect with clinical risks, including malaria, hypertension, anemia, and inadequate antenatal care [9], [10]. Preterm birth (<37 weeks) and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) are the primary contributors, often exacerbated by infections, lack of iron supplementation, and multiple pregnancies [11]. In Ghana, maternal age, primiparity, and HIV infection further compound risks, emphasizing the need for integrated prenatal care [12], [13], [14].

This study examined the factors associated with low birth weight among women delivering at the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST) Hospital, which serves Ayigya and surrounding communities in Kumasi. This study examined maternal sociodemographic, obstetric, and clinical factors associated with low birth weight among deliveries at KNUST Hospital. The findings provide facility-specific evidence that may inform clinical decision-making and service planning within hospitals and similar healthcare settings. Community-level social, cultural, behavioural, and healthcare access factors were beyond the scope of this study and warrant investigation through future population-based primary research. The findings would also inform localized strategies to mitigate LBW incidence, align with global health targets, and reduce neonatal mortality in underserved settings.

Methods and material

Study design

This study utilized facility-based matched case-control design based on routinely collected electronic hospital records. Data were extracted specifically for this study using a structured data extraction sheet (Supplementary Material 1) to investigate whether there was any difference between the cases and controls in their exposure to previous possible risk factors. The case-to-control ratio was 1:2. Cases were individually matched with two controls by infant sex and maternal age within ±1 year. Cases were defined as babies born during the study period with a birth weight of less than 2.5 kg, while controls were those born in the same study period with a birth weight ≥ 2.5 kg.

Study setting

The study was conducted at the University Hospital located within the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, Kumasi. Initially established as a dressing station in 1952, the KNUST hospital has expanded into a fully functional hospital with a capacity of 125 beds. It now serves a population of over 200,000, including approximately 42,000 students, 30,000 staff members and their dependents, as well as around 150,000 individuals from over 30 nearby communities such as Ayigya, Bomso, Ayeduase, Kotei and Boadi.

While the hospital was originally intended to meet the healthcare needs of university staff, their families, and students, it has since expanded to serve the general public and surrounding communities, functioning as a District Hospital. It provides general medical care and specialized services.

Study population

In this study, the population consisted of babies delivered at the KNUST hospital between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2023.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All infants born during the study period were included. Infants born with birth defects and those born from multiple births were excluded from the study. This decision was made due to the understanding that these cases carry distinct risks and causes for LBW and premature birth. Stillbirths and patients with incomplete data were excluded from the analysis.

Data source

The data for this study were obtained through a review of electronic clinical records from KNUST Hospital. Information was extracted from the Aksoft system records, focusing on maternal and newborn characteristics, using a structured data extraction sheet (Supplementary Material 1) between January 15 and February 15, 2024. Two health information officers who were briefed on the study's eligibility criteria and the specific variables required for extraction were responsible for extracting data from the system. Permission to access the data was obtained from the hospital administration (Approval Number: UH/51). To ensure confidentiality, all extracted data were anonymized using hospital record numbers instead of patient names. These records were used to identify and classify the cases and controls for the study. Access to the dataset was restricted exclusively to the principal investigator to maintain data security and confidentiality.

Sample size and sampling methods/techniques

Data on all babies born at KNUST Hospital from January 1, 2023, to December 31, 2023, were used for the study. Whenever a case was identified, two controls were selected from babies born on the same day who did not have low birth weight and who met the matching criteria for infant sex and maternal age. Controls were selected systematically based on the order of births recorded in the delivery register, with the next eligible baby meeting the matching criteria being selected as the control for each case. Overall, there were 1249 deliveries throughout the study period, of which 86 had birth weights below 2.5 kg and 1163 had birth weights ≥2.5 kg. 52 were excluded due to various reasons: 8 due to missing information, 20 based on multiple births, 21 due to macerated and fresh stillbirths, and 3 congenital abnormalities, leaving a total of 1197 babies. Finally, 67 cases were matched with 134 controls at a 1:2 ratio. Study variables

The dependent variable in this study was the birth weight. Babies weighing less than 2.5 kg were classified as having low birth weight, while those with a birth weight of 2.5 kg or more were considered to have normal birth weight, regardless of their gestational age. Based on this classification, birth weight was coded as a dichotomous variable, with 1 representing “low birth weight (< 2.5 kg)” and 0 representing “normal birth weight (≥ 2.5 kg).”

The independent variables in this study were grouped into maternal sociodemographic characteristics and maternal obstetric and clinical factors. Maternal age at delivery was recorded as a continuous variable (in years). Because maternal age was used as a matching variable, it was included to describe the study population rather than as an exposure variable in the regression analyses. Education level was classified and coded as 0 for ‘non-formal education (no formal schooling or adult literacy programs)’, 1 for ‘primary/Junior High School (basic)’, 2 for ‘secondary’, and 3 for ‘tertiary education’. Mothers' employment was categorized and coded as 0 for ‘unemployed’, 1 for ‘non-formal employed (self-employed, petty trading, farming, artisan work, and other informal sector occupations)’, and 2 for ‘formal employed (government or registered private sector employment with a regular salary)’. Parity was categorized as 0 for ‘0–1 deliveries’ and 1 for ‘≥ 2 deliveries’. The total number of antenatal care (ANC) visits during pregnancy was classified as fewer than eight or eight or more visits according to the 2016 WHO ANC model. Gestational age at delivery was obtained from maternal obstetric records and recorded in completed weeks. It was determined based on the documented estimated gestational age, which relied on the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP) when this information was known and deemed reliable. Obstetric ultrasound assessments were performed whenever possible. The gestational age documented in the maternal record at the time of delivery was extracted for the study and was categorized and coded as preterm 0 (< 37 completed weeks) or term/post term 1 (≥ 37 completed weeks). For the regression analysis, gestational age was treated as a continuous variable measured in completed weeks. Mode of delivery was also categorized and coded as 0 ‘spontaneous vaginal delivery’, 1 ‘cesarian section’. Maternal Hb at registration (first ANC visit) was classified and coded as anemia 1 (< 11 g/dl) normal 0 (≥ 11 g/dl). Hypertension in pregnancy was categorized as no hypertension when systolic blood pressure (SBP) was <140 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was <90 mmHg, and as hypertension when SBP was ≥140 mmHg and/or DBP was ≥90 mmHg, based on the documented hospital diagnosis or blood-pressure criteria.” Thus, an SBP of exactly 140 mmHg, a DBP of exactly 90 mmHg, or elevation of either SBP or DBP was classified as hypertension.

Data management and analysis

The data were extracted into an Excel template, where they were checked for completeness and accuracy before being imported into Stata version 17 for recoding and further analysis. Maternal sociodemographic, clinical, and obstetric factors and infant weight categories were summarized using frequencies with percentages and means with their respective standard deviations presented in tables. As part of the data quality assessment, a scatter plot of birth weight against gestational age at delivery was generated separately by infant sex to identify potentially implausible combinations of gestational age and birth weights. Gestational age was recorded in completed weeks, and birth weight was recorded in kilograms. Observations with unusual gestational age–birth weight combinations identified through visual inspection were flagged for verification against the original clinical records. Observations were not excluded solely based on their position on the plot; exclusion was considered only when verification of the source records indicated an erroneous value. Bivariable conditional logistic regression models were used to assess the effects of independent variables on the dependent variable. All variables were retained in the final model, except for the two matched variables: maternal age and sex of the baby.

A sensitivity analysis was performed in which gestational age was classified as preterm (<37 completed weeks) or term/post-term (≥37 completed weeks). A separate conditional logistic regression model was fitted using the classified gestational age variable instead of continuous gestational age (Supplementary Table S1). Assessment of multicollinearity showed that the variance inflation factor (VIF) values for the independent variables were within acceptable limits (<5), indicating low-to-moderate correlation among the predictors and no evidence of problematic multicollinearity that would preclude their inclusion in the multivariable regression model. Interactions between independent variables were also tested, and no interactions were observed. The likelihood ratio test was used to assess the goodness of fit of the model.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval (CHRPE/AP/331/24) was obtained from the Committee on Human Research, Publications, and Ethics, KNUST. Permission to access the hospital records was granted by KNUST Hospital. Because anonymized routinely collected records were used, individual informed consent was waived in accordance with the institutional ethical guidelines.

Mothers with hypertension during pregnancy had 290% higher odds of delivering a low birth weight infant than those without hypertension (AOR = 3.90, 95% CI: 1.61–9.47). Each one-week increase in gestational age at delivery was associated with 41% lower odds of low birth weight (AOR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.45–0.78). As a sensitivity analysis, gestational age was further categorized as preterm (<37 completed weeks) and term/post-term (≥37 completed weeks), where delivery at ≥37 completed weeks was associated with 91% lower odds of low birth weight compared with preterm delivery (AOR = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.03–0.26). This finding was consistent with the primary analysis, in which gestational age was modelled as a continuous variable (Supplementary Table S1).

For mode of delivery, the estimated odds of low birth weight were similar for spontaneous vaginal delivery and cesarean section (AOR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.50–2.57). After adjusting for other variables in the model, parity, maternal employment status, and educational status were not significantly associated with low birth weight. This conclusion is supported by the fact that the 95% confidence intervals for their adjusted odds ratios included the null value of 1. Discussions

Among the 1249 births recorded at KNUST Hospital in 2023, 86 infants had a birth weight below 2.5 kg, corresponding to an overall LBW proportion of 6.9% before the application of the study exclusion criteria. Following the exclusion of records that did not meet the eligibility criteria, 67 LBW cases remained and were included in the matched case-control analysis. The overall LBW proportion of 6.9% was lower than estimates reported in national and other studies conducted in Ghana (15,16). National household surveys have indicated that approximately 16.8% of infants are classified as having low birth weights (16). The comparatively lower proportion observed at KNUST Hospital may be due to differences in the populations studied, referral patterns, maternal risk profiles, gestational age distributions, methods of weighing infants, exclusion criteria, and level of healthcare provided across various study settings. Therefore, caution should be exercised when comparing hospital-based estimates to national survey estimates, as the underlying populations and methodologies for determining low birth weight may vary significantly. This study aimed to identify predictors of low birth weight at KNUST Hospital, focusing on maternal obstetric and clinical characteristics, as well as sociodemographic factors such as maternal education and employment status. The findings revealed that the sociodemographic factors examined were not significantly associated with low birth weight. However, certain maternal obstetric and clinical factors were associated with an increased occurrence of low birth weight.

Maternal education is closely linked to adverse birth outcomes, such as small for gestational age, low birth weight, and preterm birth. It serves as a key factor influencing birth outcomes because of its impact on health behaviors, access to healthcare, and overall socioeconomic standing [15]. In this study, no association was found between maternal education and low birth weight (LBW), indicating that maternal educational attainment did not demonstrate a measurable influence on the likelihood of delivering an LBW infant. Although maternal education is widely regarded as a beneficial determinant of maternal and child health, its effect on birth weight outcomes in this context may be limited or potentially confounded by other more influential factors, such as socioeconomic conditions, access to healthcare, or environmental influences. The absence of a significant association between maternal education and LBW in the present study is consistent with recent studies that reported similar findings [[16], [17]]. However, this result differs from much of the existing literature, which shows that mothers with higher education levels are more likely to access and use prenatal care services, follow healthy eating habits, and avoid risky behaviors such as smoking and excessive alcohol use [[18], [19], [20]]. The lack of association in this study could be explained by the relatively high educational attainment among the participants, with many having completed secondary or tertiary education, which may reduce the variation in birth outcomes based on the educational level. Additionally, most women who delivered at the university hospital were from urban areas (within Kumasi) and had similar educational backgrounds. The relative similarity in educational and urban-access characteristics among participants may have reduced the variability needed to detect differences in LBW by educational level. Similar access to health information, medical services, and nutritional information among women delivering at the hospital may also have contributed to the lack of an observed association [21].

Maternal employment status during pregnancy has been studied as a potential factor influencing low birth weight (LBW). This is especially relevant because certain job conditions, such as physically demanding tasks, long working hours, and job insecurity, may negatively impact pregnancy outcomes. [22]. In this study, maternal employment status was not significantly associated with low birth weight (LBW). Specifically, the likelihood of LBW did not differ significantly among mothers who were formally employed, those in non-formal employment, and those who were unemployed. This finding indicates that employment status, as categorized in this study, was not an independent predictor of LBW in the population examined. Similar results were reported by Sharma et al. [23] and Adjei-Gyamfi et al. [24].

Maternal health plays a crucial role in fetal development. It is a sensitive indicator that can predict the outcome of newborn health. The health of newborns largely depends on the health of the mother during and after pregnancy. When mothers experience health challenges during pregnancy, such as malnutrition, poor health, or insufficient prenatal support, their infants are more likely to suffer from various adverse birth outcomes [25]. The analysis of maternal obstetric and clinical factors in this study in relation to LBW revealed that antenatal care visits, gestational age at delivery, hemoglobin levels at registration, and hypertension during pregnancy were associated with LBW.

During antenatal care visits, mothers who attended fewer than eight antenatal care visits were found to be at a higher risk of delivering LBW babies than those with more frequent visits. This finding aligns with other studies that have explored the link between maternal factors and LBW [[24], [26]] and emphasizes the importance of adequate ANC visits for monitoring fetal development and mitigating potential complications during pregnancy [27]. However, another study found no association between ANC attendance and LBW [23].

This study also found a relationship between hemoglobin level at registration and LBW. Mothers with hemoglobin levels below 11 g/dl at registration were at an increased risk of giving birth to an LBW infant, likely because it is linked to decreased oxygen supply to the fetus, resulting in intrauterine growth restriction [28]. Other studies have reported that a decline in a pregnant woman's hemoglobin level increases the chances of delivering an underweight child [[18], [22]]. Hence, addressing maternal anemia through nutritional supplementation and regular monitoring is vital for improving birth outcomes.

Hypertension during pregnancy was also associated with LBW. The results further indicate that the odds of giving birth to an LBW baby increase when a pregnant woman's blood pressure during pregnancy is high. Other scholarly studies have reported hypertension as an important risk factor for poor birth outcomes [[8], [23], [29], [30], [31]]. Consequently, hypertension during pregnancy increases the likelihood of inadequate fetal nutrition, leading to impaired fetal growth and ultimately contributing to LBW [32]. Thus, monitoring and controlling blood pressure before and during pregnancy should be a priority among health workers, which will help prevent these adverse birth outcomes.

In this study, gestational age at delivery was associated with low birth weight. Each additional week of gestation was associated with lower odds of delivering a low birth weight infant. This observation is consistent with previous studies that have reported that an increase gestational age is accompanied by improved fetal growth and a lower likelihood of low birth weight. [[17], [33]]. This relationship is biologically plausible because a substantial proportion of fetal weight gain occurs during the final weeks of pregnancy. Consequently, pregnancies that continue to later gestational ages allow more time for fetal growth and weight gain before delivery [34].

Study limitations

This study had some limitations. First, the study used secondary data, which could have some errors due to data entry mistakes, which could affect the findings. In addition, the study was conducted in a single hospital, which limits the ability to generalize the findings. Again, because the data were extracted from the electronic system and not collected directly from the study subjects, some important variables were not evaluated (malaria in pregnancy, birth spacing, birth order, maternal height, socioeconomic status, maternal nutrition, etc.) which may have affected the outcome of interest. The hospital-based nature of the study may have introduced selection bias, as both cases and controls were drawn from the KNUST Hospital records. Women who deliver at these facilities may differ significantly from those who deliver at other facilities or outside the healthcare system. Consequently, the study participants may not accurately represent the broader population of pregnant women in the area, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

The study found no associations between maternal sociodemographic factors and low birth weight, contrasting with the existing literature that typically associates higher education and formal employment with better birth outcomes. Key maternal factors, such as antenatal care visits (using the WHO proposed 2016 ANC model), hemoglobin levels, gestational age at delivery, and hypertension during pregnancy, were associated with LBW, emphasizing the importance of regular healthcare access and management of maternal health conditions during pregnancy.
