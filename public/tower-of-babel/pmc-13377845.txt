Ghana’s 2025 physical activity report card: children, adolescents and CAWD

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRegular physical activity in childhood and adolescence supports cardiometabolic health, mental wellbeing, and learning, yet many countries lack routine surveillance and actionable, benchmark-linked accountability tools. The Active Healthy Kids Global Alliance (AHKGA) “Report Card” approach synthesizes the best available national evidence to grade physical activity behaviours and their determinants, enabling cross-country comparisons and policy dialogue. Ghana last published a Report Card in 2018; since then, new evidence and policy developments warrant an updated appraisal, including attention to children and adolescents with disabilities and/or chronic conditions (CAWD).MethodsWe compiled and synthesized Ghana-specific evidence used for the 2025 Ghana Report Card within the AHKGA Global Matrix 5.0 framework. Ten common indicators were graded for the regular population (ages 5–17 years) using the Global Matrix 5.0 rubric bands (A + to F; INC for insufficient evidence). Data sources included national school-based surveillance (Global School-based Student Health Survey (GSHS) 2012 fact sheets/microdata), peer-reviewed studies (2009–2022), theses, policy documents, and program-based budgets. When multiple eligible estimates were available, we applied transparent synthesis rules (e.g., unweighted means for Active Transportation). CAWD grades were assigned only when disability-relevant, benchmark-aligned evidence or defensible structured consensus supported grading.ResultsGhana’s highest grades were Sedentary Behaviour (B+) and Active Transportation (B), while Overall Physical Activity was graded D-. Key benchmark-linked estimates included: 25.0% meeting an MVPA proxy guideline (Overall Physical Activity), 27.6% participating in sport ≥ 3 times/week (Organized Sport), 73% active travel to/from school (Active Transportation; unweighted mean), and 78.4% below the “high sedentary time” threshold (Sedentary Behaviour). Physical Fitness was graded C- (mean percentile ~ 40–42 across three field tests). School (D) and Community & Environment (D-) reflected low effective PE delivery and limited access to acceptable, publicly accessible spaces in an Accra-based audit proxy. Government was graded B based on rubric-aligned policy, implementation, and funding/accountability scoring (mean score 67%). For CAWD, 7/10 indicators were INC; assignable grades were Family & Peers (C-), Community & Environment (F), and Government (D+).ConclusionsGhana’s 2025 Report Card highlights persistently low physical activity levels alongside gaps in school delivery, community environments, and surveillance systems. The CAWD results are dominated by evidence insufficiency, underscoring an urgent need for disability-disaggregated surveillance and inclusive infrastructure, alongside strengthened PE implementation and safer active travel systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27777-4.

Background

Physical activity (PA) during childhood and adolescence is foundational for healthy growth, cardiometabolic risk reduction, mental wellbeing, and the development of movement competence and lifelong activity habits [1–3]. Despite clear guideline recommendations, many populations do not achieve sufficient moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), and in settings such as Ghana, rapid urbanization, changing transport patterns, and increasing digital exposure may further reshape movement and sedentary behaviours. Countries also vary in the extent to which schools, communities, and government systems enable active living.

The Active Healthy Kids Global Alliance (AHKGA) Report Card model was created to translate disparate evidence into a concise, comparable indicator “grade profile” spanning behaviours (e.g., overall PA, sedentary behaviour), settings (e.g., school), and enabling systems (e.g., government). A central feature is the use of a harmonized rubric to grade the proportion of children and adolescents meeting benchmark definitions, producing an accessible accountability tool for policymakers and stakeholders [4, 5].

Ghana last published a national Report Card in 2018 [6]. That profile was valuable as a national benchmarking and advocacy tool, but several grades were based on sparse quantified evidence and expert interpretation, particularly for sedentary behaviour, physical fitness, and family and peer support. The present update is therefore not intended simply to replace one set of grades with another; rather, it provides a more transparent evidence-to-grade reassessment using newer subnational studies where available, clearer benchmark alignment, and explicit synthesis rules within the Global Matrix 5.0 framework.

Ghana also presents a distinctive sociocultural and policy context for interpreting child and adolescent physical activity. In the Ghanaian evidence base, children’s movement is shaped not only by leisure-time exercise, but also by daily routines such as walking to school, participation in school sport and physical education, informal play, and other context-dependent forms of movement. At the same time, these opportunities are increasingly influenced by rapid urbanization, traffic exposure, changing school demands, unequal access to safe recreation space, and growing digital exposure. The policy environment has also evolved since the 2018 cycle, including continued attention to physical education within the pre-tertiary curriculum and the publication of Ghana’s National Non-Communicable Diseases Policy (2022), making updated appraisal timely and policy-relevant [7–18].

A critical equity dimension is the inclusion of children and adolescents with disabilities and/or chronic conditions (CAWD). Disability-inclusive measurement remains rare in many low- and middle-income countries; without disability-disaggregated surveillance, it is difficult to assign accountability or track progress. Consequently, the Global Matrix 5.0 approach explicitly encourages parallel CAWD grading when evidence permits [19–21].

Accordingly, the added value of the 2025 Report Card lies not only in updating grades where possible, but also in making clearer which indicators remain anchored to older national data, which are newly informed by more recent subnational evidence, and where major surveillance gaps persist.

Study aim

To present the results of Ghana’s 2025 Report Card on Physical Activity for Children and Youth, including (where possible) the corresponding CAWD grades, using Global Matrix 5.0 benchmarks and grading rubric rules.

Methods

Study design

This study is a structured evidence synthesis and benchmarking exercise undertaken as part of Ghana’s participation in the Active Healthy Kids Global Alliance (AHKGA) Global Matrix 5.0 process. Rather than collecting new primary data, we audited, compiled, and graded the best available Ghana-specific evidence against predefined benchmark definitions and rubric bands for each indicator. This design is intended to provide a transparent evidence-to-grade trail in a context where nationally representative surveillance is incomplete and available evidence is often distributed across peer-reviewed studies, surveillance outputs, policy documents, theses, and other grey sources. The purpose of the Methods was therefore not only to identify evidence, but also to make explicit the decision rules used to judge benchmark comparability, extract defensible estimates, and assign grades in a reproducible way.

Indicator framework

We graded the 10 common AHKGA indicators for the regular population of children and adolescents aged 5–17 years: (1) Overall Physical Activity; (2) Organized Sport; (3) Active Play; (4) Active Transportation; (5) Sedentary Behaviour; (6) Physical Fitness; (7) Family & Peers; (8) School; (9) Community & Environment; and (10) Government. For CAWD, we applied the same indicator framework and Global Matrix 5.0 rubric (Table S2).

Grading rubric

Grades were assigned according to the Global Matrix 5.0 grading rubric bands (A + to F) mapped to the percentage of children/youth meeting the indicator benchmark; INC was assigned when evidence was insufficient or not benchmark comparable [5].

Evidence sources and identification

Evidence was compiled within the Ghana Global Matrix 5.0 audit process (Appendix Table S1, Table S2), with priority given to sources that could contribute benchmark-comparable and extraction-ready information for one or more Global Matrix indicators. Evidence streams included: (i) national surveillance outputs, especially the Global School-based Student Health Survey (GSHS) 2012 Junior High and Senior High fact sheets and microdata-based analyses; (ii) peer-reviewed Ghanaian studies published between 2009 and 2024; (iii) postgraduate theses and dissertations; (iv) technical reports and other grey literature containing measurable indicator-relevant information; and (v) policy, curriculum, and programme-based budget documents relevant to the Government indicator [7–18, 22–35]. Sources were retained only when they provided sufficient methodological detail to judge benchmark fit, extract denominators or decision-relevant content, and support transparent grading.

The evidence-identification process combined structured searching and targeted audit retrieval. Searches were undertaken across bibliographic databases and search platforms commonly used in the report-card process, together with Google Scholar, reference-list screening, and targeted retrieval of Ghana-relevant policy and technical documents. Search concepts were adapted to each indicator, but typically combined terms related to physical activity or the relevant indicator domain, children or adolescents, and Ghana. Where necessary, the team supplemented database searching with hand-searching of known Ghana sources and indicator-specific audit materials. Records were screened for relevance, benchmark comparability, and extractability, and where multiple publications were drawn on the same dataset, the most complete or methodologically informative source was retained. The overall identification, screening, and inclusion process is summarized in Fig. 1, while the included sources and their contributions are detailed in Appendix Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

For each indicator, we applied overarching eligibility criteria. Evidence had to be Ghana-specific, relevant to children and adolescents aged 5–17 years (or to a closely overlapping school-age population) and sufficiently described to allow judgment of benchmark comparability. For behavioural indicators, we prioritized estimates with clearly defined denominators, transparent measurement approaches, and the closest alignment to the corresponding Global Matrix benchmark definition (Table S2). Where multiple sources were available, national evidence was preferred over subnational evidence, and benchmark-aligned estimates were preferred over indirect or weakly specified measures. Studies or documents were excluded when they lacked extractable denominators, could not be aligned meaningfully to the benchmark, focused on populations outside the target age range without separable child/adolescent data, or provided only narrative discussion without decision-relevant measurable content. Where dated evidence remained the only benchmark-linked source, such evidence was retained but interpreted explicitly as time-sensitive rather than fully contemporary.

Data extraction

For each included data source, we extracted study year(s), setting/region, sampling frame, age range, sample size, operational definition and measurement approach, benchmark-aligned prevalence estimate (or test percentile interpretation for Physical Fitness), and subgroup estimates when reported (Appendix Table S1).

In addition, each source was classified according to its role in grading (primary, supporting, contextual, or policy-specific), and this classification is shown in Appendix Table S1 to clarify how individual sources informed each assigned grade.

CAWD grading approach

CAWD grades were assigned only when disability-relevant evidence was sufficient to support a defensible judgment. Where no benchmark-aligned disability-disaggregated quantitative prevalence data were available, the indicator was graded INC. For indicators relying on policy or structural evidence (e.g., Government; Community & Environment), we required explicit disability-relevant inclusion, accessibility, or implementation features before awarding credit. Where qualitative evidence informed grading, this was used conservatively and only when it provided consistent Ghana-specific information relevant to the indicator construct. In those cases, qualitative findings were not treated as prevalence estimates; instead, they were used to support a structured expert-consensus judgment about whether the balance of evidence was most compatible with a low, mid-range, or more supportive grade band. This approach was adopted because the available CAWD evidence base was sparse and uneven, but the resulting grades should be interpreted with caution given the unavoidable subjectivity introduced by qualitative-to-grade translation.

Comparison with Ghana’s 2018 report card

To contextualize changes across report-card cycles, we extracted Ghana’s 2018 grades from the published 2018 Report Card and compared them directionally with the 2025 grades. This comparison was intended as an interpretive aid rather than a formal trend analysis. Because grades may change as a function of new evidence, different indicator-specific data sources, clearer benchmark alignment, or revised synthesis decisions, cross-cycle differences were interpreted cautiously and were not assumed to indicate true behavioural or system change.

Behaviour indicators

*Overall Physical Activity (D-): The only nationally comparable benchmark-linked estimate indicated that 25.0% of senior high school students met an MVPA proxy guideline (≥ 60 min/day on ≥ 5 days in the past week). Sex differences were observed (male 29.0% vs. female 21.4%) [7, 22]. However, this estimate is derived from GSHS 2012 and should therefore be interpreted as a time-sensitive indicator signal rather than a precise estimate of current national MVPA compliance among Ghanaian adolescents.

Organized Sport (D): In urban schoolchildren aged 9–15 years, 27.6% participated in sporting activity ≥ 3 times/week. The estimate varies by school type (private 26.2%; public 32.0%) and does not fully distinguish organized sport from informal sport [25].

Active Play (INC): No Ghana source reported a benchmark-comparable prevalence estimate for unstructured active play with a clearly defined denominator.

Active Transportation (B): Four eligible sources yielded benchmark-comparable estimates of active travel to and/or from school: Sunyani (Bono) 97% (85% walking; 12% cycling); Upper East and Upper West 98.96%; peri-urban Accra (Adentan) 53.7% walking to school; and Tamale 40.3% active travel [8–12]. Using the prespecified unweighted synthesis rule, the mean prevalence was 72.5% (rounded to 73%), corresponding to grade B. Because the contributing studies differed substantially in sample size, setting, and regional coverage, this value should be interpreted as an audit-based national signal derived from heterogeneous subnational evidence rather than as a population-weighted national prevalence estimate.

*Sedentary Behaviour (B+): In the national GSHS 2012 fact sheets, “high sedentary time” (≥ 3 h/day sitting outside school/homework/sleep) was 19.0% in Junior High and 23.8% in Senior High. Pooling strata with sample-size weights yielded 21.6% with high sedentary time, implying 78.4% below the threshold [23, 24]. However, because this benchmark-linked grade is anchored to nationally representative data from 2012, it should be interpreted cautiously. More recent subnational evidence suggests that sedentary exposure may now be higher in some settings, underscoring the need for renewed national surveillance rather than assuming that the current grade reflects present-day behaviour.

Fitness, social support, and settings

Physical Fitness (C-): Fitness evidence aligned to three field tests was synthesized using percentile interpretations. Cardiorespiratory fitness was weak (mean 20-25th percentile), standing long jump was comparatively higher (mean 54th percentile), and handgrip strength was interpreted as 40-45th percentile. The mean percentile across tests was 40–42 [26–28].

Family & Peers (C): In the absence of direct PA-support measures, five GSHS protective factors were averaged: close friends (86.7%), peer support/school climate (35.2%), parental supervision (42.2%), parental bonding (40.2%), and parental connectedness (45.5%). The mean was 49.96% (50%) [22, 29, 30].

School (D): Teachers in public primary schools across six circuits reported 43 min of engaged PE time out of 140 min allocated per week (30.7% of allocated time delivered) [13, 31].

Community & Environment (D-): From the Mapping Accra parks/public spaces audit, we defined an accessible and acceptable-condition proxy (free/public and rated OK/GOOD). Nine spaces met the proxy, with a combined 1 km catchment population of 362,657 over an Accra-core denominator of 1,565,694 (23.2%) [14–17, 32, 33].

Because this proxy is derived from an Accra-based audit, it should be interpreted as an urban-specific signal rather than a nationally representative measure of environmental support for physical activity.

Government (B): Rubric-aligned scoring yielded policy presence/quality 100%, implementation/coverage 45%, and funding/accountability 55% (mean 66.7%, rounded to 67%) [1–3, 18, 34, 36, 37].

CAWD results

Seven CAWD indicators were graded INC due to a lack of benchmark-aligned, disability-disaggregated prevalence data (Overall Physical Activity, Organized Sport, Active Play, Active Transportation, *Sedentary Behaviour, Physical Fitness, and School).

Three CAWD grades were assignable: (i) Family & Peers (C-), based on Ghana qualitative evidence in inclusive schools indicating mixed peer support, peer victimization, and limited family/teacher support; (ii) Community & Environment (F), reflecting pervasive accessibility barriers; and (iii) Government (D+), based on rubric scoring requiring explicit PA-relevant inclusion clauses and funded implementation [19–21, 32, 33, 37, 38]. These assignable grades should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously because they are based on limited disability-relevant evidence and, in some cases, structured expert judgment rather than benchmark-aligned quantitative prevalence estimates.

Discussion

Principal findings

Ghana’s 2025 Report Card presents a mixed and uneven profile. On one hand, the available evidence suggests relatively high active school travel in several settings and a historically favourable sedentary-behaviour profile in the last nationally representative benchmark-linked dataset. On the other hand, overall MVPA compliance remains low, physical education delivery is weak, community and environmental supports are limited, and the CAWD profile is dominated by incomplete evidence. These findings indicate that Ghana’s child movement-behaviour system is shaped by both behavioural deficits and structural implementation gaps. At the same time, the 2025 profile should be interpreted as the best available benchmark-linked synthesis rather than as a fully current national surveillance dashboard, because several key grades remain anchored to older data or to heterogeneous subnational sources.

Interpretation in context

The combination of Active Transportation (B) and Overall Physical Activity (D-) should be interpreted carefully. The available evidence suggests that active school travel remains common in several Ghanaian settings, but this does not translate automatically into sufficient total daily MVPA for most children and adolescents. One plausible explanation is that routine walking to school may be frequent but short, unevenly distributed, or constrained by distance, traffic exposure, safety concerns, household time pressure, and other contextual factors documented in the Ghanaian and related urban literature [7–12]. Accordingly, the findings do not imply that active travel is unimportant; rather, they suggest that active school travel alone may be insufficient to compensate for wider deficits in daily movement opportunities.

The *Sedentary Behaviour (B+) grade requires especially cautious interpretation because it is anchored to nationally representative data collected in 2012. Given rapid urbanization, increasing smartphone ownership, changing screen practices, and broader digitalization of daily life in Ghana, sedentary exposure among children and adolescents may now differ substantially from the pattern reflected in that historical dataset. More recent subnational evidence already suggests that sedentary time may be higher in some settings. For this reason, the grade should not be interpreted as confirming a currently favourable national sedentary profile; instead, it highlights the continuing absence of renewed benchmark-aligned national surveillance [23, 24, 35].

The same caution applies to interpretation of change across report-card cycles. Although Table 2 presents 2018 and 2025 grades side by side, these differences should not be read as a formal trend analysis. In several domains, grade changes likely reflect new evidence sources, clearer benchmark alignment, or more transparent synthesis rules rather than confirmed behavioural or systems improvement. The comparative value of the 2025 cycle therefore lies less in proving temporal change than in clarifying where evidence has improved, where it remains weak, and where accountability gaps persist.

Schools and community environments

The School (D) grade underscores a persistent implementation challenge: physical education is present in curriculum and policy frameworks, but effective delivery appears to be constrained by competing academic demands, staffing limitations, and weak implementation monitoring. This matters because school is one of the few settings that can reach large numbers of children regardless of household resources. The recommendation to protect and audit PE delivery time therefore follows directly from the finding that only a limited proportion of allocated PE time appears to be translated into engaged instructional time [13, 31].

The Community & Environment (D-) grade should be interpreted more cautiously than a conventional national environmental indicator because it is based on an Accra-derived proxy. Even so, it provides a useful signal of the kinds of environmental deficits that may constrain activity in urban Ghana, including limited access to safe, publicly accessible, acceptable-condition spaces. We therefore frame the recommendation for improved public open space provision, maintenance, and accessibility not as a claim about all of Ghana, but as an evidence-informed response to the best available urban audit data in a context where nationally comparable environmental surveillance is lacking [14, 16, 17].

CAWD: evidence insufficiency and inclusion

The CAWD results are dominated by INC grades, indicating that Ghana currently lacks benchmark-aligned disability-disaggregated evidence for most report-card domains. This should not be interpreted as evidence that children and adolescents with disabilities are inactive across these indicators; rather, it reflects a surveillance failure that limits planning, budgeting, accountability, and inclusive policy design. Where CAWD grades were assignable, they were often informed by limited structural or qualitative evidence and, in some cases, conservative structured expert judgment rather than benchmark-aligned quantitative prevalence estimates. These grades are therefore useful as warning signals of likely inclusion barriers, but they should also be interpreted cautiously because the underlying evidence base remains sparse and uneven [19–21].

This has an important implication for future surveillance. Disability inclusion should not be treated as an optional extension once the main report card is complete. It should be built into routine measurement from the outset through disability-disaggregated indicators, accessible data-collection strategies, and indicator definitions broad enough to capture assistive mobility, inclusive participation, and environmental accessibility.

Policy and practice implications

The policy and practice implications follow directly from the pattern of findings. First, renewed national surveillance is needed because several grades remain anchored to older or incomplete evidence, especially for Overall Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour, and because the CAWD profile is dominated by incomplete grades. Second, the School grade supports stronger protection and monitoring of PE delivery time since weak implementation—not merely lack of curricular intent—appears to be a central barrier. Third, the Active Transportation findings support safer and more equitable school-travel systems, particularly in urban settings where traffic exposure, safety concerns, and constrained travel environments may limit the health benefit of otherwise common walking. Fourth, the Community & Environment findings support investment in safe, free-to-access, and accessible public spaces, while also underscoring the need for broader national environmental monitoring beyond Accra. Finally, the CAWD results support mainstreaming disability inclusion into routine surveillance, infrastructure standards, and physical activity programming rather than addressing it only after evidence gaps become visible.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of a harmonized Global Matrix rubric, explicit indicator-specific synthesis rules, clearer documentation of evidence sources through Fig. 1 and Appendix Table S1, and the inclusion of a CAWD perspective within the same national report-card framework. Several limitations should also be emphasized. First, key behavioural indicators rely on dated nationally representative surveillance, which limits the contemporary validity of some grades. Second, some indicators required indirect or proxy measures, including Family & Peers, School, and Community & Environment. Third, the Active Transportation grade is derived from an unweighted synthesis of heterogeneous subnational studies and should not be interpreted as a population-level estimate. Fourth, the Community & Environment grade is based on an Accra-derived proxy and therefore has limited national generalizability. Finally, for CAWD, several grades remain incomplete and some assignable grades depend partly on qualitative evidence and structured expert judgment, introducing unavoidable subjectivity. These limitations do not negate the value of the report card, but they do require that the 2025 profile be interpreted as an evidence-informed accountability tool rather than a complete national surveillance system.

Conclusions

Ghana’s 2025 Report Card suggests that active school travel remains common in several settings and that sedentary exposure was historically relatively favourable in the last nationally representative benchmark-linked dataset, but overall MVPA compliance is low and delivery systems—particularly schools and community environments—require strengthening. For CAWD, the dominant finding is insufficient benchmark-aligned evidence, coupled with structural inclusion barriers where grading is feasible. Ghana’s next gains will likely come from improved surveillance (including disability disaggregation), protected PE delivery, safer active travel infrastructure, and accessible community spaces.
