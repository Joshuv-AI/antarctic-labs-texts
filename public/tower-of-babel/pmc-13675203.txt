Immune-related gene expression links anxiety, depression, and pain outcomes in adolescence
Authors: Anh Cong Tuan Le, Tobias Banaschewski, Arun L W Bokde, Rüdiger Brühl, Sylvane Desrivières, Hugh Garavan, Penny Gowland, Antoine Grigis, Andreas Heinz, Jean-Luc Martinot, Marie-Laure Paillère Martinot, Eric Artiges, Dimitri Papadopoulos Orfanos, Luise Poustka, Michael N Smolka, Nathalie Holz, Nilakshi Vaidya, Henrik Walter, Robert Whelan, Paul Wirsching, Gunter Schumann, Herta Flor, Frauke Nees, IMAGEN Consortium

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Chronic pain and internalising disorders such as anxiety and depression frequently co-occur during adolescence. Although prospective associations between internalising symptoms and later pain have been reported previously, the biological mechanisms underlying individual differences in this relationship remain poorly understood. Immune- and oxidative stress-related pathways, including the NRF2 gene, may contribute to such variability. This study investigated whether polymorphisms in the NRF2 gene (rs6721961 and rs35652124) are related to the association between anxiety and depression symptoms at age 14-15 and pain complaints two years later at age 16-17.Methods:Data were drawn from the IMAGEN longitudinal study (N = 724). Anxiety and depression symptoms were assessed using the Development and Well-Being Assessment (DAWBA), and pain complaints were measured via the Children’s Somatization Inventory (CSI). Genotyping was conducted for NRF2 SNPs rs6721961 and rs35652124. Regression analyses tested main effects, followed by genotype-stratified analyses.Results:Consistent with previous literature, higher levels of anxiety and depression at age 14-15 predicted increased pain complaints at age 16-17. Carriers of the TG/TT genotype of rs6721961 had significantly lower anxiety scores and reported fewer pain complaints compared to those carrying the GG genotype of rs6721961. Notably, the association between anxiety and later pain was significant only among individuals with the GG genotype.Conclusion:While replicating established associations between adolescent early internalising symptoms and later pain, this study suggests that genetic variation in NRF2 is related to the anxiety-pain association. Specifically, rs6721961 is associated with differences in susceptibility to pain among adolescents with elevated anxiety symptoms.Subject terms: Predictive markers, Genetic predisposition to disease

Introduction

Adolescence is a critical developmental period marked by substantial changes in brain structure and function, particularly in regions involved in affective processing, cognitive control, and pain modulation1,2. During this time, individuals become increasingly vulnerable to both internalising symptoms (e.g., anxiety and depression) and somatic complaints, including recurrent and chronic pain3,4. Epidemiological studies indicate a high prevalence of pain among adolescents, with over 60% reporting headaches annually and approximately one-third affected by musculoskeletal or chronic pain conditions5,6.

Importantly, pain in adolescence is not merely a physical phenomenon - it is closely linked to psychological stress and affective dysregulation7. Co-occurrence of anxiety, depression, and pain is well-documented, and prospective studies suggest that internalising symptoms may increase the risk of later pain complaints. However, not all adolescents with elevated anxiety or depression develop persistent pain, indicating substantial interindividual variability in vulnerability. The biological mechanisms underlying these individual differences remain poorly understood.

One biological pathway increasingly implicated in both affective and pain disorders is the immune system, particularly neuroimmune interactions that shape brain development during adolescence8. For example, elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α have been associated with both anxiety and depression9,10, while interventions targeting inflammatory pathways have shown therapeutic effects in mood disorders11. Similarly, immune dysregulation is reported to contribute to pain chronification via glial activation, cytokine release, and sensitisation of central nociceptive pathways12,13. Collectively, this evidence suggests that immune-related mechanisms may influence susceptibility to both internalising symptoms and pain, as well as the relationship between them.

A candidate molecular regulator in this context is nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) - a transcription factor that orchestrates antioxidant and anti-inflammatory responses14. Activation of NRF2 has been shown to mitigate both pain behaviours and affective symptoms in animal models15,16, while our recent work suggests that NRF2 may contribute to individual susceptibility and resilience to chronic pain and its affective comorbidity17. At the genetic level, promoter-region polymorphisms such as rs6721961 and rs35652124, which are linked to different regulatory motifs, have been associated with altered NRF2 expression and transcriptional activity, potentially influencing inflammatory and oxidative stress responses18–21. These variants, therefore, represent plausible candidates for moderating the link between internalising symptoms and later pain outcomes. Despite accumulating evidence for prospective associations between internalising symptoms and pain, and growing evidence implicating NRF2 in both affective and pain-related processes, it remains unknown whether genetic variation in NRF2 is associated with individual differences in the strength of this association during adolescence.

The present study addresses this gap using data from a large adolescent cohort. We investigated whether two common NRF2 polymorphisms (rs6721961 and rs35652124) are related to the association between anxiety and depression symptoms at age 14–15 and pain complaints two years later at age 16–17. Specifically, we were interested in whether the genotype groups differ in this association. Based on previous literature, we expected higher internalising symptoms to be associated with greater pain complaints at follow-up and hypothesized that NRF2 genetic variation would be related to individual differences in susceptibility to this affect-pain relationship.

Consistent with previous literature, we found that greater levels of anxiety and depression at age 14–15 are associated with increased pain complaints at age 16–17. Individuals carrying the TG/TT genotype of rs6721961 report significantly lower anxiety scores and fewer pain complaints than GG genotype carriers. Notably, in the genotype-stratified analyses, the association between early anxiety and later pain reaches statistical significance among individuals with the GG genotype, but not among those with the TG/TT genotype.

Methods

Participants

We leveraged data from the IMAGEN study (“Reinforcement-related behaviour in normal brain function and psychopathology”; grant LSHM-CT-2007-037286; data collection started in 2008)22,23, in which healthy adolescents were recruited from the general population via school visits, flyers, and registration offices in Germany, the United Kingdom, Ireland, and France. Exclusion criteria for study participation were: contraindications for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations, pregnancy, severe medical conditions, and previous head trauma with unconsciousness. For the present analyses, a subset of participants with complete baseline (BL) (age 14–15) and follow-up 1 (FU1) (age 16–17) data was used (N = 724) (Table 1).

The study was approved by the local ethics committees, including the institutional ethics committee of King’s College London (PNM/10/11-126), University of Nottingham (D/11/2007), Trinity College Dublin (SPREC092007-01), Technische Universitat Dresden (EK 235092007), Commissariat a l’Energie Atomique et aux Energies Alternatives, INSERM (2007-A00778-45), University Medical Center at the University of Hamburg (M-191/07) and in Germany at medical ethics committee of the University of Heidelberg (2007-024N-MA) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. After a complete study description, written informed consent was obtained from both adolescents and their parents.

Assessment of anxiety and depression

The Development and Well-Being Assessment (DAWBA)24 was utilized to assess major depressive disorder (MDD) and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), with definite symptoms obtained by structured questions to the child and parent. The answers to those questions were fed into a computerized diagnostic algorithm, which predicts the likelihood that an experienced clinical rater would assign the child operationalized DSM-IV and ICD-10 diagnoses. The computer prediction provides output as a 6-point scale, ranging from less than 0.1% likely to more than 70% likely. Data on GAD and MDD were used at BL.

Assessment of pain symptoms

The participants completed the Children Somatization Inventory (CSI)25 to assess the severity of somatic symptoms, including pain. The CSI consists of 35 items, including the somatic symptoms defined by DSM-III-R (American Psychiatric Association, 1987), the Hopkins Symptom Checklist (HSCL)26, and additional symptoms common in functional gastrointestinal disorders. The response format is a 5-point scale ranging from “not at all” (0) to “a whole lot” (4), which results in a range of sum scores from 0 to 140, and the standard period for symptom reports is 2 weeks. Items that are related to pain and motor weakness symptoms were selected based on ref. 27. Participants completed the CSI online from home using Psytools software (Delosis Ltd, London, United Kingdom). In this study, we included data on pain symptoms at FU1.

DNA extraction, genotyping, and quality control

Whole blood samples (~10 mL) were collected and stored in BD Vacutainer EDTA tubes (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Oxford, United Kingdom). DNA extraction was performed using Gentra Puregene Blood Kit (QIAGEN Inc., Valencia, CA), while genotyping was done at 582,982 markers within the context of the IMAGEN study22 using the Illumina HumanHap610 Genotyping BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Single nucleotide polymorphisms were excluded if call rates were below 98%, minor allele frequency under 1%, or significant deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (P ≤ 1 × 10−4). Additionally, individuals with excessive missing genotypes (failure rate > 2%), outlying heterozygosity (more than three standard deviations from the mean), or an ambiguous sex code were also excluded. Before the following analysis, closely related individuals with an identity-by-descent greater than 0.1875 were excluded. Population stratification for the GWAS was examined by principal component analysis with EIGENSTRAT software28 and the four HapMap II populations as references. Additionally, individuals with divergent ancestry (from CEU [Utah residents of northern and western European ancestry]) were also excluded29.

For this study, we report data on 589 GG genotype carriers of rs6721961, 130 TG, and 5 TT genotype carriers. Due to a limited number of TT alleles, TG and TT will be merged (N = 135). Meanwhile, the SNP rs35652124 consists of 337 TT, 312 CT, and 75 CC. Please refer to Table 2 for more details on the SNPs. The present hypotheses were not pre-registered. However, the selection of NRF2 and its SNPs was guided by a priori biological considerations and based on the established role of NRF2 in oxidative stress and immune response regulation implicated in both pain and affective symptoms.

Statistics and reproducibility

Association between anxiety/depression and pain complaints during adolescence

For the prediction of pain complaints, ordinary least-squares regression was used, with anxiety/depression measured at BL as a regressor for painful symptoms at FU1. Anxiety and depression scores were treated as continuous variables in the regression models, while sex and centre sites were added as covariates - Eq. (1–2).

The contributions of SNPs rs35652124 and rs6721961 to the associations

The prediction models were tested in the whole group and separately for the allele groups of rs6721961 and rs35652124. Moreover, we analyzed possible differences in pain complaints and early-in-life anxiety/depression symptoms between the allele groups using either analysis of variance (ANOVA) or independent t-tests if the data met parametric assumptions. Otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test or the Mann–Whitney U test was employed.

Statistical significance, multiple comparison correction, and identified outliers

All tests were performed with a significance level of α = 0.05, two-tailed. Data were presented as mean (SD). Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied for multiple comparisons. In this study, significant outliers were defined using Cook’s distance of 4/(n – k – 1), where n is the sample size and k is the number of independent variables. Analyses were performed in Python 3.9.6 and R 4.6.0.

To access the robustness and specificity of the outcome, analyses were also conducted using NRF2 rs2886161 as a negative control, supplemented with permutation testing (Tables S1–S3 and Fig. S1). Compared to the two main SNPs, the negative control is located in a different region (Intron 1) in the NRF2 gene and has few prior studies linking it with pain and comorbidities30.

Discussion

In this longitudinal study, we replicated previous findings showing that symptoms of anxiety and depression in mid-adolescence are associated with greater pain complaints two years later. This observation is consistent with earlier population-based studies and further supports the established link between internalising symptoms and later pain outcomes during adolescence31,32. While this prospective relationship is increasingly well documented, considerably less is known about the biological factors that may explain why some adolescents with elevated internalising symptoms develop pain, whereas others do not. The principal novelty of the present study, to the best of our knowledge, is that anxiety symptoms and later pain association were linked with genetic variation in NRF2, a transcription factor involved in oxidative stress regulation and immune signaling. Specifically, carriers of the rs6721961 GG genotype exhibited higher anxiety levels and more pain complaints, and the association between anxiety and pain was significant in this genotype group. In contrast, no significant association between anxiety and later pain outcomes was found in carriers of the TG/TT genotypes. These findings suggest that NRF2-related pathways may be potentially associated with individual differences in vulnerability to pain among adolescents experiencing internalising symptoms. This appears consistent with preclinical work identifying NRF2 as a key regulator of susceptibility to affective symptoms and pain-related behaviours in animal models16,17,33. Our results also align with evidence indicating that the TG/TT variants of rs6721961 are associated with higher NRF2 expression18, and therefore potentially a greater capacity to regulate neuroinflammatory and oxidative stress responses.

We observed sex differences, with females reporting higher anxiety, depression, and pain - consistent with existing literature34,35. However, sex did not moderate the anxiety-pain association or differ across genotype distributions, suggesting that the observed genetic effects are not confounded by sex.

Together, these findings point to the NRF2 rs6721961 polymorphism as a potential genetic factor that is related to the relationship between early anxiety and later pain. While exploratory, these results contribute to emerging efforts to identify genetically based susceptibility and resilience factors for pain and affective comorbidity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the absence of baseline pain assessment within the IMAGEN dataset at age 14 restricted our ability to determine the extent to which the observed affect-pain association is independent of pre-existing pain symptoms. This should be investigated in future studies. Second, while our sample was large and longitudinal, genotype frequencies limited the statistical power for certain subgroup analyses. Pain and affective symptoms were self-reported, and clinical diagnoses or biomarkers (e.g., cytokine levels) would strengthen the findings. We focused on two NRF2 SNPs; other polymorphisms or epigenetic factors may also play a role. Additionally, limitations inherent to candidate gene studies, such as multiple testing issues and low reproducibility, should not be overlooked. Finally, adjusting the regression models for other pain-sensitive factors, such as childhood adversity and sleep, may further improve the models.

In addition to including baseline pain assessment, future studies should examine the neurobiological correlates of these genetic effects using neuroimaging to assess structural and functional connectivity in key brain regions, including the anterior cingulate cortex, insula, amygdala, and hippocampus. Integration with inflammatory biomarkers would also clarify the immune signaling pathways involved.

In conclusion, the present findings suggest that the NRF2 gene variant rs6721961 is associated with the relationship between early internalising symptoms and later pain during adolescence. Although these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that rs6721961 significantly moderates the association, they highlight the importance of considering genetic heterogeneity when examining complex relationships between psychological and pain-related outcomes and may provide a basis for future research into underlying mechanisms. In addition, while the explained variance is moderate (7.6%), these results align with growing evidence implicating immune and oxidative stress pathways in both emotional and somatic health outcomes. They also support further investigation of NRF2-related genetic variation as a potential contributor to pain vulnerability and as a candidate pathway for understanding early risk trajectories among youth experiencing internalizing symptoms.
