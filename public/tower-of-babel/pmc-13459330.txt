Predictive value of diaphragmatic ultrasound for weaning outcomes in mechanically ventilated patients with acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaoqing Zhou, Yingnan Xu, Qianjun Yan, Ruilin Chen, Xin Lv, Zhen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWeaning failure in mechanically ventilated patients with acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) remains a critical ICU challenge that substantially increases mortality. Although point-of-care diaphragmatic ultrasound is widely proposed to guide weaning decisions, its diagnostic accuracy remains uncertain, and the optimal parameter for this high-risk population has yet to be established.MethodsWe systematically searched eight databases through April 5, 2026, for studies evaluating diaphragmatic ultrasound for predicting weaning outcomes in AECOPD. After methodological quality was appraised with the QUADAS-2 tool. Pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR + and LR−), and diagnostic odds ratios were estimated using bivariate random-effects models, and overall diagnostic performance was summarized by the area under the summary receiver operating characteristic curve. Fagan nomograms were constructed to illustrate changes from pre-test to post-test probability. Threshold effects and heterogeneity were assessed using Spearman correlation and the Q test with the I² statistic, respectively. Exploratory meta-regression, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, and Deeks’ test were used to investigate sources of heterogeneity, assess result stability, and evaluate funnel-plot asymmetry.ResultsTwenty-three studies comprising 2,000 patients (1,310 successes, 690 failures) were included. Of 14 distinct ultrasound parameters identified, four provided sufficient data for quantitative synthesis. Diaphragmatic excursion (DE, n = 16) showed the most robust and balanced performance, with a pooled sensitivity of 0.85, specificity of 0.85, and an AUC of 0.92. Diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF, n = 13) also showed good accuracy (sensitivity 0.84, specificity 0.77, AUC 0.88). The diaphragmatic rapid shallow breathing index (D-RSBI, n = 8) showed high specificity (0.88) but moderate sensitivity (0.74; AUC 0.85), whereas diaphragmatic contraction velocity (DCV, n = 4) showed only moderate accuracy (AUC 0.70). Substantial heterogeneity was observed for DE, DTF, and D-RSBI.ConclusionsDiaphragm ultrasound, particularly DE, may provide useful prognostic information for weaning outcomes in mechanically ventilated patients with AECOPD, while D-RSBI may help identify patients at high risk of weaning failure. However, given the small, predominantly single-centre studies, post hoc study-specific thresholds, substantial heterogeneity, and geographical concentration of the evidence, these findings represent apparent prognostic performance rather than externally validated diagnostic accuracy. Diaphragm ultrasound should therefore be used as an adjunct rather than the sole basis for weaning decisions, pending multicentre external validation and interventional trials demonstrating improved patient-important outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-026-06240-1.Keywords: Diaphragmatic ultrasound, Ventilator weaning, Weaning failure, Acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Intensive care units, Diaphragmatic excursion, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) represents a severe clinical state characterized by the rapid deterioration of airway function and a worsening of respiratory symptoms [1]. As a major driver of critical illness, it is a leading cause of ICU admission and mortality among COPD patients and imposes a substantial economic burden on global healthcare systems [2]. While severe AECOPD episodes frequently necessitate invasive mechanical ventilation for respiratory failure [3], the subsequent ventilator weaning process remains a major clinical challenge. Current evidence indicates that despite advances in respiratory care, the weaning failure rate in AECOPD patients remains high, ranging from 20% to 30% [4]. Weaning failure and subsequent reintubation prolong ICU stay, increase the risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia, exacerbate diaphragmatic dysfunction, and raise mortality [5]. Consequently, early and accurate prediction of weaning outcomes is clinically important for optimizing weaning timing and reducing failure rates.

Distinct from the general ICU population, patients with AECOPD exhibit a particularly pronounced imbalance between an elevated respiratory workload and limited diaphragmatic pump capacity during ventilator weaning. Accumulating clinical evidence highlights diaphragmatic dysfunction as a key mechanical determinant of weaning failure in this population [6]. Importantly, diaphragmatic dysfunction in patients with COPD may already exist before acute exacerbation as a consequence of chronic hyperinflation and disease-related respiratory muscle remodeling. Chronic pulmonary hyperinflation alters diaphragmatic geometry by shortening and flattening the diaphragm, thereby placing it at a mechanical disadvantage and reducing its efficiency in generating inspiratory pressure. During acute exacerbations, severe airflow limitation and dynamic pulmonary hyperinflation further increase the end-expiratory lung volume, generating intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEPi) [7]. Consequently, during the transition to spontaneous breathing, these patients must exert substantial inspiratory effort to overcome this threshold load of PEEPi, resulting in a markedly increased inspiratory work of breathing [8]. Increased recruitment of accessory respiratory muscles may partially compensate for reduced diaphragmatic contribution and help maintain ventilation; however, this compensatory mechanism may also weaken the direct relationship between diaphragm-specific function and overall respiratory performance, potentially influencing the diagnostic interpretation of diaphragm ultrasound parameters. Furthermore, pre-existing diaphragmatic impairment and acute mechanical disadvantage are often compounded by ventilator-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction (VIDD) resulting from disuse atrophy during mechanical ventilation [9]. Diaphragmatic pump reserve refers to the available margin between the diaphragm’s maximal pressure-generating capacity and the inspiratory pressure required to overcome the prevailing respiratory load. Therefore, accurate assessment of diaphragmatic pump reserve before weaning may help determine whether patients with AECOPD have sufficient diaphragmatic capacity to tolerate the transition from mechanical ventilation to spontaneous breathing.

Traditional weaning predictors, such as the rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI) [10], blood gas analysis, oxygenation index, maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) [11], and conventional spontaneous breathing trials (SBT) [12], primarily evaluate global respiratory function. They are unable to directly and dynamically quantify diaphragmatic contractile capacity and reserve, and their suboptimal sensitivity and specificity limit their clinical utility [13]. In contrast, diaphragmatic ultrasound, as a non-invasive, real-time, and reproducible bedside imaging technique, is increasingly being applied to assess respiratory muscle function. Commonly used diaphragmatic ultrasound parameters include diaphragmatic excursion (DE), diaphragmatic contraction velocity (DCV), diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF), and the diaphragmatic rapid shallow breathing index (D-RSBI) [14]. Several studies [15, 16] have suggested that these parameters are valuable predictors for weaning outcomes. However, individual clinical trials often present varying results due to differences in SBT modalities, measurement timing, and cut-off values [17, 18], underscoring the need for a comprehensive pooling of available data to establish a more robust and generalized estimate of their diagnostic accuracy. Moreover, whether diagnostic performance estimates derived from heterogeneous ICU populations are directly applicable to patients with AECOPD remains uncertain.

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have evaluated diaphragmatic ultrasound for predicting weaning outcomes, but they have predominantly included heterogeneous general ICU populations. No previous meta-analysis has specifically synthesized the diagnostic accuracy of diaphragmatic ultrasound in patients with AECOPD undergoing invasive mechanical ventilation. Therefore, to our knowledge, this study is the first systematic review and diagnostic accuracy meta-analysis specifically focused on this population. We aimed to quantify the diagnostic performance of diaphragmatic ultrasound in predicting weaning outcomes, thereby providing disease-specific evidence to inform weaning assessment in patients with AECOPD.

Method

Registration and reporting guidelines

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement [19] and the PRISMA-DTA extension [20]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO before study commencement (Registration No. CRD420261282089).

Literature sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across eight electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, the Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, VIP Chinese Science and Technology Journal Database, and the China Biology Medicine database. The retrieval period extended from the inception of each database to April 5, 2026.

The search strategy was structured around four predefined concepts: COPD/AECOPD, diaphragmatic ultrasonography, mechanical ventilation, and ventilator weaning. Controlled vocabulary terms and free-text terms were combined and adapted for each specific database. In PubMed, the principal Medical Subject Headings included “Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive,” “Diaphragm,” “Ultrasonography,” “Respiration, Artificial,” and “Ventilator Weaning”. Because no single dedicated MeSH term exists for diaphragmatic ultrasonography, diaphragm-related and ultrasound-related terms were combined. The complete search strategy for PubMed is provided in Appendix 1.

To enhance retrieval sensitivity, supplementary search procedures were performed. The reference lists of all included studies and relevant review articles were manually screened to identify additional eligible records. Forward citation searching of the included studies was conducted using Web of Science. In addition, ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform were searched to identify ongoing, completed but unpublished, or otherwise unreported studies relevant to the review question.

Selection criteria

Studies were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) Study design: original clinical studies, including prospective or retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, or diagnostic accuracy studies; (2) Population: adult patients with AECOPD who received invasive mechanical ventilation and underwent weaning assessment; (3) Index test: diaphragmatic ultrasonography utilized to assess diaphragmatic function, including DE, DTF, or other relevant ultrasound parameters; (4) Reference standard: weaning outcomes were clearly reported as either weaning success or weaning failure; (5) Data sufficiency: diagnostic thresholds for diaphragmatic ultrasound parameters were reported with sufficient data to extract or reconstruct a 2 × 2 diagnostic contingency table.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) non AECOPD populations, or mixed populations from which AECOPD data could not be separately extracted; (2) patients did not receive invasive mechanical ventilation or did not undergo weaning/extubation assessment; (3) diaphragmatic ultrasonography was not used, or no clear diagnostic threshold was reported; [4] weaning-related outcomes were not reported; (5) only AUC, between-group differences, or correlation analyses were reported, without sufficient data to construct a 2 × 2 diagnostic contingency table; (6) reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, expert consensus statements, editorials, letters, conference abstracts, case reports, animal or experimental studies, and duplicate publications.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers (Xiaoqing Zhou and Yingnan Xu) using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted information included the first author, publication year, country, study design, study population, sample size, ultrasound parameters, diagnostic cut-off values, timing of ultrasound assessment, reference standard for weaning outcomes, and diagnostic data. When available, true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true negatives were directly extracted; otherwise, they were reconstructed from reported sensitivity, specificity, sample size, and outcome data. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (Zhen Wang).

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (Xiaoqing Zhou and Yingnan Xu) using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool, implemented in Review Manager (RevMan, version 5.4). Risk of bias was evaluated across four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing; applicability concerns were assessed for the first three domains. Each domain was rated as low, high, or unclear risk of bias or applicability concern, as appropriate. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (Zhen Wang).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 19 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). For each ultrasound parameter, pooled sensitivity and specificity with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using a bivariate random-effects model, which inherently accounts for the potential correlation between sensitivity and specificity. Forest plots were generated to display study-specific and pooled estimates of sensitivity and specificity. Based on the extracted 2 × 2 diagnostic contingency tables, positive likelihood ratios (PLRs), negative likelihood ratios (NLRs), and diagnostic odds ratios (DORs) were calculated and pooled. Summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves were constructed, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess overall diagnostic accuracy. According to the predefined criteria, the AUCs of ≥ 0.80, 0.70–0.79, and < 0.70 were interpreted as good, moderate, and poor diagnostic accuracy, respectively. Fagan nomograms were additionally constructed to illustrate the potential clinical utility of each ultrasound parameter by estimating post-test probabilities based on the pooled likelihood ratios.

Potential sources of heterogeneity were explored using meta-regression analyses when sufficient studies and covariate data were available for the corresponding ultrasound parameter. The covariates examined were study design (prospective vs. retrospective), definition of weaning failure, and diagnostic threshold. Each covariate was entered into a separate model, and each analysis included all studies with available data for the relevant ultrasound parameter and covariate. Retrospective studies and studies in which spontaneous breathing trial failure was not incorporated into the definition of weaning failure were used as the reference categories. Diagnostic thresholds were standardized to consistent units and analyzed as continuous covariates. Other potentially relevant clinical and methodological variables, including the specific spontaneous breathing trial protocol, timing of ultrasound assessment, duration of mechanical ventilation, severity of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and ventilator settings, were considered as candidate covariates but were not analyzed because they were incompletely or inconsistently reported, lacked standardized definitions, or were available in too few studies. Given the limited number of studies and incomplete covariate data, the meta-regression analyses were considered exploratory.

Between-study heterogeneity in sensitivity, specificity, and DOR was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. A Q-test P value < 0.10 or an I² value ≥ 50% was considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity. The distribution of point estimates and 95% CIs in the forest plots was also visually inspected to further assess the consistency of results across studies. Threshold effects were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation analysis between sensitivity and the false-positive rate (1 − specificity). A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant threshold effect, suggesting that variations in diagnostic cut-off values may have contributed to between-study heterogeneity.

The robustness of the pooled estimates was assessed using leave-one-out sensitivity analyses. Each study was sequentially omitted, and the pooled estimates were recalculated to determine whether any individual study had a disproportionate influence on the overall results. Potential publication bias was evaluated using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test, with a P value < 0.10 considered indicative of statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry.

Results

Literature search and study selection

The systematic search yielded 484 records from the electronic databases, with no additional records identified from supplementary sources. After the removal of 159 duplicates, 325 unique records were subjected to title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 239 citations. The remaining 86 articles underwent full-text assessment; 63 of these were subsequently excluded for incomplete or non-extractable 2 × 2 table data (n = 32), inappropriate index test (n = 8), inappropriate study design (n = 2), inappropriate study group (n = 6), or inappropriate participants (n = 15). Ultimately, 23 studies fulfilled all eligibility criteria and constituted the final evidence base for both qualitative synthesis and quantitative diagnostic meta-analysis. Methodological quality assessment via the QUADAS-2 tool revealed an overall low-to-high risk of bias and minimal applicability concerns (Fig. 2), with study-specific results (Fig. 3). Overall, the included studies demonstrated good applicability to the target clinical question, with all trials rated as having low concerns regarding applicability across the patient selection, index test, and reference standard domains. In terms of the risk of bias, the primary methodological limitations were concentrated within the patient selection and index test domains. Within the patient selection domain, 13 studies carried an unclear risk of bias, 8 had a low risk, and 2 exhibited a high risk, primarily because these trials failed to explicitly state whether patient enrollment was consecutive or randomized, or because potential selective enrollment could not be definitively ruled out. Within the index test domain, 2 studies were classified as low risk, 1 as unclear risk, and 20 as high risk. This high risk was predominantly driven by the fact that diagnostic thresholds for diaphragmatic ultrasound parameters were determined post hoc using study-specific ROC curves or the Youden index rather than being predefined, potentially introducing threshold selection bias. The reported cutoff ranges and the most frequently used threshold for each parameter are summarized in Table 2, with study-level details provided in Appendix 2. For the reference standard domain, 20 studies showed a low risk of bias, and 3 had an unclear risk. For the flow and timing domain, all 23 studies showed a low risk of bias. While the evidence base demonstrated good clinical applicability, incomplete reporting of consecutive enrollment and the widespread use of data-driven, non-prespecified thresholds constituted the main methodological limitations.

Pooled diagnostic performance

Quantitative synthesis was conducted for four diaphragmatic ultrasound parameters for predicting weaning failure: DE, DTF, DCV, and D-RSBI. The number of included cohorts per parameter ranged from 4 to 16, with cumulative sample sizes ranging from 365 to 1,344 patients. Overall, pooled sensitivity ranged from 0.69 to 0.85, pooled specificity from 0.64 to 0.88, pooled DOR from 3.87 to 32.38, and SROC-derived AUC from 0.70 to 0.92 (Fig. 4).

Diagnostic performance of DE

Sixteen studies evaluated the diagnostic performance of DE for predicting weaning failure. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.85 (95% CI, 0.78–0.90) and 0.85 (95% CI, 0.77–0.91), respectively (Fig. 5A). The pooled positive likelihood ratio (LR+) was 5.73 (95% CI, 3.62–9.07), whereas the pooled negative likelihood ratio (LR−) was 0.18 (95% CI, 0.11–0.27) (Fig. 5B). The pooled DOR was 32.38 (95% CI, 14.87–70.50) (Fig. 5C), and SROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI, 0.89–0.94) (Fig. 5D), indicating high overall discriminatory ability.

At a pre-test probability of 35%, the Fagan nomogram showed that a positive DE result increased the post-test probability of weaning failure to 76%, whereas a negative result reduced it to 9% (Fig. 5E). Nevertheless, substantial heterogeneity was observed for sensitivity, specificity, LR+, and LR− (I² = 75.70%, 83.85%, 72.19%, and 80.32%, respectively). The relatively wide 95% prediction contour also indicated considerable between-study variability. Overall, DE produced the largest and most balanced changes in post-test probability among the evaluated diaphragm ultrasound parameters; however, the pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously because of the substantial heterogeneity.

Diagnostic performance of DTF

Thirteen studies assessed the diagnostic performance of DTF for predicting weaning failure. The pooled sensitivity was 0.84 (95% CI, 0.77–0.90), and the pooled specificity was 0.77 (95% CI, 0.67–0.85) (Fig. 6A). The pooled LR + and LR− were 3.74 (95% CI, 2.51–5.56) and 0.20 (95% CI, 0.14–0.29), respectively (Fig. 6B). The pooled DOR was 18.55 (95% CI, 10.25–33.59) (Fig. 6C), while the SROC analysis demonstrated an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI, 0.85–0.91) (Fig. 6D).

Assuming a pre-test probability of 35%, a positive DTF result increased the post-test probability of weaning failure to 67%, whereas a negative result decreased it to 10% (Fig. 6E). Considerable heterogeneity was present for sensitivity and specificity (I² = 70.81% and 92.88%, respectively), as well as for LR + and LR− (I² = 88.97% and 67.87%, respectively). The broad 95% prediction contour further reflected substantial variability across studies. These findings suggest that DTF provides clinically meaningful changes in the probability of weaning failure, particularly after a negative result, although its performance remains insufficiently consistent to support its use as a standalone diagnostic test.

Diagnostic performance of DCV

Four studies evaluated the diagnostic performance of DCV for predicting weaning failure. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.69 (95% CI, 0.57–0.79) and 0.64 (95% CI, 0.55–0.71), respectively (Fig. 7A). The pooled LR + was 1.89 (95% CI, 1.51–2.38), and the pooled LR − was 0.49 (95% CI, 0.35–0.69) (Fig. 7B). The pooled DOR was 3.87 (95% CI, 2.29–6.55) (Fig. 7C), and the SROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.70 (95% CI, 0.66–0.74) (Fig. 7D), indicating limited-to-moderate discriminatory performance.

At a pre-test probability of 35%, a positive DCV result increased the post-test probability of weaning failure to 50%, whereas a negative result reduced it to 21% (Fig. 7E). Moderate heterogeneity was observed for sensitivity (I² = 49.91%), whereas no statistical heterogeneity was detected for specificity, LR+, or LR− (all I² = 0%). However, these heterogeneity estimates remain imprecise because only four studies were available. The relatively small changes in post-test probability suggest that DCV has limited clinical value when used alone, and further validation in larger prospective cohorts is required.

Eight studies investigated the diagnostic performance of D-RSBI for predicting weaning failure. The pooled sensitivity was 0.74 (95% CI, 0.61–0.83), and the pooled specificity was 0.88 (95% CI, 0.73–0.95) (Fig. 8A). The pooled LR + was 6.26 (95% CI, 2.78–14.11), whereas the pooled LR − was 0.30 (95% CI, 0.21–0.43) (Fig. 8B). The pooled DOR was 20.92 (95% CI, 9.36–46.79) (Fig. 8C), and SROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.85 (95% CI, 0.82–0.88) (Fig. 8D).

At a pre-test probability of 35%, a positive D-RSBI result increased the post-test probability of weaning failure to 77%, whereas a negative result reduced it to 14% (Fig. 8E). Substantial heterogeneity was observed for sensitivity and specificity (I² = 81.14% and 91.30%, respectively), as well as for LR + and LR− (I² = 76.09% and 83.96%, respectively). The wide 95% prediction contour also demonstrated marked between-study variability. Thus, D-RSBI appears more useful for increasing the probability of weaning failure following a positive result than for excluding failure following a negative result. However, its clinical application is limited by substantial heterogeneity and the need for standardized measurement and calculation methods.

Heterogeneity and sensitivity analyses

Substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed across the parameter-specific analyses, particularly for DE, DTF, and D-RSBI, as reflected by the wide prediction contours in the SROC plots. To evaluate whether this cross-study variability arose from a threshold effect, Spearman correlation analysis was performed between the logit of sensitivity and the logit of the false-positive rate (1 − specificity). No significant threshold effect was detected for DE, DTF, or D-RSBI (Spearman’s ρ = −0.235, 0.121, and 0.190; P = 0.381, 0.694, and 0.651, respectively). For DCV, although the correlation coefficient was high (ρ = 0.800), it did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.200). However, because Spearman correlation analysis has limited power when only a small number of studies are available, particularly for DCV, which was informed by only four cohorts, these findings do not exclude threshold-related heterogeneity. Variation in reported cut-off values and the widespread use of post hoc, study-specific threshold selection therefore remained plausible contributors to the observed heterogeneity.

To further explore potential sources of between-study heterogeneity, univariable meta-regression analyses were performed for DE, DTF, and D-RSBI. Study design, definition of weaning failure, and the reported diagnostic cutoff were evaluated separately as study-level covariates. The reported cutoff was treated as a continuous variable in an exploratory analysis of its linear association with the log diagnostic odds ratio; this analysis was distinct from the threshold-effect analysis, which assessed the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. For DE, study design was significantly associated with diagnostic performance, with prospective studies showing a higher DOR than retrospective studies (β = 1.388; R-DOR = 4.01, 95% CI 1.55–10.36; P = 0.004). Neither the definition of weaning failure nor the reported diagnostic cutoff was significantly associated with the diagnostic performance of DE. For DTF and D-RSBI, no statistically significant associations were identified for any of the examined covariates. The absence of a statistically significant association for the reported cutoff should not be interpreted as excluding threshold-related heterogeneity, because these exploratory analyses involved relatively few studies, used aggregate study-level data, and may have been affected by confounding between threshold selection and other study characteristics. Overall, among the covariates examined, study design was the only factor significantly associated with diagnostic performance, and this association was observed only for DE. Meta-regression was not performed for DCV because only four cohorts were available. Other potentially relevant variables, including the specific SBT protocol, timing of ultrasound assessment, duration of mechanical ventilation, COPD severity, and ventilator settings, could not be reliably evaluated because they were insufficiently or inconsistently reported across the included studies. Detailed meta-regression results are provided in Appendix 3.

Given the clinical and methodological diversity across studies—and the limited number of cohorts for certain parameters, which precluded meta-regression or subgroup analysis—leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the stability of the pooled estimates. The recalculated pooled DOR for DE, DTF, DCV, and D-RSBI remained stable across iterations (Fig. 9). For DE, DTF, and D-RSBI, the point estimates clustered closely around the original pooled values, confirming that the overall findings were not driven by any single study. For DCV, greater fluctuation was observed as expected due to the smaller number of included studies; however, no single exclusion altered the overall interpretation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that, despite statistical heterogeneity—which likely reflects the unstandardized clinical practice of bedside diaphragmatic ultrasonography—the pooled diagnostic accuracy estimates are stable and robust.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test (Fig. 10). The P values were 0.94 for DE, 1.00 for DTF, 0.82 for DCV, and 0.60 for D-RSBI, and none of the tests reached statistical significance. However, Deeks’ test has limited statistical power when fewer than 10 studies are included. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with particular caution for DCV (n = 4) and D-RSBI (n = 8), and the absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of the absence of publication bias. The possibility of publication bias or small-study effects cannot be excluded.

Discussion

Weaning from mechanical ventilation is a critical phase of intensive care, and both premature and delayed extubation are associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Diaphragm dysfunction is a major and frequently underrecognized contributor to weaning failure, particularly in patients with AECOPD, in whom diaphragm ultrasound has emerged as a promising noninvasive tool for bedside assessment. However, the diagnostic performance of individual ultrasound parameters has been inconsistent across studies. This systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 studies involving 2,000 patients synthesizes evidence across multiple diaphragmatic suggests that the prognostic performance of diaphragm ultrasound varies across parameters, which may inform parameter selection for prognostic assessment in this population.

The relative performance of these parameters is physiologically plausible in patients with AECOPD. Dynamic hyperinflation and intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure increase the inspiratory threshold load and place the diaphragm in a shortened and mechanically disadvantaged position [44]. DE reflects displacement of the diaphragmatic dome and integrates diaphragmatic contraction, respiratory loading, and pulmonary hyperinflation, although it may also be influenced by ventilator mode, pressure-support level, and PEEP settings [45–47]. These characteristics may partly explain its relatively high and balanced diagnostic performance. DTF more directly reflects active diaphragmatic thickening and contractile reserve, but its precision may be limited because small absolute measurement errors are amplified when expressed as a percentage, particularly given the small thickness of the diaphragm [48, 49]. DCV incorporates a temporal component but may reflect both contraction and respiratory drive; therefore, low values may indicate weak contraction, whereas high values may reflect increased neural drive rather than adequate contractile reserve [50]. This ambiguity, together with the limited number of available studies, may partly explain its comparatively weak performance.

D-RSBI may have particular physiological relevance in AECOPD because accessory inspiratory muscle recruitment can temporarily preserve tidal volume despite reduced diaphragmatic contribution. Consequently, the conventional RSBI may appear reassuring even when breathing is energetically inefficient and unlikely to be sustained [51–53]. By replacing tidal volume with DE, D-RSBI may identify patients who breathe rapidly despite restricted diaphragmatic excursion. Its relatively high specificity suggests that an elevated D-RSBI may be more useful for identifying patients at increased risk of diaphragm-related weaning failure than for broadly excluding such risk. These considerations support disease-specific assessment but do not establish that diaphragm ultrasound performs better in AECOPD than in other critically ill populations.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous meta-analyses suggesting that diaphragm ultrasound may predict weaning outcomes with acceptable accuracy [17, 54]. Song et al. reported a higher AUC for D-RSBI than for conventional RSBI during pressure-support SBTs [55], and a recent meta-analysis of D-RSBI reported a pooled sensitivity of 0.88, specificity of 0.82, and AUC of 0.93 [56]. However, Fossat et al. found that DE, DTF, respiratory rate, and accessory muscle activity discriminated 7-day extubation success better than RSBI and D-RSBI, neither of which was predictive in their cohort [57]. Previous reviews predominantly pooled general or mixed ICU populations with heterogeneous causes of respiratory failure. The present review extends this evidence by restricting the analysis to patients with AECOPD and comparatively evaluating DE, DTF, DCV, and D-RSBI within a common disease-specific framework. This distinction is clinically relevant because the disease-specific respiratory mechanics of AECOPD may modify the relationship between diaphragmatic activity and weaning outcomes. Consequently, estimates and proposed thresholds derived from mixed ICU populations may not be directly transferable to patients with AECOPD. Our analysis therefore provides population-specific estimates while showing that performance varies according to the ultrasound parameter selected.

Nevertheless, the high pooled accuracy observed for DE should be reconciled with conflicting evidence from methodologically rigorous studies. Vivier et al., in a prospective multicentre study using a prespecified methodology, found that diaphragm ultrasound did not predict weaning failure [59], in contrast to the pooled AUC of 0.92 observed for DE in the present analysis. An important explanation for this discrepancy may be the predominance of data-driven threshold selection among the included studies. Twenty of the 23 studies derived their diagnostic cut-off values post hoc from study-specific ROC curves or the Youden index, whereas only three evaluated prespecified thresholds. Selecting an optimal threshold and estimating its performance in the same dataset may inflate sensitivity, specificity, and AUC, particularly in small, single-centre derivation cohorts. In contrast, a multicentre study using prespecified definitions provides a more stringent assessment of reproducibility and transportability. Differences in populations and outcome definitions may also have contributed to the discrepant findings. Vivier et al. evaluated a mixed ICU population at high risk of reintubation after successful completion of an SBT, whereas the present review was restricted to patients with AECOPD and included studies assessing broader and variably defined weaning outcomes. COPD-specific physiological characteristics may modify the association between ultrasound measurements and weaning outcomes; however, this remains a plausible explanation rather than evidence of superior performance in AECOPD. We examined the feasibility of a sensitivity analysis restricted to studies using prespecified thresholds, but the three eligible studies did not provide a sufficient number of studies evaluating the same ultrasound parameter to support a reliable parameter-specific bivariate or hierarchical summary ROC model. Whether the pooled performance would be maintained after excluding studies with post hoc threshold selection therefore remains uncertain. The negative findings of Vivier et al. reinforce the possibility that the present pooled estimates overstate the accuracy achievable when fixed thresholds are prospectively applied in independent populations. Accordingly, the summary estimates should be regarded as apparent performance derived predominantly from threshold-generating studies rather than as externally validated diagnostic accuracy.

Relatively high I² values and wide prediction contours in the SROC plots were observed, most notably for DE, DTF, and D-RSBI, indicating substantial between-study heterogeneity and uncertainty regarding the performance that might be expected in a new clinical setting. Although Spearman correlation analysis did not identify a statistically significant across-study threshold correlation, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the test may be underpowered when only a small number of cohorts are available, particularly for DCV, which was evaluated in only four cohorts. A nonsignificant Spearman correlation therefore does not exclude threshold-related heterogeneity. Importantly, the use of non-prespecified, data-driven cut-offs represents threshold-related variability at the individual-study level, because the operating threshold was selected from the same data used to estimate diagnostic performance. Such within-study threshold effects may not be adequately captured by either an across-study Spearman correlation or aggregate-level meta-regression. In exploratory univariable meta-regression, study design was the only examined covariate significantly associated with diagnostic performance, with prospective studies showing higher performance for DE. No significant associations were identified for the definition of weaning failure or the reported cut-off, or for the examined covariates in the DTF and D-RSBI analyses. However, these analyses involved relatively few studies, were based on aggregate study-level data, and could not adequately account for confounding between threshold selection and other study characteristics. Threshold-related differences therefore remain a plausible contributor to heterogeneity, although their relative contribution could not be quantified.

Additional clinical and methodological differences may also have contributed to heterogeneity. SBT protocols differed with respect to the use of pressure support, continuous positive airway pressure, or T-piece breathing, and residual ventilatory assistance may influence DE, DCV, and D-RSBI measurements, particularly in patients with AECOPD, in whom altered respiratory loading may affect diaphragmatic motion [58, 59]. Ultrasound timing also varied in relation to the initiation or completion of the SBT and the decision to extubate. Other potential contributors included the duration of mechanical ventilation, ventilator settings, COPD severity and phenotype, causes of acute exacerbation, respiratory muscle weakness, operator experience, probe positioning, and measurement technique [47]. Definitions of weaning failure were inconsistent and included SBT failure, reintubation, initiation of non-invasive ventilation or high-flow nasal cannula, tracheostomy, or death within different follow-up periods [17, 60, 61]. Several of these clinically relevant factors could not be reliably examined in subgroup or meta-regression analyses because they were insufficiently or inconsistently reported. Consequently, although study design may explain part of the heterogeneity in the DE analysis, a substantial proportion of the observed heterogeneity remained unexplained.

The QUADAS-2 assessment was consistent with these methodological concerns, with the principal risks of bias concentrated in the patient-selection and index-test domains. Enrollment was frequently not reported as consecutive or random, and 20 of the 23 studies were judged to be at high risk of index-test bias because diagnostic thresholds were selected post hoc. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses showed that the pooled estimates were not driven by any single study; however, this finding only indicates that the results were not dependent on one influential dataset and cannot address systematic biases shared by most included studies, particularly post hoc threshold selection. Similarly, although Deeks’ tests did not identify statistically significant publication bias, their power was limited by the small number of studies available for several parameters. The apparent stability of the pooled estimates should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that they are unaffected by threshold-selection bias, publication bias, or other common methodological limitations. Accordingly, confidence in the magnitude and generalizability of the pooled estimates remains limited, and the findings for DCV and D-RSBI, which were supported by relatively few cohorts, require particularly cautious interpretation [62].

The geographical concentration of the available evidence represents an important limitation. Of the 23 included studies, 21 were conducted in China and the remaining two in Egypt; consequently, the pooled estimates primarily reflect patient populations, clinical practices, and healthcare systems from a limited number of geographical settings. Differences across regions and institutions in operator training and experience, ultrasound acquisition and measurement protocols, timing of assessment, sedation practices, spontaneous breathing trial modalities, ventilator-weaning procedures, and extubation-readiness criteria may influence diaphragmatic measurements and their optimal diagnostic thresholds. The use of prophylactic non-invasive ventilation or high-flow nasal oxygen after extubation, as well as institutional thresholds for reintubation and definitions of weaning failure, may also affect observed diagnostic performance. In addition, differences in COPD severity, comorbidity patterns, referral pathways, ICU admission practices, and baseline risk of weaning failure may alter the predictive values of these parameters. The predominance of small, single-centre studies, together with potential geographical differences in publication practices and selective reporting of positive findings, may further limit external validity. Therefore, although the present pooled estimates summarize the best available evidence in patients with AECOPD, they should not be considered universally applicable benchmarks and require external validation in large, prospective, multicentre cohorts across geographically and clinically diverse healthcare settings.

From a prognostic perspective, a high D-RSBI may indicate an unfavorable load-capacity balance [63], while after a successful SBT, abnormal diaphragm ultrasound indices can help identify patients who remain at high risk of weaning failure. Whether patients identified as high risk by diaphragm ultrasound would benefit from altered monitoring, post-extubation respiratory support, airway management, or modification of the weaning plan remains uncertain and requires prospective interventional evaluation [64, 65]. Current evidence therefore supports diaphragm ultrasound primarily as a prognostic and risk-stratification tool but does not establish that modifying weaning management on the basis of ultrasound findings improves patient outcomes, because this question has not been adequately evaluated in prospective interventional trials. Accordingly, diaphragm ultrasound should not currently be used as the sole basis for weaning decisions. Weaning failure is multifactorial, and diaphragmatic dysfunction is only one contributing component. Cough capacity, airway secretion burden, neurological status, lung aeration, cardiac function, and fluid balance all remain important determinants of weaning outcomes [66]. Diaphragm ultrasound may therefore be considered as one component of a broader multimodal assessment incorporating SBT performance, lung ultrasound, cardiac ultrasound, and airway assessment, to provide more comprehensive risk stratification. However, the clinical benefit of management strategies guided by such integrated assessments remains to be established [67].

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and diagnostic accuracy meta-analysis specifically evaluating diaphragm ultrasound for predicting weaning outcomes in patients with AECOPD undergoing invasive mechanical ventilation and comparatively synthesizing the performance of DE, DTF, DCV, and D-RSBI within a unified disease-specific framework. Nevertheless, several limitations constrain the interpretation of the findings. The evidence base consisted predominantly of small, single-centre observational studies, most of which selected diagnostic thresholds post hoc, and was geographically concentrated in China. These characteristics increase the risk of optimistic accuracy estimates and limit the transportability of the proposed cut-off values. The small number of cohorts evaluating DCV and D-RSBI also reduced the precision and reliability of their pooled estimates. In addition, the absence of individual-patient data precluded patient-level adjustment and detailed assessment of potential effect modifiers, including COPD severity and phenotype, duration of mechanical ventilation, ventilator settings, cause of exacerbation, timing of ultrasound assessment, and post-extubation respiratory support. Several study-level variables were also insufficiently or inconsistently reported, leaving a substantial proportion of the observed heterogeneity unexplained. A sensitivity analysis restricted to studies using prespecified thresholds could not be performed reliably because too few studies evaluated the same ultrasound parameter; therefore, the extent to which post hoc threshold selection inflated the pooled estimates remains uncertain. The available evidence does not support a universal clinical threshold for any parameter, and the reported cut-off ranges should be regarded as provisional rather than definitive decision limits. Future studies should use prospective multicentre and multinational designs, standardized ultrasound acquisition and reporting protocols, blinded interpretation, prospectively prespecified thresholds, and consistent definitions of weaning failure, as emphasized in the EXODUS consensus statement [68]. Threshold derivation and external validation should be conducted in separate populations. Future investigations should also assess whether diaphragm ultrasound provides incremental prognostic value beyond established clinical variables and whether ultrasound-guided multimodal weaning strategies improve patient-important outcomes.

Conclusion

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that diaphragmatic ultrasound provides potentially useful prognostic information regarding weaning outcomes in mechanically ventilated patients with AECOPD. Among the evaluated parameters, DE demonstrated the highest pooled diagnostic accuracy in the currently available literature, whereas D-RSBI may help identify patients at high risk of weaning failure because of its relatively high specificity. However, these estimates were derived predominantly from small, single-centre studies using post hoc, study-specific thresholds and should be interpreted as apparent prognostic performance rather than externally validated diagnostic accuracy. Substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, the geographical concentration of the evidence, and the limited number of studies for some parameters further restrict the generalizability and routine clinical application of the findings. Current evidence does not support a universal cut-off value or the use of diaphragm ultrasound as the sole basis for weaning decisions. It should instead be considered an adjunct to comprehensive clinical assessment. Prospective multicentre and multinational studies using prespecified thresholds and independent external validation are required, together with interventional trials evaluating whether ultrasound-guided management improves patient-important outcomes.
