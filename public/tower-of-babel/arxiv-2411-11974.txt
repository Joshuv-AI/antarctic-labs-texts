A Novel Bispectrum Estimator of the Kinematic Sunyaev-Zel’dovich Effect using Projected Fields
Raagini Patki, Nicholas Battaglia, J. Colin Hill

Abstract
With the advent of current and future high-resolution CMB experiments, the kinematic Sunyaev-Zel’dovich (kSZ) effect has become a unique observational probe of the distribution of baryons and velocities in the Universe. In this work, we propose a novel binned bispectrum of the form temperature-temperature-density to extract the late-time kSZ effect from cleaned CMB maps. Unlike ‘kSZ tomography’ methods, this estimator can use any tracer of the large-scale structure density field projected along the line-of-sight and does not require individual redshifts. With our method, we forecast signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) of ∼ \sim 100-200 for the upcoming Simons Observatory (SO) and CMB-S4 correlated with a galaxy sample from WISE that is restricted to the linear regime. We also extend galaxy modes into the non-linear regime and explore this harmonic space to show that the SNR peaks for squeezed triangles that have a short (linear) density mode and long temperature modes in harmonic space. The existing kSZ 2 − {}^{2}- density projected-fields estimator compresses the rich information contained in this bispectrum across various scales and triangle shapes. Moreover, we find that the lensing correction to this estimator’s signal is relatively small for high-SNR squeezed triangle configurations. We study the dependence of this kSZ signal on Λ \Lambda CDM parameters for SO and CMB-S4 and forecast initial constraints on the sum of neutrino masses while restricting to the linear galaxy bias regime. Our work illustrates the potential of the projected-fields kSZ bispectrum as a novel probe of baryonic abundance and beyond- Λ \Lambda CDM cosmology with upcoming precision measurements.

II
Theoretical formalism

We begin with an overview of the kSZ effect and the existing kSZ
{}^{2}\times\delta_{g}
estimator that has been used to detect it using projected-fields of matter overdensity. We then define our novel projected-fields kSZ bispectrum and detail its implementation with a binned bispectrum approach.

The kSZ effect gives rise to a secondary shift in the CMB temperature anisotropies
\Theta^{\mathrm{kSZ}}\left(\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right)\equiv\Delta T^{\mathrm{kSZ}}/T_{\mathrm{CMB}}\left(\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right)

\Theta^{\mathrm{kSZ}}\left(\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right)=-\int d\eta\hskip 1.42271ptg(\eta)\hskip 2.84544pt\mathbf{p_{e}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}},

(1)

g(\eta)=e^{-\tau}d\tau/d\eta
is the visibility function, and

\left(d\tau/d\eta\right)=\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}\,n_{e}/(1+z)

is the Thomson scattering cross-section and

is the free electron number density, so that

\Theta^{\mathrm{kSZ}}\left(\mathbf{\hat{n}}\right)=-\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}\int\frac{d\eta}{1+z}e^{-\tau}n_{e}\left(\mathbf{\hat{n}},\eta\right)\mathbf{v_{e}}\left(\mathbf{\hat{n}},\eta\right)\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}.

(2)

In this work, we consider galaxies as a tracer of matter in the LSS. However, the same formalism can be similarly applied to any other tracers of the underlying matter density field, such as quasars, 21-cm fluctuations (e.g.
[

38

\delta_{g}(\mathbf{\hat{n}})
is the galaxy overdensity field

along the line-of-sight (LOS), and is given by:

\delta_{g}(\mathbf{\hat{n}})=\int_{0}^{\eta_{\max}}d\eta\hskip 1.9919ptW^{g}(\eta)\hskip 1.9919pt\delta_{m}(\eta\mathbf{\hat{n}},\eta),

(3)

where

is the matter (fractional) overdensity field in 3D.
\eta_{\max}
is the maximum comoving distance of the galaxy sample, and its projection kernel is
W^{g}(\eta)=b_{g}p_{s}(\eta)

Since the kSZ effect has the same (blackbody) frequency dependence as the primary CMB, it cannot be isolated using component-separation methods alone. Thus, we follow previous kSZ works utilizing projected-fields

14
]
and avoid additional contributions from the integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) effect by not considering the large scales where it is found to be significant (
\ell<100

In practice, telescopes observe the CMB through a finite beam

modeled by a Gaussian as:

II.1
The Existing Projected-Fields kSZ Power Spectrum
\langle\mathrm{kSZ}^{2}\times\delta_{g}\rangle

Free electrons in the LSS are equally likely to be moving towards or away from the observer along the LOS. Given this
\mathbf{v_{e}}\rightarrow-\mathbf{v_{e}}
parity, under statistical isotropy, the cross-power spectrum
\langle\Theta^{\mathrm{kSZ}}\times\delta_{g}\rangle
between a cleaned CMB map and a projected LSS field is statistically zero at our scales of interest (
\ell>100
), due to cancellations among equally likely positive and negative contributions to the kSZ effect when summed along the LOS (although the linear-ISW effect can have detectable contributions at
\ell<100

[
7
]
). To avoid this naive cancellation, the so-called ‘projected-fields’ or ‘kSZ
{}^{2}\times\delta_{g}
’ estimator
[
33
,
34
]

squares
a cleaned CMB temperature map before cross-correlating with
\delta_{g}
. This
real
space squaring operation necessitates applying a Wiener filter to the cleaned CMB map to preferentially select angular scales dominated by the kSZ contribution, given by:

F(\ell)=\left(C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{kSZ}}/C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{tot}}\right)
,
where
C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{kSZ}}
is the template (theoretical) kSZ power spectrum. The overall filtered CMB map is then given in harmonic space by:

\Theta_{f}(\mathbf{\ell})=F(\ell)b(\ell)\Theta(\mathbf{\ell})\equiv f(\ell)\Theta(\mathbf{\ell}),

(5)

where
f(\ell)\equiv F(\ell)b(\ell)
. Thus, the existing ‘projected-fields’ estimator is a power spectrum defined as:

\langle\Theta_{f}^{2}(\mathbf{\ell})\,\delta_{g}(\mathbf{\ell^{^{\prime}}})\rangle=(2\pi)^{2}\delta_{D}(\mathbf{\ell}+\mathbf{\ell^{^{\prime}}})C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{kSZ}^{2}\times\delta_{g}}.

(6)

Using the Limber approximation
[
46
,
47
]
, this
\mathrm{kSZ}^{2}
-galaxy cross-power spectrum of projected fields is written as
[
33

34
,
43

7

44
,
11

14

C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{kSZ}^{2}\times\delta_{g}}=\int_{0}^{\eta_{\max}}\frac{d\eta}{\eta^{2}}W^{g}(\eta)g^{2}(\eta)\mathcal{T}\left(j=\frac{\ell}{\eta},\eta\right),

(7)

where the ‘triangle power spectrum’
\mathcal{T}
is

\mathcal{T}(j,\eta)=\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{2}}f(q\eta)f(|\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{q}|\eta)\,B_{p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}\delta}(\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{j}-\mathbf{q},\mathbf{j}).

(8)

The key theoretical statistic here is
B_{p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}\delta}
-
the 3-point correlation function (or ‘bispectrum’ in Fourier space)
between two electron momenta projected along the LOS (
p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}=\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}
) and one matter overdensity field. Thus,
\mathcal{T}(j,\eta)

compresses
the rich information contained in
B_{p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}\delta}
by summing over all triangles in a constant-redshift plane (at a distance

pointed out in
[

II.2
The Projected-Fields kSZ Bispectrum
\langle\Theta^{\mathrm{kSZ}}\times\Theta^{\mathrm{kSZ}}\times\delta_{g}\rangle

We now propose a novel bispectrum estimator for the kSZ that again utilizes projected-fields of LSS tracers of matter, but circumvents the drawbacks of the existing power spectrum estimator that we have described above. It thus retains the advantage of not requiring accurate inidividual redshifts of the LSS tracers, making it applicable to photometric galaxy surveys (with significant photo-z errors), as well as
galaxy/CMB weak lensing convergence, 21-cm fluctuations, etc.

Here, instead of squaring the (filtered) CMB map as done earlier

\langle\Theta_{b}(\mathbf{\ell_{1}})\,\Theta_{b}(\mathbf{\ell_{2}})\,\delta_{g}(\mathbf{\ell_{3}})\rangle=(2\pi)^{2}\delta_{D}(\mathbf{\ell_{1}}+\mathbf{\ell_{2}}+\mathbf{\ell_{3}})B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}},

(9)

where
\Theta_{b}\equiv b(\ell)\Theta(\mathbf{\ell})
and
\delta_{g}
are the same as before. Taking a cross-correlation of cleaned CMB maps in Fourier space allows us to avoid the real space squaring operation that leads to a convolution over the filter in the existing

estimator. This also means that the full bispectrum approach does not require Wiener filtering of the CMB maps, because the LSS is only correlated with the kSZ secondary anisotropies, and not with the primary CMB (in the regime where ISW is negligible). Furthermore, the rich information contained in the
B_{p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}\delta}

magnitude
of the lensing correction is significant only for triangles having
1500\lesssim\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\lesssim 4000
, since lensing dominates in CMB maps at these angular scales. While
|\Delta B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}|
itself peaks for some squeezed triangles with a short
\delta_{g}
mode (see Fig.
5
), it is
relatively
small when compared to the corresponding
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
signal in most of these high-SNR bins, as we discuss in Section
IV
. Moreover, extending the range of
\delta_{g}
up to 4200 in Fig.
3
, we show that the projected-fields kSZ bispectrum and its lensing contribution dominate for different triangle shapes.
On the other hand, the existing
C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{kSZ}^{2}\times\delta_{g}}
estimator compresses not only the full
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
signal considered here, but also its lensing correction across all triangle shapes.

II.2.3
Analytical Covariance Matrix

As with several bispectra studies done previously (e.g.
[
49
,
50
,
59
]
),
we proceed to use a simplified analytical expression for the covariance matrix of the estimator for forecasting, since this does not require accurate simulated maps for its estimation.
From Eq.(
32
), the covariance for a single triplet is given by:

\displaystyle\langle B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}\ell_{2}^{\prime}\ell_{3}^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\Delta}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}N_{\Delta}^{\ell_{1}^{\prime}\ell_{2}^{\prime}\ell_{3}^{\prime}}}}\sum_{m}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}^{\prime}&\ell_{2}^{\prime}&\ell_{3}^{\prime}\\
m_{1}^{\prime}&m_{2}^{\prime}&m_{3}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\langle a^{\Theta_{b}}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a^{\Theta_{b}}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a^{\delta_{g}}_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}a^{\Theta_{b}*}_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}m_{1}^{\prime}}a^{\Theta_{b}*}_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}m_{2}^{\prime}}a^{\delta_{g}*}_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}m_{3}^{\prime}}\rangle.

(16)

In the limit of weak non-Gaussianity and using Wick’s theorem, the 6-point function of spherical harmonic coefficients above has a decomposition that is dominated by products of three power spectra. From Eq.(
12
), it follows that in the weak non-Gaussianity limit, the covariance matrix is diagonal, so that the variance of the binned
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
bispectrum is:

\displaystyle V_{abc}^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1}\in\Delta_{a}\\
\ell_{2}\in\Delta_{b}\\
\ell_{3}\in\Delta_{c}\end{subarray}}\left(N_{\Delta}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}g_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}C_{\ell_{1}}^{\bar{T}\bar{T},b}C_{\ell_{2}}^{\bar{T}\bar{T},b}C_{\ell_{3}}^{\delta_{g}\delta_{g}}\right)}{N_{abc}^{2}\,f_{\mathrm{sky}}},

(17)

where we have accounted for the effect of the overlapping sky fraction
f_{\mathrm{sky}}
of the considered surveys. The power spectrum of the observed, cleaned CMB map is computed as (see Section
III.1
for details)
C_{\ell}^{\bar{T}\bar{T},b}\equiv b^{2}(\ell)C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{tot}}=b^{2}(\ell)\left(C_{\ell}^{TT}+C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{kSZ}}+N_{\ell}\right)
, and the projected galaxy density power spectrum is given by
[
47
,
60
]
:

C_{\ell}^{\delta_{g}\delta_{g}}=\int_{0}^{\eta_{\max}}\frac{d\eta}{\eta^{2}}\,[W^{g}(\eta)]^{2}P_{\delta\delta}\left(k=\frac{\ell}{\eta},\eta\right)+\frac{1}{\bar{n}},

(18)

where
\bar{n}
is the projected number of galaxies per steradian, appearing in the shot noise term
(1/\bar{n})
. The symmetry factor
g_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
appearing in Eq.(
17
) is 2 if the two kSZ multipoles are equal in magnitude (i.e. if
\ell_{1}=\ell_{2}
), and 1 otherwise.

We now consider the effect of CMB lensing on the estimated covariance matrix of the
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
bispectrum. One part of this effect is already accounted for above, given that
C_{\ell}^{TT}
is the lensed primary CMB power spectrum. Additionally, as pointed out recently
[
59
]
(in the context of primordial non-Gaussianity), the covariance could potentially receive a non-Gaussian lensing contribution of the form
\langle a^{\Theta_{b}}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a^{\Theta_{b}}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a^{\Theta_{b}*}_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}m_{1}^{\prime}}a^{\Theta_{b}*}_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}m_{2}^{\prime}}\rangle\langle a^{\delta_{g}}_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}a^{\delta_{g}*}_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}m_{3}^{\prime}}\rangle
. This contribution is sourced by the
connected
trispectrum of CMB temperature, which becomes non-zero in the presence of lensing.

We do a calculation analogous to
[
59
]
to derive this non-Gaussian contribution to the variance of
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
in Appendix
C
(see Eq.(
38
)).
However, we expect this contribution to be relatively small for our analysis with the
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
bispectum, because (i) Its overall amplitude due to the multiplicity of possible permutations of
a^{\Theta}
s is lowered by a factor of 9: the average multiplicity is just 4 in our case (as compared to 36 in the context of primordial non-Gaussianity with the primary CMB
\langle TTT\rangle
bispectrum in
[
59
]
), and (ii) for squeezed triangles in the ‘default’ regime assumed in this work (see Sec.
III.2
), we find that the lensing contribution to the
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}

signal
itself is relatively small (see Fig.
2
); so, we intuitively expect its variance to receive a similarly small non-Gaussian contribution due to lensing (while also noting the similarity between the expressions in Eqs (
38
) and (
14
)). Although we have provided its theoretical expression in Appendix
C
for completeness (Eq.(
38
)), we drop this term from our forecasts since it is expected to be small in the regime considered and computationally expensive to evaluate. Future work with the
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
estimator could potentially include a more accurate estimate of its covariance matrix, including the non-Gaussian contribution due to lensing, by applying our binned bispectrum approach to simulated maps (see e.g.
[
50
]
) to quantitatively validate the impact of lensing described in this section.

III.2
Analysis Choices

We make a few key analysis choices while implementing the projected-fields kSZ bispectrum as introduced in Section
II.2
. To begin with, we use broad redshift bins of width
\Delta z=0.1
since the estimator only requires a rough
z
-distribution of the galaxies; all LOS integrals (including Eq. (
10
)) are thus replaced by sums spanning the redshift range (
z<1
here). Following earlier works
[
7
,
14
]
, we only consider modes with
\ell>\ell_{\mathrm{\min}}\equiv 100
in all 3 fields, to avoid picking up an additional signal from the (linear) ISW effect that is expected to be significant at very large (
\ell<100
) scales
[
69
,
72
,
7
]
.

Unlike the existing implementation employing the
C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{kSZ}^{2}\times\delta_{g}}
estimator
[
43
,
7
,
44
,
11
,
14
]
, we
choose
to use an
\ell
-cut in the LSS field, without restricting the
\ell
-range of kSZ modes (which dominate at smaller scales
\ell\gtrsim 4000
). In our ‘default’ analysis, we choose a conservative
\ell_{\mathrm{\max}}
of 700 for the
\delta_{g}
field, restricting to large scales where the galaxy density field is expected to be well-modeled by a linear galaxy bias
b_{g}
(see e.g.
[
73
,
74
]
). The best-fit value for WISE galaxies is
b_{g}=1.13
, as determined earlier using
Planck
maps
[
7
,
43
]
. Working within the linear regime for
\delta_{g}
enables us to circumvent complications due to the HOD and non-linear galaxy bias terms, which makes our results more robust.

For binning the predicted
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
signal (Section
II.2
), we choose a uniform bin width of
\Delta\ell=100
in each of the three harmonic dimensions, which is broad enough so that there are a sufficient number of bins that contain a non-zero number of triangle modes within them.

III.3
Numerical Implementation

Accurately predicting the
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
signal involves computing the underlying key statistic,
B_{p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}\delta}
(Eq. (
10
)) using its improved model
[
14
]
given by Eqs. (A1-A5). Following
[
34
,
14
]
and several earlier works on the kSZ, we make the simulation-based phenomenological substitution
[
75
]
for each velocity field appearing in these equations; the non-linear matter density field
\delta_{m}
is substituted into the linear continuity equation:

\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{k})=i\frac{faH\delta_{m}(\mathbf{k})}{k}\mathbf{\hat{k}},

(19)

which is a good description of peculiar velocities, since most of their power comes from large scales in the linear regime. Here,
a,f
, and
H
are the scale factor, linear growth rate, and Hubble parameter at that redshift respectively. The resulting non-linear matter power spectra
P_{\delta\delta}
are calculated for the fiducial
\Lambda
CDM model with CAMB and the standard Halofit prescription
[
76
]
.

In Section
V.1
, we will consider an extension to the
\Lambda
CDM model with another free parameter: the sum of neutrino masses (
\Sigma m_{\nu}
), which is known to be non-zero from flavor oscillation detections
[
77
,
78
]
. These independent experiments permit for both, a normal hierarchy of neutrinos (
m_{1}

\simeq

m_{2}

\simeq
0
\ll

m_{3}
; with a minimum
\Sigma m_{\nu}
of
\sim 58
meV), and an inverted hierarchy (
m_{3}\ll m_{1}

\simeq

m_{2}
; with a minimum
\Sigma m_{\nu}
of
>100
meV)
[
79
,
80
]
. In our forecasts, we assume a fiducial value of 60 meV. The presence of massive neutrinos suppresses the clustering of matter at scales smaller than their free-streaming scale, which is defined as
k_{\mathrm{fs}}(z)=0.018\,\Omega_{m}^{1/2}(z)[m_{\nu}
/1 eV] h Mpc
-1
.(e.g.
[
81
,
80
]
). They also introduce a small scale-dependence in the growth rate
f
, which has two powers appearing in our estimator (from Eq.(
19
)). This dependence is well-modeled by a fitting function
[
82
]
:
f(k,z)\approx\mu(k)\Omega_{m}^{\alpha}(z)
with
\alpha\approx 0.55
for
\Lambda
CDM, and

\mu(k)=1-A(k)\Omega_{\Lambda}f_{\nu}+B(k)f_{\nu}^{2}-C(k)f_{\nu}^{3},

(20)

where the coefficients
A(k),B(k),C(k)
are obtained via interpolation (see Table II in
[
82
]
).
f_{\nu}=\Omega_{\nu}/\Omega_{m}
is the fractional contribution of neutrinos to the total matter density (
\approx
0.0045 fiducial value), and
\Omega_{\Lambda}
is the dimensionless dark energy density.

The non-linear matter bispectrum
B_{\delta\delta\delta}
is computed by following earlier works
[
7
,
43
,
44
,
14
]
and using the following fitting function
[
83
]
for simplicity:

B_{\delta\delta\delta}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=\sum_{cyc}2F_{2}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})P_{\delta\delta}(k_{1})P_{\delta\delta}(k_{2}),

(21)

where
\sum_{cyc}
denotes a cyclic sum over
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
. The scale and redshift dependence of the kernel
F_{2}
are fitted using
\Lambda
CDM-only N-body simulations, although the formula itself is general enough to be applicable at the first-order for certain
\Lambda
CDM-extensions, due to the weak cosmological dependence of
F_{2}

[
83
]
. The effect of massive neutrinos on the matter bispectrum is via the suppression of the matter power spectrum, which is consistent at first order with results from second-order perturbation theory
[
84
]
and simulations
[
85
,
53
]
.

While the binned bispectrum approach is relatively fast when applied directly to maps, in the absence of accurate simulated maps, we calculate the theoretical prediction by summing over all possible triplets (Eq.(
12
)). In practice, since the projected-fields kSZ bispectrum is smooth, we speed up the process within each
\ell
-bin indexed by
(a,b,c)
by directly computing
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
for multipole-triplets separated by a (3D) sub-binwidth of
\sim 10
, and summing over the weights (
N_{\Delta}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
) within each sub-bin to obtain the final binned signal
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}_{abc}
. The numerical computation of the statistic
B_{p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}\delta}
in each redshift bin is sped up by employing the same emulators
6
6

6

https://github.com/dylancromer/ostrich

[
86
]
based on Gaussian Process Interpolation that were used in the previous work on
C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{kSZ}^{2}\times\delta_{g}}

[
14
]
.

VI
Discussion and Future Outlook

In this work, we have proposed a novel kSZ (‘full’) bispectrum of the form
\langle TTg\rangle
that cross-correlates two powers of a cleaned CMB temperature map (‘
T
’) with one field of a tracer of LSS density projected along the LOS (‘g’) in harmonic space. Since it does not require individual redshifts of objects, it is applicable to any LSS tracer with a known statistical redshift distribution.

In Section
II
, we implemented our kSZ estimator using the binned bispectrum formalism, improving upon the existing projected-fields power spectrum (
C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{kSZ}^{2}\times\delta_{g}}
) by avoiding its convolution over the CMB filter and its compression of information across different triangle shapes. Thus, our new
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
estimator contains richer information that is better separated across different scales, given that it is a ‘full’ bispectrum that goes beyond simply squaring and cross-correlating the CMB map. Therefore, we expect that scale-dependent cosmological signatures impacting the signal at intermediate scales (such as
\Sigma m_{\nu}
above) are less likely to be biased by uncertainties in the baryonic distribution that dominate at small scales, and vice versa. Moreover, this bispectrum approach gives a better handle over some systematics; for example, choosing to restrict the galaxy modes to linear scales makes our results immune to modeling uncertainties in nonlinear bias and HOD, without compromising on the SNR.

The
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
estimator can provide complementary constraints on neutrino masses (as demonstrated in Section
V
) and potentially on dark energy or modified gravity models via their impact on the linear growth rate. Although not explicitly shown here, the
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
bispectrum could alternatively be used to measure the late-time baryon abundance (
f_{b}
) or baryon density profile in a robust way, improving upon the
C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{kSZ}^{2}\times\delta_{g}}
method
[
43
,
44
,
11
]
. The baryon dependence could be modeled more accurately by incorporating the improved theoretical model (Appendix
A
) for this estimator in a Halo Model framework using the code
class_sz
10
10

10

https://github.com/CLASS-SZ
.

Moreover, inferences from future measurements of the
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
signal using MCMC analyses will require a further speed-up of our numerical implementation, possibly by utilizing the
cosmopower
emulation framework
[
96
,
97
]
. We leave these improvements to future work. A necessary follow-up for interpreting the signal from future measurements with the proposed estimator would be to apply it to accurate simulated maps (e.g. Websky
[
98
]
or Agora
[
99
]
) and validate its pipeline. Certain key aspects to be quantitatively tested using simulations include estimating the kSZ bispectrum’s total covariance matrix and the amount of non-Gaussian contribution to it due to CMB lensing, as well as quantifying the level of contamination due to residual foregrounds, particularly residual CIB, which may be present in post-ILC CMB maps and could bias the measured kSZ signal.

Various photometric samples could be used in place of the WISE catalog for the LSS field, including those spanning higher redshifts such as the unWISE green or red subsamples
[
36
]
, DESI photometric LRGs
[
42
]
, or from the upcoming Rubin Observatory (VRO)
[
37
]
or SPHEREx
[
100
]
surveys, among others. Such measurements should also account for a contribution due to the magnification bias of galaxies, which may become significant at higher redshifts (
z\gtrsim 1
), as computed and suggested in
[
44
]
. A possible refinement would be to use a more optimal redshift weighting for these deeper surveys
[
7
]
, since the projected bispectrum can otherwise receive a majority contribution from
z<0.5
or so
[
51
,
33
,
14
]
.

While galaxies are expected to yield the highest detection significance with this estimator, any LSS tracer of matter density can be used instead, such as galaxy/CMB weak lensing convergence (as shown for the kSZ
2
estimator in
[
11
,
7
]
) or quasars. In the future, the same framework could potentially be applied to 21-cm maps as the tracer, as suggested in the related work
[
38
]
, although we note that it is necessary to use the improved model for this bispectrum (Appendix A and
[
14
]
) in such analyses to accurately interpret the kSZ signal contained in super-squeezed triangles from the epoch of reionization. Moreover, in its current form, most of the relevant 21-cm-kSZ-kSZ bispectrum modes will be lost due to foreground wedge removal in 21-cm data; this issue can be avoided by considering the kSZ
{}^{2}\times\delta_{g}^{2}
trispectrum instead, as suggested recently in
[
101
]
.

In conclusion, we have proposed a novel binned bispectrum to detect the late-time kSZ effect in cleaned CMB maps that uses a projected density field, thus making it applicable to a variety of LSS tracers. We forecasted high cumulative SNRs (
\sim
100-200) for the upcoming SO and CMB-S4 with this estimator, when combined with the WISE galaxy field restricted to linear scales. We showed that the SNR peaks for squeezed triangles in harmonic space that have a short (linear) LSS density mode and two long CMB temperature modes, while the lensing correction is found to be small compared to the signal for such high-SNR triangle configurations. We further illustrate the future potential of this kSZ bispectrum beyond measuring the baryonic abundance at cosmic times, by forecasting initial constraints on the sum of neutrino masses. Thus, the estimator proposed in this work contributes further to the ever-increasing scope of analyses employing the kSZ effect as a probe of baryonic astrophysics and beyond-
\Lambda
CDM cosmology.

Appendix A
Improved Model of
B_{p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}\delta}

The improved model of the
B_{p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}\delta}
statistic that underlies our
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
estimator was derived in the previous work
[
14
]
, which showed that it leads to significant (
\sim
10-15
\%
level) scale-dependent changes in the predicted signal for the related
C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{kSZ}^{2}\times\delta_{g}}
estimator, in the context of upcoming measurements with SO and CMB-S4.
Here, we summarize the improved model and some of its key aspects for completeness and refer the reader to
[
14
]

Under the Limber approximation, the LOS bispectrum
B_{p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}p_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}\delta}=(1/2)B_{p_{\perp}p_{\perp}\delta}

[
102
,
103
,
34
,
14
]
, where
p_{\perp}
is the transverse component of the electron momentum
\mathbf{p}\sim\delta\mathbf{v}
. Thus, the statistic is essentially a 5-point function of the form
\langle\delta\mathbf{v}\delta\mathbf{v}\delta\rangle
, whose (tree-level) Wick expansion consists of
\binom{5}{2}=10
different terms
[
34
]
, out of which four are non-zero (see Table 1 in
[
14
]
).

A rigorous derivation accounting for the directions of electron velocity fields leads to the following expression for the leading order (or ‘usual’) term:

\displaystyle B_{p_{\perp}p_{\perp}\delta}^{\mathrm{usual}}(\mathbf{k_{1}},\mathbf{k_{2}},\mathbf{k_{3}})=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}k}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\left[\sqrt{1-\mu_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{1-\mu_{2}^{2}}\right]P_{vv}(k)B_{\delta\delta\delta}(\mathbf{k_{1}}-\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k_{2}}+\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k_{3}}),

(25)

where
\mu_{\alpha}\equiv\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{k}}
for
\alpha
= 1, 2. The other three non-zero terms are referred to as the ‘extra
-i
’ terms, because they each have a rough geometric scaling
\propto 1/k_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
. Two of these are symmetric in
(\mathbf{k_{1}},\mathbf{k_{2}})
and are given by:

B_{p_{\perp}p_{\perp}\delta}^{\mathrm{extra-1}}(\mathbf{k_{1}},\mathbf{k_{2}},\mathbf{k_{3}})\\
=\int\frac{d^{3}k^{\prime}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\left[\sqrt{1-\mu_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{1-\mu_{2}^{2}}\frac{k^{\prime}}{|\mathbf{k^{\prime}}+\mathbf{k}_{1}|}\right]\\
P_{\delta v}(k^{\prime})B_{v\delta\delta}(\mathbf{k_{1}}+\mathbf{k^{\prime}},\mathbf{k_{2}}-\mathbf{k^{\prime}},\mathbf{k_{3}}),

(26)

where
\mu_{\alpha}\equiv\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{k^{\prime}}}
for
\alpha
= 1, 2, and

B_{p_{\perp}p_{\perp}\delta}^{\mathrm{extra-2}}(\mathbf{k_{1}},\mathbf{k_{2}},\mathbf{k_{3}})\\
=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\left[\sqrt{1-\mu_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{1-\mu_{2}^{2}}\frac{k}{|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}_{2}|}\right]\\
P_{v\delta}(k)B_{\delta v\delta}(\mathbf{k_{1}}-\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k_{2}}+\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k_{3}}),

(27)

where
\mu_{\alpha}\equiv\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{k}}
for
\alpha
= 1, 2. The extra
-3
term has a slightly different form since
\mathbf{k_{3}}
corresponds to
\delta_{g}

\displaystyle B_{p_{\perp}p_{\perp}\delta}^{\mathrm{extra-3}}(\mathbf{k_{1}},\mathbf{k_{2}},\mathbf{k_{3}})

Appendix B
Analytical Binned Bispectrum in the Flat-sky Limit

We analytically defined the binning of our projected-fields
B^{\mathrm{kSZ},\mathrm{kSZ},\delta_{g}}
bispectrum in the flat-sky limit in Section
II.2
. Here, we show that this definition (Eq.(
12
)) generalized is consistent with previous works following the binned bispectrum approach
[
52
,
49
,
53
,
50
]
.

In general, any 2D field across the sky (e.g. primary CMB maps,
\Theta_{f}
,
\delta_{g}
) can be decomposed using spherical harmonics (
Y_{\ell m}(\mathbf{\hat{n}})
).
The all-sky bispectrum between any such maps
(X,Y,Z)
is then defined as the ensemble average of the products of their spherical harmonic coefficients and can be written (e.g.
[
49
,
50
]
) using the Gaunt integral
11
11

11

The Gaunt integral is expressed using Wigner-3j symbols as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{n}Y_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(\mathbf{n})Y_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(\mathbf{n})Y_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(2\ell_{1}+1)(2\ell_{2}+1)(2\ell_{3}+1)}{4\pi}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N_{\Delta}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

(30)

as:

\mathscr{B}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{X,Y,Z}=\sqrt{N_{\Delta}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}}\sum_{m}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}\langle a^{X}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a^{Y}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a^{Z}_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}\rangle.

(31)

(The all-sky bispectrum can also be defined without the
\sqrt{N_{\Delta}}
factor
[
48
]
, but we follow the above convention throughout).

On the other hand, the flat-sky or ‘reduced’ bispectrum is defined as in Eq.(
9
) and is given in general by:

B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{X,Y,Z}=\frac{\sum_{m}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}\langle a^{X}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a^{Y}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a^{Z}_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}\rangle}{\sqrt{N_{\Delta}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}}}

(32)

so that for a single multipole triplet, the flat-sky and all-sky bispectra are related
[
48
,
49
]

B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{X,Y,Z}=\left(\mathscr{B}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{X,Y,Z}/N_{\Delta}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\right)
.

Now, by binning the harmonic space in 3D similar to Section
II.2
, the all-sky binned bispectrum
\mathscr{B}_{abc}^{X,Y,Z}
is effectively an average over all possible
\mathscr{B}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{X,Y,Z}
that lie within the 3D
\ell
-bin
[
52
,
49
]
. Substituting the relation between flat-sky and all-sky bispectra for each multipole triplet, we recover our definition for a binned flat-sky bispectrum (Eq.(
12
)) for fields
(X,Y,Z)
in general:

B^{X,Y,Z}_{abc}=\frac{1}{N_{abc}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1}\in\Delta_{a}\\
\ell_{2}\in\Delta_{b}\\
\ell_{3}\in\Delta_{c}\end{subarray}}N_{\Delta}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{X,Y,Z}\right).

(33)

It is thus consistent with the all-sky formalism
[
52
,
49
]
, and is a
weighted
average of all
B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{X,Y,Z}
within the bin.

The binned approach has also been extended to apply directly to (simulated or real data) maps in the flat-sky limit
[
53
,
50
]
, which is implemented by ‘filtering’ the maps to select only those
\ell
modes that lie within the bin:
W^{Z}_{c}(\mathbf{n})=\sum\limits_{\ell\in\Delta_{c}}\sum\limits_{m}{Y}_{\ell,m}(\mathbf{n})a^{Z}_{\ell,m}
. The flat-sky bispectrum is then estimated as:

\hat{B}^{X,Y,Z}_{abc}=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{n}\frac{1}{N_{abc}}W^{X}_{a}W^{Y}_{b}W^{Z}_{c},

(34)

which is seen to be equivalent to our analytical definition Eq.(
33
) by using the Gaunt integral.

\psi

\langle\Theta_{b}(\mathbf{\ell_{1}})\,\Theta_{b}(\mathbf{\ell_{2}})\,\delta_{g}(\mathbf{\ell_{3}})\rangle=b(\ell_{1})b(\ell_{2})\langle\Theta(\mathbf{\ell_{1}})\,\Theta(\mathbf{\ell_{2}})\,\delta_{g}(\mathbf{\ell_{3}})\rangle.

Expanding up to first order in
\psi

\displaystyle\langle\Theta(\mathbf{\ell_{1}})\,\Theta(\mathbf{\ell_{2}})\,\delta_{g}(\mathbf{\ell_{3}})\rangle=\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\{\langle\tilde{\Theta}(\mathbf{\ell_{1}})\,\tilde{\Theta}(\mathbf{\ell_{2}})\,\delta_{g}(\mathbf{\ell_{3}})\rangle
