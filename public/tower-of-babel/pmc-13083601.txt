Prevalence, Etiology, and Antifungal Resistance Profiles of Ocular Fungal Infections in Ghanaian Ophthalmic Patients: A Multicenter Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Isaiah Osei Duah Junior, Abigail Oforiwaa Boateng, Kwadwo Owusu Akuffo, Lord Gyimah, Kingsley Badu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsOcular fungal infections are an important but often underrecognized cause of ocular morbidity, particularly in resource‐limited settings. Delayed diagnosis and inappropriate treatment can lead to poor visual outcomes and increased disease burden. This study aimed to determine the prevalence, etiology, and antifungal susceptibility patterns of ocular fungal infections among patients attending three ophthalmic facilities in Ghana.MethodsA multicenter cross‐sectional study was conducted among 248 ophthalmic patients presenting with clinically suspected ocular infections of fungal origin. Biographical and ophthalmic data were collected using a structured questionnaire. Ocular specimens were obtained and subjected to standard microbiological culture and laboratory identification of fungal pathogens. Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed against commonly used antifungal agents. Logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate associations between participants' demographic and clinical characteristics and fungal culture positivity, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.ResultsFungal pathogens were isolated from 9.36% of ocular specimens. Candida albicans was the most frequently identified organism. Antifungal susceptibility testing demonstrated a high level of multidrug resistance, including reduced susceptibility to amphotericin B, fluconazole, itraconazole, and terbinafine. None of the demographic or clinical characteristics examined were significantly associated with fungal culture positivity.ConclusionThe findings indicate a measurable burden of ocular fungal infections in this Ghanaian ophthalmic population, with Candida species being the predominant pathogens. The observed multidrug resistance to commonly used antifungal agents raises concerns about the effectiveness of empirical treatment and highlights the need for targeted therapy based on laboratory confirmation. The absence of significant demographic or clinical predictors suggests that clinical presentation alone may not reliably identify fungal infections. Routine microbiological diagnosis, antifungal susceptibility testing, and strengthened surveillance systems are therefore essential to guide appropriate management and improve outcomes for patients with ocular fungal infections in Ghana.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, Candida albicans, fungi eye infections, multidrug resistance, ocular mycosis

Introduction

Ocular mycoses, also referred to as fungal eye infections or oculomycosis, are caused by fungal pathogens that infect ocular structures including the conjunctiva, cornea, eyelids, adnexal tissues, and intraocular compartments [1, 2]. These infections manifest as a spectrum of clinical conditions such as mycotic conjunctivitis, keratitis, blepharitis, and endophthalmitis [1, 2]. Although less frequently reported than bacterial ocular infections, fungal infections can result in severe complications including corneal perforation, permanent vision loss, and, in advanced cases, enucleation if diagnosis and treatment are delayed [3, 4, 5].

Compared with bacterial ocular infections [6, 7, 8], epidemiological data on ocular mycoses remain limited. This gap largely reflects diagnostic challenges and frequent misclassification as bacterial disease in routine clinical practice [9, 10]. Nevertheless, fungal keratitis alone is estimated to account for more than one million cases globally each year, with the highest burden occurring in tropical and subtropical regions, particularly across Asia and Africa [11, 12]. Several factors increase susceptibility to ocular fungal infections, including ocular trauma or surgery, contaminated contact lens use, prolonged topical corticosteroid or antibacterial therapy, and environmental exposures influenced by geographic and seasonal conditions [4, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Despite these recognized risk factors, reliable epidemiological data on ocular mycoses remain scarce in Ghana.

Clinically, ocular fungal infections often present with nonspecific symptoms such as ocular pain, redness, tearing, photophobia, discharge, and reduced vision, which frequently overlap with manifestations of bacterial infections [4, 5, 16]. As a result, misdiagnosis is common and may lead to inappropriate treatment. Delayed or incorrect therapy can allow infection to progress into deeper ocular structures, increasing the risk of irreversible visual impairment [4, 11, 12]. Accurate diagnosis is therefore essential for effective clinical management. However, in many low‐resource settings, including Ghana, diagnosis often relies primarily on clinical assessment rather than laboratory confirmation [17, 18]. Advanced diagnostic approaches such as fungal culture, rapid molecular assays, and high‐throughput pathogen detection are rarely available, and even basic microscopy remains underutilized [17, 18].

Consequently, clinicians frequently prescribe empirical broad‐spectrum antimicrobial therapy without identifying the causative pathogen. While this approach may provide temporary symptom relief, it can delay initiation of appropriate antifungal treatment and may contribute to the growing global threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and multidrug resistance (MDR) [17, 18, 19]. Although increasing resistance to commonly used antifungal agents has been reported in several regions, data on antifungal susceptibility patterns of ocular fungal pathogens in Ghana remain largely undocumented [20, 21, 22]. Establishing baseline susceptibility profiles is therefore essential to guide treatment strategies and reduce the risk of therapeutic failure [23].

To address these gaps, this study aimed to determine the prevalence and etiological spectrum of ocular fungal infections in a Ghanaian ophthalmic population and to assess the antifungal susceptibility profiles of the identified fungal isolates. The findings are expected to provide region‐specific epidemiological data and inform evidence‐based management of ocular mycoses in Ghana and similar resource‐limited settings.

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Population, and Setting

A multicenter cross‐sectional study was conducted from May 9 to June 15, 2023, among ophthalmic patients with suspected fungal eye infections at three Ghanaian health facilities: Anglican Eye Hospital, Jachie; St. Michael's Hospital, Pramso; and Kumasi South Hospital, Agogo. Biographic and clinical data were collected using a structured questionnaire. Conjunctival swabs and corneal scrapings were obtained for fungal analysis. Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed on recovered ocular fungal strains against commonly used antifungal agents.

Patients seeking ophthalmic care for ocular or periocular infections at the study sites during the study period were eligible. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or from caregivers for minors. A purposive sampling strategy was used to enroll patients presenting with external ocular infection signs and symptoms. Patients attending for optical correction, receiving systemic or topical antibiotics, or who had eye surgery within the prior week were excluded.

The study sites were selected for their high outpatient volume, broad catchment areas, and mix of rural and urban populations. Anglican Eye Hospital and St. Michael's Hospital are located in rural area in Bosomtwe, while Kumasi South Hospital is in an urban center in Asokwa. All facilities provide comprehensive eye care, including visual assessment, management of anterior and posterior segment pathologies, prescription of medications, and scheduled surgeries. St. Michael's and Kumasi South Hospitals serve as referral centers for nearby private and polyclinics, with tertiary referral to Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital for emergencies. None of the study facilities have ocular microbiology laboratories, and clinicians rely on empirical diagnosis for external ocular infections.

Study Variables and Operational Definitions

Blepharitis was defined as gritty, itchy, or sore eyes with crusting or collarettes at the eyelash bases, meibomian gland clogging, and eyelash loss. Conjunctivitis was characterized by conjunctival lesions with hyperemia, chemosis, purulent discharge, or hemorrhage. Blepharoconjunctivitis presented as red, dry, and scaly eyelids accompanied by itchiness and burning. Keratoconjunctivitis was associated with dryness, itching, and mucous discharge affecting both the cornea and conjunctiva. Keratitis was defined as corneal lesions with edema, cellular infiltration, pain, redness, and photophobia. Hordeolum presented as a swollen, tender eyelid with acute pain, photophobia, and mild epiphora. Ophthalmia neonatorum, occurring in neonates within the first 28 days of life, was characterized by eyelid edema, erythema, and purulent discharge. Preseptal and orbital cellulitis presented with painful eyelid swelling, with orbital cellulitis distinguished by decreased vision and pain on eye movement [17].

Data Collection

Sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and health information were collected using structured questionnaires. Comprehensive vision assessments, including visual acuity, slit‐lamp biomicroscopy, and ophthalmoscopy, were performed by a registered optometrist. Patient medical history, primary and secondary diagnoses, and prescribed antimicrobial therapy were documented. A total of 237 ocular samples were collected aseptically. Conjunctival samples were obtained by gently rolling a saline‐moistened cotton bud over the lower tarsal plate and conjunctival fornix. Corneal scrapings for keratitis and ulcer cases were collected using slit‐lamp biomicroscopy and topical anesthesia (0.5% proparacaine). No lacrimal sac or eyelid swabs were collected, as no patients had dacryocystitis or blepharitis. Samples were placed in sterile nutrient broth and transported within 1–3 h in triple‐packaged iceboxes to the laboratory.

Laboratory Methods

Specimens were cultured on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) at 37°C for 96 h. Positive cultures were subcultured on PDA containing 100 µg/mL streptomycin and 100 µg/mL penicillin to inhibit bacterial growth. Morphological and direct microscopic identification was performed, with PCR confirmation used selectively for one representative species to validate the morphological identification. Selection of these isolates was based on morphological diversity to ensure coverage of the predominant fungal types observed. Each isolate was processed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and reliability of the results.

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Quick‐DNA Fungal/Bacterial MiniPrep Kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentration was determined using a Nanodrop ND‐2000 (Thermo Scientific). The fungal ITS region (ITS1‐5.8S rDNA‐ITS2) and D1/D2 domain of 26S rRNA gene were amplified using ITS1/ITS4 and NL1/NL4 primers. PCR reactions were performed in 20 µL volumes containing 1 µL template DNA, 12.5 µL 10× DreamTaq Green Buffer, 0.5 µL of each primer, and 5.5 µL nuclease‐free water. Cycling conditions included initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 25 cycles of 95°C for 1 min, 49°C–52°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 2 min, with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. PCR products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide.

Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed using the agar well diffusion method with antifungal agents such as amphotericin B, terbinafine, itraconazole, and fluconazole. Fungal inocula were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard (1 × 107 CFU/mL) and spread on 20 mm PDA plates. Zones of inhibition were measured in millimeters and breakpoint determined using the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [24].

Quality Assurance and Control

A modified structured questionnaire was used to collect demographic and clinical data. Questionnaires were written in English and administered by the principal investigator and a trained research assistant. For participants unable to comprehend English, the questionnaire was explained in the local dialect. All collected data were double‐checked for accuracy and completeness.

Ocular samples were carefully labeled to prevent mismatches. Laboratory reagents and culture media were checked for expiration dates, and sterility controls were performed to ensure media integrity. Media functionality was verified using American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) Standard Reference Strain Candida albicans ATCC 10231.

Data Protection and Management

Hard copies of completed questionnaires were securely stored in a locked location, accessible only to the principal investigator and research advisor. Nonaggregated electronic data sets were protected with strong alphanumeric passwords. Research reports presented to facilities or for publication were aggregated, ensuring that individual participants could not be identified.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval followed a hierarchical protocol. Written permission was obtained from the authorities of Anglican Eye Clinic, St. Michael's Hospital, and Kumasi South Hospital. The study protocol, adapted from a previous investigation focused on bacterial ocular infection [17], was approved by the Committee on Human Research, Publication, and Ethics (CHRPE) of Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology and Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (Reference: CHRPE/AP/116/24). The aspects of the previous protocol that were adopted included the structured questionnaire, ocular swabbing techniques, and specimen transport procedures, which were tailored to the local context to ensure feasibility and adherence to best practices [17]. However, sample identification and characterization were modified to suit the fungal isolates of interest in this study. Written informed consent was obtained from adult participants, and for minors, consent was obtained from caregivers after thorough explanation of the study objectives, procedures, and potential risks. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all laboratory procedures, including direct microscopic examination, morphological identification, and selective PCR confirmation, followed Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines.

Statistical Analysis

Data were initially entered in Microsoft Excel and later exported to IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 for analysis. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess normality. Biographic, medical, and ophthalmic characteristics were summarized, and gender differences were examined using χ 2 analysis. Cross‐tabulations with frequencies and percentages were employed to describe clinical diagnoses, culture status, and antifungal therapy. Antifungal susceptibility experiments were performed in triplicate, with zones of inhibition reported as mean ± standard deviation. Bivariate logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between sample characteristics and the prevalence of fungal eye infections, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patterns in the Distribution of Ocular Fungal Isolates Across Patient Demographics and Medical History

Out of 248 participants, 22 (9.3%) had ocular fungal isolates. Females represented 62.9% of participants, with 6.9% testing positive compared to 5.0% of males (p = 0.182). The prevalence varied significantly by age (p = 0.003), with adults ≥ 45 years (3.6%) and children/adolescents 0–18 years (3.2%) showing the highest proportions, while adults 19–44 years had the lowest (2.0%). Educational level (p < 0.001) and occupation (p = 0.009) were also significantly associated, with higher prevalence among individuals with no or lower education and nonfarmers. Herbal concoction use was significantly associated with presence of fungal ocular isolates (p = 0.006), though conventional medications, comorbidities, sex, ethnicity, residence, religion, and marital status showed no significant effect. Overall, age, education, occupation, and herbal use influenced ocular fungal prevalence (Table 1).

Distribution of Ocular Fungal Isolates by Ophthalmic Characteristics

Distribution analysis revealed that fungal presence was significantly related to the right eye (oculus dexter, p = 0.024), eye pain (p = 0.011), whitish discharge/deposition (p = 0.008), and eyelid health status (p = 0.001). Although risk factors such as previous eye infections (63.7%), prior antimicrobial use (44.1%), ocular trauma (31.0%), spectacles use (23.8%), and ocular surgery (10.1%) were noted, their distribution did not differ significantly. The most frequent clinical presentations were conjunctivitis (68.0%), keratoconjunctivitis (14.9%), and keratitis (8.3%), but their distribution did not differ between fungal‐positive and fungal‐negative groups. Similarly, involvement of the conjunctiva, cornea, sclera, anterior chamber, pupils, iris, and lens showed no significant distribution differences. Associated conditions such as dry eye syndrome showed a borderline distribution trend (p = 0.066), while headaches, pinguecula, and pterygium showed no significant differences (see Table 2).

Fungal Etiology of Ocular Infections

The distribution of fungal pathogens across different eye infections showed that C. albicans was the predominant isolate, accounting for 6.5% (16 cases), mainly in conjunctivitis (4.8%, 12 cases), followed by keratoconjunctivitis (0.8%, 2 cases), hordeolum (0.4%, 1 case), and keratitis (0.4%, 1 case). Other fungi were rarely detected: Aspergillus fumigatus (0.4%, 1 case, keratoconjunctivitis), Aspergillus nomius (0.4%, 1 case, preseptal cellulitis), Candida auris (0.4%, 1 case, conjunctivitis), Candida krusei (0.4%, 1 case, keratoconjunctivitis), and Penicillium citrinum (0.8%, 2 cases, conjunctivitis). No fungal isolates were identified in blepharoconjunctivitis, ophthalmia neonatorum, ocular trauma, endophthalmitis, or uveitis. Overall, C. albicans emerged as the leading fungal pathogen, with conjunctivitis as the primary clinical manifestation, while other fungi appeared only sporadically (see Table 3).

Antifungal Susceptibility Patterns of Clinically Relevant Ocular Fungal Isolates

The antifungal susceptibility of six fungal isolates—A. fumigatus, A. nomius, C. albicans, C. auris, C. krusei, and P. citrinum—was assessed using the agar well‐diffusion method in triplicates. A. fumigatus was resistant to amphotericin B, itraconazole, and fluconazole, but highly susceptible to Terbinafine (35.33 ± 1.66 mm). A. nomius showed moderate susceptibility to amphotericin B (16.11 ± 1.45 mm) and fluconazole (19.22 ± 1.09 mm), while being resistant to terbinafine and itraconazole. Among the Candida species, C. albicans displayed moderate inhibition to all agents, with fluconazole (20.17 ± 1.07 mm) being most effective, whereas C. auris exhibited broad susceptibility, particularly to fluconazole (23.22 ± 1.09 mm). C. krusei and P. citrinum were resistant to amphotericin B, itraconazole, and fluconazole but showed sensitivity to Terbinafine (15.11 ± 0.6 mm and 14.78 ± 1.09 mm, respectively). Overall, terbinafine demonstrated the broadest activity across isolates, whereas fluconazole was particularly effective against Candida species. Species‐specific resistance patterns highlight the need for tailored antifungal therapy in managing ocular fungal infections as shown in Table 5.

Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence and etiology of ocular fungal infections and antifungal susceptibility in an ophthalmic population in Ghana. Approximately 10% of ocular specimens were positive for fungal pathogens, with C. albicans being the predominant species. MDR was observed against commonly used antifungal agents, fluconazole, itraconazole, and amphotericin B, while terbinafine remained effective.

Oculomycosis is a leading cause of ocular morbidity and blindness, often misdiagnosed as bacterial infections by ophthalmic clinicians [10]. Such misdiagnoses lead to inappropriate treatment, fueling AMR, worsening patient outcomes, and increasing financial and systemic burdens. Laboratory confirmation is therefore critical for accurate diagnosis, effective therapy, and improved prognosis.

The estimated prevalence (~10%) in our study aligns with findings from global studies, though prevalence varies widely across populations and regions, from as low as 0.05% to nearly 97% [13, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Our findings are comparable to reports from the United States [27], United Kingdom [30, 31], and Japan [32], where C. albicans predominates, but contrast with regions such as Brazil [33], China [34], Ethiopia [35], and India [29, 36] where Aspergillus and Fusarium species are more common [29, 33, 34, 35, 36]. The higher burden of C. albicans may reflect host factors such as female predominance, immunocompromised conditions, and use of immunosuppressants, as well as its strong virulence attributes [37, 38, 39].

Resistance patterns in this study are of significant concern. A. fumigatus exhibited resistance to fluconazole and itraconazole, consistent with growing reports of secondary azole resistance, and also showed resistance to amphotericin B, which may reflect intrinsic resistance mechanisms [40, 41, 42, 43]. A. nomius exhibited resistance to itraconazole, consistent with the known azole resistance in Aspergillus species [40]. Of concern, however, was its reduced susceptibility to terbinafine, an antifungal valued clinically for its safety, efficacy, and affordability [44]. Although C. albicans and C. auris were generally susceptible, their reduced inhibition zones against amphotericin B and itraconazole indicate MDR, underscoring the need for ongoing AMR surveillance in the management of ocular infections in Ghana. The observed resistance of P. citrinum to amphotericin B, as indicated by its no zone of inhibition, may be attributed to the altered genetic makeup of clinical isolates resulting from its broad‐spectrum clinical use [45]. Similarly, the increased resistance of the aforementioned ocular isolates to azoles could be due to genetically encoded resistance mechanisms, including biofilm formation and efflux pumps, which help them withstand high concentrations of these antifungal agents [39, 46].

This work provides the first comprehensive estimates of ocular fungal burden and antifungal susceptibility in Ghana, offering essential data to guide clinical management and inform surveillance strategies for emerging resistance. However, some limitations exist. Standard antifungals such as natamycin and voriconazole, widely recommended for ocular infections, were unavailable at the study sites. In particular, the unavailability of natamycin prevented assessment of susceptibility to this first‐line agent, limiting direct comparison with international treatment guidelines. Additionally, the absence of molecular sequencing of isolates restricted species‐level characterization, although the combined use of standard culture and PCR‐based techniques provided adequate sensitivity and specificity for determining the fungal etiology implicated in the presenting ocular infections.

Conclusion

Oculomycosis affects a notable proportion of ophthalmic patients in Ghana, with C. albicans as the dominant pathogen. The observed MDR to commonly used antifungals highlights limitations in current therapeutic options and underscores the need for routine fungal diagnostics, antimicrobial surveillance, and the development of more effective treatment strategies to improve clinical outcomes.
