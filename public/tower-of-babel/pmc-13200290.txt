Demographic variation of styloid process morphology in panoramic radiography
Authors: Oliver Schuck, Florian Dudde, Martin Gosau, Ralf Smeets, Thomas Beikler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElongation of the styloid process (SP) is frequently discussed in the context of Eagle syndrome (ES), with a length threshold of ≥ 30 mm commonly cited as indicative of elongation. However, this criterion is largely derived from heterogeneous imaging modalities and may not adequately account for demographic variability or the projection-related limitations of panoramic radiography. Given that panoramic radiographs (PRs) represent the most common first-line imaging modality in dental and maxillofacial practice, interpretation of SP morphology in this setting warrants critical evaluation.The aim of this study was to characterize demographic variation in SP morphology on standardised PRs and to explore whether commonly cited length thresholds should be interpreted with caution in this imaging modality.Materials and methodsA retrospective analysis of 350 standardised PRs was performed. Bilateral measurements included SP length, width, angulation, distance to anatomical reference points, segmentation according to the Langlais classification, and calcification patterns. Data were stratified by sex and age (≥ 40 vs. <40 years). Statistical comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test and chi-square test (α = 0.05). A post hoc power analysis confirmed adequate statistical power for primary outcome comparisons.ResultsSignificant morphological variability was observed across demographic groups. Mean SP lengths exceeded the frequently cited 30 mm threshold in all subgroups. Older individuals demonstrated greater elongation, width, angulation, and advanced calcification patterns compared to younger individuals (p < 0.05). Males exhibited significantly greater linear dimensions than females (p < 0.05). Bilateral elongation was common within the cohort.ConclusionsSP morphology showed substantial demographic variation on standardised PRs. In this non-symptom-selected cohort, measurements frequently exceeded the commonly cited 30 mm threshold, suggesting that length-based cut-offs should be interpreted cautiously on panoramic imaging.Clinical correlation remains essential, and advanced imaging may be considered in selected cases.

Introduction

Eagle syndrome (ES) is a clinical entity traditionally associated with elongation of the SP or ossification of the stylohyoid ligament complex. Since its original description in 1937, a wide range of symptoms – including dysphagia, cervical pain, foreign body sensation, otalgia, and neurovascular complaints – have been attributed to morphological variations of the SP [1–6]. Despite decades of investigation, the relationship between radiographic elongation and symptomatic presentation remains incompletely understood.

Eagle syndrome refers to a symptomatic clinical condition, whereas radiographic elongation represents an anatomical finding.

A length threshold of ≥ 30 mm is frequently cited in the literature as indicative of elongation [6, 7]. However, this cut-off has been derived primarily from radiographic studies using heterogeneous imaging modalities and populations. Importantly, elongated SP are commonly observed in asymptomatic individuals, while symptomatic patients may present with measurements below this threshold [6–8]. These inconsistencies raise fundamental questions regarding the validity of fixed numerical criteria, particularly when applied to two-dimensional imaging modalities.

In routine dental and maxillofacial practice, PRs represent the most common first-line imaging modality in which elongation or calcification of the SP is identified – often as an incidental finding [6, 9]. Due to their wide availability, low radiation exposure, and simultaneous visualization of both SP, PRs remain integral to everyday diagnostic workflows. Consequently, clinicians frequently face the challenge of interpreting SP morphology on panoramic imaging and deciding whether further evaluation, such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), is warranted.

However, panoramic imaging is inherently limited by projection distortion, magnification variability, and head positioning effects [6, 9]. While three-dimensional imaging provides superior anatomical precision, it is not routinely performed in asymptomatic patients or incidental findings. Therefore, understanding the demographic and anatomical range of SP morphology as visualised under standardised panoramic conditions is clinically relevant to avoid overinterpretation and unnecessary escalation of diagnostic procedures.

The present study seeks to characterize the demographic variability of SP morphology within a standardised panoramic imaging environment. By providing internally consistent reference data stratified by age and sex, this study aims to contribute to a more nuanced interpretation of panoramic findings and to inform future research integrating radiological morphology, three-dimensional validation, and clinical correlation.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study analysed PRs of 350 individuals, selected randomly from a database of patients who underwent routine dental or maxillofacial radiography. For inclusion, individuals were required to each have a bilateral panoramic radiograph, ensuring that both SPes could be adequately assessed. Patients with significant craniofacial deformities, prior surgical intervention on the SP, or insufficient image quality were excluded from the study. No formal sample size calculation was performed due to the retrospective and exploratory design of the study.

Age threshold

The age threshold of 40 years was selected based on previous reports describing progressive ossification and age-associated morphological changes of the stylohyoid complex becoming more prevalent in middle adulthood [10, 11]. Dichotomization at this threshold allowed investigation of potential age-dependent trends while maintaining balanced group sizes for statistical comparison [11–13].

Radiographic measurements

PRs were assessed for key measurements of the SP, including length, width at the base and tip, distance to the spina nasalis, and angular deviation relative to the horizontal plane (Fig. 1). These measurements were taken bilaterally (right and left sides) and stratified by sex and age (≥ 40 and < 40 years).

Exemplaric measurement

Distance to the spina nasalis anterior and angular measurements were included as descriptive spatial parameters intended to characterize SP position relative to a reproducible craniofacial reference structure on PRs. These parameters were not analysed as diagnostic markers of Eagle syndrome but as anatomical descriptors of radiographic morphology.

All PRs were acquired using a PLANMECA ProMax 3D Mid (SN: NTP670469; 04/2013; PLANMECA OY, Asentajankatu 6, 00880 Helsinki, FINLAND) panoramic unit. Exposure parameters were adjusted according to patient size within the manufacturer’s standard operating range (Five Categories ranging from “XS” to “XL”, see Table “Technical Specifications of the X-Ray Device PLANMECA ProMax 3D Mid”). The nominal magnification factor provided by the manufacturer was [1,2 ≈ 120%]. Patients were positioned according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol using bite block stabilization and midsagittal alignment. The Software used to store the digital images was PLANMECA Romexis (v 6.4.1.82; PLANMECA OY, Asentajankatu 6, 00880 Helsinki, FINLAND).

Measurements of the styloid process

SP length (from base to tip), width (at base and tip), distance to the spina nasalis, and angulation relative to the horizontal plane were measured bilaterally.

A standardised calibration for magnification or dimensional scaling was not performed, which may have introduced minor variation in absolute measurements. However, all radiographs were obtained using the same imaging protocol and equipment, ensuring consistency across the sample. All measurements were performed by two trained and calibrated examiners (oral and maxillofacial surgeons with > 3 years of radiologic experience). Classification was performed according to Langlais et al. (1986), who described three morphological types based on segmentation characteristics of the SP [14–16].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was used to display patients baseline characteristics. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and binary variables are using absolute and relative frequencies. Comparison of continuous variables was performed by Student’s t-test. Chi-square test was used for analysis of binary variables. Statistical analysis was performed to compare measurements between males and females, as well as between different age groups (≥ 40 and < 40 years). Left and right SP were analysed separately to descriptively capture side-specific morphological variation. As measurements were obtained bilaterally from the same individuals, potential within-subject correlation cannot be excluded. However, given the descriptive and exploratory nature of the study, no formal adjustment for intra-individual dependency was applied. To assess intra-observer reliability, a subset of PRs was re-evaluated after a one-month interval. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated for continuous measurements, with values above 0.82 indicating good reproducibility. Agreement for categorical variables (Langlais classification and calcification patterns) was assessed qualitatively based on repeated evaluations. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS version 28.0 statistical package (IBM, Markham, Canada). A post hoc sensitivity analysis for the primary comparison of SP length between age groups (n = 155 vs. 195, two-sided α = 0.05) indicated that the study had 80% power to detect a standardised mean difference of approximately Cohen’s d = 0.30. Based on the observed pooled standard deviation, this corresponds to an absolute difference of approximately 2.5 mm. The observed group differences were slightly smaller (d ≈ 0.26–0.27), indicating moderate statistical power below 80%.

Results

Overall findings

The study included 350 participants, with a mean age of 41.19 years (± 18.74). Of the total cohort, 63.7% were males (n = 223), and 36.3% were females (n = 127) (Table 1). The general analysis of the SP length, width, distance to key anatomical landmarks, angular measurements, and calcification patterns provided important insights into anatomical variations in the population.

The angle between the SP and the horizontal plane at the base of the right SP was 43.92° (± 29.05°), while the left side had a similar angle of 44.03° (± 29.02°) (Tables 2 and 3). Angles at the tip were 80.21° (± 50.42°) on the right and 79.22° (± 50.74°) on the left (Tables 2 and 3). The most common classification across the population for both the right and left SPs was Type 1 (uninterrupted SP), with 51.7% classified as such on the right side and 51.4% on the left (Tables 2 and 3). Type 2 (pseudoarticulated SP) and Type 3 (segmented SP) were observed in 30.6% and 17.7% of the population on the right, and 31.4% and 17.1% on the left, respectively (Tables 2 and 3). Calcification of the SP was observed in varying degrees. On the right side, 32.3% of the cohort showed no calcification (Type A), while the rest demonstrated partial to complete calcification: Type B (24.9%), Type C (25.1%), and Type D (17.7%) (Tables 2 and 3). Similar distribution patterns were found on the left side.

Analysis by age

When analysed by age, significant differences were found in the anatomy of the SP between individuals aged ≥ 40 years and those < 40 years (Table 2). Older individuals (≥ 40 years) exhibited longer SP on both sides compared to younger individuals (Table 2). On the right, the average length was 33.63 mm for older individuals, compared to 31.45 mm for those under 40 (p = 0.016) (Table 2). A similar trend was observed on the left side, with lengths of 34.43 mm in older individuals and 32.17 mm in younger individuals (p = 0.013) (Table 2; Figs. 4 and 5). The SP was also wider in older people. On the right, the width at the base measured 5.98 mm in the older group compared to 4.63 mm in the younger group (p < 0.001) (Table 2). The tip width was 1.58 mm in older individuals and 1.32 mm in younger ones (p = 0.001) (Table 2). Furthermore, the left side showed similar results, with a base width of 6.36 mm in the older group and 5.10 mm in the younger group (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Age also impacted the distance between the SP and the spina nasalis (Table 2). For the right SP, older individuals revealed a mean distance of 110.08 mm at the base compared to 102.20 mm in younger individuals (p = 0.005) (Table 2). The tip distance also increased with age, measuring 95.74 mm in older participants and 88.61 mm in younger ones (p = 0.006) (Table 2). Older individuals showed more pronounced angles between the SP and the horizontal plane. On the right side, the base angle was 49.80° in older participants compared to 39.23° in younger participants (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Similarly, the angle at the tip of the right SP was larger in older individuals (89.95°) than in younger ones (72.47°) (p = 0.001) (Table 2). The left side showed similar trends for both base and tip angles (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Calcification was notably more common and advanced in older individuals. On the right side, Type D calcification (complete ossification) was present in 38.7% of older individuals, compared to only 1.0% of younger individuals (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Analysis by sex

Significant differences were also observed between males and females, particularly in terms of SP length, width, and positioning (Table 3; Figs. 4 and 5). Males had consistently longer SPs compared to females (Table 3). On the right side, males had an average SP length of 33.20 mm, compared to 31.04 mm in females (p = 0.021) (Table 3). On the left, the length was 34.29 mm in males and 31.21 mm in females (p = 0.001) (Table 3). Males also exhibited a wider SP (Table 3). On the right, the base width was 5.39 mm in males compared to 4.95 mm in females (p = 0.018), while the tip width was 1.51 mm in males and 1.32 mm in females (p = 0.026) (Table 3). On the left side, males had a base width of 5.92 mm compared to 5.20 mm in females (p = 0.017), with tip widths of 1.70 mm in males and 1.36 mm in females (p = 0.005) (Table 3). Males revealed greater distances between the SP and the spina nasalis compared to females. Furthermore, on the right side, the distance at the base was 108.27 mm in males and 101.17 mm in females (p = 0.015), while the tip distance was 95.46 mm in males and 85.27 mm in females (p < 0.001) (Table 3). In addition to that, similar sex differences were found on the left side, with males showing greater distances at both the base (p = 0.008) and tip (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Males also exhibited more pronounced angles between the SP and the horizontal plane. The right-side base angle was 46.37° in males compared to 39.61° in females (p = 0.036), while the left-side base angle was 46.21° in males and 40.18° in females (p = 0.031) (Table 3). However, the angles at the tip did not show significant sex differences (Table 3). No significant sex differences were observed in the classification of SP types (Table 3). Both males and females had similar distributions of Type 1 (normal, uninterrupted SP), Type 2 (pseudoarticulated), and Type 3 (segmented SP) SP (Table 3). Males and females demonstrated similar calcification patterns, with no significant sex differences in the distribution of calcification types across the population (Table 3).

Discussion

The present study was designed to characterize SP morphology under standardised panoramic imaging conditions and to critically examine the interpretative implications of applying fixed linear thresholds to a biologically variable anatomical structure. Rather than seeking to validate diagnostic criteria for Eagle syndrome (ES), this analysis addresses a more fundamental methodological question: how should morphologic variability observed in two-dimensional imaging be interpreted in the absence of symptom correlation and three-dimensional validation [1, 5].

The ≥ 30 mm threshold frequently cited in the literature has become deeply embedded in clinical discourse surrounding ES [6, 7]. However, the origin of this value stems from heterogeneous anatomical and radiographic studies using diverse imaging modalities and small sample sizes. When applied uniformly to panoramic radiography, which is subject to projection distortion and magnification variability, the epistemological validity of a strict cut-off becomes questionable [6, 9].

In the present cohort, mean SP lengths exceeded the commonly referenced threshold across all demographic subgroups. Importantly, this occurred in a general population without symptom-based stratification. These findings suggest that reliance on a universal linear criterion may systematically overestimate the prevalence of “elongation” in asymptomatic individuals [7, 8, 10].

This observation raises a broader issue: anatomical variation does not inherently equate to pathology. The classification of elongation based solely on length reflects a reductionist interpretation of a complex three-dimensional structure visualised in a two-dimensional projection modality [5, 6].

Significant age- and sex-related differences were observed in length, width, angulation, and calcification patterns. Older individuals demonstrated progressive elongation and ossification, findings consistent with previously described age-associated ossification processes of the stylohyoid complex [11, 12]. Similarly, males exhibited greater linear dimensions, likely reflecting general skeletal size differences rather than pathological alteration. Sex-related variation in craniofacial skeletal dimensions has been widely reported and may influence morphometric measurements of craniofacial structures [17, 18].

These findings emphasize that SP morphology is influenced by biological variables and is not static across the lifespan. The application of a uniform cut-off value that does not account for demographic variation may therefore lack biological plausibility. More importantly, elongation may represent a continuum of anatomical diversity rather than a binary pathological state. Previous radiographic studies have similarly highlighted the wide morphological variability of the stylohyoid complex within asymptomatic populations [8, 19].

Length alone is unlikely to capture the clinical relevance of SP morphology. The study demonstrates considerable variability in angulation, segmentation (Langlais classification), and calcification patterns. While the absence of clinical correlation precludes symptom association, these parameters may plausibly influence spatial relationships with adjacent neurovascular structures [5, 20]. However, these parameters should be interpreted as descriptive radiographic coordinates rather than clinically validated diagnostic variables.

Future diagnostic refinement should therefore move beyond single-parameter linear thresholds and incorporate multidimensional assessment, potentially integrating three-dimensional orientation, spatial proximity to vascular and neural structures, angulation vectors, degree and pattern of ossification and demographic stratification. Such an approach may provide a more physiologically grounded framework for interpreting SP morphology.

Recent studies utilizing three-dimensional imaging have emphasised the importance of spatial relationships between the SP and adjacent vascular structures in the pathophysiology of Eagle Syndrome [21–24].

The exclusive use of panoramic radiography constitutes both a limitation and a deliberate focus. Panoramic imaging remains the most widely utilised first-line modality in dental and maxillofacial practice due to its accessibility, relatively low radiation exposure, and ability to visualize bilateral craniofacial structures within a single examination [6]. Most incidental SP findings are initially detected on PRs, not CBCT. Therefore, understanding the variability observable under standardised panoramic conditions is clinically relevant for real-world decision-making. However, panoramic radiography is inherently susceptible to projection-related distortion and magnification effects, particularly in the horizontal plane, and lacks depth information. These factors limit anatomical precision and represent an important methodological constraint when performing two-dimensional morphometric assessments. Absolute values derived from PR should therefore not be interpreted as definitive anatomical measurements but rather as modality-specific representations [6, 9].

The study intentionally preserves internal consistency by utilizing a single imaging system and standardised protocol, which reduces variability related to acquisition conditions. While external generalizability of numeric values may be constrained, intra-cohort comparisons remain methodologically valid.

The findings caution against equating radiographic elongation with clinical disease. Given the high prevalence of apparent elongation within a non-symptom-selected population, indiscriminate referral for advanced imaging based solely on linear measurements may contribute to overdiagnosis and unnecessary radiation exposure [5, 8]. A stepwise diagnostic approach incorporating clinical symptoms, physical examination, and selective three-dimensional imaging appears more rational than reliance on isolated length thresholds [20]. Importantly, this study does not dispute the existence of Eagle syndrome.

In the present non-symptom-selected cohort, morphological elongation on panoramic imaging alone did not allow any conclusion regarding the presence or absence of Eagle syndrome. Accordingly, panoramic radiographic elongation should not be interpreted in isolation from clinical findings and, where indicated, three-dimensional imaging.

The broader implication of these findings extends beyond SP morphology. They illustrate a common diagnostic challenge in radiology: the tendency to transform continuous anatomical variation into categorical pathology based on simplified numerical criteria.

Moving forward, diagnostic frameworks for ES may benefit from demographic-specific reference intervals, modality-adjusted interpretative ranges and integration of clinical and radiological parameters. Such a paradigm shift would align interpretation more closely with biological variability and reduce the risk of misclassification.

The retrospective design precludes symptom correlation and prognostic evaluation. Absence of CBCT validation limits anatomical precision. Furthermore, lack of cross-device calibration restricts transferability of absolute values. These limitations are acknowledged and define the scope of inference. Additionally, based on the estimated minimal detectable difference of approximately 2.5 mm for SP length between age groups, smaller anatomical variations may not have been reliably detectable within the present study design. Furthermore, bilateral measurements were analysed as independent observations, although measurements within the same individual may be correlated. While this approach allowed for detailed side-specific descriptive analysis, it may introduce statistical dependency that was not formally accounted for. In addition, no multivariate analyses were performed to adjust for potential confounding effects of age and sex. Given the descriptive nature of the study, analyses were limited to predefined group comparisons.

In summary, this study provides descriptive data on demographic variation of SP morphology on standardised PRs in a non-symptom-selected cohort. The findings indicate that linear measurements and calcification patterns vary with age and sex and that values above commonly cited thresholds are not uncommon in routine panoramic imaging.

While three-dimensional imaging provides improved anatomical detail, it is not routinely required and may be considered in selected clinical scenarios. The present findings should be interpreted within the limitations of panoramic imaging and without direct clinical correlation.
