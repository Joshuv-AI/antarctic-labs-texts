Identification of Key Amino Acid Residues in the Adenylation Domains of Polymyxin Synthetase Responsible for l‑Threonine Adenylation in Paenibacillus polymyxa NBRC3020
Authors: Wataru Ando, Yuya Uemura, Mai Nemoto, Taichi Mano, Minjae Lee, Satoshi Yuzawa, Toshihisa Mizuno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) research, expanding our understanding of the relationship between active-site amino acid residues in adenylation (A) domains and their adenylation activity and substrate specificity remains essential, as detailed biochemical characterization is still limited. In this study, we investigated two l-threonine (l-Thr)-specific A domains, A2 and A10, from the NRPS responsible for polymyxin D biosynthesis in Paenibacillus polymyxa NBRC 3020. To preserve enzymatic functionality, these domains were heterologously expressed in Escherichia coli BAP-1 as larger modules containing adjacent functional domains. Adenylation activity was evaluated indirectly by quantifying pyrophosphate generated during the reaction. Both modules not only exhibited the highest activity toward l-Thr but also showed measurable activity toward structurally related amino acids, including l-serine (l-Ser), l-allo-threonine (l-allo-Thr), and d-alanine (d-Ala), indicating relatively broad substrate specificity. Docking simulations based on AlphaFold3-predicted structures identified three residues within the 10-amino-acid Stachelhaus codePhe, Met, and Hisas key determinants of l-Thr side-chain recognition. Alanine substitution of these residues caused marked reductions in adenylation activity, confirming their essential roles experimentally. Comparative analysis of A domains with strict versus broader l-Thr specificity revealed that, although first-shell residues were highly conserved, distinct differences were present in second-shell residues influencing active-site geometry. Guided by these observations, introduction of the corresponding double mutation into A10 enhanced its selectivity for l-Thr over l-Ser. These findings provide new insights into the molecular basis of substrate recognition in NRPS A domains.

Introduction

Nonribosomal peptides (NRPs) represent a structurally diverse class of natural products with a wide range of biological activities, including antibiotic, antifungal, and immunosuppressive effects. These compounds are biosynthesized by large, multimodular enzyme complexes known as nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs), which assemble peptide scaffolds in a ribosome-independent, template-driven manner. The modular organization of NRPSs enables the incorporation of both proteinogenic and nonproteinogenic amino acids, thereby generating the remarkable structural diversity of NRPs. Within each module, the adenylation (A) domain determines substrate specificity by selectively recognizing an amino acid substrate, catalyzing its ATP-dependent activation, and transferring the resulting aminoacyl adenylate to the 4′-phosphopantetheinyl arm of the thiolation (T), or peptidyl carrier protein, domain. Because substrate selection occurs at this initial activation step, the A domain is regarded as the primary determinant of substrate specificity in NRPS assembly lines.

Early studies identified a set of ten specificity-conferring residues within A domains, commonly referred to as the Stachelhaus code, enabling prediction of substrate preference from amino acid sequences. However, subsequent biochemical and structural studies have demonstrated that this code alone cannot fully explain substrate specificity, and numerous discrepancies have been reported between predicted and experimentally determined substrate preferences. These findings indicate that substrate recognition involves additional structural determinants that remain incompletely understood.

One particularly challenging issue is how A domains discriminate between structurally similar amino acids. l-Serine (l-Ser) and l-threonine (l-Thr), for example, differ only by a single methyl group at the β-position, yet many NRPSs selectively incorporate one amino acid while excluding the other. Previous studies have identified A domains exhibiting strict specificity toward either l-Ser or l-Thr in SyrB1A, PvdD1A, and MbtB as well as enzymes capable of activating both substrates in Thr1A. Although structural and biochemical analyses have suggested that subtle differences in the substrate-binding pocket contribute to this selectivity, the molecular basis for precise discrimination between l-Thr and closely related amino acids remains largely unresolved. Polymyxin D synthetase from Paenibacillus polymyxa NBRC3020 provides an attractive model for investigating this question because its second and tenth adenylation domains (A2 and A10) are both responsible for incorporating l-Thr into the peptide product. However, the substrate selectivity of these domains has not been characterized in detail, and the structural features responsible for l-Thr recognition remain unknown.

In this study, we expressed recombinant A2 and A10 proteins in Escherichia coli BAP-1 and systematically characterized their substrate specificities using adenylation assays. We further compared their amino acid sequences and AlphaFold3-predicted structures with those of previously reported l-Thr-specific A domains to identify structural features associated with substrate discrimination. This study provides new insights into the molecular basis of l-Thr recognition in NRPS adenylation domains and contributes to a better understanding of substrate specificity with potential implications for future NRPS engineering and rational enzyme design.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Restriction enzymes, alkaline phosphatase, TaKaRa Ex-Premier DNA Polymerase, and In-Fusion Snap Assembly Master Mix were purchased from Takara Bio Inc. (Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). Tris­(hydroxymethyl) aminomethane (Tris, molecular biology grade), isopropyl-β- d -thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, ≥99% purity), agar, Agarose ME, Ampicillin Sodium, and lysogeny broth medium (Lennox) were purchased from Wako Chemicals (Osaka, Japan). Tryptic Soy Broth was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Oligo DNAs for genetic mutations and cloning by PCR were purchased from Eurofins Genomics Co, Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). BAP-1 E. coli strain was kindly gifted from B. A. Pfeifer. Paenibacillus (P.) polymyxa NBRC3020 strain was supplied from National Institute of Technology and Evaluation (NITE) (Tokyo, Japan). ATP, amino acids used as substrates, and other biochemical reagents were of analytical grade or higher and were purchased from Wako Chemicals (Osaka, Japan). Ni-NTA resin (His-bind resin) was purchased from Novagen (Madison, WI, USA). Other chemicals were used without further purification.

Cloning and Insertion of Genes for Modules 2 and 10 in the pET-21d Expression Vector

The genes for each module were cloned using Direct PCR (TaKaRa Ex-Premier DNA Polymerase, Takara Bio Inc. Shiga, Japan) from the P. polymyxa NBRC3020 strain, with primers designed based on the genomic information on modules 2 and 10 from the P. polymyxa E681 strain. To introduce each gene into the multicloning site of the pET-21d vector, primers were designed with an additional 20 bp homologous to the pET-21d vector for Gibson assembly. The pET-21d vector was linearized using the NcoI restriction enzyme (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan), and each gene, flanked by additional Gibson assembly sequences at both ends, was inserted using Gibson assembly (In-Fusion Snap Assembly Master Mix, Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan). The assembled constructs were transformed into E. coli JM109 cells, and colonies containing the introduced genes were selected through colony screening. Expression vectors carrying each gene were then prepared, and DNA sequencing analysis was performed using a total of five primers to verify the DNA sequences of each gene.

Protein Expression and Purification of Modules 2 and 10

Expression vectors containing each module gene were introduced into chemically competent E. coli BAP1 cells. By utilizing Gibson assembly, the genes for each module were introduced into the multiple cloning site of the pET-21d­(+) vector (Novagen, Madison, WI, USA). As a result, each module protein was expressed with a His6-tag sequence at the C-terminal side. After preincubation in LB Lennox medium at 37 °C, protein expression was induced by adding IPTG (≥99% purity, Wako Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) to a final concentration of 0.1 mM and incubating at 20 °C for 18 h. The bacterial cells were harvested and resuspended in lysis buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4; 0.1 mM NaCl; 10% glycerol by weight). The cells were then lysed by ultrasonication, and the target proteins were extracted into the supernatant. This extract was subjected to affinity chromatography using Ni-NTA resin (His-bind resin, Novagen, Madison, WI, USA) to isolate and purify the target module proteins. After binding to the resin, nonspecifically bound components were eluted using 10 vol. of the binding buffer (5 mM imidazole, 0.5 M NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9)) and 6 vol. of the wash buffer (60 mM imidazole, 0.5 M NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9)). The target protein was then eluted with 2 vol. of 0.1X, 0.2X, 0.3X, and 0.5X of the elution buffers (1 M imidazole, 0.5 M NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9)), respectively, and the purity of each fraction was analyzed by 15% SDS-PAGE. Fractions containing the purified target protein were concentrated using ultrafiltration (Amicon Ultra 50 kDa centrifugal filter units, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The purified protein was stored at 4 °C in the buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4; 0.1 mM NaCl; 10% glycerol by weight). Activity assays were performed within 3 days of isolation and purification. The purity of each sample, adequate for enzyme activity evaluation, was confirmed by 15% SDS-PAGE analysis. The calculated purities of Module 2 and Module 10 were 90% and 90%, respectively. Although His-tag affinity purification did not completely eliminate nonspecifically bound protein fractions, the resulting purity was considered adequate for the subsequent adenylation activity assays. The yields of recombinant Module 2 and Module 10 proteins were 10.3 ± 0.5 mg and 56 ± 11 mg per liter of culture medium, respectively.

Evaluation Activity of Adenylation for Substrate Amino Acids by the A Domain in the Module

The adenylation activity of the A domains contained in each module was measured with reference to the methods reported by Xia et al. and Li et al. The reaction mixture consisted of 50 μL of reaction buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 5 mM MgCl2), to which 12.5 μL of 32 mM ATP aqueous solution (≥99% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 13.2 μL of 300 mM NH2OH (aq.), and 12.5 μL of a 32 mM aqueous solution of l-Thr, l-Ser, l-allo-Thr, or other substrate amino acids (Wako Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) were added, followed by preincubation at 25 °C. The enzymatic reaction was initiated by adding 11.8 μL of an 84.7 μM solution of module 2 or module 10, respectively, to achieve a final protein concentration of 10 μM and was allowed to proceed at 25 °C for 1 h. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 1 mL of Solution 1 (20 mM Na2MoO4, 0.6 M HCl, in CH3CN), allowing the produced pyrophosphate (PPi) to form a complex with a molybdate ion. Subsequently, 20 μL of Solution 2 (50 mM bis­(triphenylphosphoranylidene)­ammonium chloride (BTPPACl) in CH3CN) was added to precipitate the PPi–molybdate complex, and the resulting yellow precipitate was collected by centrifugation (16,000 × g, 20 min). After dissolving the yellow precipitate in 200 μL of CH3CN, 20 μL of Solution 3 (0.5 M ascorbic acid, 2 M HCl, in CH3CN) was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 20 min to reduce this complex. The concentration of produced PPi was determined by measuring the absorbance of this solution at 620 nm. The enzymatic parameters (k cat and K m) were determined by conducting the reaction at varying concentrations of l-Thr or a reference substrate amino acid (final concentrations of 0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 3.0, and 5.0 mM), calculating the initial rate of PPi production at each concentration, and analyzing the resulting data using a Lineweaver–Burk plot.

Structural Prediction and Docking Simulation of Modules 2 and 10 with ATP and l-Thr

The tertiary structure of each module, which contains ATP and Mg2+, was predicted using the AlphaFold Server based on the amino acid sequence determined by DNA sequencing. To discover the affinity between each substrate amino acid and the enzymes, the basic docking simulation was implemented using AutoDock Vina. Docking simulations were conducted by simultaneously docking the ligand and one explicit water molecule into the enzyme binding site. The structural data of the l-Thr (THR), l-Ser (SER), l-allo-THR (ALO), d-Ala (DAL), l-Ala (ALA), and l-Cys (CYS) were obtained from RCSB, and the input file for the water molecule was generated using a theoretically optimized geometry. Moreover, out of the module of an enzyme, only the adenylation domain was used for docking simulation with each substrate amino acid. The ATP–Mg2+–enzyme complex, predicted using the AlphaFold Server, was used for the docking simulation with each substrate amino acid. All docking simulations were performed using a grid box with dimensions set to x = 13, y = 13, and z = 13, centered at x = −0.986, y = 2.616, and z = 0.239 for Module 2, and at x = −1.687, y = 3.138, and z = 1.326 for Module 10. Then, the hydrogen bonds between l-DAB, ATP, and enzymes were confirmed from the angle and distance of the atoms.

Evaluation of Adenylation Activities of Ala Mutants

Based on the predicted structures obtained from docking simulations with l-Thr, the following alanine mutants of A10 in module 10 were prepared. Mutants targeting residues Phe466, Met561, and His570 (F466A, M561A, and H570A), which were predicted to be involved in the recognition of the side-chain methyl and hydroxyl groups of the substrate amino acid l-Thr, as well as a mutant targeting Asp465 (D465A), which was anticipated to participate in the recognition of the α-amino group of l-Thr, were constructed. The corresponding mutations were introduced into the gene encoding A10, and the mutated genes were incorporated into pET-21d using the Gibson assembly method to construct expression vectors. These expression vectors were then transformed into E. coli BAP-1 cells, and the mutant proteins were obtained through IPTG-induced protein expression. After purification, the adenylation activities of each mutant were evaluated using the same method employed for the A2 and A10 activity assays in the evaluation of adenylation activity.

Evaluation of Adenylation Activity of A10 Mutants with Substitutions in the Second-Coordination Sphere Residues

The A10 mutants F488Y, N508S, and F488Y/N508S, carrying substitutions in second-sphere amino acid residues predicted by docking simulations to be involved in l-Thr recognition, were prepared by introducing the corresponding mutations into the gene and incorporating the mutated genes into pET-21d using the Gibson assembly method to construct expression vectors. These expression vectors were subsequently transformed into E. coli BAP-1, and protein expression was induced to obtain the mutant proteins. After purification, the adenylation activities of each mutant were evaluated by using the same method employed for the A2 and A10 activity assays.

Results

Gene Cloning and Structural Features of Modules 2 and 10, Including the Adenylation Domain for l-Thr from the Genome DNA of the P. polymyxa NBRC3020 Strain

In our previous study, we characterized the adenylation (A) domains responsible for l-2,4-diaminobutyric acid (l-Dab) adenylation in NRPSs involved in polymyxin D biosynthesis in P. polymyxa NBRC3020. Specifically, A1, A5, A8, and A9, corresponding to modules 1, 5, 8, and 9 and responsible for l-Dab incorporation, were heterologously expressed as recombinant proteins and evaluated up to the adenylation step, enabling determination of their kinetic parameters and identification of key amino acid residues involved in substrate recognition and catalysis. Since polymyxin D additionally contains l-Thr, d-Leu, and d-Ser residues, the present study focused on the A domains responsible for l-Thr adenylation, A2 and A10. Specifically, we analyzed their amino acid sequences, evaluated their adenylation activities, and identified the key residues within their catalytic sites. For A10, we previously elucidated its amino acid sequence and partially characterized its adenylation selectivity toward l-Thr and structurally related amino acids through comparison with A domains exhibiting l-Dab adenylation activity. In the present study, we further extended this analysis by comparing A10 with A2 and previously reported l-Thr-activating A domains in terms of amino acid sequence, structural characteristics, substrate selectivity, and enzymatic parameters, with the aim of gaining deeper insight into the determinants of l-Thr recognition. Although several l-Thr-specific A domains have been identified, the molecular basis by which they discriminate l-Thr from structurally related amino acids, such as l-Ser, remains unclear. One possible reason for this limited understanding is the scarcity of studies reporting the successful recombinant expression of l-Thr-specific A domains together with detailed biochemical characterization including kinetic analyses of their adenylation activities.

The A2 and A10 domains from the NRPS of Paenibacillus (P.) polymyxa NBRC 3020 could not be obtained as water-soluble proteins when expressed individually in E. coli. Therefore, following the strategy successfully applied in our previous study on l-Dab-specific A domains, A2 and A10 were expressed at the module level together with their flanking domains. For A2, an A2–T2–C2 construct comprising the adjacent T and C domains was designed and designated as module 2. In contrast, A10 was expressed as a COM–A10–T10–TE construct containing the COM, T, and TE domains, designated as module 10. Notably, module 10 corresponds to the full-length protein encoded by the pmxB gene.

Each gene fragment was amplified from the genomic DNA of P. polymyxa NBRC 3020 by PCR and cloned into the pET-21d expression vector using Gibson assembly. The amino acid sequences of the resulting modules, as determined by DNA sequencing, are summarized in Figures S1 and S2. On the basis of these sequences, structural models were predicted using AlphaFold 3, and the resulting models are shown in Figure . The predicted structures clearly contained all of the expected domains (A, T, and C domains in module 2; COM, A, T, and TE domains in module 10). Furthermore, the pLDDT confidence scores indicated high reliability for each individual domain, excluding the interdomain linker regions, thereby supporting the validity of the predicted structural models.

Predicted tertiary structures and pLDDT scores of module 2 (a) and module 10 (b), generated using AlphaFold 3. pLDDT values are color-coded as follows: blue, >90; cyan, 70–90; yellow, 50–70; and orange, <50. Structures were rendered using PyMOL (Schrödinger, version 2.5.0). Domain colors are assigned as follows: A_core, green; A_sub, dark green; T, cyan; C, yellow; COM, pink; and TE, blue.

The A2 and A10 domains exhibit a high degree of sequence identity (approximately 91.5%; Figure a), and strong conservation is observed among the residues constituting their predicted active sites based on the AlphaFold 3 models (Figure b). In A domains, ATP and the substrate amino acid are simultaneously accommodated within the active site. The region highlighted in light blue in Figure b represents the predicted cavity that accommodates ATP and l-Thr. Residues involved in recognition of the α-amino and α-carboxyl groups of the substrate are known to be broadly conserved across most A domains. These residues are also present in the predicted active sites of A2 and A10: Asp225 in A2 and Asp465 in A10 are predicted to recognize the α-amino group, whereas Lys517 in A2 and Lys757 in A10 are likely responsible for recognition of the α-carboxyl group (Figure b, orange and green). When the structures of module 2 and module 10 were predicted by AlphaFold 3 in the absence of ATP, Mg2+, and l-Thr, the Lys residues located in the A-sub domain (Lys517 in A2 and Lys757 in A10) were predicted to adopt conformations oriented away from the l-Thr-binding site (Figure S3b,d). In contrast, when structural predictions were performed in the presence of ATP and Mg2+ but in the absence of l-Thr, these Lys residues (Lys517 in A2 and Lys757 in A10) were predicted to be appropriately positioned within the l-Thr-binding site in both module 2 and module 10 (Figure S3a,c). These results suggest that ATP and Mg2+ binding is important for the formation of the l-Thr-binding site.

In contrast, residues potentially involved in recognition of the l-Thr side chain were uniquely identified in A2 and A10. Specifically, Met321 in A2 (corresponding to Met561 in A10), His330 in A2 (His570 in A10), and Phe226 in A2 (Phe466 in A10) are predicted to contribute to l-Thr side-chain recognition, consistent with the Stachelhaus code (Figure b, blue). In the corresponding l-Dab-specific A domains (A1, A5, A8, and A9) from P. polymyxa NBRC 3020, these residues are replaced by Ala, Asp, and Glu, respectively. These observations further support the hypothesis that Met321/Met561, His330/His570, and Phe226/Phe466 in A2 and A10 play key roles in the specific recognition of l-Thr.

Protein Expression and Isolation of Each Module

Each module protein was expressed in E. coli BAP-1 cells transformed with an expression vector harboring the corresponding gene. The E. coli BAP-1 strain constitutively expresses sfp, a 4′-phosphopantetheinyl transferase that enables post-translational attachment of the 4′-phosphopantetheine (PPant) group to the T domain. The PPant moiety, derived from coenzyme A, is covalently attached to a conserved serine residue in the T domain, allowing isolation of the module proteins in their holo forms. Following preincubation, protein expression was induced with 0.1 mM IPTG at 20 °C for 16 h. Under these low-temperature conditions, all module proteins were obtained in good yields as soluble proteins. Cells were lysed by sonication, and the proteins were purified by Ni–NTA affinity chromatography, yielding sufficient purity and quantity for further analysis (Figure ). Module 2, composed of the A2–T2–C2 domains, was predicted to have a molecular weight of 119.9 kDa, whereas module 10, consisting of the COM–A10–T10–TE domains, was estimated to have a molecular weight of 124.6 kDa. In both cases, bands corresponding to the expected molecular weights were confirmed by 15% SDS–PAGE (Figure ). The purified proteins were subsequently buffer-exchanged and concentrated by ultrafiltration prior to evaluation of their adenylation activities.

Characterization of Adenylation Activities of the A Domains in Isolated Modules 2 and 10

To characterize the adenylation activity of the l-Thr-activating A domains A2 and A10 contained within modules 2 and 10, respectively, we evaluated their substrate specificity and apparent kinetic parameters (k cat, K m, and k cat/K m). Adenylation activity was quantified using a colorimetric assay for released pyrophosphate (PPi), following the method reported by Townsend et al. In this assay, PPi released as a byproduct of aminoacyl-AMP formation is detected through formation of an 18-molybdopyrophosphate complex ([(P2O7)­Mo18O54]4–). This complex is subsequently reduced by ascorbic acid to yield a blue-colored solution, enabling spectrophotometric quantification of PPi. We first assessed the substrate specificity of the adenylation reaction toward the putative substrate l-Thr (Figure ). The reaction was carried out in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) containing 10 μM module 2 or module 10, 4 mM amino acid, 5 mM ATP, 10 mM MgCl2, and 40 mM hydroxylamine (NH2OH). Hydroxylamine was included to remove the amino acid loaded onto the T domain, thereby promoting enzymatic turnover. Reactions were initiated by addition of the enzyme and incubated at 25 °C for 1 h, followed by termination through addition of an acidic molybdate solution, which simultaneously enabled colorimetric quantification of PPi. As expected, both modules 2 and 10 exhibited the highest adenylation activity toward l-Thr, confirming that l-Thr is the preferred substrate for both A2 and A10 (Figure ).

However, A2 in module 2 also showed appreciable activity toward l-allo-Thr, the C3 epimer of l-Thr, as well as l-Ser, both of which bear a hydroxyl group, with activities clearly above the background level (AA(−), ATP only, no amino acid). The background PPi release is likely attributable to intrinsic ATP hydrolysis by the A domain in the absence of substrate amino acid. This tendency was even more pronounced for A10 in module 10, which additionally exhibited activity toward d-Ala. Notably, the activity toward l-Ser was relatively high; the ratio of PPi production (l-Thr/l-Ser) after incubation at 25 °C for 1 h was 1.61 for A2 and 1.04 for A10. These results indicate that the substrate selectivity of both A domains for l-Thr is relatively low.

To further characterize this broad substrate specificity, apparent kinetic parameters (k cat, K m, and k cat/K m) were determined for the three substrates exhibiting the highest PPi production in the specificity assay shown in Figure (module 2: l-Thr, l-Ser, and l-allo-Thr; module 10: l-Thr, l-Ser, and d-Ala). Adenylation reactions were performed using several different initial substrate concentrations for each amino acid. The kinetic parameters were obtained by applying the experimentally determined initial velocities and substrate concentrations to Lineweaver–Burk plot analysis (Figure S4). The resulting values are summarized in Table . Consistent with expectations, A2 in module 2 showed the highest preference for l-Thr across all kinetic parameters (k cat, K m, and k cat/K m). For example, the ratio of the k cat/K m values for l-Thr and l-Ser was approximately 5.0. In contrast, although A10 in module 10 exhibited the highest catalytic efficiency (k cat/K m) toward l-Thr, comparable values were observed for k cat with l-Ser and for K m with d-Ala. For example, the ratio of the k cat/K m values for l-Thr and l-Ser was approximately 1.4. This trend was consistent with the results of the substrate specificity assay (Figure ) and further supports the conclusion that both A2 and A10 exhibit broad substrate tolerance toward amino acids structurally similar to l-Thr, particularly A10.

Previous studies have reported several l-Thr-activating A domains, including Thr-1, derived from a free-standing 4-chlorothreonine NRPS-like enzyme from Streptomyces sp. OH-5093 and characterized by Scaglione et al.; SyrB1A, a component of the syringomycin E NRPS SyrB2 from Pseudomonas syringae pv syringae B301D, reported by Vaillancourt et al.; PvdD1A, derived from the pyoverdine NRPS of Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1, reported by Ackerley et al.; and MbtB, a component of the mycobactin NRPS in Mycobacterium tuberculosis mc2 6020. Interestingly, SyrB1A, PvdD1A, and MbtB exhibit high substrate selectivity for l-Thr over l-Ser, in contrast to A2 and A10 examined in the present study. Although detailed kinetic parameters have been reported only for SyrB1A, the ratio of its k cat/K m values for l-Thr relative to l-Ser was reported to be 58.4. By contrast, Thr-1 exhibits broad adenylation activity not only toward l-Thr but also toward d-allo-Thr, l-Ser, and d-Thr, indicating relatively low substrate specificity similar to that of A2 and A10 from the polymyxin D NRPS. Consistent with this broader substrate tolerance, the ratio of the k cat/K m values for l-Thr and l-Ser in Thr-1 was approximately 5.4.

Impacts of Alanine Mutation for the Predicted Key Residues at the Active Site on the Adenylation Activity for l-Threonine and Other Amino Acids

Both A2 and A10 exhibited adenylation activity not only toward l-Thr but also toward l-Ser and l-allo-Thr or d-Ala, indicating that these domains do not possess strict substrate selectivity. However, no detectable adenylation activity was observed for the other tested amino acids. To identify the key residues responsible for conferring activity toward l-Thr and structurally related substrates, alanine-substituted mutants were constructed, and their effects on adenylation activity were evaluated.

First, docking simulations of l-Thr with A2 and A10 were performed using AutoDock Vina based on structural models predicted by AlphaFold 3. The results are shown in Figure a,b. The simulations suggested that the Asp residues (D225 in A2 and D465 in A10), previously identified as characteristic active-site residues in A2 and A10 (Figure b), are involved in recognition of the backbone amino group of l-Thr. In addition, Phe, Met, and His residues (F226, M321, and H330 in A2; F466, M561, and H570 in A10; blue in Figure a,b) were predicted to participate in recognition of the side-chain hydroxyl group of l-Thr through interactions mediated by coordinated water molecules (red in Figure a,b). To date, among NRPS A domains involved in l-Thr adenylation, Thr-1 is the only domain for which a detailed three-dimensional structure has been determined. This structural study revealed that one water molecule participates in the recognition of the side-chain hydroxyl group of l-Thr. Therefore, we performed AutoDock Vina docking simulations using l-Thr together with one water molecule placed within the grid box. Interestingly, when two water molecules were introduced into the grid box for docking simulations, no converged structure containing a second water molecule with sufficient binding stability was obtained. In contrast, when docking simulations were performed without including any water molecules in the grid box, converged A domain–l-Thr complex structures were also obtained; however, the docking model containing one coordinated water molecule exhibited a lower predicted binding free energy (ΔG), suggesting greater thermodynamic stability (Figure S5). These results suggest that, similar to Thr-1, the involvement of one water molecule in recognition of the l-Thr side-chain hydroxyl group is a plausible feature of the A2 and A10 domains investigated in this study.

On the basis of these findings, alanine-substituted mutants targeting these residues (M561A, H570A, and F466A for residues involved in l-Thr side-chain recognition, and D465A for recognition of the backbone amino group) were constructed for A10 in module 10. The corresponding mutant genes were introduced into the pET-21d vector using Gibson assembly, and each module 10 variant carrying the alanine substitutions was expressed in E. coli BAP-1. The resulting adenylation activity profiles toward each substrate amino acid are summarized in Figure c. All mutants (M561A, H570A, F466A, and D465A) exhibited marked reductions in adenylation activity, particularly toward l-Thr, experimentally confirming that these residues serve as key determinants of l-Thr adenylation. In particular, the pronounced decrease in adenylation activity caused by alanine substitution of the Met residue (M561A), which was predicted to contribute substantially to coordination of a water molecule, supports the proposed model in which the coordinated water molecule plays an important role in recognition of the side-chain hydroxyl group of l-Thr.

We first examined residues that directly interact with the substrate l-Thr, including the Asp and Lys residues responsible for recognition of the backbone α-amino and α-carboxyl groups, as well as the Phe, His, and Met residues identified in this study as key determinants of l-Thr side-chain recognition. These residues were found to be highly conserved in both the strictly selective SyrB1_A and the less selective A2, A10, and Thr-1. These findings suggest that amino acid residues directly interacting with l-Thrthe so-called first coordination spheredo not substantially contribute to the strict discrimination between l-Thr and structurally similar substrate amino acids.

We therefore extended our structural comparison to residues in the second coordination sphere, which interact with the substrate-recognizing residues. Notably, residues positioned adjacent to the first sphere Phe, Met, and His residues were found to differ between the strictly selective and low-selective A domains: in A2, A10, and Thr-1, these positions are occupied by Phe and Asn, whereas in SyrB1_A, they are substituted by Tyr and Ser, respectively. Specifically, the Tyr residue is positioned to interact with the first sphere His and Met residues, while the Ser residue interacts with a conserved Trp residue next to the one of the key residues, Phe. This Trp residue is also one of the amino acid residues included in “Stachelhaus code” for l-Thr-selective A domains (Asp, Phe, Trp, Asn, Ile, Gly, Met, Val, His, Lys) and is conserved not only in A2, A10, and Thr-1 but also in SyrB1_A.

To evaluate the contribution of these second-sphere residues to l-Thr/l-Ser selectivity, we generated A10 variants in which the second-sphere Phe (F488) and Asn (N508) residues were individually or simultaneously mutated to Tyr and Ser, respectively. The corresponding mutant genes (F488Y, N508S, and F488Y/N508S) were expressed in E. coli BAP-1, and their adenylation activities were evaluated. As shown in Figure , neither the single Phe-to-Tyr nor the single Asn-to-Ser substitution improved l-Thr/l-Ser selectivity (Figure a,b). In contrast, the double mutant (F488Y/N508S), containing both substitutions, exhibited enhanced selectivity for l-Thr over l-Ser (Figure c). These results suggest that the second-sphere Tyr and Ser residues play critical roles in enhancing substrate discrimination against l-Thr analogs in l-Thr-specific A domains (Figure d). Docking simulations suggested that the benzene ring of the Phe side chain, one of the key first-sphere residues, is positioned to enable a CH−π interaction with the side-chain methyl group of l-Thr (Figure a,b). It was anticipated that the identity of the second-sphere residue influencing the conformation of this Phe would have a substantial impact on l-Thr-specific substrate recognition, particularly whether this position is occupied by Asn or Ser. Although further validation using additional low-l-Thr/l-Ser-selectivity A-domain mutants will be necessary, the importance of these second-sphere residues represents a novel finding that has not previously been recognized as part of the canonical “Stachelhaus code” governing l-Thr recognition in NRPS A domains.

Discussion

The substrate specificity of NRPS adenylation (A) domains is a key determinant of nonribosomal peptide diversity, and understanding the molecular basis underlying substrate discrimination is essential for both evolutionary studies and the rational engineering of NRPS systems. Among l-threonine (l-Thr)-activating A domains, both highly selective domains that preferentially recognize l-Thr and relatively broad-specificity domains that also activate structurally related amino acids, such as l-serine (l-Ser) and l-allothreonine, have been reported. In this study, A2 and A10 from the polymyxin D NRPS of P. polymyxa NBRC3020 were found to belong to the latter category, exhibiting relatively broad substrate tolerance while maintaining a preference for l-Thr.

Previous studies have identified several l-Thr-selective A domains, including SyrB1A, PvdD1A, and MbtB, which exhibit strong discrimination against l-serine. In contrast, Thr-1, an NRPS-like enzyme involved in 4-chlorothreonine biosynthesis, displays broader substrate tolerance similar to that of A2 and A10. These differences suggest that l-Thr recognition by NRPS A domains is not governed by a single conserved mechanism but rather by multiple layers of molecular determinants that have evolved to achieve different levels of substrate selectivity.

In this study, comparative structural analyses between highly selective and broad-specificity l-Thr-activating A domains revealed that residues directly interacting with l-Thr (first coordination sphere) are highly conserved among these domains. This observation suggests that differences in substrate selectivity cannot be explained solely by residues directly in contact with the substrate. Instead, our mutational analyses indicate that residues located in the second coordination sphere, which influence the conformation and local environment of the substrate binding pocket, contribute to the fine-tuning of substrate selectivity. In particular, the enhanced l-Thr/l-Ser discrimination observed in the A10 variant containing combined second-sphere substitutions highlights the importance of indirect structural regulation in NRPS substrate recognition.

Current prediction of NRPS substrate specificity is largely based on the Stachelhaus code, which consists of ten residues located within the substrate-binding pocket. Although this approach has been highly valuable for predicting substrate preferences, our results suggest that residues outside the canonical specificity code can also contribute to substrate discrimination. Incorporation of such second-sphere interactions into future predictive models may improve the accuracy of substrate specificity prediction and facilitate the rational engineering of A domains with the desired substrate preferences.

Several limitations of this study were considered. The structural interpretations presented here are based on AlphaFold 3-derived models and molecular docking simulations; therefore, further validation using experimental structural approaches, such as X-ray crystallography or cryoelectron microscopy, will be necessary. In addition, molecular dynamics simulations would provide further insight into the dynamic behavior of the active site and the stability of water-mediated interactions proposed in this study. Such analyses may contribute to a more detailed understanding of protein flexibility and the dynamic aspects of substrate recognition in NRPS adenylation domains. Furthermore, only two A domains from the polymyxin D NRPS were experimentally investigated in this study. Characterization of additional l-Thr-activating A domains with diverse substrate selectivity profiles will be required to determine whether the second-sphere mechanism identified here represents a more general feature of NRPS substrate recognition.

Overall, this study expands the current understanding of l-Thr recognition by NRPS A domains by demonstrating that substrate selectivity is influenced not only by direct substrate-contacting residues but also by surrounding residues that modulate the local active site environment. These findings provide new insights into the molecular evolution of NRPS substrate specificity and establish additional structural considerations for the future protein engineering and directed evolution of NRPS systems.
