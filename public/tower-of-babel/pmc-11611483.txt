Evaluation of window trajectory in trans-iliac foraminotomy using an individualised 3D printing guide in cadaveric dogs
Authors: Jongyeol Kim, Jongchan Ko, Yong Yu, Suyoung Heo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ImportanceIn veterinary orthopedics, addressing foramen stenosis in the L7-S1 region in dogs presents significant challenges due to limited access and visualization with conventional surgical techniques.ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate endoscopic foraminotomy through the iliac window, using three-dimensionally (3D) printing guide based on the preoperative plan, in cadaveric dogs.MethodsTrans-iliac foraminotomies were performed on each side of five canine cadavers using and individualized 3D printing guide. The intervertebral foramen (IVF) area was divided into entrance, middle, and exit zones and the trajectory angle was calculated from the computed tomography data.ResultsTrans-iliac foraminotomy with an endoscope through the iliac hole using a 3D printed guide provide good visual access to the L7-S1 region. The variations in IVF in three zones were not significantly different after surgery. The angle of deviation of the guide was not significantly different caudally or ventrally after surgery. The mean trajectory angle after surgery was 14.06 ± 4.61°caudally and 13.07 ± 6.79° ventrally.Conclusions and RelevanceOur results suggest that surgical site were directly visualized during foraminotomy by the lateral approach through the spinal endoscope. However, other procedure including corpectomy and discectomy were not performed in this study.

INTRODUCTION

Canine degenerative lumbosacral stenosis syndrome (DLSS) is characterised by compression of the cauda equina in the lumbosacral (LS) joint and is associated with back pain and neurological dysfunction. DLSS is a multifactorial disorder characterized by varying combinations of Hanse type II intervertebral disc protrusion, hypertrophied soft tissue, articular facet joint osteophytosis, LS spondylosis, and instability [1]. In many cases, the compression results from intervertebral disk protrusion and proliferation of the dorsal interarcuate ligament proliferation, which is effectively managed through dorsal laminectomy [2]. However, Unrecognized compression within the foramen, or nerve compression resulting from tissue proliferation lateral to the foramen, frequently lead to the recurrence of clinical signs in DLSS patients [234].

Several studies have investigated foraminotomy in dogs for decompression of foraminal stenosis at the LS joint. Although dorsal laminectomy, with or without facetectomy, is often combined with foraminotomy, this technique offers limited access to laterally localized foraminal compression and may result in postoperative instability [56]. Lateral approach over the ilium address compressive lesion within the foramen, but both instrumental and visual access is limited by the position of the iliac wing [6]. The other lateral approach utilizing iliac osteotomy provide good postoperative outcome, but this approach requires extensive soft tissue damage [7]. Another described approach in the literature involves drilling a hole in the iliac wing. This approach allows direct exposure of the L7 nerve, foramen and intervertebral disk and endoscopic technique provide enhanced observation. However, additional procedure, such as partial removal of the sacral wing and the extension of surgical window, are often required due to individual anatomical variation and small iliac window [2].

Minimal invasive spine surgery is a procedure that optimizes access to the target area of spine while minimizing iatrogenic trauma, without compromising the surgical goal. This requires modifications in access strategies to target area and specialized retractor and endoscopic or magnification assistance [8]. Although endoscope-assisted foraminotomy has already been performed in the DLSS. The experience with minimal invasive surgery in the LS region remains limited.

In this cadaver study, we used a trans-iliac approach based on minimal invasive spinal surgery in human medicine. We decided the iliac hole size and approach based on the human study [910]. We hypothesised that iliac hole drilling, guided by a three-dimensionally (3D) template based on preoperative computed tomography (CT) imaging, could effectively minimise procedural modifications and iatrogenic trauma while enhancing visual access with the spinal endoscope.

METHODS

Signalment data

This study included four beagles and one mixed cadaver dog. All dogs were euthanised for reasons unrelated to the study (Jeonbuk National University IACUC: NON2023-05-024). Median body weight was 10.1 ± 2.12 kg. Iliac holes were created on each side of five dogs (n = 10) for trans-iliac foraminotomy. Each iliac window size and angle were planned individually using 3D printing technique.

3D guide design for iliac wing drilling

A preoperative CT was performed (1 mm slice thickness; Alexion; TSX-034A; Toshiba Medical System, Japan) to acquire digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) images while the dogs were in ventral recumbency with flexed hips and stifle joints and straightened vertebrae using an immobilisation mattress (VACCUMAT, Genia, France). CT data were imported into the Mimics 17.0 software (Materialise, Belgium). The bone was separated by threshold segmentation, and the stereolithography (STL) file of the bone was exported to computer-aided design software (Fusion 360, Autodesk, USA). The 3D model of the caudal vertebra was repositioned vertically and horizontally along the vertebral axis in fusion 360. This position aligns the disk end plate parallelly, facilitating the precise identification of points along the iliac window track in both lateral and dorsal view. A section analysis was conducted to mark the mid-point of the intervertebral disc region and the foramen of L7-S1. Each mid-point was equidistantly positioned within the rectangle, corresponding to the level of the dorsal aspect of the disc and the lateral aspect of the foramen (Fig. 1). The axis of the line connecting the two mid-points was selected for endoscopic foraminotomy. The guide position in the ilium was repositioned if the osteotomy line was too close to the iliac crest or if the sacral wing was damaged. If the dog had an unfused iliac crest, this was avoided in the guide plate design. The 3D guides consist of two parts, as shown in Fig. 2. Part 1 was a saw guide, a cylindrical form with a 2 mm thickness wall to receive a cylindrical saw. The inner diameter of the saw guide was 0.3 mm larger than that of each cylindrical saw (New Generation Device, USA). Part 2 consisted of a guide plate firmly anchored to the iliac crest to avoid sliding along the slope of the iliac bone surface [11]. A dilator to split the muscles and a hole angle guide to measure the trajectory of the window and the working sleeve were designed. The guide was printed using a resin 3D printer (Pixel One, Zerone, Korea) with a dental surgical guide resin (SG-100, Graphy, Korea).

Surgical procedure

The dogs were placed in ventral recumbency with the hind limbs flexed [26]. and the vertebra extended to the feasible extent because flexion of the LS joint may underestimate the degree of the foraminal stenosis [12]. The position was maintained as consistently as possible with the CT scan to minimize errors in the 3D printed guide based on preoperative imaging. LS angles were calculated to ensure standardization [4]. A skin incision through the cranial iliac crest was extended sufficiently to position the guide. The muscles of the ilium were dissected. Ring retractors provided sufficient space to place the 3D guides properly. The iliac crest was dissected medially and laterally to fit the guide. The position of the guide was confirmed until there was no motion between the guide and the bone surface. The ilium was sawed using a guide, while applying manual pressure caudomedially. The bone fragment was carefully removed from the soft tissue using curved scissors or blades. A K-wire was inserted under the C-arm to target the caudal pedicle of the L7 vertebral or articular process until bone resistance was observed. Serial dilators and operating sleeve were positioned over the wire, while maintaining contact with the bone. A spinal endoscope (Richard Wolf GmbH, Germany; outer diameter 5.9 mm, usable length 207 mm) was introduced through the sleeve. A small part of the iliocostalis and longissimus muscles in the dilator window was removed using a micro-rongeur (Richard Wolf GmbH) to clear the surgical site. Foraminotomy was performed using a high-speed surgical drill with a 2.8 mm round carbide drill bit (Endospine bit, ABLE Inc.) and a micro-rongeur (Richard Wolf GmbH) (Fig. 3), the foramen was enlarged cranially toward caudo-dorsal aspect of the transverse process and dorsally toward ventral aspect of the articular facet [6]. Continuous irrigation through the endoscopic working channel was maintained with a 0.9% NaCl solution via a pressure infusion bag. After foraminotomy, the spinal cord, L7 nerve root, and disc were identified to confirm iatrogenic damage (Fig. 4).

Postoperative CT analysis

Postoperative CT scans were performed in all dogs in the ventral recumbent position, as described preoperatively. Image analysis was performed using commercially available DICOM image processing software (Radiant, 22.1.1, Medixant, Poland) and 3D Mimics (Materialise). The multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) tool was used to realign the image along the axis of the vertebral canal to minimise the error between the 3D STL file and the DICOM image. LS angle was measured in the mid-sagittal plane. The LS angle was measured using two intersecting lines. The first line was drawn from the dorsal margin of the L7 vertebral body to the endplate of L7. A second line was drawn from the endplate of L7 to the mid-dorsal margin of the sacral vertebral body [1314].

The intervertebral foramen (IVF) area was divided into three groups: entrance, middle, and exit. The landmark for dividing the groups on the MPR image was the L7 pedicle. The central portion of the L7 pedicle in the dorsal planar image was considered to middle zone. Based on the classification method used in a previous study, the entrance and exit zones were defined as the medial and lateral cortices of the L7 pedicle in the IVF area, respectively (Fig. 5) [4]. The measurements for each foramen area were performed by a hand-trace tool in the image processing software in para sagittal images. We also measured the guide angle to assess the deviation before and after surgery. Another window angle measurement guide was designed and tightly fitted to the ilium hole to measure the postoperative angle. The window angle guide has a central hole to receive the K-wire, which represents the angle of the axis. This guide was embedded into the iliac hole with a K-wire after surgery. Postoperative CT data were imported into the Mimics software, and the bone was separated. The MPR tool was used to reposition all images parallel to the vertebral canal axis. The caudal and ventral angles of the guide were measured separately using each of the two crossed lines in the dorsal planar and transverse images (Fig. 6), and the line was drawn along the mid-point of the K-wire. The first line of the caudal angle extended from the ilium to the intervertebral space. The second line was parallel to the vertebral axis from the mid-point of the K-wire in the intervertebral space. The ventral angle was calculated using the same method for the transverse image.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 29.0; IBM, USA). All data were measured thrice by a single investigator. Reliability and reproducibility analyses were performed using a two-way randomised intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). A normality test of the IVF area and guide angle was conducted using the Shapiro Wilk test. Differences in the three zones between the preoperative and postoperative IVF areas were calculated. One-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the differences in IVF areas between the preoperative and postoperative foramina. A paired t-test was conducted to evaluate the differences in the guide angle after surgery. Data was reported as mean and SD. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

The ICC for the LS angle, IVF area, and guide angle were obtained, and the correlation coefficients ranged from 0.912 to 0.992 (mean = 0.96740, SD = 0.03). The p value of all data were greater than 0.8, indicating excellent reproducibility.

IVF area variation after surgery

Each foramen was classified into three zones. The median preoperative IVF area of the entrance, middle, and exit zones was 40.09 mm2(range, 33.08–45.75 mm2), 35.02 mm2 (range, 26.12–39.52 mm2) and 40.295 mm2 (range, 29.55–45.65 mm2) respectively. The median postoperative IVF area of the entrance zone, middle zone, and exit zone was 53.12 mm2 (range, 42.23–63.50 mm2), 52.35 mm2 (range, 39.61–61.44 mm2), and 59.63 mm2 (range, 41.46–66.93 mm2) respectively. The preoperative IVF areas in the exit and entrance zones differed significantly from those in the middle zone (95% CI, p = 0.04). The preoperative IVF area was the smallest in the middle zone. The variation in the IVF area was calculated by subtracting the preoperative IVF area from the postoperative IVF area. The mean variation of the entrance, middle, and exit zones was 15.83 ± 5.86 mm2, 20.19 ± 5.52 mm2, and 18.52 ± 4.730 mm2 respectively. Although the mean variation in the entrance zone was the smallest among the three groups, one-way ANOVA revealed that there was no significant difference among the three groups after surgery (p = 0.208).

LS angle

Five LS angles were measured to evaluate the repeatability between the pre and postoperative positions. The median LS angles in the preoperative and postoperative positions were 168.06° (range, 166.1–170.63) and 166.83° (range, 163.63–172.56), respectively. The mean variation in the LS angle between preoperative and postoperative surgery was 3.21° (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.73–5.03).

Guide angle

The median angles of guide design in 3D software (n = 10) were 15.3° (range, 6–20°) caudally and 14.8° (range, 5.3–21.2°) ventrally. The measured median angles after surgery were 14.50° (range, 9.06–21.53°) caudally and 15.18° (range, 1.79–20.20) ventrally. The median angle deviation was 5.02° (range, 0.34–8.3°) caudally and 1.96° (range, 0.33–5.70) ventrally. Paired t-test revealed that the angle deviation before and after surgery was not significantly different. The p values for the caudal and ventral angles were 0.728 and 0.388, respectively.

DISCUSSION

Several studies have been conducted on dogs with foraminal stenosis to decompress the foramen through dorsal laminectomy with an endoscope, a lateral approach with or without ilium osteotomy, and distraction stabilisation techniques [2456715]. Wood et al. [4] reported an endoscopic-assisted foraminotomy with a mini-dorsal laminectomy to overcome postoperative instability from the dorsal laminectomy. In this technique, the entry and middle zones of the IVF were surgically enlarged; however, the lateral aspect of the foramen was challenging to access. This can lead to limitations in the lateralised foraminal stenosis lesion [46]. The distraction stabilisation techniques could improve clinical signs with DLSS; however, other cadaveric studies have reported that the decompression capacity for the cranial foraminal sub-compartment where the L7 nerve root could be entrapped was lower than that of lateral foraminotomy [1215]. From a lateral approach over the ilium, improved access to the lateral aspect of the foramen and disc was achieved. However, the wing of the ilium impeded physical and visual access to the foramen [27].

In humans, the lateral foramen can be approached via the supra-iliac or trans-iliac approach [16]. The recommendation between those approaches could be selected according to the location and distance of the iliac crest to the L5 spine. The trans-iliac approach is recommended for high iliac crest cases in which the iliac crest is above the mid-L5 pedicle on lateral radiography [917]. Although there is a lack of anatomic studies on the relative position between the lumbar spine and ilium in dogs, all dogs in our study met those criteria during the preoperative planning of the trans-iliac hole. However, further studies may be required to evaluate the application of those criteria in dogs because the anatomic difference between the two species that originally humans have wider and more laterally oriented ilium than dogs should be considered [18].

Other studies on foraminotomy in dogs have investigated the trans-iliac approach via iliac drilling or osteotomy. In this approach, additional procedures, such as sacral wing removal and enlargement of the iliac hole or iliac plating could be required [27]. We focused on solving these issues through preoperative planning of the trajectory of the iliac window for foraminotomy. These windows allowed visual access to the foramen, nerve, disc, and articular processes without impeding sacral wing.

In previous trans-iliac studies, the saw size was reported as 18mm. In our study, which involved smaller dogs, it was measured at 11 mm. However, in human studies, a 10 mm diameter for the iliac window was adequately safe. the location and trajectory of the iliac window are vital to access target area for surgical goal in trans-iliac endoscopic surgery. This is because the window in the bony tunnel limits the mobility of the cannula [9]. we designed the trajectory between the mid-point of the foramen to the mid-point of the disc to visualize the overall anatomical structure of the foramen. But, in human studies, the target area was within the disc space to directly visualize the annular tear and remove the disc fragment [9]. In foraminotomy as a stand-alone procedure, the iliac window size may be decreased because the main region of the foramen that decompressed the L7 nerve root was from the caudo-dorsal origin of the transverse process to the ventral aspect of the articular facet in dogs [12]. To further investigate these postulation, clinical studies investigating the trans-iliac window size are needed in large breed dogs.

In 2 out 10 approaches, there was no remaining iliac crest. There were several obstacles to guide positioning. First, entrapment of the soft tissue remaining under the guide could occur if the iliac crest was not sufficiently dissected to fit the guide. Second, the guide couldn’t be sufficiently stabilised during sawing if the degree of exposure of ilium for the guide plate was insufficient. However, the degree of exposure and fit balance remained under investigation [11]. Lastly, errors in the guide design could occur because the degree of remaining bone could not be predicted.

Although the mean increase in the entrance zone was smaller than those in the other zones, the increases in the entrance, middle, and exit zones (entrance zone = 40.5%, middle zone = 59.4%, and exit zone = 47.4%) were not significantly different (p = 0.208). In the previous study with the lateral approach by Godde and Steffen, the exit and middle zone were more easily accessible and enlarged with a successful outcome [6]. Although there was no quantitative comparison with other zones in this study, several factors affecting the accessibility to the entrance zone were different. First, in a previous study, the position of the iliac wing limited visual and instrument access in the dorso-oblique direction. This steep window slope could disrupt the visualization of the entrance zone. Secondly. Using an endoscope enabled structures within the canal to be easily identified in our study. The visual image of the endoscope improved access to the inner part of the foramen in the LS region.

The accuracy of the window trajectory angle was evaluated using the preoperative 3D design and postoperative CT data. Pin interruption from both sides of the ilium can affect the guide angle accuracy. Each pin from both sides of the ilium entered the mid-point of the disc. However, the deviation from the guide angle before and after surgery was not significantly different. The postoperative median angles were a mean of 14.50° caudally and 15.18° ventrally, similar to the angle in the extreme lateral approach for disc herniation in humans [19]. This low ventral angle to the vertebral axis improves the vision of the spinal nerves in humans. However, further studies are required to investigate the angle depending on the location of the lesion in dogs.

The position was ventral recumbency with the hind limbs flexed and the vertebra extended. In other studies utilizing a dorsal approach, the LS junction was flexed to open LS joint. however, this flexion complicates the assessment of foramen stenosis during procedure. In this study, the position was ventral recumbency with the hind limbs flexed as in the other studies [320]. The vertebrae were extended to prevent underestimation of the foramen stenosis. This position protocol was repeatedly maintained during operation and CT scan using a vacuum mattress and evaluated using the LS angle for standardization [4]. The median LS angles in the preoperative and postoperative positions were 168.06° and 166.83°, respectively, which were comparable to the neutral position angle in the other studies [13]. The range of motion was almost 32.8° in the LS joint, and positioning variation could result in dynamic variation of the IVF area [2122].

In a previous study, the LS angle was used to evaluate potential sources of variability. They reported that a variation in the LS angle within 5° was accepting, with a theoretically small change in the foramen [4]. In our study, the variation in the LS angle in the two cases was close to the 5° limit. This degree of variation in position can affect the IVF results. However, other studies reported no significant linear relationship between the LS angle and the IVF area in dogs without clinical signs, which was hypothesised to be due to a normal supportive structure to maintain the L7-S1 foraminal dimensions [14]. Further studies investigating the relationship between the LS position and IVF area are needed to establish standardisation. This study had several limitations. First, the nature of the cadaveric study limited the clinical results and other complications associated with the surgical approach. Second, healthy cadavers were investigated. There was no knowledge of the other obstacles to the approach associated with soft tissue proliferation. Large breeds may have different anatomies from small breeds. The long trajectory length required to target the region in large breeds could make a difference in instruments insertion. Based on preoperative CT data, sacroiliac joint motion could affect the guide angle. The sacroiliac joints were stiff, with much less motion than the adjacent LS and hip joints [23]. However, pressure on the ilium during the operation could interrupt the standardisation of ilium motion, resulting in guide angle deviation.

In conclusion, minimally invasive surgery is gradually replacing conventional surgery to minimise iatrogenic trauma to normal tissues and enable an early return to normal activity. Trans-iliac foraminotomy through a predesignated iliac window in this study could reduce additional procedures. The findings of this study revealed that the trajectory of the window for targeting the lesion was adequate to decompress the foramen stenosis. Further studies are required to evaluate the feasibility of this approach in patients with clinical DLSS.
