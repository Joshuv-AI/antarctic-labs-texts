Metrics vs Surveys: Can Quantitative Measures Replace Human Surveys in Social Robot Navigation? A Correlation Analysis
Stefano Trepella, Mauro Martini, Noé Pérez-Higueras, Andrea Ostuni, Fernando Caballero, Luis Merino, Marcello Chiaberge

Abstract
Social, also called human-aware, navigation is a key challenge for the integration of mobile robots into human environments. The evaluation of such systems is complex, as factors such as comfort, safety, and legibility must be considered. Human-centered assessments, typically conducted through surveys, provide reliable insights but are costly, resource-intensive, and difficult to reproduce or compare across systems. Alternatively, numerical social navigation metrics are easy to compute and facilitate comparisons, yet the community lacks consensus on a standard set of metrics. This work explores the relationship between numerical metrics and human-centered evaluations to identify potential correlations. If specific quantitative measures align with human perceptions, they could serve as standardized evaluation tools, reducing the dependency on surveys. Our results indicate that while current metrics capture some aspects of robot navigation behavior, important subjective factors remain insufficiently represented and new metrics are necessary.

Precisely, these qualitative aspects make the evaluation of human-aware navigation complex. In addition to traditional performance metrics (e.g. time, distance), several quantitative metrics for social navigation have been proposed
[
5

6

. These metrics are easy to compute in controlled experiments and enable efficient comparisons between approaches. However, adopting a wide set of contrastive measures is often misleading in clearly understanding the quality of an experiment. Furthermore, there is no agreement on a set of metrics that reliably capture social aspects such as comfort or legibility. Such aspects are typically assessed through participant surveys, which, while informative, are costly, time-consuming, and challenging to scale.

This work considers the question of whether it is possible to infer the qualitative aspects from quantitative metrics that can be measured in experiments. We approach the problem by analyzing the correlations of quantitative evaluations and qualitative human judgment from surveys in experiments with real robots and human participants. Our goal is to identify quantitative metrics that align with human perceptions, paving the way for simpler, standardized quantitative evaluations that reduce the reliance on surveys. An overall scores comparison is finally carried out to demonstrate that the set of metrics identified through correlation can obtain human-like evaluation trends for social navigation.

The contributions of this work can therefore be summarized as follows:

1.

A novel framework to perform a joint analysis of surveys and quantitative metrics, offering insights on the correlations between them through clustering and statistical tests.

2.

The identification of a subset of quantitative metrics that results most relevant to obtain human-like evaluation trends for social navigation experiments.

3.

A dataset of robot and people trajectories in eight different social scenarios collected through real world experiments with an accurate ground truth and survey evaluation, that can be used for development and benchmarking of future research.

Related Work

In the last decade, several studies have made significant contributions to defining and evaluating social navigation in robotics. The studies of Francis et al.
[

3

[

Classical quantitative benchmarks are usually derived from the analysis of the Proxemics of human-human interaction distances
[

[
9

, allow quantifying the social impact of robot motion using Social Forces and Social Work, considering velocity and directions of agents.

In recent years, considerable effort has been made to enhance classical robotics simulators with improved human navigation capabilities and benchmarks for human-aware navigation. Some interesting examples are HuNavSim
[
10

, SEAN2.0
[
11
]
, SocNavBench
[
12
]
or Arena 4.0
[
13

. These simulators exploit trajectory ground truth and repeatability facilities to compute quantitative metrics for both classical navigation performance and social navigation.

The most recent research promotes the adoption of new complete metrics to incorporate both efficiency, safety, and human perception in unique scores. Among them
[
6
]
,
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
propose a suite of novel metrics that aim to capture human-centric perceptions. The latter evaluates robot behavior from the perspective of human comfort and social acceptability, combining objective measurements with qualitative data based on surveys. This dual perspective allows researchers to better analyze whether conventional metrics completely capture critical aspects of human-robot interaction.
In addition to defining new metrics, a recent study also highlighted the importance of assigning the correct level of complexity to social scenarios
[
16
]
.

In general, the most recent studies highlight a remaining challenge: aligning quantitative benchmarks with more subjective human evaluations of robot navigation in social contexts. This work analyzes the correlation between currently used quantitative metrics and survey-based human assessment and poses the question whether these hidden patterns can be exploited to search for a new complete evaluation protocol that integrate human feedback with algorithmic metrics.

Data collection

III-C

Quantitative and human-centric metrics

The metrics used in this study can be categorized into two distinct types:

itemQuantitative metrics (QM):

a set of eleven numerical metrics used in the literature and compiled in standard social navigation benchmarks, such as HuNavSim
[
10
]
. This QM set is composed of four metrics focused on classic navigation aspects, and seven metrics for social behavior:

•

Time to goal
(
TTG
) [s]

•

Path length
(
PL
) [m]

•

Cumulative heading changes
(
CHC
) [rad]

•

Average robot linear velocity
(
ARV
) [m/s]

•

Social work
(
SW
)

•

Social work per second
(
SW_{s}
)

•

Average minimum distance to closest person
(
AMD
) [m]

•

Proxemics occupancy
: intimate space (
PR_{I}
), personal space (
PR_{PE}
), social space (
PR_{S}
), public space (
PR_{PU}
).

CHC
represents the total angular distance spanned by the robot during the path. The quantity of social work is adopted from the Social Force Model (SFM) of interaction between a crowd of agents proposed in
[
9
]
.
The social work
SW_{s}
is computed at each time step for the robot according to the following expression, considering the velocity module and direction of each agent around the robot:
SW_{s}=W_{r}+\sum_{i}W_{p,i}
, where
W_{r}
is the sum of the modulus of the robot social force (
F_{P}
) and the robot obstacle force (
F_{O}
) along the trajectory according to the SFM, while
W_{p}
is the sum of the modulus of the social forces generated by the robot for each pedestrian
i
along the trajectory in a maximum range of 5 meters. Social work is included both as an average per second and as an overall sum throughout the duration of the experiment.

The proxemics metrics are four complementary percentages that represent the time spent by the robot in a given zone: closest to the person (intimate), close to the person (personal), reasonable distance from the person but still in a range of possible interaction (social), completely away from the agent (public).

itemHuman metrics (HM):

a set of four human-centric qualitative metrics used to rank the experiments in relevant aspects, as proposed in the work of Hirose et al.
[
18
]
. They are obtained through questionnaires sent to participants using the 5-point Likert scale.

•

Unobtrusiveness
: the robot’s ability to move in the environment without being noticed by humans.

•

Friendliness
: the level of social disturbance of the robot in its trajectory.

•

Smoothness
: the robot’s smoothness in its trajectory.

•

Avoidance foresight
: the robot’s ability to adjust its motion in advance to avoid possible future collisions with moving people.

III-D

Survey Questionnaire

The human metrics have been collected through a survey based on real robot experiments conducted in the lab. For each required assessment, the social navigation scenario is described, explaining the robot task, the situation from the pedestrians perspective, and the metrics significance. Then, the animation of the real experiment reproduced at natural speed is shown, as can be seen in the survey questionnaire form
2
2

2

https://forms.gle/qyKHhzeYnaH8TPvQ7
. A total of 24 experiments have been evaluated with the survey. Fig.
3
shows the four maps used for the experiments and trajectories examples obtained in four different runs of Passing, Crossing 3, Narrow turn and Mixed scenarios. For the special case of Scenario
Curious person
, the unobtrusiveness score has not been collected in the survey, since the intrusive behavior of the human makes it lose relevance.

The survey involved a population of 70 participants from Italy, Spain and France of ages between 20 and 60 years old, with a mean age of 31 years old, and a percentage of about 40% of female participants. The background knowledge in robotics and autonomous navigation has been declared low for almost 50% of the people (up to 2 out of 5), and high for about 30% of the people (4 or 5 out of 5).
All HM metrics were rated between 1 and 5 using the Likert scale and they were finally obtained by taking the mean and the standard deviation for each metric over the total collection of answers.

IV
Analysis

In this section, we describe the methods adopted to process the data extracted from the social navigation experiments and the surveys. We aim to investigate the correlation between metrics and to propose a new practical evaluation protocol based only on quantitative aspects.

The framework is summarized in Fig.
1
. A total of 24 social navigation experiments have been conducted in real world and evaluated through a survey. Metrics correlation with humans scores is investigated to identify the most representative quantitative metrics through a clustering approach, together with a statistical analysis for single-metric correlation. Finally, an overall scores comparison demonstrates that quantitative metrics set identified through correlation show evaluation trends that follow human assessment.

IV-A

Metrics Pre-Processing

Given the different measurement units and numerical ranges of the adopted QM metrics, they were linearly scaled between 0 and 1. In this way, it is possible to perform a direct numerical comparison of the metrics also between experiments presenting diverse geometric layouts, number of humans, and motion control algorithms used. For each scenario, the run with the best value of a particular QM was assigned a value of 1, while the other two were assigned a lower linearly scaled value, depending on their share of that particular metric. The definition of best value changes depending on the specific metric evaluating navigation efficiency or safety. For example, Social Work is better when minimized, and the same holds for disturbance metrics such as intimate space occupancy, while average distance to people and social space occupancy are considered better if higher. Similar considerations were made for all the other QM metrics.
The HM metrics have also been scaled in
[0,1]
by dividing by 5, making these data ready for aggregation, comparison, and clustering.

IV-B

Clustering

The QM and HM metrics data represent two distinct feature sets of the same 24-experiment sample. In this section, we investigate the overall similarity of the two metric sets by trying to group the experiments, using QM and HM feature spaces separately. For this task, we employ the K-means clustering algorithm
[
19
]
, an unsupervised learning technique that uses distances in feature space as a criterion to group items. The 24 samples were thus labeled using the k-means algorithm using both feature sets.

The clusters obtained are first analyzed based on their internal composition to find the best number of groups, K. Then, clustering accuracy is used to investigate whether the QM and HM feature spaces generate the same groups of experiments.

The internal composition analysis was performed employing the silhouette score, a metric that quantifies the coherence of clusters. The K values tried to group the 24 experimental samples are in the range
[2,5]
.

The precision of the clustering was evaluated using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)
[
20
]
, frequently used in data cluster analysis. ARI is a coefficient that quantifies the similarity between two divisions of a dataset by comparing the assignment of data points to clusters. For our analysis, the labels assigned to the experiments by clustering with the HM survey metrics were considered the ground truth.

The ARI accuracy computation was performed for the entire set of quantitative metrics and with every possible combination of QM, except for the empty set. This process yielded a total of
2.047
combinations.
The objective of this analysis is to identify the most prevalent metrics in the combinations that produce the highest ARI. For this purpose, a cumulative ARI score has been computed for each metric, summing the ARI values obtained over the entire QM combination.

The final output, i.e., the cumulative ARI for each metric, represents the relevance of these metrics in achieving the same groupings obtained with the HM features space using K-means clustering.

IV-C

Statistical analysis for metrics correlation

The correlation between QM and HM sets of metrics is estimated by adopting two non-parametric statistical methods: Spearman’s Rho and Kendall’s Tau. Both approaches are ideal for data that do not necessarily meet the assumptions of normality, making them well-suited for ordinal or rank-based evaluations standard in social navigation studies. Combining these approaches not only provides complementary views of the correlation structure but also enables a comprehensive evaluation of how well the quantitative metrics capture trends observed in human assessments.

itemSpearman:

This method transforms raw metric values into ranks and then evaluates the monotonic relationship between paired sets. Specifically, for each set of observations, all data points are independently ranked. Spearman’s Rho is computed from the difference between the paired ranks, obtaining a coefficient that varies from -1 (perfect inversely monotonic) to +1 (perfect directly monotonic), thereby describing the strength and direction of the association between the two metrics. From the evaluations, we considered correlations resulting with strength
|\rho>0.4|
and a p-value
p<0.05

itemKendall:

Kendall’s Tau considers each pair of observations to determine if the ranks are concordant or discordant. For every possible pair of data points, the evaluation checks whether the ordering in the first metric agrees with the ordering in the second metric. The Tau coefficient is then calculated as the difference between the number of concordant and discordant pairs divided by the total number of pairs. This approach provides a more intuitive measure of association, especially beneficial in smaller sample sizes, as it directly reflects the proportion of concordant pairs. From the evaluations, we considered correlations resulting from strength
|\tau>0.25|
and a p-value
p<0.05
.

IV-D

Overall metrics matching

As a final analysis, to gain practical insight from the correlation study, an aggregate score was calculated for HM and QM metrics across each social scenario. The overall scores are computed averaging equally the metrics in the HM set, in the initial complete QM set, and in the correlation-driven optimal QM set. Hence, in Section
V
, these aggregated values are directly compared to demonstrate how the correlation analysis conducted can lead to the identification of a minimal set of relevant QM metrics that can be used to evaluate a social navigation experiment preserving the human-level assessment. The goal of this comparison is to demonstrate that the reduced QM set can be used to represent the overall evaluation trends and general ranking obtained through the survey. Thus, the complete proposed study can serve as a benchmarking protocol to evaluate new experiments using only the QM, enabling a quicker process compared to a costly and time-consuming survey to be conducted on a large population.

V-B

Statistical analysis for metrics correlation

A statistical analysis has been conducted to investigate the single-metric correlation between the QM and HM sets, with the aim of identifying the most representative quantitative metrics for each of the human factors considered. We considered correlations that obtain a Spearman strength
|\rho>0.4|
with a p-value
p<0.05
, and a Kendall strength
|\tau>0.25|
with a p-value
p<0.06
.
The correlations that satisfy both the strength and the statistical significance criteria defined for the Spearman and Kendall tests are reported on a heat map in Fig.
5
in terms of the average absolute Spearman-Kendall strength.

The resultant set of QM metrics with the highest correlation scores with human HM is: Time-to-goal, Proxemics-Social-Space, Proxemics-Intimate-Space, Avg-robot-linear-speed, Avg-min-distance-person. These findings confirm the results obtained with the cumulative ARI score for clustering (Fig.
4
).
An unexpected result is the absence of the Social Work in the table of strongly correlating metrics, which, among others, can be considered the most complete and advanced social metrics, mixing distance and velocity effect according to the Social Forces generated by the interaction among agents. However, it does not match single HM aspects.
The proxemic score and the minimum distance to people clearly correlate with unobtrusiveness, friendliness, and avoidance foresight. The average robot linear velocity is only tied to avoidance capability, but does not reflect the level of friendliness in the behavior.
Smoothness is a subtle aspect to evaluate, and it seems to be less represented by current metrics. The cumulative heading changes metric was expected to correlate more with that aspect. Instead, only time-to-goal shows a sufficient level of matching, showing it can be related to multiple aspects of navigation.

V-C

Overall Evaluation Comparison

The last analysis conducted consists of directly comparing the average aggregated scores of the quantitative QM values and the survey-based HM values representing each experiment.
The aggregate average scores calculated for each scenario can be seen in Fig.
6
as histograms, where the HM survey data are reported with the mean and standard deviation obtained from the responses of the participants.
Upon examining each scenario separately, the complete original QM metrics group (green column) provides flat overall results, making the three runs not easily distinguishable in terms of final evaluation. Thus, demonstrating that using a richer series of quantitative metrics does not provide an overall better ability to discern good and bad social navigation experiments. In contrast, there is an evident score gradient in the HM metrics obtained from the survey (light blue column) that clearly identifies the best and the worst runs in most scenarios.
The correlation analysis conducted led to the selection of an optimal set of QM metrics comprising time-to-goal, average robot velocity, proxemics in intimate and social space, and average minimum distance to people. These metrics are the one presenting a high cumulative ARI score in the clustering analysis (Fig
4
), and the ones emerging in the statistical correlation with a score greater than 0.4 (Fig.
5
).
With the optimal set of QM metrics found through correlation (blue column), the evaluation trends result significantly clearer and similar to the human assessments, enabling the identification of best and worst experiments in the majority of the cases. A clear trend over the three runs of a social scenario is considered the most impactful result in terms of practical usage of the evaluation study, and a relevant advancement over the complete QM set. Besides the trends, the scores obtained with the optimal QM metrics often fall inside the statistical range of the HM values, although a natural difference exists and a small scaling factor could better align the two results.

Nonetheless, some exceptional cases emerged. For instance, longer and more populated scenarios that present partially good and bad behavior of the robot are more challenging to evaluate overall. The results of the survey demonstrate that humans express severe assessment in case of partially bad trajectories, as emerge in Run 3 of Crossing 2, Run 2 of Crossing 3 scenario, and Run 3 of Mixed scenarios. In those advanced cases, limitations still exist in expressing overall evaluation of social navigation comparable to humans.

VI
Conclusions

This work presents an in-depth analysis of social navigation metrics, investigating existing correlations between current quantitative metrics and qualitative human assessment, with the aim of exploiting hidden data patterns to avoid costly surveys. The study proposed a clustering method to identify a QM feature space where experiments are grouped. Then, a statistical analysis of correlation have been performed to inspect individual links between metrics. Our findings show that by selecting relevant QM sets from the correlation results, it is possible to obtain an overall experimental benchmarking representative of human-like assessment. Indeed, optimal QM sets demonstrate clearer scoring trends compared to the complete ensemble of QM, better discerning good runs from bad ones. Nonetheless, advanced and mixed social scenarios still represent a challenge due to the complexity of correctly evaluating a navigation experiment that is only partially acceptable, leaving room for future improvement.

Future works will build on these findings to further highlight hidden relationships between QM and HM, also in longer and mixed scenarios. Different sets of QM less commonly used in the literature, but also interesting, could be evaluated. Learning and regression methods will be investigated with the aim of inferring human assessment from quantitative metrics, particularly focusing on the importance and explainability of characteristics. This can be possible by enriching the experiments dataset to have more samples, and grounding the trajectories with a specific environmental context.
