Safety of Dupilumab and Risk of Cutaneous T‐Cell Lymphoma in Pediatric Patients With Atopic Dermatitis: A Data‐Driven Guide to Counseling Patients and Families
Authors: Maria Gnarra Buethe, Tyler Sy, Lawrence F. Eichenfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent publications reporting increased cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma (CTCL) risk with dupilumab in atopic dermatitis (AD) have sparked debate, amplified by media coverage linking dupilumab to lymphoma. These concerns have reached pediatric populations, where we observe increasing parental hesitancy about initiating dupilumab for their children. This hesitancy is particularly acute given that dupilumab was only approved for infants aged 6 months and older in 2022—the first cohort now approaching 3–4 years of exposure. Parents understandably question the long‐term effects of decades of IL‐4/IL‐13 blockade initiated during critical immune development periods. The diagnostic overlap between AD and mycosis fungoides (the most common CTCL) complicates assessment. We examine evidence regarding dupilumab safety and CTCL risk specifically in pediatric patients, incorporating recently presented long‐term open‐label extension data, and provide recommendations for monitoring, distinguishing AD from CTCL, and counseling families navigating this complex topic.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease affecting up to 20% of children worldwide, with a significant impact on patients' and caregivers' quality of life [1]. Dupilumab, a fully human monoclonal antibody that inhibits IL‐4 and IL‐13 signaling through blockade of IL‐4Rα, is the first biologic therapy approved for moderate‐to‐severe AD in patients aged 6 months and older [1]. Phase 3 clinical trials have demonstrated significant improvements in disease severity, pruritus, and quality of life in pediatric populations, with an acceptable safety profile consistent with observations in adults [1, 2].

The relationship between AD and CTCL has long been recognized as complex. Mycosis fungoides (MF), the most common CTCL subtype, can clinically mimic AD, and Sézary syndrome can present similarly to atopic erythroderma [3, 4]. This diagnostic overlap, combined with reports of CTCL diagnoses following dupilumab initiation in adult patients, has prompted investigation into whether dupilumab poses an oncogenic risk or whether it unmasks pre‐existing malignancy [5, 6].

In this narrative review, we aim to examine the evidence regarding dupilumab safety and CTCL risk specifically in pediatric patients, incorporating recently presented long‐term open‐label extension data and current clinical recommendations to properly inform clinicians how to counsel pediatric patients and their families, including sample language for these discussions. We conducted a comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Embase, and http://clinicaltrials.gov through April 2025 using the terms “dupilumab,” “atopic dermatitis,” “cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma,” “mycosis fungoides,” and “pediatric,” supplemented by review of relevant conference abstracts, FDA drug labels, and pharmacovigilance databases.

Epidemiology of CTCL in Pediatric Populations

CTCL is predominantly a disease of older adults, with incidence increasing dramatically with age [7]. The overall incidence of CTCL is estimated at 8.8 cases per million persons, with MF accounting for approximately 5.2 cases per million [8]. Pediatric CTCL is exceedingly rare, representing less than 1.25% of all primary cutaneous lymphoma cases [9].

When CTCL does occur in children, it demonstrates distinct characteristics compared to adult disease. The hypopigmented variant of MF is very common in pediatric populations, and Sézary syndrome is extremely rare in children [1, 10]. The overwhelming majority of pediatric patients present with early‐stage disease, and progression to advanced stages is uncommon [10]. Importantly, the diagnosis of CTCL in children is frequently delayed due to clinical mimicry of common inflammatory dermatoses, including AD [1, 11]. However, CTCL in children has an excellent prognosis and is readily treatable; the majority of children respond well to skin‐directed therapies such as topical corticosteroids and phototherapy, with 5‐year and 10‐year disease‐specific survival rates of 100% in recent series [10, 11].

Atopic Dermatitis and Lymphoma Risk

Population‐based studies have demonstrated an association between AD and increased lymphoma risk, particularly in patients with severe disease. A meta‐analysis found that AD was significantly associated with elevated lymphoma risk (OR 2.56, 95% CI: 1.75–3.74), with T‐cell lymphoma showing the highest risk (OR 4.25, 95% CI: 1.94–9.33) [12]. In a large UK and Danish cohort study, severe AD was associated with a 2‐fold increased risk of non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL adjusted HR 2.08, 99% CI: 1.42–3.04). Severe AD is associated with a 2–3‐fold increased risk of lymphoma, including CTCL, even after adjusting for disease severity and drug exposure [13, 14].

In pediatric populations specifically, a large UK cohort study found that, excluding CTCL, severe AD in children was associated with increased lymphoma risk (HR 3.18, 95% CI: 1.41–7.16), though overall malignancy risk did not differ significantly between children with and without AD [14].

Several mechanisms have been proposed for the association between AD and lymphoma risk. Chronic immune stimulation and persistent T‐cell activation in severe AD may promote clonal T‐cell expansion. Additionally, shared cytokine signaling pathways—particularly IL‐13 and IL‐4—play roles in both AD pathogenesis and CTCL tumor biology. The diagnostic overlap between severe AD and early‐stage CTCL also raises the possibility that some of the observed associations reflect misclassification rather than a true causal relationship, as investigators have cautioned that misdiagnosis between severe AD and early‐stage CTCL could not be ruled out even after long follow‐up periods [13].

Dupilumab Clinical Trial Data in Pediatric Populations

The dupilumab clinical development program includes multiple phase 3 randomized trials and extension studies across pediatric age groups. In children aged 6 months to younger than 6 years with moderate‐to‐severe atopic dermatitis (AD), dupilumab demonstrated significant clinical benefit with an overall safety profile comparable to placebo, including lower serious treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) in the dupilumab arm [1].

The LIBERTY AD PRESCHOOL phase 3 trial enrolled 162 children aged 6 months to younger than 6 years (the youngest patient in the dupilumab group was aged 10 months); patients received dupilumab or placebo every 4 weeks for 16 weeks, with eligible patients subsequently enrolling in the LIBERTY AD PED open‐label extension (OLE) study [1]. In the OLE, 180 patients from this age group were followed for up to 2 years (104 weeks), with 106 completing the week 104 visit; the safety profile remained consistent with the parent study, with no new safety signals detected [1, 15]. Longer‐term open‐label extension data in children aged ≥ 6 to 12 years similarly support a stable safety profile over extended follow‐up [5].

Importantly, across the dupilumab pediatric AD clinical program (6 months–17 years), there have been no reported TEAEs of cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma (CTCL), including mycosis fungoides (MF), in controlled trials or available extension studies [2]. These pediatric findings are reassuring given the rarity of CTCL in childhood and the clinical overlap between severe or erythrodermic AD and early CTCL.

CTCL Reports in Adult Dupilumab‐Treated Patients

In adults, sporadic CTCL diagnoses have been reported during dupilumab therapy, prompting concern for either de novo oncogenesis or diagnostic unmasking of pre‐existing CTCL. In the adult AD open‐label extension program with follow‐up to 260 weeks (5 years), mycosis fungoides was reported in three (0.1%) dupilumab‐treated patients; investigators assessed these events as unrelated to the study drug [1].

A recent comprehensive review reported that 124 patients developed lymphoproliferative disorders after dupilumab, with a median time to diagnosis of 5 months after dupilumab initiation; 39% of cases presented with advanced‐stage disease [2]. A contemporaneous clinical review emphasizes that the observed association is most consistent with diagnostic unmasking or accelerated recognition of underlying CTCL in a subset of patients with refractory dermatitis, rather than a causal carcinogenic effect [6].

Proposed Mechanisms: Unmasking Versus Oncogenesis

Current evidence does not support a direct oncogenic effect of dupilumab. The proposed mechanism for CTCL emergence during dupilumab therapy involves the unmasking of pre‐existing malignant T‐cell clones rather than de novo transformation. IL‐4Rα blockade leads to increased IL‐13 availability in the local microenvironment, which may stimulate CTCL progression through IL‐13 receptor alpha 2 (IL‐13Rα2) signaling on tumor cells [2, 3, 6].

This hypothesis is supported by observations that: (1) Many patients diagnosed with CTCL after dupilumab had histopathologic evidence of MF on pre‐treatment biopsies when retrospectively reviewed; (2) The median time to diagnosis (5 months) is shorter than would be expected for de novo malignancy development; (3) Patients often present with advanced‐stage disease, suggesting pre‐existing occult malignancy [2, 4].

IL‐13 and its receptors IL‐13Rα1 and IL‐13Rα2 are highly expressed in the clinically involved skin of CTCL patients, and malignant lymphoma cells produce IL‐13 and express both receptors [3]. IL‐13 induces CTCL cell growth in vitro through IL‐13Rα1 signaling, and antibody‐mediated neutralization of IL‐13 or soluble IL‐13Rα2 molecules can lead to inhibition of tumor‐cell proliferation, implicating IL‐13 as an autocrine factor in CTCL [3].

A comprehensive review concluded that “no evidence is available to support a direct oncogenic risk of transforming AD into lymphoma by the treatment, and current literature suggests the role of IL‐4Rα/IL‐13R inhibition in unmasking pre‐existing malignant T cell clones through increased IL‐13 availability” [6].

Regulatory Assessment

Regulatory bodies have reviewed post‐marketing reports of CTCL in dupilumab‐treated patients. The available evidence does not support a causal association between dupilumab and CTCL, with the observed association likely representing diagnostic unmasking in a population (severe AD) already at elevated baseline risk for CTCL, rather than a drug‐induced oncogenic effect [6].

Distinguishing AD from CTCL: Pediatric‐Specific Considerations

Given the extreme rarity of pediatric CTCL, routine pre‐treatment biopsy is not indicated for typical pediatric AD. However, persistent treatment‐refractory dermatitis, atypical lesions, systemic symptoms, or unexplained lymphadenopathy should prompt biopsy and multidisciplinary evaluation [10, 16]. It is worth noting that lack of response to conventional AD therapy is itself a criterion for dupilumab initiation; therefore, treatment refractoriness should be interpreted in context—specifically, clinicians should consider biopsy when the clinical presentation is atypical or when there is a qualitative change in disease morphology or distribution after initiating dupilumab, rather than solely on the basis of prior treatment failure.

In pediatric patients specifically, the hypopigmented variant of MF is overrepresented and may mimic vitiligo or pityriasis alba, while folliculotropic MF can present with follicular accentuation or keratosis pilaris‐like lesions that may be mistaken for common pediatric dermatoses [10]. A high index of suspicion is necessary for accurate diagnosis, particularly in children with refractory eczematous dermatitis.

Comparative Risks with Other Systemic Therapies for Younger Patients

Prior to the availability of dupilumab, systemic treatment options for severe atopic dermatitis were limited to conventional immunosuppressants such as methotrexate (MTX) and cyclosporine, which are associated with well‐documented malignancy risks. MTX has been associated with increased risks of basal cell carcinoma (OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.20–1.38), squamous cell carcinoma (OR 1.61, 95% CI 1.37–1.89), and melanoma (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.13–1.61), with evidence of dose–response relationships for nonmelanoma skin cancers [17]. Additionally, methotrexate carries a recognized risk of methotrexate‐associated lymphoproliferative disorder (MTX‐LPD), an iatrogenic lymphoproliferative condition most commonly of B‐cell lineage that can develop during chronic MTX therapy; while predominantly described in rheumatoid arthritis populations, MTX‐LPD is relevant to any discussion of lymphoma risk with systemic AD therapies, as it may regress upon MTX withdrawal but can also require chemotherapy in a subset of patients [18]. Cyclosporine carries established risks of cutaneous and lymphoproliferative malignancies, particularly with prolonged use; in renal transplant recipients, dose‐dependent increases in cancer risk begin at 2 years and continue through 7 years of treatment, with malignancy representing the most common cause of death in long‐term studies [19, 20]. These conventional immunosuppressants also require intensive monitoring for nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, bone marrow suppression, and other serious adverse effects [19, 20, 21].

Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, including upadacitinib (approved for AD in patients ≥ 12 years) and abrocitinib, represent another class of systemic therapy now available for adolescents with moderate‐to‐severe AD [22, 23]. While JAK inhibitors have demonstrated high efficacy, they carry FDA boxed warnings for serious infections, malignancies (including lymphoma), major adverse cardiovascular events, and thrombosis, based primarily on data from the ORAL Surveillance trial in older rheumatoid arthritis patients [22, 24]. The relevance of these warnings to younger AD populations remains uncertain. Still, they underscore the importance of contextualizing the theoretical CTCL concern with dupilumab against the established risk profiles of alternative systemic agents [21, 22].

The theoretical CTCL concern—unsupported by pediatric data—should be weighed against demonstrated benefits.

Conclusions

For pediatric patients, the evidence is reassuring: no CTCL cases in any pediatric dupilumab trial, including open‐label extension data with up to 2 years of follow‐up. The extreme rarity of childhood CTCL, absence of reported cases, and regulatory conclusions support continued confidence in dupilumab's pediatric safety profile. Effective counseling requires acknowledging parental concerns—including legitimate questions about long‐term safety in children started on biologics since infancy—providing accurate context‐appropriate information and engaging in shared decision‐making. Clinicians should feel confident reassuring families while maintaining appropriate diagnostic vigilance. Withholding effective therapy based on theoretical concerns unsupported by pediatric data would not serve patients' best interests, particularly given the substantial burden of uncontrolled AD on children and their families.
