A partial converse to the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma for Bessel–Fourier series of order zero
Ryan L. Acosta Babb

Abstract
It is known that the Bessel–Fourier coefficients f m f_{m} of a function f f such that x ​ f ​ ( x ) \sqrt{x}f(x) is integrable over [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] satisfy f m / m → 0 f_{m}/\sqrt{m}\to 0 . We show a partial converse, namely that for 0 ⩽ α < 1 / 2 0\leqslant\alpha<1/2 and any non-negative a m → 0 a_{m}\to 0 , there is a function f f such that x α + 1 ​ f ​ ( x ) x^{\alpha+1}f(x) is integrable and its Bessel–Fourier coefficients f m f_{m} satisfy m − α ​ f m ⩾ a m m^{-\alpha}f_{m}\geqslant a_{m} and m − α ​ f m → 0 m^{-\alpha}f_{m}\to 0 . Introduction

The Riemann–Lebesgue Lemma is a classic result in Fourier analysis,
which tells us that the Fourier coefficients of an integrable function,
f
,
decay to 0 at high frequencies, i.e.
\widehat{f}(n)\to 0
as
\left|n\right|\to\infty
.

By increasing the regularity of
f
, say
f\in C^{k}
, we can obtain faster rates
of decay of the coefficients,
viz.

\left|n\right|^{k}\widehat{f}(n)\to 0
.
But if we merely assume that
f\in L^{1}
, then the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma
is the best possible result we can expect.
Indeed, we can find integrable functions whose Fourier coefficients
decay as slowly as we like, as the following theorem shows
(see, e.g.,
Grafakos,, 2014
, Theorem 3.3.4)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
a_{n}\geqslant 0
be a sequence such that
a_{n}\to 0
as
\left|n\right|\to\infty
.
Then, there is a function
f\in L^{1}([0,1])

whose Fourier coefficients satisfy
\widehat{f}(n)\geqslant a_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

This may seem ‘obvious’: if we have the coefficients
a_{n}
, can we not just set

f(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}nx}?

The answer is no, since there is no guarantee that this series will converge to an
L^{1}
function.
Recall that the
Fejér kernel
is given by

F_{N}(x)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\left(1-\frac{\left|n\right|}{N+1}\right)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}nx}\equiv\frac{1}{N+1}\frac{\sin(\pi(N+1)x)^{2}}{\sin(\pi x)^{2}},

(1.1)

and consider the function

f(x):=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{m}}F_{2^{2^{2^{m}}}}(x).

Since
F_{N}(x)\geqslant 0
and
\left\lVert F_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{1}({[0,1]})}=1
for all
N
,
this function is clearly well-defined and integrable.
However, as Exercises 4.2.3 and 4.2.4 in
Grafakos, (2014)
show,
the partial sums of the Fourier series of
f
diverge (in the
L^{1}
norm)
like
N\log(N)
.

Note that the Fourier coefficients of
f
are the nasty-looking

\widehat{f}(n)=\sum_{m=\lceil\log_{2}\log_{2}\log_{2}(\left|n\right|)\rceil}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2^{m}}-\frac{\left|n\right|}{2^{2^{2^{n}}}+1}\right),

which nevertheless tend to
0
as
\left|n\right|\to\infty
by the Riemann–Lebesgue Lemma.

In some situations, Fourier series may not be the ideal choice to expand our functions.
For instance, when working on the disc

\mathbb{D}:=\{(r\cos(2\pi\theta),r\sin(2\pi\theta)):r,\theta\in[0,1]\},

it is convenient to expand
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
using eigenfunctions of the Laplacian on
\mathbb{D}
.
This leads to the (2-dimensional)
Bessel–Fourier series
of
f
,

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f_{n,m}J_{\left|n\right|}(j_{\left|n\right|,m}r)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}n\theta},

(1.2)

where
J_{n}
are Bessel functions of the first kind (of order
n\in\mathbb{N}
),

j_{n,m}>0
(
m\in\mathbb{N}
) are the zeros of
J_{n}
, and
f_{n,m}
are the
Bessel–Fourier coefficients

f_{n,m}:=\frac{2}{J_{\left|n\right|+1}(j_{\left|n\right|,m})^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f(r,\theta)J_{\left|n\right|}(j_{\left|n\right|,m}r)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}n\theta}r\,\mathrm{d}{r}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}.

Note the
r\,\mathrm{d}{r}
in the measure coming from the use of polar coordinates on
\mathbb{D}
.

If
f
is radial, i.e.
f(r,\theta)\equiv f(r)
independently of
\theta
,
then the only non-zero (angular) Fourier coefficient occurs for
n=0
.
In this manner, we obtain a one-dimensional Bessel–Fourier series for
f
of order zero:

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f_{m}J_{0}(j_{m}x),

(1.3)

where we have set
j_{m}:=j_{0,m}
for notational convenience,
and the Bessel–Fourier coefficients now take the from

f_{m}:=\frac{2}{J_{1}(j_{m})^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}f(x)J_{0}(j_{m}x)x\,\mathrm{d}{x}.

The corresponding ‘Riemann–Lebesgue lemma’ for the
f_{m}
is as follows
(
Young,, 1920
)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix
0\leqslant s\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
.
If
x^{1/2+s}f(x)\in L^{1}([0,1])
and
f_{m}
are the Bessel–Fourier coefficients of
f
,
then

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{f_{m}}{j_{m}^{1/2+s}}=0.

This can be proved by recalling that
J_{0}(x)
behaves asymptotically like
\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\cos(x)
;
the
\sqrt{x}
is controlled by the condition
x^{1/2+s}f(x)\in L^{1}
,
and one can apply the classical Riemann–Lebesgue Lemma for cosines to the function
x^{s}f(x)
.

Of particular interest are the following special cases:

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}f(x)\in L^{1}

\displaystyle\implies\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{f_{m}}{\sqrt{j_{m}}}=0

\displaystyle xf(x)\in L^{1}

\displaystyle\implies\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{f_{m}}{j_{m}}=0.

In light of
Theorem

1.2
, we might suspect that we can find
L^{1}
functions
whose coefficients can be made to blow up
at any given rate below
\sqrt{j_{m}}
(whenever
\sqrt{x}f(x)\in L^{1}
) or even
j_{m}

(if
xf(x)\in L^{1}
).
The following example supports our suspicions.

Example 1.3
.

Consider the functions
f^{(\alpha)}(x)=x^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha>0
, so that

xf^{(\alpha)}(x)\in L^{1}
if, and only if,
\alpha<2
.
Taking
\alpha=2-\varepsilon
, the functions
f^{(\alpha)}
are in
L^{1}(x\,\mathrm{d}{x})

for all
\varepsilon>0
.
Their Bessel–Fourier coefficients can be estimated as follows
(where ‘
\approx
’ is a shorthand for ‘neglecting constants’):

\displaystyle f^{(\alpha)}_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{J_{1}(j_{m})^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2+\varepsilon}J_{0}(j_{m}x)x\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle\approx j_{m}\int_{0}^{j_{m}}j_{m}^{1-\varepsilon}\frac{J_{0}(y)}{y^{1-\varepsilon}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}{y}}{j_{m}}

\displaystyle\approx j_{m}^{1-\varepsilon}\left(\int_{0}^{j_{1}}\frac{J_{0}(y)}{y^{1-\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}{y}+\int_{j_{1}}^{j_{m}}\frac{\cos(y-\pi/4)}{y^{3/2-\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}{y}+\int_{j_{1}}^{j_{m}}O(y^{-5/2+\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}{y}\right)

The first integral is clearly finite;
the third is clearly bounded uniformly in
j_{m}
.
Finally, one can exploit the oscillations in the cosine to show that the second integral is also bounded
(see
Section

5
or
Zygmund,, 2003
).
Therefore,
f^{(\alpha)}_{m}=O(j_{m}^{1-\varepsilon})
.

In particular, taking
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}
, we have
f^{(1/2)}(x)=x^{-3/2}
,
so that
xf^{(1/2)}(x)\in L^{1}
but
\sqrt{x}f^{(1/2)}(x)\not\in L^{1}
.
In this case, the coefficients decay as
\sqrt{j_{m}}
.
This example shows that
\sqrt{j_{m}}
is a ‘limiting rate of growth’ for functions in
L^{1}(\sqrt{x}\,\mathrm{d}{x})
.

The decay of Bessel–Fourier coefficients has been exploited recently by
Saadi and Daher, (2022)
;
Saadi and Daher, (2023)

to study when a function belongs to a Lipschitz class.

In this paper we prove the following analogue of
Theorem

1.1
for
Bessel–Fourier series,
which is a partial converse to
Theorem

1.2
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
0\leqslant\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
and

a_{m}\geqslant 0
be a sequence with
a_{m}\to 0
.
Then, there is a function
f
, with
x^{\alpha+1}f(x)\in L^{1}
,
whose Bessel–Fourier coefficients of order zero,
f_{m}
, satisfy

0\leqslant a_{m}\leqslant\frac{f_{m}}{j_{m}^{\alpha}}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad m\to\infty.

Furthermore,
f
can be chosen so that
f_{2m}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
or so that
f_{2m+1}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

We also show, in
Section

6
, how to construct an
L^{1}
function on the disc whose Bessel–Fourier
series diverges in norm for any possible truncation of the partial sums,
assuming that
Theorem

1.4
can be improved to coefficients with
a_{m}=o(\sqrt{j_{m}})
.
Here, the condition
f_{2m+1}=0
will be crucial.

Note that
Theorem

1.4
does not prove that
Theorem

1.2
is ‘sharp’.
Indeed,

•

in
Theorem

1.2
we need only assume that
x^{\alpha+1/2}f(x)\in L^{1}

to get a decay of
f_{m}=o(j_{m}^{\alpha+1/2})
, for all
0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
;

•

in
Theorem

1.4
we construct an
f
with
x^{\alpha+1}f(x)\in L^{1}

with a decay of
f_{m}=o(j_{m}^{\alpha})
for
0\leqslant\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
.

Our result requires an extra
\sqrt{x}
, loses a factor of
\sqrt{j_{m}}
in the decay,
and leaves the end-point
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
open.

However, assuming that a kernel analogous to
F_{N}
is positive, we can show a stronger result,
namely, that for any
0\leqslant\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
, there is an

f
with
\sqrt{x}f(x)\in L^{1}
whose coefficients decay as
f_{m}=o(j_{m}^{\alpha})
,
which is in line with
Theorem

1.2
for
s=0
.
This positivity conjecture is supported by numerical calculations.

The paper is organised as follows:
in
Section

2
we record some necessary facts about
Bessel functions and their zeros.
In
Section

3
, we give a rigorous proof of
Theorem

1.4
,
without assuming any additional results;
while in
Section

4
we formulate and prove a stronger
version of
Theorem

1.4
, assuming a plausible conjecture.

Section

5
contains a proof of a technical, but crucial
lemma used in the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.
Finally, in
Section

6
we discuss how these methods
could be applied to construct an
L^{1}
function on the disc
whose Bessel–Fourier series diverges regardless of how one chooses
to truncate the partial sums.

Notation.

Throughout, we will employ the following asymptotic notation:

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim g(x)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle f(x)\leqslant Cg(x)\text{ for some absolute constant }C>0

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\approx g(x)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle f(x)\lesssim g(x)\quad\text{and}\quad g(x)\lesssim f(x)

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=O(g(x))

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\left|f(x)\right|\lesssim g(x)

\displaystyle a_{m}

\displaystyle=o(b_{m})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\frac{a_{m}}{b_{m}}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad m\to\infty.

We adopt the convention whereby
\mathbb{N}
is the set of
positive
integers,
with
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

2
Bessel functions and their asymptotics

In order to prove
Theorem

1.4
, we will need the following facts about
the Bessel function
J_{0}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
r>0
, we have

J_{0}(r)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\cos(x-\pi/4)}{\sqrt{x}}+R(r),

(2.1)

where

\left|R(r)\right|\lesssim\int_{0}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-rt}\sqrt{t}\,\mathrm{d}{t}+\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-rt}}{t}\,\mathrm{d}{t}.

(2.2)

Furthermore,

\left|R(r)\right|\lesssim r^{-3/2}\quad\text{for}\quad r\geqslant 1.

(2.3)

These are well-known results; see for instance Appendinx B in
Grafakos, (2014)
.
The specific form of the bound (
2.2
)
is derived in detail in
Acosta Babb, (2024)
.

We will also use the following information about the zeros of
J_{0}
,
and how they interact with
J_{1}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
j_{m}
denote the
m\textsuperscript{th}
positive zero of
J_{0}
. Then

j_{m}>m\quad\text{for all}\quad m\geqslant 1.

(2.4)

Furthermore,

J_{1}(j_{m})=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{j_{m}}}\right).

(2.5)

Proof.

The estimate in
Eq.

2.5
follows from the asymptotics
for
J_{1}
; see
Tolstov, (1976)
.
On the other hand, (
2.4
) is easily obtained from the following inequality
(
Hethcote,, 1970
, Theorem 3)

j_{m}\geqslant\pi m-\frac{\pi}{4}\quad\text{for all}\quad m\geqslant 1.\qed

Remark
.

Numerical calculations suggests that the sequence
J_{1}(j_{m})
oscillates
and that
\left|J_{1}(j_{m})\right|
is decreasing.
We do not at present have a proof of these facts,
but will refer to them again later when discussing possible generalisations and applications
of our results.

Finally, we record two useful formulas, which will be needed in several calculations.
The first is

J^{\prime}_{0}(x)=-J_{1}(x)\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(2.6)

and can be found in Appendix B to
Grafakos, (2014)
.
The second is

\int_{0}^{j_{m}}J_{0}(y)y\,\mathrm{d}{y}=j_{m}J_{1}(j_{m})\quad\text{for all}\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.7)

To see this, recall that
J_{0}
satisfies Bessel’s differential equation:

x^{2}J_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)+xJ_{0}^{\prime}(x)+x^{2}J_{0}(x)=0,

and that
J_{0}^{\prime}(x)\equiv-J_{1}(x)
.
Integrating the differential equation therefore leads us to
Eq.

2.7
.

3
‘Weak’ decay theorems

In order to construct a function whose Bessel–Fourier coefficients have the desired
decay properties, we will follow the argument of
Theorem 3.3.4 in
Grafakos, (2014)
.

Recall that a sequence
(c_{m})_{m}
is
convex
if

c_{m}\geqslant 0\quad\text{and}\quad c_{m}+c_{m+2}\geqslant 2c_{m+1}\quad\text{for all}\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.

The following two lemmas are proved in Chapter 3 of
Grafakos, (2014)
.

Lemma 3.1

(Lemma 3.3.2 in
Grafakos,, 2014
)
.

Given a sequence
a_{m}\geqslant 0
tending to zero as
m\to\infty
,
there is a convex sequence
c_{m}\geqslant a_{m}
that also tends to zero.

Lemma 3.2

(Lemma 3.3.3 in
Grafakos,, 2014
)
.

Given a convex decreasing sequence
c_{m}\to 0
and a fixed integer
k\geqslant 0
,

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(l+1)(c_{l+k}+c_{l+k+2}-2c_{l+k+1})=c_{k}.

Let us write

S^{\alpha}_{m}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}j_{m}^{\alpha}J_{0}(j_{k}x).

We will need the following ‘modified Fejér kernel’ for the Bessel–Fourier series:

K^{\alpha}_{M}(x):=\frac{1}{M+1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}S^{\alpha}_{m}(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(1-\frac{m}{M+1}\right)j_{m}^{\alpha}J_{0}(j_{m}x).

We will also set
K^{\alpha}_{0}(x)\equiv 1
when
M=0
.

As we saw in
Eq.

1.1
, the classical Fejér kernel for Fourier series
has a nice closed form, which shows it to be non-negative for all
x
and
N
;
this makes computing
L^{1}
norms much easier.

To prove
Theorem

1.4
, it suffices to obtain uniform lower bounds on

x^{\alpha+1}K^{\alpha}_{M}(x)
for
0\leqslant\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
0\leqslant\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
, there is a constant
C>0
such that

x^{\alpha+1}K_{M}^{\alpha}(x)\geqslant-C\quad\text{uniformly in}\quad M\in\mathbb{N}\quad\text{and}\quad x\in[0,1].

We will prove
Lemma

3.3
in
Section

5
.
Here, we will re-state and prove
Theorem

1.4
.

Theorem 3.4

(
=

Theorem

1.4
)
.

Let
0\leqslant\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
and

a_{m}\geqslant 0
be a sequence such that
a_{m}\to 0
.
Then, there is a function
f
, with
x^{\alpha+1}f(x)\in L^{1}
,
whose Bessel–Fourier coefficients of order zero,
f_{m}
, satisfy

0\leqslant a_{m}\leqslant\frac{f_{m}}{j_{m}^{\alpha}}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad m\to\infty.

Furthermore,
f
can be chosen so that
f_{2m}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
or so that
f_{2m+1}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.1
there is a decreasing convex sequence
c_{m}\geqslant a_{m}

such that
c_{m}\to 0
.
(Note that we may need to take
c_{0}=2c_{1}
, say, in order
to have a decreasing, convex sequence defined for
m\geqslant 0
.)
We use
(c_{m})_{m\geqslant 0}
to construct the function

f(x):=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(l+1)(c_{l}+c_{l+2}-2c_{l+1})K_{l}^{\alpha}(x).

(3.1)

We claim that
x^{\alpha+1}f(x)\in L^{1}
.

First, by
Lemma

3.3
,
x^{\alpha+1}K^{\alpha}_{M}(x)+C\geqslant 0
, so we have

\left\lVert x^{\alpha+1}K^{\alpha}_{M}\right\rVert_{L^{1}({[0,1]})}\leqslant C+\int_{0}^{1}(K^{\alpha}_{M}(x)x^{\alpha+1}+C)\,\mathrm{d}{x}=2C+\int_{0}^{1}K^{\alpha}_{M}(x)x^{\alpha+1}\,\mathrm{d}{x}.

Writing
K^{\alpha}_{M}
out as a sum, and changing variables to
y=j_{m}x
in the integral, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}K^{\alpha}_{M}(x)x^{\alpha+1}\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(1-\frac{m}{M+1}\right)j_{m}^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}J_{0}(j_{m}x)x^{\alpha+1}\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(1-\frac{m}{M+1}\right)\frac{j_{m}^{\alpha}}{j_{m}^{\alpha+2}}\int_{0}^{j_{m}}J_{0}(y)y^{\alpha+1}\,\mathrm{d}{y}.

(3.2)

We now split the integral into the ranges
0\leqslant y\leqslant 1
and
1\leqslant y\leqslant j_{m}
,
and use the asymptotics from
Lemma

2.1
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}}\int_{0}^{j_{m}}J_{0}(y)y^{\alpha+1}\,\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}J_{0}(y)y^{\alpha+1}\,\mathrm{d}{y}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}}\int_{1}^{j_{m}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cos(y-\pi/4)y^{\alpha+1/2}\,\mathrm{d}{y}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}}\int_{1}^{j_{m}}O\left(y^{\alpha-1/2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{y}.

(3.5)

Now, for (
3.3
), we immediately have

\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}J_{0}(y)y^{\alpha+1}\,\mathrm{d}{y}\lesssim\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}},

and for (
3.5
),

\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}}\int_{1}^{j_{m}}O\left(y^{\alpha-1/2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{y}\lesssim\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}}\left(j_{m}^{\alpha+1/2}-1\right)\lesssim\frac{1}{j_{m}^{3/2-\alpha}}.

To get suitable decay in (
3.4
), we have to integrate by parts.
Neglecting nuisance factors of
\frac{2}{\pi}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}}\int_{1}^{j_{m}}\cos(y-\pi/4)y^{\alpha+1/2}\,\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}}\left[\sin(y-\pi/4)y^{\alpha+1/2}\right]_{1}^{j_{m}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\alpha+1/2}{j_{m}^{2}}\int_{1}^{j_{m}}\sin(y-\pi/4)y^{\alpha-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{j_{m}^{\alpha+1/2}}{j_{m}^{2}}+\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}}+\frac{1}{j_{m}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{j_{m}^{3/2-\alpha}}.

(For the boundedness of the sine integral, see the argument in
Section

5
.)
Since
\alpha<1/2
, the terms in
Eqs.

3.3
,
3.4
and
3.5
are summable,
and, therefore, so is (
3.2
).
In other words,
\left\lVert x^{\alpha+1}K^{\alpha}_{M}\right\rVert_{L^{1}({[0,1]})}=O(1)
uniformly in
M
,
and so, from
Eq.

3.1
and
Lemma

3.2
, we see that

\left\lVert x^{\alpha+1}f\right\rVert_{L^{1}({[0,1]})}\lesssim\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(l+1)(c_{l}+c_{l+2}-2c_{l+1})=c_{0}<\infty,

completing the proof of our claim.

We can now mimic the argument in
Grafakos, (2014)

to show that

f_{m}=j_{m}^{\alpha}c_{m}\geqslant a_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{f_{m}}{j_{m}^{\alpha}}=c_{m}\to 0.

A slight modification of that argument shows that we can find such an
f

with the additional property that
f_{2m+1}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
It suffices to replace the kernel
K^{\alpha}_{M}
with the ‘even kernel’

K^{\alpha,e}_{M}(x):=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(1-\frac{m}{M+1}\right)j_{m}^{\alpha}J_{0}(j_{2m}x).

As can be seen from the proof of
Lemma

3.3
below,
we still have that
x^{\alpha+1}K^{\alpha,e}_{M}(x)\geqslant-C

uniformly in
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in[0,1]
.
We construct
f
as in
Eq.

3.1
and show that it satisfies
x^{\alpha+1}f(x)\in L^{1}
,
just as before.
Then, the same computation of the
f_{m}
shows that

f_{2m}=j_{m}^{\alpha}c_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{2m+1}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.6)

Indeed,

\displaystyle(K^{\alpha,e}_{M})_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left(1-\frac{k}{M+1}\right)\frac{2j_{m}^{\alpha}}{J_{1}(j_{m})^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}J_{0}(j_{m}x)J_{0}(j_{2k}x)x\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left(1-\frac{k}{M+1}\right)j_{m}^{\alpha}\delta_{m,2k},

which vanishes whenever
m>2M
or
m
is odd. Thus,

(K^{\alpha,e}_{M})_{m}=\begin{cases}\left(1-\frac{m/2}{M+1}\right)j_{m/2}^{\alpha},&\text{if $m\leqslant 2k$ is even};\\
0,&\text{if $m$ is odd or $m>2k$}.\end{cases}

Eq.

3.6
now follows.

By making the obvious adjustments, one can also construct an
f
satisfying

f_{2m}=0\quad\text{and}\quad f_{2m+1}=j_{m}^{\alpha}c_{m}\quad\text{for all}\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.\qed

and

0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
—see

We therefore make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

For all,
0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
,
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in[0,1]
,
K^{\alpha}_{M}(x)\geqslant 0
.

Assuming this conjecture, we can prove the following strengthening of
Theorem

1.4
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
Conjecture

1
is true.
Let
a_{m}\geqslant 0
be a sequence such that
a_{m}\to 0
and fix
0\leqslant\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
.
Then there is a function
f
such that
\sqrt{x}f(x)\in L^{1}
,
whose Bessel–Fourier coefficients of order
0
,
f_{m}
, satisfy

0\leqslant a_{m}\leqslant\frac{f_{m}}{j_{m}^{\alpha}}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad m\to\infty.

Furthermore,
f
can be chosen so that
f_{2m}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
or so that
f_{2m+1}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Construct
f
as before, using
Eq.

3.1
.
We now claim (assuming
Conjecture

1
) that
\sqrt{x}f(x)\in L^{1}
.

By assumption (i.e.
Conjecture

1
),
K^{\alpha}_{M}(x)\geqslant 0
, so

\left\lVert\sqrt{x}K^{\alpha}_{M}\right\rVert_{L^{1}({[0,1]})}=\int_{0}^{1}K^{\alpha}_{M}(x)\sqrt{x}\,\mathrm{d}{x}.

Writing
K^{\alpha}_{M}
out as a sum and changing variables in the integral again,
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}K^{\alpha}_{M}(x)\sqrt{x}\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(1-\frac{m}{M+1}\right)j_{m}^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}J_{0}(j_{m}x)\sqrt{x}\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(1-\frac{m}{M+1}\right)\frac{j_{m}^{\alpha}}{j_{m}^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{j_{m}}J_{0}(y)\sqrt{y}\,\mathrm{d}{y}.

(4.1)

We now split the range of integration into two pieces.
When
0\leqslant y\leqslant 1
, we have
J_{0}(y)\sqrt{y}=O(1)
.
When
1\leqslant y\leqslant j_{m}
, we can use the asymptotics for
J_{0}
in
Lemma

2.1
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{j_{m}^{\alpha}}{j_{m}^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{j_{m}}J_{0}(y)\sqrt{y}\,\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{j_{m}^{\alpha}}{j_{m}^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{1}J_{0}(y)\sqrt{y}\,\mathrm{d}{y}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{j_{m}^{\alpha}}{j_{m}^{3/2}}\int_{1}^{j_{m}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cos(y-\pi/4)\,\mathrm{d}{y}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{j_{m}^{\alpha}}{j_{m}^{3/2}}\int_{1}^{j_{m}}O\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{y}.

(4.4)

Now, using the asymptotics for
j_{m}
in
Lemma

2.2
, we have

(\ref{eqn:int01})\lesssim\frac{1}{m^{3/2-\alpha}},\quad(\ref{eqn:int1jmcos})\lesssim\frac{1}{m^{3/2-\alpha}}\quad\text{and}\quad(\ref{eqn:int1jmo})\lesssim\frac{\log{m}}{m^{3/2-\alpha}}\lesssim\frac{1}{m^{3/2-\alpha/2}},

since
m^{-\alpha/2}\log{m}\to 0
.
For
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
, all of the above terms are summable.
Combining and summing these terms in
Eq.

4.1
, we deduce that the norms

\left\lVert\sqrt{x}K^{\alpha}_{M}\right\rVert_{L^{1}({[0,1]})}
are bounded uniformly in
M
.
Therefore, from the definition of
f
in (
3.1
)
and
Lemma

3.2
we see that

\left\lVert\sqrt{x}f\right\rVert_{L^{1}({[0,1]})}\lesssim\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(l+1)(c_{l}+c_{l+2}-2c_{l+1})=c_{0}<\infty,

completing the proof of our claim.

The computation of the
f_{m}
, including the modifications
to ensure that
f_{2m+1}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
are identical to those in the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.
∎

which should remain bounded as
M\to\infty
by the Alternating Series Test.
It is then clear that, defining
f
as in
Eq.

3.1
with
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}

would yield

, and the computation of the coefficients

5
Bounds on
x^{\alpha+1}K^{\alpha}_{M}(x)

In this section, we prove
Lemma

3.3
.
First we will need another technical lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
\alpha>0
and
u>0

\max_{m>0}\left\{m^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-mu}\right\}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\mathrm{e}u}\right)^{\alpha}\lesssim u^{-\alpha}.

Proof.

Let
h(y):=y^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-yu}
and differentiate twice to find

h^{\prime}(y)=\alpha y^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{e}^{-yu}+-uy^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-yu},

and

h^{\prime\prime}(y)=\left[\alpha(\alpha-1)y^{\alpha-2}-2\alpha uy^{\alpha-1}+u^{2}y^{\alpha}\right]\mathrm{e}^{-uy}.

It is then easy to see that the only solution to
h^{\prime}(y)=0
is
y=\frac{\alpha}{u}

and that

h^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\alpha}{u}\right)=-\frac{\alpha^{\alpha-1}}{u^{\alpha-2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha}<0,

so that
\max_{y>0}h(y)=h(\alpha/u)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\mathrm{e}u}\right)^{\alpha}
.
∎

On to the proof of
Lemma

3.3
.

To begin, note that it is sufficient to prove that
x^{\alpha+1}S^{\alpha}_{M}(x)\geqslant-C
uniformly in
x
and
M
.
Using the asymptotics from
Eq.

2.1
, we have that

\displaystyle x^{\alpha+1}S^{\alpha}_{M}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{\alpha+1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}j_{m}^{\alpha}J_{0}(j_{m}x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}x^{\alpha+1/2}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\cos(j_{m}x-\pi/4)}{j_{m}^{1/2-\alpha}}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}x^{\alpha+1}j_{m}^{\alpha}R(j_{m}x)

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}x^{\alpha+1/2}\widetilde{S}^{\alpha}_{M}(x)+R^{\alpha}_{M}(x),

(5.1)

where we have defined

\widetilde{S}^{\alpha}_{M}:=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\cos(j_{m}x-\pi/4)}{j_{m}^{1/2-\alpha}}\quad\text{and}\quad R^{\alpha}_{M}(x):=\sum_{m=1}^{M}x^{\alpha+1}j_{m}^{\alpha}R(j_{m}x).

It suffices to obtain lower bounds on
x^{\alpha+1/2}\widetilde{S}^{\alpha}_{M}(x)

and upper bounds on
\left|R^{\alpha}_{M}(x)\right|

that are uniform in
x\in[0,1]
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lower bounds on
\widetilde{S}^{\alpha}_{M}
.

Since
x^{\alpha+1/2}\geqslant 0
,
it suffices to obtain lower bounds on the sums
\widetilde{S}^{\alpha}_{M}
.
To this end, we follow
Zygmund, (2003)

and relate the sum to an integral. We claim that

\widetilde{S}^{\alpha}_{M}(x)=\frac{x/2}{\sin(\pi x/2)}\int_{0}^{j_{M}+\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}+O(1).

(5.2)

To show this, note that, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{j_{m}-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{j_{m}+\frac{\pi}{2}}\cos(ux-\pi/4)\,\mathrm{d}{u}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\sin(ux-\pi/4)}{x}\right]_{j_{m}-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{j_{m}+\frac{\pi}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sin(\pi x/2)}{x}\cos(j_{m}x-\pi/4),

using the sum-to-product formula for a difference of sines

\sin(a)-\sin(b)=2\sin\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right).

Hence, integrating over all intervals
[j_{m}-\frac{\pi}{2},j_{m}+\frac{\pi}{2}]

for
1\leqslant m\leqslant M
, yields

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\int_{j_{m}-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{j_{m}+\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\int_{j_{m}-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{j_{m}+\frac{\pi}{2}}\cos(ux-\pi/4)\left(\frac{1}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{j_{m}^{1/2-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{u}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\int_{j_{m}-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{j_{m}+\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{j_{m}^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sup_{\left|u-j_{m}\right|<\frac{\pi}{2}}\left|\frac{1}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{j_{m}^{1/2-\alpha}}\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sin(\pi x/2)}{x/2}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\cos(j_{m}x-\pi/4)}{j_{m}^{1/2-\alpha}}.

By the mean value theorem, for
\left|u-j_{m}\right|<\frac{\pi}{2}
,

\frac{1}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{j_{m}^{1/2-\alpha}}=\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{j_{m}-u}{y^{3/2-\alpha}}\quad\text{with}\quad j_{m}-\frac{\pi}{2}\leqslant y\leqslant j_{m}+\frac{\pi}{2},

whence we obtain, using
Eq.

2.4
, that

\sup_{\left|u-j_{m}\right|<\frac{\pi}{2}}\left|\frac{1}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{j_{m}^{1/2-\alpha}}\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{j_{m}^{3/2-\alpha}}\lesssim\frac{1}{m^{3/2-\alpha}}.

Using this bound we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\int_{j_{m}-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{j_{m}+\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}-\frac{\sin(\pi x/2)}{x/2}

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\cos(j_{m}x-\pi/4)}{j_{m}^{1/2-\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{m^{3/2-\alpha}}=O(1)

(5.3)

since
\alpha<1/2
.

The next step is to relate

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\int_{j_{m}-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{j_{m}+\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}\quad\text{to}\quad\int_{0}^{j_{M}+\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}.

For this we need to recall the asymptotics for
j_{m}
:

j_{m}=\pi m-\frac{\pi}{4}+\varepsilon_{m},

where (according to McMahon’s asymptotic expansions for large zeros)

\varepsilon_{m}=\frac{1}{8\pi m-2\pi}+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{3}}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad m\to\infty.

(See the formula on p. 505 of
Watson, 1995
.)

Thus, if we sum over all the intervals
[j_{m}-\frac{\pi}{2},j_{m}+\frac{\pi}{2}]

for
1\leqslant m\leqslant M
, we will obtain the intergral over

[j_{1}-\frac{\pi}{2},j_{M}+\frac{\pi}{2}]
,
plus a sum of the errors obtained from the overlaps that occur around
the midpoints

\frac{j_{m+1}-j_{m}}{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_{m+1}-\varepsilon_{m}}{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right).

These errors are therefore of order

\int_{\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{1}{m^{2}}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{1}{m^{2}}}\frac{1}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}\lesssim\frac{1}{m^{2}},

and so are summable in
m
. It follows that

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\int_{j_{m}-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{j_{m}+\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}=\int_{0}^{j_{M}+\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}+O(1).

As
(x/2)/\sin(\pi x/2)>0
is bounded above and below on
[0,1]
,

Eq.

5.2
follows from this display and
Eq.

5.3
.

Since we are interested in the behaviour of this integral as
M\to\infty
,
we will replace
j_{M}+\frac{\pi}{2}
by
M
. We now claim that

\int_{0}^{M}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}\geqslant-\frac{C_{1}}{x^{\alpha+1/2}}\quad\text{for some}\quad C_{1}>0.

(5.4)

After a change of variables and by using the identity

\cos(a-\pi/4)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cos(a)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sin(a),

we obtain

\int_{0}^{M}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}=\frac{1}{x^{\alpha+1/2}\sqrt{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{Mx}\frac{\cos(v)}{v^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{v}+\int_{0}^{Mx}\frac{\sin(v)}{v^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{v}\right).

Thus, it remains to bound the indefinite integrals

G(y):=\int_{0}^{y}\frac{\cos(v)}{v^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{v}\quad\text{and}\quad H(y):=\int_{0}^{y}\frac{\sin(v)}{v^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{v}.

We deal with the cosine integral, the sine case being easier.
Since

G^{\prime}(y)=\frac{\cos(y)}{y^{1/2-\alpha}}\quad\text{and}\quad G^{\prime\prime}(y)=-\frac{\sin(y)}{y^{1/2-\alpha}}-\left(\\
\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\cos(y)}{y^{\alpha+1/2}},

and
\alpha<1/2
, we find that the local minima of
G
of occur precisely for

y_{k}=\frac{3\pi}{2}+2\pi k
(for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
).
These minima occur between consecutive zeros of the cosine.
Since
v^{\alpha-1/2}
is decreasing in
v
, the net contribution
to the integral over this period is positive,
since the negative part of the
\cos(v)
is weighted against a smaller value
of
v^{\alpha-1/2}
—see
Fig.

3
.

Figure 3
:
The function
\frac{\cos{v}}{v^{1/2-\alpha}}
.
The positive green area outweighs the negative
red area for a positive net contribution between

y_{k}
and
y_{k+1}
.

More formally,

\displaystyle G(y_{k+1})-G(y_{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{3\pi}{2}+2\pi k}^{\frac{3\pi}{2}+2\pi k+2\pi}\frac{\cos(v)}{v^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{v}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\cos(w+3\pi/2+2\pi k)}{(w+3\pi/2+2\pi k)^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{w}\quad(w=v-3\pi/2-2\pi k)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin(w)}{(w+3\pi/2+2\pi k)^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{w}\quad(\cos(a+3\pi/2)=\sin(a))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin(w)}{(w+3\pi/2+2\pi k)^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{w}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin(w)}{(w+3\pi/2+2\pi k)^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{w}

\displaystyle G(y_{k+1})-G(y_{k})

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin(w)}{(\pi+3\pi/2+2\pi k)^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{w}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin(w)}{(\pi+3\pi/2+2\pi k)^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{w}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\pi+3\pi/2+2\pi k)^{1/2-\alpha}}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin(w)\,\mathrm{d}{w}+\int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\sin(w)\,\mathrm{d}{w}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

This shows that the local minima of
G
are increasing.
Thus,
G(Mx)\geqslant G\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)
uniformly in
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in[0,1]
,
and, similarly,
H(Mx)\geqslant H\left(0\right)=0
, proving
Eq.

5.4
.

Combining
Eqs.

5.2
and
5.4
yields

x^{\alpha+1/2}\widetilde{S}^{\alpha}_{M}(x)\geqslant-C_{1}-x^{\alpha+1/2}C_{0}\geqslant-C/2

(5.5)

uniformly in
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in[0,1]
.

Bounds on
R^{\alpha}_{M}
.

Recall that
R^{\alpha}_{M}(x):=\sum_{m=1}^{M}x^{\alpha+1}j_{m}^{\alpha}R(j_{m}x)
.
By
Eq.

2.4
,
\mathrm{e}^{-j_{m}xt}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-mxt}

for all
x\in[0,1]
and
t\in(0,\infty)
.
On the other hand,
j_{m}\lesssim m
for all
m\geqslant 1
.
Taking
u=xt/2
in
Lemma

5.1
, we have

j_{m}^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-mxt}\lesssim m^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-mxt}=\frac{m^{\alpha}}{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{mxt}{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{mxt}{2}}\lesssim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{mxt}{2}}}{(xt)^{\alpha}}.

Hence, from
Eq.

2.2
we obtain the following bounds for all
x>0
:

\displaystyle\left|R^{\alpha}_{M}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant x^{\alpha+1}\int_{0}^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-mxt}\sqrt{t}\,\mathrm{d}{t}+x^{\alpha+1}\int_{2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{m^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-mxt}}{t}\,\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{mxt}{2}}\frac{x^{\alpha+1}\sqrt{t}}{(xt)^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{t}+\int_{2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{mxt}{2}}\frac{x^{\alpha+1}}{x^{\alpha}t^{\alpha+1}}\,\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-xt/2}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-xt/2}}\frac{x\sqrt{t}}{t^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{t}+\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-xt/2}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-xt/2}}\frac{x}{t^{\alpha+1}}\,\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{xt/2}-1}\frac{x\sqrt{t}}{t^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{t}+\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{xt/2}-1}\frac{x}{t^{\alpha+1}}\,\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{2}\frac{2}{xt}\cdot\frac{x\sqrt{t}}{t^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{t}+\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{2}{xt}\cdot\frac{x}{t^{\alpha+1}}\,\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{t^{\alpha+1/2}}\,\mathrm{d}{t}+\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{\alpha+2}}\,\mathrm{d}{t}\leqslant C/2.

uniformly in
x>0
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
Hence, we can take
x\to 0^{+}
and obtain uniform bounds on
\left|R^{\alpha}_{M}(x)\right|

for
x\in[0,1]
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
In conjunction with
Eq.

5.5
, we deduce the result from
Eq.

5.1
.

Remark
.

A slight adaptation of the above argument also shows that the even kernel
K^{\alpha,e}_{M}(x)

is uniformly bounded below.
In this case we use the integrals

\int_{j_{2m}-\pi}^{j_{2m}+\pi}\cos(ux-\pi/4)\,\mathrm{d}{u}=\frac{2\sin(\pi x)}{x}\cos(j_{2m}x-\pi/4)

to obtain the relation

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\cos(j_{2m}x-\pi/4)}{j_{m}^{1/2-\alpha}}=\frac{x/2}{\sin(\pi x)}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\int_{j_{2m}-\pi}^{j_{2m}+\pi}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}+O(1).

This time the intervals
[j_{2m}-\pi,j_{2m}+\pi]
almost partition the larger interval

[j_{2}-\pi,j_{2M}+\pi]
, with errors occurring around the midpoints

\frac{j_{2m+2}-j_{2m}}{2}=\pi+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right),

and so we again have

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\cos(j_{2m}x-\pi/4)}{j_{m}^{1/2-\alpha}}=\frac{x/2}{\sin(\pi x)}\int_{0}^{j_{2M}+\pi}\frac{\cos(ux-\pi/4)}{u^{1/2-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}{u}+O(1).

The remainder in this case takes on the form

\sum_{m=1}^{M}x^{\alpha+1}j_{m}^{\alpha}R(j_{2m}x),

and can be dealt with in the same way, writing

j_{m}^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-2mxt}\lesssim m^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-2mxt}=\frac{m^{\alpha}}{\mathrm{e}^{mxt}}\mathrm{e}^{-mxt}\lesssim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-mxt}}{(xt)^{\alpha}}.

6
Comments for further research

We mentioned in the introduction that our
Theorem

1.4

does not quite give us that
Theorem

1.2
is ‘sharp’.
Our result requires an extra
\sqrt{x}
, loses a factor
\sqrt{j_{m}}
in the decay, and leaves the end-point
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
open.
We saw in the proof that these ‘limitations’ essentially come from
Lemma

3.3
, since we require higher
powers of
x
to ensure that the remainder terms in the asymptotics for
J_{0}

remain uniformly bounded as
x\to 0^{+}
.

If, on the other hand, we were able to show that
K_{M}^{\alpha}\geqslant 0
directly,
then we obtain the stronger
Theorem

4.1
, where we always get
\sqrt{x}f(x)\in L^{1}

for any value of
0\leqslant\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
, and, assuming
further that
\left|J_{1}(j_{m})\right|
is decreasing,
we can show the end-point case with
xf(x)\in L^{1}
.

Based on our numerical calculations—see
Fig.

1
—we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 2
.

Given any non-negative sequence
a_{m}\to 0
,
there is an
L^{1}(x\,\mathrm{d}{x})
function
f

whose Bessel–Fourier coefficients of order
0
,
f_{m}
, satisfy

0\leqslant a_{m}\leqslant\frac{f_{m}}{\sqrt{j_{m}}}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad m\to\infty.

In order to prove the existence of an
L^{1}
function on the disc,
whose 2-dimensional Bessel–Fourier series is not summable,
we need to make an additional assumption.

Conjecture 3
.

The function in
Conjecture

2
can be chosen such that

f_{2m}=0\quad\text{and}\quad f_{2m+1}\geqslant\sqrt{j_{m}}a_{m}\quad\text{for all}\quad m\in\mathbb{N},

or with

f_{2m}\geqslant\sqrt{j_{m}}a_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{2m+1}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.

This conjecture, while a natural generalisation of
Theorem

1.2
to
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
, is less certain.
Indeed, when
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
, we were able to ‘eliminate’ the even (or odd) coefficients
because we had enough powers of
\frac{1}{j_{m}}
to ensure that the kernel
K_{M}^{\alpha}

was absolutely summable in
m
as
M\to\infty
.
But when
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
, we have to invoke oscillations in order to retain convergence,
and so both even and odd coefficients must make an appearance.
A search for an alternative proof that
K_{M}^{\alpha}\geqslant 0
might yield a way around this ‘limitation’.

Assuming both
Conjecture

2
and
Conjecture

3
, we can prove the following proposition.

Proposition 6.1
.

Assume that
Conjecture

2
and
Conjecture

3
are both true.
Then, there is a function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{D})
such that the partial sums

S_{M,N}(r,\theta)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{n,m}(f)J_{\left|n\right|}(j_{\left|n\right|,m}r)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}n\theta}

diverge in
L^{1}(r\,\mathrm{d}{r}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta})
for any choice of
M=M_{k},N=N_{k}\to\infty

as
k\to\infty
.

Proof.

If the conjectures are true, then by taking
a_{m}=\frac{1}{\log{m}}
,
we can find a function
g\in L^{1}([0,1],x\,\mathrm{d}{x})

whose 1-dimensional Bessel–Fourier coefficients satisfy and

g_{2m}=0\quad\text{and}\quad g_{2m+1}\geqslant\frac{\sqrt{j_{0,m}}}{\log(m)}.

Set
f(r,\theta):=g(r)
, so that the 2-dimensional Bessel–Fourier series of
f

is given by

S_{M,N}f(r,\theta)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}g_{m}J_{0}(j_{0,m}r),

and so

\left\lVert S_{M,N}f\right\rVert_{L^{1}({\mathbb{D}})}\geqslant\left|\int_{0}^{1}S_{M,N}f(r,\theta)r\,\mathrm{d}{r}\right|=\left|\sum_{m=1}^{M}g_{m}\int_{0}^{1}J_{0}(j_{0,m}r)r\,\mathrm{d}{r}\right|.

Using
Eq.

2.7
we have

\int_{0}^{1}S_{M,N}f(r,\theta)r\,\mathrm{d}{r}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{g_{m}}{j_{0,m}^{2}}\int_{0}^{j_{0,m}}J_{0}(y)y\,\mathrm{d}{y}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{g_{m}}{j_{0,m}}J_{1}(j_{0,m}).

Now, since
J_{1}=-J_{0}^{\prime}
, it is not hard to see that the signs of
J_{1}(j_{0,m})

alternate, with
J_{1}(j_{0,2m})<0
(i.e.,
J_{0}
is decreasing at
j_{0,2m}
)
and
J_{1}(j_{0,2m+1})>0
(i.e.,
J_{0}
is increasing at
j_{0,2m+1}
).
Since
g_{2m}=0
for all
m
, the partial sums are non-negative and therefore,
using

\frac{\left|J_{1}(j_{0,m})\right|}{\sqrt{j_{0,m}}}\gtrsim\frac{1}{j_{0,m}}

from
Eq.

2.5
, we obtain

\left\lVert S_{N,M}f\right\rVert_{L^{1}({\mathbb{D}})}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{g_{2m+1}}{\sqrt{j_{0,2m+1}}}\frac{\left|J_{1}(j_{0,2m+1})\right|}{\sqrt{j_{0,2m+1}}}\gtrsim\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{m\log(2m+1)}\to\infty,

as required.
∎
