Obturator hernia associated with inclusion body myositis: a case report
Authors: Yuma Sato, Takashi Sugiyama, Kenjiro Uchino, Hitoshi Mochizuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInclusion body myositis is a progressive muscle disease characterized by weakness, specifically in the flexor digitorum profundus and quadriceps muscles, and commonly affects men over 50 years of age. No previous reports have examined the impact of inclusion body myositis on the internal and external obturator muscles. This case study suggests that inclusion body myositis-related fat infiltration of internal and external obturator muscles may contribute to obturator hernia.Case presentationWe present the case of a 75-year-old Japanese woman admitted for management of an obturator hernia. At age 62 years, her neurological symptoms began with fatigue in the lower legs and dysphagia, and a muscle biopsy confirmed inclusion body myositis. At age 75 years, she presented to the emergency department with sudden right lower abdominal pain. Abdominal computed tomography revealed a fluid-filled small intestine segment between the right obturator externus and pectineus muscles. Thus, she was diagnosed with right obturator hernia. Following surgery, her symptoms improved, allowing for hospital discharge.ConclusionInclusion body myositis-related muscle atrophy is not well documented. Beyond its characteristic muscle weakness and dysphagia, obturator hernia may be a complication in patients with inclusion body myositis. Thus, considering the potential severity of obturator hernias, clinicians should remain vigilant for symptoms other than muscle weakness.

Introduction

Inclusion body myositis (IBM) is the most common acquired myopathy in individuals aged over 50 years, with a prevalence of 24.8–45.6/1,000,000 people [1]. It mainly causes slow, progressive weakness in the flexor digitorum profundus and quadriceps femoris muscles, although some patients also develop facial muscle weakness and dysphagia [2, 3]. Despite treatment with oral prednisolone in many cases, responses to therapy are typically limited and poor [1]. The diagnosis is based on muscle pathology, but the precise cause remains unclear.

Obturator hernia is a rare condition that affects approximately 0.1% of all patients with hernia [4]. It occurs when the small intestine prolapses between the pectineus and external obturator muscles, and it is more common in thin, elderly women owing to their wider pelvis and reduced preperitoneal adipose tissue [4].

In this case, computed tomography (CT) scans of the pelvic region in a patient with IBM revealed severe atrophy with fat infiltration of the pelvic muscles, including the external and internal obturator muscles. We hypothesized that IBM-related muscle atrophy contributes to obturator hernia. Although no previous reports link IBM to obturator hernia, we propose a potential association between the two.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 75-year-old Japanese patient with IBM who had a history of mastectomy for noninvasive ductal carcinoma at the age of 66 years, a herniated lumbar disk at age 73 years, and left obturator hernia surgery 4 months before admission. She had no other comorbidities and no family history of IBM, and had never smoked. She had experienced lower leg fatigue and dysphagia since 2010 and began neurology follow-up at a general hospital in 2012. Following magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), a T2-weighted MRI image with fat-suppression showed high signal intensities in the bilateral quadriceps femoris muscles, suggesting myositis. Furthermore, a muscle biopsy confirmed IBM. She had been taking 2–10 mg of oral prednisolone from 2012 to 2023, but she discontinued this medication from January 2023 as it was ineffective.

During her first visit to our hospital in March 2023, she exhibited slight tongue atrophy. Medical Research Council scores for her muscles were as follows: 3/3 (right/left) for the sternocleidomastoid, 3/3 for the deltoid, 5−/4 for the biceps brachii, 5−/4 for the triceps brachii, 5/5 for the wrist extensors, 4/4 for the wrist flexors, 4/4− for the finger extensors, 3/3− for the iliopsoas, 2/2− for the quadriceps, and 5/5− for the tibialis anterior. Her grip strength was 8.9/4.2 kg (right/left). She required a wheelchair for mobility. The distinct pattern of her muscle weakness, particularly in the quadriceps muscle and distal flexor muscles of the upper limbs, was consistent with IBM [3].

In 2023, she presented to the emergency department with sudden right lower abdominal pain. On admission, her height, weight, and body mass index were 158.1 cm, 44.5 kg, and 17.8, respectively. Vital signs including blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation level were 107/53 mmHg, 70 bpm, and 99% on room air, respectively; heart and respiratory sounds were normal. She had severe pain and tenderness in the right lower abdomen but no peritoneal signs. The Howship–Romberg sign was also absent.

Blood tests indicated a white blood cell count of 7,200/µL; absence of C-reactive protein content; and normal levels of aspartate transaminase, alanine transaminase, lactate dehydrogenase, and creatinine kinase. Cell blood count, total protein, and albumin levels were also normal. Moreover, electrocardiogram and chest X-ray revealed no abnormalities. Abdominal CT showed a fluid-filled small intestine segment between the right obturator externus and pectineus muscles (Fig. 1), confirming a right obturator hernia. On the same day, she underwent urgent surgery via an intraabdominal approach, through a midline abdominal incision, and after releasing the hernia, the obturator canal orifice was closed with a 3-cm square mesh. Postoperatively, her blood test results and symptoms improved substantially. She returned to her presurgery state and was discharged in a stable condition after 9 days of hospitalization. Subsequently, she has remained free of abdominal pain.

Discussion

Previous studies have investigated muscle atrophy and fat infiltration patterns in IBM. An MRI study revealed that 64 proximal and distal muscles of 12 patients with IBM exhibited atrophy and fat infiltration in the quadriceps, flexor digitorum profundus, and gastrocnemius muscles [5]. Similarly, a CT-based study reported atrophy and fat infiltration in the quadriceps, adductor, and gastrocnemius muscles [6]. However, no prior studies have examined the internal and external obturator muscles.

Figure 1 presents a pelvic CT scan of this case alongside a normal control. Although the iliopsoas and pectineal muscles showed no notable differences, the patient with IBM exhibited pronounced atrophy and a marked reduction in CT values in the internal and external obturator muscles, suggesting fat infiltration. We hypothesized that IBM contributed to fat infiltration in these muscles. Although the exact mechanism remains unclear, myeloid-derived cells are considered to play a role in fat infiltration, weakening the affected muscles [7]. The obturator foramen is structurally reinforced by a quadrilamellar musculoaponeurotic barrier comprising the internal and external obturator membranes and muscles [8].

To confirm that IBM caused the fat infiltration, it is essential to exclude other potential causes. Fat infiltration of muscle can result from the following conditions: various neuromuscular disorders, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy [9]; metabolic diseases, such as Pompe disease (glycogen storage disease type II) [10]; age-related sarcopenia (a group of muscle diseases including IBM) [11]; and disuse atrophy [12]. However, in this case, muscle biopsy findings did not indicate a metabolic disorder, and the distribution of muscle weakness was not consistent with other neurological diseases. Additionally, aging alone was unlikely to explain the changes, as the control subject of similar age showed no comparable abnormalities.

Therefore, the fat infiltration observed in the internal and external obturator muscles was attributable to IBM. IBM generally has a favorable prognosis regarding cardiac and respiratory function, but progressive weakness in walking and swallowing markedly affects the daily life of patients. Indeed, one-third of patients require a wheelchair within 14 years of onset, and all patients become wheelchair-bound within 20 years, with a median age of death of 81 years. Pneumonia caused by dysphagia is the leading cause of death [13]. On the basis of this case, we propose that IBM may predispose patients to obturator hernia, which has a reported mortality rate of 3.9% [14]. Although muscle weakness is the primary manifestation of IBM, awareness of potential nonmotor complications, such as dysphagia and obturator hernia, is crucial.

Conclusion

Herein, we report a case of obturator hernia associated with IBM. Considering that nonmotor complications, such as dysphagia and obturator hernia, can be life-threatening, careful monitoring for these symptoms is essential in patients with IBM. As this is the first reported case, further studies are needed to confirm the association between IBM and obturator hernia.
