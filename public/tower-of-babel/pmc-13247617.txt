Developing a Pediatric Pain Curriculum for Pediatric Anesthesia Fellows in Sub‐Saharan Africa: A Delphi Study
Authors: Anisa Bhettay, Rebecca Gray, Romy Parker, Nikki Allorto, Abigail Kusi Amponsah, Brian Anderson, Lillian Baranzila, Charles Carapinha, Susan Carolus, Lionel Green‐Thompson, Onica Higgins, Sonya Kolman, O' Brien Kyololo, Gillian Lamacraft, Helen Laycock, Samuel Matula, Kutlo Mutlhobogwa, Shireen Modack, Israa Modack, Timothy Mwiti, Raymond Ndikontar, Kazeem Oshikoya, Asma Salloo, Jenny Thomas, Salome Maswime
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundComprehensive pain management in children requires a specialized skillset, with a limited number of clinicians possessing the level of expertise required to successfully navigate the complexities of holistic care. The emergence of pediatric anesthesia fellowship programs in sub‐Saharan Africa presents an opportunity to embed a pediatric pain curriculum for trainees, improving the availability of specialist skill and knowledge in the field. Existing pain curricula fall short in addressing the sociocultural aspects of pediatric pain identified through research as being unique to the African context, and do not include elements of leadership and advocacy training required to navigate the complexities of resource‐constrained healthcare settings.MethodsA Delphi survey including literature review, iterative rounds of surveys and expert consensus was used to establish a pediatric pain curriculum for pediatric anesthesia fellows undertaking advanced training in sub‐Saharan Africa. The 22‐member expert panel included anesthetists, nurses, surgeons, pharmacists, pediatricians, a physiotherapist and a patient‐caregiver dyad with a lived experience of pain. After completing three rounds of surveys, a steering committee of five members was assembled to resolve outstanding items to achieve the final curriculum.ResultsThe process yielded a curriculum containing 20 knowledge items and 23 skills items. Attitudes are a key component of the curriculum and were grouped into six themes. A further aspect of the process was the identification of foundational knowledge with which trainees should enter a fellowship training program. This was termed the foundational curriculum.ConclusionUsing a Delphi method, consensus has been achieved on a pediatric pain curriculum for pediatric anesthesia fellows in sub‐Saharan Africa with potential to meet the identified need for transformative pain care in this patient population.

Introduction

There is an urgent need to advance the field of pediatric pain worldwide [1]. Children in sub‐Saharan Africa (SSA) carry a disproportionate burden of pain when compared to children in high‐income countries (HICs) [2]. With finite resources in a region characterized by a low human development index, the highest under‐five mortality rate globally, and a low healthcare workforce density, pediatric pain management in SSA is not prioritized by clinicians, scientists, policy makers, and politicians [3, 4, 5].

This global imperative is particularly urgent in SSA, where systemic constraints and a lack of specialized training infrastructure further compound the challenges of delivering effective pediatric pain care. There are inadequate numbers of pediatric pain experts and services in SSA, and no dedicated pediatric pain fellowships, resulting in limited exposure to comprehensive pediatric pain management during anesthesia and other medical specialist training. Although the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) provides curricula for pain management for healthcare professionals at undergraduate level, there is no specific pediatric pain curriculum, and no curriculum for trainees undertaking advanced training at subspecialist level [6, 7]. The Royal College of Anesthetists of the United Kingdom provides a detailed pain curriculum for anesthesia registrar training, but that curriculum was designed for a context where training is substantially longer than most training programs (seven years), with greater exposure to established pain services and experts, and a different spectrum of childhood disease within a different socioeconomic and cultural context [8].

These and other pain curricula published from high‐income settings share a broadly aligned, biopsychosocial framework [7, 8, 9, 10]. Core elements include foundational pain science, systematic pain assessment and outcome measurement, multimodal management encompassing pharmacologic, psychological, physical, and interventional strategies, and major pain conditions across acute, chronic, cancer‐related, and perioperative contexts, with explicit acknowledgment of children as a special population group. They emphasize interprofessional practice, patient safety, ethical prescribing, and progressive competency development within structured training pathways, assuming access to multidisciplinary teams and established health‐system infrastructure.

Despite their strengths, these curricula have limited contextual relevance for pediatric pain practice in SSA. Pediatric content, while explicitly mentioned, is generally not developed to the depth required for specialist pediatric pain care (e.g., neonatal and infant pain assessment, caregiver‐mediated interventions, and high‐volume procedural pain). The curricula assume consistent access to multidisciplinary teams and pediatric pain expertise, advanced monitoring, imaging, infusion devices, and interventional pain technologies, which may be unavailable [11, 12]. They provide limited guidance on task‐sharing, resource‐adapted analgesic pathways, and safe “minimum‐necessary” approaches to pain management. Dominant emphases on opioid addiction mitigation do not adequately address settings where the primary challenge is inadequate access to essential analgesics, opiophobia, fragmented supply chains, and a high burden of trauma, burns, infection‐related pain, and late‐presenting surgical disease in children [12, 13].

In light of these limitations, clinical fellowship programs present a strategic opportunity to address the training gap, and will facilitate the embedding of pediatric pain competencies within advanced anesthesia education. Clinical fellowship programs are designed to provide a comprehensive learning experience for clinicians to gain knowledge, skills, and competencies in a chosen field. Pediatric pain management, with its unique approach and special considerations, is a fundamental part of advanced training for pediatric anesthetists [14, 15]. Training in advanced pain management strategies aims to equip fellows with the knowledge and skills to positively impact pain management and attitudes to enable management with cultural humility in different contexts. The initiation of new pediatric anesthesia fellowship programs in some countries in SSA offers a timely opportunity to develop a pain curriculum for this clinician group.

This study aimed to develop a curriculum in pediatric pain management for specialist anesthetists undertaking advanced clinical training in pediatric anesthesia in SSA.

Methods

The study was approved by the University of Cape Town Faculty of Health Sciences Human Research Ethics Committee (ref 635/2021). The Delphi technique provides a structured, iterative approach that facilitates consensus‐building among a diverse panel of experts across geographical and institutional boundaries. The methodology and reporting were guided by Nasa et al. and the Conducting and REporting of DElphi Studies (CREDES) (Appendix S1) [16, 17]. Through anonymized rounds of feedback and controlled iteration, the Delphi technique supports equitable participation, moderating the influence of dominant voices, and enhances the validity and credibility of the final curriculum. The Delphi protocol was developed a priori by two of the authors (AB and RP), and registered with Open Science Framework (Appendix S2) [18].

Selection of Panelists

English‐speaking clinicians/researchers with expertise in pediatric pain were recruited from the sub‐Saharan region, supplemented by international experts where regional expertise was not available. Eligible participants included clinicians practicing in disciplines relevant to pain management in hospitalized children, including, but not limited to, anesthetists, nurses, pediatricians, pediatric intensivists, surgeons, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, pharmacists, and psychologists. Potential participants were identified by screening the authors of articles included in a scoping review on pediatric pain in SSA [12], and identifying known leaders in the field of pediatric pain. Researchers were considered eligible if they were the first author of at least one publication or the co‐author of at least two publications on pediatric pain. Clinicians were considered eligible if they had clinical experience of at least two years in pediatric pain management. Snowball sampling was used to identify other healthcare providers from within SSA with the appropriate experience.

Twenty‐three participants representing at least three disciplines were recruited to accommodate for a 5%–10% attrition rate with each round, while still allowing for adequate representation and panel size by completion of the process.

Potential panelists were invited to participate via email, which included a summary of the study's aims and objectives. Interested individuals were asked to provide their curriculum vitae for review and evaluation against the eligibility criteria. To ensure a diverse and representative panel, criteria were weighted to prioritize interdisciplinary and regional representation (Appendix S3). A patient‐caregiver dyad was also included, identified from one of the author's (AB) clinical cohort, to represent persons with a lived experience of pain.

Procedure

Preparatory steps included identifying key articles to inform the panelists' opinions, and collating existing pain curricula for comparison. Three Delphi survey rounds took place between August 2024 and March 2025 using an electronic (e‐Delphi) technique, with panelists remaining anonymous to each other and known only to the moderator (Figure 1). Two reminders were sent before concluding each round for data analysis.

Procedure: Stages of the Delphi process.

In the first round of the Delphi survey, panelists were sent five key articles identified as pertinent to informing opinion and invited to propose core topics/content and skills for inclusion into a curriculum for pediatric anesthesia fellows in SSA.

For round two, panelists were presented with the collated results from round one combined with additional content drawn from the curricula of the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP), the Royal College of Anesthetists (UK), and the Australia and New Zealand College of Anesthetists. A composite list of competencies was compiled, and panelists were asked to rate these items in terms of relevance for inclusion, using a 5‐point Likert scale (not relevant—extremely relevant). To avoid bias, no distinction was made between panelists' answers and items drawn from established curricula. Responses were coded, and median and interquartile range (IQR) were calculated for each item. Consensus on each item was reached if the IQR range was ≤ 1, indicating an agreement of more than 50%, and these items were carried forward to the next round. These items were then categorized into themes with the assistance of Perplexity.ai.

To render the emerging curriculum achievable in the context of a one‐year fellowship designed to expose fellows to all aspects of pediatric anesthesia, a protocol deviation was required: Authors AB, RP and LGT refined the outputs to recognize foundational or advanced content. The output was separated into the basic knowledge and skills trainees should be expected to have in order to enter into fellowship training (Table 2), and an advanced pediatric pain curriculum appropriate for pediatric anesthesia fellowship training (Table 3). Items were categorized into these two distinctions by consensus between authors AB and RP.

For round three, panelists were asked to consider the categorization into foundational and advanced levels to finalize the proposed curriculum. They were given options (1) Agree (with categorization), (2) Move item from advanced to foundational level, (3) Remove item (from curriculum), and where removal was suggested, rationale for this action was requested. Agreement consensus was set at 80%.

At the end of round three, four items did not reach majority agreement. This necessitated a second protocol deviation, in which a steering committee was convened to enable a moderated panel discussion to achieve resolution. Items for which > 80% majority were not reached during round three were taken to this committee at a virtual consensus meeting held in April 2025. The steering committee consisted of five members (BA, RG, LGT, RP, JT). Three members chosen from the panel had raised constructive questions/comments during round three, which served as a catalyst for the protocol deviation (BA, RG, NA, JT). One declined to participate due to time constraints. Additional members of the committee were chosen for either their content or curriculum development and medical education expertise (RP, LGT). Members were provided with the items requiring discussion beforehand. For this discussion, AB fulfilled the role of moderator.

Results

The final panel included 20 clinicians from multiple disciplines, and one dyad of two persons with a lived experience of pain (total = 22) from seven countries in SSA (Botswana, Cameroon, Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, Tanzania), two high‐income countries (HICs) (New Zealand, UK), and one low‐and‐middle‐income country from outside SSA (Guyana). The panel included eight anesthetists (five pediatric anesthetists), four nurses (three pediatric nurses), three pediatric surgeons, one burn surgeon, two pharmacists, a pediatric intensivist, a physiotherapist, and one dyad of two persons with a lived experience of pain (patient‐caregiver). One panelist was a pediatric anesthetist/intensivist and pharmacologist. Panelists' areas of research/clinical expertise spanned acute, procedural, and chronic pain. The persons with a lived experience of pain were diagnosed with a chronic pain condition in adolescence which was managed at a multidisciplinary pediatric chronic pain clinic, with multiple hospital admissions due to acute‐on‐chronic pain. Further detail on panelists' roles and areas of expertise is available in Appendix S4.

Delphi Survey Round One

The response rate for round one was 100% (22/22). Responses for core topics/content areas and skills were grouped into knowledge, skills and attitudes by applying the AfriMEDS framework (adopted by the HPCSA for medical education) [19]. This yielded 37 knowledge items, 29 skills items, and 10 attitudes.

Delphi Survey Round Two

The response rate for round two was 95.5% (21/22). Likert responses were converted to numeric codes, and median and interquartile range (IQR) were calculated for each item (Appendix S5). Consensus on each item was reached if the IQR range was ≤ 1. Six knowledge items were removed from further consideration based on responses, leaving 31 items. Two skills items did not achieve consensus, leaving 27 skills items. One attitude was removed, and the remaining attitudes were refined thematically and incorporated into the final curriculum (Table 3). The panelist who did not respond to round two was not included in round three.

Delphi Survey Round Three

There were 31 knowledge items, and 27 skills items carried forward for round three. The response rate for this round was 100% (21/21). For the knowledge items: the consensus threshold of 80% was achieved for 19 items, and 11 items achieved agreement by all panelists. For the skills items: the consensus threshold of 80% was achieved for 21 items, and three items achieved agreement by all panelists. Four items remained unresolved—no majority agreement was reached, with panelists wanting to move items from the advanced to foundational level for three of the four items, and disagreeing with the foundational level assignment for one item.

Steering Committee Meeting

After a brief discussion providing background and rationale for discussion of each item, a feasible question or curriculum item was crafted, and a poll created. Agreement of 80% (4/5) or higher by the members of the steering committee was deemed sufficient for consensus and new/modified wording was generated. Items discussed are listed in Table 1, 2, 3.

Discussion

A curriculum has emerged from this Delphi study which is intentionally designed for pediatric anesthesia fellows practicing in SSA, distinct from other published curricula—which define high‐quality pain care largely within well‐resourced systems. It is resource‐aware, culturally grounded, and leadership‐oriented, preparing fellows not only to deliver pain care, but to adapt, advocate, and build sustainable pediatric pain services in resource‐constrained settings.

There is advanced emphasis on developmental neurobiology, developmental pharmacology, and the care of highly vulnerable children. It is unique in that it operationalizes practice in resource‐constrained settings by explicitly teaching graded, context‐adapted pain management strategies, ethical decision‐making around available versus aspirational care, and safe practice in the absence of ideal infrastructure. It integrates cultural pluralism, traditional medicine, and social determinants of health as clinical competencies, and emphasizes leadership, advocacy, service development, and locally relevant research as essential skills essential for fostering transformative learning. This approach reframes pediatric pain education from the delivery of pain care within established systems to the development of clinicians equipped to adapt, lead, and build sustainable pediatric pain services in low‐resource and culturally diverse environments [21].

The Delphi method was particularly appropriate given the limited availability of context‐specific, evidence‐based training curricula and the need to integrate regional expertise and lived experience in resource‐constrained settings. The method enabled the inclusion of a diverse range of experts and educators from across the region and further afield, ensuring that the curriculum reflects both global standards and local realities. In response to the emerging data, two deviations from the protocol were required to focus the content for advanced trainees (fellows) and to finalize the curriculum.

Kalafatis et al. explored South African anesthetists' fitness for purpose, highlighting consistent gaps in non‐technical skills—particularly leadership and health advocacy [22]. These authors emphasize the importance of contextually adapted curricula and assessment frameworks [23]. Their Delphi study assessed the CanMEDS framework's fitness for purpose in the South African context [24]. An adapted CanMEDS framework was proposed with added meta‐competencies of context awareness and humaneness. This aligns with our Delphi panel's recommendations for the competencies included in the curriculum.

The curriculum developed in this study shares several structural features with the advanced pediatric pain fellowship described by Matava et al. at the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto—notably the organization of competencies across clinical, research, education, and administrative domains, and the use of an adapted CanMEDS framework to define fellowship‐level expectations [22, 23, 24, 25]. Both programs recognize research literacy and leadership as core advanced competencies. However, the Toronto fellowship was developed within a single, well‐resourced institution with reliable access to multidisciplinary teams, advanced regional anesthesia, and pain expertise. In contrast, the presented curriculum was designed for diverse sub‐Saharan African settings in which such infrastructure may be absent or inconsistent. It extends beyond clinical and academic domains to incorporate adaptive leadership, change management, health advocacy, and cultural humility—competencies indispensable for practice change in resource‐constrained and culturally diverse environments—and explicitly addresses the sociocultural dimensions of pain unique to the African context [12].

Fellows enter advanced training with differing levels of foundational knowledge and variable practice experiences. It is widely reported that undergraduate programs at medical schools lack teaching on pediatric pain management [6, 11]. Encouragingly, most respondents to a survey conducted among anesthetists in Nigeria, South Africa, Uganda, and Zambia reported receiving teaching in pediatric pain during their anesthesia registrar training [11]. The foundational curriculum of fellowship entry‐level knowledge generated as a result of this Delphi process serves as a useful benchmark and a potential blueprint for registrar training programs in anesthesia. Bridging potential gaps via online education modules (e‐learning) or pre‐fellowship “boot camps” may allow aspiring fellows to obtain the required foundational knowledge before embarking upon advanced training [6, 26, 27, 28].

The curriculum is not prescriptive in terms of content detail, implementation, or methods of assessment. Contextualization by local fellowship directors is key, and creative solutions to improving exposure to certain aspects of pediatric pain may be required ‐ e.g., virtual chronic pain clinic attendance, and virtual acute pain rounds. Partnerships and collaboration with institutions with established pain services and expertise could assist in meeting these needs [28]. For each skills domain, it is suggested that learning objectives be identified by the fellow with their mentor/supervisor to achieve the required level of competence according to the suggested adapted framework (Table 4) [29, 30].

Sub‐Saharan Africa is a region of extraordinary cultural, linguistic, and ethnic diversity, home to over 1000 languages and a mosaic of traditions, belief systems, and healthcare practices [31, 32]. These differences are deeply woven into the social fabric of communities and shape how pain is experienced, expressed, understood, and managed, particularly in children. Given this rich landscape, it is crucial that clinicians approach pain assessment and management in children with cultural humility and curiosity, and cultivate “cultural competency.” These cultural nuances must be reflected not only in clinical practice but also in the educational frameworks that prepare clinicians to deliver equitable and effective pain care.

While some of the items in the proposed curriculum are universally applicable, they still require contextualization to ensure appropriateness and relevance. Post‐fellowship anesthetic practitioners experience significant challenges, yet also encounter key enablers in their desire to effect change [33]. The inclusion of adaptive leadership training encompassing competencies in advocacy will enhance the ability for knowledge translation and practice impact.

In addition to leadership and advocacy, the ability to generate and apply context‐specific evidence is critical for driving sustainable improvements in pediatric pain care. A scoping review of pediatric pain in SSA highlighted the need for high quality research on pediatric pain in the region to inform the development of targeted interventions and policies to improve pain management [12]. The steering committee agreed that equipping fellows with the knowledge and skills to conduct robust research was a key asset in this curriculum. This echoes the IASP recommendations that every advanced curriculum embeds research literacy [7].

While the development of a robust, evidence‐informed curriculum is essential, its successful implementation depends on addressing the practical constraints faced by clinicians in resource‐limited settings. Valid discussion points were raised about the challenges of curriculum implementation, particularly relating to the lack of access to ultrasound devices and certain consumable items ‐ e.g., epidural sets, child‐friendly patient‐controlled analgesia (PCA) devices. While these concerns deserve consideration, the steering committee, reflecting on the wide range of practice environments in which anesthetic practitioners may practice, decided that the curriculum should remain aspirational, and could be used as a tool to motivate for the necessary equipment. The benefits of good regional anesthesia techniques are particularly important in low‐resource settings, and should become a focal point of advocacy.

To ensure the curriculum has meaningful impact beyond its design, attention must be given to how it is disseminated and embedded within existing educational and professional structures. Avenues and platforms for dissemination of the curriculum include the colleges and societies of anesthesia and pain within SSA. It has been translated into French for use in French‐speaking African countries (Appendix S6).

The breadth and depth of the curriculum may not be achievable in certain settings during a 12‐month fellowship designed to equip fellows with a wide range of competencies. A third tier of training may be required, specifically targeting pediatric pain competencies. Alternatively, specific gaps could be filled through intentional continued professional development after exiting fellowship training.

Positioning the curriculum within broader global efforts to transform pediatric pain care further reinforces its relevance and potential for systemic change. The Lancet Child and Adolescent Health Commission in 2020 proposed four goals aimed at achieving “transformative action in pediatric pain.” These included “making pain matter,” “making pain understood,” “making pain visible,” and “making pain better.” [1] These simple statements represent challenges that lie at the core of global realization. By mapping closely onto the IASP core domains while deliberately expanding into advocacy, change management and resource‐limited practice, the curriculum is positioned to produce clinicians who can both deliver and scale evidence‐based pediatric pain care.

Limitations

Limitations were analyzed using the CREDES reporting guidance. One notable limitation was the restriction to English‐speaking panelists, which may have excluded valuable perspectives from Francophone and Lusophone regions of SSA. Although the curriculum was later translated into French by author RN (Appendix S6), only one panelist represented Francophone Africa during the Delphi process, potentially limiting the applicability and cultural relevance of the curriculum for these countries.

Conclusions

Through a structured Delphi process, consensus was achieved on a contextually relevant pediatric pain curriculum tailored for fellows in pediatric anesthesia in SSA. The curriculum defines core competencies in knowledge, clinical skills, and professional attitudes necessary for effective pediatric pain management in resource‐constrained and culturally diverse settings. By aligning with global standards while addressing regional realities, it offers a strategic framework to strengthen clinical training, support advocacy, and guide resource allocation. Importantly, the curriculum has the potential to contribute meaningfully to the realization of the four transformative goals proposed by the Lancet Child and Adolescent Health Commission: to make pain matter, understood, visible, and better, by equipping clinicians with the tools to deliver and scale evidence‐based, compassionate pediatric pain care across the region.
