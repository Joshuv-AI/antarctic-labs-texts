Survival analysis of left-truncated and right-censored HIV data: comparison of Cox regression and alternative models
Authors: Tuba Çakır, Yüksel Terzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examined the impact of delayed entry in HIV cohorts by analyzing left-truncated and right-censored (LTRC) survival data from 69 HIV-positive male patients (24 deaths) followed at a tertiary infectious diseases center. The primary objective was to empirically compare commonly used survival models in estimating survival and identifying prognostic factors under LTRC conditions.MethodsThree LTRC-adapted modeling frameworks were applied with identical covariates: the semiparametric Cox proportional hazards model, accelerated failure time (AFT) models, and parametric proportional hazards models. Median survival time was estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods under both right-censoring-only and LTRC specifications to assess truncation-related differences. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained from the LTRC-adjusted Cox model. Model performance was evaluated using information criteria (AIC, BIC, HQIC).ResultsIgnoring left truncation substantially inflated median survival estimates (3,885 vs. 2,626 days). In the LTRC-adjusted Cox model, age (HR = 1.049, 95% CI: 1.011–1.089) and log-transformed HIV RNA (HR = 1.214, 95% CI: 1.055–1.400) were significant predictors, whereas CD4 count and comorbidity status were not. Among the evaluated models, the Cox model showed the lowest information criterion values within this dataset.ConclusionsAppropriate risk-set specification under left truncation is essential for reliable survival estimation in delayed-entry HIV cohorts. Within this empirical dataset, the LTRC-adapted Cox model showed favorable performance relative to AFT and parametric PH alternatives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02874-5.

Introduction

Survival analysis comprises a set of statistical methodologies used to model the time until the occurrence of an event, such as death, disease relapse, or treatment failure. These methods are widely applied across disciplines—including medicine, engineering, economics, and social sciences—and are especially important in clinical research for evaluating patient outcomes [1]. A major methodological challenge in survival data arises from censoring and truncation, which occur when event times cannot be fully observed.

Right-censoring occurs when individuals leave the study before experiencing the event of interest or when the study ends while they remain event-free; thus, the exact event time is only known to exceed a certain point [2]. In contrast, left-truncation arises when subjects enter the study after becoming at risk. As a result, only individuals who survive beyond the truncation time are included in the sample [3, 4]. If not properly accounted for, this selective inclusion mechanism leads to sampling bias and distorted survival estimates.

Several studies have demonstrated that ignoring left-truncation results in inflated and biased survival estimates. For example, Wolfson et al. [5] reported that the median survival of dementia patients was 6.6 years when truncation was ignored but only 3.3 years when truncation was properly accounted for. Asgharian and Wolfson [6] further showed that disregarding truncation can bias both survival time estimation and regression coefficients. Consequently, appropriate modeling of truncation and censoring is essential, particularly in epidemiological settings characterized by delayed entry [7, 8].

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection weakens the immune system, increasing susceptibility to opportunistic infections and malignancies. Although antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV into a chronic, manageable condition, identifying prognostic factors associated with survival remains a critical research focus. Covariates such as age, CD4 cell count, viral load (HIV RNA), and coinfections (e.g., tuberculosis, PCP) have been widely studied for their prognostic relevance [9–11]. HIV cohort studies frequently feature both right-censoring and left-truncation due to late diagnosis or delayed initiation of clinical follow-up [12].

Commonly employed survival modeling approaches include the semiparametric Cox proportional hazards (PH) model [13], accelerated failure time (AFT) model, and fully parametric PH model. The Cox model is widely used for its flexibility and robustness when the proportional hazards assumption holds, although this assumption must be assessed carefully [14]. AFT models serve as valuable alternatives when the logarithm of survival time follows distributions such as Weibull, log-normal, or log-logistic distributions [15, 16]. Parametric PH models can also provide efficient inference when the underlying time-to-event distribution is known or can be reasonably specified [17].

The primary objective of this study is to compare Cox, AFT, and parametric PH models using left-truncated and right-censored HIV patient data to determine the most suitable modeling framework for this data structure. Additionally, the study evaluated the effects of key clinical covariates—age, CD4 + T cell count, HIV RNA level, and comorbidities—on survival time. By quantifying the bias introduced when left-truncation is ignored, the study underscores the methodological importance of appropriately handling delayed entry in survival analysis.

This study is intended as an applied empirical evaluation of survival modeling strategies under LTRC conditions rather than a formal methodological development or simulation-based investigation.

Specifically, this study makes three key contributions to the survival analysis literature.

Structured model comparison

It provides a rigorous comparison of Cox, AFT, and parametric PH models under an explicitly defined left-truncated and right-censored (LTRC) structure using identical covariates, allowing direct evaluation of model behavior under delayed-entry conditions.

Bias and inference evaluation

Beyond quantifying survival inflation when left-truncation is ignored, the study systematically examines how proper risk-set specification affects covariate significance, effect size interpretation, and overall model fit.

Practical modeling implications

The findings demonstrate that model choice and distributional assumptions can materially influence statistical inference and clinical interpretation in delayed-entry survival data. By highlighting how improper risk-set definition may alter substantive conclusions, the study underscores the applied methodological consequences of LTRC specification.

Methods

Study population and data description

This retrospective study utilized clinical follow-up data from 69 HIV-positive male patients who where diagnosed and monitored at the Department of Infectious Diseases, Ondokuz Mayıs University Faculty of Medicine (Samsun, Türkiye). The time of origin for survival analysis was defined as the date of HIV diagnosis (t = 0). The event of interest was all-cause mortality (n = 24). Patients who were alive at the end of the study or lost to follow-up (n = 7) were treated as right-censored, yielding a total of 45 censored observations.

The observation period spanned from January 1, 2006 to January 1, 2020, which served as the common right-censoring time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C$$\end{document}. Although some HIV-positive patients had been diagnosed prior to January 1, 2006, these individuals were not included in the analytical cohort because they were not under active clinical follow-up during the observation window. To account for early clinical variability in laboratory markers, a left-truncation time of 90 days post diagnosis (L = 90) was imposed. This threshold reflects the period required for stabilization of CD4 + T cell counts and HIV RNA viral load levels, reducing bias from early, highly volatile measurements.

All covariates were defined at baseline. Age corresponded to the age at HIV diagnosis. CD4 cell count and HIV RNA viral load values were obtained from the initial clinical assessment following diagnosis and prior to the application of the 90-day left-truncation threshold. Comorbidity status was recorded at baseline based on clinical documentation. All patients received antiretroviral therapy (ART) during follow-up according to routine clinical care; however, because ART exposure was universal in this cohort, it was not included as a covariate in the regression models.

To improve model interpretability and meet statistical assumptions, HIV RNA values were log-transformed using base 10, and the CD4 count was evaluated both continuously and categorically, with a clinically established cutoff of ≤ 200 cells/mm³ to reflect severe immunosuppression.

Survival models

To evaluate survival under left-truncated and right-censored (LTRC) conditions, both nonparametric and model-based approaches were considered. Specifically, the Kaplan–Meier estimator was used as a nonparametric method, alongside three modeling frameworks: the semiparametric Cox proportional hazards (PH) model, accelerated failure time (AFT) models, and fully parametric PH models.

For each approach, appropriate methods were applied to account for delayed entry due to left-truncation. In addition, analyses based on standard right-censored assumptions were also considered to enable comparison with models that properly incorporate left-truncation.

All models were fitted using identical covariates to ensure consistent comparison of effect estimates and model fit across different methodological approaches.

Cox proportional hazards model

The Cox proportional hazards (PH) model [13] is one of the most widely used semiparametric regression approaches in survival analysis. Accelerated Failure Time (AFT) models

The Accelerated failure time (AFT) model provides an important alternative to the Cox proportional hazards model, particularly when the proportional hazards (PH) assumption is violated. While there were originally popular in engineering and industrial reliability analysis, AFT models have become increasingly relevant in biomedical survival studies [19, 20].

Unlike the Cox model, which assumes a multiplicative effect of covariates on the hazard function, the AFT model assumes that covariates act multiplicatively on the survival time itself. AFT model parameters were estimated via maximum likelihood. For left-truncated and right-censored (LTRC) data, the likelihood was conditioned on survival beyond each individual’s truncation time to account for delayed entry.

LTRC-adapted AFT models were fitted under several distributions (Weibull, log-normal, log-logistic, Gompertz, and extreme value) and compared using information criteria (AIC, BIC, HQIC, CAICF) to evaluate relative model fit.

Parametric proportional hazards models

Parametric proportional hazards (PH) models assume that the baseline hazard function follows a specific distribution. When this assumption is approximately valid, parametric PH models typically provide more efficient and precise estimates than semiparametric methods such as the Cox model [23]. These models offer direct expressions for the hazard function, survival probability, and median survival time, often with reduced standard errors.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The study utilized clinical follow-up data from HIV-positive male patients treated at the Department of Infectious Diseases, Ondokuz Mayıs University Medical Faculty Hospital (Samsun, Türkiye). Female patients were excluded due to their small representation in the dataset. The recorded variables included age (years), CD4 cell count (cells/mm³), viral load (HIV RNA, copies/mL), comorbidity status (e.g., tuberculosis, PCP), and diagnosis date.

The CD4 count reflects the number of immune cells targeted by HIV, with lower values indicating greater immunosuppression. Viral load (HIV RNA) quantifies the amount of circulating virus and serves as a key indicator of treatment response. In this study, a truncation time of 90 days post-diagnosis was defined to allow for initial stabilization of CD4 and viral load measurements. The observation period covered 2006–2020, with data right-censored on January 1, 2020 (pre-pandemic).

Among these patients, 45 (65%) were censored, and 24 (35%) experienced the event of interest (death).

The inclusion of left-truncation results in shorter estimated median survival, indicating that ignoring truncation may inflate survival estimates

As shown in Table 3, incorporating left-truncation yields a substantially shorter median survival time, indicating that analyses ignoring delayed entry may lead to overestimation of survival duration.

The apparent discrepancy between mean and median survival estimates reflects their different statistical sensitivities. The median survival time is determined by the point at which the survival probability reaches 0.5 and is therefore primarily influenced by the early portion of the survival curve and risk-set redefinition under left-truncation. In contrast, the mean survival time corresponds to the area under the Kaplan–Meier curve and is affected by the entire follow-up period, including the tail of the distribution. Consequently, modifications in the early risk set may reduce the median survival estimate while simultaneously increasing the estimated mean survival.

It should be noted that the time origin for all analyses was defined as the date of HIV diagnosis (t = 0). The 90-day truncation does not imply that survival times were shifted or that 90 days were added to the estimated mean or median survival. Instead, left-truncation affects only the definition of the risk set, such that individuals contribute to the analysis only after surviving beyond the truncation time.

Interpretation and quantification of bias

Across all the models, the Cox proportional hazards model demonstrated the lowest information criterion values (AIC = 143.774; BIC = 152.710), indicating the best overall fit and parsimony for the LTRC HIV dataset. All the parametric PH and AFT models presented substantially higher AIC/BIC values—for example, the best-performing AFT model had an AIC of 437.895—suggesting inadequate representation of the underlying hazard structure. These findings identify the Cox PH model as the most robust and appropriate approach for this dataset.

A key methodological finding was the quantification of survival bias arising from ignoring left-truncation. When the Cox model was fit to right-censored data only, the estimated median survival was 3885 days. In contrast, the LTRC-adjusted Cox model produced a median survival of 2626 days, representing an artificial inflation of approximately 47%. This substantial discrepancy highlights the severe sampling bias introduced by delayed entry and underscores the necessity of employing LTRC-appropriate modeling techniques in studies of HIV survival.

Interpretation of significant covariates

In the LTRC-adjusted Cox proportional hazards model, age and log-transformed HIV RNA were identified as statistically significant predictors of mortality among HIV-positive patients (p < 0.05).

The estimated hazard ratio for age (HR = 1.05) indicates that each additional year of age increases the risk of death by approximately 5%. This finding suggests that older individuals may experience faster disease progression or diminished response to antiretroviral therapy (ART). This is consistent with previous studies reporting reduced immune recovery and increased comorbidity burden among older HIV-infected adults [25, 26].

Similarly, the hazard ratio associated with log(HIV RNA) (HR = 1.21) demonstrated that a higher viral load substantially elevates the risk of mortality. Increased HIV RNA levels reflect active viral replication and impaired immune function, which increase vulnerability to opportunistic infections. These findings reinforce the central role of achieving and maintaining viral suppression through ART to improve long-term survival outcomes.

In contrast, the CD4 cell count and comorbidity status were not statistically significant predictors in according to LTRC Cox model. This lack of significance may be attributed to the relatively small sample size or to the stabilizing effects of modern ART, which can reduce the variability of CD4 levels and mitigate the short-term impact of opportunistic infections.

Overall, the results highlight age and viral load as the two most robust prognostic factors in this cohort, which is consistent with the model selection results indicating that HIV RNA and age are the most stable predictors across all survival modeling approaches.

Discussion

This study evaluated left-truncated and right-censored survival data from 69 HIV-positive male patients followed at the Infectious Diseases Department of Ondokuz Mayıs University Medical Faculty Hospital. The primary aim was to compare the performance of Cox proportional hazards (PH), parametric PH, and accelerated failure time (AFT) models in estimating survival and identifying prognostic factors under delayed entry conditions a common feature in HIV cohorts due to late diagnosis, irregular follow-up patterns, and the time required for initial clinical stabilization.

Consistent with prior studies, younger individuals tend to present with higher baseline HIV RNA levels and delayed viral suppression [27]. In our LTRC-adjusted analyses, age emerged as a significant predictor, with each additional year increasing mortality risk by approximately 5%. This aligns with earlier studies reporting impaired immune recovery and a greater burden of comorbidities among older HIV-infected adults. Similarly, higher viral load measured using log-transformed HIV RNA—was strongly associated with increased mortality risk, reflecting its direct relationship with ongoing viral replication, immune decline, and vulnerability to opportunistic infections.

K–M analyses demonstrated the critical impact of accounting for left-truncation: median survival was overestimated (3,885 vs. 2,626 days) when truncation was ignored. Importantly, the naïve Kaplan–Meier estimator that ignores left-truncation is not targeting the same estimand as the LTRC-adjusted estimator. While the LTRC approach estimates survival conditional on individuals having survived beyond the truncation time, the naïve estimator implicitly assumes full risk-set observability from time zero, leading to a fundamentally different target quantity. This finding supports earlier research [5], which documented substantial upward bias in survival estimates when delayed entry was not modeled appropriately.

Given that the truncation specification plays a central role in defining the risk set, the choice of the truncation threshold warrants clarification. The 90-day left-truncation threshold was prespecified based on routine clinical follow-up intervals in HIV care, where CD4 and viral load assessments are typically conducted at three-month intervals during early treatment. This period is characterized by substantial early immunological and virological variability following diagnosis and treatment initiation. While alternative truncation points could be evaluated in sensitivity analyses, the modest sample size of the present cohort would substantially alter the effective risk set and event counts. Future studies with larger populations may benefit from systematically assessing robustness across different truncation thresholds.

Initial Cox regression using right-censored data suggested that CD4 categories were significant predictors. However, after left-truncation was incorporated, the CD4 count and comorbidity status were no longer significant, whereas age and log(HIV RNA) remained robust predictors. This shift illustrates the methodological importance of correcting the risk set definition; failing to account for delayed entry can lead to misleading clinical interpretations and biased covariate effects.

The relatively small sample size (69 patients, 24 events) limits statistical power, particularly for detecting moderate associations for CD4 count and comorbidity status. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance for certain covariates should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no effect. Importantly, the methodological conclusions regarding the behavior of survival models under left-truncation are conceptually independent of cohort size, whereas the clinical predictor findings are specific to this study population and should be interpreted with caution.

AFT models were explored as alternatives, particularly for scenarios where the proportional hazards assumption might be violated. Although several AFT distributions (e.g., log-logistic, Gompertz) yielded significant covariates, none demonstrated superiority to the Cox model on the basis of information criteria. The extreme value distribution performed best among the AFT models, yet its goodness-of-fit remained considerably lower than the Cox PH model. Similarly, among parametric PH models, the Weibull model provided the best fit, but remained inferior overall.

Collectively, model comparison using AIC, AICc, BIC, CAICF, and HQIC confirmed that the semiparametric Cox model was the most parsimonious and best-fitting approach for this dataset. It should be noted that information criteria derived from partial likelihood (as in the Cox proportional hazards model) and full likelihood (as in fully parametric survival models) are not strictly equivalent from a theoretical standpoint. The Cox model leaves the baseline hazard unspecified and is estimated using partial likelihood rather than a complete parametric likelihood formulation [18]. In contrast, AFT and parametric PH models rely on fully specified likelihood functions. Because likelihood-based information criteria are formally grounded in full likelihood theory [28], the model comparison presented in this study should be interpreted pragmatically as an empirical assessment of relative fit within the observed dataset, rather than as a definitive inferential ranking across fundamentally different likelihood structures. These findings highlight the flexibility and robustness of the Cox model particularly when appropriately adapted to left-truncated and right-censored survival data structures.

Importantly, these methodological considerations have practical implications for public health. Accurate survival estimation is essential for monitoring progress toward the World Health Organization’s 2030 goals, which include eliminating new HIV infections and reducing HIV-related mortality. As Turkey is still in an early phase of its HIV epidemic, rigorous survival modeling using appropriately specified risk sets is vital for guiding clinical decision-making and policy development.

In addition, this study may be subject to length-biased sampling due to delayed entry. Although individuals with survival times shorter than 90 days were excluded to reduce early mortality bias, patients who survived longer were more likely to enter the cohort, while individuals with shorter survival times may have died before observation. Therefore, the observed survival estimates may be higher than the true survival experience in the target population.

Future research could benefit from expanding the sample size and incorporating additional covariates such as sex, substance use, ART adherence, and comorbidity burden. Such enhancements would strengthen predictive accuracy and improve generalizability across more diverse HIV populations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that properly accounting for left-truncation is essential for obtaining unbiased survival estimates in HIV cohorts with delayed entry. When left-truncation was incorporated, estimated survival times decreased substantially and the set of significant predictors changed, underscoring the methodological importance of accurate risk-set specification.

Among the evaluated models, the Cox proportional hazards model showed the most favorable information criterion values within this dataset relative to the AFT and parametric PH alternatives.

Age and log(HIV RNA) count emerged as the strongest prognostic factors, reinforcing the clinical importance of early diagnosis and effective viral suppression strategies. These findings support the continued need for targeted interventions aimed at reducing the viral load and improving long-term outcomes in HIV-infected individuals.

Importantly, this analysis reinforces that ignoring delayed entry can introduce significant upward bias in survival estimation and distort covariate effects. The present findings underscore the necessity of incorporating left-truncation mechanisms in future HIV survival analyses to avoid biased conclusions and misleading clinical recommendations.

Although the present study focuses on empirical evaluation within a real-world HIV cohort, future research may extend these findings through simulation-based investigations to systematically examine estimator performance and truncation-induced bias under varying data-generating scenarios.

Future studies with larger, more diverse cohorts—and with expanded clinical and behavioral variables—are recommended to further improve the prediction accuracy and support health policy planning in alignment with the global 2030 HIV targets.
