Response of continuous cropping cowpea and root-zone soil to composite microbial agents: plant growth promotion and nitrogen-fixing bacterial community regulation
Authors: Jie Zhu, Jiachen Chen, Jie Zhang, Qiangchuan Hou, Kai Luo, Yuqi Li, Peng Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Continuous cropping obstacle of cowpea is an urgent problem to be solved for its sustainable production. A field experiment was performed from April to June 2024 to study the effects of four commercially microbial agents on the growth and yield of continuously cropping cowpea plants and nitrogen-fixing bacterial community structure in root-zone soil by high-throughput sequencing technology. Four commercially microbial agents used were BS (Bacillus subtilis), TH (Trichoderma harzianum), AB (Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, Brevibacillus laterosporus, Bacillus mucilaginosus and Enterobacter ludwigii), and CP (T. harzianum, B. amyloliquefaciens, Bacillus licheniformis and B. laterosporus). The results showed that four microbial agents enhanced soil organic matter, nitrate nitrogen and available potassium content in soil. The application of BS resulted in an increase in the relative abundance of Proteobacteria in root-zone soil of cowpea, while Actinobacteria and Firmicutes showed a downward trend compared to the control (CK). The treatments of BS, TH and AB significantly increased the relative abundance of Bradyrhizobium in soil by 109.4%, 38.5%, and 20.6% compared with CK, respectively. Furthermore, TH and CP increased the relative abundance of Halorhodospira in soil by 47.4% and 98.5%, respectively. The yield of cowpea plants increased by 9.6% with CP treatment compared to CK (P < 0.05). The BS treatment significantly promoted the main stem width of cowpea plants. The AB treatment enhanced protein, vitamin C and organic acid contents in cowpea plants. Overall, CP treatment is a better choice, as it has potential advantages over other agents in terms of cowpea growth and yield, and can improve the structure of the soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial community.

Introduction

Continuous cropping is one of the critical challenges for sustainable agricultural development, which can result in soil nutrient imbalance, disrupting soil microbial communities, and reduction of crop yield [1]. Studies have shown that long-term continuous cropping not only reduces soil organic matter (SOM) content, but also alters the diversity and abundance of soil microbial communities [2, 3]. Among them, the particularly pronounced change is in the structure of nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities, which in turn affects soil nitrogen availability and crop nitrogen uptake efficiency [4]. Moreover, continuous cropping may cause soil acidification and salinization, thereby exacerbating soil degradation processes [5]. These directly impact crop growth and yield, and have become bottlenecks constraining sustainable agricultural development [6].

Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata subsp. sesquipedalis (L.) Verdc.), an important legume vegetable crop, is highly valued for its rich nutritional profile [7]. Researches [8, 9] have shown that tender pods parts of cowpea plants are rich in protein, crude fiber, carbohydrates, vitamin C, and mineral elements such as calcium and iron, which can enhance human appetite and immune function. Nowadays, cowpea plants are cultivated in more than 100 countries. Its production was approximately 9.8 million tonnes in 2020, and expected to jump to 12.3 million tonnes by 2030 [10]. In China, cowpea planting area has exhibited a consistent upward trend driven by escalating market demand [11]. The same plot of land is usually used repeatedly for cowpea plants cultivation. However, long-term continuous cropping of cowpea faces the dilemma of root-zone soil quality degradation, nitrogen supply shortages and nitrogen-fixing bacterial community imbalance [12]. These factors severely restrict the improvement of cowpea yield and nutritional quality.

Cowpea belonging to legume crops can establish symbiotic relationships with nitrogen-fixing bacteria from the Rhizobium genus, fixing otherwise unavailable atmospheric nitrogen through biological nitrogen fixation [13, 14]. However, the efficiency of this symbiosis is influenced by environmental factors, including soil acidity, soil nutrient imbalance and salinization due to continuous cropping [15]. These factors affect processes, such as rhizobia survival, nodule formation, and nitrogenase activity, ultimately determining the growth and productivity of legumes [16]. In addition, long-term continuous cropping may lead to the accumulation of root exudates from leguminous plants in the soil, further inhibiting the invasion ability and nodulation efficiency of rhizobia, thereby weakening symbiotic nitrogen fixation [17]. Therefore, studying how to improve cowpea growth and nitrogen-fixing bacterial community structure in continuous cropping soils has important practical and theoretical significance for the sustainable development of agriculture.

Currently, with growing emphasis on soil health and sustainable utilization, biological controls have emerged as a promising strategy to address continuous cropping obstacles [18]. Among them, the development and application of microbial agents play a key role in biological controls [19]. The application of these microbial agents not only inhibits the growth of pathogenic bacteria, but also promotes the growth of beneficial microorganisms such as soil nitrogen-fixing bacteria, which in turn builds a microbial community favorable to plant growth [1]. The proper application of microbial agents can effectively increase the diversity of soil nitrogen-fixing bacteria, which can help to improve the growth of crops [20]. The study of Rigobelo et al. [21] found that combined application of Bacillus subtilis and Trichoderma harzianum enhanced soybean plant growth and promoted plant-associated microbial groups. In addition, Bacillus inoculation can diminish the negative influence of high temperatures on cowpea nodulation and community changes in cowpea nodule bradyrhizobial composition [22]. However, there are few reports on the application of microbial agents into cowpea continuous cropping soil for improvement of plant growth and soil environment.

In this research, a field experiment was conducted to investigate effects of four commercially microbial agents BS (B. subtilis), TH (T. harzianum), AB (Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, Brevibacillus laterosporus, Bacillus mucilaginosus and Enterobacter ludwigii), and CP (T. harzianum, B. amyloliquefaciens, Bacillus licheniformis and B. laterosporus) on the growth and yield of continuously cropping cowpea, soil physicochemical properties, and nitrogen-fixing bacterial community structure in root-zone soil by high-throughput sequencing. The experimental results will provide a guidance for high-quality and sustainable cultivation of cowpea plants.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

“E cowpea No. 8” cowpea was used as plant materials in this experiment. Four commercially microbial agents used were as follows: BS (Effective viable counts ≥ 2.0 × 1010 CFU/g), TH (Effective viable counts ≥ 2.0 × 1010 CFU/g), AB (B. amyloliquefaciens and B. laterosporu with effective viable counts ≥ 2.0 × 108 CFU/g, B. mucilaginosus and E. ludwigii with effective viable counts ≥ 1.0 × 109 CFU/g), and CP (Effective viable counts ≥ 1.0 × 109 CFU/g). The BS, TH and CP were purchased from Shandong Yitai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China. The AB was provided by the Institute of Oilseed Crops, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China.

Experimental methods

This experiment was conducted from April to June 2024 at Tuanshan Experimental Field of Xiangyang Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Hubei province, China (31.54° N, 112.11° E). Figure 1 shows that the change of average temperature and rainfall during the experimental period. The soil of the experimental site is brown loam with uniform fertility and has been planted with cowpea for three consecutive years. Cowpea seeds used for continuous planting were not specially treated. Cowpea seeds harvested in the previous year were used as seeds for planting in the next year. Soil background values at the beginning of the experiment were as follows: soil pH 6.23, SOM content 33.2 g/kg, total nitrogen (TN) 1.86 g/kg, alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen (AN) 131.9 mg/kg, ammonium nitrogen (NH4+-N) 26.5 mg/kg, nitrate nitrogen (NO3−-N) 28.4 mg/kg, available phosphorus (AP) 175.0 mg/kg and available potassium (AK) 237.0 mg/kg.Fig. 1Average temperature and rainfall from April to June 2024

Average temperature and rainfall from April to June 2024

Four treatments of BS, TH, CP and AB with CK were set up in this experiment. The BS, TH and CP were treated at 1 kg per acre and AB at 2 kg per acre, respectively. Before treatment, each microbial agent was diluted 1000 times with an equal amount of brown sugar and fermented for 4 h. The CK was treated with equal amounts of water by watering the cowpea before sowing and after emergence, followed by spraying at intervals of 2 weeks. The experiment was arranged in randomized blocks with four replications, giving a total of 20 plots, each 7.2 m long and 3.3 m wide, with an area of 23.76 m2. Isolation strips were placed between plots to prevent the microbial agents from being affected by factors such as rainfall and irrigation. Sowing of cowpea was conducted on 17 April 2024 at 58 holes per plot with 3 seeds per hole. After growing two true leaves, one cowpea seedling was preserved per hole. Cowpea vines were guided to climb up the shelf on 14 May 2024. No additional irrigation, fertilization or pesticide spraying was carried out throughout the entire experiment.

Soil collection and determination of soil physicochemical properties

Root-zone soil samples were collected on 13 June 2024. Ten sample points were selected by serpentine sampling in each plot, and soil cores were taken with a soil auger at depths of 0–20 cm. The cores were mixed well and placed in a self-sealing bag to form a soil sample. The samples were stored in an insulated box containing ice packs, sent to the laboratory immediately, and divided into two parts after root picking and impurity removal. One part of the fresh samples was stored at −80 °C for subsequent molecular biology analysis. The other part was air-dried at room temperature, milled and passed through a 2 mm sieve to determine soil physicochemical properties.

Soil physicochemical properties were determined according to the method described by Bao [23]. The soil pH was measured using a portable pH meter (pHTestr20; Oakton Instruments, Vernon Hills, USA) according to a 1:5 soil/water (w/v) ratio. The SOM and TN content were measured using a carbon nitrogen analyzer (VarioMAX, Elementar, Germany). Soil AN content was determined using alkaline-hydrolysis diffusion method. The NH4+-N and NO3−-N in soil were extracted using 2 mol/L of potassium chloride solution and determined using a colorimetric method. Soil AP was determined by using molybdenum-antimony resistance colorimetry after sodium bicarbonate extraction. Soil AK was extracted using 1 mol/L of ammonium acetate and determined by using atomic absorption spectrophotometry.

Soil DNA extraction, high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

The total DNA was extracted from each root-zone soil sample using a MoBio PowerSoil™ DNA Isolation Kit (MoBio Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The total DNA extracted was stored at −80°C for subsequent sequencing analysis. The diversity of nitrogen-fixing bacteria was determined by amplifying the nifH gene using primers nifH-F 5’-AAAGGYGGWATCGGYAARTCCACCAC-3’ and nifH-R 5’-TTGTTSGCSGCRTACATSGCCATCAT-3’ [24]. Paired-end was sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, PE300, USA) according to the standard protocols by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The measured raw sequences were spliced using FLASH after truncating the Barcode sequence and primer sequences to obtain the raw Tags data. Subsequently, the raw Tags were quality filtered using QIIME (version 1.9.1) to obtain high quality Tags (clean tags). Next, the clean tags were compared to a species reference database to detect and discard chimeric sequences therein [25]. At the same time, residual primer sequences and low-quality sequences were removed to finally obtain effective tags. The USEARCH11-uparse algorithm was used to cluster effective tags of all samples with a similarity threshold of 97% to obtain the operational taxonomic units (OTUs), and the sequence with the highest frequency of occurrence in each OTU was selected as its representative sequence. The representative sequences of OTUs were analyzed for species annotation (with a confidence threshold set at 0.7) based on the RDP classifier in conjunction with the SILVA 132 SSURef NR99 database in order to obtain taxonomic information on OTUs for each sample [26, 27]. Subsequently, microbial community composition was calculated for each sample at the taxonomic level. Phylogenetic relationships of all OTUs representative sequences were obtained by rapid multiple sequence comparison using MUSCLE software (version 3.8.31). The normalization processing was finally performed using the sample with the lowest sequence number in the dataset as the standard, and all subsequent analyses were based on the normalized data. Species number, Chao1 index, Shannon’s index and Simpson’s index were calculated using QIIME software.

Determination of Cowpea growth, yield and quality

The indices of cowpea growth were investigated in 13 June 2024. Meanwhile, samples of cowpea fruits in each plot were collected for the determination of quality indices [28]. The content of soluble sugars in cowpea fruit was determined by anthrone colorimetric method, soluble proteins using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 method, vitamin C using 2,6-dichlorophenol indophenol titration method, organic acid using acid-base titration method, chlorophyll using a colorimetric method after ethanol extraction, and soluble solids using a handheld refractometer. Cowpea yield in each plot was calculated based on the sum of the weight of cowpea fruits harvested each day.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of one-way ANOVA was performed for soil physicochemical properties, plant growth and yield, and alpha diversity index of nitrogen-fixing bacteria using SPSS 22.0 software. Pearson correlation analysis was carried out between dominant groups of soil nitrogen-fixing bacteria and environmental factors. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and analysis of similarities were performed using R 3.5.3 software to analyze the composition of soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities. The redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed using Canoco 5.0 software for main controlling factors of soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial diversity and community structure. The high-throughput amplicon sequencing data are available in the NCBI SRA (accession number PRJNA1305160).

Nitrogen-fixing bacterial community abundance

As can be seen in Fig. 2 BS treatment significantly reduced the Shannon index of soil microbial community compared to CK (P < 0.05), whereas there was no significant difference in the other treatments. All treatments resulted in an increase in Chao1 index, with the AB treatment reaching a significant level of elevation (P < 0.05). The TH, CP, and BS treatments had a higher Chao1 index than CK but not reaching a significant difference. The number of species was not found to be significantly different between the treatments (P > 0.05).Fig. 2Effect of microbial agents on the alpha diversity index of soil nitrogen-fixing bacteria

Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in soil samples were classified according to OTU, resulting in 10 phyla, 17 classes, 35 orders, 54 families and 77 genera. At the phylum level, taxa with an average relative abundance of less than 1% were classified as other, yielding three taxa, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria and Firmicutes (Fig. 3a). The Proteobacteria has an absolute advantage in all samples, accounting for 91.86% − 97.67% of the relative abundance of nitrogen fixing bacteria. Compared to CK, the BS treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Proteobacteria, while decreased the relative abundance of Actinobacteria and Firmicutes. Interestingly, none of the TH, CP and AB treatments had a significant effect on the relative abundance of these major phyla.Fig. 3Analysis of differences in soil nitrogen-fixing bacteria at the phylum (a) and genus (b) levels under different treatments

Analysis of differences in soil nitrogen-fixing bacteria at the phylum (a) and genus (b) levels under different treatments

At the genus level, the top ten genera of nitrogen-fixing bacteria ranked in relative abundance were selected for comparison (Fig. 3b). The BS, TH and AB treatments increased the relative abundance of Bradyrhizobium by 109.4%, 38.5% and 20.6% compared to CK, respectively. TH and CP treatments resulted in 47.4% and 98.5% increases in the relative abundance of Halorhodospira. Burkholderia had a relative abundance of 0.01% in the CK and 6.8% in the CP treatment, while the genus was not detected in the other treatments. The relative abundance of Dechloromonas in the AB treatment was 5.5 times that of the CK. All treatments reduced the relative abundance of Azospirillum, Frankia, Paenibacillus and Azoarcus compared to the CK.

Soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial community structure

The results of principal component analysis (PCoA) showed that different microbial agent treatments had a significant effect on the structure of soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities in the root-zone of cowpea plants (Fig. 4a). The 1 st and 2nd principal components of the PCoA analysis explained 26.91% and 14.88% of the community variation, respectively, indicating that the composition of the root-zone soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities was different among different microbial agents. Based on redundancy analysis, it was further found that the RDA1 and RDA2 axes explained 20.19% and 10.00% of the variation, respectively, and cumulatively explained 30.19% of the total variation in nitrogen fixing bacterial community structure (Fig. 4b). SOM and AK were the main environmental factors affecting the distribution of soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities in the root-zone soil of cowpea plants (P < 0.05). Specifically, BS was positively correlated with pH, SOM and AK. Positive correlations were observed between TH and pH, SOM and TN. The AB was positively correlated with NH4+-N, and CP, AB and CK were all positively correlated with AN. The NMDS analyses showed that there was a clear separation of the nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities between different microbial agents and CK (Fig. 4c). Interestingly, the differences in nitrogen-fixing bacteria under BS treatment were significant with TH, CP and AB, whereas there were no significant differences among TH, CP and AB.

The relationship between microbial agents and soil physicochemical properties was analyzed using the Mantel test for different microbial agent environmental drivers (Fig. 4d). The results showed that there was an extremely significant positive correlation between AP and AB, and negative correlations between AB and TN, AK and SOM. Interestingly, AB and AN are not related. In addition, CP was significantly positively correlated with pH, while NH4+-N, NO3−-N and SOM were negatively correlated with CP. Next, SOM showed a highly significant positive correlation with BS, while AN showed a highly significant negative correlation with BS. In addition, TN and SOM were positively correlated with TH, while NO3−-N was extremely negatively correlated with TH. Finally, AK and NO3−-N were significantly positively correlated with the composition of soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities, while AP and NO3−-N were significantly negatively correlated.Fig. 4PcoA (a), RDA (b), and NMDS (c) analysis of effects of microbial agents on the structure of soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities, and Mantel test analyses (d) of the relationship between the structure of soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities and soil physicochemical properties

Quality of Cowpea plants

The quality properties of cowpea plants are key factors in their edible and commercial value, affecting consumer acceptance, market competitiveness and the economic benefits of the industry. As illustrated in Table 3, effects of treating cowpea plants with different microbial agents on quality properties were significantly different compared with CK (P < 0.05). Compared to the CK, CP treatment significantly increased soluble sugar content, while BS, TH and AB significantly reduced it (P < 0.05). In term of soluble protein and vitamin C content, TH and CP treatments resulted in significant reductions compared with CK, while BS and AB treatments led to significant increases (P < 0.05). Compared with CK, four microbial agents resulted in a significant increase in organic acids, as well as a decrease in chlorophyll and soluble solids (P < 0.05). Overall, cowpea under AB treatment performed excellently in several key indicators, while BS and CP treatments had advantage in soluble solid and soluble sugar contents, respectively. These results demonstrated that microbial agents can effectively improve quality traits of cowpea plants. Discussion

Continuous cropping of cowpea triggers its growth disorders, leading to plant stunting, plant yield reduction and deterioration of the soil environment [29, 30]. Microbial agents have exhibited advantages in mitigating continuous cropping obstacle of crops, such as the improvements of soil environment, plant nutrient use efficiency, and crop yield and quality [31–33]. Effects on soil physicochemical properties

Based on the results of this experiment, the increases of SOM and NO3−-N provided an abundant carbon and nitrogen sources for the growth of nitrogen-fixing bacteria, thereby promoting the activity of nitrogen-fixing bacterial community. These findings were consistent with the results of previous researches [34, 35], indicating that microbial agents used in this study have a significantly effect on the improvement of soil physicochemical properties. Studies have shown that B. subtilis, B. amyloliquefaciens and E. ludwigii can improve carbon and nitrogen sources, promoting microbial community activity [36–38]. It is worth noting that TH and CP significantly improved the content of AK in soil. The enhance of AK indicated that microorganisms of agents may have potassium-solubilizing function, which is particularly important for soil in potassium deficiency. The study has suggested that strains such as B. mucilaginosus and B. licheniformis can break down insoluble potassium minerals in soil, converting them into plant-available forms [39, 40]. It can be seen that microbial agents have a positive effect on soil environment.

Effects on nitrogen-fixing bacterial community abundance and structure

Compared with CK, the Shannon index was significantly decreased in the BS treatment (P < 0.05), while TH, CP, and AB treatments were not significant. This result suggested that the BS treatment resulted in a decrease in the abundance of nitrogen-fixing bacterial community, and that perhaps overpopulation of advantage species suppressed other species [41]. Furthermore, Chao 1 index was significantly increased in the AB treatment compared with CK (P < 0.05), while there was no significant difference in BS, TH, and CP treatments. This finding indicated a significant increase in microbial abundance in the soil and an enhancement in the potential functional diversity of the community [42, 43]. It is worth noting that there was no significant difference in the number of microbial species in the soil after the application of the four microbial agents compared to the CK (P < 0.05). This indicated that microbial agents may promote the reproduction of beneficial bacteria and inhibit harmful bacteria, thereby maintaining a relative balance in the number of species [44].

Research has found that the Proteobacteria was the most important bacterial phylum in peanut cultivation [45]. Copiotrophs bacteria with faster growth rates dominate the microbial community in the root-zone soil of crops, such as Proteobacteria, Firmicutes and Actinobacteria [46]. These bacteria can release and help plant roots absorb potassium, phosphorus, and other micronutrients that plants can use [47]. At the genus level, compared with the CK, BS treatment significantly increased the abundance of Bradyrhizobium. Bradyrhizobium can participate in the nitrogen-fixing process by assimilating sulfate [48], and an increase of their abundance may be related to improved sulfur availability in the soil. Furthermore, the relative abundance of Halorhodospira was significantly increased by TH and CP treatments. This finding indicated that microbial agents may provide a suitable environment for the growth of Halorhodospira by improving soil salinization [49]. Azospirillum has symbiotic nitrogen-fixing capability and can form root nodules with leguminous plants to provide stable supply of nitrogen for the plants [50]. At the same time, indole acetic acid can be produced, which is beneficial for plant root-zone extension and promotes the division of lateral root meristem, thereby accelerating the absorption and conversion efficiency of soil nutrients by plants [51]. Frankia not only exists in free state in soil, but also forms symbiotic relationships with plants root nodules. Frankia possesses nitrogen-fixing capabilities in both status, and unique vesicle structure can accelerate its nitrogen-fixing efficiency [52]. These changes revealed distinctions in the structure of nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities between different treatments. According to the report [34], the application of microbial agents can alter the composition and diversity of plant root-zone microbial community. Results of this research further confirmed that the four microbial agents had different effects on the composition and structure of soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities.

Interplay between microbial agents and soil factors

Previous studies have shown that there are close correlations between soil environmental factors and soil microorganisms [30, 53, 54]. This study based on the Beta diversity, RDA and Mantel test, indicated that microbial agents can have significantly effects on soil physicochemical factors. Conversely, the SOM and AK were the main environment factors affecting on nitrogen-fixing bacterial community distribution. The treatments of BS and TH were positively correlated relationship with SOM and AK, indicating that these microbial agents can improve soil SOM and AK [55]. This conclusion was similar with previous results [1, 56, 57], which showed that long-term application of organic fertilizers to increase SOM content changed the abundance and diversity of soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities. The treatments of CP and AB were positively correlated with AN and NO3−-N in the soil, demonstrating that they may influence the structure of nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities by regulating soil nitrogen cycling [58]. The CP also showed an extremely significant positive correlation with pH, indicating that it may provide a suitable growth environment for nitrogen-fixing bacteria by improving the acid-base balance of the soil [59].

Effects on Cowpea growth and quality

All four microbial agents promoted the growth of cowpea plants, with the CP treatment significantly increasing the yield of cowpea plants compared with CK (P < 0.05). The treatment of CP improved the yield of cowpea plants by 9.6%, indicating that composite agents had potential advantages in the improvement of crop growth and yield. These results may be related to the significant increase in the content of AK and NO3−-N in soil under CP treatment, as the adequate supply of these nutrients can provide favorable conditions for the growth of cowpea plants. The main fungus in CP treatment, T. harzianum, can form a “protective layer” at the roots of plants to prevent the invasion of pathogens [60]. In addition, B. laterosporus contributed to its antifungal and insecticidal activity, thereby promoting plant growth [61]. The study of Chohan et al. [62] found that Trichoderma asperellum and T. harzianum can promote the growth of chickpea plants. AB treatment significantly increased the soluble protein and vitamin C content, demonstrating its unique advantages in improving crop quality. The increase in vitamin C content may be related to the metabolic products of microorganisms in the agents, which can promote the antioxidant capacity and nutrient synthesis of plants [51]. In addition, AB treatment significantly increased organic acid content, while CP treatment performed best in terms of soluble sugar content. Some studies have shown that microbial agents can modulate the metabolic pathways of organic acids and sugars in crops, thereby improving crop quality [63–65]. The research results indicated that the importance of microbial agents for crop growth and quality was self-evident.

Conclusions

This study investigated the growth, yield and quality of continuous cropping cowpea plants, physicochemical properties of root-zone soils, and changes in the structure of soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial community under treatments of four microbial agents. These microbial agents can significantly increase SOM, NO3−-N and AK contents in soil and alter the structure of soil nitrogen-fixing bacterial communities. BS treatment significantly reduced Shannon index of soil microbial community, whereas AB treatment increased Chao1 index compared to CK (P < 0.05). In the phylum level, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria increased under BS treatment. In the genus level, BS, TH, and AB treatments significantly promoted the relative abundance of Bradyrhizobium, while TH and CP treatments increased the relative abundance of Halorhodospira. CP treatment improved the main stem thickness and yield of cowpea compared with CK. Soluble protein and vitamin C contents in cowpea fruits under AB treatment increased significantly, while soluble sugar content was high under CP treatment compared to CK. Overall, CP treatment has a better effect on continuous cropping cowpea and soil nitrogen-fixing bacteria community than others. The results of this study also provided a practical guidance for alleviating the challenges of continuous cropping cowpea plants cultivation. In addition, it is necessary that experiments under different conditions should be conducted in future research to evaluate effects of these microbial agents on cowpea growth and soil microorganisms.
