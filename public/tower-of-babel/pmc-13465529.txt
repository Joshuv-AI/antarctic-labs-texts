The comparative effects of prenatal exposure to methadone versus buprenorphine for opioid use disorder on neonatal outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Lindsay A Wilson, Samuel Tobias, Preet Gandhi, Bohdan Nosyk, Jane A Buxton, Lianping Ti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBoth methadone and buprenorphine are considered safe for use for the treatment of opioid use disorder, but the comparative effectiveness of these regimens during pregnancy has not been well-established. The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to identify and synthesize published evidence from randomized controlled trials and observational studies to evaluate the impacts of methadone and buprenorphine on neonatal health outcomes.MethodsWe searched Medline, Embase, PsycINFO, and CINAHL databases for published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or observational studies that compared outcomes among individuals who received methadone during pregnancy to individuals who received buprenorphine (with or without naloxone) during pregnancy. Outcomes of interest were preterm birth, low birthweight, small for gestational age, stillbirth, neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) diagnosis, and NOWS treatment. We conducted separate pooled analyses for the RCTs and observational studies to generate pooled unadjusted risk ratios (RR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and 95% prediction intervals. Risk of bias was evaluated using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale and the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias-2 tool.FindingsA total of 27 studies (22 observational, 4 RCTs, and one study that included an observational arm and an RCT arm) were included for data extraction and synthesis. The studies included a combined total of 14,978 individuals exposed to buprenorphine and 8,358 individuals exposed to methadone. In the pooled unadjusted analyses of the observational studies, compared with methadone, buprenorphine was associated with lower risk of preterm birth (unadjusted RR 0.63; 95% CI: 0.57–0.70); low birthweight (unadjusted RR 0.48; 95% CI: 0.36–0.64), small for gestational age (unadjusted RR: 0.82; 95% CI 0.77–0.89), and NOWS treatment (unadjusted RR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.61–0.78). Our findings suggested no difference between groups in terms of NOWS diagnosis or stillbirth, although stillbirth was rare.ConclusionsIn this meta-analysis, exposure to buprenorphine during pregnancy was associated with lower unadjusted risk of several adverse birth outcomes. The quality of the available evidence was limited by a lack of adjustment for key covariates, heterogeneity in the included analyses, and small sample sizes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09256-1.Keywords: Buprenorphine, Meta-analysis, Methadone, Neonatal health, Opioid agonist treatment, Opioid use disorder, Pregnancy, Systematic review

Introduction

Opioid agonist treatment (OAT) is commonly prescribed for the treatment of opioid use disorder (OUD), as it can alleviate withdrawal symptoms associated with opioid use cessation and is associated with better clinical outcomes than managed withdrawal alone [1–6]. Compared with untreated OUD among pregnant people, OAT is associated with a lower risk of adverse neonatal health outcomes (e.g., stillbirth, preterm birth), greater uptake of prenatal care, and decreased birthing parent mortality [5, 7–11]. Indeed, a 2021 study of pregnant people with OUD in British Columbia, Canada found that, compared to discontinuing OAT during pregnancy, continuous OAT was associated with 40% lower odds of preterm birth and 60% lower odds of low birthweight [12]. Similarly, in a study of 13,320 pregnancies in the United States, Krans and colleagues (2021) found that for each additional week of OAT use, the odds of drug poisoning (i.e., drug overdose) decreased by 2%, the odds of preterm birth decreased by 1%, and the odds of continued post-partum OAT use increased by 95% [11].

In many jurisdictions, OAT is the standard of care for pregnant people with OUD, with methadone and buprenorphine (which can be administered with or without naloxone) representing the most commonly prescribed forms of OAT [13–15]. Compared with managed withdrawal and untreated OUD, both methadone and buprenorphine are considered safe for use during pregnancy [16–18], but the comparative effectiveness of these regimens during pregnancy has not been well-established [19]. Moreover, clinical guidelines for OUD treatment during pregnancy are variable between jurisdictions [1, 16, 20], and are often based on low-quality evidence [21]. In many cases, dedicated guidelines specific to OAT during pregnancy do not exist. Previous studies have found that healthcare providers often report discomfort, confusion or a lack of knowledge regarding clinical guidelines for managing OUD during pregnancy, and may rely on outdated evidence in their clinical decision-making [22–24]. There is therefore a need for up-to-date clinical guidelines based on the best available evidence that can help to inform treatment decisions between pregnant people and their healthcare providers.

Previous systematic reviews have attempted to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of methadone vs buprenorphine during pregnancy, but these reviews have been limited by small numbers of studies, few participants, or heterogeneity between studies or outcome definitions [25–27]. In addition, available studies rarely present adjusted analyses, introducing the potential for unmeasured confounding or biased estimates. Crucially, many studies were unable to account for factors such as dosage, duration or timing of exposure, each of which may play an important role in each regimen’s effectiveness. As several large-scale studies have recently been published on the comparative effectiveness of prenatal OAT regimens, the objective of this study was to incorporate these newer studies and update the existing evidence base by identifying and synthesizing published evidence from both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies on the comparative effectiveness of methadone and buprenorphine on neonatal health outcomes.

Reporting for this systematic review follows the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 Checklist [34].

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

Our search strategy was designed in consultation with a medical librarian at the University of British Columbia. We searched four study databases (Medline, Embase, PsycINFO, and CINAHL) for published randomized clinical trials or observational studies (i.e., cohort studies, case–control studies) that compared individuals who received methadone during pregnancy to individuals who received buprenorphine (with or without naloxone) during pregnancy. We also included conference abstracts published within the last two years. Databases were initially searched on May 12, 2022, and the search was updated on March 13, 2024. Only studies published in English were included. No date or setting restrictions were imposed. Search strategies are presented in Supplementary Tables 1A–D.

The population of interest in our review was pregnant people with OUD and their neonates. Eligible interventions were methadone, buprenorphine (excluding long-acting buprenorphine), or buprenorphine/naloxone received during pregnancy. Outcomes of interest were preterm birth (i.e., gestational age < 37 weeks), low birthweight (i.e., < 2500 g), small for gestational age birthweight (i.e., birthweight < 10th percentile for gestational age), stillbirth, NOWS diagnosis and NOWS treatment. While OAT is known to impact other neonatal and birthing parent health outcomes, these outcomes, all of which are common among pregnant people with OUD [12, 35, 36], have clearly established clinical definitions, which reduces between-study heterogeneity and increases the feasibility of meta-analysis. In the case of NOWS, diagnostic thresholds and terminology may differ across settings, but this outcome was included due to its high clinical relevance and potential role in guiding treatment decisions. We sought to mitigate the potential for heterogeneity by including both NOWS treatment and NOWS diagnosis as separate outcomes. Study screening and data extraction

Screening was conducted using Covidence [37], which automatically removed duplicate publications. Two reviewers (LW and PG) independently reviewed titles, abstracts and full texts for eligibility. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus between the two reviewers. Data extraction was conducted independently and in duplicate by LW and ST, using a pre-specified data extraction form within the Covidence platform. In addition to data on the study population, design, intervention, and outcomes, we also extracted information on study setting and eligibility criteria, as well as information on participant characteristics (e.g., age, parity, duration of prior drug use).

Risk of bias (RoB) was assessed by LW and ST. We assessed RoB for observational studies using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale, which evaluates each study in terms of selection (i.e., how study groups were selected), comparability (i.e., how comparable the groups are) and outcome (i.e., how the presence or absence of each outcome was ascertained) [38]. Studies receive a star rating on each of these domains, with a maximum rating of nine stars (i.e., up to 4 stars for selection, 2 stars for comparability, and 3 stars for outcome). Studies with a larger number of stars have a lower risk of bias. Clinical trials were assessed using the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias-2 tool, which scores studies as “low risk”, “some concern” or “high risk” based on the risk of bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported results [39].

Study results were synthesized using RevMan Web v9.1.0 [40]. Because several studies combined patients who received buprenorphine and buprenorphine/naloxone into one category, we were unable to analyze buprenorphine and buprenorphine/naloxone separately. Therefore, all comparisons were between methadone and buprenorphine ± naloxone. When a study reported results for both buprenorphine and buprenorphine/naloxone, these groups were combined for the purposes of our analysis. Where more than one publication reported on the same study population, participants were counted only once in the total sample size, and outcome data were extracted to avoid double-counting of participants or events in any analyses.

For each outcome, we developed forest plots illustrating the pooled number of events across all studies and generated a pooled unadjusted risk ratio (RR), 95% confidence interval (CI), and 95% prediction interval (PI), incorporating between-study heterogeneity. Observational studies and RCTs were pooled separately to account for differences in study design [41]. For studies that reported results for multiple distinct populations, each population was included as its own line in the forest plot. Specifically, Nechanska and colleagues (2018) [19] reported results separately for individuals from Norway and the Czech Republic; Coulson and colleagues (2021) [42] reported separate results for individuals who received OAT at high doses and those who received OAT at low to moderate doses; and Metz and colleagues (2011) [43] conducted both an RCT and an observational analysis, each of which was reported separately. As there was no overlap in the participants included in the RCT and observational analyses conducted by Metz and colleagues (2011) the study appears in both the pooled observational and pooled RCT analyses, with only the relevant study arm reported for each analysis. These stratified estimates were retained to improve transparency in our analyses and preserve the distinct exposure definitions and study populations reported in the original studies. They are presented as descriptive, exploratory strata within studies; specific subgroup analyses were not conducted.

When adjusted odds ratios (ORs) or RRs were available, we generated forest plots of these adjusted estimates for each outcome. Studies that reported adjusted RRs and adjusted ORs were presented separately, as OR may not approximate RR when the outcome is relatively common [44]. To account for heterogeneity between studies and the fact that the adjusted measures of effect adjusted for different variables between studies, we used a random-effects model across all analyses (Restricted Maximum-Likelihood Model). For the unadjusted analyses, pooled confidence intervals were calculated using the Wald-type method. As adjusted estimates were available for only ≤ 3 studies for each outcome, the unadjusted pooled results were presented as our primary analyses, and pooled results were not generated for adjusted estimates. Heterogeneity was measured within RevMan using the I2 statistic. We generated funnel plots to evaluate publication bias when at least 10 studies were available for an outcome [41].

Confidence in the body of evidence was assessed with the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [45] using the GradePro GDT software [46].

Results

Our search yielded 5,520 results (Fig. 1), 2,111 of which were removed as duplicates. After title and abstract screening, 150 full texts were screened for eligibility. A total of 89 studies met our initial eligibility criteria, but 62 of these were excluded when the decision was made to exclude studies that did not include both methadone and buprenorphine as comparators. Ultimately 27 studies were included for data extraction and meta-analysis. No conference abstracts were included in our analyses. Characteristics of these studies are presented in Table 2.

The 27 studies included a combined total of 14,978 individuals who had been exposed to buprenorphine and 8,358 individuals who had been exposed to methadone. Five studies reported on four different RCTs [43, 51–54], and the remainder were cohort studies. Nine studies reported adjusted results [19, 27, 42, 54, 61, 63, 65, 66, 68].

Of the five publications describing RCTs, two (Jones and colleagues [2010] [54] and Holbrook and colleagues [2012] [52]) reported on data gathered from the same study (i.e., the MOTHER trial). All of the RCTs had a low risk of performance bias, detection bias, and reporting bias, but an unclear risk of selection bias and a high or unclear risk of attrition bias (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among the observational studies, the median score on the Newcastle Ottawa Scale was 7 (interquartile range: 7–9; Supplementary Table 3). Most studies scored well in terms of sample selection (e.g., individuals exposed to methadone vs buprenorphine were drawn from the same community) and outcome ascertainment, but scored poorly in terms of comparability, usually due to a lack of adjustment for potential confounders. Due to the small number of studies reporting each outcome, publication bias could only be assessed for preterm birth, NOWS treatment, and small for gestational age (Supplementary Figs. 2A–2C). No substantial asymmetry was detected for these outcomes.

Preterm birth

Nearly all studies (24, 89%) reported results for preterm birth (Fig. 2A). Within the observational studies, preterm birth occurred among 14.2% of individuals who received buprenorphine (n = 2,038) and 23.9% of individuals who received methadone (n = 1,871). Three studies [47, 55, 59] reported a lower risk of preterm birth among birthing parents who received methadone, whereas 16 found a lower risk among individuals who received buprenorphine [19, 35, 43, 48, 56–58, 60–63, 65–69]. One study by Coulson and colleagues (2021) reported a lower risk of preterm birth among individuals who received buprenorphine when the two regimens were compared at high doses, but a lower risk among individuals who received methadone when compared at low doses, but both estimates were imprecise, with confidence intervals including the null value [42]. In the pooled analysis, birthing parents who received buprenorphine had a 37% lower risk of preterm birth (unadjusted RR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.57–0.70; 95% PI: 0.50–0.80) (Fig. 2A).

Forest plots illustrating unadjusted risk of adverse neonatal health outcomes

Preterm birth was reported in four RCTs [43, 51, 53, 54]. Within these studies, preterm birth occurred among 7.5% of individuals who received buprenorphine (n = 7) and 19.3% of individuals who received methadone (n = 21). In the pooled analysis, participants who received buprenorphine had a 60% lower risk of preterm birth than individuals who received methadone, but the prediction interval was wide (unadjusted RR: 0.40; 95% CI: 0.18–0.85; 95% PI: 0.07–2.15).

Three studies reported adjusted ORs [19, 63, 66] and two reported adjusted RRs [48, 65] (Supplementary Fig. 3 A and 4 A). All five of these studies reported reduced risk of preterm birth with buprenorphine, but only three reported confidence intervals that excluded the null value [48, 65, 66].

Low birthweight

Seven studies (26%) reported results for low birthweight (Fig. 2B). This outcome occurred among 8.1% of individuals who received buprenorphine (n = 966) and among 14.7% of individuals who received methadone (n = 856) All studies reporting low birthweight were observational. All but one study found a lower risk of low birthweight among individuals who received buprenorphine than among individuals who received methadone, with a pooled unadjusted RR of 0.48, but the prediction interval crossed 1 (95% CI: 0.36–0.64; 95% PI: 0.20–1.15). Adjusted results were reported only for Wang and colleagues (2023) (adjusted OR: 0.14; 95% CI: 0.04–0.46) [66] and Suarez and colleagues (2022) (adjusted RR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.50–0.62) [65] (Supplementary Fig. 3B and 4B).

Small for gestational age

Twelve studies (44%) reported results for small for gestational age (Fig. 2C). Among the observational studies, small for gestational age occurred among 12.5% of individuals who received buprenorphine (n = 1,686) and 15.0% of individuals who received methadone (n = 1,042). Most studies found a lower risk of small for gestational age among individuals who received buprenorphine, but these results were imprecise, except in the study by Suarez and colleagues (2022) [65]. In the pooled unadjusted analysis of observational studies, individuals who received buprenorphine had an 18% lower risk of small for gestational age (unadjusted RR: 0.82; 95% CI: 0.77–0.89; 95% PI: 0.74–0.91). In the one RCT reporting small for gestational age (Holbrook 2012) [52], the risk was also lower among individuals who received buprenorphine, but the CI was wide (unadjusted RR: 0.63, 95% CI 0.06–6.77).

Nechanska and colleagues (2018) [19], Petrich and colleagues (2022) [63], and Wang and colleagues (2023) [66] reported adjusted ORs for small for gestational age, and Suarez and colleagues (2022) [65] and Brogly and colleagues (2018) [48] reported adjusted RRs. Differences between treatments were only identified by Petrich and colleagues (2022) [63] and Suarez and colleagues (2022) [65], both in favor of buprenorphine (adjusted OR: 0.58; 95% CI: 0.34–0.98 and adjusted RR: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.69–0.81) (Supplementary Fig. 3 C and 4 C).

Stillbirth

Stillbirth was reported in only four studies (15%), all of which were observational. This outcome was rare, with only 3 stillbirths occurring among individuals who received buprenorphine (0.6%), and 10 among individuals who received methadone (1.9%) (Fig. 2D). A lower risk of stillbirth was found among individuals who received buprenorphine in three studies: Brogly and colleagues (2018) [49], Lacroix and colleagues (2011) [59], and Nechanska and colleagues (2018) (in both Norway and the Czech Republic) [19]. Kelty and colleagues (2017) reported a lower risk among individuals who received methadone [56]. However, in all of these studies, the estimates were imprecise, and all confidence intervals crossed 1. This finding remained consistent in the pooled analysis, and the prediction interval was very wide (unadjusted RR: 0.41; 95% CI 0.11–1.50; 95% PI: 0.01–11.9). No studies reported adjusted results for stillbirth.

Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (diagnosis)

Nine studies reported on NOWS diagnosis (33%) (Fig. 2E). All studies reporting on NOWS diagnosis were observational. NOWS diagnosis occurred among 68.2% (n = 3,730) and 51.3% (n = 5,559) of infants who were exposed to methadone and buprenorphine, respectively. All nine studies found a lower risk of NOWS diagnosis among infants exposed to buprenorphine than among infants exposed to methadone. In the pooled analysis, the risk of NOWS diagnosis was 31% lower for infants exposed to buprenorphine (unadjusted RR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.47–1.00; 95% PI: 0.07–6.41). However, heterogeneity was extremely high (I2: 99%), leading to a very wide prediction interval.

Adjusted ORs were reported by Wang and colleagues (2023) [66] and Nechanska and colleagues (2018) (Norway population only) [19]. An adjusted RR was reported by Suarez and colleagues (2022) [65]. No differences were identified between treatments, except in the study by Suarez and colleagues (2022), which reported a lower adjusted risk of NOWS diagnosis among infants exposed to buprenorphine (adjusted RR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.72–0.75) (Supplementary Figs. 3D and 4D).

Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (treatment)

Fifteen studies (55.6%) reported on NOWS treatment. Among the 12 observational studies, NOWS occurred among 59.9% of infants exposed to methadone (n = 1,558) and among 38.9% of those exposed to buprenorphine (n = 653) (Fig. 2F). Nearly all observational studies found a lower risk of NOWS treatment in the buprenorphine group, except Welle-Strand and colleagues (2013) [67]. However, the findings reported by Welle-Strand and colleagues were imprecise. In the pooled unadjusted analysis, the risk of needing treatment for NOWS was 31% lower for infants exposed to buprenorphine (unadjusted RR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.61–0.78; 95% PI: 0.48–0.98).

Four RCTs reported NOWS treatment results. Within the RCTs, NOWS treatment was needed among 52.7% of infants exposed to buprenorphine (n = 49) and 56.9% of infants exposed to methadone (n = 62). Jones and colleagues (2005 and 2010) reported a lower risk of NOWS treatment among infants exposed to buprenorphine, while Fischer (2006) and Metz (2011) reported a lower risk among infants exposed to methadone. None of these studies identified differences between treatments, and the prediction interval crossed 1 (pooled unadjusted RR: 0.98; 95% CI: 0.77–1.25; 95% PI: 0.63–1.53).

Coulson and colleagues (2021) [42], Petrich and colleagues (2022) [63], and Wiegand and colleagues (2015) [68] reported adjusted ORs for NOWS treatment. All three studies identified lower odds of NOWS treatment among infants exposed to buprenorphine, and Petrich and colleagues (2022) and Wiegand and colleagues (2015) reported a confidence interval that excluded the null value (adjusted OR: 0.29; 95% CI: 0.19–0.43; and adjusted OR: 0.39; 95% CI: 0.20–0.76, respectively). Lemon and colleagues (2018) [61] reported an adjusted RR, which also suggested a lower risk of NOWS treatment among infants exposed to buprenorphine (adjusted RR 0.75; 95% CI: 0.65–0.85) (Supplementary Figs. 3E and 4E).

Heterogeneity assessment

In the unadjusted analyses of observational studies, heterogeneity was low for preterm birth (I2: 16%), small for gestational age (I2: 0%), and stillbirth (I2: 13%). Moderate heterogeneity was identified for low birthweight (I2: 27%) and NOWS treatment (I2: 53%), and high heterogeneity was identified for NOWS diagnosis (I2: 99%). Among the RCTs, heterogeneity was low for preterm birth (I2: 0%) and NOWS treatment (I2: 14%).

Confidence in the body of evidence

The summary of findings from the GRADE assessment is presented in Table 3. For all of the observational studies, risk of bias was serious due to the lack of adjustment for confounding in the majority of studies. The quality of evidence for low birthweight and preterm birth was higher than for the other outcomes in the observational studies, due to the large number of studies and participants, and due to the strength of the observed associations. In the RCTs, the quality of evidence was higher due to the studies’ randomized designs and lack of other serious sources of bias.

Discussion

The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis of 27 studies suggest that, compared to individuals who received methadone during pregnancy, individuals who received buprenorphine (with or without naloxone) during pregnancy had a lower risk of preterm delivery and of delivering an infant with low birthweight, small for gestational age, or needing treatment for NOWS. No differences between treatment groups were identified in terms of the risk of NOWS diagnosis or stillbirth, though stillbirth was rare. Notably, PIs were wide for several outcomes, particularly NOWS diagnosis and stillbirth, suggesting uncertainty in these effects in new settings. By contrast, the PIs for preterm birth, small for gestational age, and NOWS treatment were narrower, suggesting more consistent effects across studies and settings. Our findings did not vary substantially between observational studies and RCTs. Although these findings also remained consistent in several of the adjusted analyses, very few studies reported adjusted results, limiting our ability to draw conclusions about each outcome.

This study builds upon the findings of previous systematic reviews, which have also suggested that buprenorphine exposure during pregnancy may be associated with lower risk of several adverse neonatal health outcomes [25–27]. By including both RCTs and observational studies, and by including several studies that have been published recently and were not included in the previous systematic reviews [63, 65, 66], the present study corroborates the findings of prior reviews, while also reiterating the need for higher quality evidence surrounding this topic. In their 2020 review of existing RCTs, Minozzi and colleagues found no significant differences between buprenorphine and methadone in terms of birthweight or NOWS, but argued that the available evidence was very poor due to substantial heterogeneity, inconsistency in results, high rates of participant drop-out, and small sample sizes [26]. These issues remained present in our analyses, despite efforts to control for heterogeneity by focusing on outcomes with standardized definitions and our inclusion of observational studies.

A lack of adjustment for potential confounding has been raised as an issue in several previous systematic reviews [25, 26, 70], and remained an issue in our study. Although our unadjusted analyses consistently suggested that buprenorphine was associated with a lower risk of several adverse neonatal outcomes, the small number of available adjusted analyses limits confidence in the magnitude of these effects. Although the adjusted analyses were consistent in reporting a lower risk of adverse neonatal outcomes among individuals treated with buprenorphine, no more than three adjusted analyses were available for each outcome, and those that were available suffered from substantial heterogeneity that limited our ability to draw conclusions about the pooled results. Specifically, no studies adjusted for the same set of covariates, and the small number of events within each study means that many study teams would have been unable to adjust for all relevant covariates without model overfitting. For example, Wang and colleagues (2023) [66], reported that the OR was adjusted for maternal age, race, year of birth, multiple gestation, pre-existing comorbid conditions, and healthcare resource utilization at baseline, but the total sample size was only 200. As a result, the reported adjusted OR was 14.49 (95% CI 3.20–65.57), suggesting potential model overfitting. These findings suggest that while the direction of association between buprenorphine exposure and improved neonatal outcomes is relatively consistent, the true magnitude of effect remains uncertain due to unmeasured or residual confounding. Large, population-based target trials should be conducted to enable more robust analyses that can control for a wider range of potential confounders.

Another major issue present across the included studies is the lack of adjustment for timing, duration or dosage of exposure to each OAT regimen. Although several studies included in this review suggest that buprenorphine is associated with lower risk of adverse neonatal outcomes, issues have also been documented with long-term adherence to treatment with buprenorphine [12, 54, 71]. Indeed, Jones and colleagues (2010) reported that individuals who received buprenorphine dropped out of the study more frequently than individuals who received methadone (28/86 [33%] vs 16/89 [18%], respectively) [54]. These adherence challenges could have substantial implications for the real-world effectiveness of buprenorphine. The lack of information on OAT dosage also presents an issue regarding the ways in which the amount of OAT received and at what gestational age it is received may impact the risk of adverse outcomes. Moreover, timing of exposure was unavailable in all studies except one [65]. Suarez and colleagues (2022) evaluated the risk of several outcomes following exposure to OAT during early pregnancy (through 19 gestational weeks) and during late pregnancy (from 20 gestational weeks until delivery; within 30 days prior to delivery), but the authors were unable to account for duration of exposure or dosage in their analysis [65]. There remains an urgent need for future research that can account for all of these elements and the impact of their interactions on the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Limitations

This review has limitations. First, the majority of studies that were included were observational studies, and most of these did not adjust for the presence of confounding, likely leading to unmeasured confounding that may lead to more extreme or biased effect estimates. Confounding by indication may be of particular concern for studies that were conducted in the US, where methadone is not available in office-based settings, and is instead dispensed by opioid treatment programs [72]. It is therefore possible that individuals who are prescribed methadone in the US may have more severe OUD and be of lower socioeconomic status than individuals who receive buprenorphine. These differences could meaningfully impact our findings, potentially overestimating the effectiveness of buprenorphine compared to methadone. Second, we were unable to adequately distinguish buprenorphine monotherapy from buprenorphine administered with naloxone, as several studies pooled these categories together. Although buprenorphine is administered with naloxone as standard of care in some jurisdictions [16, 73], other jurisdictions continue to recommend buprenorphine monotherapy during pregnancy due to a lack of data on the use of naloxone exposure during pregnancy [74, 75]. As a result, our inability to differentiate between these formulations may limit the relevance of our findings in settings where buprenorphine monotherapy is still recommended. Third, our results are substantially impacted by the results from Suarez and colleagues (2022), as this study had a much larger sample size than any of the other studies included in our analysis. However, the Suarez study was well-designed and had a low risk of bias. Fourth, the heterogeneity between studies, particularly those that reported NOWS diagnosis, limited our ability to conduct pooled analyses for the adjusted effect estimates. As a result, only unadjusted pooled estimates could be generated. Finally, by excluding single-arm studies that focused only on methadone or buprenorphine alone, the evidence base was narrowed, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, this restriction helped to reduce the potential for bias introduced through indirect comparisons.

Conclusion

Across the studies in this meta-analysis, exposure to buprenorphine during pregnancy was associated with lower unadjusted risk of several different adverse neonatal health outcomes. Given the large number of studies and participants included in this analysis, these findings offer compelling evidence into the potential benefits of buprenorphine during pregnancy. However, the quality of the available evidence was limited by a lack of adjustment for key covariates, heterogeneity in the included analyses, and small sample sizes. Given the urgency of the ongoing opioid crisis in North America, high-quality observational research that can address these limitations should be prioritized so clear, evidence-based guidelines for OUD management during pregnancy can be developed.
