Life satisfaction in later life: the role of economic security, health, and religious belief among urban Iranian older adults
Authors: Fardin Alipour, Toktam Paykani, Hassan Rafiey, Meroe Vameghi, Shekoufeh Ahmadi, Vahid Rashedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLife satisfaction in later adulthood is a central indicator of successful aging and reflects the overall evaluation individuals make of their quality of life. Previous research suggests that subjective well-being in older age is shaped by a combination of socioeconomic conditions, physical health, and psychosocial or cultural factors. However, the relative contribution of these domains, particularly in non-Western contexts, remains insufficiently understood. This study aimed to examine the associations between socioeconomic resources, self-rated health, and religious identity with life satisfaction among Iranian older adults.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed data from 2,248 community-dwelling adults aged ≥60 years recruited from urban settings across Iran. Participants were recruited using proportional stratified cluster sampling with probability proportional to size (PPS). Data were collected through structured questionnaires assessing life satisfaction, self-rated health status, socioeconomic characteristics, and belief in God. Ordinal logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with life satisfaction.ResultsBased on an ordinal logistic regression analysis of 2,248 Iranian adults aged 60 years and older, life satisfaction was significantly associated with socioeconomic factors, self-reported health status, and religious belief. Greater income adequacy, higher educational attainment, and home ownership were each associated with higher life satisfaction. Regarding religious belief, individuals who reported not believing in God or being in doubt had lower odds of reporting higher life satisfaction than those who reported believing in God.ConclusionLife satisfaction in later life is associated with a multidimensional interplay of structural resources, health status, and existential orientation. Socioeconomic security and religious belief emerged as significant predictors of well-being, after controlling for health status. These findings highlight the importance of integrated policies addressing both material and psychosocial predictors of aging well.

Introduction

Population aging has become one of the most important demographic transformations of the twenty-first century. Improvements in healthcare, sanitation, nutrition, and living conditions have substantially increased life expectancy worldwide, resulting in a rapid growth in the proportion of older adults [1]. Iran is experiencing this demographic transition at an accelerated pace. Over recent decades, declining fertility rates and increasing longevity have shifted the population structure toward older age groups, raising important social, economic, and public health concerns. In this context, promoting successful and healthy aging has become a major policy priority, and understanding the determinants of well-being in later life is increasingly important for researchers, clinicians, and policymakers [2].

Life satisfaction is widely recognized as a central indicator of subjective well-being and successful aging. It reflects an individual’s overall cognitive evaluation of life circumstances and achievements according to personal expectations, values, and standards. Among older adults, life satisfaction is associated with numerous positive outcomes, including better physical health, lower mortality risk, improved mental health, greater resilience, and enhanced social participation. Conversely, low life satisfaction has been linked to depression, disability, social isolation, and poor quality of life [3]. Because older adults often face multiple age-related challenges—including chronic illness, retirement, bereavement, reduced social networks, and economic vulnerability—identifying the factors that shape life satisfaction in later life is particularly important [4].

Previous studies have demonstrated that life satisfaction in older age is a multidimensional construct influenced by socioeconomic, psychological, health-related, and cultural factors [5]. Among these determinants, economic security has consistently emerged as one of the strongest predictors of well-being in later life. Financial resources influence access to healthcare, nutrition, housing, social participation, and supportive services, all of which contribute to quality of life. Economic insecurity may increase stress, dependency, and social exclusion, thereby undermining psychological well-being among older adults. Recent evidence indicates that older individuals with higher income and better financial security report substantially greater well-being, confidence, and social engagement compared with economically disadvantaged older adults [6]. The present study is also informed by Cumulative Advantage Theory, which proposes that social and economic advantages and disadvantages accumulate throughout the life course, resulting in increasingly divergent trajectories of health, well-being, and quality of life in older age. Educational attainment, financial security, and stable housing are not merely contemporaneous socioeconomic indicators but also reflect accumulated life-course opportunities and resources that continue to influence subjective well-being in later life. This theoretical perspective provides a useful framework for understanding why socioeconomic inequalities may remain strongly associated with life satisfaction among older adults.

In Iran, economic conditions may play an especially important role in shaping life satisfaction among older adults due to inflation, economic instability, limited pension adequacy, and unequal access to welfare resources. Home ownership, education, and perceived income adequacy may therefore represent important indicators of socioeconomic security in later life. A recent Iranian study reported that subjective socioeconomic status (SES) was positively associated with life satisfaction, independent of other psychosocial factors [7]. Similarly, research conducted among Iranian older adults has shown that financial sufficiency and social resources contribute significantly to successful aging and psychological well-being [6]. These findings suggest that socioeconomic inequalities may substantially influence subjective well-being in later life within the Iranian sociocultural context.

Health status is another major determinant of life satisfaction in old age. Aging is frequently accompanied by chronic diseases, functional limitations, sensory impairments, pain, and declining physical capacity, all of which can reduce autonomy and quality of life. Self-rated health, in particular, has been recognized as a robust and reliable predictor of both subjective well-being and mortality among older populations. Older adults who perceive their health as poor are more likely to experience depressive symptoms, reduced social participation, and lower life satisfaction. Recent evidence from Iran demonstrated that poor self-rated health had a strong negative relationship with life satisfaction among adults and older individuals [7]. Furthermore, studies among chronically ill older adults have shown that declining physical and psychological health substantially reduces satisfaction with life and overall well-being [8].

Beyond physical health and economic circumstances, religiosity and spiritual beliefs may also influence how older adults evaluate their lives. Religion often provides meaning, emotional support, coping strategies, hope, and a sense of social belonging, particularly during periods of illness, loss, and existential uncertainty [9]. In many societies, religiosity has been associated with greater psychological resilience, lower levels of depression and anxiety, and higher life satisfaction among older adults. A growing body of literature suggests that spirituality may buffer the negative effects of stress and illness by fostering adaptive coping mechanisms and emotional regulation [10].

The role of religion may be especially relevant in Iran, where religious beliefs and cultural values remain deeply embedded in social life and personal identity. Older Iranian adults often rely on religious practices and spiritual beliefs as sources of emotional comfort and social integration. Previous research has indicated that religiosity is positively associated with hope, resilience, and life satisfaction among older populations [10]. Nevertheless, the relationship between religiosity and life satisfaction is complex and may vary depending on cultural norms, personal beliefs, and social contexts [11]. In addition, relatively few studies have examined the experiences of individuals who identify as non-believers or who are reluctant to disclose their religious beliefs, particularly within Middle Eastern societies. Understanding these distinctions may provide important insights into the social and psychological dimensions of aging and well-being. Most studies examining religion and successful aging have focused on religious involvement, spirituality, or faith among individuals who identify as believers, implicitly assuming that religious belief is a common characteristic of older populations. Consequently, relatively little attention has been paid to older adults who identify as non-believers or who express uncertainty regarding religious belief, particularly in societies where religion constitutes an important social and cultural norm. Examining this subgroup extends the successful-aging literature by considering whether the absence of religious belief may represent not only a difference in personal worldview but also a distinct social and psychological context associated with subjective well-being.

In addition to economic security, health, and religiosity, demographic characteristics such as marital status and education have also been linked to life satisfaction in later life. Marriage and social companionship may provide emotional support, caregiving, and financial stability, whereas social isolation and widowhood may negatively affect well-being. Educational attainment may improve health literacy, socioeconomic opportunities, and coping skills across the life course, ultimately contributing to greater satisfaction in old age [12]. Previous studies among older adults have shown that married individuals and those with higher educational attainment generally report greater life satisfaction than socially or economically disadvantaged groups [13].

Despite the growing international literature on aging and subjective well-being, important conceptual questions remain insufficiently explored in non-Western societies. In Iran, where religious belief is deeply embedded in cultural and social life while older adults increasingly face economic insecurity and health-related challenges, it remains unclear whether religious belief functions as a psychological resource that helps preserve life satisfaction under adverse circumstances. Conversely, little is known about whether older adults who report non-belief or uncertainty regarding belief in God experience additional social or psychological disadvantage beyond the effects of socioeconomic hardship and poor health. Addressing this question is particularly important because most previous studies have focused primarily on religious populations and have rarely examined the experiences of non-believers or individuals expressing doubt within highly religious societies.

Based on the existing theoretical and empirical literature, we formulated the following hypotheses:H1: Older adults with greater socioeconomic resources, including higher educational attainment, adequate household income, and home ownership, will report higher levels of life satisfaction.H2: Better self-reported health status will be positively associated with life satisfaction.H3: Older adults who report belief in God will have higher life satisfaction than those who report non-belief or uncertainty regarding belief in God.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to investigate the associations between socioeconomic resources, health status, and religious belief and life satisfaction among Iranian adults aged 60 years and older. Using ordinal logistic regression analysis, we examined the independent contributions of income adequacy, educational attainment, housing status, marital status, health status, and belief in God to life satisfaction. By identifying the major determinants of subjective well-being in later life, this study may contribute to the development of culturally appropriate policies and interventions designed to promote healthy aging and improve quality of life among older adults in Iran.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study utilized data derived from a survey conducted by the authors to investigate social health and its predictors in urban Iran. The present analysis focused specifically on older adults and included 2,248 community-dwelling individuals aged 60 years and older residing in urban settings across the country.

Sampling procedure

Participants were recruited using a proportional stratified cluster sampling design combined with probability proportional to size (PPS) methodology to maximize nationality and demographic coverage. Sampling strata were defined according to province, county, and population demographic characteristics, including age and sex distributions.

Initially, the total sample was proportionally allocated across Iran’s 31 provinces based on the size of the adult population within each province according to the most recent national census data. Subsequently, between three and five counties from each province were selected using PPS sampling procedures. Within the selected counties, urban neighborhoods were identified and sampled proportionally according to predefined age and sex distributions to ensure consistency with population demographics.

At the final stage of recruitment, quota-based street-intercept sampling was employed within selected neighborhoods. Trained interviewers approached potential participants in public and residential environments, including streets, parks, public squares, and surrounding neighborhood areas. Eligibility criteria included residence within the selected neighborhood and being aged 60 years or older. Recruitment continued until the predetermined demographic quotas for each county were fulfilled. This sampling strategy was selected to balance methodological rigor, demographic representativeness, and logistical feasibility in a large-scale national survey [14, 15].

To minimize temporal selection bias and improve participant diversity, data collection was conducted during two standardized daily intervals: 10:00–13:00 and 16:00–20:00. These time windows were chosen to increase the likelihood of capturing individuals with varying daily routines and activity patterns.

Measures

Life satisfaction

Life satisfaction, the primary outcome variable, was assessed using a single-item global measure asking respondents: “Overall, to what extent are you satisfied with your life?” Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “very low” to “very much.” Single-item measures of life satisfaction have been widely used in population-based surveys and have demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability for assessing subjective well-being in older populations [16].

Self-reported health status

Participants were asked to evaluate their current overall health status (“All in all, how would you describe your current health status?”). Response options included: (1) healthy; (2) new illness (≤ 6 months); (3) long-term illness (> 6 months); and (4) chronic condition or disability (physical or mental). In the regression models, health status was operationalized as a binary variable, distinguishing individuals who reported being healthy (coded as 1) from those who reported any disease condition, including new, long-term, or chronic illness or disability (coded as 0).

Belief in God

Religious belief was assessed using a single item self-reported scale evaluating participants’ belief orientation. Response options included: “do not believe in God,” “in doubt,” “believe in God,” “believe in God, but no religion”, and “prefer not to answer.” To improve conceptual clarity and analytic stability, responses were recoded into three categories. Participants who identified as religious or who reported belief in God despite lacking formal religious affiliation were categorized as believers. Respondents who selected “prefer not to answer” were excluded from the analyses.

Socio-demographic variables

Age was measured as a continuous variable in years. Sex, marital status, educational attainment, and housing tenure were included as categorical variables. Household income adequacy was assessed using a subjective single-item measure evaluating whether respondents perceived their household income as sufficient to meet living expenses. Response options included: “income inadequate for expenses,” “income adequate for expenses,” and “income exceeds expenses.” Subjective income adequacy measures are considered particularly informative in aging research because they capture perceived financial strain beyond objective income indicators [17, 18].

Data collection procedures

Data were collected using structured self-administered paper-based questionnaires. In cases where participants experienced literacy or physical limitations, trained interviewers provided assistance while maintaining response confidentiality and neutrality. All field researchers underwent standardized training covering research ethics, participant recruitment procedures, informed consent practices, questionnaire administration, and quality assurance protocols.

Prior to participation, all respondents received a detailed explanation of the study objectives, confidentiality procedures, and the voluntary nature of participation. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection commenced. Participant anonymity was preserved throughout the study, and no personally identifying information was included in the analytical dataset.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized participants’ characteristics. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with all values below 1.6 (mean VIF = 1.25), indicating no evidence of problematic collinearity.

Ordinal logistic regression models with listwise deletion were used as the primary analytical approach to examine factors associated with life satisfaction among adults aged ≥ 60 years. The initial model included age, sex, marital status, education, household income adequacy, housing tenure, and self-rated health status. A second model additionally included belief in God. Model fit statistics (log-likelihood, AIC, BIC, and McFadden’s pseudo-R²) are reported for both models.

To assess the proportional odds assumption, a generalized ordered logit model was estimated using the gologit2 command in Stata (Williams, 2006). The global Wald test of the parallel lines assumption was not significant (χ²(36) = 28.20, p = 0.820), indicating no overall evidence of violation of the proportional odds assumption. Therefore, the ordered logistic regression model was considered appropriate and retained as the primary analytical approach due to its greater parsimony and interpretability.

Among the 3,064 participants aged ≥ 60 years, 2,248 participants had complete information on all variables included in the final regression models and were included in the analyses; therefore, 816 participants were excluded due to missing information on one or more study variables. We examined the distribution of missing values and compared participants with complete and incomplete data. The observed differences in participant characteristics were generally small. Given the low proportion of missing data for individual variables and the available analytic sample size, a complete-case analysis was applied.

Results

The mean age of participants was 66.76 years (SD = 6.42), with little variation across life satisfaction categories (range of means: 66.66–67.05 years). Overall, 51.9% of participants were male. The majority were married (65.8%). More than one-third (37.0%) had less than a high school diploma. Nearly half (46.8%) reported that their household income was less than their living costs, and most participants (62.1%) were homeowners (Table 1).

In Model 1, compared with married participants, single older adults had approximately 39% lower odds of reporting higher life satisfaction (OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.46–0.81), whereas divorced/separated and widowed participants did not differ significantly from married participants. Compared with participants with less than a high school diploma, those with a high school diploma had approximately 1.4 times the odds of reporting higher life satisfaction (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.13–1.67), and those with a university education had approximately 1.7 times the odds (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.39–2.05). Economic security demonstrated the strongest association among the socioeconomic factors examined. Compared with participants whose income was less than their living costs, those whose income was equal to their living costs had approximately 2.5 times the odds of reporting higher life satisfaction, while those whose income was greater than their living costs had approximately 2.9 times the odds (OR = 2.52, 95% CI: 2.12–2.98; and OR = 2.90, 95% CI: 2.17–3.87, respectively). These associations were larger in magnitude than those observed for educational attainment and homeownership, with university education and homeownership associated with approximately 1.7 and 1.4 times the odds of higher life satisfaction, respectively (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.39–2.05; and OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.11–1.66). In addition, participants classified as healthy had nearly twice the odds of reporting higher life satisfaction compared with those reporting a disease condition (OR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.62–2.26).

In Model 2, the associations between socioeconomic characteristics, health status, and life satisfaction remained largely unchanged after adjustment for belief in God. Compared with participants who believed in God, those who did not believe had approximately 55% lower odds (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.31–0.65), and those who were uncertain about their belief had approximately 56% lower odds (OR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.30–0.65) of reporting higher life satisfaction. These findings indicate that belief in God was independently associated with life satisfaction after accounting for sociodemographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, housing status, and self-reported health.

Discussion

This study examined the association between socioeconomic resources, self-rated health, and religious identity with life satisfaction among Iranian older adults, using a large and nationally relevant sample of individuals aged 60 years and older. The findings demonstrate that life satisfaction in later life is associated with multiple domains, including socioeconomic resources, health status, and religious belief, whereas demographic characteristics such as age and sex were not significantly associated with the outcome after adjustment. Overall, the findings support a multidimensional perspective on aging well, demonstrating that socioeconomic resources, health status, and religious belief are independently associated with life satisfaction in later life.

Socioeconomic resources and cumulative advantage in later life

A central finding of this study is the robust and graded association between SES and life satisfaction. Older adults with adequate and especially more-than-adequate income reported significantly higher levels of life satisfaction compared to those with insufficient income. In addition, home ownership and higher educational attainment were positively associated with life satisfaction, reinforcing the broader evidence that socioeconomic advantages accumulate across the life course and continue to exert protective effects in old age. The magnitude of the association between perceived income adequacy and life satisfaction should also be interpreted within the contemporary Iranian socioeconomic context. During recent years, persistent inflation, rising living costs, and reduced purchasing power have placed considerable financial pressure on many households, particularly older adults who rely on fixed pensions or limited retirement income. Under these conditions, perceived income adequacy may capture not only material resources but also an individual’s ability to meet essential daily needs, maintain independence, and avoid financial insecurity. Consequently, the strong association observed in this study likely reflects the central role of economic security in preserving subjective well-being among older adults living in an environment characterized by sustained economic uncertainty.

Consistent with the cumulative advantage perspective introduced earlier, our findings suggest that early and midlife socioeconomic disparities tend to widen over time, leading to diverging trajectories of health and well-being in later life. Financial security in older age likely reduces psychological stress related to meeting basic needs, increases access to healthcare and supportive services, and enhances autonomy in daily decision-making. Home ownership may further contribute to a sense of stability, identity continuity, and perceived control, all of which are known contributors to subjective well-being in older populations [19, 20].

Importantly, the strength of the association between income and life satisfaction in this study suggests that economic security remains a foundational predictor of well-being even in contexts where family and social support networks may partially buffer material deprivation. This finding highlights the continuing relevance of structural inequalities in shaping aging experiences.

Self-rated health as a predictor of life satisfaction

Older adults reporting poor health exhibited a dramatic reduction in the likelihood of higher life satisfaction, indicating that subjective health perception is a central axis around which overall well-being is organized.

This finding is consistent with extensive gerontological literature showing that self-rated health is a powerful summary indicator of both objective health status and subjective interpretation of physical functioning. In later life, health limitations often directly constrain independence, social participation, and engagement in meaningful activities. Consequently, deteriorations in perceived health are likely to have immediate and profound effects on evaluative judgments of life quality [21, 22].

The magnitude of the association observed in this study suggests that health may function not only as one domain among many but as a primary organizing factor in older adults’ overall assessment of life satisfaction. This reinforces the importance of preventive health care, chronic disease management, and rehabilitation services in promoting successful aging.

Religious identity and existential resources

The findings also indicate that religious belief is significantly associated with life satisfaction. Individuals who reported non-belief or doubt in God exhibited lower life satisfaction compared to believers. This pattern suggests that religious identity may operate as an important psychosocial resource in later life, potentially providing meaning, existential security, and coping mechanisms in the face of aging-related challenges.

Religious involvement and belief systems have been widely documented as sources of psychological resilience in older adults [23]. They may provide interpretive frameworks that help individuals make sense of suffering, decline, and mortality, thereby buffering the negative emotional impact of aging-related losses. Additionally, religious communities often serve as sources of social integration, emotional support, and structured engagement, all of which contribute to enhanced well-being [24]. Beyond its role as a source of personal meaning and psychological coping, religious belief may also operate through broader social mechanisms. In Iran, where religious institutions and practices remain closely integrated with community life, religion can facilitate social participation, interpersonal trust, and access to informal support networks. From this perspective, the higher life satisfaction observed among participants reporting belief in God may reflect not only the psychological benefits of religious belief but also the advantages associated with greater social integration and social capital. Conversely, older adults reporting non-belief or uncertainty regarding belief in God may experience reduced access to these socially embedded resources, which could contribute to lower levels of subjective well-being.

The finding that non-believers report lower life satisfaction may reflect the absence of these psychosocial and community-based resources in contexts where religious affiliation is culturally normative. However, caution is warranted in interpreting this result, as religiosity may also be intertwined with unmeasured cultural, familial, or personality-related factors.

Implications

The findings have several important implications. First, policies aimed at improving life satisfaction among older adults should prioritize economic security, including income support and housing stability. Second, health promotion and chronic disease management should remain central pillars of aging policy, given their profound influence on subjective well-being. Third, psychosocial and cultural resources, including religious and meaning-making systems, should be acknowledged as important components of healthy aging frameworks.

At the community level, interventions that strengthen social integration and provide opportunities for meaningful engagement may help mitigate the negative effects of both economic disadvantage and health decline. Importantly, the differential pattern observed among non-believers suggests that inclusive approaches are needed to support diverse existential orientations in aging populations.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and reverse causality cannot be ruled out, particularly regarding the relationship between health and life satisfaction. Self-reported measures may also introduce reporting bias. In addition, religiosity was measured through a single item scale, which may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of religious experience, including practice, spirituality, and institutional participation. Also, the study did not assess social participation, social capital, or engagement with religious communities. Consequently, the mechanisms through which religious belief may influence life satisfaction could not be directly examined. Future research would benefit from longitudinal designs and more nuanced measures of religiosity and psychological well-being.

Although missing-data patterns were assessed and participant characteristics were compared between those with complete and incomplete data, some differences were observed, suggesting that missingness may not have been completely random. Therefore, the possibility of bias due to missing information and the use of a complete-case approach should be considered when interpreting the findings.

The sampling design was proportionate at the provincial level and quotas were aligned with census age–sex distributions, however, the final stage of street-based recruitment may have resulted in selection bias. Older adults with limited mobility, those spending less time in community settings, or those living in care facilities may have been less likely to be included. Data collection during two daily time periods was intended to improve participant coverage; however, it may not have fully addressed this potential limitation. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously when extending conclusions to older adults with substantial health or functional limitations.

Responses indicating “prefer not to answer” for belief in God were excluded from the regression analyses because they could not be assigned to a specific belief category. However, given that non-response to sensitive topics may reflect characteristics of the respondents, the possibility of bias due to the exclusion of these responses cannot be completely ruled out.

Although the models adjusted for health status using a four-category measure (healthy, new illness, long-term illness, and chronic condition or disability), some potentially important confounders, including living arrangement, objective chronic disease burden, functional status, and depressive symptoms, were not assessed. These unmeasured factors may have contributed to residual, and the observed associations should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that life satisfaction among Iranian older adults is shaped by an interplay of socioeconomic resources, perceived health status, and religious identity. Socioeconomic security, including income adequacy, education, and home ownership, further contributes to higher life satisfaction, highlighting the enduring influence of structural advantage in aging.

Religious belief also plays a meaningful role, with non-belief and doubt regarding God both associated with lower life satisfaction.

The findings of this study have several practical implications for policies promoting healthy aging in Iran. Efforts to improve the financial security of older adults through adequate pension support and measures that reduce economic vulnerability may substantially enhance life satisfaction. Given the strong association between perceived health and subjective well-being, expanding access to age-friendly primary healthcare, preventive services, and chronic disease management should remain a public health priority. In addition, the observed differences associated with religious belief suggest the importance of ensuring that all older adults, including those who identify as non-believers or express uncertainty regarding religious belief, have opportunities for meaningful social engagement. Community-based programs, senior day-care centers, and social participation initiatives delivered through social welfare agencies and local community organizations may help reduce social isolation and promote psychological well-being irrespective of religious affiliation. Such inclusive approaches may complement existing community resources while ensuring equitable support for diverse groups of older adults.
