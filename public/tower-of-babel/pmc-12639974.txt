Entomological impact of three years of clothianidin-based indoor residual spraying in two high malaria endemic districts in Côte d’Ivoire
Authors: Joseph Chabi, Constant Victorien Ako Edi, Sylvester Coleman, Guy Constant N’Guessan Gbalegba, Allassane Foungoye Ouattara, Brice Renaud N’Guessan Broudje, Ruth Marie Adjoua Kouame, Firmain N’Dri Yokoly, Dramane Soro, Lucien Yao Konan, Roméo N’Tamon N’Tamon, Emmanuel Tia, Roseline Monsan Yapo, Guillaume Koffi Konan, Alexis Sea Tea, Maurice Adja Akre, Didier Stephane Mintokapieu Kpan, Antoine Mea Tanoh, Ndombour Gning Cissé, Cecilia Flatley, Bernard Loukou Kouassi

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndoor residual spraying (IRS) represents a major malaria vector control strategy to reduce malaria burden. The first major IRS campaign for malaria control in Côte d’Ivoire was implemented by the US Presidents Malaria Initiative in two malaria endemic districts between 2020 and 2022. This study report on the effect of clothianidin-based IRS on key entomological indices of malaria transmission in the IRS districts following three rounds of IRS with clothianidin-based insecticide formulations.MethodsPre- and post-IRS adult mosquito collections were conducted monthly in two IRS districts (Sakassou and Nassian) and two control districts (Beoumi and Dabakala) using human landing catches (HLCs) and pyrethrum spray catches (PSC) from April 2019 through December 2022. Mosquitoes collected were morphologically identified, further analysed for sibling species using PCR, and tested for sporozoite infection using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). A proportion of the Anopheles gambiae sensu lato mosquitoes collected from HLCs were also dissected to assess their parity rates.ResultsAnopheles coluzzii and An. gambiae were the main malaria vectors collected in the study area. In the IRS arm HBR declined progressively across spray rounds, with a statistically significant reduction of about 60% by the third round (IRR = 0.40; p = 0.002) but not in the controls (all p > 0.5). The odds of sporozoite infection decreased by 49% after the first (OR = 0.51; p = 0.023) and second (OR = 0.51, p = 0.043) at the IRS sites but did not show any further decline by the third round (p = 0.51). These reductions resulted in four-fold decrease in EIR from 3.92 infective/bites/person/night pre-IRS to 1.25 ib/p/n post-IRS. EIR in control declined only marginally from 0.91ib/p/n pre-IRS to 0.58 ib/p/n post-IRS.ConclusionsClothianidin-based IRS was associated with a clear reduction in An. gambiae and An. coluzzii biting densities in the IRS districts and, more importantly, a significant reduction in EIR after accounting for changes over the same period in the neighbouring control areas. These findings are consistent with the epidemiological findings reported in the same sites and demonstrates the utility of IRS as an important tool for malaria control in a highly endemic setting like Côte d’Ivoire.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05663-5.

Background

Malaria morbidity and mortality have been drastically reduced across the world through effective vector control strategies and implementation of new tools during the last two decades [1, 2]. Indoor residual spraying (IRS) and mass distribution of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) are the key contributors to the reduction of malaria incidence in most of the endemic countries [1, 3]. However, both strategies require significant investments to reach expected outcomes and protect the entire populations. Therefore, national malaria programmes of countries relied on external funding and support from stakeholders to achieve their goals of malaria reduction or elimination. IRS implemented in the majority of endemic countries was reported to have drastically decreased both malaria incidences and entomological indices of transmission, mostly when the spraying was maintained for several years [4–9].

In Côte d’Ivoire, malaria accounts for the greater proportion of all visits to heath facilities across the country despite the interventions deployed by the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) and partners. The entire population is at risk of contracting the disease since about 71% of the population live in high endemic areas (incidence between 200 and 5000 per thousand inhabitants (%)) [10]. Furthermore, the incidence of malaria has increased from 154.6 to 173.0‰ in the general population and from 286.9 to 4410‰ in children under 5 years of age between 2016 to 2020 [10, 11].

Malaria incidence is disparate across the country with some districts like Sakassou and Nassian leading malaria case reports between 2015 and 2020 [10]. Regarding malaria transmission, four Anopheles species have been incriminated in malaria transmission across the country with geographical location and season specific [12–15]. However, Anopheles gambiae and Anopheles coluzzii represent the main vectors responsible for most infections across the country and Plasmodium falciparum being the primary parasite transmitted (99%) [10]. Anopheles funestus sensu lato (s.l.) played a second role in some of the location while Anopheles nili was still a sporadic and geo-located vector. In addition, malaria transmission is stable throughout the year including peaks observed during the rainy season [12–15].

To ensure that appropriate and sustainable vector control interventions are used, the NMCP and stakeholder decisions were made strategically and driven by entomological data to inform vector control selection, planning and implementation [16]. The Côte d'Ivoire NMCP has continuously implemented ITN mass distribution since 2011 across the country and reached the fourth and fifth distribution campaigns in 2021 and 2024 conducted using an insecticide resistance data-based stratification of ITNs to appropriately target malaria vectors and reduce malaria incidence across the country. In the 2016–2020 National Strategic Plan [17], the NMCP decided to adopt IRS as a major strategy to be implemented in the districts with the highest malaria burden to complement ITNs in an effort to further reduce the malaria burden in the country. Thus, three consecutive years of IRS were conducted in Sakassou and Nassian in 2020, 2021 and 2022 using clothianidin-based IRS insecticides formulation (SumiShield® 50WG and Fludora® Fusion). This IRS campaign represented the first large scale IRS implemented in Côte d'Ivoire. Hence there was the need to evaluate both entomological and epidemiological endpoints to help guide decision-making by the NMCP prior to any scale up of IRS to other endemic district.

To evaluate the entomological impact of the IRS in both districts, entomological vector surveillance was conducted before and after IRS in Sakassou and Nassian. In parallel, two other non-sprayed districts were selected in the same geographical area with similar malaria incidences to serve as control. The data collection was based on specific approaches enabling comparing baseline and post-IRS data between sites and, trends between IRS and control sites in terms of vector species and density, parity, entomological inoculation rate and indoor resting density.

Methods

Study sites

Entomological monitoring was conducted in four sites including two IRS sites (Sakassou and Nassian) and two unsprayed sites (Beoumi and Dabakala) serving as control of the IRS sites (Fig. 1). Distribution of pyrethroid-only ITNs was the sole vector control intervention that was implemented in all sites before IRS in Sakassou and Nassian. The pairs of districts (Beoumi-Sakassou and Dabakala-Nassian) had similar eco-geographical characteristics enabling comparison of entomological indicators. In all the districts, malaria transmission was permanent with an upsurge during the rainy season [15].

Sakassou (7º 27′ 20'' N; −5º 17′ 25'' W) and Beoumi (7º 40′ 27'' N; −5º 34′ 54'' W) are in the pre-forested savannah zone in the Region of Gbêke in Central Côte d’Ivoire. Sakassou has an area of approximately 1820 km2 and a population of 126,468 inhabitants while Beoumi district covers 1130 km2 and a population of 178,145 inhabitants. The climate is tropical with two rainy seasons (March–July and September–October) and two dry seasons (July–August and November–February). However, in recent years, there have been variations in rainfall due to climate change with rainfall varying between 900 and 2000 mm.

Nassian district (8º 27′ 10'' N; −3º 28′ 17'' W) in the Region of Bounkani and Dabakala (8º 21′ 52'' N; −4º 26′ 11'' W) is in the Region of Hambol are in the North-East of Côte d'Ivoire. Nassian covers an area of 2810 km2 with a population of 80,264 inhabitants and Dabakala 1940 km2 where about 238,746 inhabitants live. The climate of both sites is of Sudanese type, with two seasons: a dry season which lasts from 5 to 6 months extending from November to April in recent years, and a rainy season lasting 6–7 months, from April to October. The annual average rainfall varies between 900 and 1200 mm.

IRS was conducted once annually from 2020 to 2022 in Sakassou and Nassian, representing three consecutive IRS campaigns. During the 2020 and 2021 campaigns, SumiShield was sprayed in Nassian and Fludora Fusion in Sakassou. Initially planned to be strategically conducted in May–June, the 2020 and 2021 campaigns were shifted to August due to the Corona Virus (Covid-19) pandemic. In 2022, the campaign was conducted in May 2022 and IRS insecticides were switched between sites with Fludora Fusion being sprayed in Nassian and SumiShield in Sakassou. During the three campaigns, a mean of 34,146 household structures were found and 31,696 (94.6%) were sprayed in Sakassou and mean of 26,478 structures in Nassian out of which 25,533 (97.2%) were sprayed. The overall mean population protected during the three IRS campaigns was 128,817 inhabitants in Sakassou and 79,012 in Nassian with some fluctuations each year of IRS due to population movement.

Adult mosquito collection

Adult mosquitoes were collected monthly from April 2019 to December 2022 using human landing catch (HLC) and pyrethrum spraying catch (PSC) methods. Forty-three collection months were conducted from May 2019 through December 2022. Furthermore, May 2019 to July 2020 represented the baseline collection period while from August 2020 to December 2022 were used as post spray collection months with IRS campaigns implemented in August 2020 and 2021 and May–June 2022. HLCs were conducted indoors and outdoors in four houses per site during two consecutive nights per month between 6:00 pm and 6:00 am to collect adult mosquitoes landing on human baits while PSCs were conducted in 30 houses per site per month between 6:00 a.m. and 8:00 a.m. Mosquito collections were performed in the same houses for HLCs throughout the longitudinal monitoring period, while PSC collections were conducted in randomly selected houses, depending on the availability of households. All collected mosquitoes were morphologically identified using the key of Gillies and Coetzee [18] and Coetzee [19]. Furthermore, all An. gambiae s.l. were ovary dissected per site and per collection night during monthly collections when the number collected by HLC is below 100 mosquitoes while a percentage was selected for dissection when the density was high. The ovaries were examined for parity by observing the coiling degree in the ovarian tracheoles [20]. All mosquitoes were stored individually in Eppendorf tubes on silica gel, labelled and sent to the laboratory for molecular analyses.

Identification of malaria vector species and detection of circumsporozoite infection

The DNA of each individual mosquito was extracted using the protocol designed by Collins et al. [21]. Anopheles gambiae complex species were identified following the short-interspersed element protocol described by Santolamazza et al. [22]. The Plasmodium falciparum infection was determined using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay protocol for identification of P. falciparum circumsporozoite [23, 24] and all positive mosquitoes were boiled and retested for confirmation [25].

Data analysis

Data capture, cleaning & processing

A District Health Information Software (DHIS2)-based database was used for entry of field paper collected data and management, data workflows, technical reviews, data cleaning and exported for analyses. The primary entomological indicators investigated include the Human biting rates (HBR), sporozoite rate (SR), indoor resting density (IRD), and entomological inoculation rates (EIR). The HBR is defined as the mean number of bites per person per night calculated using the vector density collected over the number of person-night used as mosquito collectors, SR is defined as the proportion of infected mosquitoes (i.e. the sporozoite-positive) out of the total number tested by ELISA. The EIR is a standard metric of transmission intensity calculated as the product of the SR and the HBR, whilst the IRD represents the number of mosquitoes collected resting indoors divided by the number of houses sprayed during PSCs.

Two main effect measures were compared, including: i) between-treatment arm effect, which compared differential change between the IRS and the control sites over the period and ii) within-treatment arm effect; comparing pre- vs post-IRS within each treatment arm. The pre -IRS period covers the 14-month baseline period before IRS (April 2019–August 2020), whilst the post-IRS period covers 28 months after IRS (September 2020–December 2022); split into 12 months post-IRS 1, 10 months post 1RS 2 and 6 months post IRS 3.

R version 4.5.1 (RTeam, 2025) was used to perform the trend analysis of the indoor and outdoor EIRs. Graphical representation of time series data was done using the ggplot2 package (Wickham, 2016), which provided clear and detailed visualizations of the data. The LOESS (Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing) model based on a polynomial function to estimate local trends in the data was used for the trend analysis. Confidence intervals for the smoothed estimates were calculated to assess the precision of the identified trends. Confidence intervals are typically constructed using a standard error approach, with typical margins of error ± 1.96 × SE, where SE is the standard error of the estimate at each point.

Statistical analysis

Impact of IRS on entomological indices

The impact of each IRS round on HBR and sporozoite rate were analyzed using mixed-effects regression models fitted in glmmTMB. The baseline measurements were used as the comparator. HBR was modelled with a Tweedie GLMM and log link, because it is a continuous rate variable containing zeros and non-normal dispersion. Sporozoite positivity, expressed as the number of infected mosquitoes out of the total tested, was modelled with a Binomial GLMM and logit link. In both models, IRS round, mean daily precipitation (mm day⁻1), and mean temperature (°C) were entered used as fixed effects, while month and site were included as random intercepts to account for temporal and spatial variations.

Difference-in-Differences (DiD)

The impact of IRS on the entomological outcomes was evaluated using a difference-in-differences (DiD) method that compared IRS sites with neighboring control sites across two periods pre-IRS and post-IRS using R version 4.5.1 (RTeam, 2025). The primary entomological outcome was the HBR (mean bites per person per night) and the EIR defined as product of HBR and the sporozoite rate (SR), we estimated SR internally (via a binomial generalized linear mixed model (GLMM)) to construct EIR.

A random intercept for month accounted for within-month clustering. Response-scale marginal means for each arm × period cell were obtained with means (regridded to the response scale).

Human biting rate and biting time of Anopheles gambiae s.l.

The overall mean HBR of An. gambiae s.l. was highest in Sakassou (196.4 bpn), followed by Dabakala (47.1 bpn) and Beoumi (28.4 bpn) over the study period. Nassian recorded the lowest HBR (13.7 bpn) during the four-year collections (Supp. data 2). Anopheles gambiae s.l. biting time was similar in all sites with the peak biting observed between 0:00 am and 3:00 am, before and after IRS and both indoors and outdoors.

During the baseline collection, higher HBR of An. gambiae s.l. was observed in Sakassou (mean of 303.8 bpn), followed by Dabakala (56.5 bpn), Beoumi (35.5 bpn) and Nassian (17.5 bpn). The same trend was observed during the three consecutive years of IRS with an overall mean HBR of 142.6 bpn in Sakassou, 42.5 bpn in Dabakala, 24.9 bpn in Beoumi and 11.8 bpn in Nassian. Furthermore, when comparing the trend in each site over the collection period, there was a significant decrease of the mean HBR recorded after IRS in Sakassou (p = 0.0004) compared to the baseline collection while no significant difference was recorded in the other sites (Nassian (p = 0.5394), Beoumi (p = 0.5483) and Dabakala (p = 0.4713)). However, the overall mean HBR of the combined IRS sites (Sakassou and Nassian) was significantly decreased by about 53% (163.2 bpn vs 77.2 bpn; p = 0.0003) after the three years of IRS, compared to the baseline in both sites, while the HBR in the control sites was reduced by about 27% but was not statistically different from the baseline collections (46.0 vs 33.7 bpn; p = 0.5483) (Supp. data 2).

At IRS sites, HBR declined progressively across spray rounds, with a statistically significant reduction of about 60% by the third round (Rate Ratio (RR) = 0.40, 95% CI 0.23–0.72; p = 0.002) (Supp. data 1, Table S-3). Rainfall showed a modest positive association with biting rate (Rate Ratio = 1.09 per mm day⁻1; p = 0.005), while temperature was negatively associated (RR = 0.72 per °C; p = 0.003). In the control sites, biting rates showed no significant change over time (all p > 0.1), although higher temperature increased biting activity (RR = 1.51; p = 0.03).

In Sakassou (51.9%; p = 0.068) and Dabakala (51.1%; p = 0.068), biting occurred similarly indoors and outdoors. The mean indoor HBR during the baseline was 310.0 bpn in Sakassou with the highest peaks recorded in November 2019 (423.0 bpn) and in June 2020 (492.0 bpn). This significantly decreased to 133.2 bpn (p = 0.034) over the period between August 2020 and July 2021 after the first round of IRS. Entomological inoculation rates

A total of 14,632 (representing about 6.0%) of all the collected An. gambiae s.l. were tested for Plasmodium circumsporozoite proteins including 7751 from control sites and 6881 from IRS sites. The mean SR of An. gambiae s.l. was similar between Sakassou and Beoumi (1.9% vs 2.0%, respectively; p = 0.485) as well as between Nassian and Dabakala (5.4% vs 2.9%; p = 0.1129) in 2019. However, a significant decrease of the SR was observed in Sakassou after IRS compared to the baseline (1.9% vs 0.6%; p = 0.0067) while similar SRs were observed in Nassian (5.4% vs 4.2%; p = 0.3210), Dabakala (2.9% vs 1.8%; p = 0.7623) and Beoumi (2.0% vs 1.7%; p = 0.7087).

Impact of IRS round on sporozoite infectivity

The binomial GLMM showed a significant reduction in the odds of sporozoite infection following the first and second rounds of IRS. In comparison to the baseline the sporozoite rates in the IRS sites decreased by approximately 49% after the first and second rounds (IRS 1: OR = 0.51, 95% CI 0.29–0.91; p = 0.023 and IRS 2: OR = 0.51, 95% CI 0.26–0.98; p = 0.043). No further decline was observed in the third round (OR = 0.82; p = 0.505). In contrast, the control sites showed no significant change in sporozoite rate across the same period (all p > 0.05).

Comparing the overall mean EIRs

The cumulative mean EIRs of An. gambiae s.l. was significantly higher in both IRS sites (2.918 infected bites/person/night (ibpn)) compared to the control sites (0.796 ibpn); (p = 0.0134) during the baseline collection in 2019. Conversely, there was a significant decrease in the overall and cumulative EIRs (p = 0.048) in IRS sites (from 2.918 to 0.507 ibpn) compared to that of the control site from 0.796 to 0.554 ibpn); after all spray years (Supp. data 2). Like the SR, the overall mean EIR in Sakassou decreased significantly after spraying (4.909 ibpn vs 0.784 ibpn; p = 0.0005) and was statistically lower than all other three sites: Nassian (0.784 ibpn vs 0.231 ibpn; (p = 0.1422)), Dabakala (1.028 ibpn vs 0.755 ibpn; (p = 0.9787)) and Beoumi (0.564 ibpn vs 0.352 ibpn; (p = 0.4082)). The overall percentage reductions were 84% in Sakassou and 71% in Nassian, compared with 27% and 38% in the control sites of Dabakala and Beoumi, respectively.

When comparing each IRS site and its control, the mean EIR was significantly higher in Sakassou (4.909 ibpn) than Beoumi (0.609 ibpn); (p = 0.0017) at the baseline. After IRS, both site EIRs were not significantly different (0.784 ibpn vs 0.352 ibpn; (p = 0.3851)). Parity rate

A total of 38,307 non-bloodfed female An. gambiae s.l. collected by HLCs were examined for parity status over the study period. At baseline, the mean parity rate in the IRS sites (Nassian and Sakassou) was 69.9% and was similar to the parity rate in the control sites (66.7%), (Beoumi and Dabakala) (p = 0.5818). When comparing the trends in each individual site, the mean parity significantly increased in Sakassou (65.4%–76.4%; p = 0.0266), and Dabakala (63.8%–81.4%; p = 0.0119) while no significant difference was recorded in parity rates in Beoumi (69.6%–80.0%; p = 0.2482) and Nassian (74.7%–68.8%; p = 0.4696).

However, when compared between pre and post IRS, the overall mean parity rate significantly increased in control sites (66.7%–80.7%; p = 0.004) while no significant difference was observed in the IRS sites (69.9%–72.7%; p = 0.5410). Over the duration of the study the overall mean parity rate of An. gambiae s.l. was 73.1% (13,914/19,028) at the IRS sites and 76.7% (14,778/19,279) at the control sites. A Z-test for the difference in proportions showed a significant difference in parity rates between mosquitoes from the IRS (73.1%) and control areas (76.7%) (pooled sample proportion = 0.7490, SE = 0.00443, Z = − 7.97, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Clothianidin-based IRS was associated with a clear reduction in An. gambiae and An. coluzzii biting densities in the IRS districts and, more importantly, a significant reduction in vector parity, sporozoite infectivity and EIR after accounting for changes over the same period in the neighbouring control areas. These findings are consistent with the epidemiological impact reported by Hilton et al. [28] in the same sites. The study showed that the malaria incidence in IRS sites declined significantly after IRS deployment with more than 126,160 malaria cases averted in the two sprayed sites while no significant change occurred in the control sites [28]. However, our findings contrast with the heterogenous impact of clothianidin-based IRS on malaria cases reported in Ghana, and the seemingly lack of efficacy in Uganda [29–31].

For IRS to be effective, it is most important that the vectors bite and rest indoors and are susceptible to the insecticide sprayed [32]. In addition to these vector-specific characteristics, properties of the IRS product (formulation and residual efficacy), community acceptance, type of spray surface, and timing of the spray campaign, are seen as important considerations that influence the efficacy of IRS [33, 34]. All these factors might have contributed to the observed efficacy of clothianidin-based IRS in Nassian and Sakassou following the strategic deployment of the intervention.

Anopheles gambiae and An. coluzzii were the main malaria vectors found across the sites and throughout the study period. Even though there were differences in the predominance of the sibling species between Nassian (99% An. gambiae) and Sakassou (100% An. coluzzii) the effect of IRS was comparable between the two sites suggesting that both vectors were equally impacted by the intervention. This impact could be linked to the observed host-seeking behaviour of these vectors in the two IRS districts. Anopheles gambiae in Nassian was found to be predominantly endophilic while An. coluzzii in Sakassou bites equally indoors and outdoors. These trends in host seeking were observed before and after IRS and was the basis for the continued IRS campaign in both districts. The plasticity in host seeking behavior of An. coluzzii from Sakassou is consistent with reports of behavioural plasticity in An. coluzzii [35]. Host availability has often been found to shape such plastic host-seeking behaviours [36]. While such plastic behaviour is seen as a challenge to the efficacy of most conventional control tools (IRS and ITNs), no decline in IRS efficacy was observed in Sakassou. In fact, the greatest impact of clothianidin-based IRS on EIRs was recorded at Sakassou when compared to Nassian which had predominantly An. gambiae.

The higher IRDs of vectors in Sakassou suggests that, although feeding occurred both indoors and outdoors, An. coluzzii still rested indoors at some point in the day, likely due to unfavourable daytime conditions outdoors characteristic of tropical savannah zone [37]. Unfavorable daytime conditions may compel exophilic vectors to rest temporarily indoors or under sprayed eaves for shade, as reported elsewhere [38]. There are also instances (Sri Lanka and Iran) where outdoor feeding vectors were still found to be vulnerable to control with IRS, because they tended to rest indoors after taking their blood meal outdoors [39]. We hypothesize that this could have been the case for An. coluzzii in Sakassou and might explain the impact of IRS in this area. Before IRS, the mean indoor and outdoor HBRs were significantly higher in the IRS sites but declined during the post-IRS collections while both indoor and outdoor HBRs remained similar throughout the study period in the control sites. Although climatic factors such as rainfall may influence mosquito populations, no differences in rainfall patterns were observed between IRS and control sites. Therefore, the observed declines in IRS areas are likely attributable to the intervention itself.

The main entomological indicators that have historically been impacted after IRS implementation are adult vector density, longevity/parity and decrease the transmission intensity of the disease they target [40]. Vector parity represents one of the key parameters associated with IRS impact on malaria vectors. The parity rates recorded through mosquito ovary dissection were similar in both control and IRS sites during the baseline collection period. After IRS, a rise in parity across both IRS and control sites was observed, however the rates in IRS sites were significantly lower post-IRS. The marginal increase in parity post-IRS could be attributed to the mode of action of clothianidin. It is known that neonicotinoid insecticides have a delayed effect or slow-acting properties due to their mode of action [41]. They do not have a knock down effect on mosquitoes and may allow mosquitoes to survive at least up to their first or second egg laying or become parous. Perhaps a proper differentiating of parity into early parous and older parous females could have allowed for a better assessment of the impact of clothianidin on parity.

Increased parity is expected to result in a surge in the vectorial capacity of the vectors. However, this was not the case in this study area. Despite a modest increase in parity suggesting increased adult longevity, the clothianidin-based IRS led to a pronounced reduction in biting density and two-fold larger proportional reductions in EIR in the IRS sites vs control sites. This suggest that in this study IRS suppressed malaria transmission primarily by reducing biting risks rather than through direct adult mortality or decreased survival alone.

A comparison of the relative decline in the vector biting rates relative to the baseline shows that the largest decline (60%) was recorded post IRS 3. This significant decline can in part be attributed to the shift in the timing for the IRS campaign. The first two rounds of IRS (IRS 1 in 2020 and IRS 2 in 2021) were implemented in August, which coincided with the peak or midway through the major rainy season. These spraying schedules were made due to the Covid-19 pandemic that shifted the initial plan to spray in May 2020. IRS 3 on the other hand was implemented between May–June 2022, which represents the start of the rainy season in the Central and Savannah regions of Côte d’Ivoire. The finding of the current study is consistent with some studies that have shown that the best time for IRS is just before mosquito populations peak [43, 44]. By simulating IRS to start earlier than was normally implemented, Worall et al. [45] found that spraying earlier in the rainy season could significantly reduce malaria case burden by an additional 114, 400 [45]. Additionally, starting spray campaigns before the rains peak has the additional programmatic benefit of increased coverages [45]. A delayed start to IRS campaigns can leave portions of the population unprotected throughout the malaria transmission period. To maximize coverage and impact, IRS programs should be implemented prior to the onset of peak rains. Historically, there were concerns that spraying too early in the season could result in mosquito populations peaking just as the residual efficacy of earlier insecticides (which typically lasted 4–6 months) was waning. In contrast, the newer generation of IRS products including SumiShield and Fludora Fusion, have much longer residual life (ranging from 8 to 12 months) and can provide sustained protection throughout the transmission season [46]. However, the delayed start of the IRS campaign may not have been the sole factor contributing to the marginally reduced entomological impact observed during the first two rounds of IRS. The 60% reduction in vector biting rates observed after the third IRS round could be a confirmation of the cumulative impact of repeated IRS applications. These results are consistent with previous findings from West and East Africa showing that IRS can significantly impact entomological indices of transmission [6–8]. However, prolonged implementation of insecticide-based interventions may lead to unintended consequences, such as altered ecology of malaria vector communities in favour of species with more plastic feeding and resting behaviour [47].

A shift in vector species composition or replacement by exophilic or resistant species overtime can negatively impact the efficacy of IRS in this area, as previously reported by other studies [48]. In Western Kenya, An. funestus—a highly anthropophilic and endophilic species, was rapidly replaced by Anopheles arabiensis after just one IRS round. While this replacement may reflect IRS effectiveness, the emergence of a more zoophilic and exophilic vector raises concerns about sustained transmission risk and control challenges [48, 49]. The failure of clothianidin-based IRS in Uganda for example was temporally correlated with the replacement of An. gambiae by An. funestus as the primary malaria vector. This was possible because it exhibited high tolerance to clothianidin compared to An. gambiae [30]. For now, there is no indication of tolerance or resistance of the predominant vector species in the study area to clothianidin. The significant decline in IRD post IRS could be an indication that the vectors that rested in sprayed rooms were killed. There is also no indication of vector population replacement post IRS since the vector species composition has remained similar over the years. However, we observed a significant increase in An. funestus s.l. population, replacing An. gambiae towards the end of the rainy season of each year especially in Nassian. This is consistent with previous studies that have shown that the geographical location of Nassian periodically provides suitable environmental conditions for An. funestus s.l. [12]. This trend in Nassian should be monitored over time, given the report from Uganda of a reduced impact of clothianidin-based IRS on An. funestus s.l. This study findings appear consistent with this observation, as IRS in Nassian had a weaker effect on An. funestus s.l. than on An. gambiae s.l. The reduction in EIR for An. funestus s.l. (44%) was smaller than that observed for An. gambiae (71%). It is also worth noting that, although a substantial proportion of transmission occurred indoors, some transmission still took place outdoors beyond the reach of IRS and ITNs. This was particularly evident during the third round of IRS when it appeared that despite the reductions in vector density the impact on sporozoite infectivity had plateaued. The lack of a further reduction during the third round suggests residual transmission, likely maintained by vectors resting outdoors or avoiding contact with sprayed surfaces or vectors that might be tolerant to clothianidin.

Additional data on vector susceptibility and behavioural responses to clothianidin are needed to clarify species-specific responses and guide the deployment of clothianidin-based IRS in Côte d’Ivoire and countries where IRS remain a key strategy for malaria vector control intervention.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that IRS using clothianidin-based formulations effectively reduced vector density and infection rates, leading to a measurable reduction in the transmission potential of vectors. The simultaneous absence of comparable declines at control sites reinforces the causal effect of IRS. Additionally, these findings confirm the effectiveness of IRS in reducing malaria burden in highly endemic areas of Africa, when implemented optimally with the right insecticide, targeted against the appropriate vector, and sustained at high coverage levels.

Limitations

The selection of IRS control sites were based on malaria case incidences but not on vector density and malaria transmission indicators. Though the IRS sites and their respective controls are in the same administrative and regional area, there was a huge difference in vector density in Sakassou compared to Beoumi in terms of HBR comparisons.
