Plasma IL-6 level predict the risk of in-hospital mortality in HIV-associated pneumocystis pneumonia
Authors: Huan Xia, Jing Song, Yue Hu, Lei Li, Liying Gao, Ping Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe study aimed to determine plasma immune-inflammatory biomarkers that may predict in-hospital mortality in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-infected individuals diagnosed with pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP).MethodsThis study prospectively included 125 antiretroviral therapy (ART)-naïve, HIV-infected patients with PCP. Biomarkers involving clinical variables and 8 pre-selected plasma inflammatory cytokines (IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, IL-17, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) were evaluated at time of admission. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors substantially associated with in-hospital mortality. The ROC curve was used to determine the predictive value of biomarkers for in-hospital mortality.ResultsOur results show a hospital mortality rate of 12.8% (16/125). When compared to surviving AIDS PCP patients, non-survivors had substantially higher levels of C-reactive protein, IL-6, aspartate aminotransferase, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and lower levels of albumin (ALB), PO2, and CD4 count. We found a significant association between increased IL-6 levels and hospital mortality using multivariable logistic regression analysis (adjusted odds ratio per 20 pg/mL increase, 1.127; 95% CI, 1.047–1.230; p = 0.004). The plasma IL-6 levels had the largest area under the curve (AUC) (0.883; 95%CI, 0.812–0.953), compared to CD4+ T cell (AUC, 0.789; 95%CI, 0.697–0.881), ALB (AUC, 0.776; 95%CI, 0.661–0.892), and LDH (AUC, 0.703; 95%CI, 0.573–0.832).ConclusionsElevated plasma IL-6 levels at admission are significantly associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality in HIV-infected patients with PCP.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, HIV, IL-6, Mortality

Introduction

Pneumocystis jirovecii is an opportunistic fungus capable of causing life-threatening pneumonia. It is a human-specific disease that exhibits parasitic behavior, as per the natural history of P. jirovecii. While healthy individuals are frequently exposed, they are more likely to experience mild or asymptomatic infections; pneumonia and severe infections are more likely to occur when there is immunological dysfunction [1]. Patients with compromised immune systems, especially those living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), are more vulnerable to pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP). Before the introduction of antiretroviral therapy (ART) and PCP prophylaxis, PCP was common in 70–80% of HIV/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) patients, leading to significant mortality (20%-40%) and recurrence rates of as high as 50% within the first year of diagnosis [2]. When the CD4 count dropped under 200 cells/µL, PCP would appear, particularly in individuals who had unexplained weight loss, recurrent bacterial pneumonia, or oral thrush [3]. P. jirovecii infections have reduced dramatically after the advent of efficient and early ART, PCP prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMZ), and HIV screening [4, 5]. Patients diagnosed with PCP have a substantially higher long-term life expectancy since they receive ART. Nevertheless, the severity of the disease is underscored by the fact that in-hospital mortality for PCP remains comparable, despite major improvements in critical care [2]. According to a recent nationwide cohort study conducted in China, PCP remained the most predominant cause of AIDS-related deaths between 2013 and 2022, comprising 15.4% to 19.9% of cases [6]. This emphasizes the necessity of conducting study that specifically examines the features and prognostic factors of PCP patients.

It is well acknowledged that one of the main mechanisms beneath HIV-associated PCP is immune response imbalance. Cytokines are soluble proteins generated by a wide variety of cells that facilitate interaction and recruitment amongst immune system components [7]. As a prognostic indication for P. jirovecii infections, pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine levels could lead to excessive inflammation that damages host tissue and influences the patients’ clinical outcomes [8, 9]. There have been very few studies on the role of cytokines in the prognosis of HIV-associated PCP patients, most of which included small patient cohorts. As a predictive indicator for mortality risk and the possibility of severe respiratory impairment, interleukin (IL)-8 in bronchial alveolar lavage fluid (BALF) has a direct correlation to the severity of HIV-infected patients with PCP [10]. Another study identified that plasma IL-8 and the IL-6/IL-10 ratio have been correlated with disease severity and could predict adverse hospital outcomes in AIDS PCP patients [9]. In the present study, we assessed eight pre-selected inflammatory cytokines to identify whether they could be useful in predicting in-hospital mortality of PCP in HIV-positive individuals.

Methods

Study population

Our study is a prospective, single-center cohort study of HIV patients diagnosed with PCP who were admitted to Tianjin Second People’s Hospital between January 2021 and January 2024. The following criteria were used to make the PCP diagnosis: (1) clinical signs such as dry cough, fever, and progressive dyspnea; (2) bilateral diffuse ground glass opacities on chest computed tomography imaging; (3) detection of P. jirovecii cysts in respiratory specimens (sputum or BALF) by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or nucleic acid by metagenomics next-generation sequencing (mNGS). A PCP diagnosis was made for a patient who met the above specified criteria. Exclusion criteria were: (1) younger than 18 years; (2) co-infection with COVID-19; (3) co-infection with influenza A/B virus; (4) pregnant women; and (5) inadequate data. All patients in our cohort were newly diagnosed with HIV during this hospitalization and were ART-naïve at the time of PCP diagnosis. Every participant provided written informed consent. The Ethics Committee of Tianjin Second People’s Hospital approved the study (No.: 2020–021), which was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and definitions

The following information was obtained from the electronic medical record system: age, gender, clinical symptoms, time from symptom start to hospital admission, and the existence of co-infections. White blood cell (WBC), lymphocyte, neutrophil, monocyte, hemoglobin (HGB), platelet (PLT), albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBIL), creatinine, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), C-reactive protein (CRP), (1,3)-ß-D-Glucan, partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), CD4/CD8 cell counts, and HIV viral load were among the laboratory results.

In-hospital mortality was defined as death from any cause during the same hospitalization in which PCP was diagnosed. Survivors were those discharged alive from the hospital. Co-infections were diagnosed using a combination of clinical specimens, including BALF, sputum, and blood cultures, as clinically indicated. Microbiological confirmation was based on culture, PCR, or mNGS.

Management

In accordance with the Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Opportunistic Infections in Adults and Adolescents with HIV, all diagnosed patients in our research received standard treatment dosages of TMP-SMZ (TMP 15–20 mg and SMZ 75–100 mg)/kg/day for 21 days [2]. The recommended alternate therapy for those who are unable to take TMP-SMX is clindamycin with either caspofungin or primaquine. Adjuvant glucocorticoids (recommended dosage of prednisone or methylprednisolone) were administered to all patients with moderate-to-severe PCP. All patients in our cohort were ART-naïve, HIV treatment was initiated within a week using bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide.

Cytokine measurements

Peripheral blood samples for cytokine measurement were collected at the time of hospital admission, prior to the initiation of corticosteroid or antimicrobial therapy. The EDTA anticoagulant tube was used to collect a 5 mL sample of peripheral blood. After centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 20 minutes, the plasma was frozen for storage at −80 °C until it was analyzed. The levels of cytokines in plasma were evaluated using fluorescence-labeled microbeads with 8-plex Fixed Panel (Weimi Biotech, Changsha, China) and a flow cytometer FACS Canto Plus (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA). Analytes examined concurrently included IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, IL-17, TNF-α, and IFN-γ.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables are presented as numbers with percentages. The χ2-test, Fisher’s exact test, or Mann Whitney U test were employed to conduct comparisons between survivors and non-survivors. To assess the potential correlation between in-hospital mortality and selected cytokine levels, while considering our limited number of patients, we included baseline variables that were considered clinically relevant and that had statistically significant differences in a multivariate logistic regression model. Variables having a p value < 0.05 in univariate analysis were added to a backward stepwise multivariable model. The results are shown as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC) was used to evaluate the predictive value for in-hospital mortality. Area under the curves (AUC) together with their 95% CI, sensitivity, specificity, and Youden index were calculated. Survival analysis was generated from the log-rank test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. SPSS software (version 26.0) for Windows was used for data analysis (SPSS, Chicago, IL).

The median age was 42 years, 92.0% of the participants were male, and the median symptom onset to admission was 25 days (IQR 15–35). Additionally, 43.2% of the participants were current smokers. Cytomegalovirus and bacterial infections were co-infected in 28.0% and 23.2% of PCP patients, respectively.

Predictors of in-hospital mortality

Based on comparison of the indicators of the two groups (non-survivors and survivors) and clinically relevant, we chose CD4 count, levels of albumin, levels of LDH and levels of IL-6 as variables. In multivariate logistic regression analysis that included clinically relevant risk factors and significant variables, the baseline IL-6 and LDH levels were positively associated with high in-hospital mortality [IL-6 per 20 pg/mL increase: aOR (95%CI): 1.127 (1.047–1.230), p = 0.004; LDH: aOR (95%CI): 1.006 (1.002–1.011), p = 0.009; respectively, Table 3]. Conversely, the ALB and CD4 count were negatively associated with it [ALB: aOR (95%CI): 0.853 (0.718–0.991), p = 0.048; CD4 count: aOR (95%CI): 0.897 (0.811–0.959), p = 0.010; respectively, Table 3].

Predictive power of IL-6 and other indicators for in-hospital mortality

The plasma IL-6 levels had a maximum area under the AUC (0.883; 95%CI, 0.812–0.953), compared to CD4+ T cell (AUC, 0.789; 95%CI, 0.697–0.881), ALB (AUC, 0.776; 95%CI, 0.661–0.892), and LDH (AUC, 0.703; 95%CI, 0.573–0.832) (Fig. 1). The AUC of IL-6 combined with other indicators (AUC, 0.924; 95%CI, 0.875–0.973) were higher than that of IL-6 alone, suggesting that the combination of plasma IL-6 with other clinical factors was more effective in predicting in-hospital mortality.

Discussion

Our investigation of HIV-associated PCP patients revealed a correlation between higher plasma IL-6 concentrations on admission and in-hospital mortality, confirming IL-6 as a crucial indicator of poor clinical outcomes.

All cases of PCP in our cohort presented in individuals who were undiagnosed with HIV infection, were not receiving ART at the time of PCP diagnosis, and had a CD4 count < 200 cells/µL. These findings were consistent with those reported in previous studies [11, 12]. The mortality rate of AIDS PCP in our cohort was 12.8%, which was marginally lower than that of previous reports [6, 13], perhaps owing to easy access to potent HIV treatment. In PCP patients with HIV infection, several risk factors have been associated with mortality, including age, PCP episodes, low baseline PaO2 and low HGB level upon hospital admission, and pre-existing comorbidities [14–16]. Our results revealed that low CD4 count and ALB, as well as high levels of LDH, were related with increased in-hospital mortality, indicating that identifying patients with such mortality risks at the time of disease presentation is critical for physicians to make enlightened decisions regarding treatment.

Identifying appropriate biomarkers that accurately represent the inflammatory and immunological balance in PCP hosts is critical for predicting adverse outcomes. Certain lung samples, such BALF, are acquired by invasive methods, which include a risk of problems during collection, particularly in individuals who have respiratory problems [17]. Therefore, it is necessary to assess the inflammatory reactions using non-invasive samples. Since most of the patients in our cohort did not undergo bronchoscopy, we solely investigated the predictive value of blood cytokine levels for HIV-associated PCP.

IL-6 is a pro-inflammatory mediator that triggers the acute phase response, causing leukocyte recruitment, immune activation, and hemodynamic impacts. IL-6 plays a crucial role in the response to acute injury and has been widely studied as a predictive biomarker in sepsis and other acute organ damage [18–21]. IL-6 has been proved to have a substantial role in inflammation associated with pathogen-induced pneumonia, including that results from Escherichia coli, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and COVID-19 [22–25]. Levels of IL-6 in broncho alveoli and/or plasma have been demonstrated to be early indicators of lung damage and predictors of organ failure, extended mechanical ventilation, morbidity, and death in pulmonary diseases [26, 27]. Our research found that IL-6 was the most efficient predictor for mortality among PCP patients with HIV infection, outperforming other cytokines. In PCP, Pneumocystis jirovecii infection may induce alveolar epithelial damage and macrophage activation, leading to excessive IL-6 release. This cytokine storm can contribute to diffuse lung injury, impaired gas exchange, and multi-organ dysfunction, thereby increasing the risk of mortality. Thus, IL-6 may not only be a biomarker but also a mediator of pathological inflammation in severe PCP.

In the present study, LDH levels were notably higher and CD4 counts were considerably lower in non-survivors compared to survivors. In multivariate analysis, we found lower CD4 count and higher LDH levels upon admission as clinically relevant risk factors for mortality, which was consistent with another study of HIV-negative patients with PCP [28]. Low CD4 counts frequently correlate with a poor prognosis; thus, continuing lymphocyte count monitoring is critical to understanding the increased risk of PCP with HIV infection. As expected, elevated blood LDH levels are a risk factor influencing the outcome of PCP. LDH is regarded as both a predictor of disease severity and a marker for monitoring patients’ status throughout therapy. Higher levels of LDH have also been recognized as an independent factor for mortality post the diagnosis of PCP [29]. Following the pathogen-induced lung damage, PCP patients may have elevated blood LDH, which is secreted from cells following cytoplasmic membrane disruption.

Our findings indicate that plasma IL-6, either alone or in combination with CD4 count, albumin, and LDH levels, could predict in-hospital mortality. The combination of these four parameters yielded a high predictive accuracy (AUC 0.924), suggesting that an integrated assessment is superior to any single marker. Given the strong independent association of these four variables with mortality, we derived a preliminary predictive formula based on the multivariable logistic regression model. The log-odds of in-hospital mortality can be estimated as: Log-odds = [Intercept] + (0.006 × IL-6) + (0.006 × LDH) - (0.159 × Albumin) - (0.109 × CD4 count). This formula, which requires future validation in an independent cohort, represents a foundational step towards a clinically applicable composite score. Future prospective, multi-center studies with larger cohorts are warranted to develop and validate a simple points-based score that compiles these results for direct use at the bedside to guide therapeutic strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, our study was carried out in a single center with a small number of patients, which may restrict the generalization of the results. Validation across different clinical centers is critical. Second, this finding cannot be used to predict PCP mortality in non-HIV individuals. Third, there is a restricted selection of cytokines. We predetermined cytokines based on their biological plausibility; nevertheless, a non-targeted method could find more cytokines indicative of adverse outcomes. Furthermore, since the cytokines were not tracked, the dynamic changes in cytokine responses in PCP patients with HIV infection could not be examined. Finally, due to the study’s observational character, we were only able to analyze correlation rather than causation.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings imply that plasma IL-6 levels could predict in-hospital mortality in PCP patients with HIV infection. Using readily available test biomarkers for assessment and risk stratification upon admission may be particularly advantageous for PCP management.
