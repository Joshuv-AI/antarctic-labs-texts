Phase transition for conditional covariance matrices estimated by importance sampling, and implications for cross-entropy schemes in high dimension
J. Beh, J. Morio, F. Simatos

Abstract
Motivated by the estimation of covariance matrices by importance sampling arising in the cross-entropy (CE) algorithm, we study a random matrix model Σ ^ = 𝐗 ​ L ​ 𝐗 ⊤ \hat{\Sigma}={\bf X}L{\bf X}^{\top} with two distinct features: 𝐗 \bf X and L L are dependent, and L L is heavy-tailed. In the high-dimensional regime d → ∞ d\to\infty , we prove under suitable assumptions that a phase transition occurs in the polynomial regime n = d κ n=d^{\kappa} , with n n the sample size. Namely, we prove that ∥ Σ ^ − 𝔼 ​ Σ ^ ∥ op ⇒ 0 \lVert\hat{\Sigma}-\mathbb{E}\hat{\Sigma}\rVert_{\rm op}\Rightarrow 0 if and only if κ > κ ∗ \kappa>\kappa_{*} for some threshold κ ∗ \kappa_{*} determined by the behavior of the maximum likelihood ratios. Moreover, we identify general situations where κ ∗ = 1 / λ 1 \kappa_{*}=1/\lambda_{1} , with λ 1 \lambda_{1} the smallest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix of the auxiliary distribution used to estimate Σ ^ \hat{\Sigma} by importance sampling. This suggests that importance sampling will work better with covariance matrices having a large smallest eigenvalue. We carry this insight into recent CE schemes proposed to estimate the probability of high-dimensional rare events. Through numerical simulations, we demonstrate that better CE schemes are also the ones with larger smallest eigenvalue, even though these algorithms were not designed to smooth the spectrum. This new spectral interpretation raises stimulating questions and opens research directions for the design of efficient high-dimensional algorithms.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation: cross-entropy in high dimension

Consider the problem of estimating the probability
p=\mathbb{P}(X\in A)
of an event
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the reliability context where
p
is small, i.e.,
A
is rare, the naive Monte Carlo method is not efficient and one has to resort to better numerical schemes. for instance adaptive splitting methods such as subset simulation or adaptive importance sampling methods such as cross-entropy. Importance sampling is a general technique widely used in this context. It estimates
p
from
{n_{p}}
i.i.d. samples
X_{i}
drawn according to an auxiliary distribution
g
in the following way:

\hat{p}=\frac{1}{{n_{p}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{p}}\frac{f(X_{i})}{g(X_{i})}\xi_{A}(X_{i})

(1)

where
\xi_{A}
is the indicator function of the set
A
and
f
is the original density of interest, so that
p=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\xi_{A}f
.

The choice of the auxiliary distribution
g
is of uttermost importance for the efficiency of the importance sampling scheme. Here we are specifically interested in the behavior of the prominent cross-entropy (CE) algorithm, which is an iterative algorithm that outputs a family of auxiliary distributions
(\hat{g}_{t},t=0,1,\dots)
. CE works for rare events of the form
A=\{x:\varphi(x)\geq 0\}
for some measurable function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, so that the sought probability can be written as
p=\mathbb{P}(\varphi(X)\geq 0)
. The idea is to sequentially approach the set
\{\varphi\geq 0\}
by simpler sets
\{\varphi\geq q_{t}\}
, with probabilities
\mathbb{P}(\varphi(X)\geq q_{t})
approaching
p
. A deterministic version of CE is presented in Algorithm
1
showing how the
q_{t}
’s are iteratively computed: at iteration
t
with current auxiliary distribution
g_{t}
,
q_{t}
is computed as the
\rho
-quantile of
\varphi(X)
under
g_{t}
, and the next auxiliary distribution
g_{t+1}
is then the Gaussian distribution with mean and variance that of
f
conditioned on
\varphi\geq q_{t}
. This deterministic version of CE involves the unknown parameters
g_{t}
,
q_{t}
,
A_{t}
,
\mu_{t}
and
\Sigma_{t}
. The true CE scheme, presented in Algorithm
2
, is simply a stochastic version of Algorithm
1
where these unknown parameters are replaced by estimations
\hat{g}_{t}
,
\hat{q}_{t}
,
\hat{A}_{t}
,
\hat{\mu}_{t}
and
\hat{\Sigma}_{t}
.

We are interested in the behavior of this algorithm in the high-dimensional regime where
d\to\infty
, so all quantities of interest now implicitly rely on
d
. For instance, the original data
A
and
f
depend on the dimension, but this is not reflected in the notation to ease the readability. In high dimension, standard importance sampling techniques typically behave poorly. It is often observed in practice that the importance weights
\frac{f(X_{i})}{g(X_{i})}
in (
1
) degenerate, in the sense that one weight takes all the mass. In CE, the issue stems from the estimation (
2
) of the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{t}
in step
4
of Algorithm
2
. Indeed, it is known that estimating high-dimensional covariance matrices is a very difficult problem
[
14
]
, as it amounts to estimating a quadratic (in the dimension
d
) number of parameters. Thus, except if the sample size
{n}
is large, the estimation
\hat{\Sigma}_{t}
of
\Sigma_{t}
will be noisy.

To overcome this problem, a recent stream of literature has proposed to reduce the dimension of the problem by projecting on subspaces of small dimensions. The general idea is to replace
\hat{\Sigma}_{t}
in step 4 of Algorithm
2
by a covariance matrix that only updates variance terms in a small number of directions: typically, this amounts to replacing
\hat{\Sigma}_{t}
by
{\textnormal{{Proj}}}_{r}(\hat{\Sigma}_{t},{\bf v})
where we define the following projection operator with
{\bf v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})
an orthonormal family:

{\textnormal{{Proj}}}_{r}(\Sigma,{\bf v})=\sum_{k=1}^{r}(\lambda_{k}-1)v_{k}v_{k}^{\top}+I\ \text{ with }\ \lambda_{k}=v_{k}^{\top}\Sigma v_{k}.

(3)

In words,
{\textnormal{{Proj}}}_{r}(\Sigma,{\bf v})
keeps the same variance as
\Sigma
in the directions
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
(the
\lambda_{k}
’s), while setting variance in the orthogonal subspace to the identity. The subscript
r
refers to the dimension of the subspace which in practice is typically less than
3
. One should thus think of
{\textnormal{{Proj}}}_{r}(\Sigma,{\bf v})
as a finite-rank perturbation of the identity, the so-called the spiked model. Algorithm
3
presents a typical version of CE with projection: the main difference with Algorithm
2
is step 4, where the full covariance matrix
\hat{\Sigma}_{t+1}
estimated by importance sampling is replaced by a simpler covariance matrix
\hat{\Sigma}^{\textnormal{{proj}}}_{t+1}
obtained by projection. In particular, we see that in versions of CE with projection, the covariance matrices used in the intermediate Gaussian auxiliary densities
\hat{g}_{t}^{\textnormal{{proj}}}
are finite-rank perturbations of the identity.

Another motivation for considering spiked covariance matrices is that the rare event
A
has often, in practice, a small intrinsic dimension, meaning that it can be described by a linear subspace of a much lower dimension than the dimension
d
of the input. When
A=\{\varphi\geq 0\}
this means that
\varphi(x)=\varphi(Mx)
for some
d\times d
matrix
M
with small rank (the intrinsic dimension). In this case, it is natural to only try and evaluate covariance terms in a small number of directions deemed influential. Such dimensionality-reduction behavior is widespread in signal processing, learning theory or data assimilation, to name only a few examples, see for instance the literature overview in
[
1
]
.

Various propositions have been made for the projection directions
{\bf v}
, see for instance
[
13
,
12
,
18
]
and the discussion in
[
5
, Section
2.3
]
. In practice this choice has an important impact on the accuracy of the resulting estimator, but, to our knowledge, no theoretical result to date has assessed its influence on the performance of the final estimator
\hat{p}
.

1.2
Estimation of covariance matrices by importance sampling

As explained above, the high-dimensional performance of CE is strongly driven by the quality of the estimation of
\Sigma_{t}
by importance sampling in (
2
). Neglecting the rank-one term
\hat{\mu}_{t}\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}
,
\hat{\Sigma}_{t}
follows a random matrix model that can be written as

\hat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}{\bf X}\hat{L}{\bf X}^{\top}

(5)

with
{\bf X}=[X_{1}\cdots X_{n}]
the
{n}\times d
matrix with columns
X_{i}
and
\hat{L}
the
n\times n
diagonal matrix with entries
\hat{\ell}(X_{i})
. This model, called the covariance matrix model, is quite standard in random matrix theory. It has mostly been studied assuming that
\hat{L}
and
\bf X
are independent with
\hat{L}
light-tailed: under these assumptions, the relevant regime is where
n
and
d
grow proportionate, i.e.,
d/n\to c
for some
c\in(0,\infty)

[
2
,
6
,
8
,
10
]
. However, these two assumptions do not hold here:
\hat{L}
and
\bf X
are not independent, and in high dimension,
\hat{L}
is typically heavy-tailed. To the best of our knowledge, no existing result covers this case, and our main theoretical result makes progress in this direction. Namely, we show (see Theorem
2.1
for a precise statement) that, when
A
has a finite intrinsic dimension, an original phase transition occurs in the polynomial regime
n=d^{\kappa}
: for
\hat{\Sigma}
as in (
5
), the approximation
\hat{\Sigma}\approx\mathbb{E}(\hat{\Sigma})
is accurate if and only
\kappa>\kappa_{*}
for some threshold
\kappa_{*}
linked to the behavior of the maximum likelihood ratios
\hat{\ell}(X_{i})
. From a technical standpoint, what allows us to control
\hat{\Sigma}-\mathbb{E}\hat{\Sigma}
is that, under the spiked model and assuming that
A
has a finite intrinsic dimension,
\hat{L}
and
\bf X
are only slightly dependent, and so we can essentially reduce the problem to the independent case where we can then use recent concentration results for the sum of independent random matrices
[
7
]
.

1.3
Paper organization

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the simplified random matrix model that serves as a proxy for covariance matrix estimation in CE schemes, and presents and discusses our main theoretical results. Section
3
connects these results back to CE schemes through numerical experiments on classical benchmark examples. This provides a new perspective on the effect of projection strategies on their performance. Section

.

2.2
Main results

In the rest of the paper, we consider
f=N(0,I)
and
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
measurable with
p=\mathbb{P}_{f}(X\in A)
. Let
\Sigma_{A}
be the covariance matrix of
f
conditioned on
A
:

\Sigma_{A}=\mathbb{E}_{f}(YY^{\top}\mid Y\in A)-\mu_{A}\mu_{A}^{\top}\ \text{ with }\ \mu_{A}=\mathbb{E}_{f}(Y\mid Y\in A).

We are interested in the estimation of
\Sigma_{A}
by importance sampling using as auxiliary distribution a centered Gaussian distribution whose covariance matrix is a finite-rank perturbation of the identity. More precisely, we will consider the following estimator of
\Sigma_{A}
:

\hat{\Sigma}_{A}=\frac{1}{np}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(X_{i})\xi_{A}(X_{i})X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\mu_{A}\mu_{A}^{\top}

(6)

with
\ell=f/g
and the
X_{i}
’s i.i.d. sampled according to
g=N(0,\Sigma)
. We assume that
\Sigma
satisfies the following assumption, which states that it follows the spiked model, i.e., it is a finite-rank perturbation of the identity. Note that this assumption allows for
\Sigma=I
by taking
r=\lambda_{1}=1
: this case corresponds to the standard Monte Carlo scheme where
g=f
.

Assumption 1
.

For each
d
, the covariance matrix
\Sigma
is given by

\Sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{r}(\lambda_{k}-1)v_{k}v_{k}^{\top}+I

(7)

for some
r\geq 1
,
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{r}
and some orthonormal family
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})
, whose span is defined as
V=\text{span}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})
. Moreover, as
d
varies we have that:

•

\sup_{d}r<\infty
;

•

\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)
is independent of
d
and satisfies
0<\lambda_{1}\leq 1
;

•

\sup_{d}\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)<\infty
.

The estimator (
6
) is inspired by the estimation step (
4
) in CE with projection. The main differences are that 1/ we assume in (
6
) that the probability
p
and the mean
\mu_{A}
are known, whereas they are estimated in (
4
), and 2/ the auxiliary distribution is not centered in (
4
). The simplified model (
6
) allows to focus on the main difficulty in (
4
), and our results could probably be extended with estimations of
p
and
\mu_{A}
and non-centered
g
at the expense of more involved technical derivations but with no additional insight.

Our second assumption concerns the rare event of interest
A
: for reasons outlined in the introduction, we assume that it has a finite intrinsic dimension.

Assumption 2
.

For each
d
, there exists a linear subspace
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ x\in A\Leftrightarrow P_{U}x\in A.

(8)

Moreover, as
d
varies we have
\sup_{d}\dim(U)<\infty
.

Thus, two subspaces play a key role:

•

U
, which is enough to describe the set of interest
A
;

•

and
V
, the set of directions in which variance terms are updated in the auxiliary distribution
g
.

Intuitively, we expect the estimation
\hat{\Sigma}_{A}\approx\Sigma_{A}
to be better when
V
is aligned with
U
. Indeed, Assumption
2
implies that
\Sigma_{A}x=x
for
x\in U_{\perp}
(see the forthcoming proof of Lemma
5.6
), which means that directions in the orthogonal of
U
do not influence
\Sigma_{A}
. Thus, only the directions in
U
are influential and so one should try to take the
v_{k}
’s belonging to
U
.

However, although different choices for
\bf v
have been proposed in the literature, the influence of this choice on the quality of the estimation has never been assessed theoretically. Here we make progress in this direction by studying the two extreme cases where either
V\subset U
(“good” case) or
V\subset U_{\perp}
(“bad” case). Our main result shows that, in both cases, a phase transition occurs in the polynomial regime
n=d^{\kappa}
: the estimation will be accurate if
\kappa>\kappa_{*}
and not accurate if
\kappa<\kappa_{*}
. We show that the value of the threshold
\kappa_{*}
is linked to the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of
\Sigma
through the behavior of the maximum likelihood ratio appearing in (
6
). To control the latter, we will make the following technical assumption.

Assumption 3
.

There exists
\gamma_{*}\geq 0
such that:

\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\xi_{A}(X_{i})\ell(X_{i})\Rightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{ if }\gamma>\gamma_{*},\\
+\infty&\text{ if }\gamma<\gamma_{*}\end{array}\right.

(9)

where the
X_{i}
’s are i.i.d. drawn according to
g
.

In the non-triangular case (no dependency on
d
), the fact that the maximum of
n
i.i.d. random variables
Z_{i}
grows polynomially is quite standard. More precisely, in the non-triangular case, (
9
) holds with
\gamma_{*}=0
for light-tailed random variables, and when
Z_{i}
is in the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable distribution (
\alpha<2
), then it holds with
\gamma_{*}=1/\alpha
(in this case,
\max_{i=1,\dots,n}Z_{i}
grows like
n^{1/\alpha}s(n)
for some slowly varying function
s
). Thus, we assume that this standard behavior continues to hold in the triangular regime
d\to\infty
, which can be seen as a regularity assumption as the dimension increases. We can now state our main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
hold, and let
\kappa_{*}=1/(1-\gamma_{*})
. Assume in addition that
\inf_{d}p>0
and that either
V\subset U
or
V\subset U_{\perp}
. Then in the regime
n=d^{\kappa}
, the following phase transition holds:

•

if
\kappa>\kappa_{*}
, then
\lVert\hat{\Sigma}_{A}-\Sigma_{A}\rVert_{\rm op}\Rightarrow 0
;

•

if
\kappa<\kappa_{*}
, then
\lVert\hat{\Sigma}_{A}-\Sigma_{A}\rVert_{\rm op}\Rightarrow\infty
and more precisely, we have
\lambda_{\max}(\hat{\Sigma}_{A})\Rightarrow\infty
while
\sup_{d}\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{A})<\infty
.

Moreover, if
V\subset U_{\perp}
then
\kappa_{*}=1/\lambda_{1}
while if
V\subset U
, then
1\leq\kappa_{*}\leq 1/\lambda_{1}
.

The assumption
\inf_{d}p>0
is crucial for our results. This assumption is common in previous work studying the high-dimensional behavior of importance sampling, see the discussion in
[
5
, Section
2.4
]
. To our knowledge, the case
p\to 0
is essentially unknown, and widely different behavior requiring significant different arguments could arise. Importantly, it was proved in
[
5
, Corollary
4.2
]
that the assumption
\inf_{d}p>0
implies that

\sup_{d}\lVert\mu_{A}\rVert<\infty\ \text{ and }\ 0<\inf_{d}\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{A})\leq\sup_{d}\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{A})<\infty.

(10)

The next result shows that the range
[1,1/\lambda_{1}]
for
\kappa_{*}
in the case

is sharp, i.e., every value can be attained.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that
A
is of the form
A=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\lvert\langle u,x\rangle\rvert\leq K\}
for some
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\lVert u\rVert=1
and
K>0
. Then Assumption
2
is satisfied with
U={\rm span}(u)
, and for any fixed
\lambda_{1}\in(0,1)
, Assumption
1
is also satisfied if
V=U
. Moreover, if
K=1+\sqrt{2\alpha\lambda_{1}\log n}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
, then
\inf_{d}p>0
and Assumption
3
is satisfied with
\gamma_{*}=\alpha(1-\lambda_{1})
.

Of course, this result is very artificial as we have
p\to 1
, nonetheless it serves its purpose of showing that every value between
1
and
1/\lambda_{1}
is possible for
\kappa_{*}
. A slightly more interesting example is when
A=\{x:\langle x,u\rangle\geq K\}
for some fixed
K\in\mathbb{R}
, not depending on

. In this case, even in the “good” case
V={\rm span}(u)
, it is not hard to prove that
\kappa_{*}=1/\lambda_{1}
. In particular, it is striking to realize that, depending on the set

, the good choice

may not improve the performance of the bad choice

, with both needing

to behave correctly.

3
Efficiency of recent CE schemes: a new spectral interpretation

3.1
Implications of Theorem
2.1
for CE schemes with projection

Theorem
2.1
has direct implications for CE schemes with projection described in Algorithm
3
. Indeed, step 2 of Algorithm
3
involves the estimation of the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{t+1}=\Sigma_{A_{t}}
by importance sampling with auxiliary distribution
\hat{g}^{\textnormal{{proj}}}_{t}=N(\hat{\mu}^{\textnormal{{proj}}}_{t},\hat{\Sigma}^{\textnormal{{proj}}}_{t})
: since
\hat{\Sigma}^{\textnormal{{proj}}}_{t}
is obtained by projection (
3
), by construction it follows the spiked model of Assumption
1
. In other words, Theorem
2.1
sheds light on CE schemes with projection when considering
A=A_{t}
and
\Sigma=\hat{\Sigma}^{\textnormal{{proj}}}_{t}
. In particular, the prime importance of
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)
discussed after Theorem
2.1
suggests that Algorithm
3
will behave better if
\lambda_{\min}(\hat{\Sigma}^{\textnormal{{proj}}}_{t})
is larger: indeed, a small smallest eigenvalue translates to a large sample size needed to accurately learn the target covariance matrix. To a lesser extent, the assumption
\sup\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)<\infty
in Assumption
1
also suggests that a small
\lambda_{\max}(\hat{\Sigma}^{\textnormal{{proj}}}_{t})
is beneficial. But in practice, projection schemes almost always only update variance terms in directions of small variance, leading to
\lambda_{\max}(\hat{\Sigma}^{\textnormal{{proj}}}_{t})=1
.

On this background, several projection directions
\bf v
have recently been proposed. However, the rationale for this projection step is not to smooth the spectrum of the auxiliary covariance matrix, but rather to decrease the number of parameters to be estimated, thereby reducing noise while focusing on relevant parameters. Projection directions are then chosen according to some optimality criterion, typically minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence with
f|_{A}
.

In the following, we take a new look at these algorithms and pay special attention to the influence of the choice of projection directions on the spectrum of the estimated covariance matrices. Numerical results presented below show that projection directions that work well are also the ones with the largest smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}(\hat{\Sigma}^{\textnormal{{proj}}}_{t})
. This is quite surprising since, as explained above, these algorithms were not designed to act on the spectrum. Nonetheless, this gives credit to our main result, Theorem
2.1
, for the understanding of the performance of importance sampling schemes, and opens the way for the design of efficient importance sampling schemes in high dimensions.

First, we have so far focused on the influence of projecting, but simply comparing CE and iCE without projection suggests that our results may reach further. The performance of CE compared to that of iCE is displayed in Figure

Second, although our results allow to understand the potential benefits of projection methods in CE schemes, they actually challenge the relevance of importance sampling in high dimension. Indeed, consider the following corollary to Theorem
2.1
, directly obtained with
g=f
.

Corollary 4.1
.

Consider the Monte Carlo scheme, i.e.,
\Sigma=I
, in the regime
n=d^{\kappa}
for some
\kappa>0
. If Assumption
2
holds and
\inf_{d}p>0
, then
\lVert\hat{\Sigma}_{A}-\Sigma_{A}\rVert_{\rm op}\Rightarrow 0
for any
\kappa>1
.

Thus on the one hand, Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.2
suggest that importance sampling with auxiliary distribution
g=N(0,\Sigma)
may require
n\gg d^{1/\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)}
to be consistent, even in good cases where
V\subset U
, while Corollary
4.1
suggests that
n\gg d
is sufficient for Monte Carlo. Overall, this suggests that Monte Carlo behaves better than importance sampling in high dimension. Of course, this is contradicted by numerous numerical results which show that, in practice, Monte Carlo fails to estimate small probabilities in high dimension while importance sampling with a suitable auxiliary distribution may succeed. Thus, our results call for a theoretical justification of the improvement of importance sampling over Monte Carlo in high dimension which, in our view, is still lacking.

Third, our results shed light and complement earlier results. Consider in particular the following theoretical result on CE without projection.

Finally, although we believe that the spectral behavior of the estimated covariance matrices is an important ingredient for explaining the performance of a given iterative importance sampling scheme, we emphasize that this is not necessarily the whole story. For instance, Uribe et al.

have proposed projecting on the so-called Failure Informed Subspace (FIS). Preliminary results suggest that a naive implementation of this method as in Algorithm

as

Let us now prove that

(17)

and so the independence between

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume that Assumption
1
holds and let

, any
\alpha<(1-\lambda_{1})^{-1}

Proof.

Since

\sup_{d}\mathbb{E}_{g}\left(\ell(X)^{\alpha}\right)<\infty\ \text{ and }\ \sup_{d}\mathbb{E}_{g}\left(\lvert\langle w,X\rangle\rvert^{\beta}\right)<\infty.

Since

\mathbb{E}_{g}(\ell(X)^{\alpha})=\mathbb{E}_{f}(\ell(Y)^{\alpha-1})

\mathbb{E}_{f}(\ell(Y)^{\alpha-1})=\lvert\Sigma\rvert^{(\alpha-1)/2}\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{r}(\lambda_{k}^{-1}-1)\langle Y,v_{k}\rangle^{2}\right)\right].

A straightforward extension of Lemma
5.4
implies that

(and therefore

\eta
for some fixed
\eta>1

n^{\prime}/(qn)\to 1
in L

\inf_{d}q>0

, the next lemma implies that

. For lack of reference to this probably standard result (a triangular version of the law of large numbers), we provide its proof.

Lemma 5.5
.

For each
d\geq 1
, let
\xi_{n,d}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i,d}
where
\zeta_{1,d},\ldots,\zeta_{n,d}
are centered and i.i.d. with
\sup_{d}\mathbb{E}\lvert\zeta_{1,d}\rvert^{\eta}<\infty
for some
\eta>1
. Then
n^{-1}\xi_{n,d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{L\textsubscript{1}}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.

Proof.

Let
\zeta_{d}=\zeta_{1,d}
and
C=\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert^{\eta}
, which by assumption is finite. If
\lVert X\rVert_{\eta}=[\mathbb{E}(\lvert X\rvert^{\eta})]^{1/\eta}
denotes the
\eta
-norm, we have

\lVert\xi_{n,d}\rVert_{\eta}=\frac{1}{n}\Big\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i,d}\Big\rVert_{\eta}\leq\lVert\zeta_{d}\rVert_{\eta}=[\mathbb{E}(\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert^{\eta})]^{1/\eta}\leq C^{1/\eta}

which shows that
\xi_{n,d}
is uniformly integrable. Thus, in order to show that
\xi_{n,d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{L\textsubscript{1}}}}}{{\to}}0
, it is enough to show that
\xi_{n,d}\Rightarrow 0
. If
\eta\geq 2
this is immediate by considering the variance, so let us assume
\eta<2
. According to
[
11
, Theorem 2.2.11]
, since the
\zeta_{i,d}
are i.i.d., it is sufficient that

(i)

n\mathbb{P}(\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert>n)\to 0
,

(ii)

\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{d};\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert\leq n]\to 0
, and

(iii)

n^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{d}^{2};\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert\leq n]\to 0
.

The first two terms are bounded by
C/n^{\eta-1}
. Indeed, for (i) this directly comes from Markov inequality. For (ii), since
\mathbb{E}(\zeta_{n})=0
it follows that
\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{d};\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert\leq n]=-\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{d};\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert\geq n]
and so

\left\lvert\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{d};\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert\leq n]\right\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}[\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert;\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert\geq n]\leq\frac{C}{n^{\eta-1}}.

For (iii), writing
\zeta_{d}^{2}=\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert^{\eta}\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert^{2-\eta}
and using
\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert^{2-\eta}\leq n^{2-\eta}
when
\lvert\zeta_{d}\rvert\leq n
(and
\eta<2
) leads to the bound
C/n^{\eta-1}
. In either case, we see that all three terms vanish as desired.
∎

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\text{{tr}}\left((S-\mathbb{E}S)^{2q}\right)\right]^{1/2q}\leq

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ +\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\text{{tr}}\left(G^{2q}\right)\right]^{1/2q}-(\text{{tr}}\otimes\tau)(\lvert X_{\text{free}}\rvert^{2q})^{1/2q}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ +\left\lvert(\text{{tr}}\otimes\tau)(\lvert X_{\text{free}}\rvert^{2q})^{1/2q}\right\rvert.

By definition,
G
and

do not depend on the Gaussian assumption, and so the bounds
2\sigma(S)

The authors then control the terms

Thus in order to prove Proposition

(and, for

\sigma(S)\leq\frac{1}{\min p_{j}}\Big\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\text{\rm Tr}[\Sigma_{i}]\Sigma_{i}+\Sigma_{i}^{2}\right)\Big\rVert_{\rm op}^{1/2},\ v(S)\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\min p_{j}}\Big\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma_{i}^{2}\Big\rVert_{\rm op}^{1/2}

R_{q}(S)\leq\frac{1}{\min p_{j}}n^{1/q}\max_{i\leq n}\left(\text{\rm Tr}\Sigma_{i}+q\lVert\Sigma_{i}\rVert_{\rm op}\right).

We start with the extension for

which is straightforward. Following the proof of

7

, we have

R_{q}(S)\leq 2n^{1/q}\max_{j\leq n}\mathbb{E}\left(\lVert Z_{j}\rVert_{\rm op}^{2q}\mid Z_{j}\in A_{j}\right)^{1/q}\leq 2n^{1/q}\max_{j\leq n}\frac{1}{p_{j}^{1/q}}\mathbb{E}\left(\lVert Z_{j}\rVert_{\rm op}^{2q}\right)^{1/q}

Let us now prove the desired bound on
v(S)
. By definition,

v(S)^{2}=\sup_{(\text{\rm Tr}\lvert M\rvert)^{2}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\text{\rm Tr}[M(S-\mathbb{E}S)]\right)^{2}\right].

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[\text{\rm Tr}\left(M(S-\mathbb{E}S)\right)\right]^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[\text{\rm Tr}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top})\right)\right]^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\text{\rm Tr}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top})\right)\right]^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\text{\rm Tr}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top})-\mathbb{E}(\text{\rm Tr}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top}))\right)^{2}\right]

using the definition of
S
for the first equality, the linearity of the trace for the second one, and the fact that the
T_{j}
are independent, so that the variance of the sum is the sum of variance, for the third one. If
Z_{j}\sim N(0,\Sigma_{j})
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\text{\rm Tr}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top})-\mathbb{E}(\text{\rm Tr}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top}))\right)^{2}\right]\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\text{\rm Tr}(MZ_{j}Z_{j}^{\top})-\mathbb{E}(\text{\rm Tr}(MZ_{j}Z_{j}^{\top})\mid Z_{j}\in A_{j})\right)^{2}\mid Z_{j}\in A_{j}\right]

and so the variational characterization of the mean gives that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\text{\rm Tr}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top})-\mathbb{E}(\text{\rm Tr}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top}))\right)^{2}\right]\\
\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\text{\rm Tr}(MZ_{j}Z_{j}^{\top})-\mathbb{E}(\text{\rm Tr}(MZ_{j}Z_{j}^{\top}))\right)^{2}\mid Z_{j}\in A_{j}\right]

which leads to

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\text{\rm Tr}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top})-\mathbb{E}(\text{\rm Tr}(MT_{j}T_{j}^{\top}))\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{p_{j}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\text{\rm Tr}(MZ_{j}Z_{j}^{\top})-\mathbb{E}(\text{\rm Tr}(MZ_{j}Z_{j}^{\top}))\right)^{2}\right].

This bound allows to resume the original proof in the Gaussian setting, with no conditioning, at the expense of an additional multiplicative
1/\min p_{j}
term.

Finally, the bound on
\sigma(S)
proceeds with the same arguments as for
v(S)
, but in a multi-dimensional case. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(S)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\left(S-\mathbb{E}(S)\right)^{2}\right]\right\rVert_{\rm op}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(T_{j}T_{j}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}(T_{j}T_{j}^{\top})\right)^{2}\right]\right\rVert_{\rm op}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top}\mid Z_{j}\in A_{j})\right)^{2}\mid Z_{j}\in A_{j}\right]\right\rVert_{\rm op}.

The following lemma is a multi-dimensional generalization of the inequality used previously to control
v(S)
.

Lemma A.3
.

For any random matrix
M
and any event
E
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(M-\mathbb{E}(M\mid E)\right)^{2}\mid E\right]\leq\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(E)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(M-\mathbb{E}(M)\right)^{2}\right]

where
A\leq B
means that
B-A
is positive.

Proof.

We have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(M-\mathbb{E}(M)\right)^{2}\mid E\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\left(M-\mathbb{E}(M\mid E)\right)^{2}\mid E\right]=\left[\mathbb{E}(M)-\mathbb{E}(M\mid E)\right]^{2}

implying that
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(M-\mathbb{E}(M\mid E)\right)^{2}\mid E\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(M-\mathbb{E}(M)\right)^{2}\mid E\right]
.
∎

Thus

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top}\mid Z_{j}\in A_{j})\right)^{2}\mid Z_{j}\in A_{j}\right]\\
\leq\frac{1}{\min p_{j}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top})\right)^{2}\right]

and so

\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top}\mid Z_{j}\in A_{j})\right)^{2}\mid Z_{j}\in A_{j}\right]\\
\leq\frac{1}{\min p_{j}}\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top})\right)^{2}\right].

Since
\lVert A\rVert_{\rm op}\leq\lVert B\rVert_{\rm op}
when
A,B
are positive with
A\leq B
, we obtain

\sigma(S)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\min p_{j}}\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\top})\right)^{2}\right]\right\rVert_{\rm op}

and so we are back to the Gaussian setting.
