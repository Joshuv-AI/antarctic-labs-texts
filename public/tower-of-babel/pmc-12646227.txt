Therapeutic hypothermia reprograms nanocarrier protein corona via apolipoprotein C1 enrichment for precision cardiovascular therapy
Authors: Chenwen Li, Guanli Zhao, Yuantong Qi, Menglong Sun, Ling Zhong, Qinghua Yang, Kaiyao Hu, Juan Cheng, Yin Dou, Yingxue Hao, Jianxiang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe treatment of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) is persistently plagued by poor drug targeting and systemic adverse effects. While innovative nanotherapeutic approaches offer enhanced drug delivery through targeting and stimulus-responsive release, their clinical translation remains constrained by inadequate tissue specificity and manufacturing limitations. We establish a paradigm leveraging therapeutic hypothermia (TH) to potentiate targeted nanotherapy delivery for the precision management of CVDs. Crucially, we find that TH-mediated selective enrichment of apolipoproteins creates a biological framework for enhanced nanocarrier tropism. The further developed plasma or apolipoprotein C1 preconditioning strategy enables facile functionalization of diverse nanocarriers, achieving tissue-precise delivery of therapeutic payloads through lipoprotein-mimetic engineering for treating CVDs.

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) encompass a group of disorders affecting cardiovascular and cerebrovascular systems, including heart failure, myocardial hypertrophy, atherosclerosis, and stroke (1). As the leading causes of global mortality and disease burden, CVDs pose significant challenges, particularly due to increasing life expectancy and an aging population (2). Pharmacological interventions remain a cornerstone in the management of CVDs. For example, drugs capable of addressing cardiac dysfunction and alleviating symptoms have been widely used as patient-friendly and clinically desirable treatment options for heart failure, including diuretics, β-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and angiotensin II receptor blockers (3, 4). However, their clinical utility is often limited by short retention times, low accumulation in injured cardiovascular tissues, and substantial side effects arising from nonspecific distribution and dose-limiting toxicities.

To address these limitations, advanced drug delivery systems have been developed to overcome the constraints of existing therapies and enhance clinical outcomes, by leveraging their functional advantages, such as excellent targeting capability, high delivery efficiency, prolonged circulation time, and programmed and on-demand drug release (5–8). Depending on their size, surface charge, and shape, nanoparticles (NPs) passively target injured tissues via leaky vasculature or incomplete vasculature (9). Furthermore, NPs can be specifically engineered with functional biomaterials to selectively release therapeutic payloads at the diseased hearts, vessels, or brains, in response to pathological microenvironments characterized by abnormally elevated levels of biochemical molecules like enzymes and reactive oxygen species (ROS) (9–12). In this context, nanotherapies based on ROS-responsive NPs have demonstrated superior therapeutic benefits in various murine models of CVDs, such as heart failure, myocardial hypertrophy, arterial restenosis, atherosclerosis, abdominal aortic aneurysm, and stroke (7, 10, 13). To further enhance delivery efficiency, NPs have been functionalized with specific ligands, antibodies, or adhesive biomolecules, such as cardiac-targeting peptides (CTP), ischemic myocardium-targeting peptides, stroke-homing peptides, and vascular cell adhesion molecule one-binding motifs. These modifications are rationally designed to enhance nanotherapeutic accumulation at pathological sites, including heart failure lesions, myocardial infarction zones, aneurysmal tissues, and atherosclerotic plaques (6, 14–18). Additionally, biomembranes derived from different cells or extracellular vesicles have been employed to functionalize nanotherapies to potentiate their in vivo performance (19, 20). A notable example is the use of platelet membrane-cloaked NPs, which enable selective adhesion to damaged vasculature (21). This approach substantially enhanced efficacy of docetaxel in a rat model of coronary restenosis by facilitating site-specific therapeutic delivery. In other cases, cell membranes derived from platelets, macrophages, and cardiomyocytes have been utilized to engineer nanotherapies and mitochondria for precision treatment of atherosclerosis, aortic aneurysm, and ischemic heart disease (22–24). Beyond biochemical targeting strategies, synthetic NPs, extracellular vesicles, and therapeutic cells have been oriented for treating vascular disorders and cardiomyopathy by harnessing physical forces such as shear stress, magnetic fields, ultrasound-derived mechanical forces, and photoacoustic signals (5, 25–27).

Despite these advances, there are tremendous challenges in developing translational, high-targeting nanocarriers for precision treatment of CVDs (28). To date, few nanomedicines have achieved successful clinical translation, and none have been approved for marketing to treat CVDs (29). Efficient targeted delivery of nanotherapeutics in vivo remains a major obstacle to realizing their transformative potential. In particular, current molecular moiety-based targeting strategies face several limitations, including limited specificity, potential off-target effects, and variability in biochemical signals across different types of CVDs. Additionally, emerging cell membrane coating technologies encounter concerns such as potential stability issues due to membrane degradation, difficulties in scalable production, stringent storage conditions, and the risk of unintended immune responses. Moreover, achieving precise control over membrane coating thickness and uniformity remains a significant hurdle. Further, excessively intricate nanomedicine designs, coupled with technically complex fabrication requirements and unsustainable production costs, present major barriers to clinical translation (30). Accordingly, there is an urgent need for new, simple, convenient, and efficient targeting strategies to enable nanotherapies to efficaciously treat multiple types of CVDs.

Herein, we report a therapeutic hypothermia (TH) treatment strategy that can significantly enhance the targeting specificity of different types of nanocarriers to pathological sites across multiple animal models of CVDs, thereby substantially augmenting therapeutic efficacy of engineered nanotherapies (Fig. 1). Mechanistically, this innovative approach promotes the selective enrichment of apolipoproteins C1 (ApoC1) and C4 within the protein corona on NP surfaces, which facilitate targeted delivery through specific recognition of disease-associated receptors upregulated in damaged cardiac tissues. We thus propose that both TH intervention and direct ApoC1 functionalization represent robust yet straightforward strategies to enhance NP accumulation in injured cardiovascular tissues, thus potentiating therapeutic benefits of loaded drugs for CVD treatment.

Results

TH Treatment Enhanced In Vivo Targeting Capability of Poly(Lactic-Co-Glycolic Acid) (PLGA) NPs in Multiple Animal Models of CVDs.

PLGA NPs (PNs) were initially used as candidate nanocarriers. Cyanine5 (Cy5)-labeled PNs (Cy5-PNs) were fabricated by a nanoprecipitation/self-assembly method (Fig. 2A). Observation by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) indicated a nearly spherical shape of Cy5-PNs, with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 233 ± 8 nm and ζ-potential of −33 ± 2 mV (Fig. 2 B and C). To examine the effects of TH treatment on the tissue targeting ability, various animal models of CVDs were employed. First, a mouse model of heart failure was established by intraperitoneal injection of doxorubicin (DOX) (10), which was validated by notable cardiac atrophy, reduced heart index, and histological examination of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained myocardial tissue sections (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–D). Then we investigated the effect of TH (maintaining a rectal temperature at 32 to 34 °C) on the cardiac targeting capability of Cy5-PNs in heart failure mice (Fig. 2D). Following intravenous (i.v.) injection of Cy5-PNs, mice in the TH group were anesthetized and hypothermia was induced by physical cooling, while mice in the normothermia (NT) group were maintained a physiological rectal temperature of 37.5 °C. At 24 h after administration, ex vivo imaging revealed substantial cardiac accumulation of Cy5-PNs in two groups. Notably, the TH group exhibited a significantly enhanced myocardial distribution of Cy5-PNs compared to the NT group (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis indicated that the TH group showed relatively high colocalization of Cy5-PNs with multiple cardiac cell populations, including CD31+ endothelial cells, CD68+ macrophages, collagen I (COL-1)-positive cardiac fibroblasts, and α-actinin-positive cardiomyocytes in myocardial cryosections (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Importantly, this TH-mediated enhancement in cardiac targeting efficiency was consistently observed across species, as evidenced by significantly greater accumulation of Cy5-PNs in cardiac tissues of Sprague-Dawley rats with heart failure compared to the NT controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

We next evaluated cardiac targeting capability of Cy5-PNs in a mouse model of myocardial hypertrophy with or without TH intervention (Fig. 2F). Myocardial hypertrophy in mice was induced by subcutaneous injection of isoproterenol (ISO) (31). Cardiac accumulation of Cy5-PNs was assessed in hypertrophic mice under different thermal conditions. At 24 h post i.v. injection of Cy5-PNs, ex vivo imaging revealed remarkably elevated fluorescence signals in the myocardial tissues of TH-treated animals compared to NT controls (Fig. 2G). Notably, quantitative analysis showed that the TH group exhibited a 1.9-fold increase in cardiac fluorescence intensity relative to the NT group, indicating substantially improved nanovehicle accumulation under hypothermic conditions.

The above results demonstrate that TH treatment of animals can effectively enhance the cardiac targeting efficiency of PNs under pathological myocardial conditions. To validate the broader applicability of TH-mediated targeting enhancement across CVDs, we extended our investigation to three distinct vascular pathology models, including atherosclerosis, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and ischemic stroke. Atherosclerosis was induced in apolipoprotein E-deficient (ApoE−/−) mice by feeding a high-fat Western diet for 12 wk (Fig. 2H). At 12 h after i.v. injection of Cy5-PNs, the fluorescence intensities in the aortas of TH-treated atherosclerotic mice were significantly stronger than those of control mice (Fig. 2I). Notably, TH treatment resulted in a 2.4-fold increase in aortic Cy5-PNs accumulation relative to the NT group, demonstrating the substantial impact of hypothermia on nanocarrier targeting efficiency in atherosclerotic vessels. In mice with FeCl3-induced DVT, quantitative fluorescence analysis revealed significantly enhanced Cy5-PNs accumulation in thrombus-containing vessels. Specifically, the inferior vena cava segments from TH-treated mice exhibited a 2.8-fold increase in fluorescence intensity compared to NT controls at 6 h after i.v. administration of Cy5-PNs (Fig. 2 J and K). Similarly, in mice with middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO)-induced ischemic stroke, TH treatment significantly enhanced cerebral accumulation of Cy5-PNs compared to NT-treated control mice. Quantitative analysis at 6 h post i.v. administration revealed that the fluorescence intensity in the ipsilateral hemisphere of TH-treated mice was 2.6-fold higher than that of NT-treated animals (Fig. 2 L and M). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TH treatment can significantly enhance the targeting efficiency of PNs across multiple well-established CVD models, suggesting its broad applicability for improving nanovehicle delivery in various pathological conditions.

TH Intervention Increased the Tissue-Specific Accumulation of Other Hard NPs in Pathological Cardiovascular Tissues across Multiple Disease Models.

Building upon these promising findings, we reasonably speculate that the TH-mediated enhancement of nanovehicle targeting might represent a universal phenomenon applicable to diverse nanocarriers. To test this hypothesis, we first evaluated the cardiac targeting efficiency of 200 nm polystyrene NPs (PS NPs) in mice with DOX-induced heart failure under TH or NT conditions. Quantitative analysis at 1 h post i.v. administration revealed significantly increased accumulation of Cy5-labeled PS NPs in myocardial tissues of TH-treated mice compared to NT controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This finding is consistent with our previous observations using Cy5-PNs.

Recent advances in nanomedicine have highlighted the potential of ROS-responsive NPs as superior delivery platforms for targeted treatment of CVDs, particularly given the pivotal role of oxidative stress and inflammation in their pathogenesis. Among them, NPs fabricated from phenylboronic acid ester-containing materials have emerged as promising candidates for precision CVD treatment (7, 10, 11, 24). Consequently, we further evaluated the TH-mediated targeting enhancement of ROS-responsive NPs based on a phenylboronic acid ester (PBAP)-conjugated β-cyclodextrin (OCD) material (SI Appendix, Fig. S5) (32). Spherical Cy5-labeled OCD NPs (Cy5-ONs) were also prepared by nanoprecipitation/self-assembly (Fig. 3 A and B), showing a mean diameter of 105 ± 2 nm and ζ-potential of −35 ± 2 mV (Fig. 3C). Then in vivo targeting performance of Cy5-ONs was examined in mouse models of heart failure, myocardial hypertrophy, and ischemic stroke. At 24 h after i.v. delivery of Cy5-ONs, quantitative analysis demonstrated significantly enhanced fluorescence intensity in cardiac tissues of TH-treated heart failure mice compared to NT controls (Fig. 3D). Particularly, the TH group exhibited threefold higher nanocarrier accumulation relative to the NT group. In mice with ISO-induced myocardial hypertrophy, TH treatment also significantly enhanced cardiac deposition of Cy5-ONs, as evidenced by fluorescence imaging analysis (Fig. 3E). Similarly, TH-treated mice exhibited remarkably higher accumulation of i.v. administered Cy5-ONs in the brain tissues with MCAO-mediated ischemic stroke, in comparison to the NT group (Fig. 3F).

Furthermore, we prepared Cy7.5-labeled NPs (defined as Cy7.5-TPTNs) based on a ROS-responsive/scavenging material, following our previously established method (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–C) (31). Cy7.5-TPTNs exhibited an average diameter of 89 ± 2 nm and ζ-potential of −33 ± 0.4 mV (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D and E). To evaluate the cardiac targeting efficiency of Cy7.5-TPTNs under TH conditions, a rat model of ventricular fibrillation was induced by transesophageal electrical stimulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6F) (31, 33). At 6 h post i.v. injection, ex vivo imaging indicated significantly higher fluorescent intensity in cardiac tissues isolated from TH-treated rats compared to NT controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S6G). Additionally, i.v. delivered Cy7.5-TPTNs demonstrated superior cardiac accumulation in TH-treated mice with ISO-induced myocardial hypertrophy relative to NT-treated animals (SI Appendix, Fig. S6H). These findings support the generalizability of TH-mediated targeting enhancement in CVDs across different hard NPs.

TH-Mediated Enhancement of Liposomal Targeting Efficiency in Mouse Models of CVDs.

Beyond investigations with hard NPs, we further assessed the effect of TH treatment on the targeting efficiency of liposomes in representative CVD models. As a well-recognized type of soft NPs, liposomes have been extensively utilized as versatile delivery platforms for diverse therapeutic agents (34–37). Notably, the clinical translation potential of liposomal technology is evidenced by the regulatory approval of more than 16 liposome-based formulations, which have been successfully employed in the treatment of tumors, fungal infections, and pain (38, 39). Cy5-encapsulated liposomes (Cy5-LPs) were fabricated using distearoyl phosphatidylcholine, cholesterol, and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphatidylethanolamine-poly(ethylene glycol) by a thin-film hydration method. TEM observation confirmed the characteristic lipid bilayer structure, with a mean diameter of 121 ± 1 nm and ζ-potential of −26 ± 3 mV (Fig. 3 G–I). We subsequently evaluated in vivo targeting capability of Cy5-LPs in three CVD models. In DOX-induced heart failure mice, i.v. injection of Cy5-LPs resulted in 1.9-fold higher fluorescence intensity in the isolated cardiac tissues of the TH group compared to the NT group (Fig. 3J). Consistent with this finding, TH treatment significantly enhanced Cy5-LPs accumulation in pathological tissues of mice with myocardial hypertrophy or MCAO-induced ischemic stroke (Fig. 3 K and L).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that TH can significantly enhance the tissue-specific targeting efficiency of diverse nanocarriers across multiple CVD models. Importantly, this TH-mediated targeting enhancement represents a universal phenomenon that is independent of the intrinsic physicochemical characteristics of nanocarriers, including their size, surface charge, and material composition.

TH-Mediated Augmentation of In Vivo Therapeutic Efficacy of Dexrazoxane (DEX)-Loaded NPs in Heart Failure Mice.

Given the demonstrated enhancement in cardiovascular targeting efficiency across various NPs under TH conditions, we further hypothesize that TH can serve as a strategy to augment therapeutic efficacy of nanotherapies. As a proof of concept, DEX, an FDA-approved cardioprotective agent for DOX-induced cardiotoxicity (40), was selected as a model drug. DEX-loaded PNs (i.e., DPNs) were prepared using our established nanoprecipitation/self-assembly protocol (Fig. 4A). Physicochemical characterization revealed uniform NPs with an average diameter of 239 ± 1 nm, ζ-potential of −32 ± 1 mV, and a DEX loading content of 10.9 wt% (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). We next evaluated in vivo therapeutic effects of DPNs in mice with DOX-induced heart failure (Fig. 4C). DOX-treated mice showed significant pathological manifestations, including substantial weight loss, notable cardiac atrophy, and a reduced heart weight to tibia length (HW/TL) ratio, compared to healthy controls (Fig. 4 D–F). Treatment with DPNs (at 40 mg/kg of DEX) under both NT and TH conditions demonstrated superior therapeutic outcomes compared to free DEX (at 200 mg/kg), as implicated by the effectively mitigated weight loss and increased HW/TL ratio. Echocardiography indicated that DPNs combined with TH treatment significantly ameliorated DOX-induced left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, as evidenced by enhanced left ventricle ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricle fraction shortening (LVFS) (Fig. 4 G–I). By contrast, therapy with free DEX or DPNs under NT conditions showed limited therapeutic benefits. Consistently, the combined therapeutic regimen of DPNs and TH afforded the most substantial reduction in both myocardial tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α expression and circulating cardiac troponin-I (cTnI, a specific biomarker of cardiac injury) levels (Fig. 4 J and K). Examination of H&E-stained myocardial sections revealed pronounced nuclear enlargement and extensive cytoplasmic vacuolization in cardiomyocytes of DOX-treated mice. These pathological manifestations were markedly reversed after treatment with DPNs and TH (Fig. 4L). These results suggest that DPNs combined with TH treatment exerts superior cardioprotection against myocardial injury and inflammation compared to either free DEX administration or DPNs treatment under NT conditions.

Extensive research has established that oxidative stress, mediated by excessive ROS generation, is closely associated with the pathogenesis of DOX-induced myocardial injury (41). In vivo ROS quantification in mice with DOX-induced heart failure revealed a significantly higher ROS level in the injured myocardium of model mice compared to that in the healthy myocardium (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). In contrast, ROS levels in nontarget tissues including the liver, spleen, lung, kidney, brain, and vasculature, were comparable between model and healthy mice. This result confirms that the pathological elevation of ROS is highly localized to the injured cardiac tissue in the DOX-induced heart failure mouse model, while nontarget tissues maintain ROS levels similar to those in the healthy state. Consequently, the tissue-specific distribution of ROS further supports the targeting specificity of our ROS-responsive nanotherapeutic strategy. We then examined in vivo efficacy of the ROS-responsive DEX nanotherapy based on ONs (i.e., DONs) in heart failure mice under TH conditions. DONs, fabricated by the nanoprecipitation/self-assembly approach as aforementioned, exhibited an average diameter of 95 nm (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B). In vitro release tests confirmed the ROS-responsive drug release behavior of DONs (SI Appendix, Fig. S9C). Notably, accelerated DEX release was observed even at a relatively low H2O2 concentration of 50 μM, indicating the high sensitivity of DONs to oxidative stress. Subsequently, therapeutic effects of DONs (at 20 mg/kg of DEX) were evaluated (Fig. 5A). At day 5 postadministration, the combined treatment with DONs and TH resulted in less body weight loss and a higher HW/TL ratio, compared to DEX (at 200 mg/kg) or the same dose of DONs under NT conditions (Fig. 5 B–D). Echocardiographic assessment revealed substantial cardiac functional recovery, with marked improvements in LVEF and LVFS after the DONs-TH combination therapy (Fig. 5 E–G). Furthermore, this combined therapeutic approach more remarkably reduced serum biomarkers of cardiac injury, including cTnI and creatine kinase (CK), along with significant decrease in myocardial malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, a key indicator of oxidative stress (Fig. 5 H–J). Histopathological analysis confirmed that the DONs-TH combination therapy effectively mitigated cardiomyocyte apoptosis and restored myocardial structures to a greater extent than either free DEX or DONs treatment under NT conditions (Fig. 5K). Notably, DONs administered at 20 mg/kg of DEX exhibited therapeutic efficacy comparable to DPNs at 40 mg/kg, clearly demonstrating the superior advantage of the ROS-responsive nanotherapy (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

In a parallel study, therapeutic effects of DEX-containing liposomes (DLPs) were also examined. To this end, DLPs with an average diameter of 150 ± 2 nm and a DEX loading content of 11.5% were fabricated (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). In vivo evaluation in mice with DOX-induced heart failure demonstrated that the combination of DLPs and TH yielded superior therapeutic outcomes compared to either free DEX or DLPs under NT conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Specifically, the DLPs-TH combined regimen significantly attenuated body weight loss, improved cardiac functional recovery, reduced myocardial injury biomarkers, and mitigated oxidative stress at day 5 posttreatment.

Collectively, these in vivo studies provide compelling evidence that TH can serve as a potent strategy to enhance therapeutic performance of various NP-based delivery platforms for targeted treatment of CVDs. The observed therapeutic benefits strongly correlate with the enhanced in vivo targeting efficiency of different nanocarriers under TH conditions, as demonstrated by in vivo targeting results.

Mechanisms Underlying TH-Mediated Targeting Enhancement of Nanocarriers to Pathological Cardiovascular Tissues.

Given the promising therapeutic outcomes, we next sought to elucidate mechanisms underlying TH-mediated targeting enhancement effects. Notably, when NPs interface with plasma, they rapidly absorb proteins to form a protein corona, a well-documented phenomenon that critically determines biodistribution profiles and therapeutic effects of nanotherapies (42, 43). The physicochemical characteristics of the biomolecular corona, particularly its compositions, are pivotal determinants of nanomedicine fate in vivo, which critically govern systemic stability, biodistribution patterns, cellular uptake efficiency, and ultimately therapeutic efficacy (42, 44–46). Previous studies indicate that temperature can modulate the formation of protein coronas by altering the surface hydrophobicity of NPs, thereby affecting the quantity and composition of adsorbed proteins (47, 48). Moreover, temperature also influences protein binding kinetics and stability, and these factors collectively regulate the overall characteristics of protein coronas (49, 50). Consequently, we first verified whether the targeting ability of NPs varies after incubation with plasma at different temperatures (Fig. 6A). In this case, Cy5-PNs were incubated with fresh mouse plasma at 4, 15, 25, 33, or 37 °C for 2 h. Physicochemical characterization revealed no significant alterations in the particle size across different incubation conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). We also observed that the amount of proteins adsorbed onto the surface of PNs remained constant over a 72 h period, which confirms the stability of the protein corona (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). Subsequently, the heart-targeting ability of different Cy5-PNs was evaluated in heart failure mice after i.v. administration. Strikingly, ex vivo fluorescence imaging indicated that plasma-preincubated Cy5-PNs exhibited enhanced cardiac accumulation compared to native PNs, regardless of the incubation temperature (Fig. 6 B and C). Notably, PNs preincubated at 33 °C showed the maximal enrichment in the injured heart. Similarly, in mice with induced myocardial hypertrophy, Cy5-PNs preconditioned with plasma at 33 °C also demonstrated superior targeting efficiency compared to controls (Fig. 6 D and E). Besides Cy5-PNs, Cy5-labeled liposomes preincubated with plasma at 33 °C similarly achieved the highest cardiac accumulation in heart failure mice (Fig. 6 F and G). Intriguingly, 33 °C falls within the therapeutic temperature range used in clinical hypothermia protocols, suggesting a potential translational relevance of this finding.

To delineate the mechanism underlying plasma pretreatment-mediated targeting enhancement, we conducted comparative proteomic profiling of NP-associated corona proteomes. PNs were incubated in mouse plasma under controlled conditions to form a protein corona at a therapeutically relevant temperature (33 °C) versus the physiological temperature (37 °C) for 2 h. Cryogenic TEM (Cryo-TEM) images revealed that, following incubation of PNs with mouse plasma at 33 °C and 37 °C, protein clusters appeared as regions of enhanced electron density distributed on the PN surfaces. In contrast to the untreated PNs, this distinct morphological feature provides direct evidence for the formation of a protein corona (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). To further characterize the protein corona composition, the proteins absorbed on the surface of PNs were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and analyzed via liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Quantitative proteomic analysis revealed distinct protein absorption patterns, with 768 plasma proteins identified in the NT group and 780 proteins detected in the TH group. Notably, 765 proteins were found to be common to both treatment conditions (Fig. 6H and SI Appendix, Fig. S16). Among the corona proteins, extracellular proteins constituted the largest proportion (~34%), followed by cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). The volcano plot highlighted 36 differentially expressed proteins between the NT and TH groups, with 7 downregulated proteins and 29 upregulated proteins in the TH-treated group compared to the NT group (Fig. 6I). The most abundant upregulated proteins were visualized in the heatmap (Fig. 6J). Importantly, PNs exhibited enhanced absorption of apolipoproteins, specifically ApoC1 and ApoC4, after TH treatment (Fig. 6 K and L). This finding is particularly significant, as apolipoproteins are known to play critical roles in cellular uptake, blood circulation half-life, and tissue distribution of nanocarriers (42, 44, 45, 51–55). Therefore, we conclude that TH treatment can remodel the protein corona by modulating the protein binding affinities, with pronounced enrichment of ApoC1 and ApoC4.

ApoC1, the smallest apolipoprotein, plays a crucial regulatory role in lipoprotein transport and metabolism (56, 57). In contrast, while ApoC4 has been implicated in lipid metabolism (58), its precise biological functions remain poorly understood. To experimentally validate our hypothesis that TH-induced protein corona remodeling enhances NP accumulation in injured cardiovascular tissues via ApoC1/ApoC4 enrichment, we engineered Cy5-PNs coated specifically with ApoC1 (ApoC1-Cy5-PNs) and evaluated their cardiac targeting capability in heart failure mice. ApoC1-Cy5-PNs were obtained by directly incubating Cy5-PNs with ApoC1 at varying temperatures for 2 h. The adsorption of ApoC1 onto Cy5-PNs was confirmed by LC-MS analysis (SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2). ApoC1-Cy5-PNs maintained comparable morphology and hydrodynamic diameter to unmodified Cy5-PNs (Fig. 6 M and N). Biolayer interferometry (BLI) measurements indicated that the calculated equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) between ApoC1 and very low-density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR) was 1.5 × 10−8 M and 0.9 × 10−8 M before and after ApoC1 absorption onto PNs, respectively. This suggests that ApoC1 retains its functional conformation and preserves the ability to bind to the receptor after being adsorbed onto the surface of PNs (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). The surface density of ApoC1 adsorbed onto each PN after incubation at 33 °C was approximately 165 molecules per particle. The loading efficiency of ApoC1 on PNs was 46.8 ± 0.1%. The amount of ApoC1 loaded onto the NP surface remained relatively stable during the first 2 h of incubation and decreased slightly from 468.4 ng/mg NPs to 374.8 ng/mg NPs after 24 h of incubation in serum (SI Appendix, Table S3).

Compared with ApoC1-Cy5-PNs obtained at 25 and 37 °C, ApoC1-Cy5-PNs prepared at 33 °C exhibited the highest myocardial accumulation after i.v. injection in mice with heart failure (Fig. 6 O and P), which was twofold higher than that of native Cy5-PNs. Additionally, we compared the targeting capacity of PNs preincubated with plasma or ApoC1 at 33 °C. At 24 h after i.v. delivery of Cy5-PNs, ex vivo imaging revealed notably higher fluorescent signals in injured hearts of mice treated with ApoC1-Cy5-PNs compared to those treated with plasma-incubated Cy5-NPs (Fig. 6 Q and R). The fluorescence intensity in the heart tissue of the ApoC1-Cy5-PNs group was 1.6-fold higher than that of the plasma-Cy5-PNs group. Moreover, immunofluorescence analyses revealed the considerable localization of Cy5-PNs in endothelial cells, macrophages, cardiac fibroblasts, and myocardial cells in heart cryosections (SI Appendix, Fig. S19). This result well agrees with our in vivo cardiac targeting findings in heart failure mice under TH conditions (Fig. 2E).

Furthermore, to demonstrate the cardiac-targeting advantage of ApoC1 modification, we designed comparative experiments using a well-recognized CTP and a mitochondrial-targeting peptide (SS31) as control targeting moieties. Of note, both CTP and SS31 have been extensively studied as targeting ligands for the treatment of heart diseases (6, 59–61). CTP-functionalized Cy5-PNs (CTP-Cy5-PNs) and SS31-coated Cy5-PNs (SS31-Cy5-PNs) were prepared using similar methods. The formulated different PNs exhibited comparable particle sizes (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). After i.v. injection in heart failure mice, ex vivo imaging revealed distinct cardiac accumulation patterns, with significantly stronger fluorescent signals detected exclusively in heart tissues from the CTP-Cy5-PNs and ApoC1-Cy5-PNs groups. Notably, ApoC1-functionalized PNs showed the highest heart-targeting efficiency, while SS31-Cy5-PNs and unmodified Cy5-PNs displayed minimal cardiac accumulation (Fig. 6 S and T). These findings suggest that ApoC1 functionalization represents a promising targeting strategy for precision nanomedicine, potentially revolutionizing therapeutic delivery to injured tissues in CVDs through enhanced tissue-specific accumulation.

ApoC1 Functionalization Enhanced Targeting Efficiency of NPs in Pathological Cardiovascular Tissues via Receptor–Ligand Interaction.

Compelling evidence demonstrates that apolipoproteins (in particular, ApoA, ApoB, and ApoE) enriched in the NP protein corona dictate in vivo biodistribution through specific interactions with receptors of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and/or very LDL (VLDL) (42, 44, 45, 51–55). Building on this established mechanism, we propose that ApoC1-functionalized nanocarriers similarly achieve enhanced specific accumulation in pathological cardiovascular tissues via recognition by these lipoprotein receptor pathways. Consequently, we examined the expression profile of lipoprotein-associated receptors in myocardial tissues of heart failure mice. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed notable upregulation of key lipoprotein receptors, including VLDLR, LDL receptor (LDLR), LDLR-related protein 1 (LRP1), and scavenger receptor B1 (SR-B1) in cardiac cryosections of diseased mice compared to healthy controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S21A). Subsequent in vivo tracking of intravenously delivered ApoC1-Cy5-PNs in the heart failure model indicated remarkable receptor-specific colocalization, with strong spatial correlation observed between ApoC1-Cy5-PNs and VLDLR, LDLR, and SR-B1 in the diseased myocardium, while minimal interaction was detected with LRP1. Particularly, both the fluorescence intensity and degree of colocalization with corresponding receptors were notably higher in the ApoC1-Cy5-PNs group, compared to the control Cy5-PNs group (SI Appendix, Fig. S21B). Collectively, these results substantiate that ApoC1 decoration can enhance nanocarrier accumulation in the injured cardiovascular tissues through receptor-mediated uptake, primarily facilitated by the upregulated expression of VLDLR, LDLR, and SR-B1 in CVD states.

Discussion

Herein, we demonstrate that the targeting capability of diverse nanocarriers, such as PLGA NPs, ROS-responsive NPs, PS NPs, and liposomes, to diseased cardiovascular tissues can be remarkably enhanced under TH conditions. This enhancement was validated across multiple murine models of CVDs, including heart failure, myocardial hypertrophy, ventricular fibrillation, atherosclerosis, DVT, and ischemic stroke. Using DEX as a model drug, we found that TH treatment significantly potentiated therapeutic effects of DEX-containing nanotherapies based on PLGA NPs, ROS-responsive NPs, and liposomes in heart failure mice, compared to treatments performed at physiological temperature. Consistent with enhanced in vivo targeting effects and improved therapeutic outcomes under TH, preincubation of NPs with mouse plasma at 33 °C, a clinically relevant temperature for TH, significantly increased their targeting efficiency in heart failure mice, outperforming both native NPs and NPs pretreated with plasma at other temperatures.

Further proteomic analysis indicated that temperature can modulate the plasma protein composition of nanocarrier coronas, consequently altering their in vivo biodistribution. Notably, corona enrichment with apolipoproteins, particularly ApoC1, markedly enhanced nanocarrier accumulation in injured cardiovascular tissues. Correspondingly, precoating NPs with ApoC1 under TH conditions effectively improved their targeted delivery to pathological myocardial tissues in mice. Compared to the commonly used cardiac-targeting peptide CTP and the mitochondrial-targeted peptide SS31, ApoC1-functionalized NPs demonstrated significantly enhanced accumulation in injured cardiac tissues. This targeting enhancement is primarily mediated by the upregulated expression of lipoprotein receptors (e.g., VLDLR, LDLR, and SR-B1) in the pathologically altered myocardium, which facilitate receptor-specific uptake of ApoC1-modified NPs. In contrast to existing NP targeting strategies, such as physicochemical parameter optimization (e.g., size, shape, and surface charge), ligand/antibody conjugation, biomimetic membrane coating, and endogenous targeting mechanisms (35), our innovative approach utilizing TH-conditioned pretreatment with plasma or ApoC1 offers multiple advantages, including streamlined formulation protocols, straightforward preparation procedures, broad applicability across nanoplatforms, cost-effectiveness, and superior targeting precision in cardiovascular pathologies.

It should be noted that ApoC1-decorated NPs face dynamic challenges in vivo that can alter their protein corona. Shear stress in the circulatory system may disrupt weak interactions that stabilize the binding between ApoC1 and NPs, potentially leading to ApoC1 detachment, conformational changes, or exposure of epitopes that facilitate the binding of other plasma proteins (62). Additionally, enzymatic degradation by proteases (e.g., serine proteases) can reduce the abundance of ApoC1, and the resulting fragmented proteins may lose their functionality or interfere with the integrity of the protein corona. Besides, opsonization could exacerbate these changes, as opsonins (e.g., C3b, IgG) compete with ApoC1 for binding sites, forming mixed protein coronas and thereby altering immune recognition and clearance processes (63). Despite these concerns, our experimental results demonstrate that the targeting specificity of ApoC1-modified NPs remains intact throughout the in vivo process.

Meanwhile, TH can modulate immune function, with reported immunosuppression in post–cardiac arrest patients, evidenced by reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, impaired phagocytic activity of neutrophils and macrophages, altered lymphocyte proliferation, and an increased risk of secondary infections (64). Nevertheless, controlled and time-limited TH, particularly when applied for less than 48 h, appears to carry a lower risk of immune system disruption (65). In this study, the hypothermic condition was maintained at 32 to 34 °C for 2 h, a duration that is unlikely to induce significant immune alterations. Moreover, our findings demonstrate that the in vitro hypothermia-based approach for decorating ApoC1 onto NPs for targeted delivery avoids the immune perturbations associated with in vivo TH treatment.

In summary, we establish a paradigm wherein TH potentiates targeted nanotherapy delivery for precision management of CVDs. Capitalizing on the extensive clinical applications of TH in managing cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction, neonatal encephalopathy, stroke, traumatic brain injury, and spinal cord injury (66–68), TH represents a clinically feasible approach to amplify the targeting efficiency and therapeutic efficacy of nanotherapies for CVDs. Crucially, our findings reveal that TH-mediated selective enrichment of apolipoproteins creates a biological framework for enhanced nanocarrier tropism. The developed plasma/ApoC1 preconditioning strategy enables facile functionalization of diverse nanocarriers, achieving tissue-precise delivery of therapeutic payloads through lipoprotein-mimetic engineering for treating CVDs.

Materials and Methods

Detailed experimental procedures are provided in SI Appendix. Briefly, all carrier materials, including PLGA, OCD, and TPCD were either commercially sourced or synthesized from commercially available reagents. Fluorescent or drug-loaded PLGA NPs, OCD NPs, and liposomes were prepared using established protocols, with their particle size, size distribution, and ζ-potential characterized. All animal studies strictly adhered to ethical guidelines approved by the Animal Ethical and Experimental Committee of Army Medical University (Chongqing, China; Approval No. AMUWEC2019122). Cardiovascular disease models were established based on modified literature methods (detailed in SI Appendix): DOX-induced heart failure and ISO-induced myocardial hypertrophy in mice, atherosclerosis in ApoE−/− mice, FeCl3-induced DVT in mice, and transesophageal electrical stimulation-induced ventricular fibrillation in rats. In vivo targeting of nanocarriers in murine cardiovascular disease models was evaluated through ex vivo fluorescence imaging combined with immunofluorescence analysis. Therapeutic effects of nanotherapies were assessed in the murine heart failure model. Protein corona composition of NPs was analyzed via cryo-TEM, proteomic analysis, and BLI assay following various treatments.
