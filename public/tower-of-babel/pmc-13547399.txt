Identification of a membrane transporter for dimethylsulfoniopropionate uptake in marine eukaryotic phytoplankton
Authors: Rafel Simó, Jean-François Mangot, Valerie Vergé, Jean-Claude Lozano, Violeta Saló, Clara Ruiz-González, Daffne López-Sandoval, Yaiza M Castillo, Ronald P Kiene, Stephen D Archer, François-Yves Bouget, José M González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarine microalgae obtain essential sulfur through energetically costly, assimilatory reduction of sea-salt sulfate, so the ability for osmotrophic incorporation of reduced sulfur would confer them adaptability in energy-limiting conditions. Dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP) is a common marine osmolyte whose pervasive occurrence in seawater makes it as good a candidate for supplying reduced sulfur to marine eukaryotes as it is to prokaryotes. Despite earlier recognition of DMSP uptake by some phytoplankters, the mechanism and its taxonomic and geographic distributions have remained unknown. Here we show that DMSP uptake occurs in taxonomically diverse eukaryotic phytoplankton harbouring PaDT (for picoalgal DMSP transporter), a gene within the betaine/choline/carnitine transporter (BCCT) family whose transcription was upregulated by DMSP additions. Confirmation that PaDT encodes a functional DMSP transporter was obtained by restoring DMSP uptake in a BCCT-deficient mutant of Escherichia coli complemented with picoalgal PaDT. Increase of picoalgal DMSP uptake and PaDT transcription under decreasing light supports exogenous DMSP incorporation as an energy-saving strategy. The global and taxonomic spread of PaDT expression in the surface ocean indicates that eukaryotic phytoplankton are not only the main DMSP source but also a ubiquitous sink, reinforcing their role in regulating marine sulfur emissions.Subject terms: Element cycles, Microbial ecology

Introduction

Dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP) is ubiquitously produced in the oceans and coastal marine environments mainly by phytoplankton but also by macroalgae, bacteria, corals, and plants1,2. With production accounting for 5–9% of global phytoplankton carbon fixation3 and with intracellular concentrations in the micromolar to hundreds of millimolar range4,5, DMSP plays important ecophysiological roles in its producer, acting as osmolyte, cryoprotectant, antioxidant and elemental buffer1,4. Through release and catabolism, it also provides carbon and sulfur to marine microbes1,2, mediates communication and interactions among microorganisms, macroalgae, and invertebrate and vertebrate animals6–8, and fuels the emission of volatile sulfur into the marine atmosphere9,10, with consequences for olfactory-based animal foraging11, aerosol formation, cloud albedo and climate9,12,13.

Globally, the vast majority of DMSP biosynthesis is thought to occur in phytoplankton1,13 using sulfur acquired through assimilatory sulfate reduction14; most of its consumption is assumed to be conducted by prokaryotes, mainly by heterotrophic bacteria2,13,15 and to a lesser extent by cyanobacteria16–18. This is how the DMSP cycle is currently represented in regional and some Earth System models19. However, these classical roles are blurring as genes for DMSP synthesis are increasingly found in heterotrophic bacteria14,20, and scattered evidence has shown that some eukaryotic phytoplankton take up exogenous (dissolved) DMSP, both in cultures and in nature15,21–24. This dual role of phytoplankton as producers and consumers is not yet incorporated into current understanding and modelling, essentially because the DMSP uptake mechanism in eukaryotic algae, its taxonomic spread and its quantitative importance are unknown. In this work, we use radioisotope assays, molecular tools and bioinformatics to identify a widespread membrane DMSP transporter in marine microalgae and elucidate the diversity and environmental distribution of DMSP uptake by eukaryotic phytoplankton.

Results

DMSP uptake by eukaryotic phytoplankton cultures

We tested a number of cultured phytoplankton strains for their ability to take up 35S-DMSP added to the growth medium at sub-nanomolar concentrations (Table 1). Uptake occurred in the assayed strains of the pico-sized Mamiellophyceaen species Micromonas commoda, Ostreococcus lucimarinus, Ostreococcus clade B members, Ostreococcus tauri, and Bathycoccus prasinos, but not in Micromonas pusilla. It also occurred in the assayed strain of the diatom Phaeodactylum tricornutum, which agrees with previous reports of DMSP uptake by the diatoms Thalassiossira pseudonana, Thalassiossira oceanica15, and Thalassiossira gravida23. In contrast, the diatoms Skeletonema marinoi and Chaetoceros sp. did not show measurable 35S-DMSP uptake, the latter in agreement with previous observations with other Chaetoceros strains23. The dinoflagellates Prorocentrum minimum and Karlodinium armiger7 showed uptake, whereas the chlorophycean Dunaliella tertiolecta and two assayed strains of the prymnesiophycean Emiliania huxleyi did not. In total, 35S-DMSP uptake was observed in 11 of the 19 phytoplankton strains tested, with no clear distinction between taxonomic classes.

A BCCT carrier as the candidate DMSP transporter in cultured eukaryotic phytoplankton

Mining the sequences of the subgroup of strains whose genomes (Table S1) or transcriptomes25 have been fully sequenced (Table 1), we found that all the 35S-DMSP-incorporating strains had a betaine/carnitine/choline transporter (BCCT)-encoding gene that was absent in the M. pusilla and D. tertiolecta strains that did not show uptake.

In prokaryotes, BCCT carriers are known transporters of DMSP and glycine betaine, among other substrates26,27. Addition of 10 µM of glycine betaine suppressed 35S-DMSP uptake by the mamiellophycean B. prasinos (Fig. S1), suggesting that DMSP and glycine betaine shared the same membrane transporter (hence presumably a member of the BCCT family), as we had previously suggested for two diatoms of the genus Thalassiosira15. Therefore, based on the absence of a BCCT-encoding gene in the algal strains that did not incorporate 35S-DMSP, and the competitive inhibition of DMSP uptake by glycine betaine, we hypothesized that a BCCT was the candidate DMSP transporter in eukaryotic phytoplankton.

Confirmation of the BCCT-member PaDT as the DMSP transporter

One of the mamiellophycean species that showed 35S-DMSP uptake was O. tauri (Table 1). Despite its absence in large metagenomics and metatranscriptomics datasets of open-ocean plankton28, O. tauri is used as a model species for functional studies through genetic engineering29,30. The small genome of O. tauri contains two genes predicted to encode BCCT homologs, located in two different chromosomes, which we arbitrarily numbered BCCT-1 and BCCT-2.

A heterologous expression experiment with Escherichia coli was conducted to decipher whether the O. tauri BCCT genes encoded DMSP transporter proteins (Fig. 1a). Full-length coding sequences of BCCT-1 and BCCT-2 were amplified from reverse-transcribed RNA of O. tauri RCC745 and cloned into the expression vector pBAD33 to generate the expression plasmids pBAD33-BCCT-1 and pBAD33-BCCT-2 that were used in a complementation of E. coli MKH13 mutant. The wildtype E. coli strain DH5α harbours a BCCT carrier, which we confirmed, confers the capability to incorporate DMSP at 4% salinity, i.e., similar to that of seawater (Fig. 1b). MKH13 is a proline betaine carrier (ProP- ProU-)-deficient mutant31, hence expected to be incapable of DMSP uptake. We confirmed this by introducing the empty plasmid pBAD33 into MKH13, followed by 35S-DMSP addition assays. This resulted in no detectable DMSP uptake, as the amount of cell-retained radioisotope was indistinguishable from that of a killed, untransformed E. coli DH5α (Fig. 1b). Complementation of MKH13 with pBAD33-BCCT-1 at 4% salinity restored 36% of the 35S-DMSP uptake activity relative to wildtype DH5α strain (Fig. 1b). Conversely, complementation with pBAD33-BCCT-2 did not result in any noticeable 35S-DMSP uptake, suggesting that O. tauri BCCT-1 functions as a DMSP carrier while BCCT−2 does not. We termed the BCCT-1 carrier PaDT (for “picoalgal DMSP transporter”), and its corresponding gene PaDT.

Homologs of the O. tauri PaDT genes are present in the genome of M. commoda, another mamiellophycean with a cosmopolitan distribution in the world’s oceans32. Unlike O. tauri, which has its two PaDT genes on different chromosomes, M. commoda harbors a single PaDT gene on chromosome 5, predicted to have two splicing variants33. We grew the M. commoda RCC299 cultures with and without daily additions of 500 nM DMSP, and quantified PaDT expression at two growth phases (mid-exponential and early-stationary, Fig. S2), and in dark (end of the night) and light (one hour after light onset) conditions (Fig. 2). In our RNA-seq data, the two predicted splicing variants could not be distinguished, and expression is therefore reported at the gene level. We also quantified the expression of the DMSP synthesis gene proposed14 for Mamiellophyceae (DSYE) in the same samples. This way, we wanted to ascertain which conditions of growth, light, and exogenous DMSP availability favoured uptake over de novo synthesis, and vice versa.

In mid-exponential growth, when nutrients and vitamins were still at replete concentrations, addition of DMSP slightly upregulated the transcription of PaDT, yet only in the dark (Fig. 2a). Transcripts were largely suppressed in the light, regardless of whether DMSP was added or not. This contrasted with the transcription of the DMSP synthesis DSYE gene homolog, which was higher in the light and lower in the dark and downregulated by DMSP addition (Fig. 2b). At the beginning of the stationary phase, when nutrients and vitamins became scarce and self-shading largely reduced light availability, DMSP addition greatly upregulated the transcription of PaDT, especially in the dark (Fig. 2a), while reducing the transcription of DSYE, especially in the light (Fig. 2b). All in all, upregulation of the PaDT gene expression of M. commoda upon exposure to exogenous dissolved DMSP provided further support for the role of PaDT as a DMSP transporter, acting primarily in the absence of light.

Light regulation of DMSP uptake by cultured and natural phytoplankton

The deleterious effect of light exposure on PaDT expression was further tested through radioisotope uptake experiments with cultures of M. commoda as well as B. prasinos, another cosmopolitan mamiellophycean (Fig. 3). Both experiments were performed during the exponential growth phase under a range of light conditions: dark, low light (15 μmol photons m−2 s−1), and high light (100 μmol photons m−2 s−1). Consistent with the observed pattern of PaDT transcription in M. commoda, the uptake of 35S-DMSP by both strains was highest in the dark and decreased with increasing light.

The question remained as to whether DMSP uptake, clearly demonstrated with cultured microalgal strains, is anecdotal or widespread in surface-ocean phytoplankton assemblages. During a summer bloom of the high-DMSP producing prymnesiophycean Phaeocystis sp. in the Arctic, we used microautoradiography22 to identify and count bacterial and phytoplankton cells that took up 35S-DMSP after additions at picomolar concentrations and incubations for 10 h in dimmed light. More than 80% of morphologically identified Micromonas-like cells co-occurring with the blooming Phaeocystis sp. were labelled with 35S (Fig. S3). We later performed a series of similar experiments in the Mediterranean Sea, but quantified total incorporation of 35S-DMSP as the radioactivity retained and precipitated on filters instead of quantifying single-cell uptake through microautoradiography. After 6–7 h of incubation with picomolar additions of 35S-DMSP, and precipitation of macromolecules with trichloroacetic acid, 60–90% of the retained radioisotope was retrieved on filters of 1.2 µm of pore size, with the remaining 10–40% being contained in 0.2–1.2 µm size particles (Fig. S4). Thus, more 35S ended up in the fraction corresponding to protists and aggregates than in the fraction of free-living bacteria, currently thought to be the main DMSP consumers34. Across three sampling locations, the relative contribution of larger particles to total incorporation increased with chlorophyll a concentration, suggesting a major role for phytoplankton, yet we do not know how much corresponded to osmotrophic uptake. Incorporation of 35S by the larger size fraction was on average twice as high in nocturnal compared to diurnal incubations, while it was nearly the same for the small size fraction (Fig. S4). This supports that, as in the previous experiments with mamiellophycean cultures, absence of light at night enhanced DMSP uptake by natural Mediterranean phytoplankton.

Spread of PaDT occurrence and expression across marine eukaryotic phytoplankton

Surveying the genomes and transcriptomes of the Marine Microbial Eukaryote Transcriptome Sequencing Project (MMETSP) and EukProt databases (Figs. 4 and S5), we identified 361 BCCT-like transporters from marine eukaryotes, substantially surpassing the 57 eukaryotic BCCT homologs previously reported35. These transporters, belonging primarily to pico- and nanosized algae and named PaDT for their homology to the mamiellophycean gene, form a phylogenetic group distinct from known prokaryotic BCCT-like transporters (Fig. 4). PaDT genes clustered into five clades (arbitrarily named A–E), with clade A being the most diverse, comprising transporters from chlorophytes (notably Mamiellophyceae), Cryptophyceae, and Prymnesiophyceae, several of which have shown DMSP uptake in culture assays (Table 1). Clade B includes phototrophic dinoflagellates and Pelagophyceae; clade C combines multicellular organisms and some dinoflagellates; and clades D and E are associated with diatoms and dinoflagellates, respectively.

To assess the ecological significance of eukaryotic phytoplankton PaDT genes, we investigated the biogeographic distribution of their occurrence and expression in surface ocean waters using metagenomic and metatranscriptomic datasets from the Ocean Microbial Reference Gene Catalog (OM-RGC.v2; 0.22–3 μm size fraction) and the Marine Atlas of Tara Oceans Unigenes (MATOU; 0.8–2000 μm) (Fig. S6). From mapped distributions of transcripts, PaDT genes collectively proved to be ubiquitous in the global ocean but showed differences in their latitudinal patterns of expression by clade (Fig. 5). In OM-RGC.v2 picoplankton, transcripts from clades C, D, and E were absent, consistent with their association with larger organisms such as diatoms and dinoflagellates. Clade A transcripts were more abundant in high-latitude regions (> 50°N and 50°S), associated mainly with Mamiellalophyceae (north) and Prymnesiophyceae (south) (Fig. 5a), while Clade B PaDT showed more homogeneously distributed expression, with a large peak in low-latitude waters off Peru (~ 10°S), exclusively associated with Pelagophyceae (Fig. 5b). In MATOU, transcripts of all PaDT clades were detected: while clades A (largely contributed by Chlorophyta, mainly Mamiellophyceae) and D (mainly diatoms) both peaked in southern sub-polar and polar regions, the dinoflagellate-associated clades B, C, and E showed relatively homogeneous distribution across latitudes (Fig. 5c–h).

We compared the latitudinal patterns of total PaDT transcription in surface ocean waters with the satellite-derived total DMSP concentrations (DMSPt_sat) co-located by latitude and month (Fig. 6). Both DMSPt_sat (p = 9.3 × 10−32, Kruskal–Wallis test) and PaDT transcripts in OM-RGC.v2 (p = 1.6 × 10−7) showed latitudinal structuring, whereas MATOU exhibited only a marginal pattern (p = 3.3 × 10−3). Post-hoc analyses indicated broadly higher OM-RGC.v2 transcript abundance at high versus low latitudes, while MATOU showed weak differentiation and lacked data north of 45°N. Overall, PaDT transcription roughly mirrors the predicted distribution of DMSPt_sat, suggesting that phytoplankton assemblages most exposed to DMSP are better primed for, and more active in, DMSP uptake.

Discussion

As a dissolved reduced sulfur compound, DMSP is ubiquitously available in the euphotic ocean at background concentrations in the nM range34,36, which can increase to µM in patches around cells and aggregates6. In the presence of strong producers, released DMSP is enough to fuel the entire sulfur demand of heterotrophic bacteria, and prey-associated DMSP is estimated to satisfy half the sulfur demands of micrograzers37. Our results indicate that these estimates should also include the supply of an as-yet unknown proportion of the sulfur requirements of phytoplankton. In bacteria as well as protistan grazers, part of the incorporated DMSP-sulfur ends up assimilated into the consumer’s proteins37–39, thereby reducing the need for sulfate reduction. The vast majority of marine microorganisms, with few but conspicuous exceptions40, possess the enzymatic machinery to take up and reduce sea-salt sulfate, and assimilate the produced sulfide into the synthesis of organic compounds, including DMSP and amino acids. However, the double negative charge on sulfate makes it energetically costly to import, and reduction to sulfide involves multiple steps and requires eight electrons41. Therefore, having alternatives to the complete biosynthesis of organic sulfur metabolites may confer to phytoplankton a competitive advantage. Such alternatives include the incorporation of reduced forms from prey by phagotrophy42, but also by osmotrophy43 if possessing efficient transport systems, as we show here.

In marine bacteria, transporters in the families BCCT and ATP-binding cassette have been identified as carriers for DMSP uptake27,44–46. This study identifies a BCCT carrier as a widespread DMSP transporter in marine eukaryotic microalgae. The eukaryotic PaDT protein shows 32–34% similarity to the prokaryotic BCCT proteins OpuD from Bacillus subtilis45, DddT from Halomonas47, and BetP from Corynebacterium glutamicum48. An analysis of the predicted protein structure and binding site of the substrate with O. tauri RCC745 and O. lucimarinus CCE9901 PaDT in comparison to C. glutamicum BetP supports this functional similarity (Fig. S7). This is interesting, because BCCT are multipurpose transporters: they are known to transport several substrates, mainly N-osmolytes like glycine betaine, choline, carnitine, proline betaine, dimethylglycine and ectoine35,49, with known roles as compatible solutes that are analogous to DMSP.

The results of radioisotope uptake and transcriptomic tests with cosmopolitan Mamiellophyceae like Micromonas and Bathycoccus (Fig. 2) indicate that exposure to lower light or darkness impairs DMSP synthesis and upregulates uptake, whereas higher light downregulates uptake and favours DMSP biosynthesis. This suggests that DMSP uptake replaces part of DMSP synthesis under energy-limiting conditions, similar to the fact that algal uptake of amino acids is enhanced in the absence of photosynthesis, i.e., in turbid sediments or at night50. DMSP uptake is expected to be less energy-costly than biosynthesis41. The benefits of uptake, though, will be dependent on the function the cell assigns to the acquired DMSP23. In prokaryotes, DMSP not only acts as an osmolyte but its catabolic degradation supplies part of the sulfur and carbon needs of the cell16,34. The prokaryotic DmdA enzyme, in particular, is the gate towards DMSP-sulfur assimilation into protein via methionine synthesis38,51. Even though homologs of the dmdA genes are absent in the sequenced genomes of Mamiellophyceae (Table S1), the observation that in M. commoda and B. prasinos uptake increases with decreasing light suggests that these cosmopolitan algae do not only take up DMSP to potentially use it as an osmolyte but also that it fuels the metabolic machinery in a way that helps the organism overcome energy limitation. This may be especially important over the polar winter or in deep mixing events like those occurring in subpolar and tropical upwelling waters (Fig. 5). The uptake of reduced sulfur in the form of DMSP by Mamiellophyceae may help explain how these picoalgae have developed versatile adaptive strategies that make them highly successful in many different oceanic environments, from the tropics to the poles52.

Besides Mamiellophyceae, the phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 4) showed that PaDT is spread across most taxonomic groups of eukaryotic phytoplankton, as suggested by previous evidence of DMSP uptake obtained by microautoradiography22. Intriguingly, many of the microalgal taxa harbouring the PaDT gene, including most of those that showed uptake in the radioisotope assays (Table 1), are DMSP producers, with intracellular concentrations up to hundreds of mM (ref. 4, Table S2). Therefore, an apparent paradox emerges: across-membrane uptake occurs against a strong gradient between outer (nM) and inner (mM) DMSP concentrations, even if uptake elicits or coincides with downregulation of biosynthesis. This suggests either that most of the intracellular synthesised DMSP pool does not accumulate in the cytoplasm but in plastids53, mitochondria, or secretory vesicles54, or that import by PaDT confers an accumulation capability of nearly 106. At present we cannot decipher which is the most plausible explanation, but it is noteworthy that, in the bacterium C. glutamicum grown in saline medium48, the BCCT carrier BetP showed an accumulation ratio of glycine betaine of 106. To make DMSP transport against a concentration gradient energetically viable, BCCT carriers can be used as symporters or antiporters with other solutes26,49,55, e.g., H+, Na+ or other betaines, whose transport down the concentration gradient releases energy. Alternatively, since transport via BCCT carrier proteins in prokaryotes has been seen to be fully reversible48, there is the possibility that some strong DMSP-producing eukaryotes harbouring the PaDT gene use the PaDT carrier not for uptake but to release DMSP.

The capability for DMSP uptake through PaDT may elicit chemosensory behaviour in motile phytoplankton. The picoflagellate M. commoda has been shown to be chemotactic towards high-concentration patches of DMSP6, therefore accessing more favourable conditions for exploiting DMSP as a resource. Another interesting case is that of the mixotrophic dinoflagellate Karlodinium armiger, which is also chemotactic towards DMSP7. In this case, K. armiger does not only benefit from DMSP uptake but uses it as a prey-signalling cue7. Therefore, even if DMSP were not taken up by phytoplankton to build intracellular concentrations high enough to provide osmoregulatory benefits, the DMSP carrier may be part of a sensory system with osmotrophic or foraging benefits.

Our comprehension of planktonic trophic webs is essential for the assessment, prediction and management of ocean’s ecosystem services such as food production, pollution recycling, global elemental cycling or climate regulation. Also, to assess whether ecological theory (generally developed for terrestrial ecosystems) applies to the vast pelagic ocean biome. Over the last two decades, our comprehension of marine planktonic trophic webs56 has changed as classical functional roles keep blurring: e.g., many heterotrophic bacteria harvest and use light57 and fix gaseous dinitrogen58, and many primary producers phagocyte other cells59 or take up dissolved organic matter43, so that mixotrophy (either by phagotrophy or osmotrophy) is widespread among eukaryotic phytoplankton. Here, we show that many phytoplankton protists can supplement their sulfur needs by acquiring reduced sulfur from seawater in the form of DMSP released by their functional partners.

It is still unknown whether this incorporated DMSP by phytoplankton is primarily accumulated to be used as a compatible solute for osmoregulation or against oxidative stress60, or broken down for sulfur or carbon assimilation via a yet-to-know catabolic pathway, or both. Even before we fully understand the ecophysiological implications of DMSP uptake by eukaryotic phytoplankton, the broad taxonomic and geographic spread of this process challenges the way we budget and model organosulfur cycling in the surface ocean.

Methods

Phytoplankton cultures

Monoclonal cultures of the strains listed in Table 1 were grown in duplicates in their preferred medium (either f/2 or L1) at 20–22 °C under fluorescent light (100 μmol photon m−2s−1; 12:12 h light:dark cycle). The composition of f/2 and L1 media follows the original descriptions61,62. Aliquots were taken daily for cell counts by flow cytometry or epifluorescence microscopy. Duplicate 1.8 mL samples were fixed with paraformaldehyde (1%) and glutaraldehyde (0.05%) and analysed using a Becton–Dickinson FACSCalibur or a Sysmex/Partec Cube8 flow cytometer. Algal cells were counted on FL3 (chlorophyll a autofluorescence) versus side scatter cytograms. Replicates were stained with SYBR Green I (∼20 μM final concentration; Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA.), and heterotrophic bacteria were counted on FL1 (SYBR Green fluorescence) versus side scatter cytograms. The flow cytometry gating strategy used for algal and bacterial discrimination is shown in Figure. S8. Alternatively, cells were counted manually on filters by epifluorescence microscopy after 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining (1 µg mL−1). Cultures that were purchased axenic were kept with low bacterial abundances until the 35S-DMSP uptake assays. Cultures that were not axenic were treated with ad-hoc sequential additions of antibiotics: penicillin (0.9 mg mL−1) was first added to culture duplicates, which were monitored over 3 days for their response in bacterial and algal growth (see below). The replicate with a lower growth of bacteria and a higher growth of algae was diluted 1:10 for regrowth after addition of neomycin (0.25 mg mL−1). After three days, the best replicate was diluted 1:10 for regrowth after addition of kanamycin (0.9 mg mL−1). After three days, this last parent culture was further diluted into replicates to conduct the experiments. In the repetition of the experiment for gene expression with M. commoda (see below), neomycin was not used as it was detrimental to the picoalga. The efficiency of the antibiotics to arrest or reduce bacterial growth was monitored by flow cytometry and epifluorescence as described above. Since bacterial counts do not necessarily inform of heterotrophic bacterial activity, bacterial growth was checked by plating on Zobell agarose medium and monitoring the growth of colonies. We considered the algal cultures to be sufficiently free of active bacteria when agarose plates remained clear after three days of incubation and bacterial counts determined by flow cytometry were ≤106 cells mL−1.

DMSP content of cultured microalgae

Aliquots of 1–2 mL of exponentially growing cultures were placed into a 10 mL gas-tight borosilicate serum vial and filled to the brim with Milli-Q water. After addition of a NaOH pellet (ca. 0.2 mol L−1 final concentration, pH >12), the vial was septum-capped and crimped, and the DMSP was left to hydrolyse for at least 48 h, so that all DMSP was converted to equimolar amounts of DMS. Aliquots of 10–100 µL were withdrawn through the septum with a gas-tight syringe and injected into a custom-made purge and trap system. Volatiles were sparged by bubbling 40 mL min−1 of ultrapure helium during 3–5 min, and trapped in a PTFE tubing loop immersed in liquid nitrogen. For injection, the loop was immersed into water at room temperature, so that the volatiles were carried by helium into a gas chromatograph (Shimadzu GC14A) equipped with a S-specific flame photometric detector and a packed CarbopackH 60/80 mesh column (Sigma-Aldrich) held at 150 °C. Calibration was performed either by injections of known volumes of a gaseous mixture of helium and DMS released by a permeation tube (Dynacal, Valco Instruments Co. Inc.), or with standard solutions of DMS prepared by diluting weighted amounts of DMSP in milliQ water and addition of NaOH for at least 24 h. Plots of log(peak area) vs log(nmol DMS) yielded a straight line that was used for quantification. The detection limit was 3 pmol.

35S-DMSP uptake assays with phytoplankton cultures

Volumes of 20–40 mL of antibiotic-treated, exponentially growing cultures were incubated in duplicate in the light (50–100 µmol photon m−2 s−1) with additions of tracer 35S-DMSP (< 0.1 nM from 35S-DMSP batches of 130–1100 Ci mmol−1 specific activity). Substrate uptake was determined after 6–14 h in 2–3 aliquots of 2–10 mL of one of the replicate cultures by filtration through 0.2 μm nylon filters (GNWP, Milllipore), and rinsing twice with 0.2 μm-filtered seawater. Filter-retained radioactivity was measured with a Beckman scintillation counter. The other replicate was used as a killed control, in which paraformaldehyde and glutaraldehyde (5% each) were added 30 min before the radioisotope; retained radioactivity was barely distinguishable from filter blanks. To study the dependence of 35S-DMSP uptake on light, duplicate B. prasinos and M. commoda cultures were spiked with 14,200 and 28,600 dpm mL−1 of 35S-DMSP, respectively, and were incubated for 7 and 14 h, respectively, in three light conditions: dark, low light (LL, 15 µmol photon m−2 s−1) and high light (HL, 100 µmol photon m−2 s−1). To study the effect of glycine betaine (GBT) addition on light 35S-DMSP uptake, four replicates of a B. prasinos culture were spiked with 17,000 dpm mL−1 of 35S-DMSP. Two replicates were also added 10 μM of GBT, and the four replicates were incubated in the light (100 µmol photon m−2 s−1) for 24 h. Uptake was monitored as above by filtering duplicates of 1 mL of the cultures at time points 0, 4, 10, and 24 h.

Uptake assays in nature

During the SUMMIT-Med cruise to the north-western Mediterranean on board the R/V Garcia del Cid (June 2021), seawater from three stations (s1–s3) was sampled in a Niskin bottle and transferred into a 0.5 L glass bottle, from which it was split into four acid-cleaned polycarbonate vials (40 mL each). Surface waters (4 m depth) were sampled at stations s1 and s3, whereas samples from station s2 were collected at the bottom mixed layer (25–30 m depth). Paraformaldehyde (1%) was added to one vial as killed control. After 30 min, 10,000 dpm mL−1 of 35S-DMSP were added to the four vials, which were incubated on deck, in a tank with continuously flushing surface seawater to mimic sea surface temperature, and under dimmed natural light to best mimic the average light in the upper mixed layer. Light was dimmed to 36% (s1), 22% (s2), and 60% (s3) of surface irradiance by covering the tank with layers of neutral mesh, and it was monitored with a Biospherical PUV-2500 radiometer placed in the incubation tank with the samples. After 6 h of incubation, the live vials were killed with 1% paraformaldehyde and all were stored at 4 °C in the dark for at least 12 h. Aliquots (5 mL) of each vial were filtered through 0.2 μm nominal pore-size nylon filters (GNWP, Millipore), and rinsed three times with 0.2 μm filtered seawater. Vacuum was stopped, cold trichloroacetic acid (5%) was added and left to react during 5 min for macromolecule precipitation, filtration was turned on again, and the filters were rinsed with milli-Q water. Filters were stored in scintillation vials with a scintillation cocktail until analysis. The same procedure was repeated with further 5 mL aliquots on 1.2 μm nominal pore-size nylon filters (GNWP, Millipore). Blanks (same filters with no sample) were collected and treated in the same way. At each sampling station, experiments were conducted during daytime (11:00–17:00 local time) and during night-time (00:00–06:00 local time).

Microautoradiography

Surface ocean samples were collected on the RV Hesperides during the ATOS I cruise22 in the Arctic (July 2007). Samples AR3 and AR4 were incubated for 10 h in quartz bottles inside a black tank with running seawater to maintain in situ temperature, after addition of 35S-DMSP (845 Ci mmol−1, 0.04 nM final concentration). Light was dimmed to 60% surface irradiance using an optically neutral mesh. Samples were fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C in the dark, gently filtered through 5 µm polycarbonate filters (Osmonics, inc.), rinsed with Milli-Q water, air dried and stored at −20 °C until being processed for microautoradiography22. After staining with DAPI, active and inactive cells were distinguished under the epifluorescence microscope by the presence or absence of silver grain accumulations denser than the background.

Complementation of E. coli mutant with O. tauri BCCT candidates

The coding sequence of two putative BCCT carriers (BCCT-1: 2455 bp; BCCT-2: 2623 bp) were amplified from O. tauri RCC745 (OTH95) RNA by RT-PCR with SuperScript™ III One-Step RT-PCR System with Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen) using gene-specific primers, custom-synthesized by Eurofins (France) for this study (Table S3 and Fig. 1a). RT-PCR reactions were performed in a 50 µL final volume containing 2× Reaction Mix, 1 µg of RNA, 1 µl of each primer (10 µM), 2 µL of SuperScript™ III RT/Platinum Taq Mix and water. Amplification began with reverse transcription step of 60 °C for 30 min, followed by a pre-denaturation at 94 °C and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 65 °C for 30 s, extension at 68 °C for 2 min, with a final extension step of 5 min. PCR products were verified and purified from agarose gel using the NucleoSpin® Gel and PCR Clean-up Kit (Macherey Nagel). The absence of the intronic sequence in the PCR products was confirmed by verifying the lack of a restriction site located within the single intron. Constructs were assembled into the expression vector pBAD33 (ATCC 87402), linearized with SacI, using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly (New England Biolabs) following the Gibson assembly protocol. The resulting expression plasmids (pBAD33-BCCT-1, pBAD33-BCCT-2, and pBAD33 empty vector as control) were first propagated by heat shock transformation into E. coli DH5α for sequence verification and subsequently introduced into E. coli MKH13 by electroporation (Fig. 1a). Two E. coli strains were used for functional assays: DH5α, a wild-type strain, and MKH13, a transporter-deficient mutant31 lacking major compatible solute transporters (PutP, ProP, ProU, and BetT-BetIBA). After electroporation, cultures were grown aerobically at 250 rpm in Luria Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with 1% NaCl (LB 1%) and 25 µg ml−1 chloramphenicol (Cm).

Functional assays with BCCT-complemented E. coli

For functional assays, overnight cultures of E. coli DH5α (wild-type) and MKH13 transformed with either O. tauri BCCT constructs or empty vector were diluted 1:100 into fresh LB 1% supplemented with 25 µg mL−1 Cm. A subset was maintained under these conditions (LB 1%), while another subset was further supplemented with NaCl to a final concentration of 4% (LB 4%). Cultures were induced with 0.01% L-arabinose (Sigma-Aldrich). Transport activity was assessed by uptake of radiolabeled ³⁵S-DMSP (final activity: 30,000–42,000 dpm mL−1). Cultures were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark with shaking. Parallel killed controls were prepared by adding paraformaldehyde (1%) and glutaraldehyde (0.05%) 40 min before ³⁵S-DMSP addition. Samples (3 ml each; in triplicate, except for DH5α, which was in duplicate) were filtered onto 0.2 μm nylon membranes (Millipore GN), rinsed with 10 mL 0.2 μm-filtered seawater, and counted for radioactivity using a Beckman scintillation counter. Uptake values were normalized to cell density (OD₅₉₅) and compared to MKH13 + empty vector, DH5α wild-type, and killed controls.

Structural modelling and functional characterization of O. tauri BCCT candidates

The predicted structure of O. tauri RCC745 BCCT-1 (accession number: CEG01428) was generated using RoseTTAFold263 via the ColabFold64 implementation (default parameters). Structural alignment with the crystal structure of the glycine betaine symporter BetP from Corynebacterium glutamicum (Protein Data Bank ID: 2WIT)65 was performed using US-align (v.20241201)66, based on aligned Cα atoms. Structural similarity metrics, including TM-score and root mean square deviation, were calculated over aligned residues. Transmembrane helices were predicted using DeepTMHMM (v1.0)67, and oligomeric state prediction was performed using DeepSub68. Subcellular localization was predicted using DeepLoc (v2.1)69, yielding a membrane localization probability of 0.80. Structural visualization, alignment, residue analysis, distance measurements, and electrostatic surface representations were performed using UCSF ChimeraX (v.1.10.1)70.

Comparative structural analyses focused on conserved substrate-binding residues forming an aromatic box, based on alignment with BetP and analysis of homologous proteins from O. tauri RCC745 and O. lucimarinus CCE9901 (accession number: XP_001422299). Molecular docking simulations were performed using Quick Vina 2 (v1.1.2)71, with parameters –exhaustiveness 64 and –num_modes 9. The predicted BCCT carrier structure was used as the receptor, and docking was conducted with DMSP and glycine betaine. Binding free energies (ΔG) were extracted from docking outputs. Docking results were validated using GNINA (v.1.3.2)72 with parameters –autobox_ligand and –cnn_scoring rescore. Distances between ligand atoms and amino acid residues were measured in UCSF ChimeraX. Potential hydrogen bond interactions were inferred based on structural proximity. Electrostatic surface calculations were performed in UCSF ChimeraX.

Gene expression assays with M. commoda

M. commoda RCC299 were grown in duplicates under standard conditions with or without daily additions of 500 nM dissolved DMSP. Daily, 1.8 mL samples were collected to track M. commoda abundance by flow cytometry, using the protocol described above. Expression levels of the PaDT gene (averaged across the two predicted splicing variants) and the DMSP synthesis gene DSYE16 were quantified at two growth stages, mid-exponential and early stationary phase, and under both dark (end of night) and light (one hour after light onset) conditions. The experiment was repeated to recover a missing condition (mid-exponential, light-exposed treatment) from the first run. For RNA extraction, 25–50 mL of culture was filtered onto 0.2 μm pore-size polycarbonate filters (47 mm diameter), flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Filters were disrupted and vortexed with PowerSoil beads (MoBio), and RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). DNA was removed using TURBO DNA-free Kit (Ambion), and RNA was purified by ethanol precipitation and resuspended in 60 μL of 10 mmol L⁻¹ Tris-HCl. Library preparation and sequencing were conducted at Sequentia Biotech (www.sequentiabiotech.com). Ribodepletion, RNA fragmentation, and reverse transcription were performed for all samples as part of the library construction using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA protocol (Illumina). Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) with 150 bp paired-end reads ( ~ 7.5–18 Gb per sample). For the stationary-phase dark control and stationary-phase light +DMSP treatment, one replicate each failed during sequencing and was excluded from downstream analysis. The remaining replicate was successfully processed and included in all analyses.

Raw reads were quality- and adapter-trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.33)73, and ribosomal RNA reads were removed using SortMeRNA (v2.1)74 by matching against M. commoda SSU and LSU rRNA sequences from the SILVA database, with an E-value threshold of <0.01. Filtered reads were aligned to the M. commoda genome33 using Bowtie2 (v2.3.4.1)75, and alignments with mapping quality ≤10 were excluded using Samtools (v1.8)76; -q 10 -F 4). Read counts were calculated using HTSeq (v0.10.0)77 with the parameters -t CDS -r pos –nonunique all. Transcript abundance was normalized as transcripts per million (TPM), accounting for gene length and sequencing depth. Differential expression of PaDT and DSYE was retrieved and evaluated across light conditions, growth phases, and DMSP treatments. For PaDT, expression values from the two splicing variants, MRCC299_05G06500 and MRCC299_05G06510, were averaged to represent total gene expression. For DSYE, a BLASTp search was conducted using amino acid sequences of functionally characterized DSYE proteins from Ostreococcus spp16. against the M. commoda RCC299 proteome. The top hit, MRCC299_06G05600 (annotated as a sarcosine-dimethylglycine methyltransferase), showed 76% identity, 96% coverage of the O. prasinos DSYE sequence and 98% coverage of the M. commoda sequence, an E-value of 1.26 × 10⁻¹⁶, and a bitscore of 460, supporting its candidacy as a DSYE-like gene in M. commoda.

Identification of eukaryotic BCCT-like homologs in experimental strains and phylogenetic analysis

Predicted peptide sequences from the genomes of algal strains used in this study, as well as closely related species (Bathycoccus prasinos RCC1105, O. tauri RCC745 [OTH95], O. lucimarinus CCE9901 [CCMP2514], Ostreococcus sp. RCC809 [RCC141], Micromonas sp. RCC299, Micromonas sp. CCMP1545, Micromonas ASP10_1a, Thalassiosira pseudonana CCMP1335, Thalassiosira oceanica CCMP1005, and P. tricornutum CCAP 1055/1; Table S1), were downloaded from public databases (NCBI GenBank, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/; JGI Integrated Microbial Genomes, https://img.jgi.doe.gov/). BCCT carriers in these genomes were identified by querying predicted proteins using profile Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) for the BCCT family (PF02028). The HMM, built from 73 UniProt reference sequences78, was applied with HMMER (v3.3)79 using default parameters and the model-specific gathering threshold. For genomes lacking peptide annotations, genes were predicted with AUGUSTUS (v3.4)80.

To expand representation among eukaryotes for phylogenetic analyses, especially algae, previously reported eukaryotic (n = 57) and prokaryotic (n = 19) BCCT homologs35 were retrieved from NCBI GenBank and Reefgenomics81. Additional homologs were identified by querying algal genomes and transcriptomes available in the EukProt82 and MMETSP25 databases using the same HMM approach. Hits with E-values < 1 × 10⁻⁶ were retained and duplicates across datasets were removed using a custom Python script. To ensure quality, sequences shorter than 300 amino acids were excluded. Redundant sequences (> 95% identity within a genome/transcriptome) were collapsed, retaining only the best-scoring hit. Identical sequences from unrelated organisms were discarded as likely contaminants. Completeness of BCCT domains was assessed via transmembrane helix (TM) prediction using DeepTMHMM (v1.0)67; sequences with <8 or >17 predicted TMs were excluded or manually curated using InterProScan83 in GeneiousPrime® (https://www.geneious.com). A final set of 485 putative eukaryotic BCCT-like (mean ± SD = 765 ± 178 aa) and 19 prokaryotic BCCTs (mean ± SD = 581 ± 70 aa) were retained.

Phylogenetic analysis and designation as PaDT

All BCCT-like identified sequences were aligned with MAFFT-L-INS-i (v7.402)84, trimmed with trimAL (v1.4.1)85 with the –gappyout option, and manually curated using AliView (v1.27)86. Sequences with ≥50% alignment gaps were removed. Phylogenies were reconstructed with IQ-TREE (v2.0.6)87 using the LG + F + R10 substitution model (determined via ModelFinder88) and 1000 ultrafast bootstrap iterations.

Prokaryotic BCCTs were used as the outgroup for rooting purposes. Initial trees revealed several sequences clustering with prokaryotes; these were excluded as probable contaminants. One sequence with excessive branch length was also removed. The final dataset (n = 418 eukaryotic BCCT) was re-aligned and analysed as described above. Because some of the algae harboring these eukaryotic BCCT-like sequences exhibited DMSP uptake in experiments, we refer to the curated sequences as putative algal DMSP transporters (PaDT) to distinguish them from their prokaryotic homologs. We note that not all PaDT sequences have been functionally validated.

Distribution of eukaryotic PaDT in Tara Oceans datasets

To assess the distribution of eukaryotic PaDT genes and transcripts in marine environments, a custom HMM profile was built from the newly identified PaDT sequences using HMMER (v3.3). This profile was used to search the Tara Oceans OM-RGC.v289 and MATOU90 datasets via the online webserver Ocean Gene Atlas91 with default settings and an E-value of 1 × 10−30. Candidate sequences were further filtered by BLASTp to retain only those with ≥40% amino acid identity and ≥70% alignment coverage to reference sequences, yielding 3412 and 3845 putative PaDT peptides in OM-RGC.v2 and MATOU, respectively.

For taxonomic assignment, the retrieved peptides were aligned to the PaDT reference alignment using PaPaRa (v2.5)92 with default parameters, and placed on the reference phylogeny using EPA-ng (v0.3.8)93 and raxml-ng (v0.9.0)94, with the amino acid substitution model predicted with IQ-TREE for the reference tree. Branch lengths of placed sequences were compared to the maximum pairwise distances in the reference tree; none exceeded this threshold and all were retained. To ensure placement confidence, likelihood weight ratios (LWRs) were extracted from the resulting jplace files. Sequences for which the top placements did not account for ≥90% of cumulative LWR were excluded using gappa (v0.7.1)95. Final taxonomic annotations were assigned based on placement positions and the taxonomy of reference sequences.

In both metagenomic and metatranscriptomic datasets, the relative abundance of DMSP transporters genes and transcripts is expressed as percentage of mapped reads. Finally, the latitudinal distribution of PaDT transcripts was compared with that of climatological concentrations of total DMSP derived from satellite data96 for the period 1998–2012 (DMSPt_sat). DMSPt_sat estimates co-located to Tara Oceans metatranscriptome sampling stations (by latitude and longitude rounded to the nearest degree) were taken from the climatological month before, during, and after each sampling event. This resulted in three DMSPt_sat values per metatranscriptome sample.

Statistics

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed in R (v4.4.3)97. Phylogenetic analyses and tree visualization were conducted using the “ape” (v5.8−1)98, “ggtree” (v3.14.0)99, “ggtreeExtra” (v1.16.0)100 and “ggnewscale” (v.0.5.2)101 packages. Geospatial maps were generated using the “rnaturalearth” (v1.2.0)102 and “ggpattern” (v1.3.1)103 packages. Satellite-derived total DMSP concentrations were extracted from NetCDF files using the “RNetCDF” (v2.11−1)104 package. General data handling and visualization were performed using the “tidyverse” (v2.0.0)105 package.

Significant differences between the two size fractions (> 1.2 µm vs 0.2–1.2 µm) within each studied station and between daytime and night-time incubations (Fig. S4) were assessed using a two-sided Welch’s t-tests. Latitudinal patterns (Fig. 6) were evaluated using a Kruskal–Wallis test (p < 0.05 considered significant), followed by pairwise two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction.
