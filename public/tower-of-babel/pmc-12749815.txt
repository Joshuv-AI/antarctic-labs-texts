Synergistic inhibition of CHK1 and MUS81 to combat replication stress resistance in high-risk neuroblastoma
Authors: Elien Hilgert, Christophe Van Neste, Sarah-Lee Bekaert, Fien Martens, Suzanne Vanhauwaert, Ellen Sanders, Peter Verstraelen, Martijn Risseeuw, Ellen M Westerhout, Mark A Grannetia, Serge Van Calenbergh, Nadine Van Roy, Winnok H De Vos, Rob W F Wolthuis, Frank Speleman, Lisa Depestel, Kaat Durinck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-risk neuroblastoma is a pediatric tumor that originates from immature sympathetic neuroblasts and is characterized by a low mutational burden. Nevertheless, it frequently presents with highly recurrent chromosomal imbalances, including MYCN amplification and gain of chromosome 17q. Highly proliferative cancers, such as neuroblastoma, exhibit significant DNA replication stress, rendering tumor cells dependent on ATR-CHK1 signalling and DNA damage repair pathways. We previously hypothesized that gene dosage effects resulting from 17q copy number alterations of non-mutated genes involved in replication stress resistance could offer novel therapeutic opportunities. To identify critical candidate genes and pathways driving high-risk neuroblastoma, we performed an integrated bioinformatics analysis, and identified the BRIP1 gene, encoding the FANCJ protein, as top-ranked 17q candidate. FANCJ is involved in multiple processes that alleviate replication stress. In the absence of specific FANCJ-targeting compounds, we evaluated the phenotypic and molecular effects of pharmacological inhibition of the MUS81 endonuclease, which functions downstream of FANCJ in restarting stalled replication forks. When combined with CHK1 inhibition, we observed synergistic effects on neuroblastoma cell growth and survival, supporting further development of on-target MUS81 inhibitors for in vivo preclinical testing and future clinical trials aimed at overcoming replication stress resistance in high-risk neuroblastoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29898-z.Keywords: Neuroblastoma, 17q gain, Replication stress, FANCJ, MUS81, Combination therapySubject terms: Cancer genetics, Targeted therapies, Paediatric research, Preclinical research, DNA damage checkpoints, DNA damage response, Paediatric cancer, Stalled forks

Introduction

Neuroblastoma (NB) is a pediatric tumor arising from immature sympathetic neuroblasts. These tumors are characterized by a remarkable genetic and clinical heterogeneity, with high relapse rates in high-risk cases despite intensive multimodal treatment1,2. For these high-risk cases, there is an urgent medical need for effective, durable, and less toxic treatments. Given the low mutational burden of NB, precision treatment tailored to specific molecular lesions is challenging. In contrast to this low mutation frequency, NBs often present with highly recurrent focal segmental chromosomal alterations, including amplification of the MYCN oncogene (located on the distal chromosome 2p arm), which is present in 45% of high-risk NB cases, and associated with a poor prognosis3,4.

Approximately 80% of all high-risk tumors (both MYCN-amplified (MNa) and non-amplified (MNna)) share segmental 17q gain5–8. The biological contribution of 17q gain to tumor development and/or tumor phenotype is supported through different observations. First, in MYCN-overexpressing NB mouse tumors, gain of chromosome 11, syntenic to human 17q, occurs in approximately 25% of tumors9. Second, we showed that human embryonic stem cells with 17q gain exhibited enhanced growth capacity compared to the normal parental stem cells under conditions of replication stress8. Third, Saldana-Guerrero et al. revealed the impact of 17q gain on gene regulatory networks, possibly favoring an early Schwann cell precursor signature, suggesting that this alteration reinforces a developmental arrest at this immature cell stage, which makes it more prone to malignant transformation10. Altogether, a deeper exploration of the role of 17q gain implicated genes is warranted.

For several genes located on 17q, a functional role in NB has already been reported. For example, we previously established TBX2 as a transcription factor which is part of the adrenergic core regulatory circuitry and critically implicated in NB growth and survival11. Of further interest, two other 17q genes, BRCA1 and ALYREF, were shown to fulfill a key role in several processes involved in replication stress12–14. Replication stress is caused by slowing down, stalling, or even collapse of replication forks due to various factors interfering with replication fork progression, including dNTP depletion, DNA damage, secondary DNA structures such as stable G-quadruplexes, and replication-transcription conflicts such as R-loops15–17. We previously revealed a key function for RRM2, encoded on the frequently gained 2p region, in dampening of MYCN-induced replication stress levels18. Further support for the important role of replication stress signalling in NB came from the work of the Palmer lab, which recently provided compelling evidence for a direct connection between ALK activation and ATR/CHK1 phosphorylation19,20. A strong CHK1 dependency in NB was already noted through an early siRNA screen by the Maris team21. Later, Blosser et al. identified NB as the most sensitive cancer type to the CHK1 inhibitor Prexasertib within a pan-cancer cell line panel22. While CHK1 inhibition alone is initially effective, especially in MNa NB, its sustained impact is often limited due to activation of compensatory DNA repair pathways. Combination strategies are therefore required to overcome resistance mechanisms achieve durable responses. Given the important role of replication stress signalling in high-risk NB and the large size of the commonly gained 17q segments, we hypothesized that additional copy number affected genes could contribute to dampening of oncogene-induced replication stress, supporting proliferation of early transformed sympathetic neuroblasts and offering further potential for strong synergistic combinatorial drugging with CHK1 inhibitors23,24.

Fanconi anemia complementation group J (FANCJ, also known as BRCA1-interacting protein 1 (BRIP1) or BRCA1-associated C-terminal helicase 1 (BACH1), and hereafter referred to as FANCJ for both gene and protein), has been implicated in multiple functions at replication forks including (1) CtIP/TOPBP1/RPA-mediated ATR activation; (2) DNA helicase unwinding of G-quadruplexes which hinder the incoming DNA polymerase causing replication fork stalling and R-loop formation; (3) involvement in mismatch repair and interstrand cross-link repair through interaction with MLH1 and FANCD2; (4) replication fork protection through interaction with AND-1 and BLM; and (5) BRCA1-binding-mediated homologous recombination during replication fork restart25–31. The Janscak team recently reported FANCJ binding to MLH1/PMS1 as an upstream event in a replication stress controlling pathway implicating the DDX17 helicase and MUS81 endonuclease to ensure replication fork restart32. The MUS81 endonuclease activity is tightly controlled throughout the cell cycle to prevent premature cleavage of replication intermediates, which would otherwise cause DNA breaks and compromise genome stability33. Moreover, MUS81 is an interesting target for drug development, given that it acts both upstream in ATR activation and downstream at the replication fork34.

In this study, we performed an integrative bioinformatics analysis of DNA copy number data, gene expression data, and risk classification data from a large cohort of primary NBs and identified FANCJ as the top-ranked 17q candidate in both MNa and MNna high-risk NB. We show that stable FANCJ knockout negatively affects NB cell growth and increases the sensitivity to CHK1 inhibition. In the absence of drugs directly targeting FANCJ and given its recently reported role in supporting MUS81-mediated replication fork restart, we subsequently performed an in vitro evaluation of the phenotypic and molecular impact of two available experimental MUS81 inhibitors and observed synergism for both compounds in combination with CHK1 inhibition32. We propose the FANCJ-MUS81 axis as a target for further drug development to allow more in-depth in vivo preclinical analysis to pave the way for further therapeutic development.

Results

17q dosage-driven candidate dependencies in NB are enriched for genes implicated in cell cycle control, replication stress, and DNA damage response

We previously explored a publicly available dataset with DNA copy number alterations of 556 high-risk primary NB tumors to determine frequently gained and lost chromosomal alterations3. We prioritized an in-depth analysis of genes located on chromosome 17q, given that 17q gain was the most frequently occurring chromosomal imbalance, observed in approximately 80% of both MNa and MNna NB cases.

Based on the data mining approach of Łastowska et al., we scrutinized a publicly available dataset of 219 primary high-risk NB tumors (GSE85047)35 and calculated the individual frequency of each gene on 17q gain, for MNa and MNna patients separately (Supplementary Table S1, column Penetrance), which was then prioritized by two consecutive gene ranking procedures36. The first ranking was based on a linear model assessing the impact of gene expression changes driven by copy number alterations (Supplementary Table S1, column Dosagelm). The second ranking evaluated the association between gene expression levels and patient risk classification (Supplementary Table S1, column Risklm). These two rankings were then integrated (Supplementary Table S1, column Prod_pct_rank) and the combined ranking scores of the top 50 genes in the MNa and MNna groups were displayed (Fig. 1a).

A total of 35 genes were common amongst the top 50 ranked 17q genes from the MNa and MNna groups. Interestingly, these genes are mainly involved in processes related to DNA damage repair, replication stress, and cell cycle checkpoint regulatory processes (Fig. 1b). The top-ranked gene in both MNa as well as MNna groups is the DNA helicase FANCJ (Fig. 1a)25–32. Further analysis of pan-cancer gene expression data showed higher FANCJ expression in NB compared to other cancer entities (Fig. 1c). Fancj gene expression increased substantially during TH-MYCN-driven murine NB development compared to normal matching murine sympathetic ganglia (Fig. 1d)38.

Additionally, high FANCJ mRNA expression was linked to worse overall and event free survival outcomes in NB (Fig. 1e-f) and displayed elevated expression in high-risk NB cases carrying 17q gain, compared to low-risk cases, irrespective of the MYCN status (Fig. 1g)38.

FANCJ knockout NB cells have decreased growth capacity

Given the top-ranked position of FANCJ in both MNa and MNna high-risk NB cohorts, we performed transient FANCJ knockdown experiments in IMR-32, SK-N-BE(2)-C (MNa, 17q gain), and SH-SY5Y (MNna, 17q gain) NB cells using 2 independent siRNAs, but could not observe an impact on NB cell growth (Supplementary Fig. S1). Moreover, under conditions of further enhanced replication stress through exposure to low-dose CHK1 inhibition using Prexasertib, no differential impact was noted between siRNA-mediated FANCJ knockdown compared to control-transfected cells (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Given that previous studies already suggested that complete depletion of FANCJ protein induces more robust phenotypes32,39,40, we successfully generatedboth full and partial (81%) FANCJ knockout models in SK-N-BE(2)-C cells, whereas in SH-SY5Y cells only partial (62%) knockout could be achieved (Supplementary Fig. S2). While the partial FANCJ knockout models displayed similar growth rates comparable to the controls, a significant growth reduction was observed in the full FANCJ knockout model (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. S2). Moreover, only full FANCJ knockout resulted in p53 pathway activation, illustrated by upregulation of CDKN1A (encoding the p21 response protein) (Fig. 2b-c, Supplementary Fig. S2). Increased Prexasertib sensitivity was observed in full FANCJ knockout SK-N-BE(2)-C and partial FANCJ knockout SH-SY5Y cells, while partial FANCJ knockout SK-N-BE(2)-C cells responded similarly to controls (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. S2). Together these data support our hypothesis that FANCJ loss below a critical threshold compromises NB by increasing the dependency on ATR-CHK1 signalling.

Combined inhibition of MUS81 and CHK1 acts synergistically in NB

Given the lack of FANCJ inhibitors and the upstream function of FANCJ in MUS81-dependent replication fork restart, we evaluated MUS81 as a possible drug target. Interestingly, the structure-specific MUS81 requires interaction with the essential meiotic structure-specific endonucleases 1 or 2 (EME1, EME2) for its endonuclease activity, with EME1 being also one of the common top 50 ranked 17q genes (Fig. 1a). Recently, a high-throughput drug screen identified the Dynasore analogue Dyngo-4a as a novel small molecule inhibitor of the MUS81-EME1/2 interaction41. Initially, Dyngo-4a was identified as dynamin inhibitor, a key protein required for membrane fission in clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Inhibiting dynamin can trigger replication stress and DNA damage by interfering with cellular responses to growth factors and stress signals, as well as mitochondrial fission42. Indeed, Dyngo-4a treatment negatively affected cell confluence in IMR-32 and SH-SY5Y NB cells at low micromolar concentrations, whereas the non-cancerous immortalized retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells were affected only at much higher drug concentrations (Fig. 3a).

The endonuclease activity of MUS81 is strictly regulated during the cell cycle by CHK1 and WEE1 kinases, as they restrain MUS81 activity during S-phase to avoid inappropriate cleavage of replication intermediates and subsequent genome instability33,43–47. Moreover, MUS81 promotes R-loop-induced activation of the ATR-CHK1 pathway, highlighting its interplay with ATR-CHK1 signalling in DNA damage repair and replication fork restart34,48,49. Therefore, we tested combined MUS81 and CHK1 inhibition using respectively Dyngo-4a and Prexasertib using a broad concentration range and observed synergistic drug interactions in NB cells (Bliss Independent (BI) scores = 0.39 (IMR-32); 0.64 (NGP); 0.21 (CLB-GA)) (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. S3). Combined MUS81-CHK1 inhibition strongly reduced cell confluency and induced apoptosis in NB cells, while not affecting the non-cancerous RPE cells using the same concentration ranges (Fig. 3c-d, Supplementary Fig. S3). As expected, the strong reduction in cell confluency upon combination treatment was associated with a marked S-phase arrest (Fig. 3e).

Furthermore, in line with the stronger Prexasertib sensitivity observed in the FANCJ knockout NB models, the synergy between MUS81 and CHK1 inhibition was observed at lower Prexasertib concentrations in FANCJ knockout NB cells, suggesting enhanced reliance on the MUS81-CHK1 pathway upon FANCJ loss (Fig. 4a-b).

In line with the findings of Domínguez-Kelly et al. that DNA damage repair depends on MUS81 in the absence of WEE1, we also observed a synergistic interaction between Dyngo-4a and the WEE1 inhibitor MK1775 in NB cells, while RPE cells were not affected using the same concentration ranges of the combination treatment (BI = 0.29 (IMR-32); BI = 0.01 (RPE)) (Supplementary Fig. S4)44.

Recently, Collie et al. performed a fragment-based hit screening to identify novel MUS81 small molecule inhibitors and discovered compound 23, further denoted as MUS81in1, with a favourable in vitro pharmacological profile50. Similarly to Dyngo-4a, NB cells showed strong sensitivity for MUS81 inhibition with MUS81in1 (Fig. 5a). Moreover, we confirmed a strong reduction in NB cell growth and induction of apoptosis upon combined MUS81 and CHK1 inhibition using MUS81in1 and Prexasertib, respectively, while not affecting RPE cells using the same concentration ranges (BI score = 0.40 (IMR-32); BI = −0.01 (RPE)) (Fig. 5b-d). Nonetheless, Dyngo-4a shows higher potency in NB cells than MUS81in1, with a 20-fold difference in IC50 concentrations between the two inhibitors.

Combined pharmacological inhibition of the MUS81-CHK1 axis downregulates DNA damage repair and replication stress resistance-associated gene signatures

To gain deeper mechanistic insight into the observed phenotypic drug responses, we performed transcriptome profiling using bulk RNA sequencing of IMR-32 NB cells following 24 h exposure to either the combination treatment (1 µM Dyngo-4a and 2 nM Prexasertib) or Dyngo-4a as a single drug using the IC50 concentration calculated at 48 h post-treatment (2.26 µM). Differential gene expression analysis revealed 593 upregulated and 531 downregulated genes for the combination treatment, and 1,881 upregulated and 1,895 downregulated genes for the Dyngo-4a IC50 treatment (adjusted p-value < 0.05; Supplementary Table S2). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct transcriptional changes in the Dyngo-4a/Prexasertib combination treatment compared to Dyngo-4a IC50. In contrast, both single agents at the concentrations used in the combination treatment clustered closely with DMSO controls (Supplementary Fig. S5).

For both Dyngo-4a IC50 and the Dyngo-4a/Prexasertib combination treatment, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed upregulation of the p53 pathway and TNFα signalling via NFkβ, as well as downregulation of MYC, E2F, and G2-M checkpoint targets (Supplementary Fig. S5). Treatment with the IC50 of Dyngo-4a significantly disrupted the cellular energy metabolism and mitochondrial function, including the assembly of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex and oxidative phosphorylation. It also impaired mitochondrial catalytic activities, such as NADH dehydrogenase and oxidoreductase functions. Moreover, this treatment regimen induced an upregulation of cellular stress response pathways, including the unfolded protein response (UPR) and Hedgehog signalling, compared to the combinatorial drugging. These findings suggest that the Dyngo-4a IC50 drug concentration effectively targets the expected dynamin-related processes, with disruption triggering activation of the UPR to restore endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis (Fig. 6a-b, Supplementary Fig. S5)51. Notably, combining a lower dose of Dyngo-4a with CHK1 inhibition shifts the molecular responses towards pronounced dysregulation of cell cycle and cell division processes, particularly the G1-S and G2-M checkpoints, in line with the observed S-phase arrest in the combination treatment (Fig. 3e). The combination treatment also impairs DNA replication and repair pathways, including double-strand break repair and helicase activity, while reducing nuclease activity (Fig. 6a-c).

Immunoblotting confirmed increased replication stress, DNA damage and apoptosis upon combined Dyngo-4a/Prexasertib treatment, shown by upregulated protein levels of phosphorylated CHK1 at the ATR activation site (pCHK1S345), γH2AX and cleaved PARP respectively, together with the expected concomitant loss of CHK1 auto-phosphorylation (pCHK1S296) due to Prexasertib treatment (Fig. 6d). Furthermore, using quantitative immunofluorescence microscopy, we confirmed elevated DNA damage by increased γH2AX levels after Dyngo-4a/Prexasertib combination treatment (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Discussion

In this study, we developed a bioinformatics pipeline to integrate DNA copy number alterations, gene expression, and patient risk classification to identify novel dosage sensitive putative dependency genes located within the commonly gained chromosome 17q segment in high-risk NB. This culminated in the identification of FANCJ, encoded by the BRIP1 gene, as top-ranked 17q candidate in both MYCN-amplified and MYCN non-amplified high-risk tumors25–32. Full knockout of FANCJ showed significant reduction in growth capacity and p53 pathway activation, whereas partial FANCJ depletion did not reproduce these effects, suggesting that residual protein levels could blunt phenotypic effects or compensatory activity of other highly expressed proteins interacting in replication stress resistance. Indeed, at least some of the FANCJ functions can also be compensated by other helicases such as DDX11 and DHX3639,52. In this context, we noted that the list of top-ranked 17q genes included several RNA helicases (DHX40, DDX42, HELZ) or genes reported to function in R-loop processing (FTSJ3, INTS2), thus potentially also involved in mediating R-loops at stalled replication forks and replication stress control53–55. Further research is required to deeper explore the functional role of FANCJ and possible interaction with other helicases in the biology of high-risk NB.

It was recently reported that FANCJ is a key upstream mediator in resolving R-loops at stalled replication forks by cooperating with specific mismatch repair proteins to bind G-quadruplexes, recruit the DDX17 helicase for R-loop unwinding, and promote transcription and replication restart via a MUS81 structure-specific endonuclease-mediated pathway32. MUS81 has previously been proposed as promising drug target for cancer cells56–58. Given the lack of specific FANCJ inhibitors, we evaluated the sensitivity of NB cells to two different MUS81 inhibitors, Dyngo-4a and MUS81in1. Dyngo-4a was initially identified as dynamin inhibitor and subsequently also shown to inhibit MUS81 endonuclease activity by disrupting its interaction with EME1/2. Moreover, a recent study revealed that Dyngo-4a promotes neuronal features and enhances the differentiation of mouse NB cells through activation of AKT/ERK59. MUS81in1 was discovered by structure-based optimization of fragment hits that bind in the catalytic site of the enzyme. We evaluated the effect of both MUS81 inhibitors in combination with sublethal CHK1 inhibitor dosing and showed synergistic drug interaction in both MNa and MNna NB cells. In addition, FANCJ knockout resulted in increased sensitivity to CHK1 inhibition using Prexasertib and induced synergism between MUS81 and CHK1 inhibition at lower Prexasertib concentrations, suggesting that FANCJ loss enhances the dependence of NB tumors on CHK1 signalling and increases the susceptibility to replication stress–targeting combinations, including those involving MUS81.

Given that Dyngo-4a exposure resulted in a stronger phenotypic impact compared to MUS81in1, we selected Dyngo-4a to explore the molecular consequences of single drug inhibition and combinatorial drugging with Prexasertib using bulk RNA sequencing. Monotherapy with the IC50 of Dyngo-4a primarily influenced dynamin-associated processes at mitochondria, consistent with its established role in mitochondrial metabolism and membrane fission through clathrin-mediated endocytosis42. In contrast, combined treatment of Dyngo-4a and Prexasertib led to a significant reduction in NB cell confluence, accompanied by induction of apoptosis, DNA damage, and replication stress. At the molecular level, combined Dyngo-4a/Prexasertib treatment points at a reduction in gene sets implicated in nuclease and helicase activity, DNA replication initiation, and DNA damage repair as well as an upregulation of TNFα signalling via NFkβ and cell cycle related hallmarks, collectively underscoring the MUS81-targeting activity of Dyngo-4a.

Previous studies indicated that depletion of the cell cycle regulators CHK1 or WEE1 increases dependency on MUS81 for the cleavage of stalled replication forks44,45. The WEE1 kinase is a downstream CHK1 effector and functionally implicated under conditions of replication stress to mitigate premature entry into mitosis, thereby allowing replication fork restart, DNA repair, and completion of DNA replication. We therefore also tested the impact of combined pharmacological inhibition of MUS81 (using Dyngo-4a) with WEE1 inhibitor MK1775 and observed synergistic drug interaction, similar as for combinatorial drugging with CHK1. Additionally, a recent study by Malacaria et al. demonstrated that MUS81-dependent replication fork processing is also controlled by CHK2, indicating that dual inhibition of MUS81 and CHK2 may also hold promise for development of new therapeutic strategies60.

In conclusion, we identified FANCJ as top-ranked 17q dosage sensitive candidate dependency gene in high-risk NB. Pharmacological targeting of the FANCJ-MUS81 axis shows promising therapeutic potential, particularly in combination with inhibitors of the replication stress response kinases CHK1 and WEE1, demonstrating robust and strong synergistic effects. Our findings underscore the need to advance the development of in vivo applicable MUS81 inhibitors to enable further pre-clinical in-depth exploration of the therapeutic potential of this novel drug combination to combat high-risk NB.

Materials and methods

Ranking analysis of 17q dosage-driven candidate genes

Dosage-driven candidate dependency genes were ranked using the Neuroblastoma Research Consortium (NRC, GSE85047)35 dataset, which includes Copy Number Variation (CNV), gene expression, and clinical metadata. CNVs were classified using thresholds established by Depuydt et al. (GSE103123)61: gains were defined by a mean CNV ratio ≥ 0.15, losses by a mean CNV ratio ≤ −0.25, and normal CNV ratios fell between these thresholds.

The intersection of sample IDs across CNV, expression, and clinical datasets yielded 219 samples with overlapping data for analysis. Expression data initially contained probe-level identifiers, which were mapped to corresponding gene symbols. Probes that could not be mapped to known genes were excluded; when multiple probes mapped to the same gene, only the first occurrence was retained. The expression data were normalized using L2 normalization across genes to standardize expression values.

Metadata were categorized into four groups based on INSS stage and MYCN amplification: ‘highstage_amp’ (INSS stage 3 or 4; MYCN amplification), ‘highstage_sc’ (INSS stage 3 or 4; no MYCN amplification), ‘lowstage_amp’ (INSS stage 1, 2, or 4 S; MYCN amplification), and ‘lowstage_sc’ (INSS stage 1, 2, or 4 S; no MYCN amplification).

For each gene, a linear model was fitted using normalized gene expression as the dependent variable, and CNV status (gain, loss, or normal) and clinical metadata as independent variables. The output of each model was saved as a CSV file and used to calculate Dosagelm and Risklm scores for ranking.

A gene ranking was then generated, for genes located within significantly altered regions of the 17q chromosomal arm based on three biological parameters, which were combined to produce the final ranking: (1) Penetrance score (Penetrance) – calculated using a function which determines how frequently a chromosomal arm is altered across patients. This analysis is based on a combined dataset of 556 high-risk NB patients from Depuydt et al.61. Segmentation analysis of the patient-derived profiles was performed, classifying genomic regions as gains or losses based on log2 CNV thresholds. Each altered region was mapped to its corresponding genes using genomic coordinates (hg38). The penetrance (frequency) of each CNV was calculated by dividing the number of patients exhibiting the alteration by the total patient count. (2) Dosage sensitivity score (Dosagelm) – the correlation between copy number and gene expression within the NRC cohort, and (3) Risk classification score (Risklm) – the correlation between gene alterations and risk category (based on metadata classification). Each score was normalized using percentile ranking (percentile rank = rank/total count), and the normalized scores were multiplied to compute a final combined ranking score for each gene.

A detailed description, including scripts and files, is available in the following GitHub repositories: https://github.com/CVNResearch/nb_ranking and https://github.com/PPOLLabGhent/NB_Ranking.

Cell cultures

The human NB cell lines IMR-32, SH-SY5Y, NGP, and CLB-GA were grown in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS, PAN biotech (P30-3306)), penicillin/streptomycin (100 IU/mL), and L-glutamine (2 mM). The SK-N-BE(2)-C and SH-SY5Y cell lines used for FANCJ knockout were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FCS (Serana (S-FBS-MX-015)), penicillin/streptomycin (100 IU/mL), L-glutamine (2 mM), and 1% minimal essential medium non-essential amino acids (MEM NEAA). The human retinal pigmented epithelial (RPE) cell line was grown in DMEM: Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12) supplemented with 10% FCS (PAN biotech (P30-3306)), penicillin/streptomycin (100 IU/mL), and L-glutamine (2 mM). All cell lines were cultured in 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C in plastic culture plates. Short Tandem Repeat genotyping was used to validate cell line authenticity prior to performing the described experiments and screening for mycoplasma contamination occurred routinely on the cells. The origin and MYCN status of the used cell lines are provided in Supplementary Table S3, and all products used for cell cultures are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

siRNA-mediated knockdown

Cells were transfected using the Neon transfection system (MPK10096) with small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) towards FANCJ (s38384, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #4392420 (siFANCJ-84), and L-010587-00-0005, Thermo Fisher Scientific (FANCJ-pool)). As negative control, a non-targeting control (NTC) siRNA (Dharmacon, D-001810-10-05) was used. For RNA and protein analysis, cells were seeded in a T-25 flask at a density of 1.5 × 106 cells per flask and harvested at 48 h post transfection. Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, collected using scrapers and centrifugated for 5 min at 1,000 x g, 4 °C. This was followed by a wash in PBS and again centrifuged for 5 min at 1,000 x g, 4 °C. For proliferation analysis, 3,500 cells/well were seeded in a 384-well plate and analysed using the IncuCyte™ Live-Cell Imaging System, which captured images at regular intervals over a period of 100 h. These images were analysed using the IncuCyte™ Software through assessing cell proliferation by measuring the occupied area (percentage confluence) in the images over time.

CRISPR-Cas9 cell editing

In order to create FANCJ knockout NB cell models, 1 × 104 SK-N-BE(2)-C and SH-SY5Y cells were transfected twice with a LNP-like complex of mRNA-Cas9 and sgRNA. Briefly, the RNA mixture consisting of 32.47 nM mRNA-Cas9 (Trilink, CleanCap® Cas9 mRNA) and 499.97 nM sgRNA targeting FANCJ or OR10A7 (Synthego, CRISPRevolution sgRNA EZ kit - modified; AGAUUACUAGAGAGCUCCGG (FANCJ) or AGAAGAGGGACACCACAAUG (OR10A7, negative control)) was added together with in-vivo-jetRNA®+ (Polyplus) in 1:1 ratio (µgRNA:µLreagent) in RNA-buffer for a total volume of 2.5 µL per well. Single colonies were picked manually under the microscope and after 2–3 weeks of clonal expansion, genomic DNA was harvested by DirectPCR lysis reagent (Takara Bio) with Proteinase K for indel investigation. gDNA was used as a template for PCR amplification using the TerraPCR kit (Takara Bio). The primers sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S5. Samples were treated with ExoSAP PCR product cleanup reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analysed by Sanger sequencing. Indel analysis was run on a Miseq and analysed using the CRISPResso2.0 software (http://crispresso2.pinellolab.org)62. For proliferation analysis, 4,500 cells/well were seeded in a 384-well plate and analysed using the IncuCyte™ Live-Cell Imaging System as described above.

Compounds and chemicals

Dyngo-4a was obtained from Abcam (ab120689); Prexasertib was obtained from MedChemExpress (HY-18174 A); MK1775 was obtained from Selleck Chemicals (S1525). MUS81in1 (HY-163699, MedChemExpress), was synthesized according to published procedures50.

Measurement of cell viability for single-compound treatments

Cell lines were seeded in 384-well plates at a density of 3,500 (IMR-32, NGP), 4,500 (SH-SY5Y, CLB-GA), 2,000 (SK-N-BE(2)-C), or 250 (RPE) cells/well. After overnight adherence, the compounds were added in a range of concentrations using the Tecan D300e Digital Dispenser. Cell proliferation was monitored for 100 h, using the IncuCyte™ Live-Cell Imaging System. Results were normalized to the vehicle control (< 1% DMSO), and inhibitory concentration values were calculated using GraphPad Prism Software (version 10.2.3). Data of 3 biological replicates was used to perform Biological Replicate (BR) analysis with the in-house developed HTSplotter tool63. Dose-response curve analysis was also conducted through GraphPad Prism Software using non-linear regression.

Combination treatment and synergism measurements

To identify synergism, cells were seeded in 384-well plates at the same densities as described earlier. After overnight adherence, a concentration range of the compounds was added in a combination matrix, using the Tecan D300e Digital Dispenser. Cell proliferation was monitored for 100 h, using the IncuCyte™ Live-Cell Imaging System. The HTSplotter tool was used to perform BR analysis and evaluate synergism using the Bliss Independent (BI) method63,64. After identifying the concentrations with the highest BI score, the same procedure as above was repeated, but in addition to the IncuCyte™ cell proliferation assay, apoptosis levels were measured at 48 h post drugging using the Caspase-Glo™ 3/7 Assay system (Promega). This protocol was adapted, adding 25 µL of reagent to 50 µL of cells in culture media. The growth curve plots were generated using GraphPad Prism Software. As for the apoptosis assay, the data was autoscaled to the DMSO control.

Cell cycle analysis

IMR-32 cells were seeded in T25 culture flasks at a density of 1.5 × 10⁶ cells per flask and allowed to adhere for 24 h. After this incubation, compounds or vehicle (DMSO) were added, followed by an additional 48-hour incubation. Cells were washed once with PBS before collection in PBS. During each of the following wash steps, cells were centrifuged at 1,000 x g for 5 min at 4 °C and supernatant was removed. Cells were washed once in PBS before fixation in 70% ethanol for at least 1 h on ice, then washed once with PBS and incubated in PBS with ribonuclease A (RNase A, 250 µg/mL) for 1 h at 37 °C. Propidium iodide (40 µg/mL) was added, and analysis was performed on a BD LSRFortessa™ flow cytometer using BD FACSDIVA™ software. The flow cytometry results were analysed by FlowJo™ v10.8 software (BD Life Sciences), following the cell cycle instructions.

Sample collection for RNA, protein, and cell cycle analysis

IMR-32 cells were seeded in T25 culture flasks at a density of 1.85 × 10⁶ cells per flask and allowed to adhere for 24 h. After this incubation, compounds or vehicle (DMSO) were added, followed by an additional 24-hour incubation. Cells were then harvested using a cell scraper and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell pellets were washed with PBS, centrifuged again under the same conditions, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

RNA isolation, analysis and sequencing

RNA isolation was performed using the miRNeasy kit from QIAGEN including an on-column deoxyribonuclease treatment, following manufacturer’s instructions. Concentrations were measured using NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was done according to the manufacturer’s guidelines using the iScript Advanced cDNA synthesis kit from Bio-Rad. The PCR mix included 5 ng of cDNA, 2.5 µL of SsoAdvanced SYBR RT-qPCR super mix (Bio-Rad), and 0.25 µL forward and reverse primers (to a final concentration of 250 nM; Integrated DNA Technologies). The RT-qPCR cycling analysis was conducted with a LightCycler 480 device (Roche). Gene expression levels were analysed using the qBasePlus software 3.2 (www.biogazelle.com). B2M, SDHA, UBC, and YHWAZ were used as reference genes and the expression levels were autoscaled to the control (NTC siRNA, sgOR10A7, or DMSO). The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

For RNA sequencing, RNA was isolated as described above. The Fragment analyser 12 capillary array was used to ensure proper RNA quality prior to profiling. A total of 200 ng of RNA was used as input for the library preparation with the QuantSeq 3′ mRNA library prep kit (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria) with umi barcoding according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The protocol was modified to use only half of the total solvent volumes, and amplification was done using 15 PCR cycles. The Kapa Library Quantification Kit for the LightCycler 480 (Roche) was used for RT-qPCR quantification of the libraries. Sequencing of the RNA-seq libraries was conducted on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform using the NextSeq 2000 P2 Reagents 100 Cycles single-end kit (Illumina (20100981)). Sample and read quality were evaluated with FastQC (version 0.11.7). The FASTQ files were aligned to the Homo sapiens reference genome (GRCh38.96) using STAR (version 2.7.2b). Post-alignment, read counts were generated and normalized with edgeR (version 3.36.0). PCA plotting confirmed the quality of the data with no outliers shown. Differential expression analysis was performed using the Limma-Voom pipeline (version 3.50.3) with four replicates per condition. Only genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered significant. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed on genes ordered according to the differential expression statistic (t) value. GSEA utilized gene sets from MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) and custom gene lists from this and previous study (Cell Cycle genesets65. All figures were generated using ggplot2 (version 3.5.1) and pheatmap (version 1.0.12) packages in R.

Protein extraction from cells

Cell pellets were lysed in cold RIPA buffer (0.5% w/v Sodium Deoxycholate, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM TrisHCl pH 7.5, 0.1% w/v SDS, 1% w/v NP-40) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche Molecular Biochemicals) by rotating at 4 °C for 1 h. The lysates were cleared and collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm in a microcentrifuge for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were measured using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The lysates were denatured by adding 5x Laemli denaturation buffer supplemented with β-mercaptoethanol (Merck Life Science) following 10 min incubation at 95 °C.

Western blot analysis

30 µg of protein samples were loaded on an SDS-PAGE gel with 10x Tris/Glycine/SDS buffer and run at 120 V for 1 h. Next, samples were blotted on nitrocellulose membranes by running at 100 V for 1 h in a buffer containing 10% 10x Tris/Glycine buffer and 20% methanol. Protein transfer confirmation was obtained by Ponceau staining. Subsequently, the membranes were blocked for 1 h in 5% non-fat dry milk or 5% BSA in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 detergent (TBST) followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Blots were washed three times for 5 min with TBST prior to incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Visualisation of the immunoblots was done using the enhanced chemiluminescence WestDura or WestFemto (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the Amersham™ Imager 680 (BIOKÉ). Prior to detection of additional proteins, blots were stripped using the Restore™ Stripping Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantification was conducted using ImageJ software. For each sample, the area under the intensity curve was normalised to the loading protein (GAPDH) and protein levels were calculated relative to the control (NTC siRNA, sgOR10A7, or DMSO). Products and antibodies used for Western blotting are provided in Supplementary Table S6 and Supplementary Table S7, respectively.

Simple Western analysis

Simple Western analyses were conducted with the Wes instrument (Bio-Techne)66 using the 12–230 kDa Wes Separation module and the EZ standard pack reagent 1 (Protein Simple) under denaturing conditions. Protein lysates were loaded at 0.4 µg/µL and antibodies were used for detection of FANCJ, Actin and Tubulin. Origin and dilutions of the antibodies can be found in Supplementary Table S8. Chemiluminescence peaks were created by the Compass Software for Simple Western and the area was determined using the gaussian fit method. Area of the corresponding peaks were normalized to those of the loading control (Actin or Tubulin) and protein levels were calculated relative to the NTC siRNA.

Immunostaining and fluorescence microscopy

IMR-32 cells were seeded at a density of 5000 cells per well in a 96-well plate for microscopy (CellVis P96-1.5P). After overnight adherence, the compounds were added for 24 h before cell fixation in 2% paraformaldehyde.

Permeabilization was done in blocking buffer (0.1% bovine serum albumin, 10% normal horse serum (Innovative Research IGHSSER) in PBS) with 1% Triton X-100, for 5 min. Primary rabbit polyclonal anti-γH2AX (Abcam ab2893) antibody was applied at a concentration of 1 µg/mL in blocking buffer. After overnight incubation at 4 °C followed by a PBS wash, the secondary donkey-anti-rabbit-Cy3 antibody was applied at a concentration of 1 µg/mL for 2 h. HCS CellMask Deep Red stain (Invitrogen H32721) was included with the secondary antibody (1/5000 dilution). A 10 min incubation with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 2.5 µg/mL) was followed by a final PBS wash.

Multichannel Z-stacks were acquired on a Nikon spinning disk confocal microscope (Ti2 with W1 disk), with a 40X air (NA 0.95) immersion objective and standard filters for DAPI, Cy3 and Cy5. Five wells with 30 images per well were recorded for each experimental condition, and cell segmentation was done with the FIJI script CellBlocks67. Briefly, nuclei were detected in the DAPI channel using the Stardist algorithm68. In the nuclear regions of interest, the average intensity in the γH2AX channel was measured. Resulting numerical data were plotted using GraphPad Prism Software (version 10.2.3), with one data point representing one image.
