Abstract
. Creating a cast of characters by attending to their relational dynamics is a critical aspect of most long-form storywriting. However, our formative study (N=14) reveals that writers struggle to envision new characters that could influence existing ones, to balance similarities and differences among characters, and to intricately flesh out their relationships. Based on these observations, we designed Constella, an LLM-based multi-agent tool that supports storywriters’ interconnected character creation process. Constella suggests related characters (FRIENDS DISCOVERY feature), reveals the inner mindscapes of several characters simultaneously (JOURNALS feature), and manifests relationships through inter-character responses (COMMENTS feature). Our 7–8 day deployment study with storywriters (N=11) shows that Constella enabled the creation of expansive communities composed of related characters, facilitated the comparison of characters’ thoughts and emotions, and deepened writers’ understanding of character relationships. We conclude by discussing how multi-agent interactions can help distribute writers’ attention and effort across the character cast.

1.
Introduction

“The single biggest mistake writers make when creating characters is that they think of the hero and all other characters as separate individuals. […] To create great characters, think of all your characters as part of a web in which each helps define the others”

(
Truby 2008
)
.

Characters are essential building blocks of fictional stories. While the heart of the story may rest in one central character, its pulse comes to life through many. A well-constructed ensemble not only propels the plot
(
Myers 2022
)
but also creates emotional depth and thematic resonance by allowing characters to challenge, support, and transform one another
(
McKee 2021
;
Truby 2008
;
Egri 1972
;
Myers 2022
)
. Thus, crafting an interwoven cast of characters is an important activity for all forms of storytelling—novels, plays, comics, films, and drama—but is especially crucial for full-length stories where the extended scope provides greater room to develop the lives of its characters
(
McKee 2021
)
. While this process is expressed through terms such as character orchestration
(
Egri 1972
)
, interconnected web
(
Truby 2008
)
, and cast formation
(
McKee 2021
)
, we refer to it as
interconnected character creation
in this research.

Acknowledging the importance of character creation, Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) researchers have developed computational tools to aid creative writers. One line of work has implemented chatbots embodying writer-defined personas, enabling users to iteratively shape and interact with their characters through free-form conversation
(
Schmitt and Buschek 2021
;
Qin et al. 2024
;
Qin et al. 2025
)
. While these works support some relational context (e.g., roleplaying), they primarily focus on single-character development. In contrast, we aim to support character cast creation, and to do so, we see an opportunity for leveraging Large Language Model-based Multi-Agents (LLM-MA).

LLM-MA are distinguished by their focus on a variety of agent profiles, interactions between agents, and their collective decision-making processes
(
Guo et al. 2024a
)
. Prior HCI research has leveraged LLM-MA to provide users with opinions from diverse viewpoints, finding applications in domains that require a multitude of expert suggestions
(
Park et al. 2023
)
, audiences
(
Park and Choi 2023
;
Choi et al. 2025
)
, or simulated users within social computing systems
(
Park et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. Although these works are not directly related to the storytelling context, they collectively underscore the potential of LLM-MA when used for interconnected character creation. As noted by storywriting literature,
“the most important step in creating your hero, as well as all other characters, is to connect and compare each to the others”

(
Truby 2008
)
. Thus, by enabling the simultaneous exploration of contrasting character perspectives, LLM-MA can empower writers to construct a complex, multilayered cast of characters.

To design an LLM-MA tool that supports storywriters’ interconnected character creation, we first sought to understand the challenges they face during this process and ground our system in the strategies they find effective for overcoming these difficulties. To this end, we conducted a formative study with 14 writers experienced in creating full-length fictional stories. We found that writers address key challenges through the following strategies: 1) To conceptualize new characters capable of influencing existing ones, they brainstorm a wide range of related characters. 2) To balance similarities and differences between characters, they imagine each character’s inner world and how each would respond uniquely to the same situation. 3) To flesh out character relationships, they often create moments of interaction regardless of the main plot to gain insight into their dynamics.

With these observations, we derived three design goals for an LLM-MA tool to support interconnected character creation: 1) Suggest diverse characters related to existing ones, 2) support the comparison and investigation of in-depth character identities, and 3) manifest character relationships through exploratory interactions. To translate these goals into concrete system features, we drew inspiration from interaction concepts found in social media (e.g.,

(
Meta Platforms, Inc. 2025a
;
Meta Platforms, Inc. 2025b
;
Reddit, Inc. 2025
;
Cyworld 2025
)
), as they provide effective means for designing social interactions among story characters that align with each design goal. Specifically, these platforms enable users to connect with individuals beyond their usual acquaintances through potential connection points, freely express differing or similar thoughts on topics of mutual interest, and display or reinforce relationships by reacting to others’ content. We anticipated that drawing on these affordances to simulate character interactions would intuitively facilitate our design goals by echoing everyday social behavior.

Thus, we propose Constella
1
1

1

Constella is short for constellation, underscoring that characters, like stars, gain new meaning as they connect, relate, and are viewed together.
, an LLM-MA tool that supports storywriters’ interconnected character creation through social media-inspired interactions (
Figure 1
). Constella enables writers to iteratively build characters and their relationships via dedicated character profiles. Upon creating a profile, the FRIENDS DISCOVERY feature helps writers uncover new characters linked to existing ones by generating their mini profiles. Writers can also use the JOURNALS feature to contrast the inner mindscapes of characters by generating their diary entries. Lastly, writers can elaborate upon character relationships as they respond to each other’s journal entries through the COMMENTS feature. Together, these features offer resources for writers to flexibly expand, deeply inspect, and tangibly concretize their character casts throughout their development process.

To explore how our tool integrates into storywriters’ practices, we conducted a 7–8 day deployment study with 11 writers. During the study, participants used the tool through tasks that included creating multiple characters, a backstory, scenes, and a story outline, all within the context of writing a full-length fictional story. The results show that Constella supported diverse approaches to interconnected character creation. It facilitated the construction of multiple worlds populated by distinct yet interlinked characters, supported the analysis of character differences and similarities that inspired new scenes, and enabled deeper exploration of character relationships by surfacing emotional tensions. Notably, our tool encouraged writers to focus on a broader range of characters, including those that might otherwise have remained underdeveloped. Constella was also perceived to play a complementary role in the co-creative process without compromising their sense of authorial control. We conclude by discussing the potential of LLM-MA to help writers distribute creative attention across the entire character cast and how AI-infused writing tools can be designed to support authorial agency.

This paper presents the following contributions:

(1)

Constella, an LLM-MA tool that assists storywriters in constructing character casts through LLM-MA.s

(2)

Empirical insights from a deployment study illustrating Constella’s integration with storywriters’ practices.

(3)

Design implications for AI-based writing support tools to help preserve authorial agency.

As full-length fictional stories typically feature an ensemble of characters, writing guides offer suggestions for developing characters in relation to one another

Truby 2008
;
Egri 1972
;
Myers 2022
;
Eastman 2021
)
. For instance, McKee
(
McKee 2010
)
uses the metaphor of a solar system, suggesting that writers imagine the main character as a star and other characters as orbiting planets, each positioned based on their influence on the protagonist and one another. He advises writers to begin by creating the central role, followed by the incremental development of supporting roles. In contrast, Truby
(
Truby 2008
)
stresses the importance of developing characters simultaneously, encouraging writers to draw continuous comparisons and connections among them. He argues that this process highlights each character’s individuality, allowing even supporting characters to become more distinct and meaningful alongside the protagonist.

While literature on storywriting underscores the value of creating characters in an interconnected manner, it tends to focus on general principles rather than providing comprehensive explanations of the real-world challenges writers face during interconnected character creation and the underlying causes of these difficulties. Furthermore, the degree to which these theoretical approaches reflect actual writing practices remains uncertain. As prior HCI research has highlighted the diversity of writers’ practices and the variability of their needs
(
Ippolito et al. 2022
;
Guo et al. 2024b
;
Gero et al. 2023
)
, we anticipated discrepancies between the formalized processes outlined in theory and the actual practices of writers in the wild. Thus, to design a tool that effectively supports storywriters, we determined it was essential to draw not only on theoretical insights but also to engage deeply with empirical evidence. Based on this background, we grounded the development of our tool through in-depth interviews with storywriters while using the previously cited writing guides as references for developing the interview questions.

2.2.
AI Support for Character Construction

In this section, we review LLM-based tools designed for storywriters, focusing on systems that support the creation of new characters or facilitate character interactions through agents that embody writer-defined personas.

Several works utilized Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate new characters
(
Mirowski et al. 2023
;
Chung and Kreminski 2024
;
Kreminski et al. 2024
;
Swanson et al. 2021
;
Sudowrite 2025
;
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Huot et al. 2024
)
, often drawing from other elements of the story to align with the writers’ overall vision and storytelling goals. For example,
Dramatron

(
Mirowski et al. 2023
)
allows users to first define a logline
3
3

3

A logline is
“a single sentence describing your entire story”

(
Russell 2000
)
, such as what the protagonist wants or desires and what is at stake
(
Bishop 2016
)
.
, which is then used to generate not only the characters but also the title and plot.
Patchview

(
Chung and Kreminski 2024
)
enables writers to create concepts, such as good and evil, and choose positions along these spectra, guiding the system to create appropriate worldbuilding elements, including characters. However, existing works do not explicitly focus on user-specified relational ties between characters as a primary axis for character generation, despite the central role they play in character development and plot progression
(
Varotsi 2019
;
Truby 2008
;
Myers 2022
;
Eastman 2021
;
Harvey 1970
)
.

Another line of work has aimed to enable storywriter-character interactions through chatbots designed to reflect writer-defined attributes
(
Schmitt and Buschek 2021
;
Qin et al. 2024
;
Google Creative Lab 2019
;
Biermann et al. 2022
;
Stark et al. 2023
)
. Most relatedly,
CharacterChat

(
Schmitt and Buschek 2021
)
first introduced the concept of
“progressive manifestation,”
where the writers gradually shape a chatbot into their character by adding new attributes through back-and-forth conversation.
CharacterMeet

(
Qin et al. 2024
)
extends this research by utilizing an LLM and implementing a conversation context, enabling writers to roleplay as other characters. However, with the exception of those created for children
(
Chung et al. 2025a
)
, implementations of agent-based character embodiments for storywriters
(
Schmitt and Buschek 2021
;
Qin et al. 2024
;
Google Creative Lab 2019
)
have mostly concentrated on single-character development.

While we focus on providing support for fictional storywriters, prior research in the domain of interactive storytelling has proposed tools
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Lu et al. 2025
;
Han et al. 2024
)
that utilize multiple character embodiments via LLM-MA. As the emergent nature of LLM-MA poses challenges for authors to steer the direction for interactive stories (e.g., games), these works focus on providing control mechanisms that mediate between the author’s intent, the unpredictability of LLM-based agents, and player-introduced actions. For example,
Storyverse

(
Wang et al. 2024
)
translates high-level plot outlines into characters’ action sequences during playtime, while the game player can also steer the unfolding events, such as by killing a character.

Beyond interactive storytelling, several works
(
Yu et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Huot et al. 2024
)
adapt LLM-MA for fictional storywriting, but their emphasis is on proposing automated story generation pipelines, thereby providing limited support for writers’ iterative and incremental character construction processes
(
Kreminski and Martens 2022
)
. For instance, Yu et al.’s work
(
Yu et al. 2025
)
assumes that detailed character personas and the plot outline are readily available and proceeds to generate inter-character simulations that produce the detailed text for the story. Other systems
(
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Huot et al. 2024
)
limit the writer’s contribution to supplying a seed storyline; from that point onward, agents automatically detail the plot and characters, multi-agent exchanges generate the dialogue, and a final agent combines them together into the complete narrative.

While these approaches demonstrate the potential of LLM-MA in storywriting, they primarily utilize LLM-MA simulations as either real-time, emergent narratives or as components within largely automated pipelines for complete story generation. In contrast, we focus on leveraging LLM-MA to support fictional storywriters in constructing their character casts, specifically through the design of scaffolding materials (e.g., journal entries and comments) rather than final story text. This design choice reflects our deliberate intent to uphold authorial agency by preserving the writers’ role in the expressive act of the writing process. In line with this focus, we investigate how our tool supports writers through a deployment study, which, to our knowledge, is the first research to offer empirical insights into how a character-based LLM-MA tool integrates into the real-world practices of fictional storywriters.

that offers open-ended resources for storywriters. Specifically, we provide resources in social media-inspired formats such as mini profiles, diaries, and comments, which likely encourage writers to iterate over, adapt, and interpret the generated content rather than directly copying and inserting it as-is. In doing so, we aim to create room for the author’s expressive intent to flow through the interpretation of underdetermined outcomes

Kreminski 2024

We focused on identifying their approaches to interconnected character creation, the challenges they face during this process, and the strategies they use to overcome these difficulties. Insights from this study informed the three design goals for our tool. The study process was approved by our university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB No. concealed for anonymity).

We recruited writers with prior experience in creating full-length fictional stories. Considering the domain of storytelling, we aimed to gather insights across diverse story types and genres. Furthermore, we aimed to diversify participants based on their total duration of creative writing experience to gain a comprehensive understanding from individuals relatively newer to storywriting to those with greater familiarity. Lastly, we recruited participants with and without formal creative writing education, as well as those with and without publication experience. This inclusive approach was informed by Gero et al.’s work
(
Gero et al. 2023
)
, which argues that formal education does not necessarily equate to expertise and that relying on publication experience as a metric for expertise could unnecessarily marginalize writers.

Participants were recruited through online writer communities, university communities, personal contacts, and social media posts uploaded by researchers and their acquaintances. All participants were selected through purposeful sampling
(
Palinkas et al. 2015
)
to ensure maximum variation across story types, genres, and total duration of creative writing experience.

In total, 14 participants (F=8, M=6) were recruited (
Table 1
). Story types ranged from film (N=11), drama (N=10), novel (N=10), plays (N=3), comics (N=2), and animation (N=1). The total number of writing genres they had experience with reached 32, including examples such as comedy, romance, and historical fiction. The duration of their creative writing experience ranged from 2 to 26 years, with an average of 11.93 years. All participants resided in South Korea.

3.2.
Procedure

Figure 2.
Procedure of the formative study.

The formative study was divided into three parts: (1) Q&A of overall creative experience, (2) memory-recall workshop, and (3) Q&A of selected creative experience (
Figure 2
). The study with each participant was conducted either in person (N=5) or remotely via Zoom (N=9), following the participants’ preferences.

(1) Q&A of overall creative experience (40 minutes)
: After obtaining informed consent, we introduced the goal of the study. To gain overarching insights into their interconnected character creation process, we asked participants to answer the questions (
Table 5
in Appendix
A.1
) based on all their full-length stories. The questions inquired about their experiences in developing characters with differing roles, comparing characters, and building character relationships.

(2) Memory-recall workshop (20 minutes)
: To help participants recall their work in preparation for the next session, we offered worksheets that served as elicitation materials. Participants selected one of their previously created full-length stories and completed three types of worksheets: the Character List, Character Cards, and Character Relationship Map (
Figure 3
). For online participants, the worksheets were shared via FigJam
(
Figma 2025
)
, while offline participants received printed copies. Further details of the workshop materials are described in §
3.3
.

(3) Q&A of selected creative experience (30 minutes)
: In this session, we aimed to gain a deeper understanding of their interconnected character creation process through concrete, situated examples. Thus, participants answered the same set of questions (
Table 5
in Appendix
A.1
), but now contextualized to the specific story they had selected in the second session. All participants received compensation of 30,000 KRW (approx. 21 USD) for their participation.

3.3.
Materials for Memory-Recall Workshop

Figure 3.
Materials for the memory-recall workshop. (A) Character List. (B) Character Cards. (C) Character Relationship Map.

The worksheets for the memory-recall workshop were designed to help participants reflect on the characters from one of their previously created full-length stories. The
Character List
helped participants recall both main and supporting characters by listing their names. The
Character Cards
prompted focused reflection on 3–4 main characters by writing their descriptions, an approach inspired by character sheets used by storywriters
(

)
. The

guided participants in outlining relationships and interactions among the main characters, inspired by character maps discussed in storywriting literature
(

. Acknowledging that these materials may not align with their usual practices, participants were encouraged to recall their work and creation process using alternative methods if preferred.

3.4.
Analysis

We transcribed over 1,260 minutes of audio recordings from interviews with 14 participants. The worksheets completed during the memory-recall workshop were used solely as reference materials to clarify participants’ responses during the transcription process.

The interview data were qualitatively analyzed through three cycles of coding. In the first cycle, the first author used structural coding
(
Saldana 2021
)
to categorize sections of the data in relation to our study’s goal (approaches, challenges, strategies), resulting in our initial codes. Based on these initial codes, the first author developed sub-codes through process coding
(
Miles et al. 2013
)
to identify patterns in participants’ conceptual and observable actions. These sub-codes were then iteratively refined through discussions among the researchers. Finally, the first author used pattern coding
(
Saldana 2021
)
to cluster the sub-codes into three overarching categories that explain participants’ interconnected character creation practices. This last analysis process was also reviewed by the other researchers. The entire analysis process was conducted over two months.

3.5.
Findings

We report participants’ approaches to interconnected character creation, the challenges they face during this process, and the strategies they use to address these difficulties. These insights are organized into three overarching categories that encapsulate their practices.

3.5.1.
Branching Out Related Characters

Approach

Writers would often start creating their main characters, then branch out new characters in relation to these existing characters (P1, P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P8, P9, P10, P11). One specific method involved splitting the traits of the main character and distributing them among other characters (P7, P9). P9 mentioned that this method would ensure that these characters would form a cohesive whole, as each character would reflect different aspects of the main character’s personality. Another method was to think of new characters that would complement existing characters or have opposing characteristics to create conflict (P1, P5, P6, P7, P9, P10). One last notable method was to imagine specific situations where the main characters would be placed and envision other characters that would naturally exist in the same environment (P1, P7, P9).

Challenges

However, thinking of new characters, especially those who would influence the protagonist in specific ways, was often challenging for some writers (P4, P7). The guidance offered to the main character and the inner conflicts they experience depend on the supporting characters. Therefore, participants placed great importance on finding characters that could appropriately fulfill these roles.

“The character needs to act in a certain way, but who could be the essential character to influence that character towards this direction? A friend, perhaps? What kind of personality and values does this friend have, and how would they influence the protagonist? It’s not just about what they say; it’s also about the kind of relationship they’ve had until now. But who could that be?”
(P4)

P7 also shared her experience, mentioning that the process of adjusting supporting characters so they fit these roles seems
“roughly correct at first, but roughly correct is not enough. One must continuously refine these characters and repeat this process.”

Strategies

In response to this challenge, P4 mentioned that he would collaborate with a fellow writer to brainstorm a wide range of characters. P7 added that she would sometimes even change the settings of existing characters when things were not working out.

In summary, writers often expand their character casts in relation to the main characters. However, coming up with new characters that could appropriately influence these core characters was often challenging. This process led to ideating a broad spectrum of potential characters and iteratively refining them to create the perfect match. Thus, the tool should offer a wide selection of related characters with key descriptions, allowing writers to quickly explore options and iteratively adapt characters when needed.

3.5.2.
Balancing Similarities and Differences

Approach

Many participants explained that they would often construct characters by comparing their identities (P1, P2, P3, P5, P7, P8, P10, P13). This practice serves two main purposes. First, it helps ensure that each character has distinct roles and personalities. For example, P1 would start by thinking of ways their characters would contrast visually and progressively alter several attributes of each character, such as their gender, when the participant thought their identities overlapped. Second, this approach enables participants to create characters who not only contrast but also share values and beliefs. P10 emphasized the importance of this practice for creating complex characters who both attract and clash with each other.

Challenges

Despite these initial approaches, many writers found it challenging to create distinct characters, leading to overlapping character identities (P1, P2, P5, P7, P8, P10, P11, P13). For instance, P2 worried that her characters would reflect her own values and thoughts too much, further explaining that individuals created from a single source would inevitably result in similar personalities. In addition, writers explained that balancing character identities that would contrast and align in different aspects was also challenging (P7, P8, P13).

“What feels more compelling to me is exploring the sparks that fly when two equally powerful beings, each acting based on their beliefs or convictions, come into conflict. So, it’s not just about aligning their social status but also about balancing the scale of their beliefs and actions, which is what I find challenging.”
(P8)

Strategies

To create distinct character identities, writers aimed to capture essential traits that could clearly define their characters through expert interviews, reading other stories, and observing social media, among others. In addition, writers (P1, P2, P3, P5, P7, P8, P9, P10) expressed the importance of becoming immersed in the character’s inner world and deeply empathizing with them, such as by writing character backstories in the first person. To create characters that hold contrasting and similar identities, writers (P1, P2, P3, P4, P7, P8, P9) would place characters in identical situations to further establish their personalities. Some writers used predefined questionnaires and answered these questions for each character in the initial stage of characterization. Others would create such situations as scenes within their stories to continuously refine and ideate details for their characters.

In short, writers often compared character identities to create both distinguishable and similar characters. Yet, balancing character similarities and differences remained a persistent challenge. Strategies to tackle this issue included examining how several characters would respond to a common situation, as well as deeply empathizing with and understanding characters’ inner worlds. Therefore, the tool should also allow writers to explore how each character interprets identical contexts in ways that reveal their internal thought processes and subjective perspectives.

3.5.3.
Fleshing Out Relationships

Approach

Several writers mentioned using character relationship maps in the form of diagrams to organize and explore new connections between characters (P1, P5, P9, P13, P14). For example, P5 explained that she uses these diagrams to get a broader view of character relationships and often eliminates characters who remain isolated and unconnected to others. Similarly, P13 and P14 noted that these diagrams help discover new relationships between characters that they had not previously considered.

Challenges

Despite these approaches, intricately fleshing out character relationships remained a challenge for several writers (P5, P7, P9, P11, P13). For example, P9 explains:

“Characters A and B may be defined as close friends, but within that, there might be nuances, like one of them not attending the other’s wedding. Even though they might all be grouped together as close friends from the outside, the deeper you delve, the more you uncover different circumstances and histories that make the relationships more complex.”
(P9)

Strategies

To tackle these challenges, one major strategy used by writers (P9, P13, P14) was to ideate a wide range of character interactions to deepen their understanding of these relationships, even if they did not make it into the final narrative. For example, P13 referred to this technique as
“hypothetical situations”
and employed it to determine whether the main characters would get along before putting her story into action. In addition, P9 mentioned that the emotions and interactions between characters would slowly accumulate through these activities and naturally evolve into specific dialogues or episodes within the story.

To summarize, character relationship maps were utilized to identify and organize connections between characters. However, writers struggled to thoroughly build upon these relationships. Therefore, participants imagined how characters would interact with one another in various situations, regardless of the story’s overall flow. This implies that the tool should also aim to reveal character relationships through open-ended reactions towards each other.

3.6.
Design Goals

Based on these findings, we identified three design goals (DG) to build a tool to support storywriters’ interconnected character creation.

DG 1.

Suggest diverse characters related to existing ones.

To support writers in branching out related characters, the tool should focus on broadly recommending a wide range of related characters with core traits, rather than extensive information about a single character (§
3.5.1
). This goal focuses on the breadth of information.

DG 2.

Support the comparison and investigation of in-depth character identities.

To assist writers in balancing the similarities and differences between characters, the tool should illustrate how each character interprets the same context in ways that reveal their inner mindscapes (§
3.5.2
). This goal emphasizes the depth of information.

DG 3.

Manifest character relationships through exploratory interactions.

To help writers flesh out character relationships, the tool should show open-ended reactions between characters as testbeds to build and refine their dynamics (§
3.5.3
). This goal emphasizes speculative information rather than narrative progression.

Through these three design goals, we aim to provide a plethora of resources that storywriters can mold into concrete forms, use to understand their work deeply, or draw inspiration from while leaving the generated results as-is.

4.
Constella

With the derived design goals, we developed Constella, a creativity support tool designed to support storywriters’ interconnected character creation process through LLM-MA. Its core features include FRIENDS DISCOVERY, JOURNALS, and COMMENTS. FRIENDS DISCOVERY generates three characters in relation to a given character, aiding writers to expand their ensemble with diverse additions (
DG 1
). JOURNALS produces diary entries from multiple characters according to a shared theme, revealing their internal emotions, thoughts, and behaviors (
DG 2
). COMMENTS enables characters to respond to other journal entries, surfacing additional layers of character relationships (
DG 3
). Here, we first discuss our source of inspiration when translating the design goals into system features (§
4.1
). We then describe the system’s components (§
4.2
), present an example use scenario (§
4.3
), and detail the implementation and prompt design (§
4.4
).

4.1.
Metaphor and Interaction Concept

In this section, we explain the rationale behind adopting social media as an overarching metaphor to address the design goals. In addition, we further illustrate how interaction concepts from social media inspired the design of our LLM-MA tool’s main features.

Metaphors have been utilized to provide conceptual models that influence the perception of and interaction with AI systems
(
Tian et al. 2024
;
Dove and Fayard 2020
;
Lee et al. 2024
)
. Social media platforms, in particular, contain intuitive interaction concepts that facilitate social interaction. When used as a metaphor, it offers a familiar language for modeling and communicating the affordances
(
Norman 1999
)
of a system that naturally fosters networked connections, polarized identity expression, and relational communication—all of which reflect our design goals.

Specifically, social media offers unique features that allow people to easily connect with individuals beyond their usual acquaintances, creating an environment where everything becomes a potential point of connection
(
Papacharissi 2010
)
. This characteristic aligns with
DG 1
. Drawing inspiration from this, we designed FRIENDS DISCOVERY, a feature that allows writers to specify the types of connections (e.g., Harry’s long-lost friend) they wish to seek for a given character. Subsequently, the feature offers character candidates with relevant ties grounded in the defined connection point and the seed character’s personal details.

Social media often reveals personal details about people’s lives, helping to build social bonds and reinforce a sense of identity
(
Lin and Faste 2012
)
. Furthermore, when anonymity is guaranteed, individuals are more likely to engage in self-disclosure
(
Pohan and Lbs 2022
)
. This often results in a shared environment where honest and at times even polarizing
(
Lee and Kim 2023
)
reactions to a single event are openly expressed and compared. These traits match
DG 2
. Inspired by this, we designed JOURNALS, a feature that allows writers to first define a theme (e.g., Dumbledore has vanished), then generate diary entries in which selected characters articulate their inner thoughts on that shared topic.

Lastly, social media is frequently used to exhibit and strengthen relationships between users. For instance, comment threads commonly serve as a space to express interpersonal affection, remark on some mutual events, or proclaim relational status
(
Ballantine et al. 2015
)
. These characteristics map with
DG 3
. Based on this, we designed COMMENTS, a feature in which writers can select characters to freely respond to each other’s journal entries, allowing their relationships to unfold through open-ended interactions generated by this feature.

4.3.
An Example Use Scenario

To demonstrate our system, we present how Jina, an animation writer, uses Constella for her latest project, “The Conqueror.”

4.3.1.
Character Profiles

Jina has roughly conceptualized two of her main characters, “Binggu” and “Chorong.” She opens two New Character Panels (
Figure 5
) in Constella and adds several Attributes (e.g., age, goal, backstory, personality) for each character. For Binggu’s personality, she writes, “Binggu tries to appear outwardly violent and fearless, but internally, he is actually very anxious.” For Chorong she writes, “Chorong is a warm-hearted alien who cherishes peace and happiness.” She saves both characters.
Next, Jina opens their Profile Panels (
Figure 6
) and creates their Relationship Attributes by making them follow each other (
Figure 7
). Within Binggu’s profile, she writes, “Binggu finds Chorong annoying,” and decides that Binggu knows Chorong’s character description, personality, occupation, and age. Within Chorong’s profile, she states, “Chorong follows Binggu around like a puppy” and specifies that Chorong knows Binggu’s character description, personality, and current status.

4.3.2.
FRIENDS DISCOVERY

Jina now thinks it would be intriguing to have these two characters crash-land on Earth from outer space. To create a character who causes this crash, she uses FRIENDS DISCOVERY. She enters “Binggu’s greatest enemy” (
Figure 8
-A) as the input and receives three suggestions: “Metal Monster,” “Starlight Witch,” and “Scrap Metal Prince” (
Figure 8
-B). After browsing through the Mini Profiles, Jina is drawn to Metal Monster. As he was the one who wrecked Binggu’s home in the past, Metal Monster would be the perfect villain to stir up Binggu’s emotional journey. Without hesitation, Jina locks him in as the story’s antagonist by saving the character.

4.3.3.
JOURNALS

Jina is now curious about how Binggu and Chorong would react during their crash landing on Earth. Using JOURNALS, she selects both characters (
Figure 10
-C) and generates their journal entries by entering the theme “Binggu and Chorong’s spaceship crashes to Earth after being hit by Metal Monster’s bomb” (
Figure 10
-D). Through the generated entries (
Figure 11
), Jina discovers that Binggu experiences overwhelming stress and frustration in the moment of the blast. Meanwhile, Chorong views the situation with a hint of optimism, tinged even with admiration for the surreal experience. Inspired by this contrast, Jina decides to further amplify their differences by making Chorong even more naive and wonder-filled. Chorong would then perhaps even marvel at the sparkly colors of Earth’s atmosphere during the crash and express excitement about the unexpected adventure that lies ahead. Jina updates Chorong’s Attributes to reflect these changes.

4.3.4.
COMMENTS

Jina is now eager to explore how Metal Monster would respond to Binggu’s journal entry. Using COMMENTS, Jina generates Metal Monster’s comment (
Figure 12
-A) and finds that Metal Monster is still harboring resentment toward Binggu even after his bomb landed successfully (
Figure 12
-B). Seeing this, Jina starts to wonder, what unresolved emotions are fueling his hostility toward Binggu? Motivated by this question, she updates Metal Monster’s Relationship Attributes, adding details about the simmering jealousy that has quietly shaped his attitude toward Binggu all along.

4.3.5.
Future Usages

In the future, Jina can discover new friends Binggu meets on Earth using FRIENDS DISCOVERY, investigate how Metal Monster and Chorong would each react when tasting candy for the first time through JOURNALS, and witness Chorong’s teasing interrogation of Metal Monster’s journal entry through COMMENTS. Example generation results for each feature, based on this future usage, are also included in Appendix
A.2
.

4.4.
Implementation and Prompt Pipeline

4.4.1.
Implementation

Constella was developed as a web-based application with a front-end client built with React.js and a Node.js back-end. User-uploaded images were stored in Google Cloud Storage, while all other application data was managed in MongoDB.

As the participants in our user study were South Korean, we aimed to use an LLM with strong proficiency in both Korean language and cultural understanding. At the time of our research, however, there were no widely published evaluations of state-of-the-art LLMs, such as OpenAI’s GPT-4o
(
Hurst et al. 2024
)
and the Claude 3 family
(
Anthropic 2024
)
, with regard to Korean-specific performance. To address this, we referred to
(
Corca AI 2024
)
, which evaluated GPT-4 and GPT-4o on CLIcK
(
Kim et al. 2024c
)
, a dataset specifically designed to assess cultural and linguistic intelligence in Korean. Their results showed that GPT-4o outperformed GPT-4 and other models tested in the original CLIcK paper. Based on this, we selected GPT-4o and accessed it via the OpenAI API.

The static instruction guides the model to roleplay as a storywriter and generate three distinct fictional characters based on a specified relationship phrase and the given character’s Attributes. JOURNALS

The static instruction directs the model to roleplay as the given character, using both the provided character’s Attributes and Relationship Attributes. The model then generates a journal entry based on a specified theme. In alignment with
DG 2
, each entry begins with the phrase “Dear Diary,” thereby establishing the entry as an intimate monologue that reveals the character’s inner self to an imagined confidant. COMMENTS

The static instruction places the model in the character’s role by making it refer to the character’s Attributes and Relationship Attributes. Taking account of the open-endedness emphasized in
DG 3
, the model is given freedom to focus on a specific aspect of the journal entry when generating a response. In a threaded reply, the model is further guided by the preceding comment history to maintain continuity while focusing on the most recent comment in the thread.

In addition, our prompt design adopts a stateless generation approach; characters do not retain memory of previously generated journal entries beyond the current one, nor of comments outside the current thread. This design choice was motivated by the assumption that character Attributes would be iteratively revised during writers’ creative process. Persisting prior outputs could thus introduce inconsistencies with the updated character definitions and potentially burden users to micromanage previously generated content. Thus, we believed that a deliberate forgetting strategy
(
Sterman et al. 2022a
)
would help writers iterate more freely in favor of emergent ideas and reduce creative pressure by eliminating a sense of finality.

5.
User Study

We conducted a 7–8 day system deployment study to evaluate Constella. Our aim was to understand how Constella integrates into storywriters’ existing practices and how it supports their interconnected character creation process.

CSTs are often evaluated through a mix of observing user actions during the creative process and subjective experience reports
(
Anderson et al. 2024
)
(e.g., Creativity Support Index (CSI)
(
Cherry and Latulipe 2014
)
). Given that Constella was designed to assist storywriters’ creative processes within the context of full-length story creation, we chose to evaluate our tool by exploring its use through a system deployment study. This approach provides participants with the temporal and spatial flexibility to use the tool over an extended period within their natural environments and established processes
(
Remy et al. 2020
)
. To closely track their experiences throughout this period, we collected system interaction logs that stored all our participants’ actions (e.g., generations, edits, deletions) performed within Constella. We also employed a diary study, along with follow-up interviews, to revisit participants’ experiences in greater depth. Lastly, we selected the CSI to gather quantifiable subjective experience reports. The CSI is a widely used and psychometrically validated survey instrument that measures how well a system supports creative activities. We specifically used the survey to identify the strengths and weaknesses of Constella in its role as a CST. The study process was approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB No. concealed for anonymity).

5.2.
Procedure

5.2.1.
Tasks

Creating a full-length story is an extensive process that can take several months or even years. Therefore, we split the process into a set of tasks focused enough to explore system usage while aligning well with participants’ usual writing practices.

(1) Characters

We asked participants to create at least five characters (profiles) within Constella. Although the number of characters in fictional stories varies widely, Truby
(
Truby 2008
)
proposed that characters can be classified based on their roles: the hero, opponent, ally, fake-ally opponent, and subplot character. While not all stories require characters for each of these five roles, some may feature multiple characters fulfilling the same role. Thus, we did not prescribe specific character roles but requested a minimum number of characters.

(2) Character Backstory

We asked participants to write a central character’s backstory. This is a foundational activity according to storywriting literature (§
2.1
) and one that was also utilized by our formative study participants (§
3.5.2
). Given that relationships often form the basis of backstories
(
Barker et al. 2019
)
, we asked participants to include how their central character influenced or was influenced by other characters in their backstory. For this task, we asked writers to use FRIENDS DISCOVERY, as this feature was designed to support writers in discovering related characters. We included this task to ensure FRIENDS DISCOVERY could be utilized non-intrusively, eliminating the need for participants to incorporate entirely new characters in their narratives, as they may have already set clear goals for their character cast. In addition, we only asked participants to write a backstory for one character, as this task was sufficient for trying FRIENDS DISCOVERY.

(3) Scenes

Participants were asked to write three types of scenes: Scene Type A, B, and C.

Scene Type A.
Writers were requested to write a scene where several characters show contrasting or aligned perspectives. The former can be interpreted as conflict and the latter as unity, both essential elements in stories
(
Egri 1972
)
. Therefore, we believed that this scene could naturally be integrated into their narrative. We encouraged participants to use JOURNALS when writing this scene, as the feature is intended to help writers delve into and compare their characters’ thoughts, making it relevant to this task.

Scene Type B.
Participants were instructed to create a scene that shows the relationship between two characters. As character relationships are also central components in stories, we expected these requirements to cause minimal deviation from their narrative goals. We asked writers to use COMMENTS when writing this scene, as this feature was designed to help authors elaborate upon character relationships. We also note that while participants could include any number of multiple characters per scene, we asked them to focus on two-character relationships in Scene Type B, as COMMENTS mainly supports dyadic interactions (§
4.2.3
).

Scene Type C.
Participants were asked to create a scene that involved several characters. For this task, participants were informed to use at least one of the system’s AI features. We provided minimal requirements for this scene to explore additional system usage when writers were given more freedom to use Constella according to their own needs.

(4) Story Outline

We asked participants to complete a story outline for a full-length story featuring at least five characters (profiles). Before completion, we asked writers to use at least one of Constella’s AI features. This task was included because this study aimed to explore system usage within the context of writing a full-length story. The act of writing a story outline provided this overarching context, while its compressed nature, such as lacking details compared to the entire full-length story, was complemented by the aforementioned tasks.

5.2.2.
Supplementary Task Guidelines and Ethical Protocols

We provided supplementary guidelines to support task flexibility, accommodate participants’ preferred writing practices, and ensure ethical handling of intellectual property.

System Outcomes
.
Participants were asked to try the system’s features for the tasks (§
5.2.1
), but the decision to actually use the system-generated outcomes and how to incorporate them was entirely at their discretion.

Order
.
The aforementioned tasks are not sequential steps but a culmination of final results. For example, participants could begin outlining their story while creating character profiles, write Scene Type C, and then revise their outline.

Length and Format
.
Participants could freely select the story type and genre for their narrative. These choices ultimately influenced the length and format of the tasks. For instance, some writers preferred a screenplay format, often writing more pages due to dialogue and spacing conventions. Others favored detailed prose, which tended to be more textually dense despite occupying fewer pages.

Word Processor
.
Participants selected their preferred word processor (e.g., Microsoft Word) to complete the tasks.

Intellectual Property
.
Participants were informed, both during recruitment and before the start of the study, that their work (backstory, scenes, outline) would not be disclosed in its entirety to external parties. Instead, they would be used solely to provide context during our data analysis process. However, we clarified that all content, generated and manually written within Constella, could be fully disclosed for academic purposes. All participants agreed to the above terms. To formalize this process, we provided a non-exclusive intellectual property license agreement detailing these terms, which each participant chose to sign.

5.2.3.
Study Procedure

Figure 13.
Procedure of the user study.

The user study consisted of three parts: (1) introductory session, (2) main session, and (3) post-interview (
Figure 13
). We describe each part in detail below.

(1) Introductory Session (60 minutes)
: We conducted individual introductory sessions with each participant via Zoom. After obtaining informed consent, we introduced the goal of our study and provided a live tutorial of the system. The tutorial included an overview of the system’s features and their purposes, followed by a hands-on session allowing participants to explore the tool themselves. We then provided guidelines for the diary study and explained the tasks for the main session.

(2) Main Session (7-8 days)
: Over a 7-8 day period, participants were asked to complete the assigned tasks and record their thoughts in an online diary each day. For the diary, we utilized Notion
(
Notion Labs, Inc. 2025
)
, a note-taking application, to enable quick and easy documentation. The diary included questions (
Table 6
in Appendix
A.1
) to guide participants in documenting both their storywriting process and system usage experience. While our initial timeframe for the study was set at 7 days, a duration determined through a pilot study, six participants requested an additional day to complete their tasks. As a result, five participants took part in the main session for 7 days and six participants for 8 days.

(3) Post-Interview (120 minutes)
: Finally, we conducted individual semi-structured interviews with each participant to gain an in-depth understanding of their experiences. Each interview was conducted via Zoom. We began by asking about their overall experience with the system, followed by specific questions about each feature (
Table 7
in Appendix
A.1
). We then inquired about their Notion diary entries to understand the context and meaning behind their documented responses. Throughout the interview, participants were encouraged to reference their Notion diaries, the system, and completed tasks to recall and explain their experiences in detail. At the end of this session, participants completed the CSI. All participants received compensation of 350,000 KRW (approx. 251 USD) for their participation.

5.3.
Analysis

We transcribed over 1,320 minutes of audio recordings from interviews with 11 participants. We also gathered their Notion diary entries, completed tasks, Constella account data, system logs, and CSI results. The data from the interview transcriptions and diary entries were qualitatively analyzed through reflective thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2019
)
.

Our analysis process was conducted through the following procedures. First, the first and second authors repeatedly read through the entire dataset of the diary entries and interview transcriptions. Then, these two authors developed initial codes by categorizing the data based on our tool’s features. After this process, the first and second authors reviewed the initial codes while cross-referencing system logs, participants’ Constella account data, and completed tasks to identify themes. These themes were then iteratively refined through discussions among all the authors. As a result of this six-month process, we constructed themes related to use cases for interconnected character creation, the tool’s impact on writers and their writing processes, and its potential future usages, suggested improvements, and limitations. We also note that we found no significant thematic variation based on participants’ duration of creative writing experience and thus did not pursue further analysis along that dimension.

6.2.
Use Cases for Interconnected Character Creation

We report several use cases for each key feature in Constella to illustrate how these features support storywriters’ interconnected character creation process.

6.2.1.
FRIENDS DISCOVERY

Contextualized Discoveries:
Writers were able to create diverse characters linked to the central figure in contexts they desired. The feature also enabled them to uncover unexpected yet meaningful connection points.

All participants either explicitly stated or generally agreed that they were able to discover various characters in contexts they desired. For example, P10 described using this feature by first writing a detailed backstory in the protagonist’s profile. Then, by entering the simple relationship phrase “revenge” in the input field of FRIENDS DISCOVERY, he discovered three characters that not only aligned with but also expanded his preconceived conceptions of the antagonists.

Additionally, participants (P3, P5, P6, P8) expressed astonishment when the tool surfaced connections they had not initially considered. P6 shared a case in which the tool suggested a character associated with the element of “swimming.” This unexpected detail turned out to be crucial for her story, offering a natural and meaningful way for the protagonist to meet the sub-protagonist, the suggested character, for the first time.

“I had only briefly mentioned swimming as part of the protagonist’s daily routine, which I included as a small detail while writing [the protagonist’s profile]. But when it suddenly emerged [in the suggested character], I realized that the system had considered even these minor details and made connections.”
(P6)

Finally, participants (P5, P11) expressed high satisfaction with the recommended characters, noting that they were stylistically consistent with their central characters. This coherence allowed new characters to be integrated smoothly, without disrupting the story’s overall mood or tone.

“When I wanted a character with a vibe similar to Character A […] it would provide recommendations for characters that matched the similar vibe I wanted but offered slightly different variations.”
(P5)

Story World and Narrative Expansion:
Generated characters often transcended their initially envisioned roles and contributed to the narrative in new and unforeseen directions.

Participants used FRIENDS DISCOVERY to identify new characters that aligned with their initial ideas, which they then further developed based on their narrative needs. However, there were also instances where participants (P1, P3, P4, P5, P7, P8, P11) derived entirely new ideas from the recommended characters, which in turn sparked the creation of new storylines. For example, P4 shared that the subplot she developed around a supporting character, initially generated through Constella, felt even more compelling than the protagonist’s storyline she had initially conceived. P4 continued:

“Originally, creating main characters requires a lot of thought, so paying attention to supporting characters or secondary events usually happens later in the writing process. But this method feels new and helpful. As I included the recommended supporting characters in my story and they merged with my protagonist’s narrative arc, I was able to create a whole new story.”
(P4)

Some participants (P3, P8, P11) even adopted the experimental approach of generating entire communities of related characters early in their ideation process, then built their narrative around these characters. For instance, P11 described how she built two distinct story worlds, populated with a village-like network of interconnected figures, each centered around a different character.

“In my case, I initially created many supporting characters around the main character, Yoo Ji-an. I kept imagining a world connected to her, and then at some point, I wanted to create a completely different world. […] By continuing to generate related characters, I ended up with two very different worlds inhabited by distinct characters.”
(P11)

Stockpile Resources:
Generated characters often served as stockpile resources, flexible components ready to be divided, combined, or set aside for use in unexpected situations.

While generated characters were often used as individual entities, some participants (P1, P2, P3, P10, P11) selectively incorporated elements from the generated characters into existing ones or synthesized them into their ideal characters. P1 noted that this was possible because Constella provided multiple character recommendations at once.

“Since about three characters are created, I was able to combine various characteristics from them to shape the character I wanted, which was definitely helpful.”
(P1)

Furthermore, P2 saved suggested characters even if they were not immediately needed in her story, in case she might need them in the future.

“By creating and saving characters, I could bring them back later as cameos when the story hit a dead end, allowing the narrative to take a different direction.”
(P2)

6.2.2.
JOURNALS

Introspective Construction:
JOURNALS helped writers delve into the inner worlds of their characters, including those who might have otherwise remained sidelined or underdeveloped.

All participants mentioned that JOURNALS was helpful for delving into their characters’ psychology, thoughts, and emotions. In addition, some writers (P2, P4, P8, P9, P11) noted that the feature also facilitated the development of the supporting cast. For instance, P8 even wrote a scene from the villain’s perspective for his task, explaining that this was possible because the feature helped him deeply think about the character’s emotions and ideology. P8 further explains that using the feature led him to humanize the antagonist, endowing the villain with human qualities that made it difficult for him to view the villain solely with contempt. P9 similarly noted that JOURNALS was useful for concretizing the backgrounds of characters that might otherwise have been used in a throwaway manner.

“I think there are characters who inevitably end up being used in a disposable way for the protagonist. While working on the detailed background of such characters [through JOURNALS], I found that I could effectively use these characters to shape scenes or plots that could appear later on.”
(P9)

JOURNALS was also perceived to help writers (P4, P10, P11) deeply immerse themselves in several characters simultaneously. For instance, P11 noted that this helped her distribute attention and emotional investment more evenly across characters.

“When a writer keeps writing the dialogue or situation for two characters, they might end up becoming more immersed in one character or fail to fully understand the perspective of the other. […] But by creating [journal entries] first and then reading through both perspectives, I found it helped me approach the process more objectively and distribute [my] emotions more evenly between the two characters.”
(P11)

Relational Construction:
JOURNALS helped writers create characters in relation to one another, either by allowing them to view one character through others’ perspectives or by exploring how multiple characters respond differently to shared themes.

By viewing
Character B

Character A

Character B

“As I started to look at the new situation of the security guard, a supporting character, through the [main character’s] eyes, I found myself wondering, what might have happened to the man? The wind blew a lot, and it rained. Were there any difficult or troublesome complaints he had to deal with?”
(P2)

Participants (P1, P2, P4, P5, P7, P8, P9, P11) also stated that having two or more characters generate journal entries based on a shared theme helped them distinguish or create similarities between characters. Specifically, P1 noted that observing diverse thoughts across several characters aided in the creation of each of their dialogues, narrations, and non-verbal actions. P4 also explained that comparing journal entries also helped in plot creation.

“When I asked the same question to multiple characters, I noticed differences between them within the journals, and by comparing those differences, I was able to create new scenes and storylines.”
(P4)

6.2.3.
COMMENTS

Extra Layers:
COMMENTS was mainly used as an extension for the generated journal entries, allowing writers to further explore character relationships through additional responses.

Some participants found COMMENTS more effective than JOURNALS for understanding the psychological interplay between characters (P2) or for establishing character relationships (P7). While JOURNALS supported the development of individual emotional arcs through monologue-like reflections, COMMENTS was more effective for exploring interpersonal emotional dynamics, as they mimicked communicative exchanges.

In a similar vein, participants (P2, P4, P6, P7, P8, P10, P11) mentioned that observing character responses through COMMENTS helped them further elaborate on relational subtleties. For example, P4 used COMMENTS to detect subtle cues of hidden animosities or ill intent, which informed her decisions about whether those characters could plausibly collaborate as allies. In contrast, P10 generated comments to reinforce existing dynamics rather than introduce new shifts in relationships.

“I noticed through the comments that, even after such humiliation and having his Achilles tendon severed, [Lee Min-jun] doesn’t give up. That made me want to crush [Lee Min-jun] even more. I think those impressions ended up being reflected in the scenes.”
(P10)

6.3.
Impact on Writers and Their Writing Process

6.3.1.
Alignment and Changes to Writers’ Existing Practices

Alignment

All participants (N=11) agreed that FRIENDS DISCOVERY aligned well with their established practices. They explained that this feature mirrored their usual approach of conceptualizing new characters in relation to existing ones. JOURNALS also aligned well with most writers’ existing practices (N=9). P4 specifically mentioned that this feature was similar to her established method of considering how different characters would each react to a shared situation. COMMENTS received mixed feedback in terms of effective practice alignment. While participants who expressed positive remarks (N=6) mentioned the usefulness of this feature to explore character relationships, others who gave negative feedback (N=5) pointed out that COMMENTS felt unnecessary or inefficient compared to JOURNALS. This was because JOURNALS could be utilized to serve similar purposes as COMMENTS, but conveyed more extensive details due to their longer format.

Changes

Constella also introduced notable shifts in participants’ existing writing practices. Writers (P2, P3, P8, P11) who typically initiated story ideation with conflicts or a logline reported that our tool nudged them to begin instead with character development. Participants also appreciated how Constella shifted their focus towards deeper engagement with a broader range of characters, including those they might not have even considered at all, at an earlier stage in their writing process. Furthermore, some writers (P8, P10) became curious about exploring the perspectives of characters not directly involved in the current story situation. P10, in particular, enjoyed using this newfound ability to speculate on how other unassociated characters might react alongside those more centrally involved,
“since the rumor can spread to others.”
Lastly, Constella offered valuable insights for participants to refine their creative methods in the future. P3 came to appreciate the value of having a diverse pool of characters who could be flexibly integrated into any scenario. Other writers (P8, P9) expressed a desire to change their usual approach of organizing character attributes and relationships, inspired by the structure and layout of the Profile feature.

6.3.2.
Preserving Authorial Control

Participants perceived Constella to play a complementary role in their writing process, with some (P7, P8, P10, P11) referring to it as an
“assistant writer.”
Our analysis suggests that this perception was shaped by specific design aspects of Constella that supported writers in maintaining their creative voice within human-AI collaboration. Below, we examine these design elements in greater detail.

Constraining AI Involvement

Since FRIENDS DISCOVERY generates characters only in relation to existing ones, the responsibility of creating profiles for the initial characters, typically the main protagonists, was placed on the writers. Participants (P7, P11) noted that this feature resembled their co-writing dynamics, where they delegate secondary character creation to collaborators. They also mentioned that creating descriptions for the main character themselves helped ensure a greater sense of ownership of their work (P10, P11). Furthermore, as the tool only provides core character attributes, writers (P3, P11) noted that this created room for them to build on the suggested characters with their own ideas.

“If the main characters were also created by the tool, I think it could be like someone addicted to gambling, just staring blankly at the app, completely absorbed. But since only supporting characters are generated and there’s a certain limit, it made me snap out and think, wait a minute, I need to create this part myself.”
(P11)

Intermediary Materials

We designed JOURNALS and COMMENTS to generate text in formats that resemble diary entries and responses to these entries. Participants (P1, P3, P9, P10) mentioned that they were initially surprised because they had only heard of AI tools that generate entire outlines or stories in output-ready formats. However, for our tool, writers (P1, P2, P7, P8, P9, P10) pointed out the importance of their own role in consciously extracting meaning from the generated results. When doing so, they found these resources could scaffold them to further envision and realize their characters and narratives themselves.

“Usually, when people think of AI, they assume it just generates stories automatically when given a prompt. But in that case, AI is the one leading the process. In contrast, when I provide the input, this tool creates journals and other things, and through these journals, I create my scenes.”
(P10)

6.4.
Future Usages, Suggested Improvements, and Limitations

6.4.1.
Potential while Writing the Entire Full-Length Story

Participants suggested that Constella would be utilized even more extensively when writing the main text of the story, rather than summarized outlines. When engaged in this process, writers (P1, P3, P7, P8, P10) noted they would use the tool to delve even deeper into characters’ backstories and thoughts, concretize their relationships, and create more supporting characters. P8 also shared that using Constella to develop identities for background characters, instead of simply treating them as extras, would even help imagine their visual appearances during the illustration process.

In addition, participants (P1, P3, P8) mentioned that Constella would be more beneficial when used for large-scale stories that require expansive worldbuilding and complex relationships, such as serialized work and different stories that share the same fictional world. Considering such situations that require vast numbers of interconnected characters, updating FRIENDS DISCOVERY to generate characters associated with multiple individuals (P2), including character relationship maps (P1, P10, P11), adding a tagging feature (e.g., @name) to easily change and reference character names (P10, P11), and organizing characters into groups (P3, P6, P8) could further help writers expand and manage their character casts.

6.4.2.
Additional Support for Writers’ Linear, Non-linear, and Iterative Processes

Participants (P2, P11) requested a feature that allows characters to recall previously generated journal entries and comment threads to explore character relationships that evolve over time. Additionally, a version control
(
Sterman et al. 2022b
)
feature to track and recover changes in character profiles was suggested (P3, P4, P7, P10). Specifically, P4 expressed interest in exploring characters non-linearly by aligning profile versions with different points along the story’s timeline. While writers often created journals for a specific purpose, they also derived new meanings when reviewing entries in sequence or looking back on them later (P3, P4, P9, P11). As a result, a feature that allows users to reorganize journal entries together into new categories or story timelines was also requested (P3, P4). To better support the iterative process of editing character profiles, participants (P3, P7) suggested integrating a word processor into Constella, allowing them to seamlessly make changes while writing their stories within a single interface.

6.4.3.
Generation Quality and Intent Elaboration

Some writers (P5, P8) mentioned that JOURNALS and COMMENTS were sometimes overly constrained by character attributes, leading to a lack of novelty. For instance, P8 mentioned that the system tended to echo character descriptions too literally; if a character was described as disliking someone, that sentiment would appear in the generated text regardless of the overall context.

In addition, a common critique was that the generated results felt somewhat mundane or stereotypical (P1, P2, P3, P5, P6, P9), and the characters were depicted as excessively kind (P1, P2, P5, P6, P11). However, many of these writers (P1, P2, P9, P11) also mentioned that their perceptions shifted to varying degrees after creating more detailed character profiles or prompts. They also believed that additional efforts in doing so could have yielded better results (P1, P3, P5, P6, P11). That said, participants found the process of creating character profiles in detail challenging. Specifically, writers (P2, P3, P8, P11) mentioned a trade-off between the effort required in detailing character profiles and the benefit of obtaining consistent, high-quality generations. For example, writers (P2, P3, P8) did not input all character details (e.g., nationality, manner of speech) as they typically only write down key traits when working alone. They also mentioned that adding descriptions to achieve more aligned results felt frustrating since they had already mentally envisioned those characteristics. Continuously updating character profiles to incorporate updated information from the storyline also required considerable effort (P8, P11). We discuss potential methods to alleviate these issues in §
7.5
and §
8
.

6.4.4.
Limited Narrative Advancement

While acknowledging that this was not Constella’s intended purpose, some writers (P1, P5, P7, P11) expressed disappointment that the generated results for COMMENTS and JOURNALS were not presented in forms they found
“directly applicable to the story”
(P7) or had limited ability to
“progress the narrative”
(P11). This was explained because the outputs lacked sufficient action sequences (P5, P7, P11) or were inadequate for simulating in-the-moment conversational interactions mimicking the form of dialogues in stories (P1, P11). Instead, the outputs were focused more on characters’ thoughts, emotions, and reflections of past events. While this approach was perceived as a shortcoming by some, we offer our reflections on how it may have encouraged writers to exercise greater creative judgment in §
7.3
.

7.
Discussion

In this section, we first identify opportunities for AI interaction design in storywriting. To do so, we begin by reflecting on our system’s design goals through revisiting patterns of user appropriation (§
7.1
) and by exploring how multi-agent interactions can be intentionally designed to encourage balanced attention across the character cast (§
7.2
). We then present broader design implications for AI-based writing support tools that value authorial agency by highlighting the value of deliberate constraints and intermediary materials (§
7.3
). We also discuss the opportunities and challenges of employing metaphors in AI-based CSTs (§
7.4
). Finally, we address the challenge of intent alignment, advocating for AI assistance that reduces cognitive load without diminishing opportunities for user reflection (§
7.5
).

7.1.
Reflecting on the Design Goals

We reflect on our system design goals outlined in §
3.6
, assessing the extent to which they have been achieved through our user study. We also use participants’ acts of appropriation
(
Li et al. 2023
)
and misuse
(
Li et al. 2025
)
uncovered through the study as resources to identify design opportunities for future AI-based storywriting support tools.

DG 1: Suggest diverse characters related to existing ones

FRIENDS DISCOVERY allows writers to discover three new characters in relation to a given character by specifying their relationship. In the study, all participants reported that our tool helped them discover various characters aligned within their desired relational contexts (§
6.2.1
). As the tool offered multiple variations, we also observed that several writers (N=4) engaged in a creative process of remixing suggested characters by combining several of their attributes. This emergent use highlights a potential area for improvement: generating new character variations based on remixed attributes. For instance, a writer might select the element of a “magician” from one suggested character and the element of “sleepwalking” from another. The system could then generate further character variations based on their combinations while still aligning with the original relationship prompt. This approach could potentially expand the diversity of characters writers can explore while preserving their relational ties to existing ones.

DG 2: Support the comparison and investigation of in-depth character identities

JOURNALS provide writers with characters’ introspective reactions toward a shared theme. All participants in our study regarded the feature as a valuable aid to delve into their characters’ minds and personal viewpoints (§
6.2.2
). In addition, writers (N=8) mentioned that having multiple characters reflect on the same theme helped them better understand and concretize contrasts and similarities between characters. Lastly, we found that several writers (N=5) drew inspiration from the visual and spatial descriptions within the journal entries, treating them as expressive cues that represent characters’ internal states. In light of these use cases, we propose expanding JOURNALS to support the externalization of characters’ mindscapes through image-generative AI. Such introspective visualizations can leverage the power of ambiguity
(
Gaver et al. 2003
;
Park et al. 2025
)
to facilitate writers’ meaning-making and interpretation while faithfully expressing the emotional texture and mood of each character’s inner world.

DG 3: Manifest character relationships through exploratory interactions

COMMENTS enables writers to explore open-ended character reactions toward others’ journal entries. While many writers (N=7) found the feature helpful for surfacing inter-character dynamics (§
6.2.3
), we also observed that JOURNALS was often used for similar purposes by defining themes that prompted characters to reflect on one another (§
6.1.1
, §
6.2.2
). Therefore, to further support relational development in ways that differentiate from JOURNALS, future work could expand COMMENTS to include non-verbal interactions. For example, characters could proactively delete or edit their comments, intentionally delay their responses, or passively view other comments multiple times without replying. These open-ended forms of interaction would allow writers to explore dimensions such as hesitation or one-sided emotional investment, which can provide further inspiration when defining and portraying character relationships.

7.2.
Balancing Attention through Multi-Agent Interactions

We introduced Constella, an LLM-based multi-agent tool that supports storywriters’ interconnected character creation process. Our CSI score (§
6.1.2
), along with qualitative findings that illustrate the diverse ways writers used the tool (§
6.2
), collectively suggest that Constella complemented a range of approaches to character creation. Furthermore, one notable finding was that our tool not only supported but also actively reshaped writers’ existing practices. In particular, Constella enabled writers to more evenly distribute their attention and creative effort across the character cast, rather than focusing predominantly on a single central character (§
6.3.1
). We coin this emergent phenomenon the
balancing effect
and use this section to further examine the underlying features that contributed to it, as well as its implications as a design affordance for future storywriting support tools.

Several features of Constella played a critical role in fostering the balancing effect. FRIENDS DISCOVERY surfaced unexpected ties when generating new characters, prompting writers to explore interwoven narratives they had not initially envisioned (§
6.2.1
). We believe the process enabled supporting characters to take on firm and meaningful roles within the narrative, thereby underscoring their potential to shape story dynamics alongside central characters. Then, through JOURNALS, writers received a chorus of perspectives from their characters, encouraging them to shift focus more fluidly across the cast (§
6.2.2
). This process helped further deepen and humanize each character more equally, ultimately reinforcing the notion that every character, regardless of role, has a story worth telling.
We believe this underscores the ability of multi-agent tools to guide writers towards the creation of narratives in which each character can potentially contribute more meaningfully to the evolving story.

Thus, we invite future work to explore how multi-agent systems can be
intentionally designed
to surface underdeveloped perspectives and support more balanced forms of storywriting. For example, multi-agent tools might reimagine a completed scene or even the entire story by exploring how the narrative would change if other supporting characters took the lead. Such generative reframings could be particularly valuable in polyphonic stories, which are characterized by multiple voices, perspectives, and distributed character agency. Given that creative practitioners often adapt their tool choices to intentionally accommodate specific ways of thinking and behaviors
(
Nicholas et al. 2022
)
, and that writers increasingly use AI tools to experiment with their creative processes
(
Guo et al. 2024b
;
Ensemble Park 2025
)
, we believe such LLM-MA tools may hold value not only as creative aids that support well-established practices but also as educational or experimental scaffolds for writers seeking to explore more distributed, multi-perspective, non-traditional storytelling structures.

7.3.
AI Design for Authorial Control and Expression

Our findings indicate that two design aspects of Constella played a critical role in helping writers preserve their creative voice: constraining AI’s generative role and offering intermediary forms of text.

Deliberate Constraints

To align with the idiosyncratic values of writers, existing discourses have emphasized adaptability as a key principle for co-creative AI systems
(
Qin et al. 2024
;
Qin et al. 2025
;
Biermann et al. 2022
)
. Yet, might there also be value in applying deliberate constraints on AI within these tools? Our findings show that these constraints, such as not generating the central character and providing only a minimal set of attributes, can prevent writers from delegating creative decisions entirely to the AI system (§
6.3.2
). These imposed boundaries also helped writers avoid over-reliance on AI and encouraged more intentional and thoughtful decisions.

Echoing research advocating for meaningful limitations in technology
(
Pierce and Paulos 2014
;
Pierce 2012
;
Tatar et al. 2008
)
, we believe that carefully applied constraints can enable future writing support tools to create room for writers to exercise their creative agency while still drawing on AI’s generative potential. For example, one could imagine creating a tool that only generates the last sentence of a chapter, prompting writers to build the narrative leading up to that conclusion. Such approaches may also help address what has been described as
“the dearth of the author”

(
Kreminski 2024
)
, an issue wherein AI systems facilitate large volumes of text with minimal authorial decision-making. Then, how to constrain AI capabilities to help writers maintain authorial control while offering enough meaningful support will be a critical research question to guide the design of future AI-based writing support tools.

Intermediary Materials

As mentioned in §
6.4.4
, several writers expected our tool to create action sequences or dialogues in output-ready prose. However, writers also found that the format of JOURNALS and COMMENTS required them to actively engage with the generated materials and think critically about how to use the tool’s outputs in their own creative process (§
6.3.2
).

Based on these findings, we suggest that a key experiential quality of the intermediary materials produced by our tool is its ability to introduce
slowness
. Rather than propelling the narrative forward with polished, ready-made text, these materials encourage writers to pause, reflect, and develop a more profound understanding of their work or engage in active interpretation before deciding whether and how to incorporate the AI-generated content (§
6.2.2
, §
6.2.3
). This aligns with findings from
(
Zhou and Sterman 2024
)
, which show that incoherent or grammatically imperfect AI suggestions can prompt reflection and result in more user-generated content.

Together with prior work that advocates for preserving
“creative struggle”

(
Zhou and Sterman 2023
)
in human-AI co-writing, these insights point toward the value of AI-generated
“intermediate text”

(
Zhou and Sterman 2024
)
, outputs that are intentionally incomplete or ambiguous, as a means of fostering user reflection and adaptation
(
Kreminski and Mateas 2021
)
. We thus call for future research to further explore alternative forms of AI-based CSTs that do not merely optimize for productivity or output-readiness but instead intentionally support slow, interpretive, creative processes.

7.4.
Applying Metaphors for Designing Interactions in AI-based CSTs

Constella was designed with the social media metaphor. In this section, we examine the broader opportunities and challenges that arise when metaphors are used to structure interactions in AI-based CSTs.

Recent HCI research has increasingly explored metaphorical design in AI-powered CSTs, investigating how conceptual metaphors shape users’ mental models and interactions. In particular, intelligent writing assistants are frequently framed as
“agents,”

“tools,”
or
“hybrid”
(combinations of both)
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
. These metaphors reflect differing levels of autonomy and user control, with agents seen as collaborators and tools as extensions of user intent. Beyond these broad categories, more specific interaction metaphors have also emerged. For instance,
Patchview

(
Chung and Kreminski 2024
)
utilized the generative dust and magnet metaphor to visualize high-level concepts as magnets and the generated story elements as dust, thereby encoding semantic proximity through spatial interaction.
Toyteller

(
Chung et al. 2025b
)
introduced a toy-playing metaphor, allowing users to manipulate symbolic characters like toys in order to influence narrative generation in playful, embodied ways. As such, metaphors can unlock more imaginative approaches to designing AI systems
(
Tian et al. 2024
)
and bring forth emotionally resonant user experiences (§
6.1.2
) when drawing from playful domains such as our choice of social media.

However, metaphorical design also introduces new challenges. Anthropomorphic metaphors can inflate user perceptions of AI competence or distort users’ understanding of how AI systems function. For instance, a recent study
(
Jang 2023
)
has found that presenting agents with anthropomorphic traits can increase perceived AI creativity and possibly lead to further acceptance of AI-generated content. However, this may come at the cost of reduced critical reflection on the system’s actual generative processes. The collaboration metaphor, in particular, has been critiqued for masking the extensive and often invisible human labor embedded in the development of AI systems
(
Sarkar 2023
)
. This concern becomes especially salient when models are trained on datasets drawn from the work of creative practitioners, potentially exacerbating the issue of unacknowledged appropriation of artistic labor without consent or compensation
(
Gero et al. 2025
;
Jiang et al. 2023
)
.

While prior HCI research has examined the potential of metaphors in interactive system design more broadly, we argue that their role in AI-based CSTs warrants deeper and more critical scrutiny. In this context, metaphors do more than frame interaction; they shape how users understand authorship, agency, and creativity itself. As such, we believe that metaphorical design must be approached not only as a creative opportunity but also as an ethical responsibility and thus call forth further research to examine its implications when used for designing AI systems.

7.5.
Aligning with Evolving Intent

We found that participants encountered a trade-off when detailing character profiles: they could either invest more time to achieve generations better aligned with their envisioned characters or exert less effort and receive more generic outputs (§
6.4.3
). This trade-off may disrupt creative workflows, as writers are repeatedly forced to specify trivial details they already know instead of immersing themselves in the act of storywriting.

Prior work
(
Kreminski et al. 2024
)
has also pointed out similar issues where AI systems fail to fully understand users’ intent, as users often provide minimal prompts without fully expressing their goals or because users themselves are uncertain about their creative goals during the early stages of creation. Our tool addresses the latter challenge by helping users refine and reflect on their evolving intent by manifesting their specified characters. However, our tool’s heavy reliance on manual inputs and edits to character profiles appears to fall short in addressing the former challenge, mainly because users sometimes found it a burden to actively type in and update character details.

One potential direction to support this process would be to treat AI-generated results as resources for continuously refining character profiles. For example, when users make edits to the generated outputs from JOURNALS and COMMENTS, the system could automatically reflect those changes in the original character profiles. Additionally, a retrieval-augmented generation approach
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
could help retain useful details for new outputs, reducing the need for users to specify some aspects in character profiles. These methods can also be applied to the writer’s story, as they may have expressed these character details within their narrative, even if they were not explicitly defined in the system.

While such AI-assisted mechanisms could reduce manual burden, they also raise important questions about the impact of automation on user creativity. Manual tasks like updating character personas, while seemingly tedious, may often spark reflection or new ideas
(
Hwang 2022
)
. Writers may not just be inputting data; they may also be reflecting on the character’s arc, emotional consistency, and narrative role. If automation takes over that task, users may skip the opportunity to revisit their ideas, even when they initially feel fully formed or obvious. Future research should therefore explore how to best integrate these mechanisms in ways that preserve opportunities for writers to reflect through their acts of creation.

8.
Limitations and Future Work

Exploring Tool Usage During Entire Full-Length Story Creation

In our system evaluation study, participants created characters, a backstory, scenes, and a story outline within the context of developing a full-length story. However, these activities may not fully capture the tool’s potential when used throughout the entire process of writing a complete story. This is because writing an entire full-length story is a more prolonged and complex process, during which writers must introduce additional characters and develop their relationships in greater detail. To assess the tool’s long-term value, future work could build on the recommendations in §
6.4.1
and conduct longitudinal studies of entire full-length or even serialized story creation, where participants expect the tool to provide even greater value.

Participant Cultural and Writing Backgrounds

While we made efforts to recruit writers with diverse backgrounds, the participants in this study do not represent the full spectrum of storywriters. From a cultural standpoint, all participants were based in South Korea, and this shared cultural background may have influenced their perceptions toward AI technologies and character creation. In addition, while our recruitment aimed to include writers from a broad range of story types and genres, writers ultimately chose specific ones during our evaluation study. Future work could thus explore tool usage by focusing on writers from different cultural contexts and examining how the tool supports a broader range of genres and story types. When doing so, incorporating features suggested in §
6.4.2
may further help accommodate their varied writing processes.

Prompt Engineering and Impersonation Quality

Constella currently uses prompt-based, specifically nonparametric prompting
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
, to guide each character’s behavior within a pre-trained large language model. This technique is a common practice for roleplaying agents, offering a flexible and lightweight method for assigning personas without requiring model retraining. However, vanilla prompt engineering is prone to what has been termed the
“overuse problem”

(
Kim et al. 2024b
)
, wherein the model shows rigid adherence to a character profile and exploits persona attributes, including those that deviate from the given topic. Future work may therefore explore integrating the
PANDA framework

(
Kim et al. 2024b
)
, which detects and mitigates persona overuse through fine-grained topic mapping and context-aware scoring. Another direction to improve impersonation quality would be to investigate fine-tuning strategies, as exemplified by
CharacterGLM

(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
, which demonstrated more contextually adaptive character behaviors compared to prompt-based approaches alone.

Nevertheless, even with improved prompting strategies and fine-tuning methods, it is important to acknowledge that LLMs inherently carry biases rooted in their pre-training data
(
Kotek et al. 2023
;
Cheng et al. 2023
)
. These underlying tendencies may lead to stereotypical, homogenized, and culturally skewed outputs
(
Anderson et al. 2024
;
Mirowski et al. 2024
;
Lucy and Bamman 2021
;
Agarwal et al. 2025
)
, potentially misaligning with the writer’s creative intent. Future efforts to mitigate these issues will be crucial for enabling more inclusive, contextually sensitive, and nuanced character portrayals across a broader range of contexts.

9.
Conclusion

This paper proposed Constella, an LLM-based multi-agent tool designed to support storywriters’ interconnected character creation process. Our objective was to leverage the potential of LLM-MA in aiding the comparison of a cast of fictional characters, an aspect emphasized in storywriting literature as crucial for long-form narratives. To ground our design not only in theoretical foundations but also in the real-world practices of writers, we began with a formative interview study. The insights gained from this study informed the core concept and features of Constella. Our developed tool suggests diverse characters related to existing ones through the FRIENDS DISCOVERY feature, supports the comparison and investigation of in-depth character identities through the JOURNALS feature, and manifests character relationships through exploratory interactions through the COMMENTS feature. Our system deployment study demonstrated that Constella encouraged a more balanced distribution of creative focus across the character cast while preserving authorial control during tool use. We hope our work offers insight into the potential of multi-agent systems to shed light on sidelined perspectives and into design approaches that help maintain authorial agency in AI-assisted writing.
