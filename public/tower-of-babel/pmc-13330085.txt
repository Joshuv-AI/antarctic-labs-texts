Functional analysis of a novel splice site variant of RAB3GAP2 in a fetus with congenital cataracts
Authors: Xuemei Tan, Yuanyuan Huang, Xiaobao Wei, Qiurong Lai, Lang Pang, Jiquan Lan, Qingnan Liu, Jiangfeng Qin, Fangfang Zeng, Lizhu Chen, Dejian Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMutations in RAB3GAP2 are associated with Martsolf syndrome 1, characterized by postnatal microcephaly, congenital cataracts, and developmental delay. Two compound heterozygous variants of RAB3GAP2 were identified in a fetus with congenital cataract. This study aimed to assess the pathogenicity of the RAB3GAP2 variant c.304 + 5G > T.MethodsFetal lenses were evaluated using ultrasound. Whole exome sequencing (WES) was employed to identify splicing variants in RAB3GAP2, and Sanger sequencing confirmed the results. Pathogenicity was predicted using bioinformatics tools, including Mutation Taster, Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion, Splice AI, NetGene2, SpliceRover, and Alternative Splice Site Predictor. Reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) was conducted to detect RAB3GAP2 transcripts in peripheral blood leukocytes. A minigene assay was used to examine the variant’s impact on splicing.ResultsUltrasound examination revealed increased echogenicity in both fetal lenses, consistent with congenital cataracts. WES identified two compound heterozygous variants in RAB3GAP2: c.812–2 A > G (rs556159021) and c.304 + 5G > T. This study focused on the RAB3GAP2 variant c.304 + 5G > T. In silico analysis predicted that this variant disrupts the donor splice site in intron 3. RT-PCR analysis revealed an abnormal transcript with skipping of exon 3. The minigene assay showed that the RAB3GAP2 c.304 + 5G > T variant impaired mRNA splicing in HEK293T and HeLa cells.ConclusionsThe RAB3GAP2 splicing variant (NM_012414.4:c.304 + 5G > T) is predicted to cause aberrant splicing and truncated proteins. These findings contribute valuable information for genetic counseling regarding congenital cataracts in future pregnancies within affected families.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-026-02385-8.

Introduction

Congenital cataract is an ophthalmic disorder characterized by abnormal lens development, resulting in lens opacity during the fetal or neonatal period. Severe cataracts can lead to impaired vision or blindness in infants [1–3] and are reported as the most common ocular abnormality occurring in isolation [4, 5]. The disorder affects approximately 0.01% to 0.06% of live births [3, 6–9], with a consistent incidence between males and females. Causes of congenital cataracts include idiopathic factors, genetic disorders, metabolic diseases, intrauterine infections, and drug reactions [5, 8, 10]. Genetic factors are implicated in about one-third of cases [2, 7, 11], with most hereditary cases affecting both eyes [4, 9]. Inheritance patterns are autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, and X-linked, with autosomal dominant inheritance being the most common [11, 12]. Family inheritance was first described by Nettleship and Ogilvie in 1906 [2]. Despite numerous studies on cataracts, the molecular mechanisms underlying their development in humans remain poorly understood [2].

This study identified two compound heterozygous splicing variants in RAB3GAP2 in a fetus with congenital cataracts. RAB3GAP2, located on chromosome 1q41, consists of 35 exons encoding a 1393-amino acid protein. The non-catalytic subunit (150 kDa) encoded by RAB3GAP2 forms a heterodimeric complex with the catalytic subunit (130 kDa) encoded by RAB3GAP1 [13–15]. RAB3GAP, a Rab GTPase-activating protein, converts active Rab3 GTP to inactive GDP and plays a critical role in the regulation of hormone and neurotransmitter exocytosis, which is vital for neural development [13, 14, 16–18]. Pathogenic variants in RAB3GAP2 underlie two well-defined autosomal recessive genetic disorders: Warburg micro syndrome 2 (WARBM, OMIM: 614225) and Martsolf syndrome 1 (MS, OMIM: 212720) [19–21]. These syndromes share overlapping clinical phenotypes, including ocular, neurological, and endocrine abnormalities. However, MS presents with a milder phenotype, including less severe ocular and neurological manifestations than WARBM [19, 21–23]. In the present case, the fetus exhibited lens abnormalities without the more severe phenotypes seen in WARBM, such as microcephaly, microphthalmia, microcornea (diameter < 10 mm), progressive optic atrophy, craniofacial deformities, or cerebral malformations [24, 25]. These findings suggest a diagnosis of MS, although further evidence is needed for definitive confirmation.

The two potentially pathogenic compound heterozygous variants identified in RAB3GAP2, associated with cataracts in the fetus, were NM_012414.4:c.812–2 A > G (rs556159021) and NM_012414.4:c.304 + 5G > T. The clinical phenotype of c.304 + 5G > T remains unreported in the literature. Consequently, this study focused on this variant and its potential pathogenicity.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old pregnant woman at 24 weeks and four days of gestation presented for examination. Ultrasound revealed an ocular abnormality in the fetus, with increased echogenicity in both fetal lenses (Fig. 1A), suggesting a high likelihood of congenital cataracts. No other structural malformations were detected. The couple had no family history of genetic disorders and were not consanguineous. The woman had no history of hypertension, diabetes, anemia, or medication use. Serological screenings for toxoplasmosis, rubella, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex virus were all negative. Given the fetal ocular abnormality, the doctor recommended prenatal genetic testing to explore potential underlying genetic causes. The pregnant woman consented to the prenatal diagnosis at 24 weeks and 6 days of gestation. Whole exome sequencing (WES) revealed two RAB3GAP2 variants associated with ocular developmental defects and intellectual disability. The pregnant woman decided to continue the pregnancy after being informed of the findings. However, at 31 weeks and one day of gestation, intrauterine fetal demise was diagnosed, and the woman returned to the hospital for induction of labor. The couple agreed to further genetic testing on fetal tissues. Notably, prior to this pregnancy, the woman had undergone an induced abortion due to fetal lens abnormalities. Informed consent was obtained from the couple before conducting any experiments in this study.

Materials and methods

Amniocentesis was performed to collect amniotic fluid. After excluding maternal contamination, the amniotic fluid samples underwent both WES and karyotype analysis. Peripheral blood samples from the couple were also collected for further experiments.

Short Tandem Repeat (STR) locus analysis

To ensure the absence of maternal cell contamination in the amniotic fluid, a short tandem repeat (STR) assay was conducted. Testing was performed on both the amniotic fluid and the woman’s peripheral blood, using a total of 21 STR loci (HEALTH GENETECH, Ningbo, China). The fetal STR genotypes were compared to those of the mother. If the electropherogram of each STR locus in the amniotic fluid showed one or two peaks, and all peak origins matched the corresponding STR genotypes of the mother, this confirmed the absence of maternal cell contamination, permitting the continuation of subsequent experiments.

Whole exome sequencing

WES was conducted to detect potential genetic defects in the fetus. Genomic DNA was extracted from amniotic fluid and peripheral blood samples using the QIAamp DNA Midi Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The DNA samples were purified and digested to construct a sequencing library. Targeted probes were employed to capture the exons of nearly 20,000 human genes, along with approximately 20 bp of the upstream and downstream regions. WES was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina, CA, USA). Sequencing data were processed for quality control, including removal of adapter sequences and low-quality reads. The data were then aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome, mapping the sequences to their respective genomic positions. Comparisons between the sample sequences and the reference sequence facilitated the identification of single nucleotide variations (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (Indels). Exome mutations were screened against the dbSNP, 1000 Genomes Project, and gnomAD databases, excluding variants with a frequency exceeding 1%. Further, synonymous SNVs and intronic variants located more than 10 bp from exon regions were removed. Mutations annotated in the Human Gene Mutation Database, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), and ClinVar were also excluded if they were not associated with the clinical phenotype.

Karyotyping

Karyotype analysis was performed to detect structural chromosomal abnormalities in the fetus. Amniotic fluid cells were cultured using conventional methods, and karyotypes were harvested for G-banding and subsequent analysis. Thirty metaphase spreads at a 500-band resolution were analyzed using a Zeiss chromosome analysis system. Five karyotypes were examined, and karyotype descriptions followed the International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature (ISCN2020).

Sanger sequencing

To verify the two intronic variants in RAB3GAP2 previously detected by WES, Sanger sequencing was performed. The amplification primers were as follows: RAB3GAP2-c-304-5-F1: 5’-AGTTTTACCCACAGGAAGAAGAAGG-3’; RAB3GAP2-c-304-5-R1: 5’-CTGAGGTACCATATGGAGAGCACTA-3’; RAB3GAP2-c-812-2-F2: 5’-TGGAGTGCAGTTGGTGTGA-3’; RAB3GAP2-c-812-2-R2: 5’-AGCTCCTGACTATTGCACCA-3.

Sanger sequencing was performed by Sangon Biotech (Sangon, Shanghai, China).

In silico prediction

The pathogenicity of the variants was assessed using MutationTaster (https://www.mutationtaster.org/) and Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) (https://cadd.gs.washington.edu/). To evaluate the potential impact of the c.304 + 5G > T variant on normal RNA splicing, the following bioinformatics tools were utilized: Splice AI (https://spliceailookup.broadinstitute.org/), NetGene2 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?NetGene2-2.42), SpliceRover (http://bioit2.irc.ugent.be/splicerover/), and Alternative Splice Site Predictor (ASSP) (http://wangcomputing.com/assp/index.html).

Reverse transcription-PCR

Reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) was performed to analyze RAB3GAP2 transcripts. RNA was extracted from maternal peripheral blood using the HiPure Universal RNA Mini Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was carried out using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Japan). Specific primers were designed for PCR amplification of the cDNA, and the resulting products were subjected to Sanger sequencing (Tsingke Biotech, Beijing, China). The primer sequences for cDNA amplification were as follows: Forward primer: 5’-CTGCTACTTCCAGGACCTCC-3’; Reverse primer: 5’-CACAATGCAGGTCCAGTCAG-3’.

Minigene assay

The c.304 + 5G > T variant is located in intron 3 of RAB3GAP2. To study its effect on splicing, genomic sequences from part of intron 2, exon 3, intron 3, and part of exon 4 were amplified by PCR. The minigene vector pcMINI-C was used to construct the minigene pcMINI-C-RAB3GAP2-wt/mut through enzyme digestion, ligation, transformation, and verification of recombinant clones [26, 27]. The recombinant vectors were then transfected into HeLa and 293T cells. After 48 h, total RNA was extracted from the cells using Trizol (TaKaRa). RNA quality was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and reverse transcribed into cDNA. The cDNA was amplified by PCR, and the amplification products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Bands from the gel were excised and purified using the DNA Gel Extraction Kit (SIMGEN, Hangzhou, China), followed by Sanger sequencing to identify the sequences of the splicing products.

Results

The family pedigree is shown in Fig. 2A. STR analysis confirmed the absence of maternal cellular contamination in the amniotic fluid (supplementary material). The fetus’s karyotype was normal. Screening of WES data identified two intronic variants in RAB3GAP2, c.812–2 A > G and c.304 + 5G > T, both associated with cataracts. Sanger sequencing confirmed these variants (Fig. 2B). The c.812–2 A > G variant was inherited from the father, and the c.304 + 5G > T variant was inherited from the mother. NM_012414.4:c.812–2 A > G is found at an extremely low frequency in the Exome Sequencing Project, 1000 Genomes, and ExAC databases, consistent with the PM2 supporting evidence in the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines. This variant occurs in intron 9 at the splice acceptor site within the highly conserved GT-AG dinucleotide sequence, a canonical splice site, and is generally considered pathogenic [28–30]. According to the ACMG guidelines, RAB3GAP2: c.812–2 A > G was classified as a likely pathogenic variant (PVS1+PM2_Supporting). The c.304 + 5G > T variant is located in intron 3 of RAB3GAP2. This rare variant has no available phenotypic records. To assess its potential clinical impact, bioinformatics tools were first used to predict its pathogenicity. SpliceAI, a deep learning-based tool considered one of the most accurate methods for predicting splicing variants [31], was employed. A ΔScore ≥ 0.5 indicates a likely disruption in splicing. For NM_012414.4(RAB3GAP2): c.304 + 5G > T, SpliceAI predicted a donor loss ΔScore of 0.95 and an acceptor loss ΔScore of 0.82, suggesting a high likelihood of abnormal splicing. MutationTaster and CADD also predicted the variant to be deleterious. Additionally, NetGene2, SpliceRover, and ASSP all indicated that RAB3GAP2: c.304 + 5G > T would result in the loss of the donor splice site in intron 3.

Following the induced abortion of the fetus, an ophthalmologist performed an ocular examination of the fetal tissues. White spots with cloudy opacity were observed in the pupil area of both eyes (Fig. 1B), consistent with the prenatal diagnosis of congenital cataracts. RAB3GAP2 is widely expressed in various tissues, particularly in the brain and aorta. To investigate the effect of RAB3GAP2: c.304 + 5G > T on transcription, RNA-seq analysis was conducted on fetal tissues with high RAB3GAP2 expression. However, the RNA-seq analysis did not detect RAB3GAP2 expression. Subsequently, considering that the fetus’s mother was a heterozygous carrier of RAB3GAP2: c.304 + 5G > T, RT-PCR was performed on her peripheral blood.

To study the effect of the c.304 + 5G > T variant on in vivo transcription, peripheral blood samples from the mother, a heterozygous carrier of the variant, were analyzed. Reverse transcription PCR revealed an abnormal transcript, showing that the variant caused exon 3 skipping (Fig. 3). A minigene experiment was also conducted to further examine the impact of the variant on in vitro transcription. The results indicated that c.304 + 5G > T disrupts normal splicing of RAB3GAP2 (Fig. 4). The variant led to three abnormal transcripts in HeLa and 293T cells: (1) a 66 bp retention on the left side of intron 3, which did not alter the reading frame but retained 22 amino acids in the protein; (2) a 4 bp deletion on the right side of exon 3, resulting in a frameshift that introduced a premature stop codon, producing a truncated protein of 122 amino acids; (3) the skipping of exon 3, causing a frameshift and the generation of an early termination codon, leading to a truncated protein of 82 amino acids. The RT-PCR results aligned with those of the minigene assay. These findings demonstrate that c.304 + 5G > T causes skipping of exon 3 (124 bp), generating a premature stop codon within exon 4. This results in a frameshift in the open reading frame, producing a truncated protein of 82 amino acids. The variant is predicted to trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay [32]. Both RT-PCR and minigene assays confirmed the deleterious effect of RAB3GAP2: c.304 + 5G > T on transcription (PS3). According to the ACMG guidelines, RAB3GAP2: c.304 + 5G > T was classified as a likely pathogenic variant (PS3+PM2_Supporting+PM3) [33, 34].

Discussion

Ultrasonography is a vital tool for prenatal screening of fetal malformations, offering simplicity, effectiveness, and non-invasiveness for both the pregnant woman and the fetus [35, 36]. In this case, a systematic ultrasound screening was performed on the fetus, along with a targeted ultrasound examination of the fetal face. Multiplanar imaging of the lenses revealed abnormal acoustic patterns in both lenses, with enhanced echoes, a characteristic feature of congenital cataracts. Congenital cataracts are one of the most common congenital ocular malformations [37]. The fetal lens begins to develop by the third week of pregnancy [38], originating from ectodermal cells. The ectodermal cells in contact with the optic vesicle thicken to form the lens plate, which then invaginates and differentiates into the lens vesicle [9, 38]. On ultrasound, the normal fetal lens appears as central anechoic or hypoechoic, with circular hyperechoic edges [3, 7, 35]. In the presence of cataracts, enhanced echoes are observed within the lens. Depending on the location and morphological features of lens opacity, congenital cataracts can be classified into three types: (1) completely hyperechoic lens; (2) punctate or clustered echogenic features; and (3) a “double ring” sign, characterized by regular or irregular hyperechoic regions [3]. In this case, the bilateral lenses of the fetus displayed clusters of dense echoes, consistent with the second type of congenital cataract.

Following the detection of congenital cataracts via ultrasound, WES was performed to explore the genetic cause. Two intronic splice site variants in RAB3GAP2, c.812–2 A > G and c.304 + 5G > T, were identified. RAB3GAP2 expression during fetal development is critical for the normal formation of the ocular lens and retina, and loss of its function results in ocular abnormalities, including congenital cataracts [39–41]. Therefore, it is likely that the congenital cataracts in this fetus are linked to mutations in RAB3GAP2. No other developmental defects were observed in the fetus aside from the ocular abnormalities. Furthermore, both identified variants were splicing mutations in RAB3GAP2. Available evidence suggests that less disruptive variants in RAB3GAP2, such as missense and splice-site variants, are more commonly associated with MS, with a smaller proportion linked to WARBM [19, 23, 42]. The current phenotype of the fetus aligns more closely with MS, which is characterized by milder clinical symptoms. However, it is also possible that some symptoms are not yet apparent due to the fetus’s early gestational stage. In 2005, researchers investigating RAB3GAP2 expression in zebrafish found it to be specifically expressed in the eyes and central nervous system, highlighting its critical role in neural development [43]. Abnormalities in eye and neural development are likely related to impaired neurotransmitter exocytosis, a process in which RAB3GAP2 is involved. Studies in Drosophila melanogaster embryos have shown that mutations in RAB3GAP2 result in the blockage of neurotransmitter release, potentially disrupting the development of the optic lobe [15].

Currently, there are no reports regarding the clinical significance of the RAB3GAP2: c.304 + 5G > T variant. This study aimed to investigate the clinical pathogenicity of this variant, which is located at a noncanonical splice site. It is well-documented that variations in intronic sequences can produce aberrant mRNAs by altering the original splice sites or activating cryptic splice sites, which can lead to changes in protein function or localization [29, 44]. In silico prediction indicated that c.304 + 5G > T is deleterious and is predicted to disrupt the donor splice site in intron 3. To examine the in vivo effect of RAB3GAP2: c.304 + 5G > T on transcription, RNA sequencing was conducted on fetal tissues. Unfortunately, RNA-seq analysis failed to detect RAB3GAP2 expression in the fetus. This failure may be attributed to several factors, including technical limitations, insufficient sample collection, or RNA degradation in the specimen. Given that the mother was a heterozygous carrier of RAB3GAP2: c.304 + 5G > T, RT-PCR analysis was performed on her peripheral blood. Sanger sequencing of the variant transcript confirmed exon 3 skipping, consistent with the in silico predictions. RT-PCR analysis demonstrated that c.304 + 5G > T disrupts splicing in peripheral blood leukocytes. However, this may not fully represent the physiological splicing pattern in ocular tissues. The minigene assay further demonstrated that c.304 + 5G > T affects normal splicing of mRNA, resulting in abnormal transcripts, such as exon 3 skipping, in 293T and HeLa cells. This finding further supports that c.304 + 5G > T disrupts RAB3GAP2 splicing. The c.304 + 5G > T variant causes abnormal splicing of RAB3GAP2, resulting in mRNA with a premature termination codon, producing a truncated protein (p.Ser83Ter). This variant is predicted to trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, leading to the loss of gene function [45].

In conclusion, this study reports a case of congenital cataracts in a fetus, in which two potentially pathogenic compound heterozygous variants (c.304 + 5G > T and c.812–2 A > G) were detected in RAB3GAP2 through WES technology. The findings suggest that c.304 + 5G > T disrupts the normal splicing pattern of RAB3GAP2. Based on ACMG guidelines, this variant is classified as likely pathogenic and is strongly correlated with congenital cataracts. However, as this study is based on a single family, further cases are necessary to confirm the broader clinical impact of this variant.
