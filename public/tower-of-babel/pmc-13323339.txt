Formulation and in vitro-in vivo assessment of a novel monoammonium glycyrrhizinate controlled-porosity osmotic pump
Authors: Xuan Lu, Hongfei Yang, Jian-li Chen, Ya-jun Chen, Xing-xing Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to prepare and evaluate a novel monoammonium glycyrrhizinate (MAG) controlled-porosity osmotic pump (CPOP) with controlled-release properties. Capsule shells were prepared by the dip-coating method method, and the key variables affecting in vitro release were optimized using a Box-Behnken design-response surface methodology. In vitro release behavior was evaluated, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to examine shell morphology before and after dissolution. Pharmacokinetic behavior in rabbits, molecular docking, and solid-state characterization by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) were also performed. The optimized formulation exhibited a near zero-order release profile of MAG over 12 h in vitro, and the release curve closely matched the target profile. In rabbits, the self-prepared capsules reduced the peak plasma concentrations of MAG and its active metabolite glycyrrhetinic acid (GA) and significantly prolonged mean residence time compared with the reference formulation, indicating marked controlled-release behavior in vivo. Molecular docking confirmed stable binding of GA to nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), providing a mechanistic basis for the pharmacological benefits associated with prolonged in vivo exposure. DSC and XRD further showed that MAG remained in a stable crystalline state, with no crystal-form transition or incompatibility with the excipients during preparation. This study provides a promising advanced drug-delivery system for MAG, enhances understanding of its pharmacological advantages, and offers useful guidance for the development of controlled-release formulations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51202-w.

Introduction

Monoammonium glycyrrhizinate (MAG), a second-generation extract derived from the root of the traditional Chinese medicine Radix Glycyrrhiza (licorice), exhibits a variety of pharmacological activities, including hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects1,2. At present, its dosage forms mainly include injections, conventional capsules, and tablets3–5. However, injections are associated with poor acceptance and adherence in patients with chronic liver disease, whereas conventional oral formulations usually need to be taken more than once daily. This can be particularly challenging for patients requiring long-term therapy, especially elderly patients who may forget their dosing times. Moreover, frequent administration may lead to large fluctuations in plasma drug concentrations, potentially increasing safety risks, encouraging inappropriate medication use, and ultimately aggravating liver injury.

Controlled-porosity osmotic pump (CPOP) formulations are characterized by zero-order release kinetics and can maintain stable plasma drug concentrations, making them highly attractive oral controlled-release systems6,7. The CPOP investigated in this study is a novel capsule-based osmotic pump dosage form that integrates capsule technology with controlled-porosity osmotic pump principles. It can be prepared without tableting or coating, which simplifies the manufacturing process, lowers production costs, and allows the capsule shell and fill to be prepared separately, thereby providing flexible dosing and formulation options8,9. As an important advance in modern pharmaceutical technology, CPOP systems enable constant-rate drug delivery, enhance drug efficacy, reduce dosing frequency, and improve the overall treatment experience10,11. Their stable release pattern helps maintain effective drug concentrations in target tissues, reduces drug wastage, and alleviates the burden associated with frequent administration. These advantages are particularly relevant for patients requiring long-term treatment, as well as for elderly and pediatric patients, because they may improve adherence, therapeutic outcomes, and medication safety12. With continued advances in formulation technology, the application prospects of CPOP systems are expected to expand further.

Drug release from CPOP formulations is driven by the osmotic pressure difference across the semipermeable capsule shell and is expected to follow zero-order kinetics. Because release and disposition involve dissolution, diffusion, metabolism, and subsequent systemic exposure, metabolite characteristics are also important when evaluating efficacy and safety. In the case of MAG, the active metabolite glycyrrhetinic acid (GA) may influence drug distribution, excretion, bioavailability, and overall therapeutic response13. Therefore, metabolite-related properties should be considered during formulation design and optimization to ensure effective delivery and patient safety. Previous oral formulations of GA have shown distinct advantages, whereas a CPOP system may offer additional benefits through sustained and predictable release14,15.

Given these considerations, there is a clear need to develop a zero-order controlled-release formulation that can prolong the action of MAG, reduce dosing frequency, minimize fluctuations in plasma concentration, and improve patient compliance and safety. Accordingly, this study combined MAG with a novel CPOP dosage form. The preparation process was optimized using Box-Behnken design-response surface methodology, and the release mechanism and pharmacokinetic behavior were investigated through in vitro release studies and determination of plasma drug concentrations in rabbits.

Materials and methods

Selection of preparation methods for CPOP capsule shell

In this study, the preparation methods for the CPOP shells were meticulously selected and optimized. The experimental materials comprised cellulose acetate (CAS 9004-35-7, sourced from Shanghai Aladdin), PEG2000 (CAS 25322-68-3, obtained from Shanghai Macklin), PEG400 (also CAS 25322-68-3, but from Shanghai Aladdin), acetone (CAS 67-64-1, supplied by Shantou Xilong), anhydrous ethanol (CAS 64-17-5, acquired from Wuxi Zhanwang), and size 0 capsule shells (provided by Ningbo Jiangnan). The equipment utilized during the process included an ultrasonic cleaner (model KQ5200DE, manufactured by Kunshan), an electronic balance (Tianjin Tianma, model Jinzhi 00000297), and a drying oven (manufactured by Shanghai Boxun).

To fabricate the capsule shells, a uniform coating solution was first prepared using the aforementioned materials[16]. This solution was then employed to coat size 0 gelatin capsules through two distinct methods: dip-coating method and perfusion. In the dip-coating method method, the capsules were immersed into the coating solution, followed by drying and further processing. Alternatively, in the perfusion technique, the solution was infused into the capsules, subsequently scraped to ensure a uniform coating, freeze-dried, and then processed. Both methods were assessed for their ability to produce capsule shells with satisfactory molding and appearance quality.

After the coating process, the CPOP shells were rigorously evaluated for their molding quality, including the uniformity and smoothness of the coating, as well as the absence of defects such as cracks or bubbles. The appearance quality, including color and transparency, was also inspected to ensure that the final product met the desired aesthetic standards. These evaluations were crucial in selecting the most suitable preparation method for the capsule shells, ensuring that they possessed the necessary structural integrity and visual appeal for further use in the controlled-release drug delivery system.

Establishment of cumulative release percentage method - selection of dissolution medium

The current study aims to establish a method for determining the release profile of ammonium glycyrrhizinate and optimize its dissolution medium. Conducted by Operators A to E, the experiment utilized UV-visible spectrophotometry to investigate the full-wavelength scan and linear relationship of ammonium glycyrrhizinate in various media: pH 1.2 hydrochloric acid, pH 6.8 phosphate buffer, and distilled water. Reagents used were sodium hydroxide (Shantou Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd., CAS No. 1310-73-2), potassium dihydrogen phosphate (Shantou Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd., CAS No. 7778-77-0), hydrochloric acid (Suzhou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., CAS No. 7647-01-0), glycine and methionine (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. and Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., CAS No. 56-40-6 and 348-67-4, respectively), and ammonium glycyrrhizinate (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., CAS No. 53956-04-0). Instruments included a numerical control ultrasonic cleaner (Kunshan Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., model KQ5200DE), an electronic balance (Tianjin Tianma Hengji Instrument Co., Ltd., Jinzhi 00000297), an intelligent dissolution tester (Tianjin Chuangxing Electronic Equipment Manufacturing Co., Ltd., model ZRC-8ST), and a UV-visible spectrophotometer (Beijing Ruili Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd., model UV-1601). The study first identified the optimal absorption wavelength of ammonium glycyrrhizinate in different media via full-wavelength scanning, plotted standard curves to examine linear relationships, and determined apparent solubility to screen the optimal dissolution medium, laying the groundwork for future cumulative release percentage tests of CPOP.

Establishment of release method - precision and repeatability testing

The study aims to develop a precise and reproducible method for ammonium glycyrrhizinate release profiling, conducted by five operators using UV spectrophotometry. Materials included ammonium glycyrrhizinate standard (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., CAS No. 53956-04-0). The instruments used were an ultrasonic cleaner (KQ5200DE), an electronic balance (Jinzhi 00000297), and a UV spectrophotometer (UV-1601). Precision was assessed by measuring absorbance of standards (50, 100, 250 µg/mL) intra-day (5 times) and inter-day (5 consecutive days). Reproducibility was tested by preparing six replicate 100 µg/mL solutions and measuring absorbance, calculating the RSD.

Establishment of release rate method - sample recovery and stability test

To establish a release testing method for ammonium glycyrrhizinate, we conducted recovery and stability studies using UV spectrophotometry. Operators A, B, C, D, and E participated in the experiments. The ammonium glycyrrhizinate standard (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., CAS No. 53956-04-0, lot number not specified) was the primary reagent. The instruments used included a numerical control ultrasonic cleaner (Kunshan Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., model KQ5200DE), an electronic balance (Tianjin Tianma Hengji Instrument Co., Ltd., Jinzhi 00000297), and a UV spectrophotometer (Beijing Ruili Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd., model UV-1601). For the recovery study, ammonium glycyrrhizinate standards of 50 µg/mL, 100 µg/mL, and 250 µg/mL were prepared and spiked with additional ammonium glycyrrhizinate (100 µg/mL) in volumes of 1 mL, 5 mL, and 10 mL, respectively. The absorbance of the mixed solutions was measured to calculate the recovery rates. For the stability study, a 100 µg/mL solution of ammonium glycyrrhizinate was prepared and its absorbance was measured at 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h to assess concentration changes and calculate the RSD value. Determination method of cumulative release percentage

An ultraviolet (UV) spectrophotometry method was developed for the determination of the in vitro release of MAG according to the General Principles for Dissolution and Release Tests 0931 of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition). Three sets of 10 mg MAG standards were weighed precisely and dissolved in hydrochloric acid solution at pH 1.2, phosphate buffer solution at pH 6.8, and distilled water to form a 1 mg/mL solution, respectively. The three standards were scanned with an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (UV-1601, Beijing Ruili Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) at 190–500 nm with a scanning interval of 1.0 nm, and the UV absorption profiles were plotted, and the optimum absorption peaks were selected to determine the optimum absorption wavelengths. The linearity, accuracy, precision, reproducibility, recovery, and stability of the method were validated in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition). The in vitro drug release profile was plotted using GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0, GraphPad Software, LLC, San Diego, CA, USA; https://www.graphpad.com).

In vitro MAG content determination method

A method for the determination of MAG using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was developed through a literature review17 and preliminary experiments. The standard solution of 0.5 mg/mL monoammonium glycyrrhizinate was diluted into 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 150, 500 µg/mL with distilled water, and 10 µL was injected into a HPLC instrument (LC-20 A, Shimadzu Instruments Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan), and the chromatograms were recorded with the concentration of the injection (X) as the horizontal coordinate and the peak area of monoammonium glycyrrhizinate (Y) as the vertical coordinate. The linear regression equation was calculated and the standard curve was plotted using the feed concentration (X) as the horizontal coordinate and the peak area of monoammonium glycyrrhizinate (Y) as the vertical coordinate. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: a C18 analytical column (4.6 mm × 150 mm, 5 μm) was used, with a mobile phase composed of acetonitrile and a 0.01 mol/L phosphoric acid solution (volume ratio 40:60), a flow rate of 1 mL/min, a column temperature maintained at 30 °C, a detection wavelength of 252 nm, an injection volume of 10 µL, and a detection time of 10 min. The linearity, specificity, accuracy, precision, reproducibility, recovery, and stability of the method were validated in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition).

Single-factor experiments

With 25 mg of MAG and Cellulose acetate (CA) as the main capsule materials, the types and amounts of pore-forming agents (Polyethylene glycol [PEG] 2000, PEG4000, and PEG6000), solubilizers (PEG400 and polysorbate 80), plasticizers (dibutyl phthalate, triethyl citrate, and tributyl citrate), osmotic agents (sodium chloride, lactose, and sucrose), and fillers (microcrystalline cellulose and mannitol), as well as the amounts of swelling agents (polyethylene oxide) and retardants (sodium carboxymethylcellulose) were examined and optimized. Cumulative release percentage tests were performed for each factor to determine the degree of fit of the in vitro cumulative release of the prepared capsules to the zero-order release equation, and the factor with the best fit was selected.

Box-behnken design-response surface experiment

A series of experiments were conducted to optimize compound ammonium glycyrrhizinate osmotic pump capsules using RSM. Based on initial experiments, a Box-Behnken design was implemented with four key factors: CA (Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical), DEP (Tianjin Damao), PEO (Shandong Yousuo), and NaCl (Tianjin Bodi), each at three levels using Design Expert software(Version 10, Stat-Ease, Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA; https://www.statease.com). The coded and actual levels of these independent variables are summarized in Table S1. The objective was to identify the optimal preparation process based on cumulative release within 12 h. Capsules were prepared using specific materials sourced from various companies, and dissolution tests were conducted according to Chinese Pharmacopoeia standards. Based on single-factor experiments, four factors affecting in vitro release were selected and a Box-Behnken design experiment was conducted using Design-Expert software. The optimum formulation was validated by preparing CPOP and determining the in vitro cumulative release18.

Capsule shell surface observation and cross-section

The capsule shells before and after dissolution were thoroughly dried at 40 °C for 2 h, sprayed with gold under vacuum and the surface structure was observed under a scanning electron microscope (SEM). In addition, after cutting the optimal prescription capsule shell before and after the dissolution test to prepare the outer surface samples, the capsule shell was crushed under liquid nitrogen to prepare the cross-section samples, and the complete cross-section was obtained, dried at 40 °C for 2 h, and placed under the SEM to observe the ultramicro-morphology before and after the external dissolution of the capsule shell, and the imaging results were utilized to optimize the prescription of the compound MAG CPOP for the evaluation of the surface and the cross-section.

In vitro zero-order controlled release characteristics investigation

The cumulative release of MAG from the CPOP was evaluated at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 h. At each sampling time point, the absorbance of MAG in the release medium was measured by ultraviolet (UV) spectrophotometry, the cumulative drug-release percentage was calculated, and the in vitro release profile was plotted. The cumulative release-time data for the optimized MAG CPOP were then fitted to zero-order, first-order, and Higuchi release models. The models were compared using the correlation coefficient (R2); the closer the r value was to 1.00, the better the fit, and the release characteristics of the capsules were determined accordingly.

Pharmacokinetic study of MAG CPOP in rabbits

To establish a high-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (HPLC-Q-Tof-MS) method for MAG quantification, operators A-E optimized the m/z values of the precursor and fragment ions. Ammonium glycyrrhizinate (Shanghai Yuanye, CAS 53956-04-0) was dissolved in purified water to obtain a 100 µg/mL stock solution, diluted to 1 µg/mL, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an InertSustain AQ-C18 column (1.9 μm, 2.1 × 5 0 mm) with a water-acetonitrile mobile phase (5:5, v/v) at 0.3 mL/min and 20 °C using an HPLC-Q-Tof-MS 9030 system (Shimadzu). Full-scan (SCAN) mode (50–1000 m/z, 1 min) was used to identify the parent ion, followed by multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) to detect fragment ions at different collision energies (CEs) over 50–1000 m/z for 1 min, with specific CE settings of 35 ± 17. Turbo Spray ion source parameters included curtain gas (CUR) 40.0 psi, collision-activated dissociation gas (CAD) at medium, ion spray voltage (IS) 5500 V, source temperature (TEM) 550 °C, and Gas 1/Gas 2 at 55/50 psi. The final m/z values were confirmed by comparing the total ion current chromatograms obtained in the two acquisition modes.

A method was developed for the determination of MAG and its active metabolite GA in blank rabbit plasma using high-performance liquid chromatography quadrupole-time-of-flight mass spectrometry (HPLC-Q-TOF-MS, Shimadzu LCMS-9030). A 90 µL aliquot of blank rabbit plasma was spiked separately with 10 µL of the standard working solution containing MAG or GA, to prepare matrix-matched calibration standards and quality control samples. The mixture was mixed thoroughly, followed by the addition of 300 µL of methanol containing the internal standard glibenclamide (200 ng/mL). The mixture was vortexed for 5 min and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 20 min. A 200 µL aliquot of the supernatant was collected for LC-MS analysis.

Chromatographic Conditions: The column was an InertSustain AQ-C18 column (1.9 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm). Mobile Phase A was 5 mmol·L⁻¹ ammonium acetate aqueous solution, and Mobile Phase B was acetonitrile. Gradient elution was performed as follows: 0–1 min, 60%–10% A; 1–2 min, 10% A; 2–3 min, 10%–60% A; 3–8 min, 60% A. The flow rate was 0.3 mL·min⁻¹, the column temperature was 40 °C, and the injection volume was 3 µL.

Mass Spectrometry Conditions: Electrospray ionization (ESI) source in negative ion mode; Curtain Gas (CUR): 40.0 psi; Ion Spray Voltage (IS): 5500 V; Temperature (TEM): 550 °C; Ion Source Gas (Gas 1/Gas 2): 55/50 psi; detection in selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode with characteristic ions of m/z 410.1946 (MAG), m/z 469.3329 (GA) and m/z 492.1626 (glibenclamide). The method was validated for specificity, linearity, precision, accuracy, matrix effect, and stability in accordance with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Guidance for Bioanalytical Method Validation (2018).

Six healthy male New Zealand White rabbits, weighing 1.8 ± 0.15 kg, were purchased from Anhui Provincial Laboratory Animal Center (Hefei, China) and randomly divided into [19] two groups: a prepared MAG osmotic pump capsule group (n = 3) and a reference group of MAG tablets (Menon®, Minophagen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) (n = 3). Prior to any experimental procedures, anesthesia was induced via intravenous injection of pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 30 mg/kg to minimize rabbit stress during repeated blood sampling and ensure pharmacokinetic data accuracy. Unanesthetized rabbits would experience significant stress and movement during repeated blood collection, which could alter drug distribution and metabolism and lead to biased results. This dose is consistent with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.After 12 h of fasting, the two groups were orally administered with MAG controlled-porosity osmotic pump capsules and reference Menon® tablets, respectively, at a dose of 5 mg/kg (expressed as MAG). Blood samples (approximately 0.5 mL each) were collected from the marginal ear vein of the rabbits before administration and at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 10, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h post-administration. The blood was placed in heparinized tubes, centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 10 min to separate plasma, and the upper plasma was stored at -20 °C for subsequent analysis. Upon completion of the pharmacokinetic study, all rabbits were euthanized by an intravenous overdose of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg ). Euthanasia complied with animal welfare norms to avoid long-term pain from multiple sampling injuries.

The animal study was reported in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. The experimental protocols were approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Hefei Normal University (Approval No. 2024LLSP010) and complied with the Principles of Laboratory Animal Care issued by the National Institutes of Health (NIH Publication No. 85 − 23, revised 1996). Plasma concentrations of MAG and GA were calculated from the matrix-matched calibration curves established above, and pharmacokinetic parameters were computed using Phoenix WinNonlin software (Version 7.0, Certara USA, Inc., Princeton, NJ, USA; https://www.certara.com/software/phoenix-winnonlin/).

Solid-state characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to characterize the crystal form and physical state of the MAG bulk drug, the blank physical mixture of excipients, and the optimized MAG CPOP formulation. Measurements were obtained with a SmartLab SE X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The scanning range was 5°-60° (2θ) at 2°/min, with a tube voltage of 40 kV and a tube current of 40 mA. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was used to evaluate the thermal behavior, crystal-form stability, and excipient compatibility of MAG on a Q2000/DSC2500 calorimeter (TA Instruments, USA). Approximately 5 mg of each sample was accurately weighed and sealed in an aluminum crucible, with an empty sealed crucible used as the reference. All samples were heated from 30 °C to 300 °C at 10 °C/min under nitrogen at a flow rate of 50 mL/min.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking of MAG and GA with nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2, PDB: 1u6d) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ, PDB: 2xyj) was performed using AutoDock Vina software (Version 1.5.7; https://vina.scripps.edu).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0, GraphPad Software, LLC, San Diego, CA, USA; https://www.graphpad.com), and data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between the two groups were made using the t-test. Multi-group analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA to assess statistical differences, with P ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

In vitro release and content determination methods for MAG

The in vitro release of MAG was determined by UV spectrophotometry using water as the release medium and 254 nm as the detection wavelength. Good linearity was observed over the concentration range of 0–200 µg/mL (Fig. 1A and B). Accuracy, precision, reproducibility, recovery, and stability all met the requirements for release determination.

A high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method was developed for MAG determination. The excipients in the capsules did not interfere with the assay, and satisfactory linearity was obtained over the concentration range of 5.00–500 µg/mL (Fig. 1C, D and E, and F). The precision, accuracy, and stability of the method met the requirements of the Principles and Requirements for the Validation of Analytical Methods for Quality Control of Chemical Drugs.

MAG CPOP preparation

The capsule shells prepared by the impregnation and filling methods were compared. Shells obtained by the filling method showed poor morphology and a marked gap between the cap and body. Therefore, the dip-coating method method was selected for shell preparation because it yielded capsules with good shell-formation rates, appropriate hardness (Fig. 2A), and stable performance across batches. The effects of different excipients in both the shell and the fill were then examined in single-factor experiments. The results showed that the concentration of cellulose acetate (CA) and the amounts of diethyl phthalate (DEP), polyethylene oxide (PEO), and sodium chloride (NaCl) all influenced drug release, as judged by the goodness of fit of the cumulative in vitro release data to the zero-order equation (Fig. 2B and C).

Based on the single-factor experiments, a four-factor, three-level Box-Behnken design-response surface study was performed. The experimental design matrix and the resulting cumulative release percentages for the 29 runs are presented in Table S2. Quadratic multiple regression using Design-Expert software yielded the following cumulative-release equation: y = 0.9716–2.11a − 0.8758b + 0.4467c + 0.5092d − 1.05ab + 0.1825ac + 0.4150ad − 0.1575bc − 0.5825bd + 0.8450 cd − 5.10a2 − 3.11b2 − 0.7978c2 − 2.85d2. The statistical significance and adequacy of this quadratic model were confirmed by Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), as detailed in Table S3, which showed a highly significant model (P < 0.0001) and a non-significant lack of fit (P = 0.3012). Significant interactions were observed between CA and DEP, CA and PEO, and CA and NaCl (Fig. 3A). The optimal formulation consisted of CA, 1.86 g; acetone/anhydrous ethanol, 8:1 (v/v); DEP, 0.68 mL; polyethylene glycol 400 (PEG400), 0.2 mL; polyethylene glycol 4000 (PEG4000), 0.6 mg; MAG, 25 mg; PEO, 35 mg; microcrystalline cellulose (MCC), 40 mg; sodium carboxymethylcellulose (CMC-Na), 5 mg; and NaCl, 54 mg. The CPOP prepared with this formulation achieved a cumulative release of 99.75%, which was very close to the theoretical prediction of 100%, demonstrating the feasibility of the process. The formation of pores on the capsule shell surface is closely related to the composition of excipients (CA, DEP, PEO, NaCl). The interaction between CA and DEP regulates the pore size and distribution, PEO affects the pore connectivity, and NaCl adjusts the osmotic pressure inside the capsule, which together promote the formation of a uniform pore structure and further achieve stable zero-order release. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the optimized capsule shells before and after dissolution showed smooth outer surfaces and no cracks or pores in cross-section before dissolution, whereas after dissolution the shell remained intact but displayed a small number of surface pores (Fig. 3B). These pores were generated by dissolution of the pore-forming agent PEG4000 during in vitro release, thereby providing channels for MAG diffusion from the capsule interior into the release medium and enabling the zero-order controlled-release behavior of the formulation.

Pharmacokinetic study of MAG CPOP in rabbits

A high-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry method was developed for the simultaneous determination of MAG and its active metabolite glycyrrhetinic acid (GA) in blank rabbit plasma, and selected ion monitoring (SIM) was chosen for quantitative analysis.

The analytes were detected within 5 min (Fig. 4A and B). The specificity, linearity, precision, accuracy, matrix effect, and stability of the method met the criteria of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Guidance for Bioanalytical Method Validation.

After oral administration of the self-prepared MAG CPOP capsules and the reference Menon® tablets (5 mg/kg, expressed as MAG) to rabbits, plasma concentration-time data for MAG and GA were analyzed using a noncompartmental model in Phoenix WinNonlin software (Version 7.0, Certara USA, Inc., Princeton, NJ, USA; https://www.certara.com/software/phoenix-winnonlin/) to obtain the key pharmacokinetic parameters. In the reference group, MAG showed a peak concentration (Cmax) of 62 ng/mL and a mean residence time (MRT) of 0.91 h, whereas the CPOP group showed a lower Cmax of 46 ng/mL and a longer MRT of 2.78 h. For GA, the reference group showed a Cmax of 112.33 ng/mL and an MRT of 15.47 h, compared with a Cmax of 96.5 ng/mL and an MRT of 20.6 h in the CPOP group. Independent-samples t-tests showed that the CPOP significantly reduced the Cmax of both MAG and GA and significantly prolonged their MRT values (P < 0.05), indicating that the prepared MAG CPOP markedly prolonged in vivo residence time and produced satisfactory controlled-release effects.

Molecular docking

Based on the pharmacokinetic behavior of MAG CPOP in rabbits, especially the prolonged in vivo residence time of GA, molecular docking was performed to explore the possible pharmacological basis of the formulation. Considering the time-concentration relationship and the expected anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), two targets closely associated with the pathogenesis of hepatitis, were selected for docking on the basis of the literature20–22.

The binding energies of MAG with Nrf2 and PPARγ were − 12.37 and − 5.84 kcal/mol, respectively, whereas those of GA with Nrf2 and PPARγ were − 13.19 and − 10.34 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. 4D, using PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.5, https://pymol.org/). In general, binding energies below − 5 kcal/mol indicate stable ligand-receptor interactions. These results suggest that both MAG and GA can bind favorably to Nrf2 and PPARγ, with GA showing the stronger affinity for both targets. This finding provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for the sustained hepatoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects of the MAG CPOP formulation during prolonged in vivo exposure.

More broadly, the docking results support the view that sustained systemic exposure to MAG, and especially to GA, may contribute to prolonged pharmacological activity, including hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects23–25. The results also indicate that Nrf2 and PPARγ can recognize both ligands and form stable complexes through multiple intermolecular interactions, which may alter receptor conformation and function and subsequently trigger signaling pathways related to anti-inflammatory and antioxidant responses. Thus, the prolonged retention of the MAG CPOP in vivo, particularly the sustained effective blood concentration of GA, may be an important basis for its pharmacological benefits.

Solid-state characterization of MAG and the formulation

The XRD patterns of the different samples are shown in Fig. 5A. The MAG bulk drug exhibited sharp and distinct crystalline diffraction peaks at 2θ = 5.88°, 31.84°, and 35.44°, indicating the typical crystalline state of the raw material. The blank physical mixture of excipients also showed sharp crystalline peaks, confirming the crystalline nature of the excipients. In contrast, the optimized MAG CPOP formulation showed no sharp characteristic peaks of either MAG or the excipients, and only an amorphous diffuse halo was observed. This phenomenon may be attributed to the presence of MAG as microcrystals in the CPOP formulation, with crystal sizes below the detection limit of XRD. The DSC thermograms are shown in Fig. 5B. The MAG bulk drug exhibited a weak endothermic peak at 71.0 °C, corresponding to the glass-transition region, and a sharp endothermic melting peak at 238.8 °C, which reflects the melting process of MAG microcrystals. The blank physical mixture showed an endothermic peak at 71.4 °C, attributable to the melting of polyethylene glycol (PEG) excipients, and a strong endothermic peak at 244.8 °C, corresponding to melting/decomposition of the excipient matrix. For the optimized MAG CPOP formulation, the characteristic endothermic peaks of both MAG and the excipients were retained, with no obvious peak shift, no peak disappearance, and no new endothermic or exothermic events. These findings indicate that there was no obvious incompatibility between MAG and the excipients and that MAG maintained good crystal-form stability in the CPOP formulation.

Discussion

In this study, CPOP capsule shells were successfully fabricated by the dip-coating method method. The resulting shells showed a smooth, transparent, bubble-free appearance, as well as suitable hardness, excellent demolding performance, high molding yield, and a relatively simple preparation procedure. In particular, almost no visible morphological or dimensional deformation was observed before or after demolding. These results indicate that the dip-coating method method is suitable for preparing capsule shells with acceptable physical quality and process reproducibility.

During the single-factor experiments, the capsule shells had to be prepared simultaneously under identical conditions to avoid progressive drying and hardening, which could otherwise affect drug release. Cellulose acetate (CA) was selected as the main shell-forming material, and polyethylene glycol 4000 (PEG4000) was used as the pore-forming agent to enhance shell permeability. In the in vivo study, plasma concentrations of MAG and its active metabolite GA in rabbits were determined by HPLC-Q-Tof-MS. During optimization of the mass spectrometric conditions, both SIM and MRM modes were evaluated, and SIM provided the better response. Accordingly, MAG [M-H-NH4]²⁻ at m/z 410.19, GA [M-H]⁻ at m/z 469.33, and the internal standard glibenclamide [M-H]⁻ at m/z 492.16 were selected for quantitative analysis.

Full-scan mass spectrometry of MAG revealed consistent single-, double-, and triple-charged ions, among which the doubly charged ion [M-H-NH4]²⁻ gave the strongest response and was therefore selected for pharmacokinetic analysis. After optimization of the mass spectrometric conditions, a highly sensitive assay suitable for rabbit pharmacokinetic studies was established. The rabbit experiments showed that, compared with the reference formulation, oral administration of the MAG CPOP prolonged the residence time of both MAG and GA; in particular, the MRT of GA increased from 15.47 h in the reference group to 20.6 h in the prepared group, which is generally consistent with the report by Takeda S et al.26. Wang et al.27 reported that solid dispersions of GA with L-arginine and Soluplus improved both the solubility and bioavailability of GA, providing a promising oral anti-inflammatory formulation and offering useful insights for other drugs with low oral bioavailability. More recently, researchers28 assembled poorly soluble GA with its amphiphilic prodrug diammonium glycyrrhizinate (dG) into aqueous nanodispersions, increasing the apparent water solubility of GA to 549.0 µg/mL and achieving more than 80% cumulative dissolution within 5 min in vitro; this strategy substantially improved the dissolution behavior of GA and broadened its potential applications29. In addition, studies30 have shown that nanocrystals can enhance the oral bioavailability of GA. Other work has demonstrated that solid dispersions prepared with carboxymethyl starch (CMS) as a carrier can significantly improve the in vitro solubility and pharmacokinetic parameters of GA, including Cmax and area under the concentration-time curve from 0 to 24 h (AUC0-24 h). Compared with these solubility-enhancing approaches, the present study focused on controlled release of MAG through a CPOP system, thereby extending systemic exposure while maintaining a predictable release profile.

Furthermore, molecular docking suggested that GA can bind stably to Nrf2 and PPARγ, supporting its potential roles in hepatoprotection, anti-inflammatory activity, and immune regulation31. However, the in vivo functional effects of the prepared capsules still require validation in disease-relevant animal models. It also remains necessary to determine whether GA truly binds to Nrf2 and PPARγ in vivo and whether additional molecular targets are involved. Meanwhile, the XRD and DSC results confirmed that MAG remained physically stable in the CPOP formulation, with no evidence of crystal-form transformation or incompatibility with the excipients during preparation. Such solid-state stability is important for ensuring long-term storage stability, consistent dissolution behavior, and reliable in vivo efficacy, and thus provides an important material basis for the potential clinical application of MAG CPOP capsules.

Nrf2 and PPARγ were selected as docking targets because they play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of hepatitis and are closely associated with oxidative stress, inflammation, and lipid metabolism31–34. Nrf2 participates in cellular antioxidant defense mechanisms, whereas PPARγ is involved in regulation of fatty acid metabolism. Previous studies have shown that GA exerts significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects35, which is consistent with the present results. By characterizing the interactions of MAG and GA with these two targets, the docking analysis provides preliminary mechanistic support for the potential hepatoprotective effects of the CPOP formulation. More broadly, these findings may also be useful for the future development of more selective and effective therapeutic agents for hepatitis, with the potential to enhance efficacy while reducing adverse effects.

This study has several limitations. First, quantitative analysis of the SEM images, such as pore-size distribution and pore density, was not performed, which limits a more rigorous interpretation of the relationship between pore formation and in vitro release kinetics. Second, pharmacokinetic evaluation was conducted only in normal rabbits, and no in vivo pharmacodynamic studies were performed in liver disease models; these issues should be addressed in future work to verify the therapeutic efficacy of MAG CPOP. Third, the in vitro release test covered only 12 h, and the long-term stability of the MAG CPOP formulation remains to be investigated.

Conclusions

In summary, a novel MAG CPOP with zero-order release was successfully developed through formulation optimization. Owing to its simple preparation process and effective controlled-release design, the capsule showed satisfactory zero-order controlled-release behavior in rabbits while maintaining acceptable uniformity, permeability, and solid-state stability. The combined in vitro, pharmacokinetic, docking, and solid-state results support the feasibility of this formulation as a promising oral controlled-release delivery system for MAG. Nevertheless, further in vivo efficacy and mechanistic studies are still needed, including in vivo anti-hepatitis activity studies in animal models of hepatitis, long-term toxicity studies, and tissue distribution studies of MAG and GA, to provide more comprehensive experimental basis for the clinical application of the formulation.
