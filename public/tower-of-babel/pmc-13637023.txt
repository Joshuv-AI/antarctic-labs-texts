Non-Equilibrium Dynamics of the Time-Dependent Excitonic Coupling in Fluorescent Protein Dimers
Authors: Robson Christie, Cerys Murray, Youngchan Kim, Jaewoo Joo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We quantify the excitonic coupling in the homodimer of the dimeric Venus fluorescent protein using a quantum-classical hybrid workflow. Because the anionic chromophore carries appreciable double-excitation character, we obtain its site energy from domain-based local-pair-natural-orbital similarity-transformed equation-of-motion coupled-cluster theory (DLPNO-STEOM-CCSD), which places the in-protein bright ππ* state at 523.90 nm, in good agreement with the experimental absorption maximum near 515 nm and validated against an explicit triples-corrected EOM-CCSD­(fT) benchmark; single-excitation time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) blueshifts this state. Applying a transition-density coupling (TDC) formalism to the STEOM transition density and simulating the VenusA206 tandem-dimer construct (two β-barrels covalently joined by an inter-domain linker), the covalent tether holds the A206 interface docked throughout 1 ns of room-temperature dynamics. We obtain a thermally averaged coupling of J = 32.8 ± 1.6 cm–1 across the 1000-frame trajectory (Davydov splitting 2|J| = 65.6 ± 3.1 cm–1), representing a 19% near-field enhancement over the ensemble point-dipole estimate (J PDA = 27.6 ± 1.3 cm–1) at the 24.69 ± 0.32 Å chromophore centroid separation. Furthermore, we argue that a separation of time scales resolves the apparent theoretical tension between intermediate experimental excitonic couplings and the highly decoherent biological environment. While it has been hypothesized that the fluorescent protein β-barrel scaffold sustains coupling by shielding the chromophore from thermal fluctuations, we emphasize that the separation of time scales applies irrespective of the exact degree of environmental decoherence and dissipation. Collective photoexcitation imprints the Davydov splitting under optical-limit dielectric screening upon absorption, preceding bulk solvent relaxation and sub-picosecond environmental dephasing. To characterize the subsequent post-absorption evolution, we employ stochastic wavefunction simulations to model the transition from a delocalized exciton superposition to incoherent hopping between localized chromophore states.

Introduction

Excitation energy transfer in biological systems is conventionally understood through the competition between intermolecular excitonic coupling (J) and environmental vibrational dephasing. , When environmental fluctuations dominate, phase relationships randomize rapidly, restricting the system to the very weak-coupling regime characterized by incoherent Förster hopping. Conversely, the intermediate weak regime permits the formation of coherently delocalized excitonic eigenstates, manifesting spectroscopically as Davydov splitting, while the strong coupling regime gives rise to more pronounced modifications of the absorption spectrum. Because physiological environments are highly dynamic and structurally disordered, biophysics has historically assumed that ambient thermal noise precludes robust excitonic delocalization, confining such systems to the very weak coupling regime. ,

Homodimers of the yellow fluorescent protein variant dimeric Venus challenge this assumption. Operating at room temperature, these dimers exhibit distinct Davydov splitting in circular dichroism (CD) spectra, and photon antibunching statistics confirm that the interacting chromophores act as a collective quantum entity upon photon absorption. , These signatures indicate an interaction strength firmly within the intermediate excitonic regime. Couplings of this magnitude, and substantially larger, are documented in photosynthetic pigment–protein complexes − (for example, ∼100 cm–1 between neighboring bacteriochlorophylls in the FMO complex and ∼300 cm–1 in the LH2 B850 ring). In the FMO complex, however, this exciton and Davydov structure is resolved in linear absorption and circular-dichroism spectra recorded at 6 K, where homogeneous broadening is suppressed. The distinguishing feature of the Venus dimer is therefore not the magnitude of J but the persistence and spectral resolvability of its coherent excitonic signatures at room temperature, in an environment otherwise expected to induce strong decoherence.

To explain this phenomenon, it has been hypothesized that the fluorescent protein β-barrel scaffold plays a central role in sustaining inter-chromophore excitonic coupling by providing a structured dielectric shield that attenuates thermal fluctuations from the surrounding environment, thereby potentially extending excitonic decoherence times. While this protective shielding offers a compelling mechanism, we propose that the persistence of the excitonic coupling signatures can be reconciled by separating the physical time scales of exciton formation and subsequent dephasing, irrespective of the exact degree of environmental noise suppression. Photoexcitation acts as a collective, near-instantaneous vertical transition directly into a delocalized bright eigenstate, initially protected by the scaffold’s optical-limit dielectric screening. The Davydov splitting is an inherent property of the energy spectrum imprinted at this precise moment of absorption. As demonstrated by the stochastic trajectories in this work (see Figure ), the system subsequently undergoes strong environmental dephasing and rapid relaxation into a localized state. However, because this decoherence and the associated dielectric screening occur on sub-picosecond to picosecond time scales, they are significantly slower than the absorption event. Consequently, strong excitonic coupling and its spectroscopic signatures are observed despite the presence of strong, subsequent decoherence.

Accurately predicting the magnitude of the coupling that drives this initial delocalization requires examining the limitations of standard point-dipole interaction models. At the inter-chromophore separation in dimeric Venus (∼24.7 Å), the point-dipole approximation (PDA) provides a reasonable first-order estimate of the Coulombic interaction, but higher-order near-field multipolar contributions provide a modest correction to the overall coupling strength J. , That the PDA begins to break down once the inter-chromophore separation becomes comparable to the spatial extent of the transition density is well established; , our aim is to quantify the magnitude of this effect for the Venus dimer and, through an explicit Cartesian multipole decomposition, to evaluate how higher multipolar terms contribute. Capturing these effects rigorously requires integrating the full three-dimensional (3D) spatial distribution of the electron transition density, which maps the shape of the transition’s probability amplitude onto the 3D molecular structure.

Furthermore, because exciton formation precedes the macroscopic nuclear reorganization of the solvent (Debye relaxation τ D ≈ 8.3 ps), the initial Coulombic coupling is not damped by the static permittivity of water (εeq ≈ 78). Instead, it is screened only by the fast electronic polarization of the environment, corresponding to the optical dielectric limit (εopt ≈ 1.78). ,

Obtaining a quantitatively reliable site energy and transition density is itself non-trivial for this system: the deprotonated (anionic) chromophore of yellow fluorescent proteins carries appreciable double-excitation character, precisely the regime in which TDDFT, a single-excitation theory, is known to fail, systematically blueshifting the bright state relative to correlated wavefunction methods and experiment. We therefore compute the site energy with DLPNO-STEOM-CCSD, a correlated equation-of-motion coupled-cluster method whose treatment of double (and, in part, triple) excitations we outline in the Theoretical Framework [eq ] and detail in the Methods, and validate against an explicit triples-corrected EOM-CCSD­(fT) benchmark, evaluating the coupling from the resulting STEOM transition density.

In this work, we present a quantum-classical hybrid analysis of non-equilibrium dynamics of the time-dependent excitonic coupling between dimeric Venus molecules. By combining a transition-density coupling (TDC) formalism with a non-equilibrium dielectric framework, we quantify the near-field multipolar contributions to the excitonic coupling. We demonstrate that the interplay of optical-limit screening and transition-density geometry produces an intermediate coupling (J = 32.8 ± 1.6 cm–1, Davydov splitting 2|J| = 65.6 ± 3.1 cm–1) and show how non-equilibrium dynamics capture the subsequent decoherence and state localization.

Theoretical Framework

To describe the excitonic interactions within the dimer, we restrict our model to the single-exciton manifold, defining the local site basis as |1⟩ ≡|e L g R ⟩ and |2⟩ ≡|g L e R ⟩. This notation explicitly enforces the conservation of energy in a single-photon absorption subspace, ensuring that only a single excitation is shared between the two chromophores. The β-barrel of Venus has a diameter of ∼22 Å and a closest inter-chromophore approach of 15 Å in the crystal structure of the dimeric Venus fluorescent protein homodimer, effectively precluding significant short-range Dexter exchange interactions. Consequently, the electronic coupling J is accurately described as the Coulomb interaction between the transition densities of the individual monomers. The electron transition density, ρmg→e(r) , is obtained by integrating the product of the ground- and excited-state wavefunctions over the coordinates of N – 1 electrons in a monomer: 1 ρmg→e(r)=N∫...∫dr2...drNΨe*(r,r2,...,rN)Ψg(r,r2,...,rN) where Ψ g and Ψ e are the many-body electronic wavefunctions for the ground and excited states, respectively (m = L, R). The excited-state wavefunction Ψ e is itself a superposition of configurations obtained by promoting electrons out of the orbitals occupied in the ground-state reference Φ0 into unoccupied (virtual) orbitals 2 Ψe=c0Φ0+∑iaciaΦia+∑i<j,a<bcijabΦijab+∑i<j<k,a<b<ccijkabcΦijkabc+···, where i, j, k label occupied and a, b, c virtual orbitals: Φ i a promotes one electron (a singly excited, one-particle/one-hole configuration), Φ ij ab promotes two at once (a doubly excited, “2p2h” configuration), and Φ ijk abc three (triply excited, “3p3h”). Single-excitation theories (TDDFT and configuration-interaction singles) retain only the c i a amplitudes; when the true state carries appreciable weight in the doubly excited c ij ab block, as the anionic Venus chromophore does, they misplace it and, here, blueshift the bright ππ* state.

We therefore obtain Ψ e from DLPNO-STEOM-CCSD, a correlated coupled-cluster method that recovers the full double-excitation character, together with a partial subset of the triple excitations, that a single-excitation theory omits (the STEOM construction is summarized in the Methods). It thereby approaches the accuracy of an explicit full-triples treatment at a small fraction of the cost, which is decisive here: an explicit-triples calculation of the in-protein chromophore, embedded in the protein and solvent point-charge field, is computationally out of reach. We instead validate the method on the smaller, tractable gas-phase chromophore, where an explicit non-iterative triples correction [EOM-CCSD­(fT)] can be afforded: STEOM-CCSD reproduces the triples-corrected excitation energy and removes the overestimate of the bare doubles-level EOM-CCSD (Table ), confirming that it captures the higher-order excitation physics governing this transition.

Crucially, while the total electron density is a positive-definite observable representing the probability of finding an electron, the electron transition density implies an off-diagonal matrix element. Since ∫ρ g→e (r)d r = 0 due to the orthogonality of the ground and excited states, its spatial distribution, characterized by positive and negative phases, indicates the transition’s multipolar character. For example, weighting the transition density by the position vector yields the transition dipole moment (μ = ∫rρ g→e (r)d r), which dictates the far-field optical response. The full TDC is given by the 3D spatial integral 3 JTDC≈14πε(t)∬(ρLg⃗e(r))*ρRg→e(r′)|r−r′|drdr′ for time-dependent permittivity ε­(t). Formally, the electron transition density can be complex, and the conjugate ensures a real coupling strength. In our numerical implementation, the 3D continuous integral is evaluated following the Transition Density Cube (TDC) method introduced by Krueger et al., where space is discretized on a 3D Cartesian grid of voxel volume ΔV. The Coulomb integral is screened by a time-dependent effective permittivity ε­(t) that tracks the solvent response. The aqueous environment relaxes with a Debye time τ D ≈ 8.3 ps; because this time scale is orders of magnitude longer than the sub-picosecond time scale of excitation transfer, the solvent cannot undergo nuclear equilibration during the transfer. The permittivity is therefore initially governed by the optical dielectric constant (εopt ≈ 1.78) and only later approaches the static limit (εeq ≈ 78), as described by 6 1ε(t)=1εeq+(1εopt−1εeq)e−t/τD

Within the sub-picosecond window (t < 1 ps) over which electronic dephasing and population localization occur in our dynamical simulations, the inverse dielectric screening factor 1/ε­(t) changes only marginally, from 1/εopt ≈ 0.562 at t = 0 to ≈0.501 at t = 1 ps (an ∼11% decrease). Consequently, this small variation means that during the primary dephasing event (T 2 * = 60 fs) and initial site localization, the excitonic interaction is predominantly governed by the optical-limit coupling J(0). The slow, macroscopic solvent relaxation toward the fully screened static limit (εeq ≈ 78, where 1/ε ≈ 0.013) unfolds on the much longer τ D ≈ 8.3 ps time scale, well after coherent phase relationships have already been damped by environmental fluctuations.

Assuming a structurally identical homodimer where the ground- to first excited-state excitation energies are identical for each monomer (E L = E R = E), the excited-state Hamiltonian in the site basis is 7 Ĥsite(t)=(EJ(t)J(t)E) diagonalizing Ĥsite(t) yields the adiabatic (exciton) basis, where eigenstates are the symmetric (bright) and antisymmetric (dark) combinations such as |±⟩=12(|1⟩±|2⟩) . Photoexcitation is treated as a vertical transition directly into the delocalized bright state | + ⟩, and its energy separation is the Davydov splitting, given by ΔE(t) = 2|J(t)|. The dynamics are seeded with the optical-limit coupling at the instant of absorption, J(0). Over several picoseconds, the coupling J(t) relaxes as the solvent environment reorganizes from the initial optical dielectric response toward the static equilibrium limit, resulting in a gradual damping of the excitonic interaction.

Because the physical interactions between the system and its aqueous environment are local, we define the master equation (ME) with a Hermitian jump operator L̂=ℏγ/2σ̂z in the site basis and the pure dephasing rate γ = 1/T 2 * ( σ̂z for the Pauli-z operator and ℏ for the reduced Planck constant). For our simulations, a homogeneous pure-dephasing time of T 2 * = 60 fs was used, the rate at which environmental fluctuations randomize the relative phase of the two site states and damp the off-diagonal coherence ρ12, as distinct from the population-relaxation time T 1. While the rigid β-barrel of fluorescent proteins can partially shield chromophores from solvent fluctuations and extend coherence relative to unbound organic dyes, this conservatively fast 60 fs limit was chosen to align with empirical measurements of rapidly decaying electronic quantum coherence in solvated pigment–protein complexes at ambient temperatures. We stress that T 2 * enters as a phenomenological, order-of-magnitude parameter transferred from these analogous systems: no T 2 * has been measured for the Venus dimer, and, importantly, it is deliberately not extracted from our classical thermal trajectory. Homogeneous pure dephasing is a property of the quantum bath correlation function (it obeys detailed balance and is dominated by high-frequency intramolecular modes with ℏω ≫ k B T) and cannot be obtained rigorously from classical molecular dynamics, which samples equilibrium configurational statistics rather than the quantum bath response. What the classical ensemble does legitimately supply here is the complementary inhomogeneous broadening from the static spread of the coupling over thermally sampled geometries (the ±1.6 cm–1 distribution of J in Figure ); site-energy disorder is not sampled in the present 1 ns ensemble. Crucially, our argument never requires the precise value of T 2 *: it enters only as an upper bound on the dephasing time scale, so that the central claim, that exciton formation and the Davydov splitting are imprinted faster than the environment can dephase or dielectrically relax, holds for any T 2 * short compared with the ∼8.3 ps Debye time. We confirm this by sweeping T 2 * over 20–200 fs: the resulting coherence-decay times (∼0.02–0.2 ps) all remain orders of magnitude below the ∼8.3 ps Debye time, so T 2 * sets only the rate of localization in Figure , while the separation of time scales itself is preserved throughout. Our choice of jump operator effectively “measures” the excitation site, driving the state toward the local site basis, which emerges as the decoherence-resistant pointer basis (|1⟩ and |2⟩) since physical interactions are local. The system’s ensemble-averaged density matrix ρ̂(t) in the {|1⟩, |2⟩} basis evolves via the Lindblad equation, given by 8 dρ̂dt=−iℏ[Ĥsite(t),ρ̂]+L̂ρ̂L̂−12{L̂2,ρ̂}

Building on the Lindblad model, we extend the analysis by simulating individual pure-state trajectories in addition to the ensemble density operator. While the ME provides a deterministic description of the average behavior of a large ensemble of dimers, it cannot resolve the discrete, incoherent events experienced by a single system. To capture these dynamics, we use a stochastic Schrödinger equation (SSE), which describes the evolution of a single pure-state trajectory |ψ⟩ subject to continuous environmental monitoring. The density operator is recovered by taking the stochastic average E over many trajectories, ρ̂=E[|ψ⟩⟨ψ|] . Then, the SSE is given by 9 |dψ⟩=(−iℏĤsite−12(L̂−⟨L̂⟩)2)|ψ⟩dt+(L̂−⟨L̂⟩)|ψ⟩dW, where dW is an Itô Wiener process.

We visualize the individual pure-state trajectories (Figure b,c) by mapping the system to the Bloch vector r=(⟨σ̂x⟩,⟨σ̂y⟩,⟨σ̂z⟩) (Figure a), where ⟨σ̂x⟩=2Re(ρ12) , ⟨σ̂y⟩=2Im(ρ12) , and ⟨σ̂z⟩=ρ11−ρ22 . All the numerical simulations are initialized in the delocalized bright state | + ⟩, corresponding to a vector on the equator of the Bloch sphere. Individual trajectories exhibit incoherent hopping between sites in the bright-dark basis. These stochastic paths represent pure-state evolution restricted to the surface of the sphere, as shown in Figure a. Conversely, the ensemble-averaged dynamics governed by the ME in eq describe the rapid, smooth decay of off-diagonal coherence, leading to a statistical mixture over both sites and evolution toward the center of the Bloch sphere, shown as the solid blue line in Figure a.

Excited-State Structure and Excitonic Coupling

The chromophore possesses a single bright excitation in the visible: TDDFT (ωB97X-D3, computed on the same 44-atom QM region and point-charge field as the correlated calculation) returns a dominant bright ππ* root (f = 1.03), but as a single-excitation theory it blueshifts this state to 360.42 nm, roughly 154 nm to the blue of experiment. The electron transition density in Figure , which serves as the fundamental quantity for evaluating the Coulombic integral in eq , exhibits a complex multipolar spatial distribution in 3D that extends across the entire chromophore and adjacent residues. Note that the red and blue isosurfaces in Figure b do not represent static charge gain or loss, but rather the spatial “lobes” of the electron transition probability. The spatial overlap of these lobes between the two chromophores determines the magnitude of J TDC and captures interactions that are ignored when the density is treated as a single point-dipole vector. Concurrently, the electron density difference in Figure b illustrates the charge reorganization from the ground to the first excited state (state 1), with the deprotonated phenolic oxygen on the chromophore residue (CR2, anionic form) transferring charge to the rest of the chromophore.

For comparison, evaluating the same TDC pipeline on the TDDFT transition density gives J TDC = 27.7 cm–1 versus J PDA = 25.0 cm–1 (J TDC/J PDA ≈ 1.11); the modest near-field enhancement over the PDA (∼10–20%) is thus a robust, method-consistent feature.

The close agreement between the TDC calculation and the far-field point-dipole estimate reflects the well-separated nature of the two chromophores. At a centroid separation of 24.7 Å, the point-dipole approximation provides a reliable leading-order estimate of the coupling strength, with higher-order multipoles contributing a modest ∼10–20% enhancement. This is in quantitative agreement with independent direct coupling benchmark calculations on the Venus crystal structure (e.g., Q-Chem direct coupling yielding 48.7 cm–1 for TDC versus 43.2 cm–1 for PDA, a 13% difference). A Cartesian multipole decomposition reveals that the dipole–dipole term accounts for the dominant portion of the interaction, while dipole–quadrupole and higher multipolar terms provide the remaining ∼10–20% correction.

To identify the origin of this enhancement, we decomposed the coupling into a primitive Cartesian multipole series about each chromophore centroid (companion analysis multipole_analysis.py, evaluated on the transition density of the bright ππ* state), evaluating the transition dipole, quadrupole, and octupole moments and the corresponding inter-site interaction terms (dipole–dipole, dipole–quadrupole, quadrupole–dipole, dipole–octupole, quadrupole–quadrupole, octupole–dipole). The dipole–dipole term reproduces J PDA exactly by construction, but the truncated series converges only slowly at this geometry: summing all terms through octupole recovers just ∼28% of the full J TDC (dipole alone ∼23%, through quadrupole ∼27%). The majority of the near-field enhancement therefore does not reside in a few leading multipoles but in the short-range Coulomb coupling between the spatially extended transition densities, whose facing tails approach to ∼9⁡Å , far closer than either the ∼15⁡Å nuclear closest approach or the ∼25⁡Å chromophore centroid separation, and which only the full transition-density integral captures. This is a purely electrostatic effect between non-overlapping densities, distinct from short-range Dexter exchange, which is negligible at these separations. This sharpens the broader lesson for the PDA: when transition densities are delocalized and their nearest approach is comparable to the molecular size, even a multipole-corrected dipole model is inadequate and the full density is required. The decomposition is validated by a self-test that reconstructs an exact brute-force coupling between two compact, well-separated model densities to better than 0.1%.

Because protein conformational dynamics modulate the inter-chromophore geometry and hence J, we characterized the coupling over a room-temperature trajectory of the actual VenusA206 tandem-dimer construct (see Methods, Figure ). The covalently tethered tandem holds the A206 interface docked for the full 1 ns: the chromophore separation is 24.69±0.32⁡Å (range 23.72– 26.08⁡Å ), and the full-grid transition-density coupling is J = 32.8 ± 1.6 cm–1 across the 1000-frame trajectory (Davydov splitting 2|J| = 65.6 ± 3.1 cm–1). The corresponding ensemble point-dipole result is J PDA = 27.6 ± 1.3 cm–1, confirming a robust 19% near-field enhancement. The covalent linker of the tandem construct sustains the docked geometry throughout the dynamics, confirming that intermediate coupling is an intrinsic feature of the tethered dimer.

Computational Methods

The quantum-classical hybrid workflow is automated via a single Python orchestrator, reproduce_paper.py, that integrates structural preparation, classical relaxation, QM/MM electronic-structure calculations (TDDFT, DLPNO-STEOM-CCSD, and the EOM-CCSD­(fT)/ADC(2) validation), GPU-accelerated TDC analysis, and the thermal ensemble, spanning the ORCA and Q-Chem back-ends behind a common configuration (random seed, dielectric, ensemble size, per-stage caching). Algorithm 1 serves as a concise, reproducible map of this orchestration, linking each manuscript quantity to the routine that produces it in the released code, rather than introducing new methodology; readers interested only in the physical results may skip it.

To sample the room-temperature conformational dynamics that modulate the inter-chromophore geometry and hence J, we built the VenusA206 tandem dimer (VenusA206-TD): two Venus β-barrels covalently joined by the flexible ∼33-residue inter-domain linker used in the tandem constructs, with the barrels docked at the physiological A206 interface (residues clustered around Ala206, Leu207, Leu221, and Phe223; released tool build_tandem_dimer.py). The two CR2 sites received the complete bonded, nonbonded, RESP-charge, exclusion, and 1–4 terms from the production anionic CR2 AMBER topology rather than generic fallback parameters. We then ran 500, 000 unrestrained OpenMM NVT steps at 300 K with a 2 fs timestep (1 ns total), saving the protein and chromophores every 500 steps to give 1000 snapshots at 1 ps intervals (run_nvt.py). Periodic imaging was removed by whole-residue lattice translations without coordinate fitting or deformation. For every frame, each copy of the cap-masked STEOM transition density was placed by a rigid fit of the 19 shared CR2 heavy atoms and the mutual Coulomb integral was accumulated on an RTX 4080 over all 259, 2772 density-point pairs in FP32 (coupling_dcd_steom.py); full-grid FP64 checks on frames 1, 500, and 1000 differed by only 0.011–0.014 cm–1. The resulting separation and coupling distributions are reported with the Results (Figure ). Animations of the NVT trajectories are available at https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLbopStXxFpaQ.

Summary

We have characterized the excitonic coupling and energy-transfer dynamics in the Venus dimer using a hybrid QM/MM transition-density approach within an open-quantum-system model. Using a correlated (DLPNO-STEOM-CCSD) site energy and transition density (validated against an explicit triples benchmark) and simulating the actual VenusA206 tandem-dimer construct, we find that the covalent inter-domain linker holds the A206 interface docked throughout a nanosecond of dynamics, yielding J = 32.8 ± 1.6 cm–1 across 1000 snapshots and a Davydov splitting of 2|J| = 65.6 ± 3.1 cm–1, firmly within the intermediate excitonic regime. Distributed transition density calculations reveal that the point-dipole approximation (J PDA = 27.6 ± 1.3 cm–1) provides a reliable leading-order description at this ∼24.7 Å separation, with higher multipoles contributing a modest 19% near-field enhancement.

Our analysis validates the role of non-equilibrium dielectric screening in protein environments. Because the excitonic interaction time scale is significantly faster than the macroscopic Debye relaxation time of bulk water (∼8.3 ps), the excitonic coupling can operate in the optical dielectric limit. This inertial “freezing” of the solvent protects the initial coupling from overwhelming equilibrium screening with εeq. The stochastic trajectories illustrate that this coherence is transient and environmental interactions drive the system into a localized pointer basis on a sub-100 fs time scale. Over the sub-picosecond time scale (<1 ps) of coherent exciton transfer, 1/ε­(t) varies by only ∼11%, leaving the coupling effectively governed by J(0). This temporal separation reconciles the Davydov splitting imprinted at absorption with rapid localization and subsequent screening within picosecond time scales. The argument is not specific to Venus: because Debye relaxation of aqueous environments universally lies in the picosecond range while optical absorption and dephasing are far faster, the same optical-limit imprinting of the coupling is expected in other water-exposed multichromophore complexes, with the β-barrel here additionally suppressing the fluctuation amplitude, not the underlying separation of time scales. Although Figure accounts well for the single-photon excited-subspace phenomena in the Venus dimer, extending the time-dependent simulation of the excitonic interaction to higher energy levels would offer a more comprehensive view of non-equilibrium dynamics in fluorescent proteins.

Several aspects of the environmental treatment are deliberately simplified and bound the quantitative accuracy of J. First, the dielectric model treats the surroundings through bulk–water parameters (εeq ≈ 78, εopt ≈ 1.78, τ D ≈ 8.3 ps); a chromophore enclosed by the β-barrel is only partially solvent-exposed, so the relevant static permittivity is lower and spatially heterogeneous, and the tight inter-barrel packing makes the intervening solvent distribution asymmetric rather than the isotropic continuum assumed in the point-dipole coupling [eq ] and the effective dielectric-relaxation model. These therefore represent approximations whose effect should be probed with a position-dependent or atomistic, explicitly polarizable embedding, , which can introduce sizeable additional screening at this separation. We emphasize that the environment’s fast electronic polarizability is itself already represented, at the continuum level, by the optical dielectric εopt that screens the coupling; what such an embedding would add is the explicit, position-dependent atomistic response of the protein and solvent, rather than electronic polarizability per se. Crucially, however, the central result (the coupling imprinted at the instant of absorption, J(0)) is set by the optical dielectric εopt alone: sweeping the static permittivity εeq over 4–78 (protein interior to bulk water) leaves J(0) exactly invariant and rescales only the long-time, fully relaxed limit. The protein-versus-water dielectric question therefore bears on the late-time damping of the coupling, not on the magnitude of the Davydov splitting set at absorption. Second, the single-exponential Debye form omits the fast librational and intramolecular components of the water response; these act on tens of femtoseconds and would provide a partial polarization prior to full nuclear reorganization, so the optical limit is best regarded as an upper bound on the early-time coupling. We note more broadly that the breakdown of the point-dipole approximation reported here is the expected behavior whenever the chromophore separation is comparable to the molecular dimensions; , the present case quantifies its magnitude for a suspended fluorescent-protein dimer.

Further refinements to increase the accuracy of our simulation estimates are possible, although they would incur a significant computational cost. Such refinements include enlarging the QM region and treating the phenolic hydrogen fully quantum mechanically to capture proton tunneling. , Additionally, future work will extend this framework to incorporate explicit vibronic coupling and evaluate the impact of non-Markovian protein phonons on long-term coherence dynamics.
