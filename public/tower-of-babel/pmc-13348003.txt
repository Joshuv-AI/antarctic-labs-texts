Hydrogen-delivering biomaterial carriers for diabetic foot ulcer healing: a systematic review and meta-analysis of preclinical animal studies
Authors: Xiaona Wang, Lei Pan, Haijun Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to systematically evaluate the preclinical evidence on the therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms of hydrogen-delivering biomaterial carriers in the treatment of diabetic foot ulcer (DFU).MethodWe conducted a comprehensive search across eight databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, CBM, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP) to identify randomized animal studies investigating biomaterial-based hydrogen delivery for DFUs from database inception through November 2025. After screening, eleven studies met inclusion criteria and were included in the final meta-analysis. Data synthesis and statistical analyses were performed using RevMan 5.4; risk of bias was assessed using the SYRCLE’s tool for animal studies.ResultsThe delivery of hydrogen via biomaterial carriers effectively promotes diabetic wound repair by enhancing wound closure rates, stimulating angiogenesis, and improving collagen deposition. Smart delivery systems, such as hydrogen-generating hydrogels, responsive microneedle patches, and photocatalytic dressings, enabled spatiotemporally controlled, sustained H₂ release. This modulation effectively attenuated oxidative stress and suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6). Collectively, biomaterial-mediated hydrogen delivery confers dual therapeutic actions: direct promotion of tissue regeneration and dynamic reprogramming of the impaired wound microenvironment, supporting its potential as a targeted, mechanism-informed intervention for DFUs.ConclusionBiomaterial-based hydrogen delivery systems demonstrate multifaceted therapeutic benefits in preclinical DFU models, primarily through anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and collagen-enhancing mechanisms. The emergence of smart delivery systems enables localized, tunable, and prolonged hydrogen release, which improves bioavailability and therapeutic precision. Future research should prioritize optimization of release kinetics, pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic modeling in diabetic wound beds, and exploration of synergistic combinations (e.g., with growth factors or antimicrobial agents) to advance toward clinically translatable, precision wound therapeutics.Keywords: Hydrogen therapy, Diabetic foot ulcer, Wound healing, Meta-analysis, Animal models, Biomaterial carrier

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) arises from the interplay of multiple pathophysiological disturbances in diabetes, including vascular complications leading to local tissue ischemia, peripheral neuropathy, and mechanical stress. Secondary infection frequently complicates this compromised wound milieu, further disrupting epithelial integrity and promoting progressive tissue necrosis [1]. Globally, an estimated 18.6 million individuals live with DFU. DFU precede 80% of lower extremity amputations among diabetic patients and indicate a higher risk of death [2]. DFU management encompasses a multidisciplinary approach: sharp debridement, moist wound healing strategies, offloading, vascular assessment and revascularization when indicated, infection control, and stringent glycemic regulation, ideally coordinated through a multidisciplinary foot care center. Nevertheless, despite this comprehensive approach, healing outcomes remain suboptimal and require improvement [3].

Historically regarded as biologically inert, molecular hydrogen (H₂) was redefined in 2007 following a landmark Nature Medicine study identifying its selective antioxidant properties without disrupting physiologically essential reactive oxygen species signaling. This discovery catalyzed the field of hydrogen biomedicine [4]. Recent research [5] have robustly documented that molecular hydrogen (H₂) exerts multiple a range of biological effects in animal models, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and anti-allergic properties. At present, diabetic skin ulcers represent a persistent clinical challenge, emphasizing the necessity of investigating effective therapeutic interventions an urgent priority. Positive therapeutic indications of hydrogen have shown beneficial effects across major clinical domains, including cardiovascular disease, neurodegeneration, respiratory injury, oncology, and sepsis [6]. Notably, recent biomaterial engineering efforts have yielded innovative H₂-delivery platforms tailored for chronic wounds. A recent study [7] designed a hydrogen‑generating composite membrane using poly(lactic‑co‑glycolic acid) nanofibers loaded with iron nanoparticles. This system, functioning as an in-situ generator, reacts with the wound environment to continuously produce H₂, offering a direct biomaterial‑based delivery strategy for diabetic wounds. The proposed multi-target mechanisms by which hydrogen-based therapy modulates the diabetic wound microenvironment are illustrated in Fig. 1.

This study evaluated the therapeutic effect of a system for delivering hydrogen using bio-material carriers in the healing of diabetic ulcer wounds. Given the limited available clinical evidence, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled animal studies. The objective was to integrate the current body of evidence regarding their effectiveness and to examine potential mechanisms, with the expectation of providing reference basis for subsequent preclinical and clinical investigations.

Methods

The protocol for this systematic review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). (registration number: CRD420261286994.)

information retrieval

Two independent reviewers conducted comprehensive, parallel literature searches in both English- and Chinese-language databases. The Chinese databases consisted of CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and CBM, whereas the international databases covered PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library. To mitigate publication bias, we additionally searched grey literature (e.g., clinical trial registries, dissertations, conference abstracts). The search employed a combination of subject heading terms (e.g., MeSH) and free-text terms, with core keywords such as “hydrogen”, “molecular hydrogen (H₂)”, “diabetic foot ulcer”, and “wound healing”. The search covered all records indexed from database inception through 30 November 2025.

Study selection criteria

Eligible studies were randomized controlled animal experiments evaluating hydrogen delivery via engineered biomaterial carriers in preclinical models of DFU.

Inclusion criteria: (1) study design: randomized controlled animal studies (no restrictions on language or publication type); (2) animal models: diabetic ulcer models (any species, sex, or age); (3) Intervention: Experimental group received molecular hydrogen (H₂) delivered exclusively via a defined biomaterial carrier.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Non-randomized designs, case reports, reviews, editorials, or studies lacking concurrent controls; (2) Studies not involving diabetic wound models; (3) Hydrogen administration via non-biomaterial routes; (4) Duplicate publications reporting overlapping data from the same experimental cohort; (5) Studies with insufficient quantitative outcome data to permit effect-size calculation.

Data collection and outcome assessment

Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts, performed full-text assessments, and extracted data using a piloted, standardized electronic form. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or adjudication by a third senior reviewer. Extracted variables included: (title, year of publication, first author), animal model specifications, intervention specifics (functional element, carrier type, intervention, control measures, group details), and primary outcome measures.

The experimental groups involved the application of biomaterial-based hydrogen delivery systems for wound treatment in diabetic animal models. In the experimental design, no restrictions were imposed on the types of animals, intervention methods, treatment duration, dosage, carrier components, wound size, administration frequency or injection sites. The control groups received either non-functional dressings (such as medical gauze or blank dressings) or no treatment. This study evaluated the following primary outcomes: wound closure rate, inflammatory factors, macrophage phenotype, angiogenesis, fibrosis, and tissue repair.

All extracted numerical data were entered into a secure, version-controlled Excel database. For data available only in graphical format, numerical values were extracted using GetData Graph Digitizer (version 2.26).

Risk of Bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the SYRCLE’s risk of bias tool [8].

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted using software RevMan 5.4. Continuous outcomes were pooled using the standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), given expected heterogeneity in measurement scales across studies. The I² statistic was adopted for the quantification of between-study heterogeneity. I² ≤ 50%, which indicated low heterogeneity, promoted the adoption of a fixed-effects model; otherwise, a random-effects model was employed (I² > 50%). P < 0.05 denoted statistical significance. Publication bias was assessed visually via funnel plots. Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding individual studies and re-estimating pooled effects to evaluate result robustness.

Results

Search results

The initial database search search retrieved 127 records (15 Chinese, 112 English). After removing 8 duplicates, 119 records underwent title/abstract screening; 33 met preliminary eligibility and proceeded to full-text review. Of these, 11 studies satisfied all predefined inclusion criteria and were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis [9–19]. Result analysis

Wound closure

Wound status at days 3 and 7 post-intervention was available from eight studies [10–12, 14, 16–19]. Reported metrics included either wound closure rate or residual wound area percentage. To enable statistical pooling, residual area values were uniformly converted to closure rate using the formula:

Wound Closure Rate (%) = 100% – Residual Wound Area (%).

To ensure analytical consistency, all wound closure data were standardized to wound closure rate (%) prior to meta-analysis. Data from eight studies were pooled, with effect estimates synthesized separately for day 3 and day 7 post-intervention.

Wound closure rate at day 3: Significant statistical heterogeneity was observed across studies (P = 0.0008, I2 = 72%). Accordingly, a random-effects approach was employed. Pooled analysis demonstrated a large and statistically significant improvement in wound closure in the hydrogen-treated group versus controls (SMD = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.00 to 3.83, P = 0.0008; Fig. 4A).

Forest plots of wound healing rate. (A) Day 3; (B) Day 7

Wound closure rate at day 7: A statistical heterogeneity was present (P = 0.02, I2 = 57%), warranting use of the random-effects model. Pooled analysis of the wound closure rate at day 7 showed a statistically significant benefit in the hydrogen-treated group. (SMD = 3.47, 95% CI: 2.0 to 4.93, P < 0.00001; Fig. 4B).

Analysis of the Inflammatory microenvironment and immune response

Inflammatory factors

Relative IL-6 expression. Using interleukin-6 (IL-6), a key pro-inflammatory cytokine, to analyze the inflammatory condition of the wound microenvironment. Five studies [9, 13, 15, 18–19] exhibited moderate heterogeneity (P = 0.12, I² = 46%). In accordance with our pre-specified analysis plan (employing a fixed-effect model for I² ≤ 50%), a fixed-effect model was adopted. The pooled result for IL-6 expression demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in the intervention group (SMD = -6.12, 95% CI: -7.94 to -4.30, P < 0.00001; Fig. 5A).

Forest plots of pro-inflammatory (A-C: IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) and anti-inflammatory (D: IL-10) cytokines

Relative TNF-a expression. The pro-inflammatory wound microenvironment was assessed via tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) expression. Meta-analysis of data from two studies [15, 18] showed no significant heterogeneity (I² = 0%, P = 0.7). Therefore, we adopted the fixed-effect method. The pooled results indicated a significant reduction in TNF-α expression in the intervention group (SMD = -6.61, 95% CI: -9.64 to -3.57, P < 0.0001; Fig. 5B).

Relative IL-1β expression. The pro-inflammatory wound microenvironment was analyzed via IL-1β expression. Meta-analysis of data from three studies [9, 14–15] showed a large amount of heterogeneity among the results (I² = 80%, P = 0.007). We adopted the random-effects model according to the pre-specified approach. The meta-analysis indicated a trend toward reduced IL-1β levels among animals receiving the intervention, but the difference was not statistically significant (SMD = -3.57, 95% CI: -7.70 to 0.55, P = 0.09; Fig. 5C).

Relative IL-10 expression. To analyze the inflammatory profile of the wound microenvironment, the relative expression level of the key anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was assessed. When pooling data from three studies [13, 15, 18], the heterogeneity observed was low to moderate (I² = 45%, P = 0.16). As pre‑specified, a fixed-effects model was used (I² ≤ 50%). The pooled result ascertained a statistically significant increase in IL-10 expression in the intervention group (SMD = 8.57, 95% CI: 4.78 to 12.35, P < 0.00001; Fig. 5D).

Macrophage phenotype

To assess the changes in macrophage polarization in the wound microenvironment, the expression of classic surface markers for macrophage phenotypes were analyzed: CD86, a marker of the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, and CD206, an indicator of the anti-inflammatory/reparative M2 phenotype.

Relative CD86 expression. Across five studies [10, 13, 15, 18–19], heterogeneity was negligible (I² = 0%, P = 0.57), supporting the use of a fixed-effects model. The pooled analysis revealed a statistically significant benefit in favor of the hydrogen-treated group (SMD = -6.26, 95% CI: -8.26 to -4.26, P < 0.00001).

Relative CD206 expression. Four studies [10, 13, 15, 18] indicated substantial heterogeneity (I² = 77%, P = 0.005), promoting the use of a random-effects model. The analysis showed that the CD206 expression in the hydrogen-treated group had a statistically significant increase(SMD = 6.4, 95% CI: 1.91 to 10.89, P = 0.005).

Angiogenesis

Relative CD31 expression. CD31 expression, a specific marker for angiogenesis and indicative of new blood vessel formation, was utilized as a metric in four studies [10, 13–14, 18]. A certain degree of heterogeneity was observed (I² = 48%, P = 0.13). Since I² was below the threshold of 50%, a fixed-effect model was applied. The pooled analysis revealed a statistically significant and substantial increase in CD31 expression in the intervention group compared to controls (SMD = 4.34, 95% CI: 1.92 to 6.76, P = 0.0004; Fig. 6).

Forest plot for the meta-analysis of CD31 expression

Fibrosis

The progression of fibrosis involves three key elements: TGF-β (Transforming Growth Factor-beta) expression activates fibroblasts to transform into α-SMA-positive (alpha-smooth muscle actin) myofibroblasts, which in turn are responsible for pathological collagen deposition.

TGF-β Expression. Pooled analysis of two studies [15, 18] using a random-effects model (I² = 70%) indicated a statistically significant and substantial increase in TGF-β expression in the intervention group (SMD = 6.42, 95% CI: 3.20–9.64, P < 0.0001; Fig. 7A).

Forest plots of TGF-β (A), α-SMA (B), and collagen volume fraction (C)

α-SMA Expression. Meta-analysis of two studies [18, 19] using a random-effects model (I² =73%) demonstrated a statistically significant elevation in α-SMA expression in the intervention group (SMD = 7.90, 95% CI: 1.38–14.42, P = 0.02; Fig. 7B).

Collagen Deposition. Pooled analysis of four studies [9, 13–14, 18] using a random-effects model (I² = 79%) revealed that the collagen deposition in the intervention group had a statistically significant increase (SMD = 9.19, 95% CI: 2.04–16.34, P = 0.01; Fig. 7C).

Tissue repair

Epidermal thickness. Epidermal thickness, an indicator of re-epithelialization integrity, was assessed as an outcome measure in three studies [13–14, 18]. Given the non‑significant heterogeneity (I² = 43%, P = 0.18), a fixed‑effects model was used for pooled analysis. The meta-analysis revealed that epidermal thickness in the intervention group was significantly higher than that in the control group (SMD = 9.51, 95% CI: 5.69 to 13.33, P < 0.00001).

Granulation tissue thickness. Granulation tissue thickness, as an indicator of the proliferative stage activity and matrix filling in wound healing, was used as a result indicator in two studies [13, 18]. As substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 89%, P = 0.003), we applied a random-effects model for the pooled analysis. The meta-analysis showed no significant difference in epidermal thickness between the intervention group and control group (SMD = 5.82, 95% CI: -2.33 to 13.97, P = 0.16).

Publication Bias; sensitivity analyses

Publication bias for the wound healing rate at day 3 was assessed via a funnel plot (Fig. 8). The distribution exhibited approximate symmetry, suggesting no evident small-study effects or selective reporting bias across included studies.

Funnel plot assessing publication bias for the meta-analysis of day 3 healing rate

For most outcomes (except IL-1β), sensitivity analyses showed robust results that were unchanged in direction and statistical significance upon exclusion of any single study. For IL‑1β expression, post‑hoc sensitivity analysis identified Study [9] as the primary source of substantial heterogeneity and the overall non‑significant result. Exclusion of Study yielded a homogeneous dataset (I² = 0%, P = 0.62) and demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in IL-1β expression (SMD = -5.87, 95% CI: -8.77 to -2.78, P = 0.0002). The consistency of sensitivity results for other endpoints reinforces the reliability of our meta-analytic conclusions.

Subgroup analysis

Pooled effect estimates

Eleven studies reported wound healing rates at day 3, 7, or 14 post-intervention and were included in the primary pooled analysis. The overall pooled analysis showed a statistically significant and substantial improvement in wound healing rate in the intervention group (biomaterial-based hydrogen delivery) compared to controls. The overall effect was significant (Z = 6.14, P < 0.00001). However, moderate-to-high heterogeneity was present, prompting pre-specified subgroup investigations (I² = 65%, P < 0.0001).

To investigate the temporal efficacy profile and the sources of heterogeneity, we conducted a pre-specified subgroup analysis, which took into account the time points of assessment. The results indicated there were significant positive effects at all three time points (all P ≤ 0.02), demonstrating no significant difference in the effect size among the subgroups at the statistical level (subgroup differences P = 0.47).

Subgroup analysis

(1) Subgroup analysis based on time of assessment

A subgroup analysis of wound healing rate based on the time of assessment (day 3, 7, and 14; Fig. 9) was performed. The results confirmed statistically significant improvements in wound healing rate across all time points (all P ≤ 0.02). The point estimate of effect size appeared numerically largest (day 3). However, substantial heterogeneity (I² = 66%) was observed within each subgroup, indicating inconsistency among the included studies. The test for subgroup differences did not demonstrate statistical significance (P = 0.47, I² = 0%), suggesting that the observed treatment effect did not differ substantially across the three time points.

Forest plot of subgroup analysis for wound healing rate based on time of assessment

(2) Subgroup analysis by biomaterial carrier type

Studies were categorized according to the primary biomaterial carrier employed: carrier-delivered hydrogel, active therapeutic materials (i.e., materials possessing inherent therapeutic or sensing activity), and microneedle delivery systems. Day 3 and day 7 wound healing rates were selected as primary efficacy endpoints to capture early dynamic responses and sustained therapeutic effects.

Subgroup analysis of day 3 wound healing rate by biomaterial carrier type:

Pooled analysis revealed a significant overall improvement in day 3 wound healing rate (Z = 4.57, P < 0.00001, Fig. 10), albeit with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 72%). Subgroup-level results revealed marked differences in both magnitude and consistency of effect: hydrogel-based systems [10–12, 14] yielded a significant benefit (Z = 3.26, P = 0.001) but with high heterogeneity (I² = 80%); microneedle systems [17, 19] also demonstrated significant improvement (Z = 3.50, P = 0.0005) and low heterogeneity (I² = 18%); whereas active treatment materials [16, 18] did not reach statistical significance (Z = 1.27, P = 0.20) with considerable heterogeneity (I² = 79%).

Subgroup analysis of day 3 wound healing rate by biomaterial carrier type

Subgroup analysis of day 7 wound healing rate by biomaterial carrier type:

Meta-analysis of day 7 healing rates across subgroups revealed differences in efficacy and heterogeneity (Fig. 11). Carrier-delivered hydrogels [10–12, 14] demonstrated highly consistent and significant efficacy (Z = 3.59, P = 0.0003; I² = 47%). In contrast, active treatment materials [16, 18] remained non-significant (Z = 1.37, P = 0.17; I² = 78%), with persistent heterogeneity limiting interpretability. While microneedle delivery systems [17, 19] showed a trend toward improvement but also failed to reach statistical significance, with substantial heterogeneity (Z = 1.69, P = 0.09; I² = 82%). Collectively, comparative analysis of day 3 and day 7 outcomes indicates a distinct carrier-dependent efficacy profile associated with different biomaterial carriers, providing crucial insights for therapeutic development.

Discussion

DFU represent a prevalent, debilitating, and economically burdensome complication of diabetes. Approximately 34% of individuals with diabetes develop a foot ulcer during their lifetime, and such ulcers account for over their lifetime, and DFUs account for more than 80% of non-traumatic lower-limb amputations in this population [20].The pathogenesis of DFU is multifactorial, involving peripheral neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, biomechanical stress, and heightened susceptibility to infection [3]. Crucially, the wound healing cascade is profoundly dysregulated, characterized by chronic inflammation, excessive oxidative stress, impaired angiogenesis, dysfunctional fibroblast activity, and aberrant extracellular matrix (ECM) turnover [21]. Sustained inflammation, fueled by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), represents a major factor impairing the normal wound healing process in diabetes [22]. Conventional therapies yield suboptimal outcomes: a recent meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials reported only a 33.15% complete healing rate, highlighting an urgent unmet need for mechanistically grounded, high-efficacy interventions [23]. Molecular hydrogen (H₂) has emerged as a promising therapeutic modality for chronic wounds since its selective antioxidant properties were first reported in 2007. However, its clinical translation remains hampered by pharmacokinetic limitations, including low aqueous solubility, rapid diffusion, and uncontrollable kinetics with conventional delivery (e.g., inhalation, H₂‑rich water) [24]. To overcome these pharmacokinetic challenges, biomaterial-based delivery platforms have been engineered to enable localized, prolonged, and microenvironment-responsive H₂ release. As demonstrated, hybrid hydrogels encapsulating hydrogen-producing agents (e.g., ammonia borane–loaded Prussian blue nanoparticles [25] or piezoelectric C₃N₄ nanosheets [26] facilitate sustained, stimulus-triggered release within the wound microenvironment. These systems extend H₂ action duration and may synergistically modulate oxygen levels and suppress advanced glycation end products, thereby addressing multiple pathological features of diabetic wounds. Preclinical studies in STZ‑induced or db/db mouse models report accelerated wound closure, reduced inflammation, and improved histology with various biomaterial‑H₂ platforms [27, 7] Nevertheless, existing data are fragmented across disparate models, carriers, and outcome metrics, precluding evidence-based prioritization of one platform over another. To bridge this gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized preclinical studies in diabetic ulcer models.

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes preclinical evidence on biomaterial-mediated molecular hydrogen (H₂) delivery for diabetic ulcer treatment. Pooled analyses suggest a beneficial pro-healing effect and modulation of the wound microenvironment, characterized by accelerated wound closure, suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and a coordinated pro-fibrotic response. While re-epithelialization was robustly enhanced across studies, the effect on granulation tissue thickness remains inconclusive due to limited data and high heterogeneity, underscoring a critical knowledge gap requiring targeted experimental investigation.

Subgroup analysis revealed a pronounced time-dependent efficacy profile across biomaterial carriers. No significant difference was found among carriers at day 3, suggesting that early wound closure is primarily driven by shared physicochemical properties. By day 7, functional divergence emerged: traditional hydrogels showed robust and consistent effects (I² = 47%, P = 0.0003), while advanced systems exhibited high heterogeneity and non-significant results (I² = 78–82%), indicating that prolonged, tunable H₂ release is essential for sustaining therapeutic activity beyond the initial inflammatory phase.

This study has several limitations warranting careful interpretation and guiding future work. First, methodologically, extracting numerical data from published figures using digitization software introduces potential measurement error, despite dual-independent verification and intra-rater reliability assessment. Second, despite subgroup and sensitivity analyses, residual experimental heterogeneity likely persists due to variations in animal models, hydrogen dosing regimens, and outcome measurement methods. Third, conclusions for certain specific outcomes (e.g., particular cytokine levels or granulation tissue thickness) are based on a limited number of studies, precluding definitive inference and highlighting priorities for replication. Fourth, all evidence is derived from preclinical models; therefore, translating even optimized biomaterial systems to the more complex pathophysiology of human diabetic foot ulcers will require rigorous phase I/II clinical trials to assess safety, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy in patients. Finally, although funnel plots did not indicate significant publication bias, small-study effects cannot be entirely excluded in experimental biomedicine, where underpowered or negative-result studies are less likely to be published.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis provides robust preclinical evidence that biomaterial-based hydrogen delivery exerts multimodal therapeutic actions in diabetic ulcer models, contributing to immune modulation, angiogenesis, and tissue repair. The heterogeneity in outcomes across different carrier systems further supports a paradigm shift: optimal therapeutic outcomes may depend not on selecting a single “best” platform, but on rationally matching carrier pharmacokinetics. Interpretation of these findings must account for between-study heterogeneity.
