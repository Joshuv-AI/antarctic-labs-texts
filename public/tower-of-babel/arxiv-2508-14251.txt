The Chapman-Enskog approximation for a relativistic charged gas in the trace-fixed particle frame
Carlos Gabarrete 1 , Ana Laura García-Perciante 2 , and Olivier Sarbach 3

Abstract
In this article we provide a thorough discussion of the first-order Chapman-Enskog approximation for a relativistic simple charged gas propagating in an arbitrary fixed background spacetime and electromagnetic field. Motivated by a rigorous study of the linearized collision operator, we argue that the most natural thermodynamic frame to describe dissipative relativistic fluids is the trace-fixed particle frame. This frame determines the state variables by requiring compatibility of the first few moments of the one-particle distribution function with those of the Jüttner distribution. The resulting constitutive relations are derived, and it is shown that the corresponding transport coefficients are, in fact, frame-independent if suitably defined. Moreover, we discuss the representation freedom for these relations within the microscopic approach. As shown in previous work, when this freedom is suitably exploited, the resulting fluid theory gives rise to strongly hyperbolic and causal evolution equations for which global equilibria are stable.

I
Introduction

The kinetic theory of gases provides a formal and rigorous framework to model macroscopic phenomena based on the properties and dynamics of its individual constituents
Chapman and Cowling 1970
;
Cercignani 2012
;
Cercigniani 2014
. For dilute systems, the Boltzmann equation describes the time evolution of the one-particle distribution function when considering binary collisions, and accurately leads to the corresponding transport equations in the hydrodynamic regime when linearized around equilibrium configurations. This can be achieved by means of the perturbative approach known as the Chapman-Enskog method, which has been successfully applied to non-relativistic systems. The corresponding constitutive equations relating dissipative fluxes and forces, when truncated to first order in the gradients of the state variables, lead to the well-known Navier-Stokes equations for a compressible fluid
Chapman and Cowling 1970
;
Cercignani 2012
;
Cercigniani 2014
;
Saint-Raymond 2014
;
Saint-Raymond 2009
.

The traditional procedure to establish constitutive equations within the Chapman-Enskog approach has been systematized such that the existence of the solution of the linearized Boltzmann equation is enforced by eliminating the time derivatives of state variables appearing in the first-order correction to the equilibrium distribution function by means of the lower order Euler equations. This leads to the usual coupling laws (Fourier and Navier-Newton) between dissipative fluxes and spatial gradients of the state variables
Chapman and Cowling 1970
;
García-Perciante
et al. 2025
.

On the other hand, the usual linear relations predicted in the non-relativistic case, both by linear irreversible thermodynamics and kinetic theory, are inadequate in a relativistic scenario, where the second law of thermodynamics requires the dissipative fluxes to be coupled with time derivatives, in addition to the usual spatial gradient terms. More precisely, the phenomenological approach first developed by Eckart
Eckart 1940
leads to a heat flux driven by both the temperature gradient and the hydrodynamic acceleration. However, this proposal and its Landau-Lifshitz
Landau and Lifshitz 1987
counterpart, have been shown to lead to pathological systems, due to causality issues and the presence of generic instabilities
Hiscock and Lindblom 1983
;
Hiscock and Lindblom 1985
. This problem led to the development of extended or higher-order dissipative fluid theories (see Refs.
Ván 2020
;
Salazar and Zannias 2020
;
Gavassino and Antonelli 2021
;
Rocha et al. 2024
for recent reviews).

For the reasons mentioned above, first-order theories had been mostly discarded for decades. However, they have received renewed interest in recent years since it has been shown that they can lead to a physically sound system of equations without resorting to extended or higher-order formalisms
Bemfica et al. 2022
;
Kovtun 2019a
. This is achieved by allowing for the appearance of non-equilibrium corrections in all the components of the particle current density and the energy-momentum-stress tensor, and for these corrections to be coupled to both first-order time and spatial derivatives of the state variables. This amounts to describing the fluid in an arbitrary thermodynamic frame and constitutes one of the key features of this formalism, usually referred to as Bemfica-Disconzi-Noronha-Kovtun (BDNK) theory. Here, the choice of thermodynamic frame refers to the particular assignment of the state variables to non-equilibrium quantities. This choice is usually fixed through the so-called matching conditions which require certain moments of the distribution function (or certain macroscopic variables) to match those corresponding to the local equilibrium configuration.

1

In the context of kinetic theory, we shall refer to these conditions as compatibility conditions.
When these are left unspecified, one is led to a general first-order theory for which hyperbolicity, stability, and causality are conditioned to the fulfillment of several complicated inequalities for the parameters introduced in the proposal, and thus restricts the choice of frame.

However, it has been recently shown that when the
trace-fixed particle frame
(TFP) is chosen, only two conditions are needed in order to achieve these desirable properties
Salazar
et al. 2025a
;
Salazar
et al. 2025b

Such a description is obtained when the non-equilibrium variables are matched to the equilibrium configuration by fixing the particle current density and the trace of the energy-momentum-stress tensor. Although at the phenomenological level this choice of matching conditions may seem arbitrary, in this work we argue that it has a natural interpretation within relativistic kinetic theory.

The same matching conditions have already been introduced by John Stewart in the context of the methods of moments, see chapter 4.4 in Ref.
Stewart 1971

The microscopic foundations for the BDNK theory have been previously explored by other authors by implementing the Hilbert expansion
Hoult and Kovtun 2022
or a modified Chapman-Enskog procedure
Rocha et al. 2022
. However, in this work we perform a thorough analysis of the integral equation that is obtained when the Chapman-Enskog expansion is introduced in the relativistic Boltzmann equation. We conclude that kinetic theory does indeed predict time-derivative terms in the first-order relativistic constitutive equations when the projection method developed in Ref.
Saint-Raymond 2014
is generalized to the relativistic scenario. It is worthwhile to mention that such generalization was previously applied in the Eckart frame
García-Perciante
et al. 2025
in order to shed light on the possibility of writing the constitutive equations in terms of different combinations of time and spatial derivatives of the state variables within the same frame.
3
3

3

These different combinations correspond to a change of representation, as explained in
García-Perciante
et al. 2025
and Section
IV.6
of the present paper.
In the present work we show that the TFP frame and its corresponding constitutive equations emerge naturally from kinetic theory by restricting the linearized collision operator on the orthogonal complement of its kernel in a suitable Hilbert space. On the one hand, this restriction guarantees the existence of the solution to the integral equation by projecting the source term on this space. On the other hand, it also yields the uniqueness of the solution since orthogonality to the kernel is equivalent to the imposition of the TFP frame. Additionally, we show that the formalism here developed is not exclusive to the TFP frame. Indeed, introducing alternative compatibility conditions adds terms corresponding to homogeneous solutions of the integral equation which expresses the constitutive equations in other frames. Furthermore, it is shown how the imposition of the balance equations directly in the source term adds a freedom which corresponds to a change of representation, which is crucial for achieving hyperbolicity, stability, and causality of the resulting system of equations, as shown in Refs.
Salazar
et al. 2025a
;
Salazar
et al. 2025b
.

In order to accomplish the tasks described above, we consider a fully relativistic kinetic gas consisting of identical spinless classical point particles of mass
m
and charge
q
propagating in a curved spacetime background
(M,g)
and electromagnetic field
F
. For generality, we perform the calculations in arbitrary spatial dimensions

In this work, we focus on the weak field hydrodynamic limit, that is, the limit in which the mean free path is much shorter than both the macroscopic scale and the characteristic length associated with the electromagnetic field. Therefore, to zeroth order, the gas lies in local thermodynamic equilibrium in which the one-particle distribution function is characterized by the particle density, the temperature, and the hydrodynamic velocity of the gas. In this article we derive the dissipative fluid theory resulting from including first-order deviations from local equilibrium.

This work is organized as follows. In Section
II

Next, in Section

V

VI

VII

VIII

k\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
. Then, the function
G_{k}:(0,\infty)\to(1,\infty)
is strictly monotonously decreasing and invertible.

As a consequence of the last two lemmas on has:

Lemma 3

For fixed

defined in Eq. (

are monotonously increasing in the temperature. Furthermore,

IV
Knudsen number, hydrodynamic limit and Chapman-Enskog approximation

From here on, we focus our attention on the hydrodynamic limit. To this purpose, in Section
IV.1
, we first review the relevant length scales that are associated with the problem and derive the rescaled Boltzmann equation. In Section
IV.2
we discuss the weak field hydrodynamic approximation which is based on a formal expansion of the distribution function in terms of the Knudsen parameter. Next, in Section
IV.3
we introduce the linearized collision operator and discuss its most important properties. The Hilbert method, in which one obtains an infinite hierarchy of macroscopic equations, is described in Section
IV.4
. This method is contrasted with the Chapman-Enskog one, described in Section
IV.5
, which (at a given truncation order in the Knudsen parameter) leads to a closed set of macroscopic equations for the particle density, temperature, and hydrodynamic velocity. Finally, in Section
IV.6
we present some variants of the Chapman-Enskog method which are important for the discussion in other sections of this article.

IV.1
Rescaling of the Boltzmann equation

For the following, we introduce the relevant length scales for our problem. Note that all these quantities are defined through scalars, such that they are independent of the choice of coordinates or reference frame.

•

A characteristic length scale
\ell_{\textrm{em}}
associated with the background electromagnetic field, which can be defined through the relation

\frac{q}{m}\sqrt{|F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}|}\sim\frac{1}{\ell_{\textrm{em}}}.

(IV.1)

For example, for a purely magnetic field,
1/\ell_{\text{em}}
is related to the cyclotron frequency (recall that we work in units for which the speed of light is one).

•

The macroscopic length scale
\ell_{\text{ms}}
, defined as a typical length over which the spacetime observables vary. For example, one could define

\ell_{\text{ms}}:=\frac{n}{\sqrt{|(\nabla^{\mu}n)(\nabla_{\mu}n)|}},\quad\hbox{with}\quad n=\sqrt{-J^{\mu}J_{\mu}},

(IV.2)

the invariant particle density.
5
5

5

When in local equilibrium, this
n
coincides with the particle density
n
defined in Eq. (
III.6
).

•

The mean free path
\ell_{\text{mfp}}
whose nature is microscopic and describes the average distance traveled by a particle between successive collisions. Locally, it is defined through the relation

\sigma_{T}\ell_{\text{mfp}}n=1,\quad\hbox{with}\quad\sigma_{T}:=\int\limits_{S^{d-1}}\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}d\Omega,

(IV.3)

the total cross section.

•

The mean inter-particle distance
\ell_{\mathrm{mpd}}:=n^{-1/d}
.

•

The thermal wavelength
\lambda_{\mathrm{T}}:=h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_{B}T}
, where
h
denotes Planck’s constant and
T
the temperature.

•

The curvature radius
\ell_{\textrm{R}}
, which can be defined through a suitable inverse power of a typical curvature invariant (see, for instance, the discussion of the geometric optics approximation in Ref.
Straumann 2013
). For example, in flat spacetime,
\ell_{\textrm{R}}=\infty
, whereas for a Schwarzschild black hole of mass
M
,
\ell_{\textrm{R}}\sim\sqrt{r^{3}/M}
at a sphere of areal radius
r
.

Several of these length scales already enter when formulating the relativistic Boltzmann equation (
II.4
). For instance, it is assumed that the gas particles behave as classical point particles between collisions, which requires

\lambda_{\mathrm{T}}\ll\ell_{\mathrm{mpd}}.

(IV.4)

Furthermore, one assumes that only binary elastic interactions take place and that they can be modeled as point-like collisions. This requires that the total cross section is small compared to the corresponding scale set by the curvature radius, that is,

\sigma_{T}\ll\ell_{\mathrm{R}}^{d-1}.

(IV.5)

After these observations, we may use the remaining length scales
\ell_{\mathrm{ms}}
,
\ell_{\mathrm{em}}
, and
\ell_{\mathrm{mfp}}
to express Eq. (
II.4
) in terms of dimensionless quantities, such that

x^{\mu}=\ell_{\mathrm{ms}}\overline{x}^{\mu},\qquad p_{\mu}=m\overline{p}_{\mu},\qquad f=\frac{1}{\ell_{\mathrm{ms}}^{d}m^{d}}\overline{f},\qquad\frac{q}{m}F_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{\ell_{\mathrm{em}}}\overline{F}_{\mu\nu},\qquad\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\frac{\ell_{\mathrm{ms}}^{d}}{\ell_{\mathrm{mfp}}}\frac{d\overline{\sigma}}{d\Omega},

(IV.6)

where all the quantities with a bar are dimensionless. In this way, one obtains the rescaled Boltzmann equation:

\overline{L}[\overline{f}]+\frac{1}{\mathrm{K}_{\mathrm{em}}}\overline{V}[\overline{f}]=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Kn}}\overline{Q}[\overline{f},\overline{f}],

(IV.7)

with

\overline{L}:=\overline{p}^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{x}^{\mu}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial g^{\alpha\beta}}{\partial\overline{x}^{\mu}}\overline{p}_{\alpha}\overline{p}_{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{p}_{\mu}},\qquad\overline{V}:=\overline{F}_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{p}_{\nu}},

(IV.8)

\overline{Q}[\overline{f},\overline{f}]
denoting the dimensionless collision term, and

\mathrm{Kn}:=\frac{\ell_{\mathrm{mfp}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{ms}}},\qquad\mathrm{K}_{\mathrm{em}}:=\frac{\ell_{\mathrm{em}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{ms}}}.

(IV.9)

The quantity
\mathrm{Kn}
represents the ratio between the mean free path and the macroscopic scale and is known as the Knudsen number. Likewise,
\mathrm{K}_{\mathrm{em}}
represents the ratio between the characteristic length scale associated with the electromagnetic field and the macroscopic scale.

Depending on whether
\mathrm{Kn}
and
\mathrm{K}_{\mathrm{em}}
are small or large and depending on their relative size there are different regimes one may consider. We mention the following three scenarios:

1.

The weak field hydrodynamic approximation:

\textrm{Kn}\ll 1\lesssim\textrm{K}_{\textrm{em}}.

(IV.10)

In this case the collision term dominates over the other terms. Note that these conditions imply that
\ell_{\mathrm{mfp}}\ll\ell_{\mathrm{em}}
, such that the mean free path is much smaller than the electromagnetic length scale.

2.

The strong field hydrodynamic approximation:

\textrm{Kn}\sim\textrm{K}_{\textrm{em}}\ll 1.

(IV.11)

In this case, both the collision term and the electromagnetic terms dominate over the transport term
\overline{L}[\overline{f}]
, and
\ell_{\text{mfp}}\sim\ell_{\mathrm{em}}
.

3.

The Vlasov limit:

1\sim\textrm{K}_{\textrm{em}}\ll\textrm{Kn},

(IV.12)

in which case collisions are sub-dominant.

In the remainder of this article, we shall focus on the first of these regimes. For approaches of regimes 2 and 3 in the nonrelativistic case, see for instance Refs.
García-Colín
et al. 2007
and
Balescu 1988
.

IV.2
The weak field hydrodynamic approximation and the Hilbert expansion

In the following, we study the weak field hydrodynamic regime which is characterized by Eq. (
IV.10
) and the small parameter

. For notational simplicity we denote

(IV.13)

where

are determined recursively by solving the integral equations

\displaystyle Q[f^{(0)},f^{(0)}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(IV.14)

\displaystyle Q[f^{(0)},f^{(1)}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L_{F}[f^{(0)}],

(IV.15)

\displaystyle Q[f^{(0)},f^{(2)}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L_{F}[f^{(1)}]-\frac{1}{2}Q[f^{(1)},f^{(1)}],

(IV.16)

\displaystyle\ldots

with

The zeroth-order equation (

) implies that

must be a local Jüttner distribution function as in Eq. (

) to

and

, which implies that the right-hand side of Eq. (

) is zero and hence that

must be a collision invariant. According to Theorem 5 in

must be precisely of the form (

). At this stage, the dependency of the fields

and

on

IV.3
The linearized collision operator (formal definition)

The first-order equation (
IV.15
) is an integral equation for
f^{(1)}
, and its solution requires a careful analysis of the linearized collision operator which consists of the map
f^{(1)}\mapsto Q[f^{(0)},f^{(1)}]
. In particular, the solvability of Eq. (
IV.15
) requires that the source term
\frac{1}{2}L_{F}[f^{(0)}]
lies in the image of this operator.

To analyze this point in more detail, it is convenient to rewrite Eq. (
IV.15
) in the form

\mathcal{L}[\phi]=\mathcal{Y},

(IV.17)

where we have set
\phi:=f^{(1)}/f^{(0)}
,
\mathcal{Y}:=-L_{F}[\log(Af^{(0)})]
and the linearized collision operator
\mathcal{L}
is formally defined as

\mathcal{L}[\phi](x,p_{1}):=-\frac{2Q[f^{(0)},f^{(0)}\phi](x,p_{1})}{f^{(0)}(x,p_{1})}=\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}\int\limits_{S^{d-1}}\mathcal{F}\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}f^{(0)}_{2}\left[\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}-\phi_{1}^{*}-\phi_{2}^{*}\right]d\Omega(\hat{q}^{*})\mbox{dvol}_{x}(p_{2}).

(IV.18)

Here, we have used Eq. (
II.6
) and the fact that
f^{(0)}(x,p_{1}^{*})f^{(0)}(x,p_{2}^{*})=f^{(0)}(x,p_{1})f^{(0)}(x,p_{2})
. Multiplying both sides of Eq. (
IV.18
) by an arbitrary test function
\psi
times
f^{(0)}
and integrating over
p_{1}
yields

\displaystyle\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}\psi(x,p)\mathcal{L}[\phi](x,p)f^{(0)}(x,p)\mbox{dvol}_{x}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}\int\limits_{S^{d-1}}\mathcal{F}\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}f^{(0)}_{1}f^{(0)}_{2}\left[\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}-\phi_{1}^{*}-\phi_{2}^{*}\right]\left[\psi_{1}+\psi_{2}-\psi_{1}^{*}-\psi_{2}^{*}\right]d\Omega(\hat{q}^{*})\mbox{dvol}_{x}(p_{1})\mbox{dvol}_{x}(p_{2}).

(IV.19)

This identity can also be derived directly from Eq. (
II.7
) by substituting
\Psi=\psi
,
f=f^{(0)}
and
h=f^{(1)}\phi
in this equation. Equation (
IV.19
) shows that the linear operator
\mathcal{L}
is symmetric with respect to the scalar product

\langle\psi,\phi\rangle_{x}:=\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}\psi(p)\phi(p)f^{(0)}(x,p)\mbox{dvol}_{x}(p).

(IV.20)

Furthermore, for
\psi=\phi
it follows that

\langle\phi,\mathcal{L}[\phi]\rangle_{x}=\frac{1}{4}\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}\int\limits_{S^{d-1}}\mathcal{F}\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}f^{(0)}_{1}f^{(0)}_{2}\left[\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}-\phi_{1}^{*}-\phi_{2}^{*}\right]^{2}d\Omega(\hat{q}^{*})\mbox{dvol}_{x}(p_{1})\mbox{dvol}_{x}(p_{2})\geq 0,

(IV.21)

which shows that
\mathcal{L}
is nonnegative and that its kernel consists of collision invariants, that is,

\ker\mathcal{L}=\mbox{span}\{1,p_{\mu}\}.

(IV.22)

Since
\mathcal{L}
is symmetric, its image is contained in
(\ker\mathcal{L})^{\perp}
, the orthogonal complement of its kernel. In Section
V
we will specify conditions on the differential cross section which guarantee that (when defined on a suitable function space) the image of
\mathcal{L}
is exactly equal to
(\ker\mathcal{L})^{\perp}
. It then follows that the first-order problem (
IV.17
) has a solution if and only if
\mathcal{Y}\in(\ker\mathcal{L})^{\perp}
, and that the solution is unique up to the addition of an element in
\ker\mathcal{L}
. Moreover, it follows that at each point
x\in M
, the operator
\mathcal{L}
is invertible and self-adjoint when restricted to the Hilbert space
(\ker\mathcal{L})^{\perp}
. In the following, we shall denote by
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
the inverse of
\mathcal{L}
on
(\ker\mathcal{L})^{\perp}
.

IV.5
The Chapman-Enskog method

The Chapman-Enskog approach is slightly different from the Hilbert method discussed in the previous section. Although it is based on an expansion of the one-particle distribution function similar to Eq. (
IV.13
), the crucial difference relies on the fact that the function
\alpha(x)
and the timelike vector field
\beta^{\mu}(x)
parametrizing
f^{(0)}
through Eq. (
II.13
) are determined by the (yet unknown) full distribution function
f
through suitable compatibility conditions, instead of the Euler equations. The methodology we describe next follows the formal approach described in Ref.
Saint-Raymond 2014
, here generalized to the relativistic regime and considering a charged fluid in the presence of a background electromagnetic field.

Let
f_{\varepsilon}
be a one-parameter family of solutions of the rescaled Boltzmann equations

L_{F}[f_{\varepsilon}]=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}Q[f_{\varepsilon},f_{\varepsilon}].

(IV.39)

The Chapman-Enskog ansatz is given by
Saint-Raymond 2014
;
Saint-Raymond 2009

f_{\varepsilon}=f^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon h_{\varepsilon},

(IV.40)

where
f^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}
denotes a local Jüttner distribution function of the form of Eq. (
III.17
) and
h_{\varepsilon}
determines the off-equilibrium correction. Here, the subscript
\varepsilon
in
f^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}
indicates that the local equilibrium function
f^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}
depends on
\varepsilon
through the fields
n_{\varepsilon}(x)
,
T_{\varepsilon}(x)
, and
u^{\mu}_{\varepsilon}(x)
that parametrize it by means of suitable compatibility conditions which specify a unique frame. Hence, in contrast to the Hilbert method in which the local equilibrium contribution
f^{(0)}
is completely determined by the ideal Euler equations, in the Chapman-Enskog method the fields
n_{\varepsilon}(x)
,
T_{\varepsilon}(x)
, and
u^{\mu}_{\varepsilon}(x)
that parametrize the local distribution function
f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
cannot be obtained from the zeroth-order equations alone. Instead, they are determined from the balance equations at the end of the calculation, once
f_{\varepsilon}
(or one if its truncations) is known.

Introducing the Chapman-Enskog ansatz (
IV.40
) into Eq. (
IV.39
) and recalling the definition (
IV.18
) of the linearized collision operator yields

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\left[\frac{h_{\varepsilon}}{f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}}\right]=-L_{F}[\log(Af_{\varepsilon}^{(0)})]-\varepsilon\frac{L_{F}[h_{\varepsilon}]-Q[h_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}]}{f^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}},

(IV.41)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
refers to the linearized collision operator at
f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
. As discussed previously, the integrability condition for this equation is that the right-hand side lies orthogonal to
\ker\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
. In the Chapman-Enskog approach as described in
Saint-Raymond 2014
this is enforced by taking the orthogonal projection of the right-hand side of Eq. (
IV.41
) onto
(\ker\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})^{\perp}
. Since
Q[h_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}]/f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
is already orthogonal to
\ker\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
(see Eq. (
II.7
)), this projection is valid as long as

0=\left\langle\Psi,L_{F}[\log(Af_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}]+\varepsilon\frac{L_{F}[h_{\varepsilon}]}{f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}}\right\rangle=\left\langle\Psi,\frac{L_{F}[f_{\varepsilon}]}{f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}}\right\rangle=\int\Psi(p)L_{F}[f_{\varepsilon}](p)\mbox{dvol}(p),

(IV.42)

for all collision invariants
\Psi
, which is equivalent to the statement that
J_{\varepsilon}^{\mu}:=J^{\mu}(f_{\varepsilon})
and
T_{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu}:=T^{\mu\nu}(f_{\varepsilon})
satisfy the balance equations. Therefore, provided one ensures that the observables associated with
f_{\varepsilon}
satisfy the balance equations (
II.11
) at the end of the process, one is allowed to take the orthogonal projection of the right-hand side of (
IV.41
) on
(\ker\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})^{\perp}
, such that

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\left[\frac{h_{\varepsilon}}{f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}}\right]=-\left(L_{F}[\log(Af_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}]\right)^{\perp}-\varepsilon\left(\frac{L_{F}[h_{\varepsilon}]-Q[h_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}]}{f^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\perp},

(IV.43)

where
(\ldots)^{\perp}
denotes the orthogonal projection of
(\ldots)
onto
(\ker\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})^{\perp}
.

As discussed at the end of Section
IV.3
, when restricted to the Hilbert space
(\ker\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})^{\perp}
, the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
is self-adjoint and invertible, and we denote the inverse of this restriction by

IV.6
Changes of frame and representation

We conclude this section by mentioning two possible types of generalizations of the Chapman-Enskog method. As discussed later, these generalizations are important for the frame-invariant interpretation of first-order dissipative fluids which have attracted much attention in recent years.

The first type of generalization is related to the choice of frame. Recall that when passing from Eq. (
IV.43
) to Eq. (
IV.44
) we have completely fixed the frame by requiring that
h_{\varepsilon}/f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
is orthogonal to the kernel of the linearized collision operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
which led to the compatibility conditions (
IV.47
) and (
IV.48
). However, one can add to the right-hand side of Eq. (
IV.44
) any term of the form

f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}\psi_{\varepsilon},\qquad\psi_{\varepsilon}\in\ker\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon},

(IV.57)

since with the addition of this term Eq. (
IV.44
) still implies Eq. (
IV.43
). As discussed below Eq. (
IV.33
) such a term corresponds to a variation of the equilibrium configuration, and hence it represents a change of frame. Therefore, by choosing
\psi_{\varepsilon}
appropriately, one should in principle be able to derive the results in any desired frame. This will be exemplified in the context of the first-order theory in Section
VI.3
.

The second generalization of the Chapman-Enskog method adds the following terms to the right-hand side of Eq. (
IV.43
):

-\frac{1}{k_{B}T_{\varepsilon}n_{\varepsilon}}\left[\hat{\Gamma}_{0}(\nabla_{\mu}J_{\varepsilon}^{\mu})u_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{k_{B}T_{\varepsilon}}\left(\nabla_{\mu}T_{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu}+qF_{\mu}{}^{\nu}J_{\varepsilon}^{\mu}\right)\left(\hat{\Gamma}_{1}u_{\nu}u_{\alpha}+\hat{\Gamma}_{2}\Delta_{\nu\alpha}\right)\right]u_{\beta}(p^{\alpha}p^{\beta})^{\perp},

(IV.58)

where
\hat{\Gamma}_{0}
,
\hat{\Gamma}_{1}
, and
\hat{\Gamma}_{2}
are dimensionless free parameters that can, in principle, depend on the temperature parameter
T_{\varepsilon}
. Since
J_{\varepsilon}^{\mu}
and
T_{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu}
are required to solve the balance equations, these terms are exactly zero
a posteriori
. Furthermore, since by construction they are orthogonal to
\ker\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
, the integrability condition for passing from Eq. (
IV.43
) to (
IV.44
) is still satisfied. As discussed later, the addition of these terms corresponds to a change of representation when truncating the series to first order, which turns out to be crucial to obtain hyperbolic evolution equations.

V
Mathematical discussion of the linearized collision operator

The considerations in Section
IV.3
motivate a well-defined mathematical framework that allows one to establish rigorous results regarding the properties of the linearized collision operator
\mathcal{L}
. A detailed discussion of such results lies beyond the scope of this article and have already been analyzed elsewhere, see for instance
Dudyński and Ekiel-Jeżewska 1988
for the three-dimensional case and
Saint-Raymond 2009
and references therein for the non-relativistic limit. However, since the properties of
\mathcal{L}
play such a central role in our analysis, we wish to provide a brief sketch of the methods used and state the most important results that are known so far.

For the following discussion we fix an event
x\in M
and an equilibrium distribution function
f^{(0)}
of the Jüttner form (
II.13
) in which it is assumed that
\alpha(x)\neq 0
and
\beta^{\mu}(x)=u^{\mu}(x)/(k_{B}T(x))
is future-directed timelike with
u^{\mu}
normalized such that
u^{\mu}u_{\mu}=-1
. Next, we introduce the separable Hilbert space
6
6

6

Note that if
\psi
is represented in terms of an orthonormal frame at
x
whose timelike vector coincides with
u^{\mu}(x)
, then
\mathcal{H}_{x}
is the weighted
L^{2}
-space

\left\{\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}:\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\psi(p)|^{2}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{|p|^{2}+m^{2}}}{k_{B}T(x)}}\frac{d^{d}p}{\sqrt{|p|^{2}+m^{2}}}<\infty\right\}.

{\cal H}_{x}:=L^{2}(P_{x}^{+}(m),d\mu_{x})
consisting of measurable functions
\psi:P_{x}^{+}(m)\to\mathbb{R}
on the future mass hyperboloid
P_{x}^{+}(m)
which are Lebesgue-square integrable with respect to the measure
d\mu_{x}(p):=f^{(0)}(x,p)\mbox{dvol}_{x}(p)
. The scalar product on
{\cal H}_{x}
is defined by Eq. (
IV.20
), that is,

\langle\psi,\phi\rangle_{x}:=\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}\psi(p)\phi(p)d\mu_{x}(p),\qquad\phi,\psi\in{\cal H}_{x}.

(V.1)

The goal is to prove that the linearized collision operator
\mathcal{L}
which is formally given by Eq. (
IV.18
) defines a nonnegative self-adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}:D(\mathcal{L})\subset{\cal H}_{x}\to{\cal H}_{x}
, with domain
D(\mathcal{L})
, which possesses a spectral gap (that is, a smallest positive element
\nu_{0}>0
of the spectrum). It then follows from the spectral theory of self-adjoint operators
Reed and Simon 1980a
;
Reed and Simon 1980b
that the restriction of
\mathcal{L}
to the Hilbert space
(\ker\mathcal{L})^{\perp}\subset\mathcal{H}_{x}
is again self-adjoint and has positive spectrum contained in
[\nu_{0},\infty)
. In particular, this restriction has a well-defined inverse

\mathcal{L}^{-1}:(\ker\mathcal{L})^{\perp}\to(\ker\mathcal{L})^{\perp}\cap D(\mathcal{L}),

(V.2)

which was already denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
in the previous section. For the purpose of this article this is the most important result of this section, since it provides the basis for a rigorous treatment of the Chapman-Enskog and Hilbert methods discussed in the previous section.

In order to prove the above properties and determine
D(\mathcal{L})
one needs to make suitable assumptions on the differential cross section. To keep the discussion simple, we do not specify the most general possible conditions and restrict ourselves to the following assumptions: there are constants
\sigma_{1}>0
,
\sigma_{2}>0
, and
0\leq\rho<2
, such that for all
\mathrm{g}\geq 0

\sigma_{T}(\mathrm{g}):=\int\limits_{S^{d-1}}\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\mathrm{g},\Theta)d\Omega\geq\sigma_{1}\frac{\mathrm{g}^{\rho+1}}{1+\mathrm{g}},\qquad\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\mathrm{g},\Theta)\leq\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\mbox{Vol}(S^{d-1})}\frac{1+\mathrm{g}^{\rho+1}}{\mathrm{g}}.

(V.3)

Here, the first inequality is a consequence of the “hard interaction” assumption (4.7) made in
Dudyński and Ekiel-Jeżewska 1988
. On the other hand, in the three-dimensional case the second inequality is equivalent to the assumption (2.7) in
Dudyński and Ekiel-Jeżewska 1988
with
\alpha=1
and
\gamma=0
, and it describes a relativistic generalization of Grad’s cutoff process which tames the singularities in the cross section originating from grazing collisions. Since only the combination
\mathcal{F}\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
appears in the linearized collision operator it is convenient to multiply both sides of the inequalities in Eq. (
V.3
) by the invariant flux
\mathcal{F}=m^{2}\mathrm{g}\sqrt{1+\mathrm{g}^{2}/4}
, which leads to the equivalent conditions

\mathcal{F}\sigma_{T}(\mathrm{g})\geq c_{1}\mathrm{g}^{\rho+2},\qquad\mathcal{F}\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\mathrm{g},\Theta)\leq\frac{c_{2}}{\mbox{Vol}(S^{d-1})}\left(1+\mathrm{g}^{\rho+2}\right),

(V.4)

for constants
c_{1}>0
,
c_{2}>0
, and
0\leq\rho<2
. Integrating the second inequality over
S^{d-1}
implies that the total cross section
\sigma_{T}
satisfies

c_{1}\mathrm{g}^{\rho+2}\leq\mathcal{F}\sigma_{T}(\mathrm{g})\leq c_{2}\left(1+\mathrm{g}^{\rho+2}\right).

(V.5)

Well-known models satisfying these bounds include Israel particles and hard spheres in three dimensions (see Refs.
Israel 1963
and
Cercignani and Kremer 2002
) and hard disks in two dimensions (see Ref.
García-Perciante and Méndez 2019
). In these cases, the transport coefficients can be computed analytically.

Having discussed the assumptions on the cross section, one proceeds as follows. First, the linearized collision operator
\mathcal{L}[\phi]
is written as the sum of three terms (known as the Hilbert decomposition):

\mathcal{L}[\phi]=\mathcal{M}_{\nu}[\phi]+\mathcal{K}_{1}[\phi]-\mathcal{K}_{2}[\phi].

(V.6)

The first operator on the right-hand side is the multiplication operator
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}[\phi]:=\nu_{x}\phi
involving the function
7
7

7

corresponds to

times the collisional frequency

introduced in
Dudyński and Ekiel-Jeżewska 1988
.

\nu_{x}(p_{1}):=\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}k_{1}(p_{1},p_{2})d\mu_{x}(p_{2}),\qquad k_{1}(p_{1},p_{2}):=\mathcal{F}\sigma_{T}(\mathrm{g})=m^{2}\mathrm{g}\sqrt{1+\frac{\mathrm{g}^{2}}{4}}\sigma_{T}(\mathrm{g}),

(V.7)

\mathcal{K}_{1}

\mathcal{K}_{2}

(V.8)

and

\mathcal{K}_{2}[\phi](p_{1}):=\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}\int\limits_{S^{d-1}}\mathcal{F}\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\mathrm{g},\Theta)\left[\phi(p_{1}^{*})+\phi(p_{2}^{*})\right]d\Omega(\hat{q}^{*})d\mu_{x}(p_{2}),

Next, one analyses the properties of the multiplication operator. To this purpose, one first proves:

VI.1
The trace-fixed particle frame

As mentioned above, the compatibility conditions specify the fields

,

, and

parametrizing the local Jüttner distribution function (

Notice that, using Eq. (

9

9

VII
Entropy current density and second law of thermodynamics

This section is dedicated to a brief discussion regarding the form of the entropy current in the Chapman-Enskog approach and on the validity of the second law of thermodynamics. Of course, Boltzmann’s H-theorem guarantees a nonnegative entropy production, as discussed in Section
II
. However, when truncating the Chapman-Enskog series to first order in the Knudsen parameter
\varepsilon
, as we do in this article, the corresponding entropy current density is not guaranteed to have strict nonnegative divergence. Nevertheless, as we show in this section, the leading-order terms in the entropy production, which are quadratic in
\varepsilon
, are positive-definite. In this sense, the second law of thermodynamics holds within the range of validity of the first-order theory.

In order to compute the entropy current density defined in Eq. (
II.3
) we first recast the Chapman-Enskog ansatz (
IV.40
) into the form

f_{\varepsilon}=f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}\left(1+\varepsilon\frac{h_{\varepsilon}}{f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}}\right),

(VII.1)

which yields

f_{\varepsilon}\log(Af_{\varepsilon})=f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}\log(Af_{\varepsilon}^{(0)})+\varepsilon\left[\log(Af_{\varepsilon}^{(0)})+1\right]h_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\varepsilon)^{j}}{j(j+1)}\left(\frac{h_{\varepsilon}}{f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}}\right)^{j+2}f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)},

(VII.2)

with the series converging absolutely if
|h_{\varepsilon}|\leq f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
. From Eq. (
III.18
) we have

\log(Af_{\varepsilon}^{(0)})+1=\frac{\mu+u^{\alpha}p_{\alpha}}{k_{B}T},

(VII.3)

where for notational simplicity we have omitted the subscripts
\varepsilon
in
\mu
,
T
, and
u^{\alpha}
. From this, we obtain the following expansion of the entropy current density:

S^{\alpha}(f_{\varepsilon})=S^{\alpha}(f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)})-\varepsilon\frac{\mu J^{\alpha}(h_{\varepsilon})+T^{\alpha\beta}(h_{\varepsilon})u_{\beta}}{T}-k_{B}\varepsilon^{2}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\varepsilon)^{j}}{j(j+1)}J^{\alpha}\left(\frac{h_{\varepsilon}^{j+2}}{(f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)})^{j+1}}\right),

(VII.4)

where the zeroth-order contribution is

S^{\alpha}(f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)})=-\frac{(\mu-k_{B}T)J^{\alpha}(f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)})+T^{\alpha\beta}(f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)})u_{\beta}}{T}=nsu^{\alpha},\qquad s=\frac{h-\mu}{T}.

(VII.5)

Therefore, including first-order corrections, we obtain

S^{\alpha}(f_{\varepsilon})=S_{IS}^{\alpha}(f_{\varepsilon})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),

(VII.6)

with
S_{IS}^{\alpha}
being the Israel-Stewart entropy current density defined as
Israel and Stewart 1979
;
Israel 1989

S_{IS}^{\alpha}(f_{\varepsilon}):=\frac{1}{T}\left[\mathrm{p}u^{\alpha}-\mu J^{\alpha}(f_{\varepsilon})-T^{\alpha\beta}(f_{\varepsilon})u_{\beta}\right].

(VII.7)

Of course, one can replace
f_{\varepsilon}
with
f_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}+\varepsilon h_{0}
in
S_{IS}^{\alpha}
and Eq. (
VII.6
) is still correct. From now on, we assume that we evaluate
S_{IS}^{\alpha}
using the latter choice and omit the argument for notational simplicity.

As reviewed in Appendix B of Ref.
Salazar
et al. 2025b
, the Israel-Stewart entropy current satisfies the covariant Gibbs relation

\delta S_{IS}^{\alpha}=-\frac{u_{\beta}}{T}\delta T^{\alpha\beta}-\frac{\mu}{T}\delta J^{\alpha},

(VII.8)

for arbitrary perturbations around an equilibrium configuration. Consequently,
S_{IS}^{\alpha}
is first-order frame invariant. Furthermore, using the balance laws and the notation in Appendix
A
one obtains

\displaystyle T\nabla_{\alpha}S_{IS}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-(\mathcal{E}-ne)\frac{\dot{T}}{T}-(\mathcal{P}-\mathrm{p})\theta-(\mathcal{N}-n)T\overset{\cdot}{\left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right)}

\displaystyle-\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\alpha}-h\mathcal{J}^{\alpha}\right)\left(\frac{D_{\alpha}T}{T}+a_{\alpha}\right)-h\mathcal{J}^{\alpha}\left(a_{\alpha}+\frac{D_{\alpha}\mathrm{p}}{nh}-\frac{q}{h}E_{\alpha}\right)-\mathcal{T}^{\alpha\beta}\sigma_{\alpha\beta},

(VII.9)

which reduces to Eq. (B5) in
Salazar
et al. 2025b
in a particle frame, for which
\mathcal{N}=n
and
\mathcal{J}^{\alpha}=0
. In particular, for the constitutive relations (
VI.13
) in the TFP frame derived in the previous section, one finds

T\nabla_{\alpha}S_{IS}^{\alpha}=\varpi\left(\frac{\dot{T}}{T}+\frac{\theta}{d}\right)^{2}+\lambda\left(\frac{D^{\alpha}T}{T}+a^{\alpha}\right)\left(\frac{D_{\alpha}T}{T}+a_{\alpha}\right)+2\eta\sigma^{\alpha\beta}\sigma_{\alpha\beta},

(VII.10)

which is positive-definite, given that the transport coefficients
\varpi
,
\lambda
, and
\eta
are positive. Therefore, the theory associated with the constitutive equations (
VI.13
) has exact nonnegative entropy production! However, as discussed towards the end of the last section, in order to achieve hyperbolic evolution equations in the resulting fluid theory, one needs to consider the modified constitutive relations (
VI.32
–
VI.34
). This leads to additional terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (
VII.10
) and the resulting entropy production is not manifestly nonnegative anymore. However, these additional terms are third-order in the gradients, and hence they constitute higher-order terms. In this sense, the second law of thermodynamics still holds within the limit of validity of our first-order theory.

We end this section with two comments. The first one refers to the decomposition
S_{IS}^{\alpha}=\mathcal{S}u^{\alpha}+\phi^{\alpha}
of the Israel-Stewart entropy current for which (in the notation of Appendix
A
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=ns+\frac{\mathcal{E}-ne}{T}-(\mathcal{N}-n)\frac{\mu}{T},

(VII.11)

\displaystyle\phi^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{Q}^{\alpha}-\mu\mathcal{J}^{\alpha}}{T},

(VII.12)

such that one can rewrite Eq. (

) in the form

\displaystyle T\nabla_{\alpha}S_{IS}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-(\mathcal{S}-ns)\dot{T}-(\mathcal{N}-n)\dot{\mu}-(\mathcal{P}-\mathrm{p})\theta

\displaystyle-T\phi^{\alpha}\left(\frac{D_{\alpha}T}{T}+a_{\alpha}\right)-h\mathcal{J}^{\alpha}\left(a_{\alpha}+\frac{D_{\alpha}\mathrm{p}}{nh}-\frac{q}{h}E_{\alpha}\right)-\mathcal{T}^{\alpha\beta}\sigma_{\alpha\beta}.

(VII.13)

The second comment refers to the comparison of our results with the Eckart frame, for which
\mathcal{E}=ne
in addition to
\mathcal{N}=n
and

. In this case, Eq. (
VII.11
) yields

, and thus it follows either from Eq. (

,

that

T\nabla_{\alpha}S_{IS}^{\alpha}=\zeta\theta^{2}+\kappa\left(\frac{D^{\alpha}T}{T}+a^{\alpha}\right)\left(\frac{D_{\alpha}T}{T}+a_{\alpha}\right)+2\eta\sigma^{\alpha\beta}\sigma_{\alpha\beta},

(VII.14)

which is also positive-definite. The resulting theory is not hyperbolic; however it can be made hyperbolic by changing the frame and representation (again at the cost of introducing third-order terms in the entropy production), see

. Note that the fact that

T

\mathcal{E}-ne
nor

are zero off equilibrium and thus

VIII
Conclusions

In this article we provided a detailed microscopic derivation of the first-order theory for relativistic dissipative fluids put forward and shown to be hyperbolic, causal, and stable in Refs.
Salazar
et al. 2025a
;
Salazar
et al. 2025b
. Our derivation is based on a Chapman-Enskog approximation for the near-equilibrium solutions of the general relativistic Boltzmann equation. In contrast to previous works (see for example
Israel 1963
;
de Groot et al. 1980
;
Cercignani and Kremer 2002
;
Rocha et al. 2022
), we have developed a new scheme within this approximation which follows the lines of
Saint-Raymond 2014
;
Saint-Raymond 2009
;
García-Perciante
et al. 2025
and is based on inverting the linearized collision operator on the orthogonal complement of its kernel in a suitable Hilbert space. The selection of this space guarantees the existence and uniqueness of the solution at each order. Furthermore, as we have discussed, this space leads naturally to the trace-fixed particle frame adopted in
Salazar
et al. 2025a
;
Salazar
et al. 2025b
. However, as explained in this work, the resulting theory can be translated to any hydrodynamic frame.

Moreover, we have demonstrated that the force-flux representation-freedom (i.e. the freedom to add combinations of the Euler equations to the constitutive relations) can be incorporated in our Chapman-Enskog scheme by adding a suitable term to the Boltzmann equation which is zero on-shell. As explained in
Salazar
et al. 2025a
;
Salazar
et al. 2025b
exploiting this representation-freedom is fundamental to achieve hyperbolicity and stability of global equilibria.

Although only applied here to derive the first-order theory, our scheme can in principle also be used to derive a fluid theory to any desired order of the Knudsen parameter. It should be interesting to investigate whether the second- or higher-order approximations obtained in this way, together with the representation-freedom, still lead to physically sound fluid theories.

We hope that this work provides the foundations for a new understanding of the Chapman-Enskog approximation in the relativistic regime and its applications to derive physically sound equations of relativistic dissipative fluids. It should be interesting to generalize it to consider more general situations including mixtures of gases or an investigation of the strong field hydrodynamic regime.

\displaystyle(p^{\alpha}p^{\beta})^{\perp}\Delta_{\alpha\beta}{}^{\mu\nu},

(E.12)

\displaystyle p^{\mu}p^{\nu}u_{\mu}\Delta_{\nu}{}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(p^{\mu}p^{\nu})^{\perp}u_{\mu}\Delta_{\nu}{}^{\alpha}-mG_{1}(z)p^{\nu}\Delta_{\nu}{}^{\alpha},

(E.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(p^{\mu}p^{\nu})^{\perp}u_{\mu}u_{\nu}+mc_{1}p^{\nu}u_{\nu}+m^{2}c_{2},

(E.14)

where we have introduced the projection onto the spatial trace-free part

\Delta_{\alpha\beta}{}^{\mu\nu}:=\Delta_{\alpha}{}^{\mu}\Delta_{\beta}{}^{\nu}-\frac{1}{d}\Delta_{\alpha\beta}\Delta^{\mu\nu},

(E.15)

and the coefficients

and

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle 2b_{1}-b_{3}=\frac{d}{z^{2}G_{-1}^{\prime}(z)}\left[G_{1}(z)\frac{c_{v}}{k_{B}}-\left(G_{1}(z)-\frac{1}{z}\right)\frac{c_{\mathrm{p}}}{k_{B}}\right],

(E.16)

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-b_{2}=-\frac{1}{z^{2}G_{-1}^{\prime}(z)}\frac{c_{v}}{k_{B}}.

In this appendix, we define the functionals
\mathcal{W}_{n,k}[\phi]
used in Section

\phi
of the variable
\gamma

(F.1)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}\phi(\gamma)\mbox{dvol}_{x}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0,0}[\phi],

(F.2)

\displaystyle m^{2}\left(u_{\mu}u_{\nu}\mathcal{W}_{2,0}[\phi]+\frac{1}{d}\Delta_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{W}_{0,1}[\phi]\right),

(F.4)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{P_{x}^{+}(m)}p_{\mu}p_{\nu}p_{\sigma}\phi(\gamma)\mbox{dvol}_{x}(p)

\displaystyle m^{3}\left(u_{\mu}u_{\nu}u_{\sigma}\mathcal{W}_{3,0}[\phi]+\frac{3}{d}\Delta_{(\mu\nu}u_{\sigma)}\mathcal{W}_{1,1}[\phi]\right),
