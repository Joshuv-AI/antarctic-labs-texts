Phylogenetic reconciliation supports a methanogenic ancestor of the Archaea and a derived origin for host-associated lineages
Authors: Wen-Cong Huang, Nina Dombrowski, Tara A. Mahendrarajah, Noah Wahl, Alexandros Stamatakis, Gergely J. Szöllősi, Tom A. Williams, Anja Spang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The phylogeny of the Archaea continues to be revisited and revised as new groups are discovered and phylogenetic methods improve, but key questions about their early evolution remain unanswered. It has been suggested that the root of the Archaea may lie on, or potentially within, any of three major groups - the Euryarchaeota, TACK+Asgard clade, and DPANN, the last of which includes many host-associated and genome-reduced lineages. These root hypotheses make starkly different predictions about the nature of early archaeal evolution: for example, a root on or within DPANN might suggest a small-genome and host-associated ancestor, with methanogenesis, the hallmark metabolism of the Archaea, evolving later. Here, we investigate the position of the archaeal root and the nature of the last archaeal common ancestor using a range of phylogenetic approaches, including the best available site- and branch-heterogeneous substitution models, and new gene tree-species tree reconciliation models that capture changes in rates of gene duplication, loss and transfer across the phylogeny. Our analyses converge on a narrow archaeal root region at or near the base of the Euryarchaeota, supporting hypotheses in which the Last Archaeal Common Ancestor (LACA) was a complex, free-living (hyper-)thermophilic methanogen. We recover DPANN as the sister group to TACK and Asgard archaea, and suggest that their genome evolution has been characterised by episodes of genome streamlining and expansion, driven by gene loss and transfer.

Introduction

Over the past 15 years, developments in environmental genomics have led to the discovery of major new lineages that have fuelled new interest in, and debates about, the deep structure of the tree of life1–7. In particular, the discovery of clades comprising genetically diverse, small-genome-sized, host-associated archaea and bacteria—i.e., the “DPANN” archaea (an acronym of the first described phyla: Diapherotrites, Parvarchaeota, Aenigmarchaeota, Nanoarchaeota and Nanohaloarchaeota, now referred to as Nanobdellati8 and hereafter informally as DPANN) and the “CPR” bacteria (Candidate Phyla Radiation bacteria, now termed Patescibacteria9/Minisyncoccota10 and hereafter informally referred to as CPR)—has catalysed new controversies about the antiquity of symbiosis and parasitism in life’s history. Initial phylogenetic analyses placed DPANN and CPR at the base of their respective domains4, potentially implying archaeal and bacterial ancestors of only moderate size and cellular and metabolic complexity. Subsequent analyses using improved phylogenetic methods resolved CPR as a derived clade related to Chloroflexota11–15 and suggested that the last bacterial common ancestor was likely a complex and free-living cell akin in complexity to modern bacteria12. However, resolving or approaching a similar degree of consensus on the position of DPANN within Archaea has proven more challenging, with published analyses recovering phylogenies that have starkly different consequences for our understanding of archaeal origins and the sequence of metabolic evolution within this domain16–20.

The primary question is whether DPANN branch at the base of the archaeal domain, or alternatively whether DPANN occupy a derived position, as sister to or branching within one of the other major lineages, which include “Euryarchaeota” (also referred to as Methanobacteriati8 and hereafter informally as Euryarchaeota), Thermoproteota (previously referred to as “TACK” Archaea21, now as Thermoproteati8 and hereafter as TACK) and “Asgard” archaea (Asgardarchaeota22/ Promethearchaeota23; hereafter referred to as Asgard)4,14,17–20,24. Resolving the placement of DPANN in the archaeal tree is critically important because it informs the nature of LACA and the tempo and mode of archaeal genome evolution. This is because DPANN predominantly, though not exclusively24–28, comprise organisms with reduced genomes and apparently limited metabolic capabilities. Indeed, the first cultivated representatives29–36 of DPANN were shown to be obligate archaeal ectosymbionts depending on distinct archaeal24–26,37 and potentially even bacterial hosts38. If the DPANN branch at the base of the Archaea, this could suggest that LACA was a relatively simple and small-genome-sized organism, with methanogenesis—the hallmark metabolism of the Archaea39–41—evolving later, after the divergence of DPANN from Euryarchaeota, TACK and Asgard archaea. Alternatively, if the archaeal root lies within one of those alternative lineages with DPANN being derived, LACA may instead have been a bona fide complex, free-living organism.

Specifically, determining the position of DPANN within the archaeal phylogeny has proven challenging. Some recent studies have resolved DPANN as the earliest-diverging branch in the archaeal tree13,14,18, or with, or within, Euryarchaeota17, while others have suggested that DPANN might actually be polyphyletic, evolving multiple times from within the Euryarchaeota via parallel events of genome reduction42–44. Each of these inferred positions has been suggested to be induced by various phylogenetic artefacts. For example, the distinctive evolutionary mode of DPANN—which, as symbionts, may both evolve fast and lose many genes otherwise conserved in archaea—might artifactually draw them to the root of the tree due to long branch attraction (LBA)17,19,20,45. On the other hand, alternative DPANN placements at derived positions within the archaeal tree have also been suggested to result from phylogenetic artefacts. For example, the placement of DPANN with Euryarchaeota in some analyses has been suggested to result from unmodelled horizontal gene transfer (HGT) between DPANN symbionts and their euryarchaeotal hosts46,47, or from systematic phylogenetic error48. A recent analysis using a newly-developed non-stationary phylogenetic model that captures variation in sequence composition across both the sites of the alignment and the branches of the tree—two pervasive but often un-modelled features of sequence evolution—provided support for a derived position of DPANN branching sister to Euryarchaeota, with the root of the archaeal tree lying between the TACK+Asgard archaea and all other archaea17.

The studies summarised above used a range of distinct approaches to root the archaeal phylogeny. The most widely used rooting approach is to use an outgroup— that is, to include a bacterial outgroup to root the archaeal tree on the inter-domain branch. This approach is conceptually straightforward but may be problematic for rooting entire domains, because the long inter-domain branch might induce errors in the within-Archaea topology49,50. In particular, the basal position of DPANN in published trees might result from long-branch attraction between the DPANN and inter-domain stems. As an alternative to outgroup rooting, non-reversible or non-stationary models can be used to root the tree based on capturing directional aspects of the evolutionary process, such as long-term trends in amino acid substitution patterns or changing amino acid compositions over time51–55. However, the power of these approaches to root trees depends on the strength of the time-dependent signal in the data and the ability of the model to adequately describe the underlying processes53. These signals are sometimes weak for anciently diverged sequences51. A third approach is to use phylogenetic reconciliation, which can assess distinctly rooted species trees based on inferred evolutionary events that have a clear direction in time, such as gene duplications, losses and transfers12,18,56,57. However, as with substitution models, the accuracy of the results depends on the adequacy with which the model captures the underlying processes12. In addition to these modelling concerns, different studies have used distinct sets of phylogenetic “marker” genes—genes that are thought to provide a reliable trace of vertical archaeal evolution—and protein families in the case of reconciliations with the differing properties of these datasets introducing additional variation into the results13,17–20,45,48. While none of the available rooting methods or datasets are conclusive, the hope is that, as methods improve, they may converge on a consistent root position supported—or at least not strongly rejected—by distinct analytical approaches.

Here, we investigate the root of the archaeal tree, using the best available outgroup-rooting methods and recently developed non-reversible and non-stationary substitution models51,58,59. For the first time, we also apply a new class of more flexible and better-fitting phylogenetic reconciliation methods that we recently developed56, and which allow the model of genome evolution to vary across the rooted species tree. The reconciliation method not only allows us to assess the support for various root hypotheses but also to reconstruct historical patterns of genome evolution across Archaea.

Results

Outgroup rooting has only limited power to resolve the root of Archaea

We re-visited suitable markers and outgroup rooting on an archaeal tree using a focal dataset of 257 Archaea and 103 Bacteria (Supplementary Data 1) based on 25 vertically-evolving markers that did not violate the requirement of domain monophyly (Supplementary Data 2)13. Specifically, we applied several rounds of manual curation to assemble a dataset of 257 phylogenetically representative, high-quality (low contamination, high completeness) genomes sampled from across the known diversity of Archaea (Supplementary Data 1) (see “Methods”). The maximum likelihood tree under the best-fitting site-heterogeneous model (LG + C60 + F + R; chosen via the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)) placed the archaeal root between DPANN and all other archaea (Fig. S1, DPANN root, 93.3 and 61.18 ultrafast bootstrap support60, respectively, for the monophyly of DPANN lineage and the monophyly of Euryarchaeota+TACK+Asgard), consistent with some previous analyses4,13,18,24,61. However, previous work has shown that there is sometimes limited information about the archaeal root position in such “tree of life”-scale analyses12–14,18,62, so that the best-known maximum likelihood tree is not necessarily significantly better than trees with alternative root positions. To test this possibility on our data, we generated a range of rooting hypotheses (Supplementary Data 3), including key hypotheses from the literature as well as other plausible root positions (e.g., between each of the major archaeal taxonomic clades), by relocating the bacterial outgroup's attachment point to the archaeal ingroup. For each candidate root, we inferred the best-known ML tree while remaining consistent with that root hypothesis using a constrained tree search in IQ-TREE 263, and then assessed whether the likelihoods of these trees were significantly worse than the unconstrained ML tree using an approximately unbiased (AU) test64. This analysis rejected some hypotheses, such as a root between the Asgard archaea and all other Archaea (P < 0.05; Supplementary Data 3). However, various other hypotheses, some of which received literature support, e.g., DPANN root18, a root between TACK+Asgard archaea and all other archaea (hereafter, TACK+Asgard root)17,45, a root between a clade comprising Halobacteriota and Thermoplasmatota and all other archaea (hereafter, Halobacteriota+Thermoplasmatota root)19,20, and a root between Euryarchaeota and all other archaea (hereafter, Euryarchaeota root), remained statistically indistinguishable (Supplementary Data 3). These results are also consistent with another recent analysis of the tree of life using site-heterogeneous models62, in which the maximum likelihood analysis supported TACK+Asgard on one side of the archaeal root, and Euryarchaeota+DPANN on the other (“Topology 1” in ref. 64); however, this root was statistically indistinguishable from a root between DPANN and all other Archaea based on the AU test.

Inferring an unrooted species tree of Archaea

Given the lack of resolution in the outgroup rooting analyses, we next inferred an unrooted phylogenetic tree for subsequent use of outgroup-free rooting using non-stationary and phylogenetic reconciliation models17,56–59,65. Since tree search is computationally prohibitive under these models, we followed a two-step procedure in which we initially estimated an unrooted archaeal species tree using a concatenation of phylogenetic marker genes, then searched for the root position on this fixed tree using a variety of approaches. We extracted the sequences for a set of 51 marker genes previously suggested to evolve vertically within the Archaea61 from our archaeal focal dataset of 257 representative genomes; manual inspection (see “Methods”) suggested that 45 of those markers were suitable for concatenation (i.e., they had no indication for inter-domain HGT and phylogenetic incongruence) (Supplementary Data 4). We then inferred an unrooted tree using the best-fitting model (LG + C60 + F + R; chosen by BIC; see “Methods”), which accounts for different rates of change among amino acids and variation in evolutionary rate, as well as sequence composition across the sites of the sequence alignment. This phylogeny recovered the clanhood (that is, monophyly conditional on a root outside the group66) of three archaeal groups with maximal or near-maximal support based on the ultrafast bootstrap (UFBOOT)60 and Shimodaira–Hasegawa approximate likelihood ratio test (SH-aLRT)67 metrics: Euryarchaeota (100/99), TACK+Asgard archaea (100/100) and DPANN archaea (100/100) (Fig. 1). To evaluate whether the inferred topology might be affected by across-branch variation in amino acid composition—a feature of sequence evolution not accounted for by the LG + C60 + F + R model—we incrementally filtered out 10%–80% of the most compositionally-biased sites from the alignment and re-inferred the species trees, which produced highly congruent species trees that all recovered the three archaeal groups (Fig. S2). This suggests that key features of our topology are not artefacts of biased sequence composition. Phylogenetic inference using an evenly downsampled, phylum-level dataset produced highly congruent species trees that consistently recovered these three groups and archaeal phylum relationships, indicating that topologies reported here are not affected by taxon sampling or imbalance (Fig. S3). Furthermore, we used Astral-Pro368, a gene tree summary approach, to infer a species tree from all 7059 arCOG (archaeal Cluster of Orthologues) families, including the 45 core genes used for concatenation and 241 additional archaeal gene families found in at least 80% of archaeal genomes (Fig. S4). The inferred topologies were in agreement with previous analyses—for example, recovering Euryarchaeota, TACK+Asgard and DPANN clans, as well as many of the major relationships within each of those groups14,17,18,61,62. Finally, to evaluate the impact of differing evolutionary histories among genes introduced by horizontal gene transfers, we fitted a mixture across sites and trees (MAST)69 model to the same concatenation. One lineage that has proven difficult to place in previous analyses is the Altiarchaeota, which branch with DPANN but do not appear to be genome-reduced and are predicted to be capable of free-living growth27. We recovered Altiarchaeota as a member of DPANN Cluster 1 (a clade comprising Altiarchaeota, Micrarchaeota, Iainarchaeota and B1Sed10-23)61 (Fig. 1), in agreement with several previous studies61,70–73. By contrast, other recent studies placed the Altiarchaeota as the earliest-diverging branch within DPANN as a whole17,19. These studies made use of an alternative set of marker genes with a range of functions beyond information processing, including metabolic genes such as subunits of ATP synthase. While an expanded marker set is valuable, a recurrent challenge is the higher rate of horizontal gene transfer in non-ribosomal proteins13. We re-analysed these genes and, after filtering out marker genes with instances of HGT within Archaea and between Archaea and Bacteria, recovered Altiarchaeota at the base of Cluster 1 (see Supplementary Note 1 for further details, Fig. S6 and Supplementary Data 5).

Analysis with non-reversible and non-stationary substitution models supports a root within Euryarchaeota

We next used non-reversible models to infer a rooted species tree and a non-stationary substitution model to infer the root position on the fixed unrooted species tree. We first performed analyses under the non-reversible models51,74 implemented in IQ-TREE 263. In these models, exchangeabilities between amino acid states are non-reversible; for example, the probability of change from D (aspartic acid) to E (glutamic acid) is different to the probability from E to D; as a result, trees inferred under the non-reversible models are intrinsically rooted, with the root information derived from long-term trends in protein evolution (a long-term difference in the rate at which exchanges occur between pairs of amino acids; e.g., the relative rate of change of amino acid from D to E was 0.45, while the rate from E to D was 0.19, in our dataset). When applying such considerations to the 257 archaeal species, the 45-marker gene dataset inferred the best known maximum likelihood root between Korarchaeota and all other archaea (Korarchaeota root, rootstrap support: 42.8)51 under the NONREV + R10 model (Fig. S7A) which provided the best model fit among the models tested (Supplementary Data 6). As in the outgroup-rooting analyses, we used an AU test64 on site-wise likelihoods to evaluate whether the Korarchaeota root provided a significantly better fit to the data than alternative positions that are rerooted at every branch of the inferred maximum likelihood tree. As with outgroup rooting, the analysis indicated that a variety of root hypotheses could not be rejected under the NONREV + R10 model for these data, including roots on Korarchaeota, Thermoproteota, DPANN, Euryarchaeota and TACK+Asgard archaea (Supplementary Data 6 and Fig. S7A).

Non-stationary models can also be used to infer the root of a tree, accounting for changes in the substitution process over time. One such model is the GFmix model17,58,59, which models changing nucleotide or amino acid sequence compositions over the branches of the phylogenetic tree. This kind of compositional change is manifest in real data. For example, halophilic archaea were reported to be enriched in D and E while depleted in I (isoleucine) and K (lysine) compared to non-halophilic archaea48,75. GFmix is particularly appealing for analyses of deep microbial evolution because it combines the modelling of across-site compositional heterogeneity (as in the site-heterogeneous C60 model described above) with across-branch compositional variation—both pervasive features of these datasets—in a single analysis. When applied to our 45-gene supermatrix, a root within the Euryarchaeota, i.e., the Halobacteriota+Thermoplasmatota root19,20 had the highest likelihood with all other tested roots being rejected by an AU test (P < 0.05, Supplementary Data 7). By contrast, a recent analysis of a deep archaeal phylogeny using GFmix and a different marker gene set, tested a range of root hypotheses and reported that a root between TACK+Asgard and all other Archaea had the highest likelihood among the trees tested, with DPANN being derived from within a paraphyletic Euryarchaeota17. We repeated this analysis on the original dataset, but including a broader set of root hypotheses (9 more positions in addition to the DPANN root and TACK+Asgard root) (Supplementary Data 8) and found that several of these had significantly higher likelihoods. Among those we tested, the Halobacteriota+Thermoplasmatota root19,20 had the highest likelihood in agreement with our new results from the 45-gene concatenate (being 8.6 likelihood units higher than the TACK+Asgard root; see Supplementary Data 7). All other tested roots, including a root between TACK+Asgard and all other Archaea17, were rejected by the AU test (P < 0.05; see Supplementary Data 8).

Overall, our analyses of these datasets using outgroup rooting, non-reversible, and non-stationary models suggest that the root of the Archaea remains a challenging problem, yielding rather similar likelihoods for a variety of plausible root hypotheses, most of which are within 3 nodes of one another on the unrooted species tree. To complement these analyses, we explored rooting using gene tree-species tree reconciliation, which makes use of a substantially larger fraction of the available genome data and might therefore have greater resolving power to assess contrasting root hypotheses12,18,65.

Improved modelling of archaeal gene family evolution using branch-heterogeneous phylogenetic reconciliation models

Early reconciliation analyses of the archaeal root made use of a limited set of archaeal diversity (e.g., 60 genomes in Williams et al.18), and a model that made the simplifying assumption that rates of duplication (D), transfer (T) and loss (L) do not vary across the branches of the species tree. However, various lines of evidence suggest that D, T and L rates do vary among archaeal clades, reflecting differences in the fixation of D, T and L events among lineages. Note that here and in what follows, DTL rates and events refer to fixations in the population (which are detectable from gene tree-species tree reconciliation), not rates at which Ds, Ts or Ls first appear in a population; the latter would require population-genetic data estimates. Thus, DTL rates have the same interpretation as substitution rates in traditional phylogenetic models. Our analyses indicate that duplications appear to be more common in Asgard archaea18,76, while losses are more common among DPANN lineages18. In principle, a failure to accommodate these varying gene fixation rates might mislead phylogenetic analyses that rely on reconciliation methods, just as failure to accommodate varying evolutionary rates or compositions among alignment sites can induce high support for the incorrect trees in traditional sequence-based phylogenetics58,77,78. Both of these limitations can now be overcome: the genome sampling of the archaeal domain is far richer than previously, and we have recently developed more flexible phylogenetic reconciliation models that relax the assumptions of constant D, T and L rates across the species tree implemented in AleRax56.

To begin, we fit a three-parameter branch-homogeneous reconciliation model (one D, T and L rate, respectively, common to all branches, referred to as global in what follows) to the dataset of 257 archaeal genomes and 7059 gene family phylogenies, and used an AU64 test to compare support for a range of potential root positions that were also assessed in the outgroup rooting analyses (Fig. 1, 1−15). The result provided strong support for a root between DPANN Cluster 261—a clade within DPANN—and all other archaea (referred to as DPANN Cluster 2 root, Fig. 1A) (Supplementary Data 9). To gain insight into the nature of the signal for the DPANN Cluster 2 root, we investigated the properties of the gene families that favoured the DPANN Cluster 2 root over alternative root positions, using gene family filtering to explore root signals12. We noticed that the preference of DPANN Cluster 2 root came from gene families with a small number of species and a biased taxonomic representation (Fig. S8A, B), consistent with a “small genome attraction (SGA)” artefact, where the model prefers a root that separates smaller genomes from larger ones on the tree18. In principle, such a bias might arise in analyses under a branch-homogeneous DTL model if gene absences in genome-reduced lineages are over-interpreted as evidence that a given lineage never had a gene, overlooking the possibility that genes might be absent due to secondary loss. Consistent with this possibility, DPANN Cluster 2 clades predominantly comprise symbionts with reduced genomes, suggesting that they might exhibit a higher-than-average gene loss rate26. To test whether the assumption of a single, global loss rate might drive support for the DPANN Cluster 2 root, we added one more parameter to allow for a different loss (L) rate for DPANN archaea (DPANN_L), and reassessed root supports using an AU test64. Adding a DPANN-specific loss rate substantially improved reconciliation model fit (BIC: 3284636.6, Supplementary Data 10), and, as predicted, the DPANN loss rate was inferred to be higher than that for the other clades (DPANN: 0.40-0.45, other clades: 0.26-0.27). Interestingly, this analysis expanded the set of plausible roots that could not be rejected by the AU test under the global model, including two alternative root positions that are located nearby on the unrooted tree: Euryarchaeota root or TACK+Asgard root (P > 0.174; Supplementary Data 11), suggesting an amelioration of SGA in the analysis.

The best-fitting reconciliation model supports a root at or near the base of Euryarchaeota

Given the shift in root support when explicitly modelling the higher loss rate in DPANN, we next explored a series of more complex reconciliation models with additional lineage-specific D, T and L parameters. Our aim was to identify a model that adequately captured lineage-specific variation in D, T and L rates across Archaea by using the BIC to assess model fit and to prevent overfitting, while also evaluating how root support varies when different aspects of genome evolution are taken into account (Supplementary Note 2).

These analyses indicated that the best-fitting reconciliation model for these data was a model in which each of the major clades/phyla of Archaea had their own gene-family D, T and L rates (Supplementary Data 10, assessed by BIC). The final model had 72 parameters (DTL_br2), with lineage-specific DTL rates for various Euryarchaeota, TACK+Asgard and DPANN archaeal lineages (see Methods). Under this 72-parameter model (Supplementary Data 10), an AU test rejected all roots except the Euryarchaeota root and a root within Euryarchaeota (between a clade comprising Methanobacteriota_A(B), Hydrothermarchaeota and Hadarchaeota, hereafter MHH root), both with a monophyletic DPANN emerging as sister to the Asgard+TACK group (Fig. 1B, P ≥ 0.48, Supplementary Data 12). Support for the Euryarchaeota root and the MHH root under the branch-heterogeneous model was broad and particularly concentrated in the most widespread and taxonomically balanced families (Fig. S8I), with the Euryarchaeota/MHH root remaining the maximum likelihood root until the top 2% most widespread and balanced families had been filtered out of the analyses (Fig. S8I), in contrast to the gene family-removal analysis described above which found support for the DPANN Cluster 2 root derived from gene families with limited taxa representation present mainly on one side of the root (Fig. S8B). As another experiment, we further increased model complexity on the 257-genome dataset by using additional sets of DTL parameters for groups within the MHH clade (an 81-parameter model—see Supplementary Note 2). This further improved model fit slightly as judged by BIC (BIC: 3264897.57) and placed the root on the branch leading to Euryarchaeota (AU test, P = 0.99, Supplementary Data 13), providing evidence against the adjacent MHH root (AU test, P = 0.024, Supplementary Data 13). However, root support across gene families was less robust under this model, with the increased confidence for the Euryarchaeota over the MHH root deriving from patchily-distributed gene families (Supplementary Note 2), potentially resulting from the challenge of adequately modelling both relatively recent, lineage-specific evolution and DTL regimes on the deepest branches of the tree. Our conservative conclusion from these results, based on both improved model fit and the robustness of support across families in the 72-parameter model, is that the Euryarchaeota and MHH roots are the best supported roots from our reconciliation analyses (Supplementary Note 2).

Given the improved model fit and change in root support observed with the better-fitting, branch-heterogeneous reconciliation models on the 257-genome dataset, we also reanalysed the 60-genome dataset from Williams et al.18, which had provided support for a DPANN root when analysed with an earlier branch-homogeneous reconciliation model. As in our focal dataset, allowing D, T and L parameters to vary across clades substantially improved model fit (BIC: 462712.66, Supplementary Data 14) and shifted support from the original DPANN root to a root between Euryarchaeota and all other Archaea (P = 1, Supplementary Data 15, Supplementary Note 3). Taken together, our results on new and previous datasets demonstrate that improved modelling of variation in D, T and L rates across the tree substantially improves reconciliation model fit and provides support for an archaeal root at or near the base of Euryarchaeota (Fig. 1B).

Dynamics of archaeal genome evolution

Given the impact of model complexity on root inference, we evaluated the effect of model parameterisation on ancestral reconstructions. Ancestral reconstructions are highly similar across different models (Pearson's r: >0.98; Supplementary Note 4, Fig. S9). Therefore, we performed ancestral reconstructions on the 72-parameter model with the origination rate of deep nodes optimised (see “Methods”). Our analyses suggest that archaeal gene family evolution has been mostly vertical, with a mean verticality (i.e., the proportion of genes at the bottom of a branch whose ancestry traces straight up through the same branch) estimated to be 79% (Supplementary Data 16). Transferability of gene families varies significantly across functional categories under both the Euryarchaeota root and the MHH root (H = 885.19, P = 1.13×10−174 and H = 878.0, P = 3.75 × 10−173, degree of freedom: 20, Kruskal–Wallis test, Supplementary Data 17 and 18), with genes involved in defence mechanisms (such as restriction endonuclease, COG category V), and cell wall biogenesis (such as glycosyltransferase, COG category M), being the most frequently transferred, and those involved in cell cycle and informational processing the least (Fig. S10 and Supplementary Data 19), in line with previous findings18,79. Gene transfer has a strong impact on archaeal evolution: it outnumbers duplication events across nearly all (507/501 out of 513 branches for the Euryarchaeota/MHH root), and loss events on many branches (177/154 out of 513 branches). Specifically, we found that gene transfers are on average 10.87-11.18 times more frequent than duplication events in archaeal evolution (Supplementary Data 20), in agreement with the finding that transfers are more frequent than duplications in prokaryotic genomes80,81. In line with previous studies18,76, Asgard archaea had the highest inferred duplication rate compared to the other archaeal clades (Fig. 2A, B), whose duplication events (normalised by speciations) appeared significantly higher than its sister lineage Thermoproteota (Conover's test, P < 0.012, Supplementary Data 21), while the transfer and loss events of Asgard archaea (normalised by speciations) did not appear to be significantly different from what we observe for other archaea with the exception of DPANN lineages (Fig. 2A, B, Conover´s test, Supplementary Data 22 and 23). Our analyses inferred significantly higher normalised loss and transfer events in DPANN Cluster 1 and DPANN Cluster 2 archaea compared to euryarchaeal and thermoproteotal lineages (Conover´s test, P < 0.05, Supplementary Data 22 and 23), suggesting that genome evolution of DPANN was shaped by gene exchanges and losses, consistent with their reduced genomes, predicted symbiotic lifestyle and host dependencies. In fact, the branch leading to the common ancestor of DPANN archaea (LDCA) experienced 639.82 /592.64 loss events, followed by another 490.19/588.91 events on the branch leading to Undinarchaeota and DPANN Cluster 2 archaea under the Euryarchaeota/MHH root (Supplementary Data 20; Figs. Notably, while the duplication is significantly lower in DPANN Cluster 2 archaea, SpSt-1190 lineages appeared to have the highest normalised duplication events among DPANN Cluster 2 archaea (Fig. 2A, B and Supplementary Data 21). Additionally, we observed a high number of duplication and acquisition events (transfers and originations) at the branch leading to Halobacteria, whose members are suggested to have evolved from a methanogenic ancestor into aerobic/light-harvesting heterotrophs82 and are reported to be polyploid83 with duplicated genes predicted to be involved in the osmotic stress response for adaptation to hypersaline environments (Figs. The Last Archaeal Common Ancestor was a (hyper-)thermophilic methanogen

Branch-wise reconciliation analysis provides an inference of gene family evolution and an estimate of ancestral genome content across the tree, allowing the reconstruction of the metabolic potential of LACA. Compared to previous work18, this reconstruction sampled broader archaeal diversity and made use of a more realistic, better-fitting reconciliation model to reconstruct archaeal genome evolution according to the BIC (Supplementary Data 10). On the basis of the relationship between number of arCOG gene families and genome size in modern archaeal genomes (Asgard archaea: Pearson's correlation coefficient: 0.967, P = 3.27 × 10−12, all other Archaea: 0.9587, P = 2.57 × 10−130, Fig. S13 and Supplementary Data 24), we estimated the genome size of LACA to having been 1.58 Mb (95% bootstrap confidence interval, BCI: 1.54 Mb to 1.61 Mb) for the Euryarchaeota root and 2.06 Mb (95% BCI: 2.01 Mb to 2.11 Mb) for the MHH root (Fig. 2C, D). Based on the inferred presence probabilities (PP) of all arCOG families, we further predicted that LACA encoded between 800 to 1219 arCOG gene families for the Euryarchaeota root, and 1190 to 1709 gene families for the MHH root (Supplementary Data 25 and 26). This estimate is obtained by integrating over the LACA PPs for all gene families: some gene families have LACA PPs equal to zero, suggesting that they originated later in archaeal evolution (e.g., ammonia monooxygenase subunit A, arCOG08676; for succinctness, PP values of Euryarchaeota/MHH root are reported as x/y unless specified otherwise; here, 0/0); others have moderate root PPs ( ≥ 0.5), suggesting they may have been present early in archaeal evolution (e.g., glucokinase, arCOG04280 with PP = 0.54/0.54), while others have high root PPs ( ≥ 0.75), representing strong evidence that the gene was already present in LACA (e.g., DNA-directed RNA polymerase subunit A, arCOG04527, PP = 0.99/1). Due to phylogenetic uncertainty, stochastic error and the mosaic ancestry of genomes, PP support for the constituent genes of a metabolic pathway is often variable. For example, while most canonical genes encoding proteins involved in replication84,85 (Fig. S14; Supplementary Data 25 and 26) and translation machinery86 (Fig. S15; Supplementary Data 25 and 26) were inferred to have been present in LACA, some had low support. This incompleteness of certain modern metabolic pathways is expected, given the evolutionary changes through time and limitations of our analysis pipeline, including phylogenetic noise. Hence, we interpret our reconstruction of LACA's metabolism based on the inference of pathways using the same PP cutoff ( ≥ 0.5) as in previous analyses12,18,62.

The inferred gene content of LACA suggests that it already had most of the components of modern archaeal DNA replication, transcription, translation and protein quality control machinery, in agreement with previous work18 (Figs. S14–S18). We also inferred an FtsZ-based cell division system, components of secretion systems and various transporter genes (Supplementary Data 25 and 26). Additionally, we identified most of the proteins required for archaellum biosynthesis87 and an archaeal/vacuolar-type ATP synthase (Supplementary Data 25 and 26; Fig. S19). The improvement of the reconciliation model and expanded archaeal diversity furthermore allowed us to map the presence of many additional components to LACA, including proteins involved in the biosynthesis of key cellular building blocks, such as nucleotides and lipids, which have been missed previously18. For example, a complete mevalonate pathway (MVA) via anhydromevalonate 5-phosphate, along with the genes for archaeol biosynthesis, including glycerol-1-phosphate dehydrogenase (GldA, arCOG00982, PP = 1/0.99) (Fig. 3; Fig. S19, Supplementary Data 25 and 26), were mapped back to LACA. Further, we identified almost all components of the lower glycolytic, non-oxidative pentose phosphate and ribulose monophosphate pathways with high PP support (Fig. 3; Fig. S19, Supplementary Data 25 and 26). Several genes in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle were also mapped to LACA. (Fig. S19; Supplementary Data 25 and 26). Among the various carbon fixation pathways operating in archaea88–92, the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway (WLP) has been suggested to represent an ancient metabolic pathway18,93,94 potentially tracing back to the last universal common ancestor62,95,96. In line with this, our results traced almost all components of the tetrahydromethanopterin methyl- and carbonyl-branch of the WLP to LACA (Fig. 3; Fig. S20, Supplementary Data 25 and 26). Our reconstructions also indicated the presence of nearly all subunits of the key enzyme of methanogenesis/methane oxidation, i.e., methyl(acyl)-coenzyme M reductase (M(A)cr) (McrA, arCOG04857, PP = 0.5/0.58, McrB, arCOG04860, PP = 0.61/0.56, McrG, arCOG04858, PP = 0.39/0.82, McrD, arCOG04859, PP = 0.78/0.96, and McrC, arCOG03225, PP = 0.74/0.94) (Fig. 3; Supplementary Data 25 and 26) in LACA. Moreover, we identified many methanogenesis marker proteins and genes involved in methanofuran, coenzyme F430, coenzyme F420, coenzyme B, coenzyme M and molybdopterins biosynthesis in LACA (Fig. 3; Fig. S20, Supplementary Data 25 and 26). This provides the first support for a methanogenic LACA based on ancestral genome reconstructions, in agreement with the geological record97. LACA was furthermore inferred to have encoded two out of eight subunits of the tetrahydromethanopterin S-methyltransferase (Mtr) complex (MtrA, arCOG03221, PP = 0.92 and MtrH, arCOG04336, PP = 0.98) under the Euryarchaeota root (Fig. 3; Fig. S20 and Supplementary Data 25), and six out of eight subunits under the MHH root (MtrA, arCOG03221, PP = 0.99; MtrB, arCOG04867, PP = 0.65; MtrC, arCOG04868, PP = 0.58; MtrE, arCOG04870, PP = 0.6; MtrG, arCOG03380, PP = 0.71; MtrH, arCOG04336, PP = 0.97) (Fig. 3; Fig. S20, Supplementary Data 26). This enzyme is used by many hydrogentrophic methanogens for sodium ion translocation40,98. High support for the presence of methanol-corrinoid protein: coenzyme M methyltransferase (MtbA, arCOG03325, PP = 0.96/0.94 and methanogenic corrinoid protein (MtbC, arCOG02028, PP = 0.77/0.99) at the root suggests that LACA might have been able to use methylated compounds for methanogenesis. In addition, acetate-CoA ligase (AcdA, arCOG01340, PP = 0.60/0.85) and acetyl-CoA synthetase (Acs, arCOG01529, PP = 0.94/0.97) were also recovered at the root, indicating the potential to metabolise acetate. Thus, our analyses indicate the antiquity of the WLP and methanogenesis in Archaea39,99–101, with the potential for CO2-reducing methanogenesis being root-dependent.

Our reconstruction indicated high PP support for a group 3 [NiFe]-hydrogenase (PP = 0.98/0.99) (Supplementary Data 27and 28, Figs. S20, S21, Supplementary Note 5), among which we were able to map a group 3c [NiFe]-hydrogenase (PP = 0.66/0.68) (Supplementary Data 27 and 28) and heterodisulphide subunits (HdrA, arCOG02236, PP = 0.73/0.81; HdrC, arCOG00964, PP = 0.87/0.99) to LACA with at least moderate support (Supplementary Data 25 and 26). This suggests that LACA might have had the ability to use a group 3c [NiFe]-hydrogenase to bifurcate electrons from H2 to reduce ferredoxin and heterodisulfide of coenzyme B and M, coupled with the Mcr complex to form methane. Furthermore, our analyses indicated that an ancestral group 4 [NiFe] hydrogenase might have been present in LACA (PP = 0.9/0.99; Supplementary Data 27 and 28; Fig. S20). Group 4 [NiFe]-hydrogenases comprise various respiratory membrane-bound enzymes in modern organisms and may have been involved in energy conservation in the last universal common ancestor102–104. These results are consistent with the view that [NiFe]-hydrogenases are ancient enzymes103. Additionally, we recovered a subunit of heterodisulfide reductase (HdrD, arCOG00333, PP = 0.99/0.97), which is present in various modern archaea, including methanogens105,106, and might facilitate electron transfer.

Finally, we estimated the optimal growth temperature of LACA based on the observation that the amino acid composition of our 45-gene supermatrix showed a significant correlation with the optimal growth temperature (Pearson's r: −0.87, P = 8.951 × 10-20) of modern Archaea (Fig. S22). We used the branch-heterogenous CoaLA substitution model107 to sample 100 ancestral sequences reconstructed at nodes of the trees and inferred the temperature optima for each node18. The analysis suggested that LACA may have been a (hyper-)thermophile (median 78.78°C, 95% CI: 74.15–83.52°C under the Euryarchaeota root; median 79.40°C, 95% CI: 74.18–84.30°C under the MHH root; Supplementary Data 29), consistent with previous work18,107 and with the inference that reverse gyrase, a specific hallmark of (hyper-) thermophiles, may have been present in LACA under the Euryarchaeota/MHH roots (arCOG01526, PP = 0.68/PP = 0.75, Supplementary Data 25 and 26).

The DPANN ancestor was a free-living organism

Our rooted species tree resolves DPANN as a monophyletic sister clade of TACK and Asgard. We reconstructed a broadly similar metabolic repertoire of the LDCA and the last TACK+Asgard common ancestor, with the LDCA appearing to encode fewer genes in vitamin biosynthesis (Supplementary Note 6; Figs. S19 and S20). Further, the LDCA under the Euryarchaeota and MHH root, respectively, was estimated to have encoded a genome of 1.32 Mb/1.68 Mb (95% CI: 1.28 Mb to 1.36 Mb / 95% CI: 1.64 Mb to 1.72 Mb), with 546-827/820-1152 arCOG families (Supplementary Data 24). This is comparable to modern Altiarchaeota genome sizes and gene repertoires (1.17 to 2.41 Mp; 1219 to 2776 total coding sequences) (Supplementary Data 1), suggesting that the LDCA may have been a free-living organism similar to modern Altiarchaeota27,108. Indeed, our inference of the ancestral metabolic content of the LDCA is compatible with a free-living lifestyle. For instance, our analyses indicate the presence of almost all components of archaeal ether lipid biosynthesis and de novo purine and pyrimidine biosynthesis pathways in the LDCA (Fig. 4; Supplementary Data 25 and 26). A number of genes involved in vitamin and amino acid biosynthesis were also recovered (Fig. 4; Supplementary Data 25 and 26). This contrasts with many DPANN Cluster 2 representatives, including co-cultivated symbionts such as members of Nanoarchaeales34–36,109 like Nanoarchaeum equitans29,110, and members of Nanohaloarchaeota30,31, such as Nanohaloarchaeum antarcticus30, that do not encode lipid, nucleotide, amino acid or cofactor biosynthesis pathways. We also identified components of central carbon metabolic pathways, including the lower glycolytic pathway, pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and TCA cycle in the LDCA (Fig. S19). We inferred an ongoing trend of convergent genome reduction during the evolution of DPANN archaea (Fig. 2C, D; Fig. S23 and Supplementary Data 24). In particular, DPANN Cluster 2 archaea are characterised by genome streamlining that appears to have occurred in parallel in different lineages61,112. Specifically, our reconstructions inferred a reduction in genome size from 1.32/1.68 Mb in LDCA to 0.92/1.26 Mb in the DPANN Cluster 2 common ancestor (LDC2CA, Supplementary Data 24). The reduction in genome content included genes encoding enzymes of the WLP, ribulose monophosphate pathway (RuMP), vitamins, amino acids, nucleotides and lipid biosynthesis pathways, which indicates dependency on external sources by the LDC2CA (Fig. 4; Fig. S19, Supplementary Data 25 and 26, Supplementary Note 7). However, we identified most genes of the lower glycolytic pathway and acetate-CoA ligase in the LDC2CA, in line with experimental support indicating that this machinery may be indispensable for these symbionts in the absence of host-derived ATP113. We also inferred that most of the archaellum subunits87 of the motor complex (ArlHIJ) were present in the LDC2CA, which were found in extant representatives of DPANN Cluster 2 archaea (Supplementary Data 25-26)113,114. Similarly, we identified most of the components of DNA replication, transcription and translation machinery in LDC2CA and the last common ancestor of Nanoarchaeota, in line with extant representatives24,25,115,116. In contrast to this general trend, we inferred that some clades within Woesearchaeales and Pacearchaeales experienced genome expansion (Fig. S23). For example, we inferred an increase of 0.34 to 0.39 Mb in one Woesearchaeales clade, and an increase of 0.26 to 0.27 Mb in one Pacearchaeales clade, compared to the last common ancestor of Woesearchaeales and Pacearchaeales, respectively (Fig. S23 and Supplementary Data 24). The genome content expansion of the Woesearchaeales clade includes genes involved in amino acid transport and metabolism, as well as energy production and conversion, in line with a previous inference112. For example, several subunits (1–4) of the archaeal/vacuolar-type ATP synthase were inferred to have been re-acquired during this expansion (Supplementary Data 30). In contrast, the majority of genes that contributed to genome expansion in Pacearchaeales were inferred to be involved in translation, transcription, and replication machinery (Supplementary Data 31). For example, acquisitions of transcriptional regulators were inferred under both the Euryarchaeota and MHH root (Supplementary Data 31) for the latter lineage.

Discussion

The archaeal phylogeny continues to be revisited and revised as genomes from new groups become available, and phylogenomic methods improve1,3,5,6,76,117,118. While many studies consistently support the clanhood of DPANN and TACK+Asgard archaea13,14,18,19,24,25,39,62, the clanhood of Euryarchaeota remains debated. Some analyses recover Euryarchaeota as a monophyletic group14,18,24,25,39,62,117, whereas others suggest it to be paraphyletic, with (hyper-)thermophilic lineages, Thermococci and/or Hadarchaeota, branching basal to TACK+Asgard17,19,48,119. These two lineages were proven difficult to place in previous work13,14,17,119–121, and are placed at the base of Euryarchaeota in the phylogeny summarised from all gene trees (Fig. S4). Nevertheless, our analyses consistently recovered the clanhood of Euryarchaeota under the progressive removal of compositionally biased sites, under the best-fitting MAST model69, and from the summary of all gene trees using Astral-Pro 368, in line with a previous observation in which support for the clanhood of Euryarchaeota emerged upon site filtering17. While our analysis recovered Hydrothermarchaeota within Methanococci, Methanopyri and Methanobacteria with low support UFBOOT/SH-aLRT support (21.6/83) in the untreated dataset (Fig. 1), an AU test could not reject a topology in which Methanococci, Methanopyri and Methanobacteria are sister to Hydrothermarchaeota (P = 0.5). This is consistent with previous work either recovering the latter topology17,99,119,122 or placing Hydrothermarchaeota within Methanobacteriota13,61. While some studies placed Altiarchaeota at the base of all DPANN archaea17,19,48, our analyses supported the placement of Altiarchaeota within DPANN as a member of DPANN Cluster 1 once gene families strongly affected by horizontal evolution (including interdomain transfer) were removed, suggesting that the alternative placement was induced by including non-vertical markers (Supplementary Note 1). Finally, although many previous studies placed the Korarchaeota inside or basal to Thermoproteota19,21,123–125, we recovered Korarchaeota as a sister lineage of a monophyletic clade of TACK+Asgard after the removal of non-vertical markers (Fig. S6). This placement was consistent across analyses, including the progressive removal of compositionally biased sites, the application of the best-fitting MAST model, and gene tree summary approach using Astral-Pro 3, in agreement with recent work61,78,126,127 and the latest assignment of Korarchaeota as a phylum-level lineage in GTDB128.

We applied various methods to root the archaeal tree and assessed the confidence of various root placements51. Our results reveal that outgroup rooting and non-reversible models are unable to distinguish among a broad set of potential root placements (Supplementary Data 3 and 6). By contrast, the GFmix model, which accounts for variation in amino acid frequencies across sites and branches, supported a Halobacteriota+Thermoplasmatota root, in line with a previous study using a divide-and-conquer phylogenomic approach19,20. While these analyses are typically based on information from a small set of marker genes, phylogenetic reconciliation allows to incorporate additional information based on gene evolutionary histories12,18,57,65,129. Earlier attempts to resolve the root of Archaea using gene tree-species tree reconciliation approaches were based on a small taxon set and relied upon a simple reconciliation model that did not incorporate variation in rates of duplication, transfer and loss between different lineages18—a limitation that can induce artefacts, especially when including a diverse set of reduced genomes from symbionts. Analyses using better-fitting models placed the archaeal root on or within Euryarchaeota, with DPANN emerging as a sister group to TACK+Asgard. The root recovered in the GFmix analysis was one node away from these two adjacent branches, defining a small “root region” on the archaeal tree at or near the base of the Euryarchaeota. These results emphasise the importance of integrating parameter-rich, realistic models of evolution that improve model fit and resolve deep archaeal phylogeny.

By including only sequences from archaea and avoiding the need for a bacterial outgroup, the reconciliation approach enables many more gene families to be used to resolve the archaeal root, and reduces the impact of long branch attraction artefacts on the results. However, a limitation of the archaeal focus is that inter-domain gene transfers between archaea and bacteria in individual arCOG gene families will not be detected, as the reconciliation method only sees the archaeal subfamily. Our analyses indicated that the same root is recovered on that subset of gene families least prone to inter-domain transfer (Supplementary Data 32), suggesting that this does not cause issues in practice. In principle, a reconciliation analysis including both prokaryotic domains would provide a higher-resolution map of gene family origins (though still limited by genome sampling of extant lineages and the now-extinct lineages in which gene families most likely originated), albeit at the expense of gene family tree and root inference accuracy.

Our reconciliation approach simultaneously allowed reconstructing ancestral gene sets along the rooted archaeal tree, resolving a relatively complex LACA that was likely a (hyper-)thermophilic methanogen18,84–86,130,131. The inferred metabolic content of LACA is consistent with methyltrophic methanogenesis or potentially CO2-reducing methanogenesis39,94,99,132. We hypothesise that such a hyperthermophilic ancestor could have thrived in environments analogous to hydrothermal vents or geothermal subsurface systems with strong reducing conditions, abundant in H2, CO2 and methylated compounds such as methanol133,134.

While various studies have recovered a deep split between DPANN and all other archaeal lineages14,18,24, more recent work has supported DPANN forming a derived lineage within Euryarchaeota in a tree rooted between TACK+Asgard and all other archaea17. Our analyses also recovered DPANN as a derived lineage, albeit branching sister to the TACK+Asgard (Fig. 1). These results suggest that the symbiotic lifestyle of DPANN archaea is a secondary adaptation rather than an ancestral trait. In fact, our analysis suggests that the LDCA might have been a free-living organism, with an ancestrally reduced vitamin biosynthesis repertoire, potentially representing an early step towards dependency within a microbial community, and that subsequent diversification of DPANN lineages was shaped by high rates of gene loss and transfer. While genome reduction in both DPANN and CPR can thus be considered as a derived trait, their evolutionary trajectories differ. Reduction in CPR likely occurred early on the stem lineage12, whereas DPANN gene loss was more episodic and, in part, occurred convergently in different lineages. However, genome reduction in the stem leading to DPANN Cluster 2 exhibits analogous patterns as observed for CPR, involving the loss of genes coding for proteins involved in central carbon and energy metabolism, including the ATP synthase, as well as amino acid biosynthesis12. The loss of metabolic pathways is also a common feature in reductive genome evolution of bacterial endosymbionts of insects24,25,135. In contrast to those endosymbionts, DPANN archaea appear to retain genes for proteins that are involved in DNA repair and transcription. This difference may reflect their distinct symbiotic strategies: all DPANN symbionts that have been successfully co-cultivated have episymbiotic stages29–31,33,113,136. Therefore, they likely experience stronger selection to maintain key components for an information processing machinery than strictly vertically inherited intracellular endosymbionts. Furthermore, the ectosymbiotic lifestyle of DPANN archaea might explain the observed genome expansion in certain DPANN lineages, such as Woesearchaeales and Pacearchaeales, through high rates of horizontal gene transfer. Prospective analysis of the sources and mechanisms of these gene transfers may allow for identifying host organisms for the vast diversity of uncultivated DPANN.

Methods

Taxa selection

Contigs and protein files of archaeal genomes were downloaded from NCBI in August 2021 (n = 7,967). If protein files were unavailable, gene calling was performed using Prokka (v1.14, settings: --kingdom Archaea --addgenes --increment 10 --compliant --centre UU --norrna --notrna)137. Taxonomic classifications were assigned to all genomes using GTDB-Tk using GTDB release 202138, and the original NCBI tax ID was converted into an NCBI taxonomy string using ncbitax2lin (https://github.com/zyxue/ncbitax2lin). Genome completeness was estimated using CheckM2 (v0.1.3)139.

Genomes with completeness >50% and contamination <5% were used for an initial phylogenetic analysis using the PhyloSift marker set140. In brief, homologues of 34 PhyloSift marker proteins were identified in each genome using the PhyloSift v1.0.1 “find” mode (settings: –besthit). Sequences were aligned with MAFFT (v7.407, settings: –reorder)141, trimmed with BMGE (v1.12, settings: -t AA -m BLOSUM30 -b 2 -h 0.55)142. The trimmed alignments were concatenated using catfasta2phyml.pl (https://github.com/nylander/catfasta2phyml) and the concatenated protein alignment was used to reconstruct a phylogenetic tree using IQ-TREE (v1.6.7, settings: -m LG + G -nt AUTO -bb 1000 -alrt 1000)143. Based on this tree, a sub-selection of 1,711 archaeal reference genomes was conducted based on high genome completeness, low contamination and taxonomic diversity.

To further thin out the taxon selection, two rounds of species tree inferences were performed using 51 universal, single-copy marker proteins suitable for archaeal phylogenetic analyses61. Specifically, a customised TIGR database was queried against a database generated from all archaeal proteins using hmmsearch (v3.1b2)144,145. The output was parsed to only retain hits with an e-value łe≤ 1E-3 and then to only include the best hit per protein selected using the lowest e-value and the highest bit score. Selected sequences were aligned with MAFFT (v7.407, settings: –reorder)141, and alignments were trimmed with BMGE (v1.12, settings: -t AA -m BLOSUM30 -b 2 -h 0.55)142. Individual alignments were concatenated using catfasta2phyml.pl, and a phylogenetic tree was generated using IQ-TREE (v1.6.7, settings: -m LG + C20 + F + R -bb 1000 -alrt 1000)143 and visualised using FigTree (v1.4.4). The tree was manually inspected, and an additional taxa subset was selected based on genome completeness and contamination statistics while maintaining a taxonomically diverse set of genomes with a specific focus on the DPANN archaea. This workflow was repeated, resulting in a selection of 513 taxa that were further reduced to the focal taxa set of 257 genomes with balanced taxa representation of Euryarchaeota, TACK+Asgard and DPANN (Supplementary Data 1). Njordarchaeia was not included in the analyses owing to the timing of genome availability146 (August 2021) and the difficulty of accommodating its compositional bias under conventional C-series models76,78.

Genome annotations

For further functional annotation, proteins from 257 archaeal genomes were compared against several databases, including COGs (using the 2020 update)147, arCOGs (version from 2020)148, the KO profiles from the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS; downloaded April 2019)149, the Pfam database (Release 31.0)150 and the TIGRFAM database (Release 15.0)144. Individual database searches were conducted as follows by querying these databases against all archaeal genomes using hmmsearch v3.1b2 (settings: --tblout --domtblout --notextw)145. The output was parsed to only include the best hit for each protein using the lowest e-value and the highest bit score value. Additionally, all proteins were scanned for protein domains using InterProScan (v5.29-68.0; settings: --iprlookup --goterms)151.

Inferring an unrooted archaeal species tree

The focal taxon selection was used to infer a maximum likelihood phylogeny using 51 universal, single-copy marker proteins as described above, but with minor adjustments to improve the quality of the species tree61—i.e., markers with more than 10% duplicated proteins were discarded, leaving 45 markers. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT or MAFFT-L-INS-i for gene families with more than or fewer than 1000 sequences, respectively, and trimmed with BMGE. Single gene trees were generated with IQ-TREE 2 (v2.1.2, settings: -m LG -T AUTO -keep-ident --threads-max 2 -B 1000 -bnni)63. A custom script (sequence_correler.py, https://zenodo.org/records/18701544) was run to identify multi-copy proteins15. Each single gene tree was visually inspected to identify the correct protein sequences per marker and remove long-branching sequences. After removing problematic sequences, trimmed alignments were generated using the procedure described above and concatenated using catfasta2phyml.pl (https://github.com/nylander/catfasta2phyml). The best fitting phylogenetic model was estimated using IQ-TREE 2 (v2.1.2, settings: -m MF -mset LG -madd LG + C10,LG + C10 + G,LG + C10 + R,LG + C10 + F,LG + C10 + R + F,LG + C10 + G + F,LG + C20,LG + C20 + G,LG + C20 + F,LG + C20 + G + F,LG + C20 + R,LG + C20 + R + F,LG + C30,LG + C30 + G,LG + C30 + R,LG + C30 + F,LG + C30 + R + F,LG + C30 + G + F,LG + C40,LG + C40 + G,LG + C40 + R,LG + C40 + F,LG + C40 + R + F,LG + C40 + G + F,LG + C50,LG + C50 + G,LG + C50 + R,LG + C50 + F,LG + C50 + R + F,LG + C50 + G + F,LG + C60,LG + C60 + G,LG + C60 + R,LG + C60 + F,LG + C60 + R + F,LG + C60 + G + F --score-diff all), via the Bayesian Information Criterion152, and a phylogenetic tree was inferred using IQ-TREE 2 (v2.1.2, settings: -m LG + C60 + F + R -B 1000 -alrt 1000)63 and visualised using ggtree (v3.14.0)153. The species tree was re-rooted at 15 different key positions (positions from literature and between each major lineage) using a custom script (root_w_ete3.py, https://zenodo.org/records/18701544).

To assess the clade support for major archaeal lineages, compositionally biased sites were filtered from the alignment in a progressive manner (from 10-80%, in 10% increments) using alignment_pruner.pl (https://github.com/novigit/davinciCode/blob/master/perl/alignment_pruner.pl; settings: --chi2_prune). For each pruned alignment, a phylogenetic tree was inferred with IQ-TREE 2 (v2.1.2, settings: -m LG + C60 + F + R -bb 1000 -alrt 1000)63. All tree topologies are highly congruent (Fig. S2; all trees share quartet similarity above 0.99154), so we used the phylogenetic tree based on the full alignment for all further analyses.

To assess the impact of taxonomic balance at the phylum level on the topology of our inferred species tree, we downsampled the 257 archaeal dataset to 53 taxa, retaining 3 representatives for each GTDB phylum. The marker sequences of these taxa were realigned using MAFFT-L-INS-i155 and trimmed using BMGE v1.2142 (-m BLOSUM30 -h 0.55 -t AA). The species tree was inferred under the best-fitting LG + C60 + F + R model using IQ-TREE v2.1.263. We also inferred a species tree using ASTRAL-Pro 368 (settings: -r 16 -s 16) from single-gene trees of 241 (present in more than 80% of the archaeal species) and 7059 (all families) arCOG gene families (See arCOG gene families for ancestral gene content reconstruction).

Further, we assessed the statistical support for the placement of Thermococci and Hadarchaeota, and the monophyly of Euryarchaeota, and Korarchaeota using the AU test64 as implemented in CONSEL v0.2156, and the best-fitting MAST model69 for the 257 archaeal dataset. Specifically, we constructed 3 constrained topologies: Euryarchaeota paraphyletic with Thermococci branching sister to Hadarchaeota+TACK+Asgard (compatible with a recent study17), Hadarchaeota and Thermococci branched basal to other Euryarchaeota (compatible with the Astral-Pro 3 topology), Koarchaeota branches inside Thermorproteota (compatible with pre-GTDB 10 classification). Subsequently, we performed a constrained topological search using IQ-TREE v2.1.2 (-g TOPOLOGY -wslr -m LG + C60 + F + R). Six MAST models (TMIX{LG + FO + R,LG + FO + R,LG + FO + R,LG + FO + R} + T, TMIX{LG + FO,LG + FO,LG + FO,LG + FO} + R + T, TMIX{LG + F + R,LG + F + R,LG + F + R,LG + F + R} + T, TMIX{LG + F,LG + F,LG + F,LG + F} + R + T, LG + FO + TMIX{R,R,R,R} + T, LG + FO + R + T) were fit to the data using the unconstrained tree (Fig. S5), and the trees containing three alternative hypotheses via the -te option using IQ-TREE v3.0.1157. The BIC identified TMIX{LG + FO + R,LG + FO + R,LG + FO + R,LG + FO + R} + T as the best-fitting model.

Reciprocal outgroup rooting of an archaeal species tree with bacteria

To perform outgroup rooting of archaea, we selected 25 vertically evolving marker proteins shared between bacteria and archaea13 (Supplementary Data 2). COG marker sequences were identified using hmmsearch v3.1.2 from 257 archaeal and 103 bacterial genomes. Alignments were generated using MAFFT-L-INS-i and trimmed using BMGE (-m BLOSUM30 -h 0.55 -t AA). Single gene trees were inferred based on LG + G using IQ-TREE 2 (v2.1.2) and inspected to remove long-branching sequences and false paralogues. Sequences were realigned using MAFFT-L-INS-i, trimmed using BMGE (-m BLOSUM30 -h 0.55 -t AA), and concatenated into a supermatrix. Finally, we inferred an ML tree under the LG + C60 + F + R model, which recovered a DPANN root (Fig. S1). Subsequently, we generated 14 additional root hypotheses by relocating the bacterial outgroup attachment point to the archaeal ingroup (Supplementary Data 3), and conducted maximum likelihood based tree inferences under these topological constraints using the LG + C60 + F + R model in IQ-TREE 2 (v2.1.2) (-g root -wlsr). AU tests were performed using CONSEL v0.2156 using 1,000,000 bootstrap samples.

Rooting of archaeal species tree using a non-reversible model

Additionally, we inferred maximum-likelihood phylogenies from the same concatenated alignment using three non-reversible models implemented in IQ-TREE 2 (v2.3.6, setting: -m NONREV + R10/ NONREV + G/NQ.pfam+R10). NONREV + R10 provided the best model fit to the data. The likelihood of trees being rooted on every branch of the ML tree from three models was evaluated using the “–root-test” option, and AU tests were performed for those trees using the option: -zb 10000 -au. ML phylogenies inferred using these three models are shown in Fig. S7. The results representing the 15 root positions that were tested with outgroup-rooting and reconciliation analysis are provided in Supplementary Data 6.

Rooting of archaeal species tree using a non-stationary model

We also used the GFmix model (v1.2)17,58,59 with LG + C60 + F + G model to assess the 15 different rooting hypotheses based on the same concatenated alignment. Site-wise likelihoods were computed using the: -l options, and AU tests were conducted using CONSEL v0.2156 with 1,000,000 bootstrap samples. The GFmix rooting analyses were also performed on the alignment and treefile from a recent study17 by allowing the ratio of FIMNKY-to-GARP to vary across branches (settings: -d -l). Afterwards, an AU test was performed.

Gene tree/species tree reconciliations to the root archaeal species tree and reconstruct ancestral gene content

arCOG gene families for ancestral gene content reconstruction

Gene families for ancestral reconciliations were generated based on the arCOGs, a COG-based orthology database that captures archaeal protein family evolution148. COG-based orthology has been shown to perform better than de novo clustering approaches in reconciliation analyses of bacterial genome evolution12. To this end, proteins from an initial taxon set with 513 archaeal genomes (relatively large representation of DPANN) were combined into a protein database that was used in an hmmsearch against the arCOG database (settings: --tblout --domtblout --notextw)147,148. Hits with e-values above 1e-3 were discarded from the domain results table, and the output was further parsed to only include the best hit for each protein based on the lowest e-value and highest bit score. The number of predicted genes that were assigned to an arCOG family is provided in Supplementary Data 1 for each genome. Note that in our focal set of 257 taxa, the proportion of predicted genes that can be assigned to an arCOG ranges from 47.08% to 99.8%, with a median of 82.3%.

We first identified and split potentially fused proteins comprising more than one protein domain, each assigned to a distinct arCOG (from now on called “splits”), using a custom pipeline to avoid incorrect inference of gene loss events (because each protein can only be assigned to its best arCOG hit). Proteins were further investigated for secondary domain hits after subtracting the position of the first domain hit from the full protein using bedtools subtract (v2.26.0)158. Secondary domains were only considered if they were assigned to a different arCOG than the first domain. This process was repeated until proteins were investigated for up to four domains, and the original protein was split into individual domains using the positional domain information with bedtools getfasta. For reference, unsplit proteins were given the extension “a0” and the split proteins were given the extension “a1” to “a4",” indicating the primary to quarterly domain, in the generated trees.

All protein sequences were combined, and sequences with ambiguous amino acids were identified and discarded using a custom Python script (remove_seq_with_specific_char.py, https://zenodo.org/records/18701544). Afterward, a first round of alignments was generated using MAFFT or MAFFT-L-INS-i for gene families with more than or fewer than 1000 sequences, respectively (v7.407, settings: --anysymbol --reorder)141. Alignments were trimmed using trimAl (v1.4.rev15, settings: -gappyout)159. Sequences with equal to or greater than 50% of gaps were identified and discarded using custom python scripts (faa_drop.py and screen_list_new.pl, https://zenodo.org/records/18701544). These steps allow to minimise the biases in phylogenetic inference induced by missing data160–162. Only clusters with at least four sequences were considered for further analysis. Based on these initial selections, a second alignment was performed with MAFFT and MAFFT-L-INS-i as described above, and sequences were trimmed with BMGE (v1.12, settings: -t AA -m BLOSUM30 -b 2 -h 0.55)142. The trimmed alignments were used to generate a first round of single gene trees using IQ-TREE 2 (v2.1.2, settings: -m LG + G -T 1)63. To minimise the potential reconciliation noise introduced by the LBA artefacts in single gene trees, single sequences or clusters of sequences on long branches were identified using a custom script and using a branch length cutoff of two (cut_gene_tree.py, https://zenodo.org/records/18701544)15. The validity of this cutoff was confirmed on the basis of a random number of single gene trees that were manually inspected after adding the KO mapping, PFAM, and arCOG mappings as well as taxon information to the tip labels. Single sequences on long branches were discarded, and clusters of at least four sequences were assigned to a separate cluster using a custom script (screen_list_new.pl, https://zenodo.org/records/18701544). Finally, sequences within each cluster were subset to the focal taxon set of 257 archaeal genomes with a balanced representation of Euryarchaeota, TACK+Asgard and DPANN lineages, amenable to complex gene-tree and species-tree reconciliation models (https://zenodo.org/records/18701544).

A third round of alignments was generated with MAFFT and/or MAFFT-L-INS-i and trimmed with BMGE as described above. The trimmed alignment was used to perform a model test with IQ-TREE 2 (v2.1.2, settings: -m MF -mset LG -madd LG + C10, LG + C10 + G, LG + C10 + R, LG + C10 + F, LG + C10 + R + F, LG + C10 + G + F, LG + C20, LG + C20 + G, LG + C20 + F, LG + C20 + G + F, LG + C20 + R,LG + C20 + R + F --score-diff all) as well as to infer guide trees (settings: -m LG + G + F --score-diff all -nt 1 -wbtl -B 1000 -bnni). Depending on the best model chosen, single gene trees were inferred using either the posterior mean site frequency (PMSF) model (settings: -s trimmed_aln.faa -m best_model -ft guide_tree.treefile -T 1 -wbtl -B 1000 -pers 0.2 -nstop 500)163 or the default model settings of IQ-TREE 2 (v2.1.2, settings: -s trimmed_aln.faa -m best_model -T 1 -wbtl -B 1000 -pers 0.2 -nstop 500) depending on whether or not a C-series profile mixture model was selected as the best model, respectively. In total, 7059 single gene trees were generated for the arCOG-based clusters, respectively, and used as input for the ancestral reconciliations as described below.

Gene tree-species tree reconciliation

To account for the incomplete nature of some MAGs, genome completeness estimates from CheckM v2 were used in reconciliation139 (Supplementary Data 1). Bootstrapped single gene trees (1000 per gene family), inferred from 7,059 arCOG-based clusters (see arCOG gene families for ancestral gene content reconstruction), were reconciled with 15 different rooted archaeal species trees (Fig. 1A) using the distinct reconciliation models implemented in AleRax v1.1.156: These models include a global rate model (3 parameters, one D, one T and one L homogeneous to all branches), a per-family DTL rate model (21177 parameters, one D, one T and one L per gene family), and different branch-wise DTL models to capture differences in genome-evolving rates across archaeal lineages via the --model-parametrization option. All optimisations were performed using a limited-memory BFGS algorithm following recommendations from Williams et al.65. We built a series of branch-heterogeneous gene family reconciliation models on top of a global-rate model as baseline (Supplementary Data 33). First, we introduced DPANN-specific rate classes: a different loss rate (DPANN:L; 4 parameters), different duplication+loss rates (DPANN:DL; 5 parameters), different transfer+loss rates (DPANN:TL; 5 parameters) and different duplication+transfer+loss rates (DPANN:DTL; 6 parameters). We then extended the DTL heterogeneity to major clades—Euryarchaeota, TACK+Asgard, DPANN and three DPANN sublineages (Woesearchaeales+SCGC-AAA011-G17, Pacearchaeales, Micrarchaeota), resulting in 21 parameters (DTL_br1). Building on this, we allowed clade-specific origination (O) rates for DPANN, Euryarchaeota and TACK+Asgard (25 parameters, DTL_br1_O). Next, we let D, T and L vary freely for Euryarchaeota lineages (i.e., for each Halobacteriota; Thermoplasmatota; and a clade comprising Methanobacteriota_A(B), Hydrothermarchaeota and Hadarchaeota), for TACK+Asgard lineages (i.e., for each Korarchaeota, Thermoproteota, Asgardarchaeota), and for DPANN lineages (i.e., for each Micrarchaeota; B1Sed10-29; Iainarchaeota; Altiarchaeota; Undinarchaeota; Aenigmarchaeota; PWEA01; EX4484_52; Nanohaloarchaeota; SpSt-1190; parvarchaeal-related lineages; UBA10117; Pacearchaeales; Woesearchaeales+SCGC-AAA011-G17; Nanoarchaeales; plus two internal nodes representing the last common ancestor of all DPANN and of DPANN Cluster 1 archaea, respectively), yielding 72 parameters in total (DTL_br2). Based on this model, in another experiment, we further allowed the D, T and L for Methanobacteriota_A, Methanobacteriota_B, Hydrothermarchaeota and Hadarchaeota to vary freely, resulting in 81 parameters. Model fit across models is assessed using the BIC and provided in Supplementary Data 10. The likelihoods of 7059 families under all of the above models were compared and tested using AU tests as implemented in CONSEL v0.20 with 1,000,000 bootstrap samples156. In addition, we reanalysed data generated by Williams et al, 201718 using a global model, a per-family DTL model, and a branch-wise model allowing Altiarchaeota, Micrarchaeota, Iainarchaeota, Nanohaloarchaeota, Aenigmarchaeota, Parvarchaeota, Nanoarchaeales, Woesearchaeales, Pacearchaeales, Halobacteriota, Thermoplasmatota, Asgardarchaeota, Korarchaeota, Thermoproteota to evolve at different DTL rates, while we used different DTLO rates for nodes representing the LCA of DPANN, Euryarchaeota and TACK+Asgard, respectively (Supplementary Data 14 and 33). For ancestral genome content reconstruction, we allowed heterogeneous origination by estimating independent rates for the root, its two immediate descendants, and the DPANN lineage using --origination OPTIMISE, based on the reconciliation model with 72 parameters, which is highly correlated with the best-fitting model, DTL_br3 (Pearson's r: 1) (Supplementary Note 4).

Gene family filtration

To evaluate the underlying support for competitive root candidates, we performed gene family filtration similar to Coleman et al.12. We ordered gene families by species number and taxonomic bias score (defined as |⅓-%(DPANN) | + | ⅓-%(TACK+Asgard)|+|⅓-%(Euryarchaeota)|), which quantifies the deviation from equal representation of three archaea groups in a gene family. To assess the potential of gene families to induce the SGA, we compared the differences in likelihoods between competing root hypotheses and the best-supported maximum-likelihood root as gene families were sequentially excluded from the analysis (Supplementary Note 2).

Ancestral sequence reconstruction and temperature estimates

To estimate the ancestral optimal growth temperature of LACA, we reconstructed ancestral sequences based on the supermatrix using the LG + G4+COaLA[2] model (best-fitting model from LG + G4+COaLA[1-3]107 based on BIC; [x] refers to the number of independent axis positions per branch and on the root) implemented in Bio + + (v2.3.1)164. We sampled 100 sequences from the posterior distribution of ancestral states (i.e., amino acid) at each node to account for reconstruction uncertainty. Correspondence analyses on amino acid compositions were performed using the Prince165 package (v0.13.0) Python3166. Axis 2 of amino acid compositions was found to be significantly correlated with the optimal growth temperatures in extant archaeal taxa (Pearson's r: −0.87, p-value: 8.951 × 10-20, Fig. S22). A linear regression model was fitted using the OLS function implemented in statsmodels (v0.13.5)167, and the fitted (R2: 0.75) regression line was used to predict the OGT for ancestral nodes and extant taxa without known OGT. Confidence intervals were computed to account for uncertainty in the regression line parameter estimates.

Genome size estimation

The number of arCOG families present at the ancestral nodes of interest was obtained by counting families with posterior probabilities (PP) ≥ 0.5 (moderate support) and ≥0.75 (strong support). To estimate ancestral genome size, we used all 7059 gene families. Specifically, we applied locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) to model the relationship between protein number and genome size across extant genomes and predicted ancestral genome sizes from the fitted curve. Prediction uncertainty was quantified by bootstrap resampling (1000 replicates).

Statistical tests

Statistical significance of transfer propensity and gene duplication, loss and transfer events of different groups was tested using a Kruskal–Wallis test, a one-sided statistical test implemented in scipy.stats (v1.16.2), followed by a post hoc pairwise test for multiple comparisons of mean rank sums (Conover´s test) using scikit_posthocs (v0.11.2) with P-values adjusted via the Holm–Bonferroni method. The correlation between ancestral state reconstructions inferred via distinct reconciliation models was computed using the corrcoef function implemented in numpy (v1.26.4), and hierarchical clustering of the reconciliation models was performed using the linkage function implemented in scipy (v1.16.2) with “average” method.
