Pf41 serological profiling reveals potential malaria exposure in low transmission setting in the Menoreh Hills, Central Java, Indonesia
Authors: M Ilyas, Mutiara Rizqi Yuwanita, Surya Natanegara, Muhammad Muzhaffar Daud, Aprillia Andika Rahayu, Derico Hitipeuw, Ajeng Dwi Wusono, Raisha Nuranindita, Fadhila Fitriana, Jadidan Hada Syahada, Bo-Young Jeon, Hojong Jun, Martini Martini, Dion Zein Nuridzin, Feng Lu, Jin-Hee Han, Fauzi Muh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPf41 serological profiling may serve as an additional sero-epidemiological tool to characterize past malaria exposure, particularly in low-transmission settings where routine parasitological methods have limited capacity to detect historical exposure. In the Menoreh Hills, Central Java, where malaria transmission is low and unstable, such profiling can reveal exposure history that are not captured by conventional parasitological methods. This study evaluates the utility of Pf41 antibody responses as an exposure marker in a low-transmission context, with the Menoreh Hills serving as an epidemiologically relevant field site.MethodsA cross-sectional seroepidemiological study was conducted from January to June 2025 in four malaria-endemic villages: Sedayu, Kemejing, Kembaran, and Wadas. Serum samples from 120 individuals in malaria endemic villages and 24 healthy non-exposed controls were analyzed for anti-Pf41 IgG antibodies using a protein microarray. Demographic and behavioral data were collected through structured questionnaires, and statistical analyses were performed to assess differences in antibody responses and their associations with demographic and exposure-related factors.ResultsThe results revealed a high overall Pf41 seropositivity rate of 71% (85/120) among individuals in malaria endemic villages, with marked heterogeneity across villages: Kembaran 93%, Sedayu 83%, Wadas 60%, and Kemejing 47%. Mean standardized MFI was significantly higher in all exposed groups compared to controls (p < 0.001). At the individual level, Pf41 antibody responses were significantly associated with selected behavioral factors in a site-specific manner. In Sedayu, behavioral factors showed a site-specific pattern, with frequent mosquito bites during outdoor activities at night being associated with higher Pf41 antibody levels. In Kemejing, higher antibody levels were observed among individuals who fed livestock at night. In the pooled analysis across all study sites, nighttime livestock feeding and the use of repellent was significantly associated with increased Pf41 antibody levels.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that serological evidence of past malaria exposure remains detectable in the Menoreh Hills despite a decline in reported clinical cases, with antibody responses varying across villages and reflecting differences in behavioral risk profiles. The integration of Pf41 serology into malaria surveillance systems could complement existing approaches by contributing to the identification of populations or areas with serological evidence of prior malaria exposure in low-transmission settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05206-1.Keywords: Malaria, Pf41, Seroepidemiology, Protein microarray, Menoreh Hills

Introduction

Malaria remains a critical public health issue that continues to threaten millions of lives globally, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. The number of malaria cases worldwide in 2024 was estimated at 282 million, with 610,000 death [1]. Despite substantial progress towards malaria elimination, residual malaria exposure remains a significant challenge for surveillance and control programs, as low-density infections and asymptomatic infections often go undetected by routine diagnostic tools and may complicate malaria surveillance and elimination efforts [2]. In Southeast Asia, Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax are responsible for most malaria infections, with varying transmission intensities across different countries [3]. Indonesia, which was once highly endemic, has achieved significant reductions in malaria incidence through enhanced control and surveillance measures; however, malaria transmission has historically persisted in several provinces, including parts of Java. Purworejo Regency, situated in the Menoreh Hills of Central Java, has historically been endemic for malaria. Surveillance data from the Purworejo Regency Health Office documented a substantial decline in reported malaria cases from 1,411 in 2015 to 5 in 2020, followed by a notable resurgence in 2021–2022 (535 and 558 cases, respectively) before declining again to 49 cases in 2023 [4]. In 2024 and 2025, only imported cases were reported (11 and 14 cases, respectively), with no indigenous clinical malaria cases documented in the study villages of Sedayu, Kemejing, Kembaran, and Wadas during the study period. Despite this reported reduction, the area remains ecologically suitable for malaria transmission, highlighting the need for alternative surveillance tools capable of detecting residual exposure. Conventional diagnostic tools, such as microscopy and rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), have limited sensitivity in detecting low-density exposures typical of asymptomatic carriers [5]. In low transmission and pre-elimination settings, conventional that rely on parasite detection may fail to accurately capture the full extent of malaria because infections are often asymptomatic, of low density, or have been cleared by the time of sampling. In this context, serological markers provide tools for identifying cumulative or historical exposure within the population [6]. Although molecular methods such as PCR offer enhanced sensitivity, they are resource-intensive and not always feasible in field settings [7]. Consequently, seroepidemiological approaches that assess host antibody responses to malaria antigens have gained significance in elucidating the exposure history and transmission dynamics [8]. Among the wide range of candidate serological markers, well-established antigens such as merozoite surface protein 1 (MSP1), apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1), and glutamate-rich protein (GLURP) are highly immunogenic and elicit long-lived antibody responses that primarily reflect cumulative malaria exposure. While these markers are valuable for estimating overall transmission intensity, their strong and persistent immunogenicity may limit their ability to differentiate subtle variation in exposure across low-transmission or heterogeneous settings. In this context, Pf41 is investigated as a complementary serological marker that may contribute additional resolution to exposure profiling when used alongside established antigens, rather than as an indicator of recent infection or current transmission [9, 10]. Pf41 is a member of the 6-Cys (s48/45) protein family of P. falciparum, has emerged as a promising complementary serological markers due to its distinct biological and immuno-epidemiological characteristics [11]. Pf41 forms a complex with Pf12, exposing epitopes recognized by host antibodies during erythrocyte invasion [12]. Pf41 is a merozoite surface protein that forms a functional complex with Pf12 during erythrocyte invasion, exposing epitopes that are recognized by host antibodies at a critical stage of the parasite life cycle [12]. Previous studies have demonstrated natural seropositivity to Pf41 in endemic regions, ranging from 32 to 88%, underscoring its potential as a biomarker of malaria exposure [13, 14]. This variability likely reflect differences in cumulative malaria exposure and immune acquisition in endemic populations. Evidence from multi-antigen sero epidemiological studies further supports the immunogenicity of Pf41 and its association with cumulative malaria exposure in endemic settings [13]. However, the Pf41 serological evidence from the Indonesian population remains scarce. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of anti-Pf41 antibodies among individuals exposed to malaria in endemic areas of the Menoreh Hills, Purworejo Regency, Central Java where conventional methods for parasite-based surveillance may exhibit limited sensitivity in detecting malaria exposure. By characterizing antibody responses and their associations with demographic and behavioral factors, this study primarily assesses the utility of Pf41 as a serological marker in a low-transmission setting, while providing complementary epidemiological context regarding malaria exposure.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted from 10 January to 20 June 2025 as part of a seroepidemiological survey. The study period coincided with a post-transmission phase in a malaria elimination setting, thereby enabling the assessment of serological responses that reflect cumulative and historical malaria exposure rather than active clinical transmission. Samples were collected from four malaria-endemic villages in the Menoreh Hills: Sedayu, Kemejing, Kembaran, and Wadas (Fig. 1). These villages were selected to represent a range of historical malaria transmission intensities within the Menoreh Hills region, based on routine surveillance data and previously reported malaria incidence. In 2017, the region reported an annual parasite incidence of approximately 4.0 per 1,000 population, with malaria transmission predominantly driven by Plasmodium falciparum [15, 16]. Other malaria species were reported only sporadically and were largely associated with imported cases reflecting substantial P. falciparum transmission prior to the transition toward malaria elimination [16]. The Menoreh Hills form a geographically continuous, cross-border region spanning Kulon Progo Regency in the Special Region of Yogyakarta, Purworejo Regency and Magelang Regency in Central Java Province. At the time of the study, Purworejo Regency remained in the malaria elimination phase, while the neighboring regencies of Kulon Progo and Magelang had achieved malaria elimination certification. The Menoreh Hills are characterized by a heterogeneous landscape comprising forested areas, agricultural land, and diverse hydrological features. Located at elevations of approximately 200–800 m above sea level with an annual rainfall of 2,000–3,000 mm, the area provides ecological conditions that are favorable for the breeding and resting of Anopheles mosquitoes.

Study population

A total of 120 individuals were recruited from four villages in the Menoreh Hills that have been historically classified as malaria-endemic based on routine surveillance records, reflecting areas with documented malaria transmission in previous years. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling based on predefined inclusion criteria. Eligible individuals were required to have resided in the study villages for more than six months, be older than five years of age, be capable of providing informed consent, and show no clinical signs of malaria at the time of sample collection. Children under five years of age were excluded due to potential immaturity of antibody responses and the influence of maternally derived immunity [17]. According to routine surveillance records from the Purworejo Regency Health Office, no indigenous clinical malaria cases were reported in the study villages at the time of sample collection (January–June 2025). The exclusion criteria encompassed any history of antimalarial treatment within the preceding three months and the presence of severe comorbidities defined as chronic or systemic diseases such as HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, diabetes melitus, chronic hepatic/renal impairment that might confound immune response assesment. In addition, 24 healthy participants from non-malaria-endemic areas in South Korea were included as controls to establish baseline antibody reactivity. These areas are characterized by the absence of local malaria transmission, and thus the population is considered non-exposed to malaria vectors. All participants used in this study were subsequently screened using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and the results confirmed that they were negative for malaria infection at the time of sampling. The study protocol was reviewed with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Public Health, Universitas Diponegoro (No. 144/EA/KEPK-FKM/2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For participants under 18 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian.

Sample collection

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula for prevalence studies, with additional consideration of field feasibility in a low-transmission setting, resulting in a fixed sample of 30 participants per village. Participants were enrolled with the assistance of local health centers. A health questionnaire was designed to capture key demographic variables, including age, gender, educational level, and occupation, as well as behavioral risk factors associated with malaria exposure (Supplementary 1). Behavioral variables included history of mosquito bites, travel to other malaria-endemic areas, use of bed nets, use of mosquito repellents, engagement in outdoor activities during night hours, and contact with livestock at night. Travel history was recorded as a binary variable (yes/no) indicating whether participants had ever traveled to other malaria-endemic areas, which may differ in transmission intensity from the study villages, and was included as a proxy for additional malaria exposure. Preventive behaviors, including bed net and repellent use, were assessed through self-reported practices to avoid mosquito bites and were analyzed as binary variables without detailed information on timing or frequency. Similarly, self-reported malaria history was recorded as an ever/never variable with specification of timing or number of episodes. Each individual voluntarily donated 3–5 mL of venous blood, which was collected in an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tube. The serum was separated by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was carefully transferred into sterile microtubes. The serum aliquots were subsequently stored at –20 °C to preserve immunoglobulin integrity until further serological analysis.

Pf41 recombinant protein expressions and purifications

The Pf41 recombinant protein construct was designed based on the P. falciparum 3D7 strain sequence PF3D7_0404900 (21-378aa) resulting in an expected molecular weight of 47 kDa. This construct incorporated all relevant non-synonymous mutations necessary for antigenicity screening. The DNA fragments encoding those proteins were amplified from Pf3D7 with primer pairs Pf41_F: 5’-CTTGGAGCGGCCGCAAAGAGCCACAAGTGCGAC-3’; Pf41_R: 5’-GTTCTCTGGCGCGCCGCTGGTCTTGAAGCTGTTCAGC-3’. Recombinant proteins were expressed using HEK293 EBNA1-6E cells, a human embryonic kidney-derived suspension cell line expressing EBNA1. The cell line was originally developed by researchers at National Research Council Canada and was cultured under standard conditions according to established protocols. The codon-optimized genes were amplified with specific primers and subcloned into the pTT5-8 × His expression vector using the In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA). Transfection of HEK293 EBNA1-6E cells was performed using linear polyethylenimine hydrochloride as a transfection reagent, and the secreted recombinant protein was harvested from the culture supernatant after five days. Purification was carried out using Ni–NTA agarose (QIAGEN) with an elution buffer containing 350 mM imidazole, 50 mM HEPES, 5% glycerol, and 150 mM NaCl. The purified protein (1 µg) was prepared with a reducing buffer and resolved using a 4–12% gradient Bis–Tris Mini Protein Gel (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The gel was subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Sigma-Aldrich) to confirm protein purity and integrity.

Protein microarray

To evaluate the antigenic variation of recombinant proteins, a protein microarray technique was employed [18, 19]. Antigens were systematically immobilized on aminopropyl-coated slides as previously described [19]. Briefly, the recombinant proteins were applied at an optimized concentration of 100 ng/µL per spot and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C to ensure proper antigen attachment. The slides were then blocked with a 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution in phosphate-buffered saline with Tween 20 (PBS-T) for 1 h at 37 °C to minimize non-specific binding. These sera were diluted 1:25 in PBS-T and applied in duplicate to each antigen spot, followed by incubation for 1 h at 37 °C. The secondary antibody was detected using Alexa Fluor 546-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA,10 ng/µL) in PBS-T, with a subsequent 1-h incubation at 37 °C. The microarrays were scanned using an InnoScan 300 (INNOPSYS, Carbonne, France). Positive antigenic responses were defined using a cut-off threshold calculated as the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) plus two standard deviations (mean + 2SD) of the healthy non-exposed controls group. Samples with MFI values exceeding this cut-off were classified as positive. Normalized MFI values were calculated by dividing each sample’s raw MFI by the corresponding cut-off value (mean + 2SD), allowing comparison of antibody responses across samples. The healthy non-exposed control group therefore served as the negative baseline reference against which the statistical significance of antibody responses among study participants was evaluated. Seropositivity was defined as the proportion of individuals with a positive antigenic response within the study population.

Statistical analysis

For data analysis, variables were categorized as follows: age was categorized into productive (15–64 years) and nonproductive (< 15 or > 64 years), based on the World Health Organization (WHO) standard and groups based on their epidemiological relevance to malaria exposure risk, as productive-age individuals; educational level was classified as low (education below senior high school) and high (completion of senior high school or higher); and occupation was categorized based on the primary work environment; outdoor category included jobs predominantly performed outside (e.g., farmers, laborers, project workers) and indoor category comprised roles primarily based indoors (e.g., housewives, entrepreneurs, village officials, private employees, village heads, pensioners, and merchants). Protein microarray data and demographic risk factors were systematically analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism v10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) to ensure comprehensive interpretation. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The primary outcome variable, Pf41 mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), showed a right-skewed distribution in several subgroups based on Kolmogorov–Smirnov normality tests (p < 0.05). Because the linear regression assumes approximately normally distributed residuals and homoscedasticity, MFI values were log₁₀-transformed to reduce skewness and stabilize variance prior to regression analysis. To compare antibody responses between the study population and healthy non-exposed controls, protein array results were analyzed using either the Mann–Whitney U test or the independent t-test. Results were considered statistically significant if the p-value was < 0.05 (95% confidence interval). To address non-normality and stabilize variance, MFI values were log₁₀-transformed prior to analysis. Associations between demographic factors (age, gender, and education level) and behavioral factors (outdoor mosquito exposure at night, indoor mosquito exposure, feeding livestock at night, checking livestock at night, use of repellents, use of mosquito nets, travel history to malaria-endemic areas, and self-reported malaria history) and log₁₀(MFI) were examined using General Linear Models (GLM) in SPSS. All independent variables were entered simultaneously into each model (sociodemographic characteristics and behavioral risk factors, Tables 1 and 2) to adjust for potential confounding effects, thereby estimating adjusted regression coefficients. Analyses were conducted separately for each study site (Sedayu, Kemejing, Kembaran, and Wadas; n = 30 per site) to account for potential heterogeneity in malaria transmission intensity and exposure patterns across villages. Site-stratified modeling allowed evaluation of village-specific associations without assuming homogeneity of effects across locations. Additionally, pooled analyses including all sites (n = 120) were performed to evaluate overall associations. Additionally, pooled analyses were performed to evaluate overall associations, taking into account the effects at the village level. Village was included as an adjustment variable in the pooled analysis. The primary analyses were regression-based using GLM, results are presented as unstandardized regression coefficients (B) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) and where p-values represent between-group differences in log₁₀(MFI). Model fit was evaluated using R2 values, which quantify the proportion of variance in log₁₀(MFI) explained by the predictors included in each multivariable model, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Geographical and ecological characteristics

This location was selected based on its historical prevalence of malaria transmission and environmental conditions conducive to the proliferation of Anopheles mosquitoes. The region is characterized by undulating hilly terrain, featuring a topographical mix of rain-fed fields, plantations and secondary forests (Fig. 1). These conditions facilitate the formation of slow-moving streams, puddles, and seasonally stagnant pools in a humid climate. In Sedayu and Kembaran, the ecological landscape is dominated by stable natural springs that serve as the primary perennial breeding sites for Anopheles mosquitoes. In contrast, Kemejing exhibits a more diverse hydrological profile, with notably higher habitat diversity, including slow-moving river streams and seasonal ponds. Wadas shares similar hilly characteristics but is distinguished by its proximity to denser forest edges and a higher prevalence of seasonal water stagnation. Beyond these natural factors, anthropogenic activities also play a significant role, as residents commonly position livestock pens adjacent to or directly behind their homes. The majority of the population are farmers reliant on dryland cultivation and forest product management, in addition to being livestock breeders. Epidemiologically, this region is classified under low to residual malaria transmission status.

Expression and purification of Pf41

The recombinant Pf41 external domain (estimated molecular weight ~ 47 kDa) was successfully expressed in HEK293 cells and purified. The quality of the purified protein was evaluated using SDS-PAGE (Fig. 2). After elution with imidazole, prominent and clearly separated protein bands were observed in the elution fractions (E1–E4). The highest yield of purified Pf41 protein was obtained in the initial elution fractions (E1 and E2), indicating efficient elution of the protein during the initial imidazole wash, whereas concentrations increased in the subsequent fractions (E3 and E4). The absence of visible contaminant bands and the clean flow-through fraction (FT) indicate that the resin binds well and the purification process is efficient. These results confirmed that Pf41 was obtained in a soluble, whole, and pure form. This confirms that the HEK293 expression system is suitable for producing antigens for subsequent serological analysis.

Antigenicity of anti-Pf41 antibodies

Significant differences in antibody responses were identified between the exposed populations and healthy non-exposed controls across all study sites (p < 0.001 for all). Individuals from the endemic villages, Sedayu 83% (25/30), Kemejing 47% (14/30), Kembaran 93% (28/30), and Wadas 60% (18/30) (Table 3), exhibited substantially higher seropositivity rates and normalized MFI values compared to healthy non-exposed controls from non–malaria-endemic areas. Pf41 seropositivity varied across study villages, with Kembaran showing an observed seropositivity rate of 93% (Fig. 3). The normalized mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) values for exposed-individuals ranged from 1.08 to 1.89, whereas the control group consistently showed lower values around 0.65 to 0.70 (Table 3). Among the endemic sites, Kembaran exhibited the highest antibody response (nMFI = 1.89), followed by Sedayu (nMFI = 1.29), Wadas (nMFI = 1.17), and Kemejing (nMFI = 1.08). This significant elevation in antibody levels is consistent with increased Pf41 seroreactivity in malaria-exposed populations compared to healthy non-exposed controls.

Behavioral risk factors and antibody response

Linear regression analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between behavioral risk factors and log₁₀-transformed Pf41 antibody levels (anti-Pf41 antibody level) (Table 2). All regression models utilized predefined reference categories and were adjusted for village. The distribution of Pf41 antibody levels across behavioral categories within each village is depicted in Figs. 5 and 6 for descriptive visualization of its antibody level. The coefficients of determination showed (R2 = 0.306) in Sedayu, (R2 = 0.381) in Kemejing, (R2 = 0.092) in Kembaran, and (R2 = 0.289) in Wadas; combined analysis across all study sites yielded an (R2 = 0.332). Significant associations were only observed specifically in Sedayu and Kemejing. In Sedayu, frequent mosquito bites during outdoor nighttime activities were significantly associated with lower anti-Pf41 antibody levels (p = 0.017). However, this finding should be interpreted with caution given the relatively small sample size and the potential for bias, including recall bias and misclassification of mosquito exposure, which may have influenced the observed association. In contrast, in Kemejing, feeding livestock at night was significantly associated with higher anti-Pf41 antibody levels (p = 0.027). In the pooled analysis across all locations (n = 120), feeding livestock at night remained independently associated with increased anti-Pf41 antibody (p = 0.027). Additionally, not using repellent was independently associated with lower log₁₀(MFI) Pf41 levels compared with repellent use (p < 0.05). This result should be interpreted cautiously, given the imbalance in repellent use among participants, with a substantially smaller proportion reporting repellent use than non-use, which may have affected the observed association. Other behavioral and historical variables including nighttime outdoor activity, frequency of indoor mosquito bites, bed net use, travel to malaria-endemic areas, and previous malaria infection history were not significantly associated with anti-Pf41 antibody levels in the regression models (all p > 0.05), although several showed coefficient directions consistent with expected exposure patterns.

Discussion

The present study revealed a high seroprevalence of anti-Pf41 antibodies among residents living in Menoreh Hills, suggesting substantial historical or cumulative malaria exposure even in settings with low reported clinical incidence [20]. The observed variation in antibody responses among different villages suggests local-scale heterogeneity in prior malaria exposure. This variation likely reflects differences in behavioral risk factors and historical exposure patterns, rather than uniform cumulative exposure across the region. Although heterogeneity in Pf41 responses was not formally quantified, the village-specific patterns are consistent with previous observations that malaria exposure can vary across discrete local foci, even within a single regency experiencing broader declines in reported cases [21]. Accordingly, Pf41 seropositivity is best understood as an indicator of past or cumulative exposure to Plasmodium falciparum, rather than as a definitive marker of active or residual malaria transmission. Antibodies against Plasmodium falciparum merozoite surface antigens have shown to persist for several months and, in some cases, up to approximately one year following infection, even in the absence of microscopically or molecularly detectable parasitemia [10]. The heterogeneity observed among the study sites corresponds with the microfocal patterns of malaria exposure described across Southeast Asia [22]. Village-level differences in Pf41 antibody responses may plausibly reflect variation in human–vector contact histories. In the present study, higher seropositivity and antibody intensity were observed in Kembaran compared with other villages, whereas Kemejing exhibited lower overall antibody responses. Such patterns are more likely attributable to heterogeneous exposure histories than to ongoing differences in transmission intensity. The absence of a notable link between age and Pf41 antibody levels indicates that the serological responses in this group might represent a relatively uniform cumulative exposure across different ages. While age is often used as an indicator of cumulative malaria exposure in areas where the disease is common, this connection can weaken in regions with decreasing or varied transmission rates [23]. In such areas, antibody responses can persist beyond the initial infection and are not strictly tied to age. Numerous influential sero-epidemiological studies have shown that IgG responses to blood-stage antigens can remain detectable for extended periods and may not consistently show strong age-related patterns in low-to-moderate transmission settings [24].

In Sedayu, frequent mosquito bites during nocturnal outdoor activities were associated with higher antibody responses. This evidence suggests that behavioral risk mapping, as indicated by the correlation between nocturnal outdoor activities and Pf41 antibody responses, can enhance sero-surveillance for identifying micro-scale infection foci in low-transmission settings [6]. A different pattern was observed in Kemejing feeding livestock at night was significantly associated with higher antibody responses. These findings suggest that particular behavioral practices represent plausible pathways for malaria exposure in specific village contexts, rather than evidence that such factors uniformly sustain transmission across all sites. In contrast, in the pooled analysis across all study sites, nighttime livestock feeding was associated with higher antibody levels, while repellent use was associated with lower antibody levels, suggesting distinct exposure dynamics across behavioral groups. These findings align with research conducted in Cambodia, Vietnam, and Myanmar, where individuals involved in nocturnal agricultural or forest-related activities demonstrated a higher prevalence of likelihood of malaria exposure due to increased contact with outdoor-biting mosquito vectors [25–27]. Similarly, studies in Indonesia have reported persistent malaria transmission in communities characterized by significant outdoor exposure and livestock-related activities [28, 29]. While ecological features such as proximity between human dwellings, livestock shelters, and potential mosquito breeding sites may contribute to increased exposure risk [29, 30], these characteristics were not systematically quantified in the present study and therefore cannot be interpreted as direct evidence of ongoing residual transmission. The observed exposure patterns are biologically plausible when interpreted in light of previously reported behavioral characteristics of malaria vectors in other endemic settings, including species with early evening, exophagic (outdoor-biting), and zoophilic (animal-biting) tendencies. These behaviors may be associated between behavioral risk factors and serological responses rather than as direct evidence of local vector ecology [31, 32]. Their exophagic (outdoor-biting) and and zoophilic tendencies may facilitate overlapping transmission cycles between humans and livestock, whereby the presence of animals can function either as diversionary hosts (zooprophylaxis) or as transmission-enhancing attractants (zoopotentiation), depending on livestock shelter structure and proximity to human dwellings [32]. In rural settings where animal pens are commonly adjacent to houses, this ecological configuration likely increases human–vector contact, thereby contributing to amplified antibody responses among residents [33].

Given the cross-sectional design of this study and the lack of longitudinal data on Pf41 antibody kinetics, the observed seropositivity cannot be interpreted as evidence of current or residual infection [34]. Therefore, Pf41 antibody responses in this study could be interpreted as markers of prior malaria exposure at an undetermined time point, rather than indicators of ongoing parasitemia or active transmission. Individuals residing in endemic areas who exhibit asymptomatic or previously exposed immune profiles are increasingly recognized in the literature as populations that harbor immunological evidence of past infection and and therefore serve as markers of residual malaria exposure, particularly in low-incidence settings where routine surveillance systems primarily capture symptomatic cases [2, 35, 36]. This poses challenges for malaria elimination, as conventional passive surveillance systems may fail to detect these infections. The high immunoreactivity observed in our study adds to evidence supporting Pf41 as both a biomarker of malaria exposure and a potential target for future interventions [14].

The integration of Pf41-based serology into malaria surveillance frameworks may complement existing monitoring approaches by identifying populations with serological evidence of prior malaria exposure that are not captured by routine parasitological methods. Beyond its diagnostic utility, serology serves as an epidemiological tool for assessing intervention efficacy, monitoring long-term transmission dynamics, and identifying hidden transmission foci that evade detection by microscopy or rapid diagnostic tests [37, 38]. Collectively, these findings affirm the potential of Pf41 serology as a complement to conventional surveillance strategies, thereby bolstering malaria elimination efforts in areas characterized by transmission heterogeneity. In this research, antibody responses specific to Pf41 were used as markers of possibility to routine malaria exposure. Examining reactions to particular antigens can reveal differences in transmission rates, immune history, and the immunogenicity of certain antigens, thus providing insights into exposure patterns in areas where multiple diseases are present [39]. Furthermore, using a combination of antigens or species-specific markers could improve the understanding of malaria exposure dynamics, particularly in regions with low transmission rates or where elimination efforts are in progress [40].

An important limitation of this study is the absence of comparator serological assays targeting established malaria antigens such as MSP1, AMA1, and GLURP. Without parallel measurement of antibody responses to commonly used markers, it is not possible to directly compare Pf41 with other serological indicators of malaria exposure or to assess its relative performance against antigens with well-established immunological track records. As a result, the present findings should be interpreted as describing Pf41 antibody responses within the sampled communities rather than validating Pf41 as a superior or equivalent exposure marker. Future studies incorporating multi-antigen panels and longitudinal designs would be valuable for contextualizing Pf41 responses and assessing their added value in malaria surveillance. Additionally, the temporal dynamics of anti-Pf41 antibody responses, including decay kinetics and duration of seropositivity following infection, have not been established. This precludes distinguishing recent from distant exposure based on serological data alone and limits the interpretation of seropositivity to an indicator of prior exposure at an undetermined timepoint.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Pf41 elicits varied antibody responses among residents of malaria-prone villages in Menoreh Hills, Central Java, reflecting different exposure levels in a low-transmission setting. High seropositivity was observed among those exposed, with differences between villages, while controls showed no responses, highlighting the possibility of Pf41's specificity as an exposure marker. Sociodemographic analyses revealed no significant associations with Pf41 antibody levels across the study sites. Behavioral factors showed varied associations in Sedayu and Kemejing only. In the combined analysis across all sites, feeding livestock at night and the use of repellent were significantly associated with Pf41 antibody levels. Other behavioral variables showed no significant associations. The findings underscore the potential of Pf41 serology as a supplementary surveillance tool, capable of detecting both cumulative and potentially hidden malaria exposure in regions with low transmission and those on the brink of elimination. By revealing variations in exposure within micro-epidemiological contexts, Pf41 could help identify residual transmission hotspots that conventional surveillance methods might miss. Moreover, the antibody responses to Pf41 seem to reflect specific patterns of malaria exposure shaped by local environmental and behavioral factors, thereby confirming its value in surveillance systems focused on elimination. These findings suggest that Pf41 serology reflects cumulative malaria exposure influenced by local contexts and indicates its potential as surveillance tool in elimination settings. However, the study focused exclusively on Pf41 antibody responses without assessing other malaria antigens or species, which may limit the comprehensiveness of exposure assessment in co-endemic settings. Finally, the lack of longitudinal follow-up restricts understanding of antibody persistence and dynamics over time.
