Utility and clinical relevance of multiplex PCR panels for diagnosis of central nervous system infections
Authors: Merve Gürler, Çisem Karaoğlu, Ebru Evren Yurtcu, Ebru Us, Zeynep Ceren Karahan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesMultiplex molecular panels for meningitis and encephalitis (M&E) allow rapid detection of multiple pathogens directly from cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples. Although their use is expanding, real-world data on their diagnostic yield and clinical relevance remain limited. This study aimed to assess the diagnostic performance, clinical relevance, and usage patterns of multiplex PCR panels for diagnosis of meningitis and encephalitis over a five-year period in a tertiary care hospital.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed CSF samples tested with multiplex M&E panels between January 2019 and December 2023. Clinical data, test results, and subsequent clinical actions were reviewed. Only the first positive sample per patient was included. Clinical relevance was defined as a documented change in diagnosis or treatment attributed to the panel result.ResultsOut of 1,491 CSF samples, 122 (8.2%) were positive for at least one pathogen. Viral agents accounted for most detections, with human herpesvirus-6 (n=34, 27.9%), enterovirus (n=14, 11.5%), and herpes simplex virus type-1 (n=10, 8.2%) being most common. Streptococcus pneumoniae (n=13, 10.6%) was the leading bacterial pathogen. Co-detections were observed in (n=18, 14.7%) of positive cases. Only 41% (n=50) of positive results were associated with a clinically relevant action. Repeat testing after an initial negative result had low additional diagnostic yield (n=7, 7.2%), and several tests were ordered for conditions not covered by the panels.Interpretation and conclusionsMultiplex PCR panels offer rapid pathogen detection in CNS infections, but inappropriate test utilisation and limited follow up may reduce their clinical impact. Clearer testing criteria and clinician awareness regarding panel contents are essential to improve diagnostic efficiency.

Central nervous system (CNS) infections such as meningitis and encephalitis pose significant diagnostic challenges because of overlap in their clinical presentations.1

Recent advances in molecular diagnostics, particularly syndromic multiplex PCR panels, have improved the diagnostic work-up of CNS infections. These panels allow simultaneous detection of multiple pathogens directly from cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples, offering rapid results with high analytical sensitivity and specificity.2 Compared to conventional methods, they provide several advantages: faster turnaround time, broader coverage, and reliable performance even in patients who have already received empirical antimicrobial treatment, which often reduces culture sensitivity.3,4

Despite their benefits, multiplex panels are not without limitations. High costs and the potential for detecting clinically irrelevant or latent pathogens may complicate interpretation. For example, the detection of human herpesvirus-6 (HHV-6) may reflect chromosomally integrated viral DNA rather than active infection, particularly in immunocompetent individuals. Similarly, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) positivity in CSF may indicate latent infection without CNS involvement.1,5,6​

Currently, two FDA-approved multiplex panels are widely used for the diagnosis of meningitis and encephalitis: the FilmArray Meningitis/Encephalitis (M&E) Panel (BioMérieux, France) and the QIAstat-Dx Meningitis/Encephalitis Panel (Qiagen, USA). The FilmArray system detects 14 targets, including six bacteria (Escherichia coli K1, Haemophilus influenzae, Listeria monocytogenes, Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus agalactiae, and Streptococcus pneumoniae), seven viruses (herpes simplex viruses 1 and 2, varicella zoster virus, cytomegalovirus, HHV-6, enterovirus, and parechovirus), and one fungus (Cryptococcus neoformans/gattii), typically within one hour.7 The QIAstat-Dx panel additionally includes Streptococcus pyogenes and Mycoplasma pneumoniae, but does not target cytomegalovirus. It also provides access to amplification curves and cycle threshold (Ct) values.8

As the clinical use of multiplex panels becomes increasingly common, especially in emergency and intensive care settings, the principles of diagnostic stewardship are gaining importance. Unrestricted use without clinical correlation may result in diagnostic confusion, overtreatment, and increased healthcare costs. Effective communication between clinicians, infectious disease specialists, and microbiologists is essential to optimise test utility and interpretation.9

We aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance, clinical relevance, and utilisation patterns of multiplex PCR panels for meningitis and encephalitis over a five-year period in a tertiary-care academic hospital, using three different commercial platforms.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the division of Molecular Microbiology, Ankara University Faculty of Medicine, İbn-i Sina Hospital, Ankara, Turkey. The laboratory serves two tertiary-care campuses, providing approximately 70,000 inpatient bed-days and 1.5 million outpatient visits annually. All multiplex M&E panel test requests between January 2019 and December 2023 were included in the study period. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Ankara University Faculty of Medicine and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Since this was a retrospective analysis using anonymised laboratory and medical record data, individual informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All patients, regardless of age or sex, who underwent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) testing for suspected meningitis or encephalitis were eligible. Only the first positive CSF sample per patient was included in the analysis to avoid duplication. Cases with missing clinical or demographic information were excluded.

Data collection

Demographic, clinical, and microbiological data were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. The collected variables included age, sex, clinical indications for testing, requesting departments, detected pathogens, and follow up actions after positive results, such as initiation of targeted antimicrobial or antiviral therapy or modification of empirical regimens. For patients with initially negative results, the rationale and outcome of any repeat testing were also reviewed.

Study population

Patients were categorised into two age groups for comparative analysis: children (<18 yr) and adults (≥18 yr).

M&E panel testing platforms

During the study period, different commercial multiplex PCR panels were employed. In 2019, the Neuro 9, Neonatal Meningitis, Viral Meningitis, and Bacterial Meningitis panels (FastTrack Diagnostics, Siemens Healthineers, Germany) were used. Between 2020 and 2021, testing was performed using the BioFire® FilmArray® Meningitis/Encephalitis Panel (bioMérieux, France), while in 2022–2023 the QIAstat-Dx® Meningitis/Encephalitis Panel (Qiagen, Germany) was implemented. Each CSF sample was collected aseptically and processed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

All CSF samples were processed in a biosafety level-2 molecular microbiology laboratory by trained personnel. Approximately 200 µL of uncentrifuged CSF was used per assay. Samples were kept refrigerated (2–8°C) and tested within 4 h of receipt; when delayed, aliquots were stored at −80°C until analysis. Each multiplex system (FastTrack, BioFire® FilmArray®, and QIAstat-Dx®) utilises a closed, single-use cartridge containing built-in internal inhibition controls and automated nucleic acid extraction and amplification steps, thereby minimising the risk of human error and cross-contamination. For the QIAstat-Dx® Meningitis/Encephalitis Panel, amplification curves and cycle threshold (Ct) values were available; however, Ct data were not consistently recorded in the laboratory system and were therefore not included in the quantitative analysis.

Definition of clinical relevance

A positive panel result was considered ‘clinically relevant’ when at least one of the following conditions was met: initiation or change of targeted antimicrobial or antiviral therapy; additional diagnostic investigations prompted by the panel result (e.g., brain imaging, repeat lumbar puncture); or a confirmed clinical diagnosis consistent with the detected pathogen. Results were classified as ‘clinically non-relevant’ when the detected pathogen was reviewed but judged not to explain the clinical presentation (e.g., latent viral detection). Results were classified as ‘not evaluated’ when no clinician follow up or documentation regarding test interpretation was available.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as median (range) because they did not follow a normal distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All P-values were reported exactly (to three decimal places), and effect sizes (Cramer’s V or odds ratios with 95% CI) were calculated where applicable. As only pairwise group comparisons were conducted, no correction for multiple comparisons was applied. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study cohort and demographics

A total of 1,491 CSF samples from unique patients were analysed. The median patient age was 34 years (range: 1 day to 87 yr), with n=486 (32.6%) of cases in the paediatric group and n=1,005 (67.4%) in adults. The most common clinical indications for M&E panel testing were fever, altered level of consciousness, and headache.

Among the 1,491 patients, 122 (8.2%) had a positive result for at least one target on the M&E panel. Of these, n=73 (59.8%) were male and n=49 (40.2%) female. Paediatric patients accounted for 58.2% (n=71/122) of the positive cases, whereas adults comprised 41.8% (n=51/122). The positivity rate was significantly higher in the paediatric population (14.6%, 71/486) compared to adults (5.1%, 51/1,005; P<0.001). The mean age of patients with positive results was 25 years (range: 7 days–87 years). Fever was the most common presenting symptom in paediatric patients (n=37, 52.1%), while altered consciousness was most frequent in adults (n=19, 37.2%).

The departments most frequently requesting M&E panels were paediatrics (n=486, 32.6%), neurology (n=465, 31.2%), and emergency (n=116, 7.8%). The highest positivity rate was observed in paediatric patients (n=71, 14.6%), followed by emergency (n=11, 9.5%) and neurology (n=24, 5.2%).

Viruses were the predominant pathogens detected. Figure 1 shows the specific pathogens. Viral detections were more common in paediatric cases, predominantly HHV-6 and enterovirus, while bacterial pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis were more frequently observed in adults. The proportion of clinically relevant results did not differ significantly between paediatric and adult groups (43.7% vs. 38.9%, P=0.32).

Co-detections

Multiple pathogens were detected in 18 out of 122 positive samples (14.7%). The most frequent co-detection was HHV-6 with parvovirus B19 (50% of co-detections). Repeat testing was performed in six of these co-detection cases. In five cases, repeat tests confirmed the original result. One patient who initially tested positive for Neisseria meningitidis, EBV, and CMV tested positive only for EBV and CMV nine days later, suggesting N. meningitidis as the likely true pathogen. Among the 18 cases with co-detections, repeat testing was clinically requested in six patients using newly obtained CSF samples collected within 1–10 days of the initial test. Five of these six repeat tests (83.3%) confirmed the same pathogen profile, while one showed clearance of N. meningitidis upon follow up, likely reflecting treatment response. Therefore, this analysis reflects clinical evolution rather than analytical reproducibility. Notably, 16 out of 18 co-detections (88.9%) occurred in 2019, when the FastTrack Diagnostics® multiplex PCR panel was in use.

Indications for repeat testing

The most common indications for repeat testing included persistent fever (with or without headache), altered mental status, and seizures (42.3%). Imaging findings suggestive of encephalitis accounted for 12.3% of repeat testing requests. Other indications (33.6%) included neonatal prematurity, sepsis, ventriculoperitoneal shunt-related infections, unexplained vomiting, skin rashes, neuropsychiatric symptoms, cerebrovascular disease, trauma, and suspected autoimmune or infectious neurological disorders (e.g., multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children [MIS-C], neurobrucellosis, neurosyphilis, and neuro-Behçet’s disease).

Some repeat M&E panel tests were requested in clinical scenarios where suspected pathogens, such as Brucella spp., Treponema pallidum, or staphylococcal species associated with shunt infections, were not covered by the available test panels. These cases reflect inappropriate test utilisation and limited clinician awareness of panel contents, potentially resulting in unnecessary testing, reduced diagnostic yield, and increased healthcare costs.

Discussion

Timely and accurate diagnosis of CNS infections is essential to avoid unnecessary antimicrobial therapy, reduce diagnostic uncertainty, limit hospital stay, and ultimately prevent morbidity and mortality.10,11 In this five-year retrospective study, we evaluated the diagnostic performance and clinical relevance of multiplex PCR panels used for meningitis and encephalitis in a tertiary-care setting. The findings demonstrate that although these panels provide rapid and broad pathogen detection, their real-world clinical utility is limited by suboptimal interpretation and follow up. Only a portion of positive results led to documented changes in clinical management, underscoring a need for improved diagnostic stewardship and clinician awareness regarding test selection and interpretation.

The observed trends in test utilisation reflected institutional practices, with most requests originating from paediatric and neurology departments, in line with earlier reports from Türkiye,12 but differing from international data, where use is highest in emergency settings.13 This variation suggests that institutional practices and department-specific workflows strongly influence test utilisation. The clinical indications, including fever, altered mental status, and headache, were consistent with global findings.13 Fever was more common among paediatric patients, whereas altered consciousness predominated in adults, reflecting age-specific clinical patterns. The overall positivity rate of multiplex panels in our cohort was comparable to that reported in previous BioFire® FilmArray® studies,10,14-16 and positivity was notably higher in paediatric patients, confirming their higher diagnostic yield in children.10,15 Fluctuations observed across individual years may be partly explained by variations in hospital admission patterns and diagnostic practices during the COVID-19 pandemic, as well as inter-platform differences in analytical sensitivity, which should be considered when interpreting temporal trends. The inter-annual differences in positivity rates were largely attributable to changes in diagnostic platforms rather than true epidemiological shifts. The FastTrack Diagnostics® panels yielded the highest positivity, likely due to broader viral coverage and increased susceptibility to cross-reactivity, while the BioFire® and QIAstat-Dx® systems demonstrated more consistent performance with lower co-detection rates. This underscores the importance of interpreting longitudinal data within the context of assay-specific variability.

Among viral pathogens, HHV-6 predominated, a result similar to that reported by Radmard et al14 although its clinical relevant remains uncertain, especially in immunocompetent patients.5,6 Although HHV-6 was the most frequently detected virus in our cohort, its clinical relevance remained low. This finding is consistent with previous studies and can be explained by the detection of latent or chromosomally integrated HHV-6 DNA rather than active CNS infection, particularly in immunocompetent individuals. The high analytical sensitivity of multiplex PCR panels may also contribute to the detection of clinically insignificant viral DNA, underscoring the importance of correlating HHV-6 results with clinical findings and CSF parameters before interpretation. Streptococcus pneumoniae was the most frequently detected bacterial pathogen, consistent with previous international studies,10,13,14,17,18 reinforcing its clinical importance despite vaccination programmes. Co-detections were more common in our data than in most previous reports from the United States and India,10,18 particularly during the period when the FastTrack Diagnostics® panel was in use. This finding supports previous observations that analytical design and target range may affect co-detection frequency.19 These variations highlight the need for critical clinical correlation, especially when low-yield or latent viral targets such as HHV-6, EBV, or parvovirus B19 are detected.6

Test positivity and clinical impact varied by department. The higher yield in paediatric units corresponds to the higher incidence of viral CNS infections and the greater use of molecular diagnostics in children, while lower positivity among neurology and emergency departments may reflect broader diagnostic uncertainty, where CNS infection is suspected in a wider range of non-infectious conditions. Understanding such service-specific testing behaviours is essential for optimising diagnostic yield, avoiding unnecessary panel use, and implementing targeted diagnostic stewardship.

Pathogens such as Neisseria meningitidis, E. coli, and Streptococcus pyogenes were associated with 100% clinical relevance, demonstrating that panel results can directly guide appropriate therapy, particularly in bacterial CNS infections. However, other targets such as HHV-6 and EBV had limited clinical significance in our setting. These findings suggest that a more targeted testing approach may enhance clinical utility and reduce unnecessary downstream interventions. Existing proposals, such as modified Reller criteria for HSV-1 and stepwise algorithms for low-yield viral targets, may be useful in guiding test use.5,6 Another important aspect of our study was the evaluation of repeat testing. Although only a small proportion of patients underwent repeat M&E panel testing, the overall yield was low, and less than half of repeat positives were clinically relevant. While some repeat tests were justified by evolving symptoms or imaging findings, others were ordered in clinical scenarios where the suspected pathogens, such as Brucella spp., Treponema pallidum, or staphylococcal spp, were not covered by the panels. This highlights an important area for improvement through clinician education and clearer testing guidelines.

This study has several strengths, including a large sample size spanning both paediatric and adult populations, the inclusion of high-risk and immunocompromised patients, and the assessment of test relevance in real-world clinical practice. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The use of three different multiplex PCR platforms during the study period may have introduced variability in target detection and analytical sensitivity. Furthermore, as a single-centre, retrospective analysis, the findings may not be generalisable to other institutions with different patient populations or testing algorithms. The lack of outcome measures such as antimicrobial use or length of stay also limits the ability to fully evaluate clinical and economic impact.20 These parameters could not be consistently retrieved due to incomplete retrospective documentation; however, the proportion of clinically relevant results provides an indirect measure of test impact on patient management. Future prospective studies incorporating treatment and outcome data are planned to address this gap. Although multivariable modelling could further clarify predictors of panel positivity, the limited number of positive cases precluded reliable regression analysis. Future multicentre studies with larger datasets are warranted to explore independent determinants of test performance and clinical relevance.

In conclusion, multiplex PCR panels substantially improve the speed and breadth of pathogen detection in suspected CNS infections; however, their true clinical benefit becomes evident only when results are interpreted within a structured clinical framework. In our cohort, many positive results did not alter management, highlighting a gap between analytical detection and actionable decision-making. These findings underscore that the panels are most effective when used in patients with a high pre-test probability, such as those with acute bacterial meningitis, viral encephalitis in children, or immunocompromised hosts, where rapid pathogen identification can directly inform antimicrobial initiation, escalation, or de-escalation. Integrating multiplex PCR testing into evidence-based clinical pathways, supported by diagnostic stewardship and close communication between clinicians and microbiologists, may therefore enhance its real-world impact. Ultimately, targeted and clinically contextualised use of these panels has the potential to shorten time to appropriate therapy, reduce unnecessary antimicrobial exposure, and improve outcomes in patients presenting with suspected CNS infections.
