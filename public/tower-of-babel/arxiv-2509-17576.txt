Abstract
Protocols for distributed quantum systems commonly require the simultaneous availability of n n entangled states, each with a fidelity above some fixed minimum F app F_{\mathrm{app}} relative to the target maximally-entangled state. However, the fidelity of entangled states degrades over time while in memory. Entangled states are therefore rendered useless when their fidelity falls below F app F_{\mathrm{app}} . This is problematic when entanglement generation is probabilistic and attempted in a sequential manner, because the expected completion time until n n entangled states are available can be large. Motivated by existing entanglement generation schemes, we consider a system where the entanglement generation parameters (the success probability p p and fidelity F F of the generated entangled state) may be adjusted at each time step. We model the system as a Markov decision process, where the policy dictates which generation parameters ( p , F ) (p,F) to use for each attempt. We use dynamic programming to derive optimal policies that minimise the expected time until n n entangled states are available with fidelity greater than F app F_{\mathrm{app}} . We observe that the advantage of our optimal policies over the selected baselines increases significantly with n n . In the parameter regimes explored, which are based closely on current experiments, we find that the optimal policy can provide a speed-up of as much as a factor of twenty over a constant-action policy. In addition, we propose a computationally inexpensive heuristic method to compute policies that perform either optimally or near-optimally in the parameter regimes explored. Our heuristic method can be used to find high-performing policies in parameter regimes where finding an optimal policy is intractable.

In this work, we find protocols that optimise the rate of entanglement packet generation, by adaptively varying a rate-fidelity trade-off mechanism available due to the entanglement generation scheme.

Here, we consider a setting with two nodes that attempt entanglement generation sequentially.
In our model we assume that time is divided into discrete, uniform time steps, where in each time step, a single entanglement generation attempt is performed.

We use dynamic programming

By computing

MDP-based techniques have previously shown their value for the optimisation of a range of quantum network protocols. For example, they have been used to find optimal entanglement swapping policies in repeater networks

,
19
,
20

. Approximate reinforcement learning approaches, which efficiently find approximate solutions to problems formulated as MDPs, have also been utilised in the context of quantum networks, for example for designing entanglement routing schemes

]
, optimising quantum repeater chains for secret key distribution

]
or entanglement distribution

III
Methods

III-A

Constructing the Markov decision process

An MDP is defined as a 4-tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathrm{P},R)
where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space,
\mathrm{P}
is the transition function, and
R
is the reward function.
In the following, we elaborate on each component of the MDP for our system.

III-A
1
State space

The fidelity of each link in memory is fully characterised by the time it will survive before being discarded, which we refer to as the
time-to-live
(TTL) of a link.
Suppose that a link has fidelity
F>F_{\mathrm{app}}
. Recalling our decoherence model (
1
), the TTL of the link is given by

t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(F)=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\Gamma}\ln{\frac{F - \frac{1}{4}}{F_\mathrm{app} - \frac{1}{4}}}\right\rceil.

(9)

The ceiling function is taken because we work in discrete time. Letting the maximum fidelity of a newly generated link be denoted by

F_{\max}=\max\{F:(p,F)\in\mathcal{A}\},

the maximum TTL is given by

t_{\max}=t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(F_{\max}).

(10)

The state
S_{t}
of the system at time
t
characterises the relevant system information. For our system, the state is the number of links stored in memory and their TTLs (corresponding to link fidelities). If there are
m
links in memory, the state
s
is given by

s=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\},

(11)

where
t_{i}\in[t_{\max}]
is the TTL of the
i
th link, and
[t_{\max}]=\{1,\dots,t_{\max}\}
. Formally,
s
is a multiset that may contain multiple elements of the same value, because it is possible that
t_{i}=t_{j}
for
i\neq j
. Since the
n
memories are assumed to be identical, the ordering of the TTLs in any given state is assumed to be decreasing without loss of generality, i.e.
t_{i}\geq t_{j}
for
i<j
.

As an example, if
S_{t}=\{3,2\}
then at time
t
there are two links in memory, one of which will be discarded after three time steps, and the other after two time steps. The first link has a higher fidelity than the second link, because it will be discarded later.

We denote the set of all states with
m
links in memory as

\mathcal{S}_{m}=\big\{\left\{t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\right\}:t_{i}\in[t_{\mathrm{max}}],t_{1}\geq\dots\geq t_{m}\big\}.

(12)

We denote the state with no links in memory as the empty set
\varnothing\equiv\{\}
. The process is completed when there are
n
links in memory, or equivalently, when it reaches a state
s\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
. We also refer to
\mathcal{S}_{n}
as the set of
absorbing states
. See Figure
1
for an illustration.
The full state space is given by

\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{m=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{S}_{m}\cup\{\varnothing\}.

(13)

We also denote the combined state space with the absorbing states as

\mathcal{S}^{+}=\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}_{n}.

(14)

The time
T_{\pi}
until completion (when policy
\pi
is employed) may then be written explicitly as

T_{\pi}=\min\{t:S_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\}.

(15)

III-A
2
Action space

The action space
\mathcal{A}
is the set of generation parameters (
2
). The possible generation parameters depend on the specific entanglement generation scheme used. We note that
\mathcal{A}
is without loss of generality finite (see Section
IV-C
). In Section
IV-C
we give an example of
\mathcal{A}
for the single-click protocol.

III-A
3
Transition function

Suppose that at time
t
, the system is in state
s
, and that action
a\in\mathcal{A}
is chosen. Then, the transition function
\mathrm{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)
determines the probability of transitioning to state
s^{\prime}
in time step
t+1
. We now write down the transition function
\mathrm{P}
explicitly for our system.
We suppose that
s=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\}
and that the action chosen is
a=(p,F)
. There are two transitions that can occur. The first is when the entanglement generation attempt succeeds, which occurs with probability
p
. In this case, a new link is generated with TTL
t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(F)
, as given by (
9
). All other links decohere by one time step and are discarded if the fidelity falls below
F_{\mathrm{app}}
, or equivalently when the TTL becomes zero. Since the TTL (
9
) is the inverse of the decoherence map (
1
), decoherence over a single time step simply causes all TTLs to reduce by one.
In the event of entanglement generation success, the state in the next time step is therefore given by

s^{\prime}_{\mathrm{succ}}=\{t_{j}-1:t_{j}\in s,t_{j}>1\}\cup\{t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(F)\}.

(16)

The second possible transition is when entanglement generation fails, which occurs with probability
1-p
. The state in the next time step is given by

s^{\prime}_{\mathrm{fail}}=\{t_{j}-1:t_{j}\in s,t_{j}>1\}.

(17)

We therefore have

\mathrm{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)=\begin{cases}p,&\text{ if }s^{\prime}=s^{\prime}_{\mathrm{succ}}\\
1-p,&\text{ if }s^{\prime}=s^{\prime}_{\mathrm{fail}}\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(18)

Similarly, the transitions from the state
\varnothing
are given by

\mathrm{P}(s^{\prime}|\varnothing,a)=\begin{cases}p,&\text{ if }s^{\prime}=\{t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(F)\}\\
1-p,&\text{ if }s^{\prime}=\varnothing\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(19)

The transition function is fully defined by (
18
) and (
19
).

III-A
4
Reward

Since our objective is to minimise the expected completion time
\mathbb{E}[T_{\pi}]
, the reward is
R(s,a)=-1
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}^{+}
.

III-B

Dynamic programming

We use a dynamic programming algorithm known as
policy iteration
to compute optimal policies. We let
R_{t}\coloneqq R(S_{t},a)
be the reward at time step
t
, given that the state is
S_{t}
and action
a
is taken. Then, the
value
of a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
under policy
\pi
is defined as

v_{\pi}(s)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{T_{\pi}-t}R_{t+k}\Bigg|S_{t}=s\right].

(20)

This is the definition value for episodic problems with no discounting factor – see e.g. Chapter 3 of
[
15
]
for more details of how the value is defined for other systems.
In our system,

\displaystyle v_{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{T_{\pi}-t}-1|S_{t}=s\right]

(21)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}[T_{\pi}-t|S_{t}=s]

(22)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}[T_{\pi}|S_{0}=s].

(23)

Then,
v_{\pi}(s)
is simply the expected completion time, given that the process starts in state
s
and policy
\pi
is employed.

For all
s\in\mathcal{S}
and any policy
\pi
, we can calculate
v_{\pi}(s)
, which is a step known as
policy evaluation
. We do this with
iterative policy evaluation
, where the update rule

v_{k}(s)\coloneqq-1+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{P}(s^{\prime}|s,\pi(s))v_{k-1}(s^{\prime})

(24)

is recursively applied. Letting
v_{k}=v_{k-1}=v_{\pi}
, (
24
) is known as the
Bellman equation
for
v_{\pi}
. The sequence
\{v_{k}\}
obtained can be shown to converge to
v_{\pi}

[
15
]
.

Policy iteration computes an optimal policy
\pi^{*}
by starting with an arbitrary policy
\pi_{0}
, and then iteratively updating it until the value
v_{\pi}(s)
of each state
s
has been maximised.
Let
\pi_{k}
be the policy at the
k
-th iteration.
The policy is then updated by maximising the value with respect to all
a\in\mathcal{A}
for each state, which again can be achieved by considering the Bellman equation for
v_{\pi_{k-1}}
,

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(s)

\displaystyle=\underset{a\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathrm{argmax}\,}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\textrm{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)(-1+v_{\pi_{k-1}}(s^{\prime}))

(25)

\displaystyle=\underset{a\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathrm{argmax}\,}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\textrm{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)v_{\pi_{k-1}}(s^{\prime}).

(26)

Since both
|\mathcal{S}|
and
|\mathcal{A}|
are finite in our MDP, policy iteration converges to an optimal policy
\pi^{*}
after a finite number of iterations
[
15
]
.

IV
Results

IV-A

Analytical solution for
n=2

We now analyse the case
n=2
, for which we are able to find a closed-form expression for the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
and its expected waiting time. This will help to provide intuition for the properties we expect from optimal policies for larger
n
.

We recall the definition of the state space (
13
).
For
n=2
, the state space is written explicitly as

\mathcal{S}=\left\{\varnothing,\{1\},\{2\},\dots,\{t_{\max}\}\right\}.

(27)

For now, we write
\pi^{*}(\varnothing)=(p_{\varnothing},F_{\varnothing})\in\mathcal{A}
. The precise choice of
\pi^{*}(\varnothing)
will be fixed later.
We then notice that, if a single link is present in memory, one must maximise the success probability. This is because the process will be completed if the second link is generated before the first is discarded. Then, although maximising the success probability also minimises the fidelity of the generated link, this would not matter because the second link does not impact future behaviour. In particular, we define the action

\displaystyle(p_{\max},F_{\min})=\underset{(p,F)\in\mathcal{A}}{\text{argmax}}\{p\}.

(28)

Note that by the rate-fidelity trade-off (
3
) assumed in
\mathcal{A}
, we also have
F_{\min}=\min\{F:(p,F)\in\mathcal{A}\}.
We then set

\pi^{*}(s)\coloneqq(p_{\max},F_{\min}),\text{ for }s\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\setminus\big\{\{1\}\big\}.

(29)

In (
29
), we have not assigned the same action to
\pi^{*}(\{1\})
because, when there is a link with TTL of one, the link will be discarded in the next time step. Thus, there is no chance of completing the process in the next time step. One should therefore not maximise the success probability, as we do for the other states in (
29
). In particular,
\varnothing
and
\{1\}
both contain zero
viable links
, which are the number of links that have a non-zero probability of surviving until the system reaches an absorbing state
s\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
. We therefore view
\varnothing
and
\{1\}
as equivalent states, and they are assigned the same action

\pi^{*}(\{1\})=\pi^{*}(\varnothing)=(p_{\varnothing},F_{\varnothing}).

(30)

Equivalent states may also be identified when
n>2
to perform a reduction of the state space
\mathcal{S}
(see Appendix
A
), which can speed up the computation of optimal policies with dynamic programming.

Given the structure of the optimal policy from (
29
) and (
30
), we now
explicitly compute the expected completion time
\mathbb{E}[T_{\pi^{*}}]
. Assuming that the system starts in the state
S_{0}=\varnothing
, it will alternate between two phases until completion:

(A)

There are no viable links in memory (the state is either
\varnothing
or
\{1\}
). The system attempts entanglement generation with parameters
(p_{\varnothing},F_{\varnothing})
until a link is successfully generated.

(B)

The newly generated link survives in memory for
t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(F_{\varnothing})
time steps, until it is discarded. During the first
t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(F_{\varnothing})-1
time steps when the link is in memory, the system attempts entanglement generation with parameters
(p_{\max},F_{\min})
.
If at least one of these attempts is successful, the process is completed.
If none of these attempts are successful, then in the final time step, the state is
\{1\}
and the system returns to phase (A).

The expected completion time may then be computed using exactly the same method as the one used in Section III-B of
[
17
]
. The method makes use of properties of the geometric distribution. We obtain

\mathbb{E}[T_{\pi^{*}}]=\frac{1}{p_{\max}}+\frac{1}{p_{\varnothing}\left(1-(1-p_{\max})^{t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(F_{\varnothing})-1}\right)},

(31)

and the solution for
\pi^{*}(\varnothing)
and
\pi^{*}(\{1\})
is therefore given by

(p_{\varnothing},F_{\varnothing})=\underset{(p,F)\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\left\{\frac{1}{p\left(1-(1-p_{\max})^{t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(F)-1}\right)}\right\}.

(32)

We have now fully defined the optimal policy
\pi^{*}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, and this completes the analytical solution. We have also derived a formula for its performance in (
31
).

The analysis for
n=2
offers valuable insights into the expected behaviour of the optimal policy for
n>2
.
As we extend to cases with larger
n
, we anticipate that the optimal policy will continue to select the action
(p_{\max},F_{\min})
for states with
n-1
viable links in memory. For other states, the action chosen by the optimal policy must correctly balance the probability of generation
p
with the time-to-live
t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(F)
. This is because a link must be generated quickly, but there must also be sufficient time for the remaining links to be generated while that link is in memory. For
n=2
, the correct balance is captured by (
32
).

IV-B

Heuristic policy

The size of the state space
|\mathcal{S}|
scales exponentially with
n
, meaning that for large
n
computing optimal policies with policy iteration (
subsection III-B
) is intractable. See Appendix
A
for a detailed analysis of the scaling. With our setup, we are only able to compute optimal policies for
n\leq 7
in the parameter regimes that we explore in Section
IV-C
. Here, we propose a heuristic policy
\pi_{\mathrm{h}}
that is efficient to compute for large
n
and that performs close to optimally for
n\leq 7
(see Section
IV-C
). One can therefore expect
\pi_{\mathrm{h}}
to exhibit high performance for
n>7
.

Recalling the notion of a viable link that was introduced in
subsection IV-A
, we define the function

N_{v}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}

such that
N_{v}(s)
outputs the number of viable links in state
s
. For example, recalling the discussion in
subsection IV-A
, we have
N_{v}(\varnothing)=N_{v}(\{1\})=0
. More generally, given the state
s=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\}
, the
m
-th link with TTL
t_{m}
is viable if there is a chance of completing the process (i.e. generating
n-m
remaining links) before the link expires. Therefore, the link is viable if
t_{m}>n-m
. Recall from Section
III-A1
that the labelling of the TTLs is assumed to be decreasing, i.e.
t_{i}\geq t_{j}
for
i<j
. If the
m
-th link is viable, then for all
k\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}
, we therefore have
t_{k}>n-m
and all other links are viable. The total number of viable links in the state
s
is given by

N_{v}(s)\coloneqq\max\{j:t_{j}>n-j\}.

(33)

Given the state
s=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\}
, we define a second function
v:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
such that

v(s)=\begin{cases}\{t_{1},\dots,t_{N_{v}(s)}\}&\text{ if }N_{v}(s)\geq 1\\
\varnothing,&\text{ if }N_{v}(s)=0.\end{cases}

(34)

The state
v(s)
contains the viable links of
s
. Identifying
{s\equiv v(s)}
can be used to reduce the size of the state space — see Appendix
A
for more details.

We now define the heuristic policy
\pi_{\mathrm{h}}
. As we did in Section
IV-A
for the case
n=2
, we set the actions for states with zero viable links to an arbitary value,

\pi_{\mathrm{h}}(s)\coloneqq(p_{\varnothing},F_{\varnothing})\in\mathcal{A}\text{, if }N_{v}(s)=0.

(35)

Further, we use the intuition established in Section
IV-A
and enforce that the policy must choose the maximum-probability action (
28
) when there are
n-1
viable links in memory,

\pi_{\mathrm{h}}(s)\coloneqq(p_{\max},F_{\min}),\text{ if }N_{v}(s)=n-1.

(36)

We now consider states
s
such that
0<N_{v}(s)<n-1
.
For such states, the heuristic policy chooses the highest-probability action that generates a link TTL greater than or equal to the smallest TTL of a viable link in memory. Explicitly, we set

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{h}}(s)\coloneqq\underset{(p,F)\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathrm{argmax}}\big\{p:\;t_{\mathrm{TTL}}

\displaystyle(F)\geq t_{N_{v}(s)}-1\big\},

(37)

\displaystyle\text{if }0<N_{v}(s)<n-1.

In particular, we see that the action taken in a given state
s
is only dependent on its viable links
v(s)
,

\pi_{\mathrm{h}}(s)=\pi_{\mathrm{h}}(v(s))\text{ for all }s\in\mathcal{S}.

(38)

In (
36
) and (
37
), we have now fixed all actions apart from
(p_{\varnothing},F_{\varnothing})
, from (
35
). The choice of
(p_{\varnothing},F_{\varnothing})
may be fixed by either policy evaluation or performing a Monte Carlo simulation of the system while employing
\pi_{\mathrm{h}}
with each
(p_{\varnothing},F_{\varnothing})\in\mathcal{A}
. Then, one may select the value that minimises
\mathbb{E}[T_{\pi_{\mathrm{h}}}]
. This step involves a maximum of
|\mathcal{A}|
policy evaluation steps (either carried out exactly by solving the Bellman equations (
24
), or approximately by performing a Monte Carlo simulation). The complexity of policy evaluation scales exponentially with
n
, but in practice the possibility to evaluate performance with simulation allows for the computation of the heuristic and its performance for regimes with larger state spaces than the optimal policy (see Section
IV
).

The policy
\pi_{\mathrm{h}}
is particularly simple in the case where, for any
t\in\{1,\dots,t_{\max}\}
, there exists
(p,F)\in\mathcal{A}
such that
t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(F)=t
. In other words, in
\mathcal{A}
there are generation parameters that can produce a link with any
\mathrm{TTL}
. In such a case, (
37
) is simply a
matching heuristic
, which ensures that all viable links have the same
\mathrm{TTL}
, while maximising the success probability as much as possible.

The intuition behind our heuristic policy is as follows. With (
37
) the heuristic policy ensures that, when at least one viable link in memory will soon expire (small TTL), the heuristic policy tries to quickly generate all links within the remaining time. On the other hand, if all links in memory have a large TTL, the heuristic policy tries to generate similarly long-lasting links. This is a property we will also see in
section IV
in the structure of optimal policies: it is a trend that, as the links in memory decohere, the optimal policies prioritise higher-probability generation parameters. In fact, for one of the two parameter regimes considered, we see that the heuristic policy is in fact optimal.

\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{ran}}(S_{t})=(p,F)

\displaystyle\text{ for all }(p,F)\in\mathcal{A},\;\;t\geq 0.

IV-C
2
Parameter regimes

In the performance evaluation, we consider generation parameters that correspond to batched executions of the single-click protocol.
When photon loss is high, one execution of the single-click protocol has a very low success probability of generating entanglement
[
11
]
. Therefore, it can be beneficial for a single entanglement generation attempt to consist of
M
executions of the single-click protocol, which increases the probability of success while minimising overhead due to communication with higher layers of the software stack
[
28
]
. We also refer to these as
batched attempts
. The attempt is declared successful if at least one of the
M
single-click executions succeeds.
In Appendix
B
, we show that for batched single-click attempts, the trade-off between
p
and
F
is accurately approximated as

F=\lambda\ln(1-p)+1.

(41)

Here,
\lambda=1/(2p_{\mathrm{det}}M)
is a fixed parameter that depends on the probability of detecting an emitted photon
p_{\mathrm{det}}
and the batch size
M
.
The relation (
41
) holds when
M\sim p_{\mathrm{det}}^{-1}
is large.
The trade-off (
41
) is valid for a sufficiently small range
p\in(0,q]
. Given that all newly generated links must have fidelity
F
such that
F>F_{\mathrm{app}}
, by (
41
) the maximum success probability
q
satisfies

q<1-e^{\frac{F_{\mathrm{app}}-1}{\lambda}}.

(42)

Noting that (
41
) enables a continuous choice of
(p,F)
, we discretise the action space as follows. The maximum TTL of a newly generated link given the trade-off (
41
) is given by

t_{\max}=\lim_{p\rightarrow 0}t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(\lambda\ln(1-p)+1)=t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(1).

(43)

Similarly, we let

t_{\min}=t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(\lambda\ln(1-q)+1)

(44)

be the minimum TTL of a newly generated link.
Then, for each
i\in\{t_{\min},\dots,t_{\max}\}
, we define
p_{i}
as the maximum probability with which one can generate a link with TTL
i
,

p_{i}=\max\{p:t_{\mathrm{TTL}}(\lambda\ln(1-p)+1)=i\}.

(45)

We then work with the finite action space

\mathcal{A}=\left\{(p_{i},\lambda\ln(1-p_i)+1):i\in\{t_{\min},\dots,t_{\max}\}\right\}.

(46)

We now compare the performance of our optimal and heuristic policies to our baselines in two parameter regimes. The first parameter regime has a high decoherence rate
{\Gamma=0.19}

\Gamma=0.1

F_{\mathrm{app}}=1/2

The batch size in current experiments is

V
Conclusion and future work

We have considered a scenario where entanglement generation attempts are sequential in time and the generation parameters may be chosen in each time step.
By formulating the problem as an MDP, we have found policies that minimise the expected time to generate multiple simultaneously-existing links. In the parameter regimes explored, we have seen that our optimal policies provide a significant improvement over the constant-action and random-action baselines.
We have also found a heuristic method to compute policies that exhibit either optimal or close-to-optimal performance in all parameter regimes explored. The heuristic method is more efficient than finding optimal policies with dynamic programming, and we therefore expect it to be useful in situations where the optimal policy cannot be computed due to the scaling of the state space (e.g. when the number of required links
n
is large). Our work highlights that adaptive protocols leveraging the rate-fidelity trade-off inherent to the entanglement generation scheme can be extremely helpful in improving quantum network performance. Moreover, in certain important entanglement generation schemes, the rate-fidelity trade-off is easily tunable (for example, by varying the bright-state parameter or mean photon number
[
11
,
13
]
), and so our adaptive protocols are readily implementable.

Our model does not assume a specific entanglement generation protocol. In our results, we have used the example of a batched single-click protocol. It would also be interesting to study optimal policies for different entanglement generation schemes. A different entanglement generation scheme may result in a different action space, and therefore optimal policies with distinct properties to the batched single-click case.

For parameter regimes in which finding the optimal policy is not feasible with dynamic programming, a fruitful direction of research may be deep reinforcement learning, which finds approximate solutions.

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}|

(50)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{t_{\mathrm{max}}}{m}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)

(51)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}{t_{\textrm{max}}+m-1\choose m},

(52)

, in the second step we have used (
49
), and in the last step we have used the formula

\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)={n+k-1\choose k}.

(53)

We can further simplify (
52
) using the hockey-stick identity
[
32
]
,

\sum_{j=0}^{w-r}\binom{j+r}{r}=\binom{w+1}{w-r}

(54)

for
w\geq r
. Then,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\binom{t_{\textrm{max}}-1+m}{m}

(55)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\binom{t_{\textrm{max}}-1+m}{t_{\textrm{max}}-1}

(56)

\displaystyle=\binom{t_{\textrm{max}}+n-1}{n-1}.

(57)

We can then bound the state space size as follows,

\displaystyle{t_{\textrm{max}}+n-1\choose n-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{n-2}\frac{t_{\textrm{max}}+n-1-i}{n-1-i}

(58)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{i=0}^{n-2}\frac{t_{\textrm{max}}+(n-1)}{n-1}

(59)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{t_{\textrm{max}}}{n-1}\right)^{n-1}.

(60)

To obtain (
59
), we have used the fact that

y=\frac{A-x}{B-x}

(61)

is an increasing function of
x
, for
A>B
.

By definition of our problem, we have
t_{\textrm{max}}\geq n-1
. Then, by (
60
) we see that
|\mathcal{S}|\geq 2^{n-1}
, i.e. the size of the state space scales exponentially with
n
(within the feasible region
n\leq t_{\textrm{max}}
).

We now quantify the reduction in the size of the state space that may be obtained by identifying states only with their viable links. Recall the function
N_{v}
that counts the number of viable links, defined in (
33
). We also recall the function
v
, such that
v(s)
only contains the viable links of
s
, defined in (
34
). We define the reduced state space
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{red}}\subset\mathcal{S}
by identifying
s\equiv v(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Then,

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{red}}=\{s\in\mathcal{S}:v(s)=s\}.

(62)

We now compute
|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{red}}|
. For
m=0,\dots,n-1
, we define

\mathcal{S}_{m,\mathrm{red}}\coloneqq\{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{red}}:N_{v}(s)=m\}

(63)

where we note that for
s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{red}}
, we simply have
|s|=N_{v}(s)
, or equivalently all links are viable.
The set
\mathcal{S}_{m,\mathrm{red}}
is interpreted as the set of states containing exactly
m
viable links.
We note that
\mathcal{S}_{0,\mathrm{red}}=\{\varnothing\}
. We also note that
\mathcal{S}_{m,\mathrm{red}}\subset\mathcal{S}_{m}
.
We then have

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{red}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\bigcup_{m=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{S}_{m,\mathrm{red}}\right|

(64)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\left|\mathcal{S}_{m,\mathrm{red}}\right|,

(65)

where in the second step we have used the fact that
\mathcal{S}_{i,\mathrm{red}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{j,\mathrm{red}}
are mutually exclusive for
i\neq j
. We now compute
|\mathcal{S}_{m,\mathrm{red}}|
. Recalling from Section
IV-B
that the state
s=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\}
contains
m
viable links when
t_{m}>n-m
, it follows that

\mathcal{S}_{m,\mathrm{red}}\\
=\{\{t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\}:t_{i}\in[t_{\mathrm{max}}]\setminus[n-m],t_{1}\geq\dots\geq t_{m}\}.

(66)

Then, we deduce
|\mathcal{S}_{m,\mathrm{red}}|
by the same counting argument as was used above for
|\mathcal{S}_{m}|
, to find

|\mathcal{S}_{m,\mathrm{red}}|=\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{t_{\mathrm{max}} + m - n}{m}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right).

(67)

Combining with (
65
), we then have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{red}}|

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{t_{\mathrm{max}} + m - n }{m}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)

(68)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}{t_{\textrm{max}}+2m-n-1\choose m},

(69)

where we again made use of (
53
).

Appendix B
Trade-off relation for batched single-click scheme

Here, we derive a trade-off relation between
p
and
F
for batched attempts of the single-click entanglement generation protocol
[
7
]
.
In the limit of high photon losses (
\eta\ll 1
), if the success probability of a single execution of the single-click protocol is
p_{\mathrm{succ}}
, the fidelity of a generated link is

F=1-\frac{p_{\mathrm{succ}}}{2p_{\mathrm{det}}},

(70)

where
p_{\mathrm{det}}
is the probability that an emitted photon is detected
[
33
]
. When
p_{\mathrm{succ}}\ll 1
, as is typically the case for NV centres, it can be beneficial to perform attempts in batches
[
28
]
in order to avoid overhead in the software stack (the outcome of each attempt must be communicated to higher layers of the software stack). We now consider the case where one entanglement generation attempt corresponds to
M
executions of the single-click protocol. If at least one of the
M
execution succeeds, then the entanglement generation attempt is successful. The success probability of an individual attempt is therefore given by

p=1-(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})^{M}.

(71)

Letting
\lambda\coloneqq 1/(2p_{\mathrm{det}}M)
, from (
70
) we have that

p=1-\left(1-\frac{(1-F)}{\lambda M}\right)^{M}.

(72)

Then, using the fact that
\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}(1+\frac{x}{K})^{K}=e^{x}
, when
M\sim p_{\mathrm{det}}^{-1}
is large the above is approximated as

p\approx 1-e^{-\frac{1-F}{\lambda}},

(73)

which results in the trade-off relation

F\approx 1+\lambda\ln(1-p).

(74)

The relation (
74
) is used in the illustration of our results in Section
IV-C
. The trade-off relation
F\approx 1-\lambda p
that was found in
[
17
]
can be seen as the first-order approximation of (
74
) at
p=0
.

We now show that (
74
) is an accurate approximation in the regime where
M\sim p_{\mathrm{det}}^{-1}
is large.
To bound the error of the approximation, we use the following inequality:

\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)^{y-1}>\frac{1}{e}>\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)^{y},\;\;\;\text{ for }y>1.

(75)

The proof of (
75
) is given in Appendix
C
. We recall that the true trade-off is given by

\tilde{p}=1-\left(1-\frac{(1-F)}{\lambda M}\right)^{M}

(76)

and the approximate trade-off

p=1-e^{-\frac{1-F}{\lambda}}.

(77)

We firstly claim that
p<\tilde{p}
. Letting
r=(1-F)/\lambda
and
y=M/r
, we have
r>0
. Then,

\displaystyle\tilde{p}-p

\displaystyle=e^{-r}-\left(1-\frac{r}{M}\right)^{M}

(78)

\displaystyle=e^{-r}-\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)^{yr}>0,

(79)

where in the final step we have made use of the upper bound from (
75
). We now bound the difference
|p-\tilde{p}|
. We have

\displaystyle|\tilde{p}-p|

\displaystyle=e^{-r}-\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)^{yr}

(80)

\displaystyle<e^{-r}-\left(e^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)\right)^{r}

(81)

\displaystyle=e^{-r}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)^{r}\right)

(82)

\displaystyle<1-\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)^{r},

(83)

where in the second inequality we have used the lower bound from (
75
), and in the final inequality we have used
e^{-r}<1
(since
r>0
). In particular, making use of the power series expansion, we see that

|\tilde{p}-p|\approx\frac{r}{y}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{M}\right),

(84)

i.e. as long as
M\sim p_{\mathrm{det}}^{-1}
is large, our approximation remains accurate.

Appendix C
Proof of (
75
)

To prove the left inequality of (
75
), we firstly take the logarithm of both sides, to obtain

(y-1)\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)>-1,\text{ for }y>1.

(85)

For ease of notation, we let

f(y)\coloneqq(y-1)\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right).

(86)

To prove (
85
), we firstly claim that

\lim_{y\rightarrow\infty}f(y)=-1.

(87)

Letting
z\coloneqq 1/y
, (
87
) is equivalent to

\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\left(\frac{1-z}{z}\right)\ln\left(1-z\right)=-1,

(88)

which one may verify with L’Hôpital’s rule: differentiating both the numerator and denominator,

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\frac{(1-z)\ln\left(1-z\right)}{z}

\displaystyle=\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\frac{-\ln\left(1-z\right)-1}{1}

(89)

\displaystyle=-1.

(90)

Having shown (
87
), to prove (
85
) it suffices to show that the function
f(y)
is decreasing. We have

\displaystyle\derivative{f}{y}

\displaystyle=\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)+(y-1)\frac{\frac{1}{y^{2}}}{1-\frac{1}{y}}

(91)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)+\frac{1}{y}.

(92)

Since

\ln(1+z)<z\text{ for }z>-1,

(93)

by letting
y=-1/z
it follows from the above that
\derivative{f}{y}<0
, which suffices to prove the left inequality of (
75
).

We now show the right inequality of (
75
), which taking the logarithm of both sides is equivalent to

-\frac{1}{y}>\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right),\text{ for }y>1.

(94)

Again, letting
y=-1/z
, by (
93
) this holds.
