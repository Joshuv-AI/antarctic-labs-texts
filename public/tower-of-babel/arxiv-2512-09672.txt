Pattern Based Quantum Key Distribution using the five qubit perfect code for eavesdropper detection
Mehedi Hasan Rumi

Abstract
I propose a new quantum key distribution protocol that uses the five qubit error correction code to detect the presence of eavesdropper reliably. The protocol turns any information theoretical attacks into a classical guess about the pattern. The logical qubit is encoded with a specific pattern into a block of five physical qubits. The security of the protocol relies on the correct pattern choice of Alice and Bob. Decoding with any wrong pattern choice increases multi qubit error rate and the 5 qubit code transforms an eavesdropper’s logical disturbance into a signature that is detectable and distinguishable from natural channel noise up to a certain distance.



I
Introduction

Quantum key distribution [1] is a communication method used to distribute keys between two parties, Alice and Bob. There are several types of attack that an eavesdropper can perform during transmission to gain the information about Alice’s bit. Introducing a new security mechanism so that any meaningful strategy to learn Alice’s bit reduces to a classical guess makes the communication very resilient against security threat. The five qubit perfect code has characteristics that can achieve that. [2,3] The encoded state hides the logical qubit because the ensembles are identical, but the correct, key-dependent decoding operation recovers the logical qubit deterministically. And it ensures that no information leakage occurs without decoding the state. So an eavesdropper has to guess the correct pattern of the encoded state out of 120 possible patterns. So, randomly guessing the correct pattern of the encoded state is also difficult to achieve. Alice and Bob agrees beforehand to use only computational basis or hadamard basis for their communication. They agree with a specific 5 qubit code circuit and a pattern set with two unique elements out of 120 possible patterns for their communication. The protocol is conceptually like BB84 protocol. But instead of basis ambiguity, the protocol uses encoded pattern ambiguity. For Eve to reduce the protocol into a BB84 protocol, she has to learn the Alice and Bob’s pre agreed basis choice, the correct circuit operation of their system and the pre agreed pattern set. Even if Eve manages to learn them, her decoding attempt to learn Alice’s logical qubit is equivalent to intercept resend attack in BB84 protocol.

II
Protocol Description

The protocol is built conceptually on the principles of BB84 scheme but introduces a new security mechanism. Instead of making the arbitrary qubit in any of the 4 states:
\ket{0},\ket{1},\ket{+},\ket{-}

\ket{0}
or
\ket{1}
(or 2 states
\ket{+}
or
\ket{-}

The patterns are chosen to have at least three positions that differ from one another. Alice and Bob agree beforehand on a small secret set S of two distinct 5 qubit encoding patterns (permutation of positions
\{1,2,3,4,5\}
) , where the secret set, S =
\{P0,P1\}
. And the set of all permutations is defined by P. So S
\subset
P. Each pattern is a fixed ordering that determines how the 1 logical qubit is mapped onto the five physical qubits (i.e., the permutation that defines the layout/decoding order). The two patterns in S are chosen so that they differ in at least 3 positions. This ensures they are sufficiently distinct. For each transmission block, Alice chooses a classical bit, a
\in\{0,1\}
randomly and picks a pattern p uniformly in S. She encodes the logical qubit into 5 physical qubits and sends the encoded state through the quantum channel to Bob. Bob picks q uniformly in S to decode. He records his measurement results and the syndrome bits. Alice and Bob reveal over the authenticated classical channel which pattern index they used for that block. Alice reveals the index, Bob reveals whether his chosen index matched. They keep Only those bits where Bob used the same pattern as Alice. From the sifted bits, they take a random subset to estimate the multi qubit error rate, M (MQER). When the bits from the subset mismatch, the syndrome bit corresponding to the mismatched bit is treated as multi qubit error. If the MQER is below a threshold, they continue with classical error correction and privacy amplification. Otherwise, they abort. Here, if Alice and Bob sees that the multi qubit error rate is around 50% or above, it tells them that Eve has no idea about their pattern set. However, if the multi qubit error rate is much below 50% but above the threshold, Alice and Bob can also learn that their pre agreed pattern set has been compromised.

Alice and Bob’s secret set S contains exactly 2 patterns and Eve doesn’t know S. So with this assumption, Bob knows S but not Alice’s per block choice p
\in
S. Bob selects q
\in
S uniformly at random. So, Bob’s probability of choosing the correct pattern for any block,
P_{B}
= 1/2. Eve with no knowledge of S, has to guess a permutation uniformly at random from 120 possibilities. The probability that she guesses the correct pattern set,
P_{E}
= 1/6540
(^{120}C_{1}\times^{109}C_{1}=6540)
. If she does not guess the exact permutation, her decoding operation is applied in the wrong order and will produce a logical outcome uncorrelated with Alice’s bit – effectively random. Worst case scenario: If Eve somehow learns the two patterns in S, the protocol looks like a BB84 protocol. In this scenario, she can restrict her guesses to the two patterns. Even if she learns the pair of secret patterns,
S=\{P0,P1\}
the protocol retains a strict advantage over plain BB84. Knowledge of S doesn’t remove the per block ambiguity. Alice still randomly selects one of the two patterns for each block, which forces Eve to guess the used pattern on every block with probability at 1/2. So any attempt to learn the information looks like Intercept resend attack.

No classical information about the logical qubit can be extracted from the quantum state.
Although the logical codewords
\ket{0_{L}}
and
\ket{1_{L}}
are orthogonal and hence distinguishable in principle, an observer lacking knowledge of the physical encoding pattern perceives both logical values as identical ensemble states. Indistinguishability arises only from classical uncertainty over the encoding pattern.
Consequently, the total density matrices corresponding to logical 0 and logical 1 are equal,
\rho_{0}=\rho_{1}
.
Since Eve’s state is fully characterized by the density matrix, neither the specific encoding pattern nor the gate history constitutes a physically observable quantity. Thus, despite the fact that the patterns
P0
and
P1
are orthogonal and distinguishable in principle, the physical states corresponding to logical values a= 0 and a=1 are indistinguishable from Eve’s perspective.
It follows that the Holevo quantity vanishes,
\chi=0
and the logical variable is information-theoretically inaccessible to any observer lacking knowledge of the encoding pattern. In particular, any measurement performed on the physical system—even globally on all five qubits—cannot yield outcome statistics dependent on the logical value. Furthermore, any fixed unitary operation acting on the physical qubits and independent of the unknown encoding pattern preserves the indistinguishability of the states. This conclusion does not apply to decoding operations conditioned on classical pattern information, which lie outside the scope of operations acting solely on the ensemble-averaged state. In particular, a decoding operation assuming the wrong pattern is equivalent to a unitary applied without pattern knowledge and therefore cannot extract logical information; its output remains fully mixed on each physical qubit.

V
Intuitive Observation on Misapplied Error Correction

As an intuitive observation, the five-qubit code is specifically constructed to correct single-qubit errors relative to a fixed code space. An observer who lacks knowledge of the physical encoding pattern does not have access to the correct stabilizer structure or syndrome map. Consequently, any attempt to perform error correction based on an incorrect encoding results in recovery operations that do not correspond to the actual error structure of the state. Such misapplied error correction acts effectively as a multi-qubit error on the encoded state, rather than correcting it, further limiting an adversary’s ability to extract information. [7]

VI
Implementation-Level PNS Attack Considerations

Photon‑number splitting (PNS) attacks arise in photonic implementations when multiple photons carry the same quantum information. The relevance of such attacks depends entirely on the physical realization of the protocol.

Quantum Transducer Implementation:
In principle employing a quantum transducer, each physical qubit is deterministically converted into a single photon. As a result, multi‑photon pulses are physically forbidden, rendering PNS attacks impossible. This approach removes the need for weak coherent pulses and avoids Poissonian photon‑number statistics altogether. In realistic implementations, occasional multi-photon events are possible but extremely rare and can be suppressed with current technology. [8,9]
Due to the code’s distance-three structure [3], at least three photons from the same logical block are required for an adversary to gain any information. Combined with the near-deterministic single-photon emission of quantum transducers, this ensures that multi-photon events capable of leaking information are extremely rare.
Moreover, deterministic two‑qubit gates such as CNOT operations are generally challenging to implement with high fidelity in purely photonic architectures. In contrast, quantum transducer–based implementations allow the encoding and decoding operations to be performed in matter‑based platforms where deterministic entangling gates are available, making this approach both secure against PNS attacks and practical for realizing the proposed protocol.

Direct Photonic Encoding:
If the five‑qubit logical code is encoded directly into photons using weak coherent pulses, the photon number in each pulse follows Poissonian statistics, we can figure out the probability of getting photons using Poisson distribution:
P(n)=\frac{\mu^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-\mu}}{n!}
where
\mu=
mean photon number per pulse

Here, multi‑photon pulses may occur. In this case, PNS attacks are possible in principle. However, as before, due to the distance‑three structure of the five‑qubit code, [3] Eve must obtain at least three photons from the same logical block to gain any information about the encoded logical qubit. Intercepting fewer than three photons yields no information, as the corresponding reduced state remains independent of the logical value.
Consequently, even in direct photonic implementations, the protocol substantially suppresses Eve’s information gain, since successful PNS attacks require correlated access to multiple photons within a single logical block.

In summary, quantum transducer implementations makes the possibility of PNS attacks extremely rare, while direct photonic implementations retain strong resistance due to the code’s distance‑three structure. The requirement that Eve obtain at least three photons from the same logical block significantly constrains her ability to extract information.

VII
Conclusion

The protocol introduced in this work provides robust security during transmission. The five-qubit error-correction code not only corrects the dominant single-qubit errors but also serves as an intrinsic detector of adversarial intervention. If an eavesdropper cannot correctly guess the encoding pattern set, her presence introduces logical errors that manifest as multi-qubit errors, with a rate approaching 50% in a nearly deterministic manner. In the event that the eavesdropper succeeds in learning the correct pattern set, the multi-qubit error rate is reduced but may still exceed the error threshold, provided that the intrinsic noise of the channel is sufficiently low. In such cases, the pattern set can be updated to maintain secure communication.
