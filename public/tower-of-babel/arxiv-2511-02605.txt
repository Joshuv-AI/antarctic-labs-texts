Adaptive GR(1) Specification Repair for Liveness-Preserving Shielding in Reinforcement Learning Conference: Proc. of the 25th International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (AAMAS 2026); May 25 – 29, 2026; Paphos, Cyprus #434
Tiberiu-Andrei Georgescu ∗, Alexander W. Goodall ∗, Dalal Alrajeh, Francesco Belardinelli, Sebastian Uchitel

Abstract
. Shielding is widely used to enforce safety in reinforcement learning (RL), ensuring that an agent’s actions remain compliant with formal specifications. Classical shielding approaches, however, are often static, in the sense that they assume fixed logical specifications and hand-crafted abstractions. While these static shields provide safety under nominal assumptions, they fail to adapt when environment assumptions are violated. In this paper, we develop the first adaptive shielding framework – to the best of our knowledge – based on Generalized Reactivity of rank 1 (GR(1)) specifications, a tractable and expressive fragment of Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) that captures both safety and liveness properties. Our method detects environment assumption violations at runtime and employs Inductive Logic Programming (ILP) ( Muggleton 1991 ) to automatically repair GR(1) specifications online, in a systematic and interpretable way. This ensures that the shield evolves gracefully, ensuring liveness is achievable and weakening goals only when necessary. We consider two case studies: Minepump and Atari Seaquest; showing that (i) static symbolic controllers are often severely suboptimal when optimizing for auxiliary rewards, and (ii) RL agents equipped with our adaptive shield maintain near-optimal reward and perfect logical compliance compared with static shields.

1.
Introduction

Recent advances in deep learning
(
LeCun et al. 2015
)
and reinforcement learning (RL)
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
)
have enabled highly optimized autonomous systems in diverse domains, including self-adapting robots
(
Koos et al. 2013
;
Chatzilygeroudis et al. 2018
;
Bongard et al. 2006
)
, autonomous vehicles
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
, and coordinated multi-robot teams for search-and-rescue
(
Petrlik et al. 2022
)
. In such safety-critical scenarios, providing strong safety assurances is imperative.
Interpretability
is also a crucial part of enabling trust in autonomous systems, in for example, diagnosing model misspecification
(
Camilli et al. 2022
)
.

A promising approach for enforcing logical constraints and safety in RL is
shielding

(
Alshiekh et al. 2018
)
, which typically involves synthesizing a runtime monitor, the shield, that overrides the agent’s actions, only allowing those that keep the system within a safe subset of the state space
(
Bloem et al. 2015
;
Alshiekh et al. 2018
)
. If the agent attempts an action that would violate the safety constraint, the shield can override it with a safe alternative, thereby preventing any unsafe outcomes. This stands in contrast with many other safe RL approaches (e.g., constrained optimization of policies
(
Achiam et al. 2017
;
Ray et al. 2019
)
or reward penalties for unsafe behaviour
(
Thomas et al. 2021
)
), which cannot guarantee absolute safety and instead aim to reduce the probability of unsafe behaviour.
Classical shielding approaches typically rely on a safety abstraction of the environment (a formal model of relevant state dynamics), enabling the synthesis of a correct-by-construction controller that ensures the system remains within a corresponding winning region – the set of allowable states and actions satisfying the safety property
(
Bloem et al. 2015
)
. Crucially, shields are designed to exercise minimal interference with the agent’s behaviour
(
Alshiekh et al. 2018
)
, only overriding actions that would lead to safety violations, leaving control to the RL agent otherwise.

However, a fundamental limitation of existing shielding methods for RL is the assumption that the environment’s behaviour conforms to the design-time model. In many works on safe RL with shielding
(
Alshiekh et al. 2018
;
Bloem et al. 2020
;
Könighofer et al. 2023
;
Jansen et al. 2020
)
, the safety-relevant dynamics of the environment are handcrafted and assumed
a priori
. The resulting shield is synthesized under these assumptions and guarantees safety only as long as the environment behaves consistently with them. If the actual environment violates the assumed dynamics, due to model misspecification or unexpected changes, the shield’s guarantees may no longer hold. Worse still, some approaches do not make any assumptions explicit, nor do they check whether the specification is realizable under realistic environmental behaviour. In such cases, the assumptions are not eliminated but rather become implicit, as in, it would be impossible to achieve all system guarantees unless the behaviour of the environment follows certain rules that were not specified. When those hidden assumptions fail, the shield may become overly conservative, unnecessarily blocking safe but beneficial behaviour, or even compromise liveness guarantees by preventing the agent from completing its tasks
(
Könighofer et al. 2017
;
Könighofer et al. 2020
)
. These limitations expose a broader challenge: designing shielding frameworks that not only guarantee safety but also make assumptions explicit and support adaptive repair of their underlying specifications, preserving safety and liveness in the presence of unexpected environment behaviour.

In this paper, we address these critical limitations, by proposing the first framework for adaptive shielding, which to the best of our knowledge, (i) makes explicit the environment assumptions that must hold for the correctness of the shield, by using a declarative form (LTL/GR(1))
(
Bloem et al. 2012
)
; (ii) synthesizes an initial shield (implementing the winning-region) based on the system’s desired behaviour (guarantees) and the expected behaviour of the environment (assumptions); and (iii) automatically repairs the specification when assumption violations are observed at runtime; upon detecting a violation, our framework employs ILP to adapt the GR(1) specification to the new environment dynamics, and a new shield controller is synthesized on-the-fly. Since GR(1) synthesis is polynomial-time (unlike full LTL synthesis, which is double-exponential
(
Pnueli and Rosner 1989
)
), these adaptations can be performed online during deployment. Importantly, because specification repair is logic-based and expressed in GR(1), the shield’s evolution is transparent: the system provides human-readable explanations of how and why the safety rules were modified. This combination of adaptability and interpretability enhances trustworthiness, aligning with recent efforts in explainable and self-explainable adaptive systems
(
Hussain et al. 2021
;
Khalid and Qureshi 2021
)
.

2.
Background

2.1.
Reinforcement Learning

We model agent-environment interaction via the
Markov Decision Process
(MDP). Formally, an MDP is
a tuple
M=(S,s_{0},A,P,R,\gamma)
, where
S
is a finite set of states, with
initial state

s_{0}
,
A
is a finite set of actions,
P:S\times A\to\Delta(S)
is the probabilistic transition function, with
\Delta(S)
denoting the set of distributions over
S
,
R:S\times A\to\mathbb{R}
is the reward function, and
\gamma
is the discount factor. The agent’s policy
\pi:S\to\Delta(A)
is a probabilistic function mapping states to action distributions, with the goal of learning the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
, that maximizes the
expected discounted cumulative reward
:
V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(S_{t},A_{t})\mid S_{0}=s\right]
, also called the value function. The Q-function, similarly defines the policy’s expected discounted cumulative reward, from state
s
taking action
a
,
Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(S_{t},A_{t})\mid S_{0}=s,A_{0}=a\right]
. Q-learning algorithms such as
deep Q-network
(DQN)
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
, iteratively approximate and converge to the optimal Q-function,
Q^{*}=\max_{\pi}Q^{\pi}
, thus extracting the optimal policy via,
\pi^{*}:=\arg\max_{a}Q^{*}(s,a)
. In contrast, policy-gradient methods, e.g.,
Proximal Policy Optimization
(PPO)
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
, approximate the value function
V^{\pi}
of the policy and iteratively update
\pi
in the direction of the policy-gradient.

2.2.
Linear Temporal Logic

Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)
(
Pnueli 1977
)
is a formal language for specifying dynamics properties over reactive systems.
Over a finite set of atomic propositions
AP
, LTL formulas are generated by the following syntax,

\varphi\;::=\;\top\mid\bot\mid p\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid X\,\varphi\mid\varphi\;U\;\varphi

where
p\in AP
is an
atomic proposition
, either True (
\top
) or False (
\bot
) in a given stat, negation (
\neg
) and conjunction (
\land
) are the familiar propositional operators, next (
X
) and (strong) until (
U
) are the temporal operators.
As usual, disjunction,
\varphi\lor\psi\equiv\neg(\neg\varphi\land\neg\psi)
, implies,
\varphi\to\psi\equiv\neg\varphi\lor\psi
, eventually (finally),
F\,\varphi\equiv\top U\varphi
, and always (globally),
G\,\varphi\equiv\neg F\,\neg\varphi
can be derived in the usual way from the grammar above.

LTL allows us to write properties about sequences of states (or traces) and constrains the infinite execution trace of a system, Let
\tau=s_{0}s_{1}s_{2}\ldots
be an infinite word over
2^{AP}
and let
(\tau,i)\models\varphi
denote satisfaction at position
i\geq 0
. Then the semantics of LTL formulas is given as follows:

\displaystyle(\tau,i)

\displaystyle\models p

iff

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ p\in s_{i}\;\text{(for $p\in AP$)}

\displaystyle(\tau,i)

\displaystyle\models\neg\varphi

iff

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \neg(\tau,i)\models\varphi

\displaystyle(\tau,i)

\displaystyle\models\varphi\land\psi

iff

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ (\tau,i)\models\varphi\land(\tau,i)\models\psi

\displaystyle(\tau,i)

\displaystyle\models X\,\varphi

iff

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ (\tau,i{+}1)\models\varphi

\displaystyle(\tau,i)

\displaystyle\models\varphi\,U\,\psi

iff

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \exists k\geq i.\;(\tau,k)\models\psi\;\land\;\forall j\in[i,k)\!:\;(\tau,j)\models\varphi

For notational convenience we also consider the following past-time operators:
yesterday

Y
expresses that a formula held in the previous state, and the
historically
operator
H
expresses that a formula has always held in the past. Formally, their semantics are defined as,
(\tau,i)\models Y\,\varphi\;\text{iff}\;(i>0)\land(\tau,i{-}1)\models\varphi
and
(\tau,i)\models H\,\varphi\;\text{iff}\;\forall j<i.\;\pi,j\models\varphi
.

While full LTL is an expressive language (e.g., safety, liveness), controller synthesis from arbitrary LTL formula is doubly-exponential in the size
|\varphi|
of the formula. For practical synthesis, researchers often focus on a fragment of LTL.
In this paper we consider Generalized Reactivity of rank 1 (GR(1))
(
Bloem et al. 2012
)
.

2.3.
GR(1) Synthesis and Realizability

GR(1) is an assume-guarantee specification that describes the interaction between two players: the
environment

\mathbf{e}
and the
system

\mathbf{s}
. Written as a pair
\Phi=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{G}\rangle
, GR(1) specifications provide a logical structure for the environment’s behaviour, the assumptions
\mathcal{A}
, and the desired system properties, the guarantees
\mathcal{G}
. We have (Boolean) variables
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}
partitioned into
inputs

\mathcal{X}
(environment
\mathbf{e}
) and
outputs

\mathcal{Y}
(system/controller
\mathbf{s}
). A GR(1) specification has the assume-guarantee structure,

\Phi\;=\;\Big(\theta^{\mathbf{e}}\;\land\;G\,\rho^{\mathbf{e}}\;\land\;\bigwedge_{i\in I^{\mathbf{e}}}GF\,J^{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\Big)\;\to\;\Big(\theta^{\mathbf{s}}\;\land\;G\,\rho^{\mathbf{s}}\;\land\;\bigwedge_{i\in I^{\mathbf{s}}}GF\,J^{\mathbf{s}}_{i}\Big),

where,

•

\theta^{\mathbf{e}},\theta^{\mathbf{s}}
are
initial
propositional conditions over
\mathcal{V}
;

•

\rho^{\mathbf{e}},\rho^{\mathbf{s}}
are
transition
relations over current/next variables (one-step invariants, wrapped by
G
);

•

J^{\mathbf{e}}_{i},J^{\mathbf{s}}_{i}
are
justice
(fairness) predicates over current variables (liveness, wrapped by
GF
).

Equivalently, we can write
\mathcal{A}=\theta^{\mathbf{e}}\land G\rho^{\mathbf{e}}\land\bigwedge_{i\in I^{\mathbf{e}}}GFJ^{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
and
\mathcal{G}=\theta^{\mathbf{s}}\land G\rho^{\mathbf{s}}\land\bigwedge_{i\in I^{\mathbf{s}}}GFJ^{\mathbf{s}}_{i}
.
The specification takes the form of a turn-based two-player GR(1) Game
\langle\mathcal{V},\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\theta^{\mathbf{e}},\theta^{\mathbf{s}},\rho^{\mathbf{e}},\rho^{\mathbf{s}},\varphi\rangle
, where
\varphi
is the fairness formula,
\bigwedge_{i\in I^{\mathbf{s}}}GF\,J^{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\rightarrow\bigwedge_{i\in I^{\mathbf{s}}}GF\,J^{\mathbf{s}}_{i}
. GR(1) synthesis solves the GR(1) game on states
\nu\in 2^{\mathcal{V}}
where,

(1)

The environment
\mathbf{e}
picks inputs
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
consistent with
\mathcal{A}
.

(2)

The system/controller
\mathbf{s}
picks outputs
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
to satisfy
\mathcal{G}
.

We say a GR(1) specification is
realizable
iff the
strict realisability formula
of the GR(1) game holds. Furthermore, if a specification is realisable, then there exists a
winning strategy

\sigma
for the system
\mathbf{s}
to ensure the fairness formula. This strategy is encoded through a symbolic controller in the form of a fairness-free Fair Discrete System (FDS), which can be synthesised in polynomial-time (see
(
Bloem et al. 2012
)
). The set of states from which
\mathbf{s}
has a
strategy

\sigma
ensuring the fairness formula
\varphi
is called the
winning region

\mathcal{W}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{V}}
.

It is important to emphasize the role of environment assumptions in this process. The assumptions
\mathcal{A}
represent prior knowledge or design-time modelling of the environment. The controller
\mathbf{s}
(or shield) relies on these assumptions to guarantee the system properties
\mathcal{G}

(
Bloem et al. 2012
)
. In practical terms, this means that if an assumption is violated at runtime, the shield’s behaviour is undefined with respect to the original specification.

Example (Minepump).

The minepump system is a classic case study for reactive controller synthesis
(
Kramer et al. 1983
;
Joseph et al. 1996
)
. It models a control system responsible for preventing flooding in a mine while avoiding ignition hazards due to methane. The controller manages a pump that extracts water from the mine. Safety requirements (the guarantees
\mathcal{G}
) demand that the pump should run when water levels are high, but must remain off when methane is present, as pumping under methane conditions risks an explosion. Additionally, domain assumptions (
\mathcal{A}
) capture how the physical environment behaves: when the pump is active, water levels gradually decrease; and for simplicity, we assume methane and high water do not occur simultaneously. Formally, the variables are:
\mathcal{X}=\{\texttt{highwater},\texttt{methane}\}
(environment) and
\mathcal{Y}=\{\texttt{pump}\}
(system). The initial spec is given as follows,

guarantee1:

\displaystyle G\big(\texttt{highwater}\to X(\texttt{pump})\big)

guarantee2:

\displaystyle G\big(\texttt{methane}\to X(\neg\texttt{pump})\big)

assumption1:

\displaystyle G\big((Y(\texttt{pump})\land\texttt{pump})\to X(\neg\textit{highwater})\big)

assumption2:

\displaystyle G\big(\neg\texttt{highwater}\lor\neg\textit{methane}\big)

In our two-player game here, the environment
\mathbf{e}
chooses values for
\mathcal{X}=\{\texttt{highwater},\texttt{methane}\}
, while respecting the assumptions
\mathcal{A}
, the system
\mathbf{s}
chooses values for
\mathcal{Y}=\{\texttt{pump}\}
that respect the guarantees
\mathcal{G}
. The winning region
\mathcal{W}
consists of all states (valuations of
\mathcal{X}
) from which a winning strategy exists. Since, in the winning region
\mathcal{W}
,
highwater
and
methane
cannot be simultaneously true, the system
\mathbf{s}
always has a strategy,

•

If
methane
then set
\texttt{pump}=0
.

•

If
highwater
then set
\texttt{pump}=1
.

•

Else set
\texttt{pump}=0
.

which keeps the system within the winning region
\mathcal{W}
. Notice how this strategy is fixed (e.g., static symbolic controller), lifting this strategy to the full winning region
\mathcal{W}
gives us a maximally permissive shield, allowing the pump to be applied when
highwater
is not true; this may be desirable in the context of RL when we are trying to optimize some auxiliary reward (e.g., variable pricing strategies). However, if
highwater
and
methane
were to both evaluate to true simultaneously during execution (violating
assumption2
), the shield’s behaviour would be undefined, as we have left the winning region
\mathcal{W}
, highlighting how brittle this initial specification is.

3.
The
RepairRL
Shielding Approach

Our overall approach is a hybrid
reactive synthesis
+
reinforcement learning
framework called
RepairRL
(see Figure
1
). The main components are: (1) an RL agent training to maximise rewards provided by the environment; (2) a reactive shield synthesized from a GR(1) specification of the environment assumptions and system guarantees that enforces constraints on the agent’s actions; (3) an
Environment Checker
that monitors the traces of the combined system (RL agent + environment) for any violation of the environment assumptions; (4) a
SpecRepair
module that uses ILP, as described in
(
Buckworth et al. 2023
)
, to adapt the specification (learn new assumptions and guarantees, then re-synthesise the shield) when a violation is detected. We describe each aspect of our approach below, including concrete details explaining the division of responsibilities between the learning agent and the synthesized shield.

.

3.1.
GR(1) Winning Region as Shield

We remember a GR(1) specification may be synthesised as a symbolic controller. This can take the form of a fairness-free FDS, which we define as

\mathcal{D}=\langle\mathcal{V},\theta,\rho\rangle
,
where
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{X}\bigcup\mathcal{Y}
is defined as before,
\theta:2^{\mathcal{V}}\rightarrow\{true,false\}
is an
initial condition
, and
\rho:2^{\mathcal{V}}\times 2^{\mathcal{V}}\rightarrow\{true,false\}
is a
transition relation
.

As stated in
(
Bloem et al. 2012
)
, the fairness-free FDS is a symbolic representation of the Mealy machine, or finite-state reactive system, for those familiar with shielding literature
(
Alshiekh et al. 2018
)
.

Given a specification
\Phi
, if
strict realisability
holds, we may then extract a
maximally permissive safety-shield
as the fairness-free FDS
\mathcal{S}h=\langle\mathcal{V},\hat{\theta},\hat{\rho}\rangle
, where
\hat{\theta}=\theta^{e}\rightarrow(\theta^{s}\land\mathcal{W})
and
\hat{\rho}=(\mathcal{W}\land\rho^{e})\rightarrow\rho^{s}
. Given the realisability formula holds, a symbolic controller implementing
\mathcal{S}h
has the following property:

Corollary 3.1 (Shield Closure under the Winning Region).

Given a realisable specification
\Phi
synthesised as a fairness-free FDS
\mathcal{S}h=\langle\mathcal{V},\hat{\theta},\hat{\rho}\rangle
, a run of consecutive states
(\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\dots)\in(2^{\Sigma})^{\omega}
that satisfies the initial environment condition
\nu_{0}\models\theta^{e}
and transition relation
\forall i\in\mathbb{N}.(\nu_{i},\nu_{i+1})\models\rho^{e}
, will belong to the winning region
\forall i\in\mathbb{N}.\nu_{i}\in\mathcal{W}
.

The aforementioned ensures that, for every state
\nu
belonging to a run, there is always a strategy
\sigma
that can satisfy the fairness goal
\varphi
, as long as our environment behaves as expected. This allows our shield to act not only towards
safety enforcement
, but also towards
liveness protection
, preventing actions that may force the system into a liveness-violating ergodic component (e.g. an infinite loop that never visits at least one of the goal states).

3.2.
Integration with Reinforcement Learning

To integrate RL agents, based on the MDP formulation, with logic-based shields, we need a concrete way to map MDP state to the Boolean predicates used by the shield. We therefore introduce the following labelling functions:

•

State labelling function:

L_{\{S\}}:S\to 2^{\mathcal{X}}
assigns to each state
s\in S
of the MDP a valuation over the environment inputs
\mathcal{X}
of the GR(1) assumptions.

•

Action labelling function

L_{\{A\}}:A\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
maps each concrete agent/MDP action
a\in A
to a valuation over the Boolean output variables
\mathcal{Y}
used in the GR(1) guarantees.

Definition of Safe Action

Correctness, with respect to a GR(1) specification, is split into 2 parts:
safety
and
liveness
. In the context where all environment assumptions are satisfied, a system action is considered
correct
if (1) it adheres to the safety invariants
G\rho_{s}
and (2) it does not allow the environment to force the violation of any liveness property
GFJ^{\mathbf{s}}_{i}
.

Derived Safe Action Set

At runtime the set of
allowable (safe) actions
from MDP state
s
and shield state
\nu
is formally defined as,

A_{\text{safe}}(s,\nu)=\{a\in A\mid\big(\nu,(L_{\{A\}}(a),L_{\{S\}}(s))\big)\models\hat{\rho}\}

The shield
\mathcal{S}h
, thus only permits actions
a\in A_{\text{safe}}(s,\nu)
. If the agent proposes an action outside this set, the shield overrides it with a permissible alternative, either using an arbitrary partial ordering of the actions
A
(e.g., via Q-function), or randomly sampling from
A_{\text{safe}}(s,\nu)
. This guarantees that the joint execution trace respects the guarantees
\mathcal{G}
of the GR(1) specification, provided the environment adheres to the assumptions
\mathcal{A}
. This is formalized hereafter.

Corollary 3.2 (Correctness and Minimal Deviation).

Let
\Phi=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{G}\rangle
be realizable with winning region
\mathcal{W}
, and assume the environment satisfies
\mathcal{A}
.

•

Correctness:
The shield
\mathcal{S}h
synthesized from
\Phi
ensures that the safe action set
A_{\text{safe}}(s,\nu)
is non-empty for every reachable state
(s,\nu)
from the initial conditions
(s_{0},\nu_{0})
. Hence, the agent’s execution is deadlock-free and satisfies the safety invariants consistent with
\rho^{s}
.

•

Minimum deviation
: The shield deviates from the policy
\pi
if and only if the next state transitions out of the winning region
(\nu,L_{S}(s),(L_{A}(\pi(s))))\not\models\hat{\rho}
. Thus, liveness remains
achievable
from every visited state
(s,\nu)
(there exists a strategy to realize
\bigwedge_{i\in I^{\mathbf{s}}}GF\,J^{\mathbf{s}}_{i}
), although it is strictly not enforced by
\mathcal{S}h
.

Division of Responsibilities

The RL agent optimizes reward over the
restricted
action space via policy optimization; the shield ensures correctness by filtering out precisely those actions that would violate one-step safety or leave
\mathcal{W}
. For optimal behaviour, training with the shield in-the-loop aligns learning with deployment, prevents reliance on disallowed actions, and naturally confines exploration to the permissible
winning region

\mathcal{W}
.

3.3.
Online Shield Adaptation

Initial Shield Synthesis

Before deployment, we assume that a system engineer or domain expert provides an initial formal specification
\Phi=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{G}\rangle
, that captures what the system is supposed to do and what environment assumptions it relies on. We then use a GR(1) synthesis tool (such as Spectra
(
Maoz and Ringert 2021
)
) to compute the winning region
\mathcal{W}
of the safety game between environment
\mathbf{e}
and system
\mathbf{s}
. From this, we can derive the set of allowed (safe) actions, given the agent’s trajectory through the environment. This set of allowed actions is used to filter the agent’s choices.

Deployment and Monitoring

Once the RL agent and the initial shield are deployed, at each timestep the
Environment Checker
observes the environment’s state and transitions. If it detects any event that violates the environment assumptions
\mathcal{A}
, it raises a flag indicating an environment failure. For example, if both
methane
and
highwater
are simultaneously true, this violates
assumption2
in the initial spec of the Minepump case study. At the moment of detecting a violation, the system’s formal guarantees may no longer hold, and continuing operation with the old shield could lead to an imminent safety failure. We then pause the agent’s normal operation and initiate the
SpecRepair
module.

Specification Repair

The goal of the
SpecRepair
module is to produce a new realizable specification
\Phi^{\prime}=\langle\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\rangle
that accounts for the observed environment behaviour and is realizable under the current violating trace
\tau
. We employ a counterexample-guided inductive learning approach, which follows a “learner-oracle” framework
(
Jha et al. 2010
)
. In our implementation, inspired by the algorithm proposed by Buckworth et. al. in
(
Buckworth et al. 2023
)
, the learner is an ILP learner (we use ILASP
(
Law et al. 2014
)
) and the oracle is a GR(1) synthesizer (Spectra
(
Maoz and Ringert 2021
)
). A higher level overview of the repair algorithm is outlined in Algorithm
1
(see
(
Buckworth et al. 2023
)
for further details). The steps taken are as follows:

(1)

Weaken assumptions
: At line 1, using the violation trace
\tau
(the sequence of states/actions leading up to the assumption failure) as a positive example, the learner proposes a revised set of environment assumptions
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
that is weaker than the original
\mathcal{A}
and permits the new observed behaviour. For example, for the Minepump case study, a trivial way to weaken the violating assumption (
assumption2
) is to remove it.

(2)

Realizability check
: At line 2, the oracle (synthesizer) checks whether the system guarantees
\mathcal{G}
are still realizable under the new assumptions
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
. If
\langle\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{G}\rangle
is realizable, it means the system can still fulfil the original guarantees even with the environment’s new behaviour, so we synthesise the new specification’s winning region
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
at line 3.

(3)

Weaken guarantees (if needed)
: If
\langle\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{G}\rangle
is not realizable, it means that the environment’s deviation has made it impossible for all the original guarantees to be satisfied. In other words,
\mathcal{G}
is too strict under the new set of assumptions
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
. Therefore, at line 5, we extract a
counter-strategy
– an example of how the environment can force a violation of
\mathcal{G}
. The logic-based learner then uses this counter-strategy to learn
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, a set of weaker system guarantees, that no longer considers the proposed counter-strategy as a failure. We present this process at line 6 as a single-shot task, but actually this is an iterative process, where multiple counter-strategies may be generated to guide the learning process. For a detailed description of how counter-strategies aid in guiding the learning process for the weakened guarantees
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, we refer the reader to

(
Buckworth et al. 2023
)
. In the Minepump case study, after removing
assumption2
, either
guarantee1
or
guarantee2
need to be weakened. Assuming turning the pump on after methane is observed is highly dangerous behaviour,
guarantee1
is weakened to:
G(\texttt{highwater}\land\neg\texttt{methane}\to X\texttt{pump})
.

(4)

Re-synthesise
: Finally, at line 7, the oracle synthesises the new winning region
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
from the weakened specification
\langle\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\rangle
. This new shield now enforces the modified guarantees
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
under the updated assumptions
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.

Algorithm 1

Specification Repair (
SpecRepair
) under Assumption Violations

1:

Input:
Violating trace
\tau
, current spec
\Phi=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{G}\rangle

2:

Output:
Realizable spec
\Phi^{\prime}=\langle\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{G}\rangle^{\prime}

3:

A^{\prime}\leftarrow\text{learner.weaken\_assumptions}(A,\tau)

4:

if

\text{oracle.is\_realisable}(\langle A^{\prime},G\rangle)

then

5:

return

\text{oracle.get\_controller}(\langle A^{\prime},G\rangle)

6:

else

7:

cs\leftarrow\text{oracle.counter\_strategy}(\langle A^{\prime},G\rangle)

8:

G^{\prime}\leftarrow\text{learner.weaken\_guarantees}(A^{\prime},G,\tau,cs)

9:

return

\text{oracle.get\_controller}(\langle A^{\prime},G^{\prime}\rangle)

Notably this adaptation happens online and autonomously – there is no need for a human to rewrite the specification or manually intervene when an assumption is broken.
Moreover, since the repair is based on formal counterexamples and logic-based learning, the new specification
\Phi^{\prime}=\langle\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\rangle
is sound. Under the soundness of the repair approach, we formalise this below.

Corollary 3.3 (Soundness of SpecRepair).

Given a realizable specification
\Phi=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{G}\rangle
and a violating trace
\tau\not\models\mathcal{A}
, a sound SpecRepair procedure produces a new specification
\Phi^{\prime}=\langle\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\rangle
such that:

(1)

\tau\models\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
(the violating trace is consistent with the repaired assumptions);

(2)

\Phi^{\prime}
is realizable, i.e., there exists a strategy for the system ensuring all guarantees
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
under all environment behaviours satisfying
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
;

(3)

\forall\sigma\in\Sigma^{\omega}
,
\sigma\models\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\sigma\models\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
(any infinite trace formerly allowed by the previous set of assumptions is allowed by the new set of assumptions. In other words, the new set of assumptions is equivalent or weaker to the old one);

(4)

\forall\sigma\in\Sigma^{\omega}
,
\sigma\models\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\sigma\models\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
(equivalent to property (3), but for the set of guarantees).

Hence, the repaired shield synthesized from
\Phi^{\prime}
both enforces
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
under
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and guarantees safe continuation of the ongoing execution without interruption. Crucially, we can extract the following from properties (1) and (4) of the above:

Corollary 3.4.

The winning region
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
induced by
\Phi^{\prime}
contains the current execution state reached by
\tau
. This ensures that the agent’s trace remains
deadlock-free
after repair.

Furthermore, the use of GR(1) also keeps the computational burden manageable, so that adaptation can occur within a reasonable time during deployment.

4.1.
Minepump

Environment Details.

We extend the canonical GR(1) Minepump specification into a stochastic MDP with both exogenous disturbances and auxiliary rewards. In the
training environment
, we assume (as in the initial specification) that
methane
and
highwater
never occur simultaneously. In the
deployment/evaluation
environment, this assumption may be violated:
methane
and
highwater
can arise together due to stochastic transitions. This setup reflects a practical design-deployment mismatch, where training relies on simplified physical models, but the deployed system must contend with unanticipated interactions.

Water inflow evolves as a three-state Markov chain (c.f., Appendix
B.3
) over rates
{0,0.5,2}
, producing bursts of high inflow. Methane follows a two-state geometric process with switching probabilities
p_{\text{on}\to\text{off}}=0.25
and
p_{\text{off}\to\text{on}}=1/6
, leading to intermittent but persistent episodes. Energy prices evolve as a two-state Markov chain with persistence 0.7, yielding low (1.0) and high (4.0) tariffs that directly scale the per-step cost of pumping.

The environment defines a strict
highwater
threshold
H=10
and a water cap
W_{\max}=18
. The pump removes
R=6
units per step, which ensures
assumption1
; if the pump runs for two consecutive steps,
highwater
will eventually clear, since outflow dominates inflow in expectation. The reward combines: (i) switching costs (
0.5
for pump-on,
0.1
for pump-off); (ii) tariff costs scaled by the current energy price; and (iii) a
highwater
penalty (
-1.0
). Optimal behaviour may involve pumping early, during low tariff windows or just after methane clears, to plan for costly
highwater
and high-tariff scenarios.

Liveness Extension.

We also considered a liveness extension requiring that
highwater
is always eventually cleared. Using a standard GR(1) pattern, we introduce a system variable
highwater_flag
that is raised whenever
highwater
occurs. Guarantees are then:

•

G(\texttt{highwater}\to\texttt{highwater\_flag})
, ensuring the flag is raised whenever highwater occurs;

•

GF(\neg\text{highwater\_flag})
, requiring that the flag is infinitely often cleared.

Together, these enforce the liveness property that “whenever water rises above the threshold, it must eventually be brought down again.” However, this specification is unrealizable under the original assumptions: in the safety game, the environment can always maintain
\text{methane}=1
, preventing the pump from ever operating and thus blocking liveness. To restore realizability, we add the liveness assumption::

•

GF(\neg\texttt{methane}\land X(\neg\texttt{methane}))

ensuring methane eventually clears for at least two consecutive steps, providing the system with a window in which it can activate the pump to remove water, and thereby satisfying:

•

GF(\neg\text{highwater\_flag})
.

An important subtlety is that liveness assumptions are difficult to monitor directly, since they are interpreted over infinite traces. In practice, the Environment Checker can issue warnings if no two-step methane-free window occurs for many timesteps, flagging that the assumption may be invalid. Such alerts improve
trust and transparency
, making engineers aware of hidden assumptions underpinning realizability without triggering premature repairs.

We benchmark our adaptive shielding against symbolic baselines and RL agents with different static shields. The following static controllers are:

•

Static Symbolic Controller 1
: the nominal winning strategy for the initial spec (assumes
methane
and

2.3
.

•

Environment Details.

We modify the emulator of the class Atari 2600 Seaquest game (provided as part of the

Formalizing the System Dynamics

First we specify the initial GR(1) specification that captures the engineers best guess at the relevant dynamics and guarantees for the Seaquest environment. The full specification makes use of Boolean variables and cannot be provided in full here (c.f., Appendix
B.5
), rather we provide a more concise abstraction below:

•

The environment variables
\mathcal{X}
, correspond the the current
oxygen
level and
depth
of the submarine.

•

The system variables
\mathcal{Y}
correspond to the direction of the action use to move the submarine, in particular,
up
or
down
.

The key environment assumptions (
\mathcal{A}
) are:

•

– oxygen depletion rate:
G((\texttt{depth}>0\land\texttt{oxygen}>0)\to X(\texttt{oxygen})=\texttt{oxygen}-1)

•

– movement of the submarine:
G((\texttt{depth}>0\land\texttt{up})\to X(\texttt{depth})=\texttt{depth}-4)

The key system guarantees (

•

The initial shield can be synthesized from the formal specifications

using Spectra
(

We use the same evaluation metrics as before:

,

,

,

,
evaluation overrides
. All metrics in Table

are reported over 5 random seeds with standard error (SE). In the (expected) training environment all of the different shields are able to successfully enforce
guarantee1
(never run out of oxygen), whereas, the unshielded PPO baseline fails
>80\%
of the time. The shielded agents also obtain better training reward across the board, likely because the goal of maximizing reward and not running out of oxygen are reasonably well aligned, with the shield acting as a useful inductive bias. In the (unexpected) evaluation environment the outlook changes, both the
Naive
and

Both the
pre-emptively repaired shield
and our
adaptive shield
approach successfully enforce
guarantee1

However, in all cases, the reward obtained by the agents in the evaluation environment dramatically drops off. This can be explained two-fold: (1) with a higher rate of oxygen depletion introduced in the environment, the opportunity for collecting rewards becomes more challenging; (2) due to the deterministic nature of the Atari environments, the agents trained in the expected environment might have memorized the optimal actions/path to take in order to achieve a high reward, leaving little room for generalizing to different dynamics. At the very least, compliance is guaranteed, but re-training might be required to restore optimality in this case.

5.
Related Work

The concept of shielding in RL originates from the
simplex architecture
, where an “undependable” controller (e.g., a learning agent) is overseen by a “dependable” safety controller that guarantees critical properties
(
Weiss et al. 2018
)
. Shield synthesis
(
Könighofer et al. 2017
;
Könighofer et al. 2020
)
formalizes this idea by using logical specifications to define safe behaviour and automatically synthesizes a reactive system that enforces them. Introduced by Bloem et al.
(
Bloem et al. 2015
)
, shielding ensures that LTL safety properties are maintained during execution by correcting any action that would otherwise lead the system into an unsafe state. Alshiekh et al.
(
Alshiekh et al. 2018
)
extended this idea to
reinforcement learning
(RL) by synthesizing shields from formal safety specifications and integrating them with deep RL agents, enabling provable safety guarantees while still allowing the agent to explore and optimize their behaviour.

However, most shielding approaches assume a known safety abstraction of the environment, represented by logical rules or automata that capture relevant dynamics and constraints
(
Alshiekh et al. 2018
;
Bloem et al. 2020
;
Jansen et al. 2020
;
Könighofer et al. 2023
)
. Given such an abstraction and desired specification, the shield is constructed by solving a
two-player safety game

(
Bloem et al. 2015
)
between the environment and system, with the environment attempting to force the system into a deadlock state (no available actions) and the shield ensuring play remains within the winning region of states.

A major limitation is that the environment’s abstraction is assumed to be
correct and complete
. In practice, safety-critical dynamics are often uncertain or misspecified, meaning that a static shield may fail under unexpected behaviours. Recent work has begun to relax this assumption:
approximate shielding
learns or refines safety-relevant models from data
(
Goodall and Belardinelli 2023
;
He et al. 2022
;
Xiao et al. 2023
)
, but such methods provide only probabilistic or bounded safety guarantees. In continuous control, shielding has been extended via safety filters that exploit control barrier functions and Hamilton-Jacobi reachability analysis to ensure constraint satisfaction under known dynamics
(
Bastani et al. 2021
;
Banerjee et al. 2024
;
Ames et al. 2019
)
. However, these techniques rely on accurate system models, leaving open the question of how to maintain
formal safety and liveness guarantees
when environmental assumptions break at runtime.

Despite the progress in shield synthesis for RL, most logic-based shielding frameworks for RL either do not consider the possibility of environment changes or terminate if an unexpected behaviour is observed
(
Bloem et al. 2015
;
Alshiekh et al. 2018
)
. Pranger et al. developed TemPest – a shield synthesis tool that can handle certain types of probabilistic environments and switch between different shield strategies
(
Pranger et al. 2021
)
. TemPest and related works
(
Bloem et al. 2020
)
focus on adapting to different notions of shielding or recovering from agent failures (when the agent temporarily violates a rule and must recover within a fixed number of steps), rather than necessarily revising the environment assumptions. Buckworth et al.
(
Buckworth et al. 2023
)
recently proposed an approach to adapting formal specifications in the event of environment changes, but their work was limited to relatively constrained, low-complexity environments and they only consider the synthesis of static-symbolic controllers, rather than shields lifted to the full winning-region for RL.

Other techniques that enforce safety via minimal interference, adopt controllers that intervene only as a last resort
(
Pham et al. 2018
)
, or via specification revision/repair where the agent’s reward function or constraints are adjusted when safety issues arise
(
Bharadhwaj et al. 2020
)
. These approaches typically lack the formal guarantees that our synthesized shield provides. By using a reactive synthesis shield, we ensure that safety rules are never violated (even after repair, which always preserves realizability).

6.
Conclusion

Our results show that adaptive GR(1) specification repair is a practical way to keep deep RL agents both safe and effective when environment assumptions break at runtime. By deploying agents with an
Environment Checker
and invoking the ILP-guided
SpecRepair
module only upon detected assumption violations, we can re-synthesize shields online with transparent, human-readable changes to assumptions and guarantees. The explicitness of our approach improves both interpretability and trust, as the assumptions that underpin realizability are no longer hidden in the synthesized controller but are exposed, and when necessary, weakened minimally and visibly. In Minepump, we further illustrated how liveness can be expressed and checked within GR(1) by introducing a liveness assumption on methane clearance; we emphasized that liveness violations are not refutable on finite traces, motivating warnings rather than immediate repair and giving engineers clear diagnostics about which assumptions ensure realizability.

Looking forward, several limitations point to natural extensions of our framework. One notable limitation is the expressiveness of the specification formalism: we rely on LTL in GR(1) form, which requires a finite, discrete state abstraction. While this worked well for propositions such as oxygen-level or depth in Seaquest, domains with rich continuous dynamics may defy a clean logical discretization.
Future work could therefore investigate more expressive logics, such as Signal Temporal Logic (STL) or hybrid-system formalisms that mix discrete and continuous dynamics, enabling adaptive synthesis directly over real-valued signals. In addition, although GR(1) synthesis itself is efficient, the iterative learner-oracle loop (e.g., ILASP + Spectra) can become a computational bottleneck when specifications grow large or if frequent repairs are needed. Developing faster repair algorithms would help scale adaptive shielding to more complex tasks and longer deployments.

A.1.
Proof of Corollary
3.1

Proof sketch

The proof of closure follows from the definition of strict realisability from
(
Bloem et al. 2012
)
:

\varphi_{G}=(\theta^{e}\rightarrow\theta^{s})\land(\theta^{e}\rightarrow\,G((H\rho^{e})\rightarrow\rho^{s}))\land((\theta^{e}\land\,G\rho^{e})\rightarrow\varphi)

Where
\varphi
is the fairness goal defined in section
2
.
In lemma 2 from
(
Bloem et al. 2012
)
, the winning region
\mathcal{W}
is defined as the set of states in which
\varphi
is true. We leverage the proof of FDS completeness from
(
Bloem et al. 2012
)
, to extrapolate that our shield is complete with respect to
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{V}
. Finally, let us consider any arbitrary finite computation
\sigma=\nu_{0},\nu_{1},...,\nu_{n}
of shield
\mathcal{S}h
. We assume this trace satisfies
\nu_{0}\models\theta^{e}
and
\forall i<n.\,(\nu_{i},\nu_{i+1})\models\rho^{e}
to show that all states within the trace belong to the winning region
\mathcal{W}
.
From the definition of
\hat{\theta}
, it follows that
\nu_{0}\models\theta^{s}
and
\nu_{0}\models\mathcal{W}
.
Through induction, take an arbitrary
\nu_{i}\models\mathcal{W}
. Since any
(\nu_{i},\nu_{i+1})\models\rho^{e}
, by definition of
\hat{\rho}
, we have
(\nu_{i},\nu_{i+1})\models\rho^{s}
. Furthermore, from the definition of realisability, if we extend our finite trace with an infinite suffix
\sigma^{\prime}\in(2^{\mathcal{V}})^{\omega}
such that
\sigma^{\prime}\models\,G\rho^{e}
, and the suffix represents an infinite computation of some winning strategy (similar to the definition of
\mathcal{D}
from
(
Bloem et al. 2012
)
), then, by definition of strict realisability, we have
\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime}\models\varphi
. Therefore,
\nu_{i+1}\in\mathcal{W}
.
From the above, corollary
3.1
follows.

Discussion

Interestingly for the Minepump experiments there are two distinct types of learning dynamics among the algorithms. The first group: DQN and DQN +
Static 2
, converge much slower than the second group: DQN
Static 1
,
Static (*)
and DQN +
Adaptive
. The only difference here is that the second group all strictly enforce
guarantee1
, i.e.,
G\big(\texttt{highwater}\to X(\texttt{pump})\big)
. This strict adherence to
guarantee1
acts as a sort of inductive bias that appears to speed up convergence, and interesting insight that demonstrates if the spec is well-aligned with the reward objective then learning can be sped-up. A similar picture is painted in the Seaquest learning curves: all algorithms that are constrained by, even a suboptimal shield during learning, achieve better performance and converge more quickly than the unconstrained RL baseline.

In the training environment all shielded PPO agents maintain

and spend 100% of the time in the winning region. The unconstrained PPO spends only 74% of the time in the winning region, meaning at least 25% of the time it is essentially doomed and will inevitably run out of oxygen. As expected,

B.5.
Seaquest Spec (full)

First we describe all Boolean variables
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}
.

•

Environment variables
\mathcal{X}
:

oxygen0
,
oxygen1
,
oxygen2
,
oxygen3
,
oxygen4
,
oxygen5
,
oxygen6
(7 oxygen bits for encoding 64 different oxygen values),
depth0
,
depth1
,
depth2
,
depth3
,
depth4
(5 oxygen bits encoding 24 different depth levels, corresponding to the multiples of 4 from 0-92 inclusive),
diver_at_depth1
,
diver_at_depth2
,
diver_at_depth3
,
diver_at_depth4
(environment variables that are asserted when there is a diver at each of the 4 possible levels).

•

System variables
\mathcal{Y}
:

up
(agent actions corresponding to upward movements),
down
(agent actions corresponding to downward movements),
rescues1
,
rescues2
,
rescues3
,
rescues4
(system variables that are asserted to rescue a diver at each of 4 the possible levels).

Now we describe the initial conditions
\theta^{\mathbf{e}}
and
\theta^{\mathbf{s}}
of the environment
\mathbf{e}
and system
\mathbf{s}
respectively.

•

Environment
\theta^{\mathbf{e}}
:

\texttt{depth}=0
(encoded via
depth0
-
depth4
),
\texttt{oxygen}=0
(encoded via
oxygen0
-
oxygen6
),
diver_at_depth1
-
\texttt{diver\_at\_depth4}=false
(no divers present initially).

•

System
\theta^{\mathbf{s}}
:

\texttt{up}=false
and
\texttt{down}=false
(the submarine is initially stationary),
rescues1
-
\texttt{rescues4}=false
(no divers initially rescued).

Now we describe the environment invariants
\rho^{\mathbf{e}}
.

•

assumption1
– oxygen depletion rate:
G((\texttt{depth}>0\land\texttt{oxygen}>0)\to X(\texttt{oxygen})=\texttt{oxygen}-1)
. This can be encoded via binary variables, for example,

\displaystyle G((\texttt{depth0}\lor\texttt{depth1}\lor\ldots\lor\texttt{depth4})\land(\texttt{oxygen0}\land\texttt{oxygen1}\land\texttt{oxygen2}\land\ldots\land\texttt{oxygen6}\land\neg\texttt{oxygen7})

\displaystyle\quad\to X((\neg\texttt{oxygen0}\land\texttt{oxygen1}\land\texttt{oxygen2}\land\ldots\land\texttt{oxygen6}\land\neg\texttt{oxygen7})))

i.e., the oxygen level goes from 63 to 62.

•

assumption2
– movement of the submarine:
G((\texttt{depth}>0\land\texttt{up})\to X(\texttt{depth})=\texttt{depth}-4)
(i.e., an
up
movement) can also be encoded via binary variables, for example,

G((\texttt{depth0}\land\texttt{depth1}\land\ldots\land\texttt{depth3}\land\neg\texttt{depth4})\land\texttt{up}\to X(\neg\texttt{depth0}\land\texttt{depth1}\land\ldots\land\texttt{depth3}\land\neg\texttt{depth4}))

i.e., the depth decreases from 15 to 14 when
\texttt{up}=true
.

•

assumption3
– analogous movement of the submarine but for
down
:
G((\texttt{depth}<92\land\texttt{down})\to X(\texttt{depth})=\texttt{depth}+4)
. For brevity we omit any further binarization here.

•

assumption4
– stationarity of the submarine when neither
\texttt{up}=true
or
\texttt{down}=true
:
G(\neg\texttt{up}\lor\neg\texttt{down}\to X(\texttt{depth})=\texttt{depth})

•

assumption5
– oxygen increases at the surface:
G((\texttt{depth}=0\land\texttt{oxygen}<64)\to X(\texttt{oxygen})=\texttt{oxygen}+1)
.

•

assumption6
– diver is rescued if the submarine is present at the corresponding depth. Noting there are 4 possible locations for the diver to be present which divide the depth levels evenly. Thus, we have the following:

–

G((\texttt{depth}=6\land\texttt{diver\_at\_depth1})\to X(\neg\texttt{diver\_at\_depth1}))

–

G((\texttt{depth}=12\land\texttt{diver\_at\_depth2})\to X(\neg\texttt{diver\_at\_depth2}))

–

G((\texttt{depth}=18\land\texttt{diver\_at\_depth3})\to X(\neg\texttt{diver\_at\_depth3}))

–

G((\texttt{depth}=24\land\texttt{diver\_at\_depth4})\to X(\neg\texttt{diver\_at\_depth4}))

i.e., for each diver depth level.

•

assumption7
– persistent diver:
G((\texttt{diver\_at\_depth1}\land\neg\texttt{depth}=6)\to X(\texttt{diver\_at\_depth1}))
(diver does not disappear unless rescued), defined analogously for
diver_at_depth1
-
diver_at_depth4
.

And the environment justice goals
J_{i}^{\mathbf{e}}
.

•

assumption8
– the environment must always eventually present the system with divers. Formally this is written as:

–

GF(\texttt{diver\_at\_depth1})
.

–

GF(\texttt{diver\_at\_depth2})
.

–

GF(\texttt{diver\_at\_depth3})
.

–

GF(\texttt{diver\_at\_depth4})
.

Now we describe the system invariants
\rho^{\mathbf{s}}
.

•

guarantee0
– we can never move up and down simultaneously:
G(\neg(\texttt{up}\land\texttt{down}))
.

•

guarantee1
– never run out of oxygen: we don’t have to explicitly write “never run out of oxygen”, i.e.,
G(\neg(\texttt{oxygen}=0))
. This was an oversimplification written in the main text. Rather, we just restrict the movement of the submarine when
\texttt{oxygen}=0
. If the submarine is not operational then it can’t pursue all its goals, so any state with
\texttt{oxygen}=0
would lie outside the winning region
\mathcal{W}
anyway, thus we write (in binary form),

G((\neg\texttt{oxygen}1\land\ldots\land\neg\texttt{oxygen7})\to(\neg\texttt{up}\land\neg\texttt{down}))

Finally the system goals
J_{i}^{\mathbf{s}}
,

•

guarantee2
– rescue divers at a given depth segment:

G(\texttt{diver\_at\_depth}[i]\to F(\texttt{depth}=i))
, i.e., ensures we always eventually reach divers at a given depth. Formally we write,

–

GF(\texttt{rescues1})
(for
rescues1
-
rescues4
).

–

G((Y(\texttt{diver\_at\_depth1})\land\texttt{diver\_at\_depth1})\to\texttt{rescues1})
(for
rescues1
-
rescues4
/
diver_at_depth1
-
diver_at_depth4
analogously). This is technically a system invariant
\rho^{\mathbf{s}}
enforcing the
rescues1
flag can only be true when a diver has disappeared.

–

Further,
G(\texttt{diver\_at\_depth1}\to\neg\texttt{rescues1})
(for
rescues1
-
rescues4
/
diver_at_depth1
-
diver_at_depth4
analogously) asserts the
rescues1
flag cannot be on when there is a diver to be rescued (also a system invariant
\rho^{\mathbf{s}}
).
