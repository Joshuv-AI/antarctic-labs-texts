Access to health services and perceived quality of primary care in rural Ancash, Peru
Authors: Edgar Agustin Mendez Nieto, Janett V. Chávez Sosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary health care (PHC) is a key strategy to achieve universal health coverage; however, significant access barriers persist in rural settings, potentially compromising the quality of care. In Peru, these challenges are particularly evident in Andean rural areas characterized by geographic dispersion and structural limitations of first-level health facilities. Objective: To analyze the association between accessibility to health services and the perceived quality of primary care in a rural population from the district of Llumpa, Áncash, Peru.MethodsAn analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among 497 adults attending first-level health facilities between July and October 2025. Accessibility to health services was assessed using the Tanahashi Health Service Coverage questionnaire, which evaluates accessibility, acceptability, contact, and availability. Primary care quality was measured using the short adult version of the Primary Care Assessment Tool (PCAT-A10). Descriptive analyses were performed, followed by Spearman’s rho correlation and Generalized Linear Models (GLM) with robust estimation adjusted for sociodemographic variables.ResultsPerception of poor quality of primary care was identified in 93.6% of participants, while 87.7% presented poor access to health services. A significant negative correlation was observed between access to health services and primary care quality (Rho = − 0.205; p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, access to health services remained independently associated with primary care quality (B = − 0.133; p < 0.001). Additionally, the availability and contact dimensions showed significant negative associations with primary care quality.ConclusionsIn this rural Andean population, barriers to access to health services are significantly associated with poorer perceived quality of primary care, reflecting persistent structural gaps in the health system. Strengthening service availability and effective patient–provider contact is essential to improve primary health care performance in rural settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14784-9.

Introduction

Within the framework of universal health coverage, the international community recognizes that strengthening primary health care (PHC) is an essential means of guaranteeing equitable and efficient services. Despite this, deep gaps persist: in 2021, more than half of the world’s population lacked full coverage of essential health services, indicating structural challenges to achieving effective, timely access [1, 2]. Faced with this scenario, the Astana Declaration reaffirms PHC as the axis of the health system and as a gateway to provide comprehensive, accessible, and people-centered care [3].

However, expanding coverage is not enough if services do not ensure quality. Quality of care is understood as the degree to which services increase the probability of favorable outcomes and align with current professional knowledge [4]. A widely used framework for its evaluation is the structure-process-result model, which allows analysis of everything from the conditions of the establishment to clinical interactions and their effects on health. In low- and middle-income countries, evidence shows that focusing solely on outcomes can mask specific failures; therefore, it is recommended to strengthen measurement of care processes (e.g., diagnosis, counseling, referral), especially in PHC platforms where most health needs are addressed [5]. Globally, the persistence of low-quality care is associated with a high burden of avoidable mortality and significant social and economic losses, which reinforces the need to prioritize quality as a central component of universal coverage [6].

In Latin America, the concern for quality is especially relevant. A multicenter study reported that, although access may be relatively high, only about a third of people identified a source of high-quality care, evidencing inequalities between groups and territories [7]. This perception of low quality is linked to barriers that affect the user-service relationship, including problems of acceptability, accessibility, contact, and availability. In addition, PHC reforms in the region have made progress but continue to confront fragmentation, financing, governance, and territorial equity [8].

In Peru, PHC (first-level of care) is the fundamental access point to the system and accounts for most of the available establishments; however, it faces critical limitations that can compromise the quality of care. A sectoral diagnosis by the Ministry of Health reported that 94.47% of first-level establishments had inadequate installed capacity in 2022, reflecting significant gaps in infrastructure and equipment [9]. These limitations are more evident in territories with greater geographical barriers and less availability of human resources, as is often the case in rural areas. In fact, inequality within countries is often expressed in the unequal distribution of establishments and qualified personnel, especially affecting rural and socially marginalized communities [10]. In the Peruvian case, the SERUMS program seeks to contribute to the distribution of personnel in rural and marginal urban areas; however, working conditions and limitations at the first level can impact performance and the continuity of care [11].

In Peru, the health system is segmented into the Comprehensive Health Insurance (SIS), which is government-funded for the vulnerable population, and EsSalud, a social security system for formal workers. Despite this, access to services remains a key barrier to the timely use of Primary Health Care (PHC) [12]. According to the National Household Survey (ENAHO 2023), for the July-September 2023 quarter, less than half of the population with a health problem who sought care did so in a formal health establishment; a significant proportion instead resorted to pharmacies or drugstores as their primary place of consultation [13]. This pattern suggests limitations in effective access to the first level, which may be associated with factors such as distance, availability of personnel, waiting times, and service supply, aspects that tend to be more pronounced in rural environments [10, 14].

In this context, it is a priority to study the relationship between access to health services and the perceived quality of primary care in rural populations. The Ancash region, with dispersed Andean territories and connectivity challenges, presents a key context for understanding how access barriers can translate into experiences and outcomes of primary care perceived as insufficient.

Materials and methods

An analytical cross-sectional study was carried out. The population consisted of residents of the Llumpa district, located in the province of Mariscal Luzuriaga, department of Ancash, Peru, who go to the jurisdiction’s first-level care establishments.

The Llumpa district is characterized by being a predominantly rural area, with a dispersed population in communities far from each other and with geographical conditions typical of the Andean highlands, such as limited access roads, long travel times, and restrictions on transport to health facilities, factors that directly influence timely access to primary care services. The district has a total population of 6,447 inhabitants. In this remote mountain region, healthcare is provided exclusively through a network of five Ministry of Health (MINSA) facilities. Since there are no Social Security (EsSalud), private clinics, or alternative medical services available, this network serves as the sole point of formal convergence for the entire population’s health-seeking behavior. Consequently, these health posts represent the universal healthcare reality for all residents, regardless of their insurance status.

The target population consisted of 2,022 registered users who came to receive care at the five first-level health facilities managed by two micro-networks: Piscobamba (Llumpa health post) and Yanama (Chinguil, Lluichocolpan, and Yurma health posts) during the period July-October 2025. The sample size was estimated using the formula for finite populations, with a confidence level of 95% (Z = 1.96), a margin of error of 5% (E = 0.05), and an expected proportion of 0.5, yielding a minimum sample size of 323 participants.

Participants were selected using non-probabilistic convenience sampling, approaching individuals in the waiting rooms of the five facilities. A total of 560 users were initially invited to participate; however, 63 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria—primarily due to minority age, lack of active health insurance (SIS), physical or intellectual disabilities, or voluntary refusal. Consequently, a final sample of 497 inhabitants was reached, as detailed in the selection flow diagram (Figure S1). This sample size increased the accuracy of the estimates and the statistical power of the study, resulting in an effective margin of error of ≈ 4.0% and an approximate statistical power of 90% to detect small to moderate associations (r ≥ 0.13), with a level of statistical significance of 0.05.

Data collection was conducted using a structured questionnaire that included sociodemographic variables and two validated instruments. Access to health services was assessed using the Health Service Coverage questionnaire, proposed by Tanahashi (1978) and replicated in Peru by Chávez et al. (2022) [15]. The instrument consists of 25 items, distributed across four dimensions: availability, which refers to the physical presence of health resources; accessibility, understood as the geographic and economic distance to the facility; acceptability, defined as the alignment between service characteristics and the community’s social expectations; and contact, which represents the actual use of the service. These were measured with a five-point Likert-type scale (1 = never to 5 = always). In this study, higher scores on this scale indicate a greater presence of perceived barriers to health services. The complete set of items is provided as Supplementary Material (Appendix 1). It presented an adequate internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.738). For the interpretation of the results, access to health services was classified as deficient (74–96 points) and good (50–73 points). Although these thresholds differ from previous studies [15], they were adjusted to reflect the score distribution of this specific rural population where higher values represent more frequent obstacles, as shown in the distribution histogram provided in the Supplementary Material (Figure S2).

The quality of primary care was evaluated from the user’s perspective using the Primary Care Assessment Tool short version for adults (PCAT-A10), an adaptation of the Primary Care Assessment Tool–Adult Edition, validated by Rocha et al. (2021) [16] and replicated in the Peruvian population by Becerra (2023) [17]. The instrument consists of 9 items, with response alternatives ranging from 1 (do not know) to 5 (yes, without a doubt) and presents an adequate reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.73). This tool evaluates perceived quality based on essential primary care attributes: longitudinality, which implies a continuous relationship between patient and provider; coordination, referring to the integration of care; and comprehensiveness, defined as the facility’s capacity to address diverse health needs. In this scale, higher values represent a better perception of primary care quality. No reverse-scored items were used in this short version. The complete set of items is available in the Supplementary Material (Appendix 2). The score was used to classify primary care quality as deficient (16–29 points) or good (30–42 points). The appropriateness of these cut-off points is further supported by the score distribution histogram included in the Supplementary Material (Figure S3), which illustrates the clustering of responses in the lower end of the scale.

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. Categorical variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies. For the quantitative variables, the normality of the distributions was previously evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test; those that did not show normality (p < 0.05) were described using the median and interquartile range (Supplementary Material, Figures S2 and S3).

For the bivariate analysis, the association between the study variables was evaluated using Spearman’s Rho correlation, establishing a significance level of p < 0.05. For this purpose, the total continuous scores of each instrument were used, while the categorical versions (good/deficient) were employed exclusively for the descriptive characterization of the population. Likewise, a multivariate analysis was performed using a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) with a linear response and a robust estimator for the covariance matrix. The total score of perceived primary care quality was defined as the dependent variable, assuming a normal distribution and an identity link function. The models included the sociodemographic characteristics (sex, age, marital status, level of education, employment status, economic income, presence of children, and travel time to the health facility) and access to health services, evaluated both as a total score and by its dimensions.

The goodness-of-fit of the models was evaluated using the deviance, Pearson chi-square, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating a better fit. The omnibus likelihood-ratio contrast was used to compare each adjusted model with the intercept-only model, and the significance of the individual effects was evaluated using the Wald statistic, with p < 0.05.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The heads of the Piscobamba Health Microred and the Yanama Microred authorized the study. It was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Postgraduate School of the Universidad Peruana Unión (RS 2025-CEEPG-00080). Participation was voluntary, and the confidentiality and anonymity of the information were guaranteed by signing the informed consent form.

Results

The sample consisted of 497 inhabitants, of whom 53.3% were women and 46.7% were men, with a median age of 35 years (IQR: 25). Regarding marital status, 58.1% were married or cohabiting, and 41.9% were single. Regarding educational level, 52.5% had basic education, 31.6% had higher education, and 15.9% had no formal education. Regarding employment status, 92.2% reported being working. 94.2% reported a monthly economic income of less than 1,130 soles. Likewise, 72.6% indicated having children. The travel time to the health facility presented a median of 50 min (IQR: 25.5) (Table 1).

The perceived quality of primary care was classified as poor in 93.6% of the participants and as good in 6.4%, with a median of 23 points (IQR: 3). Regarding access to health services, 87.7% presented high levels of perceived barriers (deficient access) and 12.3% had low levels of barriers (good access), with a median of 80 points (IQR: 11). When analyzing the dimensions of access, availability was classified as poor in 92.8% of cases, contact in 82.5% and acceptability in 92.4%. In contrast, the accessibility dimension was rated as good by 53.7% of participants (Table 2).

Bivariate analysis identified a significant negative correlation between access to health services and the perceived quality of primary care (Rho = − 0.205; p < 0.001). Likewise, significant associations were observed between the perceived quality of primary care and the dimensions of accessibility (Rho = − 0.106; p = 0.018), contact (Rho = − 0.165; p < 0.001), and availability (Rho = − 0.200; p < 0.001). The acceptability dimension showed no statistically significant association (p = 0.073) (Table 3).

The multivariate analysis showed that access to health services was significantly associated with the perceived quality of primary care (B = − 0.133; 95% CI: −0.192 to − 0.075; p < 0.001), after adjusting for sociodemographic variables. This indicates that, for each one-point increase in the score for access to health services, the quality score of primary care decreased on average by 0.13 points, holding the other variables in the model constant. This result indicates that as the perception of barriers increases (higher score), the perceived quality of care significantly decreases. Likewise, travel time to the health facility showed a significant association (B = 0.011; 95% CI: 0.003 to 0.019; p = 0.005), suggesting that each additional minute of travel was associated with a 0.01-point increase in the primary care quality score. No statistically significant associations were observed with sex, age, marital status, educational level, employment status, economic income, or child ownership (Table 4).

On the other hand, in the model that incorporated the dimensions of access to health services, it was identified that availability (B = − 0.202; 95% CI: −0.349 to − 0.054; p = 0.008) and contact (B = − 0.210; 95% CI: −0.383 to − 0.037; p = 0.017) were significantly associated with the perceived quality of primary care. In practical terms, for each one-point increase in the dimensions of availability and contact, the quality score of primary care decreased by approximately 0.20 points, respectively. Acceptability and accessibility did not exhibit statistically significant associations. The travel time to the health facility maintained a significant association in this model (B = 0.014; 95% CI: 0.006 to 0.023; p = 0.001), indicating that each additional minute of travel was associated with an average increase of 0.01 points in the primary care quality score (Table 5).

Discussion

The study showed that access to health services was significantly, though modestly, associated with the perceived quality of primary care, even after adjusting for sociodemographic variables. Multicenter studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries have documented that deficiencies in access often coexist with low levels of quality, especially in domains related to the user experience and the resolving capacity of services [5]. In a complementary way, qualitative research developed in rural communities in Africa and North America has shown that structural barriers, such as the shortage of health personnel, the shortage of medicines, the limited supply of services, and the geographical dispersion of the population, simultaneously affect access and the perceived quality of care, generating fragmented and unsatisfactory care experiences [18, 19].

Likewise, studies conducted in rural populations in Asia have shown that lower access to primary care services is associated with greater health vulnerability and more unfavorable evaluations of the health system, especially among socially vulnerable groups, such as people with lower educational levels and lower incomes [20]. Concurrently, reviews of disparities between rural and urban areas have documented that residing in rural areas is associated with lower service availability, lower continuity of care, and a worse perception of care quality compared to urban contexts [21].

Along the same lines, the positive deviation analysis conducted by Lewis et al. (2023) across 2,929 health facilities in six low- and middle-income countries showed that, even in resource-limited contexts, some centers deliver superior-quality primary care. This improved performance was mainly explained by organizational and systemic factors - such as efficient management, strong institutional leadership, and greater community participation - rather than by individual characteristics of users [22]. Together, these findings reinforce the interpretation that the gaps in the perceived quality of primary care observed in the Llumpa district are fundamentally driven by structural conditions of the health system, closely linked to access to and availability of services.

In the Peruvian context, these results are consistent with those reported in the Amazon, where it has been observed that users who face greater access barriers - particularly those related to geographical distance and limitations in the infrastructure of establishments - report lower levels of satisfaction with the attention received [23].

On the other hand, the finding that availability and contact were the dimensions of access most consistently associated with the perceived quality of primary care is consistent with the international literature. Qualitative studies conducted from the perspective of providers in rural areas have identified that staff shortages, high professional turnover, and work burnout affect the continuity of care and weaken the link between users and health services [18]. Likewise, research on service quality in primary care has shown that insufficient human and material resources limit the services’ ability to meet users’ expectations, deteriorating the users’ global perception of care quality [24]. These findings align with studies across various rural contexts, which show that discontinuity of care and limited service availability negatively affect the user experience [19].

One outcome that warrants special attention is the positive association between travel time to the health facility and the perceived quality of primary care. Although the effect size was small, this finding could be interpreted in light of the ‘Distance-Decay Paradox’ or a ‘selection bias’ inherent to extreme rural settings like Llumpa. Inhabitants who overcome the greatest geographic barriers (often traveling through difficult Andean terrain for over an hour) likely do so only when they perceive a high need for care or have a specific trust in the facility’s resolving capacity. This ‘survivor effect’ suggests that those who successfully reach the health post are more invested in medical encounters, potentially leading to a more favorable evaluation compared to those with easier access but less urgent needs. While previous studies in the Peruvian Amazon [23] and international reviews [21] have reported that greater distances usually correlate with lower satisfaction, our findings suggest that in high-altitude rural territories, the physical act of reaching a provider can be perceived as a ‘success’ that positively biases the user’s perception. These differences highlight how specific territorial and cultural factors mediate the relationship between distance and perceived quality.

Finally, the results of this study must be interpreted in light of certain limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships between access to health services and the perceived quality of primary care. Secondly, the use of instruments based on users’ perceptions may be subject to recall or social desirability biases. Likewise, the application of non-probability sampling limits the generalization of the results to other populations. However, the study provides relevant evidence from an unexplored Andean rural context, with an adequate sample size and an analytical approach that allowed the identification of structural factors associated with the perceived quality of primary care. In conclusion, the findings suggest that significant gaps in access to and perceived quality of primary care persist in rural districts such as Llumpa, particularly related to the availability of services and effective contact with health personnel, highlighting the need to strengthen primary care with a territorial approach, aimed at reducing structural inequalities and improving the experience of users in rural areas.
