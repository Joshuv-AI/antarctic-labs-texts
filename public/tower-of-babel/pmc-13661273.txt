Tissue flow acts as a guidance cue for immune cell polarization and directional migration
Authors: Hoang Anh Le, Jonas Hartmann, Ricard Alert, Roberto Mayor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmbryonic myeloid (EM) cells are the first immune-cell population to emerge during development and must disperse throughout the embryo to act as the first line of defence against infection. Although EM cells migrate directionally toward wounds, how they navigate through unwounded tissues during early colonization remains unclear. Here we show that EM cell dispersion in Xenopus embryos is driven, at least in part, by cell-on-cell migration, an important yet underappreciated phenomenon, guided by dynamic tissue flows. We have established a new ex vivo EM cell migration system that allows for live imaging, computational analyses and optogenetic manipulation. We find that local ectodermal tissue flows repolarize EM cell protrusions and bias their directional migration. Disrupting these flows, both ex vivo and in vivo, either genetically or mechanically, impairs EM cell dispersion. Our findings reveal that mechanical cues generated by surrounding tissue flows coordinate immune-cell migration during development, highlighting an overlooked mechanism by which collective tissue dynamics guide individual cell behaviour.Subject terms: Collective cell migration, Embryology, Amoeboid migration, Monocytes and macrophages

Main

When studying cell migration, we often disproportionally focus on cell–matrix interactions1–7. However, within the crowded tissue environment in vivo, migrating cells also encounter other cells. Historically, studies on cell–cell interactions have been more successful in vivo. In Drosophila melanogaster, border cells form E-cadherin adhesions with surrounding nurse cells to migrate8,9. In zebrafish embryos, the posterior lateral line primordium (pLLP) cells form focal adhesions to the basal surface of the skin as it moves10–13. Studies in this regard have laid the foundation for an important but often overlooked concept, whereby cells can serve as a living substrate for other cells to interact with and migrate along13–16, a phenomenon called cell-on-cell migration.

In vertebrates, embryonic myeloid (EM) cells are among the first to populate early embryos. They clear apoptotic corpses17,18, respond to bacterial infections17, are essential to organ development19–21, and form various tissue-resident macrophage lineages22,23. In Xenopus laevis embryos, EM cells originate from the anterior ventral blood island (aVBI) near the head (the equivalent of the anterior lateral plate mesoderm (ALPM) in zebrafish and the yolk sac in mammals24–26). EM cells initiate their dispersion around stage 20, just after the closure of the neural folds, and populate the entire embryo by stage 27—one of the final stages in the early tailbud phase. Unlike other migratory embryonic cell populations, such as the neural crest, which migrate collectively27–29, EM cells disperse as single cells25. They move along the anterior–posterior and ventral–dorsal axes. Traditionally, the mechanisms governing single-cell migration have often been explored in the context of cancer, where cells use a chemical gradient (such as lysophosphatidic acid30,31) or a stiffness gradient32 as guidance cues. However, the mechanisms that guide single immune-cell dispersion, such as within a developing embryo, remain largely unexplored.

Early Xenopus embryos lack a functional circulatory system, similar to zebrafish17, so dispersing EM cells can migrate in between and underneath ectodermal cells, in between mesodermal cells, and later invade into the deeper endodermal tissue24–26 (Fig. 1a). Cross-sectional images of later-stage embryos show confined bundles of fibronectin separating the ectoderm from the mesoderm, and the mesoderm from the endoderm33–35, rather than interdigitating between these cells, suggesting that EM-cell dispersion through tissue is primarily a cell-on-cell migration process. To dissect the mechanism of how EM cells navigate through dense tissue, we have developed an ex vivo culturing system from Xenopus embryos that preserves some aspects of the natural tissue anatomy and the developmental dynamics of the EM cells while also being highly reproducible and easy to set up. Using live imaging, we show that the ectoderm is necessary to maintain the normal polarized morphology and directional migration of EM cells. Correlation analyses, mathematical modelling and optogenetic experiments reveal that EM-cell protrusion dynamics and directionality are physically biased by the local flow of neighbouring ectodermal cells. Disrupting this flow ex vivo or in vivo substantially disrupts EM-cell dispersion. Overall, our results demonstrate that tissue flow can act as a mechanical cue to guide the migration of embedded single migrating immune cells.

Results

The ectoderm enables EM cell polarity and radial migration

We first established an ex vivo culture of EM cells (Fig. 1b(i)). We labelled the EM cells by microinjecting fluorescent dextran into two C1 blastomeres36,37. The aVBI24–26,37, which contains both ectoderm and mesoderm layers, was dissected and cultured on a fibronectin-coated dish. As the ectoderm adhered and spread, EM cells also radiated from the mesodermal tissue at the centre of the explant (Fig. 1b(ii)). To check whether these EM cells developed normally, we compared their relative emergence time from explants from embryos of different stages (14–15, 19 and 21)38. We found that stage-14–15 EM cells emerged around 6–8 h later than those from stages 19 and 21, which is roughly equal to the time difference found in embryos grown at 16.5 °C (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Video 1). High-resolution imaging showed that EM cells within explants migrated in a 2.5-dimensional (2.5D) to 3D environment within the ectoderm (Extended Data Fig. 1c, magenta arrows and Supplementary Video 2). EM cells were often seen moving up and down and making frequent contacts with the surrounding ectodermal cells and the fibronectin substrate underneath, similar to their distribution in vivo (Extended Data Fig. 1d). In our ex vivo model, the EM precursor cells (Fig. 1b(ii), pink cluster), which are a part of the mesodermal tissue, are less motile than the ectodermal layer, and thus remain at the centre of the explant. When EM cells emerge from this mesodermal tissue, they enter the environment of a moving ectodermal layer. These observations suggest that our explant system faithfully recapitulates the natural development of EM cells and the interactions between EM cells and ectodermal cells found in vivo.

Given the striking radial migration pattern of EM cells following the spreading ectoderm, we first asked how EM cells would behave in the absence of the ectoderm. We fragmented the explants into smaller pieces to release EM cells into the surrounding space outside. We refer to them as free EM cells (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Video 3). Free EM cells are much less persistent and less radially directional than native EM cells (Fig. 1d–f and Extended Data Fig. 1e). Free EM cells also present characteristics of low-adhering cells, with round cell bodies and a jumping motion39,40, whereas native EM cells move in a more streamlined manner. This is reflected in a higher average speed as well as a higher total turning angle for free EM cells (Extended Data Fig. 1f,g). Shape analysis also showed that free EM cells remained relatively round, with a smaller cell area, whereas native EM cells were more elongated with higher average cell area over time (Fig. 1g and Extended Data Fig. 1h).

The difference in shape suggests a change in polarity. Accordingly, we next expressed green fluorescent protein-tagged myosin light chain (GFP-MLC) in EM cells (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Video 4) and found an almost random GFP-MLC distribution in free EM cells, whereas native cells had a clear polarized rearward signal (Fig. 2b–d and Supplementary Video 5).

Overall, these observations suggest a role of the ectoderm in enabling the polarized morphology and the striking radial migration of EM cells ex vivo.

Artificial compression cannot completely recapitulate native EM cells’ morphology and radial migration

As the native EM cells were more elongated than the free EM cells, we hypothesized that this could be due to the compression exerted by the ectodermal layer. We used a glass coverslip to gently compress free EM cells cultured on a 19-kPa polyacrylamide gel crosslinked with fibronectin41,42 (Fig. 3a). Compressed EM cells exhibited a flatter appearance with a large frontal protrusion, and were significantly faster and more persistent than uncompressed cells (Fig. 3b–d and Supplementary Video 6), in agreement with previous reports43–45. This prompted us to ask whether compression alone could recapitulate the distinctive radial migration pattern seen in native EM cells. We performed a side-by-side comparison of compressed versus native EM cells (Fig. 3e). We noticed that while native EM cells still had their streamlined shape, compressed cells had the very stereotypical morphology of a migrating amoeboid cell, with a small round rear end and a prominent frontal protrusion (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Video 7)45–47. High-resolution imaging showed that although both conditions have a rear GFP-MLC signal distribution, compressed EM cells appeared to have a much more compact GFP-MLC signal, unlike the broader, more spread-out signal seen in native EM cells (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b and Supplementary Video 8). Compressed EM cells often had their nucleus at the front instead of being closer to the back as in native EM cells, which is indicative of increased actomyosin contractility48–50 (Extended Data Fig. 2c,d). Compressed EM cells were also significantly faster, possibly due to their exclusive amoeboid mode of migration51, but surprisingly, they remained less persistent and less radially directional than native EM cells (Fig. 3g,h(i,ii,iii)).

Ectodermal tissue flow guides EM cells’ directional migration

We next asked whether the radial migration of EM cells could be explained by chemotaxis. Much like at a wound site, we first hypothesized that the edge of dissected explants might release adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to attract EM cells to migrate outwards radially. Treating explants with the ATPase apyrase affects neither the persistence nor the speed of EM cells (Extended Data Fig. 2e(i,ii) and Supplementary Video 9), suggesting that ATP is unlikely to be involved. Another major chemotactic molecule for macrophages is the macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF or CSF1)52. However, treatment with an anti-CSF1 receptor antibody53,54 only resulted in a marginal reduction in the overall speed, with no significant change in the persistence index of EM cells (Extended Data Fig. 2f(i,ii) and Supplementary Video 10). This suggests that CSF1 is unlikely to contribute significantly to the observed directional migration of EM cells ex vivo. However, ATP and CSF1 are only two of the many possible secreted chemoattractants55,56. So, to tackle the problem in a more general manner, we introduced a constant flow of liquid by slowly draining out the solution at a rate of 3 ml h−1 (Extended Data Fig. 2g and Supplementary Video 11). We reasoned that if any secreted chemoattractant molecules were present, they would be washed out, and this should alter EM cells’ migration behaviour. However, we did not detect any significant change in either the persistence index or the mean speed (Extended Data Fig. 2h(i,ii)). To further rule out any possible attraction coming from the explant edge, we reasoned that if the attraction coming from the edge was notable, excluding EM cell tracking near the edge would significantly decrease the overall persistence index (Extended Data Fig. 2i). Excluding the last frame, the last five frames, or the last ten frames of the migration trajectories of all EM cells indeed reduced the overall spatial coverage57,58 as expected, but did not affect their overall persistence index (Extended Data Fig. 2j(i,ii)). General inhibition of GPCR signalling, another major chemotaxis pathway59, by pertussis toxins (PTX) only marginally reduced EM cells’ mean speed, but again did not change their persistence index (Extended Data Fig. 2k(i,ii) and Supplementary Video 12). Although it is possible that there might be incomplete inhibition, the observed effects are small enough to suggest that chemotaxis is probably not a major player controlling the radial dispersion of EM cells ex vivo.

We next consider the direct interaction between EM cells and ectodermal cells as a possible mechanism. To this end, we treated our explants with heparanase, an enzyme that digests the sugar heparan sulfate that is attached to many transmembrane proteins, which mediates cell–cell interactions and haptotatic signalling60–63. Indeed, EM cells in heparanase-treated explants have a significantly slower mean speed and persistence index compared to untreated explants (Extended Data Fig. 2k(i,ii) and Supplementary Video 12). This suggests that direct interactions between EM cells and ectodermal cells may be important. We then hypothesized that the outward flow of the spreading ectodermal tissue could play a role. To test this, we plated aVBI explants on a low-fibronectin surface64. In this condition, the ectoderm struggled to stably spread and exhibited frequent retractions and slippages, which resulted in a smaller normalized spread area over time compared to the control (Fig. 4a(i,ii) and Supplementary Video 13). We quantified the degree of disorder caused by the reduced fibronectin using Ilastik65 and particle image velocimetry66,67 (Extended Data Fig. 3a(i,ii,iii) and Supplementary Videos 14 and 15). Indeed, both ectodermal tissue and EM cells displayed a more disordered flow field in the low-fibronectin condition compared to the control, represented by a higher average line (the orange dotted line compared to the blue dotted line) in both plots (Extended Data Fig. 3a(ii,iii)). Importantly, EM cells in the low-fibronectin condition became less persistent compared to the control, but their migration speed was not affected (Fig. 4b(i,ii,iii)), suggesting that lowering fibronectin did not directly affect the capacity of EM cells to migrate. We also disrupted ectodermal spreading by either inhibiting focal adhesion kinase (FAK)—a key cell–matrix adhesion molecule with a translation-blocking morpholino—or by scratching away the fibronectin around the explant. All of this resulted in a decreased persistence index, while also decreasing the average speed of native EM cells (Extended Data Fig. 3b–e and Supplementary Videos 16 and 17). These data suggest that destabilizing the ectodermal flow impacts the directional migration of native EM cells.

One hypothesis for how the ectodermal flow could influence EM cell migration is through the remodelling of fibronectin. We first depleted fibronectin in one-half of each embryo using a morpholino and compared the number of dispersed EM cells in the control and injected sides (Fig. 4c(i)). Embryos bent towards the injected side, similar to those injected with fibrillin morpholinos35,68 (Fig. 4c(ii)). Pairwise comparison between the two sides showed no statistically significant difference in the number of EM cells (Fig. 4d), suggesting that fibronectin is not required for in vivo EM cell dispersion. This is consistent with previous studies showing immune cells can still migrate even when focal adhesions cannot be formed69,70. We confirmed the efficacy of the knockdown using immunofluorescence (Extended Data Fig. 3f(i,ii)). Although these observations indicate that EM cells’ dispersion is unlikely to require fibronectin in vivo, we nevertheless examined the idea of fibronectin remodelling by the ectoderm ex vivo. We fixed and stained aVBI explants with a Xenopus-specific anti-fibronectin antibody to assess whether the ectoderm could remodel endogenous fibronectin (Fig. 4e(i)). We found that the endogenous fibronectin was located exclusively within the mesodermal layer with virtually no overlap with EM cells in the ectoderm region (Fig. 4e(ii)). We next stained the explants with a cross-reactive antibody that recognizes the exogenous bovine fibronectin used for coating (Extended Data Fig. 3g). The entire surface was homogeneously stained except for only a few fibronectin fibres located exclusively in the mesoderm, which has already been described as a site of fibronectin remodelling71 (Extended Data Fig. 3G). Most of the EM cells do not colocalize with any anti-fibronectin signal (Fig. 4e(ii)). We then performed live cell imaging using a fluorescently labelled Xenopus-specific fibronectin monoclonal antibody. The antibody, again, showed a strong signal for fibrous fibronectin in the mesoderm of the explants. Labelled EM cells were seen interacting with these fibronectin fibres, but once they left the mesoderm, no fibres were seen associating with these cells (Fig. 4f(i) and Supplementary Video 18). Quantifying the amount of fibronectin within the mesoderm versus the ectoderm shows that virtually all fibronectin fibres remain within the mesoderm, even as time progresses, and the explants expand (Fig. 4f(ii)). We then plated aVBI explants onto a Rhodamine-fibronectin-coated surface to look for signs of exogenous fibronectin remodelling (Fig. 4g). Most explants showed no sign of fibronectin remodelling apart from a small portion of explants that appeared to contain some fibrous structures (Fig. 4g,h, white arrowheads and Supplementary Video 19). However, these fibres were rare and confined to the mesoderm, with little to no interaction with the EM cells. If the ex vivo radial migration of EM cells were to be explained by the ectoderm remodelling of fibronectin, we would have expected to observe a more extensive network of fibres associated with the EM cells, particularly in the ectoderm region. However, this was not the case in all the experiments we performed. We thus conclude that fibronectin does not play a major role in EM cells’ migration in vivo, and that ex vivo, the ectoderm layer does not exhibit any apparent fibronectin remodelling during EM cells’ migration.

To assess the direct mechanical link between ectoderm spreading and EM cell migration, we performed migration correlation analyses between the ectoderm and EM cells ex vivo. We first calculated the trajectory deviation of both ectodermal cells and EM cells relative to their respective Euclidean outward trajectories (Fig. 4i). Unlike raw movement vectors, trajectory deviations take into account the explant’s radial geometry, allowing for an unconfounded assessment of local correlations between EM cells and ectoderm movements (Extended Data Fig. 3h). We performed correlation analyses between ectodermal cells and the local ectoderm averages as an internal control (blue line) and between EM cells and the local ectoderm averages (orange line) (Fig. 4j(i)). As expected for collectively migrating cells, there was a strong correlation between ectodermal cells and their neighbours, represented by a prominent peak (blue line) at 0 min. Interestingly, a smaller but statistically significant peak was also observed for the correlation between EM cells and the neighbouring ectodermal cells (orange line) (Fig. 4j(i,ii) and Supplementary Video 20). There was a shift of ~5 min between the two correlation peaks, suggesting a delay in the response from EM cells to changes in the local ectodermal directionality. This hinted at a causal relationship between local ectodermal flow and EM-cell trajectories. The detected correlation was reproducibly present in both the control and diluted fibronectin conditions, which suggests that this correlation was an innate relationship between EM cells and the ectodermal cells rather than the result of experimental conditions. We found similar behaviour in the cosine similarity (the angles between vectors) between EM cells and the local ectodermal cells, but no correlation was found in the speed component (Extended Data Fig. 3i–l and Supplementary Video 20). To check whether these correlations are also present in vivo, we applied the same analysis pipeline to live embryos. We found no speed correlation but detected a statistically significant correlation in the directionality between the two cell populations (Extended Data Fig. 3m,n and Supplementary Video 21), consistent with previous results. These observations show that ectodermal tissue flow can influence EM cells’ directionality, but not their speed, both ex vivo and in vivo.

We next incorporated EM cells into our model. We assumed that EM cells had their own polarity and velocity vectors. Without ectodermal flow, individual EM cells would be polarized randomly, as observed in our data (Figs. 1c and 2a); hence, there would be no net radial polarity of the EM-cell population. However, ectodermal flow would align EM cells’ polarities in the radial direction. Interestingly, unlike the ectoderm, the radial velocity profile of the EM cell population is rather flat (Fig. 4l(ii)). Local ectodermal flow induces protrusion bias in neighbouring EM cells

Next, we investigate how flow-induced EM-cell polarization happens at the cellular level. We first asked whether ectodermal retraction events could affect the directional migration of EM cells by plating aVBI explants on a low-fibronectin surface. When the ectoderm retracted, the nearby EM cells lost their protrusion and polarity (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Video 22). There was often a slight delay in EM cells’ response before a sudden drop in their protrusion length (Fig. 5b). They then regained their protrusion as the ectoderm spread out again. This suggested that local ectodermal flow could influence EM cells’ directionality by altering protrusion dynamics.

Next, to control tissue flow on demand, we turned to optogenetics. We used a relatively new system called Opto-ILID-p63DH (hereon Opto-IP)81–83, which can induce local contractility and therefore a flow of tissue towards the contracting cell (Fig. 5c,d). We hypothesized that a local contraction-induced tissue flow of the ectoderm should bias the protrusion dynamics of neighbouring EM cells (Fig. 5e). Indeed, when a contraction was activated near the side of a forward-moving EM cell, the cell subsequently changed the direction of its main protrusion to head towards the activated area (Fig. 5f(ia) and Supplementary Video 23). We confirmed that the contraction indeed caused a shift in the position of the ectodermal cells (Fig. 5f(ib)) with a speed of ~0.89 μm min−1 (Extended Data Fig. 4a), which is of the same order of magnitude as a native spreading ectoderm (~0.6 μm min−1) (Extended Data Fig. 4b(i,ii)). Analysis of segmented EM cells clearly shows a change in their protrusion direction84,85 (Fig. 5f(ic) and Supplementary Video 23). A contraction-induced flow from behind is often enough to reverse the EM cell’s migration direction (Fig. 5f(iia,iib,iic) and Supplementary Video 24). To quantify the degree of directional change, we measured the angle α between the cell’s major protrusion axis and the line connecting the cell’s centroid to the centre of the photoactivation region over time (Fig. 5g). The angle α changed from around 60° (before activation), which reflects the lateral position of the photoactivation site relative to the selected EM cell (Fig. 5h(i)), to around 30° at peak contraction (Fig. 5h(i,ii) and Extended Data Fig. 4c(i)), and in many cases, the EM cell’s protrusion became directly aligned with the direction of the contraction-induced flow. We also calculated the average global protrusion velocity of EM cells over time and found an added increase in protrusion activity following photoactivation, suggesting that contraction-induced tissue flow can drive protrusion formation (Extended Data Fig. 4c(ii)). We then asked whether this change in EM cells’ protrusion direction was passive, that is, cells simply being dragged along, or active, that is, cells actively responding to force cues. To test this, we performed the same optogenetic experiment with EM cells expressing the fluorescent filamentous actin reporter Ruby-LifeAct (Extended Data Fig. 4d(i) and Supplementary Video 25). We activated a region of the ectoderm behind a moving EM cell and observed new actin protrusions formed from what was the rear end of the cell (Extended Data Fig. 4d(i,ii,iii)), suggesting an active repolarization mechanism.

Finally, we took our optogenetic system in vivo (Fig. 5i). Upon photoactivation, the ectoderm contracts with an average speed of 0.6 μm min−1, in line with the speed of a moving ectoderm in wildtype embryos (Fig. 5j(ia),(ib) and Extended Data Fig. 4e(i,ii)). This contraction biased a pre-existing protrusion and simultaneously induced a new protrusion in a neighbouring EM cell (Fig. 5j(ic) and Supplementary Video 26). Importantly, this response was not observed in the control condition where no contraction-induced flow was present (Extended Data Fig. 4f and Supplementary Video 27). Measuring the area of protrusion extension and retraction in EM cells over time shows no change in both parameters in the control condition (Fig. 5j(ii), dotted lines), while significant increases over time were observed in the photoactivated condition (Fig. 5j(ii), solid lines). The angle of alignment α of EM cells was similarly decreased from ~80° to ~30°, but not in control embryos (Fig. 5j(iii), green line and blue line).

Overall, these data suggest that local ectodermal tissue flow can induce active repolarization and protrusion bias in neighbouring EM cells both ex vivo and in vivo.

As expected, DD1 expression results in shorter embryos with a curving dorsal side (Extended Data Fig. 5a). Mechanically restricting embryo elongation impairs EM-cell migration in vivo

Although DD1 inhibits convergent extension mainly on the dorsal side, some level of elongation still occurs on the ventral side. We therefore established a strategy to completely inhibit embryo elongation by mechanically constraining them (Fig. 7a). We found that in the unconstrained condition, EM cells freely dispersed in the embryos as they grew longer. However, in the constrained condition, EM cells initially migrated out from the aVBI but, strikingly, once the embryos reached the boundary of the mould and stopped growing, EM cells abruptly lost their protrusions and stopped migrating altogether (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Video 30). There was a trend towards a lower persistence index in constrained embryos but this was not statistically significant, and was probably caused by the initial directional migration before their total loss of motility (Fig. 7c(i)). However, there is a statistically significant drop in the average speed and spatial coverage (Fig. 7c(ii,iii)), and a faster decline in the instantaneous speed over time (Fig. 7d, purple line) in the constrained condition. To observe the co-migration of the ectoderm and the EM cells, as well as the impact of the constraint, we performed high temporal live imaging of constrained embryos labelled for both cell populations. Initially, as the embryo elongated normally, the ectoderm spread from the ventral to the dorsal side, which was followed by the EM cells dispersing in the same direction (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Video 31). However, when the embryo reached the boundary of the mould (cyan dotted line, red box), the ectoderm stopped and was followed soon after by the EM cells (Fig. 7f(i,ii) and Supplementary Video 31). Quantifying the average instantaneous speed of EM cells before and after constraint shows a significant drop (Fig. 7f(iii)). Importantly, the defective migration phenotype could be rescued if the constrained embryos were released and left to recover, as evidenced by the increase in the number of dispersed EM cells, albeit not to the same level as in the unconstrained condition (Fig. 7g,h). Similar to before, no difference in the expression of the differentiation markers spiba (both S and L homologues) and mpo was observed between the three conditions, indicating that mechanically constraining embryos probably did not affect the normal development of EM cells within the time frame of the experiment (Extended Data Fig. 6). A correlation analysis showed a positive linear relationship, with unconstrained embryos having the highest number of single cell count, constrained embryos the lowest, and rescued embryos in the middle (Fig. 7i).

Discussion

In this Article we provide both ex vivo and in vivo evidence to support the hypothesis that local ectodermal tissue flow can act as a guidance cue for the migration of EM cells. The current working model is summarized in Fig. 8. EM cells start off as a cluster of mesenchymal cells at the aVBI. As the embryo grows, EM cells leave the cluster, perhaps in part driven by contact inhibition of locomotion101,102. In the absence of an intact ectoderm, EM cells assume a rounded-up morphology with a scattered myosin signal distribution. In the presence of a non-moving artificial compression, these cells become polarized with a distinctively large frontal protrusion and a concentrated myosin signal at the rear. Their morphology and kinetics, however, do not entirely resemble those seen in native EM cells. Only in the presence of an intact ectoderm can EM cells assume their natural streamlined morphology. The collective movement of the surrounding local ectodermal cells can directly influence EM cells’ protrusion polarization and therefore their direction of migration both ex vivo and in vivo. This flow of tissue is most prominent during embryo elongation, and the directionality of EM cells and the ectodermal cells is correlated with a 5-min delay. Genetically or mechanically interfering with this normal elongation impairs EM cells’ dispersion. Although our experiments were performed in Xenopus embryos, a similar challenge arises in the dispersion of embryonic macrophages in other systems. This includes the migration of equivalent immune cells from the anterior lateral plate mesoderm (ALPM) in zebrafish and from the yolk sac in mammals24–26. In all these cases, these cells may also employ a similar cell-on-cell migration mechanism. Because of the reliance of EM cells’ polarization and directional migration on the surrounding moving tissue, we coin the term ‘collective-assisted directional migration’ as a subset of cell-on-cell migration to describe this phenomenon.

The current working model.

EM cells originate as a cluster from mesodermal tissue at the aVBI in embryos. By a yet to be defined signal (perhaps contact inhibition of locomotion), EM cells start leaving the cluster as single cells. Without an ectoderm, EM cells assume a round shape with a random distribution of MLC signal. In the presence of an artificial compression such as a piece of glass, EM cells assume a fast but less radially migrating amoeboid migration phenotype with a large frontal protrusion, anterior-located nucleus, and a small, localized MLC signal at the rear end, which is indicative of a pressure-driven mode of migration. This phenotype does not resemble the phenotypes observed in native EM cells. Within an intact and moving ectoderm, EM cells move more directionally with the highest radial directionality index. They align with the direction of the surrounding ectodermal cells. They have a more elongated morphology, with an actin-rich frontal protrusion and broader and less constrained MLC localization at their rear. The spreading of ectoderm ex vivo reflects the movement during embryo elongation, which provides an added guidance cue (in addition to other potential cues such as chemotaxis, haptotaxis and durotaxis) to direct EM cell dispersion in the developing embryo.

It is worth noting that EM cells have their own intrinsic motility and do not need a moving ectoderm to be able to migrate. Rather, we propose that the co-movement of the surrounding tissue acts as an additional guidance cue to EM cells, which makes their initial dispersion in embryos more efficient. It is also important to note that our experiments have not specifically considered the co-movement of the endoderm or mesoderm. We cannot visualize beyond two or three cell layers deep in a Xenopus embryo, so analysing their correlation in vivo is not possible. Ex vivo culture might be a possible approach; however, accurately preserving the anatomical organization of all three germ layers poses another challenge. Another question is whether passive dragging could explain collective-assisted directional migration. However, the fact that EM cells actively form actin-rich protrusions in response to the local tissue flow, and that there is a 5-min delay in their response, speaks against this being a passive process. This, however, would make an interesting question for future studies.

We also noted that although we did not find a strong contribution of chemotaxis on EM cells’ dispersion ex vivo, we cannot rule out its contribution in vivo. A gradient of secreted or bound chemoattractants61,103 could still be present within an intact embryo, or EM cells could create their own path through a self-generated gradient104–106. One of the interesting observations is the decrease in both the migration speed and directionality of EM cells when explants were treated with heparanase. Heparanase digests the sugar chain heparan sulfate, which is a typical post-translational modification for cell-surface proteins107,108. Heparan sulfate has many different roles, including regulating protein functions109, protein–protein interactions through binding to and patterning of morphogens62,110, and cell–cell interactions such as during transendothelial migration60,63. EM cells seem to respond to the loss of heparan sulfate, which could be an indication of the existence of a haptotactic gradient of unknown molecules bound to the surface of ectodermal cells111,112. It is also possible that the loss of heparan sulfate directly affects cellular activities or EM cell–ectodermal cell interactions by disrupting glycosylated transmembrane receptors such as syndecan109. Coupled with the fact that EM cells’ migration was altered when the ectoderm was disrupted both ex vivo and in vivo, this supports the hypothesis that physical interactions between EM cells and ectodermal cells are more relevant to their migration in our system.

Although cell-on-cell migration is a widespread phenomenon that has been observed in different model organisms, the exact molecular mechanism for our collective-assisted directional migration remains elusive. In previously described scenarios, cadherin-mediated cell–cell contacts and focal adhesions played an important role in guiding cells along each other8,9. In our case, the molecular identity of the EM cell–ectodermal cell interaction is unknown. EM cells appear to use a mixture of amoeboid and mesenchymal migration113, evidenced by the presence of both lobopodia-like pseudopods114,115 and actin-rich lamellipodia protrusions. This makes identifying a specific cell–cell interaction molecule challenging. However, the idea that particles can align themselves with the motion of surrounding particles has been described and studied extensively in physics72,116,117. Hence, given that EM cells are in such proximity to the moving ectodermal tissue, a non-specific friction-like interaction between the two cell populations could be a possible explanation118,119. This friction could, perhaps, affect EM cells’ actin retrograde flow, which has been strongly correlated with migration persistence120. A recent study also found that the directionality, but not the speed, of the endoderm is correlated with the movement of the mesoderm in zebrafish embryo development121. Expanding on this idea, macrophages have been shown to sense tension within the extracellular matrix created by contracting fibroblasts from a distance122. So perhaps a moving or contracting tissue can generate a differential tension gradient between cells that EM cells can sense and respond to. Alternatively, the flow of tissue could induce polarization of proteins inside EM cells, which can lead to protrusion formation, similar to how ADIP complexes during neural tube closure in Xenopus123, and the proteins Fat2 and Lar124 during Drosophila follicular epithelium development are polarized. Altogether, these examples suggest that collective-assisted directional migration might be conserved in many different biological contexts121.

We also noticed that the behaviour of EM cells is reminiscent of neutrophils during transmigration. In the presence of a shear flow, neutrophils roll on the vessel lumen before forming catch bonds with the endothelial tissue using integrins such as αLβ2, αMβ2 or α4β1125–129. They then flatten out and form actin-rich protrusions to search for a weak spot to transmigrate. On the one hand, free EM cells flatten when entering the ectoderm, which could be due to the interactions with the glycocalyx coat and integrin receptors on ectodermal cells, much like those on blood vessels130. On the other hand, there are also distinct differences between these two scenarios. Unlike luminal fluid, tissue can respond to changes exerted on it from other cells, so the relationship between EM cells and ectodermal cells is most likely two-way. Neutrophils are also not embedded within a moving sheet of heterogeneous and viscoelastic ectodermal cells, which means the physics that act on them can be very different from what is experienced by EM cells. Therefore, it is possible that some of the molecular mechanisms are shared between EM cells and neutrophils, while others may not be, something that future research could address.

Beyond development, collective-assisted directional migration can also be present in other scenarios, such as during wound healing. As mechanical tension could be transmitted through matrix fibres to attract distant macrophages122, a similar mechanism could be at play near the wound edge, where the initial damage chemicals attract distant immune cells, while a mechanical tension propagating between cells could act like a ‘honing-in’ signal to the exact site of injury. During collective cancer invasion, individual cells can align with their neighbours like a nematically active fluid7. In the lymph node, dendritic cells61, T cells and B cells131 have been shown to rely on direct stromal-cell interaction as guidance for their migration. These examples suggest that collective-assisted directional migration might apply to a much broader range of biological contexts.

Overall, our results provide some added insights into how naive immune cells receive and interpret physical cues to navigate the complex environment within embryos. We hope that our findings shed some light on the complex and elusive phenomenon of cell-on-cell migration.

Methods

Our research complies with all relevant ethical regulations.

Xenopus laevis husbandry and in vitro fertilization

Female X. laevis (from NASCO, USA) were preprimed with 100 IU of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG; Intervet) before being induced by injecting with 700 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (Chorulon, Intervet) at least 72 h later. Embryos were collected from at least two female Xenopus laevis frogs on the day of the experiment and fertilized in vitro according to the standard protocol.

Microinjection and labelling of EM cells

Fertilized embryos were dejellied. The EM cells were labelled by microinjecting fluorescent dextran or mRNA into two C1 blastomeres at the 32-cell stage. Embryos were left to heal in FICOL solution for at least 1 h at 18 °C before being transferred to 0.1× Marc’s modified Ringer’s (MMR) solution. The embryos were left to develop at 14.5 °C for ~48 h to reach stage 18. Embryos were selected for correct labelling based on the location of the fluorescence signal at the anterior ventral blood island. The vitelline membrane was then peeled in normal amphibian medium 3/8 (NAM3/8) solution, and explants were dissected.

To specifically target the ectoderm, only the animal blastomeres at the 8-cell stage were injected. Embryos were left in FICOL to recover as described above.

Animal licences were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Board (WERB) at University College London and issued by the Home Office, UK.

aVBI explant system

Explant culturing

The aVBI was dissected from stage-18 embryos in NAM3/8 buffer. The explant is composed of the ectoderm and mesoderm layer, which contains the EM cell population. The dissected explants were then transferred onto a dish coated with 10 μg ml−1 of fibronectin and containing Danilchik’s For Amy (DFA) solution overnight. Explants were oriented using an eyebrow knife so that the ectoderm layer faced up and the mesoderm layer contacted the fibronectin to mimic how the tissues organized in vivo. A glass coverslip was placed on top of the explant, slightly pushed down to promote the adhesion of the explant and to prevent the explants from curling up due to actomyosin contractility from the ectoderm. Explants were left to adhere for at least 3 h at 14.5 °C before the coverslips were carefully removed. The attached explants could be used for downstream experiments within two days.

For dewetting experiments, explants were plated on a surface coated with 0.5 μg ml−1 to 0.6 μg ml−1 fibronectin. Subsequent manipulation was as described above.

Explant immunofluorescence

Cultured aVBI explants were fixed in 4% formaldehyde in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, explants were washed three times in 1× PBS to get rid of excess formaldehyde and were permeabilized by incubation in a solution containing 20 mM glycine and 0.1% Triton-X100 in PBS for 15–30 min at room temperature. The explants were washed three times with 1× PBS, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) with 0.1% Triton-X100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The explants were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in the same blocking solution, with the added primary antibody at 1 in 50 dilution for the anti-Xenopus fibronectin or 1 in 100 dilution for the cross-reactive anti-fibronectin. Next day, the explants were washed three times in PBS and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the secondary antibody, mixed in with the same blocking solution. On the final day, the explants were washed three times with PBS and imaged immediately after.

Long-term time-lapse

The attached explants were imaged using an upright Leica SP8 confocal microscope or an inverted Axiovert 200M microscope for multi-condition experiments. Explants were cultured in 10 ml of DFA solution and were imaged using a ×10 water immersion lens (HC APO 10×/0.3 W, 506142) or ×20 water immersion lens (HCX APO L20×/0.5 W, 506147). Green fluorescence was excited with a 488-nm laser, and red fluorescence was excited with a 568-nm laser, at 3% power for the ×10 lens and 5% laser power for the ×20 lens, and with 800 digital gains. Images were acquired at 1,024 × 1,024 pixels, averaging at 2, every 5 min for 15–20 h in total.

Generating fluorescently labelled antibody and live imaging of fibronectin fibres in aVBI explants

To generate the fluorescently labelled version of the anti-Xenopus fibronectin antibody (4H2), we first purified the antibody from 5 ml of serum at a concentration of 19 μg ml−1 using the affinity chromatography Nab Protein A/G spin kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting antibody eluate was buffer-exchanged using Zeba Spin desalting columns. The resulting eluate was then concentrated to ~100 μl using a Pierce Protein Concentrators PES system with 3-kDa cutoff, before proceeding to be labelled with AlexaFluor 555 using the AlexFluor antibody labelling kit (according to the manufacturer’s protocol).

To label fibronectin in vivo and for live imaging in explants, we microinjected around 20 nl of the purified AlexaFluor 555-labelled anti-Xenopus fibronectin into the mesoderm of the aVBI region in stage-18 embryos. The embryos were left to incubate with the antibody and to heal at 12.5 °C for 1–2 h. The aVBI was then dissected and plated onto a fibronectin-coated dish and imaged with a ×20 water immersion lens on an SP8 confocal microscope as described above.

Protrusion dynamic assay

Explants with labelled EM cells were cultured in fibronectin at a concentration of 0.5–0.6 μg ml−1 to induce dewetting transition. The explants were imaged with the SP8 every 2 min for 20 h using the same set-up as described above. During analysis, only positions with ectoderm retraction events were considered. A line was drawn from a fixed point inside the explant towards the edge of the ectoderm, and this distance was measured. The frame where the ectoderm retracted the furthest was considered frame 0, and the distance was tracked for at least 12 frames before and 12 frames after and normalized to this frame 0. The length of the EM cells near this retraction point was measured and normalized in the same manner.

Optogenetic experiments

Explant optogenetics

For each embryo at the 8-cell stage, two ventral animal blastomeres were injected with 10 pg of Lck-mTurquoise2-iLID-SsrA and 10 pg of SspB-mCherry-p63DH mRNA in FICOL. The embryos were then left to develop into the 32-cell stage before 25 pg of either the membrane-localised red fluorescent protein (RFP-membrane) or Ruby-LifeAct mRNA was injected into the C1 blastomeres. The concentrations of these component mRNAs was optimized to allow for normal embryonic development. One caveat of this approach was that the expression of the optogenetic constructs would be less homogenous. However, the procedure was well-tolerated by the embryos, and we could completely avoid potential cross-contamination if the constructs were also injected into dorsal animal blastomeres, where they could leak into the C1 blastomeres from which EM cells were derived. Because the activation wavelength was in the green channel and we did not have access to a far-red fluorescence protein, we decided to use a red fluorescence protein, either RFP or Ruby, to label the EM cells. We carefully optimized the dose so that the signal from the EM cells was not too dim (which could make it hard to distinguish from the mCherry signal of the Opto-IP constructs) and not so bright that it would saturate any structural detail of the cells. The embryos were left to develop to stage 18 before their aVBI explants were dissected and cultured on 0.5–0.6-μg-ml−1 fibronectin substrate overnight. The low level of fibronectin helped increase the contraction induced by the optogenetic system. To perform optogenetic induction, a region of the ectoderm at around 60° angle to the direction of migration of the EM cell was targeted for contractility induction (Zeiss880 confocal mode, ×40 oil lens, wavelength 454 nm, 5% laser power, 1-s interval, started at frame 16 and repeated every five frames until the end). Both the ectoderm and EM cells were imaged with 568-nm wavelength, 5% laser power and a digital gain of 700.

To measure the angle, the centroid of the activated region was first acquired. Then, by using the angle tool, the angle was measured between the line connecting the centre of the activated region and the centroid of the cell (manually estimated) and the line connecting the cell’s centroid and the cell’s major protrusion. This angle was measured every ten frames.

To estimate the contraction speed, ectodermal cells at frame 15 or 16 (just before or just after optogenetic induction occurs) were manually segmented. The choice of frame 15 or 16 depended on whether a cell boundary could be easily identified and was under the assumption that the positions of ectodermal cells at the induction site were still relatively unchanged. The same cells were segmented at the final frame for each movie. The displacement distance between the centroid of each cell between the first frame and the last frame was measured. A z-interval of 1 μm was used to ensure EM cells in the deeper tissue layer were captured. Imaging conditions are as follows: Zeiss880 confocal mode, ×40 oil lens, wavelength 454 nm, 10% laser power, consecutive interval, started at frame 2, and repeated every frame until the end. Single images

The cytoplasm and membrane images were fed into the Fiji plugin ADAPT, where the protrusion dynamics were detected and analysed. The overall protrusion velocity for each cell was plotted over time.

Flow experiment

Labelled explants were cultured in a row on a 10-μg-ml−1 fibronectin-coated dish containing 10 ml of DFA solution. The explants were left at 21.5 °C for a few hours to allow for the initiation of EM migration. An outlet was connected to one side of the dish. The explants were imaged for ~3 h before the draining of the DFA solution started at a rate of 3 ml h−1 until all the liquid was drained and the imaging stopped.

Compression experiment

To make polyacrylamide gels, glass slides were first coated with a bind-silane solution overnight. The glass slides were washed three times in 70% ethanol and left to air dry, then 13-mm glass coverslips were incubated with repel-silane solution overnight and left to air dry the next day. For a 19-kPa polyacrylamide gel, 100 μl of solution mixture was made by mixing these solutions in order: 63.4 μl of distilled H2O, 20 μl of 40% acrylamide solution, 13.2 μl of 2% bis-acrylamide, 2.4 μl of 6-acrylamidohexanoic acid (AHA), 0.5 μl of tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) and 0.5 μl of 10% ammonium persulfate solution. For each gel, 10 μl of the mixture was pipetted onto the bind-silane glass slide before a repel-silane coverslip was placed on top. The gel was left to solidify for 12 min, then the coverslips were gently removed. The gels were briefly washed three times with 100% methanol before being rehydrated three times with 200 μl of 1× PBS, 5 min each at room temperature. For each gel, 50 μl of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) was added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Each gel was washed three times with 1× PBS for 5 min each, before being coated in 100 μl of a solution of 100-μg-ml−1 fibronectin in 1× PBS overnight at 4 °C in a humidity chamber. On the day of the experiment, the gels were equilibrated in 100 μl of DFA solution. No PBS should be left on the gel as it is not compatible with Xenopus cells.

Labelled aVBI explants were dissected from stage-18 embryos, then chopped into smaller pieces. The chopped explants were transferred into 1 ml of DFA to equilibrate. Excess DFA on the gels was drained. Using a P20 Gilson pipette, 10 μl of the chopped explant in DFA suspension was added onto the gel, then a 22-mm × 22-mm square glass coverslip with grease around the edges was placed on top and gently pressed down.

In vivo grafting experiment

Acceptor embryos were either wildtype or injected with 300 pg of the dominant negative Wnt signalling protein (derived from a mutant Dishevelled protein called DD1) into four animal blastomeres. Donor embryos were whole labelled with fluorescence dextran by injecting at the 2-cell stage. Both donor and acceptor embryos were at stage 16 before grafting. Acceptor embryos were partially immobilized in plasticine moulds. The aVBI explant was dissected from the donor embryos and grafted onto the aVBI region of the acceptor embryos in NAM3/8. A small piece of glass was placed on top to promote healing. The grafted embryos were left to recover in NAM3/8 at 14.5 °C for at least 1 h before being imaged with the SP8 confocal microscope. The embryos were positioned in channels made from agarose. A layer of 3% methyl cellulose dissolved in 0.1× MMR was carefully pipetted on top to reduce embryo twitching and movement during the imaging period, then 10 ml of NAM3/8 mixed with tricaine (0.24 mg ml−1) was carefully pipetted on top. The embryos were left to settle before being imaged with an SP8 confocal system. The imaging set-up was as follows: ×10 water immersion lens, 488-nm laser at 6% laser power, z-stack with 25-μm interval, acquired every 9 min for 15–20 h. Only healthy embryos with enough labelled EM cells were included in the analysis. Healthy embryos were defined as those that remained intact for the majority of the imaging period.

Ex vivo grafting experiment

To recapitulate the same conditions as used in the in vivo grafting experiment, aVBI explants with labelled EM cells were placed adjacent to explants taken from the lateral region of the acceptor embryo on a dish coated with 10 μg ml−1 of fibronectin in DFA labelled with RFP-membrane. The explants were left to adhere and spread for at least 2 h before being imaged with the SP8 confocal microscope.

Embryo constraining experiment

aVBI explants from labelled donor embryos were grafted onto wildtype acceptor embryos as already described. Stage-18 embryos were placed in plasticine moulds with a glass coverslip placed on top to prevent them from slipping out. Unconstrained embryos were left in moulds with a free opening for normal elongation, and constrained embryos were placed in moulds with this end blocked. Embryos were imaged in NAM3/8 with tricaine as described above. Only healthy embryos with enough labelled EM cells were included in the analysis. Healthy embryos were defined as those that remained intact for most of the imaging period. Only healthy constrained embryos that exhibited no significant damage were included in the rescue experiment.

Quantifying cell numbers in ISH embryos

Images of ISH embryos from the side were quantified. aVBI was defined as the region near the head just below the cement gland in the embryo. This region was excluded from the analysis. Single EM cells outside the aVBI were counted using the Find Maximum function with an appropriate threshold in Fiji.

To quantify the number of dispersed EM cells between the control and fibronectin morpholino-injected side, only embryos with a midline that could be confidently defined were included in the analysis to avoid potential biases.

Cryosectioning

The embryos were dehydrated in a 10% sucrose solution overnight at 4 °C. The next day, they were placed in cryosection plastic moulds filled with a 1:1 mixture of OCT and 10% sucrose, oriented to the desired orientation, then transferred to −80 °C to freeze overnight. Sections of 30-μm thickness were collected and adsorbed onto a glass slide. The slides were baked at 37 °C for 1 h or until dry. If the embryos were double-fixed in formaldehyde prior to cryosectioning, an antigen retrieval step was performed before any immunofluorescence staining was performed. To retrieve antigens, the slides were incubated in 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) in 1× PBS for 10 min at room temperature then washed with copious amounts of 1× PBS to ensure no SDS remained. The samples were permeabilized in 1× PBS + 0.1% TritonX-100 for 15 min at room temperature and blocked in 1× PBS + 0.1% TritonX-100 + 5% BSA for 1 h. The samples were incubated with the appropriate primary antibody at 4 °C overnight in a humidified chamber to prevent drying out. Next day, the samples were washed three times in 1× PBS + 0.1% TritonX-100 + 5% BSA before being incubated with the secondary antibody at 4 °C overnight. Finally, samples were washed three times in 1× PBS + 0.1% TritonX-100 and mounted in mounting medium.

To quantify the stained fibronectin signal between the control and the fibronectin morpholino-injected side, a straight line going from the notochord to the ventral side was drawn to divide the cryosection in half. The threshold was manually adjusted to detect and measure the fibronectin area between the two halves. Only sections with a relatively intact ectoderm were included in the analysis.

MATLAB vector analysis

aVBI explants were labelled with H2B-mCherry for the ectodermal cells and AlexaFluor 488 fluorescence dextran for EM cells. Ectodermal nuclei and EM cells were segmented using Ilastik, and the resulting images were converted into binary. The binary images were imported into the PIVlab app (v3.06) in MATLAB (vR2024b 24.2.02740171) to generate vector direction plots. The angle between the velocity vectors of the cells and the coordinate system of the input image, which starts at the top left corner with the x axis (u-component) pointing right and the y axis (v-component) pointing down, was calculated and plotted from 180° to −180°. For a smooth and persistent migrating explant, we expected to see a more homogeneous and stable vector direction field, whereas for a more disordered migrating explant, we expected to see a more heterogeneous vector direction field. To quantify the degree of disorder, the colour maps of the vector field were converted into binary, and the area where the direction changed was quantified over time and converted to percentages.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed with the PowerUp SYBR Green master mix (Applied Biosystems, 25742). Trajectory deviation, speed correlation and cosine similarity analyses

EM cells were manually tracked using the MTrackJ plugin, while the ectoderm was automatically tracked using TrackMate133,134 with StarDist135. For processing and analysis with Python, tracks were ingested into pandas data frames, and ectoderm track stubs (<10 time points) were removed. For ex vivo data, quality control excluded one outlier explant due to it being unhealthy. For in vivo data, where tracking was much harder, only samples where a sufficient number of EM cells (≥10) could be tracked for a sufficiently long time (≥20 frames) were included, resulting in n = 7 embryos out of 11 that were imaged across two separate experiments. Average local ectoderm velocity vectors were derived for the neighbourhood of each ectoderm cell and each EM cell by applying a Gaussian kernel (σ = 40 µm) to the surrounding ectodermal cell velocities, which favours the contribution of nearby ectodermal cells to the local average. Time-shift correlations (for trajectory deviations and for cell speeds) and cosine similarities measurements (for directional angles) between ectodermal cells and local ectoderm averages and between EM cells and local ectoderm averages were computed using the pandas corr method and scikit-learn’s paired_cosine_distances function, respectively. When calculating averages of these metrics over time, early time points (<80 ex vivo, <70 in vivo) and late time points (>180 ex vivo, >140 in vivo) were truncated away to preclude boundary effects. To calculate radial profiles from ex vivo data for comparison to model predictions, cluster centres were determined as the centroid of all ectoderm positions, rays from the centre to each cell were constructed, and all positions and vectors were rotated around the centre such that the centre-cell rays collapsed onto a single axis. All Python code to reproduce this analysis is available under the MIT open source software license on GitHub at https://github.com/WhoIsJack/ectoderm-myeloid-tracking-analysis.

Statistical analysis

All graphs were plotted using the ggplot2 R package or Matplotlib from Python. Two-tailed unpaired t-tests were used to compare between two groups. ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison was used to compare between three or more conditions. The n (number of cells analysed) and N (number of embryos) values are shown in each figure legend. We anticipated that the effect size of the observed phenotype would be in the range of small to medium at the explant level based on preliminary data. We calculated the hypothetical number of explants (or embryos) needed to detect an effect size of 0.3 (medium) at a significance level (alpha) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.8 to be 175 explants (or embryos). This was impractical to perform. Therefore, to reduce the use of animals in experimentation, as well as to capture the full spectrum of variables between individual cells, we decided to use the n number for statistical analyses (unless specified otherwise). To reduce the chance of a false positive and ensure the consistency and reproducibility of the observed effects, we drew our conclusions from experiments that were performed in multiple different contexts and experimental set-ups, as well as from embryos collected from at least three different female frogs on separate days. Analyses were carried out blind whenever possible, with embryos or explants being represented as numbers while being analysed, and embryos randomly mixed from different frogs to ensure maximal variations were captured. Nevertheless, treatments with morpholinos often result in changes in their morphology so blind analyses may not always be possible. The data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. Statistical tests were chosen following guidance from the Handbook of Biological Statistics136.
