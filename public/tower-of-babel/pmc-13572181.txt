Chicken hepatic injury and food safety: From etiological drivers to pathogenic mechanisms
Authors: Qi Liu, Xiangyu Xiao, Minghui Li, Ke Ding, Zuhua Yu, Jian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First systematic classification of etiological drivers of chicken liver injury.•Common mechanisms: oxidative stress, inflammation, lipid disorders, cell death via gut-liver axis.•Highlights food safety risks and proposes control strategies along production chain.

Introduction

Chicken liver ranks among the world’s most widely consumed edible offal, with a per capita annual consumption of approximately 0.5 kg in Europe (Czarnowska-Kujawska et al., 2020). It is rich in highly bioavailable heme iron, vitamin A and vitamin B12, and is extensively used in infant complementary foods, nutritional supplements for pregnant and lactating women, as well as pet food (Goluch et al., 2025; Beal et al., 2021; Zuhairini et al., 2025; Pinto et al., 2022; Sezerotto et al., 2025). Full utilization of poultry liver and other by-products can effectively reduce food waste and mitigate global hunger. Especially in developing countries, edible poultry offal helps alleviate malnutrition and enhance food security (Alao et al., 2017). Nevertheless, as a core organ responsible for detoxification, metabolism and immune regulation in chickens, the liver is highly vulnerable to damage induced by various environmental stressors and pathogens (Zaefarian et al., 2019). In recent years, the rapid expansion of intensive farming has exposed chickens to an increasingly complex and diverse array of hepatotoxic stressors. These factors can be systematically categorized as follows: nutritional imbalances (e.g., high-energy low-protein diets, amino acid and vitamin deficiencies), mycotoxins (aflatoxin B₁, ochratoxin A, deoxynivalenol, zearalenone), heavy metals (mercury, lead, cadmium, arsenic), irrational use of veterinary drugs (florfenicol, azithromycin), organic pollutants (decabromodiphenyl ether, acetochlor), microplastics, as well as infectious pathogens (avian leukosis virus, Marek’s disease virus, fowl adenovirus, avian hepatitis E virus, Salmonella, Escherichia coli, Pasteurella multocida, and parasites such as Histomonas meleagridis and Leucocytozoon spp.). These stressors often act alone or in combination, triggering chronic liver injury, metabolic dysfunction and even hepatic tumors in chickens. The consequences of chicken liver injury extend far beyond the lesion of the organ itself, also posing food safety risks (Table S1). On the one hand, impaired detoxification function leads to toxin residues, which directly contaminate poultry liver products. On the other hand, affected chickens exhibit marked reductions in feed intake and average daily gain, resulting in substantial economic losses (Sousa et al., 2020; El-Abd et al., 2025). Nevertheless, despite the growing number of clinical cases, current systematic understanding of the etiological spectrum, molecular mechanisms and interactions underlying chicken liver injury remains highly fragmented. It remains unclear whether diverse stressors induce liver damage via shared cell death pathways, including apoptosis, pyroptosis and ferroptosis, and what the key regulatory nodes are for the progression from hepatic steatosis to fibrosis and tumorigenesis.

This narrative review systematically integrates existing knowledge concerning the etiology, pathogenic mechanisms, and food safety impacts of hepatic injury in chickens. Peer-reviewed studies published between 1980 and 2026 were retrieved from scientific databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Search keywords were as follows: chicken liver, hepatic injury, hepatotoxicity, mycotoxins, toxic contaminants, nutritional-metabolic disorders, infectious diseases, oxidative stress, inflammatory response, apoptosis, ferroptosis, lipid metabolism, gut-liver axis, and food safety.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they investigated naturally occurring or experimentally induced hepatic injury in chickens, reported alterations in physiological, pathological, metabolic, or immune indicators, or evaluated the effects of nutritional factors, toxic contaminants, environmental stressors, or pathogenic infections on hepatic damage. Studies directly relevant to poultry health, production performance, molecular mechanisms, and food safety risks were prioritized, consolidating evidence from toxicology, pathology, immunology, molecular biology, and food safety disciplines.

This review systematically classifies the etiological factors and pathogenic mechanisms underlying chicken hepatic injury, with particular emphasis on the molecular signaling cascades and regulatory networks induced by hepatotoxic contaminants, and further addresses the associated food safety risks throughout the production chain, thereby providing a theoretical basis for integrated risk control and safety assessment.

Etiological overview of chicken hepatic injury: a multifactorial syndrome

With the rapid development of intensive farming, modern commercial chickens are exposed to increasingly complex and diverse hepatotoxic stressors. These etiological factors can be systematically classified into four major categories: nutritional-metabolic factors, toxicant exposure, pathogenic microbial infections, and environmental stress. Although the etiological spectrum is very broad, these seemingly distinct causes ultimately activate highly overlapping downstream pathological pathways, mainly oxidative stress, inflammatory response, lipid metabolic disturbance, and programmed cell death. This characteristic of divergent etiologies and convergent pathways provides a theoretical basis for developing intervention strategies targeting these common pathways.

Nutritional-metabolic factors

Nutritional-metabolic factors represent important triggers of non-infectious hepatic injury. High-energy and low-protein diets can induce hepatocyte damage and hepatic hemorrhage, accompanied by excessive lipid accumulation and elevated inflammatory cytokines (Bona et al., 2018; Du et al., 2024). This process involves riboflavin and pantothenic acid deficiency induced by disturbed gut microbiota metabolism, which further triggers lipid metabolic disorders and oxidative stress (Mi et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024). Amino acid imbalance also exerts marked hepatic effects: excess lysine may competitively inhibit arginine transport, interfere with nitric oxide synthesis and promote oxidative stress (Jia et al., 2019); methionine deficiency simultaneously elicits oxidative stress and reduces hepatic lipid export, thereby aggravating lipid accumulation (Ma et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2018). Vitamin and mineral deficiencies should also not be overlooked: riboflavin deficiency causes extensive hepatic hemorrhage and impaired yolk lipid mobilization in chicken embryos (Cogburn et al., 2018); folate deficiency promotes de-novo fatty-acid synthesis and reduces triglyceride hydrolysis and export by perturbing the insulin/insulin-like growth factor (IGF) signaling-phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT)-mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)-sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP-1c) pathway, DNA methylation and autophagy (Liu et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019; Du et al., 2023); selenium deficiency induces hepatocyte pyroptosis through reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) pathway activation (Gu et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025a). Low calcium combined with high phosphorus induces development-specific necrosis of liver tissue in chickens (Ono et al., 1986). Fatty liver syndrome and its progressive form, fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome, are typical manifestations of nutritional-metabolic imbalance. Their pathogenesis involves lipid metabolic disorders and inflammatory responses mediated by estrogen, high-energy low-protein diets and choline deficiency (Shini et al., 2020; Cui et al., 2024; Yadav et al., 2024).

Toxicant exposure

Toxicant exposure represents another major category of etiological factors for chicken hepatic injury, including mycotoxins, heavy metals, organic pollutants, veterinary drug residues and microplastics. Among mycotoxins, aflatoxin B₁ is bio-activated by hepatic phase-I metabolic enzymes to exert potent toxicity, triggering hepatocyte degeneration and necrosis as well as liver fibrosis, with the processes involving oxidative stress, necroptosis and suppression of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling pathway (Liu et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2025). Ochratoxin A induces free-radical generation and down-regulates the expression of most Nrf2-dependent genes, leading to insufficient antioxidant defense and mitochondrial dysfunction (Han et al., 2019; Kövesi et al., 2025). Deoxynivalenol elicits oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation via inhibiting protein synthesis, and exerts its toxic effects mainly through the Ras-associated protein 1 (Rap1)/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway (Zhuang et al., 2026). Zearalenone can simultaneously activate oxidative stress, inflammatory response and apoptosis (Xu et al., 2021). Heavy metals including mercury, lead, cadmium, arsenic and copper exhibit well-defined hepatotoxicity. Their shared mechanisms involve disturbance of the Nrf2-Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) pathway, oxidative stress and endoplasmic-reticulum stress, which further induce autophagy or apoptosis (Ma et al., 2018a; Wang et al., 2016; Shao et al., 2014; Li et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024). Organic pollutants such as decabromodiphenyl ether, trimethyltin chloride and acetochlor provoke hepatic injury through aberrant activation of the MAPK/wingless-related integration site (WNT) pathway or endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-Ca²⁺/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) signaling-mediated ferroptosis (Yang et al., 2023; Su et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b). Improper application of veterinary drugs, such as exposure to high-dose azithromycin, florfenicol and furazolidone, can impair liver function via oxidative stress and apoptosis (Fotouh et al., 2024; Han et al., 2020; Sasaki et al., 2013). As an emerging class of environmental pollutants, microplastics can disrupt the intestinal vascular barrier, promote lipopolysaccharide (LPS) accumulation, and modulate patterns of programmed cell death in hepatocytes, thereby triggering hepatic immune responses and lipid metabolic disturbance via the gut-liver axis (Yin et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2025).

Pathogenic microbial infections

Pathogenic microbial infections also pose a serious threat to the liver of poultry. Subgroup J avian leukosis virus (ALV-J) infection causes focal lymphocyte infiltration in the chicken liver, a process tightly regulated by m⁶A methylation modification (Xu et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022). The incidence of Marek’s disease virus (MDV)-induced liver tumors is second only to that in the spleen, and disease onset is accompanied by extensive reprogramming of lipid, carbohydrate and amino-acid metabolism as well as the p53 signaling pathway (Zhi et al., 2025). Fowl adenovirus serotype 4 (FAdV-4) triggers outer mitochondrial membrane rupture and cytochrome c release, inducing hepatocyte apoptosis, autophagy and robust inflammatory responses (Niu et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2025). Avian hepatitis E virus (aHEV) activates the TLR-JNK-NF-κB signaling pathway and the caspase-1 inflammasome (Zhang et al., 2023). Reticuloendotheliosis virus (REV) infection can induce hepatic neoplastic lesions, but the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood (Xu et al., 2020; Shosha et al., 2024). Bacterial pathogens including Salmonella, avian pathogenic Escherichia coli, Pasteurella multocida and Campylobacter activate the NLRP3 inflammasome via the LPS-Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) axis, further triggering pyroptosis and necroptosis (Coble et al., 2013; Shi et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2017; van Engelen et al., 2026). At the parasite level, infections with Histomonas meleagridis and Leucocytozoon give rise to multifocal necrotizing hepatitis and granulomatous inflammation (Lotfi et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2016).

Environmental stress factors

In terms of environmental stress, with the continuous rise in global temperature, heat stress has become a major bottleneck restricting the efficient production of poultry. As a form of environmental stress, heat stress can induce secondary hepatic metabolic disorders. Chronic heat stress activates the TLR4-NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways, up-regulates pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, and triggers endoplasmic reticulum stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. It modulates the Nrf2-Keap1 pathway and the expression of ferroptosis-related genes, ultimately aggravating hepatic oxidative damage (Liu et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2022; Jing et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024).

Core pathogenic mechanisms: a common endpoint converged by multiple pathways

Oxidative stress

Oxidative stress is one of the earliest and most universal pathological events observed during chicken hepatic injury. Because the liver is the primary metabolic and detoxification organ in poultry, hepatocytes possess abundant mitochondria and cytochrome P450 enzymes that continuously generate ROS during physiological metabolism. Under normal conditions, ROS are efficiently eliminated by endogenous antioxidant systems. However, excessive ROS production or impaired antioxidant defense disrupts redox homeostasis, resulting in oxidative stress that subsequently triggers lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and multiple downstream pathological processes. Heavy-metal exposure and FAdV-4 infection (Chi et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2025) all induce mitochondrial dysfunction accompanied by massive ROS accumulation. Poultry exhibit high activity of phase-I metabolic enzymes yet relatively weak detoxification capacity of phase-II detoxifying enzymes such as glutathione S-transferase (GST), rendering them highly susceptible to aflatoxins (Neal et al., 1981; Klein et al., 2002). AFB₁ undergoes biotransformation by CYP450, with concurrent generation of reactive oxygen species (Wang et al., 2023). Furazolidone can up-regulate the expression of multiple CYP450 isoforms, accelerating metabolic activation of exogenous toxicants and elevating ROS levels (Sasaki et al., 2013). OTA activates NADPH oxidase in chicken heterophils to promote ROS release (Han et al., 2019). Salmonella can cause chronic hepatic infection in chickens and trigger abundant free-radical-derived ROS production, which damages cell membranes (Biazus et al., 2017). Selenium deficiency can also induce the production of ROS (Zhang et al., 2025a).

The Nrf2-Keap1 pathway serves as the core regulatory cascade for endogenous antioxidant defense. Under physiological conditions, Nrf2 stably binds to Keap1 and undergoes continuous degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Upon oxidative-stress stimulation, cysteine residues of Keap protein are oxidatively modified; dissociated Nrf2 translocates into the nucleus and binds to ARE cis-acting elements to initiate transcription of antioxidant enzymes and phase-II detoxifying enzymes, including SOD, CAT, glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1). Multiple hepatic-damaging stimuli suppress this pathway to varying degrees. Among mycotoxins, aflatoxin B₁ not only blocks nuclear translocation of Nrf2 but also down-regulates the expression of chaperone proteins v-maf avian musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma oncogene homologs F and K (MafF and MafK), which prevents Nrf2 from forming functional heterodimers and consequently hampers transcription of downstream antioxidant genes (Ye et al., 2025). Ochratoxin A broadly inhibits mRNA transcription of Nrf2 target genes, directly resulting in defective antioxidant systems (Han et al., 2019; Kövesi et al., 2025; Kulcsár et al., 2025). Mercury exposure persistently disturbs Nrf2-Keap1 homeostasis (Ma et al., 2018a). Florfenicol administration and chronic heat stress also repress Nrf2 pathway activation and exacerbate the progression of hepatic oxidative injury (Han et al., 2020; Jing et al., 2023).

Following functional disruption of antioxidant defense systems, excess ROS triggers irreversible chemical modification of various biomacromolecules. ROS attack polyunsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes to produce lipid peroxides such as malondialdehyde and 4-hydroxynonenal, disrupting the bilayer structure and permeability properties of cell membranes (Zhao et al., 2019; Kulcsár et al., 2025). Oxidative modification alters protein side-chain groups and induces protein carbonylation, directly impairing catalytic activity of enzymes and cytoskeleton stability (Sun et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2019). Continuous accumulation of misfolded proteins further provokes endoplasmic reticulum stress. ROS attack nucleic-acid bases to generate oxidative-damage markers such as 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (Gao et al., 2022). Sustained DNA damage increases the risk of genomic mutation, which is a process potentially associated with the progression from chronic hepatic injury to liver tumors. Lacking histone protection and with limited DNA-repair capability, mitochondria are more vulnerable to ROS assault. Mitochondrial damage further impairs respiratory-chain operation and establishes a self-amplifying injury loop (Niu et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2025).

Beyond direct biomacromolecular damage, oxidative stress acts as a redox-sensitive signal to modulate downstream inflammatory and cell-death pathways. ROS activates signaling molecules related to NF-κB and the NLRP3 inflammasome to mediate local hepatic inflammatory responses. Meanwhile, it increases mitochondrial outer-membrane permeability to trigger cytochrome C release and initiate apoptotic cascades. It participates in iron-dependent lipid peroxidation to govern ferroptosis, continuously consumes intracellular reduced glutathione, and heightens the susceptibility of hepatocytes to diverse forms of programmed cell death. In vivo intervention studies in poultry have confirmed that targeted modulation of the Nrf2 pathway can alleviate hepatic oxidative damage induced by diverse etiological factors (Li et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017; Ding et al., 2023).

Inflammatory response

There exists a bidirectional interplay between oxidative stress and inflammation. ROS initiates multiple inflammatory signaling pathways, while activated macrophages and heterophils undergo respiratory burst to generate abundant ROS, forming a sustained positive-feedback regulatory loop. Mild initial injury can progressively develop into extensive hepatic tissue destruction under this cyclic effect. The TLR4/NF-κB signaling axis constitutes a core regulatory cascade for the initiation of hepatic inflammation in poultry, which can be activated by diverse endogenous and exogenous stimuli. Intestinal-derived LPS serves as a classical activating mediator (Morrison and Ryan, 1987). High-energy low-protein diets, zearalenone, polystyrene microplastics, arsenic exposure and avian pathogenic Escherichia coli infection all disrupt intestinal epithelial barrier integrity and reshape gut microbiota composition, facilitating translocation of Gram-negative bacterial LPS to the liver via the portal vein (Mi et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2022). Lipopolysaccharide is then recognized by TLR4 on the surface of Kupffer cells, triggering myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88)-dependent signal cascades and ultimately driving NF-κB activation (Huang et al., 2017). Damaged hepatocytes can also release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to trigger inflammatory pathways. Strong positive HMGB1 signals have been detected in the nuclei of hepatocytes and Kupffer cells surrounding necrotic foci during Pasteurella multocida infection (Tang et al., 2017). AFB₁-induced necroptosis is accompanied by plasma membrane rupture and leakage of cytoplasmic contents (Li et al., 2022), and OTA also causes hepatocellular and nuclear injury (Han et al., 2019). These injuries may further amplify inflammatory responses via DAMP release. Activated NF-κB translocates into the nucleus and initiates transcription of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, as well as various chemokines and adhesion molecules. Abundant inflammatory factors recruit peripheral neutrophils and monocytes to infiltrate the hepatic parenchyma, leading to distinct inflammatory foci (Cheng et al., 2017). Heat-stress-induced pathway activation exhibits time-dependent characteristics: short-term heat exposure rapidly elevates pathway activity, whereas adaptive negative-feedback regulation is triggered under prolonged stimulation, with the activation levels of NF-κB and NLRP3 gradually reaching a steady state (Liu et al., 2022).

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a key multiprotein complex mediating inflammatory amplification, and its full activation requires two sequential signals. The priming signal, typically triggered by pattern-recognition receptor ligands (represented by TLR ligands), up-regulates the basal expression of NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β. The activation signal is derived from danger-associated stimuli including reactive oxygen species accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, intracellular potassium efflux, and lysosomal rupture. These two signals cooperatively drive inflammasome assembly and subsequent caspase-1 activation (Broz and Dixit, 2016). Activated caspase-1 cleaves pro-IL-1β, pro-IL-18 and gasdermin D (GSDMD). GSDMD-N mediates plasma membrane pore formation, facilitating the release of mature IL-1β and IL-18 and triggering pyroptosis (Shi et al., 2024). Heat stress delivers the second signal via mitochondrial ROS and endoplasmic-reticulum stress (Ma et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Selenium deficiency induces calcium homeostasis disturbance and massive ROS generation through the miR-138-5p/SelM pathway to sustain NLRP3 activation (Sun et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025a). Avian pathogenic Escherichia coli, ZEA, aHEV and ALV-J can induce inflammasome assembly and activation through distinct intermediate links (Shi et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023a; Liu et al., 2016a).

Programmed cell death

Apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis and ferroptosis have all been demonstrated in studies of poultry liver injury, and these modes of cell death exhibit distinct features in morphology, molecular mechanisms and immunological consequences. Apoptosis is mediated by caspase-family proteases without marked inflammatory exudation. Its typical morphological features include cell shrinkage, nuclear fragmentation and apoptotic-body formation, and apoptotic bodies can be cleared by adjacent phagocytes (Elmore, 2007). Lead exposure up-regulates Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax) and down-regulates B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), disturbs the Bax/Bcl-2 expression balance, alters mitochondrial outer-membrane permeability, promotes cytochrome-c release, and sequentially activates caspase-9 and caspase-3 (Wang et al., 2016; Chi et al., 2017). Cadmium exposure induces apoptosis in chicken hepatocytes, accompanied by altered GRP78 expression and disrupted intracellular calcium homeostasis (Shao et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2017). Zearalenone up-regulates the expression of caspase-3 and caspase-11, thereby inducing hepatocyte apoptosis (Xu et al., 2021). Trimethyltin chloride increases the expression of caspase-3, caspase-8 and caspase-9 (Su et al., 2024). FAdV-4 causes mitochondrial outer-membrane rupture and cytochrome-c release, and triggers hepatocyte apoptosis (Niu et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2025). Pasteurella multocida induces caspase-3 up-regulation and activates apoptosis (Tang et al., 2017; Vucur et al., 2013).

Pyroptosis is mainly characterized by caspase-1-dependent inflammasome activation. Activated caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD to form pores in the cell membrane, driving robust release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 and amplifying local inflammatory responses (Shi et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2025). Selenium deficiency triggers ROS production and NF-κB pathway activation, up-regulates the NLRP3 inflammasome, and induces hepatocyte pyroptosis (Gu et al., 2022). Avian pathogenic Escherichia coli-derived LPS challenge markedly increases the mRNA levels of NLRP3, caspase-1 p20, IL-1β and IL-18 in chicken liver and induces pyroptosis (Shi et al., 2022). Heat stress activates the NLRP3 inflammasome to trigger hepatocyte pyroptosis (Ma et al., 2022), and HSP90-regulated mitophagy suppresses heat-stress-evoked pyroptosis (Shi et al., 2024). Polystyrene microplastic exposure up-regulates caspase-8 and promotes the switch of cell-death mode from apoptosis to pyroptosis and necrosis (Yin et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2025).

Necroptosis is mediated by the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL pathway and is independent of caspase activity; cell rupture releases abundant DAMPs to provoke inflammation. Aflatoxin B₁ markedly up-regulates the mRNA expression of RIPK1, RIPK3 and MLKL in chicken liver, triggering hepatocyte necroptosis accompanied by cell-membrane rupture, leakage of cytoplasmic contents and mitochondrial swelling (Li et al., 2022). Pasteurella multocida-derived LPS synergizes with TNF-α to activate RIPK1/RIPK3-dependent necroptosis. Strong positive HMGB1 signals are detected in the nuclei of hepatocytes and Kupffer cells surrounding necrotic foci, together with infiltration of IL-6-positive inflammatory cells (Tang et al., 2017).

Ferroptosis is a non-apoptotic form of programmed cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and GPX4 inactivation, with ultrastructural features including mitochondrial shrinkage, increased mitochondrial membrane density and reduced mitochondrial cristae (Chen et al., 2021). OTA markedly down-regulates GPX4 expression and increases cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis (Li et al., 2026; Kulcsár et al., 2025). Heat stress modulates the transcription of ferroptosis-related genes (Zhao et al., 2024). Acetochlor promotes ferritin degradation and free-iron release through the ER-CaMKII pathway to elicit lipid peroxidation (Zhang et al., 2025b). Copper mediates ferroptosis by inhibiting the Nrf2-SLC7A11 and FSP1 pathways (Zhong et al., 2024). Hepatic lipid accumulation heightens hepatocyte vulnerability to ferroptosis, and lipid peroxides concurrently activate inflammatory signaling.

These four forms of programmed cell death are not mutually exclusive. Instead, oxidative stress frequently serves as the common upstream trigger, whereas inflammation determines the intensity of pyroptosis and necroptosis. Crosstalk among these cell-death pathways collectively determines the severity and progression of hepatic injury.

Lipid metabolic disturbance

Hepatic lipid accumulation represents a common metabolic abnormality induced jointly by nutritional, toxic and environmental stressors. Lipid deposition increases hepatic susceptibility to oxidative and inflammatory stimuli and drives the continuous progression of hepatic lesions. High-energy low-protein diets, methionine deficiency, folate insufficiency and elevated estrogen levels all disrupt lipid homeostasis (Du et al., 2024; Mi et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019; Du et al., 2023; Shini et al., 2020; Cui et al., 2024). DON, as well as free gossypol and cyclopropene fatty acids in cottonseed cake, can also provoke hepatic lipid metabolic disorders (Moon et al., 2007; Vörösházi et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2021). High-energy low-protein diets up-regulate de-novo lipogenesis and suppress fatty-acid oxidation. Methionine deficiency impairs hepatic very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL)-mediated lipid export (Peng et al., 2018). Folate promotes lipogenesis via the IGF-PI3K-AKT-mTOR-SREBP-1c pathway and reduces triglyceride hydrolysis through autophagy-dependent processes (Liu et al., 2019; Du et al., 2023). Oestrogen increases the expression of lipogenesis-related genes, constituting an important contributor to the high incidence of lipid disorders in laying hens (Shini et al., 2020; Cui et al., 2024). DON globally inhibits protein synthesis, reduces apolipoprotein expression and impedes triglyceride efflux toward peripheral tissues (Moon et al., 2007). Cottonseed-derived anti-nutritional factors alter hepatic fatty-acid profiles, elevate cholesterol contents and perturb lipid homeostasis (Yang et al., 2021).

Hepatic lipid homeostasis is coordinately maintained by de-novo lipogenesis, fatty-acid β-oxidation and lipoprotein export. SREBP-1c acts as the master transcription factor for lipogenesis and governs the expression of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), fatty acid synthase (FASN) and stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD). Aberrant folate supply, ACC gene methylation and abnormal H3K27me3 modification can all enhance the activity of this lipogenic cascade (Liu et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2016b; Cui et al., 2024). AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a core regulator of hepatic fatty-acid oxidative catabolism. AMPK mitigates lipid accumulation by increasing the transcriptional activity of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), while AMPK is down-regulated in copper-exposed chicken liver (Chen et al., 2024). SLCO1B3, which exerts an inhibitory effect on lipid deposition, is down-regulated in diseased livers, suggesting that it serves as a key target for deciphering the pathogenesis of fatty liver (Zhu et al., 2023). Apolipoprotein B mediates VLDL secretion; methionine deficiency and DON exposure suppress apolipoprotein synthesis and block lipid outward transport (Peng et al., 2018; Moon et al., 2007).

Lipid accumulation, oxidative stress and inflammation constitute a persistent bidirectional regulatory loop. Lipid overload increases mitochondrial burden and promotes reactive-oxygen-species production, while providing substrates for lipid peroxidation. Excessive ROS inhibits AMPK and activates NF-κB, further facilitating lipogenesis. Free fatty acids and lipid peroxides trigger the TLR4 and NLRP3 pathways. TNF-α and IL-6 repress insulin signaling and up-regulate SREBP-1c, continuously aggravating lipid deposition. This vicious cycle exacerbates hepatic lesions, which are often refractory to spontaneous recovery. Therefore, lipid metabolic disturbance is not an isolated pathological event but rather functions as an amplifier that links oxidative stress, inflammation, and ferroptosis during the progression of hepatic injury.

Gut-liver axis-mediated regulation

As a physiological regulatory system for bidirectional communication between the intestine and liver, the gut-liver axis acts as a critical intermediate bridge by which most hepatic-damaging factors mediate hepatic pathological alterations. Various stimuli can disrupt intestinal barriers and disturb gut microbiota composition, alter the secretion of intestinal metabolites, and transmit injurious signals to the liver through the portal venous system, thereby triggering a spectrum of pathological changes including oxidative stress, inflammation and lipid metabolic disturbance. High-energy low-protein diets perturb caecal microbiota homeostasis and lead to insufficient synthesis of riboflavin and pantothenic acid. As key co-factors for hepatic energy and redox balance, deficiency of these two B-group vitamins directly provokes hepatic lipid metabolic disorders and oxidative injury (Mi et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024). Zearalenone, polystyrene microplastics and arsenic exposure all disrupt the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier and reshape gut-microbiota profiles, promoting portal translocation of LPS derived from Gram-negative bacteria (Wang et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). Upon reaching the liver, LPS activates the TLR4/NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways in Kupffer cells and amplifies hepatic inflammatory responses. Ochratoxin A induces oxidative stress, autophagy and ferroptosis in the jejunum, accompanied by altered gut-microbiota structure; secondary bacterial translocation following intestinal injury aggravates hepatic toxic damage (Li et al., 2026). The liver in turn modulates intestinal homeostasis via bile secretion. Arsenic exposure up-regulates hepatic CYP7A1 and disturbs bile-acid synthesis, and aberrant bile acids further exacerbate intestinal injury, establishing a reciprocal gut-liver injury cycle (Li et al., 2025). Based on the regulatory logic of the gut-liver axis, concurrent preservation of intestinal barrier function and modulation of gut microbiota can mitigate hepatic pathological injury induced by diverse causative factors at the upstream level.

Food safety implications of hepatic injury in chickens

Chicken liver: a high-risk edible organ prone to contaminant accumulation

The liver acts as the central organ in poultry responsible for xenobiotic metabolism, detoxification, nutrient storage and immune regulation. Owing to these physiological properties, chicken liver is not only highly vulnerable to pathological lesions but also prone to enrich diverse environmental contaminants and veterinary chemicals (Zaefarian et al., 2019). Accordingly, the adverse outcomes induced by hepatic injury extend beyond poultry health and productive performance, and directly compromise the safety and nutritional quality of edible chicken liver products (Sousa et al., 2020). The liver receives dual blood supply from the hepatic artery and portal vein, enabling continuous exposure to exogenous contaminants absorbed via the gastrointestinal tract (Peng et al., 2026). Heavy metals, certain mycotoxins, veterinary pharmaceuticals and persistent organic pollutants readily accumulate in hepatic tissues. This phenomenon arises partly from hepatic metabolic biotransformation of toxicants and partly from the relatively long biological half-lives of these contaminants. Novel particulate pollutants such as microplastics are retained in the liver via physical entrapment (Aljohani, 2023; Bonerba et al., 2024; Ali et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2020; Abd El-Hack et al., 2025). Notably, not all exogenous substances accumulate extensively in the liver; some compounds undergo hepatic metabolism and are rapidly eliminated via bile and faeces (Riahi et al., 2021). Moderate to severe hepatic injury disturbs cytochrome P450 activity, glutathione metabolism and biliary excretion, impairing systemic detoxification and clearance capacity (Amminikutty et al., 2025). Compromised detoxification further facilitates hepatic contaminant retention, establishing a vicious cycle: toxicant accumulation exacerbates hepatic damage, and impaired liver function in turn reduces the efficiency of contaminant elimination (Li et al., 2025). Chicken liver is therefore classified as an edible tissue with elevated food safety risks. Such risks are further pronounced under intensive poultry production systems, where birds are concurrently subjected to multiple environmental and nutritional stressors. Nevertheless, contaminant accumulation is dose-dependent, and detectable excessive residues may not occur under low-level exposure (Chelenga et al., 2025).

Bioaccumulation and potential consumer exposure risks

Residues accumulated in chicken liver act as a direct link between poultry health and human food safety. Dietary hazards associated with chicken products fall into two main categories. The first category comprises zoonotic pathogens such as Salmonella and Campylobacter jejuni, which colonize and accumulate in chicken liver and trigger human gastrointestinal infections upon consumption if thermal processing is insufficient (Lanier et al., 2018). The second category covers various chemical contaminants that tend to concentrate in edible visceral tissues and enter the human food chain via daily intake (Soumaoro et al., 2021).

Beyond chemical contaminants, chicken liver serves as a critical target organ for zoonotic pathogen colonization. Salmonella, Campylobacter jejuni and other pathogens persistently inhabit the liver during chronic poultry infection, resulting in visceral bacterial carriage. Inadequate washing and thermal processing of chicken liver allow viable bacteria to enter the human body through diet and induce acute gastrointestinal symptoms including vomiting and diarrhea; severe complications such as sepsis may develop in immunocompromised individuals, constituting a typical biological food safety risk (Cox et al., 2007; Qu et al., 2024; Edwards et al., 2014).

Heavy metals warrant particular concern. These substances resist biodegradation and possess strong bioaccumulation potential. Under long-term environmental exposure, cadmium, mercury, lead and arsenic gradually accumulate in hepatic tissues. Unable to be eliminated via routine metabolic pathways, these metals build up in the human body through repeated dietary intake. Chronic exposure elicits nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity, disrupts immune function, and elevates cancer risks (Aljohani, 2023).

Mycotoxins also merit urgent attention. Multiple feed-borne mycotoxins including aflatoxins, ochratoxin A, deoxynivalenol and fumonisins undergo biotransformation in broiler liver to generate toxic intermediates that trigger hepatocellular oxidative and DNA damage. Long-term feeding of contaminated diets causes organic hepatic lesions and systemic immunosuppression in poultry. Where dietary toxin concentrations exceed regulatory limits, multiple mycotoxins and their metabolites accumulate in chicken liver and enter the human body via ingestion. Chronic low-level exposure induces hepatic and renal injuries and immune dysfunction, while increasing cancer susceptibility, with infants and immunocompromised populations suffering more severe adverse health outcomes (Olariu et al., 2025).

Veterinary drug residues represent a major source of human dietary exposure risk. Improper medication, overdosing, and failure to observe mandatory withdrawal periods prior to slaughter lead to massive accumulation of antibiotics such as enrofloxacin, oxytetracycline and tylosin in chicken liver. Long-term consumption of residue-contaminated chicken liver not only causes direct toxicological effects including human hypersensitivity and gastrointestinal disorders, but also accelerates the emergence of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria, posing latent public health threats to clinical anti-infective therapy (El Tahir et al., 2021).

Microplastics and nanoplastics have attracted extensive research attention as emerging contaminants. Animal studies demonstrate that orally ingested microplastics interfere with hepatic function via the gut–liver axis, triggering toxic effects such as oxidative stress, inflammatory responses and lipid metabolic disorders. Existing research also indicates potential adverse human health impacts of microplastic exposure, including inflammatory and immune disturbances, metabolic disruption, DNA damage, neurotoxicity and even carcinogenesis. Nevertheless, the long-term human exposure levels and chronic health outcomes remain insufficiently characterized. Given the environmental persistence and ubiquitous distribution of microplastics, continuous surveillance of microplastic contamination in food and corresponding health risk assessments are still necessary (Abd El-Hack et al., 2025).

Stability of contaminants during food processing

Food processing can markedly reduce microbial contamination risks, yet it generally exerts limited removal efficiency against numerous chemically stable contaminants accumulated within chicken liver tissues. Thermal treatment efficiently inactivates pathogenic bacteria and viruses to lower the hazards of foodborne infections. Studies on major foodborne pathogens in chicken livers demonstrate that both Salmonella and Campylobacter jejuni exhibit temperature-dependent inactivation profiles; their viable counts drop significantly with rising heating temperatures. Among them, Salmonella possesses stronger heat resistance, with D-values in chicken liver 3–4 times higher than those of C. jejuni under identical thermal conditions. Internal chicken liver temperatures of 70–73.9°C achieve over 7-log reduction of Salmonella, verifying that adequate thermal processing effectively mitigates microbial risks in chicken livers (Qu et al., 2024).

Nevertheless, thermal processing shows distinct limitations in managing chemical contaminants. Heavy metals are thermally stable and cannot be decomposed by heat; their concentrations may even rise during processing due to factors such as cooking water and metal cookware. Persistent organic pollutants (POPs) feature rigid chemical structures and thus resist efficient degradation via domestic cooking procedures (Raters and Matissek, 2008).

The thermal stability of mycotoxins varies by type. Systematic evaluations on the thermal degradation of aflatoxin B₁ (AFB₁) and ochratoxin A (OTA) reveal that OTA retains high stability even at 180°C, whereas AFB₁ undergoes nearly complete degradation at temperatures ≥160°C. Moreover, carbohydrate and protein fractions in food matrices further modulate mycotoxin thermal breakdown. Although high-temperature processing may cut the levels of certain mycotoxins, variable cooking temperatures, durations and food matrices in household preparation prevent thorough elimination of tissue-embedded contaminants (Hashem and Abdaljaleel, 2024; Perelló et al., 2009; Tan et al., 2017).

In summary, food processing primarily functions to mitigate microbial contamination risks, while its efficacy against heavy metals, persistent organic pollutants and several heat-stable mycotoxins accumulated in chicken livers and other animal tissues remains constrained. Accordingly, food safety governance should prioritize source control during breeding and production stages to curb contaminant uptake in livestock and fundamentally reduce consumer exposure risks, rather than relying on post-processing as a remedial mitigation measure.

Food safety control throughout the entire poultry production Chain

The sources of hepatic injury and contaminant accumulation are complex and diverse. Hence, effective food safety management must cover the whole poultry production chain instead of merely relying on end-product testing. At the feed manufacturing stage, strict monitoring of mycotoxins, heavy metals and environmental pollutants is required to minimize initial contaminant exposure in poultry. Nutritional interventions that maintain intestinal barrier integrity and systemic antioxidant capacity can also reduce the susceptibility of the liver to toxic lesions (Zhang et al., 2023b).

During poultry rearing, veterinary drugs shall be used prudently with strict adherence to withdrawal periods; meanwhile, environmental stress such as heat stress should be alleviated to preserve normal hepatic physiological functions and minimize residue formation. Husbandry regimens sustaining intestinal microbiota homeostasis can also reduce endotoxin translocation via the gut-liver axis (Ringseis and Eder, 2022; Ennab et al., 2026).

Post-slaughter, systematic residue surveillance should be conducted using highly sensitive analytical techniques including liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) and rapid immunoassays. Combining these detection methods with routine pathological examination facilitates early identification of high-risk chicken livers destined for the food supply chain (Panzenhagen et al., 2016; Lešić et al., 2025; Rehan et al., 2023).

Ultimately, food safety shall be implemented under a full-chain "farm-to-table" strategy integrating feed quality control, environmental remediation, rational medication, residue monitoring, post-mortem inspection and post-market supervision. A comprehensive whole-industry-chain management system delivers more robust safety assurance than single-intervention measures targeting individual contaminants.

Conclusion and prospect

Chicken liver injury is a multifactorial syndrome in which diverse etiological agents converge on a limited set of core pathways, including oxidative stress, inflammation, lipid metabolism disorders, and programmed cell death (Figure 2). Accumulating evidence indicates that distinct liver injury-inducing factors frequently activate similar downstream signaling pathways. High-energy low-protein diet, heat stress, selenium deficiency, aflatoxin B₁, ochratoxin A, lead and decabromodiphenyl ether exposure all trigger oxidative stress, and some of these factors mediate this process via disruption of the Nrf2-Keap1 signaling pathway (Jing et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2018b). Zearalenone and FAdV-4 infection also induce prominent inflammatory responses (Xu et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2025). Lipid metabolism disorder represents another convergent mechanism: high-energy low-protein diet, methionine deficiency, folate deficiency, and cyclopropene fatty acids derived from cottonseed collectively contribute to hepatic steatosis and fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome in chickens. Notably, the gut-liver axis acts as a critical intermediate mediator. A high-energy low-protein diet reshapes cecal microbiota and reduces the levels of riboflavin and pantethine, whereas zearalenone, microplastics, and arsenic impair intestinal barrier integrity, facilitating the translocation of LPS into the liver (Hu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2023). The fact that distinct etiological agents activate convergent downstream pathways—such as the Nrf2-Keap1 axis, inflammasomes, and lipid metabolism regulatory networks—suggests that, theoretically, intervention strategies targeting these shared pathways may offer greater cost-effectiveness and broader applicability than measures directed at individual causative factors.

Nevertheless, several urgent research gaps remain to be filled. First, current studies are dominated by single-factor experimental designs, while commercial chickens are constantly exposed to multiple concurrent stressors such as combined mycotoxin and heat stress. The synergistic or antagonistic interactions among these coexisting factors remain largely unknown. Second, although oxidative stress has been extensively reported, its upstream triggers including calcium overload, endoplasmic reticulum stress and mitophagy, as well as their temporal hierarchical relationships, have been severely neglected. Third, research on the functions of DNA methylation and m6A RNA modification in chronic liver injury and tumorigenesis is still in its infancy. Additionally, omics-based biomarkers and therapeutic targets have not been translated into practical tools for field application. Fourth, the stability of hepatotoxic contaminants during cooking and the establishment of practical residue monitoring systems warrant further investigation.

Hepatic injury not only impairs liver physiological functions, but also deteriorates growth performance, accompanied by decreased feed intake and average daily gain (El-Abd et al., 2025). As a widely consumed edible by-product, chicken liver with accumulated chemical residues poses potential threats to food safety. From the perspective of public health, hepatic damage induced by heavy metals, microplastics, bacterial and parasitic pathogens deserves particular attention. These pathogenic triggers not only cause liver lesions in chickens, but also compromise the edible safety of chicken liver. Heavy metals and microplastics tend to persist and bioaccumulate in hepatic tissues, and their residues can enter human bodies via food chains, imposing long-term health hazards on consumers (Aljohani, 2023; Hu et al., 2025). Unlike most viral diseases that rarely infect humans, bacterial and parasitic infections carry zoonotic transmission risks and may serve as reservoirs of foodborne pathogens, increasing cross-species transmission risks. Even non-human-tropic viral infections can trigger hepatic dysfunction and metabolic disorders, substantially reducing the nutritional value of chicken liver. The contents of highly bioavailable heme iron, vitamins and essential phospholipids decline markedly, which indirectly degrades the quality of this nutrient-dense food. Accordingly, guaranteeing the safety of chicken liver products requires systematic monitoring and control of chemical residues, as well as stringent supervision on pathogenic microorganisms and environmental contaminants throughout the whole production chain. A farm-to-fork framework—integrating feed safety, flock health, slaughter hygiene, processing controls, and end-product surveillance—is essential for this purpose.

In conclusion, chicken liver injury is a multifactorial syndrome driven by diverse etiological factors that collectively induce oxidative stress, inflammation, lipid metabolic dysregulation, and programmed cell death. The gut–liver axis acts as a central regulatory network connecting intestinal homeostasis with hepatic metabolic and toxic responses. Future studies addressing the current knowledge gaps will deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying liver injury and its implications for poultry production. More importantly, such advances will provide a scientific basis for developing targeted nutritional and management strategies to safeguard liver function, improve meat quality and nutritional value, minimize food safety concerns, and support the sustainable supply of high-quality poultry products. Future studies should also address multi-stressor interactions and the long-term health impacts of low-level contaminant exposure through dietary chicken liver consumption.
