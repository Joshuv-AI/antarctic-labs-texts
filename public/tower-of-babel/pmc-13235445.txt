Endovascular repair of a type IA endoleak following ALTO endograft placement using a custom-made fenestrated device
Authors: Marc-Antoine Bety, Stéphane Elkouri, Laura M. Drudi, Jean-François Blair, Philippe Charbonneau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ALTO endograft (Endologix, Inc) was developed to expand endovascular aneurysm repair eligibility. However, type IA endoleak remains a devastating complication associated with this device. Few reports describe management of endoleaks after ALTO implantation, and published cases are limited to open surgical explantation. We report a case of type IA endoleak following endovascular aneurysm repair using an ALTO device, which was successfully treated with a custom-made three-fenestration endograft with an integrated bifurcation. The procedure was performed with fusion imaging and carbon dioxide angiography. Technical success was achieved. One-year follow-up imaging demonstrated no residual proximal endoleak or aneurysm sac enlargement.

Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) is the preferred treatment for anatomically suitable abdominal aortic aneurysms, particularly in frail or high-risk patients.1 However, unfavorable proximal neck morphology or inadequate access vessels may preclude EVAR. The ALTO abdominal stent graft system (Endologix, Inc) was developed to expand EVAR eligibility by incorporating a polymer-filled sealing ring and a low-profile delivery system designed to accommodate complex anatomy, including a short infrarenal neck length up to 7 mm.2 Although early results have demonstrated favorable technical success in selected patients, standard EVAR-related complications such as type IA endoleak have been described.3 This report presents a novel endovascular approach for the treatment of a type IA endoleak following ALTO endograft placement. The patient expressed consent for the publication of this case report.

Case report

A 78-year-old male with significant comorbidities, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart failure, coronary artery disease, and stage IV chronic kidney disease with an atrophic, nonviable left kidney, was referred for management of an enlarging abdominal aortic aneurysm sac associated with a type IA endoleak following ALTO endograft implantation performed 1 year prior (Fig 1). At that time, the ALTO device was selected based on a short infrarenal neck (11 mm) and a reverse-tapered aortic morphology, consistent with ALTO instructions for use. Following an interdisciplinary meeting with our aortic team, an endovascular strategy was chosen, considering the patient’s comorbidities. The procedure was performed in a hybrid operating room under general anesthesia. Iodinated contrast, fusion imaging, and carbon dioxide aortography (Angiodroid System; Angiodroid, Srl) were used for vessel localization. Bilateral percutaneous common femoral access and percutaneous right axillary access were obtained, followed by administration of an intravenous heparin bolus. A CMD fenestrated endograft (Cook Medical) with two fully covered proximal sealing stents and three planned fenestrations for the celiac trunk, superior mesenteric artery (SMA), and right renal artery (RRA) was advanced and partially deployed after fusion-based alignment. The SMA was catheterized via axillary access, and an 8 × 27 mm BeGraft Peripheral stent graft (Bentley InnoMed, GmbH) was positioned. After unsuccessful attempts to catheterize the remaining fenestrations, the main body was fully deployed. Contralateral gate cannulation was challenging but was achieved from the left femoral access using a steerable sheath. Two 10 × 57 mm BeGraft Plus stents were deployed in a kissing configuration to bridge the CMD to the ALTO iliac limbs (Fig 3). The celiac trunk was then catheterized using a steerable sheath, and a 10 × 38 mm BeGraft Peripheral stent was deployed and flared. The SMA bridging stent was also expended. After multiple attempts, the RRA was ultimately catheterized via the axillary approach, and a 6 × 28 mm BeGraft Peripheral stent was deployed. Completion aortography demonstrated patency of all target vessels without residual endoleak (Fig 4). All access sites were closed without complication. A total of 70 mL of iodinated contrast was used. The postoperative course was notable for transient acute renal failure that resolved without intervention. Renal duplex ultrasound was unremarkable, and renal function returned to baseline within days. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 10. Discussion

CMD fenestrated endografts are a well-established solution for unfavorable proximal neck anatomy.4 However, urgent presentation or anatomic constraints such as limited access, vessel tortuosity, or small renal arteries may preclude their use.5 In these situations, the polymer-based ALTO endograft may represent an alternative while avoiding the complexity of fenestrated EVAR. In our experience, this endograft may be appropriate for patients who are poor candidates for open repair or fenestrated EVAR. The ALTO endograft allows simple endovascular repair, often performed under regional or local anesthesia, in patients with short neck, limited iliac access (<6 mm), and/or severe iliac tortuosity. Despite its low incidence (1.3%), early proximal type IA endoleak may occur following ALTO implantation,6 especially in off–instructions-for-use neck morphologies. In the present case, proximal neck calcification impaired ring apposition, resulting in a type IA endoleak. Usually, endovascular treatment options include infrarenal cuff extension, fenestrated or branched CMDs, endoanchors, giant Palmaz stents, or parallel grafts.7 However, the ALTO device’s polymer-based sealing rings limit the applicability of these approaches. Few reports describe treatment of type IA endoleak after ALTO endograft repair, which is usually explanted using an open surgical approach.8

In our case, open conversion was considered high risk secondary to the patient’s comorbidities and frail status, making an endovascular strategy preferable. Placement of an additional infrarenal cuff extension was not possible because of the absence of a sealing zone between the ALTO device and the lowest renal artery. Considering the relatively long ALTO suprarenal fixation stent, an extensive visceral segment coverage was deemed necessary to achieve adequate sealing. A proximal cuff extension using a fenestrated CMD was therefore preferred. Although well-described for conventional endografts, this approach requires careful preoperative planning to minimize complications, including renal failure, visceral stent thrombosis, and iliac limb occlusion. Although uncommon, perioperative mortality has been reported, with 30-day mortality rates ranging from approximately 3.3% to 5.3%.9,10

In this case, the unique design of the ALTO endograft, combined with the patient’s comorbidities, introduced additional technical challenges for deployment of a three-fenestration CMD. The presence of stage IV chronic kidney disease necessitated minimization of iodinated contrast; accordingly, carbon dioxide angiography was used as an adjunctive imaging modality, as previously shown to be safe and effective in complex endovascular procedures in patients with impaired renal function.11 Uncertainty also existed regarding the interaction between the CMD and the short, restrictive main body of the ALTO device. An integrated bifurcated CMD was therefore selected to avoid the need for an inverted limb configuration and to reduce the risk of type III endoleak. The use of 10-mm CMD gates permitted deployment of 10-mm BeGraft Plus stents as bridging limbs, providing sufficient radial force at the level of the flow divider to promote complete expansion, optimize apposition, and reduce the risk of limb thrombosis, while ensuring adequate distal sealing within the prior endograft (Fig 3). Given anatomic constraints, the RRA fenestration was positioned at the level of the CMD flow divider, further complicating cannulation due to limited maneuverability. In addition, contralateral gate catheterization was challenging; consequently, kissing iliac stents were deployed prior to RRA cannulation. This strategy allowed advancement of a large steerable sheath into the visceral aorta, providing the stability required for successful catheterization and stenting of the remaining vessels.

Reports describing the treatment of endoleak after ALTO endograft implantation remain limited and, to our knowledge, are restricted to open explantation.8 Therefore, our approach represents a novel endovascular strategy for the management of type IA endoleak following ALTO implantation.

Conclusions

This case highlights key technical challenges that must be carefully considered during preoperative planning. Thorough evaluation of the ALTO device’s unique design features is essential to optimize outcomes. With meticulous planning, this advanced endovascular approach may offer a safe, reproducible, and effective solution in selected patients.
