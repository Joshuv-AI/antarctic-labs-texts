Linked lives and work-family dynamics: gendered workload, family support, and mental well-being among urban Chinese dual-earner households
Authors: Meixuan Li, Lara Stas, Christophe Vanroelen, Deborah De Moortel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In China, debates on achieving work-family balance have intensified with the rise of the dual-earner model. Balancing paid and unpaid work is stressful for dual-earner couples resulting in poor mental well-being outcomes for both partners. While prior mental well-being research mainly focused on individual associations, our study investigates associations of paid and unpaid workloads on spousal mental well-being within married dyads. Moreover, given that family support is an important resource in accordance with Chinese cultural and social norms, we investigate the mitigating role of family support within this dyadic relationship. Using coupled data from the Chinese Family Panel Study (CFPS) Wave 5, we employed Actor-Partner Interdependence Model (APIM) to explore crossover mechanisms of multiple workloads (total, paid, and unpaid) and spousal mental well-being. Mental well-being was evaluated by utilizing a scale for depressive symptoms and for subjective well-being. We found that both genders’ higher levels of depression were border-significantly related to their own total workload (i.e. the sum of paid and unpaid labour), yet spousal associations were unobserved. For women, paid workload is negatively associated with subjective well-being while controlling for unpaid workload. The moderation models revealed, for men, that family support received from their wives’ family attenuates the positive association between workloads and depression. Our study advances our knowledge of the gender dynamics influencing work-family balance and strengthens the rationale for the adoption of family-friendly workplace regulations in China.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02687-w.

Introduction

Research on the intersection of work and family dynamics and its impact on the health of men and women has gained attention in light of changing gender roles in the workforce and family structures [1]. Comparable to other countries, the dual-earner model – with shared responsibilities in work and family domains – has become prevalent among Chinese families, especially in urban areas in line with their institutional transition and emerging market economy [2]. It is well-established that workers’ mental well-being is impacted by the number of paid and unpaid working hours, as well as the division of work patterns among dual-earner couples [3]. Dual responsibilities for work and family domains amount to individual’s (high) ‘total workload’, which can be conceived as the sum of paid and unpaid work [4, 5].

Previous studies have highlighted detrimental effects of long working hours on mental well-being, simultaneously encompassing paid and unpaid labour [6, 7]. Notwithstanding rising gender equality, married women continue to bear a heavier household labour burden, often performing a ‘second shift’ or experiencing the ‘double burden’ of paid and unpaid work [8, 9]. This imbalance may predispose married women to a ‘mental well-being penalty’ due to their extended total workload [10]. Importantly, gender norms shape how individuals allocate their resources like time, effort, and energy across work and family domains [11–13]. In China, family values are characterized as patriarchal and family-oriented, with women bearing the primary responsibility for unpaid housework, leading to gender disparities in relative resources and (household) bargaining power [14, 15].

Studies in the domain of work-family interference so far mostly adopted an individualistic approach, neglecting the ‘dyadic interrelations’ between the spouses’ workloads and their mental well-being. Stress arising from total workload in daily life may, however, spillover or crossover between spouses, impacting their mental well-being. The spillover-crossover model has already been employed as a conceptual framework elucidating the interconnections between work and family (e.g., job demands and work-family conflict) and mental well-being in other contexts, but not in China [16, 17]. Westman [18] noted that couple-level stress affects mental health through work-family interactions, aligning with Conservation of Resources (COR) theory. Individuals protect key resources like time and energy to avoid depletion from excessive workloads, which can reduce family engagement and harm well-being [19, 20]. Heavy workloads with long hours limit personal time, reducing couple interactions, communication, and shared leisure activities, ultimately harming mental health [20, 21].

Social support, however, may perform a vital role in comprehending the association between (un)paid workloads and the mental well-being of dual-earner families. Unlike COR theory’s focus on direct impact of resources on mental outcomes, the stress buffering theory emphasizes accumulating external resources (e.g., support received from family members) to enhance coping capacity during stressful events [22]. Both theories argue that family support, emotional or instrumental, may mitigate the detrimental impact of stressors [23]. Within marital relationships, the reciprocal exchange of emotional and instrumental support has been linked to improved health outcomes [24]. When managing their multiple responsibilities, urban Chinese dual earner couples are likely to rely on assistance from their parents, because of traditional family values. Ta et al. [25] argue that intergenerational co-residence in China may address gender imbalance in the division of labour through supplying assistance with childcare and household chores. Given the limited focus of previous studies on the role of family support in dyadic associations - despite evidence suggesting its connection to mental well-being [26, 27] - it is of the utmost importance to investigate family support as a moderating factor and as a stress-buffering mechanism, notably in China.

This study aims to enhance our understanding of diverse mental well-being outcomes among Chinese urban dual-earner couples by examining the extent of couples’ involvement in work and family responsibilities and the protective role of family support. Diverging from prior research that respectively delved into the mental consequences of paid and unpaid workloads [28], our study adopts a dyadic approach to not only capture the effects of paid and household work but also examines the role of the total workload. Measuring total workload—as opposed to studying paid and unpaid work respectively—allows for a better evaluation of how couples respond to their own and their partner’s multiple demands. It also highlights gender differences related to the totality of workload, which might be missed when only examining paid or unpaid work separately.

Utilizing Actor-Partner Interdependence Models (APIMs [29]), we investigate how unpaid and paid workloads associate with own’s mental well-being and that of partners using the Chinese household dyadic data. Additionally, we explore the role of family support as a potential buffering factor contributing to mental well-being for both spouses. While some scholars have examined workload and mental well-being crossover associations, their research has predominantly focused on Western contexts [7, 18, 28] and has often neglected the complexities of work-family challenges faced by Eastern couples. Our study thus centres on Chinese urban, heterosexual dual-earner couples, shedding light on the mental well-being implications of gender dynamics in the Chinese context. The findings of our research will contribute to the ongoing conversation about the enduring labour division dynamics within Chinese dual-earner couples and the factors influencing mental well-being related to workload, both between and within couples. Furthermore, our findings can support the implementation of family-friendly measures in the workplace and other societal domains.

Theoretical background

Work and family life within the Chinese context

Owing to industrialization, urbanisation, and increased global integration—especially in the economic sphere—the Asian countries have gradually established a more gender egalitarian society. Movements towards gender equality, however, still encounter institutional barriers and ingrained historical-cultural traditional beliefs [30]. On top, Eastern collectivistic cultures place more value on interpersonal belonging, group identification, and emotional comfort than do Western individualistic notions [31]. Spanning the period from Maoist era until the post-reform time, a unique version of collectivism with Confucian tendencies has not only become deeply entrenched in family-oriented values but has also been applied to labour organisations.

Workers in China endure excessively long working hours, as the collectivistic culture highly values employment and regards it as a fundamental family obligation. The shift to a market economy in post-socialist China, coupled with intensified global competition, has rendered traditional work hour standards economically unfeasible for numerous employers. According to the Labour law code of China, the legally mandated standard for daily working hours should not exceed 8 h, with an average weekly limit of 44 h [32]. Nevertheless, to satisfy production and business purposes, employers may require that workers extend their working hours beyond these prescribed standards. The collectivistic working culture contributed to China’s rapid economic growth and has directly or indirectly led to the implementation of prolonged working hours aimed at cost-saving, rather than the recruitment of additional staffs [33]. This prevailing work culture diminishes time spend in the private sphere [34], and, in turn, has influenced the gender gap in labour participation and occupational segregation [35]. The “danwei” system prior to the reform provided ample employment opportunities within state-owned enterprises for the majority of citizens. This system proved highly effective, with approximately 90% of urban women participating in the labour force. It specifically functioned as the social security system in terms of employment benefits and affiliated welfare, allowing women to progressively reduce their domestic responsibilities through childcare [36].

Nevertheless, the privatization of the market economy during economic reforms has hastened the erosion of the welfare system, leading to increased involvement of women in family activities as state support for care diminishes. Simultaneously, private sector employers are less inclined to hire women [36]. Despite decreased female labour participation due to institutional changes, dual-earner couples remain prevalent, constituting the largest group among various family formations [35].

Gendered labour at the work-family interface in urban China

The societal expectations surrounding specific roles in the home and workplace make it challenging to between work and family obligations [37, 38]. Gender division of labour theories, such as role theory [39–41], identity theory [42], and relative resources theory [43], shed light on how tensions between work and family lead to gender disparities in the distribution of paid and unpaid work. Furthermore, gender norms often lead women to do more unpaid household work, while men dedicate more time to paid employment [5]. On top, regardless of the regional contexts, the ‘greedy workplace’ requires extended working hours, leading to conflict between work and non-work domains [44]. Especially in China where a strong work ethic is rooted in the collectivist culture [31], employees suffer from the improper work pressure given by employers. As such, an individual’s paid and unpaid labour accumulates to a high extent, leading to a high “total workload” [45].

Chinese women have a relatively high participation in the labour force, particularly in urban area. As Chinese urbanization progresses rapidly, there is an exacerbation of the dual burden on urban women, but also urban men are increasingly taking on household responsibilities [46]. Still, Chinese urban women continuously struggle to balance work and domestic roles, worsened by the dissolution of the socialist era danwei system [25]. A multitude of factors, such as relatively lower occupational status and education levels for women, and patriarchal gender ideologies, increase the likelihood of women taking on a higher domestic role, compared to men [30]. Therefore, our first hypothesis is that women in dual-income households bear a higher total and unpaid workload than men.

Hypothesis 1

Wives’ total workload and unpaid workload exceed those of husbands among Chinese dual-earner couples.

Workload, mental well-being and crossover effects between spouses

Research on the association between work demands and health indicates that high job demands, including overwork, contributes to negative psychosocial outcomes such as burnout and exhaustion [47, 48]. High housework demands are associated with lower levels of mental well-being [3]. Juggling demands from both domains affects multiple outcomes of mental well-being of married individuals, including life stress [49], marital dissatisfaction [50], depression [51], and reduced subjective well-being [23]. Therefore, we formulate our second hypothesis as follows:

Hypothesis 2

Workloads (i.e., paid, unpaid and total) are positively related to own depressive symptoms, and negatively related to own subjective well-being among men and women. (Actor effects)

Amongst dual-earner couples, a ‘crossover effect’ might also be present, meaning that a spouse’s workload can significantly affect the partner’s mental well-being [18, 52]. Prior research has indicated that individual consequences of role-conflicts in the context of high work demand are associated with crossover effects to spousal marital satisfaction [53], life satisfaction [54, 55], and psychological distress [56]. Li et al. [57] suggested that strain experienced by the role sender (e.g., husband or wife) caused by work stressors could spill over to the spouse resulting in resource depletion, further resulting in psychological distress. Therefore, we expect a crossover effect in Chinese dual-earner couples:

Hypothesis 2a

Wives’ workloads (i.e., paid, unpaid and total) are positively related to husbands’ depression, and negatively related to husbands’ subjective well-being. (Partner effects for men)

Hypothesis 2b

Husbands’ workloads (i.e., paid, unpaid and total) are positively related to wives’ depression, and negatively related to wives’ subjective well-being. (Partner effects for women)

Gender differences, however, can be visible in these associations. Evidence indicates that among dual-earner couples, women continue to face high demands from both professional and domestic roles, exacerbated during motherhood [58]. Empirical findings report that the continued challenges women encounter in balancing work and family responsibilities, lead to subsequent lower mental well-being in women, compared to men [3]. Plus, women exhibit heightened sensitivity of their spouses’ work-related stress as they devote more time to handling family responsibilities [59]. For example, Westman et al. [60] discovered that stress is transmitted unidirectionally from husbands to wives. Therefore, the third hypothesis goes as follows:

Hypothesis 3a

Women exhibit a stronger relationship between their own workloads and mental well-being compared to men. (Gender difference in actor effects)

Hypothesis 3b

Women exhibit a stronger relationship between spouses’ workloads and their own mental well-being compared to men. (Gender difference in partner effects)

Family support as a moderator to buffer the stressors

Social support has been recognized as a protective factor for well-being, acting as a buffer against the detrimental effects of stressors [61, 62]. According to Cohen and Wills [22], social support constitutes a social resource that functions on coping with stressful events. Occupational stress has been found to be positively moderated by support from family members, including spouses, friends, and relatives [63]. Support from close family members promotes individuals’ mental well-being.

Family support can be mainly manifested in two forms, given the multi-faceted nature of the construct: instrumental and emotional supports [64]. Instrumental support, for instance, pertains to assistance with childcare and housework [22]. Informal help from relatives or formal assistance can facilitate work-family balance when family members struggle to manage household tasks due to hectic work schedules [65]. In China, the gendered division of labour under conservative has led to an increased role of parents(-in-law) in providing support to their adult children in managing work-family dynamics, often involving assistance with grandchildren and daily chores [36]. Re-negotiation of the division of labour in the dual-earner households might be achieved by the availability of intergenerational co-residence and the provision of extra help from their parents(-in-law) [25]. We, therefore, assume that family support lessens the negative impact of stressors—such as workloads—by preserving resources related to household assistance, thereby safeguarding the psychological well-being of couples.

Hypothesis 4a

Family support weakens the positive relations between an individual’s workload and their own depression (moderation in actor effect) as well as between their partner’s workload and the individual’s depression (moderation in partner effect).

Hypothesis 4b

Family support strengthens negative relations between an individual’s workload and their own subjective well-being (moderation in actor effect) as well as between their partner’s workload and the individual’s subjective well-being (moderation in partner effect).

Methodology

Data and sample

The study relies on data from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS, [66]), a national longitudinal survey initiated and funded by the Institute of Social Sciences of Peking University since 2008. The CFPS aims to comprehensively track societal changes in China, covering social, economic, demographic, educational, and health-related aspects at individual, household, and community levels. Its primary goal is to provide high-quality microdata for research in demographics, sociology, and policy analysis in China, utilizing a multi-stage probability-proportional sampling method with implicit stratification across 25 provinces [67].

For our core analysis, we have used the fifth wave which was collected in 2018. The CFPS data collection is done every two years. Considering the impact of the pandemic on the work and family lives of dual-earner couples, particularly in China [68], the last round of data collected before the pandemic, was employed in this study. It is regarded as the most reliable data, due to its pre-pandemic status. The CFPS 2018 adult questionnaire contains extensive information about demographic characteristics, marriage, work-related behaviors (e.g., paid, and unpaid work hours per week), and health indicators (e.g., depression, subjective well-being, and self-reported health status). Individual and family data were integrated into a dyadic dataset, matched using unique identifiers and family codes. Our eligibility criteria included married, heterosexual, working couples residing in urban or suburban areas. Employment status (i.e., employed/unemployed) and work time were surveyed in work module of wave 5 questionnaire. Cohabiting couples were excluded due to legal non-recognition compared to marriage in China and their minimal representation in CFPS 2018. Residence was determined by current living location, excluding domicile residence to account for potential migration. We focused on the working age group (21 to 60 years), resulting in an unweighted sample of 1,344 couples (2,688 individuals) after removing observations with missing data (approximately 1%, n = 96) on relevant variables (See Appendix Table 3). Given that Ledermann et al. [69] advise using samples larger than 100 dyads, our sample with 1,344 dyads has adequate statistical power.

Measures

Mental well-being

The central focus of this study revolves around two distinct forms (i.e., positive, and negative) of mental well-being: depression and subjective well-being.

Depressive Symptoms Measurement Scale. The CFPS questionnaire utilizes the simplified the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) 8-item scale, originally proposed by Radloff [70] to measure depression, encompassing eight items reflecting depressive symptoms. Respondents rate their agreement on a Likert scale, ranging from 0 (‘none’) to 3 (‘most of the time’) for each item. Two positive items are reverse-coded. Statements like “I feel depressed” and “My sleep was restless” contribute to a cumulative depression measure from 0 to 24, indicating severity, where high values indicate more severe depression. Both female and male respondents demonstrated satisfactory reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients (women: 0.749, men: 0.748), affirming the scale’s credibility and accurate representation of the variable.

Subjective well-being (hereinafter SWB). Subjective well-being is the second indicator in assessing mental well-being through individual self-evaluation, recognized as a particular positive facet of mental well-being [71]. Each participant measured SWB by responding to the question: “Overall, do you perceive yourself as content and satisfied with your life?” Responses ranged from 1 (strong dissatisfaction) to 5 (strong satisfaction). The SWB question, without specific temporal reference, has been consistently used in multiple waves of CFPS, which allows us to explore the respondents’ life well-being.

Workload

Workload (Paid, Unpaid and Total). Extending the design of Liu et al. [72], our key explanatory variables encompass three continuous indicators derived from survey questions related to paid and unpaid work activities. These questions inquired about the average daily or weekly hours that respondents spent on these activities. We concentrate on individuals who worked for at least one hour during the week prior to the survey. Paid workload includes paid normal working time and unpaid extra hours except the lunch break, performed during one week.

Questions regarding unpaid work addressed at least two aspects: time allocated to housework and caregiving for children, the sick, and the elderly. Respondents reported their average daily housework hours on weekdays, and a similar question about weekend housework time was used. To maintain consistency with similar parameters used in the study by Liu et al. [72], we subsequently coded a variable representing the total weekly hours spent on housework.

The total workload variable, as already used in earlier studies (e.g., [45, 73]), represents the sum of hours dedicated to both paid and unpaid work per week for each partner individually. For APIM main analyses, we note that the total workload is computed by summing the coefficients for paid and unpaid workload.

Family support

The potential buffering role of family support in each of the studied associations was examined. This moderator variable was derived from two questions in the CFPS survey: (1) “Does your mother assist you with household chores?” (2) “Does your father assist you with household chores?” Responses were coded as either “Yes” or “No.” If a respondent answered “Yes” to at least one of the two questions, the family support variable was coded as 1, i.e., “having family support”. If a respondent answered “No” to one question while the other response was missing, the variable was coded as 0, i.e., “not having family support”. Since both spouses are treated in the crossover analyses, the moderation effects are considered separately for each individual within one couple unit. Control variables

We incorporated age of respondents at interview year (namely, the 2018 year), educational attainment (categorized as low, middle, high), occupational class (ISCO-88: low (categories 6–9), middle (categories 4–5), high (categories 1–3)), and the number of children under 12 as covariates, selected based on their known associations with primary study variables [74]. Educational levels in the CFPS 2018 dataset, ranging from 1 (Illiterate/Semi-literate) to 8 (Master’s degree or equivalent), were grouped into three tiers for analysis: low (1–2), middle (3), and high (4–8), with no respondents holding a doctoral degree. Occupational status, classified using ISCO-88 and contextualized for the Chinese labour market, was categorized as low (6–9), middle (4–5), and high (1–3), considering its potential influence on mental well-being mechanisms [75].

Analytical strategy

Descriptive analyses are presented in Table 1: i.e., mean (M) and standard deviation (SD) for workload, levels of depression, and SWB of Chinese dual-earner couples. Paired t-tests were conducted to assess significant gender differences in these variables. As a second step, we adopted the Actor-Partner Independence Model (APIM, [76, 77]), a dyadic analytical approach that enables us to quantify the crossover effects on mental well-being of workload between partners. This model allows for a dyadic analysis in family (e.g., married couples) or interpersonal relationship research (e.g., teacher and student), capturing dyadic interdependence from an interpersonal perspective. The APIM comprises two components that can be estimated simultaneously on a certain outcome: actor effects and partner effects, as illustrated in Fig. 1. H indicates husband, while W means wife. Actor effects (e.g., X(W)- > Y(W)) represent the direct effect of an individual’s predictor variable on their own mental well-being outcome (horizontal paths labelled ‘a’). Partner effects (e.g., X(W)- > Y(H)) refer to the extent to which one’s own predictor variable has an effect on one’s partner mental well-being outcome (diagonal paths labelled ‘p’). We account for correlations between independent variables (double-headed arrow labelled ‘b’) and for residual nonindependence of the dependent variables (double-headed arrow labelled ‘c’).Fig. 1Actor-partner interdependence model for measuring the associations among dual-earner couples

Actor-partner interdependence model for measuring the associations among dual-earner couples

All APIM analyses were carried out through structural equation modelling (SEM). The code from the online apim_sem app [78] was adapted to the moderation setting in R Studio using the lavaan package [79]. SEM provides an effective approach for estimating the nonindependence between distinguishable partners within the APIM framework. In particular, the default APIM is an identified model with 0 degrees of freedom, making traditional model-fitting statistics noninformative. By employing the Actor-Partner Interdependence Moderation Model (APIMoM, [80]), the study aims to explore the moderating role of family support in the relationship between workloads and mental well-being outcomes for both genders in the specific context of China. Additionally, our model encompasses a between-dyad variable (i.e., number of children) and age, educational level, occupation level and family support are included as mixed within-dyad variables. All exogeneous variables were grand mean centred. To address missing data in confounding variables, FIML estimation with robust standard errors were used, aligning with prior research [81]. Prior interpreting the results, the model fit is inspected. Adhering to Hu and Bentler’s [82], criteria for reasonable model fit, the following criteria were used: non-significant Chi-squared test, RMSEA < 0.06, SRMR < 0.08, and CFI/TLI values approximately > 0.95.

Testing the moderating role of social support

By means of the Actor-Partner Interdependence Model of Moderation (APIMoM), our analysis contends that family support—defined as parental assistance with household chores—may moderate the relationship between workloads and mental well-being. This results in four interaction effects for each partner, eight interactions per moderated model (models 3 and 4 in Table 6), aligning with Garcia et al. [80] framework.

Discussion

While previous studies have investigated how paid, unpaid, and total workload affects personal mental health outcomes [11, 45, 83], little attention has been paid to the impact of workload on spousal well-being. The present study investigated how paid, unpaid and total workload influences the mental well-being of married couples in China from the couple perspective, inspired by COR theory and studies on crossover mechanisms [18, 28]. In addition, by examining total workload as an important factor, the present study generates a more holistic picture of work-family demands for dual-earner families. Our findings contribute to the exisitingpertinent literature by emphasizing the importance of analyzing work-family dynamics from a linked lives perspective [18, 28, 52].

Our empirical evidence confirms a pronounced gender-based disparity in household workload, with married women spending nearly twice as much time on housework as their husbands. This division reflects deeply ingrained gender norms, positioning men as primary earners and women as primary caretakers [25, 46, 84]. Among these dual-earner couples, however, the discrepancy in paid workload is less significant.

Our dyadic analysis further corresponds with our expectation that higher paid workload correlates with higher depression in both genders and with lower SWB for women. Previous research has indicated that employment often provides mental well-being benefits for women, such as financial independence and social engagement [85]. Reduced investment in family responsibilities given the increased employment-related workload may, however, have a detrimental impact on women’s mental well-being [86, 87]. The lack of a male association with SWB is consistent with earlier insights suggesting that gender disparities in mental well-being may be linked to differences in leisure time availability [12, 83, 88]. Women, who dedicate less time to leisure activities due to dual demands of paid work and domestic responsibilities, may be at greater risk of experiencing negative mental health and well-being. Moreover, although there is plenty evidence linking housework to depression and SWB [3, 89], particularly in societies with traditional gender norms, our analysis of crossover cross-over mechanisms surprisingly failed to detect any evidence linking unpaid workload to mental well-being outcomes. One possible explanation is that Chinese married women may not perceive the gendered division of domestic labour as inequitable given that their husbands fulfil their expected breadwinning role, making them more committed to household work [30]. Delving further, the absence of empirical results about partner paths did not align with extant studies on reciprocal crossover mechanisms between partners [28], potentially attributed to the heterogeneity of dual-earner couples in various socio-cultural contexts.

Moreover, gender differences in actor paths were observed, with paid workload having a stronger relationship with depression in men than in women. This could demonstrate how employment could help mothers’ and women’s mental well-being, because of access to social support, income, skill acquisition, and self-identity [1]. This shift towards employment in women, however, may provide less support to the family. Prior research investigating on the crossover of work hours and stress between partners has suggested that longer work hours for wives predict higher stress in their spouses, leading to increased depression in men as which to the loss of spousal emotional support [90].

A supportive family environment did not appear to buffer the adverse relation between workload and mental well-being outcomes, in most cases. Coinciding with stress-buffering hypothesis [22], we expected that social support serves as a resource buffering the stress process [62, 64]. Chinese adult children and their parents have turned to one another for assistance in the face of widespread workplace competitiveness and limited formal access to family benefits and services. Yet, the protective role of family support is debated, as some studies highlight its benefits in reducing work-family conflicts and promoting stability [91], while others suggest that intergenerational instrumental support can negatively affect the well-being of both parents and children [92]. Our findings are most in line with the latter. Nevertheless, our findings also indicate that instrumental support from parents-in-law played a protective role in the relationship between total workload effects (i.e., paid + unpaid) and depression in husbands. It implies that assistance from wives’ family alleviates some of the stress associated with overwhelming workloads, enabling husbands to maintain a better balance between work and family life. A significant role of a wife’s family support may reflect the persistence of patrilineal systems in Chinese society, where family ties and obligations are often traced through the male lineage [93]. Interestingly, family support did not buffer negative effects of workload on women’s mental well-being. This discrepancy might be derived from the fact that, while men may benefit from instrumental support provided by their in-laws, it is less likely for women to experience the same relief. This may be related to entrenched expectations that women remain primarily responsible for household labour regardless of external support.

Likewise, the presence of wives’ family support amplified the negative association between wives’ unpaid workload and husbands’ SWB. This might be explained by the husband’s need to conform to societal norms (i.e., the role of primary breadwinner). Husbands may experience increased pressure to concentrate on paid work when their wives and families offer substantial support, which may result in stress. While wives do more domestic work, and with additional help from their extended family, husbands may experience heightened mental burden due to their detachment from domestic life and intensified focus on economic responsibilities.

Strengths and limitations

The study is subject to certain limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents us from determining the causal relationship between workload and mental well-being outcomes. Future research could explore crossover effects over time and consider the interdependence between couples [94]. Second, for key variables including SWB and workload, this study relied on single-dimensional measurements rather than multidimensional or validated scales. This approach might have lowered the data’s variance captured [28]. In addition, our dataset was unable to measure workload beyond paid and unpaid activities, factors such as cognitive and emotional labour, were not thus considered. The exclusion of emotional support, a crucial aspect of family support, further restricts insights into gendered coping differences [64]. Lastly, findings are based on an urban sample, limiting their generalisability to rural settings where agricultural labour dominates. In the future, the perspective might be enlarged to rural areas and look at work-family interaction patterns according to various occupational structures. Chinese society, where the gendered division of labour is more evident and the wives’ role in the family is more central, implicates a stronger reflection of the conventional pattern of “the man is the supporting pillar, and the woman is in charge of the household” [95]. The findings brought forward in current study might also be distinct in Western societies, which are more individualistic (and less relying on traditional gender roles). Therefore, it is imperative that future research focuses on this issue.

This study also has several strengths. First, it fills the empirical gap in the existing literature by focusing on Chinese urban dual-earner couples grounded in the cross-domain transmission perspective. To the best of our knowledge, this research is the first study to unravel the complex, reciprocal within-couple relationship and mental well-being outcomes in Chinese context, considering all types of workloads. By applying APIM techniques, we were able to simultaneously assess the relation of paid, unpaid, and total workload effects with mental well-being for both husbands and wives. Lastly, we measured both positive and negative components of mental well-being.

Policy implications

Our study justifies interventions aimed at improving the mental health and well-being of dual-career couples from several angles. The adverse relation of total and paid workload with well-being outcomes and the lack of evidence that family support eases that relation underscore the necessity of family-friendly workplace policies. The limited relief that parental assistance provides for dual-earner couples in private sphere, even in family-oriented societies such as China, emphasises the necessity of supportive public policies. In the context of Chinese cultural norms, paid employment is often prioritized as a means of securing family stability. Yet, the widespread overtime culture reduces employees’ capacity to fulfil family responsibilities, contributing to stress transmission within households [96]. Innovatively, employers may consider work characteristics of employee’s spouses—such as job type, hours, and contract status—when designing policies to optimize work schedules and enhance employees’ work-family facilitation.

Previous studies have shown that telecommuting, flexitime, and compressed workweeks aid employees in balancing work and family demands [97]. To prevent unnecessary overtime and ensure employees have enough rest and recovery time, employers need to foster a supportive work culture and reduce overtime incentives [98]. Government policies ought to discourage excessive working hours and support employers in the implementation of reasonable flexible working arrangements. Targeted mental support programmes can also be established to address work-related mental health concerns effectively [99].

Conclusion

Our study, conducted among Chinese dual-earner couples using a dyadic approach, sheds light on the crossover effects of various workloads on individual and spousal mental well-being, thereby enhancing our comprehension of gender disparities in mental well-being within couples. Our findings indicate that higher levels of depression are associated with higher paid workload, particularly among men. Regarding SWB, the results show that only women’s SWB is related to their own paid workload. Notably, the unpaid workload of both genders appears not to influence mental well-being outcomes. A supportive family environment, where extended family members help with housework, plays a part in reducing the relation of workloads with depression among husbands, thus contributing to attaining work-family balance. This research underscores the importance of implementing family-friendly workplace policies in terms of work hours and schedules, particularly in contexts such as China, to promote better work-family balance and mental well-being outcomes within couples.
