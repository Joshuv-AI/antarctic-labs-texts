Targeted genetic screening for identifying carrier status and early-onset disease risks in Chinese newborns
Authors: Chanjuan Hao, Xuyun Hu, Ruolan Guo, Zhan Qi, Limin Xie, Feng Jin, Huimin Li, Xin Ni, Wei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Expanded newborn genetic screening can identify a wide range of inherited conditions early in life. However, the prevalence and distribution of pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants in large-scale cohorts remain underexplored in the Chinese population.Methods:We screened 33,894 newborns using targeted sequencing of 465 genes associated with monogenic disorders. Variants were classified with locally optimized American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines, focusing on pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants. We analyzed cumulative carrier rates, disease-specific prevalence, and regional differences.Results:Here we show that among 33,894 newborns, 16,687 (49.2%) carry at least one pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant. In total, 22,457 such alleles are detected across 427 (91.8%) genes. Detection rates are higher in the south (52.1%) than in the north (48.5%). On average, each newborn carries 0.7 variants. The most frequent genes (allele frequency >1%) include GJB2 (5.56%), PAH (1.41%), and SLC26A4 (1.30%). The cumulative predicted incidence of 57 inborn errors of metabolism included in newborn tandem mass spectrometry screening is 1/2,177, with PAH, MMACHC, SLC22A5, MMUT and SLC25A13 as the main contributors. Regional analysis reveales significant geographic variation, with three inborn errors of metabolism (e.g., methylmalonic aciduria, phenylketonuria) more common in the north, five disorders (e.g. G6PD deficiency, thalassemia) more common in the south.Conclusions:This large-scale study highlights the utility of targeted genetic screening for identifying carrier status and early-onset disease risks in newborns. The findings provide a critical foundation for integrating genetic screening into routine newborn care and for optimizing public health strategies.Subject terms: Paediatrics, Epidemiology, Metabolic disorders, Genetic testing, Population screening

Introduction

Newborn screening (NBS) programs have significantly evolved since their inception in the 1960s, initially focusing on detecting treatable inborn errors of metabolism such as phenylketonuria (PKU). The introduction of tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) expanded the scope of these programs to encompass dozens of inborn errors of metabolism (IEMs), emphasizing the early diagnosis and intervention for conditions with severe health implications1–4. Despite these advances, many early-onset monogenic diseases with actionable outcomes remain undetected by conventional biochemical methods, highlighting the need for more comprehensive approaches.

The advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS) has revolutionized genetic diagnostics by offering a high-throughput, accurate, and cost-effective platform capable of identifying a wide spectrum of genetic variants5–8. Targeted sequencing panels curated for specific diseases have demonstrated the potential to overcome limitations of traditional NBS by expanding the scope of detectable conditions and identifying carrier states that may prompt parental screening or family genetic counseling, thereby indirectly informing future reproductive decisions9–12. Moreover, genetic data can facilitate closed-loop management by integrating diagnosis, genetic counseling, and interventions, thereby potentially improving the standard of care for affected families13,14. In our previous studies, targeted sequencing of selected gene panels was employed to screen newborns for relatively common and severe early-onset diseases. These efforts demonstrated the feasibility and clinical utility of incorporating genetic approaches into routine NBS programs9,10.

Building on these findings, we undertake a large-scale genetic screening initiative to assess the prevalence and spectrum of pathogenic variants across 465 genes in 33,894 newborns. We identify 22,457 pathogenic variants across 427 genes, further emphasizing the value of comprehensive genetic analysis in uncovering the genetic landscape of monogenic diseases in diverse populations. This study provides an in-depth analysis of carrier frequencies, predicted disease prevalence, variant distribution, disease spectrum in a large cohort of Chinese newborns, thereby contributing foundational data for understanding population genetics and informing future public health strategies.

Methods

Study population

This study enrolled a total of 33,894 newborns from 31 hospitals across 14 provinces in China between 2017 and 2024. The cohort was designed to represent a broad demographic, including newborns from diverse geographic regions, ensuring a comprehensive view of the genetic landscape across the country. The ratio of the number of newborns from northern provinces to those from southern provinces is ~4:1 (27,174/6720, using the Qinling-Huaihe Line as the boundary).

Blood samples were collected from each participant within 72 h of birth. The first dried blood spot card was used for routine national newborn screening (e.g., phenylketonuria and congenital hypothyroidism), following standardized protocols. For this study, a second dried blood spot card was collected using the same procedure. After receiving pre-test counseling by trained medical staff at the participating hospitals, voluntary pregnant women participated in this study before delivery. Informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants, and the study received an approval from the institutional review board of Beijing Children’s Hospital (2017-K-39). The inclusion criteria for this study were based on standard newborn screening protocols, with no exclusions based on gestational age, birth weight, or other clinical parameters. Detailed demographic information, including sex, ethnicity (parental report), and region of birth, was recorded for each participant.

Gene panel, sequencing protocol and variant interpretation

A custom-designed targeted sequencing panel was developed to cover 465 genes associated with early-onset, severe, and actionable genetic disorders. A detailed list of the genes included in the panel, their inclusion criteria, and information on library structure, sequencing, quality control criteria, variant calling, and interpretation can be found in our previously published results (Supplementary Fig. 1)9. Sequencing was performed at >200× depth with high target coverage (>95%) and base quality (Q20 > 90%; Q30 > 80%). The raw sequencing data were analyzed using standard bioinformatics pipelines to identify variants in the target genes. Variants were classified according to the locally optimized American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1)9,15. Only variants classified as pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) variants were included in the final analysis. Variant of uncertain significance (VUS) were not included in the prevalence calculations.

Statistics and reproducibility

Descriptive statistics were used to calculate carrier rates, predicted incidence, cumulative carrier rate (CCR), and the overall prevalence of P/LP variants in the cohort. For specific diseases such as IEMs, related genes were grouped together. The combined carrier rate (CCR) was then calculated as the probability of carrying a P/LP variant in any of the genes within that group. For autosomal recessive conditions, the predicted incidence for a gene was calculated as the square of the cumulative allele frequency of all P/LP variants identified within that gene ((Fgene)2), assuming Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. For regional analyses, statistical tests, including chi-square tests and Fisher’s exact test, were used to examine the differences in variant distribution between different regions and demographic groups. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall prevalence and distribution of P/LP variants

Among the 33,894 newborns from 31 hospitals across 14 provinces in China (Fig. 1A), 16,687 individuals (49.2%) were identified as carriers of P/LP variants. Sanger sequencing validation was performed for 926 variants when confirmation of inheritance (e.g., de novo status or phasing of compound heterozygotes) was requested during post-test genetic counseling. All tested variants were confirmed, demonstrating a 100% concordance rate with next-generation sequencing (NGS) findings. The positive detection rates in the northern and southern populations were 48.5% (13,185/27,174) and 52.1% (3502/6720), respectively. The comparison showed a statistically significant difference (p = 1.4346 × 10−7). Given the observed effect size and the respective sample sizes, a post-hoc power analysis indicated that the study had greater than 99% power (α = 0.05, two-sided) to detect this difference, confirming that the sample size was adequate for this regional comparison. A total of 22,457 P/LP variant detections were identified, involving 6,019 distinct variants across 427 of the 465 genes analyzed. On average, each newborn carried 0.7 P/LP variants, with 5 individuals carrying 6 variants, 18 carrying 5 variants, and 11,809 carrying only one variant (Fig. 1B).

Gene-specific allele frequencies and variant spectrum

There were 38 (8.2%) genes without any detected P/LP variants. Separately, 28 genes had an allele frequency (AF) greater than 0.2% (Supplementary Table 2). The most frequently affected genes were GJB2, PAH, and SLC26A4, all with allele frequencies exceeding 1%. GJB2, associated with nonsyndromic hereditary hearing loss, was the most commonly affected gene, accounting for 21.1% (3615/16,687) of positive individuals. The predominant variant in GJB2 was c.109G>A, which had an AF of 4.24% (2875/67,788). Across the 465 genes analyzed, a total of 6019 distinct P/LP variants were identified, of which 1032 (17.1%) were previously unreported. Among these variants, 45% were missense variants, while truncating variants (including frameshift, nonsense, and splicing variants) accounted for 51% (Fig. 1C). On average, 13 distinct P/LP variants were detected per gene. The genes with the highest mutational heterogeneity were USH2A, DNAH11, PAH, and DNAH5 (Fig. 1D).

Disease screening results and risk estimation

When categorizing the variants by disease type, IEMs and hearing loss accounted for the largest proportions. Fifty-seven IEMs screened through MS/MS, accounted for 21.7% of all detected variants (4883/22,457). The most frequently affected genes in this category were PAH, MMACHC, SLC22A5, and MMUT (Supplementary Table 2). The predicted overall incidence of these 57 IEMs was 0.46 per 1000 live births (1/2177), with a CCR of 13.7% (Supplementary Table 3). This result aligns closely with the overall incidence of IEMs reported by the Maternal and Child Health Surveilance of China (www.mchscn.cn/). Among IEMs, those with highest incidence rates include the hyperphenylalaninemias (screened for PAH, PTS, QDPR, GCH1, and PCBD1, with a predicted incidence of 1/5,011) and methylmalonic aciduria (screened for MMACHC, MUT, MMAA, MMAB, and MMADHC, with a predicted incidence of 1/7649) (Supplementary Table 3).

Hereditary hearing loss, primarily caused by P/LP variants in GJB2 and SLC26A4, made up 35.6% of the variants (8015/22,457). Notably, the GJB2 c.109G>A variant has a high frequency and incomplete penetrance. Therefore, when excluding GJB2 c.109G>A, the proportion of hearing loss variants decreased to 22.8% (5140/22,457). Thus, the overall incidence of autosomal recessive (AR) hearing loss was 0.48/1000 (1/2105), with a CCR of 13.6%. However, if GJB2 c.109G>A is included, the incidence rate and CCR rise to 3.4/1000 (1/295) and 21.1%, respectively. The CCR for all AR diseases screened was 46.9% (Supplementary Table 3).

In addition, a total of 542 P/LP alleles were identified across 34 genes of inborn errors of immunity (IEIs), with the top three affected genes being PRF1, TNFRSF13B, and ATM. Since some IEIs can present with either AR or AD inheritance patterns (e.g., common variable immunodeficiency 2 caused by TNFRSF13B mutation, thrombophilia caused by PROC mutation, among others), the predicted incidence of IEIs ranged between 1/321 and 1/5826. Predicted incidences of other common genetic disorders included spherocytosis at 1/5619, albinism (both nonsyndromic and syndromic) at 1/22,776, nephrotic syndrome at 1/30,563, primary ciliary dyskinesia at 1/66,990, epidermolysis bullosa at 1/351,935, nephronophthisis at 1/352,015, Bartter syndrome at 1/1,510,092, and Perrault syndrome at 1/1,210,859.

High-frequency variants analysis in the population

The analysis of detected P/LP variants revealed key high-frequency variants across multiple genes. Among the most frequently identified variants, GJB2 c.109G>A was the most dominant, detected in 2763 individuals, with an AF significantly higher than other variants, followed by GJB2 c.235delC (611 individuals) and SLC26A4 c.919-2A>G (394 individuals). Additionally, genes associated with IEMs, such as SLC25A13, MMACHC, SLC22A5, also harbored high-frequency variants like SLC22A5 c.1400C>G and SLC25A13 c.852_855delTATG, which were frequently observed among affected individuals. All the 47 high-frequency P/LP variants that occurred more than 50 times in the population are listed in Fig. 2A.

For the ten most frequently affected genes, variant distributions highlighted distinct spectra (Fig. 2B). Given the exceptionally high carrier rate and incomplete penetrance of GJB2 c.109G>A, we excluded it from the analysis of GJB2 mutation spectrum. As shown in Fig. 2B, the top three P/LP variants in 9 out of 10 genes (excluding PAH) accounted for more than 50% of the total affected individuals. Notably, the most common variants in three genes, GJB2 (c.235delC), SLC22A5 (c.1400C>G), and OCA2 (c.1363A>G), each accounted for ~70% of all affected individuals. The PAH gene had 93 distinct P/LP variants, with the top five variants accounting for only 37%, reflecting the greater genetic heterogeneity of phenylketonuria (PKU).

We further compared the AFs of all 47 high-frequency variants in our cohort with the overall and East Asian AFs from the gnomAD v4 database. The results showed that, with the exception of MYO6 c.2751dupA, all other variants had significantly higher AFs in our cohort compared to the gnomAD overall AFs (p < 0.0001), particularly the eight variants MMACHC c.609G>A, PAH c.1197A>T, MMUT c.1663G>A, SLC45A2 c.478G>C, G6PD c.1466G>T, CFTR c.2909G>A, USH2A c.8559-2A>G, and MMACHC c.80A>G, all of which had odds ratios (OR) > 100 (Supplementary Table 4).

Out of the 47 high-frequency P/LP variants, 33 variants had AFs significantly higher (p < 0.0001) than those in the gnomAD v4 East Asian population, while the other 14 variants had significantly lower AFs (p < 0.0001), reflecting the unique mutation spectrum in the Chinese cohort. Notably, 12 variants with significantly increased frequencies had OR values > 4, and these 12 variants completely encompass the 8 most significantly elevated variants compared to the overall AFs listed above. The remaining 4 variants include TYR c.896G>A, G6PC c.446G>A, PAH c.728G>A, and MMACHC c.658_660delAAG (Supplementary Table 4).

Regional differences in disease prevalence and genetic variations

We performed a regional distribution analysis of the most common genetic diseases listed in Supplementary Table 2. We calculated the frequencies of P/LP variants and the OR for northern and southern populations in China. The threshold for identifying genes with regional differences was set as p-value < 0.0001 and |log2 OR|> 1.

The analysis revealed significant differences in disease prevalence between northern and southern populations. Notably, three IEMs (methylmalonic aciduria and homocystinuria (CblC type), phenylketonuria, and short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency) exhibited OR > 2, indicating that the prevalence of these diseases was significantly higher in the northern population compared to the southern population. Five other diseases showed significantly higher prevalence in the southern population, including glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency and beta-thalassemia (Table 1). Notably, the GJB2 gene mutation showed an elevated prevalence in the southern population, primarily due to a significant increase in the detection rate of the specific variant c.109 G>A in the south. After excluding this variant, the difference in GJB2 mutation prevalence between the two populations became considerably less pronounced (Table 1).

We further analyzed the mutational spectra of the diseases mentioned in Table 1 and identified significant regional differences in both variant frequencies and profiles for the PAH, OCA2, and HBB genes between northern and southern populations. For example, as previously mentioned, the PAH gene had higher genetic heterogeneity, and its variants spectrum showed that the most common variant in the north was c.728G>A, representing 15.4% of PAH variants, whereas in the south, this variant accounted for only 9% (ranking third). In contrast, the most common variant in the south, c.516G>T (13.0%), was much less prevalent in the north, with a frequency of only 3% (ranking tenth). In addition, two hearing loss genes, GJB2 and OTOF, while sharing the same most common variants in both regions, exhibited substantial differences in their frequencies. The GJB2 c.109G>A and OTOF c.5098G>C variants were significantly more frequent in the southern population than in the northern population (90.0% vs. 66.5% and 83.6% vs. 48.8%, respectively, Table 1).

Discussion

This study represents one of the largest newborn genetic screening efforts in China, spanning a diverse cohort of 33,894 newborns across 13 provinces. By using NGS, we identified 22,457 P/LP variants across 427 genes, with nearly half (49.2%) of the newborns carrying at least one variant. Interestingly, we identified 1032 different novel variants (17.1% of all variants), which significantly enriches the mutation spectrum for these diseases. Our findings also revealed regional differences in genetic variant prevalence and disease profiles between northern and southern populations, providing critical insights into the genetic landscape of monogenic diseases in China.

One of the key aspects of this study is the estimation of the predicted incidence rates for various genetic diseases, particularly IEMs and hereditary hearing loss. These estimates were based on the prevalence of P/LP variants in the cohort, providing valuable insights into the burden of these diseases in the population. For the 57 IEMs that can also be detected through MS/MS, the predicted incidence rate was found to be 1/2177 live births. The predicted incidence rate is consistent with previously reported biochemical screening results for inherited IEMs (ranging from 1/1000 to 1/4000 with a higher prevalence observed in northern regions compared to southern regions)16–21. The CCR for these disorders was calculated to be 13.7%, indicating that a significant proportion of the population carries at least one P/LP variant in genes associated with metabolic diseases, highlighting the necessity of newborn screening throughout China, especially in the north.

It is worth noting that although the ranking is generally consistent with previously reported MS/MS-based studies, the predicted genetic incidence rates for hyperphenylalaninemia and methylmalonic aciduria are slightly higher than nationwide levels through MS/MS screening16. While this discrepancy may be partially attributed to differences in sample sources, it also suggests that newborn screening based on genetic sequencing may offer higher sensitivity compared to traditional biochemical methods. For example, genetic sequencing can directly identify the underlying cause in cases with atypical biochemical features or mild phenotypes, and detect diseases not included in current biochemical panels22,23.

The second-highest incidence in the screening was observed for hereditary hearing loss. The incidence for hearing loss varied significantly depending on whether the GJB2 c.109G>A, a highly prevalent variant with incomplete penetrance, was included in the calculation24. Considering that the biallelic c.109G>A variant in GJB2 leads to hearing loss in less than 20% of newborns10,25, our predicted results are consistent with the reported incidence of hereditary hearing loss26,27. Based on our findings, the most common genes associated with hearing loss include GJB2, SLC26A4, USH2A, OTOF, and MYO6. Pathogenic variants identified in these genes account for 71.2% of all hearing loss variants, and if GJB2 c.109G>A is excluded, the proportion is 21%. Thus, a broader panel of hearing loss genes, including but not limited to USH2A, OTOF, and MYO6, are recommended for NBS program based on their high prevalence and mutational heterogeneity observed in our cohort.

Our study also provides valuable insights into the mutation spectrum of more common inherited conditions in China. A comparison with the gnomAD database reveals that the frequencies of the 47 most common variants in the Chinese population are significantly higher than the gnomAD overall frequencies. For instance, although the PAH gene exhibits high genetic heterogeneity compared to other frequently affected genes, 3 of its top 5 variants were initially reported in the Chinese population28–30. Approximately 60% of the studies depositing these variants in the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD, 2024.4) were conducted in Chinese cohorts. Notably, several other high-frequency variants, such as SLC45A2 c.478G>C, OCA2 c.1363A>G, MMUT c.1286A>G, and OTOF c.5098G>C, have only been reported in the Chinese population31–34. Furthermore, the 17.1% novel variant discovery rate in this study is also noteworthy, as it not only expands the known genetic spectrum of several disorders but also further suggests that the genetic diversity within the Chinese population may differ significantly from other ethnic groups. This population-specific pattern is further supported by a detailed comparison of variant frequencies with data from the Japanese ToMMo (Tohoku Medical Megabank Organization) database (Supplementary Table 4)35.

The regional analysis in our screening cohort reveals significant differences in disease profiles and incidence rates between northern and southern populations. This division was based on the well-established Qinling-Huaihe Line, which serves as a traditional geographical, climatic, and cultural boundary in China, usually associated with distinct population genetic background. For instance, G6PD deficiency and beta-thalassemia were significantly more prevalent in the southern population, whereas certain IEMs, such as methylmalonic aciduria and phenylketonuria, exhibited higher prevalence rates in the north. This is consistent with previous studies reporting regional genetic background differences across China36. Although factors such as sampling variations and recruitment periods may play a role, the significant regional genetic differences observed in our study are predominantly accounted for by genetic diversity shaped by ancestral population histories. Additionally, environmental factors, including exposure to region-specific risk factors, may also contribute. A typical example is the long-standing selective pressure of malaria in southern China, which has led to the persistence and high prevalence of protective variants, such as those related to G6PD deficiency and beta-thalassemia37–39. The observed difference in positive detection rates between the northern and southern populations (48.5% vs 52.1%, p = 1.4346 × 10⁻⁷) may be largely explained by the substantially higher prevalence of G6PD deficiency and HBA/HBB-related variants in the south.

Notable differences in mutation spectrum was also observed in other genes. For example, in the PAH gene, which is associated with PKU, exhibited distinct regional variations: in the northern population, the c.728G>A variant is predominant, whereas in the southern population, c.516G>T is more common. Similarly, in the GJB2 gene, particularly the c.109G>A variant, was significantly more frequent in the southern population, contributing substantially to the overall incidence of hearing loss in the south. Such regional differences may be attributed to historical population bottlenecks or founder effects40.

The observed regional differences may inform the design of public health strategies, although the impact of population migration and admixture must be carefully considered to ensure effectiveness and equity. In our cohort, the identification of region-specific genetic profiles and the high prevalence of certain high-frequency variants not only deepens our understanding of the genetic landscape across different regions of China but also offers practical benefits for optimizing screening strategies. Our findings have profound implications for public health policy, particularly in the design and implementation of both newborn and carrier screening programs in China and internationally. Integrating genetic screening into existing NBS programs (especially for conditions with high carrier frequencies or significant regional variability) can provide critical information for risk assessment by prompting parental screening or family genetic counseling, ultimately reducing the burden of genetic diseases in future generations. Policy-makers should carefully balance screening costs and benefits by prioritizing diseases with high incidence and those amenable to early intervention while progressively expanding the screening panels to include additional conditions. As the technology for genetic screening becomes more affordable and widely accessible, incorporating NGS into routine screening could lead to more personalized and effective healthcare management.

In addition, cost-effectiveness analysis is crucial for guiding public health policies. Although a formal economic evaluation was beyond the scope of this study, our findings carry important implications. The high prevalence of certain conditions (e.g., GJB2-related hereditary hearing loss, thalassemias, and G6PD deficiency) suggests that sequencing-based newborn screening could be particularly cost-effective in China, as it enables the simultaneous detection of multiple high-burden disorders in a single test. If confirmed by future cost-effectiveness analyses, such efficiency would support the integration of genetic screening with traditional biochemical methods in nationwide newborn screening programs.

Expanded genetic screening, while offering significant benefits for early diagnosis, introduces complex ethical considerations. A key challenge is obtaining truly informed consent from parents for genetic information that may not have immediate health implications for their child, potentially impacting the child’s future “right not to know”41. Identifying asymptomatic carriers can lead to parental anxiety or stigmatization. While our panel primarily targets genes associated with pediatric-onset conditions (e.g., ATM for ataxia-telangiectasia), carrier status in some genes may still carry implications for adult-onset health risks or reproductive planning. Addressing these issues requires robust genetic counseling, clear communication of results, and careful consideration of long-term follow-up strategies and data privacy, especially for information not immediately actionable in infancy42.

Despite the substantial insights provided by our study, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, although our screening cohort was large and geographically diverse, it may not fully capture the genetic heterogeneity present across all Chinese populations, particularly among underrepresented regions or minority groups. The sample size from the north was higher than the south (4:1), and the proportions of samples from each province were not equal, which may lead to bias in AF or spectrum comparison. While this imbalance primarily reflects recruitment distribution, it may also limit the generalizability of regional comparisons; future studies with more balanced sampling or sensitivity analyses will be necessary to validate these regional patterns. Second, the study focused on P/LP variants, potentially overlooking VUS, which could provide additional contribution to disease risk. The exclusion of VUS was to maintain a high level of stringency in our reported prevalence figures. However, VUS represent an important area for future research and reclassification as more evidence becomes available. Similarly, from a technical perspective, our current gene panel does not cover non-coding regions or copy number variations (CNVs), which may result in the omission of certain pathogenic variants. Expanding the scope of genetic testing could enhance the comprehensiveness of screening and improve the accuracy of disease risk prediction. Moreover, although SMN1 was included in the panel, only SNVs were analyzed due to technical limitations. As its main pathogenic mechanism involves CNVs, which were not reliably detectable at the time of this study. Future expanded genetic screening panels should ideally incorporate CNV analysis to capture such important genetic disorders. Third, the incidence estimation only counts for the most representative disease-causative genes, without counting the genes with very rare incidences or uncovered genes. Thus, incidence of some diseases with genetic heterogeneity may be underestimated. Finally, as a cross-sectional study, our research lacks long-term follow-up data to evaluate the clinical outcomes of newborns identified with P/LP variants. This limits our ability to assess genotype-phenotype correlations, actual disease manifestation, and the clinical utility of the screening. Such follow-up would involve detailed clinical assessments and ongoing monitoring, helping to clarify genotype-phenotype correlations, disease severity, and the effectiveness of early interventions. Key outcomes to be tracked in future longitudinal studies include disease onset and severity, age at diagnosis, clinical response to early interventions, and long-term health trajectories. Our study serves as an important step, pinpointing individuals who could benefit most from such future, intensive follow-up. Future studies incorporating longitudinal data will be essential to assess the clinical utility, cost-effectiveness, and feasibility of implementing expanded newborn genetic screening at a population scale, particularly in resource-limited settings, and to further refine screening strategies based on real-world outcomes.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study is the first large-scale investigation of monogenic disease carrier rates in a Chinese newborn cohort, and uses these findings to predict local disease incidence, a significant improvement over earlier estimates based largely on Western populations. Our findings provide a comprehensive overview of the prevalence, carrier rates, and mutation spectra of P/LP variants across China, revealing distinct regional differences between northern and southern populations that must be considered in newborn screening strategies. Furthermore, the carrier rates and high-frequency variants information in our study offer vital insights for genetic counseling and clinical practice, enabling informed reproductive planning and personalized risk management. These findings also provide a solid foundation for targeted drug development and precision medicine initiatives. Overall, these results enhance our understanding of the genetic landscape of monogenic disorders in China and lay a robust foundation for tailored screening programs and public health initiatives.
