Pharmacokinetics and Tissue Distribution in Rats After Oral Administration of Tibetan Medicine Wuwei Leze Powder
Authors: Ping Wang, Peng Wang, YiRui Wu, ShuiYing Wu, HuiJuan Xu, WenYu Yang, Ya Tu, Chuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWuwei Leze Powder (WLP, སླྱེ་ཏྱེསལྔ་ཐང།) is a classic Tibetan medicine formula with clinical efficacy against rheumatoid arthritis (RA), but its in vivo pharmacokinetic (PK) behavior is unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the absorption, metabolism, pharmacokinetics, and tissue distribution of WLP in rats. UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS and UPLC‐QQQ‐MS were used to qualitatively and quantitatively analyze WLP components in rat plasma and tissues. Fifty‐six plasma constituents (21 prototypes, 35 metabolites) were identified. Nine compounds—syringin, cordifolioside A, sweroside, mangiferin, norswertianolin, isovitexin, ellagic acid, amarogentin, and arjungenin—were quantified. All were detectable in plasma within 15 min, with peak concentrations at 0.75–2 h ellagic acid and mangiferin showed the highest systemic exposure (AUC 0→t: 5007.26 and 3354.21 µg/L·h; Cmax : 1491.27 and 749.00 µg/L). Half‐lives ranged from 2.63 to 5.26 h. All nine compounds distributed to all tested tissues within 0.5 h, with residues significantly decreased by 8 h and no accumulation observed. The nine compounds are rapidly absorbed and broadly distributed, particularly to synovium, liver, and kidney. Amarogentin, mangiferin, ellagic acid, cordifolioside A, and arjungenin are identified as primary in vivo exposed compounds, supporting the potential pharmacodynamic material basis of WLP against RA.

Introduction

Wuwei Leze Powder (WLP) (སླྱེ་ཏྱེསལྔ་ཐང།) is a traditional Tibetan medicine (TTM) formula in the Tibetan medicine classics “The Four Medical Tantras” [1]. Currently, WLP has been included in the “Catalogue of Ancient Classic Prescriptions (Second Batch)‐Tibetan Medicine” (No. 22) issued by the National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine, and is simultaneously incorporated into the promulgated Tibetan medicine standards [2]. WLP is composed of five Tibetan herbal medicines, including Tinospora sinensis, Swertia chirayita, Terminalia bellirica, Terminalia chebula, and Phyllanthus emblica. It possesses anti‐inflammatory properties and dry huang‐shui, and is used to treat Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [3]. T. sinensis acts as the “jūn” (principal) herb, effectively clearing heat and regulating pathological disorders. S. chirayita is the “chén” (minister) herb, specifically clearing heat from the liver and gallbladder. T. bellirica, T. chebula, and P. emblica are the “zuǒ” (assistant) herbs and have the effect of harmonizing the three factors [1].

The selection of these medicinal plants as anti‐RA agents is grounded in both long‐standing traditional Tibetan medical practice and accumulating modern pharmacological evidence. Modern studies have demonstrated that T. sinensis extract significantly alleviates paw swelling, reduces synovial proliferation, and inhibits inflammatory cell infiltration in collagen‑induced arthritis (CIA) rats. Tinosporine, a bioactive compound from T. sinensis, exhibits anti‑inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects and has been shown to alleviate CIA by modulating the gut microbiota‑metabolome‑immunity axis [4]. Notably, the use of medicinal and edible homologous plants (MEHPs) for managing inflammatory diseases, including RA, has gained increasing attention in recent years. Phenolic acids, a crucial group of natural bioactive substances widely distributed in MEHPs, exhibit significant anti‐inflammatory activities through the regulation of multiple signaling pathways such as NF‐κB, MAPK, NLRP3, Nrf2, TLRs, and IL‐17, making them promising candidates for the development of anti‐inflammatory drugs and functional foods [5]. Beyond phenolic acids, numerous edible plants have been demonstrated to possess anti‐arthritic potential. For instance, the bioactive fraction of Camellia sinensis var. assamica (Assam variety white tea), rich in epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) and epigallocatechin (EGC), has been shown to exert remarkable anti‐RA activity by inducing apoptosis of synovial fibroblast cells, downregulating TNF‐α and IL‐6, and inhibiting p‐STAT3 expression [6]. These findings underscore the broad potential of edible and medicinal plants as sources of anti‐RA agents, supporting the rationale for investigating the pharmacodynamic material basis of WLP.

Most existing studies focus on the anti‐RA activity of single herbs or isolated monomers, while the in vivo process of WLP as a holistic compound prescription remains poorly understood. Specifically, the absorption and metabolic profiles of multiple components after oral administration of WLP, their pharmacokinetic (PK) characteristics in systemic circulation, and their distribution patterns in RA target tissues (especially the synovial membrane, the primary lesion site) have not been systematically elucidated. This knowledge gap hinders the identification of the core pharmacodynamic substances and the interpretation of the multicomponent synergistic mechanism of the formula. Clinical trial results indicate that WLP demonstrates significant efficacy in alleviating symptoms in patients with RA, with an efficacy rate exceeding 90% [7]. Our previous studies have shown that the WLP exerts anti‐RA effects in a collagen‐induced arthritis (CIA) rat model by modulating inflammatory responses and metabolic pathways mediated through the Wnt/RANKL/NF‐κB axis, effectively suppressing synovium inflammation and improving bone erosion [8]. In addition, Tinosporine from the “jūn” (principal) herb T. sinensis modulated the gut microbiota‐metabolome‐immune axis to suppress inflammatory responses in CIA rats. This intervention corrected abnormalities in gut microbiota and differential metabolites while maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier [4]. However, the material basis of WLP is not clear.

The material basis of drug efficacy is a cornerstone of TTM's connotation and a key goal and technical challenge in its modernization research. The efficacy of TTM and its compound prescriptions is characterized by multicomponent, multi‐target, multifunctional, and multilevel properties [9]. Clarifying their complex system and mechanisms of action with just one or a few components is challenging. Prior to this, we identified 109 components from WLP using the UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS method. These components included 24 clerodane furanoditerpenoids, 15 xanthones, 15 tannins,14 flavonoids, 9 alkaloids, 7 iridoids, 7 triterpenoids, 3 phenylpropanoids, and 15 other types of compounds [8]. Clerodane furanoditerpenoids were primarily sourced from the the “jūn” (principal) herb T. sinensis, the xanthones were chiefly derived from the “chén” (minister) herb S. chirayita, and the tannins were obtained from the “zuǒ” (assistant) herbs T. bellirica, T. chebula, and P. emblica. In addition, we used the UPLC‐QQQ‐MS/MS method to conduct rapid quantitative determination of 13 chemical components (gallic acid, syringin, cordifolioside A, corilagin, sweroside, mangiferin, isorientin, norswertianolin, jatrorrhizine, isovitexin, amarogentin, ellagic acid, arjungenin, etc.) in WLP. However, there is currently a lack of PK models for WLP physiological and pathological situations, as well as tissue distribution (concentration‐time distribution) for quantitative and predictive analysis.

Generally, TCM ingredients can be absorbed into the bloodstream and reach and maintain effective concentrations in target organs, and are key medicinal substances that play a role in vivo [10]. In the clinical practice of TTM, WLP is mainly administered orally in the form of powder. The specific surface area and dispersion degree of drugs in powders are usually larger than those of other solid preparations, and the contact area with biofilm is larger, and the absorption is relatively rapid [11]. Due to the limited level of pharmaceutical production in ancient TTM, it was unable to evaluate and compare the effects of herbal prescriptions in vivo, especially in terms of drug absorption (A), distribution (D), metabolism (M), and excretion (E) [12]. In recent years, researchers have employed chromatography‐mass spectrometry to investigate the PK parameters of TTM compound prescriptions, such as area under concentration‐time curve (AUC), elimination half‐life (t 1/2), maximum concentration (C max), peak time (T max), and tissue distribution, clarifying the interactions among the components of the active substances of TTM in vivo [13]. Especially for the target organs on which each herb or compound exerts therapeutic effects in vivo. Based on our previous phytochemical characterization of WLP and preliminary qualitative analysis in plasma, nine representative compounds were selected for quantitative PK investigation according to four predefined criteria: (1) Confirmed absorption into plasma after oral administration; (2) representation of the major chemical classes and the five constituent herbs of WLP; (3) reported pharmacological relevance to rheumatoid arthritis or inflammatory regulation in previous studies; and (4) availability of high‐purity reference standards for reliable quantitative LC‐MS/MS determination. Therefore, we hypothesized that conducting PK and tissue distribution studies on WLP and assessing the in vivo exposure of its components (as prototypes and/or metabolites) would enable identification of the key pharmacodynamic material basis of WLP.

We adopted a comprehensive strategy combining UPLC‑Q‑TOF‑MS and UPLC‑QQQ‑MS to qualitatively detect prototype compounds and absorbable metabolites in plasma after oral WLP administration, and to quantitatively determine the PK characteristics of the main compounds in plasma and their tissue distribution in target organs. This study aims to provide PK evidence supporting the potential pharmacodynamic material basis of WLP.

Materials and Methods

Chemical Reagents and Materials

The medicinal materials including Tinospora sinensis (Lour.) Merr., Swertia chirayita (Roxb. ex Flemi) Karsten., Terminalia bellirica (Gaertn.) Roxb., Terminalia chebula Retz., and Phyllanthus emblica L. were all acquired from the Qinghai Provincial Tibetan Hospital (Xining, Qinghai, China) in February 2023. All the herbal materials were identified by Prof. Ya Tu of the China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (identification date: March 2023) and are stored in the laboratory of the School of Food and Biological Engineering at Xihua University, Chengdu, Sichuan Province. Voucher specimens (voucher nos. WLP‑2023‑001 through WLP‑2023‑005) have been deposited at the herbarium collection of the School of Food and Bio‐engineering, Xihua University, Chengdu, China. Cordifolioside A (#AFDG1504), sweroside (#AFBG1333), mangiferin (#AFCC1652), quercetin (#AFDB1959), norswertianolin (#AFDI1203), isovitexin (#AFBL2852), ellagic acid (#AFCH2257), amarogentin (#AFDG1002), syringin (#AFBG1364), arjungenin (#AFDI1202), and chloramphenicol (#AF20100214, internal standard, IS) were procured from Chengdu Alfa Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). The purity of each of the compounds that were used in the experiment exceeded 98%. Formic acid (HPLC grade) was procured from Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd., Tianjin, China. Anhydrous ethanol was bought from Chongqing Chuandong Chemical Co., Ltd. Methanol (LC‐MS grade) made available by Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, USA).

Animals

Wistar rats (male, 6 weeks, weighing 200 ± 20 g) were supplied by Chengdu Dasuo Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (China, No. China (SCXK (chuan) 2020‐0030). All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for the Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council). The experimental protocol was granted approval by the Animal Ethics Committee, Xihua University, China (XH‐2023‐02‐01). All rats were housed individually in well‐ventilated cages under ambient conditions maintained at 24°C ± 2.0°C and 50% ± 5% relative humidity. The experimental protocol followed a 12 h light‐dark cycle. Prior to the experiment, rats underwent a one‐week adaptation period during which fresh food and water were provided.

Preparation of WLP Sample

WLP extracts were prepared in our laboratory. The WLP formula is prepared from the following ingredients in a 1:1:1:1:1 ratio: T. sinensis, T. bellirica, T. chebula, P. emblica, and S. chirayita. Briefly, grind the above herbs into fine powder (100 mesh), take about 10 g of each, weigh them up, and place them in a round‐bottomed flask. Then, add 10 times 70% ethanol, soak for 60 min, and heat and reflux for 60 min. Finally, the filtrate was collected and vacuum freeze‐dried to obtain the freeze‐dried powder of the extract for later use. The extraction yield (freeze‑dried powder vs. crude herbal material) was determined to be approximately 42% (w/w).

Preparation of Solutions

Cordifolioside A, sweroside, mangiferin, quercetin, norswertianolin, isovitexin, ellagic acid, amarogentin, syringin, and arjungenin were precisely weighed, dissolved in methanol, and prepared into a stock solution with a concentration of 5 mg/mL. Transfer a portion of the stock solution into a volumetric flask and dilute it with methanol to prepare a mixed solution with a concentration of 10 µg/mL. Finally, dilute this mixed solution with 70% methanol to prepare a series of standard solutions with varying concentrations. Accurately weigh the chloramphenicol reference, dissolve and prepare a stock solution at 5 mg/mL. Before use, dilute with 70% methanol to achieve the required internal standard (IS) working concentration.

Administration and Sample Collection

Preparation of dosage: The recommended clinical daily dose of WLP for adults is 5 g/day. Assuming a standard adult body weight of 60 kg, the human equivalent dose corresponds to approximately 83.3 mg/kg. According to the body surface area normalization method (FDA guidance), the equivalent dose for rats is 0.525 g/kg (approximately 6.3‐fold higher than the human dose on a body weight basis). Therefore, three dose levels (0.263, 0.525, and 2.625 g/kg) were selected for the PK study.

Plasma sample collection: The 18 Wistar rats were divided into three groups (n = 6 per dose group: 0.263, 0.525, and 2.625 g/kg). The same six animals in each dose group were used for serial blood collection at all time points (0, 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h post‑dose) via the retro‑orbital venous plexus. The samples were placed in heparin sodium tubes for anticoagulation. After centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min, the plasma was separated and placed in a refrigerator at −80°C for analysis.

Tissue sample collection: Thirty Wistar rats were separated into five groups based on specific time points, with each group containing six rats. A high dose (2.625 g/kg) was selected for continuous intragastric administration for six days. The high dose was selected for the following reasons: (1) The primary purpose of this study was to comprehensively map the whole‐body distribution of WLP components and their metabolites; (2) pre‐experiments demonstrated that the medium human‐equivalent dose (0.525 g/kg) resulted in analyte concentrations close to or below the LLOQ in poorly perfused tissues such as adipose tissue, brain, and skeletal muscle, which could not support reliable quantitative analysis; (3) WLP components exhibited linear PK characteristics within the tested dose range, and the distribution pattern at the high dose was representative of the therapeutic dose. Rats were anesthetized by the intraperitoneal injection of 0.3% sodium pentobarbital solution. At 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h after administration, the organs and tissues (heart, liver, spleen, synovium, and kidney) of the rats were collected in batches. Subsequently, the tissues were immediately placed in normal saline to wash away the residual blood, weighed, and finally each tissue sample was stored in a refrigerator at −80°C for analysis.

Processing of Plasma and Tissue Samples

Plasma: At each time point, 50 µL of plasma was mixed with 5 µL of IS solution. An eightfold volume of methanol‑acetonitrile (2:8, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 1 min to precipitate proteins. After centrifugation at 4°C and 12,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was transferred to an Eppendorf tube and evaporated to dryness at 37°C. The residue was redissolved in methanol, vortexed for 1 min, ultrasonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged twice at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The final supernatant was collected for analysis.

Tissue: Frozen tissue samples were thawed on ice, blotted dry with filter paper, and accurately weighed. Tissue homogenates were prepared by adding ice‑cold normal saline (1:3, w/v, i.e., 1 g tissue to 3 mL saline) and homogenizing using a high‑speed tissue homogenizer at 50 Hz for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C to remove debris. One thousand microliters of the resulting supernatant (tissue extract) was transferred to a fresh tube and mixed with 10 µL of IS solution. After vortexing, the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 rpm, and the supernatant was evaporated to dryness at 37°C. The residue was redissolved in 100 µL of 50% methanol and processed further as described for plasma samples with the same protein precipitation and centrifugation steps.

Chromatographic and Mass Spectrometric Conditions

Qualitative Mass Spectrometry Analysis

Qualitative analysis was performed using UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS. Chromatographic separation employed an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 µm), with the column temperature set at 30°C. The mobile phase consisted of aqueous solution containing 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B), with the following gradient elution program: 2%–6% B (0–1.1 min); 7%–12% B (1.1–3.5 min); 12%–16% B (3.5–9.5 min); 16%–30% B (9.5–15.5 min); 30%–35% B (15.5–19.5 min); 35%–70% B (19.5–24 min); 70%–85% B (24–31.5 min); 85% B (31.5–33.5 min); 85%–95% B (33.5–36 min); 2% B (36–40 min). Flow rate was set at 0.25 mL/min, with an injection volume of 2 µL.

This UPLC system is used in conjunction with the SYNAPTTM G2 HDMS Q‐TOF tandem mass spectrometer. The mass spectrometer is equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source (Waters, Manchester, UK). In cation mode, the ESI source is operated at a capillary voltage of 3 kV, while in anion mode it operates at a capillary voltage of −2.5 kV. In both modes, the sample cone voltage was set to 25 V. The source temperature was remained at 120°C, in contrast to the desolvation temperature reaching 400°C. The cone gas flow rate and the desolvation gas flow rate were selected at 50 and 1000 L·h−1, depending on the mode. In the data acquisition process, the MSE mode was adopted, with the collision energy of the low‐energy region trap set at 5 eV, while the energy gradient in the high‐energy region was gradually increased from 20 to 50 eV. The collision‐induced dissociation experiments in this study employed argon as the collision gas. The mass spectrometer was calibrated using sodium formate solution within the 100–1200 Dalton range to ensure measurement accuracy and result reproducibility. Data analysis was performed using MassLynx software (V4.2, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) to extend relevant information from spectra that were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes.

Quantitative Mass Spectrometry Analysis

Quantitative analysis was performed using the 8045 LC‐MS/MS (Shimadzu 8045, Japan). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Shim‐pack Scepter (C18) column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.9 µm, Shimadzu Co., Japan). Mobile phase A consisted of an aqueous solution containing 0.1% formic acid, while mobile phase B was methanol. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0.01–8 min: 20%–40% B; 8–10 min: 40%–50% B; 10–12 min: 50%–70% B; 12–13 min: 70%–95% B; 13–13.1 min: 95%–20% B; 13.1–16 min: 20% A. The flow rate was fixed at 0.3 mL/min, with a sample injection volume of 1 µL. Throughout the entire analysis, the cuvette temperature was maintained at 4°C.

The ESI mass spectrometer operates in both positive and negative ion scanning modes, utilizing multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) technology to achieve efficient ion acquisition. The optimized MS parameters listed in Table S1 are as follows: drying gas flow rate: 10.0 L/min; atomizing gas flow rate: 3.0 L/min; heating gas flow rate: 10.0 L/min; DL temperature: 250°C; interface temperature: 300°C; desolventization temperature: 500°C; heating block temperature: 400°C; CID air pressure: 230 kPa.

Method Validation

In accordance with the FDA Bioanalytical Method Validation Guidance (Food and Drug Administration, 2018) and guiding principles for the quantitative analysis of biological outlined in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, 2020 (The State Pharmacopoeia, 2020), we conducted comprehensive validation of the established method. This included assessments of specificity, linearity, accuracy, precision, stability, recovery rate, and matrix effects.

Data Analysis

The PK parameters were analyzed by a noncompartmental method using the DAS 2.3 PK program (Chinese Pharmacology Society); The concentration data were introduced into GraphPad Prism 8.0 software to draw plasma concentration‐time curves and histograms. All findings are presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Differences between groups were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Metabolites Identification

Cordifolioside A‐related metabolites and syringin‐related metabolites were the primary potential exogenous metabolites in plasma. By comparing with reference standards and detecting the characteristic ions m/z 504.1842 and m/z 372.1420, Cordifolioside A and syringin were successfully identified. Taking cordifolioside A as an example, in the negative ion mode, the retention time of this compound was detected to be 5.76 min, the excimer ion peak was m/z 503.1732 [M‐H]−, and the error was 0.7 ppm. It is speculated that its molecular formula is C22H32O13. The fragment ions in its secondary mass spectrum are m/z 293.0844, 209.0793, 195.0615, and 181.0514 correspond, respectively, to [M‐H‐C11H12O4]−, [M‐H‐C11H18O9]−, [M‐H‐C11H18O9‐CH2]−, and [M‐H‐ C11H18O9‐2CH2]−. Based on the database, reference substances, it can be inferred that the compound is cordifolioside A. Its cleavage pathway and secondary mass spectrum are shown in Figure 3. In our research, three cordifolioside A‐related metabolites (M1, M2, M3) were detected. M1 (t R = 3.22 min) exhibited a deprotonated molecular ion at m/z 679.1403 (176 Da (C6H8O6)), and product ions of m/z 651.1547, m/z 503.0548, and m/z 209.0335 were obtained, indicating that M1 is a glucuronide conjugate of cordifolioside A. M2 (t R = 4.05 min) presented a deprotonated molecular ion at m/z 547.0418, and product ions of m/z 371.0205, m/z 355.0929, and m/z 179.0865 were obtained, indicating that M2 was the cordifolioside A+glucuronidation+dexylulofuranose. M3 (t R = 4.48 min) displayed a deprotonated molecular ion at m/z 582.9975, and product ions of m/z 451.2295, m/z 391.0610, and m/z 289.0310 were obtained, indicating that M3 was the cordifolioside A+sulfation. Correspondingly, according to the cracking law, syringin‐related metabolites resulting from metabolic pathways mainly involving glucuronidation (M2, M7), reduction (M4), acetylation (M5, M10), deglucosylation (M6), hydroxylation (M9), dedimethylation (M8), demethylation(M11), and sulfation(M12) (as shown in Figure S1).

Standard Curve Regression Equation, Lower Limit of Detection (LLOD), and Lower Limit of Quantification (LLOQ)

Standard curve regression equation, Linear range, LLOD, and LLOQ of the nine components are shown in Table S3. The correlation values (r≥0.9990) of all the calibration curves of the nine components indicate that within a wide detection range, the concentration of the analytes has a good linear relationship with the peak area. The LLOD and LLOQ of nine components in plasma and tissue were within the range of 0.7–25.3 and 0.2–7.6 ng/Ml, respectively. These results are sufficient to meet the requirements for PK studies and tissue distribution studies.

Precision and Accuracy

The intra‐day and inter‐day precision and accuracy of nine compounds in rat plasma and tissues were both less than 15%. The RSD ranges of intra‐day and inter‐day precision were 1.71% to 13.59% and 1.07% to 13.31%, respectively, and the RE ranges of intra‐day and inter‐day accuracy were −9.35% to 14.05% and −13.31% to 13.04%, respectively. The precision and accuracy are within ± 15%, as shown in Table S4, which meets the relevant requirements for biological sample testing.

Stability

This study investigated the stability of nine analytes when stored at room temperature for 6 h, at −20°C for one month, and during three freeze‐thaw cycles. The results show that all RSD values are less than 15% and all RE values are between −14.30% and 14.54%, as shown in Table S5, which can maintain good stability under validation conditions.

Extraction Recovery and Matrix Effect

The extraction recycling rates of these nine compounds in rat plasma and tissues all exceeded 80%, with a matrix effect range of 90% to 110%. Both the extraction recycling rate and the RSD of the matrix effect are below 10%, as shown in Table S6, meeting the relevant requirements for testing biological samples.

PK Study

All PK doses refer to the freeze‐dried extract rather than crude herbal powder.

Administration Dose of Nine Compounds

At a dosage of 2.625 g/kg, the specific doses for the nine compounds are detailed in Table S7. Among the 56 plasma‐related constituents identified by UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS, nine compounds were selected for quantitative PK analysis. The selection was based on the following considerations. First, all nine compounds were unequivocally identified as blood‐exposed prototype constituents after oral administration. Second, these analytes represent the major phytochemical classes of WLP, including phenylpropanoids, iridoids, xanthones, flavonoids, tannins, and triterpenoids, thereby reflecting the chemical diversity of the formula. Third, these compounds have been reported to possess anti‐inflammatory or antirheumatic activities in previous pharmacological studies. Finally, authentic reference standards with sufficient purity were commercially available, enabling reliable quantitative determination using the validated UPLC‐QQQ‐MS method.

PK Parameters and Plasma Concentration‐Time Profiles

Following administration of WLP to Wistar rats, nine compounds were detected in their plasma. The PK parameters of these compounds are detailed in Table 2, with plasma concentration‐time curves depicted in Figure 5. Nine blood‐entering components could be detected in rat plasma within 15 min, and the peak time of the maximum blood drug concentration was all within 0.75–2 h, which was consistent with the drug‐time curve. This indicates that these components were rapidly absorbed in the blood after oral administration. The T max values of sweroside, mangiferin, and isovitexin were all around 2 h, while the T max values of the other six compounds were all less than 1 h, approximately 0.8 h. This suggests that peak concentrations of these nine compounds can be reached more quickly in rats. The in vivo exposure levels of nine compounds were evaluated by AUC 0→t and C max. The AUC 0→t results indicated that ellagic acid and mangiferin had the highest levels, reaching 5007.26 and 3354.21 µg/L*h, respectively, and the C max values reached 1491.27 and 749.00 µg/L, respectively. It indicates that the bioavailability of both is relatively good. The apparent volume of distribution (Vd) values of cordifolioside A, norswertianolin, and syringin were the largest, reaching 782.80, 746.95, and 600.09 L/kg, respectively, indicating that the distribution degree of the three compounds in the body tissues was wide. The t 1/2 of all compounds were mostly within 5 h. The t 1/2 of sweroside, ellagic acid, and Mangiferin were relatively large, reaching 5.26, 4.97, and 4.22 h, respectively, while the t 1/2 value of syringin was the smallest, at 2.63 h. It indicates that syringin is eliminated relatively quickly in plasma, while sweroside, ellagic acid, and mangiferin are eliminated relatively slowly. The CL also showed similar results. The CL values of ellagic acid and mangiferin were as low as 3.88 and 5.95 L/h/kg, respectively, reflecting the low efficiency of drug elimination.

Tissue Distribution Study

This study established an UPLC‐QQQ‐MS/MS method for analyzing the distribution of nine compounds across various tissues, including heart, liver, spleen, synovium membrane, and kidney. The distribution of the compounds in various rat tissues at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h post‐administration is seen in Figure 6. All nine compounds were detectable in every tissue within 0.5 h of administration, indicating their rapid and extensive distribution characteristics in vivo. The drug concentration in each tissue reached C max within 1 or 2 h after administration. The nine compounds presented unique tissue distribution characteristics in rats. The distribution of sweroside in various tissues is as follows: kidney > liver > spleen > synovium membrane > heart. At 8 h post‐administration, the concentration of this substance in various tissues decreased significantly, indicating its rapid metabolism in vivo. Syringin and cordifolioside A were not detected in the heart tissue, but had a relatively high content in the kidney and synovium tissue. It reached C max in the synovium and spleen tissues within 2 h and C max in the kidney within 1 h. After 8 h, the content in each tissue was low, indicating that their metabolism is relatively fast. The tissue distribution of amarogentin, mangiferin, norswertianolin, ellagic acid, cordifolioside A, and arjungenin is relatively consistent. Specifically, they are mainly distributed in the kidneys, synovium membranes, and liver tissues, with the highest concentration in the kidneys and the second highest in the synovium membranes. It is particularly noted that cordifolioside A has the highest concentration in synovium tissue, norswertianolin is not distributed in cardiac tissue and spleen tissue, and isovitexin is not distributed in spleen tissue. The above shows that the drug concentration in the kidney, liver, and lung tissues is significantly higher than that in the heart and spleen organs, among which the concentration level in the kidney is the highest, indicating that it may act as the main excretory organ to participate in the drug clearance process. From the perspective of the temporal distribution characteristics of drug content in tissues, five components, namely, sweroside, mangiferin, ellagic acid, isovitexin, and arjungenin, showed relatively high contents in the tissues (C max >100 ng/mL). However, the peak concentrations of four components, namely syringin, amarogentin, cordifolioside A, and norswertianolin, were relatively low (C max <100 ng/mL). Finally, the tissue residues of all components were significantly reduced at 8 h, and no obvious tissue accumulation phenomenon was observed.

Discussion

In Tibetan clinical practice, TTM demonstrates unique therapeutic approaches and a long history in treating RA. We previously indicated that Tibetan medicine, by virtue of its multicomponent and multi‐target features, exerts therapeutic effects on RA through synergistic actions by modulating immune responses, metabolic components, and gut microbiota in multiple ways [4, 8]. However, after the active ingredients of Tibetan medicine enter the human body, they may undergo biotransformation through processes such as absorption, distribution, and metabolism [14]. This study exploring the changes in the effective components of WLP during the ADME process in vivo helps to elucidate the material basis of its anti‐RA effects and the rationality of the TTM peiwu of WLP [13].

We ultimately detected a total of 56 compounds, including 21 prototype compounds (as shown in Figure 2) and 35 metabolites (Table S2). From the perspective of TTM peiwu, we found that clerodane furanoditerpenoids (tinosineside A, tinosineside B, tinocordiside) and phenylpropanoids (cordifolioside A, syringin, rosmarinic acid) derived from the “jūn” (principal) herb T. sinensis were successfully detected in plasma, consistent with previous reports that these components are the core bioactive substances absorbed into the bloodstream after oral administration of T. sinensis extract [15, 16]. Xanthones (mangiferin, norswertianolin) and iridoid glycosides (sweroside, amarogentin) from the “chén” (minister) herb S. chirayita were also identified as major prototype components, which aligns with the phytochemical profile and in vivo exposure characteristics of this herb [17, 18]. Meanwhile, phenolic acid compounds (5‐O‐galloyl‐1,4‐galactarolactone, Chebulanin, (‐) ‐shikimic acid 5‐O‐gallate), triterpene saponin compounds (Arjungenin,4‐Epi‐hederagenin, Arjunolic acid) is derived from the “zuǒ” herbs T. bellirica, T. chebula, and P. emblica [19, 20, 21]. Liu et al. showed that Chebulanin exerts anti‐RA effects by inhibiting NF‐κB and MAPK activation in CIA mice, but the in vivo process is lacking [22]. In terms of metabolic profiles, phase II conjugation reactions including glucuronidation and sulfation, together with deglycosylation, demethylation, and hydroxylation, were the main metabolic pathways for these components in vivo, which is consistent with the general metabolic pattern of glycosides and polyphenols in traditional medicine formulas [23]. Collectively, the multisource absorbed components and their diverse metabolites collectively constitute the in vivo chemical exposure profile of WLP, reflecting the holistic and synergistic characteristics of TTM compound prescriptions

The PK results revealed that all nine target compounds were rapidly absorbed into the systemic circulation after oral administration, with detectable plasma levels within 15 min and T max values ranging from 0.71 to 2.00 h. This rapid absorption feature aligns with the pharmaceutical characteristics of powder preparations, which have a large specific surface area and high dispersion, facilitating gastrointestinal dissolution, and absorption [11]. Among the nine compounds, ellagic acid and mangiferin exhibited the highest systemic exposure, with AUC0→t values of 5007.26 and 3354.21 µg/L·h, respectively, indicating their dominant position in in vivo chemical exposure. Notably, the oral bioavailability of mangiferin has been shown to be as low as 1.2% [24]. This low bioavailability may be due to the inherent properties of mangiferin, such as low hydrophobicity and low gastrointestinal absorption [25], its exposure level in this study was relatively high under the compound formula environment. This phenomenon may be attributed to coexisting components in WLP that improve its dissolution, promote intestinal absorption, or inhibit its metabolic elimination, reflecting the potential PK interactions within the TTM formula. In terms of elimination, sweroside, ellagic acid, and mangiferin showed relatively long half‐lives (4.22–5.26 h) and low clearance rates, suggesting they may sustain effective blood concentrations for a longer period and contribute to the sustained therapeutic effect of WLP. In contrast, syringin had the shortest half‐life (2.63 h) and highest clearance, which may be related to its rapid degradation by intestinal enzymes and extensive first‐pass metabolism [26, 27]. The elimination half‐life of mangiferin in this study (4.22 h) was notably longer than the value (1.09 h) reported by Suryawanshi et al. [16]. This discrepancy may arise from multiple factors, which are critically analyzed as follows. First, dose‐dependent nonlinear kinetics may play a key role. Mangiferin is primarily cleared via UGT‐mediated glucuronidation and biliary excretion; at the higher mangiferin dose delivered by WLP in this study, the metabolic enzymes and efflux transporters may become partially saturated, reducing systemic clearance and prolonging the elimination half‐life. Second, the multicomponent WLP formulation may modulate mangiferin disposition. Coexisting ingredients in WLP may inhibit hepatic and intestinal UGT activity and biliary transporter function, slowing the metabolic and excretory clearance of mangiferin and extending its in vivo residence time, whereas pure mangiferin was administered in the previous study without such modulatory effects. Third, differences in experimental design contribute to the variation. The previous study employed a shorter sampling period, which may not have fully captured the terminal elimination phase and thus underestimated the half‐life. In contrast, our 24‐h sampling schedule covered the complete elimination phase, providing a more robust estimation of terminal t1/2 . Differences in sample processing and analytical methodologies between studies may also introduce additional variability. Additionally, cordifolioside A, norswertianolin, and syringin showed large apparent volumes of distribution, indicating their extensive distribution in peripheral tissues rather than being restricted to the systemic circulation

The tissue distribution study further provided critical information linking in vivo exposure to target organ efficacy. All nine compounds could distribute to the heart, liver, spleen, synovium, and kidney within 0.5 h, showing a rapid and widespread distribution pattern. Consistent with common drug excretion rules, the highest concentrations of most compounds were found in the kidney and liver, indicating that these two organs are the main sites for drug metabolism and excretion [28]. Most notably, all nine components were detectable in the synovial membrane, the core lesion tissue of RA, and several components maintained relatively high concentrations there. Specifically, cordifolioside A showed the highest concentration in synovial tissue among all tested tissues, while mangiferin, amarogentin, ellagic acid, and arjungenin also exhibited considerable synovial exposure. Given that RA is pathologically characterized by synovial inflammation, hyperplasia, and subsequent bone erosion [29], the effective distribution of these components in synovial tissue provides direct tissue‐level evidence supporting their potential anti‐RA effects. Combined with existing pharmacological evidence that mangiferin inhibits synovial pyroptosis and inflammatory activation [30, 31], ellagic acid suppresses synovial fibroblast proliferation and oxidative stress [32], and cordifolioside A exerts anti‐inflammatory and immunomodulatory activities [33], we propose that cordifolioside A, mangiferin, amarogentin, ellagic acid, and arjungenin are promising candidate core pharmacodynamic substances of WLP against RA, warranting further functional validation, considering their favorable in vivo exposure, effective synovial distribution, and proven anti‐arthritic activities. Furthermore, all components showed significantly reduced tissue residues at 8 h without obvious accumulation, suggesting good in vivo safety and low risk of tissue toxicity after repeated administration. Therefore, the selected analytes were not intended to represent all absorbed constituents of WLP, but rather representative compounds with favorable analytical feasibility and pharmacological relevance for quantitative PK evaluation.

Certain limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, all PK and tissue distribution experiments were conducted in healthy rats, while the pathological state of RA may alter gastrointestinal function, hepatic and renal metabolic capacity, and tissue vascular permeability, which could further affect the ADME behaviors of drug components. Subsequent studies in CIA rat models are warranted to reveal the in vivo process of WLP under pathological conditions. Second, this study focused on prototype components for quantitative analysis, while the pharmacological contribution of abundant phase I and phase II metabolites remains to be evaluated. Third, the synergistic interactions among multiple active components at the PK and pharmacodynamic levels still require in‐depth investigation. Fourth, the tissue distribution analysis focused on the major systemic organs (heart, liver, spleen, synovium, and kidney) but did not include intestinal tissues, feces, or urine. Given that WLP is administered orally and several of its components (e.g., syringin, sweroside) are known to undergo intestinal metabolism and bacterial biotransformation, intestinal distribution and excretion profiling would provide valuable information on presystemic clearance and the role of the gut in component disposition. This aspect will be addressed in our future studies. In future work, we will combine pathological PK studies with nanotechnology‐based delivery system optimization to further improve the targeting, metabolic stability, and therapeutic efficacy of WLP's active components [34].

Conclusion

This study systematically characterized the in vivo absorption, metabolism, PK profiles, and tissue distribution of WLP after oral administration in rats using a combined UPLC‐Q‐TOF‐MS and UPLC‐QQQ‐MS approach. A total of 56 plasma constituents, including 21 prototype compounds and 35 metabolites, were identified, revealing the holistic in vivo chemical exposure profile of WLP. The nine major active components were rapidly absorbed and widely distributed across multiple tissues, with no obvious tissue accumulation observed. Most importantly, all components effectively reached the synovial membrane, the key target tissue of RA. Among them, cordifolioside A, mangiferin, amarogentin, ellagic acid, and arjungenin were identified as major in vivo exposure components and potential pharmacodynamic constituents of WLP, with favorable systemic exposure and synovial tissue distribution. This study provides the first systematic evidence supporting the potential pharmacodynamic material basis of WLP against RA.
