Combined MEK1/2 and Autophagy Inhibition Suppresses Tumor Growth via STING‐Mediated Type I Interferon Response in iCCA
Authors: Chengqiang Sun, Zheng Gao, Enfu Dong, Liangxia Ding, Shanru Feng, Jiafeng Chen, Pascal Kwangwari, Yinghong Shi, Weiren Liu, Xin Zhang, Ao Huang, Jian Zhou, Sheng Wang, Jia Fan, Xiutao Fu, ZhenBin Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe RAF–MEK–ERK pathway contributes to many human cancers, including intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA). Although MEK is an important therapeutic target, MEK inhibitors (MEKis) have limited efficacy as monotherapy in iCCA, and the underlying adaptive mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we show that MEK inhibition induces protective autophagy in iCCA cells. Mechanistically, MEK inhibition suppressed ERK–RSK signaling, activated the LKB1–ULK1 pathway, and promoted autophagy. MEK inhibition also increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and activated PINK1/Parkin‐mediated mitophagy. This autophagic response limited activation of the cGAS–STING–TBK1 pathway. Pharmacological or genetic inhibition of autophagy during MEK inhibition enhanced STING‐mediated type I interferon signaling, increased IFN‐α and IFN‐β expression, and sensitized iCCA cells to MEKi treatment. Consistently, combined MEK and autophagy inhibition suppressed tumor growth in xenograft‐bearing nude mice. These findings identify a link between MAPK signaling, autophagy, and innate immune sensing and support targeting the MEK‐autophagy‐STING axis to improve MEKi efficacy in iCCA.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is a heterogeneous malignancy that accounts for approximately 15% of primary liver cancers [1]. Its global incidence and mortality have increased in recent years. Anatomically, CCA is classified into intrahepatic CCA (iCCA), perihilar CCA, and distal CCA [2]. Surgical resection remains the only potentially curative treatment; however, most patients with iCCA are diagnosed at an advanced stage, and only 20%–30% are eligible for resection [3]. Therefore, defining the molecular mechanisms underlying iCCA progression and developing effective targeted therapies remain important clinical priorities.

The RAS/mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway is aberrantly activated in many human cancers, and several of its components function as oncogenic drivers [4]. KRAS mutations are among the most common molecular alterations in iCCA, occurring in approximately 8.6%–24.2% of patients [5]. These mutations cause constitutive activation of the RAF–MEK–ERK cascade, which regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival [6]. Therapeutic inhibition of this pathway has been investigated in multiple cancers, including non‐small cell lung cancer and pancreatic cancer, using MEK1/2 or ERK1/2 inhibitors alone or in combination with other treatments to overcome resistance [7, 8, 9]. However, MEK1/2 inhibitors have not shown broad efficacy in iCCA [10].

Autophagy is a conserved cellular degradation process with context‐dependent roles in tumorigenesis and therapeutic resistance [11, 12, 13]. Although MEK inhibition has been reported to induce protective autophagy in other malignancies, the molecular mediators of this response and its downstream tumor‐intrinsic consequences remain unclear in iCCA [14, 15].

In this study, we identify a signaling mechanism by which MEK inhibition induces protective autophagy through the RSK1/2–LKB1–ULK1 cascade in iCCA. We also define a link between mitophagy and innate immune sensing. Specifically, MEK inhibition promotes ROS accumulation and PINK1/Parkin‐dependent mitophagy, which suppresses activation of the cGAS–STING pathway. Concurrent inhibition of autophagy enhances STING‐mediated type I interferon (IFN) responses, indicating a functional interaction between autophagy and innate immune signaling in iCCA. These findings provide a rationale for combining autophagy inhibition with MEK1/2 inhibition to improve therapeutic efficacy in iCCA.

Statistical Analysis

Differences between two groups were evaluated using paired or unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test, while multiple groups were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance. Representative results from three independent experiments were used in the study analysis. The Kaplan–Meier method with the log‐rank test was used for survival analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analysis of data was performed by GraphPad Prism 10.

Results

RAF–MEK–ERK Signaling Pathway Is Dysregulated in iCCA and Correlated With Poor Prognosis

To examine the association between the RAS–MEK–ERK pathway and iCCA prognosis, we analyzed clinical data from 244 patients with iCCA in the GEO dataset GSE33327. We assessed the relationship between overall survival and the expression of KRAS, MAP2K1, and MAPK3, which encode KRAS, MEK1, and ERK1, respectively. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that higher expression of these genes was associated with shorter overall survival in patients with iCCA (Figure 1A). In the TCGA‐CHOL cohort, KRAS, MAP2K1, and MAPK3 expression levels were significantly increased in iCCA samples (Figure 1B).

Consistently, IHC and western blot analyses showed increased p‐ERK levels in iCCA tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (Figure 1C–E). Together, these findings indicate that RAS–MEK–ERK pathway activation is associated with iCCA progression and poor patient survival, providing a rationale for evaluating MEK inhibition in iCCA.

MEK1/2 Inhibition Restricts iCCA Cell Growth and Induces Autophagy Activation

To determine whether inhibition of the RAS–MEK–ERK pathway suppresses iCCA cell proliferation, we used two human intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) cell lines, HuCCT1 and RBE. Cells were treated with the MEK1/2 inhibitors cobimetinib or trametinib, and cell viability was assessed using the CCK‐8 assay. After 24 h of treatment, the IC50 values of cobimetinib were 25.31 μM in HuCCT1 cells and 18.06 μM in RBE cells, whereas those of trametinib were 71.38 μM and 74.69 μM, respectively (Figure 2A). After 48 h, the IC50 values of cobimetinib were 19.17 μM and 16.24 μM, respectively, whereas those of trametinib were 79.53 μM and 86.10 μM, respectively (Figure 2B). These relatively high IC50 values suggest that MEK inhibition alone has limited anti‐proliferative activity in iCCA cells. Based on these results, cobimetinib and trametinib were used at 20 μM and 40 μM, respectively, in subsequent experiments. Furthermore, at these fixed drug concentrations, the proliferation of HuCCT1 and RBE cells was moderately inhibited over time (Figure 2C). At these fixed concentrations, cobimetinib and trametinib moderately inhibited the proliferation of HuCCT1 and RBE cells over time (Figure 2C).

Because MEK1/2 inhibition has been reported to induce autophagy in other cancers [16], we next assessed autophagy activation in iCCA cells. Immunofluorescence analysis showed an increase in LC3B‐positive puncta in HuCCT1 and RBE cells after treatment with cobimetinib or trametinib (Figure 2D). We next examined p‐ERK and autophagy‐related markers by western blotting 6 h after MEK1/2 inhibitor treatment. Both cobimetinib and trametinib markedly reduced p‐ERK levels, confirming inhibition of MEK–ERK signaling (Figure 2E,F). Although high‐dose trametinib nearly abolished ERK phosphorylation at this early time point, cell proliferation was only partially inhibited at 24 and 48 h (Figure 2A,B), indicating that acute MAPK pathway inhibition is insufficient to fully suppress iCCA cell growth. In parallel, MEK1/2 inhibition increased the LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 2E,F). These findings indicate that MEK–ERK inhibition induces autophagy in iCCA cells, which may act as an adaptive response to ERK pathway suppression.

Inhibition of Protective Autophagy Sensitizes iCCA Cells to the Antitumor Effects of MEK1/2 Inhibitors

Based on these findings, we examined whether autophagy inhibition enhances the anti‐proliferative effects of MEK1/2 inhibitors in iCCA cells. Cells were treated with cobimetinib or trametinib, alone or in combination with the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ). CCK‐8 assays showed that CQ further enhanced the inhibitory effects of cobimetinib and trametinib on cell proliferation after 24 h of treatment (Figure 3A). Similarly, crystal violet staining showed that CQ alone had limited effects, whereas combined MEK1/2 and autophagy inhibition markedly increased the sensitivity of tumor cells to MEK1/2 inhibition (Figure 3B,C).

(B, C) HuCCT1 and RBE cells were treated with increasing concentrations of MEKi, CQ, or their combination for 24 h, followed by crystal violet staining. We next used siRNAs targeting ATG5 and ATG7 to knock down these genes in HuCCT1 and RBE cells; the siRNA sequences are listed in Table S1. Western blotting confirmed efficient depletion of the corresponding autophagy‐related proteins (Figure 3D). Crystal violet staining showed that siRNA‐mediated knockdown of ATG5 or ATG7 further suppressed iCCA cell proliferation when combined with MEK1/2 inhibitors, compared with MEK1/2 inhibitor treatment alone (Figure 3E). Together, these findings suggest that MEK1/2 inhibition induces protective autophagy in iCCA cells and that inhibition of this response enhances the anti‐proliferative effects of MEK1/2 inhibition.

The RSK1/2–LKB1–ULK1 Axis Mediates Protective Autophagy and Supports iCCA Cell Survival

To define the mechanism by which MEK1/2 inhibition induces autophagy in iCCA cells, HuCCT1 cells were treated with cobimetinib, and total protein lysates were analyzed using a human phospho‐kinase array. Cobimetinib treatment markedly reduced ERK1/2 phosphorylation at T202/Y204 and T185/Y187, confirming effective inhibition of MEK–ERK signaling. The array also showed a significant reduction in RSK1/2 phosphorylation at S221/S227 after cobimetinib treatment (Figure 4A). Because RSK1/2 act downstream of ERK1/2 [17], we further assessed RSK1/2 phosphorylation by western blotting. Increasing concentrations of cobimetinib reduced ERK1/2 phosphorylation and decreased RSK1 phosphorylation at T359 and S380 and RSK2 phosphorylation at S227 (Figure 4B). Previous studies have shown that ERK1/2 can regulate LKB1 through phosphorylation at S428, and that LKB1 modulates autophagy via the AMPK/ULK1 axis [18, 19]. To determine whether RSK1/2 regulates autophagy through LKB1, we depleted RSK1/2 using siRNA. Western blotting confirmed RSK1/2 knockdown and showed increased LKB1 phosphorylation, elevated ULK1 phosphorylation, and a higher LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio (Figure 4C). These results suggest that MEK inhibition induces autophagy through the RSK1/2–LKB1–ULK1 axis. To assess the functional relevance of this axis, we performed colony formation assays. Treatment with cobimetinib (20 μM) or the ULK1 inhibitor SBI‐0206965 (5 μM) alone had limited effects on cell growth. In contrast, the combined treatment markedly reduced the colony‐forming capacity of HuCCT1 and RBE cells (Figure 4D). These findings indicate that the RSK1/2–LKB1–ULK1 axis contributes to resistance to MEK inhibition in iCCA cells.

Proteomic analysis of cobimetinib‐treated HuCCT1 cells followed by KEGG enrichment analysis showed significant enrichment of the mitophagy pathway after MEK inhibition (Figure 4E). Mitophagy is a key mitochondrial quality‐control process that can be triggered by mitochondrial stress, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation [20]. ROS detection assays showed increased intracellular green fluorescence intensity after cobimetinib or trametinib treatment, indicating that MEK inhibition promotes ROS accumulation in iCCA cells (Figure 4F). Mitophagy can be mediated by ubiquitin‐dependent or ubiquitin‐independent pathways. GO enrichment analysis showed that the differentially regulated proteins were significantly enriched for the molecular function “ubiquitin binding” (GO:0043130) (Figure 4G), suggesting involvement of ubiquitin‐related processes after MEK inhibition. Western blot analysis further showed increased cellular ubiquitination after cobimetinib or trametinib treatment, supporting the involvement of ubiquitin‐dependent mitophagy in response to MEK inhibition (Figure 4H). We next examined the canonical PINK1/Parkin mitophagy pathway. Western blot analysis showed that cobimetinib or trametinib treatment increased PINK1 and Parkin protein levels (Figure 4I), supporting activation of PINK1/Parkin‐mediated mitophagy after MEK inhibition.

Co‐Inhibition of MEK1/2 and Autophagy Enhances STING/Type I Interferon Signaling

Because protective autophagy promotes iCCA cell survival during MEK inhibition, we investigated how inhibition of this response suppresses cell growth. Impaired autophagy has been reported to promote cytosolic DNA or damaged organelle accumulation, thereby activating tumor‐intrinsic innate immune signaling [21]. To examine this mechanism, we performed proteomic analysis of iCCA cells treated with cobimetinib, chloroquine, or their combination. GSEA of the proteomic data showed enrichment of the type I interferon pathway after cobimetinib, chloroquine, and combined treatment (Figure 5A). Because cGAS–STING activation can induce cytokines, chemokines, and type I interferons [22], we next examined whether STING‐related signaling was altered after MEK inhibition. RNA‐seq analysis of cobimetinib‐treated HuCCT1 cells showed enrichment of autophagy‐related pathways, accompanied by increased STING1 transcript levels (Figure 5B). These bioinformatic results suggest that MEK inhibition may promote STING pathway activation. Western blotting showed that cobimetinib or chloroquine alone increased intracellular cGAS levels and TBK1 phosphorylation, whereas combined MEK and autophagy inhibition further enhanced TBK1 phosphorylation (Figure 5C). Similarly, ATG5 or ATG7 knockdown combined with cobimetinib increased STING pathway activation and downstream signaling (Figure 5D). We next used a genetic knockdown approach to assess whether the anti‐proliferative effect of the combination treatment depends on STING activation. Colony formation assays showed that siRNA‐mediated STING knockdown substantially attenuated the growth inhibition induced by combined MEK and autophagy blockade (Figure 5E). These results indicate that STING pathway activation is required for the growth‐inhibitory effect of combined MEK and autophagy inhibition in iCCA cells.

To determine whether combined MEK and autophagy inhibition activates the STING pathway and induces a type I interferon response, RNA was extracted from HuCCT1 cells treated with cobimetinib, chloroquine, or their combination. qRT‐PCR was performed to measure the mRNA levels of IFN‐α, IFN‐β, and IFN‐γ using the primers listed in Table S2. Combined cobimetinib and chloroquine treatment significantly increased IFN‐α and IFN‐β mRNA expression, whereas IFN‐γ expression was unchanged (Figure 5F). Based on these findings, we examined whether STING activity is required for interferon induction by combined MEK and autophagy inhibition. HuCCT1 cells were treated with the STING agonist diABZI or the STING inhibitor H‐151 in combination with cobimetinib and chloroquine. diABZI further increased IFN‐α expression in cells receiving combined cobimetinib and chloroquine treatment, whereas H‐151 reduced the combination‐induced expression of IFN‐α and IFN‐β (Figure 5G). Similarly, siRNA‐mediated STING knockdown decreased IFN‐α and IFN‐β expression following cobimetinib and chloroquine treatment (Figure 5H). These results support the requirement of STING signaling for type I interferon induction by combined MEK and autophagy inhibition. To determine whether the observed growth inhibition was mediated by type I IFN signaling, iCCA cells were treated with B18R, a vaccinia virus‐encoded type I IFN‐neutralizing protein [23, 24]. B18R treatment significantly attenuated the anti‐proliferative effects induced by combined cobimetinib treatment and autophagy inhibition (Figure 5I). These results suggest that STING‐mediated type I IFN signaling contributes to the growth‐inhibitory effect of combined MEK and autophagy inhibition in iCCA cells.

Autophagy Inhibition Enhances the Anticancer Activity of MEK1/2 Inhibition In Vivo

To assess whether autophagy inhibition enhances the efficacy of MEK1/2 inhibition in vivo, we established a HuCCT1 cell‐derived subcutaneous xenograft model in nude mice. Treatment was initiated 2 weeks after tumor cell inoculation. No overt toxicity was observed, and body weight did not differ significantly among the treatment groups (Figure 6A). Tumor growth was reduced in both the cobimetinib‐ and CQ‐treated groups compared with the vehicle group, with cobimetinib showing a stronger inhibitory effect than CQ. Combined cobimetinib and chloroquine treatment significantly reduced tumor volume and tumor weight compared with either monotherapy (Figure 6B–D).

(C, D) Tumor volume (C) and resected tumor weight (D) in the subcutaneous tumor model (n = 4 per group). Histopathological examination of the xenograft tumors showed a predominantly solid growth pattern, with high cellularity, marked nuclear pleomorphism, and prominent nucleoli. Typical glandular or tubular structures were not evident. In the combined cobimetinib and chloroquine treatment group, tumors showed extensive necrotic areas with abundant karyorrhectic nuclear debris, consistent with coagulative necrosis and nuclear fragmentation (Figure 6E). Together, these findings indicate that autophagy inhibition enhances the antitumor effect of MEK1/2 inhibition in vivo.

Discussion

MEK inhibitors, including cobimetinib and trametinib, have been approved for several cancers, such as melanoma and colorectal cancer [25]. However, clinical trials in advanced cholangiocarcinoma have shown limited efficacy [26, 27, 28]. The mechanisms underlying MEK inhibitor resistance in iCCA remain incompletely defined, limiting the effectiveness of targeted therapy. Therefore, identifying strategies that improve the response of cholangiocarcinoma cells to MEK inhibition remains clinically relevant.

Consistent with the clinical relevance of MAPK pathway dysregulation, our analysis showed that high KRAS, MAP2K1, and MAPK3 expression was associated with poor prognosis in iCCA, and that p‐ERK levels were elevated in tumor tissues compared with adjacent tissues. These findings support the involvement of MAPK pathway activation in iCCA. However, the relatively high IC50 values of cobimetinib and trametinib in HuCCT1 and RBE cells, together with incomplete growth inhibition despite near‐complete suppression of ERK phosphorylation, suggest that MEK inhibition alone may be insufficient to effectively suppress iCCA cell proliferation. This is consistent with previous studies showing that adaptive feedback and compensatory survival pathways can limit the efficacy of MEK inhibitors, supporting the use of combination strategies [29]. In our study, MEK inhibition increased LC3‐II accumulation in iCCA cells, consistent with autophagy activation. To directly assess the role of autophagy, we inhibited autophagy pharmacologically with chloroquine and genetically by RNA interference targeting the essential autophagy genes ATG5 and ATG7. Consistent with findings in pancreatic cancer showing that autophagy inhibition enhances the efficacy of MEK inhibitors [15], autophagy suppression sensitized iCCA cells to the anti‐proliferative effects of MEK inhibition. Our study identifies the RSK1/2–LKB1–ULK1 axis as a mediator of this adaptive response. MEK inhibition suppresses ERK–RSK signaling, which may relieve inhibitory regulation of the LKB1–ULK1 pathway and promote protective autophagy in iCCA cells. Disruption of this autophagic response sensitizes iCCA cells to MEK inhibition. We also found that MEK inhibition increased intracellular ROS levels. Tumor cells often exhibit elevated ROS due to metabolic reprogramming and changes in nutrient or oxygen availability within the tumor microenvironment. MAPK signaling has been reported to regulate antioxidant defense genes [30]; therefore, MEK inhibition may increase ROS accumulation by suppressing these antioxidant programs. ROS can also trigger PINK1/Parkin‐dependent mitophagy [31], which is consistent with our observation that MEK inhibition activated the PINK1/Parkin mitophagy pathway.

Previous studies have suggested that MEK inhibition promotes cytosolic DNA accumulation and activates cGAS–STING signaling [32]. In our study, autophagy inhibition further enhanced STING signaling during MEK inhibition in iCCA cells, as shown by increased IFN‐α and IFN‐β expression. A possible explanation is that MEK inhibition‐induced autophagy promotes the degradation of cytosolic DNA and STING [33], thereby limiting STING activation. However, chloroquine treatment or ATG5/ATG7 knockdown alone did not induce a strong STING‐dependent type I interferon response, which may reflect the relatively low basal autophagic activity of iCCA cells [34]. In the absence of therapeutic stress, the cGAS–STING pathway in iCCA cells may remain largely inactive because of limited endogenous triggers, such as cytosolic dsDNA. MEK inhibition may alter this state by inducing stress signals associated with STING activation, including ROS accumulation and mitochondrial perturbation [35]. At the same time, MEK inhibition activates an RSK/LKB1/ULK1‐dependent autophagic response, which may attenuate these signals and limit STING pathway activation.

STING‐mediated type I interferon signaling can exert tumor‐cell‐intrinsic anti‐proliferative effects in addition to its role in antitumor immunity [36, 37]. In our study, combined MEK and autophagy inhibition enhanced cGAS–STING–TBK1 pathway activation and increased IFN‐α and IFN‐β transcription in iCCA cells. Pharmacological inhibition of STING with H‐151 or STING knockdown reduced IFN‐α and IFN‐β induction, supporting the involvement of STING‐dependent innate immune signaling in this response. In addition, blockade of type I interferon signaling with B18R attenuated the anti‐proliferative effect induced by combined MEK and autophagy inhibition. These findings indicate that the STING–type I interferon axis functionally contributes to the growth‐inhibitory effects of combined MEK and autophagy inhibition in iCCA cells. In this context, autophagy may limit the persistence of cytosolic danger signals induced by MEK inhibition, whereas autophagy blockade allows these signals to accumulate and sustain STING–TBK1 activation and type I interferon‐dependent growth inhibition.

This study has several limitations. First, most mechanistic experiments were performed in two iCCA cell lines. Validation in additional genetically diverse iCCA models, organoids, or patient‐derived xenografts would improve the generalizability of our findings. Second, although the subcutaneous xenograft model confirmed the in vivo efficacy of combined MEK and autophagy inhibition, the use of immunodeficient mice limited the evaluation of STING‐dependent type I interferon signaling in antitumor immunity. Because type I interferon signaling is closely linked to antitumor immune responses [38], enhanced STING–IFN signaling induced by combined MEK and autophagy inhibition may have implications for immunotherapy in iCCA. This possibility requires further validation in immunocompetent or humanized models, particularly in combination with immune checkpoint blockade.

In summary, our findings show that MEK inhibition increases STING‐dependent type I interferon signaling in iCCA cells. Concurrent autophagy inhibition further enhances activation of the STING–type I interferon pathway and strengthens the growth‐inhibitory effect on iCCA cells. These results support the potential use of autophagy inhibitors in combination with MEK inhibitors as a therapeutic strategy for iCCA.
