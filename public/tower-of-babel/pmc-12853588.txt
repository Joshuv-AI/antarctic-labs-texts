Complete chloroplast genome of Ecklonia maxima and comparative analysis with related species
Authors: Ying Ji, Yingying He, Kunpeng Wang, Hancheng Zhao, Changfeng Qu, Jinlai Miao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEcklonia maxima, an ecologically and economically critical brown alga, holds substantial value in social, economic, and ecological contexts. While it is garnering growing interest for its applications in aquaculture and blue carbon sequestration, its genomic characteristics remain poorly characterized, particularly those of its chloroplast (cp.) genome, including its structure, genetic variation, and adaptive mechanisms. To address this knowledge gap, we sequenced, assembled, and annotated the complete cp. genome of E. maxima to investigate its phylogenetic relationships and conduct a comparative analysis with related species within the Ecklonia genus.ResultsWe sequenced and assembled the complete cp. genome of E. maxima. The assembled genome is 130,900 bp in length and exhibits the typical quadripartite structure, consisting of a large single-copy (LSC) region of 76,991 bp, a small single-copy (SSC) region of 42,941 bp, and a pair of inverted repeat (IR) regions (each 5,484 bp). Genome annotation identified 169 genes, comprising 137 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 29 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and 3 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. Non-coding sequences accounted for 25.27% of the genome. Simple sequence repeat (SSR) analysis revealed 47 SSRs, which were predominantly mononucleotide and tetranucleotide repeats with a distinct AT bias. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that E. maxima forms a well-supported sister group (96% bootstrap) to a clade containing its congeners E. radiata, E. cava, E. cava subsp. stolonifera and E. radicosa, indicating their close evolutionary relationship. Comparative genomic analysis among the six Ecklonia species (including E. maxima) showed high sequence conservation, with the IR regions being more conserved than the LSC and SSC regions. The Ka/Ks (nonsynonymous/synonymous substitution rate) analysis indicated that petF, rpl24, and syfB were under positive selection, while the majority of genes were under purifying selection. The signatures of positive selection and the high nucleotide diversity (e.g., petF, PI = 0.038) observed in these genes provide direct evidence supporting adaptive evolution to environmental fluctuations.ConclusionsThe sequencing and analysis of the complete cp. genome of E. maxima in this study provide a valuable genomic resource for future studies on species identification and for elucidating the evolutionary and phylogenetic relationships among species within the genus Ecklonia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12448-2.

Background

Brown algae play a crucial role in coastal marine ecosystems. In particular, large brown algae belonging to the orders Laminariales, Dictyotales, Fucales, and Desmarestiales are dominant members of intertidal and shallow subtidal ecosystems. They form complex underwater forests, significantly increasing the structural complexity of marine ecosystems and regulating environmental factors such as light, fluid dynamics, sedimentation, and food supply [1]. Additionally, large brown algae play a vital role in marine ecosystems by providing food and habitat for numerous marine organisms. Through photosynthesis and growth, brown algae absorb nitrogen and phosphorus, contributing to the maintenance of nutrient balance in water bodies [2]. They are also a key source of coastal productivity, enhancing secondary productivity in nearshore ecosystems [3].

Chloroplasts (cp.) are the key site of photosynthesis in plants and algae, encoding mainly genes for photosynthesis and metabolism, and are extensively involved in several metabolic pathways [4]. The cp. is not only the site of sugar and pigment synthesis, but also serves as a site for the synthesis of amino acids and fatty acids, making it an important bioreactor [5]. In recent years, with the rapid development of high-throughput sequencing technology, cp. genomes have garnered significant attention in phylogeny as important tools for molecular systematics and evolutionary research. An increasing number of brown algal cp. genomes have been sequenced, revealing their structural features, gene composition, and evolutionary characteristics. Most cp. genomes are closed-loop, but linear, D-loop and lasso configurations also exist [6]. Overall, cp. genomes are divided into four regions, consisting of a LSC region, a SSC region and a pair of IR regions [7]. The cp. genome can autonomously encode genetic information and transmit genes. Compared to the nuclear genome, the cp. genome exhibits a simpler and more stable structure, with highly conserved sequences and a slower evolutionary rate. These characteristics make it an ideal material for studying the systematic evolution and phylogenetic relationships among species as well as within intraspecific taxa [8]. Consequently, they are widely used in phylogenetic analyses and species identification across different taxonomic levels in plants and algae. Additionally, the rearrangement rate of cp. genomes is higher than that of mitochondrial genomes, while the replacement rate of their coding genes is relatively conserved; this combination makes cp. genomes ideal for investigating macroevolutionary events such as gene loss and endosymbiotic gene transfer [9]. Brown algal cp. genomes are typically circular and range from 124,000 to 140,000 bp. Owing to their high copy numbers and sequence conservation, cp. genomes have been widely applied in fields such as population genetics, evolutionary biology, phylogenetics, and species identification [10–12].

E. maxima is a species belonging to the genus Ecklonia, family Lessoniaceae, order Laminariales, and class Phaeophyceae [13]. The species is primarily distributed along the west coast of South Africa [14] and has garnered attention for its unique morphology, structure, important ecological functions as well as its economic value [15]. It has a complex and highly specialized algal structure. It possesses a long, erect stipe that can reach 10–15 m in length. One or more pneumatocysts are located at the junction between the stipe and the blade, maximizing buoyancy for light exposure [16]. Its cell walls are rich in alginate, which is widely used as a thickening and stabilizing agent in the food industry, as well as a wound dressing and a slow-release material for drugs in the pharmaceutical field [17]. The species is also processed into organic seaweed fertilizers, rich in potassium, nitrogen and plant hormones, which enhance crop resistance [13]. Meanwhile, its leaves are a major source of feed for the Haliotis midae farming industry [18].

However, a significant geographical bias exists in current genomic resources [19]. While numerous Northern Hemisphere brown algae (e.g., Saccharina japonica, Ectocarpus siliculosus) have been sequenced [20, 21], genomic studies on Southern Hemisphere endemics, particularly within the genus Ecklonia, lag. Although the cp. genome of its relative E. radiata has been reported [22], the absence of a sequenced cp. genome for E. maxima has hindered comparative genomics and phylogenetics within the genus Ecklonia. To address this knowledge gap, we sequenced, assembled, and annotated the complete cp. genome of E. maxima for the first time. We then conducted a comprehensive analysis, including genome structure characterization, codon usage, repeat elements, and a robust phylogenetic reconstruction incorporating all available congeners, thereby providing a resolved framework for the genus. Furthermore, we performed comparative genomics with five additional Ecklonia species to assess sequence divergence, structural variation, and selective pressures. This work not only provides a valuable genomic resource but also delivers insights into the evolutionary dynamics and adaptive potential of this ecologically significant kelp.

Materials and methods

Materials and sources

In this study, E. maxima was sampled from Saldanha Bay, South Africa (approximate coordinates: 33°02′05″ S, 18°00′35″ E). We deposited voucher specimens at the First Institute of Oceanography, Ministry of Natural Resources (Website: https://www.fio.org.cn/, Contact: Jinlai Miao, Email: miaojinlai@fio.org.cn) under the voucher number FIO2023031608. Cp genome extraction and sequencing

The brown alga E. maxima was collected, and cpDNA was isolated by gradient centrifugation. To minimize contamination from nuclear and mitochondrial DNA, the enriched cpDNA fraction was treated with DNase I (Promega, Madison, USA). The enriched cpDNA was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. For library construction, cpDNA was fragmented to 300–500 bp using a Covaris M220, purified with the TIANgel Midi purification kit, and processed with the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Sequencing generated 8.03 Gb of raw paired-end (2 × 150 bp) reads. The raw data were subsequently processed and used for cp. genome assembly.

Cp genome assembly and annotation

Raw data were quality-trimmed using the software Trimmomatic v0.39 (http://www.usadellab.org/cms/?page=trimmomatic) as follows: adapter sequences were removed from reads; bases containing non-AGCT characters at the 5’ end were clipped; read ends were trimmed using a quality threshold of Q20; reads with an N-containing ratio exceeding 10% were discarded; and finally, fragments shorter than 75 bp after adapter removal and quality trimming were discarded. To ensure more accurate subsequent assembly, the cp. genome was assembled using GetOrganelle v1.7.5 (https://github.com/Kinggerm/GetOrganelle). Target-related reads were iteratively recruited using a seed database, and the genome was assembled with SPAdes. The cp contigs were ordered and oriented by aligning them to the NCBI NT database using BLASTn, and the arrangement with the highest consistency and coverage was selected as the final genome. The start position, orientation, and partitioning structure (LSC/SSC/IR) were determined based on a reference genome, yielding the E. maxima cp genome sequence.

Annotation was performed using GeSeq (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/geseq.html) with the following parameters: protein search identity set to 60%, rRNA/tRNA/gene search identity set to 35%, and the third-party tRNA annotator set to tRNAscan-SE v2.0.7 (http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/). Predicted genes were deduplicated, and their start/end positions, as well as exon/intron boundaries, were manually corrected for accuracy. Circular genome maps were generated using OGDRAW (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html) [12], combining assembled sequences with coding gene predictions to visualize the genome structure. Additionally, AT-skew and GC-skew were calculated to quantify base distribution asymmetry, using the standard formulas: AT-skew = (A - T)/(A + T) and GC-skew = (G - C)/(G + C) [23].

Analysis of codon usage

Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) refers to the relative probability of a codon being used to encode its corresponding amino acid among synonymous codons, reflecting the degree of codon usage bias [24]. An RSCU value of 1 indicates that the codon is used at the average frequency; values greater than 1 suggest higher usage, while values less than 1 indicate lower usage. The RSCU was calculated for all PCGs using the cusp program within the EMBOSS package (v6.6.0.0) to analyze codon usage bias [25].

Analysis of repeated sequences

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) are typically repetitive sequences with a length of 1–6 bp, where 2–3 nucleotides serve as the main repeat units. Microsatellite loci in the contig sequences generated by sample assembly were analyzed using MISA (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/) [26]. The parameters were set as follows: mononucleotide (≥ 10 repeats), dinucleotide (≥ 5 repeats), trinucleotide (≥ 4 repeats), tetranucleotide (≥ 3 repeats), pentanucleotide (≥ 3 repeats), and hexanucleotide (≥ 3 repeats); with a minimum distance of 100 bp between SSRs; SSRs located closer than this were classified as a single compound microsatellite. Additionally, primers were designed for the identified SSR loci using Primer3 to facilitate future experimental validation (http://www.simgene.com/Primer3) [27].

Dispersed repetitive sequence analysis was conducted using REPuter (http://bibiserv.techfak.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/). The parameters were set as follows: Minimal Repeat Size was 30 bp, Hamming distance was 3, and Maximum Computed Repeats was 5000. Four types of repeats were identified: F (Forward), R (Reverse), C (Complement), and P (Palindromic).

Phylogenetic analysis

To elucidate the phylogenetic position of Ecklonia maxima, we performed a phylogenomic analysis. The cp. genome sequences of 16 algal species (belonging to 6 families, class Phaeophyceae) were obtained from the NCBI database as ingroups: Family Lessoniaceae: Ecklonia maxima (PP471197), Ecklonia cava (NC_085303.1), Ecklonia arborea (MZ156038.1), Ecklonia radicosa (MZ156040.1), Ecklonia stolonifera (ON552965.1), Ecklonia radiata (NC_070408.1), Lessonia flavicans (NC_056288.1); Family Costariaceae: Costaria costata (NC_028502.1); Family Alariaceae: Alaria praelonga (NC_058772), Alaria esculenta (NC_058770), Alaria marginata (NC_058769), Alaria crispa (NC_058768); Family Laminariaceae: Saccharina japonica (NC_018523.1); Family Scytosiphonaceae: Colpomenia sinuosa (NC_081925.1), Scytosiphon lomentaria (NC_057081.1); Family Dictyotaceae: Dictyopteris divaricata (NC_036804.1). Haslea silbo (MW645082.1), a diatom (class Bacillariophyceae), was selected as the outgroup. Diatoms and brown algae (Phaeophyceae) share a common evolutionary ancestor within the stramenopiles lineage, but have undergone extensive divergence, making Haslea silbo a phylogenetically distant and appropriate outgroup for rooting the tree of brown algal species.

To establish the dataset for phylogenetic reconstruction, we conducted a pan-genome analysis of the protein-coding genes. All annotated protein-coding sequences from the 17 cp. genomes were clustered using CD-HIT v4.6.1 with parameters set to sequence identity > 50% and alignment coverage > 50%. This analysis identified a total of 2,397 initial gene clusters. The pan-gene set contained 256 gene families. Among these, we identified 69 core gene families that were present as single-copy orthologs in every genome. Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using this conserved set of 69 core genes. The amino acid sequences of each core gene were aligned using MUSCLE v3.8.31. The resulting individual alignments were then concatenated to generate a supermatrix alignment. A Maximum Likelihood (ML) tree was reconstructed from this supermatrix using PhyML v3.0 (http://www.atgc-montpellier.fr/phyml/), with Bayesian-based correction applied and branch support assessed by 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Comparative and rearrangement analyses of genome structures

The cp. genomes of five additional species within the genus Ecklonia (E. cava, NC_085303.1; E. radiata, NC_070408.1; E. arborea, MZ156038.1; E. radicosa, MZ156040.1; E. stolonifera, ON552965.1) were downloaded from the NCBI website. We compared the cp. genome structure of E. maxima with those of these five congeners using Proksee (https://proksee.ca/). Nucleotide diversity (PI) of coding and non-coding regions was analyzed using DnaSP v5.10 (http://www.ub.edu/dnasp/), revealing genomic divergence hotspot regions that can serve as candidate regions for DNA barcoding. The parameters were set as follows: window length = 300 bp, step size = 200 bp. Perl-IRscope (https://github.com/xul962464/perl-IRscope) was used to visualize the cp. genome’s IR and SC regions and compare differences in IR expansion or contraction across the six species. Finally, to visualize conserved and variable regions within the genus, a comparative analysis was performed using mVISTA (https://genome.lbl.gov/vista/index.shtml) [28] in the Shuffle-LAGAN mode [29], with the annotation of E. maxima serving as the reference.

Adaptive evaluation analysis

To assess the selective pressures acting on the cp. genome of E. maxima, we performed a pairwise Ka/Ks analysis with five other Ecklonia species. Coding sequences (CDS) of the shared PCGs were aligned using MUSCLE v3.8.31. The non-synonymous substitution rate (Ka) and synonymous substitution rate (Ks) for each gene were calculated pairwise between E. maxima and each of the other five Ecklonia species using KaKs_Calculator v2.0 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/kakscalculator2/) to obtain the Ka/Ks ratio [30]. This pairwise approach was adopted to provide a direct comparison of selective pressures between E. maxima and its close relatives.

Results

Characteristics of the cp. genome of E. maxima

The complete cp. genome of E. maxima (GenBank No. PP471197) was sequenced, assembled, and annotated. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating a total of 8.03 Gb of raw paired-end (2 × 150 bp) reads. From this dataset, 1.12% (~ 90 Mb) of the reads were successfully recruited for cp. genome assembly. Sequencing depth analysis confirmed an average coverage of 690.07× for the assembled 130.9 kb genome, with depths ranging from 3× to 1,989×. The continuous and uniform read coverage across the entire genome, without abrupt drops at the junctions, provides strong support for the assembly of a complete, circular DNA molecule (Figure S1).

The assembled, complete cp. genome of E. maxima is a circular, double-stranded DNA molecule of 130,900 bp. It comprises a LSC region of 76,991 bp, a SSC region of 42,941 bp, and a pair of IR regions, each 5,484 bp (Fig. 2). The overall AT content of the genome is 69.02%, with the IR regions (56.22%) having a lower AT content than the LSC (70.2%) and SSC (70.17%) regions. A total of 169 genes were annotated, including 137 PCGs, 29 tRNA genes, and 3 rRNA genes. Non-coding sequences comprise 33,073 bp, accounting for 25.27% of the genome (Table 1).

Characteristics of E. maxima cp. genome

The nucleotide composition and skewness of the E. maxima cp. genome were analyzed in detail (Table S1). The overall base composition was asymmetric, with A (34.46%) and T (34.56%) predominating over G (15.62%) and C (15.36%). This composition varied across functional elements, with PCGs exhibiting an AT content of 68.30%, while tRNAs and rRNAs had lower AT contents of 48.16% and 58.16%, respectively. The calculated AT-skew and GC-skew were − 0.001 and 0.008, respectively. A pronounced codon usage bias was evident, as the AT content at the third codon position (82.40%) substantially exceeded that of the first (57.23%) and second (65.27%) positions. Functional categorization assigned 48 genes to photosynthesis, 81 to self-replication, and 40 to other or unknown functions (Table S2).

Codon usage bias

To explore codon usage patterns in the E. maxima cp. genome, we analyzed codon usage bias in the E. maxima cpDNA by calculating the RSCU values for all PCGs (Fig. 3 and Table S3). We found that most amino acids exhibited distinct codon preferences. For alanine (Ala), the RSCU values of GCT and GCA were 1.8126 and 1.5473, respectively, while those of GCC and GCG were 0.3410 and 0.2989. For leucine (Leu), the RSCU value for TTA was the highest (3.9392), considerably higher than that of all other leucine codons. For arginine (Arg), RSCU values ranged widely: AGA had an RSCU of 2.3368, whereas CGG had an RSCU of only 0.1736. Tryptophan (Trp) had only one codon (TGG) with an RSCU value of 1.0000.

Interspersed repeats and SSR of cp. genomes

A total of 22 pairs of large repeats were identified in the E. maxima cp. genome. These consisted of 16 palindromic repeats and 6 forward repeats, while no reverse or complementary repeats were detected. The lengths of these repeats ranged from 30 to 5,484 bp, distributed as follows: 11 repeats were 30–38 bp in length, another 11 were 43–148 bp, and a single repeat was 5,484 bp (Fig. 4, Table S4).

Phylogenetic inference

To elucidate the phylogenetic position of E. maxima, a ML tree was constructed based on the aligned amino acid sequences of single-copy core genes identified from the complete cp. genomes of 17 species, with the diatom Haslea silbo serving as a phylogenetically distant outgroup. The resulting tree showed strong nodal support, with 11 out of 15 nodes having bootstrap values ≥ 95% (6 nodes at 100%) (Fig. 6).

Fig. 6Phylogenetic relationships analysis of E.maxima cp. genome

Phylogenetic relationships analysis of E.maxima cp. genome

Within the Ecklonia clade, E. maxima was resolved as the sister group to a well-supported subclade (bootstrap values = 96%) comprising E. radiata, E. cava, E. cava subsp. stolonifera, and E. radicosa. This topology indicates that E. maxima, E. radiata, E. cava, and E. radicosa form a closely related group within the genus, with E. arborea representing an earlier diverging lineage. This refined phylogenetic framework provides greater resolution for understanding the evolutionary relationships among Ecklonia species.

Notably, Costaria costata (family Costariaceae) was resolved as sister to the clade comprising the families Lessoniaceae and Laminariaceae, a placement consistent with recent multi‑gene phylogenetic studies that have redefined the family boundaries within the order Laminariales [31]. Furthermore, the relationships among other taxa are fully congruent with modern molecular systematics [3, 32], which primarily relied on plastid markers (rbcL, psaA, psbA, atpB) alongside mitochondrial (cox1, cox3, nad1, nad4, atp9) or nuclear markers (LSU rDNA). The species Colpomenia sinuosa and Scytosiphon lomentaria formed a strongly supported clade, consistent with their classification within the family Scytosiphonaceae (Order Ectocarpales). In contrast, Dictyopteris divaricata (Family Dictyotaceae) formed a distinct, early-diverging lineage within the separate order Dictyotales, which is evolutionarily distant from the Ectocarpales. Overall, this phylogenomic analysis based on complete cp. genomes provides a highly resolved and reliable framework for understanding the evolutionary history of E. maxima and its relatives.

Comparative analysis of the cp. genomes of E. maxima and its related species

Initial GCview comparison demonstrated high structural similarity between the E. maxima cpDNA and its congeners (Fig. 7). To quantitatively assess sequence divergence, we calculated the PI values across 177 coding and non-coding loci (Fig. 8, Table S5). The PI values ranged from 0 to 0.038322, with a mean of 0.00409, indicating overall high sequence conservation. Notably, six genes—petF (0.038322), ycf37-exon2 (0.0157576), petJ (0.0152152), psaE (0.0139785), rpl24 (0.0132275), and psaL (0.0126332)—were identified as hypervariable regions (PI > 0.012). These genes were distributed across different genomic regions: psaL and petJ in the LSC; rpl24, psaE, and petF in the SSC; and ycf37 in the IRa. The prevalence of low PI values among the six studied species underscores the high evolutionary constraint on the cp. genome in these Ecklonia congeners, while the identified hotspots provide valuable markers for species-level phylogenetics.

To further understand structural variation beyond sequence divergence, we compared the IR/LSC and IR/SSC boundary positions across all six Ecklonia species to assess the extent of their IR expansion or contraction (Fig. 9A). Among these six cpDNAs, the E. arborea genome (130,965 bp) was the longest, and E. cava (130,837 bp) was the shortest; these size differences were primarily due to variations in the LSC and SSC regions, while the IR region was relatively stable in length (5,479 bp to 5,484 bp), showing a high degree of conservatism. The average lengths were 77,016 bp for the LSC region, 5,480 bp for the IRa/b region, and 42,917 bp for the SSC region. Analysis of the boundary genes revealed a consistent pattern across all six species: the trnL gene was located at the LSC/IRb junction; the rns and rrn5 genes were situated within the IR regions; and the ycf17 gene spanned the SSC/IRa boundary, with the majority of its sequence in the SSC region (165 bp) and a short segment extending into the IRa region (6 bp). No other genes were annotated at the IR-SSC boundaries in the comparative analysis.

Adaptive evaluation analysis

We used KaKs_Calculator to compute the Ka/Ks ratios shared between E. maxima and five other Ecklonia species (Table S6). Results showed that Ka/Ks values between E. maxima and other Ecklonia species ranged from 0.001 to 1.28146. Specifically, the Ka/Ks values of gene petF between E. maxima and E. radicosa (1.21664), E. cava (1.28146), E. stolonifera (1.28146); rpl24 between E. maxima and E. radicosa (1.08755); and syfB between E. maxima and E. radiata (1.16654) were greater than 1, indicating positive selection on these genes. The remaining genes had Ka/Ks < 1, indicating strong purifying selection pressure in the genus Ecklonia (Fig. 10).

Discussion

Genome structure and evolutionary conservation

The cp. genome of E. maxima exhibits a highly conserved architecture typical of brown algae and its congeners. Its size (130,900 bp), quadripartite structure, and gene content are remarkably similar to those of other Ecklonia species, such as E. radiata and E. arborea. Such size conservation aligns with the narrow range (125.0–140.0 kb) of brown algal cp. genomes [33]. This structural conservation underscores the slow evolutionary rate of cp. genomes and highlights the strong functional constraints acting on essential photosynthetic and metabolic processes [5, 8]. This finding supports the hypothesis that cp. genome size is relatively conserved in closely related algal lineages, as observed in other marine macroalgae [34]. The high AT content (69.02%) is a shared feature among Laminariales, which may influence gene expression regulation and DNA flexibility, potentially as an adaptation to the marine environment [35]. Notably, the IR regions in E. maxima and its congeners are highly stable in both length and gene content, contrasting with the substantial expansions or contractions observed in orders like Fucales [36]. This stability suggests that IRs play a crucial role in maintaining genomic integrity within the Laminariales, possibly through recombination-mediated repair mechanisms [37, 38]. This study represents the first report of the complete cp. genome sequence of E. maxima, filling the gap in cp. genomic resources for Southern Hemisphere-endemic Ecklonia species and providing a foundation for developing species-specific DNA barcodes (e.g., based on hypervariable genes like petF and rpl24).

Phylogenetic position and biogeographic implications

Our comprehensive phylogenomic analysis, incorporating all six Ecklonia species and based on a conserved set of cp. protein-coding genes, robustly resolves the phylogenetic position of E. maxima. The analysis reveals that E. maxima forms a well-supported sister group to a subclade comprising E. radiata, E. cava, E. cava subsp. stolonifera and E. radicosa. This closely related ancestry, likely originating from a common ancestor that was fragmented by glacial sea-level fluctuations in the Southern Hemisphere, is characterized by limited divergence in the cp. genome. This provides the essential genetic prerequisite for potential introgression or hybridization among these closely related species, as suggested by recent studies in contact zones [39]. Furthermore, the monophyly of the genus Ecklonia and the clear delineation from other families demonstrate the utility of complete cp. genomes in resolving phylogenetic relationships across different evolutionary depths [3, 32]. The phylogenetic insights provided by this study form a valuable basis for future phylogenetic analyses of species within the genus Ecklonia.

Signatures of positive selection and adaptive evolution to the fluctuating upwelling environment

Although the Benguela Upwelling Zone is nutrient-rich, it is characterized by significant ‘pulse-like’ fluctuations in key environmental factors such as light availability (due to tidal cycles, turbidity, and deep nutrient upwelling) and nutrient supply [40]. Our comparative genomic and evolutionary analysis identified molecular signatures that may underpin E. maxima’s adaptation to this dynamic habitat. We discovered that despite overall high sequence conservation (mean PI = 0.00409), several genes, including petF, petJ, psaE, psaL, rpl24, and ycf37-exon2, exhibited notably high nucleotide diversity (PI > 0.012), marking them as hypervariable regions. Crucially, to distinguish neutral genetic drift from adaptive evolution, we performed a Ka/Ks analysis. Ka/Ks > 1 denotes positive selection, indicating that mutations are beneficial and promote adaptive evolution; Ka/Ks = 1 denotes neutral selection, where mutations are not subjected to selection; and Ka/Ks < 1 denotes purifying selection, indicating that the gene is functionally conserved, with most mutations being deleterious and removed [41]. The results demonstrated that three of these genes—petF, rpl24, and syfB—have undergone positive selection (Ka/Ks > 1) in specific pairwise species comparisons. The convergence of high sequence variability and signals of positive selection strongly implicates these genes as key targets of adaptive evolution in Ecklonia.

The biological implications of these positively selected genes can be directly interpreted in the context of the unique physiological challenges posed by the upwelling environment. Firstly, adaptations to fluctuating light are evident. The gene petF (ferredoxin), a crucial electron carrier, exhibits signatures of positive selection. This likely fine-tunes its interactions with photosystem I (PSI) and ferredoxin-NADP + reductase, optimizing electron transfer efficiency under rapidly changing light conditions [42, 43]. The positive selection on petF is likely a direct molecular adaptation to the extreme light fluctuations inherent to the Benguela Upwelling. Here, tidal cycles and turbid upwelled water create a “light pulsing” environment [44]. An optimized petF could enhance the kinetics of electron flow from PSI, thereby minimizing photodamage during sudden high-light exposure and maximizing energy harvest during brief light periods, a critical advantage in this dynamic habitat. Concurrently, the elevated variability in psaE, psaL (structural components of PSI), and ycf37-exon2 (a PSI assembly factor) may collectively enhance the stability and repair of PSI, a primary site of photoinhibition [45, 46]. Secondly, a key adaptation to pulsed nutrient supply involves the ribosomal protein gene rpl24. Under positive selection, rpl24 may enhance the efficiency and fidelity of ribosomal assembly and protein synthesis [47]. This adaptation aligns with the “feast-or-famine” nutrient regime of upwelling systems [48]. We hypothesize that adaptive evolution in rpl24 enhances translational efficiency, allowing E. maxima to swiftly produce the proteins necessary for cell division and nutrient assimilation during the transient nutrient pulses characteristic of the Benguela Upwelling, thus conferring a competitive edge during these critical growth windows. Finally, the positive selection on syfB points to an adaptation for maintaining cellular homeostasis. This gene encodes the beta subunit of phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase, a core component of the plastid translation machinery [49]. Selection on syfB likely safeguards translational accuracy under the stressful conditions accompanying upwelling fronts (e.g., shifts in osmolarity or temperature), ensuring the production of functional proteins even when metabolic rates are high and conditions are fluctuating. This is particularly relevant as upwelling fronts are associated not only with nutrient pulses but also with ancillary stresses such as rapid temperature shifts and oxidative stress. A more robust syfB could therefore be a fundamental adaptation to safeguard essential cellular functions under a spectrum of co-occurring environmental challenges.

In summary, our analysis description by identifying not only variable genes but also those under positive selection. We propose a plausible eco-physiological model where adaptations in petF and PSI-related genes enhance photoprotection and light-use efficiency, while changes in rpl24 and syfB optimize resource allocation and growth in response to nutrient pulses. This provides a genetic foundation for how E. maxima may have evolved to thrive in the specific habitat of the Benguela Upwelling System, offering testable hypotheses for future functional studies.

It is important to note that the Ka/Ks analysis in this study was conducted through pairwise comparisons between E. maxima and its congeners, which serves as an effective initial screen for identifying candidate genes under selection. We acknowledge that future studies employing more powerful phylogenetic codon models (e.g., branch-site models in PAML or HyPhy) on a broader taxonomic scale would provide more robust statistical support and help to precisely identify the specific amino acid sites underpinning this adaptive evolution. Furthermore, functional studies through protein engineering or gene expression analysis under simulated environmental stresses would be crucial to confirm the physiological roles of these adaptive mutations.

Repetitive elements and codon usage reflect evolutionary forces

The analysis of repetitive sequences and codon usage provides insights into the evolutionary dynamics of the E. maxima cp. genome. We identified 47 SSRs in the E. maxima cp. genome, predominantly mononucleotide and tetranucleotide repeats. This profile differs from other brown algae like Saccharina japonica, which is rich in trinucleotide repeats [50], suggesting lineage-specific mutational or selective regimes. These species-specific SSR patterns are valuable for developing molecular markers for population genetics and phylogeographic studies [51, 52]. Furthermore, our analysis revealed a pronounced codon usage bias in the E. maxima cp. genome. Codon usage bias refers to the non-uniform usage of synonymous codons for an amino acid, and our findings of distinct preferences for specific codons (e.g., TTA for Leu and AGA for Arg) reflect the evolutionary pressures acting on the genome. A clear bias towards A/T-ending synonyms was observed, a pattern that is primarily driven by the overall high AT-content (69.02%) of the genome, as is commonly observed in other brown algal cp. genomes [21]. This genome-wide AT-richness exerts a strong mutational pressure that shapes codon selection [53]. However, this non-random codon usage is likely shaped by a combination of such mutational bias and natural selection for translational efficiency and accuracy [54]. The preferential use of codons that may correspond to the most abundant tRNA pools could enhance the synthesis rates of critical photosynthetic proteins, providing a fitness advantage [55]. Although synonymous codons encode the same amino acid, their frequency of use varies significantly; the patterns we observed in E. maxima likely contribute to fine-tuning gene expression under its specific environmental conditions. The repertoire of repetitive sequences and the distinct codon usage bias together underscore the interplay of mutational and selective forces in shaping the E. maxima cp. genome.

Conclusions

This study systematically characterized the cp. genome of E. maxima and explored its evolutionary and adaptive features through comparative and phylogenetic analyses. The cp. genome of E. maxima exhibits a typical four-part structure (LSC, SSC, IRa, IRb), with a genome size of 130,900 bp, a total of 169 genes, and a high AT content of 69.02%, consistent with the conserved characteristics of cp. genomes in the genus Ecklonia and the order Laminariales. SSR primarily consists of single-nucleotide and tetranucleotide repeat sequences, providing important markers for population genetics studies. Phylogenetic inference based on conserved cp. genes clarified the relationships within Ecklonia, revealing that E. maxima is sister to a clade containing E. radiata, E. cava, E. cava subsp. stolonifera and E. radicosa with strong support (96% bootstrap). This confirms their close evolutionary relationship. Comparative genomic analysis indicates high sequence conservation among Ecklonia species, with overall low nucleotide diversity (average PI = 0.00409), while six genes (petF, ycf37, petJ, psaE, rpl24, and psaL) exhibit high variability, suggesting they could serve as molecular markers for species differentiation. Codon usage bias and Ka/Ks ratios indicate that purifying selection predominates, with a few genes (such as petF) undergoing positive selection, which may help adapt to fluctuations in light and nutrient conditions in the Benguela Upwelling Zone. Overall, this study provides comprehensive genomic resources for E. maxima, clarifies its phylogenetic position within the genus Ecklonia, and reveals both conserved and adaptive features in its cp. genome. These findings enhance our understanding of the evolutionary dynamics and environmental adaptability of brown algae, providing a valuable genomic resource for future studies in kelp taxonomy, population genetics, and adaptive evolution.
