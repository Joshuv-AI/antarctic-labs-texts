Kidney injury associated with direct oral anticoagulants: a comparative study of direct thrombin inhibitors and factor Xa inhibitors based on literature and database analysis
Authors: Songhua Chen, Can Shi, Xiang Li, Xia Wang, Ren Guo, Tian Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are widely used, limited real-world studies have explored kidney injury linked to DOACs, particularly differences between direct thrombin inhibitors (DTIs) and factor Xa inhibitors (FXaIs). We conducted a comprehensive analysis of kidney injury cases from the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS, 2008–2024) and systematically reviewed published case reports, comparing DTIs (dabigatran) with FXaIs (rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban). Among 8,116 reports of suspected DOAC-associated kidney injury, 1,784 were linked to DTIs and 6,332 to FXaIs. Significant reporting rates were observed for DTIs [reporting odds ratio (ROR) = 2.081, 95% CI: 1.979–2.189; proportional reporting ratio (PRR) = 2.044, CI: 1.947–2.147)] and FXaIs (ROR = 1.483, 95% CI: 1.444–1.523; PRR = 1.472, CI: 1.434–1.510). Notably, substantial intra-class variation existed among FXaIs, with edoxaban showing the highest risk signal (ROR = 4.453, 95% CI: 4.021–4.932; PRR = 4.212, 95% CI: 3.831–4.632). Multivariable logistic regression analysis confirmed that significantly lower kidney injury reporting disproportionality signals with FXaIs versus DTIs after adjustment (ROR = 0.687, 95% CI: 0.650–0.727, P < 0.001). Analysis of 32 detailed cases revealed distinct clinical patterns: DTI-associated injury featured higher proteinuria rates (P = 0.029), while FXaI-associated injury occurred more frequently in settings of polypharmacy (P = 0.006) and was associated with poorer renal recovery (P = 0.023). This study demonstrates distinct kidney injury risks among DOAC classes and individual agents, with DTIs and edoxaban showing particularly strong signals. These findings emphasize the need for individualized DOAC selection and enhanced renal monitoring in clinical practice.

Introduction

With the aging population and changes in lifestyle, the global incidence of thrombotic diseases has been rising, becoming the leading cause of death worldwide. Oral anticoagulation therapy is a key strategy for the prevention and treatment of various thrombotic diseases. Compared with warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) offer advantages such as rapid onset, shorter half-life, food-independent efficacy, fixed dosage, and no need for routine monitoring of coagulation function [1]. These advantages have established DOACs as first-line therapy for venous thromboembolism and systemic embolism in patients with non-valvular atrial fibrillation, as recommended by major clinical guidelines [2–4]. In recent years, the application scope of DOACs has been extended to the arterial field, such as stable angina and peripheral artery disease [5], leading to growing clinical adoption. However, with their widespread use, reports of DOACs-related adverse reactions, primarily hemorrhage, have increased [6–10]. Additionally, non-hemorrhage complications, such as kidney injury, are gaining more attention [11].

DOACs are categorized according to their molecular targets into direct thrombin inhibitors (DTIs, such as dabigatran etexilate) and factor Xa inhibitors (FXaIs, such as rivaroxaban, edoxaban and apixaban) [12]. This mechanistic distinction carries important implications for renal safety. DTIs inhibit thrombin-mediated activation of protease-activated receptor (PAR)-1, -3, and -4 [13], whereas FXaIs additionally block the pro-inflammatory and potentially kidney-injurious PAR-2 pathway, which may confer a renal protective advantage [13,14]. Furthermore, DTIs exhibit greater reliance on renal clearance compared to FXaIs [15,16]. We therefore hypothesize that DTIs are associated with a higher risk of kidney injury than FXaIs. Despite the widespread clinical use of DOACs, comparative evidence regarding the renal safety profiles of DTIs versus FXaIs remains limited [17]. To address this evidence gap, this study utilizes the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database of the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in conjunction with published case reports to compare DTIs and FXaIs in the reporting disproportionality signals, clinical characteristics, and management of anticoagulant-associated kidney injury.

Methods

Pharmacovigilance study

In this study, we conducted a retrospective, disproportionality, and pharmacovigilance analysis. Using data retrieved from the publicly available FAERS database in FDA website from the 2008 Quarter 1 to the 2024 Quarter 4, we evaluated the reporting disproportionality signals of kidney injury with the use of different DOACs. The study compared two DOAC classes: DTIs (dabigatran etexilate) and FXaIs (rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban). Case identification utilized both brand and generic names, including records where these agents were designated as ‘primary suspected’ or ‘secondary suspected’ drugs. Renal adverse events (AEs) were identified using MedDRA (v24.0) Preferred Terms, encompassing: acute kidney injury [10069339], blood creatinine increased [10005483], blood urea abnormal [10005846], glomerular filtration rate (GFR) decreased [10018358], renal impairment [10062237], oliguria [10030302], anuria [10002847], dialysis [10061105], proteinuria [10037032], nephropathy toxic [10029155], nephritis allergic [10029120], and tubulointerstitial nephritis [10048302]. Signal detection was performed using the reporting odds ratio (ROR) and proportional reporting ratio (PRR) based on principles of Bayesian and non-proportional analysis. The ROR and PRR were calculated as (a × d)/(b × c) and [a/(a + b)]/[c/(c + d)], respectively, with complete calculation methodologies detailed in the Supplementary Table S1. A significant signal required: ROR/PRR >1, lower 95% confidence interval (CI) >1, and ≥3 supporting cases.

Descriptive study

We conducted a systematic review of published case reports and case series according to the PRISMA 2020 statement. A comprehensive search of multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Wanfang, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Web of Science, was performed from January 2008 to January 2025, with no language restrictions. The search terms were ‘dabigatran etexilate’, ‘Pradaxa’, ‘rivaroxaban’, ‘Xarelto’, ‘edoxaban’, ‘Lixiana’, ‘apixaban’, ‘Eliquis’, ‘acute kidney injury’, ‘blood creatinine increased’, ‘blood urea abnormal’, ‘glomerular filtration rate decreased’, ‘renal impairment’, ‘oliguria’, ‘anuria’, ‘dialysis’, ‘proteinuria’, ‘nephropathy toxic’, ‘nephritis allergic’, and ‘tubulointerstitial nephritis’. Case reports and case series were included, while reviews, mechanistic studies, animal studies, and articles for which the full text was unavailable were excluded. Two independent reviewers screened records, extracted data, and assessed the risk of bias using the Joanna Briggs Institute Checklist for Case Reports. The collected data included patient location, age, sex, indication of DOACs, medical history, time to onset, clinical manifestations, laboratory tests, histopathological examinations, treatment, and prognosis of DOACs-induced kidney injury, where time to onset was defined as the period from DOACs initiation to the occurrence of the associated kidney injury. A narrative synthesis was then performed, stratified by drug class (DTIs versus FXaIs).

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous non-normal data were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. To identify independent risk factors associated with kidney injury and to compare the renal safety profiles of DTIs and FXaIs, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. The results are presented as adjusted ROR with their corresponding 95% CIs. To account for potential over-reporting bias related to the Weber effect, we conducted two sensitivity analyses. Firstly, we excluded AE reports from the first two years post-marketing and repeated the analysis using data from year three onward. Secondly, we included ‘years since approval’ as a covariate in the regression model. Analyses were conducted in SPSS (v20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. As the analysis included all available eligible reports, conventional sample size estimation was not applicable.

Results

DTIs and FXaIs-associated kidney injury in the FAERS database

From 2008 Quarter 1 to 2024 Quarter 4, the FAERS database recorded 46,224 AE cases linked to DTIs compared with 228,668 cases associated with FXaIs (rivaroxaban 94,707; apixaban 128,571; edoxaban 5,390) (see Supplementary Figure 1). Among these, 8,116 reports (3.0% of total DOAC-related AEs) primarily suspected or secondary suspected kidney injury as an adverse reaction, of which 1,784 (3.9% of total DTIs-related AEs) were dabigatran etexilate and 6,332 (2.8% of total FXaIs-related AEs) were FXaIs (Supplementary Table S2). FXaI subgroup analysis revealed rivaroxaban accounted for 3,152 reports (3.3% of all rivaroxaban-related AEs), followed by apixaban (n = 2,773; 2.2% of apixaban-related AEs) and edoxaban (n = 407; 7.6% of edoxaban-related AEs). There were statistical differences in indication [atrial fibrillation (AF), deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism (DVT/PE), and prophylaxis], concomitant drugs [furosemide, pantoprazole, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors (RAASi), and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)], and outcomes (hospitalization, disability, and life-threatening) between DTIs and FXaIs. Patients with DTI-associated kidney injury were more commonly indicated for AF or prophylaxis, and were more likely to use furosemide, RAASi, or NSAIDs concomitantly. Additionally, they had significantly higher rates of hospitalization, disability, or life-threatening events (Table 1).

Disproportionality analyses

Disproportionality analyses identified 12 unique preferred terms for kidney in the database. A statistically significant increase in kidney injury events was reported for both DTIs and FXaIs, with corresponding RORs of 2.081 (95% CI: 1.979–2.189) and 1.483 (95% CI: 1.444–1.523), and PRRs of 2.044 (95% CI: 1.947–2.147) and 1.472 (95% CI: 1.434–1.510), respectively. Among FXaIs, significant heterogeneity in kidney injury reporting disproportionality signals was observed (Tables 2 and 3). The disproportionality analysis revealed that the association signal for edoxaban was substantially stronger than those for rivaroxaban and apixaban. Specifically, edoxaban’s ROR of 4.453 (95% CI: 4.021–4.932) and PRR of 4.212 (95% CI: 3.831–4.632) were approximately two to four times higher than the corresponding values for rivaroxaban (ROR: 1.817; PRR: 1.793) and apixaban (ROR: 1.120; PRR: 1.118).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

To further identify independent risk factors associated with kidney injury and to compare the renal safety profiles of DTIs and FXaIs, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. The model was adjusted for the following covariates: age (as a continuous variable per 10-year increment), sex, year since approval, indication, and concomitant medications. The results confirmed that the significant difference in kidney injury risk between DTIs (dabigatran) and FXaIs remained statistically significant after adjusting for these potential confounders (adjusted ROR for FXaIs vs. DTIs: 0.687, 95% CI: 0.650–0.727, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Tables S2–S4 and Table 4).

Descriptive analysis

A total of 29 studies (28 case reports and 1 case series) with 32 individual cases were identified in the published literature (see Supplementary Figure 1). Of these, 62.5% involved DTIs and 37.5% FXaIs (apixaban 18.8%, rivaroxaban 15.6%, edoxaban 3.1%). The patient sex ratio (male/female) was 1.5, with median age 78 years (range, 59–89 years). The main indication for DOACs therapy was AF (30 cases, 94.0%). Comparative analysis revealed patients that FXaI-associated kidney injury had significantly higher rates of polypharmacy (P = 0.006), and RAASi use (P = 0.044) than those with DTI-associated cases. Details are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Clinical manifestations and laboratory features

Among the 32 included cases, 24 (75.0%) presented with symptoms. The most common initial symptoms were hematuria (n = 13, 40.6%) and proteinuria (7 cases, 21.9%), followed by fatigue (n = 4, 12.5%), anemia (n = 1, 3.1%) and dysuria (n = 1, 3.1%). DTIs demonstrated a significantly higher incidence of proteinuria compared to FXaIs (DTIs: 35.0% [7/20] vs FXaIs: 0% [0/12]; P = 0.029). The time from DOAC initiation to symptom onset was less than 3 months in 40.6% (n = 13) and more than 3 months in 34.4% (n = 11) of cases, with no significant difference between DTIs and FXaIs. Hypertension (systolic BP >140 mmHg) occurred in 28.1% (n = 9) of patients, with all severe cases (≥160 mmHg, including one extreme value >240 mmHg) occurring exclusively in DTI users.

The laboratory profile for DOACs-induced kidney injury cases is summarized in Table 5. Prolonged prothrombin time (PT) or international normalized ratio (INR) were observed in 78.6% (11/14) of tested DTI cases versus 100% (3/3) of FXaI cases, while elevated activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT, n = 11) and thrombin time (TT, n = 3) were exclusively observed in DTI recipients. Renal impairment was evidenced by elevated creatinine in 87.5% of cases (28/32; DTIs: n = 17, FXaIs: n = 11) and increased BUN in 43.8% (14/32; DTIs: n = 6, FXaIs: n = 8). Urinalysis demonstrated proteinuria in 21.9% (7/32; DTIs: n = 5, FXaIs: n = 2) and hematuria in 37.5% (12/32; DTIs: n = 5, FXaIs: n = 7) of cases, with no significant intergroup differences in these parameters.

Renal biopsy

Renal biopsy results were reported for 18 patients (DTIs: n = 10; FXaIs: n = 8). The predominant histological finding was acute tubular injury (44.4%, 8/18), followed by red blood cell casts in cortical tubules (38.9%, 7/18), interstitial fibrosis (22.2%, 4/18), and IgA nephropathy (11.1%, 2/18). No statistically significant differences in histopathological patterns were observed between DTI- and FXaI-associated kidney injury cases (Table 5).

Treatment and prognosis

Sixteen patients discontinued DOACs after kidney injury. Among the 32 included patients, 12 patients were treated with steroids (including prednisone and methylprednisolone), 7 patients were treated with dialysis, 4 patients received idarucizumab, and 3 patients were treated with diuretic therapy (including furosemide, torasemide and mannitol). Overall, 68.8% (22/32) achieved complete recovery, while 21.9% (7/32) developed persistent renal dysfunction and 9.4% (3/32) required ongoing hospitalization. FXaI-associated kidney injury demonstrated significantly worse renal recovery compared to DTI-associated cases (P = 0.023). Among recovered patients, 27.3% (6/22) showed improvement within 2 weeks (DTIs:5 vs FXaIs:1), 4.5% (1/22) at 2–4 weeks (DTIs only), and 27.3% (6/22) beyond 4 weeks (DTIs:5 vs FXaIs:1), suggesting differential recovery trajectories between DTIs and FXaIs (Table 6).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first real-world pharmacovigilance study to integrate data from both the FAERS database and published case reports, and provide evidence that kidney injury profiles differ significantly between DTI and FXaIs. Our analysis indicates lower disproportionality reporting signals associated with FXaIs compared to DTIs. This disparity in reported risk remained statistically significant after adjusting for potential confounders, affirming the concept of class-specific renal risks among DOACs. These findings highlight clinically important safety distinctions between DTIs and FXaIs that warrant consideration in therapeutic decision-making.

The higher renal risk observed with dabigatran can be largely attributed to its distinct pharmacokinetics [18]. Approximately 80% of the drug is eliminated unchanged by the kidneys, resulting in a direct dependence of its clearance on renal function [19]. This profile explains the prolonged half-life and elevated plasma concentrations in patients with renal impairment, which may precipitate acute kidney injury [20]. Proposed mechanisms for dabigatran’s nephrotoxicity include dose-dependent formation of intratubular erythrocyte casts and potential aggravation of underlying renal conditions like IgA nephropathy [18,21,22]. In contrast, the renal elimination of FXaIs is considerably lower (25–50%), forming the pharmacokinetic basis for their more favorable renal safety profile[19,20,23,24]. Beyond pharmacokinetic differences, this mechanistic distinction carries important implications for renal safety: while dabigatran directly inhibits thrombin-mediated activation of PAR-1, PAR-3, and PAR-4, FXaIs not only indirectly attenuate PAR-1, -3, and -4 activation by reducing thrombin generation but also directly block factor Xa–mediated PAR-2 signaling [13,14]. Given that PAR-2 activation is generally linked to pro-inflammatory and kidney-injurious pathways [25,26], its inhibition by FXaIs may confer additional renal benefits.

It is important to interpret our findings within the context of real-world clinical prescribing patterns. Our data revealed that patients receiving FXaIs had a significantly higher prevalence of heart failure and chronic kidney disease—both established risk factors for acute kidney injury [27,28]. These comorbidities represent important confounders that may partly explain the observed kidney injury signals associated with this drug class. However, due to the absence of structured comorbidity data in our primary FAERS analysis and the limited sample size of the case series, we were unable to fully adjust for these confounding factors. Therefore, the identified association should be interpreted with caution.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the inherent biases of spontaneous reporting systems—including under‑reporting, over‑reporting, duplicate reporting and confounding by indication—preclude the establishment of causal relationships. Although we performed multivariate adjustments, residual confounding by unmeasured factors, such as baseline renal function, remains possible. Therefore, our findings should therefore be interpreted as signals of disproportionate reporting rather than measures of absolute risk. Second, the descriptive case series component of our investigation included only 32 patients. Although statistical methods such as Fisher’s exact test were applied to explore differences between DTIs and FXaIs, the small sample size limited our ability to adjust for all potential confounders or detect rare AEs. In addition, publication bias in the case series part should be mentioned and addressed, as case reports describing positive or unusual findings are more likely to be published, which may skew the pooled description of outcomes. Thus, this analysis should be regarded as exploratory, serving as a foundation for future large-scale, prospective studies. Furthermore, the differential nephrotoxicity profiles between DTIs and FXaIs require confirmation through rigorously designed prospective cohorts or randomized controlled trials to establish causality and evaluate dose–response relationships.

Conclusion

This study confirms that DOACs are associated with kidney injury, with DTIs demonstrating a stronger association than FXaIs. Furthermore, considerable variation exists within the FXaI class, as edoxaban shows a particularly strong risk signal. Therefore, these findings warrant both heightened clinical vigilance and further investigation through prospective studies.
