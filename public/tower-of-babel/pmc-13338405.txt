Microbiome responses to anthelmintic treatment depend on pre-treatment helminth infection status in young Ethiopian children
Authors: Polina Tikhonova, Zeleke Mekonnen, Kenneth D Belanger, Ahmet Ay, Emily R Davenport, Bineyam Taye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMass deworming programs using anthelmintic drugs are a common public health strategy for controlling soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections in high-risk, low-income countries. However, whether routine deworming induces long-term changes in gut microbiome composition remains unclear. We used longitudinal data to examine how anthelmintics alter the gut microbiome profiles of Ethiopian children.MethodsFecal samples were collected from school-aged children enrolled in a mass deworming program in Jimma, Ethiopia, before and after receiving a 400 mg dose of albendazole. Baseline samples were collected from 132 participants between April and May 2020, prior to treatment. Follow-up samples were obtained one year later from 67 children who remained in the study.ResultsWe observed a significant change in gut microbiome diversity metrics one year following treatment, characterized by fewer observed taxa and increased community evenness (Shannon and Simpson indices, p < 0.01), indicating substantial perturbation of microbial community structure. Furthermore, we identified six significantly enriched genera post-treatment (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05) among participants who were helminth-negative at the baseline, including Blautia, Senegalimassilia, Coprococcus 1, Marvinbryantia, Clostridium sensu stricto 1, and Lachnospiraceae ND3007 group. In contrast, individuals who had at least one egg count of Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura, or hookworm before treatment exhibited enrichment in five taxa (Senegalimassilia, Lachnospiraceae ND3007 group, Lachnospiraceae FCS020 group, Collinsella, and Intestinimonas).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that baseline helminth infection status influences the microbiome changes induced by anthelmintic treatment. Further controlled animal studies are needed to clarify how anthelmintic treatment affects taxon shifts in gut microbiota.Subject terms: Microbial communities, Gastrointestinal diseases

Introduction

The use of anthelmintic products is a widely accepted public health intervention1. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends periodic mass administration of anthelmintic drugs to control soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections2, including roundworms (Ascaris lumbricoides), hookworms (Ancylostoma duodenale and Necator americanus), and whipworms (Trichuris trichiura), affecting the world’s poorest communities3. These interventions involve administering a single oral dose of albendazole (400 mg) or mebendazole (500 mg) to preschool and school-age children, either annually or biannually, in areas where at least 20% to 50% of children are infected with STHs4,5. While this intervention primarily aims to decrease the prevalence and severity of STH infections, studies looking at the long-term impacts of mass deworming yield conflicting results6–9. Awasthi et al.6 found that dewormed children experienced significant weight gain, whereas Moshi et al.7 reported decreased or no change in their weight and height. Furthermore, two comprehensive reviews and meta-analyses by Taylor-Robinson et al. and Welch et al. indicated no improvement in nutritional status, hemoglobin levels, cognition, school attendance, or overall physical well-being after deworming8,9.

The ongoing investment and advocacy for mass deworming of school children, despite the conflicting reports, has raised several concerns about this strategy. First, relying solely on mass deworming is unlikely to eliminate helminthiasis without improved access to clean water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH)10,11. Second, while this intervention decreases the prevalence and intensity of STH infections, its impact on other gut microbial communities sharing the same ecological niche has not been thoroughly evaluated. Earlier studies examined the effects of antibiotics on altering gut microbiome diversity and composition12–15. However, more recently researchers have also investigated the influence of commonly prescribed non-antibiotic medications on gut microbiome communities and signatures16,17. A study by Vich Vila et al.18 revealed that 19 out of 41 commonly prescribed drugs were associated with altered microbial features across three independent cohorts after controlling for antibiotics and other drugs that have a strong association with microbiome features. Others have reported that using proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), drugs used to inhibit stomach acid production, has been associated with either an increase in the less healthy gut microbiome or altered overall microbial diversity and composition19–21. The interaction between gut microbes and non-antibiotic drugs is intriguing, complex, and bidirectional22. The composition of the gut microbiome can be affected by drugs, and conversely, the gut microbiome can also influence how a person responds to a drug by enzymatically changing the drug’s structure and affecting its bioavailability, bioactivity, or toxicity23,24.

Anthelmintic drugs present a particularly intriguing case for investigating secondary effects of drugs on microbiome composition. Anthelmintic drugs given to children either as part of a mass deworming program or by prescription, and administered orally, pass through the upper gastrointestinal tract and proceed into the intestinal tract25. Throughout this journey, these drugs interact with the diverse microbial communities inhabiting the gut through both direct and indirect mechanisms, potentially altering intestinal microenvironments and affecting bacterial growth, composition, and function, as observed in people who have taken other non-antibiotic drugs (e.g., proton pump inhibitors)22. Importantly, both direct and indirect effects of the drug could cause these shifts in microbiome composition, with direct effects driven by the drug itself and indirect effects coming from the removal of helminthic species from the gut environment. Animal studies identify both short- and long-term alterations in the gut microbiome following anthelmintic administrations26–28, but data from humans remain much more limited. Data from one epidemiological study in Kenya have shown that anthelmintic drugs, such as albendazole, are associated with an increase in the proportion of Clostridiales and a reduction of Enterobacteriales for at least three months following treatment29. However, a placebo-controlled anthelmintic trial in Indonesia found that albendazole alone did not affect microbiota composition in the overall study population but did in helminth-infected individuals30. Therefore, further study is needed to identify whether routine anthelmintic treatment leads to long-term microbiome composition changes in the relevant populations, particularly when taking helminth infection status into account.

Here, we used longitudinal data to examine the impact of exposure to anthelmintic treatment on the gut microbiome profiles of Ethiopian school-age children. Specifically, we were interested in identifying whether routine mass administration of an anthelmintic using 400 mg albendazole resulted in long-term changes in microbiome composition in individuals with and without STH infections. We observe differences in microbiome diversity and composition one year after treatment that vary according to infection status at the time of treatment. These findings reveal an important connection between anthelmintic drug use and microbiome composition, highlighting the need to carefully consider secondary effects when targeting helminth infections.

Methods

Sample collection

A detailed study design was described previously31. Briefly, microbiome composition before and one year after anthelmintic treatment was examined in 132 participants of a larger epidemiological project32. Participants enrolled in the study are school-age children in Jimma Town, Ethiopia. At the baseline (April to May 2020), fecal samples were collected from 132 study participants prior to mass administration of anthelmintic treatment (400 mg albendazole). One year later, follow-up fecal samples were collected from a subset of 67 children who remained in the longitudinal study. Our demographic analysis indicated no evidence of selection bias (Fig. 1D).

Sample collection and DNA extraction have been described previously31. Briefly, each collected fecal sample was divided into two parts: one for STH egg count examination and one for microbial DNA extraction. Microscopic examination of helminthic eggs was performed using the Kato Katz technique at Jimma University Institute of Health Neglected Tropical Disease Laboratory within 2–4 hours after sample collection. Samples for microbial DNA extraction were transferred to Norgen Stool Nucleic Acid Collection and Preservation Tubes (Norgen Biotek Corp), delivered to Jimma University (Jimma, Ethiopia), and frozen within 2 h of collection. Frozen tubes were then transported to Colgate University (Hamilton, NY, USA) and stored at −80 °C.

Microbiome data generation

DNA extraction was performed using DNeasy PowerSoil Pro kit (Qiagen, Germany, cat no. 47014) as described previously31. Briefly, up to 250 mg of fecal sample was used per extraction. Homogenization was performed using two 30-second pulses on a Beadbeater (BioSpec, Inc., OK, USA) at 4 °C. The concentration and purity of isolated DNA were measured using a NanoDrop One (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA). Gel electrophoresis was performed on a 0.8% agarose gel with ethidium bromide staining to test the integrity of the genomic DNA. The V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was PCR amplified using 515 F (GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA) and 806 R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT) primers. Extracted DNA material was stored at −80 °C and shipped for sequencing to the MR DNA molecular laboratory (MR DNA, TX, USA). Amplicons were sequenced paired-end to a length of 250 bp using the Illumina MiSeq platform following standard Illumina protocols. Raw sequences were demultiplexed using FASTQ Splitter 64 bit v19.07.10.

Data processing

Raw sequences were processed using the DADA2 v1.30.0 pipeline33. The first 12 nucleotides of the reads were trimmed to remove low-quality bases. Then, after filtering and chimeric reads removal with default settings, sequences were clustered into Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs). Sequence annotation was performed against SILVA version 138.2 using the assignTaxonomy and addSpecies functions of DADA2 with the default settings. ASVs assigned to “Mitochondria” and kingdoms other than Bacteria and Archaea were filtered out.

Statistics and Reproducibility

To visually observe taxonomic differences between treatment subgroups, stacked barplots of the top 20 genera grouped by Family were created, using the fantaxtic R library v0.2.134. To compare bacterial alpha diversity metrics (Observed, Shannon35, Simpson36) were calculated using the microbiome v1.24.0 package37 on ASV counts rarefied to a depth of 100,000 reads/sample. Statistical differences were assessed using paired Wilcoxon tests.

Additionally, based on the rarefied dataset, we conducted a presence analysis for each ASV within groups of subjects with at least one detected STH egg and without detected STH eggs (+STH and -STH). Specifically, for each group, we filtered out ASVs present in fewer than four samples to remove individual-specific taxa or those that might be the result of technical error. For each ASV passing the filtering step, we assessed the odds of its presence in the baseline samples compared to the follow-up samples within the corresponding group of study participants. The odds of both groups were then combined, and a threshold for depletion calculated as a 95% quantile (Fig. S1). The resulting ASVs were agglomerated at the Family level, families represented by only one depleted ASV were combined in one group named ‘Others’, and the phylum represented by ASVs present only in -STH group were discarded.

To compare broad compositional differences between groups, Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was calculated by agglomerating ASVs to the genus level using vegdist from the vegan v2.6.4 package38. Differences in Bray-Curtis dissimilarity were assessed using Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) via adonis2 from the vegan v2.6.4 library38. Differences between samples of baseline and follow-up groups were visualized using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) via pcoa from the phyloseq v1.46.0 package39. To identify the genera with the most influence on the principal component axis, the covariance of each taxon with the first two principal coordinates was computed. Out of the top five genera with the largest covariances, we selected one genus with the largest covariance per quadrant to visualize via PCoA plot. The covariance computation code was adopted from the biplot.pcoa function of the ape v.5.8-1 library40.

Differential abundance analysis was performed to assess differences in microbial relative abundance between baseline and follow-up time points for three comparisons: (i) all paired samples; (ii) samples that had at least one detected STH egg at the baseline; (iii) samples that did not have any STH stages at the baseline. The genus-agglomerated counts were clr-transformed using aldex.clr function from the ALDEx2 v1.34.0 library41. ALDEx2 (paired Wilcoxon tests, aldex.ttest function) was used to account for compositionality in differential abundance analysis. To account for the presence of the different helminthic infections, we conducted a similar ALDEx2 analysis based on microbial residuals for all longitudinal pairs of samples. The microbial residuals were calculated using generalized linear models, where clr-transformed microbial abundance of each genus was modeled using the egg presence parameters for three major helminths: Ascaris, Trichuris, and hookworm. To include the residuals in the ALDEx2 model, they were transformed to integer counts by adding the lowest residual value across the dataset. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to account for multiple tests.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of Colgate University, Hamilton, NY, USA (IRB Reference number: FR-S20-03/20) and Jimma University Institute of Health, Ethiopia (IRB Reference number: IHRPGD/786/20). After informing the participants and their parents/legal guardians of the procedures and aims of this study, we obtained written or fingerprint-informed consent from the participants’ representatives.

Results

Study design

To assess long-term changes in gut microbiomes after anthelmintic treatment, we built on our previously published study of Ethiopian school children31. Our previous study described the baseline sampling of the cohort, which consisted of fecal samples collected from 132 Ethiopian school children before mass administration of anthelmintic treatment. Using these baseline samples, we identified compositional differences between individuals with and without helminthic infections, identifying microbial signatures unique to each parasitic agent. For the study reported here, we obtained follow-up stool samples from 67 participants in the original study cohort31 one year after they were administered an anthelmintic treatment (Fig. 1A). The follow-up cohort presented the same distribution of key demographic and socioeconomic parameters as in the original study: sex (Χ² =0.072, p = 0.79), age (Χ² =1.215, p = 0.54), household size (Χ² =0.573, p = 0.75), residence (Χ² =0.404, p = 0.82) and maternal education (Χ² =1.105, p = 0.89) (Fig. 1B). The mean age of participants in the follow-up group was 10.6 ± 2.5 years, and 57% of the participants (n = 38) were male. Complete information, including sampling sites, survey, and sample collection procedures, was described previously31,32. For the remainder of the study, we only consider individuals who participated at both baseline and during the 1-year follow-up (n = 67).

Briefly, each collected stool sample was divided into two portions: one for microscopic examination of helminthic eggs at Jimma University (Ethiopia) and the other to perform microbial sequencing of the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene at Colgate University (USA). A sample was considered STH-positive (+STH) for soil-transmitted helminths (STHs) infection if the parasitological examination detected at least one egg from any of the three major STHs: Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura, or hookworm; and STH-negative (-STH), otherwise. In total, 59 samples were reported as STH positive (Fig. 1C). Of these, 52 samples were collected as part of the original study (baseline), and nine samples were collected one year after the anthelmintic treatment. The initial survey of the baseline cohort also gathered data about prior anthelmintic treatment events. In total, 83 participants reported receiving a deworming tablet within one year prior to entering the study. Of these, 46 participants were included in the follow-up cohort (Fig. 1D).

Microbial diversity at baseline varies between children with and without STH infection

To assess differences in bacterial diversity between baseline and follow-up samples, we conducted pair-wise statistical testing (two-sided Wilcoxon test) of microbial richness (Observed) and evenness (Shannon and Simpson) metrics applied to the datasets, rarefied to 100,000 reads per sample. The Wilcoxon test showed a significant decrease in the observed number of taxa among follow-up samples (p < 0.006), as well as a significant increase in evenness consistent across both metrics (Shannon: p < 0.015; Simpson: p < 0.005, Fig. 2A). We then found that the increase in the community evenness was the most prominent among participants, who were +STH at the baseline (Shannon: p = 0.023, paired Wilcoxon test; Simpson: p = 0.006, paired Wilcoxon test), but not significant among the -STH participants at the baseline (Shannon: p = 0.44, paired Wilcoxon test; Simpson: p = 0.38, paired Wilcoxon test) (Fig. 2B). There were no additional significant differences when accounting for the STH status at the follow-up time point (Fig. S2).

B Microbial diversity differences one year following treatment differ depending on the subject’s egg count status prior to the treatment. For A and B p-values for the paired two-sided Wilcoxon test are marked as ** (p < 0.01), * (p < 0.05), ns (not significant).

We further analyzed the microbial differences between samples using the Bray-Curtis distance metric and Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) (Fig. 2C). We observed a significant difference in microbial community composition between samples collected at the baseline and one year after mass treatment administration (p = 0.006, PERMANOVA) (Fig. 2C). The top genera covarying with the first and second principal coordinates were Segatella and Succinivibrio, respectively. These observations suggest that the microbial community underwent compositional perturbations during the year after the anthelmintic treatment. In particular, the gut microbiomes of the participants with a helminth infection at baseline showed significant increases in diversity (Fig. 2B) one year after treatment.

ASVs from the Bacillota phylum had the highest vanishing rates after the treatment

We next investigated shifts in specific genera across timepoints. Across timepoints, the Bacillota phylum is the most diverse among the top three most abundant phyla – Bacteroidetes, Bacillota, and Pseudomonadota (Fig. 3A). Based on visual observation, Segatella, Faecalibacterium, and Succinivibrio were the most abundant genera across samples among these major phyla. Notably, baseline samples from participants with detected helminthic eggs ( + STH) appeared to have higher abundances of Segatella and Succinivibrio. Additionally, microbiomes of +STH subjects had a consistently lower abundance of Ruminibacter prior to and after the anthelmintic treatment. On the contrary, the relative abundance of Blautia (Bacillota) is consistently higher across all samples collected one year after the anthelmintic treatment administration. Moreover, upon visual inspection, we detected a consistent increase in the relative abundance of genera from less abundant phyla in follow-up samples compared to the baseline.

B ASV counts of vanishing taxa (>quantile 95%, Fig. S1) agglomerated at a family level. (-STH = no detected helminthic eggs; +STH = at least one detected egg of Ascaris, Trichuris, or hookworm; ASV amplicon sequence variants).

Our microbial diversity analysis shows a significant decrease in the observed number of ASVs in samples following the anthelmintic treatment (Observed: p < 0.05; Wilcoxon paired test). To explore which microbial families were most susceptible to depletion after the treatment, we compared the presence rates of ASVs between baseline and follow-up samples to identify vanishing ASVs. In agreement with the microbial diversity results, we observed that the overall depletion rate was lower among subjects who were+STH at the baseline than among participants who were -STH at the baseline (41 vs 67 ASVs significantly depleted; Fig. 3B). In both groups, the Bacillota phylum was the most susceptible to the treatment. The majority of all vanishing ASVs were from the Lachnospiraceae family followed by Ruminococcaceae and Prevotellaceae families (Fig. 3B). The full list of taxonomically annotated ASVs is presented in Supplementary Data 3. Therefore, we observe greater changes in vanishing ASVs in individuals without evidence of STH infection at baseline.

An increase in the relative abundances of certain microbial taxa was observed across participants during the follow-up year

We then assessed changes in taxonomic relative abundance in participants’ gut microbiomes one year after treatment. To do so, we used ALDEx241, a compositional data analysis tool that accounts for variability and compositional constraints through centered log-ratio transformation and Monte Carlo sampling. We identified 12 genera that consistently increased in relative abundance during the year of the experiment across participants (adjusted p < 0.05) (Fig. 4A, Fig. S3A, Supplementary Data 4).

To account for changes in gut microbiota associated with STH infection, we stratified by STH status at the baseline and applied a paired Wilcoxon test via ALDEx2 to identify significant relative abundances differences. Two genera, Senegalimassilia and Marvinbryantia remained significantly increased in all conditions (Fig. 4B, C, Fig. S3B, C, Supplementary Data 4). A total of six genera, including Blautia, Anaerobutyricum, Clostridium, and Fusicatenibacter, show reproducible significant increases one year after the treatment in the stratum comprised of 43 participants -STH at the baseline (Figs. 4B, S3B). Also, we observed that four genera: Senegalimassilia, Collinsella, Intestinimonas, and Marvinbryantia increased only in the follow-up samples of participants who were +STH at the baseline (Figs. 4C, S3C, Supplementary Data 4). To account for physiological differences across helminthic infection types, we conducted additional differential abundance testing using ALDEx2 on microbial residuals, controlling for different STH egg presence (Fig. S4, Supplementary Data 5). This analysis reproduced a significant increase of Senegalimassilia, Blautia, Marvinbryantia, and 10 other genera in gut microbiomes one year after the treatment. As a result, we identified a significant reproducible increase in Senegalimassilia, and Marvinbryantia relative abundances one year after anthelmintic treatment among all participants, and increases in Blautia, Anaerobutyricum, Clostridium, Fusicatenibacter, Collinsella, and Intestinimonas genera, which depended on the STH status of the participant at the baseline.

Discussion

In this study, we tracked how gut microbial communities shift after deworming in children living in Jimma, Ethiopia, showing divergent patterns based on initial infection status. We found a significant decrease in the observed number of taxa and an increase in evenness in gut microbiome composition one year after anthelmintic treatment. However, these trends were dependent on STH status at the baseline. Specifically, we observed a decrease in observed taxa in individuals without helminth infection one year following treatment, but not in individuals with a helminth infection. Conversely, we observed higher evenness in individuals with a helminth infection at baseline compared to those without. These trends highlight that anthelmintic treatment effects on the microbiome differ depending on infection status at the time of administration.

These diversity differences are largely driven by the perturbations within the Bacillota phylum. Specifically, we observe a significant loss of the Lachnospiraceae family diversity, but a reproducible increase of two genera of the family: Blautia and Marvinbryantia, both across STH status of the participant at a baseline, and after controlling for the presence of STH eggs, suggesting a potential recolonization of the available niche. Blautia and Marvinbryantia are both acetogens and producers of vitamin B1242, which utilize different energy sources43. Interestingly, as a result of metabolism, Marvinbryantia produces an excess of succinate43. At the same time, rural African populations were previously characterized by another succinate-producing genus: Treponema succinifaciens44. Thus, the increase of Marvinbryantia genus after the treatment might serve as an indicator that the administration of the anthelmintic treatment could be a contributing factor to the industrialization of the gut microbiomes of the Ethiopian population.

When we stratified our analysis based on the STH status at the baseline, four other genera, namely Senegalimassilia, Anaerobutyricum, Clostridium, and Fusicatenibacter, were significantly higher one year after the treatment among -STH subjects. However, an increase in the abundance of only two genera (Senegalimassilia, Marvinbryantia) was reproduced among +STH participants. This highlights the interplay between active helminth infection and anthelmintic treatment on microbiome composition.

Taxa in the Clostridiales order show mixed trends in individuals given anthelmintic treatment in prior studies29,45–47. A study among Ecuadorian schoolchildren showed no effect on overall microbiome composition or the abundance of specific taxa following albendazole treatments46. Easton et al.29 documented a significant increase in Clostridiales relative abundance following albendazole treatments against STHs in five rural Kenyan villages, suggesting that clearance of STHs by anthelmintic drugs altered the gut microbiome. At the same time, a study in Ghana has shown a significant increase in of Clostridia abundance only in cases of failed anthelmintic treatment48. However, we only observed a significant increase in the Clostridium relative abundance among subjects who were -STH at the baseline, which suggests that helminths’ presence might not be the only factor explaining the change of abundances of the genera from Clostridiales order after the treatment. While there have been previous reports of Bacteroides and Clostridium species ability to metabolize the anthelmintic drug levamisole49, a follow-up metabolic study might be necessary to identify the interplay between STH infections, anthelmintic treatment and Clostridiales species.

The difference in the microbiome configurations observed following anthelmintic treatment appears to depend on the baseline STH infection status. This suggests that +STH subjects at baseline might respond to the drug by producing unique parasite-induced drug metabolites, which may alter gut microbiome interactions with the treatment compared to -STH subjects50. Alternatively, the observed increase in the abundance of microbial taxa following anthelmintic treatment could be due to the ability of the microbiome to metabolize different drugs49,51,52. A previous study reported that Bacteroides and Clostridium species metabolized the anthelmintic drug levamisole49, which could partly explain the higher Clostridium sensu stricto 1 after anthelmintic treatment in this study. Furthermore, the Clostridia class has been associated with albendazole treatment failure in subjects infected with hookworms48. It is plausible that a drug metabolized by microbes could lose its potency and/or toxicity51. Additionally, drug-microbe interactions could alter the gut barrier function, potentially leading to deregulated drug translocation into the body53. While our study did not specifically examine the drug’s efficacy, the observed increase in Clostridium abundance following anthelmintic treatment warrants further investigation to determine if the increased specific taxa associated with the low efficacy of albendazole, particularly in mass deworming settings like Ethiopia, are relevant. The low efficacy of albendazole in treating soil-transmitted helminth infections54, particularly in subjects infected with Trichuris trichiura, has been documented in multiple studies54–56. The increase in the Lachnospiraceae FCS020 group, Collinsella, and Intestinimonas after anthelmintic treatment among STH-positive subjects at baseline in this study agrees with the studies in Indonesia57 and Taiwan58, which reported an increased abundance of Collinsella among helminth-infected subjects treated with anthelmintic drugs. Our analysis also revealed an increase in Intestinomonas after anthelmintic treatment, likely due to restructuring of the gut microbiome following treatment, which may favor taxa that thrive without helminths59,60. Intestinimonas is a common commensal bacterium associated with converting dietary lysine and fructoselysine into butyrate, a compound linked to heat-processed foods and potential health risk61. A study in Kenya also reported clearance of helminth infections 3 months post-treatment associated with a microbial shift29. It is plausible that the removal of helminth parasites by anthelmintics likely opens up new ecological niches and resources for certain bacterial taxa, leading to some bacteria to increase while others decline. However, further controlled animal studies are needed to clarify how anthelmintic-driven helminth removal influences specific gut microbiota taxa.

In this study, we observed a significant decrease in the number of unique taxa and an increase in evenness with Shannon and Simpson indices after treatment. This suggests that the microbial community underwent compositional perturbations, with few unique taxa but a more balanced distribution of the remaining taxa after one year of treatment. The increase in evenness and decrease in observed taxa after treatment may indicate a change in the previously dominant taxa that might be favored by helminth infection or helminth-induced immune environment in the gut62, indicating the gut microbiome has not fully recovered its original diversity. However, as these data are observational and correlative, we cannot assign a direct biological mechanism or health relevance to these shifts. Nonetheless, this provides a basis for the hypothesis that the helminth-induced immune environment plays a key role in structuring the microbial community.

Studies that evaluate microbial diversity following anthelmintic treatment showed conflicting results29,30,63,64. A study by Gandasegui et al.63 reported no difference in alpha diversity metrics (Shannon index, Chao1, Gini-Simpson, and PD parameters) among hookworm-infected subjects who were cured compared to those who were not cured using albendazole treatment. Similarly, studies in Indonesia30 and Malaysia64 reported no significant differences in microbial diversities at pre- and post-treatment, suggesting that anthelmintic treatment did not induce a shift in microbial diversity. In contrast with these findings, a study in western Kenya29 found a significant posttreatment decrease in Chao1 richness, which agrees with our analysis.

Several factors could possibly explain the observed inconsistency in the published literature regarding the impact of anthelmintic treatments on microbial diversity. First, each study area has a different helminth infection rate and dominant helminth species, although this effect was inconsistent across the study areas. Second, we cannot exclude the possibility that variation in environmental and lifestyle factors among these studies impacts reported microbial diversity. Differences in bacterial diversity and evenness are evident in the microbiome when comparing samples from Western and developing societies65–67 or rural and urban populations68. Urban residents tend to be more exposed to antimicrobial drugs than rural residents, which may select for bacteria with higher resistance68. Furthermore, variations in study design, stool collection methods, and the statistical techniques used make it challenging to compare studies across different geographical locations.

The findings of this study must, however, be interpreted considering the following limitations: First, due to the study design and ethical considerations, all participants of the baseline cohort received the albendazole treatment, resulting in the absence of the control untreated group in the follow-up cohort. Second, a selection bias may result from the smaller “follow-up” sample size resulting from the challenges inherent in capturing everyone enrolled at the baseline during our follow-up data collection, due to either the child changing school or out-migrating from the original lived address. To counteract this shortcoming, we compared the socio-demographic characteristics of the baseline and the follow-up data. We found no significant difference between the baseline and follow-up, suggesting selection biases are unlikely to be the case in our analysis. Third, the deworming status during data collection was ascertained historically from school records and the interview-led administration questionnaire, which limited our ability to rule out the possibility that children may have received similar or other forms of anthelmintic drugs from other health care providers. However, the study area is a low-income country with limited access to standard treatment, making this an unlikely source of bias. Moreover, while a period of one year allowed us to minimize the potential effects of seasonal factors, we cannot exclude the influence of the year-to-year variation and other environmental exposures, medical prescriptions during the year, etc. Lastly, a small cohort size also might have led to the reduced power of the statistical results, especially at the +STH and -STH group comparisons.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the impact of anthelmintic treatment as part of a mass-deworming program using a longitudinal design in Ethiopian schoolchildren. The microbiomes of the Ethiopian population remain largely understudied69. Considering the previous studies on gut microbiomes of the African population determined geographic factor as one of the largest predictors of microbial variability44, our study provides a valuable baseline data representing the Ethiopian population. Moreover, current studies conducted on the Ethiopian population mainly collected fecal samples from children younger than 5 years old70–72. However, the focus of this study is school-age children, another population of particular interest for studies on the effects of anthelmintic treatment on the gut microbiome, due to being one of the most vulnerable populations included in the mass deworming program5. Importantly, the collected dataset also allows exploration of changes in microbiome composition at the longitudinal time point in the same population following the treatments. Short follow-up periods (two to four weeks), often utilized in the previous studies comparing the post-anthelmintic treatment samples to the pretreatment states29,46–48, were considered to be a limitation for assessment of the anthelmintic treatment effects on microbial perturbations29,48. Thus, we believe that the design of this study helps to address current concerns in the field. In addition, our findings have public health policy implications, for example, suggesting that microbiome-associated actions be considered in the ongoing blanket deworming program. For instance, school children enrolled in the deworming program could be allowed to take prebiotic foods and probiotic supplements to maintain gut microbiome health, as such interventions have been shown to reduce antibiotic-induced dysbiosis in animal models73.

Conclusions

Here, we conducted the first longitudinal study investigating gut microbiome composition differences following anthelmintic drug administration to school children in Ethiopia. We identify significant shifts in microbiome composition and diversity one year following treatment that differ depending on infection status at treatment. These results highlight the need to identify whether there may be any long-term effects of mass deworming treatments on the microbiome, particularly for individuals not infected at the time of administration.
