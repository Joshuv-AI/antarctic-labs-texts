Abstract
Let Ω \Omega be a bounded, convex, centrally symmetric in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} with a connected C 2 , ϵ C^{2,\epsilon} ( ϵ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \epsilon\in(0,1) ) boundary. We show that, if the following overdetermined elliptic problem − Δ ​ u = α ​ u ​ in ​ Ω , u = 0 ​ on ​ ∂ Ω , ∂ u ∂ n = c ​ on ​ ∂ Ω -\Delta u=\alpha u\,\,\text{in}\,\,\Omega,\,\,u=0\,\,\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\,\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}=c\,\,\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega has a nontrivial solution corresponding to a sufficiently large eigenvalue α \alpha , then Ω \Omega is a disk, which is the partially affirmative answer to the Berenstein conjecture. Similarly, we show that, if Ω \Omega has a Lipschitz connected boundary and the following overdetermined elliptic problem − Δ ​ u = α ​ u ​ in ​ Ω , ∂ u ∂ n = 0 ​ on ​ ∂ Ω , u = c ​ on ​ ∂ Ω -\Delta u=\alpha u\,\,\text{in}\,\,\Omega,\,\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}=0\,\,\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\,\,u=c\,\,\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega has a nontrivial solution corresponding to a sufficiently large eigenvalue α \alpha , then Ω \Omega is also a disk, which is the partially affirmative answer to the Schiffer conjecture.

A domain

The problem of classifying regions based on whether they possess the Pompeiu property is known as the

This problem originated from harmonic analysis first proposed by Polish mathematician Pompeiu

A notable application of the Pompeiu problem lies in computer-aided tomography (CT), an essential medical imaging technique used to detect tumors and lesions. In CT scanning, a narrow beam of mono-energetic X-ray photons is directed through an object. By measuring the intensity of the beam before and after transmission, the average density along its path can be determined. Mathematically, this process involves reconstructing a density function

Another application arises in remote sensing, as highlighted by Willms

Several classes of domains are known to possess the Pompeiu property. These include squares

Williams conjecture.

If
\partial\Omega
is homeomorphic to the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, then
\Omega
has the Pompeiu
property if and only if it is not a ball.

The conjecture is closely related to the following Schiffer conjecture.

Schiffer conjecture.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
be a bounded regular simply-connected domain. If
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

is a nontrivial solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u+\alpha u=0&\text{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}u=0&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\\
u=\text{const}&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

where
\alpha
is a parameter and
\nu
is the unit outer normal vector to
\partial\Omega
, then
\Omega
is a ball and

is radially symmetric.

For a bounded, regular, simply-connected domain
\Omega
, the Williams conjecture is equivalent to the Schiffer conjecture. In fact, Williams
[
56

showed that the Pompeiu problem is also equivalent to the Schiffer conjecture, thereby reformulating the Pompeiu problem in terms of the Schiffer conjecture. Specifically, he established that
\Omega
fails to have the Pompeiu property if and only if equation (
1.1

Let

Theorem 1.2.

Let

1.1

The lower bound

Section 4 presents the proof of Theorem 1.2. Given the structural similarities to Theorem 1.1, we focus primarily on elucidating the essential differences.

2
Preliminary and argumentation ideas

In this section, we first present two preliminary tools: asymptotic expansion for an oscillatory integral and a Leibniz type formula. Following those, we outline our main argumentation ideas.

2.1
Asymptotic expansion for an oscillatory integral

We begin by showing an asymptotic expansion for an oscillatory integral
[
7
,
29
]

Let

be a real valued
C^{\infty}
function defined in a compact neighborhood
U
of the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{N}

has only a non-degenerate critical point at the origin in

f(x)=f(0)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}k_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{N}A_{ijk}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}+O\left(|x|^{4}\right),

direction at zero,

is symmetric in all three indices.
For convenience, we write

\begin{array}[]{c}\left|\int_{U}u(x)e^{i\lambda f(x)}\,\text{d}x-e^{i\lambda f(0)}\left(\prod\limits_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\lambda k_{j}}{2\pi i}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(u(0)+\frac{L(u)}{\lambda}\right)\right|\leq C\lambda^{-2}\sum\limits_{|\alpha|\leq 4}\sup\left|D^{\alpha}u\right|\end{array}

L(u)=-i\left[\frac{1}{2}\Box u(0)+\frac{1}{2^{2}}\frac{1}{2!}\Box^{2}(gu)(0)+\frac{1}{2^{3}}\frac{1}{2!}\frac{1}{3!}\Box^{3}\left(g^{2}u\right)(0)\right]

\Box=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{k_{i}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}.

\frac{|t|}{\log\lambda}\rightarrow 0.

2.2
A Leibniz type formula

As is well known, Leibniz’s formula is used to calculate higher-order derivatives of the product of two functions. However, this formula does not directly generalize to higher-order derivative operators. For instance, the second derivative of the product of two functions includes a term involving the product of their first derivatives. To estimate the product of two functions within oscillatory integral expansions, we employ the second-order derivative operator
\Box
. This necessitates deriving a Leibniz-type formula for
\Box
, which we establish in this work.

For two differentiable nonconstant functions
u
,
v
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we introduce the following double derivative operator

\diamondsuit(uv)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{k_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}u\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}v.

Then, we can verify that it is commutative with the operator
\Box
. In particular, for functions
u
,
v
of one variable, we have that

\displaystyle\diamondsuit^{2}(uv)=\Box u\Box v.

When
k
is even, one has that

\displaystyle\diamondsuit^{k}(uv)=\Box^{\frac{k}{2}}u\Box^{\frac{k}{2}}v.

While, for odd
k
, one has that

\displaystyle\diamondsuit^{k}(uv)=\diamondsuit\left(\Box^{\frac{k-1}{2}}u\Box^{\frac{k-1}{2}}v\right).

With this new operator in hands, we have the following Leibniz type formula.

Proposition 2.2.

For
2n
-order differentiable (nonconstant) real functions
u,v
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, one has that

\Box(uv)=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{1}C_{1}^{\alpha}\Box^{1-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+2\diamondsuit(uv),

\Box^{2}(uv)=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{2}C_{2}^{\alpha}\Box^{2-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+4\diamondsuit\sum_{\alpha=0}^{1}C_{1}^{\alpha}\Box^{1-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+4\diamondsuit^{2}(uv)

and

\displaystyle\Box^{n}(uv)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n}C_{n}^{\alpha}\Box^{n-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+2n\diamondsuit\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n-1}C_{n-1}^{\alpha}\Box^{n-1-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^{n}d_{k}^{n}\diamondsuit^{k-1}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n-k+1}C_{n-k+1}^{\alpha}\Box^{n-k+1-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+2^{n}\diamondsuit^{n}(uv),

where

d_{3}^{n}=2(n-2)(n+1)+4\,\,\text{for}\,\,n\geq 3,

d_{n}^{n}=n2^{n-1}\,\,\text{for}\,\,n\geq 4

and

d_{k}^{n}=2\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}d_{k-1}^{j}+d_{k}^{k}

for
4\leq k\leq n-1
.

Proof.
The first formula can be directly validated through definitions.
Using the first formula, we have that

\displaystyle\Box^{2}(uv)=

\displaystyle\Box\Box(uv)=\Box\left(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{1}C_{1}^{\alpha}\Box^{1-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+2\diamondsuit(uv)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=0}^{2}C_{2}^{\alpha}\Box^{2-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+4\diamondsuit(\Box uv)+4\diamondsuit(u\Box v)+4\diamondsuit^{2}(uv)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=0}^{2}C_{2}^{\alpha}\Box^{2-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+4\diamondsuit\sum_{\alpha=0}^{1}C_{1}^{\alpha}\Box^{1-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+4\diamondsuit^{2}(uv),

where we have used combination number formula
C_{n}^{m}+C_{n}^{m+1}=C_{n+1}^{m+1}
.
The desired conclusion can be verified by mathematical induction for the case of
n\geq 3
.
To facilitate the reader’s understanding, we adopt an analytical approach to derive the formula.
We assume that the coefficient of the
k
th term in the
n
th formula is
d_{k}^{n}
. Clearly, the coefficient of the first term in every formula is always
1
and
it is not difficult to check that
d_{2}^{n}=2n
.
Indeed, the coefficient of the second term is obtained
by adding twice the coefficient of the first term in the previous formula to the coefficient
of the second term.

For the convenience of identifying other coefficients, we may as well calculate a few more terms

\displaystyle\Box^{3}(uv)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=0}^{3}C_{3}^{\alpha}\Box^{3-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+6\diamondsuit\left(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{2}C_{2}^{\alpha}\Box^{2-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v\right)+

\displaystyle 12\diamondsuit^{2}\left(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{1}C_{1}^{\alpha}\Box^{1-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v\right)+8\diamondsuit^{3}(uv),

\displaystyle\Box^{4}(uv)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=0}^{4}C_{4}^{\alpha}\Box^{4-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+8\diamondsuit\left(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{3}C_{3}^{\alpha}\Box^{3-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v\right)+24\diamondsuit^{2}\left(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{2}C_{2}^{\alpha}\Box^{2-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v\right)+

\displaystyle 32\diamondsuit^{3}\left(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{1}C_{1}^{\alpha}\Box^{1-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v\right)+16\diamondsuit^{4}(uv),

\displaystyle\Box^{5}(uv)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=0}^{5}C_{5}^{\alpha}\Box^{5-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v+10\diamondsuit\left(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{4}C_{4}^{\alpha}\Box^{4-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v\right)

\displaystyle+40\diamondsuit^{2}\left(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{3}C_{3}^{\alpha}\Box^{3-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v\right)+80\diamondsuit^{3}\left(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{2}C_{2}^{\alpha}\Box^{2-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v\right)+

\displaystyle 80\diamondsuit^{4}\left(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{1}C_{1}^{\alpha}\Box^{1-\alpha}u\Box^{\alpha}v\right)+32\diamondsuit^{5}(uv).

Because the coefficient of the last term is always twice the coefficient of the last term in the previous formula, it is not difficult to see that

.

We now derive

,

This rule also holds for the general

\displaystyle 2\left(2d_{n-2}^{n-2}+2^{n-2}\right)+2^{n-1}=\cdots

\displaystyle 2^{n-3}d_{3}^{n-k+3}+(n-3)2^{n-1}.

Using formula

By analyzing the formula for

\displaystyle d_{4}^{5}=2d_{3}^{4}+d_{4}^{4}.

This rule also holds for the general

Similarly, we have that

\displaystyle d_{k}^{n}=2\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}d_{k-1}^{j}+d_{k}^{k}.

for

.
∎

2.3
Argumentation ideas

We first recall the following definition of constant breadth which is given by Berenstein and Yang
[
7
]
and then state our main argumentation ideas.

Definition 2.1.
For each

Main idea:

We first use the stationary phase method to derive a key geometric property of

[

, which is based on a finite expansion of an oscillatory integral. A central difference, however, lies in the fact that we only require the existence of one sufficiently large eigenvalue¡ªnot a sequence. This simplification arises from our use of a contradiction argument in establishing the equality of width, in contrast to a limiting procedure. More precisely, we carry out the finite expansion, derive the corresponding estimates, and then use these estimates to reach a contradiction. This constitutes a key distinction between our proof and that in
[

By combining the constant width of

Proof of Theorem 1.1.
