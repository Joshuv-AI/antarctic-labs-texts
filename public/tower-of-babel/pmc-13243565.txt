Aqueous and methanol extracts of Paullinia pinnata improve MSUc uptake in macrophages and in vitro antioxidant effects
Authors: Pius Pum Tseuguem, Télesphore Benoît Nguelefack, Elvine Pami Nguelefack-Mbuyo, Ines Diane Tchio Kenne, Judith Manialeu Pouadjeu, Basile Nganmegne Piegang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracts of Paullinia pinnata have demonstrated therapeutic effects in monosodium urate (MSU)-induced gout in rats, notably by reducing inflammation and hyperalgesia, as well as enhancing antioxidant activity in vivo. However, their in vitro antioxidant potential and their effects on MSU-stimulated macrophages remain insufficiently explored. This study aims to investigate the in vitro antioxidant properties of P. pinnata, with a particular focus on MSU uptake in stimulated macrophages. The in vitro antioxidant activities of aqueous (AEPP) and methanolic (MEPP) extracts of P. pinnata leaves were evaluated using a range of assays, including DPPH radical scavenging, hydroxyl radical scavenging, nitric oxide inhibition, reducing power, hydrogen peroxide-induced hemolysis of rat red blood cells, hydrogen peroxide-induced brain lipid peroxidation, as well as the determination of total flavonoid and polyphenol content. Peritoneal macrophages isolated from rats were pretreated with AEPP and MEPP (10, 30, and 100 µg/mL) for 1 h, followed by stimulation with MSU (10 mg/dL) for 24 h. Subsequently, cell viability, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release, nitric oxide (NO) production, MSU uptake, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, and reduced glutathione (GSH) levels were measured in the culture medium. AEPP and MEPP significantly inhibited DPPH radicals, with IC50 values of 59.20 and 13.91 µg/mL, respectively, and also scavenged hydroxyl radicals (IC50: 1.45 and 5.39 µg/mL) and nitric oxide (IC50: 139.60 and 138.20 µg/mL). Both extracts exhibited weak reducing power. Hydrogen peroxide-induced red blood cell hemolysis was inhibited, with IC50 values of 12.29 µg/mL for AEPP and 30.00 µg/mL for MEPP, while H2O2-induced brain lipid peroxidation was also reduced (IC50: 21.82 µg/mL for AEPP and 18.22 µg/mL for MEPP). MEPP contained higher concentrations of flavonoids and polyphenols than AEPP. In peritoneal macrophages, both AEPP and MEPP increased cell viability (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01), inhibited LDH release (P < 0.001), enhanced MSU uptake (P < 0.001), suppressed nitric oxide production (P < 0.001), and elevated SOD activity (P < 0.05 to P < 0.01), but had no significant effect on GSH levels. AEPP and MEPP exhibit in vitro antioxidant activity and enhance MSU uptake by macrophages, suggesting their potential use as adjuvant agents in the management of gout.Keywords: MSU uptake, Antioxidant, Paullinia pinnata, MacrophagesSubject terms: Biochemistry, Drug discovery, Medical research, Plant sciences

Introduction

Gout is a metabolic disorder caused by impaired purine metabolism, primarily affecting the joints. Its incidence ranges from 0.58 to 2.89 per 1000 person-years and is higher in men than in women1. The disease arises from elevated serum urate levels, which lead to hyperuricemia and the subsequent deposition of monosodium urate crystals (MSUc) in and around the joints1,2. Monosodium urate, the sodium salt of uric acid, is the predominant crystalline form of urate under physiological conditions. When serum uric acid concentrations exceed its solubility threshold, nucleation occurs, resulting in crystal deposition within tissues.

MSUc deposition in joints and surrounding tissues activates resident macrophages, initiating a robust inflammatory response3–5. Activated macrophages opsonize MSUc and undergo an oxidative burst, producing large amounts of reactive species, including nitric oxide (NO). As innate immune cells derived from circulating monocytes, macrophages play a central role in host defense, tissue homeostasis, and inflammation. They are highly specialized for phagocytosis, enabling them to recognize, internalize, and degrade pathogens, apoptotic cells, and particulate matter.

The progression of gout and the pathogenic effects of urate lead to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in oxidative stress in gouty arthritis. Oxidative stress has been shown to play a critical role in the pathogenesis of the disease and is considered a key factor contributing to gout-related complications6–8. It can induce irreversible cellular damage7,9 and exacerbate inflammation by promoting the release of pro-inflammatory mediators.

Inflammation in gout is triggered when monosodium urate crystals (MSUc) are phagocytosed by macrophages or other joint-resident cells. Maintaining serum uric acid levels below 6.0 mg/dL is associated with a reduced frequency of gout flares10. Accordingly, clinical management focuses on lowering circulating uric acid and minimizing flare-ups. Prophylactic agents, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, colchicine, lesinurad, and corticosteroids, are commonly employed to control disease activity11; however, their use may be limited by intolerance and adverse side effects12.

Paullinia pinnata is a medicinal plant from which crude AEPP and MEPP are obtained. Both AEPP and MEPP have been shown to exhibit analgesic effects in MSUc-induced gout in rats, as well as antioxidant activity in vivo13; however, the mechanisms underlying these effects remain unclear. These extracts have also demonstrated in vivo anti-inflammatory activity in rat models of gout and in vitro anti-inflammatory effects in lipopolysaccharide-stimulated macrophages14. Previous studies have identified several bioactive compounds in P. pinnata extracts, including alkaloids, phenolics, flavonoids, tannins, and terpenoids15,16, which are known for their antioxidant properties. Despite these findings, the effects of AEPP and MEPP on MSUc-stimulated macrophages, particularly with regard to in vitro antioxidant activity, remain unknown. Oral administration of AEPP and MEPP has been reported to reduce MSUc-induced inflammation and oxidative stress, suggesting that these extracts may also exert direct cellular antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects and may influence MSUc uptake by macrophages. Emerging evidence indicates that AEPP and MEPP hold significant therapeutic potential for the treatment of inflammatory conditions such as gouty arthritis. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the in vitro antioxidant effects of AEPP and MEPP, as well as their impact on MSUc uptake by macrophages and the associated intracellular antioxidant response.

Materials and methods

Plant material and extract preparation

Leaves of P. pinnata were collected in the Koung-khi Department (West Region, Cameroon) and shade-dried. The plant material was authenticated at the National Herbarium in Yaoundé by M. Tadjouteu Fulbert through comparison with a voucher specimen deposited under reference number 10701/SRF.Cam.

The dried leaves of P. pinnata were ground into a fine powder. The AEPP was prepared by decoction of 500 g of the powder in 3.5 L of demineralized water for 15 min. The resulting mixture was filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The residual plant material was re-extracted in 2 L of distilled water for an additional 15 min and filtered using the same procedure. The combined filtrates were subsequently evaporated in a ventilated oven at 40 °C for 72 h, yielding 89.54 g of AEPP (17.91%).

Five hundred grams (500 g) of P. pinnata leaf powder were macerated in 3.5 L of methanol for 72 h to obtain the MEPP. The mixture was filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The residual plant material was subsequently re-extracted in 2 L of methanol for 24 h and filtered using the same procedure. The combined filtrates were concentrated to yield 111.04 g of MEPP (22.21%).

AEPP and MEPP were dissolved in 4% DMSO and filtered through a 0.2 μm membrane. A 1 mL aliquot of each filtered solution was completely evaporated in an oven, and the resulting residue was weighed to determine the extract concentration. The extracts were then diluted in culture medium (RPMI) prior to use in the experiments.

Orthophosphoric acid and Tris were purchased from BDH Chemicals Ltd. (Poole, England), while sulfuric acid was obtained from RPE Reagent (Carlo Erba). Sodium carbonate was purchased from Central Drug House (India).

Biuret reagent, Griess reagent, and carbonate buffer were prepared using reagents obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Sodium bicarbonate and sodium carbonate were also obtained from Merck KGaA (Germany), and ferric chloride (FeCl3) was purchased from Fisher Scientific (France). Animals

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Animal Biology, Faculty of Science (approval no. 025/13/304FSa), and by the Directorate of In Vivo Animal Research. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the European Community guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals (Directive 86/609/EEC) and complied with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. Albino Wistar rats (150–200 g) were bred in the animal facility of the Department of Animal Biology, Faculty of Science, University of Dschang. The animals were housed in plastic cages under standard laboratory conditions, with free access to food and water. Environmental conditions were maintained at a temperature of 24 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 50–60%, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. A total of five (n = 5) rats were used in this study.

Macrophage isolation and culture

Peritoneal macrophages were isolated according to the method described by Tseuguem et al.14. Briefly, rats (n = 3) were euthanized by cervical dislocation following intraperitoneal administration of ketamine (10 mg/100 g body weight). This procedure ensures rapid loss of consciousness due to immediate disruption of the spinal cord and cessation of cerebral perfusion. Macrophages were harvested from the peritoneal cavity by lavage with 40 mL of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (PBS-EDTA; pH 7.2).

The cell suspension was centrifuged at 20 °C for 10 min at 1500 rpm and washed once with the same buffer. The collected macrophages were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 96-well plates, with five replicates per treatment. Cells were then incubated for 3 h in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (RPMI-1640) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. During this period, peritoneal macrophages adhered to the plastic surface, while non-adherent cells and debris were removed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Following incubation, cells were treated with P. pinnata extracts (AEPP/MEPP) at concentrations of 10, 30, and 100 µg/mL, corresponding to final DMSO concentrations of 0.007%, 0.02%, and 0.06%, respectively. Allopurinol (100 µg/mL) was used as a reference compound. After 3 h of treatment, cells were stimulated with monosodium urate crystals (MSUc) at a concentration of 10 mg/dL (pH 7.0–7.4), prepared in RPMI-1640 and sterilized by filtration through a 0.2 µm membrane17.

The selected concentrations of the plant extracts were determined based on preliminary dose–response experiments, cytotoxicity assays, and previously published studies14. Initial screening identified a concentration range that elicited measurable biological effects without inducing cytotoxicity, which was further confirmed by viability assays. The corresponding DMSO concentrations were kept low and consistent across treatments to minimize solvent-related effects. This approach ensured the use of biologically relevant, non-cytotoxic concentrations, thereby supporting the validity and reproducibility of the experimental outcomes.

Cells were incubated with the test materials for 24 h in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The final volume in each well was adjusted to 300 µL with RPMI-1640. The experiment was conducted using two 96-well plates.

Twenty-four hours after incubation, the culture medium from each well of the first plate was collected for the assessment of uric acid, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and nitric oxide (NO). Attached cells were washed with PBS and subjected to the MTT assay to evaluate cell viability. In the second plate, treated identically to the first, the medium was discarded, and cells were lysed by adding 100 µL of lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4). The resulting lysates were used to measure the enzymatic activities of reduced glutathione (GSH) and superoxide dismutase (SOD). Attached cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with MTT to assess viability. Nitric oxide levels were determined as described previously18.

Nitric oxide assay

Nitric oxide (NO) levels in tissue homogenates were determined using the Griess reagent according to the method of Giustarini et al.22. In this assay, nitrite ions react with sulfanilamide and N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine (NED) to form a chromophore, which is measured spectrophotometrically at 540 nm.

For nitric oxide (NO) measurement, 350 µL of the homogenate (sample) was mixed with 350 µL of 1% sulfanilamide prepared in 1% orthophosphoric acid. The mixture was incubated in the dark for 5 min. Subsequently, 350 µL of 1% N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine (NED) prepared in Tris-hydroxymethylamine was added, and the reaction was incubated for an additional 5 min protected from light. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a Helios Epsilon spectrophotometer within 30 min after incubation. Nitric oxide concentration was determined from a sodium nitrite standard curve.

Uric acid assay

The uric acid assay was performed in two steps: the preparation of a standard curve and the measurement of uric acid in the cell culture medium.

The standard curve was constructed following the method of Wells23. Briefly, 200 µL of monosodium urate crystal (MSUc) solutions at concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 160 µg/mL were mixed with 200 µL of 2 M glycine buffer and 100 µL of 2% phosphotungstic acid reagent. The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 15 min, and the absorbance was measured at 710 nm using a spectrophotometer to generate the standard curve.

For the determination of uric acid in culture medium, 200 µL of medium was mixed with 200 µL of 2 M glycine buffer and 100 µL of 2% phosphotungstic acid. After 15 min of incubation at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 710 nm. Uric acid concentrations in the culture medium were calculated using the standard curve and expressed as µg/mL per cell equivalent (µg/mL/cell Eq).

In vitro antioxidant effects of P. pinnata extracts

Free radical scavenging activity on DPPH

The DPPH free radical scavenging activity of P. pinnata extracts was assessed following the method of Nguelefack-Mbuyo et al.24. Briefly, 150 µL of AEPP, MEPP, or ascorbic acid (1–300 µg/mL) was added to 850 µL of ethanol, and the optical density (D1) was measured at 517 nm against a blank (ethanol). Subsequently, 500 µL of DPPH solution (0.063 mg/mL in ethanol) was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark for 20 min. Hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging assay

The hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging activity of P. pinnata extracts was evaluated using the ascorbic acid-iron-EDTA system, which generates hydroxyl radicals in an entirely aqueous medium. The assay was performed following the method of Kunchandy and Rao25.

The reaction mixture (1 mL) consisted of 100 µL of 28 mM 2-deoxy-d-ribose in 20 mM KH2PO4–KOH buffer (pH 7.4), 500 µL of AEPP, MEPP, or gallic acid at various concentrations (1–300 µg/mL), 100 µL of 1.04 mM EDTA, 100 µL of 100 µM FeCl3, 100 µL of 1 mM H2O2, and 100 µL of 1 mM ascorbic acid. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of the reaction mixture was added to 500 µL of 1% orthophosphoric acid and 500 µL of 1% thiobarbituric acid, followed by incubation at 100 °C for 20 min. After cooling, absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a Helios Epsilon spectrophotometer against a blank. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Reducing power assay

The reducing power of P. pinnata extracts was evaluated using a modified method of Athukorala et al.26. Briefly, 0.2 mL of AEPP, MEPP, or ascorbic acid (1–300 µg/mL) was mixed with 0.5 mL of 200 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.6) and 0.5 mL of 30 mM potassium ferricyanide, followed by incubation at 50 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of the reaction mixture was combined with 0.5 mL of 600 mM trichloroacetic acid, incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min.

The supernatant (0.5 mL) was then mixed with 0.5 mL of distilled water and 0.1 mL of 6 mM FeCl3, and the absorbance was measured at 700 nm using a Helios Epsilon spectrophotometer. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Higher absorbance values indicate stronger reducing power. Since the extracts exhibited intrinsic color at higher concentrations, absorbance corrections were made by subtracting the blank absorbance of each extract at the corresponding dilution from the measured values.

Total polyphenol quantification

The total polyphenol content of P. pinnata extracts was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method as described by Mohamed and Al-Okbi27. Briefly, 0.5 mL of AEPP or MEPP was mixed with 0.5 mL of 0.0484 N Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of 9% sodium carbonate was added, and the mixture was further incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a spectrophotometer. Total polyphenol content was expressed as quercetin equivalents (µg quercetin equivalent/mg of extract), with quercetin used as the standard. All measurements were performed in quintuplicate.

Total flavonoids quantification

The total flavonoid content of P. pinnata extracts was determined using the method of Zhishen et al.28. Briefly, 500 µL of AEPP or MEPP (100 µg/mL) or quercetin standard solutions (0, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 µg/mL) were added to test tubes. To each tube, 1500 µL of deionized water and 150 µL of 0.5 M NaNO2 were added, followed by incubation at room temperature for 5 min. Subsequently, 150 µL of 10% AlCl3 was added and incubated for 6 min at ambient temperature. Finally, 500 µL of 4% NaOH was added, and the mixture was gently mixed. The optical density was measured at 510 nm. Flavonoid content was determined from the quercetin standard curve and expressed as µg quercetin equivalent per mg of extract. All measurements were performed in quintuplicate.

Lipid peroxidation assay on rat’s brain

Two rats were sacrificed by decapitation under ether anesthesia applied to a cotton ball in a Plexiglas jar. The brains were carefully removed and homogenized (10% w/v) in cold 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4 °C. The filtered homogenate was used as a source of polyunsaturated fatty acids to assess lipid peroxidation.

For the assay, 0.5 mL of brain homogenate was mixed with 0.5 mL of AEPP, MEPP, or gallic acid (used as a reference) at concentrations of 1–300 µg/mL. Lipid peroxidation was initiated by adding 0.5 mL of 25 mM H2O2, and the mixture was incubated for 60 min. Subsequently, 100 µL of the reaction mixture was added to 1 mL of TBARS reagent (1% w/v thiobarbituric acid dissolved in 1% trichloroacetic acid and 1% orthophosphoric acid, slightly heated to ensure complete dispersion). The mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 20 min, cooled, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min29. The resulting pink chromogen was measured at 532 nm using a Helios Epsilon spectrophotometer. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Anti-hemolytic activity on rat’s red blood cells

The effect of P. pinnata extracts on H2O2-induced hemolysis was assessed following the method of Zhang et al.30. Briefly, a healthy rat was anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of thiopental (5 mg/100 g body weight), and heparinized blood was collected from the abdominal aorta. The rat subsequently succumbed due to anemia. Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated by centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 10 min, washed three times with PBS (125 mM NaCl, 10 mM NaH2PO4, pH 7.4), and suspended at 4.8% hematocrit in PBS.

For the assay, 500 µL of RBC suspension was incubated with or without AEPP, MEPP, or gallic acid (1–300 µg/mL) for 15 min at 37 °C. Effects of P. pinnata extracts on Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) released by MSUc-stimulated macrophages

Stimulation of macrophages with MSU crystals (MSUc) induced cell death, as indicated by increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity in the culture medium. Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the mechanisms by which P. pinnata extracts promote gout crystal clearance, as well as their in vitro antioxidant activities. The effects of AEPP and MEPP were evaluated in macrophages exposed to MSU crystals (MSUc) to mimic gouty conditions. Treatment with AEPP and MEPP protected cultured macrophages from MSUc-induced stimulation, which typically leads to cell death. This protective effect was evidenced by a reduction in cell death and decreased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) leakage into the culture medium. Additionally, P. pinnata extracts significantly reduced nitric oxide (NO) production (P = 0.00003), enhanced uric acid uptake (P = 0.00003), and increased SOD activity (MEPP), while having no significant effect on glutathione (GSH) content in treated cells.

When macrophages encounter MSUc, they attempt to opsonize the crystals and undergo an oxidative burst to eliminate them. At the same time, the crystals can interact directly with membrane lipids and proteins, disrupting the cellular membrane through cross-linking of glycoproteins7,31. The present results indicate that P. pinnata extracts enhance phagocytosis of MSUc crystals while preventing both oxidative burst and cell death. The effects observed with AEPP and MEPP are comparable to those reported for chondroitin sulfate15,32. Furthermore, inhibition of oxidative burst suggests that P. pinnata extracts may exert intracellular antioxidant activity, potentially through enhancement of SOD activity, supporting previously reported in vivo findings13.

The enhanced opsonizing activity observed with MEPP may be mechanistically linked to its high flavonoid and polyphenol content. Polyphenols, particularly those containing pyrogallol, are known to modulate innate immune responses by activating macrophages and neutrophils through redox-sensitive signaling pathways. These compounds may enhance phagocytosis by modulating intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) at sub-toxic concentrations, where ROS act as secondary signaling molecules that facilitate cytoskeletal rearrangement and promote phagosome formation. Future studies involving comprehensive physicochemical and biological characterization of the MSUc compound used in this study will be necessary to more precisely elucidate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced phagocytic activity observed following MEPP treatment.

Several phytochemicals have been reported to enhance phagocytosis through caspase-mediated regulation, particularly via non-apoptotic activation of caspase-3 and caspase-8, contributing to actin remodeling and increased phagocytic cup formation. The flavonoids enriched in MEPP may engage similar mechanisms, promoting phagocytic efficiency without inducing cell death33. In this context, the observed increase in SOD activity, together with stable GSH levels, supports a redox-balanced environment that favors signaling rather than oxidative damage, a condition known to enhance phagocytic competence.

Surprisingly, AEPP and MEPP did not increase GSH content in macrophages. This finding contrasts with our previous results, which showed that these extracts elevated GSH levels in the spinal cord of arthritic animals14. An increase in SOD activity reflects a robust cellular response to excessive free radical production. Reduced glutathione (GSH) directly neutralizes free radicals; therefore, maintaining stable GSH levels is essential for preserving cellular integrity and overall antioxidant function. AEPP and MEPP also prevented cell death induced by exposure to MSUc, suggesting that P. pinnata extracts protect the cellular membrane, either by inhibiting lipid peroxidation or preventing glycoprotein denaturation.

Alternatively, P. pinnata extracts may act upstream by modulating the primary sources of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, thereby reducing the overall oxidative burden. In this context, enhanced SOD activity may serve a protective or priming role, while the absence of excessive peroxide accumulation removes the need for increased GSH synthesis or recycling. This interpretation is consistent with an in vitro environment in which oxidative challenges are controlled and may not reach a threshold sufficient to trigger maximal activation of the glutathione system.

Given that P. pinnata possesses intracellular antioxidant effects, we aimed to evaluate its in vitro antioxidant activity. Both the aqueous (AEPP) and methanol (MEPP) extracts of P. pinnata exhibited concentration-dependent anti-radical activity against the DPPH radical, with MEPP showing the highest activity, exceeding that of vitamin C. The antioxidant activity of a substance on DPPH is attributed to its ability to donate a hydrogen ion, thereby stabilizing the radical34,35. The ability of AEPP and MEPP to stabilize DPPH suggests that these extracts function as hydrogen ion donors.

The hydroxyl radical is a highly reactive oxygen species that contributes to lipid peroxidation and widespread biological damage. As the most unstable ROS, it can induce oxidative damage to DNA, lipids, and proteins36,37 and is strongly implicated in the pathophysiology of diseases such as arthritis38. In this study, MEPP exhibited strong hydroxyl radical scavenging activity, with an efficiency index of 16.99, exceeding that of gallic acid (15.53), whereas AEPP was less active. These results suggest that AEPP contains few or no compounds capable of effectively neutralizing hydroxyl radicals and indicate that the antioxidant effects of AEPP and MEPP against different free radicals are likely mediated by distinct chemical constituents.

Nitric oxide (NO) is a crucial free radical produced by endothelial cells, macrophages, and neurons, playing a key role in the regulation of numerous physiological processes. However, excessive NO contributes to various pathological conditions, including cell damage, neuronal death, DNA fragmentation, and diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and rheumatoid arthritis38. In this study, the methanol extract of P. pinnata (MEPP) exhibited strong in vitro inhibition of NO production, whereas the aqueous extract (AEPP) showed minimal activity. The potent inhibitory effect of MEPP is likely due to its high content of phenolic compounds and flavonoids, which are well-known free radical scavengers39,40. Specifically, MEPP contained approximately 2.3 times more phenolic compounds and twice the flavonoid content compared with AEPP, explaining the observed differences in activity. These findings indicate that MEPP can effectively inhibit NO formation in vitro and may hold considerable potential for preventing the deleterious effects of NO in vivo13,14,41.

Given that these extracts exhibited anti-radical activity against DPPH, hydroxyl, and nitric oxide radicals, we further evaluated their reducing power. The results demonstrated that both AEPP and MEPP have low reducing power. These findings suggest that a molecule’s radical-scavenging activity against DPPH does not necessarily correlate with its reducing capacity.

To evaluate the effects of P. pinnata extracts on living systems, their activity was assessed in models of hydrogen peroxide-induced erythrocyte hemolysis and polyunsaturated lipid peroxidation in rat brain. The results demonstrated that both AEPP and MEPP exert anti-hemolytic effects, likely due to their ability to scavenge free radicals responsible for hemolysis. Free radicals induce hemolysis by causing oxidative damage through peroxidation of lipids and proteins in the erythrocyte membrane42. Specifically, in the presence of hydrogen peroxide, the Fenton reaction generates hydroxyl radicals (·OH), which, under aerobic conditions and physiological temperature, initiate lipid peroxidation of red blood cell membranes, ultimately leading to cell lysis.

Based on this mechanism, the effects of AEPP and MEPP likely result either from their ability to inhibit hydroxyl radical formation during the Fenton reaction or from the inhibition of lipid and membrane protein peroxidation. This aligns with the observation that P. pinnata enhances MSUc uptake by macrophages in vitro. While the first hypothesis is plausible for MEPP, it is unlikely for AEPP, which exhibited very weak inhibitory activity against hydroxyl radical formation. Therefore, it can be inferred that both extracts primarily act by inhibiting lipid peroxidation, supporting the second hypothesis. To test this, AEPP and MEPP were evaluated for their effects on the peroxidation of unsaturated lipids in rat brain. The results demonstrated that P. pinnata extracts strongly inhibit lipid peroxidation, suggesting that their anti-hemolytic effects are mainly mediated through this mechanism.

These findings are consistent with previous reports on P. pinnata extracts by Jimoh et al., Karim et al., and Onah et al.15,16,43, with the exception of the low reducing power observed in the present study. Further structural characterization of the active compounds would provide deeper mechanistic insight into redox modulation and antioxidant enzyme regulation; microscopic visualization of particles within the phagolysosome, increased lysosomal enzyme activity, and the requirement of specific receptors for internalization will serves as proof for MSUc uptake. Moreover, identification of key bioactive markers would facilitate extract standardization, improve reproducibility across studies, and support the development of MEPP as a well-defined phytochemical intervention.

Conclusion

Paullinia pinnata extracts reduce macrophage mortality induced by MSU, decrease nitric oxide production, and enhance cellular antioxidant activity in vitro. The reduction in MSUc-induced macrophage death may be attributed to the antioxidant activity of P. pinnata extracts at the cellular level, with MEPP showing greater efficacy in enhancing SOD activity and inhibiting lipid peroxidation, and AEPP demonstrating effects on cellular hemolysis. The observed effects of both AEPP and MEPP on MSUc uptake, coupled with their in vitro antioxidant activities, suggest that P. pinnata could serve as a potential adjuvant in the treatment of gout and as a source of antioxidants against oxidative stress. Collectively, these results highlight that qualitative and quantitative differences in phytochemical composition contribute to significant functional divergence between P. pinnata extracts. Further characterization of the MSU crystals used, including their crystalline form (monohydrate) and size distribution, will help clarify their influence on anti-inflammatory responses and the proof of MSUc uptake efficacy.
