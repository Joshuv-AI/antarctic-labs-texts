Pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP) promotes regeneration of corneal epithelial cells possibly via the NR4A1 signaling pathway
Authors: Michio Yamashita, Tomoya Nakamachi, Junko Shibato, Hitoshi Hashimoto, Dora Reglodi, Ichiro Takasaki, Randeep Rakwal, Seiji Shioda, Fumiko Takenoya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP) is a neuropeptide that promotes tear secretion from the lacrimal gland. PACAP is known to be involved in corneal injury repair, but as the mechanism underlying its effects remains unclear, we used a mouse corneal wound-injury model and cultured human corneal epithelial cells to investigate further. PAC1 receptor (PAC1-R) mRNA and associated immunoreactivity were detected in the mouse corneal epithelium. In corneal wound-injury model mice, PACAP eye drops significantly reduced the injured area 12 h after the wound insult compared to control, and this effect was abolished by co-treatment with a PACAP receptor antagonist. Corneal healing was delayed in the heterozygous PACAP knockout and PAC1-R knockout mice. Despite attenuation of corneal healing following the surgical removal of the lacrimal gland in wild-type (WT) mice, PACAP eye drops significantly improved corneal repair. In vitro, PACAP treatment significantly reduced the injury area in cultured human corneal epithelial cells, and this effect was abolished by Ara-C, a cell proliferation inhibitor. Results of DNA whole-genome microarray analysis suggested that the nuclear receptor NR4A1 is crucial for corneal epithelial proliferation. THPN, which induces the nuclear export of NR4A1, suppressed the PACAP-induced proliferation of human corneal epithelial cells and inhibited corneal repair in mice. These data suggest that PACAP stimulates corneal repair by promoting corneal epithelial proliferation via PAC1-R and NR4A1 transcriptional activity. These findings suggest that PACAP could be a promising candidate for eye-drop medication to treat corneal injuries, including dry eye syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54554-5.Subject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Medical research, Molecular biology

Introduction

The cornea is the outermost of the three optical layers that are essential for clarity of vision. While the integrity of the cornea is essential for maintaining normal eyesight, as the outermost layer it is exposed to environmental insults that may be biological, chemical, physical, or mechanical in nature. Indeed, corneal diseases, such as trachoma, onchocerciasis, corneal ulceration, corneal dystrophies, and xerophthalmia, are major causes of blindness worldwide1. The cornea consists of a thin stratified squamous non-keratinizing epithelium that exhibits a rapid wound healing response, which is crucial for the maintenance of vision. When the corneal epithelium is injured, keratinocytes undergo apoptosis and new cells proliferate from the border of the wound2. A proper balance between apoptotic and proliferation-stimulating pathways is critical for normal wound healing3,4. Several growth factors, transcription factors, and cytokines have been identified to contribute to this process2,5–7. However, impaired corneal wound healing and/or excessive apoptosis are observed in numerous pathological conditions, including diabetes, contact lens-induced injuries, and complications of refractive surgery3,8,9. Pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP), which is widely distributed in the central nervous system and peripheral tissues of mammals10, has pleiotropic functions in the central nervous system, including neuroprotection11,12, neurodevelopment13,14, glial regulation15,16, feeding regulation17,18, and stress responses19,20. PACAP binds to three types of G protein-coupled receptors: PAC1-R, VPAC1-R, and VPAC2-R, with the highest affinity shown for PAC1-R21. We discovered that PACAP promotes tear secretion from the lacrimal glands and reported that homozygous PACAP knockout mice spontaneously develop corneal damage, which can be suppressed by the continuous administration of PACAP eye drops22. Furthermore, PACAP is present in human tears fluid23, and PAC1-R immunoreactivity has been observed in the human cornea23, suggesting that PACAP may directly act on the cornea. Recent studies have shown that the addition of PACAP promotes corneal wound healing both in vitro and in vivo24,25. However, it not only remains unclear whether endogenous PACAP is also involved in corneal healing, but the mechanisms by which exogenously applied PACAP promotes corneal healing are still unknown. The aims of the present study were thus to investigate the possible accelerating effects of PACAP on corneal epithelial wound healing and to further elucidate the underlying signaling pathway(s) by which this may occur.

Results

PACAP and PAC1-r expression in the corneal epithelium and their effect on corneal wound healing

PACAP mRNA was not detected in the whole cornea or corneal epithelium of the mouse (Fig. 1A). PAC1-R and VPAC1-R, but not VPAC2-R, mRNA signals were detected in mouse whole cornea and corneal epithelium (Fig. 1A). Immunostaining experiments showed that PAC1-R immunoreactivity was present on the basal side of corneal epithelial layer in the mouse (Fig. 1B). In human corneal tissue, PAC1-R immunoreactivity was also detected in the epithelial layer23. No PAC1-R immunoreactivity was identified in sections from the antigen absorption test (Fig. 1B).

In the mouse corneal wound-injury model, the injury area visible by fluorescein staining gradually decreased at the 12 and 24 h timepoints after injury in all groups (Fig. 1C). Compared with the saline-treated group, the injury area was significantly reduced in the PACAP 10–11 M- and 10− 9 M-treated groups at 12 h and in all PACAP-treated groups at 24 h after injury (Fig. 1D). Histopathological observations showed that when only the epithelium was removed, there seemed to be less damage to the stroma area after injury, and that PACAP treatment appeared to stimulate recovery of the epithelium (Fig. 1E). In the case of rats, PACAP eye drops also stimulated corneal repair26.

To clarify which type of PACAP receptors are active in the PACAP-induced corneal healing effect, animals were pretreated with the PACAP receptor antagonists PACAP6-38 and VIP6-28. Pretreatment with PACAP6-38, a PAC1-R and VPAC2-R antagonist, significantly suppressed the PACAP-induced corneal healing at the 12 hours’ time point after injury, but not at the 24 hours’ time point (Fig. 1F). On the other hand, pretreatment with VIP6-28, a VPAC1-R and VPAC2-R antagonist, following PACAP treatment did not significantly change the PACAP-induced corneal healing (Fig. 1G). These results suggest that PACAP stimulates corneal healing via PAC1-R.

PACAP KO and PAC1-R KO mice and lacrimal gland removal study

To reveal the effect of endogenous PACAP on corneal healing, corneal injury was induced in heterozygous PACAP KO mice and in WT mice, and the extent of healing was compared. In heterozygous PACAP KO mice, the damaged corneal area showed no significant difference compared to WT mice at the 12-hour time point after injury. However, at 24 hours’ post-injury, significant improvement was observed only in WT mice (Fig. 2A, B). This data implies that endogenous PACAP stimulates corneal healing in mice. To elucidate whether the corneal epithelial healing effect of PACAP is mediated through PAC1-R, corneal injury was induced in PAC1-R knockout mice, and the injury area was evaluated. The area of corneal injury in PAC1-R KO mice was significantly greater than that in WT mice at 12 and 24 h after wounding (Fig. 2C, D). These results further support the notion that the corneal wound healing effect of PACAP is mediated by PAC1-R.

We previously reported that PACAP eye drops act as a stimulator of tear secretion from the lacrimal gland (22). As such, the corneal healing effect of PACAP may be mediated by an increase in tear volume. To confirm the corneal healing pathway of PACAP, corneal injury was induced in WT mice in which the lacrimal gland had been surgically removed. These mice showed a nearly 90% reduction in tear secretion compared to the pre-surgery condition, and we found that PACAP eye drops did not stimulate tear secretion in these animals (Supplementary Fig. 1). Further to this, the corneal injury area was significantly greater in these animals at 12- and 24-hours post-injury (Fig. 2E) compared to PACAP-treated sham-operated mice, which showed a significantly reduced injury area in line with the results obtained in intact mice (Fig. 2E). On the other hand, PACAP treatment of lacrimal gland-removed mice significantly reduced the corneal injury area compared with saline-treated lacrimal gland-removed mice at 12 h, but findings were not significant at 24 h (Fig. 2E). These results suggest that PACAP stimulates corneal healing directly in the absence of lacrimation.

Healing mechanism of PACAP on corneal epithelium

The results of an in vitro scratch assay revealed that the scratch area in HCEC2 cells was reduced in the presence of PACAP compared to control-treated cells (Fig. 3A, B). Furthermore, the reduction was significant at all tested PACAP concentrations, and particularly so at 10− 9 M. A similar effect of PACAP on reduction of scratch area in vitro was shown in SV40-human corneal epithelium cell line (Supplementary Fig. 2). To determine whether this healing effect was due to cell proliferation or chemotaxis, a scratch assay was performed in the presence of Ara-C, which inhibits cell proliferation by interfering DNA synthesis. The addition of Ara-C did not cause any significant decrease in the scratch area, even after the addition of PACAP. These results suggest that the wound-healing effect of PACAP is due to cell proliferation (Fig. 3A, B). Next, we used EdU labeling to determine the number of mitotic and proliferative cells. EdU-positive cells were significantly and markedly decreased in the presence or absence of PACAP when Ara-C was added. However, the number of EdU-positive cells increased at a PACAP concentration of 10− 9 M compared with the control condition (Fig. 3C, D). These data led us to suspect that the wound healing effect of PACAP was most likely via a process of cell proliferation.

DNA microarray analysis of PACAP’s effect on corneal epithelium wound healing

Gene expression analysis using the whole-genome DNA microarray was performed to identify genes involved in the wound-healing effects of PACAP in corneal epithelial cell scratches. To distinguish the general transcriptional effects of PACAP from its specific role during wound healing, gene expression profiles were compared under both non-scratched conditions (non-scratch + control vs. non-scratch + PACAP) and scratched conditions (scratch + control vs. scratch + PACAP). At 3 h after PACAP addition, the non-scratched PACAP group showed 336 up-regulated and 209 down-regulated genes, whereas the scratched PACAP group showed 114 up-regulated and 337 down-regulated genes. At 18 h after PACAP addition, the non-scratched PACAP group showed 117 up-regulated and 681 down-regulated genes, whereas the scratched PACAP group showed 173 up-regulated and 352 down-regulated genes (Supplementary Fig. 3).

To further evaluate the wound-healing relevance of these transcriptional changes, the differentially expressed genes were classified into GOTERM_BP_DIRECT and GOTERM_MF_DIRECT using the DAVID tool, which identified several biological terms related to cell proliferation, wound healing, inflammation, and cell adhesion (Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4). At 3 h, increased gene expression was associated with cell proliferation (GO:0061469 ~ regulation of type B pancreatic cell proliferation, GO:0048660 ~ regulation of smooth muscle cell proliferation) and transcriptional regulation (GO:0006357, GO:0000978), whereas decreased expression was associated with inflammatory response (GO:0006954 ~ inflammatory response) and cell adhesion (GO:0007155 ~ cell adhesion). At 18 h, increased gene expression was associated with cell proliferation and development (GO:0045931 ~ positive regulation of mitotic cell cycle, GO:0060038 ~ cardiac muscle cell proliferation, GO:0007420 ~ brain development, GO:0070374 ~ positive regulation of ERK1 and ERK2 cascade, GO:0008361 ~ regulation of cell size, GO:0001654 ~ eye development) as well as wound healing (GO:0009611 ~ response to wounding). In contrast, decreased expression was associated with retinal structure maintenance (GO:0001895 ~ retina homeostasis, GO:0010842 ~ retina layer formation), cell adhesion molecules (GO:0007157 ~ heterophilic cell-cell adhesion via plasma membrane cell adhesion molecules, GO:0007159 ~ leukocyte cell-cell adhesion), and collagen fibril organization (GO:0030199 ~ collagen fibril organization) terms.

Among these genes, NR4A1, also known as nerve growth factor IB (NGFIB), was categorized as related to cell proliferation in the analysis of up-regulated genes after 3 h of PACAP treatment. NR4A1 has been shown to be involved in cell proliferation and apoptosis, and recent research has indicated its role in ocular wound healing and anti-fibrosis27–34.

Effect of NR4A1 inhibition on corneal wound healing

The expression of PAC1-R and VPAC1-R in HCEC2 cells was verified by RT-PCR analysis (Fig. 4A). To test for a potential corneal epithelial wound healing effect of NR4A1, we investigated changes in NR4A1 protein expression after the addition of PACAP receptor (PAC1-R, VPAC1-R, and VPAC2-R) antagonists. To this end, there was no change in expression with VIP6-28 (a VPAC1-R and VPAC2-R antagonist), but when PA-8 (a PAC1-R antagonist) was added, the expression of NR4A1 protein was suppressed (Fig. 4B). This finding suggests that NR4A1 acts through PAC1-R.

Based on this result, we performed a scratch assay under conditions in which THPN35, which functions as a transporter of NR4A1 from the nucleus to the inner mitochondrial membrane, was added. The EdU-stained scratch assay in the presence of THPN (Fig. 4C) confirmed that THPN prevented the reduction in the wound area (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, the percentage of EdU-positive cells was found to be almost 0% following the addition of THPN (Fig. 4E). This result was also confirmed with the MTT assay (Fig. 4F), which showed no evidence of cell death. Since the mitogenic and survival effects of cells depend on the regulation of translocation within the nucleus, cell proliferation was thought to be suppressed by the nuclear export of NR4A1 by THPN. We thus consider that the inhibitory effect of THPN on wound healing was due to the suppression of cell proliferation rather than to cell death.

Localization and phosphorylation of NR4A1 in corneal epithelial cells

Localization of NR4A1 has been reported to determine cell survival or cell death (apoptosis). The nuclear localization of NR4A1 is responsible for mitogenic effects, whereas mitochondrial localization constitutes a pro-apoptotic effect, with phosphorylation involved in these localizations. We therefore confirmed the localization of NR4A1 in corneal epithelial cells and examined potential changes in the localization and phosphorylation of NR4A1 induced by PACAP (Fig. 4G, H, I). Western blot analysis of the fractions showed that most of the NR4A1 and phosphorylated NR4A1 were localized in the nucleus of the corneal epithelial cells. Scratching did not affect the abundance of NR4A1, but the abundance of phosphorylated NR4A1 increased within 5 min of scratching. Dephosphorylation of NR4A1 by PACAP was observed at 5 and 15 min after the scratch (Fig. 4G, H, I).

THPN treatment of the corneal wound-injury model

The observed effects of corneal wound healing by PACAP in the presence of THPN in the WT mouse corneal injury model are shown in Fig. 4J, K. At 12- and 24-hours post-wounding, the corneal area was significantly reduced when PACAP was added compared to saline alone, whereas the wound healing effect of PACAP in the presence of THPN was lower than that seen with saline.

Discussion

Tear fluid contains growth factors such as epidermal growth factor (EGF) and nerve growth factor (NGF), which are known to contribute to the protection and regeneration of the corneal epithelium36. We previously reported that PACAP eye drops increase tear secretion and suppress the spontaneous occurrence of corneal damage in PACAP KO mice22. There are two possible mechanisms by which PACAP eye drops suppress corneal damage: first, an increased tear secretion induced by PACAP could have protected the cornea, and second, PACAP might have acted directly on the corneal epithelium to repair the damage. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time in vivo that PACAP eye drops promote the healing of corneal damage in mice without lacrimal glands. Furthermore, PAC1-R was found to be expressed in the corneal epithelium, and PACAP stimulated wound healing in mouse cornea and cultured human corneal epithelial cells. However, these effects of PACAP were not observed in PAC1-R KO mice or in cases where PACAP 6–38 (a PAC1-R antagonist) was applied as a pretreatment. Notably, the inhibitory effect of PACAP 6–38 was evident at 12 h but less apparent at 24 h. One possible explanation is that both PACAP and PACAP 6–38 eye drops were administered only up to 12 h in the present study, and thus the inhibitory effect of PACAP 6–38 may have diminished thereafter. In addition, wound healing had progressed substantially even in the vehicle-treated group by 24 h, which may have reduced the detectability of group differences at this later time point. These results suggest that PACAP affects the corneal epithelium directly by stimulating corneal healing via PAC1-R. Given that it was previously shown that PACAP eye drops induced the growth of neuronal processes in the cornea and accelerated the recovery of corneal sensitivity in rabbits37, the present and previously published results collectively suggest that PACAP may be a potential therapeutic agent for corneal disorders. Although PACAP⁺/⁻ mice were used in the present study to evaluate the effects of reduced endogenous PACAP, the use of PACAP⁻/⁻ mice may provide more definitive genetic evidence for the role of PACAP. However, in vivo experiments using PACAP⁻/⁻ mice are technically more challenging because of their reduced viability and higher mortality. Future studies using PACAP-deficient mice will be important to further clarify the contribution of PACAP signaling to corneal epithelial regeneration.

Previous studies have investigated the use of growth factors such as EGF, NGF, and insulin for the treatment of corneal injury38–40. Among these growth factors, NGF has been the most extensively studied in clinical research. NGF is widely expressed in normal human and rat corneal tissues and is produced by corneal epithelial cells. Furthermore, NTRK1 and NGFR are broadly expressed in ocular surface cells, including corneal epithelial cells, endothelial cells, stromal cells, limbal stem cells, and conjunctival epithelial cells. NGF promotes corneal epithelial regeneration through both cell proliferation and migration. In particular, NGF plays a crucial role in epithelial healing by inducing cell migration via TrkA and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) activation41. Given these mechanisms, we examined in this study whether PACAP also promotes corneal epithelial regeneration through similar pathways. We found that PACAP administration enhanced wound healing in cultured corneal epithelial cells in a manner that was suppressed by Ara-C treatment. Additionally, PACAP treatment increased the number of EdU-positive corneal epithelial cells, and this effect was also inhibited by Ara-C. These results suggest that PACAP promotes corneal healing by stimulating corneal epithelial cell proliferation, indicating that PACAP may facilitate corneal regeneration through a pathway distinct from that of NGF, which has been the focus of previous research. In the present study, wound healing was evaluated by scratch assay based on changes in wound area over time, which indirectly reflects the integrated effects of both cell proliferation and cell migration. Under these conditions, PACAP significantly reduced the wound area, whereas Ara-C markedly attenuated this reduction, suggesting that cell proliferation is a major contributor under the present experimental conditions. However, because cell migration was not directly quantified by independent methods such as time-lapse imaging, its contribution cannot be excluded and should be addressed in future studies.

We performed experiments here based on identifying the expression of genes of interest by high-throughput whole-genome DNA microarray, followed by bioinformatics and functional analyses. Results indicated that NR4A1 signaling promotes the cell proliferation effects seen during PACAP-induced wound healing in HCEC2 cells. NR4A1 is capable of inducing both proliferation and apoptosis, depending on the stimulus and this protein’s subcellular localization42,43. These responses require both DNA binding and trans-activation functions, which involve phosphorylation of the protein which affects a variety of activities, including DNA binding, trans-activation, subcellular localization, and apoptotic activity44–47. It was reported that NR4A1 phosphorylation mainly inhibits DNA binding and transcriptional activity, and promotes NR4A1 extra-nuclear trafficking via the PI3K/AKT48,49, MEK/ERK/MAPK50–53, PKC54–56, and JNK pathways46,57. In addition, previous studies have also shown that PACAP has the ability to rapidly increase NR4A1 gene expression in neuronal cells both in vivo and in vitro. Furthermore, several recent studies reported that NR4A1 expression is regulated via the PKA-CREB pathway58–62, while forskolin treatment causes cAMP/PKA-mediated phosphorylation of CREB (p-CREB), which in turn binds to the NR4A1 promoter to strongly induce NR4A1 expression63. PACAP was also shown to enhance NR4A1 gene expression in neuronal cells and neuron-like PC12 cells64–66. In the present study, we found that PACAP increased total NR4A1 via PAC1-R signaling and was associated with suppression of phospho-NR4A1 levels during the wound-healing process, suggesting the involvement of NR4A1 signaling in the proliferation of corneal cells. More precisely, nuclear phospho-NR4A1 levels increased under both scratch + control and scratch + PACAP conditions, but this increase was significantly attenuated by PACAP treatment. In contrast, nuclear NR4A1 levels tended to decrease after scratching, whereas PACAP appeared to attenuate this decrease, although the difference was not statistically significant. Together, these findings suggest that PACAP may enhance NR4A1 signaling by increasing the overall availability of NR4A1 while suppressing the relative increase in its phosphorylated form during the wound-healing process. However, the precise molecular mechanism remains unclear. Possible explanations include altered phosphatase activity, changes in NR4A1 subcellular localization, suppression of upstream NR4A1-phosphorylating kinases, or site-specific regulation of NR4A1 phosphorylation. On the other hand, it can be suggested that the activation of cAMP/PKA via PAC1-R promotes NR4A1 expression via the phosphorylation of CREB, while reduced phospho-NR4A1 levels in the nuclei may promote DNA binding and transcriptional activity.

THPN used in this experiment is commercially available as an NR4A1 agonist and has the ability to induce the nuclear export of NR4A1 and mitochondria-dependent autophagic cell death in melanoma cells67. In addition, several other factors have been shown to promote the nuclear export of NR4A1, which subsequently interacts with mitochondria-derived factors and typically induces cell death68,45–47. We postulated that the nuclear export function of NR4A1 induced by THPN would inhibit transcriptional activity and cell proliferation, and on this basis we used it to drastically reduce the number of EdU-positive cells. Our findings suggest that the nuclear export of NR4A1 leads to the suppression of cell proliferation. This effect of THPN also suppressed the PACAP-induced increase in the number of EdU-positive cells and the extent of corneal epithelial healing. However, at the concentration of THPN used in this study, mitochondria-dependent autophagic cell death was not induced. In addition, numerous reports have shown that phosphorylation of NR4A1 inhibits DNA binding and transactivation, and that the cell growth-promoting activity requires NR4A1 to remain in the nucleus and for transcriptional activation to be achieved through NR4A1-DNA binding. These results suggest that PACAP may enhance NR4A1 signaling by suppressing the increase in phospho-NR4A1 levels, thereby promoting cell proliferation. However, because THPN was used as a pharmacological tool in the present study, potential off-target effects cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, further studies using genetic approaches, such as epithelial-specific NR4A1 knockdown or conditional knockout models, will be required to more definitively establish the role of NR4A1 signaling in PACAP-induced corneal wound healing. It should also be noted that the mechanistic link between PACAP/PAC1-R signaling and NR4A1 activation in the present study was mainly supported by in vitro experiments using HCEC2 cells. Additional in vivo analyses of NR4A1 expression, phosphorylation, and subcellular localization in the corneal epithelium will be necessary to further strengthen the proposed mechanism.

In conclusion, PACAP promotes corneal wound healing primarily by stimulating epithelial cell proliferation, possibly through PAC1-R–mediated regulation of NR4A1 signaling, leading to its transcriptional activation. Further research will be necessary to clarify the exact mechanisms underlying these actions. There is a strong need to discover ways to accelerate corneal wound healing to avoid the pain effects associated with corneal injury and the possibility of infection due to barrier breakdown. If the full mechanism of corneal wound healing by PACAP is elucidated, it may be possible to develop an effective and safe drug to promote corneal wound healing.

Methods

Animals

All mouse experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Showa University (09110, 50046) and Hoshi University (P23-085). All mice used in this study were treated in accordance with the relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations. All methods were carried out in compliance with the Animal Care and Use Committee of Showa University and Hoshi University, and all animal experiments were reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Adcyap1-/- (PACAP knockout) mice on a C57BL/6 background were established by Dr. Hashimoto in University of Osaka69. Adcyap1r1-/- (PAC1-R KO) mice (C57BL/6 N-Adcyap1r1 em1Cya, S-KO-00922, Cyagen, CA, USA) were established in our laboratory70. Animals were housed in a facility with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and were given free access to water and standard rodent chow. Eight- to twelve-week-old male mice were used in this study.

Total RNA extraction and RT-PCR

The RT-PCR protocol followed was described by us in a previous study71. For total RNA extraction experiments, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane delivered via an inhalation anesthesia apparatus (MK-A110D, Muromachi Kikai Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and subsequently euthanized by manual cervical dislocation to minimize the pain and distress. In brief, Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to isolate total RNA from mouse corneal tissue and corneal epithelium, and reverse transcription was carried out with random primers. cDNA from mouse brain tissue was also prepared as a positive control. Mouse Adcyap1 (PACAP) primers (Forward: 5’-ATG ACC ATG TGT AGC GGA GCA AGG CTG G-3’, Reverse: 5’-CTA CAA GTA TGC TAT TCG GCG TCC-3’), mouse Pac1-r (PAC1-R) primers ( Forward: 5’-CCT GTC GGT GAA GGC CCT CTA CAC A-3’, Reverse: 5’-CCC AGC CCA AGC TCA AAC ACA AGT C-3’), mouse Vpac1-r primers (Forward: 5’-GTA TGG ATG AGC AGC AAC AGA CTG-3’, Reverse: 5’-TTG ATG ATG GTG TCC CAG CAC-3’), mouse Vpac2-r primers (Forward: 5’-CAT CTC TGT GCT GGT CAA GGA C-3’, Reverse: 5’-CGC CAT CTT CTT TTC AGT TCA C-3’), and mouse Actb primers (Forward: 5’-GTG GGC CGC TCT AGG CAC CAA-3’, Reverse: 5’-CTC TTT GAT GTC ACG CAC GAT TTC-3’) were mixed with the EX taq kit (RR001A, Takara, Shiga, Japan). PCR amplicons were evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized with an illuminometer.

Immunohistochemistry

The immunostaining procedure was performed as described in a previous study72. Animals were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.) using a calibrated injection protocol. After confirming loss of reflexes, transcardial perfusion was performed with phosphate-buffered saline followed by 2% paraformaldehyde. No cervical dislocation or isoflurane anesthesia was used in this procedure. All procedures were performed by personnel trained in animal anesthesia and perfusion techniques. Eight micrometer-thick cryosections were prepared according to standard procedures and pretreated with 10 mM citrate for 30 min at 80 °C. Tissue sections were blocked in 5% normal horse serum for 1 h. Sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies, with the rabbit anti-PAC1-R antibody previously developed in our laboratory73. Alexa-546 labeled anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:400) was used as the secondary antibody. Tissue sections were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidine-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI; 1:10,000; Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Fluorescence was detected using an Axio-Imager optical sectioning microscope with ApoTome (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The specificity of the PAC1-R antibody was confirmed by an antigen absorption test where the PAC1-R antibody solution was incubated with a final concentration of 1 µM of antigens in 5% normal horse serum in PBS overnight at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 10,000 g for 20 min, the supernatant was used instead of the primary antibody.

Mouse corneal wound-injury model

In the mouse corneal wound-injury model, animals were maintained under sevoflurane anesthesia administered via a facemask, and a 1 mm diameter injury mark was created on the corneal epithelial surface using a biopsy punch. The corneal epithelium inside the injury mark was scraped off using microsurgery forceps and the injury was confirmed by HE staining. In the rat corneal wound-injury model, animals were maintained under pentobarbital anesthesia and a 2 mm-diameter injury to center of the cornea was produced with a corneal trepan8. The corneal epithelium within the damaged area was removed using microsurgery forceps. The entire procedure was performed with the animal positioned on the stage of a dissecting microscope.

PACAP treatment of the corneal wound-injury model mice

In mouse experiments, eye drops (2 µl) containing different PACAP concentrations (10-9M, 10-11M and 10-13M) were administered immediately after the injury insult and then every two hours for a total of seven doses over 12 hours’ post-injury. The same volume of saline was administered to the control group. In some experiments, PACAP receptor antagonists (PACAP6-38 (PAC1-R and VPAC2-R antagonist) and VIP6-28 (VPAC1-R and VPAC2-R antagonist)) were used as pre-treatment before application of the PACAP eye drops. To measure the area of the corneal injury, 2 µl of fluorescein solution (1 µg/µl) dissolved in saline were applied to the cornea 12 and 24 h after injury. Images were taken with a digital camera (CAMEDIA C5050, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) connected to a stereoscopic microscope immediately after application of the fluorescein eye drops, and the injury area was measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Lacrimal gland removal surgery

Surgery to remove the lacrimal gland of mice was performed as described previously22. Briefly, animals were maintained under sevoflurane anesthesia and the exorbital lacrimal gland and the lacrimal duct were exposed. The infraorbital lacrimal gland was then revealed by pulling the lacrimal duct to one side, after which both lacrimal glands were removed. The skin was then sutured, and the animals were kept warm during recovery from anesthesia. Tear secretion levels were checked before and after surgery using the cotton thread method22.

PACAP treatment of the corneal wound-injury in PAC1-R KO mouse model

In the PACAP treatment experiment, 2 µl of PACAP (10− 9 M) were administered in the form of eye drops every two hours from immediately after the injury until 12 h later. The same volume of saline was administered to the control group. Measurement of the corneal injury area was performed 12 and 24 h after the injury insult as described above.

1-(3,4,5-trihydroxyphenyl) nonan-1-one (THPN) treatment in the corneal wound-injury model

Following the injury insult to the cornea of mice, 2 µl of 10 mM THPN or vehicle (20% DMSO/saline) were applied, followed by 2 µl of PACAP (10− 9 M) eye drops. This procedure was repeated every 2 h until 12 hours’ post-injury. The control group received the same volume of saline. The area of corneal injury was measured 12 and 24 h after the injury insult as described above.

Human corneal epithelial cell culture model

Cell culture

Normal human corneal epithelial cells (HCEC2, KC-4009) were obtained from Kurabo Industries Ltd. (Osaka, Japan). According to the provider, this cell line was established and distributed in accordance with the relevant ethical guidelines and regulations, including appropriate informed consent and approval by the institutional ethics committee at the originating institution. The cells were cultivated in OcuLife BM (basal medium; LCB-LM0012, Kurabo) in a CO2 incubator (37 °C, 5%) with OcuLife LifeFactors (LCK-LS1057, Kurabo) and antibiotics (Gentamicin/Amphotericin, Kurabo). Experiments were repeated at least four times.

RNA extraction from HCEC2 cells and RT-PCR

The RNeasy Mini Kit (74104, Qiagen) was used to extract total RNA from HCEC2 cells cultured in 10 cm Petri dishes. To verify the quality of this RNA, yield and purity were determined spectrophotometrically using DS-11 (DeNovix, Wilmington, DE, USA) and confirmed using formaldehyde-agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA was synthesized using the Affinity Script QPCR cDNA synthesis kit (600559, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and the PCR reaction was performed using EmeraldAmp PCR Master Mix (RR300A, Takara). PCRs were carried out with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 minutes, followed by a post-final cycle extension at 72°C for 10 minutes. The primer sequences used were as follows: human Adcyap1 (PACAP) primers (Forward: 5’-TGA AGG AAT GAC CAT GTG TAG C-3’, Reverse: 5’-CTG TAG CTG TCC GTG AAG ATC C-3’); human Pac1-r primers (Forward: 5’-TAC TTT GAT GAC ACA GGC TGC T-3’, Reverse: 5’-GAA GTA ACG GTT CAC CTT CCA G-3’); human Vpac1-r primers (Forward: 5’-CTC CTC ACT GTG GTG GAT CAT A-3’, Reverse: 5’- TCA CCA TTG AGG AAG CAG TAG A-3’); human Vpac2-r primers (Forward: 5’-CCA GAT ACT GTT TGA GCT GTG C-3’, Reverse: 5’-AAA TCG AGT CAA TGA CAA CAC G-3’); human Actb primers (Forward: 5’-CCA CGA AAC TAC CTT CAA CTC C-3’, Reverse: 5’-CAT AGT CCG CCT AGA AGC ATT T-3’). PCR products were separated on a 1.6% agarose gel and visualized using ethidium bromide staining under UV light.

Scratch assay in HCEC2 cells

A HCEC2 cell solution containing 7–9 × 103 cells in 100 µL/well was seeded in 96-well microplates. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with basal medium (OcuLife BM), and scratches were created with a 200 µL pipette tip. PACAP was added to the cell culture medium at concentrations of 10− 7 M, 10− 9 M, or 10–11 M after scratches. When Ara-C and THPN were supplemented, 15 µM Ara-C (C3350000, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 5 µM THPN (SML1548, Sigma-Aldrich) were added after scratching, and the cell culture was incubated for 30 min before addition of PACAP. THPN was dissolved in DMSO and further diluted in PBS. A DMSO/PBS solution was used for the control group. Ara-C was dissolved in PBS and diluted. PBS was used for the control group. The cells were observed as phase-contrast images using an optical microscope BZ-X710 (Keyence, Osaka Japan) over a period of 15 to 90 h after the addition of PACAP. The extent of the scratch area was measured using ImageJ software, and scratch area at the t = 0 timepoint was taken as 100%. Finally, the results for all fields examined and for each condition were averaged (n = 4) to calculate the percentage of open wound area per condition.

MTT cell viability assay

MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) labeling reagent (Roche Life Science, Basel, Switzerland) was added at 10 µL/100 µL to 96-well microplates and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 4 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of solubilizing solution (0.01 M HCl, 10% SDS) were added to each well and incubated for 17 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After incubation, absorbance at 570 nm was measured using an iMark microplate reader (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) at a reference wavelength of 650 nm. Cell viability (%) was determined by comparing the number of viable cells with and without the addition of PACAP or THPN (referred to as the evaluation samples), with the viability of cells without the evaluation sample taken as 100%. Each measurement was repeated three times, and the average value was calculated.

EdU cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using Click-iT® Plus EdU Imaging Kits (C10639, Thermo Fisher Scientific). A 10 µM EdU solution was added to HCEC2 cells cultured in 96-well microplates and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Following removal of the culture media, 80 µL of 3.7% formaldehyde/PBS were added and cells fixed for 15 min at room temperature. After removing the fixative solution, the cells were washed twice with 100 µL of 3% BSA/PBS. Eighty microliters of 0.5% Triton® X-100/PBS were added and the cells incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed three times with 100 µL of 3% BSA/PBS. The reaction was carried out by adding 40 µL of Click-iT® Plus reaction cocktail and incubating for 30 min. The cells were washed twice with 100 µL of 3% BSA/PBS and once with 100 µL of PBS, following which 80 µL of Hoechst® 33,342 solution (1:2000 in PBS) were added and the cells maintained for 30 min at room temperature. After the reaction, the cells were washed twice with 100 µL of PBS, and images were captured using an optical microscope (BZ-X710, Keyence).

DNA microarray analysis

Total RNA extracted from HCEC2 cells for each control and treatment experiment (n = 4) was pooled for each group prior to whole-genome DNA microarray analysis (Whole Human Genome DNA Microarray 4 × 44 K, G2514F). Total RNA (400 ng) was labeled with either Cy3 or Cy5 dye using the Low Input Quick Amp WT Labeling Kit (Agilent). Fluorescently-labeled targets of control as well as treated samples were hybridized to the same microarray slide with 60-mer probes. A flip labeling (dye-swap or reverse labeling with Cy3 and Cy5 dyes) procedure was followed to nullify the dye bias associated with unequal incorporation of the two Cy dyes into cDNA. Briefly, to select differentially expressed genes by the dye-swap approach, hybridization and wash processes were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and hybridized microarrays were scanned using an Agilent G2505C Microarray Scanner. For the detection of significantly differentially expressed genes between control and treated samples, each slide image was processed using Agilent Feature Extraction software (version 9.5.3.1). This program measures the Cy3 and Cy5 signal intensities of all probes. Dye-bias tends to be signal intensity dependent; therefore, the software selected probes using a set by rank consistency filter for dye-normalization. The normalization was performed by LOWESS (locally weighted linear regression), which calculates the log ratio of dye-normalized Cy3- and Cy5-signals, as well as the final error of the log ratio. The genes with variable expression selected by the dye-swap method were classified into GOTERM_BP_DIRECT and GOTERM_MF_DIRECT by gene ontology (GO) analysis using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID 6.8, https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/).

Extraction of total soluble protein

To extract proteins, HCEC2 cells were lysed by the addition of LB-TT extraction solution, as previously described74. LB-TT was rapidly added to the culture dish and immediately mixed for 1 min at room temperature. The resulting cell lysate was transferred to new 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes and subjected to a second round of purification, involving cleanup and concentration, using the ProteoExtract Protein Precipitation Kit (539180, Calbiochem, Darmstadt, Germany). The precipitated proteins, visible as a white solid at the bottom of the tube, were resuspended in 10–100 µL of LB-TT. Protein concentrations were measured using the Pierce™ 660 nm Protein Assay Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a DeNovix DS-11 spectrophotometer (DeNovix), employing bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard.

Nucleoprotein fractionation

Nucleoprotein isolation and extraction from HCEC2 cells were performed using the Nuclear Protein Isolation-Translocation Assay Kit (NPI-1, Fivephoton Biochemicals, USA). The HCEC2 cells cultured in 10 cm Petri dishes were washed twice with PBS, following which 500 µL of Cytoplasmic Isolation Reagent were added and the Petri dish left on ice for 5 min. HCEC2 cells and the reagent were collected using a cell scraper and transferred into 1.5 mL microtubes. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant collected as the cytoplasmic fraction. Five hundred microliters of CTR reagent were added to the cell precipitates, mixed well, and centrifuged at 2500 rpm for a further 3 min at 4 °C to remove the supernatant. To the precipitates, 20 µL of NIR-1 solution was added, mixed by vortexing for 1 min, and kept on ice for 10 min. Following the reaction, the supernatant was collected as the nuclear fraction by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein purification and quantification were performed as described above using the ProteoExtract Protein Precipitation Kit, LB-TT resuspension, and Pierce™ 660 nm assay.

Western blotting

For Western blot analysis, proteins separated on 5–20% SDS-PAGE (HR mini-gel; ATTO, Tokyo, Japan) were transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Trans-Blot Turbo Midi PVDF, 0.2 μm, Transfer Packs kit; 170–4157, Bio-Rad). The following antibodies were used: 3 µg protein/lane for NR4A1 antibody (ab109180, Abcam) and P-Ser351-NR4A1 antibody (12376, Signalway Antibody, Greenbelt, MD, USA), and 5 µg protein/lane for GAPDH antibody (014-25524, Wako-Fujifilm) and ASH2L antibody (ab176334, Abcam). A Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad) was used for the electrotransfer (MIXED MW protocol; 25 V, 2.5 A, 7 min). Following the transfer of proteins onto the PVDF membrane (as also confirmed by visualizing all 10 colored molecular mass standards), the membrane was incubated in 25 mL of blocking solution for 1 h under constant slow shaking at room temperature. The blocking solution was prepared by dissolving 5.0 g skim milk (BD/Difco, NJ, USA) in 100 mL 1X TTBS [10X TTBS: NaCl 80 g; 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) 200 mL; Tween-20 10 mL/L]. The blocking solution was decanted and the membrane was washed once in 1X TTBS for 5 min, followed by incubation in 10 mL of primary antibody solution [5 µL rabbit anti-NR4A1, 3 µL rabbit anti-P-Ser351-NR4A1, 1 µL mouse anti-GAPDH, 1.5 µL rabbit anti-ASH2L] for 1 h, as above. The membrane was then washed five times with 25 mL of 1X TTBS. After decanting the last TTBS wash, the membrane was incubated in 10 mL of secondary antibody solution [0.5 µL of ECL anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked species-specific whole antibody (NA 934, GE Healthcare, IL, USA); 0.5 µL of ECL anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked species-specific whole antibody (NA 931, GE Healthcare)] for 1 h, with slow shaking at room temperature. The 1X TTBS wash step was repeated five times. For blot development, the luminol/enhancer and peroxide buffer solutions were mixed in a 1:1 ratio (170–5060, Clarity Western ECL Substrate, Bio-Rad), spread over the membrane, and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Excess solution was drained by touching one end of the membrane on a KimWipe paper towel, and the signal (cross-reacting protein bands) was visualized using a ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of the mean. Statistical comparisons were made using the ANOVA test followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc analysis. Differences with p values below 0.05 were considered significant. ***p < 0.001, **p < 0.01, *p < 0.05.
