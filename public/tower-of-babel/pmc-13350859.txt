MAPK/PMK-1 innate immune signaling protects the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans from increased intestinal colonization in an animal host-pathogen model in space
Authors: Alfredo V. Alcantara, Rocel Amor Indong, Kyoung-hye Yoon, Ban-seok Kim, Toko Hashizume, Akira Higashibata, Atsushi Higashitani, Nathaniel J. Szewczyk, Timothy Etheridge, Colleen S. Deane, Rebecca A. Ellwood, Han Sung Kim, Robert J. Mitchell, Jin I. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the recent rise in the numbers and diversity of astronauts and space travelers, health and prevention of illness in space are of primary importance. Changes in immune function among astronauts during spaceflight have been reported, but gaps remain in understanding how this may translate to increases in an in-flight risk of infection. To understand how immunity and infection are affected by microgravity, we used the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans as an animal host-pathogen model. Worms exposed to either space or simulated microgravity for several days exhibited increased Enterobacter gut colonization compared to normal gravity on Earth. Bacterial susceptibility was more severe in immunocompromised mutants of the pmk-1 gene, a conserved p38 MAPK ortholog that regulates innate immunity. RNA sequencing analysis identified several immune effector genes regulated by microgravity through MAPK/PMK-1. Silencing these genes via RNA interference identified specific immune effectors that protect C. elegans against increased Enterobacter gut proliferation, while transgenic expression of one of these effectors prevented increased colonization in immunocompromised C. elegans in microgravity. This study underscores the importance of the conserved MAPK/PMK-1 innate immune pathway in providing protection against possible infection during spaceflight.

Introduction

One of the major health risks for astronauts during long-term spaceflight is exposure to a weightless environment in space, also known as microgravity. As the human body is adapted to Earth’s gravity (1 G), which maintains our terrestrial orientation, exposure to weaker gravity can affect normal biological processes and human health in ways to which our bodies are not accustomed. Reports indicate that astronauts in space experience illness during their stay aboard the International Space Station (ISS), including skin rashes, respiratory problems, ear irritations, and other infections1, despite undergoing preparations such as health management, physical conditioning, and quarantine protocols prior to spaceflight. Gene expression related to immunity is known to be altered in cell and tissue cultures, animal models, and astronauts in space2–8. Furthermore, latent viral reactivation has been documented in astronauts, including varicella zoster virus, Epstein–Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex virus type 1, albeit without any clinical symptoms9–11. Multiple studies report changes in immune cell composition and abundance during spaceflight, pointing to impaired immune regulation2–4,12–15. Finally, spaceflight-induced imbalance in gut bacteria, known as dysbiosis, has been observed in space-flown mice16,17 and astronauts18–21. Together, these findings highlight that spaceflight-induced immune alterations have the potential to compromise astronaut health.

To determine whether altered immunity in space results in increased infection and sickness, animal host-pathogen models should be employed. Previously, space-flown microbes were shown to be harmful and infectious in mouse, fruit fly and zebrafish host-pathogen models when tested back on Earth22–25. In addition, infection of cultured intestinal cells and infection of Drosophila by parasitoids are observed in space as on the ground7,26. It remains unknown whether spaceflight-induced changes in immunity can cause increased bacterial infection in space.

To develop an infection model in space, we utilized the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans and assessed its immune gene expression together with its ability to resist infections. C. elegans serves as an advantageous model organism for space biology research, with well-established protocols for both ground-based simulations and actual spaceflight missions27–30. Additionally, C. elegans provides an excellent host-pathogen model system, as it feeds on and digests bacteria within its gut. However, certain pathogenic and pathobiont species can survive this digestive process and establish colonization in the C. elegans gut, leading to infection and host mortality31,32. To protect themselves, C. elegans possesses innate immune pathways, including p38 MAP kinase and TGFβ signaling pathways, that are conserved in mammals and humans33,34. These two molecular pathways are known to be altered in microgravity: the dbl-1 gene, a TGFβ pathway ligand, is downregulated in space aboard the ISS35, and the pmk-1 gene, which is a p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) ortholog important for environmental stress adaptation36, is activated in ground-based microgravity simulations37. These signaling pathways regulate expression of downstream immune effector genes to fight off microbial infection33.

Here, using C. elegans as a host-pathogen infection model both aboard the International Space Station and in simulated microgravity experiments on earth, we establish that microgravity increases Enterobacter colonization in the worm gut. Using a combination of genetics and gene expression analysis, we identified microgravity-specific immune effector genes downstream of p38 MAPK signaling that protect C. elegans from Enterobacter infection.

Results

Simulated microgravity increases colonization of pathogenic bacteria in C. elegans

Exposure to microgravity is known to alter normal immune functions in both animals and humans2,21. Since microgravity can also disrupt C. elegans immune pathways, we investigated whether it might affect resistance to pathogenic infections. To address this question, we used the multi-host opportunistic human pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA14, which is commonly employed in C. elegans survival assays due to its efficiency in infecting and killing nematodes31,38,39. We exposed adult C. elegans to simulated microgravity using a 3D clinostat, with a monoxenic culture of PA14 as their sole food source, ensuring that the worms fed exclusively on this bacterium. PA14 kills C. elegans via two distinct mechanisms. The first, termed “fast-killing,” involves toxin secretion by PA14, causing worm mortality within hours. The second, known as “slow-killing,” results from PA14 accumulation in the C. elegans gut, leading to intestinal distention and death over several days31. Although overall mortality did not differ between simulated microgravity-exposed and 1 G control worms, gut bacterial loads in the surviving worms were significantly higher under simulated microgravity (Fig. 1B). Intestinal accumulation of bacteria can indicate an increased infection in C. elegans and/or increased growth kinetics of the bacteria in microgravity. To determine whether simulated microgravity altered PA14 growth rate, we cultured PA14 in the 3D clinostat using the same method except without worms (axenic culture) for 48 h. Colony counts did not differ between µG clinostat and 1 G conditions (Fig. 1C), consistent with a previous report on PA14 growth kinetics in similar culture conditions40. Therefore, PA14 overabundance in the C. elegans gut under microgravity likely reflects reduced host resistance rather than intrinsic changes in final bacterial density. Despite comparable mortality rates, elevated gut abundance under simulated microgravity underscores a potential risk of enhanced infection in spaceflight environments.

C. elegans opportunistic pathogen Enterobacter hormaechei colonizes the worm gut in simulated microgravity

Since PA14 is highly pathogenic to both worms and immune-compromised humans, its use in a host-pathogen model for C. elegans microgravity experiments posed practical difficulties in safety that our MME-2 mission aboard the International Space Station was not able to address. Due to these limitations, we tested infectivity of the bacterium Enterobacter hormaechei, formerly known as E. cloacae41. Normally a commensal, non-pathogenic bacterium in wild-type C. elegans42 and considered a low colonizer43,44, E. hormaechei becomes pathogenic to immunocompromised mutant and aged worms. In mutants lacking components of the TGFβ/DBL-1 innate immune pathway such as dbl-1 mutants, the pathobiont E. hormaechei infects the intestine, leading to increased death in certain pathogenic environments45.

We exposed C. elegans to simulated microgravity with the opportunistic pathogen E. hormaechei as their sole food source (Fig. 1D–F). The E. hormaechei CEN2ent1-tdTomato (CEent1) strain used for this experiment was originally isolated from wild-type strain N2 C. elegans exposed to compost and then tagged with fluorescence45. The fluorescent signal emitted by CEent1 in the C. elegans intestine correlates with qPCR quantification of Enterobacter DNA45 and has commonly been used to indicate E. hormaechei gut colonization in C. elegans41,45,46. Three-day exposure to E. hormaechei CEent1 from L1 larvae to adults did not induce lethality in wild-type C. elegans under simulated microgravity, in contrast to the highly pathogenic PA14 strain (Fig. 1E). When we observed CEent1 fluorescence in the worm intestine, however, we saw increased colonization in simulated microgravity similar to PA14 (Fig. 1D,F). Therefore, microgravity appears to promote gut proliferation by both pathogenic PA14 and Enterobacter bacteria in C. elegans.

To rule out the possibility that increased CEent1 colonization under simulated microgravity was driven by increased bacterial growth kinetics in simulated microgravity, a similar assay was performed with CEent1 without C. elegans to feed on the bacteria. After 72 h in simulated microgravity, bacterial colony counts showed no significant difference between CEent1 exposed to µG clinostat or 1 G control conditions (Fig. 1G). A previous study using low-shear modeled microgravity (LSMMG) via rotating wall vessel (RWV) revealed similar growth kinetics with E. cloacae ATCC23355, a closely related species within the same Enterobacter cloacae complex, compared to 1 G control47. Therefore, microgravity-induced abundance of CEent1 in the C. elegans intestine is not an effect of final bacterial density, but likely a consequence of reduced resistance of C. elegans against colonization in microgravity.

Increased Enterobacter colonization in the gut of space-flown C. elegans

To further determine the effects of actual space microgravity on infection, we sent C. elegans to low Earth orbit (LEO) aboard the ISS. Due to the limitations of the MME-2 spaceflight experimental protocols and the inherent pathogenicity of PA14 bacteria, we used E. hormaechei CEent1 for the ISS experiments (Fig. 2A). In space, transcriptomic analyses of C. elegans had shown reduced dbl-1 gene expression relative to worms in the 1 G control35; (Higashitani et al., pers. communication). We sent wild-type N2 worms to LEO aboard the ISS via the Molecular Muscle Experiment 2 space mission with E. hormaechei CEent1 bacteria as a food source (Fig. 2A).

Our experimental design follows established spaceflight analog protocols (see 'Materials and Methods') and the MME-1 mission parameters48–50. The culture bags contained the initial parental generation P0 L1-stage larvae, which grew into adults by the time of arrival on the ISS and laid eggs to produce the F1 generation. After about 5.5 days on the ISS, the majority of worms were adult F1 progeny that had spent their entire lives in microgravity, mixed with a few older P0 adult worms (Fig. 2A). Only F1 generation worms were assessed; older and larger-sized P0 adults were excluded because they did not develop entirely in space microgravity. After recovery of our samples from space (Space µG), we observed a significant increase in CEent1 gut abundance in wild-type worms compared to worms cultivated in Earth gravity controls under the same conditions on the ground at Kennedy Space Center (1 G KSC) (Fig. 2B–C). This trend in gut colonization was similar to our ground-based microgravity simulations relative to 1 G control worms (Fig. 1D–E). These results, both in simulated microgravity and space, suggest that C. elegans is vulnerable to E. hormaechei colonization in microgravity. While altered gut microbiomes have been observed in rodents and humans during spaceflight16,17,19,51,52, this is the first demonstration that space directly induces bacterial colonization of an opportunistic pathogen in an intact animal during spaceflight.

The immune regulator p38 MAP kinase protects C. elegans from elevated intestinal Enterobacter accumulation in space

Given that simulated microgravity conditions did not affect CEent1 proliferation (Fig. 1F), we hypothesized that the resulting bloom in the C. elegans gut was due to altered immune responses, particularly the TGFβ and MAPK pathways that regulate immunity53,54. To determine the role of these pathways in CEent1 gut colonization under simulated microgravity, we tested mutant strains for the TGFβ-ligand dbl-1 and p38 MAPK ortholog pmk-1. As expected, we observed extensive CEent1 colonization in immunocompromised dbl-1 mutants compared to N2 wild-type, as previously reported45. However, unlike N2 wild type, we did not observe any further increase in fluorescent signal in simulated microgravity compared to 1 G control (Fig. 3B). This suggests that increase in E. hormaechei gut proliferation in microgravity is caused by disruption of the TGFβ/DBL-1 pathway. We also observed much lower CEent1 infection in N2 wild-type compared to dbl-1 mutants in simulated microgravity. This suggests that another immune pathway is protecting C. elegans from infection by compensating for the loss of DBL-1 in microgravity conditions.

One possible explanation is the PMK-1 pathway. While PMK-1 does not specifically regulate E. hormaechei colonization in C. elegans gut under normal conditions45, this gene plays an important role in environmental adaptations36 and protective functions in simulated microgravity37. Under normal Earth gravity conditions, the pmk-1 mutant is resistant to E. hormaechei infection like wild-type (Fig. 3A)45. However, we observed enhanced CEent1 colonization in pmk-1 mutants exposed to simulated microgravity similar to levels in dbl-1 mutants (Fig. 3B). To verify these results, pmk-1 mutants were sent to space aboard the ISS, and we found a similar trend of strong proliferation of CEent1 during spaceflight (Fig. 3C–D). Overall, these results suggest an important role of MAPK/PMK-1-mediated immune functions in regulating E. hormaechei in simulated microgravity and that loss of pmk-1 leads to exacerbated E. hormaechei gut colonization in simulated microgravity.

MAPK/PMK-1 responds to E. hormaechei gut proliferation in microgravity by increasing expression of specific immune effector genes

How does the immune regulator PMK-1 protect C. elegans from elevated bacterial proliferation in microgravity? C. elegans responds to intestinal infections via MAPK signaling cascades that induce antimicrobial defense factors—including C-type lectins, CUB domain–containing proteins, and antimicrobial peptides33. Consistent with this role, we observed significantly greater intestinal colonization by Enterobacter hormaechei CEent1 in pmk-1 mutants compared with wild-type N2 animals under simulated microgravity (Fig. 3B,D), suggesting that PMK-1–dependent immune defenses contribute to limiting bacterial proliferation in this environment.

To determine how simulated microgravity alters host transcriptional responses under different microbial contexts, we performed RNA sequencing on wild-type N2 and pmk-1 mutant C. elegans exposed to simulated microgravity (µG clinostat) or 1 G control conditions while fed either the standard laboratory strain Escherichia coli OP50 or E. hormaechei CEent1. Volcano plot analysis showed that wild-type animals fed E. coli OP50 exhibited a relatively limited transcriptional response to microgravity with only 57 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), whereas those fed E. hormaechei CEent1 displayed a markedly larger number of DEGs at 6,807 (Fig. 4A, B). In contrast, pmk-1 mutants fed CEent1 exhibited a distinct transcriptional profile characterized by altered DEG composition of 5,720 relative to wild-type animals (Fig. 4C). Across all conditions, differential expression was dominated by large-effect changes, such that applying a 1.5-fold change threshold ( | log₂ fold change | ≥ 0.585) captured the majority of responsive genes.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further highlighted diet- and genotype-dependent differences in host responses to microgravity. To assess functional patterns underlying these transcriptional changes, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using g:Profiler on genes meeting the differential expression criteria (raw p-value < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change | ≥ 0.585). Enrichment profiles differed substantially between dietary and genetic conditions (Fig. 4D–F).

In OP50-fed wild-type animals in simulated microgravity, enriched pathways were dominated by core metabolic and information-processing functions, including nucleotide metabolism, RNA processing, and DNA replication–associated pathways (Fig. 4D). In contrast, CEent1-fed wild-type animals showed enrichment of additional pathways associated with cellular signaling and protein turnover, including ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, endocytic trafficking, and Notch signaling (Fig. 4E).

In pmk-1 mutants fed CEent1 in simulated microgravity, KEGG enrichment revealed prominent representation of pathways involved in protein quality control, intracellular trafficking, and stress-responsive signaling, including ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, proteasome function, mTOR signaling, endocytosis, and Notch signaling (Fig. 4F). The substantial overlap in enriched pathways between CEent1-fed wild-type and pmk-1 mutant animals indicates that core transcriptional responses to microgravity are preserved across genotypes, while differences in DEG composition suggest that PMK-1 signaling modulates the structure and extent of these responses.

Together, these results demonstrate that host transcriptional responses to simulated microgravity are strongly influenced by microbial diet and immune signaling capacity, and that the transcriptional response differs when PMK-1 signaling is impaired. Rather than eliciting a uniform stress response, microgravity induces gene expression programs that differ in both magnitude and functional composition depending on host–microbe interactions.

Focusing on MAPK/PMK-1–dependent defenses, we screened 161 known antimicrobial effector genes (Fig. 5A) for microgravity-induced activation in N2 but not in pmk-1. We identified 40 genes upregulated >1.5-fold (p < 0.05) in wild-type under microgravity in CEent1; none of these were induced in wild-type in OP50, and five (abf-2, clec-199, clec-67, clec-72, clec-76) were downregulated. Of these 40, 21 failed to be induced in pmk-1 mutants in CEent1 (Table 1), which we designated as microgravity-specific PMK-1–dependent effectors. These include C-type lectins and CUB domain–containing proteins that likely play critical roles in regulating E. hormaechei colonization under microgravity. Discussion

Simulated microgravity alters the immune responses of Caenorhabditis elegans when exposed to the opportunistic pathogen Enterobacter hormaechei CEent1. Under simulated microgravity, worms exhibited reduced resistance to CEent1 gut colonization as well as PA14 colonization (Fig. 1). Although these effects were modest, the animals retained some capacity to compensate for immune disruption. Genetic experiments revealed that the p38 MAPK homolog PMK-1 is essential for defense in microgravity: unlike on Earth (1 G), pmk-1 mutants were significantly more susceptible to CEent1 than wild-type worms under both clinostat-simulated and spaceflight conditions (Fig. 3), consistent with PMK-1’s role in environmental stress adaptation36.

To identify downstream immune effectors, we conducted RNA-seq under simulated microgravity in three conditions: wild-type worms fed Escherichia coli OP50, wild-type worms infected with CEent1, and pmk-1 mutants infected with CEent1 (Fig. 4). We found 22 candidate genes, primarily C-type lectins and CUB-domain factors, that were upregulated in wild-type but not in pmk-1 mutants or the OP50 control (Fig. 5), implicating PMK-1–dependent regulation during CEent1 colonization in simulated microgravity. RNA interference of four PMK-1–dependent genes (K10D11.6, F56A4.2/clec-209, clec-67, and clec-8) significantly increased susceptibility to CEent1 (Fig. 6). In contrast, overexpression of clec-83 reduced colonization in dbl-1 mutants, and overexpression of F56A4.2/clec-209 diminished Enterobacter proliferation in pmk-1 mutants under simulated microgravity (Figs. 7 and 8). Together, these results suggest that the MAPK/PMK-1 pathway can compensate for weakened TGF-β/DBL-1 signaling in microgravity by inducing specific antimicrobial effectors that protect C. elegans from CEent1 infection (Fig. 9).Fig. 9Proposed schematic mechanism of TGFβ/DBL-1 and MAPK/PMK-1 pathways protecting C. elegans from increased Enterobacter colonization under normal Earth gravity and microgravity conditions.A Under normal gravity conditions, exposure to Enterobacter triggers TGFβ/DBL-1 signaling, which provides protection against infection in C. elegans. Previous studies using microgravity simulations and spaceflight experiments have consistently shown elevated pmk-1 expression in C. elegans37,65. Similarly, mouse macrophages cultured in RCCS exhibit p38 MAPK activation66. By contrast, the human MAPK14 gene, an ortholog of C. elegans pmk-1, undergoes significant but opposite changes in mRNA expression in two breast cancer cell lines exposed to microgravity simulated by a random positioning machine67. Moreover, analyses of p38 MAPK phosphorylation in human peripheral blood monocytes stimulated with lipopolysaccharide, used to model bacterial infection, reveal that p38 MAPK activation is maintained or slightly enhanced under spaceflight microgravity68. These findings, together with our results, suggest a general role for p38 MAPK signaling in spaceflight microgravity.

Effects of space on immune function have been suggested by previous gene and protein expression studies3,21,69,70 as well as changes in immune cell counts71. However, whether these alterations in immune function can increase the susceptibility to bacterial infection has yet to be demonstrated. Hammond et al.72,73 performed spaceflight virulence assays in C. elegans in which they assessed bacterial infection through an indirect method using residual bacterial measurements. A previous study also linked p38 MAPK attenuation to intestinal microbiome disruption under simulated microgravity; its findings originate from two separate models: Enteropathogenic E. coli–infected macrophages in RCCS and C. rodentium–infected hindlimb-unloaded mice74 lacking established direct mechanistic connections. Spaceflight decreases dbl-1 expression, and we infer that immune dysfunction allows an increase of Enterobacter infection in the gut. We did not demonstrate, however, that increased colonization of PA14 or CEent1 in simulated microgravity or space decreased survivability. Further analysis, such as longer time frames or other parameters of worm health may be necessary to establish whether pathogenicity and infection can increase in microgravity.

Furthermore, space can also increase bacterial virulence22,24,25, and alter its morphology, motility, growth and proliferation75. Although we demonstrated that microgravity does not affect E. hormaechei growth rate, we did not test whether space microgravity increases virulence of E. hormaechei on animal hosts. A previous report on E. cloacae’s proteomic profile under LSMMG revealed an increased oxidative stress response and reduced chaperone expression, suggesting potential adaptive mechanisms of Enterobacter sp. in microgravity76. Furthermore, transcriptomic analyses of astronauts’ gut samples revealed downregulation of genes essential for epithelial integrity and barrier function, resulting in increased permeability16 and potentially heightened susceptibility to intestinal microbial infection. Further experimentation is required to establish whether microgravity can alter E. hormachei virulence on animal hosts like C. elegans. It is important to note that there is no uniform response regarding bacterial responses to microgravity, as different bacteria and strains may exhibit contrasting effects77. This is an important consideration for future microgravity experiments involving various microbial strains.

Our findings highlight the critical role of PMK-1 and its downstream effectors in fine-tuning immune defenses when primary pathways, such as DBL-1/TGF-β, are compromised under microgravity. Previous transcriptomic and genetic studies have identified overlapping effectors between the DBL-1/TGF-β and PMK-1/p38 MAPK pathways, some of which remain inducible in a dbl-1 mutant background78,79 suggesting compensatory mechanisms. Although overexpressing individual genes did not fully restore resistance to wild-type levels, these data imply that multiple effectors may act synergistically or as an “antimicrobial cocktail” to regulate the gut microbiome under microgravity46. Overall, this study reveals a PMK-1–mediated response in microgravity that regulates the gut microbiome and enhances protection against CEent1 colonization.

What are the implications for humans, particularly astronauts exposed to space microgravity? Microgravity is known to alter immune function in astronauts, which could increase susceptibility to microbes during spaceflight2,21, placing astronauts at higher risk of illness in space. Our study demonstrates that immunocompromised pmk-1 mutants, despite exhibiting strong resistance to Enterobacter accumulation under normal 1 G gravity, become highly susceptible to colonization when exposed to microgravity. This finding suggests potential risks for individuals with underlying immune conditions, such as the elderly, during space travel.

In this study, we identified specific downstream immune effector genes that can ameliorate the negative effects of space microgravity upon increased expression. Identification of such immune effector genes in humans may be beneficial to protect astronauts and space travelers exposed to long-term space microgravity from infection. Thus, drugs or nutritional interventions that can potentially activate and increase expression of immune effector genes represent a viable strategy to mitigate the adverse effects of microgravity during spaceflight. Such interventions could benefit not only astronauts but also individuals with immunocompromised conditions and age-related immune decline on Earth against infection.

Methods

Worm strains and maintenance

The worm strains utilized in this study were the N2 wild-type and the following mutants: KU25 pmk-1(km25) IV, NU3 dbl-1(nk3) V, and RB2095 F56D6.2(ok2770) IV (International C. elegans Gene Knockout Consortium). All strains were obtained from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC) at the University of Minnesota, USA, which is funded by NIH Office of Research Infrastructure Programs (P40 OD010440). Worms were cultured on standard nematode growth medium (NGM) agar plates seeded with E. coli OP50 and maintained at 20°C, following established protocols80,81.

Bacterial strains

Standard E. coli OP50 was used for worm cultivation and maintenance. The fluorescently tagged E. hormaechei strain CEN2ent1-tdTomato (CEent1) was kindly provided by Michael Shapira, University of California, USA45. The pathogenic strain Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA14 and RNAi vector E. coli strain HT115 targeting the following genes were provided by the Yoon Laboratory, Yonsei University Wonju College of Medicine, South Korea: clec-8, clec-26, clec-28, clec-36, clec-56, clec-66, clec-89, clec-185, clec-187, clec-83, clec-170, clec-199, clec-209/F56A4.2, clec-225, C17H12.8, F53C11.1, H20E11.1, H20E11.3, and K10D11.6. An empty vector strain served as the control.

Bacterial culture and preparation

P. aeruginosa PA14 preparation was adapted from Anderson, Revtovich and Kirienko38. Frozen PA14 stock was streaked onto Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates and incubated for 16–24 h at 37 °C. A single colony was inoculated into 5 mL LB broth and incubated for approximately 16 h at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. Subsequently, 350 μL of the PA14 culture was spread-plated onto 9 cm Slow Kill (SK) agar plates (3 g NaCl, 3.5 g peptone, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4, 25 mL of phosphate buffer (amount per liter: 132 mL of K2HPO4 (1 M) and 868 mL of KH2PO4 (1 M)) and 18 g agar/L) and incubated for 16–24 h at 37 °C. PA14 bacteria were scraped from SK plates38 and resuspended in M9 cholesterol buffer. All procedures were performed in a Class II biological safety cabinet. Optical density (OD₆₀₀) was measured using a spectrophotometer (Eppendorf BioPhotometer or Thermo Scientific Genesys 50 UV-Vis Spectrophotometer).

E. hormaechei strain CEent1-tdTomato was inoculated in LB broth containing 100 µg/mL kanamycin and incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. The inoculum was then washed with either S-basal buffer (for spaceflight and RNAi experiments) or M9 buffer (for clinostat microgravity experiments) to obtain the bacterial pellet.

Clinostat experiments

For PA14 experiments, eggs were collected from bleached gravid adult worms81 in M9 buffer and incubated overnight at 20 °C. Approximately 200 L1 larvae were cultured on E. coli OP50-seeded NGM plates and incubated at 25 °C for ~2 days post-L1 feeding until reaching adulthood. Adult worms were then wash-transferred into 86x49x8 mm Disposable Culture Chambers (DCC or DCC-T) (Chiyoda Corporation, Japan) containing P. aeruginosa PA14 in 0.75% methylcellulose (Sigma-Aldrich, M0512-100G, 4000 cP) prepared in M9 cholesterol buffer and SK media for a total volume of approximately 4.8 mL (final adjusted OD₆₀₀ = 0.2).

For CEent1 experiments, eggs were collected from bleached gravid adults81 in M9 buffer and incubated overnight at 20 °C. Approximately 3000 L1 larvae were transferred into 86x49x8 mm disposable culture chambers (DCC or DCC-T) (Chiyoda Corporation, Japan) containing a 10x-concentrated CEent1 suspension in 0.75% methylcellulose (Sigma-Aldrich, M0512-100G, 4000 cP) prepared in M9 cholesterol buffer for a total volume of ~4.8 mL.

For clinostat microgravity conditions, one DCC was attached to the Portable Microgravity Simulator (PMS-VII; Advanced Engineering Services Co., Ltd.) set at 5.5 rpm on the X-axis and 6.5 rpm on the Y-axis for 2 days at 25° for PA14 and 3 days at 20° for CEent1. A second DCC was placed on a rocking shaker (Labogene Laboshaker R100) set at 42 rpm at 25 °C or 20 °C to serve as the Earth (1 G) gravity control. After 2 days or 3 days, worms in each DCC were observed under a stereo microscope (Olympus SZ51), and PA14 animals were considered dead when they remained immobile following mechanical stimulation. Additionally, survival analysis of worms exposed to CEent1 was conducted by examining differential interference contrast (DIC) and fluorescence images of individual worms following clinostat-simulated microgravity and 1 G control conditions. Worms were classified as deceased when they appeared visually non-viable and exhibited autofluorescence throughout the body.

Extraction and quantification of ingested Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA14

Following the clinostat microgravity experiment, surviving worms were counted and extracted from the DCC. No less than 10 live worms were transferred to unseeded NGM plates. Washing and lysis of C. elegans using a hand-held homogenizer was performed according to the protocol described by Walker, Bhargava, Vaziriyan-Sani, Brust and Czyz82 to eliminate external bacteria and extract the intestinal bacterial load. The homogenized worm mixture was serially diluted using 0.9% normal saline solution (NSS) and spread-plated on 9 cm LB agar plates in 1 to 3 ten-fold dilutions. The final wash solution and plain NSS were also plated to serve as controls. All plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. CFUs were counted and calculated by averaging CFUs per worm.

Axenic culture and colony forming unit (CFU) assays

Simulated microgravity experiments on CEent1 and PA14 were conducted using the same bacterial preparation and clinostat protocols as stated above, excluding worms. For PA14, quantification was performed according to the same protocol stated above. Bacterial suspensions of CEent1 were washed in M9 buffer, serially diluted using 0.9% normal saline solution (NSS) and spread-plated on 9 cm LB agar plates with 100 µg/mL kanamycin in 6 to 8 ten-fold dilutions. Plain NSS was also plated to serve as control. All plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. CFUs were counted and calculated by averaging cells per milliliter.

Spaceflight experiments

Spaceflight experiments were conducted under the Molecular Muscle Experiment 2 (MME-2) in collaboration with the European Space Agency (ESA). Protocols for both flight and ground controls were adapted from the initial MME mission in 201848–50. N2 wild-type and pmk-1 mutant C. elegans strains were used in this mission. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, bleach-synchronized L1 larvae were suspended in polyethylene bags containing a 10x-concentrated CEent1 culture in 6.5 mL S-basal buffer with cholesterol, sealed, then stored at 8 °C pre-launch to arrest development.

On June 3, 2021, samples were loaded into the SpaceX Cargo Dragon 2 (C209) capsule and launched aboard the Falcon 9 rocket for the CRS-22 mission, arriving at the ISS on June 5, 2021. ESA astronaut Thomas Pesquet maintained the cultures in the ISS Columbus module’s KUBIK incubator at 20 °C for ~5.5 days, during which the worm population was expected to consist of mixed P0 and F1 generation non-starved healthy adults based on analog ground experiments, before transferring the samples to the Minus Eighty-Degree Laboratory Freezer for ISS (MELFI). While discrimination between P0 and F1 worms is not definitive, our ground-based trials and prior spaceflight experiment from MME-148 have shown that the parental generation, which are ~4–5 day old adults, are noticeably larger than 1–2 day old F1 generation adults, and that the worm population was comprised predominantly of F1 worms. Frozen samples were returned to Earth and were stored at Ohio University (Ohio, USA) on July 20, 2021, along with parallel ground controls at the same conditions as spaceflight that were maintained at 1 G at NASA’s Kennedy Space Center (Florida) during the same period as the MME-2 mission. All samples were first shipped frozen to Ohio University and later shipped frozen to and analyzed at Yonsei University Mirae Campus (South Korea) in July 2022.

Fluorescence imaging

Frozen spaceflight worms were thawed and rinsed in S-basal buffer to remove surface bacteria. Live worms from clinostat microgravity and RNAi experiments were washed in M9 buffer and S-basal buffer, respectively, then immobilized with 10 mM levamisole. Specimens were mounted on 2% agarose pads, and fluorescent images were acquired on an Olympus BX50 microscope with a DP74 camera. CEent1 fluorescence was quantified in Fiji ImageJ83 using a protocol adapted from Choi, Bodkhe, Pees, Kim, Berg, Monnin, Cho, Narayan, Deller, Savage-Dunn and Shapira41. For each worm, the entire intestinal region was outlined-selected, the integrated density measured, and background fluorescence, normalized to gut area was subtracted. Because intact bacteria can be present in the pharynx, which can cause bacterial load prior to the terminal valve84, we excluded the pharynx region and quantified only the intestinal lumen distal to the pharyngeal-intestine valve. Worms that showed signs of death, such as autofluorescence throughout the body were censured during counting. All fluorescence measurements remained within the instrument’s linear dynamic range and did not saturate, as evidenced by strip plots showing centrally clustered intensities with no accumulation at the detector’s maximum readout.

RNA extraction and sequencing

RNA from the clinostat microgravity and 1 G control worms was stabilized by TRIzol reagent and extracted using the Direct-zol™ RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and quantity were assessed with a NanoDrop™ Lite Spectrophotometer (Cat. no. ND-LITE-PR). Extracted RNA was frozen and submitted to Macrogen NGS Service for sequencing, preprocessing, and analysis (TruSeq Stranded mRNA Reference Guide # 1000000040498 v00). Differential expression was assessed using the Negative Binomial Wald Test (nbinomWaldTest) in DESeq2. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed if |fold change | ≥ 2 and p < 0.05. For visualization purposes in Fig. 5A, all genes with |FC | > 0 were included without statistical criteria. For Fig. 5B, genes with |FC | ≥ 1.5 and p < 0.05 were displayed.

RNAi screening

Empty vector and RNAi sequence-carrying E. coli strain HT115 were inoculated in LB broth with 100 µg/mL ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37 °C while shaking at 200 rpm. Approximately 500 µL of the culture was seeded onto each 6 cm NGM plate and air-dried. Four to six young adults were transferred to each plate and incubated at 20 °C for 3 days to yield predominantly L4 to young adult F1 generation worms. Worms were then washed from plates with S-basal buffer and transferred to 25 cm² vented cell culture flasks (Corning Flask, canted neck with 0.2 µm vent cap) containing a 1x CEent1 suspension in 6 mL S-medium (per liter: 974 mL S-basal, 10 mL 1 M potassium citrate pH 6, 10 mL trace metals, 3 mL 1 M CaCl₂, 3 mL 1 M MgSO₄, 1 mL 5 mg/mL cholesterol in ethanol). Cultures were incubated on a rocking shaker (8 rpm) at 20 °C for 24 h. Worms were then washed and prepared for fluorescence imaging as described previously, selecting only day 1–2 adults to obtain worms that were initially exposed to CEent1 at the L4 to young adult stages.

Generation of transgenic overexpression mutant worms

Individual effector genes (K10D11.6, F56A4.2/clec-209, clec-67, clec-8, and H20E11.1), including 2 to 3 kb of upstream promoter sequences, were amplified from worm genomic DNA by PCR and subcloned into the pCR™Blunt II-TOPO™ cloning vector. Microinjection was used to overexpress individual effector genes in either dbl-1(nk3) V or pmk-1(km25) IV backgrounds. Transgenic C. elegans were generated by germline microinjection, and transgenic animals were selected based on unc-122::GFP fluorescence. Each injection mix contained 50 ng/μL of the target plasmid, 30 ng/μL unc-122::GFP as a selection marker, and 20 ng/μL empty pUC19 vector to achieve a total concentration of 100 ng/μL. Transgenic worms were maintained by picking individuals expressing unc-122::GFP fluorescence. Generated strains are listed in Table S2.

Transgenic dbl-1 mutant assay and pmk-1 mutant simulated microgravity experiments

Eggs were collected from bleached gravid dbl-1 transgenic adult worms in S-basal buffer and incubated overnight at 20 °C. About 200 L1 larvae were transferred to OP50 seeded NGM plates and incubated at 20 °C for 2 days to yield predominantly L4 to young adult worms. Worms were then washed from plates with S-basal buffer and transferred to 25 cm² vented cell culture flasks containing a 1x CEent1 suspension in 6 mL S-medium and incubated on a rocking shaker (8 rpm) at 20 °C for 24 h. Worms were then washed and prepared for fluorescence imaging as described above. Worms expressing the unc-122::GFP marker were scored as dbl-1 overexpressors, while non-fluorescent worms served as dbl-1 controls.

Eggs were collected from bleached gravid pmk-1 transgenic adult worms in M9 buffer and incubated overnight at 20 °C. Simulated microgravity experiments with CEent1 and fluorescence imaging were conducted as described above. Animals expressing unc-122::GFP marker were scored as pmk-1 overexpressors, and non-fluorescent worms served as pmk-1 controls.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses (as detailed in each figure legend) were performed using Microsoft Excel software (Microsoft, USA) and GraphPad Prism (Prism 10 for Windows 64-bit, GraphPad Software, LLC). RNA-seq data were processed by Macrogen NGS Service, and differential gene expression was analyzed using DESeq2 with a negative binomial Wald test.
