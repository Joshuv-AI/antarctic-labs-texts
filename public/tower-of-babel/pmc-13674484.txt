Noninvasive ultrashort wave neuromodulation enhances glymphatic clearance to alleviate traumatic brain edema and Alzheimer’s pathology
Authors: Jinwei Liu, Chuang Xu, Huaqiang Ruan, Kun Zhang, Fang Wang, Qiaozhen Qin, Xiaotong Li, Yue Chen, Yifei Tan, Yuanrong Qiu, Haonan Chen, Yan Wang, Zeji Yang, Yongyi Zhang, Yaqiong Zhu, Chenghao Chang, Xiaoxia Jiang, Nan Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The glymphatic system clears cerebral waste, yet strategies to enhance its function remain elusive. Here, we show that 40.68 MHz ultrashort wave (USW) rapidly enhances glymphatic bulk flow in healthy mice within 10 minutes. USW potentiates both glymphatic influx and efflux, concomitant with increased Delta wave neuronal synchronization, as confirmed by local field potentials and electroencephalograms. Chemogenetic silencing confirms that USW-driven glymphatic enhancement depends on neuronal activity, while pharmacological blockade of aquaporin-4 (AQP4) abolishes the effects, revealing dual reliance on neural dynamics and astrocytic water channels. Mechanistically, USW-induced Delta oscillations promote AQP4 polarization, coupling synchronized firing to perivascular AQP4 redistribution. Moreover, USW ameliorates traumatic brain injury–induced cerebral edema and improves cognitive deficits in Alzheimer’s mice by restoring glymphatic function. Our findings establish USW as a non-invasive neuromodulatory strategy for enhancing glymphatic clearance, offering therapeutic potential for neurological disorders characterized by glymphatic dysfunction.

INTRODUCTION

The discovery of the glymphatic system has revolutionized our understanding of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation and metabolic waste clearance within the central nervous system (CNS) (1, 2). Under physiological conditions, CSF enters the brain parenchyma via periarterial spaces, exchanges solutes with interstitial fluid (ISF) facilitated by aquaporin-4 (AQP4) channels on astrocytic endfeet, and subsequently clears metabolic waste through perivenous pathways (3, 4). Glymphatic dysfunction is implicated in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease, where impaired clearance leads to the pathological accumulation of amyloid-β and tau proteins, accelerating disease progression (5, 6). Furthermore, impaired glymphatic function is closely associated with the development of acute cerebral edema following traumatic brain injury (TBI) and stroke (7, 8). Given its critical role in the pathophysiology of diverse CNS disorders (9, 10), exploring effective strategies to modulate glymphatic function holds considerable therapeutic promise.

AQP4 serves as the gatekeeper molecule regulating fluid movement within the glymphatic system, and its polarized distribution constitutes a paramount prerequisite for proper glymphatic function (11). Traditionally, vascular pulsations constitute the dominant hydrodynamic driver propelling glymphatic flow (12, 13). However, the absence of a substantial pressure gradient between periarterial and perivenous spaces challenges the sufficiency of vascular pulsation alone to drive CSF perfusion through the high-resistance brain parenchyma (14). Emerging evidence suggests that synchronized neuronal activity may represent a dominant regulatory factor, capable of directly propelling CSF-ISF exchange within the interstitium (15) and remotely modulating vascular pulsations via neurovascular coupling (16). This mechanistic insight highlights a key direction for glymphatic modulation: strategies that target neuronal oscillations may achieve more robust enhancement of glymphatic clearance.

Physical modalities, offering advantages of non-invasiveness, safety, and minimal side effects, represent promising translational tools for glymphatic modulation (12, 13, 17–19). Representative strategies such as 40 Hz sensory stimulation and low-intensity ultrasound follow a consistent pattern of mechanistic discovery: they were initially investigated for their well-documented anti-inflammatory and cerebral hemodynamic regulatory effects in neurological disorders (20, 21); subsequent studies further revealed their capacity to enhance glymphatic flow, primarily by potentiating arterial pulsatility and promoting AQP4 polarization (13, 18). Nevertheless, most existing physical modalities act predominantly on vascular pathways, and non-invasive interventions that directly boost glymphatic function via modulating neuronal oscillations remain largely underexplored. Ultrashort wave (USW), a non-ionizing electromagnetic wave within the 30–300 MHz frequency band, is a widely used physical therapy in clinical rehabilitation. Accumulating preclinical evidence has demonstrated its multifaceted neuroprotective effects, including suppression of neuroinflammation, improvement of cerebral microcirculation, and promotion of tissue repair across multiple CNS disorder models (22–24). Following the discovery paradigm of other physical modalities, we postulate that USW may likewise exert regulatory influences on the glymphatic system—yet this possibility has not been investigated to date. TBI disrupts glymphatic function, and the consequent failure to promptly clear cellular debris activates microglia and astrocytes, thereby triggering neuroinflammation (7, 25, 26). Although prior studies have shown that USW attenuates acute neuroinflammation after TBI (22), whether this beneficial effect is mediated by improved glymphatic clearance remains an open question that warrants further investigation.

In this study, we demonstrate that USW enhances glymphatic fluid flow by potentiating Delta oscillations and increasing AQP4 polarization. In addition, increased Delta oscillation power enhances AQP4 polarization. Crucially, in mouse models of post-traumatic cerebral edema and Alzheimer’s disease, USW ameliorated disease-associated phenotypes and improved cognitive function by rectifying glymphatic impairment. These findings not only elucidate the mechanism by which USW modulates the glymphatic system but also identify a promising therapeutic strategy for glymphatic-related pathologies.

RESULTS

Ultrashort wave enhances glymphatic bulk flow

To investigate the impact of USW on overall glymphatic flow in healthy mice, a series of in vivo and in vitro experiments were performed. Given that therapeutic USW is commonly applied at 40.68 MHz and exhibits important biological effects at non-thermal doses, we evaluated the effects of different stimulation durations on glymphatic drainage to identify optimal parameters. Twenty minutes after the fluorescent tracer (OVA-647) was injected into the cisterna magna of mice, USW was applied for 10 min, 20 min, or 30 min, respectively (Fig. 1A). USW significantly accelerated the rate of tracer accumulation in the superficial cervical lymph nodes compared to controls during 60 minutes postinjection (Fig. 1, B and C). Quantification at the 60-min endpoint revealed significantly stronger OVA-647 fluorescence intensity within the superficial cervical lymph nodes of mice receiving 10-min or 20-min USW compared to controls (Fig. 1D). At the 120-min endpoint, no significant difference in tracer fluorescence intensity within the superficial cervical lymph nodes was detected across the four groups (Fig. 1E). Similarly, fluorescence intensity in the deep cervical lymph nodes was significantly elevated in the 10-min and 30-min USW groups relative to controls at 60-min endpoint (Fig. 1, F to H). Assessment of tracer accumulation across both lymph node groups indicated that a single 10-min USW session effectively enhanced glymphatic clearance, while longer durations of stimulation (20 or 30 min) did not yield additional enhancement. Therefore, the 10-min USW protocol was selected for subsequent mechanistic studies.

Next, we dissected the effect of USW specifically on the distinct influx and efflux phases of glymphatic by quantifying tracer signal intensity within the brain parenchyma at 1 h and 7 h postinjection (fig. S1, A and G). During the influx phase, mice receiving USW exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity of the intracisternally delivered tracer within the brain compared to control mice (fig. S1, B to F), indicating enhanced glymphatic influx. Conversely, during the efflux phase, the USW-treated group displayed significantly lower residual tracer signal intensity within the brain parenchyma than controls (fig. S1, H to L), demonstrating potentiated glymphatic efflux and waste clearance.

Ultrashort wave potentiates glymphatic flow by enhancing neuronal synchronization

Given that synchronized neuronal firing can drive glymphatic waste clearance (15), we investigated whether USW influences neuronal dynamics to propel CSF-ISF exchange. We implanted multi-channel micro-wire electrode arrays into the hippocampus of mice to record local field potentials (LFPs) before, during, and after USW stimulation. Analysis revealed a significant increase in low-frequency oscillation power specifically during USW stimulation compared to pre- and post-stimulation baselines (Fig. 2, A to C). Power spectral density analysis across frequency bands demonstrated a broad enhancement by USW, but the power increase within the Delta band (0.5–4 Hz) far exceeded that of other bands (Fig. 2, D and E). Furthermore, the relative power contribution of Delta oscillations increased approximately 20-fold during USW stimulation, while the contributions of mid- and high-frequency bands decreased significantly (Fig. 2F). To assess global brain activity, we recorded electroencephalograms (EEG) from the frontal and parietal cortices during USW application. Consistent with hippocampal LFP findings, EEG analysis confirmed a significant elevation in low-frequency oscillation power (fig. S2, A to C). At the whole-brain spatial scale, USW increased power across all frequency bands, yet the potentiation of Delta oscillations remained the most pronounced effect, accompanied by a significant increase in their relative power contribution (fig. S2, D to F). These results indicate that USW robustly potentiates Delta oscillation power, suggesting an alteration in neuronal firing patterns conducive to enhanced synchronization.

The marked increase in low-frequency power during USW prompted us to rigorously exclude non-neuronal artifacts. We recorded signals from the micro-wire arrays and post-mortem mice before, during, and after USW stimulation. Critically, neither the micro-wire arrays themselves nor the post-mortem brain tissue exhibited significant changes in oscillation power with or without USW (fig. S3). This confirms that the observed power increases originate specifically from the electrophysiological activity of living neurons.

To directly explore whether USW enhances glymphatic flow via Delta oscillation-induced neuronal synchronization, we employed chemogenetic inhibition of neuronal firing using pharmacologically selective actuator modules coupled with the glycine receptor (PSAMs-GlyR) (15). PSAM-GlyR was expressed in the unilateral hippocampus, and neuronal activity was significantly suppressed following administration of the ultrapotent pharmacologically selective effector molecule 792 (uPSEM792) (Fig. 3, A and B). First, we assessed the impact of neuronal silencing on USW-enhanced glymphatic influx. Mice received intraperitoneal (i.p.) uPSEM792 to inhibit neuronal firing. One hour later, OVA-647 tracer was injected intracisternally, followed immediately by USW stimulation (Fig. 3C). In control mice expressing EGFP, tracer influx was comparable between bilateral hippocampi (Fig. 3D). In contrast, PSAM-expressing mice exhibited significantly reduced tracer influx specifically within the chemogenetically silenced hippocampus compared to the contralateral, non-silenced side (Fig. 3E). Next, OVA-647 was injected intracisternally, and uPSEM792 was administered i.p. at 3 h postinjection to inhibit neuronal activity during the efflux phase. USW was applied at 6 h, and brains were collected at 7 h (Fig. 3F). Control mice showed similar residual tracer levels in both hippocampi (Fig. 3G). Conversely, in PSAM-expressing mice, the silenced hippocampal region retained significantly higher levels of residual tracer compared to the non-silenced contralateral side (Fig. 3H).

AQP4 mediates ultrashort wave-induced enhancement of glymphatic function

AQP4 serves as a critical structural component and integral effector molecule of the glymphatic system. We next investigated the role of AQP4 polarization in the USW-mediated glymphatic enhancement. Immunofluorescence co-staining for AQP4 and the endothelial marker CD31 was performed. The AQP4 polarization index was quantified by analyzing perivascular AQP4 signal intensity in randomly selected vessels within the dorsal cortex, lateral cortex, ventral cortex, and hippocampus (Fig. 4A). This analysis revealed a significantly higher AQP4 polarization index in the USW group compared to controls (Fig. 4, B and C). Furthermore, USW stimulation significantly increased total AQP4 protein expression levels (Fig. 4, D and E). To determine whether the promoting effects of USW depend functionally on AQP4, we employed the specific AQP4 inhibitor TGN-020. For the glymphatic influx phase, mice received an i.p. of TGN-020. Thirty minutes later, OVA-647 tracer was injected intracisternally, followed immediately by USW stimulation. Brains were collected 1 h post-tracer injection (Fig. 5A). Quantification showed no significant difference in tracer influx into the brain parenchyma between the USW group and controls following AQP4 blockade (Fig. 5, B to E). For the glymphatic efflux phase, tracer was first injected intracisternally. At 5.5 hours postinjection, mice received i.p. TGN-020. Thirty minutes later, USW stimulation was applied, and brains were collected at 7 hours posttracer injection (Fig. 5F). Similarly, under AQP4 inhibition, the USW group showed no reduction in residual tracer signal within the brain compared to controls, indicating impaired efflux potentiation (Fig. 5, G to J). Finally, we assessed cervical lymphatic drainage. Following TGN-020 administration and USW treatment, neither the fluorescence intensity nor the distribution area of the tracer within the superficial or deep cervical lymph nodes differed significantly between the USW group and controls (Fig. 5, K to O). Collectively, these results demonstrate that pharmacological blockade of AQP4 function completely abolishes the ability of USW to enhance glymphatic influx, efflux, and drainage.

Neuronal synchronization enhances AQP4 polarization

AQP4 polarization at perivascular sites exhibits diurnal variation, reaching its peak during the rest phase when Delta oscillation power is characteristically elevated and neuronal activity is highly synchronized (27). Notably, the degree of neuronal synchronization positively correlates with Delta oscillation power (15, 28). Our findings demonstrate that USW significantly enhances Delta oscillation power while concurrently increasing the AQP4 polarization index. This suggests a potential mechanistic link between synchronized neuronal firing and AQP4 polarization, offering a possible explanation for how neural dynamics regulate glymphatic function. USW stimulation robustly increased Delta oscillation power. However, this enhancement was significantly attenuated when USW was applied following chemogenetic suppression of neuronal firing (Fig. 6, A and B). Consequently, we utilized Delta oscillation power as an experimental variable and AQP4 polarization level as the readout to directly assess their relationship. Employing the established PSAM-GlyR chemogenetic strategy, we observed comparable AQP4 polarization in the bilateral hippocampi of EGFP-expressing control mice following i.p. uPSEM792 and subsequent USW stimulation (Fig. 6, C and D). However, in PSAM-GlyR mice, AQP4 polarization in the hippocampal region with silenced neuronal activity (characterized by low Delta power) was significantly lower than that in the contralateral unsilenced hippocampus (characterized by high Delta power) (Fig. 6, E and F). These results indicate that heightened Delta oscillation power potentiates AQP4 polarization.

Ultrashort wave mitigates post-traumatic cerebral edema and improves functional outcomes

To evaluate the clinical translational potential of USW-mediated glymphatic enhancement, we conducted further investigations using TBI and AD mouse models. OVA-647 was injected into the cisterna magna 5 minutes prior to closed-skull TBI induction, and USW was initiated immediately post-injury (Fig. 7, A and B). In vivo (Fig. 7, C and D) and in vitro experiments (Fig. 7, E and F) demonstrated impaired glymphatic drainage function following TBI, while ultrashort wave intervention significantly ameliorated glymphatic clearance. Furthermore, USW ameliorated the TBI-induced deficit in glymphatic influx (Fig. 7, G and H). Consistent with glymphatic dysfunction, TBI reduced the AQP4 polarization index. Notably, USW significantly enhanced Delta oscillation power in TBI mice (Fig. 7I, fig. S4) and partly restored AQP4 polarization distribution at perivascular sites (Fig. 7, J and K). As impaired glymphatic clearance is a major contributor to acute post-traumatic cerebral edema, we evaluated whether USW-mediated restoration of glymphatic function could mitigate brain swelling (Fig. 7L). At 1 hour post-injury, TBI increased brain water content in both cerebral hemispheres compared to sham controls. USW significantly reduced hemispheric water content in injured mice (Fig. 7M). Furthermore, repeated USW treatment over 3 days post-TBI continued to significantly reduce brain water content compared to untreated TBI controls (Fig. 7N). The reduction in cerebral edema by USW correlated with improved behavioral outcomes across both acute and chronic post-injury phases (Fig. 8A). USW-treated TBI mice exhibited enhanced motor ability (Fig. 8, B to F), improved cognitive function (Fig. 8, G and H), and ameliorated affective deficits including anxiety-like (Fig. 8D) and depression-like behaviors (Fig. 8, I and J).

Ultrashort wave enhances glymphatic clearance and ameliorates cognitive deficits in an Alzheimer’s disease model

We next evaluated the therapeutic potential of USW in the 5 × FAD mouse model of AD. Following 7 consecutive days of USW treatment, OVA-647 tracer was injected intracisternally to assess glymphatic function. Compared to untreated 5 × FAD controls, USW treatment significantly enhanced glymphatic influx into the brain parenchyma (Fig. 9, A and B) and potentiated overall glymphatic clearance efficiency (Fig. 9, C to E). Furthermore, USW treatment significantly increased Delta oscillation power (Fig. 9F, fig. S5) and augmented AQP4 polarization at perivascular sites (Fig. 9, G and H) in 5 × FAD mice, consistent with the mechanisms observed in healthy and TBI models. This enhanced glymphatic flow significantly reduced parenchymal amyloid-β burden in USW-treated 5 × FAD mice (Fig. 9, I and J). Together, improved glymphatic function and reduced amyloid-β deposition mediate the amelioration of cognitive function. USW-treated 5 × FAD mice exhibited a significantly increased preference for the novel arm in the Y-maze test and enhanced novel object recognition ability (Fig. 9K), indicating improved spatial and recognition memory. Critically, these cognitive benefits persisted for at least one month following the cessation of USW treatment (Fig. 9L).

DISCUSSION

Impaired glymphatic function, characterized by disrupted CSF dynamics and pathological accumulation of metabolic waste, is intimately linked to the pathogenesis and progression of diverse CNS disorders (29). Our study demonstrates that USW enhances glymphatic flow by potentiating low-frequency neural oscillations and augmenting AQP4 polarization (Fig. 10). Crucially, this intervention ameliorates post-traumatic cerebral edema and cognitive deficits in the Alzheimer’s disease model, presenting a promising physical modality for treating glymphatic-related pathologies.

Schematic overview of the USW-enhanced glymphatic pathway.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) enters the brain parenchyma via the perivascular spaces surrounding arteries, exchanges solutes with interstitial fluid (ISF), and subsequently removes metabolic waste through the perivascular spaces of veins. As illustrated, this glymphatic flux is markedly reduced in traumatic brain injury (TBI) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Ultrashort wave (USW) stimulation restores efficiency by synchronizing neural electrical activity and reinforcing polarized localization of the astrocytic water-channel AQP4, thereby accelerating glymphatic clearance of neurotoxic proteins.

During wakefulness, neuronal firing is typically desynchronized, predominantly manifesting as high-frequency oscillations (30). In contrast, sleep is characterized by synchronized neuronal activity dominated by low-frequency Delta oscillations (31). The power of Delta oscillations positively correlates with the degree of neuronal synchronization, which is notably higher during sleep compared to wakefulness (28). Notably, we found that USW robustly enhances Delta oscillation power in awake mice, inducing an electrophysiological signature remarkably reminiscent of that observed during sleep (32). This finding aligns with work by Kipnis and colleagues, who demonstrated that optogenetically synthesized low-frequency oscillations (1 Hz and 8 Hz) directly enhance glymphatic flow in mice (15). Similarly, electrical stimulation mimicking sleep-associated slow-wave activity (4 Hz) delivered to awake macaques improved perceptual acuity and behavioral flexibility post-stimulation (28), potentially linked to a transient boost in glymphatic clearance. Given the well-established association between enhanced low-frequency oscillation power during sleep or anaesthesia and potentiated glymphatic function (27, 33), our results suggest that USW may provide an additional clearance window for brain waste removal during wakefulness by enhancing Delta oscillations and glymphatic flow. The precise mechanism by which USW alters neuronal firing patterns to favor Delta synchronization remains to be fully elucidated. We postulate that USW may potentially alter neuronal membrane potential thresholds or intrinsic excitability, thereby modulating action potential firing rates and facilitating the coordinated, synchronous neuronal activity underlying robust Delta oscillations.

Within the CNS, neurons and astrocytes form dynamic, functionally coupled networks that cooperatively regulate higher cognitive functions, including learning, memory, and emotional processing (34, 35). While neuronal activity has been identified as a driver of glymphatic function, its precise molecular targets and downstream mechanisms remain incompletely defined. AQP4 polarization is paramount for efficient glymphatic flow (12, 18). Our findings demonstrate that USW not only enhances Delta oscillation power but also increases AQP4 polarization. Critically, chemogenetic suppression of neuronal firing significantly attenuated the USW-induced boost in Delta power and concurrently reduced the AQP4 polarization index specifically within the silenced hippocampal region compared to the contralateral, active side. This indicates a quantitative relationship between Delta oscillation power and the degree of AQP4 polarization, strongly suggesting functional coupling between synchronized neuronal firing and AQP4-dependent glymphatic facilitation. Notably, the circadian regulation of both neuronal synchronization and AQP4 polarization exhibits striking parallels. During sleep, neuronal populations engage in highly synchronized, Delta oscillation-dominant activity patterns, coinciding with significantly enhanced glymphatic clearance compared to wakefulness (32). Concomitantly, AQP4 polarization on astrocytic endfeet peaks during the sleep phase, and genetic ablation of AQP4 abolishes this diurnal variation in glymphatic flow (27). Furthermore, Hablitz et al. reported that anaesthetic-induced increases in Delta oscillation power positively correlate with glymphatic influx (36), while Wang et al. demonstrated that the dexmedetomidine augments both AQP4 protein expression and polarization, improving glymphatic flow (37). These collective observations further substantiate a potential mechanistic link between Delta oscillations and AQP4 polarization status. Given the established co-variation of neuronal synchronization, AQP4 polarization, and glymphatic clearance efficiency across both circadian cycles and anaesthetized states, combined with our observation of a specific Delta oscillation-AQP4 polarization relationship, we propose that synchronized neuronal firing acts as a pivotal orchestrator. It likely serves as a key upstream regulator of AQP4 polarization and, consequently, a master coordinator of glymphatic function.

TBI and AD represent archetypal examples of acute impairment and chronic degeneration, respectively, within the glymphatic system (15, 17). Our findings demonstrate that USW effectively restores glymphatic drainage in TBI mice, mitigating acute cerebral edema, and ameliorating deficits in cognitive and motor function, as well as anxiety- and depression-like behaviors. Similarly, in the AD model, USW enhances glymphatic flow, promotes amyloid-β clearance, and improves long-term spatial memory. Notably, while TBI disrupts both glymphatic drainage and influx (7), USW treatment also rescued the impaired influx in TBI mice. This restoration of influx is critical, as it facilitates the perfusion of fresh CSF into the parenchyma and the potential clearance of cellular debris generated by the initial injury. Under physiological conditions, the glymphatic system primarily operates during sleep to clear metabolic waste (38). Critically, both TBI and AD are intrinsically linked to sleep disruption and circadian rhythm dysfunction (39, 40). Approximately 46% of chronic TBI patients suffer from sleep disorders (41), characterized by reduced sleep spindle density and increased epileptiform spikes, severely compromising sleep quality (42). AD pathology exhibits a bidirectional relationship with sleep: amyloid-β plaques and neurofibrillary tau tangles damage sleep-regulatory nuclei like the suprachiasmatic nucleus, leading to sleep fragmentation and diminished slow-wave sleep (SWS) (40). Crucially, SWS is associated with 60% increase in CSF flow, driving the clearance of amyloid-β and tau (32); conversely, sleep deprivation impedes CSF dynamics and pathogenic protein removal (43). In both TBI and AD mice, USW stimulation induced a significant increase in Delta oscillation power – the electrophysiological hallmark of SWS. This suggests that USW may compensate for glymphatic clearance deficits caused by sleep disturbances by inducing neuronal synchronization, thereby creating a state conducive to waste removal even outside natural sleep periods. Furthermore, disrupted AQP4 polarization is a common feature in both TBI and AD pathology (17, 26, 44). USW treatment effectively restored AQP4 polarization in both disease models. Wu et al. demonstrated that flickering 40 Hz blue light exposure (1 h/day for 2 weeks) activates visual neural circuits to rescue glymphatic dysfunction in AD mice (17), while USW protocol achieved comparable glymphatic enhancement and improved cognitive performance with only 10-minute daily sessions over 7 days, indicating substantially higher temporal efficiency. Notably, whether this photostimulation paradigm alleviates acute glymphatic impairment immediately after TBI remains unexplored, raising critical questions about the universal applicability of 40 Hz neuromodulation for diverse neuropathological conditions. Collectively, our results indicate that USW modulates glymphatic function through enhancing neuronal synchronization and AQP4 polarization. Notably, this regulatory effect of USW is evident not only under physiological conditions but also across various pathological states characterized by glymphatic failure. This underscores the broad therapeutic potential of USW for glymphatic-related CNS disorders.

Several physical neuromodulation modalities have been used to regulate the glymphatic system (13, 17, 18, 45), such as 40 Hz sensory stimulation, very low-intensity ultrasound, and transcranial direct current stimulation. These physical neuromodulation modalities can ameliorate impaired glymphatic system function primarily by strengthening vascular pulsation and AQP4 polarization, thereby enhancing glymphatic flow and accelerating the clearance of endogenous or exogenous amyloid-β in the brain (13, 17). Whether these modalities affect neuronal oscillation patterns remains incompletely understood. Although transcranial direct current stimulation can enhance Delta oscillations, its glymphatic-promoting effect emerges approximately 30 minutes after stimulation, showing a delayed onset compared with the immediate enhancement induced by USW (45). Additionally, their single-session and cumulative intervention durations are relatively long, such as 40 Hz sensory stimulation (12, 17), and some require high operational expertise (18, 46). In contrast, USW can significantly enhance synchronized neuronal firing—which is more likely to play a dominant role in regulating the glymphatic system—and promote AQP4 polarization. Specifically, a single 10-minute USW session suffices to alleviate cerebral edema in TBI mice, whereas just 7 consecutive days of treatment ameliorates cognitive deficits in AD mouse models. Therefore, USW offers the advantages of brief single-session duration, short intervention cycles, and ease of administration features that not only favor clinical translation but also minimize the risk of potential adverse effects associated with prolonged or repeated exposure. Accordingly, USW therapy is anticipated to achieve high treatment adherence in future clinical applications.

This study has several limitations. First, the study only recorded neuronal population activity, without observing the modulatory effects of USW on individual neuron. Future studies employing high-resolution single-cell electrophysiological recordings will be essential to dissect how USW modulates the firing patterns of distinct neuronal subtypes, providing a more granular understanding of its neurobiological effects. Second, we did not systematically evaluate the potential impact of USW on arterial pulsatility or meningeal lymphatic vessel function. Modulation of these elements may represent important complementary mechanisms by which USW influences glymphatic flow. Third, both the TBI and AD models in this study were restricted to a single sex. Given the effects of sex discrepancy on the glymphatic system, inclusion of both sexes in future research would help strengthen the generalizability of the study conclusions.

Overall, our findings establish USW as a promising, non-invasive modality for modulating the glymphatic system. USW enhances glymphatic flow by potentiating low-frequency neural oscillations and augmenting AQP4 polarization. Crucially, under pathological conditions—exemplified by traumatic brain injury and Alzheimer’s disease—USW treatment rectifies impaired glymphatic function, mitigating post-traumatic cerebral edema, promoting amyloid-β clearance, and improving cognitive deficits and affective disorders. These results underscore the considerable therapeutic potential of USW for enhancing brain waste clearance and treating associated neurological disorders.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Wild-type male C57BL/6 J mice (8–12 weeks old) and female 5 × FAD Alzheimer’s disease model mice (6 months old) were used in this study. Mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions: temperature 21–25°C, humidity 40–65%, 12-h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. Wild-type mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (N000013), and 5 × FAD mice from Shulaibao (034848-JAX). All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Beijing Institute of Basic Medical Sciences (IA-CUC-DWZX-2021-759). The sample size was determined based on previously published studies employing comparable experimental designs and empirical data from our laboratory. The number of laboratory animals used was minimized to the greatest extent possible, under the premise of ensuring sufficient statistical power to detect biologically meaningful differences between groups. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and experiments were performed in a blinded manner wherever feasible (such as during behavioral tests). Mice that died unexpectedly during surgery/model establishment, mice with failed model induction, or mice with missing key outcome data were excluded from the final statistical analysis.

Cisterna magna cannulation and tracer injection

Mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol (400 mg/kg, i.p.). Upon loss of toe-pinch reflex, the neck was shaved and disinfected with 75% ethanol followed by povidone-iodine. Under stereotaxic guidance, the head was angled 120° relative to the body. A 1-cm midline incision exposed the neck muscles, which were bluntly dissected to reveal the cisterna magna. A PE-10 catheter connected to a 30G needle was filled with artificial cerebrospinal fluid and carefully inserted 1–2 mm through the dura. The cannula was secured with cyanoacrylate adhesive and dental cement. The catheter was heat-sealed to prevent CSF leakage. Postoperative mice were singly housed for recovery (47). For experiments requiring tracer detection ≤3 hours postinjection, mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg, i.p.). For 7-hour endpoint experiments, isoflurane (4% induction, 1 L/min O2) was used. Fluorescent tracer OVA-647 (5 μL) was infused via the cisterna magna cannula at 2 μL/min using a microinjection pump, followed by a 2-min dwell time before catheter sealing.

Glymphatic flow assessment

Influx phase: Tracer was injected intracisternally, and brains were collected at 1 h postinjection to quantify parenchymal tracer accumulation (15). Efflux phase: Tracer was injected intracisternally, and residual parenchymal tracer was quantified at 7 hours postinjection (15). Drainage phase: Tracer accumulation in superficial and deep cervical lymph nodes was monitored from 0–3 hours postinjection (48).

Ultrashort wave intervention

Non-thermal ultrashort wave was delivered using a 40.68 MHz ultrashort wave device (DL-C II ultrashort wave device, Shantou Medical Equipment Factory Co., Ltd. from China) following 5-min preheating. Two 5-cm circular electrodes were positioned bilaterally parallel to the skull surface with a 2-cm air gap. Parameters were maintained at non-thermal dosage (tuning current: 40 mA; effective power: ∼4.4 W). Intervention durations were 10, 20, or 30 min per experimental protocol. Control mice underwent identical handling without device activation.

In vivo fluorescence imaging

After shaving ventral neck fur to minimize signal attenuation, OVA-647 tracer was injected intracisternally as described. Mice were imaged using wavelengths optimized for OVA-647 (excitation/emission: 650/670 nm) with fixed exposure times to prevent saturation. Identical positioning and imaging parameters were maintained across serial timepoints (20, 40, 60, 120, 180 min). Fluorescence intensity in superficial cervical lymph nodes (sCLNs) was quantified within standardized regions of interest (ROIs).

Tissue harvesting

Following deep anesthesia, mice were positioned supine and secured. A midline incision was made through the skin from the lower mandible to the xiphoid process. After exposing the thoracic cavity by sectioning the diaphragm and bilateral rib cage, transcardial perfusion was performed: A perfusion needle was inserted into the left ventricle via the cardiac apex, the right atrium was incised, and sequential flushing with 20 mL 0.9% saline (to eliminate erythrocyte autofluorescence) followed by 20 mL 4% paraformaldehyde was administered until limb rigidity confirmed successful perfusion. For cervical lymph node isolation, the skin was incised along the ventral midline from the sternal notch to the mandible and reflected laterally toward the ears to expose submandibular glands. Superficial cervical lymph nodes were carefully dissected from the cranial borders of the submandibular glands using fine forceps and ophthalmic scissors. To access deep cervical lymph nodes, sternocleidomastoid muscles were transected bilaterally and reflected to reveal the deep nodes adjacent to the carotid sheath (49). Brains were extracted via complete craniotomy: Scissors were inserted through the foramen magnum, and calvaria was sectioned bilaterally along the occipital-temporal sutures to the olfactory bulbs, followed by careful meningeal dissection to remove the intact brain.

Immunofluorescence staining and imaging

Harvested lymph nodes and brains were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 16–18 h, followed by gradient dehydration in 15% and 30% sucrose solutions for 48 h. Tissues were embedded in OCT compound, mounted on cryostat chucks, and flash-frozen. Sectioning was performed at −22°C ± 1°C, with 20-μm coronal brain sections and maximum cross-sections of lymph nodes collected. Sections were mounted on glass slides, washed 3 times in PBS, and blocked with 10% normal goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4°C. After PBS washes (3 × 10 min), fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at room temperature protected from light. The primary antibodies included Anti-Aquaporin-4 (1:200; Proteintech, 16473–1-AP), Anti-CD31 (1:200; Abcam, ab7388) and Anti-beta-Amyloid (D54D2) (1:200; Cell Signaling, #8243). The secondary antibodies included Cy3 AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:200; Jackson ImmunoResearch, AB_2338006) and Alexa Fluor 488 AffiniPure Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (1:200; Jackson ImmunoResearch, AB_2338840). Slides were counterstained with DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium after final PBS washes.

Quantitative fluorescence analysis

Whole-slide imaging was performed using an Olympus VS200 slide scanner (20× objective, 16-bit depth) with constant exposure settings across experiments. Lymphatic drainage was quantified by measuring fluorescence intensity and distribution area in bilateral deep cervical lymph node sections. Glymphatic influx/efflux was analyzed in six defined coronal planes (Bregma +1.2 to −1.8 mm at 0.6-mm intervals) according to the Paxinos and Franklin’s the Mouse Brain in Stereotaxic Coordinates (50). Each ROI was quantified using ImageJ, and the thresholds were kept constant for each group of experiments during the calculation.

Micro-wire Array and EEG electrode implantation

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg). After shaving the scalp and applying ophthalmic ointment, animals were secured in a stereotaxic frame. The scalp was disinfected with 75% ethanol followed by povidone-iodine. A midline incision exposed the skull, and 3% hydrogen peroxide was applied to clear periosteum, revealing cranial sutures to identify bregma as the origin. Anchor screws were implanted in burr holes drilled over the frontal and parietal cortices. For hippocampal micro-wire array implantation, a craniotomy was centered at coordinates AP: −2.5 mm, ML: −2.0 mm. The bone flap was lifted, underlying dura excised, and hemostasis achieved with cotton swabs for any bleeding. A micro-wire array affixed to a micromanipulator was lowered perpendicularly at 100 μm/min until contacting the cortical surface, then advanced to a depth of DV: −1.4 mm. Electrode leads were wrapped around anchor screws and secured with dental acrylic. For EEG electrode implantation, leads were directly wound around screws following identical anesthetic, positioning, and stereotaxic protocols before dental acrylic fixation. All animals recovered on heating pads until fully conscious.

In vivo electrophysiological signal acquisition and analysis

Local field potential (LFP) and electroencephalogram (EEG) signals were digitized via wireless telemetry and processed using Python. Time-frequency analysis was performed through short-time Fourier transform (STFT) to extract signal characteristics across temporal and spectral domains, with a frequency range of 0–50 Hz. Analysis utilized 5-second Hanning windows with 50% overlap to optimize time-frequency resolution while minimizing spectral leakage. The STFT-derived spectrograms visualized localized spatiotemporal dynamics, capturing transient frequency responses to stimuli. Power spectral density (PSD) estimation employed Welch’s method with identical windowing parameters (5-s Hanning windows, 50% overlap) to quantify power distribution across the 0–50 Hz spectrum. Band-specific power was calculated by integrating spectral power within five canonical frequency bands: Delta (0.5–4 Hz), Theta (4–8 Hz), Alpha (8–13 Hz), Beta (13–30 Hz), and Gamma (30–50 Hz), enabling quantitative assessment of oscillatory power distribution across neural circuits.

Stereotaxic viral vector injection

Following identical anesthesia, immobilization, and stereotaxic coordinate protocols established for in vivo electrophysiology, a burr hole was drilled at coordinates AP: −2.7 mm, ML: −2.5 mm over the parietal cortex. After removing bone debris, glass micropipettes were backfilled with mineral oil using a syringe, mounted onto a microinjection pump, and primed to clear residual oil. Subsequently, 800 nL of viral vector was aspirated and infused into the hippocampus (AP: −2.7 mm, ML: 2.5 mm, DV: −1.5 mm from cortical surface) at 100 nL/min. A 10-minute postinjection dwell period prevented reflux before micropipette withdrawal. Scalps were suture-closed and disinfected, with mice recovering on heating pads until ambulatory. For neuronal silencing experiments, PSAM-GlyR vectors (rAAV9-hSyn-PSAM4-GlyR-P2A-EGFP) and control constructs (rAAV9-hSyn-EGFP) were procured from Brain Case.

AQP4 polarization analysis

Perivascular AQP4 profiles (approximate diameter: 5 μm, length: 30 μm) were analyzed in selected brain regions (dorsal cortex, lateral cortex, ventral cortex, and hippocampus). For each region, 2–3 qualifying perivascular structures were evaluated. Using line plot tool of ImageJ, a 50-μm perpendicular line scan was drawn across each profile. Fluorescence intensity along the scan was measured, with the average intensity within a 10-μm segment preceding the peak intensity defined as the background baseline. The AQP4 polarization index was calculated as the ratio of peak fluorescence intensity to the background baseline value (10).

Pharmacological agents and administration

uPSEM792: Three weeks postviral transduction, uPSEM792 was administered via intraperitoneal injection (3 mg/kg). Neuronal silencing commenced within 1 hour postinjection. For glymphatic influx experiments, uPSEM792 was injected 1 hour prior to intracisternal tracer delivery. For efflux assessments, administration occurred 4 hours before tissue collection (15).

TGN-020: Prepared working solution was injected intraperitoneally (100 mg/kg). For glymphatic influx experiments, TGN-020 was administered 30 min before tracer injection. For efflux experiments, injection was performed 5.5 hours posttracer delivery (13).

Western blotting

Western blot analysis was performed following established protocols (22). The primary antibodies included rabbit anti-β-Actin (1:1000; ABclonal, AC004) and rabbit anti-AQP4 (1:1000; Proteintech, 16473–1-AP), with detection using HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000; ABclonal, AS014).

Traumatic brain injury modeling

Mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane (1 L/min O2) for 60 seconds. Moderate closed-skull TBI was induced using a cortical impact device. A 3-mm tip impactor head striking at 5.2 m/s with 0.1 s dwell time and 10 mm impact depth, was applied horizontally to the mid-point between the left orbit and auditory canal (7, 44). Mildly anesthetized mice were suspended vertically by maxillary incisors on a metal ring before impact. Post-impact, mice fell onto a foam cushion to minimize secondary injury. Sham controls underwent identical anesthesia and suspension without impact.

Cerebral water content measurement

At 1 hour or 3 days post-TBI, brains were extracted following decapitation. Olfactory bulbs and cerebellum were removed using a brain matrix. Hemispheres were separated sagittally into ipsilateral (lesioned) and contralateral regions. Each hemisphere was weighed immediately (wet weight), then dried at 85°C for 72 hours to obtain dry weight. Water content was calculated as: [(wet weight − dry weight)/wet weight] × 100% (7).

Behavioral assessments

Open Field Test (OFT) was conducted in a 50 by 50 cm arena divided into a central zone (25 by 25 cm) and peripheral regions. Mice were allowed to freely explore for 5 minutes while being recorded by an overhead camera. The arena was cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials to eliminate odor cues. Total distance traveled, center zone exploration distance, average speed, and immobility time were quantified to assess locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior.

Novel Object Recognition (NOR) testing involved habituation (5-min free exploration in an empty arena) followed by a training phase where mice explored two identical objects for 5 minutes. After a 1-hour interval, one object was replaced with a novel object in the same location. Exploration time directed at novel versus familiar objects was recorded during the 5-minute test session. The discrimination index was calculated as [(Time_novel – Time_familiar)/Total exploration time] to evaluate recognition memory.

The Y maze had one arm entry permanently blocked by a guillotine door to create the novel arm. Mice were introduced into the starting arm and permitted free exploration of accessible arms for 5 minutes. After 24 hours, the previously blocked arm of the Y maze was made accessible. Mice were then reintroduced to the fully open maze, with exploration distance in the novel arm and percentage of total path distance in the novel arm recorded to assess hippocampal-dependent spatial recognition memory.

Rotarod Test required pre-training one day prior: Mice underwent three 5-minute trials on a rotating rod with speed acceleration from 5 to 15 rpm. During testing, latency to fall was recorded over 5 minutes with speed progression from 5 to 40 rpm, assessing motor coordination.

Tail Suspension Test (TST) involved securing mice 20 cm above a surface by tail tip using medical tape. Immobility time (defined as passive hanging without limb movement) was recorded during 5-minute sessions, serving as a measure of behavioral despair.

Statistical analysis

The in vivo experiment was performed as one complete animal cohort with predefined group sizes. Key phenotypic results were validated across multiple subsets of animals within the same cohort. No full independent replication of the entire in vivo experiment was conducted. For most in vivo endpoints, each individual mouse represents one independent biological replicate. For multi-channel electrophysiological recordings, each mouse serves as a biological replicate, and individual acquisition channels from the same animal are defined as observational technical replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normally distributed data underwent two-tailed paired or unpaired Student’s t tests, while non-normally distributed data were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U tests. Multi-group comparisons employed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s post hoc testing to control family-wise error rate. Linear relationships were evaluated by simple linear regression. Statistical significance thresholds were designated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.
