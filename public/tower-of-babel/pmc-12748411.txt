Targeting ferroptosis by noncoding RNAs as a novel therapeutic strategy for hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yaser Mohammadi, Mohammad Ali Mahdiabadi, Zahra Ourang, Elham Bahreini, Ali Abaszade-Cheragheali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most common primary liver cancer, is recognized as the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths. Late diagnosis and limited treatment efficacy are among the major challenges of HCC. Recent studies have introduced ‘ferroptosis’—a unique iron-dependent cell death mechanism driven by lipid peroxidation, accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and oxidative imbalance—as a promising therapeutic approach for HCC. It has also been shown that non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), play key roles in the pathogenesis of HCC and the regulation of ferroptosis. Overall, ncRNAs regulate iron metabolism, antioxidant systems, and oxidative stress responses. These interactions influence HCC proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance. This review elucidates the role of ferroptosis in HCC, examines the regulation of ferroptosis pathways by ncRNAs, evaluates their therapeutic potential, and provides insights into novel strategies to improve HCC treatment outcomes.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Ferroptosis, Non-coding RNA, Cell death

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the predominant form of primary liver cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, represents a major public health challenge due to its high prevalence, aggressive malignancy, and poor prognosis [1]. HCC commonly arises in patients with chronic liver conditions, such as cirrhosis, chronic viral hepatitis, or non-alcoholic steatohepatitis. Low survival rates are largely attributed to late-stage diagnosis and limited therapeutic options [2, 3]. While surgical resection offers a potentially curative option for early-stage HCC, sorafenib, a multi-kinase inhibitor, remains the first-line chemotherapy for advanced cases [2]. Nevertheless, widespread drug resistance often leads to recurrence, metastasis, and treatment failure [4]. Consequently, recent research has shifted toward exploring novel mechanisms of programmed cell death, with ferroptosis emerging as a unique and promising pathway for HCC suppression [5].

Cell death mechanisms play a critical role in cancer therapy, and understanding their diversity is essential for developing effective treatments. Apoptosis, for example, is well-characterized and can be targeted with caspase activators. These therapies offer precise tumor cell elimination but are often limited by resistance in HCC [6]. Necrosis, while effective in triggering inflammation to combat tumors, lacks specificity and can damage surrounding healthy tissue, posing significant drawbacks [7]. Autophagy, with its dual role in promoting or inhibiting tumor growth, remains challenging to manipulate pharmacologically due to its context-dependent nature [8]. Unlike these mechanisms, ferroptosis is less affected by cellular resistance mechanisms due to its dependence on iron-induced lipid peroxidation, and it can be proposed as a new pathway to overcome therapeutic challenges, particularly drug resistance in HCC [4, 9, 10].

Ferroptosis, triggered by the accumulation of lipid peroxides through iron metabolism, is defined by its reliance on iron, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, membrane lipid peroxidation, and disruption of the oxidative-reductive balance [11]. It is governed by intricate signaling networks, including the SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 axis and the transcription factor NRF2 (Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) [12–14]. This process contributes to tumor initiation and progression and affects chemotherapy resistance, metastasis, and HCC prognosis, making it a compelling therapeutic target [10, 15]. A deeper exploration of ferroptosis, particularly its interplay with molecular regulators, could unlock innovative treatment strategies.

In this context, non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), have emerged as pivotal regulators in HCC pathogenesis and ferroptosis modulation [16]. These molecules exert their effects through diverse mechanisms: miRNAs inhibit gene expression by binding directly to target mRNAs [17]; lncRNAs and circRNAs function as molecular sponges for miRNAs, modulating their regulatory activity [18, 19], and ncRNAs can also interact with proteins to influence downstream signaling pathways [20]. Mounting evidence indicates that ncRNAs regulate ferroptosis-related pathways—such as iron metabolism, the GPX4 antioxidant system, and oxidative stress responses—thereby influencing HCC malignant behaviors, including proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance [21–23]. For example, certain miRNAs heighten cancer cell sensitivity to ferroptosis by suppressing anti-ferroptotic genes like SLC7A11 [24], while lncRNAs may bolster resistance to this cell death mechanism by enhancing protective gene expression [25]. Meanwhile, CircRNAs, due to their stability, provide sustained regulation of ferroptosis-related processes [26]. These complex interactions underscore a dynamic regulatory network wherein ncRNAs not only respond to metabolic and oxidative stress but also shape the tumor microenvironment and disease prognosis. This review article examines the role of ferroptosis in HCC, provides a comprehensive analysis of research progress on ferroptosis-regulating ncRNAs in HCC, and explores their potential as therapeutic targets.

Search strategy

This article is a narrative review and does not follow a systematic review protocol. However, to ensure comprehensive coverage of the topic, we performed a focused literature search in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science up to June 2025 using the keywords “ferroptosis,” “non-coding RNAs,” and “hepatocellular carcinoma.” Priority was given to peer-reviewed original research articles and review papers published in English. Studies unrelated to ferroptosis or ncRNAs in HCC, conference abstracts without full data, and non-English publications were excluded. Additional references were identified through manual screening of the bibliographies of relevant articles.

Key characteristics of ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is a regulated cell death process first described in 2012 that differs fundamentally from other cell death mechanisms in its biochemical signature and morphological features [10, 27]. This iron-dependent non-apoptotic form of cell death is characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxides that occurs when the cellular antioxidant systems become overwhelmed by oxidative stress [28]. Unlike apoptosis, which features cellular shrinkage and membrane blebbing, or necrosis, which involves cellular swelling and membrane rupture, ferroptotic cells exhibit distinctive morphological changes including decreased mitochondrial volume, increased membrane density, and thickened mitochondrial membranes [29]. These alterations represent the structural consequences of excessive lipid peroxidation, which damages cellular membranes and disrupts normal cellular functions.

The biochemical hallmarks of ferroptosis include iron overload within the cellular environment, accumulation of ROS, and unrestricted lipid peroxidation that ultimately leads to cell death [28]. Iron plays a central role in this process as it catalyzes the Fenton reaction, which generates highly reactive hydroxyl radicals that initiate lipid peroxidation chains in polyunsaturated fatty acids within cellular membranes [29]. This cascade of oxidative damage progresses until membrane integrity is compromised beyond repair, resulting in cellular demise. Importantly, ferroptosis can be suppressed by iron chelators that reduce available iron, lipophilic antioxidants that neutralize reactive species, or inhibitors that directly block lipid peroxidation [30]. These intervention points highlight the distinctive nature of ferroptosis and provide valuable targets for both experimental manipulation and potential therapeutic applications.

Ferroptosis contributes to the pathogenesis of various diseases, including acute kidney injury, neurodegenerative disorders, and cancer, making it a significant focus for researchers seeking to understand and manipulate cell death mechanisms for therapeutic benefit [31]. The dysregulation of ferroptosis has been implicated in both the promotion and suppression of disease states, depending on the specific cellular context and environmental conditions [32]. This dual nature underscores the complexity of ferroptosis and its regulation, presenting both challenges and opportunities for therapeutic intervention (Fig. 1).

Molecular pathways governing ferroptosis

The execution and regulation of ferroptosis involve several interconnected molecular pathways that collectively determine cellular susceptibility to this form of death. These pathways include antioxidant defense systems, iron metabolism mechanisms, and lipid metabolism processes that interact to maintain redox homeostasis under normal conditions or drive ferroptotic death when dysregulated [11].

Antioxidant defense systems and glutathione metabolism

The primary defense against ferroptosis is the cellular antioxidant system, with glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) serving as a central regulator [33]. GPX4 uses glutathione (GSH) as a cofactor to reduce lipid hydroperoxides to their corresponding alcohols, thereby preventing the accumulation of toxic lipid peroxides that drive ferroptosis [14]. Depletion of GPX4 activity, either through direct inhibition or through depletion of its essential cofactor GSH, sensitizes cells to ferroptosis by removing this critical protective mechanism [5]. The synthesis of GSH depends on the availability of cysteine, which is primarily obtained through the cystine/glutamate antiporter Xc-system. This transmembrane transport system imports cystine into cells in exchange for glutamate, providing the rate-limiting precursor for GSH synthesis. Inhibition of Xc-system, for example, by the small molecule erastin, depletes cellular GSH levels, compromises GPX4 function, and triggers ferroptosis (Fig. 1) [30, 33].

The transcription factor NRF2 represents another important regulator of the antioxidant response that protects against ferroptosis [34]. NRF2 activates the transcription of genes containing antioxidant response elements (AREs), including those involved in GSH synthesis and regeneration, iron sequestration, and NADPH production. The activation of NRF2 enhances cellular resistance to ferroptosis by bolstering multiple antioxidant defense mechanisms, while its suppression renders cells more vulnerable to ferroptotic death [5, 34]. Many natural polyphenolic compounds exert antiferroptotic effects partly by activating NRF2, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway (Fig. 1) [35].

Iron metabolism and regulation

Iron homeostasis represents a critical determinant of ferroptosis sensitivity, as excess free iron catalyzes the formation of lipid radicals that drive peroxidation [36]. Several proteins regulate cellular iron levels, including transferrin (which transports iron), transferrin receptor (which facilitates iron uptake), ferritin (which stores iron), and ferroportin (which exports iron from cells). Dysregulation of these iron-regulatory proteins can significantly influence ferroptosis susceptibility [36, 37]. For instance, increased expression of transferrin receptor enhances iron uptake and promotes ferroptosis, while upregulation of ferritin sequesters iron and protects against ferroptotic death [38, 39].

Selective autophagy processes targeting iron-storage proteins, particularly ferritinophagy (the autophagic degradation of ferritin), play important roles in modulating ferroptosis sensitivity [40]. Ferritinophagy releases iron from ferritin storage, increasing the labile iron pool and potentially promoting ferroptosis under certain conditions [41]. Conversely, iron chelation or strategies that enhance iron storage or export can protect against ferroptosis by reducing the availability of free iron for participation in Fenton reactions and subsequent lipid peroxidation (Fig. 1) [36].

Lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis

The composition and metabolism of cellular lipids, particularly polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), are critical determinants of ferroptosis sensitivity [42]. PUFAs, characterized by multiple double bonds and bis-allylic hydrogen atoms, are highly susceptible to peroxidation due to the ease with which these hydrogens are abstracted, initiating lipid radical chain reactions [43]. The integration of PUFAs into membrane phospholipids, mediated by acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family members (ACSLs), notably ACSL4, and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3), heightens cellular vulnerability to ferroptosis by enriching membranes with peroxidation-prone substrates [39]. ACSL4, in particular, plays a pivotal role in driving ferroptosis sensitivity by preferentially incorporating polyunsaturated fatty acyl chains into phospholipids, thereby amplifying the pool of oxidizable lipids within cellular membranes (Fig. 1) [44].

The relationship between PUFA and monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) levels further modulates membrane sensitivity to peroxidation. Higher PUFA content increases the density of peroxidation-susceptible sites, enhancing the membrane’s propensity for oxidative damage. In contrast, MUFAs, with fewer double bonds, are less prone to peroxidation and can confer a protective effect by diluting PUFA-rich domains [32, 45]. This balance directly influences the degree of lipid peroxidation: membranes enriched with PUFAs exhibit greater sensitivity to ferroptosis. At the same time, an elevated MUFA-to-PUFA ratio reduces this susceptibility by stabilizing lipid bilayers against oxidative stress [32]. Lipoxygenases (LOXs) exacerbate this process by enzymatically catalyzing PUFA peroxidation, producing lipid hydroperoxides that propagate oxidative damage and amplify ferroptosis [46]. Conversely, inhibiting LOX activity mitigates ferroptosis by curtailing the generation of these reactive species [5].

In contrast, disruptions in MUFA biosynthesis, such as through the downregulation of sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP) transcription factors, shift the lipid profile toward a higher PUFA-to-MUFA ratio, thereby enhancing ferroptosis sensitivity [10, 32]. This alteration reduces the protective incorporation of MUFAs into phospholipids, leaving membranes more vulnerable to peroxidation (Fig. 1) [47]. Thus, the interplay between PUFA and MUFA levels not only dictates the structural integrity and fluidity of cellular membranes but also serves as a key regulator of their oxidative susceptibility, underscoring the nuanced lipid dynamics governing ferroptosis execution.

Ferroptosis and HCC: therapeutic implications

The unique mechanisms underlying ferroptosis present novel opportunities for cancer therapy, particularly for malignancies that demonstrate resistance to conventional treatments. Cancer cells often exhibit altered iron metabolism, increased oxidative stress, and unique dependencies on antioxidant defense systems, potentially rendering them vulnerable to ferroptosis induction [28]. The selective triggering of ferroptosis in cancer cells while sparing normal tissues represents a promising therapeutic strategy that has gained increasing attention in recent years.

Dysregulated iron metabolism in HCC

Cancer cells frequently display dysregulated iron metabolism characterized by increased iron uptake and decreased iron storage or export [48]. This iron addiction supports the elevated metabolic demands of cancer cells but also creates a potential vulnerability to ferroptosis [49]. Hepatocellular carcinoma cells, in particular, often exhibit high levels of transferrin receptor expression and reduced ferritin levels, resulting in increased labile iron pools that can catalyze lipid peroxidation when antioxidant defenses are compromised [38]. The observation that sorafenib-resistant HCC cells show significantly elevated intracellular iron levels and ROS production suggests that targeting ferroptosis may be particularly effective against treatment-resistant tumors [50].

Antioxidant dependencies in HCC

Cancer cells frequently endure elevated oxidative stress due to altered metabolism, increased generation of reactive oxygen species, and genomic instability [51]. Consequently, they often develop dependencies on specific antioxidant defense mechanisms, revealing vulnerabilities that can be therapeutically exploited. Traditionally, the glutathione (GSH)–GPX4 axis has been highlighted as a central protective mechanism preventing lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [30]. However, emerging evidence particularly in HCC demonstrates that cells also rely critically on parallel antioxidant systems, notably the FSP1–CoQ₁₀ pathway and the mitochondrial DHODH–CoQ₁₀ axis [52].

FSP1 (formerly AIFM2) reduces ubiquinone (CoQ₁₀) to ubiquinol, a potent lipid-soluble antioxidant that halts ferroptotic lipid damage, functioning independently of GPX4 [53]. In HCC, FSP1 is significantly overexpressed, correlates with poor survival, and creates a robust ferroptosis resistance mechanism that can sustain tumor progression even when the GPX4 pathway is not upregulated [54]. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of FSP1 using iFSP1 not only induces ferroptosis but also enhances anti-tumor immunity and synergizes with immunotherapy in HCC models [55]. Moreover, the lncRNA lncFAL, stabilized by HDLBP, suppresses ferroptosis in HCC cells by preventing TRIM69-mediated degradation of FSP1, further highlighting the pathway’s clinical relevance [56].

Complementing this, the mitochondrial enzyme DHODH has emerged as another key CoQ₁₀-related anti-ferroptotic system. DHODH reduces CoQ₁₀ within mitochondria, countering lipid peroxidation especially in cells with low GPX4 expression [57]. Notably, among solid tumors, HCC shows the highest DHODH expression, which correlates with more advanced disease and metastasis, suggesting its significant role in ferroptosis resistance within the hepatic context [58].

Immune regulation, angiogenesis, and tumor microenvironment

Beyond tumor cell–intrinsic mechanisms, ferroptosis in HCC exerts multifaceted effects on the tumor microenvironment. It modulates anti-tumor immunity—ferroptotic cell death can release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which enhance immune surveillance and activation; conversely, in certain contexts, inflammation driven by peroxide intermediates may foster immunosuppression or tumor progression [59, 60].

Angiogenic processes in HCC are also influenced by ferroptosis. lipid peroxidation and iron overload can regulate VEGF-related signaling and endothelial dynamics, thereby impacting tumor vascularization and growth [61].

Moreover, ferroptosis shapes the broader tumor microenvironment. for instance, ferroptotic hepatocytes can activate STING signaling in macrophages, contributing to inflammatory crosstalk that affects HCC progression [62]. These insights underscore the importance of considering the systemic consequences of ferroptosis interventions—not simply as tumor-cell killers, but as potent modulators of HCC immunobiology and microenvironment.

Ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma

Hepatocellular carcinoma represents a particularly relevant context for ferroptosis-based interventions due to its poor prognosis, limited treatment options, and unique metabolic characteristics [15]. As the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, HCC poses a significant clinical challenge, with conventional detection and treatment approaches proving inadequate to address its elevated incidence and mortality rates [63]. The liver’s central role in iron metabolism and the frequent dysregulation of iron homeostasis in HCC create a compelling rationale for exploring ferroptosis as a therapeutic strategy for this aggressive malignancy (Table 1).

However, ferroptosis in the context of HCC must be approached with caution, as it functions as a double-edged sword. A recent study highlights that while induction of ferroptosis can suppress tumor growth, under certain microenvironmental or genetic conditions, ferroptotic signaling may conversely promote carcinogenesis and tumor progression [64]. For instance, lipid peroxidation products can exacerbate chronic liver inflammation or provoke compensatory survival pathways, potentially contributing to tumor aggressiveness in predisposed settings. Incorporating this dualistic perspective enhances the conceptual depth of the review and underscores the necessity of precisely designing ferroptosis-targeting therapies in HCC.

Sorafenib resistance and ferroptosis

Sorafenib, a multi-kinase inhibitor approved as a first-line therapy for advanced HCC, has been shown to induce ferroptosis as part of its mechanism of action [2]. However, only approximately 30% of HCC patients benefit from sorafenib therapy, and resistance typically develops within six months of treatment initiation [38]. Recent evidence suggests that sorafenib resistance in HCC is associated with reduced sensitivity to ferroptosis, indicating that strategies to restore or enhance ferroptosis sensitivity might overcome treatment resistance and improve clinical outcomes [2, 38].

Several molecular mechanisms underlying sorafenib resistance through ferroptosis inhibition have been identified. For example, the transcription factor myocyte enhancer factor 2D)MEF2D) has been shown to regulate the expression of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 3 (ACSL3), a key enzyme in lipid metabolism that inhibits ferroptosis when overexpressed. In HCC, aberrant expression of ACSL3 promotes resistance to sorafenib-induced ferroptosis, suggesting that targeting the MEF2D-ACSL3 axis could potentially restore treatment sensitivity [39]. Similarly, epigenetic regulation via the ETS1/miR-23a-3p/ACSL4 axis has been implicated in modulating ferroptosis sensitivity and sorafenib response in HCC, providing additional targets for therapeutic intervention [75].

Novel therapeutic approaches targeting ferroptosis in HCC

Emerging therapeutic strategies targeting ferroptosis in HCC are now extending beyond conventional inhibitors to include a diverse array of ferroptosis inducers with distinct mechanisms of action. RSL3, a potent inhibitor of GPX4, has been demonstrated to induce dose-dependent ferroptotic death in HCC cell lines such as Huh7 and HepG2, marked by mitochondrial shrinkage, increased lipid peroxidation, and cell detachment effects that were specifically reversed by ferroptosis inhibitors (e.g., ferrostatin-1) but not by apoptosis inhibitors [76]. iFSP1, targeting the FSP1/CoQ₁₀ antioxidant axis, has shown remarkable in vivo efficacy in HCC: it effectively reduced tumor burden, induced ferroptosis, and significantly increased infiltration by dendritic cells, macrophages, and T cells while exhibiting synergy with immunotherapy in preclinical liver cancer models [54]. Brequinar, a DHODH inhibitor, has likewise enhanced ferroptosis sensitivity: in HCC, its inhibition of the mitochondrial CoQ₁₀–DHODH pathway augments sorafenib-induced ferroptosis, although preclinical data remain limited [77, 78]. Meanwhile, IKE, an imidazole ketone derivative of erastin, continues the tradition of targeting Xc-system to deplete glutathione and trigger ferroptosis, while FINO₂ and FIN56, which act through disruption of lipid peroxide detoxification and CoQ₁₀ depletion, respectively, have demonstrated ferroptosis induction in other cancer cell models, though their specific activity in HCC is still under investigation [79].

Overview of noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) in gene regulation and cancer

Noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), including miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs, constitute a diverse group of transcripts that lack protein-coding capacity yet exert profound influence over gene expression and cellular processes through mechanisms such as post-transcriptional silencing, chromatin remodeling, and molecular sponging [16, 17, 19]. MiRNAs, small RNAs processed by Drosha and Dicer, suppress gene expression by targeting mRNA 3’-UTRs within the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) [80]. While lncRNAs, transcripts over 200 nucleotides, modulate epigenetic and transcriptional events or act as miRNA sponges via interactions with complexes like polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) [81, 82]. CircRNAs, formed by back-splicing and notable for their stability, primarily regulate miRNA availability and occasionally bind proteins or encode peptides [21, 83]. Beyond these, other ncRNAs—such as piRNAs for transposon silencing, snRNAs and snoRNAs for RNA processing, eRNAs for enhancer activity, and ncNATs for antisense regulation—further enrich this regulatory landscape [84–86]. Given their established roles in cancer-related pathways, including proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis, ncRNAs have become a focal point in HCC research, where their interplay with disease-specific processes, such as ferroptosis, offer promising insights into novel therapeutic strategies.

Role of NcRNAs in HCC

ncRNAs are pivotal regulators in HCC, influencing tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance while also offering potential as diagnostic and prognostic tools. Their dysregulation can either drive oncogenesis or promote tumor suppression, impacting critical processes such as proliferation, apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis, which collectively shape HCC’s clinical trajectory. This section explores how ncRNAs, including miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs, orchestrate these dual roles and modulate cell death pathways in HCC.

NcRNAs as oncogenes or tumor suppressors in HCC

In HCC, ncRNAs exhibit a dual nature, acting as oncogenes to fuel tumor growth or as tumor suppressors to curb malignancy. For instance, miR-21, frequently overexpressed, promotes HCC proliferation and invasion by suppressing PTEN, a key tumor suppressor, thus enhancing oncogenic signaling [87]. In contrast, miR-122, a liver-specific miRNA comprising ~ 70% of the hepatocyte miRNA pool, inhibits tumor progression by targeting Wnt/β-catenin signaling and cyclin G1, underscoring its protective role. The downregulation of miR-122 correlates with poor prognosis and metastasis risk, marking it as a candidate for early detection [88]. Among lncRNAs, HULC (Highly Upregulated in Liver Cancer) accelerates HCC by acting as a ceRNA, sponging miR-200a to upregulate epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers and metastasis, HULC overexpression is linked to advanced tumor stages and reduced survival, reinforcing its prognostic utility [89], while MALAT1 (Metastasis-Associated Lung Adenocarcinoma Transcript 1) enhances migration and invasion via splicing factor regulation [90]. Conversely, circ-MTO1, often downregulated in HCC, suppresses tumor growth by sponging oncogenic miRNAs, exemplifying a tumor-suppressive circRNA [18]. These examples highlight how ncRNAs dynamically influence HCC’s malignant behavior.

NcRNAs and cell death pathways in HCC

Beyond oncogenesis, ncRNAs critically regulate programmed cell death pathways—apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis—affecting HCC progression and therapy response. In apoptosis, miR-122 enhances HCC cell death by downregulating Bcl-2 and upregulating Bax [91], while lncRNA GAS5 inhibits the PI3K/AKT pathway to sensitize cells to chemotherapy [92]. In autophagy, miR-375 suppresses tumor survival under stress by targeting ATG7 [93], whereas lncRNA HOTTIP promotes chemoresistance by upregulating LC3-II and Beclin-1 [19]. Regulation of NcRNAs in ferroptosis of HCC

MiRNAs as regulators of ferroptosis in HCC

Research suggests that miR-214-3p enhances ferroptosis in HCC cells through multiple targets. Bai et al. found that miR-214-3p increases susceptibility to erastin-induced ferroptosis by targeting activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4). Erastin, a ferroptosis inducer, inhibits Xc-system, reducing cystine import and glutathione synthesis, leading to lipid peroxidation. ATF4 upregulates SLC7A11, a component of Xc-system, and its downregulation by miR-214-3p enhances ferroptosis, as evidenced by increased malondialdehyde (MDA), ROS, and Fe2 + levels in HepG2 and Hep3B cells [25].

Guan-Nan et al. revealed a different mechanism where miR-214-3p targets GPX4, mediated by the lncPVT1. Ketamine downregulates lncPVT1, which acts as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) for miR-214-3p, allowing miR-214-3p to target GPX4, reducing its expression and inducing ferroptosis. This was confirmed by increased Fe2+, lipid ROS, and MDA levels in liver cancer cells, with ferroptosis reversed by overexpressing lncPVT1 or GPX4, or knocking down miR-214-3p [95]. This dual targeting (ATF4 and GPX4) highlights miR-214-3p’s complex role in ferroptosis regulation.

In contrast, miR-23a-3p inhibits ferroptosis, contributing to sorafenib resistance in HCC. Lu et al. showed that miR-23a-3p is epigenetically regulated by the transcription factor ETS1 and targets acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4). ACSL4 promotes ferroptosis by facilitating the synthesis of polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipids, which are prone to peroxidation. By downregulating ACSL4, miR-23a-3p reduces ferroptosis susceptibility, enhancing resistance to sorafenib, a drug known to induce ferroptosis [96]. This finding is significant for understanding drug resistance mechanisms in HCC treatment.

miR-21-5p has emerged as a significant regulator of ferroptosis in HCC. This miRNA inhibits ferroptosis by targeting MELK (maternal embryonic leucine zipper kinase) and activating the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Mechanistically, miR-21-5p overexpression increases levels of GPX4, GSH, FTH1, and xCT while decreasing HO-1 expression, ROS generation, and Fe2 + accumulation. Studies have shown that miR-21-5p is overexpressed in HCC tissues and associated with poor prognosis. Its inhibition decreases cell viability, proliferation, invasion, and migration while promoting ferroptosis [97]. Importantly, the ferroptosis inducer erastin can attenuate the protective effect of miR-21-5p against ferroptosis, suggesting potential therapeutic applications.

MicroRNA-3200-5p plays a crucial role in regulating ferroptosis in HCC through the HULC/miR-3200-5p/ATF4 axis. lncRNA HULC, which is highly expressed in HCC, functions as a competing ceRNA that sponges miR-3200-5p, preventing its inhibitory effect on ATF4. ATF4 is a key regulator of redox homeostasis and ferroptosis resistance, modulating the expression of antioxidant defense genes such as SLC7A11 and GPX4. When HULC is downregulated, miR-3200-5p is released and directly suppresses ATF4, leading to increased oxidative stress, depletion of glutathione, accumulation of lipid peroxides, and enhanced ferroptotic cell death. This mechanism not only inhibits HCC cell proliferation and metastasis but also highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting the HULC/miR-3200-5p/ATF4 axis to sensitize HCC cells to ferroptosis-inducing agents [98].

miR-490-3p plays a crucial role in the regulation of ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) through its interaction with the PART1/miR-490-3p/SLC7A11 axis. Li et al. [99] reported that the overexpression of miR-490-3p downregulates SLC7A11, leading to increased lipid peroxidation, reduced antioxidant defense, and enhanced ferroptotic cell death. Conversely, PART1 upregulation suppresses ferroptosis by maintaining SLC7A11 expression, promoting tumor progression, metastasis, and resistance to erastin-induced ferroptosis. Importantly, silencing PART1 significantly enhances the sensitivity of HCC cells to sorafenib.

Zong et al. demonstrated that miR-375-3p plays a critical role in the regulation of ferroptosis in HCC by targeting SLC7A11, a key negative regulator of ferroptosis [100].

LncRNAs and ferroptosis

In HCC, a growing body of evidence indicates that lncRNAs modulate ferroptosis by interacting with miRNAs and by regulating the expression and activity of critical ferroptosis effectors such as the SLC7A11, GPX4, and ACSL4. These lncRNAs may function as ceRNAs, “sponging” specific miRNAs that otherwise target mRNAs encoding ferroptosis regulators. In doing so, they fine-tune intracellular ROS levels, iron homeostasis, and lipid peroxidation.

Pro-ferroptotic LncRNAs

Zhu et al. has demonstrated that overexpression of MEG3 promotes ferroptosis and enhances chemotherapy sensitivity in HCC cells, particularly to cisplatin. When MEG3 is overexpressed in HepG2 and LM3 cells, it significantly suppresses cell proliferation and migration while increasing cellular ROS production and MDA levels. levels. Mechanistically, MEG3 acts as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by sponging miR-9-5p, thereby relieving its suppression of transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1), a key regulator of iron uptake. This leads to an expansion of the labile iron pool, which accelerates lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death. In addition, MEG3 downregulates the expression of ferroptosis-protective proteins such as GPX4 and FTH1, further impairing antioxidant defenses and sensitizing HCC cells to oxidative damage [101].

Studies have revealed that overexpression of EPS15-antisense1 (EPS15-AS1) in HepG2 cells inhibits cell activity and invasiveness while simultaneously increasing Fe2 + ion levels and lipid peroxidation—key indicators of ferroptosis. Mechanistically, EPS15-AS1 induces ferroptosis by inhibiting the expression of epidermal growth factor receptor substrate 15 (EPS15) and aldo-keto reductase family 1 member B1 (AKR1B1). AKR1B1, which belongs to the aldo/keto reductase superfamily, plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular redox balance. Higher levels of AKR1B1 correlate with lower survival rates in HCC patients, underscoring the clinical relevance of this pathway [102].

Zhang et al. [103] demonstrated that lncRNA HEPFAL is overexpressed in HCC and accelerates ferroptosis by promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of SLC7A11. By reducing SLC7A11 protein levels, HEPFAL enhances lipid peroxidation and sensitizes HCC cells to ferroptosis.

Wenchuan et al. reported that in HCC cells, lncRNA GABPB1-AS1, by reducing the expression of GABPB1 gene, leads to a decrease in PRDX5 level and consequently a decrease in cellular antioxidant capacity. This is followed by the accumulation of lipid peroxides and increased ROS and cell death by ferroptosis [104].

Anti-ferroptotic LncRNAs

Plasmacytoma variant translocation 1 (PVT1) is significantly upregulated in liver cancer and functions as an oncogenic lncRNA. Studies have shown that PVT1 directly interacts with miR-214-3p, acting as a molecular sponge that impedes miR-214-3p’s regulation of GPX4. By sustaining GPX4 expression, PVT1 enhances the reduction of lipid hydroperoxides to lipid alcohols, thereby preserving membrane integrity and blocking lipid peroxidation. This antioxidant effect effectively suppresses ferroptosis in HCC cells. Moreover, PVT1-mediated GPX4 stabilization reduces ROS accumulation and prevents iron-dependent oxidative damage, highlighting its role in maintaining ferroptosis resistance. Conversely, depletion of PVT1 decreases GPX4 levels, disrupts cellular redox balance, and accelerates ferroptotic cell death, thereby sensitizing HCC cells to therapy. The PVT1/miR-214-3p/GPX4 axis thus represents a key regulatory pathway in ferroptosis modulation, with significant implications for liver cancer treatment strategies [95].

LncRNA DUXAP8 is highly expressed in liver cancer tissues and associated with poor prognosis. Shi et al. [105] revealed that DUXAP8 contributes significantly to sorafenib resistance by suppressing ferroptosis in HCC cells. Mechanistically, DUXAP8 reduces HCC sensitivity to sorafenib-induced ferroptosis by interacting with SLC7A11, a critical subunit of the amino acid antiporter Xc-system. DUXAP8 facilitates SLC7A11 palmitoylation, which impedes its lysosomal degradation, thereby enhancing SLC7A11 activity and suppressing ferroptosis. RNA pull-down and immunofluorescence assays have confirmed that DUXAP8 decreases membrane translocation and promotes the sorting of de-palmitoylated SLC7A11 to lysosomes, providing detailed insights into this regulatory mechanism.

LncRNA HCG18 has been implicated in sorafenib resistance through its regulation of ferroptosis. Studies demonstrate that HCG18 is elevated in HCC cells and associated with reduced sensitivity to sorafenib. Silencing HCG18 inhibits cell proliferation, promotes apoptosis, and impairs sorafenib resistance by promoting ferroptosis. At the molecular level, HCG18 sponges miR-450b-5p to regulate GPX4 expression. Specifically, silencing HCG18 inhibits GPX4 by enhancing miR-450b-5p binding, which promotes GPX4-inhibited ferroptosis and overcomes sorafenib resistance in HCC [106].Peng et al. [107] demonstrated that LINC00654 enhances the recruitment of the transcription factor STAT3 to the SLC7A11 promoter, thereby increasing SLC7A11 transcription. Elevated SLC7A11 boosts cystine uptake and glutathione synthesis, which helps maintain the cellular antioxidant defense and prevents the accumulation of lipid peroxides—the critical trigger for ferroptosis. This suppression of ferroptosis contributes to sorafenib resistance.

Zong et al. [100] identified lncRNA NRAV as a prognostic biomarker in HCC. NRAV was shown to function as a molecular sponge for miR-375-3P, thereby relieving the miRNA-mediated repression of SLC7A11. Through this mechanism, NRAV promotes ferroptosis resistance and is associated with poorer overall survival in HCC patients.

Chen et al. [108] showed that CASC11 stabilizes SLC7A11 mRNA, reducing ferroptosis and potentially contributing to sorafenib resistance, suggesting a therapeutic target. Liu et al. [98] reported that HULC downregulation induces ferroptosis via the miR-3200-5p/ATF4 axis, providing another layer of regulation.

Dual-role LncRNAs

Beyond its direct promotion of ferroptosis, MEG3 overexpression enhances the sensitivity of HCC cells to cisplatin chemotherapy. This dual effect—both promoting ferroptosis and enhancing chemosensitivity—makes MEG3 a particularly interesting target for combination therapy approaches. The ability of MEG3 to increase cellular ROS production and MDA levels suggests that it may sensitize HCC cells to oxidative stress-inducing agents like cisplatin [101].

CircRNAs and ferroptosis

Pro-ferroptotic circrnas

Liu et al. [109] demonstrated that circIARS (hsa_circ_0008367) physically interacts with the RNA binding protein ALKBH5—a known negative regulator of autophagic flux in HCC. Under normal conditions, ALKBH5 silencing facilitates the dissociation of the BCL-2/BECN1 complex, thereby promoting autophagy and ferritinophagy, which are key to the execution of ferroptosis. However, when cIARS is depleted, this dissociation is effectively blocked, resulting in reduced autophagic activity and impaired ferroptotic cell death.

Anti-ferroptotic circrnas

Lyu et al. reported that circ0097009 is upregulated in HCC tissues and cell lines, acting as a ceRNA by sponging miR-1261, thereby upregulating SLC7A11. The knockdown of circ0097009 inhibited HCC cell proliferation and invasion, promoting ferroptosis by decreasing the GSH/GSSG ratio and deactivating GPX4, affecting Xc-system and GPX4 pathways [110].

Zhang et al. demonstrated that CircPIAS1 (hsa_circ_0007088) is overexpressed in HCC and inhibits ferroptosis by sponging miR-455-3p, upregulating NUPR1, which increases FTH1 expression. FTH1 sequesters iron, reducing its availability for the Fenton reaction, thus protecting cells from ferroptotic death. Silencing CircPIAS1 induced ferroptosis, increasing Fe²⁺ and lipid ROS levels, and decreasing GSH, reversible by ferroptosis inhibitors, highlighting its role in HCC progression [111].

Tan et al. [112] reported that circ0060467 sponges miR-6805, upregulating AIFM2 and GPX4. GPX4 reduces lipid hydroperoxides, directly inhibiting ferroptosis.

CircIL4R is upregulated in HCC and inhibits ferroptosis by sponging miR-541-3p, targeting GPX4. Its knockdown expedited ferroptosis, suggesting it facilitates tumorigenesis by enhancing GPX4 expression, similar to circ0060467 [113].

CircTTC13 functions by binding miR-513a-5p, thereby releasing the suppression of SLC7A11. The consequent upregulation of SLC7A11 impairs ferroptosis and contributes to drug resistance. Silencing circTTC13 reverses these effects, restoring sensitivity to sorafenib through the reactivation of ferroptosis [26].

Conclusions and perspectives

This comprehensive review synthesizes current evidence indicating that ferroptosis functions as a pivotal iron- and lipid peroxidation-dependent cell death mechanism in HCC, a role that is uniquely reinforced by the liver’s central involvement in iron homeostasis. Based on the body of literature examined herein, ncRNAs—including microRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, and circular RNAs—have been reported to act as critical modulators of ferroptotic pathways. Specifically, multiple studies cited in this review demonstrate that certain ncRNAs can either promote ferroptosis (e.g., miR-214-3p and MEG3) or suppress it (e.g., miR-23a-3p and PVT1), thereby exerting significant influence on tumor progression and therapeutic resistance, particularly to sorafenib. Collectively, the reviewed literature highlights the considerable potential of ncRNAs as both prognostic biomarkers (notably miR-122 and HULC) and innovative therapeutic targets for restoring ferroptosis sensitivity—a strategy that may address some of the most persistent therapeutic challenges in HCC.

Nevertheless, the existing literature presents notable inconsistencies and methodological limitations. As documented across multiple studies, the effects of specific ncRNAs on ferroptosis can be highly context-dependent, with a single ncRNA reported to exhibit pro-ferroptotic or anti-ferroptotic activity depending on cell type, tumor microenvironment, or experimental conditions (e.g., discrepancies between in vitro and in vivo models). We propose that these apparent contradictions may stem not from experimental error, but from the inherent biological plasticity of ncRNA networks in HCC. For instance, the dual role of certain ncRNAs could reflect their engagement with distinct molecular partners under varying redox or metabolic states—such as differential interaction with GPX4, SLC7A11, or NRF2 pathways depending on intracellular iron availability or lipid composition. This functional versatility underscores the need to move beyond binary classifications of ncRNAs as merely “pro-” or “anti-ferroptotic” and instead consider them as dynamic nodes within a context-sensitive regulatory circuit.

Furthermore, the interpretation of findings is often complicated by limited sample sizes, heterogeneity among HCC subtypes, and the absence of standardized assays for ferroptosis detection. These limitations, widely acknowledged in the reviewed literature, necessitate cautious interpretation when considering ncRNAs as clinical biomarkers or therapeutic targets, as their functional roles may not be universally generalizable. In light of this, we argue that future functional validation should prioritize isogenic HCC models that recapitulate defined etiologies (e.g., HBV-, HCV-, or NASH-driven subtypes) to disentangle disease-specific ncRNA–ferroptosis interactions.

Looking ahead, the synthesis of current knowledge reveals several high-priority research directions. Beyond cataloging ncRNA functions, we advocate for a mechanistic reorientation in the field: rather than merely identifying which ncRNAs regulate ferroptosis, research should focus on how their regulatory logic is rewired in therapy-resistant HCC. Future investigations should therefore focus on comprehensively delineating the context-specific, tissue-dependent, and model-dependent roles of ncRNAs in ferroptosis regulation. In particular, we hypothesize that ncRNA-mediated resistance to ferroptosis inducers may arise from adaptive feedback loops involving lipid metabolism enzymes (e.g., ACSL4, LPCAT3) or iron-regulatory proteins (e.g., IRP2, ferritin), which could be co-targeted to prevent compensatory escape mechanisms.

Concurrently, key unresolved questions include the temporal dynamics of ncRNA expression during ferroptotic cell death and the feasibility of combinatorial ncRNA-targeting strategies. Given the redundancy and compensation often observed in RNA networks, simultaneous modulation of multiple ncRNAs—such as co-inhibition of an anti-ferroptotic lncRNA (e.g., PVT1) and restoration of a tumor-suppressive miRNA (e.g., miR-214-3p)—may be necessary to achieve durable ferroptosis induction. Such strategies, however, require rigorous preclinical testing in models that preserve tumor-stroma interactions.

Moreover, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding how metabolic reprogramming in HCC cells modulates ncRNA–ferroptosis crosstalk. Notably, the shift toward glycolysis or glutaminolysis in advanced HCC may alter the availability of substrates critical for lipid peroxidation, thereby indirectly reshaping ncRNA functionality. We suggest that integrating metabolomic profiling with ncRNA expression analysis could reveal predictive signatures of ferroptosis susceptibility.

Importantly, translational considerations raised in preclinical studies underscore the need for careful evaluation of ncRNA-based ferroptosis modulation. Reported challenges include the development of safe and efficient delivery platforms (e.g., lipid nanoparticles or exosome-based carriers), ensuring ncRNA stability, minimizing off-target effects, and rigorously assessing immunogenicity and toxicity profiles. While promising, current delivery systems often lack tumor-selective targeting; we therefore propose that engineering ncRNA carriers with ligands against HCC-specific surface markers (e.g., GPC3 or ASGPR1) could enhance precision and reduce systemic toxicity.

Although early-phase clinical trials targeting ncRNAs in liver cancer have been initiated, robust clinical evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of such strategies in HCC remains scarce, reinforcing the need for well-designed, controlled clinical studies. Critically, future trials should stratify patients not only by HCC etiology but also by baseline ferroptosis competence—potentially assessed via circulating ncRNA panels or imaging biomarkers of lipid peroxidation—to identify those most likely to benefit.

The optimization of targeted delivery systems—particularly advanced nanoparticle-based platforms—emerges as a critical frontier. Additionally, we emphasize that combination regimens should be rationally designed based on mechanistic synergy rather than empirical pairing; for example, pairing a system xc⁻ inhibitor with an ncRNA that downregulates NRF2 may overcome redox adaptation more effectively than either agent alone.

Finally, integration of single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics will be instrumental in unraveling the heterogeneity of ncRNA-mediated ferroptosis regulation within the tumor microenvironment. Such approaches may reveal spatial niches—such as perivascular or hypoxic regions—where specific ncRNAs confer ferroptosis resistance, thereby guiding localized or microenvironment-modulating interventions.
