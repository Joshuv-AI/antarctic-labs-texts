Genomic Profiling of Anophthalmia/Microphthalmia‐Associated CNVs Reveals Complex Genotype–Phenotype Correlations and Incomplete Penetrance
Authors: Dong Wu, Mengting Zhang, Qian Zhang, Fengyang Wang, Qiaofang Hou, Hongyan Liu, Shixiu Liao, Hai Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnophthalmia/microphthalmia (A/M) is a severe congenital ocular malformation characterized by the complete absence or small size of the eye bulb. Interpreting copy number variations (CNVs) in A/M is challenged by variable genotype–phenotype correlations and reduced penetrance. This study investigated the genetic etiology of A/M‐associated CNVs.MethodsGenomic profiling was performed on four unrelated families presenting with ocular anomalies or harboring A/M‐susceptible CNVs. Variants were evaluated by integrating American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines with clinical phenotypes and familial segregation.ResultsAn inherited 8.13 Mb deletion (8p23.3p23.1) in Patient 1 was excluded due to genotype–phenotype mismatch. Patients 2 and 3 harbored de novo pathogenic deletions involving OTX2 (14q22.3) and SOX2 (3q26.33), causing typical A/M. Case 4 revealed a 14q22.2q23.1 deletion encompassing OTX2 in a fetus and mother without ocular anomalies, consistent with the incomplete penetrance of OTX2‐related microphthalmia. Thus, CNV‐induced haploinsufficiency causes A/M with high phenotypic variability.ConclusionAccurate CNV interpretation requires robust genotype–phenotype correlation and careful assessment of incomplete penetrance to prevent diagnostic pitfalls and improve genetic counseling.Keywords: array comparative genomic hybridization, copy number variation, haploinsufficiency, microphthalmia, next generation sequencing, penetrance

Introduction

The application of chromosomal microarray analysis and high‐throughput sequencing technologies in the diagnosis of ocular developmental disorders has led to the increasing identification of pathogenic copy number variations (CNVs) in conditions such as glaucoma (DeLuca et al. 2017), cataract (Siggs et al. 2017), iris dysplasia (Wang et al. 2018), uveal melanoma (Smit et al. 2018), and other ophthalmic diseases. Anophthalmia/microphthalmia (A/M) is characterized by the absence or reduced size of the eyeball, manifesting either bilaterally or unilaterally. While its underlying pathological mechanisms remain incompletely understood, the incidence of A/M in Chinese newborns is approximately 11.8 per 100,000 (Zhu et al. 2000). Notably, approximately 20% of these pediatric patients also present with developmental delay (Morrison et al. 2002). A/M exhibits extreme genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity; current research indicates that over 100 genes, in addition to structural variants such as CNVs resulting in haploinsufficiency, contribute to its pathogenesis (Takagi et al. 2015; Jin et al. 2020). Over the past two decades, extensive research and comprehensive reviews have substantially advanced our understanding of A/M genetics and genomic diagnostics, elucidating the complex genetic networks underlying ocular developmental pathways. Consequently, diagnostic paradigms have shifted toward comprehensive genomic evaluations. Approaches such as the “Oculome” targeted gene panel (Patel et al. 2019) and other broad next‐generation sequencing strategies (Plaisancié et al. 2019) have dramatically expanded the repertoire of A/M‐associated genes, improving both diagnostic yields and our understanding of structural ocular anomalies. Recently, the integration of comprehensive structural variant profiling has further unraveled the genetic complexity of these developmental disorders (Maftei et al. 2025).

In this study, comprehensive genomic profiling—including array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH), CNV sequencing (CNV‐seq), and trio‐whole exome sequencing (trio‐WES)—was utilized to investigate four cases of A/M. By exploring the underlying genetic etiologies, we elucidate the role of CNV‐induced gene haploinsufficiency in A/M development and underscore the necessity of rigorous genotype–phenotype correlation analysis to prevent clinical misdiagnosis.

Materials and Methods

Editorial Policies and Ethical Considerations

This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Henan Provincial People's Hospital (Approval No. 2019‐134) on December 9, 2019. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating families.

Participants

Genomic profiling was performed on four unrelated families presenting with ocular anomalies or harboring A/M‐susceptible CNVs. Patient 1 (a 8‐year‐old female) and Patient 2 (a 2‐month‐old male) were diagnosed with congenital bilateral microphthalmia, characterized by underdeveloped globes and significant visual impairment. Physical examinations revealed no other abnormalities. For Patient 3 (a fetus at 23 weeks' gestation), prenatal ultrasound revealed small bilateral orbits and an absence of normal ocular structures. For Patient 4 (a fetus at 24 weeks' gestation), prenatal ultrasound indicated a Blake's pouch cyst, a mildly hypoplastic cerebellar vermis, and ventriculomegaly (left lateral ventricle: 11.4 mm, right: 10.4 mm). The mother of Patient 4 exhibited a mild intellectual disability but had normal ocular development. Peripheral blood (2 mL) from Patients 1 and 2, amniotic fluid (20 mL) from Patients 3 and 4, and parental peripheral blood samples were collected for genetic analysis.

Methods

Extraction of Whole Genomic DNA

Genomic DNA from both peripheral blood and amniotic fluid samples was extracted using the Blood Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Cat. No. DP318, Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). The DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

CNV Detection (aCGH and CNV‐Seq)

For Patients 1 and 2, array aCGH was performed using 500 ng of qualified genomic DNA. Samples were analyzed using SurePrint G3 Human CGH Microarray 8 × 60K chips (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Following lysis, labeling with Cy3‐dUTP and Cy5‐dUTP, purification, hybridization, and washing, scanning and data extraction were conducted using an Agilent Microarray Scanner. CNVs were identified where three consecutive probe log2 values were greater than 0.25 or less than −0.25. CNVs exceeding 200 kb were routinely detected. Chip sequence information was based on the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome.

For Patients 3 and 4, CNV‐seq libraries were constructed using a high‐throughput sequencing kit (Berry Genomics, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The process involved DNA fragmentation, adapter ligation, and sample barcoding. Pooled libraries were sequenced on the NextSeq CN500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with a minimum 1× genome coverage, facilitating CNV detection at a 100‐kb resolution. Sequence reads were aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome; quality metrics, such as read depth and coverage uniformity, were evaluated to ensure data integrity. Candidate CNVs were annotated based on gene content and genomic intervals using the ClinGen, DECIPHER, ClinVar, gnomAD, and OMIM databases. Variants were filtered against population frequency databases, including DGV and ISCA. Finally, CNV interpretation followed the standardized scoring criteria of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) (Riggs et al. 2020).

Medical Exome Sequencing Analysis

To investigate potential point mutations, trio‐WES libraries for Patient 1, Patient 4, and their parents were prepared using the SureSelect Human All Exon Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The experimental process included DNA fragment digestion, library hybridization, capture library amplification, and purification. High‐throughput sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were analyzed using NextGENe software (SoftGenetics, State College, PA, USA) to assess sequencing quality and generate VCF files. Variant analysis and annotation were performed using Ingenuity Variant Analysis software (QIAGEN, Redwood City, CA, USA).

Results

CNV Analysis Results

Pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) CNVs were detected in all four patients through aCGH or CNV‐seq (see Table 1, Figure 1). In Patient 1, aCGH revealed an 8.13 Mb deletion at the 8p23.3p23.1 region. To interpret the clinical significance of the inherited 8.13 Mb deletion at 8p23.3p23.1 in Patient 1, comprehensive gene curation was conducted. Querying established genomic databases and dedicated ocular genetics resources—specifically OMIM, ClinGen dosage sensitivity maps, DECIPHER, and GeneReviews—revealed that none of the 44 protein‐coding genes within this interval exhibited established haploinsufficiency gene‐disease associations with microphthalmia or ocular malformations. In Patient 2, aCGH detected a 371 kb deletion at the 14q22.3 region. This deletion definitively encompasses the known haploinsufficient gene OTX2, which is strongly associated with syndromic and non‐syndromic microphthalmia.

Following comprehensive genetic counseling regarding the severity of the SOX2‐related phenotype, the pregnant woman and her family made the clinical decision to terminate the pregnancy.

In Patient 4, CNV‐seq identified a 4.44 Mb deletion at 14q22.2q23.1 encompassing haploinsufficient gene OTX2 and GCH1 inherited from the mother. To thoroughly investigate the phenotypic spectrum of the mother carrying the 4.44 Mb OTX2 deletion (who presented with mild intellectual disability), formal clinical evaluations were performed. Formal ophthalmologic examination confirmed normal visual acuity and structurally normal eye globes, with no evidence of subtle ocular defects such as iris/chorioretinal coloboma, optic nerve hypoplasia, or retinal dystrophy. Furthermore, clinical evaluation of endocrine function revealed routine endocrine screenings were unremarkable, corroborating that the maternal OTX2 deletion exhibits reduced/incomplete penetrance regarding structural ocular and pituitary development.

Medical Exome Sequencing Analysis Results

To rule out the possibility of underlying point mutations contributing to the phenotype in Patients 1 and 4, medical exome sequencing was performed. Trio‐WES analysis did not identify any pathogenic or LP single nucleotide variants (SNVs) or small indels associated with the microphthalmia phenotype in Patient 1 or her parents. This confirmed that the 8p23.3p23.1 deletion (detected via aCGH) was the sole significant genomic structural variation identified in this family. For Patient 4, trio‐WES analysis did not identify any pathogenic or LP SNVs or small indels associated with the intellectual disability phenotype in the pregnant woman or abnormal brain development in Patient 4.

Discussion

Microphthalmia and anophthalmia (A/M) are complex developmental anomalies driven by a multitude of environmental and genetic factors. Environmental triggers, including maternal infections (e.g., rubella, cytomegalovirus), prenatal alcohol exposure, and specific teratogens, significantly elevate the risk of A/M (Duszak 2009; Maina and Lakshmi 2009; Källén and Tornqvist 2005). Genetically, A/M exhibits extreme heterogeneity. While large chromosomal abnormalities, including trisomy 13, have been reported in a subset of individuals with syndromic A/M, the detection rate in non‐syndromic A/M remains relatively low (Källén and Tornqvist 2005). The genetic etiology of A/M predominantly involves eye‐field‐initiating transcription factors (e.g., SOX2, OTX2, and PAX6) and components of the retinoic acid signaling pathway. In this study, we utilized a combination of genomic approaches to delineate the genetic basis of four distinct A/M and related developmental anomaly cases, underscoring the critical role of CNV‐induced haploinsufficiency and the necessity of rigorous genotype–phenotype correlation. Despite the ever‐increasing diagnostic yields provided by comprehensive genomic testing and standardized frameworks like the “Oculome” (Patel et al. 2019; Harding and Moosajee 2019), navigating the expanded spectrum of A/M‐associated CNVs remains clinically challenging. As emphasized by recent comprehensive reviews (Plaisancié et al. 2019; Maftei et al. 2025), variable expressivity and reduced penetrance are hallmarks of many ocular developmental genes. Our findings perfectly illustrate this diagnostic complexity. Specifically, the genotypic‐phenotypic mismatch in Patient 1 and the direct observation of incomplete penetrance in the OTX2 deletion (Case 4) underscore that pathogenicity classifications provided by standard genomic pipelines should not be viewed as synonymous with disease causality. Even in the era of broad genomic profiling, accurate molecular diagnosis remains fundamentally dependent on phenotype‐driven interpretation and careful familial segregation.

Patient 1 presented with isolated bilateral microphthalmia. aCGH identified an 8.13 Mb deletion at 8p23.3p23.1 classified as “likely pathogenic” (LP) according to ACMG guidelines. Comprehensive clinical phenotyping and family segregation analysis excluded it as the causative variant. While the deletion partially overlaps with the 8p23.1 deletion syndrome region, it lacks the GATA4 gene, which accounts for the absence of congenital heart defects in this patient (Chien et al. 2010; Burnside et al. 2013). Furthermore, the deletion was inherited from an asymptomatic father, and the region contains no known A/M associated or imprinted genes. Subsequent trio‐WES likewise identified no pathogenic point mutations. Consequently, Patient 1 remains a genetically unsolved case. It is important to acknowledge that the true etiology for this patient may involve deep intronic variants, epigenetic modifications (such as altered methylation profiling), genetic regulatory sequence, etc., which are beyond the detection limits of standard aCGH and WES. It is also possible that the disease‐causing gene has not been identified or is not included in the routine analysis scope, resulting in a missed diagnosis. This case demonstrates that an LP classification for a structural variant does not necessarily imply causality for a specific phenotype and highlights the critical importance of integrating family history and comprehensive genomic data to prevent misdiagnosis.

The findings in Patients 2 and 4 are consistent with the incomplete penetrance of OTX2‐related microphthalmia. This gene encodes a transcription factor essential for the development of diverse ocular and anterior neural structures (Gregory et al. 2021). Pathogenic variants or deletions in OTX2 account for up to 8% of microphthalmia cases. Notably, OTX2‐related disorders exhibit marked variable expressivity; whereas some patients present with isolated ocular defects, others manifest severe syndromic features, including intellectual disability, developmental delay, or pituitary hypofunction (Schilter et al. 2011; Tajima et al. 2013).

Patient 2 presented with classic non‐syndromic bilateral microphthalmia, resulting from a de novo 371 kb deletion at 14q22.3 encompassing only OTX2. Conversely, Patient 4 carried a significantly larger 4.44 Mb deletion at 14q22.2q23.1 encompassing both OTX2 and GCH1 inherited from a mother with mild intellectual disability but normal ocular development. The fetus (Patient 4) exhibited structural brain anomalies (Blake's pouch cyst and widened lateral ventricles) rather than typical microphthalmia. This intrafamilial phenotypic divergence illustrates the incomplete penetrance of OTX2 regarding ocular phenotypes (Patat et al. 2013; Somashekar et al. 2017). The mechanisms underlying this phenotypic rescue remain complex. It is hypothesized that compensatory upregulation of the wild‐type OTX2 allele on the homologous chromosome, the presence of undefined protective modifier genes, or specific epigenetic regulations may sufficiently rescue the haploinsufficiency, allowing normal eye development in certain genetic backgrounds (Li et al. 2025). Furthermore, the co‐deletion of GCH1 (associated with dopa‐responsive dystonia and neurological phenotypes) likely contributes to a contiguous gene deletion syndrome (Wyatt et al. 2008). These findings underscore the necessity of long‐term clinical management and endocrinological monitoring for all individuals with OTX2 variants.

Patient 3, a fetus presenting with severe microphthalmia on prenatal ultrasound, was found to harbor a 1.20 Mb deletion at 3q26.33 involving the SOX2 gene. SOX2 encodes a critical SRY‐related HMG‐box transcription factor that is essential for early embryonic eye and brain development (Mercurio et al. 2022). Pathogenic variants in SOX2 are the most frequent genetic cause of microphthalmia, accounting for up to 40% of bilateral cases, and are predominantly de novo loss‐of‐function events (Gerth‐Kahlert et al. 2013; Chassaing et al. 2014). Routine prenatal ultrasonography may have limited sensitivity for detecting subtle ocular abnormalities, particularly during early gestation, many cases go undetected prenatally. The application of CNV‐seq in this instance provided a definitive and early molecular diagnosis of SOX2 haploinsufficiency, enabling the family to make an informed reproductive decision.

These findings highlight the critical importance of phenotypic evaluation in the clinical interpretation of structural variants. Rather than contradicting established protocols, our study strongly reinforces the “phenotype match” criterion emphasized by the 2019 ACMG guidelines for CNVs. A universally high quantitative pathogenicity score merely reflects the intrinsic deleterious potential of a CNV. However, as demonstrated by our cohort, final clinical reporting must be strictly gated by robust genotype–phenotype concordance and a comprehensive assessment of incomplete penetrance to prevent diagnostic pitfalls.

This study has several limitations. First, the exact base‐pair breakpoints of the identified large CNVs cannot be precisely mapped using aCGH or CNV‐seq alone, which restricts further exploration of regulatory element disruptions. Second, although prenatal ultrasound and MRI for Case 4 indicated normal ocular structures, microscopic anomalies of the retina or subtle visual impairments cannot be entirely ruled out prenatally, necessitating long‐term postnatal ophthalmological follow‐ups.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that gene haploinsufficiency resulting from CNVs is a primary genetic etiology of A/M. Integrating high‐resolution structural variant profiling (aCGH/CNV‐seq) with complementary methodologies, such as trio‐based whole‐exome sequencing (trio‐WES), represents a robust strategy for the genetic diagnosis of ocular developmental disorders. Crucially, our findings underscore that the clinical interpretation of genomic data including the exclusion of the 8p deletion in Patient 1 and the assessment of the incomplete penetrance of OTX2 in Patient 4 must be rigorously grounded in phenotypic concordance and segregation analysis to ensure diagnostic accuracy and optimize genetic counseling.
