Stationary random measures: Covariance asymptotics, variance bounds and central limit theorems
Manjunath Krishnapur, D. Yogeshwaran

Abstract
We consider covariance asymptotics for linear statistics of a general stationary random measure in terms of its truncated pair correlation measure. These asymptotics are particularly of interest in the case of hyperuniform random measures. We give exact infinite series-expansion formulas for the covariance of smooth statistics of random measures involving higher-order integrals of the truncated correlation measures and higher-order derivatives of the test functions and also, equivalently in terms of their Fourier transforms. Exploiting this, we describe possible covariance and variance asymptotics for Sobolev and indicator statistics. In the smooth case, we show that the order of variance asymptotics drops by even powers and give a simple example of a random measure exhibiting such a variance reduction. In the case of indicator statistics of C 1 C^{1} -smooth sets, we derive covariance asymptotics at surface-order scale with the limiting constant depending on the intersection of the boundaries of the two sets. We complement this with a lower bound for random measures with a non-trivial atomic part. Restricting to hyperuniform simple point processes, we prove a central limit theorem for Sobolev and Hölder continuous statistics of simple point processes satisfying a certain integral identity for higher-order truncated correlation functions. Keywords: Random measures, covariance asymptotics, variance bounds, correlation functions, hyperuniformity, linear statistics, central limit theorem. MSC2020 Classification: 60G55, 60D05 ; 60F05, 60G57.

where

\displaystyle Q_{m}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)=\begin{cases}(\lambda_{D}+I(0))\ \langle f,g\rangle&\mbox{ if }m=0,\\
\sum\limits_{\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}:\ |\gamma|=m}\frac{1}{\gamma!}I(\gamma)\langle f,\partial^{\gamma}g\rangle&\mbox{ if }m\geq 1.\end{cases}

(2.2)

Though not as strong as in the first assumption, some assumption on asymptotic decay of correlations will be present throughout the paper. The real analyticity assumption in the second one is a strong assumption on the test functions to enable us to write a clean formula for the covariance. This assumption will be relaxed. The third assumption is to ensure that the resulting series is convergent. If
\int e^{C|z|}|{\bf K}|(\mathrm{d}z)<\infty
and
\|\partial^{\alpha}g\|_{2}\leq C^{|\alpha|}
for some
C
and all
\alpha
, then this condition can be verified.

Proof.

The assumption that
f\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}
ensures that
{\mathcal{X}}(f)
and
{\mathcal{X}}(f)^{2}
have finite expectations due to (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) respectively and hence
{\cal X}(f)
has finite variance. Once
{\mathcal{X}}(f)
and
{\mathcal{X}}(g)
have finite variances, their covariance is well-defined. Now, using Campbell’s formula and the symmetry of the truncated correlation measure, we can write the covariance as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}(f),{\mathcal{X}}(g))=\int f(x)g(x)\alpha_{D}(\mathrm{d}x)+\int\!\!\!\int f(x)g(y)(\alpha_{2}^{!}-\lambda^{2})(\mathrm{d}(x,y))

\displaystyle=\left[\lambda_{D}+\int{\bf K}\right]\int fg+\int\!\!\int[f(x)g(x-z)-f(x)g(x)]{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}x

(2.3)

The absolute integrability required for reordering the integrals follows from the following bounds:

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int|f(x)|\,|g(x)|\,|{\bf K}|(\mathrm{d}z)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\left(\int|{\bf K}|\right)\left(\int|fg|\right)\leq\|{\bf K}\|\,\|f\|_{2}\,\|g\|_{2},

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int|f(x)||g(x-z)|\,|{\bf K}|(\mathrm{d}z)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\||{\bf K}|*|g|\|_{2}\|f\|_{2}\leq\|{\bf K}\|\|f\|_{2}\|g\|_{2}.

The last inequality is true because
{\bf K}
has finite total variation and
g\in L^{2}
.

In the second term in (
2.3
), use the real analyticity of
g
and expand
g(x-z)-g(x)
in power series to obtain

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int f(x)[g(x-z)-g(x)]{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\int f(x)\times\sum_{\alpha\not=0}\frac{1}{\alpha!}\partial^{\alpha}g(x)z^{\alpha}\ {\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\alpha!}\langle f,\partial^{\alpha}g\rangle I(\alpha).

To see that the sum and the two integrals can be ordered in any way (as we did), apply absolute values to the inner term and use the third assumption in the statement of the lemma. Finally, notice that the symmetry of
{\bf K}
implies that
I(\alpha)=0
if
|\alpha|
is odd to obtain (
2.1
).

\Box

Let us restate the exact formula using Fourier transforms as is commonly done in the literature. It will be a more convenient form to work with later when we try to reduce the assumptions on the test functions. First, let us fix our conventions regarding the Fourier transform.

For
f\in L^{1}
, the Fourier transform
\hat{f}
is defined by
\hat{f}(t)=\int f(x)e^{i{\langle}x,t{\rangle}}\mathrm{d}x
for
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for a signed measure
\mu
with finite total variation (e.g.,
{\bf K}
), the same definition works, i.e.,
\hat{\mu}(t)=\int e^{i{\langle}x,t{\rangle}}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)
. For
f,g\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}
, the Plancherel identity
{\langle}f,g{\rangle}=(2\pi)^{-d}{\langle}\hat{f},\hat{g}{\rangle}
holds. As
L^{1}\cap L^{2}
is dense in
L^{2}
, the Fourier transform extends continuously to
L^{2}
, and the Plancherel identity continues to hold. If
f\in L^{2}
has partial derivatives (in the weak sense) up to order
k
and all of them are in
L^{2}
, then
\widehat{\partial^{\alpha}f}(t)=(-it)^{\alpha}\hat{f}(t)
for
|\alpha|\leq k
. Conversely, if
(iz)^{\alpha}f(z)
is in
L^{2}
, then its Fourier transform is
\partial^{\alpha}\hat{f}
. These are essentially the same fact because of the Fourier inversion formula
\hat{\hat{f}}(z)=(2\pi)^{d}f(-z)
.

Just using these facts, one can rewrite
Q_{m}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)
in terms of Fourier transforms, as in (
2.4
) below. However, we rederive it from scratch, as the preliminary expressions will be useful later and also because the conditions on
{\bf K}
and
f,g
will be changed slightly.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a stationary random measure and let
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Assume that

1.

\widehat{\bf K}
is real analytic on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.

g
is smooth. Further,
f,g
and partial derivatives of
g
of all orders are in
L^{1}\cap L^{2}
.

3.

\sum\limits_{\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}}\frac{1}{\gamma!}|\partial^{\gamma}\widehat{{\bf K}}(0)|\,\,\|\partial^{\gamma}g\|_{2}<\infty.

Then (
2.1
) holds where the summands can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle Q_{m}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)=\begin{cases}(2\pi)^{-d}(\lambda_{D}+\widehat{{\bf K}}(0))\ \int\hat{f}(t)\overline{\hat{g}(t)}\mathrm{d}t&\mbox{ if }m=0,\\
(2\pi)^{-d}\sum\limits_{\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}:\ |\gamma|=m}\frac{\partial^{\gamma}\widehat{{\bf K}}(0)}{\gamma!}\int t^{\gamma}\hat{f}(t)\overline{\hat{g}(t)}\mathrm{d}t&\mbox{ if }m\geq 1.\end{cases}

(2.4)

The conclusion of the lemma is exactly the same as that of Lemma
2.1
. The equivalence of (
2.4
) with (
2.2
) is also easy to see from the fact that
\partial^{\gamma}\widehat{{\bf K}}(0)=i^{|\gamma|}I(\gamma)
and

{\langle}f,\partial^{\gamma}g{\rangle}=(2\pi)^{-d}{\langle}\hat{f},\widehat{\partial^{\gamma}g}{\rangle}=(2\pi)^{-d}(-i)^{|\gamma|}\int t^{\gamma}\hat{f}(t)\overline{\hat{g}(t)}\mathrm{d}t.

(2.5)

But a subtle change is that compared to Lemma
2.1
, in Lemma
2.2
the assumption on
{\bf K}
is stronger (that the tail of
|{\bf K}|
decays faster than any polynomial only implies the smoothness of
\widehat{{\bf K}}
, not real analyticity), while the assumption on
g
is weaker (integrability of
t^{\gamma}\hat{f}\,\overline{\hat{g}}
is ensured by the smoothness of
g
, there is no need for real-analyticity).

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Rewriting (
2.3
) and using Plancherel’s formula, we can derive

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}(f),{\mathcal{X}}(g))

\displaystyle={\langle}f,g*({\bf K}+\lambda_{D}\delta_{0}){\rangle}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}{\langle}\hat{f},\hat{g}(\widehat{{\bf K}}+\lambda_{D}){\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\hat{f}(t)\overline{\hat{g}(t)}(\widehat{{\bf K}}(t)+\lambda_{D})\mathrm{d}t.

(2.6)

Since
g\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}
and
{\bf K}
has finite total variation, we have
g*({\bf K}+\lambda_{D}\delta_{0})\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}
and hence we can use Plancherel’s identity. As
{\bf K}
is a symmetric measure,
\widehat{{\bf K}}
is real-valued and hence we omitted the conjugation on it. Now, using the real analyticity of
\widehat{{\bf K}}
, we expand
\widehat{{\bf K}}
in power series about the origin to obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}(f),{\mathcal{X}}(g))

\displaystyle=\frac{(\widehat{{\bf K}}(0)+\lambda_{D})}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\hat{f}(t)\overline{\hat{g}}(t)\mathrm{d}t\ +\ \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}:|\gamma|\in 2\mathbb{N}}\frac{\partial^{\gamma}\widehat{{\bf K}}(0)}{\gamma!}\int t^{\gamma}\hat{f}(t)\overline{\hat{g}(t)}\mathrm{d}t.

The interchange of sum and integral is justified using the third assumption and (
2.5
) and furthermore the terms with odd
|\gamma|
dropped out as
\widehat{{\bf K}}
is an even function. That the sum of the terms corresponding to
|\gamma|=2k
is equal to
Q_{2k}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)
follows from (
2.5
).

\Box

The exact formula simplifies if
{\mathcal{X}}
satisfies additional symmetries. As many random measures do have these additional symmetries, it is worth stating these formulas. While doing that, we also make equal assumptions on
f
and
g
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let the setting be as in Lemma
2.1
and in addition, assume that
f
satisfies the same assumptions as
g
.

1.

Flip invariance:
If
{\bf K}
is invariant under reflections in the co-ordinate hyperplanes, then
I(\gamma)=0
unless
\gamma\in(2\mathbb{Z}_{+})^{d}
and hence,

\displaystyle Q_{2p}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)=\sum_{|\gamma|=p}\frac{(-1)^{p}}{(2\gamma)!}I(2\gamma)\langle\partial^{\gamma}f,\partial^{\gamma}g\rangle

(2.7)

2.

Orthogonal invariance:
If
{\bf K}
is invariant under orthogonal transformations, we may write
{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z)=r^{d-1}{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}r)\sigma(\mathrm{d}\omega)
where
z=r\omega
with
r>0
,
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
\sigma
denotes the surface-area measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Here we abuse notation by using
{\bf K}
for the measure on the radial part as well but that will not cause any confusion. Then

\displaystyle Q_{2p}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)=\begin{cases}(\lambda_{D}+\sigma_{d}J(d-1))\ \langle f,g\rangle&\mbox{ if }p=0,\\
\frac{(-1)^{p}\sigma_{d}J(d-1+2p)}{p!\ 2^{p}\ d(d+2)\ldots(d+2p-2)}\langle\Delta^{p/2}f,\Delta^{p/2}g\rangle&\mbox{ if }p\geq 1.\end{cases}

(2.8)

Here
J(p):=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{p}\,{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}r)
and
\Delta
is the Laplacian, whose
\tfrac{p}{2}
power is interpreted as
\Delta^{p/2}f:=\nabla(\Delta^{q}f)
if
p=2q+1
.

{\bf K}
satisfies flip (resp. orthogonal) invariance, if
{\cal X}
satisfies flip (resp. orthogonal) invariance in distribution.

Proof.

We start with (
2.1
) and simplify it under the extra assumptions.

(1)
Invariance under reflections in the co-ordinate hyperplanes implies that
{\bf K}
is an even function of each co-ordinate. Therefore
I(\gamma)=0
unless
\gamma\in(2\mathbb{Z}_{+})^{d}
. For
\gamma=2\beta
, integrate by parts to write
{\langle}f,\partial^{\gamma}g{\rangle}
as
(-1)^{|\beta|}{\langle}\partial^{\beta}f,\partial^{\beta}g{\rangle}
to get (
2.7
).

(2)
Transforming to polar co-ordinates, we have

\displaystyle I(2\gamma)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}r^{d-1+2|\gamma|}{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}r)\right)\times\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\omega_{1}^{2\gamma_{1}}\ldots\omega_{d}^{2\gamma_{d}}\ d\sigma(\omega)\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{d}J(d-1+2|\gamma|)B_{d}(2\gamma),

with
\sigma_{d}
and
J_{p}
as in the statement and

B_{d}(2\gamma):=\frac{1}{\sigma_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\omega_{1}^{2\gamma_{1}}\ldots\omega_{d}^{2\gamma_{d}}d\sigma(\omega)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1})\ldots\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{d})}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})^{d}\ \Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+|\gamma|)}.

The last formula comes from the Dirichlet integral and the fact that on the probability space
(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\sigma(\cdot)/\sigma_{d})
, the random vector
(\omega_{1}^{2},\ldots,\omega_{d}^{2})
has Dirichlet(
\frac{1}{2},\ldots,\frac{1}{2}
) distribution. After some simplifications,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\gamma)!}I(2\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{d}J(d-1+2|\gamma|)}{2^{|\gamma|}d(d+2)\ldots(d+2|\gamma|-2)}\times\frac{1}{\gamma!}.

Now it is clear that for
Q_{0}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)
is as claimed and that for
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle Q_{2p}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{d}J(d-1+2p)}{2^{p}d(d+2)\ldots(d+2p-2)}\sum_{|\gamma|=p}\frac{1}{\gamma!}\langle f,\partial^{2\gamma}g\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{d}J(d-1+2p)}{p!\ 2^{p}\ d(d+2)\ldots(d+2p-2)}\langle f,\Delta^{p}g\rangle.

Here
\Delta=\partial_{1}^{2}+\ldots+\partial_{d}^{2}
is the Laplacian and to get there from the previous line, we just used the multinomial expansion of
\Delta^{p}
. Integrating by parts, we can write
{\langle}f,\Delta^{p}g{\rangle}
as
\langle\Delta^{p/2}f,\Delta^{p/2}g\rangle
, with the interpretation for odd
p
as in the statement of the corollary. Thus (
2.8
) follows.

\Box

2.2
Variance upper bounds

In this section, we wish to write explicit variance upper bounds for linear statistics with minimal assumptions on the smoothness of the test functions. This will be useful in transferring results from smooth linear statistics to more general linear statistics. For example, we use it in finding covariance asymptotics (Proposition
2.5
) and in proving central limit theorems (Theorem
4.3
), both with minimal smoothness assumptions.

We recall the Sobolev space
H^{k}=H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(we follow the conventions and notations in
[
34
, Chapter 2]
), the space of all functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose weak derivatives up to and including order
k
exist and are in
L^{2}
. It is a Hilbert space with norm
\|f\|_{H^{k}}^{2}:=(2\pi)^{-d}\int(1+|z|^{2})^{k}|\hat{f}(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z
. This is a standard choice for the Sobolev norm, equivalent to another standard definition as the square root of the sum of squared
L^{2}
norms of all partial derivatives up to and including order
k
. In the latter, if we take only
L^{2}
norms of partial derivatives of order equal to
k
, then it is a pseudo-norm equivalent to
|f|_{H^{k}}^{2}:=(2\pi)^{-d}\int|z|^{2k}|\hat{f}(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z
. For example, when
k=1
, then
|f|_{H^{1}}^{2}\asymp\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{2}
while
\|f\|_{H^{1}}^{2}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{2}
. It is a common convention to set
H^{0}=L^{2}
, for which of course
\|f\|_{H^{0}}^{2}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}=|f|_{H^{0}}^{2}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a stationary random measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Assuming
\|{\bf K}\|<\infty
,

{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}(f)\right)\leq(\lambda_{D}+\|\widehat{{\bf K}}\|_{\infty})\|f\|_{2}^{2}\;\;\mbox{ for any }f\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}.

If
\lambda_{D}+I(0)=0
and
I(\gamma)=0
for
|\gamma|<2k
for some
k\geq 1
, then assuming that
\widehat{{\bf K}}\in C^{2k}
and that all its partial derivatives up to order
2k
are uniformly bounded, with
\|D^{2k}\widehat{{\bf K}}\|_{\infty}:=\sup\limits_{|\gamma|=2k}\|\partial^{\gamma}\widehat{{\bf K}}\|_{\infty}
, we have

{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}(f)\right)\leq\frac{d^{k}}{(2k-1)!}\|D^{2k}\widehat{{\bf K}}\|_{\infty}\,|f|_{H^{k}}^{2}\;\;\mbox{ for any }f\in L^{1}\cap H^{k}.

Proof.

From (
2.6
), we get

\displaystyle\mbox{Var}({\mathcal{X}}(f))

\displaystyle\leq(\|\widehat{{\bf K}}\|_{\infty}+\lambda_{D})\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int|\hat{f}|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\|\widehat{{\bf K}}\|_{\infty}+\lambda_{D})\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

Now suppose
\lambda_{D}+I(0)=0
and that
I(\gamma)=0
for
|\gamma|<2k
for some
k\geq 1
. Then
Q_{j}^{{\bf K}}(f,f)=0
for
j<2k
. In (
2.6
), use the Taylor expansion of
\widehat{{\bf K}}
to order
2k-1
as

\widehat{{\bf K}}(z)=\widehat{{\bf K}}(0)+\sum_{\gamma:|\gamma|\leq 2k-1}\frac{\partial^{\gamma}\widehat{{\bf K}}(0)}{\gamma!}z^{\gamma}+\sum_{\gamma:|\gamma|=2k}\frac{2k}{\gamma!}R_{\gamma}(z)z^{\gamma}

where the remainder term
R_{\gamma}(z)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{2k}\ \partial^{\gamma}\widehat{{\bf K}}(tz)\mathrm{d}t
. As
\lambda_{D}+\widehat{{\bf K}}(0)=0
and
\partial^{\gamma}\widehat{{\bf K}}(0)=0
(recall that
\widehat{{\bf K}}(0)=I(0)
and
\partial^{\gamma}\widehat{{\bf K}}(0)
is a constant times
I(\gamma)
for
|\gamma|<2k
), (
2.6
) reduces to

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}(f)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{|\gamma|=2k}\frac{1}{\gamma!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{2k}|z^{\gamma}|\,\,|\hat{f}(z)|^{2}|\partial^{\gamma}\widehat{{\bf K}}(tz)|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}z.

Bound
1-t
by
1
and
|\partial^{\gamma}\widehat{{\bf K}}(tz)|
by
\|D^{2k}\widehat{{\bf K}}\|_{\infty}
. When
|z^{\gamma}|/\gamma!
is summed over
|\gamma|=2k
, we get
(|z_{1}|+\ldots+|z_{d}|)^{2k}/(2k)!
(multinomial expansion), which can be bounded by
d^{k}|z|^{2k}/(2k)!
(where
|z|^{2}=|z_{1}|^{2}+\ldots+|z_{d}|^{2}
). Putting these together, we arrive at

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}(f)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{k}}{(2k-1)!}\|D^{2k}\widehat{{\bf K}}\|_{\infty}\,|f|_{H^{k}}^{2},

which completes the proof.

\Box

2.3
Covariance asymptotics

For
L\geq 1
, consider the scaled random measures
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}
, defined as
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(A)={\mathcal{X}}(LA)
for sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
(we could of course define
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}
for
L>0
, but we are interested in the asymptotics as
L\to\infty
, and the uniformity of certain estimates breaks down when
L\to 0
). What to expect is made clear by Lemma
2.1
or Lemma
2.2
and we will now formalize this.

Let us denote the quantities associated to
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}
by adding the subscript
L
. From (
1.4
), we derive that
{\bf K}_{L}(A)=L^{d}{\bf K}(LA)
for any Borel
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and hence by a straightforward change of variable, we obtain that
I_{L}(\gamma)=\int z^{\gamma}{\bf K}_{L}(\mathrm{d}z)=L^{d-|\gamma|}I(\gamma)
. Thus
Q_{m}^{{\bf K}_{L}}(f,g)=L^{d-m}Q_{m}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)
. For
f,g
satisfying the assumptions of Lemma
2.1
, we therefore get

\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{d-2k}Q_{2k}^{{\bf K}}(f,g).

(2.9)

As
L\to\infty
, the first non-zero summand dominates and that determines the order of the covariance as well as the limiting covariance (of that order). Observe the surprising feature that if the covariance is
o(L^{d})
, then it must be
O(L^{d-2})
and so on, getting only alternate powers of
L
. Later in Section
2.4
, we shall see an example of random measure showing that all these orders are achievable even if
\langle f,\partial^{\gamma}g\rangle
are non-vanishing for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}
.

As it stands, the above proof only works for
f,g
satisfying the hypotheses of Lemma
2.1
, and those are too restrictive. For example, if
\lambda_{D}+I(0)=0
and
I(\gamma)=0
for
|\gamma|<2k
for some
k\geq 1
, then the above proof approach suggests that
\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}(f),{\mathcal{X}}(g))\sim L^{d-2k}Q_{2k}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)
. Considering that
Q_{2k}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)
is well-defined if
f,g\in H^{k}
, one may expect that this should be the only required condition in addition to
f,g\in L^{1}
. Indeed, our variance bound in Lemma
2.4
was proved precisely for functions in
H^{k}\cap L^{1}
.

Random measures satisfying
\lambda_{D}+I(0)=\lambda_{D}+\hat{{\bf K}}(0)=0
are said to be
hyperuniform
and in such cases, variance of linear statistics are
o(L^{d})
. There is now an extensive body of work on hyperuniform systems; see the surveys
[
59
,
14
,
39
]
. The next proposition shows some curious restrictions on the possible variance asymptotics for hyperuniform random measures.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a stationary random measure and let
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}
denote the scaled random measure defined by
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(A)={\mathcal{X}}(LA)
for
L\geq 1
. Assume that
\int|z^{\gamma}||{\bf K}|(\mathrm{d}z)<\infty
for
|\gamma|\leq 2k+1
. Let
f,g\in L^{1}\cap H^{k}
functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
k\geq 0
(with the convention that
H^{0}=L^{2}
). Then,

1.

L^{-d}\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))\to Q_{0}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)
as
L\to\infty
.

2.

If
\lambda_{D}+I(0)=0
and
I(\alpha)=0
for
1\leq|\alpha|<2k
, then

L^{-d+2k}\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))\to Q_{2k}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)
.

3.

If
\lambda_{D}+I(0)=0
and
I(\alpha)=0
for
1\leq|\alpha|\leq 2k
, then
\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))=O(L^{d-2k-1})
if one of
f
or
g
is in
C_{b}^{2k+1}
(functions with bounded, continuous partial derivatives of order
2k+1
).

We prove the proposition in two steps. First we give a proof that works when at least one of
f
or
g
is in
C_{b}^{2k+1}
. Next, we use the fact that
C_{c}^{\infty}
is dense in
H^{k}
, and the variance bound from Lemma
2.4
to extend the result to
f,g\in L^{1}\cap H^{k}
. This approximation strategy is adapted from Rider and Virág
[
52
]
who did a similar thing for the Ginibre ensemble with
k=1
.

Proof of Proposition
2.5
.

STEP 1:
Proof when one of
f,g
is in
C_{b}^{2k+1}
.

Assume that
g\in C_{b}^{2k+1}
and consider the Taylor expansion

g(x-z)-g(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}:1\leq|\alpha|\leq 2k}\frac{(-1)^{|\alpha|}}{\alpha!}\partial^{\alpha}g(x)z^{\alpha}\ +\ \sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}:|\alpha|=2k+1}\frac{(-1)^{|\alpha|}|\alpha|}{\alpha!}R_{\alpha}^{g}(x,z)z^{\alpha}

with remainder term
R_{\alpha}^{g}(x,z)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{2k+1}\partial^{\alpha}g(x-tz)\mathrm{d}t
. From this it is clear that
|R_{\alpha}^{g}(x,z)|\leq\|\partial^{\alpha}g\|_{\infty}
and that
R_{\alpha}^{g}
is continuous in
x,z
(hence the integrals below are legitimate). Apply formula (
2.3
) to
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}
and observe that
{\bf K}_{L}(\mathrm{d}z)=L^{2d}{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}(Lz))
to get (recall that
I(\alpha)=0
for odd
|\alpha|
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))=

\displaystyle L^{d}(\lambda_{D}+I(0)){\langle}f,g{\rangle}+\sum_{1\leq|\alpha|\leq 2k,|\alpha|\in 2\mathbb{N}}L^{d-|\alpha|}\frac{1}{\alpha!}I(\alpha){\langle}f,\partial^{\alpha}g{\rangle}

\displaystyle-L^{d-2k-1}\sum_{|\alpha|=2k+1}\frac{(2k+1)}{\alpha!}\int\!\!\int f(x)R_{\alpha}^{g}\left(x,\frac{z}{L}\right)z^{\alpha}{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}x.

(2.10)

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
with
|\alpha|=2k+1
,

\displaystyle\big|\int\!\!\int f(x)R_{\alpha}^{g}\left(x,\frac{z}{L}\right)z^{\alpha}{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}x\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|\partial^{\alpha}g\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{L^{1}}\int|z^{\alpha}||{\bf K}|(\mathrm{d}z).

This bound is independent of
L
, showing that the third term on the right in (
2.10
) is
O(L^{d-2k-1})
. The coefficient of
L^{d-2j}
for
0\leq j\leq k
is precisely
Q_{2j}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)
, by (
2.2
). Thus,

\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))=\sum_{j=0}^{k}L^{d-2j}Q_{2j}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)\;+\;O(L^{d-2k-1}).

All three statements of the proposition follow immediately when one of
f
or
g
is in
C_{b}^{2k+1}
. It remains to prove the second statement for more general
f,g
.

STEP 2:
Proof for
f,g\in L^{1}\cap H^{k}
.

It is well-known (Theorem 2.1.3 in
[
34
]
) that
C_{c}^{\infty}
(and hence
C_{b}^{2k+1}
) is dense in
H^{k}
. Given
f,g
, there exist
f_{n},g_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}
such that
\|f_{n}-f\|_{H^{k}}\to 0
and
\|g_{n}-g\|_{H^{k}}\to 0
.

Consider the first two cases: that either
I(0)\not=-\lambda_{D}
or
I(\gamma)\not=0
for some
|\gamma|\leq 2k
. We have proved that for fixed
n
, as
L\to\infty
,

L^{-d+2k}\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f_{n}),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g_{n}))\to Q_{2k}^{{\bf K}}(f_{n},g_{n}).

Further,
Q_{2k}^{{\bf K}}(f_{n},g_{n})\to Q_{2k}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)
as
n\to\infty
as seen from (
2.2
). Now,

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))-\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f_{n}),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g_{n}))|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g-g_{n}))|+|\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f-f_{n}),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g_{n}))|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)\right)}\sqrt{{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g-g_{n})\right)}+\sqrt{{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g_{n})\right)}\sqrt{{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f-f_{n})\right)}

\displaystyle\leq CL^{d-2k}\{|f|_{H^{k}}|g-g_{n}|_{H^{k}}+|g_{n}|_{H^{k}}|f-f_{n}|_{H^{k}}\}.

In the last line, we invoked Lemma
2.4
, using the observation that
\widehat{{\bf K}}_{L}(t)=L^{d}\widehat{{\bf K}}(t/L)
and consequently
\|D^{2k}\widehat{{\bf K}}_{L}\|_{\infty}\leq L^{d-2k}\|D^{2k}\widehat{{\bf K}}\|_{\infty}
. Here we also use that the integrability condition on
{\bf K}
implies the smoothness of
\widehat{{\bf K}}
as required in Lemma
2.4
. Clearly
|f-f_{n}|_{H^{k}}\leq\|f-f_{n}\|_{H^{k}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and similarly
|g_{n}-g|_{H^{k}}\to 0
. In particular,
|g_{n}|_{H^{k}}\to|g|_{H^{k}}
. Thus, letting
L\to\infty
and then
n\to\infty
, we see that
L^{-d+2k}\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))\to Q_{2k}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)
.

\Box

The proof in STEP 1 above can be regarded as a modification of the proof of Lemma
2.1
. Instead we could have modified the proof of Lemma
2.2
using the Taylor expansion of
\widehat{{\bf K}}
to order
2k
(similar to what we did in the proof of Lemma
2.4
) to get an almost identical result. Covariance asymptotics as above for analytic functions has been obtained recently in
[
45
, Theorem 5.1]
by more carefully analyzing (
2.6
).

2.4
Examples

We investigate the asymptotics of
\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))
explicitly for some specific random measures and simple point processes. Though the variance asymptotics for the simple point processes are derived elsewhere by the specific structure of the point process under consideration, here we show how they follow from our general result (Proposition
2.5
) and also we obtain covariance asymptotics without additional work. We also provide a simple example of a random measure that has arbitrary polynomial decay of covariance but it is more challenging to find an example of a point process with arbitrary decay of covariance as it is not easy to guarantee existence of stationary point processes with a specified truncated correlation function; see
[
2
,
10
]
. Nevertheless, using explicit analysis of structure factor (i.e.,
\lambda_{D}+\widehat{{\bf K}}(\cdot)
), point processes with arbitrary polynomial decay of structure factor (or equivalently covariance asymptotics) has been indicated in
[
26
, Section III.B]
.

We will not define the point processes formally but refer to
[
31
,
40
,
4
,
14
]
for definitions and also more examples of point processes. We also describe higher-order truncated correlations for determinantal processes and zeros of Gaussian entire function in Section
4.2
.
Though the main focus of the article is on hyperuniform random measures, we begin with the well-known example of Poisson process for completeness and to contrast with the other examples.

1.

The stationary Poisson process.
See
[
40
]
for definition. The correlations are given by

\rho_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\lambda^{k}
for some
\lambda>0
. Indeed this characterizes the stationary Poisson point process. In this case,
\kappa(z)=0
and hence
I(0)=0
. Thus,
\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))\sim\lambda L^{d}\langle f,g\rangle
. That is, the variance is volume order and in the limit we get white noise.

2.

The Ginibre example.
See
[
31
, Section 6.4]
for definition. We just recall that this is a point process on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\mathbb{C}
whose correlations are given by
\rho_{k}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=\det(K(z_{i},z_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
where
K(z,w)=\frac{1}{\pi}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|z|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|w|^{2}+z\overline{w}}
. From the first two correlations we see that
\lambda=1/\pi
and
\kappa(z)=-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}e^{-|z|^{2}}
and hence
I(0)=-\lambda
but
I(\alpha)\neq 0
for
\alpha=(2,0)
or
\alpha=(0,2)
. In fact, (since the process is isotropic) using Proposition
2.5
and Corollary
2.3
, we have that for
f,g\in L^{1}\cap H^{1}
,

\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))=-\frac{\pi J(3)}{2}\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle

where we have used

\sigma_{2}=2\pi,\quad J(3)=-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}r^{3}e^{-r^{2}}dr=-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}.

This was first derived in Rider and Virág
[
52
, Lemmas 12 and 14]
.

3.

Zeros of the G.E.F.
See
[
31
, Chapter 2]
for more details on this point process. This is another point process on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\mathbb{C}
. Let
F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}
where
a_{n}
are i.i.d. standard complex Gaussians, then
F
is a Gaussian analytic function on the whole plane with covariance
\mathbb{E}[F(z)\overline{F(w)}]=e^{z\overline{w}}
. The zeros of
F
form a stationary point process. Well-known Kac-Rice formulas allow to express the correlation functions of the zero set in terms of the covariance kernel. In particular, it has intensity
\frac{1}{\pi}
and
\rho_{2}(z,w)-\rho_{1}(z)\rho_{1}(w)=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\Delta_{z}\Delta_{w}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{1}{4m^{2}}e^{-m|z-w|^{2}}
. After some computation,

\displaystyle\kappa(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\;\sum_{m\geq 1}e^{-m|z|^{2}}(2-4m|z|^{2}+m^{2}|z|^{4}).

(2.11)

Integration by parts (transfer the Laplacians from
\kappa
to the test functions), it is clear that
I(2\gamma)=0
for
|\gamma|\leq 1
. Hence using Proposition
2.5
and Corollary
2.3
with
p=2
, we have that for
f,g\in L^{1}\cap H^{2}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}L^{2}\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi J(5)}{2!\times 2^{2}\times 2(2+2)}\times\langle\Delta f,\Delta g\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta(3)}{16\pi}\langle\Delta f,\Delta g\rangle.

In the last line, we used (
2.11
) to compute
J(5)
explicitly. This expression was derived by Forrester and Honner
[
22
]
. Also see
[
54
]
and
[
47
]
for more on variance asymptotics for various statistics (including indicator statistics) of zeros of G.E.F..

Later in Example
4.9
, we shall derive a formula for truncated correlation functions for general Gaussian analytic functions.

4.

Random measures with fast decay of covariance.
For any
p\geq 1
, we construct a random measure
{\cal X}
that is absolutely continuous w.r.t. to Lebesgue measure as in Section
1.2
such that
I(\alpha)=0
for all
0\leq|\alpha|<2p
i.e.,
L^{-d+2k}{\rm Var}\!\left({\cal X}_{L}(f)\right)\to 0
as
L\to\infty
for
f
smooth and
k<p
. In other words, there exist random measures with arbitrary fast decay of covariance.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
\int\varphi=0
,
\int|\varphi|=1
and
|\hat{\varphi}(x)|=o(\|x\|^{p})
as
x\to 0
for some
p\geq 1
. For example, we can take
\varphi
to be the
p
-fold convolution of
x\mapsto\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1_{[0,\frac{1}{2}]}(x_{i})-1_{[\frac{1}{2},1]}(x_{i})\right)
. Let
Y(x),x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a symmetric stationary stochastic process with a finite range of dependence and
|Y(x)|\leq 1
. Set
\sigma_{Y}(x)=\mathrm{Cov}(Y(x),Y(0))=\mathbb{E}[Y(0)Y(x)],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be the
covariance kernel
of
Y
. Define the random measure

{\cal X}(\cdot)=\int_{\cdot}X(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\,\text{where}\,\,X(x)=1+(Y*\varphi)(x)=1+\int Y(z)\,\varphi(x-z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

Setting
Z:=Y*\varphi
, observing that
X=1+Z
,
\sigma_{X}(z)=\sigma_{Z}(z):=\mathrm{Cov}(Z(0),Z(z))=\mathbb{E}[Z(0)Z(z)]
as it can be verified that
\mathbb{E}[Z(\cdot)]\equiv 0
. Since
|Z|\leq 1
,
X\geq 0

Clearly
{\cal X}
is a stationary random measure as
X
is a non-negative stationary random field. Thus
\rho_{1}\equiv\mathbb{E}[X(0)]=1
,
\rho_{2}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}[X(x)X(y)]=:\sigma_{X}(x-y)+1
and the truncated correlation density is
\kappa=\sigma_{X}
. We can now compute that
\hat{\sigma}_{Z}(z)=\hat{\sigma}_{Y}(z)|\hat{\varphi}(z)|^{2}
. From the above observations, we have that
{\cal X}
is a stationary random measure such that
\hat{\kappa}(z)=\hat{\sigma}_{Y}(z)|\hat{\varphi}(z)|^{2}
. Our assumption on decay of
\hat{\varphi}
yields that
|\hat{\kappa}(z)|=o(\|z\|^{2p})
as
x\to 0
and so from Lemma
2.2
, we have that
I(\alpha)=0
for all
0\leq|\alpha|<2p
as required.

5.

Perturbed lattices.
Though this example does not satisfy the common assumption on integrable truncated two-point correlation measure, still many results can be proven; see
[
27
,
24
,
51
,
64
,
44
]
for example. Let
\xi_{z},z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
U
be a uniform random vector in
[0,1]^{d}
. Then we define
{\mathcal{X}}=\{z+\xi_{z}:z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{s}=\{z+\xi_{z}+U:z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
as the perturbed lattice and stationarized perturbed lattice respectively. Though
{\mathcal{X}}_{s}
is stationary (but
{\mathcal{X}}
is not), it can be shown that it does not have an integrable truncated two-point correlation measure. Given the structure of the point process, explicit formulas are known for
\widehat{{\bf K}}
; see
[
24
]
and from which, one can conclude the variance of smooth statistics of
{\mathcal{X}}
(or
{\mathcal{X}}_{s}
) are at least of order
L^{d-2}
. In the case, when
\xi_{z}
’s are Gaussian this has been proven for
{\mathcal{X}}
but it fails for
{\mathcal{X}}_{s}
; see
[
64
, Theorem 4]
.

6.

Point process with fast decay of variance - Multiple perturbations of a lattice.
As mentioned above perturbed lattices cannot achieve variance growth of order less than
L^{d-2}
. However,
[
26
]
and
[
38
]
provided examples achieving smaller variance growth rates by using multiple perturbations of each lattice point that preserve the center of mass and higher moments. Specific constructions achieving variance growth of order
L^{d-4}
can be found in
[
26
]
and
[
54
, Pg 5]
(the example of third toy model) with the former also indicating examples achieving variance growth of orders
L^{d-6},L^{d-8}
and so on.

7.

Signed random measures.
It is also of interest to consider signed random measures and it arises naturally in many examples; for example, see
[
7
]
. We remark that it is possible to extend some of our results to many natural examples of signed random measures provided they can be written as difference of two (non-negative) random measures and applying our results individually to the two random measures. One needs to be careful as
\int f(x)\,{\mathcal{X}}(\mathrm{d}x)
is undefined in general for a signed random measure
{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
when both the positive and negative components have infinite total measure.

3
Covariance of indicator statistics

In the previous section, we derived exact formulas as well as asymptotics for
\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}(f),{\mathcal{X}}(g))
when
f,g
satisfy certain smoothness or analyticity conditions. The question remains as to what is the behaviour when
f,g
are less regular. In Section
3.1
, we first consider covariance asymptotics for the case when
f,g
are indicator functions of bounded open sets with
C^{1}
-smooth boundaries. Then in Section
3.2
, we show surface-order variance lower bound for
{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(W)\right)
for sets
W
with positive Lebesgue measure and when
{\mathcal{X}}
has a non-trivial atomic part i.e.,
\lambda_{D}>0
.

3.1
Covariance asymptotics

We begin by stating the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a stationary random measure whose truncated correlation measure
{\bf K}
has finite total variation. Assume that
A
and
B
are bounded open sets with
C^{1}
-smooth boundaries. Let
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}
be the scaled random measure defined by
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(A):={\mathcal{X}}(LA)
,
L\geq 1
. Then

1.

L^{-d}\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(A),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(B))\to Q_{0}^{{\bf K}}(f,g)=(\lambda_{D}+\int{\bf K})\,|A\cap B|,
where
|\cdot|
is the Lebesgue measure.

2.

If
\lambda_{D}+\int{\bf K}=0
and further
\int|z||{\bf K}|(\mathrm{d}z)<\infty
, then it holds that

L^{-d+1}\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(A),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(B))\to-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial A\cap\partial B}\varepsilon(x)\left[\int|z\cdot N_{A}(x)|\ {\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z)\,\right]\,\sigma_{A}(\mathrm{d}x),

(3.1)

where
N_{A}(x)
is the exterior normal to
A
at
x
and
\sigma_{A}
is the surface area measure of
\partial A
. Further,
\varepsilon(x)\in\{+1,0,-1\}
is defined as follows:
\varepsilon(x)=+1
(resp.
-1
) if
N_{A}(x)
is the exterior (resp. interior) normal to
B
at
x
, and
0
otherwise.

As the proof will show,
\varepsilon=\pm 1
a.e. on
\partial A\cap\partial B
and hence the role of
A
and
B
can be interchanged in the RHS of (
3.1
). The first statement in the above theorem is same as that of Proposition
2.5
(i) for
f,g\in H^{0}
but without requiring any further differentiability. This can be derived easily from (
2.3
). But the second statement is non-trivial and cannot be deduced from the results of the previous section. Recall that precursor results of this nature for point processes due to Lebowitz, Wieand, Buckley and Sodin were discussed in the introduction. A special case of the second statement is when
A=B
i.e., variance asymptotics. Note that even in the case of variance, the positivity of the limit is not claimed in the statement. Our upcoming Proposition
3.3
will guarantee this to be the right order of asymptotics in the case of stationary random measures with a non-trivial atomic part. We refer the reader to discussion below Proposition
3.3
and in particular, (
3.9
). We expect that the above theorem should hold for more general domains, say Lipschitz domains
or even sets of finite perimeter
.
For example, covariance asymptotics for planar isotropic hyperuniform point proceses have been shown for more general domains in
[
56
, Theorem 1.3]
. Precursors of such results for specific point processes or under restrictions on the domains can be found in
[
41
, Section III]
,
[
62
,
16
]
and
[
9
]
. Variance asymptotics for hyperuniform point processes for sets of finite perimeter or under weaker integrability conditions has been shown recently; see
[
42
]
and
[
3
, Theorem 1.5]
. The latter pre-print appeared during the revision of this paper.

Again as in (
2.8
), the limiting covariance formula has a neat product form when
{\bf K}
is isotropic. Assuming that
{\bf K}
is isotropic and abusing notation, we write
{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z)=r^{d-1}{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}r)\sigma(\mathrm{d}w)
with
z=rw
for
r>0
and
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Then, the conclusion of Theorem
3.1
(2) can be written as

\displaystyle L^{-d+1}\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(A),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(B))

\displaystyle\to-\frac{1}{2}\left[\int_{\partial A\cap\partial B}\varepsilon(x)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|w\cdot N_{A}(x)|\,\sigma(\mathrm{d}w)\,\sigma_{A}(\mathrm{d}x)\right]\times\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d}\,{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}r)

\displaystyle=c_{d}\,\int_{\partial A\cap\partial B}\varepsilon(x)\sigma_{A}(\mathrm{d}x)\times\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d}\,{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}r),

where

c_{d}:=\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|w_{1}|\,\sigma(\mathrm{d}w)=\frac{\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{2\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}.

Observe that the limiting covariance is zero if
A
and
B
do not share a boundary, even if one of them is contained in the other. On the other hand, if they share a part of the boundary and their interiors are disjoint, then the covariance is negative and proportional to the length of the shared boundary. But if one is contained in the other and they share a part of the boundary, then the covariance is positive.

Figure 1:
A
and
B
are the regions bounded by the solid and dashed contours respectively. For a small
s
, the integrand of
h_{s}(z)
with
z=(1,0)
takes value
+1
on the vertically (blue) shaded regions and
-1
on the horizontally (red) shaded regions.

The proof relies on the geometric lemma below. To state it, for nice enough sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the functions
h_{s}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
for
s\geq 0
by

\displaystyle h_{s}(z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1_{(A-sz)\setminus A}-1_{A\setminus(A-sz)})(1_{(B-sz)\setminus B}-1_{B\setminus(B-sz)})\mbox{ if }s>0,

(3.2)

\displaystyle h_{0}(z)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\partial A\cap\partial B}\varepsilon(x)|z\cdot N_{A}(x)|\ \sigma_{A}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\partial A\cap\partial B}\varepsilon(x)|z\cdot N_{B}(x)|\ \sigma_{B}(\mathrm{d}x).

(3.3)

The function
h_{0}
is almost in the form that occurs in Theorem
3.1
. It measures the signed common boundary in the direction
z
, as it vanishes unless
\partial A
and
\partial B
intersect and have a common normal on part of the intersection. To get
h_{s}(z)
, displace
A
and
B
in the direction
z
by a distance
s
(to be thought of as small), and measure the signed area common to the regions swept out (see Figure
1
for illustration). This is easiest to see for rectangles
A=[a,b]\times[c,d]
,
B=[u,v]\times[w,x]
and
z=(1,0)
. Then if
u=a
but
v\not=b
, we see that
h_{s}(z)
is equal to the length of
[c,d]\cap[w,x]
for small enough
s
. On the other hand, if
u=b<v
, then we get the same, but with a negative sign. In all cases where
A
and
B
do not share a part of their vertical edges, we have that
h_{s}(z)=0
for small enough
s
. Thus,
h_{s}=h_{0}
for small enough
s
. However, for general
z
(and general ‘smooth’
A,B
),
h_{s}
is an approximation of
h_{0}
, which is what the geometric lemma below says. If one proves it for more general sets, the covariance result Theorem
3.1
can be correspondingly generalized.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A
and
B
be bounded open sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
C^{1}
boundary. Define
h_{s}
and
h_{0}
by (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) respectively. Then
h_{s}(z)\to h_{0}(z)
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as
s\downarrow 0
.

We first prove the main theorem, assuming this lemma.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

As explained above the first statement is trivial and hence we prove the second alone. Let
f=1_{A}
and
g=1_{B}
where
A,B
are bounded open sets. Using (
2.3
) and that
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)={\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})
with
f_{L}(\cdot)=f(L^{-1}\cdot)
, we have the formula

\displaystyle L^{-d+1}\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f),{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(g))

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}L\int\!\!\int(f(x+\frac{z}{L})-f(x))(g(x+\frac{z}{L})-g(x))\,{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z)\,\mathrm{d}x\

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int h_{\frac{1}{L}}(z){\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z)

where
h_{s}
is as defined in (
3.2
).
By Lemma
3.2
, as
s\downarrow 0
, we have
h_{s}(z)\to h_{0}(z)
for each
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Further, since
|(A-sz)\setminus A|\leq Cs|z|
for small enough

and similarly for
B
, we have that
|h_{s}(z)|\leq C|z|
for
s
small. From the assumption that
\int|z||{\bf K}|(\mathrm{d}z)<\infty
, DCT applies to give

L^{-d+1}\mathrm{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}(f_{L}),{\mathcal{X}}(g_{L}))\to-\frac{1}{2}\int h_{0}(z)\ {\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z),

which completes the proof.

\Box

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

We shall break the proof into four steps for convenience.
Fix
C^{1}
domains
A,B
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
N_{A}(\cdot)
and
N_{B}(\cdot)
denote the respective outward unit normals. Let
E=\partial A\cap\partial B
.

STEP 1.

(
Common chart for
\partial A
and
\partial B
) Given
p\in E
, we can find a common chart for both
\partial A
and
\partial B
in a neighbourhood of
p
. By this we mean that there exist
r_{p}>0
, an orthonormal matrix
M_{p}
, an open set
U_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
and
C^{1}
functions
\phi_{p},\psi_{p}:U_{p}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
\varepsilon_{p}\in\{+1,-1\}
, such that

\displaystyle A\cap B(p,r_{p})

\displaystyle=\{x\in B(p,r_{p})\;:\;t>\phi_{p}(y)\}\qquad B\cap B(p,r_{p})=\{x\in B(p,r_{p})\;:\;\varepsilon_{p}t>\varepsilon_{p}\psi_{p}(y)\},

where
M_{p}(x-p)=(y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
. For

alone, the existence of a chart is the definition of

-smoothness. But we have the freedom to choose the “vertical”

-axis to be any direction that makes an angle of less than

with

. Choose the sign
\varepsilon_{p}
so that
\varepsilon_{p}N_{B}(p)
makes an angle of less than
\pi

N_{A}(p)
. Then we can choose a

with
N_{A}(p)
and with
\varepsilon_{p}N_{B}(p)

STEP 2.

(
Partition of unity to decompose

) By compactness of

, we can pick a finite set

such that
E^{\prime}:=\cup_{p\in F}B(p,\frac{1}{2}r_{p})
covers

. Choose a partition of unity
\{v_{p}\ :\ p\in F\}
subordinate to
\{B(p,r_{p})\ :\ p\in F\}
. Thus

is a smooth function with compact support inside

and such that

on

. Using this, we can write for any

and small enough

\displaystyle h_{s}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in F}\frac{1}{s}\int v_{p}(x)(1_{(A-sz)\setminus A}(x)-1_{A\setminus(A-sz)}(x))(1_{(B-sz)\setminus B}(x)-1_{B\setminus(B-sz)}(x))\;\mathrm{d}x.

(3.5)

STEP 3.

(
Local asymptotics of
h_{s}
) In this step we fix

and drop it in the subscripts for notational simplicity. We work in the rotated co-ordinates

so that
A\cap B(p,r)=\{(y,t):y\in U,\ \phi(y)<t\}
and
B\cap B(p,r)=\{(y,t):y\in U,\ \varepsilon\psi(y)<\varepsilon t\}
. Partition

into

\displaystyle G_{0}

\displaystyle=\{y\in U\ :\ \phi(y)=\psi(y)\mbox{ and }\nabla\phi(y)=\nabla\psi(y)\},

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=\{y\in U:\ \phi(y)\not=\psi(y)\},

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=\{y\in U:\ \phi(y)=\psi(y),\ \nabla\phi(y)\not=\nabla\psi(y)\}.

Applying the inverse function theorem to
\phi-\psi

is a manifold of dimension

, and hence has zero measure (under the

Now consider the summand for

). In
(y,t)
co-ordinates, the integral is over

and the integral is

\displaystyle h_{s}^{p}(z)

\displaystyle:=\int_{U}H_{s}^{p}(y)\ \mathrm{d}y\;\mbox{ where},

\displaystyle H_{s}^{p}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{p}(y,t)(1_{(A-sz)\setminus A}(y,t)-1_{A\setminus(A-sz)}(y,t))(1_{(B-sz)\setminus B}(y,t)-1_{B\setminus(B-sz)}(y,t))\;\mathrm{d}t.

Writing

in the
(y,t)

(a)

(y,t)\in(A-sz)\setminus A

(b)

(y,t)\in A\setminus(A-sz)

(c)

(y,t)\in(B-sz)\setminus B

for

s(r-\nabla\psi(y).w)_{-}
for
\varepsilon=-1
.

(d)

(y,t)\in B\setminus(B-sz)

\varepsilon(\psi(y+sw)-sr)\geq\varepsilon t>\varepsilon\psi(y)

. Hence,
H_{s}^{p}(y)\to 0
for

.

On the other hand, if

terms) or the second and fourth are the same. When
\varepsilon=-1

H_{s}^{p}(y)\to\varepsilon|r-\nabla\phi(y).w|
for

For all small

H_{s}(y)\leq C|z|
(as

) for all

\displaystyle h_{s}^{p}(z)\to\varepsilon\int_{G_{0}}v_{p}(y,\phi(y))|r-\nabla\phi(y).w|\ \mathrm{d}y.

(3.6)

r-\nabla\phi_{p}(y).w=(r,w).(1,-\nabla\phi_{p}(y))=\sqrt{1+|\nabla\phi_{p}(y)|^{2}}(z.N_{A}(x)),

where

\displaystyle h_{s}^{p}(z)\to\int_{U}v_{p}(y,\phi_{p}(y))1_{\phi_{p}(y)=\psi_{p}(y)}1_{\nabla\phi_{p}(y)=\nabla\psi_{p}(y)}\varepsilon_{p}|z.N_{A}(y,\phi_{p}(y))|\ \sqrt{1+|\nabla\phi_{p}(y)|^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}y.

Observe that for all

\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon_{p}
, where

\displaystyle h_{s}^{p}(z)

\displaystyle\to\int_{U_{p}}v_{p}(y,\phi_{p}(y))1_{\phi_{p}(y)=\psi_{p}(y)}\varepsilon(y,\phi(y))|z.N_{A}(y,\phi_{p}(y))|\ \sqrt{1+|\nabla\phi_{p}(y)|^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial A\cap B(p,r_{p})}v_{p}(x)\varepsilon(x)|z.N_{A}(x)|\ \sigma_{A}(\mathrm{d}x).

As already remarked, on

STEP 4.

(
Combining the local asymptotics
) Sum (

3.2
Variance lower bounds

In this section, we derive a lower bound for variance of stationary random measures with a non-trivial atomic part and truncated two-point correlation function in
d\geq 2
. By the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma, the existence of a truncated two-point correlation function implies that
\widehat{{\bf K}}
vanishes at
\infty
and we need this assumption.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a stationary random measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 2
whose truncated correlation measure
{\bf K}
has finite total variation and
\widehat{{\bf K}}
vanishes at
\infty
. Let
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}
be the scaled random measure defined by
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(A):={\mathcal{X}}(LA)
,
L\geq 1
. Then, there exists a constant
c>0
(depending only on
d
) such that for any bounded set
W
and for all
L\geq 1
,

{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(W)\right)\geq c\lambda_{D}\min\{|W|L^{d},|W|^{\frac{d-1}{d}}L^{d-1}\},

(3.8)

where
|\cdot|
denotes the Lebesgue measure.

This proposition reduces to that of Nazarov and Sodin
[
48
, Lemma 1.6]
if we assume that
{\mathcal{X}}
is a point process in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Similar bounds were derived by Beck
[
5
]
; see also
[
6
]
. However, essentially following the proof of
[
48
, Lemma 1.6]
, we obtain a non-trivial variance lower bound for stationary random measures with a non-trivial atomic part i.e.,
\lambda_{D}>0
. This is more widely applicable for various stochastic geometric models as explained below (
3.10
).
Although the lower bound on the right uses only the atomic part of the random measure, we are
not
claiming that if
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
is the atomic part of
{\mathcal{X}}
, then
{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(W)\right)\geq{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}_{L}(W)\right)
. In other words, the constant
c
could be smaller than
1
. As the proof shows, on the Fourier side, the atomic part dominates the diffusive part at large frequencies.

Combining the above proposition with Theorem
3.1
, we have that if
\lambda_{D}+\int{\bf K}=0
and
\int|z||{\bf K}|(\mathrm{d}z)<\infty
, then

L^{-d+1}{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(A)\right)\to-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial A}\left[\int|z\cdot N_{A}(x)|\ {\bf K}(\mathrm{d}z)\,\right]\sigma(\mathrm{d}x)\in(0,\infty),

(3.9)

where
A
is a bounded open set with
C^{1}
-smooth boundary. In case
{\mathcal{X}}
is a stationary point process, the above claim (
3.9
) follows from
[
43
, Proposition 2]
and
[
48
, Lemma 1.6]
. In special cases such as Ginibre point process or zeros of GAF, the limit (
3.9
) is known to be positive; see
[
23
, Section 3.4]
and
[
22
]
respectively. The above variance convergence also holds for perturbed lattices (see
[
64
, Theorem 5]
and
[
27
]
) even though our integrability assumption on the truncated two-point correlations does not hold for this case. Also note that without the integrability assumption, variance asymptotics of indicator statistics can be infinite for some sets
A
; see
[
35
]
and
[
64
, Section 3.1]
.

Consider purely atomic stationary random measures i.e., those for which
{\cal X}_{\text{diff}}=0
in (
1.6
). Their first two correlation functions are given in (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) respectively. If the random measure is non-degenerate i.e., the intensity
\lambda
and diagonal intensity
\lambda_{D}
are both non-zero and finite. In such cases under assumptions on
{\bf K}
, we have that
\liminf_{L\to\infty}L^{-d+1}{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(W)\right)>0
. Such a variance lower bound is often necessary for central limit theorems; for example, see
[
8
, Theorem 7.2]
.

We now give a simple illustrative example of a purely atomic stationary random measure with the above variance lower bound and thus ensuring the central limit theorem for the random measure. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the Ginibre process or zeros of G.E.F. as in Section
2.4
. Consider the random measures

{\mathcal{X}}^{ec}:=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{1}{[|X-Y|\leq 1]}\delta_{X}\,\,;\,\,{\mathcal{X}}^{el}:=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{P}}|X-Y|\mathbf{1}{[|X-Y|\leq 1]}\delta_{X},

(3.10)

representing the edge count and edge length processes respectively, associated to the random geometric graph with connection distance
1
on
\mathcal{P}
i.e.,
X,Y\in\mathcal{P}
have an edge if
|X-Y|\leq 1
. The ’locality’ of the statistic inducing the random measure can be exploited to show that the truncated correlation measure
{\bf K}
is integrable and hence
\hat{{\bf K}}
vanishes at
\infty
; see
[
7
, Theorem 1.11 and Section 2.2]
. Thus, we have that both
{\mathcal{X}}^{ec}
and
{\mathcal{X}}^{el}
satisfy surface-order variance asymptotics (
3.9
). Furthermore,
{\mathcal{X}}^{ec}_{L}(W)
and
{\mathcal{X}}^{el}_{L}(W)
satisfy central limit theorems via
[
7
, Theorem 1.14]
. Apart from the above variance lower bound,
[

also requires a moment condition. Verification of the same for

being Ginibre process or zeros of G.E.F. has been indicated in
[
7
, Section 2.1]
.

The above examples of random measures were chosen for their simplicity. More examples of such random measures and central limit theorems for the same, assuming suitable variance lower bounds can be found in
[
7

8

21

12

. The required variance lower bound in such central limit theorems follows from (

Turning to the proof, the plan is to first derive a variance lower bound for
{\mathcal{X}}(f)
when

‘high-frequencies’ of

\hat{1}_{LW}

Proof.

We shall assume
W
has positive Lebesgue measure as the claim is trivial otherwise. Let

. Recall that

Since the LHS exists and is non-negative for all

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{D}L^{2d}}{2(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{|y|\geq C}|\widehat{f}(Ly)|^{2}\mathrm{d}y

(3.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c\lambda_{D}L^{d}\int_{|y|\geq CL}|\widehat{f}(y)|^{2}\mathrm{d}y.

Assuming

without loss of generality, we will now proceed as in the proof of

to complete the proof.

Choose a non-negative multiplier
m\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
m\leq 1

in the unit ball. With
m_{L}(\xi)=m(L^{-1}\xi)

\varphi_{W,L}

Now setting

in (

) and

, we derive that

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(W)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{cL^{d}}{2}\int_{|y|\geq R}|\widehat{\mathbf{1}}_{W}(y)|^{2}\mathrm{d}y\,\geq\,\frac{cL^{d}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\mathbf{1}}_{W}(y)|^{2}(1-m_{R}(y))^{2}\mathrm{d}y

(3.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{cL^{d}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathbf{1}_{W}-\varphi_{W,R}(y))^{2}\mathrm{d}y.

Let

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathbf{1}_{W}-\varphi_{W,R}(y))^{2}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\int_{\varphi_{W,R}\leq\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{1}_{W}-\varphi_{W,R}(y))^{2}\mathrm{d}y\geq\frac{1}{4}\int_{\varphi_{W,R}\leq\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y\geq\frac{1}{4C^{\prime}R}\int_{\varphi_{W,R}\leq\frac{1}{2}}|\nabla\varphi_{W,R}|

4
Central limit theorems for statistics of point processes

In this section, we upgrade our variance asymptotics to central limit theorems for
linear
statistics of
hyperuniform
point processes under some stronger assumptions. In Section
4.1
, we introduce some necessary notation first and then state the two central limit theorems - one for Sobolev statistics and another for Hölder statistics. The central limit theorems are proved under certain assumptions on integrability of truncated correlation functions as well as an integral identity relating higher-order truncated correlation functions with lower order ones. In Section
4.2
, we discuss examples of point processes satisfying these conditions. In Section
4.3
, we prepare for our proofs by proving a key reduction formula (Proposition
4.12
) for multilinear cumulants that uses an idea due to Bálint Virág and the first author. The proofs of the two central limit theorems are provided in Section
4.4
. The proofs are by the method of moments, or more precisely by showing that if the statistics are scaled so that the variance converges, then higher cumulants vanish. By a
variant of Marcinkiewicz’s theorem
, this shows normal convergence of the scaled statistics.

4.1
The central limit theorems

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a simple point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for every
k\geq 1
, the
k
-point correlation function
\rho_{k}
exists (see (
1.3
)) and is locally-finite. The
truncated correlations

\rho_{k}^{T}
are defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}):=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(k)}(-1)^{\ell(\pi)-1}(\ell(\pi)-1)!\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\pi)}\rho_{|\pi_{j}|}(x_{\pi_{j}}),

(4.1)

where
\Pi(k)
is the collection of all set partitions of
[k]:=\{1,\ldots,k\}
and for
\pi\in\Pi(k)
having parts
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{\ell}
, we write
\ell(\pi)=\ell
(number of blocks). For
S\subseteq[k]
, we write
x_{S}
for the vector
(x_{i})_{i\in S}
(say in natural order, but it does not matter as we only apply symmetric functions to it). For instance,
\rho_{1}^{T}(x)=\rho_{1}(x)
and
\rho_{2}^{T}(x_{[2]})=\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})-\rho_{1}(x_{1})\rho_{1}(x_{2})
which is equal to
\kappa(x-y)
when
{\mathcal{X}}
is stationary. By the Möbius inversion formula on the lattice of set partitions, (
4.1
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\rho_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}):=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(k)}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\pi)}\rho_{|\pi_{j}|}^{T}(x_{\pi_{j}}).

(4.2)

Thus, truncated correlations carry the same information as correlations. From the form of (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), it is clear that truncated correlations and correlations are related like cumulants and moments. This will be seen in (
4.15
) where cumulants of linear statistics are expressed in terms of the truncated correlation functions. It is also easily seen in point processes on discrete spaces, see Appendix
B
.

Remark 4.1
.

We are sticking to a simple point processes on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with correlation functions being densities of correlation measures w.r.t. Lebesgue measure. Therefore, we have not had to clarify how correlation and truncated correlation functions are defined when
x_{i}
are not distinct. Apart from extending
\rho_{k}
by continuity on diagonals when possible, another convention for simple point processes is to set
\rho_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=0
if
x_{i}=x_{j}
for some
i\not=j
, and then the definition of
\rho_{k}^{T}
remains as stated in (
4.1
). In cases of point processes like Ginibre or zeros of G.E.F., the two conventions agree. The latter convention is the right one, for example, when considering a simple point process on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with correlation functions being densities w.r.t. counting measure on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. This definition ensures the validity of (
4.15
), which expresses cumulants of linear statistics of the point process in terms of the truncated correlations functions. To not interrupt the flow, we relegate a brief discussion to Appendix
B
.

Now we state our non-trivial assumption on truncated correlations,
which may be viewed as higher-order hyperuniformity conditions
.

Assumption 4.2
.

For all
k\geq 2

and for a.e.
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}
, we assume that
\rho_{k}^{T}
is absolutely integrable with respect to
x_{k}
and satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\mathrm{d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=-(k-1)\rho_{k-1}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}).

(4.3)

The assumption
4.2
is not satisfied by all point processes. For example,
\rho_{k}^{T}\equiv 0
for all
k\geq 2
for a Poisson process but
\rho_{1}^{T}\equiv\rho_{1}
and so (
4.3
) is violated for
k=2
. However, for stationary
{\cal X}
with intensity
\lambda
and
k=2
, (
4.3
) is the same as
\int\kappa(z)\mathrm{d}z=-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\lambda}
, which is equivalent to hyperuniformity
[
14
]
. For higher
k
, this assumption is naturally motivated by considering point processes in the discrete setting having a constant, finite number of points; see Appendix
B
. In the next subsection, we give examples of infinite point processes for which (
4.3
) does hold. The way we make use of Assumption
4.2
will be to get convenient form of cumulants of linear statistics of the point process. This will be done later in Proposition
4.12
, but for now let us illustrate the idea for the second cumulant, which is just the variance. If
f
is a nice enough test function, then by (
4.3
),

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int f^{2}(x)\rho_{2}^{T}(x,y)\ \mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\int f^{2}(y)\rho_{2}^{T}(x,y)\ \mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=-\int f^{2}(z)\rho_{1}(z)\ \mathrm{d}z,

from which and (
2.3
), we easily obtain that

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}({\mathcal{X}}(f))

\displaystyle=\int f^{2}(z)\rho_{1}(z)\mathrm{d}z+\int\!\!\!\int f(x)f(y)\rho_{2}^{T}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\!\!\!\int(f(x)-f(y))^{2}\ \rho_{2}^{T}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\ \mathrm{d}y.

(4.4)

To see the usefulness of this formula, apply it to
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}
and let
L\to\infty
. Typically
\rho_{2,L}^{T}(x,y)
concentrates in a shrinking neighbourhood around the diagonal line
\{x=y\}
(because
{\mathcal{X}}
restricted to far off regions are nearly independent). But
f(x)-f(y)
vanishes on the diagonal. Thus the above formula explains the significantly smaller variance (i.e., hyperuniformity) when (
4.3
) holds for
k=2
.

For later use, we note that by iterating (
4.3
), for all
k>r\geq 1
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k-r}}\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\mathrm{d}x_{r+1}\ldots\mathrm{d}x_{k}=(-1)^{k-r}\frac{(k-1)!}{(r-1)!}\ \rho_{r}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}).

(4.5)

If
{\cal X}
is stationary, we define
\kappa_{m-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1}):=\rho_{m}^{T}(x+z_{1},\ldots,x+z_{m-1},x)
for any
x
. For any
\tau:[m]\to[m-1]
and
\alpha^{(j)}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
,
j\leq m
, we define

I_{m}(\tau;\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(m)}):=\frac{1}{\alpha^{(1)}!\times\ldots\times\alpha^{(m)}!}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{m-1}}\kappa_{m-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}z_{\tau(i)}^{\alpha^{(i)}}\;\mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{m-1},

(4.6)

provided the integral exists.

We first give a criterion for asymptotic normality of Sobolev linear statistics of
{\mathcal{X}}
. Recall definition of
I(\alpha)
’s and
Q_{m}
’s from Section
2.1
.

Theorem 4.3
.

(CLT for Sobolev statistics)

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a simple stationary point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with truncated correlations satisfying Assumption
4.2
. Assume that for all
m\geq 2
, there exists
d_{m}\geq m
such that for all
l_{1},\ldots,l_{m-1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
with
\sum_{i}l_{i}\leq d_{m}
,

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{m-1}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m-1}|z_{i}|^{l_{i}}\;|\kappa_{m-1}|(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{m-1}<\infty,

(4.7)

and further that for all non-zero
\alpha^{(j)}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
,
1\leq j\leq m
, satisfying
\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\alpha^{(j)}|<d_{m}
, it holds that

\displaystyle I_{m}(\tau;\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(m)})=0,\,\,\forall\tau:[m]\to[m-1].

(4.8)

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{k}
for some
k\geq 1
(with the convention that
H^{0}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
). Let
\lambda_{D}+I(0)=0
and
I(\alpha)=0
for
1\leq|\alpha|<2k
. If
2(d-d_{m})<m(d-2k)
for
m
large, then

\frac{{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)]}{L^{(d-2k)/2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,Q_{2k}^{\kappa}(f,f))

Remark 4.4
.

1.

When
d_{m}=m
, the condition (
4.8
) is trivially satisfied as there are no non-zero
\alpha^{(j)}
satisfying
\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\alpha^{(j)}|<m
. This happens when
2k<d+2
, because then
2(d-m)<m(d-2k)
for all large
m
. In such cases, we only need to check the integrability condition (
4.7
). In particular, this applies to the Ginibre process with
d=2
,
k=1
,
Q_{2}^{\kappa}(f,f)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{2}
(this was shown in Section
2.4
). See Example
4.7
below for full details and generalization to stationary determinantal processes.

2.

By polarization, one can rephrase the result as saying that the random field
f\mapsto\frac{{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)]}{L^{(d-2k)/2}}
indexed by
L^{1}\cap H^{k}
functions converges, in the sense of finite dimensional distributions, to the centered Gaussian field with covariance
Q_{2k}^{\kappa}(f,g)
.
Observe that if we do not take the maximum possible value of
k
, the result holds but the limiting variance is zero.

3.

If the interest is in a particular function
f
, one may want to say that
\frac{{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}[{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)]}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,1)
. The theorem does imply this, provided
f
satisfies the assumptions of the Theorem and in addition we have
Q^{\kappa}_{2k}(f,f)\not=0
. This is because Proposition
2.5
implies that
{\rm Var}[{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})]\sim Q_{2k}^{{\bf K}}(f,f)L^{d-2k}
.

4.

When
{\mathcal{X}}
is the zero set of the G.E.F., we expect the above CLT to hold with
k=2
and
Q_{4}^{\kappa}(f,f)=\frac{\zeta(3)}{16\pi}\|\Delta f\|_{2}^{2}
. However, we are unable to verify (
4.3
) but we do show that (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) hold for some
d_{m}\geq 2m-2
. See Section
4.2
for details.

5.

As will be seen from the proof, the condition
2(d-d_{m})<m(d-2k)
is needed to prove the CLT for compactly supported smooth functions and then we extend it to functions in
L^{1}\cap H^{k}
. If one were to find an alternate condition that guarantees the CLT for compactly supported smooth functions, then our proof would readily extend it to functions in
L^{1}\cap H^{k}
by arguments as in Proposition
2.5
.

Next we state a CLT for Hölder continuous statistics of stationary point processes satisfying the integrability condition for truncated correlations and an appropriate variance lower bound. For
\alpha\in(0,1]
, let
C_{0}^{\alpha}
be the space of compactly supported
\alpha
-Hölder continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We also write
C_{0}^{0}
for the space of bounded functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

(CLT for Hölder continuous statistics)

Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a simple stationary point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying Assumption
4.2
. Assume that for all
m\geq 2
and for all
l_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}l_{i}=m
,

\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{m-1}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m-1}|z_{i}|^{\alpha l_{i}}\;|\kappa_{m-1}|(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{m-1}<\infty.

(4.9)

Suppose that
f\in C_{0}^{\alpha}
and
{\rm Var}[{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)]\geq CL^{-2\alpha+\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon,C>0
. Then CLT holds for
{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)
, i.e.,

\frac{{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}[{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)]}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,1).

This is analogous to the main asymptotic normality result of
[
47
, Theorem 1.2]
which was stated for zeros of the Gaussian entire function, and proved by a method specific to that point process. We use entirely different and general methods, but we cannot yet claim that Theorem
4.5
is a generalization of
[
47
, Theorem 1.2]
, as it remains to verify condition (
4.3
) for zeros of the Gaussian entire function, see Example
4.9
below. The variance lower bound assumption need not hold always; see the remark after Corollary 1.3 in
[
47
]
.

The proofs of both the central limit theorems are via cumulant method wherein it suffices to show vanishing of higher cumulants. The new input here is the cumulant reduction formula in Proposition
4.12
, which yields improved estimates on cumulants as suggested below Assumption
4.2
.

4.2
Examples of point processes.

We now provide examples to which the central limit theorems
4.3
and
4.5
apply. Mainly, we shall verify the non-trivial integral identity (
4.3
) and the integrability conditions (
4.7
), (
4.9
) for certain Determinantal point processes with a projection kernel and partially for zeros of G.E.F.. Although all point processes in this paper are infinite and even stationary, the cleanest way to understand the integral identity (
4.3
) is in the context of finite point processes and so we begin with this case.

Example 4.6
.

(Finite processes) Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a point process with a total of
n
points (here
n
is fixed, not random). Adopt the convention in (
4.1
) that the first part
\pi_{1}
contains
x_{k}
.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\mathrm{d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(k)}(-1)^{\ell(\pi)-1}(\ell(\pi)-1)!(n-|\pi_{1}|+1)\rho_{|\pi_{1}|-1}(x_{\pi_{1}\setminus\{k\}})\prod_{j=2}^{\ell(\pi)}\rho_{|\pi_{j}|}(x_{\pi_{j}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\Pi(k-1)}(-1)^{\ell(\sigma)-1}(\ell(\sigma)-1)!\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\sigma)}\rho_{|\sigma_{j}|}(x_{\sigma_{j}})\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\ell(\sigma)}(n-|\sigma_{j}|)\ -\ n\ell(\sigma)\right\}

\displaystyle=-(k-1)\rho_{k-1}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}).

In the second equality, we used the fact that
\sigma\in\Pi(k-1)
can arise from many different
\pi\in\Pi(k)
. One of them is the one having all the blocks of
\sigma
but in addition a singleton block
\{k\}
; in this case
\ell(\pi)=\ell(\sigma)+1
. Another
\ell(\sigma)
many set-partitions
\pi
arise by including
k
in one of the blocks of
\sigma
; in these cases
\ell(\pi)=\ell(\sigma)
.

As we think that this example conveys an important point, a different and arguably more transparent proof is given in Appendix
B
for the further special case of point processes with a fixed number of points on a countable space.

Suppose a simple point process
{\mathcal{X}}
can be “well-approximated” by finite simple point processes
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
having
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}(R^{d})=n
. By well-approximated, we mean that for any
k\geq 1
and any
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the truncated correlation function
\rho_{n,k}^{T}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})
of
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
converges in
L^{1}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k-1})
to the truncated correlation function
\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})
of
{\mathcal{X}}
. Then
{\mathcal{X}}
also satisfies Assumption
4.2
.

This example suggests that the Assumption
4.2

holds for those point processes that have a “fixed total number” of points. For infinite processes, number rigidity in the sense of Ghosh and Peres
[
30
]
is a way of making precise such a conservation of numbers. Here we just give two such classes of examples.

Example 4.7
.

(Determinantal process with a projection kernel) Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a determinantal process with kernel
K
. Then, it is known (see (4.9) in
[
57
]
) that

\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}^{\tiny cyc}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}K(x_{i},x_{P(i)})

where
\mathcal{S}_{k}^{\tiny cyc}
denotes the set of cyclic permutations of
[k]
. It is not hard to see this by checking that (
4.1
) or (
4.2
) holds
and that correlation functions are given by determinants i.e.,
\rho_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\textrm{det}\big(K(x_{i},x_{j})\big)_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
.

To verify Assumption
4.2
, we must and do assume that
K
is a projection kernel, i.e., it is Hermitian (
K(x,y)=\overline{K(y,x)}
) and satisfies the reproducing property
\int K(x,y)K(y,z)\mathrm{d}y=K(x,z)
. So, integrating the truncated correlation functions for such processes, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\mathrm{d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}^{\tiny cyc}}\int\prod_{i=1}^{k}K(x_{i},x_{P(i)})\mathrm{d}x_{k}.

For a cyclic
P
, there is some
i,j
such that
P(i)=k
and
P(k)=j
. Let
Q\in\mathcal{S}_{k-1}^{\tiny cyc}
be defined by
Q(i)=j
and
Q(i^{\prime})=P(i^{\prime})
for all
i^{\prime}\in[k-1]\setminus\{i\}
. By the reproducing kernel property

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i=1}^{k}K(x_{i},x_{P(i)})\mathrm{d}x_{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}K(x_{i},x_{Q(i)}).

Further, each
Q\in\mathcal{S}_{k-1}^{\tiny cyc}
arises from
k-1
different
P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}^{\tiny cyc}
(for a given
Q
, we can insert
k
between
i
and
Q(i)
for any
i\in[k-1]
). Therefore, we see that

\displaystyle\int\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\mathrm{d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)\sum_{Q\mbox{ \scriptsize{cyclic}}}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}K(x_{i},x_{Q(i)})

\displaystyle=-(k-1)\rho_{k-1}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})

Thus Assumption
4.2
is satisfied. Further, if
|K(z,w)|
decays faster than any polynomial in
|z-w|
as
|z-w|\to\infty
, it follows that the condition (
4.7
) and (
4.9
) are satisfied.
As explained in Remark
4.4
, if
I(\alpha)=0
for
|\alpha|<2k
where
2k<d+2
, then the condition (
4.8
) is trivially satisfied with
d_{m}=m
. For stationary determinantal processes,
k=1
works i.e.,
I(\alpha)=0
when
|\alpha|=1
. This is because
\kappa_{2}(x)
is an even function while
x^{\alpha}
is an odd function when
|\alpha|=1
.

Thus the CLT for Sobolev statistics (i.e., Theorem
4.3
) holds for stationary determinantal processes with a projection kernel that decays fast off the diagonal. If
2k<d
then the variance grows faster than a power of the mean, and the CLT result is then known even without explicit decay condition on the kernel, see
[
58
]
.

To deduce asymptotic normality for Hölder statistics as in Theorem
4.5
, the variance lower bound condition alone needs to be verified.

Example 4.8
.

(Ginibre Ensemble.) For the infinite Ginibre ensemble, we have
d=2
and from the derivation in Section
2.4
, we also have that
d_{2}=2
with
Q_{2}^{\kappa}(f,f)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{2}\neq 0
. Thus from Remark
4.4
(1),
2(d-d_{m})<m(d-2k)
holds for

. From the determinantal structure and from the exponential decay of the kernel

and the conditions (

) and (

with

Example 4.9
.

(Zeros of random analytic function) Refer to
[
31
]
for basic definitions and facts about random analytic functions, in particular Gaussian analytic functions (GAFs). Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be the zero set of a random analytic function
F
on a region
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. Then
{\mathcal{X}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Delta_{z}\log|F|
and hence, for distinct
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
,

\rho_{k}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{k}}\Delta_{z_{1}}\ldots\Delta_{z_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\log|F(z_{1})|\ldots\log|F(z_{k})|].

Therefore,

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{T}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{k}}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(m)}(-1)^{\ell(\pi)-1}(\ell(\pi)-1)!\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\pi)}\left(\prod_{i\in\pi(j)}\Delta_{z_{i}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in\pi(j)}\log|F(z_{i})|\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{k}}\Delta_{z_{1}}\ldots\Delta_{z_{k}}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(m)}(-1)^{\ell(\pi)-1}(\ell(\pi)-1)!\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\pi)}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in\pi(j)}\log|F(z_{i})|\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{k}}\Delta_{z_{1}}\ldots\Delta_{z_{k}}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}[\log|F(z_{1})|,\ldots,\log|F(z_{k})|]

(4.10)

where
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}
is the multi-linear cumulant function of real-valued random variables (recalled in the subsection
4.3
). Again, this formula is valid for distinct
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
. Singularities appear when
z_{i}
and
z_{j}
get close for some
i\not=j
. As the cumulant vanishes when one of the variables is a constant (and for
k=1
there is the Laplacian which kills constants), we may also rewrite (
4.10
) as

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{T}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{k}}\Delta_{z_{1}}\ldots\Delta_{z_{k}}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}[\log|\widetilde{F}(z_{1})|,\ldots,\log|\widetilde{F}(z_{k})|]

(4.11)

where
\widetilde{F}(z)=F(z)/\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[|F(z)|^{2}]}
is not holomorphic, but normalized to have
\mathbb{E}[|\widetilde{F}(z)|^{2}]=1
.

Is Assumption
4.2
valid? The integrability of
\rho_{k}^{T}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{k})
on
{\cal C}^{k-1}
for fixed

was proved by Nazarov and Sodin
[

]
(Claims 4.1, 4.2 together with Theorem 1.4 in that paper) for very general Gaussian analytic functions, including the G.E.F. Thus, the integral on the left side of (

) exists. Is the integral equal to the right side? For zeros of G.E.F. and

, (

) gives

\displaystyle\rho_{2}^{T}(z_{1},z_{2})=-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\;\sum_{m\geq 1}e^{-m|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}}(2-4m|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}+m^{2}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{4}).

Fixing
z_{1}
and integrating over
z_{2}
gives
\frac{-1}{\pi}
which is
-\rho_{1}^{T}(z_{1})
as required. It is worth noting that interchange of integral and sum is not valid, in fact the sum of integrals is zero!

For general

, we have not been able to carry out the computations to show that (

) holds.

C
, in particular a reduced identity (

) which would imply (

Example 4.10
.

(Zeros of the G.E.F.) In this case,

,

with
Q_{4}^{\kappa}(f,f)=C_{2}\|\Delta f\|_{2}^{2}
. Hence Remark

(5) cannot be used. We now claim that

(proven in the next paragraph), which then shows that

for all

. The integrability conditions (

) and (

) follow from

with

. Further, we also have asymptotic normality of Hölder linear statistics as in Theorem

, since the necessary variance lower bound has been proven in
[

]
. We recall that these two CLTs have been proven via different methods in
[

, Theorem 1.2 and Corollary 1.3]
.

To show that
d_{m}\geq 2m-2
, write

\displaystyle I_{m}(\tau;\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(m)})

\displaystyle=C\int_{\mathbb{C}^{m-1}}\kappa_{m-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}z_{\tau(i)}^{\alpha^{(i)}}\;\mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{m-1}.

Here
\alpha^{(i)}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{2}
for each
i
, and the natural identification of
\mathbb{C}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is used in writing

. The formula (
4.11
) gives

\kappa_{m-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})=(2\pi)^{-m}\Delta_{z_{1}}\ldots\Delta_{z_{m-1}}\Delta_{x}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[\log|\widetilde{F}(x+z_{1})|,\ldots,\log|\widetilde{F}(x+z_{m-1})|,\log|\widetilde{F}(x)|]

Remark 4.11
.

The idea behind (
4.10
) may be useful in other situations. If we can represent our point process as
{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{L}F
for a real-valued random field
F
and a linear operator (e.g., a linear differential operator)
\mathcal{L}
, then under mild conditions allowing interchange of
\mathcal{L}
and integrals,

\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\mathcal{L}_{x_{1}}\ldots\mathcal{L}_{x_{k}}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}[F(x_{1}),\ldots,F(x_{k})].

Here
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}[F(x_{1}),\ldots,F(x_{k})]
is the multi-linear cumulant of the random variables
F(x_{i})
,
i\leq k
.

When can we write a given point process
{\mathcal{X}}
as
\mathcal{L}F(x)
? A natural choice for processes on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is to define
F(x)={\mathcal{X}}(G_{d}(\cdot,x))=\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{X}}}G_{d}(y,x)
where
G_{d}(y,x)
is the Green’s function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{L}=\Delta
, the Laplacian. The catch is that there can be convergence issues for infinite point processes and additional challenges if we want
F
to be stationary, a natural requirement if
{\mathcal{X}}
is stationary. Such a construction for Poisson processes in
d\geq 5
was carried out in
[
11
]

and for fairly general hyperuniform point processes (under assumptions weaker than those of Theorem
3.1
(2)) in
[
55
, Theorem 5.1]
. In particular, this includes the Ginibre process and zeros of G.E.F.

4.3
Multilinear cumulants of linear statistics of a point process

As explained in the introduction of this section, we prove the central limit theorems by deriving bounds for the cumulants of linear statistics of the point process. These cumulants can be expressed as integrals against the truncated correlation functions. In getting upper bounds on cumulants, it becomes important to take advantage of the cancellation implicit in the signed sum in the definition (
4.1
). One key ingredient of our proof is Proposition
4.12
that extracts this cancellation efficiently, when the integration condition (
4.3
) is satisfied. This form of writing the cumulants of linear statistics was found by Bálint Virág and the first author in unpublished discussions. Some of the other combinatorial identities for cumulants that we use are more well-known, but we give a quick derivation of many of them for self-containment.

First we recall the notion of cumulants of real-valued random variables (for more details on cumulants and in particular their use in normal approximation, see
[
18
]
). The multilinear moments of a random vector
Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
are defined by

\displaystyle m_{n}[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}\ldots Y_{n}],\qquad m_{\pi}[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]=\prod_{r=1}^{\ell(\pi)}m_{|\pi(r)|}[Y_{\pi(r)}]\mbox{ for }\pi\in\Pi(m).

The (multilinear) cumulants are defined by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{n}[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(m)}(-1)^{\ell(\pi)-1}(\ell(\pi)-1)!m_{\pi}[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}],\qquad{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{\pi}[Y]=\prod_{r=1}^{\ell(\pi)}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{|\pi(r)|}[Y_{\pi(r)}].

(4.13)

We also write
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{n}(Y_{1})={\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{n}[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{1}]
for the higher cumulants of a single random variable.
Further, we use the “inner-product” notation
\langle f,g\rangle
even when
f\in L^{\infty}
and
g\in L^{1}
.

Next we state the cumulant reduction result. The motivation for expecting such a result comes from direct computations for the second and third cumulants of the linear statistic
{\mathcal{X}}(f)
. The second cumulant was derived in (
4.4
), and by a similar but more tedious computation one can show that the third cumulant is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\iiint(h(x)+h(y)-2h(z))(h(y)+h(z)-2h(x))(h(z)+h(x)-2h(y))\rho_{3}^{T}(x,y,z)\ \mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z

The point here is that typically the truncated correlation funtion decays off the diagonal (“asymptotic independence”) while the part of the integrand depending on the test function vanishes on the diagonal. Overall this leads to efficient cancellation.

Proposition 4.12
.

(Cumulant reduction,
[
37
]
)

Assume that
{\mathcal{X}}
is a simple point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying Assumption
4.2
. Let
Y_{i}={\mathcal{X}}(h_{i}),i=1,\ldots,m
for some compactly supported
continuous
functions
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},
and
Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m})
be the corresponding random vector. Then we have that,

{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[Y]={\langle}Q_{m}[(h_{i}(x_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq m}],\rho_{m}^{T}{\rangle},

(4.14)

where

Q_{m}[(y_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}]=\sum_{\tau:[m]\to[m-1]}c_{\tau}\prod_{i=1}^{m}(y_{i,\tau(i)}-y_{i,m})

with
c_{\tau}=(-1)^{m-\ell(\tau)}\frac{(\ell(\tau)-1)!\ (m-\ell(\tau))!}{m!\ (m-1)!}
.

The key point of this proposition is that
Q_{m}
is a function of the differences
y_{i,j}-y_{i,m}
. That is what gives the cancellation that we need. In contrast, here is a more familiar form often used.

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[Y]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(m)}{\langle}\bigotimes_{q=1}^{\ell(\pi)}\prod_{i\in\pi(q)}h_{i}\ ,\ \rho_{\ell(\pi)}^{T}{\rangle}

(4.15)

For
h_{1}=\ldots=h_{m}
this can be found in
[
58
, Lemma 1]
for determinantal processes and in
[
48
, Claim 4.3]
for general point processes. We give a short derivation of the identity (
4.15
) in Appendix
A

as it will be the starting point of the derivation of (
4.14
). The vital role of Assumption
4.2
is that each
\ell
-fold integral w.r.t.
\rho_{\ell}^{T}
in (
4.15
) can be extended to an
m
-fold integral w.r.t.
\rho_{m}^{T}
, by (
4.5
). That allows us to express
\kappa_{m}
as an integral of a polynomial of
Y_{i}
s against
\rho_{m}^{T}
. This polynomial is then shown to be a function of the differences alone.

Proof of Proposition
4.12
.

We start with (
4.15
) and symmetrize over variables to rewrite
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[Y]
in a more convenient manner. Observe that if
\tau:[m]\to[m]
is any function that induces the partition
\pi
(by which we mean that
\tau(i)=\tau(j)
if and only if
i,j
belong to the same block of
\pi
), then writing
\ell
for
\ell(\pi)
and
\mbox{Ran}(\tau)
for the range of
\tau
,

\displaystyle{\langle}\bigotimes_{q=1}^{\ell}\prod_{i\in\pi(q)}h_{i}\ ,\ \rho_{\ell}^{T}{\rangle}

\displaystyle=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\ell}}\prod_{k=1}^{m}h_{i}(x_{\tau(i)})\ \rho_{\ell}^{T}(x_{\mbox{\tiny Ran}(\tau)})\;\mathrm{d}x_{\mbox{\tiny Ran}(\tau)}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m-\ell}\frac{(\ell-1)!}{(m-1)!}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}(x_{\tau(i)})\ \rho_{m}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}x_{m},

where the last equality follows because of Assumption
4.2
(see (
4.5
)).
Further, there are
m(m-1)\ldots(m-\ell+1)
different mappings
\tau
that give the same partition
\pi
. Consequently, we get (write
\ell(\tau)=\#\mbox{Ran}(\tau)
)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[Y]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\tau:[m]\to[m]}\frac{(-1)^{m-\ell(\tau)}}{\binom{m-1}{\ell(\tau)-1}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}(x_{\tau(i)})\ \rho_{m}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}x_{m}

\displaystyle={\langle}Q_{m}[(h_{i}(x_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq m}],\rho_{m}^{T}{\rangle}

(4.16)

where

\displaystyle Q_{m}[(\zeta_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\tau:[m]\to[m]}\frac{(-1)^{m-\ell(\tau)}}{\binom{m-1}{\ell(\tau)-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\zeta_{i,\tau(i)}

(4.17)

If we now show that
Q_{m}
can be re-written as in the statement of the Proposition, the proof is complete.

Towards the same, we first show that
Q_{m}[(\zeta_{i,j}+t_{i})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}]
does not depend on
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}
. By symmetry, it suffices to show the lack of dependence on
t_{1}
. To this end, set
t_{1}=t
and
t_{j}=0
for
j\geq 2
and consider
Q_{m}[(\zeta_{i,j}+t\delta_{i,1})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}]
which is a linear polynomial in
t
. The coefficient of
t
in this polynomial is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!(m-1)!}\sum_{\tau:[m]\to[m]}(-1)^{m-\ell(\tau)}(\ell(\tau)-1)!(m-\ell(\tau))!\prod_{i=2}^{n}\zeta_{i,\tau(i)}.

Fix
\tau(2),\ldots,\tau(m)
, and suppose that
\ell
of them are distinct. We compute the coefficient of
\prod_{i=2}^{n}\zeta_{i,\tau(i)}
in the above expression.

1.

In the
\ell
cases when
\tau(1)=\tau(j)
for some
j\geq 2
,
\ell(\tau)=\ell
. The total contribution to the coefficient from these summands is
(-1)^{m-\ell}(\ell-1)!(m-\ell)!\times\ell
.

2.

In the
m-\ell
cases when
\tau(1)\not=\tau(j)
for any
j\geq 2
, we have
\ell(\tau)=\ell+1
. The total contribution to the coefficient from these summands is
(-1)^{m-\ell-1}\ell!(m-\ell-1)!\times(m-\ell)
.

Adding the two, we get zero and hence the derivative of
Q_{m}[(\zeta_{i,j}+t\delta_{i,1})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}]
with respect to
t
is identically zero. This shows that
Q_{m}[(\zeta_{i,j}+t\delta_{i,1})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}]
does not depend on
t
and
Q_{m}[(\zeta_{i,j}+t_{i})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}]
does not depend on
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}
as claimed.

Taking
t_{i}=-\zeta_{i,m}
, we see that
Q_{m}[(\zeta_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}]=Q_{m}[(\zeta_{i,j}-\zeta_{i,m})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}]
, and using the expression (
4.17
) for the latter gives the expression in the statement of the proposition. Also observe that if
\tau(i)=m
for some
i\in[m]
, then the corresponding summand vanishes and hence we have restricted to only
\tau:[m]\to[m-1]
.

\Box

4.4
Proofs of central limit theorems

We prove the two central limit theorems here. In structure, the proof of the first theorem is similar to that of Proposition
2.5
: We first prove for compactly supported smooth functions and then extend it to functions with less smoothness.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

STEP 1:
Proof for compactly supported smooth
f
.

We shall first derive bounds for multilinear cumulants for any compactly supported smooth functions
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},i=1,\ldots,m
and then reduce it in the case of
h_{i}=f,i=1,\ldots,m
.

From Proposition
4.12
,
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[{\mathcal{X}}(h_{1}),\ldots,{\mathcal{X}}(h_{m})]
is a linear combination of finitely many terms of the form

\int\prod_{i=1}^{m}(h_{i}(x_{\tau(i)})-h_{i}(x_{m}))\ \rho_{m}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\ \mathrm{d}x_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}x_{m},

for some
\tau:[m]\to[m-1]
. For simplicity, let us first assume that
h_{i}
are real analytic and later we will argue for compactly supported smooth functions.

Then for all
\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(m)}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
,
\int\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}|z_{i}|^{\alpha^{(i)}}|\kappa_{m-1}|(\mathrm{d}z)<\infty
and so,

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i=1}^{m}(h_{i}(x_{\tau(i)})-h_{i}(x_{m}))\ \rho_{m}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\ \mathrm{d}x_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}x_{m}

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{i=1}^{m}(h_{i}(x+z_{\tau(i)})-h_{i}(x))\ \kappa_{m-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\ \mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{m-1}\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{\alpha\not=0}\frac{1}{\alpha!}\partial^{\alpha}h_{i}(x)z_{\tau(i)}^{\alpha}\right)\ \kappa_{m-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\ \mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{m-1}\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(m)}\not=0}I_{m}(\tau;\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(m)})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\partial^{\alpha^{(i)}}h_{i}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x.

Applying this to
h_{i,L}(x)=h_{i}(x/L)
to get

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(h_{1}),\ldots,{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(h_{m})]={\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[{\mathcal{X}}(h_{1,L}),\ldots,{\mathcal{X}}(h_{m,L})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau:[m]\to[m-1]}c_{\tau}\sum_{\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(m)}\not=0}I_{m}(\tau;\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(m)})L^{d-|\alpha^{(1)}|-\ldots-|\alpha^{(m)}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\partial^{\alpha^{(i)}}h_{i}.

By definition of
d_{m}
, we see that
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[h_{1,L},\ldots,h_{m,L}]=O(L^{d-d_{m}})
and putting
h_{i}=f
for all
i
, we obtain
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}({\mathcal{X}}(f_{L}))=O(L^{d-d_{m}})
. Thus

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}\Big(\frac{{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})]}{\sqrt{L^{d-2k}}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})]}{L^{m(d-2k)/2}}

(4.18)

\displaystyle=O(L^{(d-d_{m})-\frac{1}{2}m(d-2k)}).

(4.19)

By our assumption that
2(d-d_{m})<m(d-2k)
for all large
m
, it follows that all large cumulants vanish in the limit.
Further,
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{2}\Big(\frac{{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})]}{\sqrt{L^{d-2k}}}\Big)\to Q^{\kappa}_{2k}(f,f)
as
L\to\infty
by Proposition
2.5
(2). Now using a variant of Marcinkiewicz’s theorem (see
[
32
, Theorem 1]
), we can conclude that the limiting distribution is
N(0,Q^{\kappa}_{2k}(f,f))
.

Now we fix the proof for compactly supported smooth functions
h_{i}
and more importantly only assuming integrability of
|z|^{\gamma}\kappa_{m-1}(z)
for
|\gamma|\leq l+1
with
l=d_{m}-1
. In this case, we fix
\tau:[m]\to[m-1]
as before and define
H_{x}(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(h_{i}(x+z_{\tau(i)})-h_{i}(x))
. The Taylor expansion of
H_{x}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{m-1}\to\mathbb{R}
around
0
is

\displaystyle H_{x}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})=\sum_{1\leq|\theta|\leq l}\frac{(-1)^{|\theta|}}{\theta!}\partial^{\theta}H_{x}(0)\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}z_{j}^{\theta^{(j)}}\ +\ \sum_{|\theta|=l+1}\frac{(-1)^{|\theta|}|\theta|}{\theta!}R_{\theta}(x,z)\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}z_{j}^{\theta^{(j)}}.

(4.20)

Here
\theta\in(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d})^{m-1}
is written as
\theta=(\theta^{(1)},\ldots,\theta^{(m-1)})
with
\theta^{(j)}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
, and the self-explanatory meaning
|\theta|=|\theta^{(1)}|+\ldots+|\theta^{(m-1)}|
,
\theta!=\theta^{(1)}!\ldots\theta^{(m-1)}!
and
\partial^{\theta}=\partial_{z_{1}}^{\theta^{(1)}}\ldots\partial_{z_{m-1}}^{\theta^{(m-1)}}
. The smoothness of
h
gives that
|R_{\theta}(x,z)|\leq C_{\theta},
a finite constant.

Thus, for a given
\theta\in(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d})^{m-1}
, it is easy to see that
\partial^{\theta}H_{x}(0)
is a linear combination of terms of the form
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\partial^{\alpha^{(i)}}h_{i}(x)
, where
\alpha\in(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d})^{m}
satisfies
|\alpha|=|\theta|
. Therefore, the integral of the first summand in (
4.20
) with respect to
\kappa_{m-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{m-1}
is a sum of terms of the form

I_{m}(\tau;\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(m)})\int\prod_{i=1}^{m}\partial^{\alpha^{(i)}}h_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x

over
\alpha\in(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d})^{m}
satisfying
|\alpha|\leq l
.
Since
l=d_{m}-1
, for all
|\alpha|<l
,
I_{m}(\tau;\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(m)})=0
and so we are only left with the second term in (
4.20
). Now put
h_{i,L}
in (
4.20
) and correspondingly
H_{L,x}
to obtain by a change of variables that

\displaystyle|\int H_{L,x}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\kappa_{m-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{m-1}\mathrm{d}x|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|\theta|=d_{m}}L^{d-|\theta^{(1)}|-\ldots-|\theta^{(m)}|}\frac{(-1)^{|\theta|}|\theta|}{\theta!}\int|R_{\theta}(x,\frac{z}{L})|\prod_{j=1}^{m}|z_{\tau(j)}|^{\theta^{(j)}}|\kappa_{m-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})|\mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{m-1}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=O(L^{d-d_{m}}),

where for the last bound we have used the boundedness of
R_{\theta}
and the integrability assumption on
\kappa_{m-1}
. Thus, we derive as in (
4.18
) that

{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}\Big(\frac{{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})]}{\sqrt{L^{d-2k}}}\Big)=O(L^{(d-d_{m})-\frac{1}{2}m(d-2k)}),

and thus the required central limit theorem follows.

STEP 2:
Proof for
f\in L^{1}\cap H^{k}
.

As in the second step of the proof of Proposition
2.5
, we approximate
f\in L^{1}\cap H^{k}
by compactly supported smooth functions. More precisely, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we can write
f=g+h
with
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
Q_{2k}(h,h)<\varepsilon
. Then we also have that
|Q_{2k}(f,f)-Q_{2k}(g,g)|\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, by triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. For any
L>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})]}{\sqrt{L^{d-2k}}}=\frac{{\mathcal{X}}(g_{L})-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}(g_{L})]}{\sqrt{L^{d-2k}}}+\frac{{\mathcal{X}}(h_{L})-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}(h_{L})]}{\sqrt{L^{d-2k}}}.

The second summand has zero mean and its variance converges to
Q_{2k}(h,h)<\varepsilon
. The first summand converges in distribution to
N(0,Q_{2k}(g,g))
. This shows that all subsequential limits of
\frac{{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})]}{\sqrt{L^{d-2k}}}
are within
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
of
N(0,Q_{2k}(f,f))
in the Kantorovich distance
W_{2}
. As
\varepsilon\to 0
, it follows that
\frac{{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}(f_{L})]}{\sqrt{L^{d-2k}}}
converges in distribution to
N(0,Q_{2k}(f,f))
.

\Box

Next we prove the CLT for Hölder statistics.

Proof of Theorem
4.5
.

From Proposition
4.12
and stationarity,
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f))
is a sum of finitely many terms of the kind (for some
\tau:[m]\to[m-1]
)

\displaystyle\Big|\int\prod_{i=1}^{m}(f_{L}(x+z_{\tau(i)})-f_{L}(x))\ \kappa_{m-1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\ \mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{m-1}\ \mathrm{d}x\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C^{m}L^{-\alpha m}\int_{L.B}\int_{(L.S)^{m-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}|z_{i}|^{\alpha\ell_{i}}\ |\kappa_{m-1}|(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\ \mathrm{d}x

(4.21)

where
S=B-B
where
B
is the support of
f
and
\ell_{i}=\#\{k\;:\;\tau(k)=i\}
and
C
is the
\alpha
-Hölder norm of
f
. Since
\sum l_{i}=m
, the integrability assumption (
4.9
) grants finiteness of the above integrals in (
4.21
) and so we bound the inner integral in (
4.21
) by

\int\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}|z_{i}|^{\alpha\ell_{i}}\ |\kappa_{m-1}|(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1})\ \mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{m-1}.

Denoting the supremum over all choices of
l_{i}
in the above integral by
C_{m}
, we can then bound
(
4.21
) by

C_{m}\times C^{m}L^{-\alpha m}\times L^{d}|B|=C_{m,f}^{\prime}L^{-\alpha m+d}

where
C_{m,f}^{\prime}
depends on
f
and
m
, but not on
L
. As we have assumed that
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{2}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f))\geq CL^{-2\alpha+\epsilon}
, it follows that for large
m
,
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f))=o({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{2}({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f))^{m/2})
as
L\to\infty
.
Since the second cumulant is the variance, we have that
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}(\frac{{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f))-\mathbb{E}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)\right)}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}\!\left({\mathcal{X}}_{L}(f)\right)}})\to 0
for large enough
m
. Thus again by the variant of Marcinkiewicz’s theorem (see
[
32
, Theorem 1]
), we can conclude the normal convergence.

Appendix A
Appendix: Proof of the cumulant formula (
4.15
)

Let
h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}
be compactly supported continuous functions. Using the formula for multilinear moments and the Campbell-Mecke formula, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}\ldots Y_{m}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in{\mathcal{X}}}h_{1}(x_{1})\ldots h_{m}(x_{m})\right]\;=\;\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(m)}\langle\bigotimes_{r=1}^{\ell(\pi)}\prod_{i\in\pi(r)}h_{i}\ ,\ \rho_{\ell(\pi)}\rangle

by summing according to the induced partition of
[m]
in which
i
and
j
are in the same block if and only if
x_{i}=x_{j}
. To be clear, the term corresponding to
\pi
with
\ell(\pi)=\ell
is

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\ell}}\rho_{\ell}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell})\prod_{r=1}^{\ell}\prod_{i\in\pi(r)}h_{i}(y_{r})\ \mathrm{d}y_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}y_{\ell}.

Here we have used the assumption that
\rho_{\ell}
are absolutely integrable, which ensures that the above integrals exist and are finite. This allows the use of the Campbell-Mecke formula as well.

For
\pi,\sigma\in\Pi(m)
, we write
\pi\leq\sigma
if
\pi
refines
\sigma
, i.e., each block of
\pi
is contained in a block of
\sigma
. In such a case, the restriction
\pi|_{\sigma(r)}
is a set partition of
\sigma(r)
, for each
r\leq\ell(\sigma)
.

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[Y]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\Pi(m)}(-1)^{\ell(\sigma)-1}(\ell(\sigma)-1)!\prod_{r=1}^{\ell(\sigma)}\sum_{\pi_{r}\in\Pi(\sigma_{r})}{\langle}\bigotimes_{s=1}^{\ell(\pi_{r})}\prod_{i\in\pi_{r}(s)}h_{i}\ ,\ \rho_{\ell(\pi_{r})}{\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\Pi(m)}(-1)^{\ell(\sigma)-1}(\ell(\sigma)-1)!\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(m):\pi\leq\sigma}\prod_{r=1}^{\ell(\sigma)}{\langle}\bigotimes_{s=1}^{\ell(\pi|_{\sigma(r)})}\prod_{i\in\pi|_{\sigma(r)}}h_{i}\ ,\ \rho_{\ell(\pi|_{\sigma(r)})}{\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\Pi(m)}(-1)^{\ell(\sigma)-1}(\ell(\sigma)-1)!\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(m):\pi\leq\sigma}{\langle}\bigotimes_{q=1}^{\ell(\pi)}\prod_{i\in\pi(q)}h_{i}\ ,\ \bigotimes_{r=1}^{\ell(\sigma)}\rho_{\ell(\pi|_{\sigma(r)})}{\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(m)}{\langle}\bigotimes_{q=1}^{\ell(\pi)}\prod_{i\in\pi(q)}h_{i}\ ,\ \sum_{\sigma\in\Pi(m):\sigma\geq\pi}(-1)^{\ell(\sigma)-1}(\ell(\sigma)-1)!\bigotimes_{r=1}^{\ell(\sigma)}\rho_{\ell(\pi|_{\sigma(r)})}{\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(m)}{\langle}\bigotimes_{q=1}^{\ell(\pi)}\prod_{i\in\pi(q)}h_{i}\ ,\ \rho_{\ell(\pi)}^{T}{\rangle}.

When
h_{i}=f
for all
i
, by the symmetry of
\rho_{\ell}^{T}
, this expression specializes to

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{m}[Y]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(m)}{\langle}f^{|\pi_{1}|}\otimes\ldots\otimes f^{|\pi_{\ell}|},\rho_{\ell}^{T}{\rangle}

(A.1)

in which form it occurs in the literature, for example in Claim 4.3 of
[
48
]
.

Appendix B
Appendix: Simple point processes on discrete spaces

Let
E
be a countable set and let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a simple point process with a fixed
N
number of points, i.e.,
{\mathcal{X}}(E)=N
. All information about the point process is also encoded in the collection of Bernoulli random variables
(V_{x})_{x\in E}
, where
V_{x}={\mathcal{X}}(\{x\})
. The correlation functions are defined as

\rho_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}[V_{x_{1}}\ldots V_{x_{k}}]&\mbox{ if }x_{i}\mbox{ are distinct,}\\
0&\mbox{ if not}.\end{cases}

We use this in (
4.1
) to define
\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
for any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in E
, not necessarily distinct. When
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
are distinct, from the definition of
\rho_{k}
and relationship between moments and cumulants (
4.13
), it immediately follows that

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})={\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}[V_{x_{1}},\ldots,V_{x_{k}}].

(B.1)

What happens when
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
are not distinct? The general case is outlined in Remark
B.1
below. Here we restrict ourselves to the special case where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}
are distinct and
x_{k}=x_{j}
for some
j<k
. We claim that

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},x_{j})={\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}[V_{x_{1}},\ldots,V_{x_{k-1}},V_{x_{j}}]-{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k-1}[V_{x_{1}},\ldots,V_{x_{k-1}}].

(B.2)

To see this, take
j=1
without loss of generality. Observe that in (
4.1
), the summands corresponding to
\pi
where
1
and
k
belong to the same block, vanish. Therefore,

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}[V_{x_{1}},\ldots,V_{x_{k}}]-\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(k)}^{*}(-1)^{\ell(\pi)-1}(\ell(\pi)-1)!\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\pi)}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in\pi_{j}}V_{x_{i}}\right],

where the second sum is over
\pi
in which
1
and
k
belong to the same block. As
V_{x_{1}}V_{x_{k}}=V_{x_{1}}
, the second sum remains the same if we drop
V_{x_{k}}
, which shows that it is just the same as
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k-1}[V_{x_{1}},\ldots,V_{x_{k-1}}]
. This proves (
B.2
).

Verification of Assumption
4.2
:

For distinct
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}
, using (
B.1
) and (
B.2
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{u}\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}[V_{x_{1}},\ldots,V_{x_{k-1}},V_{u}]-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k-1}[V_{x_{1}},\ldots,V_{x_{k-1}}]

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}[V_{x_{1}},\ldots,V_{x_{k-1}},\sum_{u}V_{u}]-(k-1){\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k-1}[V_{x_{1}},\ldots,V_{x_{k-1}}]

using the multilinearity of
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}
. As
\sum_{u}V_{u}=N
, a constant, the first term vanishes. Using (
B.1
) again, the second term is just
-(k-1)\rho_{k-1}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})
, which proves Assumption
4.2
.

Remark B.1
.

Using the inclusion-exclusion principle, it may be checked that for any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
,

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{r}\leq k}^{*}(-1)^{k-r}\kappa_{r}[V_{x_{j_{1}}},\ldots,V_{x_{j_{r}}}]

where the sum is over all
1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{r}\leq k
such that
\{x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{r}}\}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
(i.e., all distinct
x_{i}
s must occur among
x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{r}}
).

Appendix C
Appendix: On truncated correlations for zeros of random analytic functions

Let
F
be a random analytic function on a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and let
{\mathcal{X}}
be the point process of its zero set. Some of the manipulations below (including that
{\mathcal{X}}
is a simple point process a.s.) require some mild conditions on
F
, but they are all satisfied for Gaussian analytic functions that have no deterministic zeros and sufficient non-degeneracy (in the sense of Nazarov and Sodin
[
48
]
). From Green’s theorem,
{\mathcal{X}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Delta_{z}\log|F(z)|
in the distributional sense, and this is the source of the formulas (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) for the truncated correlations of
{\mathcal{X}}
.

In an attempt to prove (
4.3
), we rephrase it as follows (compare this with the proof in Appendix
B
for finite processes on discrete spaces).

Fix distinct
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k-1}
and write
h(z_{k})=\Delta_{z_{1}}\ldots\Delta_{z_{k-1}}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}[\log|\widetilde{F}(z_{1})|,\ldots,\log|\widetilde{F}(z_{k})|]
.
With
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{D}(z_{i},\varepsilon)
and using (
4.11
) we derive that

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{k}\int\rho_{k}^{T}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k})\mathrm{d}z_{k}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\Delta_{z}h(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}h(z_{j}+\varepsilon e^{i\theta})\ \varepsilon\mathrm{d}\theta

by Green’s theorem (assuming fast decay at infinity or at the boundary of
\Omega
). Thus, using the symmetry of the cumulant
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}
in its arguments, (
4.3
) will follow if we show that for each
k
and distinct
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k-1}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\Delta_{z_{1}}\ldots\Delta_{z_{k-1}}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k}[\log|\widetilde{F}(z_{1})|,\ldots,\log|\widetilde{F}(z_{k-1})|,\log|\widetilde{F}(z_{1}+\varepsilon e^{i\theta})|]\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{z_{1}}\ldots\Delta_{z_{k-1}}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{k-1}[\log|\widetilde{F}(z_{1})|,\ldots,\log|\widetilde{F}(z_{k-1})|].

(C.1)

The advantage of (
C.1
) over (
4.3
) is that here the contributions of the integrals are localized around the points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k-1}
. It is also similar to the derivation in Appendix
B
.

Nevertheless, we have not been able to prove (
C.1
) for G.E.F., let alone more general Gaussian analytic functions. In the main text (Example
4.9
) we sketched a proof for
k=2
for G.E.F. Here we present a proof for
k=2
for general Gaussian analytic functions.

Proof of (
C.1
) for
k=2
for Gaussian analytic functions.

Let
K(z,w)=\mathbb{E}[F(z)\overline{F(w)}]
be the covariance and let
\theta(z,w)=\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{F}(z)\overline{\widetilde{F}(w)}]=\frac{K(z,w)}{\sqrt{K(z,z)}\sqrt{K(w,w)}}
be the correlation. The assumption of no deterministic zeros implies
K(z,z)\neq 0
for all
z
and hence
\theta
is well-defined and smooth.

Now (see Section 3.5 in
[
31
]
, in particular, Lemma 3.5.2), we can write

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{2}[\log|\widetilde{F}(z)|,\log|\widetilde{F}(w)|]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\mbox{Li}_{2}(|\theta(z,w)|^{2})

where
\mbox{Li}_{2}(u)=\sum_{p\geq 1}\frac{u^{p}}{p^{2}}
is the dilogarithm function. Observe that
|\theta(z,w)|^{2}\to 1
as
w\to z
. Therefore, to write a perturbative expression, we rewrite the well-known reflection identity (as the two sides are equal for
u=0
, it suffices to show that the derivatives are equal, and that follows from
\mbox{Li}_{2}^{\prime}(u)=-\frac{1}{u}\log(1-u)
)

\displaystyle\mbox{Li}_{2}(u)+\mbox{Li}_{2}(1-u)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-(\log u)\log(1-u)

as

\displaystyle\mbox{Li}_{2}(u)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-u)^{p}}{p^{2}}(p\log(1-u)-1)

in order to write

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{2}[\log|\widetilde{F}(z)|,\log|\widetilde{F}(w)|]

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}+\frac{1}{4}(1-|\theta(z,w)|^{2})(\log(1-|\theta(z,w)|^{2})-1)+O((1-|\theta(z,w)|^{2})^{2}\log(1-|\theta(z,w)|^{2})).

Write the power series expansion
K(w+\xi,w+\zeta)=\sum_{p,q\geq 0}a_{p,q}\xi^{p}\overline{\zeta}^{q}
. A little work shows that

\displaystyle 1-|\theta(z,w)|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{1,1}a_{0,0}-a_{1,0}a_{0,1}}{a_{0,0}^{2}}|z-w|^{2}+o(|z-w|^{2})\mbox{ as }z\to w

\displaystyle=-\pi\rho_{1}(w)|z-w|^{2}+\ldots

We used the Kac-Rice formula (see page 26 of
[
31
]
) in arriving at the last line. In addition note that
a_{0,0}=K(w,w)>0
and hence
\rho_{1}(w)
is a smooth function of
w
. Therefore, as
z\to w
,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{2}[\log|\widetilde{F}(z)|,\log|\widetilde{F}(w)|]

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}-\frac{\pi}{4}\rho_{1}(w)|z-w|^{2}\log|z-w|^{2}+[\mbox{lower order terms}].

A direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle\Delta_{w}(|z-w|^{2}\log|z-w|^{2})

\displaystyle=4(2+\log|z-w|^{2}).

When we take
z=w+\varepsilon e^{i\theta}
and differentiate with respect to
\varepsilon
, this becomes
\frac{8}{\varepsilon}
.

What about the other terms in
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{2}[\log|\widetilde{F}(z)|,\log|\widetilde{F}(w)|]
? The lower order terms are not merely smaller in magnitude, they are terms involving higher powers of
|z-w|^{2}
. Hence if any of the differentiation operations is applied to them, the overall contribution is
o(1/\varepsilon)
. The same holds if any of the derivatives is applied to the
\rho_{1}(w)
factor (which is smooth as noted above). Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\Delta_{w}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{2}[\log|\widetilde{F}(w)|,\log|\widetilde{F}(w+\varepsilon e^{i\theta})|]=-\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon}\rho_{1}(w)+o(1/\varepsilon).

Multiplying by
\varepsilon
and integrating w.r.t.
d\theta/2\pi
gives
-2\pi\rho_{1}(w)
. As
\rho_{1}(w)=\frac{1}{2\pi}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{1}[\log|\widetilde{F}(w)|]
, we arrive at (
C.1
) for
k=2
.

\Box
