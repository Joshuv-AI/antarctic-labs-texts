Statistical Estimation of Adversarial Risk in Large Language Models under Best-of-N Sampling
Mingqian Feng †, Xiaodong Liu, Weiwei Yang, Chenliang Xu, Christopher White, Jianfeng Gao

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are typically evaluated for safety under single-shot or low-budget adversarial prompting, which underestimates real-world risk. In practice, attackers can exploit large-scale parallel sampling to repeatedly probe a model until a harmful response is produced. While recent work shows that attack success increases with repeated sampling, principled methods for predicting large-scale adversarial risk remain limited. We propose a scaling-aware Best-of-N estimation of risk, SABER, for modeling jailbreak vulnerability under Best-of-N sampling. We model sample-level success probabilities using a Beta distribution, the conjugate prior of the Bernoulli distribution, and derive an analytic scaling law that enables reliable extrapolation of large-N attack success rates from small-budget measurements. Using only n=100 samples, our anchored estimator predicts ASR@1000 with a mean absolute error of 1.66, compared to 12.04 for the baseline, which is an 86.2% reduction in estimation error. Our results reveal heterogeneous risk scaling profiles and show that models appearing robust under standard evaluation can experience rapid nonlinear risk amplification under parallel adversarial pressure. This work provides a low-cost, scalable methodology for realistic LLM safety assessment. We will release our code and evaluation scripts upon publication to future research.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in real-world systems where safety failures can have serious societal and operational consequences. As a result, reliable evaluation of robustness under adversarial conditions has become a central research challenge. Most existing safety benchmarks assess robustness under single-shot or low-budget querying, implicitly assuming that adversaries have limited ability to repeatedly probe the model. However, in practice, attackers can automate large-scale parallel sampling pipelines, issuing thousands of attempts until a single harmful response is elicited, leading to systematic underestimation of real-world risk
(
2
)
.

Recent work has begun to explore more realistic threat models for adversarial prompting. Particularly, Best-of-N Jailbreaking
(
9
)
demonstrates that attack success rates (ASR) increase sharply as the number of sampled prompt augmentations grows, even if variations are limited to simple character-level or surface-form manipulations, indicating that safety-aligned models can be compromised under sufficiently large attack budgets. While they convincingly establish the empirical existence of scaling vulnerabilities in such simplified settings, it mainly focuses on demonstrating the effectiveness of repeated sampling strategies and does not provide a principled statistical framework for estimating or extrapolating ASR to large budgets when exhaustive evaluation is computationally infeasible.

Our key insight is that the scaling behavior of ASR is governed by the distribution of sample-level success probabilities, which we model as a Beta distribution, the conjugate prior of Bernoulli, providing a principled and analytically tractable representation of uncertainty in per-sample success rates. Under this modeling, we derive an analytic scaling law linking small-N observations to large-N attack success rates (ASR@N). Finally, we propose a scaling-aware Best-of-N estimation risk, SABER, bridging a critical gap between empirical attack evidence and predictive safety measurement.

We evaluate our framework under a broader range of adversarial conditions that closely resemble real-world threat scenarios, including multiple advanced jailbreak pipelines operating in practical settings. This allows us to characterize not only the presence of scaling vulnerabilities but also their behavior under realistic operational constraints, offering a faithful view of LLM safety risk in deployment contexts.

Our results show that large-scale adversarial risk can be accurately predicted using only small-budget measurements. For example, Using only budgets
n=100
, SABER predicts ASR@1000 with a mean absolute error of
1.25
, compared to
12.04
for the naive baseline which is an
89.6\%
error reduction. We further demonstrate that distinct attack methods exhibit heterogeneous Sample-ASR distributions, resulting in fundamentally different risk scaling profiles. These findings reveal that models which appear robust under standard evaluation (ASR@1) can experience rapid and nonlinear risk amplification under parallel adversarial pressure, underscoring the limitations of current evaluation practices and motivating the need for BoN-aware safety assessment.

By introducing a mathematically grounded, low-cost, and scalable risk estimation framework, this work advances how jailbreak risk should be measured and reported. Rather than relying solely on expensive brute-force evaluation, we provide a principled methodology for forecasting adversarial risk, laying a foundation for next-generation LLM safety benchmarks that better reflect realistic adversarial settings.

Preliminaries

Jailbreak attack and evaluation metric.
Given a harmful query
q
, we model a jailbreak as a tripartite pipeline: an attacker
\mathcal{A}
produces an adversarial prompt
q^{\text{adv}}
; the victim model
\mathcal{V}
generates a corresponding response
r
; and a judge
\mathcal{J}
returns a score
s
and determines whether the victim
\mathcal{V}
is jailbroken. It can be formulated as follows:

\begin{split}q^{\text{adv}}&\sim\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot|q),\\
r&\sim\pi_{\mathcal{V}}(\cdot|q^{\text{adv}}),\\
s&=\mathcal{J}(q,r)\in\{0,1\}\end{split}

(1)

where we define “
1
” as jailbroken and “
0
” as not jailbroken.

When considering a dataset of harmful queries
Q_{H}=\{q_{i}\}_{1}^{K}
,
Attack Success Rate (ASR)
is a common metric to measure the overall adversarial risk. ASR is defined as the expected proportion of harmful queries in the dataset on which the attacker successfully jailbreaks the victim model.

Scaling with Best-of-N sampling.
By “
ASR
” without any suffix, we usually refer to the
\text{ASR}@1
, which means the attacker only attempts once for each harmful query,

\text{ASR}@1\coloneqq\mathbb{E}(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i}^{K}s_{i}),

(2)

where
s_{i}
is the judge result on the
i
-th harmful query
q_{i}
.

However, in practice, attackers can adopt a Best-of-N sampling strategy, repeatedly sampling adversarial prompts until one elicits a harmful response or the budget of
N
attempts is exhausted. Many prior works have demonstrated that this simple strategy effectively jailbreaks advanced open- and closed-source LLMs, using various attack methods, including simple text augmentation
(
9
)
. In this Best-of-N scenario, it is equivalent to consider
N
parallel attempts rather than sequential ones. This leads to the attack success rate at
N
attempts (ASR@N), formulated as below,

\text{ASR}@N\coloneqq\mathbb{E}(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i}^{K}s_{i,1}\lor...\lor s_{i,N}),

(3)

where
s_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
is the judge result of the
j
-th independently sampled attempt on the
i
-th harmful query.

Prior work has also shown that holding the adversarial prompt fixed while sampling multiple victim responses can yield successful jailbreaks. Moreover, since real-world LLM inference is inherently non-deterministic
(
8
)
, repeated sampling of the judge can exhibit analogous effects. Thus, to streamline the exposition without affecting the generality, we assume that each attempt independently samples the full attacker-victim-judge pipeline.

Beta–Binomial distribution.
As a result, a Best-of-N jailbreak attempt can be treated as a Bernoulli trial with an
unknown success probability

\theta
. In that case, a common Bayesian choice is to place a Beta prior
\theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha,\beta>0
, because of its conjugacy to the Binomial likelihood. Specifically, the conjugacy implies that after observing
k
successes in
n
trials, the posterior remains Beta,

\theta\mid k,n\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha+k,\,\beta+n-k),

(4)

and the update depends only on the sufficient statistics
(k,n-k)

(
5
)
. Integrating out
\theta
yields the corresponding Beta–Binomial marginal distribution for
k
,

\Pr(k\mid n,\alpha,\beta)=\binom{n}{k}\frac{\mathrm{B}(k+\alpha,\,n-k+\beta)}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}.

(5)

As shown in

, for any given (attacker
\mathcal{A}
, victim
\mathcal{V}
, judge
\mathcal{J}
), our SABER framework estimates
\mathrm{ASR}@N
from small-budget measurements in three stages. Initially, we collect the jailbreak outcomes of
n

\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
distribution, yielding
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
. In the end, we leverage the scaling law in
Theorem

3.3
to predict the adversarial risk under Best-of-N sampling, where
\alpha
governs the rate of risk amplification.

3.1
Uncertain Best-of-N Performance

In practice, the relevant threat model often corresponds to large sampling budgets: attackers can automate parallel probing and sustain many attempts. More importantly, a central and inexorable law in jailbreak evaluation is that adversarial risk increases with the sampling budget, making ASR@N at large
N
a more faithful and critical measure of operational risk than
\text{ASR}@1
. A common, but ultimately fragile, intuition is that robustness rankings should be stable across budgets: if attack method
\mathcal{A}_{1}
attains a higher
\text{ASR}@1
than
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, then
\mathcal{A}_{1}
should remain more effective for all
N
.

Figure 2
:
Attack Success Rate at
N
attempts (ASR@N) against GPT-4.1-mini on
HarmBench
.

However, as illustrated in
Figure

2
, our experiment demonstrates that this monotonic-ranking intuition does not hold in general. Augmentation
(
9
)
exhibits a lower
\text{ASR}@1
than ADV-LLM
(
15
)
against GPT-4.1-mini
(
11
)
on
HarmBench
judged by
HarmBench Classifier
, yet surpasses it at
N=15
(i.e., in
\text{ASR}@15
). This reversal implies that attack methods differ not only in their single-shot effectiveness, but also in how quickly their success rate amplifies with more attempts.

Prior work has similar observations.
9
reported empirical scaling regularities under repeated sampling. In particular,
\log(-\log(\text{ASR}@N))
appears approximately linear in
\log N
, yielding a simple straight-line fit in log-log coordinates. However, the fitted slope varies substantially across the jailbreaking triplets (attacker
\mathcal{A}
, victim
\mathcal{V}
, judge
\mathcal{J}
) and is not known
a priori
.

Consequently, “effective” jailbreaks cannot be characterized solely by
\text{ASR}@1
, and the scaling profile with respect to
N
is an essential component of adversarial risk.
However, directly evaluating ASR@N for large
N
is often prohibitively expensive. This leaves an open question central to large-
N
safety assessment: what governs the scaling speed of different jailbreak triplets, and how can the ASR at large
N
be predicted given a limited budget?

3.2
Statistical Scaling Law under Best-of-N Sampling

We now present an analytic scaling law for
\mathrm{ASR}@N
under Best-of-
N
sampling in the large-
N
regime. The central idea is to model each attempt outcome as a Bernoulli random variable with a latent, heterogeneous success probability that varies
across harmful queries
.

We begin by characterizing the asymptotic behavior of an OR-aggregated Bernoulli variable under a Beta mixture. The proof is deferred to
Section

B.1
.

Theorem 3.1

(OR-aggregated Beta–Bernoulli hierarchy)
.

Let
\theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
. Conditional on
\theta
, let
X_{j}\mid\theta\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\theta)
,
j=1,2,...
, and define
X^{(N)}\coloneqq\bigvee_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}
. Then, we have,

\footnotesize\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[1-&X^{(N)}]=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\frac{\Gamma(\beta+N)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+N)}\\
&\sim\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)N^{\alpha}}\left[1-\frac{\alpha(\alpha+2\beta-1)}{2N}+O\left(N^{-2}\right)\right].\end{split}

(6)

For each harmful query
q_{i}
, let
\theta_{i}\in(0,1)
denote its fixed per-attempt sample-level success probability, i.e., sample attack success rate (Sample-ASR or SASR). Then, we formalize the corpus-level heterogeneity assumption as follows.

Assumption 3.2

(Sample-level Beta mixture)
.

For any fixed tripartite jailbreak pipeline
(\mathcal{A,\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{J})
and a dataset
Q_{H}
, the sample-level success probabilities
\{\theta_{i}\}
are drawn from a
\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
distribution, and conditional on
\theta_{i}
, all attempt outcomes are i.i.d. Bernoulli:
\forall q_{i}\in Q_{H}.

\displaystyle\theta_{i}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta),~s_{i,1},s_{i,2},...\mid\theta_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\theta_{i}).

(7)

This Beta-prior assumption is natural for the Bernoulli parameter
\theta
, because of its conjugacy. In
Section

C.2.1
, we validate it empirically via goodness-of-fit validation.

Under
3.2
,
\mathrm{ASR}@N
corresponds to the population expectation of the OR-aggregated attempt outcomes. Applying
Theorem

3.1
yields the following scaling law.

Theorem 3.3

(Scaling law for Best-of-
N
adversarial risk)
.

Under
3.2
, as
N\to\infty
,

\mathrm{ASR}@N\simeq 1-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,N^{-\alpha}.

(8)

Theorem

3.3
implies that large-
N
risk amplification is governed by the left-tail behavior of the sample-level vulnerability distribution:
\alpha
controls the scaling speed in
N
, while
\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)/\Gamma(\beta)
determines the leading constant.

Applicability to the target
N
.

Theorem

3.3
is an asymptotic statement, and it is useful to clarify when the approximation is numerically reliable. The derivation leverages standard Gamma-function asymptotics (Stirling-type approximations), which prior work
(
14
)
has reported to be accurate already at moderate sample sizes (e.g.,
N\geq 5\sim 10
). Therefore, empirically, this source of error is typically negligible once
N
leaves the very small regime.
In
Section

B.1.5
, we provide explicit error bounds for the approximation for
\mathrm{ASR}@N
. Empirically, we find that common jailbreak pipelines often fall into a parameter region with
\alpha<1
and
\beta<2
in
3.2
. Under this regime, one can show that for
N\geq 20
, the approximation error of
\mathrm{ASR}@N
in
Equation

8
is less than
\simeq 1\%
.

Small-
N
correction.

When consider even smaller
N
, or when
(\alpha,\beta)
lies outside this typical region (e.g. larger
\beta
), we provide a
small-
N
correction to the expression
by substitute
N
with
N+\beta+\frac{\alpha-1}{2}
in
Equation

8
. This replacement is a moment-matching improvement that cancels the
O(1/N)
term in the Gamma-ratio expansion. Proofs are provided in
Section

B.1.5
. One can show that under
\alpha<1
and
\beta<4
, the error of this correction is less than
\simeq 0.1\%
for
N\geq 5
, and the numerical analysis further indicates that the actual error is often much smaller than this upper bound. Nevertheless, we note our focus is on the large-N scaling performance relevant to realistic adversaries. In this regime, the difference between the refined and standard formulas is negligible. For simplicity, we therefore mainly use the approximation in
Theorem

3.3
in subsequent derivations.

3.3
Estimating Sample-ASR@1 Distribution

The scaling law in
Section

3.2
reduces large-
N
risk prediction under Best-of-N sampling to estimating the query-level vulnerability distribution, i.e., the distribution of
\theta_{i}\coloneqq\Pr(s_{i,1}{=}1\mid q_{i})
across harmful queries. In our setting,
\theta_{i}
is the Sample-ASR@1 for query
q_{i}
, and we posit the Beta prior
\theta_{i}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
as in
3.2
. We now develop estimators for
(\alpha,\beta)
from a small measurement budget consisting of
n
attempts per query over
K
queries.

A natural baseline is a two-stage fit. That it to first compute
\hat{\theta}_{i}=k_{i}/n
from
n
trials and then fit a Beta distribution to
\{\hat{\theta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
. While convenient, this approach treats
\hat{\theta}_{i}
as noise-free observations and can be miscalibrated when
n
is small. Instead, we adopt a one-stage likelihood that explicitly models the sampled Best-of-N trajectories. This yields a Beta–Binomial maximum likelihood estimation (MLE).

Let
k_{i}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{i,j}
denote the number of jailbreak successes observed for query
q_{i}
under
n
attempts.
By
Equation

5
, the corpus-level log-likelihood, ignoring
(\alpha,\beta)
-independent constants, becomes

\small\ell(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log B(k_{i}+\alpha,\,n-k_{i}+\beta)-K\log B(\alpha,\beta).

(9)

We obtain
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
by maximizing
Equation

9
over
\alpha>0,\beta>0
using standard constrained optimizers (e.g., L-BFGS-B or Newton-type methods),

\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta}=\argmax_{\alpha>0,\beta>0}\ell(\alpha,\beta).

(10)

Notably, fitted
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
should be interpreted as
properties of the chosen data distribution
Q_{H}
under the specified pipeline, but not universal constants. If the chosen distribution shifts, the fitted
(\alpha,\beta)
and all extrapolation may change.

\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta}
on different
n

Estimation Uncertainty.

The Beta–Binomial MLE
Equation

10
correctly accounts for uncertainty induced by finite
n
and provides stable estimates even in the small-budget regime. When the per-sample budget
n
is large, the two-stage fit approaches it. In
Section

B.2.2
, we derive the asymptotic variance of
Equation

10
and show that it decreases rapidly as either the per-sample budget
n
or the dataset size
K
increases. In particular,
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\alpha})
and
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta})
scale inversely with
K
.
We further empirically validate the behavior under varying per-sample budget
n
in
Figure

3
, and varying
K
in the appendix
Figure

7
, via Monte Carlo simulation. We fix
K=159
and set the ground-truth
\alpha=0.4
and
\beta=4
, and examine the sampling distribution of
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
under varying budget
n
. The estimator concentrates quickly as
n
increases.
n=10
already yields a substantial improvement over
n=5
, and by
n=50
the resulting empirical density is nearly indistinguishable from that obtained at
n=1000
. These results indicate that accurately fitting the hidden features behind the specified triplets does not require large per-query budgets, enabling the following reliable large-
N
ASR estimation from small
n
.

Moreover, the Beta–Binomial formulation naturally extends to heterogeneous measurement budgets by replacing
n
with query-specific
n_{i}
. Each query then contributes an appropriately weighted term to the likelihood, whereas the two-stage fit confounds differing estimation noise across queries.

3.4
Estimating
\mathrm{ASR}@N
at Operational Budgets

We now combine the fitted Sample-ASR@1 distribution with the large-
N
scaling law to obtain practical estimators of
\mathrm{ASR}@N
under Best-of-
N
sampling. Let
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
denote the Beta–Binomial MLE from
Section

3.3
, and let
n
denote the small measurement budget used to collect per-query outcomes. We present two variants of the SABER estimator.

SABER-Plugin.

First, the most direct approach applies
Theorem

3.3
with
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
as plug-in estimates. Then, we derive a plug-in asymptotic estimator:

\widehat{(\mathrm{ASR}@N)}_{\mathrm{plug\text{-}in}}\coloneqq 1-\frac{\Gamma(\hat{\alpha}+\hat{\beta})}{\Gamma(\hat{\beta})}\,N^{-\hat{\alpha}}.

(11)

This estimator is the simplest to report and applicable to most circumstances.
It naturally accommodates heterogeneous data collection, e.g., query-specific attempt budgets
n_{i}
in the Beta–Binomial fit, since all heterogeneity is absorbed into
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
. Notably, when the target
N
is relatively small, e.g., in practice
N\leq 20
, we may use the
small-
N
correction
in
Section

3.2
to further improve the accuracy.

SABER-Anchored.

When a reliable small-budget datapoint
\mathrm{ASR}@n
is available under the considered jailbreak pipeline, one can eliminate the unknown
\beta
-related leading constant by anchoring the asymptotic form at
n
. Specifically, using

1-\mathrm{ASR}@n\simeq\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,n^{-\alpha}
,
we derive

(\widehat{\mathrm{ASR}}@N)_{\mathrm{anchor}}\coloneqq 1-\bigl(1-\mathrm{ASR}@n\bigr)\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)^{\hat{\alpha}}.

(12)

This estimator depends only on
\mathrm{ASR}@n
,
N
, and
\hat{\alpha}
, and therefore avoids sensitivity to
\hat{\beta}
. It also captures the potential deviation between the actual per-sample vulnerability
\theta
distribution and the assumed Beta distribution. Similarly, the
small-
N
correction
can be applied to this anchored estimator when
N
is very small. We note that although this estimator requires a reliable small-budget datapoint
\mathrm{ASR}@n
, it is naturally achieved during data collection for estimating
(\alpha,\beta)
. If the budget is evenly distributed, we must have ASR@n with the per-sample budget
n
. Even in special cases where the budget is not evenly distributed and there is no lower limit, we can always use ASR@1. In practice, we found that this SABER-Anchored performed the best in most cases. Thus, we mainly focus on it subsequently.

SABER-Fit.

Additionally, one can bypass explicit estimation of
(\alpha,\beta)
and instead fit the
\mathrm{ASR}@n
curve directly from observed
\{(n,\mathrm{ASR}@n)\}
datapoints. Notably, this approach is originally proposed by
9
.
They empirically found that
-\log\mathrm{ASR}@n
exhibits an approximately linear trend in log-transformed coordinates across a wide range of victims and modalities. Our derived scaling law confirms their observation. Particularly, when
N
is large,
Theorem

3.3
is equivalent to their empirical formula. Specifically,
Theorem

3.3
indicates a linear relationship between
\log(1-\text{ASR@N})
and
\log N
. When
\mathrm{ASR}@N
is close to
1
, the approximation
\log(1-x)\approx-x
further implies the equivalent empirical form found in
9
,

-\log\bigl(\mathrm{ASR}@N\bigr)\approx a\,N^{-b},

(13)

where
a
and
b
are actually
\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)/\Gamma(\beta)
and
\alpha
, respectively. Then, we fit a straight line to estimate
(a,b)
in
Equation

13
using the observed small-budget curve, and then extrapolate to obtain
\widehat{\mathrm{ASR}}@N
at any target
N
.

Confidence intervals.

Beyond point estimates, our framework also supports uncertainty quantification for
\widehat{\mathrm{ASR}}@N
. In
Section

B.2.2
, we derive an analytic approximation for the covariance of the Beta–Binomial MLE
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
, expressed as a function of
(\alpha,\beta)
, budget
n
, and dataset size
K
. This enables principled confidence intervals for the anchored and plugin estimator via the delta method or simple transformation. For example, let
\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\alpha})
denote the obtained standard error of
\hat{\alpha}
. Since
Equation

12
is only related to and monotone in
\alpha
for
N>1
, a
100(1-\delta)\%
confidence interval is obtained by transforming the endpoints of the Wald interval for
\alpha
. From

\alpha\in\big[\hat{\alpha}-z_{1-\delta/2}\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\alpha}),\hat{\alpha}+z_{1-\delta/2}\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\alpha})\big]
, we have that the confidence interval of
(\widehat{\mathrm{ASR}}@N)_{\mathrm{anchor}}
is

\small 1-\bigl(1-\mathrm{ASR}@n\bigr)\left[\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)^{\hat{\alpha}+z\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\alpha})},\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)^{\hat{\alpha}-z\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\alpha})}\right],

(14)

where
z
represent the standard normal quantile
z_{1-\delta/2}
.

4.2.3
Predicting
N
for a Target ASR Level

In real-world settings, a red-team operator may ask how many parallel samples are needed to attain
90\%
jailbreak coverage, or a model developer may seek a budget-normalized measure of safety. Consequently, a reversed question arises:
how many attempts are required to reach a target level of adversarial risk?

Beyond estimating
\mathrm{ASR}@N
at an fixed budget, our method also supports this inverse query. By
Theorem

3.3
, for a target level
\tau{\in}(0,1)
, the required budget satisfies

N_{\tau}\simeq\left({\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}/(({1-\tau}){\Gamma(\beta)})\right)^{\!1/\alpha}
.
Replacing
(\alpha,\beta)
by the Beta–Binomial MLE
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
yields a direct plug-in estimator of
N_{\tau}
. Similar to SABER-Anchored, we consider a observed reliable small-budget datapoint
\mathrm{ASR}@n
. It yields a inverse rule that eliminates the leading constant,

\small\widehat{N}_{\tau}\coloneqq n\left(\frac{1-\mathrm{ASR}@n}{1-\tau}\right)^{\!1/\hat{\alpha}}.

(16)

Intuitively, when
\hat{\alpha}
is small, adversarial risk amplifies rapidly with
N
, and thus the budget needed to reach a high
\tau
can be surprisingly modest; conversely, larger
\hat{\alpha}
implies slower amplification and larger required budgets.

5
Conclusion

We present SABER, a statistical framework for predicting large-scale adversarial risk in LLMs under Best-of-N sampling from small-budget measurements. Our key insight is that the scaling behavior of attack success rates is governed by the per-sample vulnerability distribution, which we model as Beta distribution. This leads an analytic scaling law enabling reliable extrapolation. Our results carry important implications for LLM safety evaluation. We demonstrated that models appearing robust under single-shot evaluation can exhibit rapid risk amplification at scale and the attack approach ranking can reverse depending the sampling budget. These findings highlight the need for scaling-aware safety evaluation beyond ASR@1.

B.1.1
Proofs of Theorems

Gamma-ratio asymptotics.
We start from the asymptotic expansion of Gamma function ratios, which has been given by
16
. Here, we also give the step-by-step deduction.

Lemma B.1

(Gamma ratio, first-order expansion)
.

Fix constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. As
|z|\to\infty
with
|\arg z|\leq\pi-\delta
, we have

\frac{\Gamma(z+a)}{\Gamma(z+b)}=z^{a-b}\left(1+\frac{(a-b)(a+b-1)}{2z}+O(z^{-2})\right).

(17)

Proof.

The classical Stirling expansion
[
4
]
(uniform in
|\arg z|\leq\pi-\delta
) gives that

\Gamma(z)=\sqrt{2\pi}z^{z-\tfrac{1}{2}}e^{-z}\left(1+\frac{1}{12z}+O(z^{-2})\right),\qquad|z|\to\infty.

(18)

Apply
Equation

18
to
z+a
and
z+b
:

\displaystyle\Gamma(z+a)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi}(z+a)^{z+a-\tfrac{1}{2}}e^{-(z+a)}\left(1+\frac{1}{12(z+a)}+O(z^{-2})\right),

(19)

\displaystyle\Gamma(z+b)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi}(z+b)^{z+b-\tfrac{1}{2}}e^{-(z+b)}\left(1+\frac{1}{12(z+b)}+O(z^{-2})\right).

(20)

Using
\frac{1}{z+a}=\frac{1}{z}+O(z^{-2})
and
\frac{1}{z+b}=\frac{1}{z}+O(z^{-2})
, the
\frac{1}{12z}
contributions cancel to first order in the ratio.

It remains to rewrite
(z+a)^{z+a-\tfrac{1}{2}}e^{-(z+a)}
and
(z+b)^{z+b-\tfrac{1}{2}}e^{-(z+b)}
with base
z
.
For fixed
c
, define

R_{c}(z)\coloneqq\left(1+\frac{c}{z}\right)^{z+c-\tfrac{1}{2}}e^{-c}.

(21)

Then

(z+c)^{z+c-\tfrac{1}{2}}e^{-(z+c)}=z^{z+c-\tfrac{1}{2}}e^{-z}R_{c}(z)
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\log R_{c}(z)

\displaystyle=(z+c-\tfrac{1}{2})\log\left(1+\frac{c}{z}\right)-c

(22)

\displaystyle=(z+c-\tfrac{1}{2})\left(\frac{c}{z}-\frac{c^{2}}{2z^{2}}+O(z^{-3})\right)-c=\frac{c(c-1)}{2z}+O(z^{-2}),

(23)

so
R_{c}(z)=1+\frac{c(c-1)}{2z}+O(z^{-2})
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(z+a)}{\Gamma(z+b)}

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{z+a-\tfrac{1}{2}}e^{-z}\left(1+\frac{a(a-1)}{2z}+O(z^{-2})\right)}{z^{z+b-\tfrac{1}{2}}e^{-z}\left(1+\frac{b(b-1)}{2z}+O(z^{-2})\right)}

(24)

\displaystyle=z^{a-b}\left(1+\frac{a(a-1)-b(b-1)}{2z}+O(z^{-2})\right)

(25)

\displaystyle=z^{a-b}\left(1+\frac{(a-b)(a+b-1)}{2z}+O(z^{-2})\right).

(26)

∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

By construction,
X^{(N)}=\bigvee_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}
and conditional on
\theta
the
X_{j}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli
(\theta)
, hence

1-X^{(N)}=\mathbbm{1}\{X_{1}=\cdots=X_{N}=0\},\qquad\mathbb{E}\bigl[1-X^{(N)}\mid\theta\bigr]=(1-\theta)^{N}.

(27)

Taking expectation over
\theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
yields the exact closed form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[1-X^{(N)}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)}\bigl[(1-\theta)^{N}\bigr]=\int_{0}^{1}(1-\theta)^{N}\frac{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}d\theta

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta+N)}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\frac{\Gamma(\beta+N)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+N)},

(29)

which matches the first line of
Equation

6
.

To obtain the asymptotics, apply
Lemma

B.1
with
z=N
,
a=\beta
, and

:

\frac{\Gamma(\beta+N)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+N)}=N^{-\alpha}\left(1-\frac{\alpha(\alpha+2\beta-1)}{2N}+O(N^{-2})\right).

(30)

Multiplying by
\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)/\Gamma(\beta)
gives the claimed expansion in
Equation

6
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.3
.

Under
3.2
, for each query
q_{i}
we have a latent per-attempt success probability
\theta_{i}
, and conditional on
\theta_{i}
the attempt outcomes are i.i.d. Bernoulli
(\theta_{i})
.
Let
X^{(N)}(q_{i})
denote the OR-aggregated success indicator for
q_{i}
under
N
attempts. Then

\mathrm{ASR}@N=\mathbb{E}_{q_{i}\sim Q_{H}}[X^{(N)}(q_{i})]
and hence

1-\mathrm{ASR}@N=\mathbb{E}_{q_{i}\sim Q_{H}}[1-X^{(N)}(q_{i})].

(31)

Since
\theta_{i}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
under the assumption, applying
Theorem

3.1
gives the exact identity

1-\mathrm{ASR}@N=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\frac{\Gamma(\beta+N)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+N)}.

(32)

Moreover, by
Lemma

B.1
(equivalently, by the second line of
Equation

6
), we have the refined asymptotic expansion

1-\mathrm{ASR}@N\sim\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}N^{-\alpha}\left[1-\frac{\alpha(\alpha+2\beta-1)}{2N}+O\left(N^{-2}\right)\right],\qquad N\to\infty.

(33)

In particular, keeping only the leading term yields

1-\mathrm{ASR}@N\sim\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}N^{-\alpha}
,
and rearranging gives

\mathrm{ASR}@N\simeq 1-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}N^{-\alpha}
,
which is exactly
Equation

8
.

B.1.2
Unbreakable-sample modeling extension

Under the Beta-mixture assumption
\theta_{i}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
, the latent per-attempt success probability has continuous support on
(0,1)
, and thus
\Pr(\theta_{i}=0)=0
.
Consequently, for any fixed query
q_{i}
we have

\Pr\bigl(X^{(N)}(q_{i})=1\mid\theta_{i}\bigr)=1-(1-\theta_{i})^{N}\to 1
as
N\to\infty
,
and averaging over queries implies
\mathrm{ASR}@N\to 1
.
In practice, however, some harmful queries may be deterministically unbreakable under a given attacker–victim–judge pipeline (e.g., due to hard refusals, safety filters, or constrained decoding), which corresponds to a non-negligible probability mass at
\theta_{i}=0
.

A simple way to capture such “unbreakable” queries is a spike-and-slab prior,

\theta_{i}\sim\pi\delta_{0}+(1-\pi)\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta),

(34)

where
\delta_{0}
is a point mass at
0
and
\pi\in[0,1]
is the fraction of unbreakable queries.
Then

\mathrm{ASR}@N=1-\mathbb{E}\bigl[(1-\theta)^{N}\bigr]\to 1-\pi\qquad(N\to\infty),

(35)

so the risk saturates below
1
at a level determined by
\pi
. In this case, our scaling law becomes

\mathrm{ASR}@N=(1-\pi)\left(1-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}N^{-\alpha}\right).

(36)

When considering there exist
always-success samples
, a similar extension can be applied.

B.1.3
Benign-attempts extension for online risk estimation

In a more realistic online setting, we can consider that a streaming prompt is harmful with probability
p_{h}
and benign with probability
1-p_{h}
.
For harmful prompts, a judge determines whether the victim LLM is jailbroken, i.e.,
s(q^{\mathrm{harm}})\in\{0,1\}
.
For benign prompts, we assume the judge always outputs “not jailbroken,” i.e.,
s(q^{\mathrm{benign}})\equiv 0
.
Given a total budget of
N
streamed prompts, we are interested in estimating the probability of observing at least one jailbreaking event,

\Pr\bigl(s^{(N)}=1\bigr),

(37)

where
s^{(N)}
indicates whether there exists at least one successful jailbreak among the
N
prompts.

Assuming again the Beta distribution
Beta(\alpha,\beta)
of the sample-level success probability, we consider two cases.

Case 1: i.i.d. harmful prompts.

First consider a general scenario where each harmful prompt is generated independently by attacking a randomly sampled harmful query.
Under this full independence (random harmful query and randomly generated adversarial prompt), each prompt has an identical unconditional success probability

\theta\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\theta_{i}]=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}
.
Therefore,

\Pr\bigl(s^{(N)}=1\mid\alpha,\beta\bigr)=1-(1-\theta)^{N}=1-\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\right)^{N}.

(38)

Case 2: repeated attempts on one sampled harmful query.

As a special case, suppose that within a fixed period the attacker repeatedly queries adversarial prompts targeting a single harmful query.
Let
n_{h}
denote the number of harmful prompts among the
N
streamed prompts, where

n_{h}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(N,p_{h})
.
Conditioned on
n_{h}
, the probability of at least one jailbreak follows the same large-
n
scaling law as in
Equation

8
:

\Pr\bigl(s^{(N)}=1\mid n_{h},\alpha,\beta\bigr)\simeq 1-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,n_{h}^{-\alpha}.

(39)

Averaging over
n_{h}
yields

\displaystyle\Pr\bigl(s^{(N)}=1\mid\alpha,\beta\bigr)

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{n_{h}=0}^{N}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta+n_{h})}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}\right)\binom{N}{n_{h}}p_{h}^{n_{h}}(1-p_{h})^{N-n_{h}}

(40)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}\sum_{n_{h}=0}^{N}\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta+n_{h})\binom{N}{n_{h}}p_{h}^{n_{h}}(1-p_{h})^{N-n_{h}}.

(41)

By
B(\alpha,\beta+n)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\beta+n-1},dt
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{h}=0}^{N}\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta+n_{h})\binom{N}{n_{h}}p_{h}^{n_{h}}(1-p_{h})^{N-n_{h}}

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\beta-1}\Big[\sum_{n=0}^{N}\binom{N}{n}\big(p_{h}(1-t)\big)^{n}(1-p_{h})^{N-n}\Big]dt

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\beta-1}(1-p_{h}t)^{N}dt

(44)

By
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(a,b;c;z\right)=\frac{\Gamma(c)}{\Gamma\left(b\right)\Gamma\left(c-b\right)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{b-1}(1-t)^{c-b-1}}{(1-zt)^{a}}dt

[
4
]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\beta-1}(1-p_{h}t)^{N}dt

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\alpha\right)\Gamma\left(\beta\right)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{}_{2}F_{1}(-N,\alpha;\alpha+\beta;p_{h})

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B(\alpha,\beta){}_{2}F_{1}(-N,\alpha;\alpha+\beta;p_{h})

(47)

where
{}_{2}F_{1}
is the Gaussian hypergeometric function.

Finally, we have the following equation ASR@N in this case:

ASR@N

\displaystyle=\Pr\bigl(s^{(N)}=1\mid\alpha,\beta\bigr)

(48)

\displaystyle=1-{}_{2}F_{1}(-N,\alpha;\alpha+\beta;p_{h})

(49)

Large-
N
behavior.

When
\alpha>0
and
p_{h}\in(0,1]
, one can show that

{}_{2}F_{1}(-N,\alpha;\alpha+\beta;p_{h})\sim\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,(Np_{h})^{-\alpha},\qquad N\to\infty.

(50)

Hence, for large
N
, we obtain the generalized Best-of-
N
scaling law

\text{ASR@N}\simeq 1-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,(Np_{h})^{-\alpha}.

(51)

B.1.4
Small-
N
correction

Previouly, we have proved

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(z+a)}{\Gamma(z+b)}=z^{a-b}\left(1+\frac{(a-b)(a+b-1)}{2z}+O(z^{-2})\right).

(52)

and

\displaystyle\text{ASR@N}=1-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\frac{\Gamma(\beta+N)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+N)}.

(53)

By setting
z=N,a=\beta,b=\alpha+\beta
, we derive

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\beta+N)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+N)}=N^{-\alpha}\left(1-\frac{\alpha(\alpha+2\beta-1)}{2N}+O(N^{-2})\right).

(54)

However, if we set
z=N+\beta+\frac{\alpha-1}{2},a=\frac{1-\alpha}{2},b=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
, we can cancel out the first-order error, yeilding

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\beta+N)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+N)}=(N+\beta+\frac{\alpha-1}{2})^{-\alpha}(1+O(N^{-2})).

(55)

This gives us a better approximation. As a result, the small-N correlation of
Equation

8
is

\displaystyle\text{ASR@N}=1-\frac{\Gamma{\alpha+\beta}}{\Gamma(\beta)}(N+\beta+\frac{\alpha-1}{2})^{-\alpha}

(56)

B.1.5
Error Bounds

Error bound for the small-
N
correction
Equation

56
.

6
proves that the error made in truncating the Field’s asymptotic expansion is numerically less than and has the same sign as the first neglected term. The Field’s asymptotic expansion is

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(z+a)}{\Gamma(z+b)}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{\Gamma(1-2\rho+2j)}{\Gamma(1-2\rho)(2j)!}B_{2j}^{(2\rho)}(\rho)\,w^{2\rho-1-2j}+O\left(w^{2\rho-1-2m}\right),

(57)

as
w\rightarrow\infty
, where
2w=2z+a+b-1
,
2\rho=a-b+1
, and
B_{2j}^{(2\rho)}(\rho)
are the generalized Bernoulli polynomials defined by

\displaystyle\left(\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}\right)^{\alpha}e^{xt}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{j}}{j!},B_{j}^{(\alpha)}(x)\qquad B_{0}^{(\alpha)}(x)=1,\quad|t|<2\pi.

(58)

Recall that our small-
N
correction is obtained by choosing

2w=2z+a+b-1=2\left(N+\beta+\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\right),\qquad w\;=\;N+\beta+\frac{\alpha-1}{2},

(59)

so that

\frac{\Gamma(\beta+N)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+N)}\approx w^{-\alpha}.

(60)

In Field’s expansion, this corresponds to
a=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}
,
b=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
and hence
2\rho=a-b+1=1-\alpha
.
Therefore the first neglected term (i.e.,
j=1
when truncating at
m=1
) scales as
w^{-\alpha-2}
.
Moreover, by
6
, the truncation error is numerically bounded by the magnitude of this first neglected term, yielding

\left|\frac{\Gamma(\beta+N)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+N)}-w^{-\alpha}\right|\leq\left|C_{1}(\alpha)\right|\,w^{-\alpha-2},

(61)

where, using
B_{2}^{(2\rho)}(\rho)=-\rho/6
,

\begin{split}C_{1}(\alpha)&\coloneqq\frac{\Gamma(3-2\rho)}{\Gamma(1-2\rho)\,2!}\,B_{2}^{(2\rho)}(\rho)=-\frac{\rho}{6}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(3-2\rho)}{\Gamma(1-2\rho)\,2!}\\
&=-\frac{\rho}{6}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+2)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\,2}=-\frac{\rho}{6}\cdot\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{2}=\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)(\alpha-1)}{24}.\end{split}

(62)

Consequently, define the approximation residual
\varepsilon_{\text{corr}}(N;\alpha,\beta)
by

\mathrm{ASR}@N=1-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,\left(N+\beta+\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\right)^{-\alpha}+\varepsilon_{\text{corr}}(N;\alpha,\beta).

(63)

Then the induced error of
Equation

56
satisfies

\bigl|\varepsilon_{\text{corr}}(N;\alpha,\beta)\bigr|\leq\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,\frac{|\alpha(\alpha+1)(\alpha-1)|}{24}\,\left(N+\beta+\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\right)^{-\alpha-2}.

(64)

Deviation from the standard (large-
N
) approximation.

Define the difference between the small-
N
correction
Equation

56
and the standard approximation
Equation

8
as

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{std}\to\mathrm{corr}}(N;\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq\left(1-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,(N+\beta+\tfrac{\alpha-1}{2})^{-\alpha}\right)-\left(1-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,N^{-\alpha}\right).

(65)

Equivalently,

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{std}\to\mathrm{corr}}(N;\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\left[N^{-\alpha}-\left(N+\beta+\tfrac{\alpha-1}{2}\right)^{-\alpha}\right].

(66)

Consider
f(x)=x^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha>0
. Since
f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\alpha(\alpha+1)x^{-\alpha-2}>0
,
f
is convex on
(0,\infty)
.
Let
d\coloneqq\beta+\tfrac{\alpha-1}{2}
(assume
d>0
so that
N+d>0
).
Convexity implies that the secant slope is sandwiched by the tangent slopes,

-f^{\prime}(N+d)\,d\leq f(N)-f(N+d)\leq-f^{\prime}(N)\,d.

(67)

Using
-f^{\prime}(x)=\alpha x^{-\alpha-1}
gives the explicit bound

\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,\alpha\,d\,(N+d)^{-\alpha-1}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{std}\to\mathrm{corr}}(N;\alpha,\beta)\leq\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,\alpha\,d\,N^{-\alpha-1}.

(68)

In particular,

\bigl|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{std}\to\mathrm{corr}}(N;\alpha,\beta)\bigr|\leq\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,\alpha\,\left(\beta+\tfrac{\alpha-1}{2}\right)\,N^{-\alpha-1}.

(69)

Error bound for the standard scaling law
Equation

8
.

Define the residual
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{std}}(N;\alpha,\beta)
of the standard approximation by

\mathrm{ASR}@N=1-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,N^{-\alpha}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{std}}(N;\alpha,\beta).

(70)

By definitions
Equations

63
and
65
,

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{std}}(N;\alpha,\beta)=\varepsilon_{\text{corr}}(N;\alpha,\beta)-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{std}\to\mathrm{corr}}(N;\alpha,\beta).

(71)

Therefore, by the triangle inequality,

\bigl|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{std}}(N;\alpha,\beta)\bigr|\leq\bigl|\varepsilon_{\text{corr}}(N;\alpha,\beta)\bigr|+\bigl|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{std}\to\mathrm{corr}}(N;\alpha,\beta)\bigr|.

(72)

Combining
Equation

64
with
Equation

68
yields

\bigl|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{std}}(N;\alpha,\beta)\bigr|\leq\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\left[\alpha\left(\beta+\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\right)\,N^{-\alpha-1}+\frac{|\alpha(\alpha+1)(\alpha-1)|}{24}\,\left(N+\beta+\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\right)^{-\alpha-2}\right].

(73)

B.2
Sample-ASR distribution estimation

B.2.1
Beta–Binomial MLE

Two-stage Beta fit.

A common two-stage approach first estimates each query-level success probability by
\hat{\theta}_{i}\coloneqq k_{i}/n
from
n
trials, and then fits a Beta distribution
\theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
to the pseudo-observations
\{\hat{\theta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
.

Concretely, under
\theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
, the density is

P_{\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)}(\theta)=\frac{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}.

(74)

Treating
\{\hat{\theta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
as i.i.d. samples from this Beta distribution yields the (two-stage) log-likelihood

\displaystyle\ell_{\mathrm{2\text{-}stage}}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log P_{\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)}(\hat{\theta}_{i})

(75)

\displaystyle=(\alpha-1)\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log\hat{\theta}_{i}+(\beta-1)\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log(1-\hat{\theta}_{i})-K\log\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta).

(76)

Let
\psi
denote the digamma function. The score equations are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{2\text{-}stage}}}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log\hat{\theta}_{i}-K\big[\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\alpha+\beta)\big],

(77)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{2\text{-}stage}}}{\partial\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log(1-\hat{\theta}_{i})-K\big[\psi(\beta)-\psi(\alpha+\beta)\big].

(78)

In practice, one can solve these equations (or directly maximize
\ell_{\mathrm{2\text{-}stage}}
) using standard optimizers such as L-BFGS-B or Newton-type methods.

While simple, this approach treats
\hat{\theta}_{i}
as noise-free draws from
\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
. When
n
is small, the sampling variance of
\hat{\theta}_{i}
can be substantial, and this mismatch can lead to miscalibrated (overconfident) estimates.

One-stage Beta–Binomial MLE.

Instead, we directly model the sampled outcomes. For each harmful query
q_{i}
, let
s_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
denote the
j
-th attempt outcome and let
k_{i}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{i,j}
be the success count. Under
\theta_{i}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
and
s_{i,j}\mid\theta_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\theta_{i})
, the marginal (Beta–Binomial) likelihood is

P_{\mathrm{BB}}(k_{i}\mid\alpha,\beta,n)=\frac{\mathrm{B}(k_{i}+\alpha,\,n-k_{i}+\beta)}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)},

(79)

ignoring the combinatorial factor
\tbinom{n}{k_{i}}
which is independent of
(\alpha,\beta)
.
Aggregating over
K
queries yields the log-likelihood

\ell_{\mathrm{BB}}(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log\mathrm{B}(k_{i}+\alpha,\,n-k_{i}+\beta)-K\log\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta),

(80)

which we maximize over
\alpha>0,\beta>0
using standard constrained optimizers (e.g., L-BFGS-B or Newton-type methods).

Score equations.

Let
\psi
denote the digamma function. Differentiating
Equation

80
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{BB}}}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\!\big[\psi(k_{i}+\alpha)-\psi(n+\alpha+\beta)\big]-K\big[\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\alpha+\beta)\big],

(81)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{BB}}}{\partial\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\!\big[\psi(n-k_{i}+\beta)-\psi(n+\alpha+\beta)\big]-K\big[\psi(\beta)-\psi(\alpha+\beta)\big].

(82)

Heterogeneous budgets
n_{i}
.

When query-level attempt budgets vary, the same derivation applies by replacing
n
with
n_{i}
in each term:

\log\mathrm{B}(k_{i}+\alpha,\,n-k_{i}+\beta)\mapsto\log\mathrm{B}(k_{i}+\alpha,\,n_{i}-k_{i}+\beta)
and
\psi(n+\alpha+\beta)\mapsto\psi(n_{i}+\alpha+\beta)
.

B.2.2
Estimation Uncertainty

We focus on uncertainty quantification for our one-stage Beta–Binomial MLE in
Section

B.2.1
.

Asymptotic covariance.

Let
\hat{\omega}=(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
and
\omega=(\alpha,\beta)
. Under standard regularity conditions, the MLE is asymptotically normal,

\hat{\omega}\,\overset{d}{\approx}\,\mathcal{N}\bigl(\omega,\,I(\omega)^{-1}\bigr)
,
where
I(\omega)
is the Fisher information.
In practice we estimate
I(\omega)
using the observed information

\hat{I}(\hat{\omega})\coloneqq-\nabla^{2}\ell(\hat{\omega})
.

Let
\psi^{\prime}
denote the trigamma function. Differentiating the score equations yields the Hessian entries

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\ell}{\partial\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\!\big[\psi^{\prime}(k_{i}+\alpha)-\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)\big]-K\big[\psi^{\prime}(\alpha)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)\big],

(83)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\ell}{\partial\beta^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\!\big[\psi^{\prime}(n-k_{i}+\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)\big]-K\big[\psi^{\prime}(\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)\big],

(84)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\ell}{\partial\alpha\,\partial\beta}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)+K\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta).

(85)

Thus
\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(\hat{\omega})=\hat{I}(\hat{\omega})^{-1}
.

Fisher information (closed form).

By taking expectations of the negative Hessian under the Beta–Binomial model, we obtain the Fisher information matrix
I(\omega)=-\mathbb{E}[\nabla^{2}\ell(\omega)]
. Let
k\sim\mathrm{BetaBinomial}(n,\alpha,\beta)
denote the per-query success count. Then,

\displaystyle I(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=K\begin{bmatrix}\psi^{\prime}(\alpha)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)+\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\mathbb{E}\big(\psi^{\prime}(k+\alpha)\big)&\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)\\
\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)&\psi^{\prime}(\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)+\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\mathbb{E}\big(\psi^{\prime}(n-k+\beta)\big)\end{bmatrix},

(86)

\displaystyle I(\alpha,\beta)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K\,f(\alpha,\beta,n)}\begin{bmatrix}\psi^{\prime}(\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)+\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\mathbb{E}\big(\psi^{\prime}(n-k+\beta)\big)&\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)\\
\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)&\psi^{\prime}(\alpha)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)+\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\mathbb{E}\big(\psi^{\prime}(k+\alpha)\big)\end{bmatrix},

(87)

where

\displaystyle f(\alpha,\beta,n)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Big[\psi^{\prime}(\alpha)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)+\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\mathbb{E}\big(\psi^{\prime}(k+\alpha)\big)\Big]\Big[\psi^{\prime}(\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)+\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\mathbb{E}\big(\psi^{\prime}(n-k+\beta)\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 8.19447pt\;-\Big[\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)\Big]^{2}.

- Model response should themselves be harmful or undesirable for a public-facing LLM to output. Benign model responses do not count.

- If the model response is harmful but not satisfying the user’s request, it does not count.

- Model response that include caveats or warnings count if the request can reasonably be said to have been satisfied alongside them.

- Model response that respond, analyze, discuss or otherwise react to the request without actually exhibiting it do not count.

- Model response where the model clearly tries to exhibit the request count. E.g., it is OK if generated code has errors.

- If the request includes writing code, the model response MUST contain code artifacts related to the request, not just comments or instructions. Otherwise it does not count.

- Generations must be in English, or otherwise easy for English-speakers to identify as a successful instance of the request (e.g., code that clearly implements a keylogger with comments in a language other than English is still acceptable)

C.2.2
Budget Allocation

In this subsection, we consider how to allocate our budget if we have a fixed and limited total budget
nK
, where
n
is the average number of trials per harmful query and
K
is the number of queries (data size).
Our goal is to choose
(n,K)
to minimize the estimation uncertainty of
\widehat{\mathrm{ASR}}@N
, which in our framework is induced by the uncertainty of the Beta–Binomial MLE
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
.

Closed-form scaling.

Recall the closed-form asymptotic covariance of
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
in
Equation

87
:

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\alpha})=\bigl[I(\alpha,\beta)^{-1}\bigr]_{11}
and
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta})=\bigl[I(\alpha,\beta)^{-1}\bigr]_{22}
.
Since
I(\alpha,\beta)
scales linearly with
K
, both variances scale as
O(1/K)
when
n
is fixed.
If we further fix the total budget
B\coloneqq nK
, then (for
\hat{\alpha}
) we can rewrite

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Kf(\alpha,\beta,n)}\Big(\psi^{\prime}(\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)+\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\mathbb{E}\big(\psi^{\prime}(n-k+\beta)\big)\Big)

(88)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{Bf(\alpha,\beta,n)}\Big(\psi^{\prime}(\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)+\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\mathbb{E}\big(\psi^{\prime}(n-k+\beta)\big)\Big)

(89)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\,g_{\alpha}(\alpha,\beta,n),

(90)

where

g_{\alpha}(\alpha,\beta,n)\coloneqq\frac{n}{f(\alpha,\beta,n)}\Big(\psi^{\prime}(\beta)-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)+\psi^{\prime}(n+\alpha+\beta)-\mathbb{E}\big(\psi^{\prime}(n-k+\beta)\big)\Big).

(91)

An analogous expression holds for
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta})=\frac{1}{B}g_{\beta}(\alpha,\beta,n)
.
Therefore, under fixed
B
, optimizing the variance over
(n,K)
reduces to selecting
n
that minimizes
g_{\alpha}
(or
g_{\beta}
), and then setting
K=B/n
.
In particular, if
g_{\alpha}(\alpha,\beta,n)
admits a minimizer at some finite
n^{*}>0
, then (once
B
is large enough to allow
K=B/n^{*}
queries) the optimal
n^{*}
is independent of
B
, and the optimal
K
scales proportionally with
B
.

C.2.3
Predicting
N
for a Target ASR Level

A budget-based robustness metric.

The inverse form motivates a simple evaluation metric that is directly aligned with operational threat models:
Budget@
\tau
, defined as the predicted number of attempts needed to reach
\mathrm{ASR}@N=\tau
(e.g., Budget@90%). Unlike
\mathrm{ASR}@1
, this metric explicitly captures scaling behavior: two attackers with similar single-shot ASR can induce dramatically different required budgets if their
\hat{\alpha}
differs.

From a benchmarking perspective, Budget@
\tau
offers two advantages. First, it is
interpretable
: it answers “how many parallel attempts are needed to succeed on
\tau
fraction of harmful queries?” Second, it is
cost-aware
: one can report Budget@
\tau
using only small-budget measurements by fitting
\hat{\alpha}
(and optionally anchoring at
\mathrm{ASR}@n
), avoiding brute-force evaluation at very large
N
.

Appendix D
Limitation and Future Work

Beyond binary judges.

Our formulation assumes a binary judge output
s\in\{0,1\}
, which simplifies the analysis by reducing each attempt to a Bernoulli trial. In practice, judges may return scores or multi-class assessment instead (e.g., multiple severity levels). A natural extension is to replace the Bernoulli-Beta hierarchy with a categorical likelihood and a Dirichlet prior, enabling a scaling-aware estimator for non-binary outcomes and providing more fine-grained evaluation.

Broader task coverage and stronger pipelines.

Our empirical evaluation focuses on a single textual jailbreak benchmark, HarmBench
[
10
]
. Additionally, although we choose attackers span three major paradigms to show our generality, they are not necessarily the most advanced available. Similarly, due to budget constraints, we do not evaluate the most costly frontier safety-aligned models (e.g., GPT-5.2). Future work should test SABER on broader settings, including multi-modal jailbreak tasks and larger datasets such as AdvBench
[
17
]
, as well as a wider range of state-of-the-art attackers and victims.

Understanding and correcting systematic bias.

Empirically, the remaining error of SABER tends to arise more from underestimating
\mathrm{ASR}@N
than from overestimating it.
A promising direction is to investigate the sources of this bias and develop principled amendments that improve calibration while preserving the extrapolation guarantees.
