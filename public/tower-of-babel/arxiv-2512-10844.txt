Modeling, Segmenting and Statistics of Transient Spindles via Two-Dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Dynamics
C. Sun, D. Fettahoglu, D. Holcman

Abstract
We develop here a stochastic framework for modeling and segmenting transient spindle-like oscillatory bursts in electroencephalogram (EEG) signals. At the modeling level, individual spindles are represented as path realizations of a two-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process with a stable focus, providing a low-dimensional stochastic dynamical system whose trajectories reproduce key morphological features of spindles, including their characteristic rise–decay amplitude envelopes. On the signal processing side, we propose a segmentation procedure based on Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD) combined with the detection of a central extremum, which isolates single spindle events and yields a collection of oscillatory atoms. This construction enables a systematic statistical analysis of spindle features: we derive empirical laws for the distributions of amplitudes, inter-spindle intervals, and rise/decay durations, and show that these exhibit exponential tails consistent with the underlying OU dynamics. We further extend the model to a pair of weakly coupled OU processes with distinct natural frequencies, generating a stochastic mixture of slow, fast, and mixed spindles in random temporal order. The resulting framework provides a data-driven framework for the analysis of transient oscillations in EEG and, more generally, in nonstationary time series.

Introduction

Studying random oscillatory signals in systems lacking a well-defined analytical structure poses significant theoretical and practical challenges. Classical tools such as Fourier transforms provide global spectral information, but fail to capture localized and transient phenomena. In contrast, wavelet transforms and related multiscale methods have been successfully used to detect local bursts, heterogeneity, and pointwise irregularities in time-series data
[
15
,
20
]
. However, beyond descriptive decomposition, understanding the emergence and variability of transient patterns in systems driven by stochastic dynamics requires the development of generative models and estimation of underlying parameters.

Electroencephalogram (EEG) signals exemplify this setting. They arise from the collective dynamics of excitatory and inhibitory neuronal populations, generating rhythmic voltage fluctuations through bursting and synaptic interactions. These fluctuations manifest as transient events across multiple frequency bands. Notably, alpha-band spindles (8–12 Hz) are short-lived oscillatory bursts that occur in various physiological states, including quiet wakefulness, early sleep, general anesthesia, meditation, and sensory-evoked activity
[
6
,
8
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) have been widely employed to model network-level neuronal dynamics
[
12
,
17
]
. In particular, such models can capture stochastic transitions between multiple attractors, often separated by a limit cycle, effectively reproducing transient oscillatory behavior and metastability
[
13
,
10
,
39
,
40
]
. These systems not only account for burst-like oscillations but also reveal, through two-dimensional phase-space analysis, how the proximity of attractors to the boundary of a limit cycle induces non-Poissonian residence-time distributions
[
17
,
9
,
10
]
.

Linearization of the dynamics in the vicinity of a stable focus attractor yields a two-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process. Under specific parameter regimes, this linear system exhibits damped oscillations with a characteristic phase delay relative to the original nonlinear limit-cycle dynamics—an effect that can be computed analytically
[
38
,
37
,
34
,
27
]
.

In contrast to this classical interpretation, the present study investigates a complementary regime in which the two-dimensional OU process, driven by stochastic forcing, gives rise to recurrent bursts of oscillatory activity—akin to EEG spindles—without the need for a nearby deterministic cycle. We show that such random bursts arise spontaneously and recurrently, centered around a characteristic frequency, as a manifestation of resonance in a linear system with rotational drift
[
29
]
. This mechanism provides a minimal yet effective model for generating transient oscillations with statistically quantifiable structure.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present the classical properties of the two-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process and highlight its capacity to generate oscillatory trajectories under stochastic forcing. Section 3 introduces a mechanism for spindle generation and presents a robust segmentation algorithm based on Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD). This algorithm identifies spindle events as intervals between two successive local minima enclosing a single maximum, enabling reliable extraction of transient oscillations
[
20
,
14
]
. In Section 4, we apply this segmentation to compute key statistical descriptors of spindles, including their duration, peak amplitude, and inter-spindle interval distributions. Section 5 focuses on characterizing the temporal asymmetry of spindles by computing the statistics of their ascending and descending phases using first-passage time analysis. In Section 6, we extend the model to a system of two weakly coupled OU processes with distinct natural frequencies. This coupling leads to the emergence of diverse spindle types—slow, fast, and mixed—appearing in a stochastic sequence without discernible temporal patterns. We conclude in Section 7 with a discussion of the implications of this modeling framework for understanding transient oscillatory activity in EEG recordings, and its potential for broader applications in stochastic signal analysis.

2.1
Two-dimensional anti-symmetric Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process generates spindle dynamics

A two-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process is governed by the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}=A\mbox{\boldmath$s$}+\sqrt{2\sigma}\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}},

(1)

where the anti-symmetric matrix is

A=\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda&\omega\\
-\omega&-\lambda\\
\end{pmatrix}

associated to the state variable
\mbox{\boldmath$s$}=(x,y)\in\hbox{\bb R}^{2}

The matrix

1
,the noise term drives the system away from the fixed point, generating stochastic trajectories that repeatedly spiral around the origin, thereby producing transient oscillations with amplitude modulated by the noise intensity.

Steady-state solution of the Fokker-Planck Equation

2.2.2
Width of the Power Spectral Density at half maximum power

To quantify the frequency dispersion induced by the spindle dynamics, we use the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the power spectral density. The FWHM is defined as the bandwidth over which the power remains above half of its peak value, providing a measure of the concentration of energy around the dominant frequency. For the power spectrum given in Eq. (
19
), the maximum occurs at the resonant frequency
f_{\mathrm{max}}=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}
, and the corresponding peak power is

\displaystyle S(f_{max})=\frac{\sigma}{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+4\omega^{2}}\right).

(21)

The width is given by the solution of equation

\displaystyle S(f)=\frac{1}{2}S(f_{max}).

(22)

The two solutions are given by

\displaystyle f_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{a(\lambda,\omega)\pm b(\lambda,\omega)}{c(\lambda,\omega)}}

(23)

where

\displaystyle a(\lambda,\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\lambda^{2}\omega^{2}+2\omega^{4},

\displaystyle b(\lambda,\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\sqrt{\lambda^{6}+8\lambda^{4}\omega^{2}+24\lambda^{2}\omega^{4}+16\omega^{6}}

(24)

\displaystyle c(\lambda,\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}+2\omega^{2}.

Finally, the width of the
\alpha-
band is given by

\displaystyle\FWHM=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\sqrt{\frac{a(\lambda,\omega)+b(\lambda,\omega)}{c(\lambda,\omega)}}-\sqrt{\frac{a(\lambda,\omega)-b(\lambda,\omega)}{c(\lambda,\omega)}}\right).

(25)

When
\omega\gg\lambda

\displaystyle\FWHM\sim\frac{\lambda}{\pi}+\frac{\lambda^{3}}{4\pi\omega^{2}}+\frac{11\lambda^{5}}{32\pi\omega^{2}}+O\left(\lambda^{6}\right)

In summary, the width of the resonance band is primarily governed by the damping parameter
\lambda
, which controls the rate of decay toward the fixed point. Our numerical simulations confirm that a two-dimensional OU process, under appropriate noise levels, can spontaneously generate spindle-like oscillations with substantial amplitude and a well-defined frequency structure. These results support the use of this minimal stochastic model for capturing the essential features of random burst dynamics observed in empirical signals.

2.3
Range of parameters leading to spindles vs oscillations

Spontaneous transient spindles emerge within a specific range of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process parameters
(\lambda,\omega,\sigma)
as defined in Eq. (
1
). To generate pronounced bursting behavior, certain conditions must be met. In particular, one must avoid regimes where the dynamics are dominated by small fluctuations around the origin, as occurs when the damping parameter
\lambda
is too small
[
34
,
36
]
. Instead, sustained spindle-like oscillations require an appropriate balance between rotational drift and stochastic excitation. This leads to the following criteria:

1.

The eigenvalues of the matrix
A
in Eq. (
1
) must be complex, i.e.,
\omega>0
, ensuring rotational dynamics around the fixed point.

2.

The system should exhibit underdamped behavior, meaning the oscillatory timescale dominates over the relaxation time:
\omega/\lambda\gg 1
.

3.

The noise amplitude
\sigma
must be sufficiently large to induce excursions away from the origin, but not so large as to destroy the coherent bursting structure.

Under these conditions, the process frequently escapes the vicinity of the fixed point, and the empirical distribution over any time window of duration greater than
1/\omega
deviates significantly from the equilibrium density centered at
(x,y)=(0,0)
.

3.1
Spindle segmentation

In the absence of a universally accepted definition of a spindle, we propose to define it as a transient oscillatory event occurring within a continuous signal, delimited by two successive local minima in its amplitude envelope. To formalize this, we express the signal
x(t)
in complex coordinates as

\displaystyle x(t)=A(t)\exp(i\phi(t)),

(30)

where
A(t)
denotes the slowly varying amplitude (envelope), and
\phi(t)
encodes the fast oscillatory phase component. The scale separation assumption
\frac{d}{dt}A(t)\ll\frac{d}{dt}\phi(t)\approx\omega
ensures that the amplitude captures low-frequency modulation, while the phase evolves on a faster timescale set by the dominant frequency
\omega
.

In practice, we shall estimate the envelope
A(t)
by applying Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD) to the signal
x(t)

[
18
,
14
]
. Based on this representation, we define a spindle as the segment of the signal between two successive local minima of
A(t)
that fall below a threshold
T_{\text{down}}
, and that enclose a single maximum exceeding a higher threshold
T_{\text{up}}
(Fig.
3
). The spindle segmentation proceeds via the following steps:

1.

Identification of local extrema.

Detect all local maxima and minima of the signal
x(t)
using Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD). The envelope of the signal is extracted via locally weighted polynomial regression (LOESS) using a second-degree model
[
19
]
.

2.

Envelope smoothing to reduce sampling noise.

Construct the upper envelope
x_{\text{up}}(t)
and the lower envelope
x_{\text{down}}(t)
by applying LOESS smoothing to the sequences of local maxima and minima, respectively.

3.

Computation of the envelope amplitude.

Define the instantaneous envelope amplitude
d(t)
as the difference between the smoothed upper and lower envelopes:

d(t)=|x_{\text{up}}(t)-x_{\text{down}}(t)|.

(31)

4.

Threshold-based segmentation of extrema.

Identify time points where the amplitude envelope reaches prominent peaks and troughs by thresholding:

•

Define a minimum threshold
T_{\min}=\sigma_{x}
and a maximum threshold
T_{\max}=3\sigma_{x}
, where
\sigma_{x}
is the standard deviation of the signal
x(t)
.

•

Identify the set of local maxima of
d(t)
that exceed
T_{\max}
:

\mathcal{S}_{T_{\max}}=\left\{t_{i}\;\middle|\;\exists\varepsilon>0,\,\forall t\in[t_{i}-\varepsilon,t_{i}+\varepsilon],\,d(t_{i})>d(t),\,d(t_{i})>T_{\max}\right\}.

(32)

•

Similarly, identify the set of local minima of
d(t)
that fall below
T_{\min}
:

\mathcal{S}_{T_{\min}}=\left\{t_{j}\;\middle|\;\exists\varepsilon>0,\,\forall t\in[t_{j}-\varepsilon,t_{j}+\varepsilon],\,d(t_{j})<d(t),\,d(t_{j})<T_{\min}\right\}.

(33)

5.

Spindle definition.

A spindle is defined as the segment of the signal between two consecutive time points in
\mathcal{S}_{T_{\min}}
that contains at least one local maximum in
\mathcal{S}_{T_{\max}}
. Formally,

\mathcal{S}_{\text{Spindles}}=\bigcup_{(t_{i},t_{i+1})\in\mathcal{S}_{T_{\min}}}\left\{x(t)\;\middle|\;t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}],\,\exists t_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{T_{\max}},\;t_{i}<t_{j}<t_{i+1}\right\}.

(34)

4
Stochastic spindle statistics

In this section, we analyze the stochastic dynamics underlying spindle amplitudes generated by the two-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process (
1
). We derive asymptotic and explicit expressions for the distributions of spindle duration, amplitude, and the increasing and decreasing phases (Fig.
3
E). Spindles are understood here as stochastic excursions in amplitude, modulated by the noisy dynamics of the OU system.

Starting from the components
(x(t),y(t))
of the OU process, we introduce polar coordinates:

\displaystyle x(t)+iy(t)=r(t)e^{i\phi(t)},

(39)

where
r(t)
is the instantaneous amplitude and
\phi(t)
is the phase. Applying Ito’s formula to the SDEs satisfied by
x(t)
and
y(t)
yields the following stochastic dynamics:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x^{2}

\displaystyle=[2x(-\lambda x+\omega y)+2\sigma]\mathrm{d}t+2x\sqrt{2\sigma},\mathrm{d}w_{1},\ \mathrm{d}y^{2}

\displaystyle=[2y(-\lambda y-\omega x)+2\sigma]\mathrm{d}t+2y\sqrt{2\sigma},\mathrm{d}w_{2},

(40)

where
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are independent Brownian motions.
Summing the two equations gives the evolution of
r^{2}(t)
:

\mathrm{d}r^{2}=(-2\lambda r^{2}+4\sigma),\mathrm{d}t+2r\sqrt{2\sigma},\mathrm{d}\omega,

(41)

where
\omega(t)
is a standard Brownian motion. Setting
u=r^{2}
, we obtain:

\mathrm{d}u=(-2\lambda u+4\sigma),\mathrm{d}t+2\sqrt{2\sigma u},\mathrm{d}w.

(42)

Applying Ito’s formula to
f(u)=\sqrt{u}=r
yields the SDE for
r(t)
:

\mathrm{d}r=\left(-\lambda r+\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\sigma},\mathrm{d}w.

(43)

This equation can be written in the form of a gradient flow:

\mathrm{d}r=-W^{\prime}(r),\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\sigma},\mathrm{d}w,

(44)

with effective potential:

W(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2}r^{2}-\sigma\log r.

(45)

The equilibrium amplitude
r_{\mathrm{eq}}
satisfies
W^{\prime}(r_{\mathrm{eq}})=0
, giving:

r_{\mathrm{eq}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\lambda}}.

(46)

The corresponding Fokker–Planck equation for the probability density
p(r,t)
reads:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}(r,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial r^{2}}(r,t)+\frac{\partial}{\partial r}[W^{\prime}(r)p(r,t)],

(47)

\displaystyle p(r,0)

\displaystyle=\delta(r),

(48)

where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function. The stationary solution satisfies:

p(r)=C\exp\left(-\frac{W(r)}{\sigma}\right),

(49)

with normalization constant:

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\int_{0}^{\infty}x\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}x^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}.

(50)

Hence, the stationary probability density function of the spindle amplitude
r
is:

p(r)=\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}r\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}r^{2}\right).

(51)

We now compute the expectation and variance of
r
. Using the standard integrals:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}e^{-br^{2}}\mathrm{d}r=\frac{1}{4b}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{b}},\ \int_{0}^{\infty}r^{3}e^{-br^{2}}\mathrm{d}r=\frac{1}{2b^{2}},

(52)

and setting
b=\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}
, we find:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[r]=\sqrt{\frac{\pi\sigma}{2\lambda}},\ \mathrm{Var}(r)=2\frac{\sigma}{\lambda}-\frac{\pi\sigma}{2\lambda}=\frac{\sigma}{\lambda}\left(2-\frac{\pi}{2}\right).

(53)

These expressions provide explicit insights into the amplitude distribution of stochastically generated spindles. In particular, they show that the mean amplitude scales as
\sqrt{\sigma/\lambda}
, while the variance is controlled by a balance between noise intensity and damping.

4.1
Estimation of inter-spindle MFPT duration

To estimate the mean inter-spindle duration, we compute the
mean first passage time
(MFPT) for the amplitude process
r(t)
to reach a threshold
T>0
, which marks the onset of a spindle. The expected time
u(y)
for the process starting at
r(0)=y
to reach
T
satisfies the following second-order differential equation (Dynkin’s formula)
[
30
]
:

\displaystyle\sigma\frac{d^{2}u}{dy^{2}}(y)-W^{\prime}(y)\frac{du}{dy}(y)

\displaystyle=-1,\quad 0<y<T,

(54)

\displaystyle u(T)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{(absorbing)},

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{(reflecting)}.

Here,
W(y)=\frac{\lambda}{2}y^{2}-\sigma\log y
is the effective potential for the radial amplitude process as derived previously in Eq. (
44
). The boundary condition at
y=0
models a reflecting origin, consistent with the fact that
r(t)\geq 0
and the process cannot cross zero.

A first integration of Eq. (
54
) yields

u^{\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{\lambda y}\left(1-\exp\left(\frac{\lambda y^{2}}{2\sigma}\right)\right).

Integrating again from a starting point
a
to threshold
T
gives:

u(a)=\int_{a}^{T}\frac{1}{\lambda y}\left(\exp\left(\frac{\lambda y^{2}}{2\sigma}\right)-1\right)dy.

(55)

In particular, when starting from
a=0
, we perform the change of variable
r=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}}y
to obtain:

u(0)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}}T}\frac{1}{r}\left(\exp(r^{2})-1\right)dr.

(56)

To analyze the behavior for small thresholds, we consider
T=\sqrt{g\sigma}
, where
g=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Expanding the exponential for small
r
gives:

\displaystyle u(0)\approx\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{g/2}}\frac{1}{r}\left(1+r^{2}+\frac{r^{4}}{2}+\cdots-1\right)dr\approx\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{g/2}}r\,dr=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\cdot\frac{g}{2}=\frac{g}{4\lambda}.

(57)

Thus, for
g=2
and
\lambda=1
, the MFPT is approximately
u(0)\approx 1/2s
. Due to the symmetry of the dynamics, the total expected time between the end of one spindle and the initiation of the next includes both the activation and return times. Hence, the mean inter-spindle interval is of order one, independent of the noise amplitude
\sigma
:

\bar{\tau}_{\text{inter-spindle}}\sim\mathcal{O}(1).

(58)

This analysis highlights that spindle generation, modeled as a threshold-crossing event by a noisy radial process, occurs on a timescale set by the system’s deterministic dynamics (e.g.,
\lambda
) rather than the noise amplitude itself. The exponential form of the potential prevents an arbitrarily long waiting time in low-noise regimes, contrasting with classical barrier crossing models where the mean exit time diverges exponentially as
\sigma\to 0
.

4.2
Mean First Passage Time for Spindle Waxing and Waning

To estimate the durations of the waxing (increasing) and waning (decreasing) phases of a spindle, we analyze the mean first passage time (MFPT) between amplitude thresholds. We assume that spindles initiate when the amplitude
r(t)
crosses a lower threshold
T_{\text{Down}}=\sqrt{2\sigma}
. As the maximum amplitude reached during a spindle is stochastic, we define the upper threshold
T_{\text{Up}}
using a quantile of the steady-state amplitude distribution:

\int_{0}^{T_{\text{Up}}}p(r),dr=1-\alpha,

(59)

where
\alpha\ll 1
. Using the distribution (
51
), this yields:

T_{\text{Up}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma}{\lambda}\log\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}.

(60)

In practice, a typical value is
\alpha=0.2
.

Waxing Phase.

To compute the mean duration of the waxing phase, we evaluate the MFPT from
T_{\text{Down}}
to
T_{\text{Up}}
using the MFPT equation (
54
) with boundary conditions:

u^{\prime}(T_{\text{Down}})=0,\quad u(T_{\text{Up}})=0.

(61)

Integrating the corresponding differential equation yields:

u_{1}^{\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{\lambda y}\left(1-\exp\left(\frac{\lambda y^{2}}{2\sigma}-\lambda\right)\right).

(62)

A second integration gives:

\displaystyle u_{1}(T_{\text{Down}})

\displaystyle=\int_{T_{\text{Down}}}^{T_{\text{Up}}}\frac{1}{\lambda y}\left(\exp\left(\frac{\lambda y^{2}}{2\sigma}-\lambda\right)-1\right)dy\

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\lambda}\int_{T_{\text{Down}}}^{T_{\text{Up}}}\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\lambda y^{2}}{2\sigma}\right)}{y}dy-\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{T_{\text{Down}}}^{T_{\text{Up}}}\frac{1}{y}dy.

Changing variables and evaluating asymptotically for
\alpha\ll 1
, we obtain:

u_{1}(T_{\text{Down}})\approx\frac{1}{2\lambda\alpha\log(1/\alpha)}e^{-\lambda}-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\log\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\right).

(63)

Waning Phase.

For the decreasing phase, we compute the MFPT from
T_{\text{Up}}
down to
T_{\text{Down}}
, solving the same MFPT equation (
54
) with boundary conditions:

u(T_{\text{Down}})=0,\quad u^{\prime}(T_{\text{Up}})=0.

(64)

The first integration yields:

u_{2}^{\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{\lambda y}\left(1-\alpha\exp\left(\frac{\lambda y^{2}}{2\sigma}\right)\right).

(65)

Integrating this expression:

\displaystyle u_{2}(T_{\text{Up}})

\displaystyle=\int_{T_{\text{Down}}}^{T_{\text{Up}}}\frac{1}{\lambda y}\left(1-\alpha\exp\left(\frac{\lambda y^{2}}{2\sigma}\right)\right)dy\

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{T_{\text{Down}}}^{T_{\text{Up}}}\frac{1}{y}dy-\frac{\alpha}{\lambda}\int_{T_{\text{Down}}}^{T_{\text{Up}}}\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\lambda y^{2}}{2\sigma}\right)}{y}dy.

For large
T_{\text{Up}}
(small
\alpha

u_{2}(T_{\text{Up}})\approx\frac{1}{2\lambda}\log\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\right)-\frac{1}{2\lambda\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}.

(66)

Total Spindle Duration.

The expected total duration of a spindle is given by the sum:

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}_{\text{Spindle}}

\displaystyle=u_{1}(T_{\text{Down}})+u_{2}(T_{\text{Up}})\approx\frac{1}{2\lambda\alpha\log(1/\alpha)}e^{-\lambda}-\frac{1}{2\lambda\log(1/\alpha)}.

(67)

These expressions quantify the inherent asymmetry in waxing and waning phases due to the nonlinearity of the potential and the shape of the amplitude distribution. As expected, higher spindle thresholds (i.e., smaller
\alpha
) lead to longer waxing durations, while the waning duration grows logarithmically in
\alpha^{-1}
.

4.6
Stability at the Interplay Between Deterministic and Stochastic Forces

We now investigate the mechanisms underlying the stability and shape of spindles generated by the two-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process. Specifically, we analyze how the interplay between deterministic drift and stochastic forcing leads to an alternation of increasing and decreasing phases in the envelope amplitude, thereby producing robust spindle-like oscillations. Our central observation is that consecutive monotonic increases or decreases in amplitude are statistically rare. Instead, the most probable trajectory involves a waxing phase followed by waning, reinforcing the notion of self-contained, transient spindles. The envelope amplitude
A(t)
evolves according to the stochastic differential equation:

\mathrm{d}A=-W^{\prime}(A),\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\sigma},\mathrm{d}w,

(93)

where the effective potential
W(A)
is given by:

W(A)=\frac{\lambda}{2}A^{2}-\sigma\log A.

(94)

This potential well exhibits a unique minimum at

A_{\text{min}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\lambda}},

(95)

which corresponds to the most probable amplitude value in the stationary regime. The concavity around this minimum ensures that deviations in amplitude are typically restored, favoring oscillatory behavior. To quantify the probability of successive amplitude increases (or decreases), we discretize Eq. (
93
) using an Euler–Maruyama scheme with fixed time step
\Delta t
:

\Delta A=-W^{\prime}(A)\Delta t+\sqrt{2\sigma\Delta t},\xi,

(96)

where
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a standard Gaussian random variable. This formulation allows us to analyze the sign of amplitude increments
\Delta A
over consecutive time steps.
We now consider two types of events:

(i)

Consecutive increases:
Two successive steps where
\Delta A_{1}>0
and
\Delta A_{2}>0
;

(ii)

Alternating behavior:
One increase followed by a decrease, or vice versa.

Since the deterministic drift
-W^{\prime}(A)
pulls the amplitude back toward the minimum
A_{\text{min}}
, the probability of two consecutive increases (or decreases) becomes significantly suppressed, especially when starting near
A_{\text{min}}
. This behavior creates a natural alternation between waxing and waning, stabilizing the spindle structure. We now consider the two cases where the steps are either consecutively increasing or decreasing.

4.6.1
Asymptotic computation for the number of event sustaining an increasing step

We consider a sequence of amplitude
A_{1},...,A_{n}
sampled from eq.(
96
) given a starting point
A_{0}
. We shall compute the asymptotic of probability for an increasing sequence event
I_{n}^{+}
: ”
A_{min}<A_{0}<...<A_{n}
” given by

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}(I_{n}^{+})=\hbox{\bb P}\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{k+1}-A_{k}>0\right).

(97)

The joint probability of
n
consecutive increments conditioned on the increment values
(A_{1},..A_{n})
is given

\displaystyle p\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{k+1}-A_{k}>0\mid A_{1},...,A_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}p(A_{k+1}-A_{k}>0\mid A_{k+1}),

(98)

where we used the independent property of the increments. Thus, using eq.(
96
), we have for
k\in\{0,...,n-1\}
,

\displaystyle p(A_{k+1}-A_{k}>0\mid A_{k+1})=p\left(\xi>\frac{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{\Delta t}}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\right)=\erfc\left(\frac{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{\Delta t}}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\right),

(99)

where
\erfc
is the complementary error function
[
1
]
.
In the small amplitude limit, we use the following expansion

\displaystyle\erfc(x)\approx\frac{\exp(-x^{2})}{x\sqrt{\pi}}\hbox{ for }x\gg 1,

(100)

thus, we have for
x=\frac{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{\Delta t}}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\gg 1
,

\displaystyle p(A_{k+1}-A_{k}>0\mid A_{k+1})\approx\frac{\sqrt{2\sigma}}{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{\pi\Delta t}}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{\Delta t}}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\right)^{2}\right).

(101)

Thus the conditional probability is

\displaystyle p\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{k+1}-A_{k}>0\mid A_{1},...,A_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2\sigma}{\pi\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\exp(-\frac{\Delta t}{2\sigma}W^{\prime}(A_{k})^{2})}{W^{\prime}(A_{k})}.

(102)

When sampling the distance from the steady-state distribution given by eq.(
51
), we have

\hbox{\bb P}(I_{n}^{+})=\int_{A_{0}}^{+\infty}...\int_{A_{n-1}}^{+\infty}p\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{k+1}-A_{k}>0\mid A_{1},...,A_{n}\right)p(A_{1})...p(A_{n})dA_{1}...dA_{n}\\
\approx\int_{A_{0}}^{+\infty}...\int_{A_{n-1}}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{{2\sigma}}{\pi\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta t}{2\sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\lambda A_{k}-\frac{\sigma}{A_{k}}\right)^{2}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{A_{k}\exp(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}A_{k}^{2})}{\lambda A_{k}-\frac{\sigma}{A_{k}}}dA_{1}...dA_{n}.

(103)

For
A_{0}
large enough, we also have that
\lambda A_{k}\gg\frac{\sigma}{A_{k}}
(i.e. the derivative of the potential is positive) and thus for all k
\geq
1, we have the following approximations

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta t}{2\sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\lambda A_{k}-\frac{\sigma}{A_{k}}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta t}{2\sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{A_{k}}{\lambda A_{k}-\frac{\sigma}{A_{k}}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}(I_{n}^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{\pi\sigma\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\int_{A_{0}}^{+\infty}...\int_{A_{n-1}}^{+\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta t}{2\sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}^{2}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}A_{k}^{2}\right)dA_{1}...dA_{n}

(104)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{2}}{\pi\sigma\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\int_{A_{0}}^{+\infty}...\int_{A_{n-1}}^{+\infty}\exp\left(-B\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}^{2}\right)dA_{1}...dA_{n},

where
B=\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta t+\lambda}{2\sigma}
. The last integral can be computed by induction as

\displaystyle\int_{A_{n-1}}^{+\infty}\exp(-BA_{n}^{2})dA_{n}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\erfc(\sqrt{B}A_{n-1})}{2\sqrt{B}}

(105)

\displaystyle\int_{A_{n-2}}^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\erfc(\sqrt{B}A_{n-1})}{2\sqrt{B}}\exp(-BA_{n-1}^{2})dA_{n-1}=\frac{\pi\erfc(\sqrt{B}A_{n-2})^{2}}{8B}.

(106)

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{A_{0}}^{\infty}...\int_{A_{n-1}}^{\infty}\exp\left(-B\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}^{2}\right)dA_{1}..dA_{n}=\frac{\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}\erfc(\sqrt{B}A_{0})^{n}}{2^{n}n!B^{\frac{n}{2}}}.

(107)

Finally, we conclude that the asymptotic probability of a n-consecutive increasing increment is given

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}(I_{n}^{+})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{2}}{\pi\sigma\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\pi^{n/2}\erfc(\sqrt{B}A_{0})^{n}}{2^{n}n!B^{\frac{n}{2}}}\approx\left(\frac{\erfc(\sqrt{B}A_{0})}{\sqrt{2\sigma B\Delta t}}\right)^{n}\frac{1}{n!},

(108)

which tends to zero as
n
tends to infinity for any fixed
\Delta t
and initial position
A_{0}>A_{min}
(minimum of the potential well). We conclude that a consecutive increasing sequence of n terms is exponentially small with n, which guarrantees that the stochastic event to reach
n
consecutive increments as
n
increases is very unlikely.

4.6.2
Asymptotic formula for number of eventa sustaining a decreasing step

In the case of a decreasing sequence, with
0<A_{n}<..<A_{0}
, a similar approach can be used to show that the asymptotic probability of such sequence becomes small as
n
increases. Following similar steps as in the previous paragraph, the conditional probability of a decrement is

\displaystyle p(A_{k+1}-A_{k}<0\mid A_{k+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\left(\xi_{k}<\frac{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{\Delta t}}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\erf\left(\frac{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{\Delta t}}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\right)\right),

(109)

where
W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})<0
. Thus, for
\frac{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{\Delta t}}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\ll-1
, we get the asymptotic expression

\displaystyle p(A_{k+1}-A_{k}<0\mid A_{k+1})\approx-\frac{\sqrt{\sigma}}{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t}}\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta t}{2\sigma}W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})^{2}\right)

(110)

Thus the conditional probability is now given by

\displaystyle p\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{k+1}-A_{k}<0\mid A_{1},...,A_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}p(A_{k+1}-A_{k}<0\mid A_{k+1})

(111)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sigma}{2\pi\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta t}{2\sigma}W^{\prime}(A_{k})^{2}\right)}{-W^{\prime}(A_{k})}.

The asymptotic probability of
I_{n}^{-}
: ”n consecutive decrements” when the distribution of distance is sampled from steady-state distributions is then given by

\hbox{\bb P}(I_{n}^{-})=\int_{0}^{A_{0}}...\int_{0}^{A_{n-1}}p\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{k+1}-A_{k}<0\mid A_{1},...,A_{n}\right)p(A_{1})..p(A_{n})dA_{1}..dA_{n}\\
\approx\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{\sigma}{2\pi\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\int_{0}^{A_{0}}...\int_{0}^{A_{n-1}}\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta t}{2\sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\lambda A_{k}-\frac{\sigma}{A_{k}}\right)^{2}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{A_{k}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}A_{k}^{2}\right)}{-\lambda A_{k}+\frac{\sigma}{A_{k}}}dA_{1}...dA_{n}.

(112)

For
A_{0}
small enough so that
A_{k}\ll 1
for all k
\geq
1, we used the following approximations

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta t}{2\sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\lambda A_{k}-\frac{\sigma}{A_{k}}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma\Delta t}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{A_{k}^{2}}\right)

(113)

\displaystyle\frac{A_{k}}{-\lambda A_{k}+\frac{\sigma}{A_{k}}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{A^{2}_{k}}{\sigma},

(114)

We set
C=\frac{\sigma\Delta t}{2}
and integrate by parts the last integral giving

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A_{n-1}}\exp\left(-\frac{C}{A_{n}^{2}}\right)A_{n}^{2}dA_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C^{\frac{3}{2}}\int_{\frac{\sqrt{C}}{A_{n-1}}}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(-u^{2})}{u^{4}}du

(115)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2C}\exp\left(-\frac{C}{A_{n-1}^{2}}\right)A_{n-1}^{5},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2C}\int_{0}^{A_{n-2}}\exp\left(-\frac{2C}{A_{n-1}^{2}}\right)A_{n-1}^{7}dA_{n-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2C)^{3}\int_{\frac{\sqrt{2C}}{A_{n-2}}}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(-u^{2})}{u^{9}}du

(116)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(2C)^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{2C}{A_{n-2}^{2}}\right)A_{n-2}^{10}.

Thus, we obtain by induction the general formula

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A_{0}}...\int_{0}^{A_{n-1}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma\Delta t}{2A_{k}^{2}}\right)A_{k}^{2}dA_{k}=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!C^{n}}\exp\left(-\frac{nC}{A_{0}^{2}}\right)A_{0}^{5n}.

(117)

Finally,

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}(I_{n}^{-})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sigma}{2\pi\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sigma^{3}\Delta t}\right)^{n}\frac{1}{n!}\exp\left(-\frac{n\sigma\Delta t}{2A_{0}^{2}}\right)A_{0}^{5n}.

(118)

which tends to zero as
n
increases. To conclude, since the asymptotic probability to have a strictly increasing or decreasing sequence becomes small as
n
increases, most events consist of a few consecutive increasing and decreasing epochs, thus preventing the spindle envelope to grow infinitely. It is interesting to note the asymmetry between the increasing and decreasing cases.

4.7
Mean number of consecutive increments

To determine the mean time of an increasing sequence starting at an amplitude
A_{0}
, we use a mean first passage approach to define the last increasing step as

\displaystyle\tilde{n}=\inf\{n\hbox{ such that }A_{q+1}-A_{q}>0,q\in\{0,..,n-1\}\hbox{ and }A_{n+1}-A_{n}<0\}.

(119)

We are interested in computing the expectation

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb E}(\tilde{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\hbox{\bb P}(\tilde{n}=n),

(120)

where
\hbox{\bb P}(\tilde{n}=n)
is the probability that there are exactly
n
consecutive increments. We can express the probability to have exactly n jumps as the average over all configuration where
A_{q+1}>A_{q}
, for all possibility when starting at
A_{0}>A_{min}
given the steady-state

\hbox{\bb P}(\tilde{n}=n)=\int_{A_{0}}^{\infty}...\int_{A_{n-1}}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A_{n}}p(A_{n+1}-A_{n}<0\mid A_{n})p(A_{n+1})\prod_{k=1}^{n}p(A_{k+1}-A_{k}>0\mid A_{k})p(A_{k})dA_{1}...dA_{n+1}.

(121)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A_{n}}p(A_{n+1})p(A_{n+1}-A_{n}>0\mid A_{n})dA_{n+1}=I_{1}+I_{2}(A_{n})

(122)

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A_{min}}p(A_{n+1}-A_{n}>0\mid A_{n})p(A_{n+1})dA_{n+1}

(123)

\displaystyle I_{2}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{A_{min}}^{A_{n}}p(A_{n+1}-A_{n}>0\mid A_{n})p(A_{n+1})dA_{n+1}.

(124)

In the first interval, the potential is negative and the other one positive.

\displaystyle I_{1}(A_{n})=\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{A_{min}}p\left(\xi_{k}<\frac{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{\Delta t}}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\right)A_{n+1}\exp(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}A_{n+1}^{2})dA_{n+1}.

(125)

In the interval
[0,A_{min}]
,
W^{\prime}<0
. We will make the approximation of
W^{\prime}(A)\sim-\frac{\sigma}{A}
so that

\displaystyle I_{1}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sigma}{2\pi\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{A_{min}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma\Delta t}{2A_{n}^{2}}\right)A_{n+1}^{2}\exp(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}A_{n+1}^{2})dA_{n+1}

(126)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sigma}{2\pi\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{A_{min}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma\Delta t}{2A_{n}^{2}}\right)A_{n+1}^{2}(1+O(1))dA_{n+1}

(127)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sigma}{2\pi\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\lambda}{\sigma^{2}\Delta t}\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma}{2\pi\Delta tA_{min}^{2}}\right)A_{min}^{5}

(128)

Similarly,

\displaystyle I_{2}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{A_{min}}^{A_{n}}p(A_{n+1}-A_{n}>0\mid A_{n})p(A_{n+1})dA_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}\int_{A_{min}}^{A_{n}}p\left(\xi_{k}<\frac{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{\Delta t}}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\right)A_{n+1}\exp(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}A_{n+1}^{2})dA_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}\int_{A_{min}}^{A_{n}}\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\erf\left(\frac{W^{\prime}(A_{k+1})\sqrt{\Delta t}}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\right)\right)A_{n+1}\exp(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}A_{n+1}^{2})dA_{n+1}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}A_{min}^{2})-\exp(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}A_{n}^{2})\approx\exp(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}A_{min}^{2})

where we approximated the erf function to 1. Thus we obtain for the integral:

\hbox{\bb P}(\tilde{n}=n)=\int_{A_{0}}^{\infty}...\int_{A_{n-1}}^{\infty}(C_{\min}\prod_{k=1}^{n}p(A_{k+1}-A_{k}>0\mid A_{k})p(A_{k})dA_{1}...dA_{n+1},

(129)

where

C_{\min}=\left(\frac{\sigma}{2\pi\Delta t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\lambda}{\sigma^{2}\Delta t}\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma}{2\pi\Delta tA_{min}^{2}}\right)A_{min}^{5}+(\exp(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma}A_{min}^{2}).

Finally,

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}(\tilde{n}=n)=C_{\min}\left(\frac{\erfc(\sqrt{B}A_{0})}{\sqrt{2\sigma B\Delta t}}\right)^{n}\frac{1}{n!}.

(130)

We conclude that the mean number of increments is given by

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb E}(\tilde{n}|A_{0})\approx C_{\min}(\exp(\frac{\erfc(\sqrt{B}A_{0})}{\sqrt{2\sigma B\Delta t}})-1)\frac{\erfc(\sqrt{B}A_{0})}{\sqrt{2\sigma B\Delta t}},

(131)

where we recall
B=\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta t+\lambda}{2\sigma}
.
Finally, it would be interesting to compute the number of decreasing steps as:

\displaystyle\tilde{m}=\inf\{m\hbox{ such that }A_{m+1}-A_{m}>0\hbox{ and }A_{q+1}-A_{q}<0,q\in\{0,..,n-1\}\}.

(132)

the mean number of steps is given by the expectation
\hbox{\bb E}(\tilde{m})=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m\hbox{\bb P}(\tilde{m}=m)
.

4.8
A general formula for the distribution of spindle amplitude

We propose here to derive a general expression for the distribution of maximum spindle amplitude. This is in principle an uneasy task to compute as computing such a distribution requires segmenting each spindle and identifying the maximum. Instead we shall compute the distribution of maximum of the radius in a spindle time interval. Due to the independent property of spindles, we will be interested in computing the probability that the maximum hits level u when starting at height
x

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}\left(\max_{s\in[0,t]}A(s)=u\mid x>0\right).

(133)

When conditioning this process on the duration
t_{s}
of the spindle, we get

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}\left(\max_{s\in[0,t_{s}]}A(s)=u\mid t_{s}=t,x\right).

(134)

so that by Bayes’ law,

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}\left(A_{max}=u\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hbox{\bb P}\left(\max_{s\in[0,t_{s}]}A(s)=u\mid t_{s}=t,x\right)\hbox{\bb P}\left(t_{s}=t\right)dt,

(135)

where
\hbox{\bb P}\left(t_{s}=t\right)
is the probability that the duration of a spindle is equal to t, that we show to be a degenerated difference of Poisson process given by

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}\left(t_{s}=t\right)=\lambda^{2}t\exp(-\lambda t),

(136)

where we consider here that the mean increasing and decreasing time are identical, thus
\lambda=\lambda_{inc}=\lambda_{dec}
. Moreover, the probability
\hbox{\bb P}\left(\max_{s\in[0,t_{s}]}A(s)<u\mid t_{s}=t\right)
is the survival of the process inside the interval
[0,u]
and can be computed from the backward Fokker-Planck equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon x}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}p(x,t)+\kappa(\theta-x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}p(x,t)

\displaystyle p(x,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(x-y)

(137)

\displaystyle p(u,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

We recall that this process has not reached the level
u
is equivalent to the hitting time being less than
t
when the process starts at
x
, thus

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}(\tau^{u}<t\mid x)=\hbox{\bb P}\left(\max_{s\in[0,t_{s}]}A(s)\leq u\mid t_{s}=t,x\right).

(138)

We get directly from eq.(
85
) the pdf of the time, condition on the initial value
x

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}(\tau^{u}=t\mid x)=\kappa\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp(-\lambda_{n}(u)t)\frac{{}_{1}F_{1}(a_{n};1;\frac{2\kappa u}{\varepsilon}x)}{\frac{\partial{}_{1}F_{1}}{\partial a}(a;1;\frac{2\kappa u}{\varepsilon})|_{a=a_{n}}}.

(139)

where the spectrum
\lambda_{n}(u)
is given by the transcendental equation with
a_{n}=-\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\kappa}

\displaystyle{}_{1}F_{1}(a_{n};1;\frac{2\kappa u}{\varepsilon})=0,

(140)

leading to
\lambda_{n}(u)
, n=1…
Thus, we obtain from relation (
135
),

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb P}(A_{max}=u\mid x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\hbox{\bb P}\left(\max_{s\in[0,t_{s}]}A(s)=u\mid t_{s}=t\right)\hbox{\bb P}\left(t_{s}=t\right)dt

(141)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda_{n}(u)+\lambda)^{2}}\frac{{}_{1}F_{1}(a_{n};1;\frac{2\kappa u}{\varepsilon}x)}{\frac{\partial{}_{1}F_{1}}{\partial a}(a;1;\frac{2\kappa u}{\varepsilon})|_{a=a_{n}}}.

It would be interesting to obtain a simplified expression and a final expression for the probability of the maximum with respect to the parameter of the Ornstein-Ulhembeck process.

5
Statistical properties of spindles generated by the OU process

In this section, we analyze the statistical properties of spindles generated by the two-dimensional OU-process that we first segmented using the method presented above (section
3.1
). our goal here is to determine the distribution of their total duration, as well as the one for the increasing phase (waxing) and decreasing phase (waning). The result are presented in Fig.
5
.

We recall the two phases: the increasing phase starts at a time
t_{j}
and ends when the process has reached a maximum amplitude
A_{j}
, lasting
\Delta_{1}
. This phase is followed by a decreasing phase starting from the maximum to the next minimum
t_{j+1}
, lasting
\Delta_{2}
(Fig.
5
A). The total spindle duration is thus the sum
\Delta=\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}
.

The statistics revealed from the stochastic simulations are presented in Fig.
5
B-C, where the increasing and decreasing phase duration distributions can be each well approximated by a single exponential curve (as discussed in section
4.4
). We thus fitted the exponential function
f_{\Delta_{1}}(t)=\lambda_{1}\exp(-\lambda_{1}t)
and
f_{\Delta_{2}}(t)=\lambda_{2}\exp(-\lambda_{2}t)
, where the optimal fits lead to
\lambda_{1}=0.54\pm 0.18s^{-1}
(
R^{2}=0.62
) and
\lambda_{2}=0.51\pm 0.16s^{-1}
(
R^{2}=0.73
). Finally, we fitted the spindle total duration by an Erlang-2 distribution
f_{\Delta}(t)=\lambda^{2}t\exp(-\lambda t)
, this fit is based on the result that the increasing and decreasing phase duration are quite similar, as we found above. The optimal fit (Fig.
5
D) yielded
\lambda=0.67\pm 0.07s^{-1}
(
R^{2}=0.91
).

We further investigated the distribution of suppression duration
\Delta_{sup}
, which is defined as the absence of spindle above the threshold
T_{down}
. We fitted a single exponential
[
30
]
leading to the estimated rate constant
\lambda_{sup}=0.76\pm 0.07s^{-1}
(Fig.
5
E). Interestingly, this mean expectation is in agreement with the estimation of the mean first passage time that we derived in subsection
4.1
.

Finally, the distribution of spindles maximum amplitude
A
computed over each spindle (Fig.
5
F) can be fitted by a Rayleigh distribution, which is the steady-state distribution that we obtained from the solution of the Fokker-Plank equation (
51
). The Rayleigh distribution is the solution of the distribution of the maximum of the envelope distance
|x_{up}-x_{down}|
that we computed from the simulation of the stochastic eq.
1
, where the optimal fits lead to
b_{A}=4.7\pm 0.3
and
b_{env}=5.3\pm 0.1
.

To conclude, we showed that the spindle
generated by the OU process can be segmented using the increasing and decreasing phase of the envelope. The increasing phase can be interpreted as driven by noise to a random maximum distance of the variable
s
, while the decreasing phase could be interpreted as a relaxation to a region where the amplitude size reaches the lower threshold
T_{down}
. We showed that the duration of increasing and decreasing phases are well approximated by single exponential distribution. We proposed a series formula for the distribution of the spindle maximum amplitude, but we could not find a closed form. However, the Rayleigh distribution seemed to be a good approximation.

6
Coupling Two OU Processes to Generate Spindles of Mixed Frequencies

To account for the diversity of spindle types observed in neural recordings—particularly with respect to their frequency content—we consider coupling multiple two-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) processes. In this section, we analyze how coupling two such processes, each tuned to a distinct frequency, can generate spindles exhibiting multiple dominant frequency bands.
Specifically, we couple two two-dimensional stochastic foci with resonance frequencies
\omega_{1}=2\pi f_{1}
and
\omega_{2}=2\pi f_{2}
. The coupling is made by a small parameter
\epsilon
as followed:

\displaystyle\dot{X_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda X_{1}+\omega_{1}Y_{1}+\epsilon X_{2}+\sqrt{2\sigma_{1}}\dot{w_{1}}

\displaystyle\dot{Y_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\omega_{1}X_{1}-\lambda Y_{1}+\sqrt{2\sigma_{1}}\dot{w_{2}}

(142)

\displaystyle\dot{X_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda X_{2}+\omega_{2}Y_{2}+\sqrt{2\sigma_{2}}\dot{w_{3}}

\displaystyle\dot{Y_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\omega_{2}X_{2}-\lambda Y_{2}+\sqrt{2\sigma_{2}}\dot{w_{4}},

where
w_{i}
are Brownian motions and
\lambda
is the decaying parameter.

A representative realization of this coupled system is shown in Fig.
6

shows two peaks (Fig.

At this stage, we identified three types of spindles as shown in Fig.

\text{min}(f_{1},f_{2})
), fast (F) (
\text{max}(f_{1},f_{2})
) and mixed (M) which contain approximately the same proportion of both frequencies. We classified spindles according to the following criteria. We label a spindle slow when more than 60% of the instantaneous frequency is less than 10 Hz, fast when more than 60% of the instantaneous frequency is greater than 10 Hz. Finally, it is considered mixed when both slow and fast frequency proportion is between 40% and 60% (Fig.
6
C).

Moreover, to investigate whether a spindle sequence could carry information (Fig.
6
E), we estimated the spindle type sequence for a fixed value of
\varepsilon
. To evaluate the randomness of the three-valued
(S,F,M)
spindle sequence, we use the Wald-Wolfowitz runs test
[
5
,
32
]
. A run is defined as a consecutive sequence of identical values. The null hypothesis
H_{0}
assumes that the sequence is random. The number of runs under H0 can be approximated by the expected number of runs given by

\displaystyle\hbox{\bb E}(R)=\frac{2n_{S}n_{F}}{n}+\frac{2n_{S}n_{M}}{n}+\frac{2n_{F}n_{M}}{n}+1

(143)

where
n_{S}
,
n_{F}
, and
n_{M}
are the counts of the slow, fast and mixed spindles and
n=n_{A}+n_{B}+n_{C}
is the total number of spindles. The variance of the number of runs is calculated as

\displaystyle\Var(R)=\frac{2n_{S}n_{F}(2n_{S}n_{F}-n)}{n^{2}(n-1)}+\frac{2n_{S}n_{M}(2n_{S}n_{M}-n)}{n^{2}(n-1)}+\frac{2n_{F}n_{M}(2n_{F}n_{M}-n)}{n^{2}(n-1)},

(144)

and the test statistic
Z
is

\displaystyle Z=\frac{R-E(R)}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(R)}}

(145)

where
R
is the observed number of runs. The value of
Z
is compared to critical values from the standard normal distribution. If
|Z|
exceeds the critical value 1.96 for
\alpha=0.05
, H0 is rejected, indicating that the sequence is not random.

We performed the runs test on 50 coupled OU process simulations of duration 20 minutes. We found that at least 90% of trials were not rejected for a large range of
\varepsilon
values (Fig.
6
E upper). These findings support the hypothesis that the sequence of spindle types—slow, fast, and mixed—emerges in a statistically random order for a broad range of coupling strengths
\epsilon
ans thus no physiological information should be expected in the EEG signal when extracting the spindle order.

Finally, we calculated the proportion of each spindle type
p_{A}
for
A\in{S,F,M}
as the ratio
n_{A}/n
. By running simulations with varying values of the coupling strength
\epsilon
, we found that the proportion of slow, mixed and fast spindles depends strongly on
\varepsilon
(Fig.
6
E lower). Indeed, the proportion of slow spindles significantly decreased when the coupling strength
\varepsilon
increases.

To conclude, by systematically varying the coupling strength
\\
epsilon
, we observed a pronounced shift in the distribution of spindle types. In the weak coupling regime (
\epsilon\approx 0
), spindles were primarily slow, reflecting the dominance of the lower frequency oscillator. As
\epsilon
increased, the fraction of mixed-frequency spindles grew, while the proportion of slow spindles declined markedly. This transition illustrates how increasing interaction between the two oscillators allows the higher frequency component to influence the output, thereby broadening the frequency content of individual spindles. At strong coupling, fast spindles dominate, confirming that coupling strength modulates the richness of frequency expression in the model. Interesting, mixed spindles seems to saturate already for
\epsilon=10
, suggesting by further increasing
\varepsilon
could have no further effect.

These results suggest a potential mechanism for frequency blending in biological systems, where weakly interacting oscillatory populations can give rise to diverse, stochastic, yet physiologically plausible spindle events.
To conclude, this analysis revealed that coupling stochastic focus can generate randomly a variety of spindles containing multiple frequencies, however, we expect no specific physiological significance of their order based on the present stochastic model. It would be interesting to generalize the present model by adding a time dependent coupling that could reflect the possible change of interaction between different frequency bands.

Discussion and Open Problems

In this work, we presented a minimal yet analytically tractable stochastic model for generating spindle-like events—transient bursts of oscillatory activity characterized by distinct waxing (increasing) and waning (decreasing) phases. We showed that such events emerge from a two-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process, where rotational drift and stochastic forcing interact to produce repeated excursions from a stable focus. We developed a method to segment these spindles and characterize their statistical properties, including the durations of their ascent and descent phases, the amplitude of peak oscillations, and the inter-spindle intervals.

We also extended the framework by coupling multiple OU processes with different intrinsic frequencies. This leads to the emergence of composite or mixed-frequency spindles, providing a mechanism to model more complex temporal patterns observed in empirical data.

Our model exhibits striking similarities to spindles found in electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings, particularly in the
\alpha
-band (8–12 Hz), which is prominent during general anesthesia, stage 2 non-REM (NREM) sleep, quiet wakefulness, and meditative states
[
25
]
. Traditionally, the
\alpha
-band appears in spectrograms as a continuous frequency trace, but closer inspection—such as illustrated in Fig.
1
A–B—reveals it is composed of successive, non-overlapping spindle events.

The current framework can be further generalized to model richer EEG spindle phenomena. For instance, by incorporating multiple coupled oscillatory modes, one may explore the coexistence and interaction of fast (
>
13 Hz) and slow (
<
13 Hz) spindles, which are notably observed during stage 2 NREM sleep
[
26
]
. These spindles are region-specific—slow spindles are more prominent in frontal cortical regions, while fast spindles tend to occur more posteriorly—and are believed to result from interactions between the thalamic reticular nucleus, the thalamus, and the neocortex. Functionally, spindles have been implicated in sensory disconnection during sleep, consolidation of learning and memory, and motor planning.

Despite the progress offered by our model, several computational and segmentation questions remain open:

1.

Spindle statistics as biomarkers:
Can deviations in the statistical distributions of spindle properties (e.g., durations, amplitudes, inter-spindle intervals) indicate physiological transitions, such as the onset of sleep stages, emergence from anesthesia, or pathological states?

2.

Spectral bandwidth from coupling:
How does coupling between multiple OU processes affect the effective width and shape of the frequency spectrum? Can one derive closed-form expressions for the power spectral density and its bandwidth in the case of arbitrary coupling?

3.

Distribution of spindle amplitudes:
While we have characterized mean amplitudes, is there a closed-form expression (or asymptotic estimate) for the full probability distribution of the maximum spindle amplitude, and how does it depend on system parameters such as noise intensity and damping?

4.

Detection robustness:
How sensitive is the EMD-based segmentation algorithm to changes in noise level, sampling rate, and the presence of other oscillatory components? Can the algorithm be adapted for real-time detection or used in closed-loop control systems?

5.

Extension to non-Gaussian noise:
Can this framework be generalized to include non-Gaussian perturbations (e.g., Lévy noise or state-dependent diffusion) to model heavy-tailed statistics observed in certain neural systems?

Addressing these problems would deepen our understanding of the stochastic origins of transient neural oscillations and may open new avenues for modeling, detection, and control in neuroscience and clinical engineering.

Appendix A: Autocorrelation Function of the OU Process

In this appendix, we compute the autocorrelation function
C_{xx}(t,s)
for the first component
x(t)
of the two-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process. The stochastic differential equation is given by

\dot{\boldsymbol{s}}=A\boldsymbol{s}+\Sigma\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}},

(146)

where
\boldsymbol{s}=(x,y)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the state vector,
A
is the drift matrix (defined in Eq. (
1
)), and
\Sigma=\sqrt{2\sigma}\,I_{2}
is the noise intensity matrix. The noise term
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=(\dot{\omega}_{1},\dot{\omega}_{2})^{T}
consists of two independent white noise processes. Assuming the process has zero mean
\mathbb{E}[x(t)]=0
, the autocorrelation function is defined as
[
16
,
30
]

C_{xx}(t,s)=\mathbb{E}[x(t)x(s)].

(147)

The solution to Eq. (
146
) can be written as

\boldsymbol{s}(t)=\exp(tA)\boldsymbol{s}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\exp((t-s)A)\Sigma\,d\boldsymbol{\omega}_{s},

(148)

where the second term is a stochastic convolution. Taking expectations, we obtain
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{s}(t)]=\exp(tA)\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
, which vanishes in the stationary limit. The covariance matrix of the process is given by

C(t,s)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\exp((t-s_{2})A)\Sigma\Sigma^{T}\exp((s-s_{1})A^{T})\,d\boldsymbol{\omega}(s_{1})\,d\boldsymbol{\omega}(s_{2})\right].

(149)

Using the
\delta
-correlation property of white noise, this simplifies to

\displaystyle C(t,s)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\min(t,s)}\exp(A(t-t^{\prime}))\Sigma\Sigma^{T}\exp(A^{T}(s-t^{\prime}))\,dt^{\prime}

(150)

\displaystyle=2\sigma\int_{-\infty}^{\min(t,s)}\exp(A(t-t^{\prime}))\exp(A^{T}(s-t^{\prime}))\,dt^{\prime}.

Next, we decompose the drift matrix
A
as

A=-\lambda I_{2}+\omega J_{2},\quad\text{where}\quad J_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

(151)

so that the exponential of
A
is given by

\displaystyle\exp(tA)

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda t}\left[\cos(\omega t)I_{2}+\sin(\omega t)J_{2}\right],

(152)

\displaystyle\exp(tA^{T})

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda t}\left[\cos(\omega t)I_{2}+\sin(\omega t)J_{2}^{T}\right].

(153)

Using
\tau=t-s
, we distinguish two cases.

Case 1:
\tau>0
.

Then, the correlation becomes

\displaystyle C(t,s)

\displaystyle=2\sigma\int_{-\infty}^{s}e^{-2\lambda(s-t^{\prime})}e^{-\lambda\tau}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\omega\tau)&-\sin(\omega\tau)\\
\sin(\omega\tau)&\cos(\omega\tau)\\
\end{pmatrix}dt^{\prime}

(154)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma}{\lambda}e^{-\lambda\tau}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\omega\tau)&-\sin(\omega\tau)\\
\sin(\omega\tau)&\cos(\omega\tau)\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(155)

Case 2:
\tau<0
.

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle C(t,s)

\displaystyle=2\sigma\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-2\lambda(t-t^{\prime})}e^{\lambda\tau}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\omega\tau)&-\sin(\omega\tau)\\
\sin(\omega\tau)&\cos(\omega\tau)\\
\end{pmatrix}dt^{\prime}

(156)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma}{\lambda}e^{\lambda\tau}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\omega\tau)&-\sin(\omega\tau)\\
\sin(\omega\tau)&\cos(\omega\tau)\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(157)

Combining both cases, the autocorrelation matrix for any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
becomes

C(\tau)=\frac{\sigma}{\lambda}e^{-\lambda|\tau|}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\omega\tau)&-\sin(\omega\tau)\\
\sin(\omega\tau)&\cos(\omega\tau)\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(158)

Component-wise autocorrelation.

Extracting the
(1,1)
component, we obtain the autocorrelation function for the first coordinate
x(t)
:

C_{xx}(\tau)=\frac{\sigma}{\lambda}e^{-\lambda|\tau|}\cos(\omega\tau).

(159)

Power spectral density.

Taking the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function (
159
), we obtain the power spectral density (PSD) of
x(t)
:

S(f)=\frac{\sigma}{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+(\omega-2\pi f)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+(\omega+2\pi f)^{2}}\right).

(160)

This expression reveals that the power spectrum is sharply peaked at the resonant frequency
f=\omega/2\pi
, and its width is governed by the damping parameter
\lambda
. This relationship can be exploited to estimate
\lambda
from data, once
\omega
and
\sigma
are known or independently estimated.

In this appendix, we compute the autocorrelation function and the power spectral density (PSD) of the first variable

System of Equations.

The coupled system is described by the following stochastic differential equations:

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\lambda X_{1}+\omega_{1}Y_{1}+\epsilon X_{2}+\sqrt{2\sigma_{1}}\,\dot{w}_{1},

(161)

\displaystyle\dot{Y}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{1}X_{1}-\lambda Y_{1}+\sqrt{2\sigma_{1}}\,\dot{w}_{2},

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\lambda X_{2}+\omega_{2}Y_{2}+\sqrt{2\sigma_{2}}\,\dot{w}_{3},

(163)

\displaystyle\dot{Y}_{2}

(164)

where

and

0&0&0&\omega_{2}\\

0&0&0&0\\

\displaystyle e^{\mathbf{A}x}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}e^{-\lambda x}\cos(x\omega_{1})&e^{-\lambda x}\sin(x\omega_{1})&\frac{\displaystyle{\epsilon}e^{-\lambda x}(\omega_{2}\sin(\omega_{2}x)-\omega_{1}\sin(x\omega_{1}))}{\omega_{2}^{2}-\omega_{1}^{2}}&-\frac{\displaystyle{\omega}_{2}\epsilon e^{-\lambda x}(\cos(\omega_{2}x)-\cos(x\omega_{1}))}{\omega_{2}^{2}-\omega_{1}^{2}}\\

We can now compute

\displaystyle e^{\mathbf{A}^{T}x}=(e^{\mathbf{A}x})^{T}

(168)

The autocorrelation matrix is given by:

(1,1)

Conclusion.

The autocorrelation function and PSD of the first oscillator are analytically tractable for both general and resonant regimes. In the resonant case
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}
, the effect of coupling
\epsilon
is amplified, leading to enhanced energy transfer and spectral power near the shared frequency
\omega
. This highlights the importance of frequency detuning and damping in controlling burst synchrony and energy distribution in weakly coupled stochastic oscillators. The analytical expression derived above are used to fit the PSD generated by the simulations of the coupled OU-systems.
