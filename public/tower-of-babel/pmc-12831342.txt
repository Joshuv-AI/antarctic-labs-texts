Guanylate-binding proteins score and a CBFA2T3-included risk model define immune microenvironment and chemosensitivity in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Zhenliang Shi, Yimeng Shen, Xin Liu, Sipei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGuanylate-binding proteins (GBPs) induced by interferons play a crucial role in inflammatory signaling. This study aimed to investigate their anti-cancer properties in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD).MethodSeven GBPs genes were obtained from the TCGA-LUAD and GSE31210 datasets. The GBPs score was computed for each patient using ssGSEA, followed by conducting WGCNA. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with the Limma package, and further refined by univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to develop a risk score model. Kaplan-Meier survival and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed with the survminer and timeROC packages, respectively. Immune microenvironment was profiled using ESTIMATE and ssGSEA algorithms, while the pRRophetic package was applied to predict chemotherapeutic sensitivity. Relevant signaling pathways were identified by GSEA. Finally, the functional roles of key genes were experimentally validated using CCK-8, wound healing, and Transwell assays.ResultsThe GBPs score was significantly higher in para-carcinoma tissues. The green-yellow module (β = 7), which contained 422 genes, was closely associated with the GBPs score. Intersection of this module with the DEGs yielded 92 overlapping candidates. A 4-gene risk model constructed from Cox regression demonstrated high predictive accuracy for 1-, 3-, and 5 year Overall Survival. Patients were assigned by the median risk score into low- and high-risk groups, with low-risk patients showing better prognostic outcomes and higher immune infiltration scores, particularly of CD4+ T cells and activated B cells. A nomogram combining multiple clinical factors exhibited robust prognostic performance. We also further identified potential drugs for treating different risk groups of LUAD. Pathways such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) and Myc target V2 were enriched in high-risk groups, suggesting potential targets for immunotherapy. In vitro assays confirmed that knockdown of the model gene CBFA2T3 significantly suppressed the migratory and invasive capacities of A549 and NCI-H838 cells.ConclusionWe analyzed the GBPs phenotype associated with LUAD progression and developed a risk score model for patient prognosis. This finding provided a reference model for clinically assessing patient prognosis, contributing to personalized treatment for LUAD patients.

Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) originates from lung epithelial cells [1, 2] and accounts for approximately 40% of all cases of lung cancer [3, 4]. The progression of LUAD is influenced by multiple risk factors, such as occupational exposure to asbestos, smoking, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and air pollution [5, 6]. Despite advancements in the treatment of LUAD [7, 8], the 5-year survival rate remains below 20% [9]. This is largely attributable to late diagnosis, acquired resistance, and ineffectiveness of the curative option [10]. As a result, discovering new therapeutic biomarkers and uncovering their molecular mechanisms are essential for improving the clinical outcomes for LUAD patients [11, 12].

Interferons could be activated by viral or microbial infections to stimulate large-scale antiviral or antimicrobial gene expression [13]. Among them, guanylate-binding proteins (GBPs), a subgroup of the guanosine triphosphatase (GTPase) family, represent some of the most high-expressed genes. They are notably upregulated by IFN-γ and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-1α, and function through interacting with guanine nucleotides to promote inflammasome signaling [14]. Mechanistically, it has been reported that GBPs catalyze GDP-to-GMP conversion or form higher-order oligomers with guanosine- triphosphate (GTP), GMP, and GDP [15]. GBPs enhance the LPS or DNA ligands of microbes, activating pyroptosis [16], whereas GBP5 regulates NLRP3 activation by the caspase-11-driven outer membrane vesicle response to LPS, soluble inflammasome priming agents, and pathogenic bacteria [17, 18]. Apart from pathogenic defense, GBPs are also involved in tumor cell adhesion, proliferation, and migration [19]. Chronic inflammation and increased proinflammatory cytokine could enhance the production of GBPs. Numerous studies have confirmed that members of the GBP family function crucially in LUAD. Specifically, GBP1 can enhance the invasiveness of LUAD by promoting cell motility [20]; GBP3 is high-expressed in lung squamous cell carcinoma [21]; GBP2 can exacerbate sepsis-induced acute lung injury [22]. Our study therefore sought to construct a GBPs-related model to provide a foundational framework for future mechanistic and translational research.

This study calculated the GBPs score for LUAD patients and systematically assessed the prognostic value of the GBPs risk model. Notably, para-carcinoma tissues had higher GBPs scores than the tumor tissue, indicating that these GBPs may affect LUAD progression. GBPs score-related gene modules were classified using WGCNA. Subsequently, 92 overlapping genes between DEGs and WGCNA module were selected. Finally, high- and low- risk LUAD groups classified by the risk score model exhibited distinct differences in prognostic differences, immune infiltration, drug sensitivity, and pathway activation. Furthermore, a nomogram model with high prediction accuracy and clinical applicability was created based on the risk score. The current discoveries could improve personalized treatment for LUAD patients.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/) was accessed to retrieve clinical data and bulk RNA-seq data of LUAD patients. Patients with a survival time longer than 0 day were retained while excluding those without survival data. The RNA-seq profiles were converted to FPKM format, and 500 tumor samples were used for further analysis. Meanwhile, Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) was accessed to obtain the dataset of GSE31210. After removing normal and tumor samples without overall survival (OS) or clinical follow-up data, probes were transformed to the ID Symbol according to the annotation file. Finally, 226 tumor samples in the GSE31210 dataset remained. In our study, the TCGA-LUAD cohort was a training set, while GSE31210 dataset served as an independent validation set. Seven GBPs genes (GBP1-7) were collected from a previous study [23].

Screening of GBPs-related modular genes

According to GBPs gene expression, the GBPs score was computed by ssGSEA method utilizing the GSVA package [24]. Then, the GBPs-related gene modules were identified by WGCNA, and the soft threshold (β) was determined by pickSoftThreshold function. Hierarchical clustering was conducted to classify the modules, with each one containing at least 60 genes (minModuleSize = 60). The module with the highest correlation was identified by the Spearman method.

Identifying DEGs and function enrichment analysis

Under the screening threshold of |log2 Fold Change (FC)|> log2(1.5) and p.adj < 0.05, DEGs between tumor and normal tissues were identified by the limma package [25]. The clusterProfiler package was employed to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis on the genes of interest [26].

Development of a risk model and validation

The risk score model was established using overlapping genes between the DEGs and GBPs-related module genes from the training cohort. First, univariate Cox regression (log-rank p < 0.01) was employed to filter genes with significant prognostic value. These candidates were then subjected to multivariable stepwise Cox regression analysis to select key genes and derive their regression coefficients employing the survival package.

Immune microenvironment analysis and development of a nomogram

Using ssGSEA in the GSVA package [24], the levels of 28 types of immune cells were computed to reflect immune cell infiltration in LUAD. Overall immune microenvironment score was calculated by the ESTIMATE algorithm [28], while the TIMER [29] algorithm was used to assess the infiltration of six types of immune cells. Significant independent prognostic factors were selected from multiple features through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses and integrated into a nomogram. Finally, calibration and decision curves were generated to test the prediction accuracy and practical clinical value of the nomogram.

Potential drug sensitivity and pathway activation analysis

The pRRophetic package was employed for drug response prediction by calculating the IC50 value [30] for the TCGA-LUAD cohort. The association between the IC50 and risk score was analyzed using the Spearman algorithm. GSEA score was conducted based on the HALLMARK pathway gene sets from MSigDB database, and differences between risk groups were assessed.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

For the qPCR assay, total RNA was isolated from cultured cells utilizing TRIzol reagent (R401-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the instructions [31]. Briefly, a total of 5 × 106 added with 1 mL RNA isolator was used for cell lysis, followed by the addition of 1/5 volume of chloroform. The mixture was vortexed vigorously for 15 s and let stand for 5 min at 4 ℃. After centrifugation (12,000 g, 15 min), the supernatant was collected, mixed with equal volume of isopropanol, and centrifuged (12,000 g, 10 min) at 4 °C. RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, centrifuged, air-dried, and resuspended in RNase-free water and stored at – 80 ℃. RNA concentration and purity were detected by a NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with all samples exhibiting an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.2. RNA integrity was further verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, the cDNA was obtained using the HiScript II 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (R211-01, Vazyme), the mixture of total RNA (2 μg), Oligo (dT)23 VN (50 μM), appropriate RNase-free ddH2O, and 2 × RT Mix (10 μL) and HiScript II Enzyme Mix (2 μL) in a total volume of 20 μL was used for reverse transcription reaction (25 ℃, 5 min; 50 ℃, 15 min; 85 ℃, 2 min). Then, using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (D7260, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), qPCR testing was performed on Bio-Rad thermocycler (CFX96, USA). Serial dilutions confirmed 90% of PCR efficiency and absence of inhibitors. An RNase-free centrifuge tube contained 2 × SYBR Green Mix (10 μL), 2 μL of forward primer (3 μM) and reverse primer (3 μM), template DNA (2 μL) and RNase-free ddH₂O (6 μL), and reaction condition was pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s, for a total of 40 cycles. Target gene expression was computed by 2–ΔΔCT method [32] and normalized to that of GAPDH. All the experiments included triplicate technical and biological replicates. CCK-8, wound healing and transwell assays

Cell viability was determined via CCK-8 assay (C0037, Beyotime). Briefly, 5 × 103 cells were plated in 100 μL of culture medium and incubated for 0, 24, 48, or 72 h. Following the incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added per well, and absorbance was read at 450 nm. Next, cell migration was measured by wound healing assay. Briefly, 5 × 104 transfected A549 cells/well were cultivated in the 6-well plates (Corning) to reach 90% confluency. A rectilinear wound was created with a 20-μL pipette tip [33]. After incubation for 48 h, the cells were rinsed in PBS and imaged under an inverted microscope (Leica). The 24-well plates with chamber inserts (Corning) were used for the Transwell experiments. Specifically, cells were seeded at 4 × 104 cells/well in the upper chamber with 300 μL serum-free DMEM, whereas the lower Transwell chamber contained 700 μL DMEM with 10% FBS. After cell culture for 48 h at 37 ℃, the invaded cells were fixed by 5% glutaraldehyde and colored by 0.5% crystal violet. An inverted light microscope (Leica, Germany) was employed for cell imaging and quantification [34].

Statistical tests

Statistical testing was performed utilizing R software (version 3.6.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 10). Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to compare difference between two continuous variables, and Spearman’s method was performed for correlation analysis. KM survival analysis was conducted utilizing survival package, and the timeROC package was used for ROC analysis [27]. Two-group comparison was performed using Student’s t test, whereas multiple group comparisons were achieved using one/two-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni's post hoc test. p < 0.05 denoted a statistical significance.

Discussion

LUAD remains a major cause of global cancer-correlated deaths, presenting considerable therapeutic challenge due to its aggressiveness and invasiveness [2, 35]. Surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy are the main options for LUAD treatment but they have limited efficacy in improving the survival. Advances in cancer genomics have identified several driver mutations, such as those in EGFR, c-MET, ALK, and KRAS, leading to the development of targeted therapies against these alteration [36]. Additionally, immunotherapy has emerged as a promising treatment modality, particularly PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [37]. Despite these advances, identifying novel biomarkers to further improve clinical prognosis in LUAD has remained a pressing need. Study has reported the prognostic value of GBPs in different cancer types [38]. This study formulated a GBPs-related risk score that can effectively predict the prognosis, immune infiltration, pathway activation, and drug sensitivity for LUAD patients with high and low risks. In addition, the risk score was confirmed as an independent factor and was incorporated into a nomogram to precisely quantify the survival probability and risk for LUAD patients.

Our present GBPs-related risk model consisted of four genes, namely, CBFA2T3, MS4A1, and P2RX1 and CD19. CBFA2/RUNX1 partner transcriptional co-repressor 3 (CBFA2T3) is a significantly overexpressed transcription factor in LUAD [39]. Zhang et al. identified CBFA2T3 as a tumor suppressor gene that could inhibit the epithelial differentiation of colitis and carcinogenesis by blocking E protein transcription in NSCLC [40]. Membrane-spanning 4-domains, subfamily A, member 1 (MS4A1) encodes the B-cell surface marker CD20. MS4A1 is involved in the TME and serves as a biomarker for LUAD prognosis [27]. This study found that MS4A1 was inhibited in A549 cells. Purinergic receptor P2X 1 (P2RX1) is expressed in multiple immune cells, including macrophages, lymphocytes, and platelets, and is involved in regulating migration, apoptosis, platelet aggregation, and cytokine release [41]. Low-expressed P2RX1 reported to be associated with poor outcomes in LUAD [27]. Furthermore, CD19 is a B-lineage-specific transmembrane glycoprotein that can promote B cell activation at low receptor densities, and it is expressed in more than 95% of tumors associated with B-cells [42]. B cell activation is closely related to interferon signaling [43], and for this reason, GBPs as interferon-inducible proteins are likely to function as upstream regulators. Some studies reported significantly reduced tumor area and weight in the LUAD mice treated with CD19 monoclonal antibodies, indicating that CD19 may play a pro-tumoral role, while others defined CD19+ B cells as a pathogenic population in the TME of LUAD [41]. In conclusion, these four GBP-related genes may collectively exert their effects on LUAD through immune-related cells such as B cells.

Analysis of these four key genes revealed that they were closely associated with immune infiltration, suggesting that the model may influence the development of LUAD by altering the patient's TME. Further research on the patient's TME will help to deepen the understanding of its underlying molecular mechanisms. The TME is a complex system containing immune, malignant, and stromal cells, with tumor-infiltrating cells playing a crucial role in the immunotherapeutic response. Many studies reported that TME components mainly consist of recruited immune cells and resident stromal cells. These components influence the therapeutic response and clinical prognosis in cancer patients [44]. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are closely linked to the 5 y OS of LUAD patients, with a low abundance of TILs indicating a worse cancer prognosis [45]. It is known that activated B cells and NK cells are two typical immune cells with extensive antitumor effects. Liu et al. revealed a worse prognosis in LUAD patients with low infiltration of B cells [46]. NK cells participate in the antitumor response by directly killing cancer cells and facilitating adaptive T-cell immunological function [47]. Moreover, study also observed that a high abundance of NK and T cells is linked to a better prognosis in various cancers [48]. In a pan-cancer analysis, significantly high infiltration of macrophages, dendritic cells, and neutrophils in LUAD is predictive of a strong immunotherapy effect [49]. In this study, the high-risk group may be more suitable for drug therapies such as pyrimethamine and lapatinib. Pyrimethamine has been shown to exert antitumor effects on NSCLCs [50], while lapatinib exhibits favorable therapeutic efficacy in HER2-overexpressing cancers [51]. Further analysis of enriched pathways suggested that the low-risk group, with its predominant enrichment in immune-related pathways such as IL6-JAK-STAT3, may be more suitable for immunotherapy. This is mechanistically supported by the close connection between Il6-Jak-Stat3 signaling pathway and PD-L1 [52], which suggested that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors may be effective to low-risk LUAD patients. However, the specific details still require further clinical validation. The present findings revealed an association between the immune microenvironment and risk score, providing a theoretical basis for subsequent improvement of GBPs-based immunotherapy and individualized management of LUAD.

It should be equally noted that this study still had some limitations. First, as a retrospective study based on public databases, the model may be influenced by potential batch effect and its applicability to other populations requires further validation. Second, although the model demonstrated strong prognostic classification efficiency in the studied cohorts, its generalizability needed to be verified in larger dataset and subsequent follow-up studies. While we established a prognostic risk model and uncovered its associations with immune infiltration and pathway activity, the precise molecular mechanisms driving LUAD progression remained to be fully elucidated. To address these limitations, future study should include multi-center and multi-subtype clinical cohorts to conduct external validation of the existing model to enhance the model’s generalizability and clinical credibility. Second, in vivo experiments and clinical studies, for example, constructing LUAD mouse models, are required to simulate the TME and delineate the regulatory roles of the key genes in LUAD development. Furthermore, clinical trials should be carried out to verify the practical application value of the model and provide direct evidence for its translation into clinical practice.

Conclusion

To conclude, we constructed a four-gene risk score model (CBFA2T3, CD19, MS4A1, P2RX1) based on the GBPs expression profile for the risk stratification of LUAD patients. Notably, the low-risk group had a high immune infiltration and was enriched in immune-related pathways, which may suggest that this group was more suitable for taking immunotherapy. The model was expected to improve the drug screening and individualized treatment of LUAD.
