The Farnesoid X receptor-bile acid axis contributes to immune evasion in gastric cancer and its inhibition enhances efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy
Authors: Cristina Di Giorgio, Michele Biagioli, Silvia Marchianò, Carmen Massa, Ginevra Lachi, Ginevra Urbani, Martina Bordoni, Rachele Bellini, Luigi Cari, Benedetta Sensini, Maria Rosaria Sette, Elva Morretta, Maria Chiara Monti, Nicola Natalizi, Luigina Graziosi, Eleonora Distrutti, Annibale Donini, Angela Zampella, Stefano Fiorucci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastric cancer ranks among the most prevalent malignancies globally and remains a major contributor to cancer-related mortality. Its progression is profoundly shaped by the tumor microenvironment (TME), a dynamic milieu comprising extracellular matrix components, soluble mediators, and diverse non-malignant immune populations that collectively influence tumor initiation and evolution. Although immunotherapeutic strategies have recently attracted increasing attention in the management of solid tumors, including gastric carcinoma, the determinants of immune evasion in this context are still not fully delineated. The Farnesoid X receptor (FXR), a key nuclear receptor for bile acids, has recently been implicated in regulating TME composition and immune cell recruitment within gastric tumors. Yet, how bile acid signaling contributes to the bidirectional interactions between malignant and immune cells remains largely undefined. This study aimed to investigate the role of FXR in modulating immune-regulatory networks associated with PD-L1/PD-1 in gastric cancer. RNA-seq analysis of paired gastric mucosa from 39 gastric cancer patients revealed significant molecular differences between intestinal and diffuse tumors. FXR was overexpressed in the intestinal subtype and correlated with poor prognosis. MS/MS analysis demonstrated enrichment of tumor tissues by cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid compared to non-neoplastic pairs, supporting the hypothesis of dysregulated bile acid metabolism. In vitro, exposure of gastric cancer cell lines and patient-derived organoids to FXR ligands increased PD-L1 expression through direct binding of FXR-responsive elements in the PD-L1 promoter. Targeting FXR may offer a therapeutic approach to improve the effectiveness of immunotherapies in intestinal gastric cancer.Keywords: Farnesoid X receptor (FXR) gastric cancer, Bile acids, PD-L1, Immune evasion, Intestinal subtype

Introduction

Gastric cancer is the fifth most common type of cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-associated mortality worldwide [1]. Despite advancements in treatment, the prognosis of locally advanced gastric cancer remains poor, and therapeutic options are limited. A critical factor in the progression of gastric cancer is the ability of cancer cells to escape the immune surveillance, primarily through the expression of immune checkpoint ligands such as the programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1). PD-L1 binds to programmed death protein 1 (PD-1) on immune effector cells, such as T cells and NK cells, leading to cellular exhaustion, immune suppression and tumor progression [2].

The development of immune checkpoint inhibitors, particularly antibodies targeting PD-1 and PD-L1, has significantly improved the survival of patients with various malignancies, including GC. Anti-PD-1 antibodies such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab have been approved for use in patients with advanced or metastatic GC following chemotherapy failure. Nivolumab is commonly used after standard chemotherapy regimens such as fluoropyrimidine and platinum-based treatments, although it can also be used as first-line treatment for patients with advanced gastric cancer in patients whose tumors express PD-L1 at higher levels (determined by a Combined Positive Score, or CPS). However, clinical responses remain heterogeneous, with objective response rates ranging from approximately 10% to 30% [3]. This variability is attributed to multiple factors, including tumor-intrinsic molecular features and the composition of the tumor microenvironment (TME). The TME contains immune effector cells such as CD8+ T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells, which are crucial mediators of anti-tumor immunity [4]. These cells can induce tumor cell death through cytolytic mechanisms; however, their activity can be inhibited by immune checkpoint signaling pathways such as PD-1/PD-L1 [2].

The gut microbiota and its metabolites, particularly bile acids, play a key role in maintaining intestinal and immune homeostasis. Bile acids and their metabolites are endogenous ligand for several transcription factors, including the Farnesoid X Receptor (FXR), Vitamin D Receptor (VDR), Liver X Receptor (LXR), and Pregnane X Receptor (PXR) among others [5]. FXR is the main bile acid sensor and is activated by primary bile acids, chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) and cholic acid (CA) [6]. In immune cells, FXR activation promotes the development of a tolerogenic phenotype and its in vivo activation attenuates intestinal and liver inflammation although in the context of cancer, it is not clear whether FXR regulates the cross talk between the cancer and immune cells [7].

However, its role in regulating tumor-immune cell crosstalk in gastric cancer remains incompletely defined.

Growing evidence suggests that FXR plays a dual role in cancer biology. In the liver FXR activation in the liver has been associated with the suppression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) by maintaining bile acid homeostasis and preventing bile acid-induced toxicity [8].

On the other hand, in gastrointestinal cancers such as colorectal cancer and GC, FXR may contribute to cancer progression by promoting an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. In CRC, reduced FXR expression or activity has been correlated with enhanced tumorigenesis, whereas activation of FXR has shown potential in reversing tumor growth [9].

FXR activation has been linked to increased PD-L1 expression, which facilitates immune evasion by inhibiting the activity of CD8+ T cells and has been associated with the recruitment of immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which further promote an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [10].

The role of bile acids in modulating immune responses is also supported by their ability to influence the differentiation of immune cells. Certain bile acid metabolites have been shown to promote the differentiation of regulatory T cells (Tregs), which play a key role in maintaining immune tolerance and suppressing inflammatory responses [11]. Conversely, other bile acid metabolites can inhibit the differentiation of pro-inflammatory Th17 cells, thereby reducing inflammation and potentially contributing to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [12]. Additionally, FXR activation in NKT cells has been shown to suppress the production of inflammatory cytokines, further highlighting the complex role of FXR in immune regulation within the cancer microenvironment [13].

Recent studies have identified alterations in bile acid metabolism in patients with GC, with an increase in primary bile acids and a decrease in secondary bile acids being particularly notable [14]. Suggesting an alteration of the catalytic activity of the intestinal microbiota, which may exacerbate cell damage and inflammation, contributing to cancer development [15]. These metabolic changes may influence the tumor microenvironment and potentially impact immune regulation in gastric cancer.

Given the emerging role of bile acids and FXR in modulating the immune microenvironment and promoting tumor immune-escape, this study aims to investigate the role of FXR agonism by primary bile acids in modulating immune-regulatory networks associated with PD-L1/PD-1 in gastric cancer. By elucidating the mechanisms through which FXR influences immune evasion, this research seeks to identify potential therapeutic targets that could enhance the efficacy of immunotherapies in treating gastric cancer.

Material and methods

Human data

Patients

Tissue specimens were collected from 39 patients diagnosed with gastric carcinoma who underwent surgical resection at the Surgery Unit of Perugia University Hospital (Italy). All patients provided informed written consent prior to surgery, and none had received chemotherapy or radiotherapy beforehand. During the surgical procedure, a trained biologist obtained paired samples from both tumor and adjacent non-tumorous tissue. The specimens were immediately preserved in RNAlater, trans-ported to the Gastroenterology Laboratory, and subsequently snap-frozen at −80 °C for further analyses. Permission to collect post-surgical samples was granted to Prof. Stefano Fiorucci by the ethical committee of Regione Umbria (CEAS), permit FI00001, n. 2266/2014, granted on February 19, 2014, and by the University of Perugia Bioethics Committee, permit FIO0003, n. 120687, granted on January 12, 2021, permit FIO0009, n.357803, granted on November 14,2024. All experimental protocols were approved by the named institutional committees. Furthermore, all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, ensuring compliance with ethical standards for the use of human tissue samples. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

The GSE66229 dataset

The GSE66229 dataset, generated by the Asian Cancer Research Group (ACRG), includes gene expression profiles from 300 gastric cancer samples and 100 non-tumorous gastric tissues.

The Cancer Genome Atlas Stomach Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-STAD)

The TCGA-STAD, cohort comprises 350 gastric cancer samples and 31 healthy tissue samples.

Cell culture

2D Culture Cell Lines. Gastric suspension from neoplastic mucosa, Human gastric cell line MKN45, Primary macrophages and T cells were employed in this study. The specific culture conditions for each cell line are provided as follows.

Gastric suspension from neoplastic mucosa

Tumor tissues from gastric resections were processed using the Tumor Dissocia-tion Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, ref. 130-095-929), following the manufacturer's protocol. The resulting single-cell suspensions were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma-Merk Life Science, Milan, Italy) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% l-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were treated with lipopolysaccha-ride (LPS, 100 ng/mL), either alone or in combination with chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA, 10 μM), for 24 h. Following treatment, RNA was extracted for gene expression analysis.

Immortalized cell lines

The human gastric carcinoma cell line MKN45 was obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (Human Science Resources Bank, Osaka, Japan), while the HepG2 hepatocarcinoma cell line was used as a hepatic model. MKN45 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium, and HepG2 in E-MEM (Sigma-Merk Life Science, Milan, Italy), both supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% l-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Mycoplasma-free status was verified using PCR-based detection (Sigma). Cells were subcultured regularly to maintain exponential growth and used within 10 passages after thawing. Before experiments, cells were seeded, serum-starved for 24 h, and then stimulated for 8, 24, or 48 h.

T cells and macrophages from donor buffy coat

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from the heparinized blood of healthy volunteers by Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation. After 24 h in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, non-adherent T cells were collected for co-culture with MKN45 cells. Adherent monocytes were differentiated into macrophages over 6 days in RPMI with 10% FBS, supplemented with either M-CSF or GM-CSF (50 ng/mL). Polarization toward an M1 phenotype was achieved by treating cells for 24 h with LPS (50 ng/mL) and IFN-γ (20 ng/mL), followed by exposure to CDCA (10 μM) and the FXR antagonist GP7 (3-(naphthalen-2-yl)-5-(piperidin-4-yl)-1,2,4-oxadiazole, 1 μM) for an additional 24 h.

Cocolture

MKN45 cells were seeded at a density of 150 × 103 cells/mL. After 24 h of serum starvation, cells were co-cultured with T cells at a density of 1,5 × 106 cells/mL and treated with CDCA (10 μM), GP7 (1 μM), or their combination. In separate conditions, cells were also exposed to CDCA or GP7 in the presence or absence of anti-PD-L1 antibody (30 μg/mL, Leinco Technologies, St. Louis, MO). At the conclusion of the treatments, RNA was isolated for analysis of gene expression.

3D Cell Lines. Three-dimensional organoid cultures were established from gastric glands isolated from both neoplastic and non-neoplastic mucosa of gastric cancer patients, as well as from the antral region of Fxr knockout and wild-type mice (C57BL6/J background, aged 4–8 weeks). Tissues were rinsed in cold PBS with antibiotics (Primocin, Invivogen), minced, and incubated in a stripping buffer (HBSS with 10% FBS, 25 mM HEPES, and 5 mM EDTA) at 37 °C for 20 min under agitation. This was followed by enzymatic digestion with collagenase (1.5 mg/mL, Gibco) and hyaluronidase (20 μg/mL, Sigma) in Advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco) for 1 h at 37 °C. The resulting gland suspensions were filtered through a 100 μm strainer, washed, and embedded in Matrigel (50,000 cells/50 μL). Organoids were cultured in Advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with HEPES, GlutaMAX, Primocin, B27, 1 mM N-acetylcysteine, EGF (50 ng/mL), R-spondin1 (200 ng/mL), Noggin (100 ng/mL), Wnt3a (100 ng/mL), FGF10 (200 ng/mL), Gastrin (10 nM), TGF-β inhibitor (0.5 mM), ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 (10 μM), and LIF (10 ng/mL).

Histological techniques

For histological examination, portions of biopsies of diffuse and intestinal gastric cancers, belonging to patients undergoing surgical resection at the Santa Maria della Misericordia Hospital of Perugia, were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin and sectioned.

Immune-fluorescence staining

The paraffin-embedded tissue sections or mouse organoid were hydrated and then subjected to antigen retrieval by incubation of the slides for 90 min in 95 °C sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and 30 min of cooling at room temperature. After washing 3 times in H2Ob and 1 time in phosphate buffered saline (PBS 1X), slides have been incu-bated with Blocking Buffer (PBS 1X, 10% horse serum and 1% BSA) for 1 h at room temperature.

Primary antibodies, anti-FXR (Orbit orb156973 1:100), and anti E-Cadherin (GeneTex GTX100443, 1:100) have been prepared in Blocking Buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C.

The next day, after washing 3 times with PBS 1X containing 0,1 % Tween 20 (PBST), slides were incubated with secondary antibody Goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (ab150077, 1:200).After washing 3 times with PBST, samples have been incubated with Sudan Black for 5 min to quench autofluorescence and then nucleus were counter-stained with DAPI 1X for 1 min in the dark and the reaction was stopped with a final wash in PBS 1X for 5 min.

Slides were mounted with ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (P36980) (Invitro-gen, Thermofisher scientific Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), sealed with nail polish and observed at fluorescence microscope Olympus BX60.

The Kaplan-Meier curve survival

The Kaplan-Meier Plotter (KM plotter) server [16] was employed to investigate the association between gene expression and survival outcomes. Gene expression data and corresponding survival information from our patient cohort, as well as from TCGA-STAD and GSE66229 series from the ACRG repositories, were uploaded onto the platform and analyzed using the Auto selection protocol to determine the optimal cutoff value [17]; then, Kaplan-Meier plots were generated and downloaded.

Transcriptome analysis

Total RNA was isolated from both non-tumorous and tumor gastric mucosa using the PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the supplier's protocol. The integrity and concentration of the extracted RNA were evaluated with the Qubit® RNA HS Assay Kit on a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer and further confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA libraries were prepared using the Ion AmpliSeq™ Transcriptome Human Gene Expression Core Panel and the Chef-Ready Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Specifically, 10 ng of RNA were reverse transcribed using the SuperScript™ Vilo™ cDNA Synthesis Kit, prior to automated library preparation on the Ion Chef™ System. Barcoded libraries were then generated through PCR amplification with the Ion Code™ PCR Plate and the AmpliSeq™ Transcriptome panel, according to standard procedures. The libraries were pooled to a final concentration of 100 pM and processed to generate Template-Positive Ion Sphere™ Particles using the Ion 540™ Kit-Chef. Sequencing was carried out on the Ion S5™ platform, and data were acquired with Torrent Suite™ Software v6. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the Transcriptome Analysis Console Software (version 4.0.2), applying a fold change threshold of < −2 or >+2 and a p-value cutoff of <0.05.

Immune deconvolution analysis

CIBERSORTx [18] was used to deconvolute the bulk RNA-Seq profiles and estimate the proportional abundance of immune cell populations in the analyzed cohort. The LM22 leukocyte reference matrix, comprising 547 curated genes that discriminate 22 human immune cell phenotypes, served as the basis for the computational inference. After running the deconvolution algorithm with 500 permutations, the resulting immune-cell fraction estimates were exported in CSV format for downstream analyses.

Bile acid determinations

Gastric cancer biopsy specimens were stored at −80 °C and subsequently lyophilized. For each sample, 20 mg of dry material was manually disrupted with a Potter pestle and solubilized in methanol to reach a concentration of 100 μg/μL, followed by a 2-h extraction. The extracts were then diluted 1:5 in a mixture containing 50% water and 50% methanol supplemented with 0.1% formic acid (FA) and 5 mM ammonium acetate (AmAc). Individual bile acid (BA) standards were prepared separately in methanol, combined, and further diluted in 50% H2O/50% MeOH with 5 mM AmAc and 0.1% FA to generate calibration curves covering 25–400 nM. Quantitative profiling was performed by UHPLC–MRM–MS on a QTRAP 6500 system (AB Sciex) interfaced with a Shimadzu Nexera LC and autosampler. Twenty-five BAs were chromatographically resolved on a Luna Omega Polar C18 column (1.6 μm, 100 Å, 50 × 2.1 mm; Phenomenex) at 40 °C with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of H2O with 5 mM AmAc and 0.1% FA, whereas mobile phase B was MeOH with identical additives. The gradient was initiated at 50% B, increased to 55% B at 3.5 min, ramped to 95% B by 19.5 min, held for 1 min, and then returned to 50% B for a 6-min re-equilibration. The QTRAP 6500 was operated in negative-ion MRM mode with the following settings: declustering potential −150 V, entrance potential −12 V, collision energy −15 V, and collision cell exit potential −30 V. Curtain gas was set to 30, ion source gases 1 and 2 to 25, and the ion spray voltage to −4500 V, with unit resolution in both Q1 and Q2. The quantified analytes included the following 25 BAs (precursor ions [M − H]-): tauro-HCA (514 m/z), tauro-CA (514), tauro-UDCA (498), tauro-HDCA (498), tauro-CDCA (498), tauro-DCA (498), tauro-LCA (482), tauro-3-oxo-LCA (480), tauro-7-keto-LCA (496), HCA (407), CA (407), UDCA (391), HDCA (391), CDCA (391), DCA (391), LCA (375), 3-oxo-DCA (389), glyco-CA (464), glyco-CDCA (448), glyco-HCA (464), glyco-DCA (448), glyco-HDCA (448), glyco-7-keto-LCA (446), glyco-UDCA (448), and glyco-LCA (432).

Relative transcript abundance was determined using the ΔCt approach. Primer pairs were generated with PRIMER3 (http://frodo.wi.mit.edu/primer3/) based on sequence information retrieved from the NCBI database. Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess whether the data conformed to a normal distribution. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired Student's t-tests with Welch's correction for normally distributed data, or the Mann–Whitney test for non-Gaussian distributions. For analyses involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was applied to data meeting the assumption of normality, with Brown–Forsythe and Welch corrections when variances were unequal, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for non-parametric data. Correlation analyses were conducted using Pearson's r for Gaussian variables and Spearman's r for non-Gaussian variables. All statistical procedures were performed using Prism 8.0 (GraphPad), with significance defined as p < 0.05.

These analyses identified the modulation of 1551 genes in intestinal-type gastric cancer compared to non-neoplastic mucosa and 278 genes in Diffuse gastric cancer compared to non-neoplastic pairs (Fig. 1C). In the intestinal subtype of gastric cancer, a downregulation of 1045 genes and an upregulation of 506 was observed. Among the downregulated genes, the analysis of differential genes (DEGs) identified a reduced expression of several key gastric epithelial markers, indicating a loss of gastric epithelial identity and a shift toward an intestinal phenotype (Fig. 1D). Specifically, ATPase H+/K + Transporting Beta Subunit (ATP4B) and Alpha Subunit (ATP4A), which are essential for acid secretion in gastric parietal cells, were significantly downregulated. This reduction is consistent with the loss of gastric function and the trans-differentiation toward an intestinal phenotype [19]. Similarly, the expression of genes encoding Pepsinogen A4 (PGA4), Pepsinogen A3 (PGA3), and Pepsinogen A5 (PGA5), which are precursors of the digestive enzyme pepsin, were also reduced. These enzymes are critical for the breakdown of proteins in the stomach, and their reduced expression reflects a shift away from the proteolytic activity characteristic of normal gastric mucosa [16]. Additionally, Gastric Intrinsic Factor (GIF), which is essential for vitamin B12 absorption, and Lipase F (LIPF), involved in fat digestion, were also downregulated, further indicating the loss of gastric functional characteristics.

Conversely, the upregulation of intestinal epithelial markers suggests a functional trans-differentiation of gastric epithelial cells towards an intestinal phenotype. For instance, Claudin-7 (CLDN7) and Claudin-1 (CLDN1), which are integral membrane proteins involved in the formation of tight junctions in epithelial cells, were significantly upregulated. These proteins play a crucial role in maintaining the barrier function and integrity of the intestinal epithelium, and their upregulation in gastric cancer indicates a shift towards intestinal-like tissue architecture [17]. Cadherin-17 (CDH17), also known as liver-intestine cadherin, is another marker that was upregulated in the intestinal subtype. CDH17 is a cell adhesion molecule typically expressed in the small intestine and colon, where it plays a role in maintaining cell-cell adhesion and tissue architecture [18].

The diffuse subtype of gastric cancer exhibited a different pattern of gene expression (Fig. 1E). The most upregulated genes in this subtype included Neuropilin-1 (NRP1) and Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase Kinase Kinase Kinase 1 (MAP4K1). NRP1 encodes a receptor involved in angiogenesis and vascular permeability, which can enhance tumor growth and metastasis by promoting blood vessel formation [20]. MAP4K1 is involved in T cell signaling and immune responses, indicating that immune modulation might be a feature of diffuse gastric cancer [21]. Among the most downregulated genes in the diffuse subtype were LOC729966, a long non-coding RNA with unknown function in cancer, and Melanocortin 2 Receptor Accessory Protein 2 (MRAP2), which is involved in energy homeostasis, suggesting that these pathways might be disrupted in diffuse gastric cancer [22].

These differential gene expression profiles highlight the molecular heterogeneity between the intestinal and diffuse subtypes of GC, with the intestinal subtype showing a clear trans-differentiation towards an intestinal phenotype, while the diffuse subtype exhibits distinct molecular alterations that may contribute to its more aggressive and invasive behavior.

Since the immune microenvironment of gastric cancer plays a pivotal role in determining the progression of the disease and the response to therapy, we have then performed immune-deconvolution analysis based on transcriptomic (Fig. 2). The analysis revealed that the intestinal subtype of gastric cancer is associated with a significant increase in immunosuppressive cell populations. Specifically, a significant increment of the frequencies of Macrophages M0 and M2 and T Regulatory Cells (T regs) in intestinal types of gastric cancer comparted to non-neoplastic pairs was observed. Moreover, B Cell Memory appeared reduced in intestinal gastric cancer while Plasma Cells were increased (Fig. 2B). Based on these findings, subsequent analyses were focused on the intestinal subtype of gastric cancer.

The results of this analysis identified 25 bile acids and neoplastic mucosa harbored a higher concentration of the total bile acids content compared to the non-neoplastic counterpart (Fig. 3A–B). Among the primary bile acids, concentrations of CA, TCA and GHCA were significantly higher in neoplastic samples than non-neoplastic pairs (Fig. 3C–E). FXR is the most potent nuclear receptor for primary bile acids, CA and CDCA. The increase in primary bile acids in neoplastic samples, likely due to altered metabolism, could induce the upregulation of FXR signaling. These findings are particularly relevant given the established role of FXR in modulating the tumor microenvironment (TME) and immune cell infiltration in GC.

In non-neoplastic mucosa TCA, THCA, GHCA and TCDCA showed a direct correlation with resting Dendritic cells (DC) and CDCA correlated directly with activated CD4+ memory T cells. This correlation was lost in neoplastic pairs, in which conversely CDCA derivatives showed a direct correlation with Treg and M2 macrophages, suggesting a shift toward an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Moreover, we have carried out a correlation analysis between primary bile acid content and FXR and SHP expression (Log2). These analyses revealed that no significant correlation was observed between FXR expression (Log2) and bile acid content in either non-neoplastic or neoplastic tissues. On the contrary, non-neoplastic tissue showed an indirect correlation between CA and CDCA and SHP expression (Log2), which was lost in neoplastic counterpart, and a direct correlation between CA and CDCA derivatives was observed (Fig. 3F–I).

To more appropriately assess FXR pathway activation in the context of bile acid enrichment, we performed correlation analyses between primary bile acid species (CA, CDCA and their conjugates) and canonical FXR-regulated transport genes, including MRP3 (ABCC3), OSTA (SLC51A), ASBT (SLC10A2), and IBABP (FABP6). In non-neoplastic mucosa, primary bile acids exhibited modest positive associations with classical FXR-dependent transport genes, consistent with physiological bile acid homeostasis and intact feedback regulation (Supplementary Fig. 1A). In contrast, neoplastic tissues displayed a partially altered or attenuated correlation pattern (Supplementary Fig. 1B), suggesting disruption or transcriptional rewiring of canonical FXR-mediated metabolic programs within the tumor microenvironment. These findings indicate that, although primary bile acids are enriched in intestinal-type gastric cancer, the classical transport-oriented FXR transcriptional program is not uniformly preserved in neoplastic tissue, potentially allowing FXR signaling to adopt alternative transcriptional outputs in the tumor context.

FXR is overexpressed in intestinal type of GC

Several studies suggest that FXR may play a pivotal role in intestinal metaplasia, being implicated in regulating gene expression patterns critical for cellular differentiation, proliferation and immune evasion [24]. In light of evidence linking FXR to the regulation of CDX2 [25], a transcription factor essential for the intestinal differentiation regulating CLDN1 and Cadherin-17 CDH17 [26], both of which were upregulated in the intestinal subtype of gastric cancer in our study. Based on this evidence, we decided to investigate the expression of FXR in our cohort.

As observed in Fig. 4A–B, FXR protein and gene (Log2) expression were significantly increased in the intestinal subtype of gastric cancer compared to matched non-neoplastic tissue and the diffuse subtype. In addition, the expression of canonical FXR target genes was evaluated. While SHP (NR0B2) expression remained unchanged, the bile salt export pump BSEP (ABCB11) was significantly reduced in neoplastic tissues (Fig. 4B), suggesting that despite FXR overexpression, components of the classical FXR-regulated bile acid transport program may be altered in the tumor context.

In addiction we have investigated the expression of several Organic-Anion-Transporting Polypeptides (OATPs) whose expression is shown in Fig. 4F. Among the various OATPs analyzed, only OATP5A1 and OATP6A1 showed a statistically significant reduction in cancerous compared to non-cancerous paired samples from patients with intestinal-type tumors (Fig. 4F). While the substrate specificity of OATP6A1 remains poorly characterized, OATP5A1 is considered a non-classical OATP, with no demonstrated transport function but potential roles in cell differentiation and proliferation regulation, as previously reported. Taken together, these alterations suggest a complex remodeling of bile acid transport machinery in intestinal-type gastric cancer rather than a simple increase in bile acid uptake.

Furthermore, the overexpression of the immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1 (CD274) (Fig. 4G) in these tumors supports the possibility that FXR contributes to immune evasion, further linking FXR to the aggressive behavior of the intestinal subtype of GC. To complement the transcriptomic findings, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of PD-L1 on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded gastric cancer tissues from our cohort, confirming the presence of PD-L1 protein expression within the tumor microenvironment in neoplastic mucosa of intestinal subtype of gastric cancer (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Together, these findings demonstrate that FXR directly regulates PD-L1 expression, thereby promoting the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Our results further suggest that pharmacological inhibition of FXR may enhance responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade in intestinal-type gastric cancer.

Discussion

Here we have shown that the bile acid sensor FXR [7] is involved in modulating the interaction between the tumor microenvironment and cancer cells, contributing to immune evasion in gastric cancer. The role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in cancer progression and therapy resistance has received increasing attention as a key determinant in oncogenesis [32]. The TME comprises a dynamic and complex network of cellular and non-cellular components, including immune cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, extracellular matrix (ECM), cytokines and growth factors, which collectively shape the tumor's interactions with surrounding tissues and modulate its response to therapeutic interventions [33].

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly those targeting PD-1 and PD-L1, have provided a substantial advancement in the treatment of various cancers, including gastric cancer. Grounded by results of the two pivotal studies, Checkmate-649 [34] and Keynote-590 [35], nivolumab and pembrolizumab have been approved for use in patients with advanced or metastatic gastric cancers, especially those who have previously undergone chemotherapy but experienced disease progression. Nivolumab, is currently approved by EMA in combination with fluoropyrimidine- and platinum-based chemotherapy for the first-line treatment of adult patients with HER2-negative advanced or metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma whose tumors express PD-L1 with a combined positive score (CPS) ≥ 5 [34]. However nivolumab does not seem to improve survival in patients with reduced PD-L-1 expression and a CPS <5, highlighting the critical role of PD-L1 expression in predicting clinical response to immunotherapy [34]. In addition to the PD-L1 status, there is increasing evidence that microsatellite instability (MSI) is predictive of response to immunotherapy. A meta-analysis conducted on four randomized clinical trials has shown a significant improvement in patient survival for MSI-high cancers compared to microsatellite stable subgroup [36].

In addition to the above-mentioned factors, the variability in gastric cancer response to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors may depend on the characteristics of the TME. The TME contains various immune effector cells, such as CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, which are pivotal in orchestrating anti-tumor immune responses [4,37], thus identification of additional mechanisms involved in regulation of these cells might have therapeutic relevance also in patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors.

In the present study, we have shown that a bile acid-FXR mediated axis might be involved in the regulation of PD-L1/PD-1 expression and function.

The transcriptomic analysis of paired neoplastic and non-neoplastic gastric cancer tissues from patients with intestinal gastric cancer revealed that FXR overexpression occurs as a distinguishing feature of the neoplastic tissues. This finding was corroborated by immunofluorescence analysis, further highlighting the specific upregulation of FXR in cancerous tissues. Moreover, a Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that FXR overexpression was statistically associated with poor patient prognosis in our internal cohort.

However, this association did not reach statistical significance in TCGA and ACRG datasets despite subtype stratification. This discrepancy may reflect cohort-specific differences in sample size, treatment exposure, sequencing platforms, and clinical heterogeneity. Therefore, the prognostic value of FXR should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger prospective cohorts.

Our findings also underscore the critical role of FXR in modulating the immune landscape within the TME [7]. Through immune-deconvolution analysis, we observed a direct correlation between FXR expression and the frequency of Tregs and alternatively activated, M2 macrophages, both of which contribute to the development of an immunosuppressive TME [38]. The direct correlation between FXR expression, gene and protein, and immunoregulatory cell populations supports the hypothesis that FXR might participate into the immune evasion by fostering an environment biassed toward cancer cell survival and growth.

The main ligands of FXR in humans are CA and CDCA, two primary bile acids generated by the liver and released in the intestine in response to food [39]. By a detailed analysis of bile acids content, in non-neoplastic and neoplastic samples from gastric cancer patients, we have been able to detect a robust accumulation of CA and its derivatives within neoplastic tissues [40,41], potentially increasing ligand availability for FXR activation. Importantly, correlation analyses between primary bile acids and canonical FXR target genes revealed a partially disrupted transport-oriented transcriptional program in neoplastic tissue, suggesting functional rewiring of FXR signaling within the tumor microenvironment. Consistent with this observation, analysis of canonical FXR target genes revealed that while SHP expression remained largely unchanged, the bile salt export pump BSEP was significantly reduced in neoplastic tissues, further supporting the concept that components of the classical FXR-regulated bile acid transport program may be partially rewired in the tumor context.

Moreover, in vitro experiments confirmed that CDCA-mediated FXR activation directly promotes the expression of PD-L1, providing mechanistic insights into how FXR modulates immune evasion pathways in gastric cancer. Importantly, additional in vivo evidence supporting the link between FXR signaling and PD-L1 modulation was observed in a murine model of metabolic liver injury, where Pd-L1 expression increased under HFD + CCl4 conditions and was significantly reduced following treatment with the FXR/GPBAR1 agonist BAR502 (Supplementary Fig. 3).These findings are consistent with the finding that fibroblast growth factor (FGF)R4 and FGF19 [42], a FXR-mediated transcription factor, promotes gastric cancer growth [43].

Previous studies have demonstrated that FXR deficiency promotes intestinal epithelial proliferation and tumorigenesis, supporting a tumor-suppressive role of FXR in colorectal cancer models [44]. In these settings, FXR maintains bile acid homeostasis and epithelial integrity, thereby limiting tumor initiation. However, nuclear receptor signaling is highly tissue- and context-dependent. Distinct roles of FXR have been reported across organs and disease stages, including hepatoprotection in cholestatic liver injury [45], metabolic regulation in the intestine, and immunomodulatory effects in inflammatory conditions [46]. Therefore, while FXR may act as a tumor suppressor in normal intestinal epithelium during tumor initiation, our data indicate that in established intestinal-type gastric cancer-characterized by bile acid accumulation and inflammatory reprogramming-FXR activation contributes to immune evasion through PD-L1 regulation. These divergent roles likely reflect differences in tissue origin, tumor stage, and transcriptional co-factor availability.

In contrast, since we have provided evidence that its activation might contribute to development of an immunotolerant phenotype in gastric cancers, FXR inhibition might represent an attractive p opportunity to rescue immune-suppressed micro-environment [47,48]. Consistent with this working hypothesis, treating cancer cell lines and cancer cells co-cultures rescue against PD-L1, FOXP3, and IL-10 induction caused by CDCA (as shown in Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9). Additionally, as shown in Fig. 7, organoids prepared from Fxr−/− mice shows an impaired maturation, that is consistent with a role for this nuclear receptor in promoting intestinal stemness [9]. The finding that FXR antagonism effectively reverses the immunosuppressive phenotype of the TME, enhancing the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapies in this experimental setting, suggest that combining FXR and immune-checkpoint inhibitors might represent a novel therapeutic strategy for patients with intestinal-type GC, who currently face limited treatment options and poor outcomes.

ChIP-qPCR supports direct recruitment of FXR to the endogenous CD274 promoter in gastric cancer cells, and pharmacological FXR agonism or antagonism functionally modulates PD-L1 expression across complementary models. Nonetheless, promoter-reporter assays with site-directed mutagenesis will be required to definitively determine the relative contribution of each putative FXR response element.

Future studies using xenograft/PDX models and genetically perturbed systems (e.g., siRNA or CRISPR-mediated targeting of NR1H4) will be required to evaluate the impact of FXR inhibition on tumor growth and response to PD-L1 blockade in vivo.

Although our study provides mechanistic evidence supporting a link between FXR activation and PD-L1-mediated immune modulation in intestinal-type gastric cancer, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the association between high FXR expression and poorer survival was significant in our internal cohort but not in TCGA or ACRG datasets despite subtype stratification. Differences in cohort size, treatment exposure, and clinical heterogeneity may account for this variability. Thus, the prognostic value of FXR requires validation in larger prospective cohorts. Second, although immune modulation was evaluated using co-culture systems and validated by intracellular flow cytometry, RNA analyses from mixed cell populations may not fully capture cell-type-specific interactions within the tumor microenvironment. Spatially resolved approaches could further refine these observations. Third, in vivo validation was not performed. While we used complementary systems including patient-derived samples, organoids, and Fxr+/+ and Fxr−/− models, studies in xenograft or genetically engineered mouse models will be necessary to assess the impact of FXR inhibition on tumor growth and response to immune checkpoint blockade. Fourth, although PD-L1 expression was confirmed by immunohistochemistry in our cohort, quantitative clinical scoring using Combined Positive Score (CPS) or Tumor Proportion Score (TPS) was not available for these retrospective samples. The analyzed specimens were collected before routine implementation of PD-L1 scoring in our institution. Therefore, future studies using prospectively annotated cohorts with standardized CPS evaluation will be important to further investigate the clinical relevance of the FXR-PD-L1 axis in gastric cancer. Finally, although ChIP-qPCR supports FXR recruitment to the CD274 promoter, promoter–reporter assays with site-directed mutagenesis will be required to define the contribution of individual FXR response elements. Despite these limitations, our integrated transcriptomic, metabolomic, and functional data consistently support FXR as a context-dependent regulator of immune evasion in intestinal-type gastric cancer.

In conclusion, our study provides evidence that FXR plays a relevant role in the pathophysiology of intestinal-type gastric cancer by modulating the tumor microenvironment and promoting immune evasion. The overexpression of FXR in neoplastic tissues, its association with immunoregulatory cell populations, and its correlation with poorer prognosis in our internal cohort highlight its potential relevance as a biomarker and therapeutic target. Given the immunosuppressive features associated with FXR activation, targeting this pathway may represent a promising strategy to improve the efficacy of immune checkpoint therapies in selected patients with intestinal-type gastric cancer.

Modulation of Pd-L1 expression in a murine model of metabolic liver injury.
