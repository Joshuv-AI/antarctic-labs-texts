Revision surgical technique for laterally placed MinuteMan® interspinous devices using a novel VertiFix® removal system through a spinous process-preserving midline approach with InSpan fusion
Authors: Sandra Thompson, Vito Lore, Erik Spayde, Taylor Headley, Jordan Acosta, Hope Estevez, Sukanya Chebrolu, Jason A Seale, Kingsley R Chin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe MinuteMan® interspinous fixation device (IFD) is a titanium implant designed for percutaneous lateral placement to provide interspinous distraction and stabilization in the treatment of lumbar spinal stenosis and interspinous fusion when combined with bone graft. With increasing use by interventional pain management physicians, standardized techniques for revision of failed lateral interspinous implants are needed. Revision is technically challenging due to dense scar tissue formation and limited access to the implant, particularly when preservation of the spinous processes is required to allow reinstrumentation. To our knowledge, there are no prior reports describing removal of a laterally placed IFD with revision using a midline interlaminar IFD. We describe a spinous process-preserving open midline surgical technique using the VertiFix® interspinous removal system for removal of a laterally placed IFD, followed by reinstrumentation with a midline interlaminar IFD (InSpan®) to facilitate interspinous fusion. The technique involves circumferential scar tissue release, controlled implant removal, decompression, and revision implant placement. Patients demonstrated 60% and 70% reductions in pain at three months. Revision was associated with implant size increases from 8 to 14 mm and 16 mm, with restoration of interspinous height. Postoperative imaging confirmed stable implant positioning without complications. This two-case technical report describes a feasible posterior midline revision approach for selected failed laterally placed IFDs. The technique may allow controlled implant removal and interspinous reinstrumentation when the posterior elements remain suitable; however, larger studies with longer follow-up and radiographic fusion assessment are needed to evaluate reproducibility, fusion, durability, and long-term clinical outcomes.Keywords: Interspinous fixation, revision spine surgery, lumbar spinal stenosis, minimally invasive spine surgery (MISS), interlaminar fixation

Highlight box

Surgical highlights

• Revision of laterally placed interspinous fixation devices is technically challenging due to dense scar tissue formation and limited implant access.

• A spinous process-preserving open midline technique using the VertiFix® system enables circumferential scar tissue release, controlled implant removal, decompression, and reinstrumentation.

• The technique allows conversion from a lateral to a midline interspinous fixation construct through a single approach.

What is conventional and what is novel/modified?

• Conventional interspinous fixation systems are typically placed via a midline approach, allowing direct visualization and preparation of the fusion bed.

• This study introduces a novel revision strategy that converts a laterally placed implant into a directly visualized posterior midline revision approach while preserving the spinous processes for reinstrumentation. Key technical elements include ligament release, implant resizing, and decortication of the lamina and facet joints with biologic graft augmentation.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Failed lateral interspinous implants should be assessed for whether spinous process-preserving revision and interspinous reinstrumentation remain feasible before selecting a more extensive revision strategy.

• Revision planning should account for implant loosening or migration, persistent stenosis, segmental instability, and structural integrity of the spinous processes.

• Dedicated interspinous removal instrumentation may facilitate controlled removal of a laterally placed failed implant and support a more consistent posterior midline revision approach.

Introduction

Background

Lumbar spine surgery has evolved from traditional open procedures to minimally invasive techniques that reduce tissue disruption and accelerate recovery (1-5). Historically, conditions such as spinal stenosis, spondylolisthesis, and degenerative disc disease were treated with open decompression procedures, including laminectomy and fusion, following the initial description of lumbar disc herniation by Mixter and Barr (6). Although effective for neural decompression, these approaches required extensive muscle dissection and were associated with prolonged recovery, increased blood loss, and higher perioperative morbidity (7,8).

Advances in surgical techniques and imaging have enabled minimally invasive spine surgery (MISS) approaches that preserve normal anatomy while achieving adequate decompression and stabilization (1,3,5,9). These techniques have been associated with reduced operative morbidity, postoperative pain, and shorter hospital stays compared with traditional open procedures (4,7). Within this evolution, interspinous fixation and stabilization devices have emerged as alternative treatment strategies for selected lumbar degenerative conditions (10-13).

More recently, interventional pain management (IPM) physicians have incorporated minimally invasive spinal stabilization procedures into clinical practice, including percutaneous lumbar decompression and interspinous fixation techniques (14-16). This reflects a broader trend toward less invasive spine care across specialties.

Devices such as the MinuteMan® interspinous fixation device (IFD) (Spinal Simplicity LLC, Overland Park, KS) gained popularity because they allow stabilization of the interspinous space through a lateral percutaneous fluoroscopically guided approach in selected patients (10-12,17). The technique involves serial dilation through the multifidus musculature followed by placement of an implant that distracts the interspinous space and stabilizes the motion segment. This minimally invasive outpatient procedure may be suitable for patients who are not optimal candidates for more extensive open surgery.

Rationale

Despite these advantages, important limitations of IFD placed laterally have been reported. The lateral approach limits direct access to key posterior bony structures, including the facets, lamina, and transverse processes which are critical for achieving robust osseous fusion. Consequently, fusion rates may be variable, and implant loosening or mechanical failure can occur in some patients (18,19). These complications may present clinically as recurrent pain, neurological symptoms, or spinal instability, often requiring revision surgery.

Revision of failed laterally placed interspinous devices presents distinct technical challenges. Loosening of these implants often results in exuberant scar tissue formation surrounding the implant and adjacent musculature. In the case of the MinuteMan® device, the lateral position of the locking nut further complicates removal, frequently necessitating increased muscle dissection and retraction. These factors increase procedural complexity and may contribute to increased surgical morbidity.

Midline interlaminar IFD such as the InSpan® interlaminar interspinous fixation device (LESSpine Innovations, Burlington, MA, USA) provide an alternative stabilization strategy that allows improved access to posterior spinal elements and facilitates interspinous fusion (20-22). This system uses a midline approach and incorporates bilateral locking set screws with a symmetrical plate design to enhance biomechanical stability (23). Midline placement also allows direct visualization and preparation of the interspinous space and adjacent bony structures, which may support improved fusion outcomes. In addition to implant design, advances in biologic graft materials, such as NanoFuse Biologics® (Amend Surgical Inc., Alachua, FL, USA), which combines bioactive glass with demineralized bone matrix, may enhance osteoconductivity and fusion potential when used with interspinous fixation systems (24,25).

Recent literature highlights the continued role of IFD in the treatment of lumbar degenerative conditions when appropriate patient selection and surgical technique are applied (20). However, standardized techniques for revision of failed lateral interspinous implants remain poorly described. To our knowledge, there are no prior reports describing removal of a laterally placed IFD with revision using a midline interlaminar IFD system.

Objective

This study describes an open midline technique using a dedicated interspinous removal system for removal of a failed lateral IFD while preserving the spinous processes, enabling reinstrumentation with a midline interlaminar IFD for interspinous and posterolateral fusion with biologic graft augmentation. Two cases illustrate the technical feasibility and application of this revision approach. We present this article in accordance with the SUPER reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0148/rc).

Preoperative preparations and requirements

Patients selected for this technique demonstrated symptomatic failure of a laterally placed IFD, defined as recurrent or worsening back and/or leg pain following an initial period of postoperative improvement. Both patients had failed at least six months of conservative management, including pharmacologic therapy and/or spinal injections. Revision was only considered when clinical symptoms correlated with imaging findings demonstrating implant-related complications, including loosening or migration (Figure 1A), persistent spinal stenosis, and preserved spinous process integrity adequate for reinstrumentation. Preoperative Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores were obtained to assess baseline pain severity. Contraindications to this technique included active infection, severe segmental instability requiring pedicle screw fixation, or inadequate spinous process integrity precluding interspinous fixation.

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the prone position on a radiolucent operating table. Fluoroscopy was utilized intraoperatively. The surgical setup included the VertiFix® interspinous removal system (NANISX LLC, Fort Lauderdale, FL, USA), decompression instruments including a decompression knife, rongeur and broach, and the InSpan® IFD. All procedures were performed by the primary author under the direct supervision of a board-certified orthopedic spine surgeon in an outpatient ambulatory surgery center. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the Western Institutional Review Board (WIRB®) now known as WIRB-Copernicus Group (WCG® IRB) (WIRB#20181251) and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Step-by-step description

Surgical technique

The posterior midline approach was selected to provide direct visualization of the implant and posterior elements while preserving the spinous processes for reinstrumentation.

Exposure

The procedure was performed using an open midline posterior approach. Under fluoroscopic guidance, a midline incision was made over the affected level and carried through the lumbodorsal fascia to expose the spinous processes. Subperiosteal dissection was performed using electrocautery to expose the posterior elements, including the spinous processes and lamina, while preserving the surrounding paraspinal musculature and ligamentous structures.

Scar tissue removal and implant exposure

Following exposure, scar tissue and adherent multifidus muscle fibers surrounding the implant were carefully released (Figure 1B). The implant was palpated along the lateral border, and a cephalocaudal sweep was performed to establish a plane between the implant and surrounding musculature. The VertiFix® decompression knife was then advanced ventral and dorsal to the implant to achieve circumferential release of scar tissue. A Leksell rongeur was subsequently used to remove residual fibrous tissue from the superior and lateral surfaces of the implant, facilitating exposure of the lateral locking nut. This stepwise dissection enabled progressive mobilization of the implant while preserving the integrity of the spinous processes.

Implant removal

After complete circumferential release of the surrounding scar tissue and residual adhesions using dedicated instruments from the VertiFix® interspinous removal system, the implant was mobilized and extracted through controlled, sequential mobilization (Figure 2, Video 1). Electrocautery was used selectively as needed while maintaining protection of adjacent soft tissues and neural elements. Care was taken to avoid levering against the cephalad or caudal spinous processes or applying excessive force, which could result in spinous process fracture. No gross osseous erosion of the superior or inferior spinous processes was observed intraoperatively, and structural integrity was maintained despite mild surface changes. Preservation of the spinous processes was prioritized to allow subsequent placement of a replacement IFD.

Decompression and fusion bed preparation

Midline decompression was performed with resection of the posterior ligamentum flavum to achieve neural decompression (Figure 3A). The interspinous space was then distracted using a sizing instrument under fluoroscopic guidance to determine the appropriate InSpan implant size and restore interspinous height. Decortication of the spinous processes was performed to prepare the interspinous fusion bed (Figure 3B).

Case 1. Fluoroscopic lateral views of the VertiFix® interspinous removal system. (A) Decompression knife positioned between spinous process and multifidus muscle. (B) Broach positioned between the spinous processes following advancement through residual scar tissue and interspinous ligaments, preparing the interspinous fusion bed for bone graft.

InSpan implant placement

The InSpan IFD was prepared by packing with autograft and NanoFuse Biologics into the implant hub. The device was inserted between the spinous processes and advanced under fluoroscopic guidance until seated anteriorly between the laminae (Figure 4). The implant was compressed against the spinous processes and secured using bilateral locking set screws. A pull-up test was performed to confirm stable fixation of the implant (20). The dorsal surfaces of the facet joints were decorticated bilaterally with the broach (Figure 5). Autograft mixed with synthetic biologic graft material was placed above the implant hub and posterolaterally over the decorticated lamina and facet joints to promote fusion.

Wound closure and postoperative care

The surgical site was irrigated with antibiotic solution prior to closure. Layered wound closure was performed including fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin. A sterile dressing was applied.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

Postoperatively, patients were monitored for neurologic status, wound healing, and implant-related complications. Follow-up radiographs were obtained to assess implant positioning and interspinous distraction. Patients were followed clinically and VAS was collected at three months postoperatively.

Tips and pearls

Successful execution of this technique depends on thorough scar tissue release to achieve adequate implant mobilization. This step is critical for controlled extraction and facilitates identification, exposure and release of the lateral locking mechanism. Careful finger dissection along the lateral border of the implant can assist in confirming separation from surrounding musculature and establishing a safe working plane. Care should be taken to avoid excessive force during implant extraction, as this may result in spinous process fracture. Over-resection of posterior elements should likewise be avoided, as this may destabilize the spinal segment and necessitate more extensive posterior instrumentation or fusion.

The midline approach provides direct access to the spinous processes and facilitates their preservation, which is essential for placement of a replacement IFD. Preparation of the interspinous fusion bed, including decortication of the lamina and facet joints with placement of biologic graft material, may enhance the likelihood of successful arthrodesis.

Revision cases may require placement of larger interspinous implants compared with the primary procedure, likely due to prior undersizing and incomplete distraction. Consideration should also be given to the potential need for larger implant hub sizes in revision settings, particularly when spinous processes are enlarged or altered by scar tissue.

Discussion

Surgical highlights

Revision of failed lateral IFD presents significant technical challenges related to implant design, scar tissue formation, and limited surgical access. In this study, we describe a novel open midline technique using the VertiFix® system for removal of a laterally placed MinuteMan® IFD while preserving the spinous processes, enabling reinstrumentation with a midline IFD (InSpan®).

In both cases, the technique allowed circumferential scar tissue release and controlled implant removal with preservation of the posterior elements. Patients demonstrated early improvement in pain, with VAS reductions of 60% and 70% at three months. Radiographic evaluation confirmed stable implant positioning and restoration of interspinous distraction without complications.

A potential key technical advantage of this approach is preservation of the spinous processes, which may help maintain posterior structural integrity and allow placement of a larger replacement implant in selected patients without requiring additional posterior instrumentation. The ability to perform decompression, stabilization, and fusion through a single midline approach may be useful in selected reveision cases when the posterior elements remain suitable for reinstrumentation. These technical objectives are consistent with the principles of Less Exposure Spine Surgery (LESS™), including direct visualization, limited soft-tissue disruption, and preservation of native posterior spinal anatomy.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strength of this study is the description of a stepwise surgical technique for revision of laterally placed IFDs, an area that remains poorly described in the literature. The use of VertiFix® dedicated instrumentation facilitated controlled implant removal with the goal of limiting disruption to surrounding tissues and preserving the spinous processes for reinstrumentation.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. This report includes only two patients treated by a single physician under the supervision of a board-certified orthopedic spine surgeon, which may limit generalizability and reproducibility across different practice settings. Follow-up was limited to three months; therefore, long-term durability, recurrent symptoms, and successful arthrodesis could not be assessed. Radiographic fusion assessment and functional outcome measures such as Oswestry Disability Index were not available. Finally, this report does not compare the described approach with alternative revision strategies, including implant removal alone, decompression without reinstrumentation, posterolateral fusion, or pedicle screw-based fixation.

Future studies involving larger patient cohorts, multiple surgeons, standardized functional outcome measures, radiographic fusion assessment, comparison groups, and longer follow-up periods will be necessary to further evaluate reproducibility, durability, safety, fusion, and long-term clinical effectiveness.

Comparison with other surgical techniques and research

Interspinous fixation systems have been increasingly utilized as an alternative to traditional pedicle screw-based fusion in selected patients with lumbar degenerative disease (10-13,19). Several studies have demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes with interspinous fixation when combined with decompression and appropriate patient selection (20-22,26,27).

Recent comparative studies have suggested that interspinous fixation may achieve similar clinical outcomes to more invasive fusion procedures while reducing operative morbidity. These techniques have also been adopted by IPM physicians performing minimally invasive spine procedures in ambulatory surgery centers (14,15).

Most interspinous fixation systems described in the literature utilize a midline approach for implantation (28). In contrast, the MinuteMan® device is uniquely designed for lateral percutaneous placement through the multifidus musculature (8,12,17). From a revision-planning perspective, lateral and midline interspinous fixation approaches differ in exposure, implant access, and access to the posterior elements. In this report, these differences are presented as technical considerations rather than evidence of clinical superiority of one approach over another.

While this approach offers a minimally invasive alternative, it preserves the interspinous and supraspinous ligaments, which may limit the ability to achieve adequate interspinous distraction and optimal implant sizing. In the present cases, revision to a midline interspinous fixation system following ligament release allowed for substantially increased implant sizing, suggesting that preservation of the posterior ligamentous complex during lateral implantation may contribute to undersizing and incomplete distraction.

Device failure and nonunion remain recognized complications of interspinous fixation systems (18,19). Despite this, there is limited literature describing revision techniques, particularly for laterally placed devices. The present study contributes to the literature by describing a midline revision approach that allows controlled removal of a lateral implant while preserving the spinous processes for replacement fixation.

Implications and actions recommended

The technique described in this study has several important clinical implications. First, it provides a stepwise method for removal of laterally placed IFDs while preserving the posterior elements, which is critical for enabling reinstrumentation with another interspinous device. Alternative revision strategies may include implant removal alone, decompression without reinstrumentation, conversion to pedicle screw-based posterior fusion, or decompression with posterolateral fusion, depending on the degree of stenosis, instability, bone quality, sagittal alignment, and integrity of the spinous processes. The technique described in this report represents one revision option for selected patients in whom the spinous processes remain structurally adequate for reinstrumentation and should not be interpreted as the preferred approach for all failed interspinous implants.

Second, the midline approach allows direct visualization of the posterior spinal anatomy, facilitating decompression, preparation of the fusion bed, and placement of biologic graft material while minimizing disruption to surrounding tissues. In addition, appropriate biologic augmentation may play an important role in achieving successful fusion in interspinous fixation procedures. In the cases presented, absence of bone graft at the index procedure may have contributed to implant loosening and failure. The use of biologic materials, including bioactive glass combined with demineralized bone matrix, may enhance osteoconductivity and support arthrodesis when combined with adequate fusion bed preparation (24). These cases highlight the need to consider graft placement and fusion bed preparation when attempting to achieve arthrodesis.

Removal of the interspinous and supraspinous ligaments during the revision procedure may allow greater interspinous distraction and improved implant seating; however, whether this translates into improved stability, fusion, or clinical outcomes requires further study.

As the use of IFDs continues to expand across specialties, the development of standardized revision techniques will become increasingly important. Dedicated instrumentation, such as the VertiFix® system, may facilitate a more controlled revision workflow, although safety and reproducibility will require evaluation in larger cohorts with longer follow-up.

Conclusions

This report describes a spinous process-preserving open midline technique using VertiFix® instruments for removal of a laterally placed MinuteMan® IFD, followed by reinstrumentation with an InSpan interspinous fixation device. In two revision cases, the technique permitted controlled scar tissue release, implant removal, decompression, and replacement fixation while preserving the spinous processes. Early postoperative findings demonstrate improved VAS pain scores and stable implant positioning at 3 months, without perioperative complications. As the use of IFD continues to expand, the development of standardized revision techniques will become increasingly important. Further studies with larger patient cohorts, radiographic fusion assessment and longer follow-up are needed to evaluate the effectiveness, reproducibility, and broader clinical applicability of this technique.
