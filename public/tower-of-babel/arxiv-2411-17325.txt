Trace inequality for B ​ V ​ ( Ω ) BV(\Omega) in smooth domains
José C. Sabina de Lis

Abstract
. This work is devoted to prove an optimum version of the trace inequality associated to the embedding B ​ V ​ ( Ω ) ⊂ L 1 ​ ( ∂ Ω ) BV(\Omega)\subset L^{1}(\partial\Omega) . Special emphasis is placed on the regularity that the domain Ω \Omega should exhibit for this result to be valid.

1.
Introduction

It has been known for a long time that an inequality of the form,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u|\leq C_{1}\int_{\Omega}|Du|+C_{2}\int_{\Omega}|u|,

(1.1)

C_{i}>0
,
i=1,2
, is satisfied for the functions
u
belonging to the space

W^{1,1}(\Omega)
provided
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain (see the pioneering work

[
10
]
, also
[
23
]
,

[
26
]
and
[
1
]
, the later for more regular domains). It expresses the continuity of the embedding

W^{1,1}(\Omega)\subset L^{1}(\partial\Omega)
while
u
in the surface
integral has the status of the ‘trace’ (the restriction) of the function on the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Moreover it follows from a standard approximation argument (see Theorem
7
below)
that (
1.1
) extends to the space
BV(\Omega)
(Section
2
) with exactly the same values
of the constants
C_{i}
. In this context, a more ambitious version of (
1.1
) was stated
in
[
4
, Sec. 3]
by allowing domains
\Omega
whose boundary satisfies
\mathcal{H}_{N-1}(\partial\Omega)<\infty
,

\mathcal{H}_{N-1}
standing for the
N-1
dimensional Hausdorff measure, and exhibits a suitable local behavior
(see
[
2
]
for a more recent account).

With respect to the constant
C_{1}
in (
1.1
), a preliminary analysis reveals that it must satisfy the restriction

C_{1}\geq 1
(Section
2
). This fact highly contrasts with the corresponding inequality for the exponent
p>1
,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u|^{p}\leq\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{p}+C_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{p},

which holds for every
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
, and where
\varepsilon>0
can be taken as small as desired.

Moreover, provided that
\Omega
is
C^{1}
then it can be shown that (
[
22
]
and Section
2
below),

\inf{C_{1}}=1,

(1.2)

where the infimum is extended to all constants
C_{1}>0
such that inequality (
1.1
) holds for some
C_{2}>0
.
A similar statement in this direction had been earlier suggested in
[
4
]
by considering a wider class
of domains encompassing the Lipschitzian ones. In this case unity in (
1.2
) should be replaced by a larger
minimizing constant (see Example
8
below).

It is therefore a natural question to decide if the better possible value
C_{1}=1
could be placed in (
1.1
).
This paper is devoted to elucidate the optimum smoothness requirements on
\partial\Omega
under which the answer is affirmative.
For the time being, we anticipate that it is not enough for

\Omega
to be merely
C^{1}
(not even
C^{1,\alpha}
for every
0<\alpha<1
) in order that this
feature to be true.

On the other hand, to clarify this technical subtlety turns out to be crucial for the validity of a
further important property. Namely, the lower semicontinuity in
BV(\Omega)
of the functional,

J(u)=\int_{\Omega}|Du|+\int_{\partial\Omega}F(x,u),

(1.3)

with respect to
L^{1}(\Omega)
. Provided that
F
is Lipschitzian in
u
with a constant
not greater than unity it was shown in
[
21
]
that the property holds true (see Theorem
6
below). Most importantly, the
proof in
[
21
]
requires
the validity of inequality (
1.1
) exactly with the critical value
C_{1}=1
. Author just reports (
[
21
, p. 493]
):
“
Let us remark that
C_{1}=1
because
\partial\Omega
is smooth
”.
He quotes
[
4
]
as a source where however there is no any
reference to this precise concern (see also Remark
5
below).

The importance of the lower semicontinuity of
J
stated in
[
21
]

becomes evident when dealing with boundary value problems involving the
one–Laplacian operator
\Delta_{1}u=\text{\rm div}\left(\dfrac{Du}{|Du|}\right)
. Specially,
when flow–type conditions are imposed on
\partial\Omega
. See for instance
[
6
]
,
[
20
]
,

[
3
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
5
]
.
In most of these works such a property is
invoked under the assumption “
\Omega
is smooth”. Nevertheless, it is widely
believed that the semicontinuity of
J
still holds
true if
\Omega
is just
C^{1}
. Thus, it should be desirable to get an insight on the
precise smoothness
degree required to
\partial\Omega
for this property to be fulfilled. As a matter of fact, it will
be also seen in Section
4
that class
C^{1}
is not enough.

The work is organized as follows. Section
2.1
recalls the background on the space

needed in this work. The geometry of the domain
\Omega

). It is discussed in Section

]

2.
Basic and technical previous results

2.1.
Functions of bounded variation

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
be a bounded domain where
L^{1}(\Omega)
designates the standard space of Lebesgue
integrable functions. As a proper subspace,
BV(\Omega)\subset L^{1}(\Omega)
stands for the functions
of bounded variation. Namely, those
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
whose gradient
Du
in distributional sense defines
a Radon measure. By designating as
\|Du\|
its associated ‘total variation’ measure it is also
required that
\|Du\|(\Omega)<\infty
. Such a number is often represented
as,

\|Du\|(\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}|Du|.

Space
BV(\Omega)
becomes complete (Banach) when endowed with the norm

\|u\|_{BV(\Omega)}=\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|Du\|(\Omega)
.
As a further concept linked to the theory, it is said that
E\subset\Omega
is a set of finite perimeter in
\Omega
if

\chi_{E}\in BV(\Omega)
,
\chi_{E}
designating the characteristic function of
E
.
In this case
\|D\chi_{E}\|(\Omega)
defines the relative perimeter
P(E,\Omega)
of
E
with
respect to
\Omega
. Let just remark to clarify that provided
Q\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is
a Lispchitz domain with
Q\cap\Omega
nonempty then
P(Q\cap\Omega,\Omega)
is given by

\mathcal{H}_{N-1}(\partial Q\cap\Omega)
. This quantity is
also denoted as
|\partial Q\cap\Omega|_{N-1}
.

By assuming that
\Omega
is a Lipschitz domain then
BV(\Omega)
possess a trace operator

\gamma:BV(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{1}(\partial\Omega)
which coincides with the restriction of functions
to
\partial\Omega
when observed in
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
. By an abuse of notation
u
will be used instead of
\gamma(u)
to mean
the trace of
u
in
\partial\Omega
. The trace operator is continuous when convergence
in
BV(\Omega)
is observed in the weaker sense of the
strict topology
. Namely,
u_{n}\to u

strictly in
BV(\Omega)
if
u_{n}\to u
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
while
\|Du_{n}\|(\Omega)\to\|Du\|(\Omega)
(
[
2
]
).
Strict topology turns out to be quite convenient when dealing with approximation affairs in
BV(\Omega)
.
On the other hand, inequality (
1.1
) extends easily to
BV(\Omega)
in the way described in the
proof of Theorem
7
below.

2.2.
Tubular neighborhoods

The analysis in Section
3
makes use of a smart coordinate system defined near
\partial\Omega
.
It is formed through a suitable family of normal segments which fills the space surrounding the
boundary of
\Omega
. To this aim we are introducing some few definitions and corresponding
properties.

Assume that
\Omega
is a bounded domain such that
\partial\Omega
is a compact
C^{1}
and
N-1
dimensional manifold.
Then it is possible to define a unit normal field
{\mathbf{n}}
attached to
\partial\Omega
which is directed towards
\Omega
.
We are assuming that
\partial\Omega
is oriented with such a field
{\mathbf{n}}
.

Any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
has at least a closer point
y\in\partial\Omega
which means that
d(x)=|x-y|
where,

d(x)=\text{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)=\inf_{y^{\prime}\in\partial\Omega}|x-y^{\prime}|.

To every
y\in\partial\Omega
a number
r(y)
, the
reach
of
y
(
[
9
]
), is defined as,

r(y)=\\
\sup\{r>0:\,\text{$\forall x\in B(y,r)$ a unique
$z\in\partial\Omega$ exists such}\\
\text{that $d(x)=|x-z|$}\}.

Geometrically,
r(y)>0
entails the existence of an inner and an outer tangent ball to
\partial\Omega
at
y
,

\overline{B}\setminus{\{{y}\}}\subset\Omega
and
\overline{B^{\prime}}\setminus{\{{y}\}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
respectively,
both of them with a radius
0<r<r(y)
. Thus
r(y)^{-1}
may be regarded as a sort of generalized
curvature of
\partial\Omega
at
y
while
r(y)
has the status of a curvature radius.

It follows from the implicit function theorem that
r(y)>0
on
\partial\Omega
provided it is a
C^{2}
manifold.
On the contrary,
r
could possibly vanish
somewhere in
\partial\Omega
if it is only of class
C^{1,\alpha}
for
0<\alpha<1
. An example of this instance is presented
in Remark
7
. That is why the next statement may be regarded as
optimal
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is a bounded
C^{1}
domain, i. e.
\partial\Omega
is a compact and
C^{1}

N-1
dimensional
manifold. Then, the following assertions are equivalent:

r(y)>0
for every
y\in\partial\Omega
,

\partial\Omega
is
C^{1,1}
, i. e.
\Omega
is a class
C^{1,1}
bounded domain.

That b) is sufficient for a) is shown either in
[
9
, Lem. 4.11]
or
[
18
, Lem. 2.1]
.
For the necessity of b) reader is referred to
[
18
, Th. 4.1]
(cf. also
[
17
, Cor. 2]
).

It can be further shown that function
r(y)
is continuous. Since
\partial\Omega
is compact it follows that,

r_{\partial\Omega}:=\inf_{y\in\partial\Omega}r(y)>0,

whenever option a) holds. Moreover,

r_{\partial\Omega}^{-1}=\sup_{x,y\in\partial\Omega\,,x\neq y}\frac{|{\mathbf{n}}(x)-{\mathbf{n}}(y)|}{|x-y|}.

If this is the case, a projection mapping,

\pi:{\mathcal{U}}\longrightarrow\partial\Omega,

is defined such that
|x-\pi(x)|=d(x)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{U}}
, where
{\mathcal{U}}={\{{x:d(x)<r_{\partial\Omega}}\}}
.
The more important features linking the previous definitions are gathered in
the following statement.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
be a bounded
C^{1,1}
domain, i. e.

\partial\Omega
constitutes a
C^{1,1}
and compact
N-1
dimensional manifold which is
oriented with the inward unit normal
{\mathbf{n}}
. Then,

\pi\in C^{0,1}({\mathcal{U}},{\mathbb{R}}^{N})
.

For each
y\in\partial\Omega
,
\pi^{-1}(y)={\{{y+t{\mathbf{n}}(y):\ |t|<r_{\partial\Omega}}\}}
.

If
{\mathcal{N}}={\{{(p,v):\,p\in\partial\Omega\,,v\in\text{\rm span}\,{\{{{\mathbf{n}}(p)}\}}}\}}
stands
for the normal bundle to
\partial\Omega
then the mapping

\begin{array}[]{cccl}{H}:&\ {{\mathcal{U}}}&\longrightarrow&\ {{\mathcal{N}}}\\
&\ {x}&\longmapsto&\ {(y,v)=(\pi(x),x-\pi(x)),}\end{array}

defines a Lipschitz homeomorphism onto its image, with Lipschitz inverse
defined as
H^{-1}(y,v)=y+v
.

The
signed
distance,

\tilde{d}(x)=(x-\pi(x))\cdot{\mathbf{n}}(\pi(x))=\begin{cases}\,d(x),&\qquad x\in\overline{\Omega},\\
-d(x),&\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\end{cases}

is
C^{1}
in
{\mathcal{U}}
.

Proof.

Assertions i), ii) and iii) are consequence of items 8), 2) and 13) in
[
9
, Th. 4.8]

while the fact that
\tilde{d}
is
C^{1}
in
{\mathcal{U}}
is the conclusion of
[
15
, Th. 1]

which is in fact the main achievement of this reference.
∎

Theorem
2
implies that
{\mathcal{U}}
becomes a tubular neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
(
[
13
]
) where
the coordinates of a point
x
are
(\pi(x),x-\pi(x))
. Alternatively, they can be expressed in the
more manageable form
(\pi(x),\tilde{d}(x))
where vector
x-\pi(x)
is replaced
by the number
\tilde{d}(x)
.

Let now
{\{{(g_{i},U_{i})}\}}
be a finite atlas for
\partial\Omega
. This means that for
every
{1}\leq{i}\leq{m}
the set
U_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N-1}

is open,
g_{i}\in C^{1,1}(U_{i},{\mathbb{R}}^{N})
is one to one and
\text{rank}\,Dg_{i}(s)=N-1
for
s\in U_{i}
.
Finally,

\partial\Omega=\cup_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}(U_{i}).

Define,

\begin{array}[]{cccl}{h_{i}}:&\ {U_{i}\times(-r_{\partial\Omega},r_{\partial\Omega})\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}}&\longrightarrow&\ {{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\\
&\ {(s,t)}&\longmapsto&\ {h_{i}(s,t)=g_{i}(s)+t{\mathbf{n}}(g(s_{i})).}\end{array}

(2.4)

Then
h_{i}
constitutes a Lipschitz homeomorphism from
U_{i}\times(-r_{\partial\Omega},r_{\partial\Omega})

onto its image
V_{i}\subset{\mathcal{U}}
, with a Lipschitz inverse
h_{i}^{-1}
. Furthermore,

{\mathcal{U}}=\cup_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}.

Therefore, pair
(s,t)
represents the local expression in
V_{i}
of the tubular coordinates
(\pi(x),\tilde{d}(x))

introduced above. In fact,
g_{i}(s)=\pi(x)
,
t=\tilde{d}(x)
for
x\in V_{i}
.

2.3.
Optimum constant in (
1.1
)

We begin by observing a basic estimate for
C_{1}
. In the next result
\Omega
is only assumed
to be Lipschitzian.

Lemma 3
.

Assume that
\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then, constant
C_{1}
in the trace inequality (
1.1
)
satisfies the lower estimate
C_{1}\geq 1
.

Proof.

If
\Omega
is Lipschitizan then there exists an increasing sequence

\Omega_{n}\subset\subset\Omega_{n+1}\subset\subset\Omega
of
C^{\infty}
strict
subdomains of
\Omega
such that
\chi_{\Omega_{n}}\to\chi_{\Omega}
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
,
while,

P(\Omega_{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{N})\to P(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{N}).

See for instance
[
19
, Th. 1]
. Define
u=u_{n}=\chi_{Q_{n}}
,

Q_{n}=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{n}
,
\chi_{Q_{n}}
meaning the characteristic
function of
Q_{n}
. By inserting
u_{n}
in (
1.1
) we get,

\mathcal{H}_{N-1}(\partial\Omega)\leq C_{1}\int_{\Omega}|D\chi_{Q_{n}}|+C_{2}|Q_{n}|=C_{1}P(Q_{n},\Omega)+C_{2}|Q_{n}|,

where
|Q_{n}|
designates the Lebesgue measure of
Q_{n}
. Then,

\mathcal{H}_{N-1}(\partial\Omega)\leq C_{1}P(\Omega_{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{N})+C_{2}|Q_{n}|.

We achieve
C_{1}\geq 1
after taking limits.
∎

Remarks 1
.

Estimate in Lemma 1 is obtained in
[
22
]
when
\Omega
is a smooth domain.
For the sake of completeness, it is shown in Section
3
that an argument as the one in
Lemma
3
can be carried out with more regular functions
u_{n}
provided

\Omega
is
C^{1}
.

Next statement asserts that
C_{1}
can be chosen as close to unity as desired when
\Omega

is
C^{1}
(
[
22
, Th. 1.7]
). For the interested reader, an independent proof of the
result can be given with the tools of Section
3
and is therefore presented
there.

Theorem 4
.

Assume that
\Omega
is a bounded
C^{1}
domain. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C_{2}=C_{2}(\varepsilon)>0

such that,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\int_{\Omega}|Du|+C_{2}\int_{\Omega}|u|,

(2.5)

holds for all
u\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
.

Remark 2
.

As it appears in
[
22
]
, Theorem
4
is not properly stated there
since it is asserted to be also valid for
piecewise

C^{1}
domains.
The proof argued in this work can only be valid if
\Omega
is
C^{1}
.
Indeed, for a piecewise
C^{1}
but Lipschitz domain,
Example
8
below shows that the optimum constant
C_{1}
is bounded away from
unity.

We now analyze an specific case where the best value
C_{1}=1
is achieved. Namely, the
situation where
\Omega
is either a ball or an annulus. It is already addressed in
[
22
]

in the space
W^{1,1}(\Omega)
.
A direct proof in the spirit of our general result in Section
3
is next included
for completeness.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\Omega
be either of the radially symmetric domains
B:={\{{|x|<b}\}}
(a ball) or
A:={\{{a<|x|<b}\}}

(an annulus). Then, the inequality (
1.1
) adopts the form,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u|\leq\int_{\Omega}|Du|+\frac{Na^{N-1}+Nb^{N-1}}{b^{N}-a^{N}}\int_{\Omega}|u|,\qquad\text{for all $u\in BV(\Omega)$,}

(2.6)

where it is set
a=0
in the case of the ball.

Proof.

By the reasons explained in the forthcoming Theorem
7
it is enough with considering

u\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
. In addition, the proof in the case of the annulus essentially encompasses
the one of the ball. Thus we only deal with
\Omega={\{{a<|x|<b}\}}
where
0<a<b
.

By employing polar coordinates we write:

v(r,y)=u(ry),\qquad r\geq 0\,,\quad|y|=1.

Thus,

-v(a,y)=-v(r,y)+\int_{a}^{r}v^{\prime}_{r}(t,y)\ dt,

what implies,

|v(a,y)|\leq|v(r,y)|+\int_{a}^{r}|v^{\prime}_{r}(t,y)|\ dt.

Then, we get by integration,

\int_{|y|=1}\int_{a}^{b}|v(a,y)|r^{N-1}\,dr\,dS_{y}\leq\int_{|y|=1}\int_{a}^{b}|v(r,y)|r^{N-1}\,dr\,dS_{y}\\
+\int_{|y|=1}\int_{a}^{b}\left(\int_{a}^{r}|v^{\prime}_{r}(t,y)|\ dt\right)r^{N-1}\,dr\,dS_{y},

dS_{y}
designating the surface area element on the unitary sphere
{\{{|y|=1}\}}
.
By changing the integration order in the last integral we arrive to,

\frac{b^{N}-a^{N}}{Na^{N-1}}\int_{|x|=a}|u|\leq\int_{\Omega}|u|+\frac{1}{N}\int_{|y|=1}\int_{a}^{b}\left(b^{N}-t^{N}\right)|v^{\prime}_{r}(t,y)|\,dt\,dS_{y}.

(2.7)

A symmetric argument starting at,

v(b,y)=v(r,y)+\int_{r}^{b}v^{\prime}_{r}(t,y)\ dt,

leads to the inequality,

\frac{b^{N}-a^{N}}{Nb^{N-1}}\int_{|x|=b}|u|\leq\int_{\Omega}|u|+\frac{1}{N}\int_{|y|=1}\int_{a}^{b}\left(t^{N}-a^{N}\right)|v^{\prime}_{r}(t,y)|\,dt\,dS_{y}.

(2.8)

By adding the inequalities (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) we obtain,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u|\leq\frac{Na^{N-1}+Nb^{N-1}}{b^{N}-a^{N}}\int_{\Omega}|u|\\
+\int_{|y|=1}\int_{a}^{b}\left(\frac{a^{N-1}(b^{N}-t^{N})+b^{N-1}(t^{N}-a^{N})}{b^{N}-a^{N}}\right)|v^{\prime}_{r}(t,y)|\,dt\,dS_{y}.

(2.9)

It is claimed now that,

\frac{a^{N-1}(b^{N}-t^{N})+b^{N-1}(t^{N}-a^{N})}{b^{N}-a^{N}}\leq t^{N-1},

(2.10)

for all
{a}\leq{t}\leq{b}
. Then, estimate (
2.6
) follows from (
2.9
), (
2.10
) and the fact that

|v^{\prime}_{r}(t,y)|=|Du(ry)|\,,\qquad{a}\leq{r}\leq{b}\,,\quad|y|=1.

To show the claim define the function
\varphi(t)
as the difference: “
t^{N-1}
minus the left hand side of
(
2.10
)”. Then
\varphi
vanishes at
t=a,b
, has a unique critical point
a<t_{c}<b
,

t_{c}=\frac{N-1}{N}\frac{b^{N}-a^{N}}{b^{N-1}-a^{N-1}},

while
\varphi^{\prime}(a)>0
and
\varphi^{\prime}(b)<0
. All these facts together prove the claim.
∎

Remark 3
.

By taking
u=1
in the trace inequality (
1.1
) it is found that constant
C_{2}
must satisfy the lower estimate,

C_{2}\geq\dfrac{\mathcal{H}_{N-1}(\partial\Omega)|}{|\Omega|}.

A nice feature of (

) is that the optimum values both for

and

2.4.

3.
The trace inequality

We are next stating the main result. Recall that the key point is the possibility of taking the unity
as the constant multiplying the second integral in (
3.11
).

Theorem 7
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
be a bounded
C^{1,1}
domain.
Then, there exists a constant
C
only depending
on
\Omega
such that the inequality,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u|\leq\int_{\Omega}|Du|+C\int_{\Omega}|u|,

(3.11)

holds for each
u\in BV(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Thanks to the version of Meyer–Serrin theorem extended to
BV(\Omega)
(
[
8
, Th. 2.2.2]
) an arbitrary function
u\in BV(\Omega)

can be approached in the strict topology of
BV
(see Section
2.1
) by a sequence of functions
u_{n}\in BV(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
This means by definition that,

\int_{\Omega}|u-u_{n}|\to 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{\Omega}|Du_{n}|\to\int_{\Omega}|Du|.

The continuity of the trace operator with respect to the strict topology (
[
2
]
) then also implies that,

\lim\int_{\partial\Omega}|u-u_{n}|=0.

On the other hand, since a smooth domain as
\Omega
satisfies either the ‘segment property’ (
[
1
]
) or alternatively the ‘graph
property’ (
[
26
]
), then any function
u\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
can be approached in this space by a sequence of functions in
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})

(by definition the restrictions to
\overline{\Omega}
of functions in
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
). Therefore, to show (
3.11
) it can be assumed
from the start that
u\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
.

We are first assuming that
\Omega
is
C^{2}
. Accordingly, we select a
C^{2}
family

{\{{(g_{i},U_{i})}\}}_{i=1,\dots,m}
of charts for
\partial\Omega
, exhibiting the properties detailed in Section
2.2
.
These charts can be furthermore arranged such that,

{\mathbf{n}}_{|g_{i}(U_{i})}=\frac{{g_{i}}_{s_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge{g_{i}}_{s_{N-1}}}{|{g_{i}}_{s_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge{g_{i}}_{s_{N-1}}|},

for all
{1}\leq{i}\leq{m}
. In this expression
{g_{i}}_{s_{k}}
stands for the partial derivative
\partial_{s_{k}}g_{i}
while the numerator
consists in the exterior product of all these derivatives.

By replacing
r_{\partial\Omega}
in (
2.4
) by a possibly smaller number
\varepsilon>0
, mappings,

\begin{array}[]{cccl}{h_{i}}:&\ {U_{i}\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}}&\longrightarrow&\ {{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\\
&\ {(s,t)}&\longmapsto&\ {h_{i}(s,t)=g_{i}(s)+t{\mathbf{n}}(g(s_{i}))}\end{array},

(3.12)

now define
C^{1}
diffeomorphisms onto their images,

V_{i}=h_{i}(U_{i}\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)).

They satisfy,

\Omega\cap V_{i}=h_{i}(U_{i}\times(0,\varepsilon)),

while,

{\mathcal{U}}_{\varepsilon}=\cup_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}\supset\partial\Omega,

becomes tubular neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
of size
\varepsilon
.
On the other hand, an open
V_{0}\subset\overline{V}_{0}\subset\Omega
can be found so that

{\{{V_{i}}\}}_{{0}\leq{i}\leq{m}}
constitutes a finite covering of
\overline{\Omega}
.

To complete this preparatory phase of the proof we introduce a partition of unity
subordinated to the covering
{\{{V_{i}}\}}_{{0}\leq{i}\leq{m}}
of
\overline{\Omega}
. It consists on
a family
{\{{\varphi_{i}}\}}_{{0}\leq{i}\leq{m}}
of
C^{\infty}
functions such that
{0}\leq{\varphi_{i}}\leq{1}

and every
\varphi_{i}
is supported in
V_{i}
. In addition,

\sum_{i=0}^{m}\varphi_{i}=1.

Thus, we can express
u\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
in the form,

u=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\varphi_{i}u=:\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}.

We now show that for every
{1}\leq{i}\leq{m}
the function
u_{i}
satisfies an inequality of
the form,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{i}|\leq\int_{\Omega}|Du_{i}|+C_{i}\int_{\Omega}|u_{i}|,

(3.13)

where the constant
C_{i}
depends on the mean curvature of
\partial\Omega
. To prove (
3.13
)
let us write,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{i}|=\int_{\partial\Omega\cap V_{i}}|u_{i}|=\int_{U_{i}}|u_{i}(s,t)|J(s,0)\ ds.

(3.14)

We are going to describe in more detail the coefficient
J(s,0)
in the surface area element.
Here and by an abuse of notation we have represented,

u_{i}(s,t)=u_{i}(h_{i}(s,t))=u_{i}(g_{i}(s)+t{\mathbf{n}}(s)),\qquad 0\leq t<\varepsilon,

where
{\mathbf{n}}(s)
stands for
{\mathbf{n}}(g_{i}(s))
.
Remark now that,

Dh_{i}(s,t)=\text{\rm col\,}(g_{s_{1}}+t{\mathbf{n}}_{s_{1}},\dots,g_{s_{N-1}}+t{\mathbf{n}}_{s_{N-1}},{\mathbf{n}}),

where subindex
i
in
g
is dropped to short,
g_{s_{j}}=\partial_{s_{j}}g
and
‘
col
’ is employed to describe the subsequent matrix by columns. If it is denoted,

J(s,t)=\det Dh_{i}(s,t),

then we observe that,

J(s,0)=\det\text{\rm col\,}(g_{s_{1}},\dots,g_{s_{N-1}},{\mathbf{n}})=|{g_{i}}_{s_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge{g_{i}}_{s_{N-1}}|.

(3.15)

Thus
J(s,t)
can be assumed to be positive for
\varepsilon
small. Moreover,
J(s,0)\ ds
is just the
expression of the surface area element on
\partial\Omega\cap V_{i}
.

On the other hand,

-u_{i}(s,0)J(s,0)=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}(u_{i}(s,t)J(s,t))\ dt\\
=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}u_{i}(s,t)J(s,t)\ dt+\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}u_{i}(s,t)\partial_{t}J(s,t)\ dt.

(3.16)

Thus,

|u_{i}(s,0)|J(s,0)\leq\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}|\partial_{t}u_{i}(s,t)|J(s,t)\ dt\\
+\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}|u_{i}(s,t)|\frac{|\partial_{t}J(s,t)|}{J(s,t)}J(s,t)\ dt.

(3.17)

By integrating in
U_{i}
with respect to
s
we obtain,

\int_{U_{i}}|u_{i}(s,0)|J(s,0)\ ds\leq\int_{U_{i}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}|\partial_{t}u_{i}(s,t)|J(s,t)\ dt\ ds\\
+\int_{U_{i}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}|u_{i}(s,t)|\frac{|\partial_{t}J(s,t)|}{J(s,t)}J(s,t)\ dt\ ds.

(3.18)

Since
J(s,0)\ ds
defines the surface area element on
\partial\Omega\cap V_{i}
we achieve the
inequality (
3.13
). To this purpose, the change of variables
x=h_{i}(x,t)
must be performed in the volumetric
integrals in the right hand side. Observe also that,

\partial_{t}u_{i}(s,t)=Du_{i}(h_{i}(s,t)){\mathbf{n}}(s),

and so
|\partial_{t}u_{i}(s,t)|\leq|Du_{i}|
. Finally,

C_{i}=\sup_{U_{i}\times(0,\varepsilon)}\frac{|\partial_{t}J|}{J}.

In this regard it should be noticed that,

\partial_{t}J(s,0)=\det\text{\rm col\,}({\mathbf{n}}_{s_{1}},\dots,g_{s_{N-1}},{\mathbf{n}})+\cdots+\det\text{\rm col\,}(g_{s_{1}},\dots,{\mathbf{n}}_{s_{N-1}},{\mathbf{n}})\\
=\text{\rm trace}\ \text{\rm col\,}({\mathbf{n}}_{s_{1}},\dots,{\mathbf{n}}_{s_{N-1}})J(s,0)=\text{\rm trace\,}D{\mathbf{n}}(s)J(s,0).

Thus,

\frac{\partial_{t}J(s,0)}{J(s,0)}=-\text{\rm trace\,}D\nu(g(s))=(N-1)H(g(s)),

where
H(x)
stands for the mean curvature of
\partial\Omega
when this surface is oriented with the
outer

unit normal
\nu=-{\mathbf{n}}
.
Reader is referred to
[
27
]
for the geometrical background and to
[
24
, Sec. 3]

for further computation details.
As a conclusion constant
C_{i}
in (
3.13
)
is directly related to the mean curvature of
\partial\Omega
.

Starting at (
3.13
) we now obtain,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi_{i}|u|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{i}|\\
\leq\int_{\Omega}|Du_{0}|+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}|Du_{i}|+\max{\{{\max_{{1}\leq{i}\leq{m}}C_{i},1}\}}\int_{\Omega}|u|.

(3.19)

In addition,

\sum_{i=0}^{m}\int_{\Omega}|Du_{i}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{i}|Du|+\sum_{i=0}^{m}\int_{\Omega}|D\varphi_{i}||u|\\
\leq\int_{\Omega}|Du|+(m+1)\max_{{0}\leq{i}\leq{m}}\|D\varphi_{i}\|_{\infty}\int_{\Omega}|u|.

(3.20)

By combining (
3.19
) and (
3.20
) we arrive to the desired inequality (
3.11
).

As for the case where
\Omega
is
C^{1,1}
, we choose a
C^{1,1}
atlas

{\{{(g_{i},U_{i})}\}}_{{1}\leq{i}\leq{m}}
for
\partial\Omega
with the properties
described in Section
2.2
. Mappings
h_{i}
in (
3.12
), where
0<\varepsilon<r_{\partial\Omega}

is regarded as a parameter, define Lipschitz homeomorphisms with Lipschitz inverses

h_{i}^{-1}
onto their images
V_{i}
. Covering
{\{{V_{i}}\}}_{{0}\leq{i}\leq{m}}
and associated
partition of unity
{\{{\varphi_{i}}\}}_{{0}\leq{i}\leq{m}}
are formed in the same way as before.

The key point to show (
3.11
) consists alike in achieving the inequality (
3.13
).
To this aim we first notice that by Rademacher’s theorem
Dh_{i}(s,t)
is defined
for all
|t|<\varepsilon
and almost all
s\in U_{i}
. Moreover,

J(s,t)=\text{det\,}Dh_{i}(s,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}J_{k}(s)t^{k},

hence
J
is a polynomial in
t
with coefficients
J_{k}\in L^{\infty}(U_{i})
.
Since
J_{0}(s)=J(s,0)
is given by (
3.15
) then
J(s,t)>0
for all
|t|<\varepsilon
and almost
all
s\in U_{i}
provided
\varepsilon>0
is conveniently reduced. In addition, equality (
3.16
) holds a. e. in

U_{i}
. Then we deduce (
3.17
) and thus, after integration in
s
, we arrive to estimate
(
3.18
).

On the other hand both the identities,

\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial{\mathbf{n}}}\right|\ dx=\int_{V_{i}}\left|\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial{\mathbf{n}}}\right|\ dx=\int_{U_{i}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}|\partial_{t}u_{i}(s,t)|J(s,t)\ dt\ ds,

and

\int_{\Omega}|u_{i}|\frac{|J_{t}|}{J}\ dx=\int_{V_{i}}|u_{i}|\frac{|J_{t}|}{J}\ dx=\int_{U_{i}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}|u_{i}(s,t)|\frac{|\partial_{t}J(s,t)|}{J(s,t)}J(s,t)\ dt\ ds,

are achieved after performing the substitution
x=h_{i}(s,t)
and employing the change
of variables formula, in its Lipschitz version
[
8
, Th. 3.3.2]
.

This concludes the proof in the
C^{1,1}
case.
∎

Remark 5
.

It should be stressed that the possibility of achieving
C_{1}=1
in (
1.1
) is
suggested in
[
12
, Rem. 1.1]
by the terse quotation: “
…if the
mean curvature of
\partial\Omega

”.

We are next exploiting the argument of the previous proof to show Theorem
4
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Unlike the case of Theorem
7
, the inner unit field
{\mathbf{n}}

is now only continuous. Anyway, a regularization
X\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},{\mathbb{R}}^{N})
of
{\mathbf{n}}
can been obtained
such that,

|X(x)-{\mathbf{n}}(x)|\leq\eta,\qquad\text{for $x\in{\{{d(x)\leq d_{0}}\}}$},\qquad d_{0}>0,

where
0<\eta<1
is a fixed small number. By denoting
x=\phi(t,y)
the solution to,

\frac{dx}{dt}=X(x),\qquad x(0)=y,

i. e.
\phi
is the flow associated to
X
,
t_{0}>0
can be further found so that,

|X(\phi(t,y))-{\mathbf{n}}(y)|\leq\eta,\qquad\text{for all $|t|\leq t_{0}$ and each $y\in\partial\Omega$}.

As in Theorem
7
we select a finite atlas
{\{{(g_{i},U_{i})}\}}_{{1}\leq{i}\leq{m}}
for

\partial\Omega
and set the mappings
h_{i}(s,t)=\phi(t,g_{i}(s))
. They are diffeomorphisms
onto their images, now denoted as
W_{i}
, when such mappings are defined in

U_{i}\times{\{{|t|<\varepsilon}\}}
with
\varepsilon>0
small.

After properly picking an open
W_{0}\subset\subset\Omega
and considering a partition of unity

\{\psi_{i}\}_{{{0}\leq{i}\leq{m}}}
associated to the covering
{\{{W_{i}}\}}_{{0}\leq{i}\leq{m}}
of
\overline{\Omega}
,
we decompose again,

u=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\psi_{i}u=\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}.

As an alternative to (
3.13
) we are now obtaining an inequality of the form,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{i}|\leq\frac{1+2\eta}{1-\eta}\int_{\Omega}|Du_{i}|+C_{i}^{\prime}\int_{\Omega}|u_{i}|,

(3.21)

for all
{1}\leq{i}\leq{m}
.

To begin with, we define,

{\widetilde{J}}(s,t)=\text{\rm det}\,\text{\rm col}(D\phi\,g_{s_{1}},\dots,D\phi\,g_{s_{N-1}},X),

where
D\phi=D_{y}\phi
and index
i
in
g
has been dropped. By taking into account that,

\frac{{\widetilde{J}}(s,0)}{J(s,0)}=\frac{{g_{i}}_{s_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge{g_{i}}_{s_{N-1}}}{|{g_{i}}_{s_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge{g_{i}}_{s_{N-1}}|}\cdot X(g(s))={\mathbf{n}}(g(s))\cdot X(g(s))\geq 1-\eta,

(3.22)

it follows in particular the positiveness of the factor
{\widetilde{J}}(s,0)
.

We next mimick the corresponding step in Theorem
7
to obtain,

\int_{U_{i}}|u_{i}(s,0)|{\widetilde{J}}(s,0)\ ds\leq\int_{U_{i}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{i}}|\partial_{t}u_{i}(s,t)|{\widetilde{J}}(s,t)\ dt\ ds\\
+\int_{U_{i}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{i}}|u_{i}(s,t)|\frac{|\partial_{t}{\widetilde{J}}(s,t)|}{{\widetilde{J}}(s,t)}{\widetilde{J}}(s,t)\ dt\ ds,

(3.23)

where
u_{i}(s,t)=u(\phi(t,g(s)))
and
g=g_{i}
.
Thus, by employing (
3.22
) we are lead to,

(1-\eta)\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{i}|\leq\int_{U_{i}}|u_{i}(s,0)|{\widetilde{J}}(s,0)\ ds.

(3.24)

Next, one observes that for every
y\in\partial\Omega
,

|X(\phi(t,y))|\leq|X(\phi(t,y))-X(y)|+|X(y)-{\mathbf{n}}(y)|+1\leq 2\eta+1.

Hence,

|\partial_{t}u_{i}(s,t)|\leq(1+2\eta)|Du_{i}|.

Accordingly,

\int_{U_{i}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{i}}|\partial_{t}u_{i}(s,t)|{\widetilde{J}}(s,t)\ dt\ ds\leq(1+2\eta)\int_{\Omega}|Du_{i}|.

(3.25)

By combining (
3.23
), (
3.24
) and (
3.25
) we arrive to the desired inequality (
3.21
).
Finally a reasoning as the one in Theorem
7
leads us to the estimate (
1.1
) with
the constant,

C_{1}=\frac{1+2\eta}{1-\eta}.

It is clear that
C_{1}
can be taken as close to unity as desired and so we are done.
∎

The proof of Lemma
3
is now performed in a
C^{1}
domain by forming a smooth
family of functions
u_{n}
. Indeed, let
\zeta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be so that
0\leq\zeta\leq 1
,
\zeta(0)=1
,

\zeta^{\prime}<0
for
0<t<1
and
\zeta=0
in
t\geq 1
. We define,

u_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\psi_{i}(x)\zeta\left(\frac{t(x)}{\varepsilon_{n}}\right),\qquad\varepsilon_{n}\to 0+,

where
x\to(s(x),t(x))
is the inverse mapping of
h_{i}(s,t)
when regarded in
W_{i}
(families

{\{{W_{i}}\}}
and
{\{{\psi_{i}}\}}
are the ones introduced in the previous proof of Theorem
4
).
We notice that
u_{n}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
while,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{n}|=|\partial\Omega|_{N-1}\,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|=o(1),\qquad\text{as $n\to\infty$.}

On the other hand,

\int_{\Omega}|Du_{n}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\psi_{i}\left|\zeta^{\prime}\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon_{n}}\right)\nabla t\right|+o(1),\qquad\text{as $n\to\infty$.}

Now observe that,

\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\psi_{i}\left|\zeta^{\prime}\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon_{n}}\right)\nabla t\right|=-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{U_{i}}\zeta^{\prime}(t)\psi_{i}(s,\varepsilon_{n}t)|\nabla t(s,\varepsilon_{n}t)|{\widetilde{J}}(s,\varepsilon_{n}t)\\
\to\int_{U_{i}}\psi_{i}(s,0)|\nabla t(s,0)|{\widetilde{J}}(s,0),\qquad\text{as $n\to\infty$.}

In addition,

{\widetilde{J}}(s,0)={\mathbf{n}}(g(s))\cdot X(g(s))J(s,0),

while a direct computation yields,

\nabla t(s,0)=\frac{1}{{\mathbf{n}}(g(s))\cdot X(g(s))}{\mathbf{n}}(g(s)).

Therefore,

\varlimsup\int_{\Omega}|Du_{n}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{U_{i}}\psi_{i}(s,0)J(s,0)=|\partial\Omega|_{N-1}.

Inequality
C_{1}\geq 1
is then obtained by setting
u=u_{n}
in (
1.1
) and passing to the limit.

4.
Some examples

Our first example shows that the conclusion of Theorem
4
is in general false
if
\Omega
is a piecewise
C^{1}
domain (cf. also
[
4
, p. 12]
).

Example 8
.

Let
\Omega
be an open domain such that for a certain small
\eta>0
its shape near
0

is described as,

\Omega\cap{\{{x_{N}<\eta}\}}=\left\{x=(x^{\prime},x_{N}):L|x^{\prime}|<x_{N}<\eta\right\},

where here and in the subsequent examples we are denoting
x^{\prime}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N-1})
.
Assume also that
\partial\Omega\cap{\{{x_{N}>\frac{\eta}{2}}\}}
is a smooth hypersurface so that

\Omega
is piecewise
C^{1}
.

We set
F_{n}={\{{L|x^{\prime}|<x_{N}<Lr_{n},\,|x^{\prime}|<r_{n}}\}}
,
u_{n}=\chi_{F_{n}}
(the characteristic
function of
F_{n}
), where
L>0
and are assuming that
r_{n}\to 0+
. Then,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{n}|=\sqrt{1+L^{2}}\,\,\omega_{N-1}r_{n}^{N-1},

\omega_{N-1}
being the volumen of
{\{{|x^{\prime}|<1}\}}
. In addition,

\int_{\Omega}|Du_{n}|=P(E_{n},\Omega)=\omega_{N-1}r_{n}^{N-1},

where the gradient of
u_{n}
has been performed in distributions sense (Section 2.1).
Since,

\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|=\frac{\omega_{N-1}L}{N}r_{n}^{N},

then constant
C_{1}
in (
1.1
) is constrained by the inequality,

C_{1}\geq\sqrt{1+L^{2}}.

Thus
C_{1}
is bounded away from unity.

Next example shows that an inequality as (
3.11
) fails to be true if the smoothness
of
\partial\Omega
is somehow weakened from
C^{1,1}
. In fact, it proves that
C^{1,\alpha}
domains,

0<\alpha<1
, are not sufficiently smooth to ensure us the validity of (
3.11
).

Example 9
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an open
C^{1,\alpha}
domain with
0\in\partial\Omega
, satisfying,

\Omega\cap{\{{x_{N}<\eta}\}}=\left\{x=(x^{\prime},x_{N}):x_{N}>\frac{|x^{\prime}|^{1+\alpha}}{1+\alpha}\right\},\qquad 0<\alpha<1,

for a certain positive and small enough number
\eta
. Fix
a sequence
r_{n}\to 0+
and define for large
n
,

E_{n}={\{{\psi(|x^{\prime}|)<x_{N}<\psi(r_{n})}\}}\subset\Omega,\qquad\psi(t)=\frac{|t|^{1+\alpha}}{1+\alpha}.

We are showing that the family
u_{n}=\chi_{E_{n}}

cannot satisfy the inequality (
3.11
) when
n\to\infty
. In fact,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{n}|=\int_{|x^{\prime}|<r_{n}}\sqrt{1+|x^{\prime}|^{2\alpha}}\ dx^{\prime}\\
=(N-1)\omega_{N-1}\left(\frac{r_{n}^{N-1}}{N-1}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{r_{n}^{N-1+2\alpha}}{N-1+2\alpha}+O(r_{n}^{N-1+4\alpha})\right)\\
=\omega_{N-1}{r_{n}^{N-1}}+\frac{(N-1)\omega_{N-1}}{2(N-1+2\alpha)}r_{n}^{N-1+2\alpha}+O(r_{n}^{N-1+4\alpha}).

As in Example
8
,

\int_{\Omega}|Du_{n}|=P(E_{n},\Omega)=\omega_{N-1}r_{n}^{N-1}.

Thus,

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{n}|-\int_{\Omega}|Du_{n}|=\frac{(N-1)\omega_{N-1}}{2(N-1+2\alpha)}r_{n}^{N-1+2\alpha}+O(r_{n}^{N-1+4\alpha}).

In addition,

\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|=|E_{n}|=\frac{\omega_{N-1}}{N+\alpha}r_{n}^{N+\alpha}.

Since
0<\alpha<1
exponent
N+\alpha
is greater than
N-1+2\alpha
.
Therefore, inequality (
3.11
) cannot be satisfied when

r_{n}\to 0+
.

Remark 6
.

Let us check that the normal curvatures of
\partial\Omega
blow up
when we approach to
0
and the boundary is oriented near
0
with the
inner normal field,

{\mathbf{n}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|x^{\prime}|^{2\alpha}}}\left\{\right(-|x^{\prime}|^{\alpha-1}x^{\prime},0)+e_{N}\}.

In fact, if
v\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
is an unitary
vector, the curve in
\partial\Omega
defined as

x=t(v,0)+\frac{|t|^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}e_{N},\qquad|t|<\varepsilon,

passes through
0
when
t=0
. It is in fact a normal section to
\partial\Omega
since each of its
points is contained in the plane
\text{\rm span\,}{\{{(v,0),e_{N}}\}}
, while
the family of normals
{\mathbf{n}}(x(t))
also lies in this plane. The expression,

\kappa=\frac{1}{|\dot{x}|^{2}}\left|\ddot{x}-(\ddot{x}.{\mathbf{t}}){\mathbf{t}}\right|=\frac{\alpha|t|^{\alpha-1}}{{\{\sqrt{1+t^{2\alpha}}}\}^{3}},\qquad{\mathbf{t}}=\frac{\dot{x}}{|\dot{x}|},

(4.26)

where ‘dots’ mean differentiation with respect to
t
, provides us the normal curvature of
\partial\Omega
at
x(t)
and the
direction
v
. It can be also checked that
\kappa
is the minimum of the principal curvatures at this point. Then, it
follows from (
4.26
) that all normal curvatures diverge to infinity as
t\to 0+
.
This also occurs to the mean curvature
H
. Thus, Example
9
is coherent with the suggestion in

[
12
]
quoted in Remark
5
.

Remark 7
.

Example
9
also provides us a
C^{1,\alpha}
manifold
\partial\Omega
with
0<\alpha<1
where
r_{\partial\Omega}=0
.
In fact we are proving that
r(0)=0
. To this proposal we take
p_{\sigma}=(0,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
and claim
that the sphere
|x-p_{\sigma}|=\sigma
possesses points outside
\overline{\Omega}
for every
\sigma>0
small.
This would imply that
\text{dist\,}(p_{\sigma},\partial\Omega)<\sigma
and so such a distance should be
achieved at infinitely many points
y\in\partial\Omega\setminus{\{{0}\}}

symmetrically distributed around the
x_{N}
axis.

To show the claim it is enough with comparing the profiles of the sphere

x_{N}=\sigma-\sqrt{\sigma^{2}-|x^{\prime}|^{2}}=:\phi(|x^{\prime}|)
and the boundary
x_{N}=\psi(|x^{\prime}|)
of
\partial\Omega

near the origin. It is clear that
\phi(t)<\psi(t)
as
t\to 0+
while
\phi(\sigma)=\sigma>\psi(\sigma)

when
0<\sigma<1
. Thus, the checking is complete.

As an extra remark, it follows from
[
9

that the distance function

d(x)=\text{dist\,}(x,\partial\Omega)
can not be differentiable at any of the points
p_{\sigma}
.
In other words,
d(x)
does not define a
C^{1}
function under the conditions of the
present example (cf.
[
15
]
).
This should be compared with the domains
\Omega
of class
C^{2}
where the signed distance

\tilde{d}(x)
defines a
C^{2}

\partial\Omega
(
[
11
, Chap. 14]
,
[
15
]
).

Finally, let us consider the functionals of type (
1.3
),

J_{\pm}(u)=\int_{\Omega}|Du|\pm\int_{\partial\Omega}|u|,

in the space
BV(\Omega)
. It is well–known that
J_{+}
is lower semicontinuous
in
BV(\Omega)
with respect to the
L^{1}
convergence
if
\Omega
is only a bounded Lipschitz domain (
[
21
, Rem. 1.3]
).
In strong contrast, we are next showing that
J_{-}
fails to exhibit this behavior even
in a more smooth domain.

Example 10
.

Let
\Omega
be the
C^{1,\alpha}
domain introduced in Example
9
.
Let
u_{n}=\chi_{E_{n}}
also be the sequence of functions considered there.
We now set,

v_{n}=A_{n}\chi_{E_{n}},\qquad A_{n}=\frac{1}{r_{n}^{N-1+2\alpha}}.

Then,

J_{-}(v_{n})=A_{n}J_{-}(u_{n})=A_{n}\left(\int_{\Omega}|Du_{n}|-\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{n}|\right)\\
=-\frac{(N-1)\omega_{N-1}}{2(N-1+2\alpha)}+O(r_{n}^{2\alpha})<0,

for large
n
. However,

\|v_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=A_{n}|E_{n}|=\frac{\Omega_{N-1}}{N+\alpha}r_{n}^{1-\alpha}\to 0.

Therefore, the conclusion of Theorem
6
