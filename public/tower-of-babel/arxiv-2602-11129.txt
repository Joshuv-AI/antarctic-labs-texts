Information-Theoretic Thresholds for Bipartite Latent-Space Graphs Under Noisy Observations
Andreas Göbel, Marcus Pappik, Leon Schiller

Abstract
. We study information-theoretic phase transitions for the detectability of latent geometry in bipartite random geometric graphs (RGGs) with Gaussian, d d -dimensional latent vectors, while only a subset of edges carries latent information, determined by a random mask with i.i.d. 𝖡𝖾𝗋𝗇 ⁡ ( q ) \mathsf{Bern}(q) entries. For any fixed edge density p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) , we determine essentially tight thresholds for this problem as a function of d d and q q . Our results show that the detection problem is substantially easier if the mask is known up-front, compared to the case where the mask is hidden. Our analysis is built upon a novel Fourier-analytic framework for bounding signed subgraph counts in Gaussian random geometric graphs that exploits cancellations which arise after approximating characteristic functions by an appropriate power series. The resulting bounds are applicable to much larger sub-graphs than considered in previous work, which enables tight information-theoretic bounds, while the bounds considered in previous works only lead to lower bounds from the lens of low-degree polynomials. As a consequence, we identify the optimal information-theoretic thresholds and rule out computational–statistical gaps. Our bounds further improve upon the bounds on Fourier coefficients of random geometric graphs recently given by Bangachev and Bresler [STOC’24] in the dense bipartite case. The techniques extend to sparser and non-bipartite settings as well, at least if the considered sub-graphs are sufficiently small. We further believe that they might help resolve open questions for related detection problems.

The model and associated testing problems

We denote by

Definition 1.1

(The distributions
\mathbb{W}(n,m,p,d)

\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)
)
.

We let
\mathbb{W}(n,m,p,d)

n\times m

\{0,1\}

\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}=(\mathbf{x}_{u})_{u\in R}

\mathbf{X}_{\text{L}}=(\mathbf{x}_{u})_{u\in L}

Define further the threshold
\tau=\tau(d,p)

\mathbb{P}(d^{-1/2}\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v}\rangle\leq\tau)=p.

Then, after drawing
(\mathbf{x}_{u})_{u\in R}
and
(\mathbf{x}_{u})_{u\in L}

W\sim\mathbb{W}(n,m,p,d)
by thresholding

4

4

Moreover, we define
\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)

Introducing sparsity via random masks of density
q
.

While this result fully characterizes the problem in terms of its feasibility as a function of
d
, it is much more challenging to find the right thresholds if the considered matrix additionally exhibits some form of
sparsity
in the sense that only a small fraction of entries actually carries information about the underlying latent vectors.
One of the most natural ways of introducing sparsity to the above problem is the so called
masked model
, as introduced in
[
4
]
.
Here, there is some partially hidden matrix
M
and we are given as input a
mask

\mathbf{M}\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,q)
together with the matrix
\mathbf{M}\odot M
(where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product), while the goal is to distinguish the following two hypotheses.

H_{0}:M\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ and }\hskip 14.22636ptH_{1}:M\sim\mathbb{W}(n,m,p,d).

Accordingly, an algorithm for solving the above testing problem only has access to the entries of
M\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)
or
M\sim\mathbb{W}(n,m,p,d)
that are not “hidden” by the mask
\mathbf{M}
(i.e., correspond to
\mathbf{M}_{u,v}=1
). Accordingly, the masked model introduces a new parameter
q
that controls the amount of sparsity and influences the associated thresholds for distinguishability. Tight information-theoretic thresholds for this masked setting as a function of
d
and
q
were given in
[
4
]
for a
continuous
version of the above problem, where a Wishart matrix is to be distinguished from a matrix with i.i.d. Gaussian entries.

Testing with known and unknown masks.

The masked setting considered so far assumes that the mask
\bf{M}
is given explicitly, which means that an algorithm has access to the information which entries in the input
M
potentially carry latent information. It is very interesting to compare this setting to the case where the mask
\mathbf{M}
is not known. To introduce these two settings formally, we define the following “masked” distributions.

Definition 1.2

(The distributions
\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d)
and
\mathbb{W}_{\mathbf{M}}(n,m,p,d)
)
.

Given a matrix
\mathbf{M}_{u,v}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m}
,
define the distribution
\mathbb{W}_{\mathbf{M}}(n,m,p,d)
as the distribution over matrixes
M\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m}
obtained by sampling
W\sim\mathbb{W}(n,m,p,d)
,
B\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)
and then setting

M\coloneqq W\odot\mathbf{M}+B\odot(1-\mathbf{M}),

where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product.
Moreover, define
\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d)
as the distribution
\mathbb{W}_{\mathbf{M}}(n,m,p,d)
resulting from drawing
\mathbf{M}
from
\mathbb{M}(n,m,q)
, i.e.,

\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{M}\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,q)}\left[\mathbb{W}_{\mathbf{M}}(n,m,p,d)\right].

Accordingly, a sample
M\sim\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d)
is obtained by choosing a matrix
W\sim\mathbb{W}(n,m,p,d)
and a mask
\mathbf{M}\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,q)
, and subsequently setting
M_{u,v}
corresponding to
\mathbf{M}_{u,v}=0
to an independent sample from
\mathsf{Bern}(p)
. This second step serves the purpose of “hiding” the mask
\mathbf{M}
by ensuring that every entry in
M
has the same marginal distribution. This can equivalently be seen as a form of introducing noise, analogous to the setting considered in
[
17
]
for (non-bipartite) RGGs on the sphere.

With this, our testing problems for known and unknown masks can be described as follows.

Problem 1.3

(Distinguishing
\mathbb{W}(n,m,p,d)
and
\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)
for unknown masks)
.

Given a matrix
M
, distinguish the following two hypotheses.

\displaystyle H_{0}:M\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)\hskip 8.5359pt\text{ and }\hskip 8.5359ptH_{1}:M\sim\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d).

Problem 1.4

(Distinguishing
\mathbb{W}(n,m,p,d)
and
\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)
for known masks)
.

Given a matrix
M
and a mask
\mathbf{M}\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)
, distinguish the following two hypotheses.

\displaystyle H_{0}:M\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)\hskip 8.5359pt\text{ and }\hskip 8.5359ptH_{1}:M\sim\mathbb{W}_{\mathbf{M}}(n,m,p,d).

Note that above, the testing problem for known masks is introduced slightly differently than in the previous paragraph in the sense that the entries “hidden” by the mask are independent
\mathsf{Bern}(p)
random variables in both hypotheses, while previously they were set to zero under both
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
. However, it is not hard to see that from the lens of distinguishability, the two settings are equivalent, assuming that the mask is part of the input. Our definitions above have the advantage that they make it easier to compare the the two settings.
We refer to
Figure

1
for an illustration of how the dependencies within a sample from
\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d)
behave and in particular become weaker as
d
grows and
q
tends to zero.

Our first main result concerns an almost tight characterization of information-theoretic distinguishability of
\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d)

\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)

Theorem 1.5

(Information-theoretic thresholds for unknown masks)
.

Consider any fixed
p\in(0,1)
. Then, the following holds whenever
d\gg\log(n)^{3}
.

If
p\neq\frac{1}{2}
then:
\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d),\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)\right)&=\begin{cases}1-o(1)&\text{if }d\ll nmq^{4}\hskip 36.98866pt\text{ or }\hskip 2.84544ptd\ll m\sqrt{n}q^{2}\\
o(1)&\text{if }d\gg nmq^{4}\log(n)\hskip 2.84544pt\text{ and }\hskip 2.84544ptd\gg m\sqrt{n}q^{2}\log(n)\end{cases}\end{aligned}

If
p=\frac{1}{2}
then:
\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d),\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)\right)&=\begin{cases}1-o(1)&\text{if }d\ll nmq^{4}\\
o(1)&\text{if }d\gg nmq^{4}\log(n)\end{cases}\end{aligned}

Here,
o(1)
denotes a function that tends to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Recall further that
m\geq n
.

Efficient tests and absence of computational-statistical gaps.
The above upper and lower bounds on the total variation distance between the two matrix ensembles are almost tight and differ only by a factor of
\text{polylog}(m)
. In the regime where
\lim\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\textsc{W},\textsc{M}\right)=1
, there are two different regimes characterized by distinct, optimal tests. If
d\ll nmq^{4}
, then counting
signed 4-cycles
distinguishes
W
and
M
, and if
d\ll m\sqrt{n}q^{2}
, then this holds for counting
signed wedges
. Formally, for a matrix
M\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m}
these are given by

\displaystyle C_{4}(M)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
i,j\in[n]\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k<\ell\\
k,\ell\in[m]\end{subarray}}(M_{i,k}-p)(M_{i,\ell}-p)(M_{j,k}-p)(M_{j,\ell}-p)\text{ and }P_{2}(M)\coloneq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k<\ell\\
k,\ell\in[m]\end{subarray}}(M_{i,k}-p)(M_{i,\ell}-p),

respectively.
Theorem

1.5
shows that once we are in the regime where none of these statistics succeeds
5
5

5

or strictly speaking, if we exceed this regime by a factor of
\log(n)
, then
no algorithm
can distinguish the two distributions. Moreover, since
C_{4}(M)
and
P_{2}(M)
are clearly efficiently computable, this implies in particular that there are
no computational statistical gaps
in our problem.

The case
p=\frac{1}{2}
vs.
p\neq\frac{1}{2}
.

Moreover,
Theorem

1.5
highlights a somewhat surprising effect arising from the choice of
p
: For
p=\frac{1}{2}
, the regime in which
\lim\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(W,M\right)=1
is significantly smaller than for the case where
p
is strictly larger or smaller than
\frac{1}{2}
. This is a consequence of the symmetry of the Gaussian distribution. In particular, if
p=\frac{1}{2}
, then
\tau=0
which has the consequence that counting wedges has no statistical power anymore (even conditional on the latent vector
\mathbf{x}_{i}
) since the two entries
M_{i,k}
and
M_{i,\ell}
will be i.i.d.
\mathsf{Bern}(\frac{1}{2})
random variables.
Theorem

1.5
shows that this effect is not specific to signed wedges, but that it is more inherent in the sense that no algorithm can distinguish
W
and
M
in the regime where signed four-cycles fail, even if signed wedges would succeed in the
p\neq\frac{1}{2}
case. Hence, choosing
p=\frac{1}{2}
makes the problem information-theoretically strictly harder compared to any other fixed choice of
p
.

Known vs. unknown masks.

In the setting of
Theorem

1.5
, we assume that the random mask determining which entries are to be re-randomized is not part of the input. We complement this assumption by a similar result concerning the setting where the mask is part of the input. The following theorem shows that the testing thresholds established in
Theorem

1.5
shift significantly under the assumption that the mask is known.

Theorem 1.6

(Information-theoretic hardness for known masks)
.

Consider any fixed
p\in(0,1)
.
Then, the following holds with probability
1-o(n)
over
\ \mathbf{M}\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,q)
whenever
d\gg\log(n)^{3}
.

If
p\neq\frac{1}{2}
then:
\begin{aligned} \hskip 22.76228pt\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{W}_{\mathbf{M}}(n,m,p,d),\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)\right)&=\begin{cases}1-o(1)&\text{if }d\ll nmq^{2}\hskip 10.81218pt\text{ or }\hskip 2.84544ptd\ll m\sqrt{n}q\\
o(1)&\text{if }d\gg nmq^{4}\hskip 2.84544pt\text{ and }\hskip 2.84544ptd\gg m\sqrt{n}q^{2}\end{cases}\end{aligned}

If
p=\frac{1}{2}
then:
\begin{aligned} \hskip 22.76228pt\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{W}_{\mathbf{M}}(n,m,p,d),\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)\right)&=\begin{cases}1-o(1)&\text{if }d\ll nmq^{2}\\
o(1)&\text{if }d\gg nmq^{2}\log(n)\end{cases}\end{aligned}

Here,
o(1)
denotes a function that tends to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Recall further that
m\geq n
.

Accordingly, comparing the thresholds given in
Theorem

5.1
and
Theorem

5.2
, we can see that switching from known to unknown masks amounts to replacing

by

.

Discrete vs. continuous models.
Note that while the setting of known masks was already considered in
[
4
]
for the continuous analogue of our model, the above theorem shows that the discrete version of the problem considered here exhibits
different
thresholds once
q
is sufficiently small, even if
p\neq\frac{1}{2}
. This is because in the continuous model, there are subtle differences in the marginal distribution of every entry
M_{u,v}
when comparing
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
. Testing for these differences allows us to distinguish the two models whenever

. In our discrete version, the marginals match exactly and convergence in total variation therefore occurs earlier. More details on the differences of the discrete and the continuous setting can be found in
Section

.

Convergence in total variation, but only for averaged masks.

Abbreviating for now
\mathbb{W}=\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d)
,
\mathbb{W}_{\textbf{M}}=\mathbb{W}_{\mathbf{M}}(n,m,p,d)
, and
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)
, then the bounds from
Theorem

1.5
and
Theorem

1.6
lead to the remarkable phenomenon that for all fixed
p
, there is a regime of the parameters
d,q
where
\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{M}\right)=o(1)
, while for the same
d
and
q
, we have
\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}(\mathbb{W}_{\textbf{M}},\mathbb{M})=1-o(1)
with high probability over the choice of
\mathbf{M}
. This means that for most fixed masks
\mathbf{M}
of density
q
(i.e.
\mathbf{M}\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,q)
), the resulting conditional distribution
\mathbb{W}_{\textbf{M}}
is efficiently distinguishable from
\mathbb{M}
, while in expectation over
\mathbf{M}
, convergence in total variation occurs. In particular, with high probability over
\mathbf{M}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{W}_{\textbf{M}},\mathbb{M}\right)=1-o(1)\text{ while }\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{M}}\left[\mathbb{W}_{\textbf{M}}\right],\mathbb{M}\right)=o(1).

In other words, the distribution
\mathbb{W}_{\textbf{M}}
is very far from
M
for most masks
\mathbf{M}
, while it is asymptotically indistinguishable from
M
when considering the averaged distribution
\mathbb{W}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{M}}[\mathbb{W}_{\textbf{M}}]
. Another point of view on this phenomenon is that every algorithm that distinguishes
\mathbb{W}
from
M
in the regime where
\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{M}\right)=o(1)
must depend on
\mathbf{M}
.

2.
Technical Contributions

2.1.
Second moment method

Like many related results from the literature, our information-theoretic lower bounds are essentially based on applying a version of the
second moment method
. Concretely, this means that given two probability distributions
\mu,\nu
, we wish to relate total variation distance
\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mu,\nu\right)
to the so-called
\chi^{2}
-divergence
\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)
. These divergences are given by

\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mu,\nu\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\underset{\xi\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)-1\right|\right]\text{ and }\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\underset{\xi\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)\right)^{2}\right]-1,

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)
denotes the probability of sampling the matrix
\xi
under distribution
\mu
divided by the probability of sampling
\xi
under
\nu
.
Now, we bound
\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mu,\nu\right)
by means of Pinsker’s inequality and by passing from relative entropy to
\chi^{2}
-divergence as

2\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mu,\nu\right)^{2}\leq\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu).

The distribution
\mu
can be expressed as a mixture
\mu=\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\left[\mu_{\phi}\right]
over some latent randomness
\phi
where
\mu_{\phi}
denotes the distribution
\mu
conditional on
\phi
.
We then expand the squared expectation into two expectations over independent copies
\phi^{(1)},\phi^{(2)}
of the latent randomness and exchange the inner and outer expectation to get

\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\underset{\xi\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)\right)^{2}\right]-1=\underset{\phi^{(1)},\phi^{(2)}\sim\varrho\otimes\varrho}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\underset{\xi\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\phi^{(1)}}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\phi^{(2)}}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)\right]\right]-1,

where
\varrho
is the law of the latent randomness.
The advantage of this re-formulation is that the inner expectation can now be computed exactly since
\nu
is a simple product distribution.

2.2.
Challenges arising from previous work

The starting point for this work is the work of Brennan, Bresler, and Huang
[
4
]
who studied a similar problem for fixed and known masks
\mathbf{M}
of density
q
. The main difference to our setting is that they study a continuous version of our problem where the goal is to distinguish a (masked) Wishart matrix
W=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}-d\mathbf{I})
from a matrix with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries, and that they only consider the setting of known masks.

Different thresholds in the continuous vs. discrete model.

While information-theoretic lower bounds for the continuous model considered in
[
4
]
directly imply the same lower bounds for the discrete model studied here (for the case of known masks), it turns out that in the presence of noise, these lower bounds are not strong enough to tightly characterize testing in the discrete setting, even if
p
is a constant (this is in contrast to the noiseless case). This can already be seen by comparing our bounds from
Theorem

1.6
with the bounds in
[
4
, Theorem 4.2]
. Concretely, in the case of relatively small
d
, the continuous model is much
less
noise sensitive than its discrete counterpart as it remains efficiently distinguishable from the i.i.d. Gaussian ensemble as long as
d\ll\sqrt{nmq}
, while our model becomes
indistinguishable
from
M
if
d\gg m\sqrt{n}q\log(n)
, i.e., at much lower levels of noise, provided that
d
is sufficiently small. Consider for example the case where
n=m
and
d
scales as some power of
\log(n)
, then the discrete model converges in total variation to
M
if
q\ll n^{-3/2}\log(n)
, while for the continuous case, convergence to the i.i.d. Gaussian ensemble only occurs for
q\ll n^{-2}
. If
p=\frac{1}{2}
, the difference is even bigger, here convergence occurs if
q\ll n^{-1}\log(n)
in the discrete setting, while the threshold stays at
q\ll n^{-2}
for the continuous model.

Handling unknown masks.

In addition to the fact that the two models provably behave differently in the presence of noise, the techniques given in
[
4
]
do not fully extend to the technically much more challenging case of unknown masks. This is because not knowing the mask results in convergence in total variation at much lower levels of noise
q
, which means that for a given
q
, the proofs need to yield convergence for asymptotically much smaller values of
d
, which requires much more careful arguments. In particular, for any fixed
q
, every column of
W\sim\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d)
will contain roughly
nq
entries that have not been re-randomized. Conditional on the random vectors
\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}
in
R
, the distribution of these
k\approx nq
entries is jointly Gaussian with a covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
dictated by the inner products of the vectors in
R
. A crucial fact exploited in the proofs in
[
4
, Section 9]
is that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
is positive definite, which ensures that the resulting distribution has a well-behaved probability density such that we can bound total variation by means of the Radon-Nikodym derivative of (two copies of)
\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}))
and the standard Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
, which arises from
\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)
when applying the second moment method.

However, to ensure a that
\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}))
has a well-behaved density, we have to require
d\gg nq
as for
d\ll nq
, it is not hard to see that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
is a matrix of rank much less than
k\approx nq
, so it has a non-trivial kernel and is
not
positive definite. This makes the resulting Radon-Nikodym derivative
infinite
and, thus, results in failure of the second moment method. For our setting, however, we cannot assume that
d\gg nq
as we wish to obtain convergence already when
d\gg nmq^{4}\log(n)+m\sqrt{n}q^{2}\log(n)
.

2.3.
Bounding total variation in terms of signed subgraph counts

While the proofs in
[
4
]
bound a continuous version of
\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)
, we proceed with discrete distributions
\mu,\nu
and use a calculation similar to the one in
[
16
]
to get a sum over the
expected signed weight

\mathbb{E}\left[\textsc{Sw}(\alpha)\right]
of all sub-graphs
\alpha\subseteq K_{n,m}
, defined as follows.

Definition 2.1

(Signed weights)
.

Let
\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v}\rangle)
be the indicator function of the event
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v}\rangle\leq\tau
. For any
\alpha\subseteq K_{n,m}
, and latent vectors
(\mathbf{x})_{u\in L\cup R}
given by the columns of
\ \mathbf{X}_{\text{L}},\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}
, we define the
signed weight
of
\alpha
as

\textsc{Sw}(\alpha)\coloneqq{\prod}_{\{u,v\}\in\alpha}(\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v}\rangle)-p).

In the setting of an unknown mask, our expression for
\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)
admits an explicit representation in terms of expected signed weights:

(2.1)

\displaystyle 1+\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\underset{\phi^{(1)},\phi^{(2)}\sim\rho\otimes\rho}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\underset{\xi\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\phi^{(1)}}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\phi^{(2)}}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)\right]\right]=1+\sum_{\emptyset\neq\alpha\subseteq K_{n,m}}\frac{q^{2|\alpha|}}{(p(1-p))^{|\alpha|}}\ \Big(\ \underset{\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{X}_{\text{L}}}{\mathbb{E}}{\big[\textsc{Sw}(\alpha)\big]}\ \Big)^{2}

and our problem reduces to showing that the above sum is
o(1)
once
d\gg nmq^{4}\log(n)+m\sqrt{n}q^{2}\log(n)
. For the details in deriving the above expression, we refer to
Section

5

2.4.
Bounding expected signed weights

The main technical challenge is now to find good bounds on the expected signed weights appearing in the above sum. We outline the main challenges arising from this approach.

Bounds given in previous work.

While signed subgraph counts for random geometric graphs have been studied in several recent works, see for example
[
3
,
2
,
1
]
, meaningful bounds have so far only been obtained for very small patterns
\alpha
with at most
\text{polylog}(n)
edges. For this work, however, we require bounds that stay meaningful for
all

\alpha\subseteq K_{n,m}
, with a number of edges of order up to
\sim nm
.

While an extension of the bounds given in the recent work of Bangachev and Bresler
[
3
]
to large
\alpha
would be no easy task on its own, it turns out that, even if this was possible and the bounds given in
[
3
]
would extend as they are to our setting, they would nonetheless be far too weak for our purposes, even in the noiseless case. The reason for this is that (as described in
[
3
, Remark 1]
) the bounds given in
[
3
]
are of order at least
(1/\sqrt{d})^{|V(\alpha)|}
, which is not enough to ensure a small sum in (
2.1
). The reason for this is that a decay of order
(1/\sqrt{d})^{|V(\alpha)|}
fails to counterbalance the number of possible
\alpha\subseteq K_{n,m}
already if
\alpha
has only
n^{\varepsilon}
many vertices. To improve upon this, we would instead require bounds that decay exponentially in
|\alpha|
(i.e. the number of edges) instead of the number of vertices
|V(\alpha)|
.

One possible approach for dealing with this issue (as also suggested in
[
3
, Remark 1]
) is to condition on a subset of the latent information. While this is indeed an important aspect of our approach, the problem with the arguments given in
[
3
]
is that, even after conditioning and switching to our bipartite setting, we would still not get an exponent that is strong enough for our purposes. Moreover, as we will see later, we do not only need a bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[\textsc{Sw}(\alpha)\right]
, but (as we will see later), we also need to make an explicit statement about how exactly this quantity
depends
on the latent vectors we condition on. This does not seem to be within reach of techniques considered so far.

Our approach.

Our main technical contribution is therefore a novel, Fourier-theoretic way of bounding expected signed weights for bipartite Gaussian RGGs that seemlessly extends to large
\alpha
while also improving on the known bounds for small patterns. It makes a statement on
\mathbb{E}\left[\textsc{Sw}(\alpha)\right]
in the conditional probability space obtained by conditioning on the high-probability event that the inner products of all right-sided random vectors are not much larger than we would expect. Concretely, we define
S_{\rho}\in\sigma(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
as

S_{\rho}\coloneqq\left\{\ \mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\text{ with columns }(\mathbf{x}_{u})_{u\in[n]}\text{ such that }\bigg|\tfrac{1}{d}\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v}\rangle-\mathbf{I}_{u,v}\bigg|\leq\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\ \text{ for all }u,v\in[n]\ \right\}.

Our main technical theorem now expresses the expected signed weight of an arbitrary star
K_{1,\alpha}
with center in
L
and leaves given by
\alpha\subseteq R
as a polynomial in the latent vectors in
\alpha
plus a lower-order correction term.

Proposition 2.1

(Bound on conditional signed weights)
.

Define
\widehat{\sigma}
such that
\Phi_{\widehat{\sigma}}(\tau)=p
, where
\Phi_{\widehat{\sigma}}
is the CDF of the distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\widehat{\sigma}^{2})
. For any given set
\alpha\subseteq R
define the random variable

\textsc{Sw}(K_{1,\alpha})\coloneqq\prod_{u\in\alpha}(\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle)-p)

where
(\mathbf{x}_{u})_{u\in\text{R}}
are the columns of
\ \mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}
.
Then, there are functions
\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}),r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
such that for all
\alpha\subseteq R
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\textsc{Sw}(K_{1,\alpha})\mid\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\right]=\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})+r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})

whenever
\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\in S_{\rho}
.
Defining
\ell\coloneqq\lceil|\alpha|/2\rceil
, we further have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})\coloneqq\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{2^{\ell}\ell!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\ldots r_{\ell}\in\alpha\times\alpha\\
{\textsc{Supp}}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell})=\alpha\end{subarray}}\ \Bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Big(\tfrac{1}{d}\big\langle\mathbf{x}_{r_{j}(1)},\mathbf{x}_{r_{j}(2)}\big\rangle-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{r_{j}(1),r_{j}(2)}\Big)\Bigg)\ \Bigg(\prod_{e\in\alpha}\phi^{(s_{e}-1)}(\tau)\Bigg)

where
{\textsc{Supp}}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})\coloneqq\{e\in\alpha\mid r_{j}(1)=e\text{ or }r_{j}(2)=e\text{ for some }j\}
is the set of edges covered by the 2-tuples
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
, where
s_{e}
is the number of tuples
r_{j}
that cover edge
e
, i.e.,

s_{e}\coloneqq|\{j\in[k]\mid r_{j}(1)=e\}|+|\{j\in[k]\mid r_{j}(2)=e\}|,

and where
\phi^{(s)}
is the
s
-th derivative of the standard Gaussian density. Moreover, there is an absolute constant
C>0
such that

|\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})|\leq\Bigg(\frac{C\rho|\alpha|}{\sqrt{d}}\Bigg)^{\ell}\text{ and }|r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})|\leq\Bigg(\frac{C\rho|\alpha|}{\sqrt{d}}\Bigg)^{\ell+1}.

Note that while we only make a statement about sub-graphs of the form
K_{1,\alpha}
, the above immediately yields an analogous bound for arbitrary
\alpha\subseteq K_{n,m}
by exploiting independence over the latent vectors in
L
. Looking at the given explicit bounds on
\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
and
r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
, it is easy to see that the conditional signed weights decay exponentially in
|\alpha|
, i.e., in the number of
edges
, instead of the number of vertices. For small
|\alpha|
, it is easy to further remove the conditioning on
S_{\rho}
and get an explicit bound on the low-degree Fourier coefficients that, in particular, improve upon the bounds given in
[
3
]
if
\alpha
is bipartite.

Corollary 2.2

(Bound on unconditional signed weights)
.

For any
\alpha\subseteq K_{n,m}
, we have

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{X}_{\text{L}},\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}}{\mathbb{E}}{\big[\textsc{Sw}(\alpha)\big]}\leq\left(\frac{C|\alpha|^{3/2}\log(d)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{|\alpha|/2}.

Proof.

We set
\rho=C\sqrt{|\alpha|\log(d)}
for a sufficiently large constant
C>0
. Then, using that each inner product is the sum of
d
i.i.d. random variables, we get from a Chernoff bound and a union bound over all of at most
|V(\alpha)|^{2}
pairs of latent vectors that
\mathbb{P}(\overline{S_{\rho}})\leq 2|V(\alpha)|^{2}\exp(-c\rho^{2})\leq(C/\sqrt{d})^{|\alpha|/2}
for some constant
c>0
and our choice of
\rho
, provided that the constant
C
is chosen large enough. Then, applying the bounds from
2.1
and the law of total expectation yields the corollary.
∎

2.5.
Information-theoretic bounds via a conditional second moment method

With
2.1
at hand, we wish to derive an upper bound on the sum in (
2.1
). However, as already mentioned, simply summing over unconditional squared expected signed weights using
Corollary

2.2
does not yield the desired bounds. Instead, we condition the right-sided latent vectors to be in line with
S_{\rho}
while setting
\rho\approx\sqrt{\log(n)}
. Then, we apply the second moment method to
\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}({\mathcal{L}}(\mu\mid S_{\rho}),\nu)
where
{\mathcal{L}}(\mu\mid S_{\rho})
is the distribution
\mu
conditional on
S_{\rho}
.

The case of
p=\frac{1}{2}
.

After conditioning, the stronger bounds from
2.1
are applicable. If
p=\frac{1}{2}
, this is essentially all we need.
However, naively plugging the bounds from
2.1
into (
2.1
) would still not give the right thresholds.
Nonetheless, it turns out that the only obstacle is given by sub-graphs
\alpha
that have leaves, i.e., vertices of degree one. If we omit the conditioning, it is easy to see that by the symmetries of our model arising for
p=\frac{1}{2}
, the expected signed weight of such
\alpha
is
0
, since the single edge incident to a leaf is always independent of all the rest.
While conditioning on
S_{\rho}
generally breaks independence between latent vectors, it is not too hard to argue that certain symmetries are preserved even after conditioning. Specifically, since
S_{\rho}
only makes a statement about the absolute value of inner products, we can still independently re-randomize the sign in front of every latent vector. Applying this “noise operator” preserves all of the inner products in absolute value, but still zeroes out signed weights of sub-graphs with leaves. After accounting for this fact, we can show that the remaining sum is small enough once we carefully account for all
\alpha\subseteq K_{n,m}
.

The case of arbitrary
p
.
For the more general case
p\neq\frac{1}{2}
,
the picture is quite a lot more complex. The most important difference to before is that sub-graphs with leaves actually have
non-zero
expected signed weight, even unconditionally. This should be no surprise, as there is a regime where counting
signed wedges
is actually a test that outperforms signed four-cycles. As a consequence, we can work under the stronger (for our purposes less restrictive) assumption that
d\gg nmq^{4}\log(n)+m\sqrt{n}q^{2}\log(n)
and we might hope that under this new assumption, the sum converges, even if including sub-graphs with leaves.

Unfortunately, this is not the case when given only the bounds from
2.1
, because the leading terms
\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
are too large for this purpose. One approach to deal with this situation would be to use similar symmetries as used for
p=\frac{1}{2}
to eliminate at least some of the leading terms we obtain from
2.1
(instead of the entire expectation) whenever we encounter a graph with leaves. However, there are two important problems with this idea. First, summing explicitly over all squared expected signed weights would be significantly more involved as before because every individual term in the sum fractures into a sum of multiple individual terms that depend on the exact degree sequence of
\alpha
and out of which some terms “zero-out” while others do not. Secondly (and more importantly), even if we could blindly omit all the leading terms from
2.1
, then the resulting sum would still not be small enough, simply due to the presence of
single edges
which actually carry a non-negligible weight, even after ignoring the associated leading terms
\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
. While this problem arises purely from conditioning, it can not simply be solved by arguing about preserved symmetries like we did for leaved graphs in case of
p=\frac{1}{2}
. This is because this time, the problem arises from fluctuations in the
length
of the latent vectors, and not their
orientation
.

However, there is a quite different way of bounding our sum that avoids both problems. To this end, we abandon the last equality in
Equation

2.1
and instead derive an upper bound on
\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)
in terms of an
exponential sum
. Concretely, we obtain

\displaystyle 1+\chi^{2}({\mathcal{L}}{(\mu\mid S_{\rho})},\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\underset{\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)},\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)}\sim S_{\rho}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(m\sum_{\emptyset\neq\alpha\subseteq R}q^{2|\alpha|}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\textsc{Sw}^{(1)}(K_{1,\alpha})\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\textsc{Sw}^{(2)}(K_{1,\alpha})\right]}{(p(1-p))^{|\alpha|}}\right)\right]

where for
k\in\{0,1\}
we define
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Sw}^{(k)}(K_{1,\alpha})]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[\prod_{j\in\alpha}(\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{j}^{(k)},\mathbf{x}\rangle)-p)\mid\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(k)}]
. In other words, we express
\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)
as an exponential sum over
conditional expected signed weights of stars
. Plugging in the bounds from
2.1
, some fortunate simplifications occur already in the exponent.

Omitting large stars and higher order terms.

First of all, it turns out the sum in the exponent is dominated only by relatively few of its leading terms. Concretely our bounds on the
conditional expectation

\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Sw}^{(k)}(K_{1,\alpha})]
already allow us to omit all terms that correspond to
|\alpha|\geq\log(m)
. Moreover, after splitting each term
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Sw}^{(k)}(K_{1,\alpha})]
into leading and remainder terms as in
2.1
, we obtain the expression
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Sw}^{(1)}(K_{1,\alpha})]\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Sw}^{(2)}(K_{1,\alpha})]=\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)})\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)})+\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)})r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)})+\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)})r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)})+r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)})r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)})
, and we can observe that the sum over all remainder terms of the form
r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)})r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)})
can be omitted as well.

Bounding the remaining exponential terms via hypercontractivity
After these simplifications, each term in the exponent can explicitly be expressed as a polynomial in the right-sided latent vectors
\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)},\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)}
we conditioned on.
After we split the exponential sum into a product via Cauchy-Schwarz, the exponent appearing in each term
\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)})
or
\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)})
from
2.1
for some small set
\alpha\subseteq R
. Using the explicit representation of
\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
from
2.1
, we can explicitly control the variance of the polynomials in the exponents over the randomness of the latent vectors
\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
.
Since the integrand (hidden in the expectation over
\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)},\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)}
) is non-negative due to the exponential, we can essentially remove the conditioning on
S_{\rho}
while still taking advantage of the fact that requiring
\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\in S_{\rho}
implies an explicit (deterministic) upper bound on the integrand. Since the
\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}
are standard Gaussian, we can use tail bounds obtained from Gaussian hypercontractivity to integrate each term over the randomness of
\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)},\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)}
up to the explicit upper bound obtained from conditioning on
S_{\rho}
.
This argument shows that each exponential is essentially of order
1+C\sigma
, where
\sigma
denotes the variance of the polynomial in the exponent.
We remark that this approach forms an extension of the ideas first considered in
[
4
, Section 9]
. The difference is that we work with different, and at the same time higher-degree polynomials than considered before.

Bounding the exponential sum over all edges

The sum resulting from this is almost small enough. The only obstacle left is formed by the exponential sum over humble
single edges
for which even the higher order terms
r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)}),r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)})
carry non-negligible contributions. Fortunately, due to the non-negativity of the exponential, we can remove the conditioning on
S_{\rho}
and expand the result as a Taylor series. Once again, we now end up with an (infinite) sum over expected signed weights, similar to the one considered in the beginning. However, since we already eliminated all patterns except for single edges from the exponent, we can now ensure that all expected signed weights that still appear in the sum correspond only to
disjoint unions of stars
. For such simple structures, the expected signed weight can be bounded explicitly, even unconditionally.

What is important to note about this final step is that the purpose of switching from a sum to an exponential only to get back at a (even larger) sum is helpful because it allows us to remove the conditioning on
S_{\rho}
which previously had caused the problems related to single edges. As explained before, this would not be as easy if we summed over all edges before switching to the exponential.

Known vs unknown masks.

So far we assumed that we compare
\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d)
to
\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)
, i.e., that we are in the case of a unknown mask. Elegantly, the exact same proof also applies to the setting of a known mask
\mathbf{M}
. To see how, we note that we have so far shown that

\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left({\mathcal{L}}(\mu\mid S_{\rho}),\nu\right)=\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{M}}\left[\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid\mathbf{M},S_{\rho})\right],\nu\right)=o(1),

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{M}}\left[\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid\mathbf{M},S_{\rho})\right]
is the distribution
\mu
conditioned on a fixed mask
\mathbf{M}
and on the event
S_{\rho}
, so
{\mathcal{L}}(\mu\mid S_{\rho})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{M}}\left[\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid\mathbf{M},S_{\rho})\right]
. On the other hand, if we pulled the expectation over
\mathbf{M}
out of the expression and could instead show

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{M}}\left[\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid\mathbf{M},S_{\rho}),\nu\right)\right]=o(1),

then
\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{W}_{\textbf{M}}(n,m,q,p,d),\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)\right)=o(1)
would follow with probability
1-o(1)
over the choice of
\mathbf{M}
, by Markov’s inequality. Remarkably, if we apply the second moment method to
\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid\mathbf{M},S_{\rho}),\nu\right)
and then take the expectation over
\mathbf{M}
, all that changes is that we only apply
one expectation
over
\mathbf{M}
instead of the expectation over
two independent replicas
of
\mathbf{M}
as before. This reflects precisely in replacing the factor of
q^{2|\alpha|}
in front of every term by a factor of
q^{|\alpha|}
while leaving the remaining proof untouched.

2.6.
Bounding signed weights after cancellations in Fourier space

It remains to outline the essential core ideas underlying all of our arguments: the proof of
2.1
. Inspired by the recent ideas presented in
[
1
]
for bounding the expected signed weight of certain pattern graphs in terms of joint cumulants via modified Edgeworth expansions, we adopt a similar Fourier-theoretic viewpoint.

To this end, we wish to bound the expected signed weight
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\textsc{Sw}(K_{1,\alpha})\mid\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\right]
of a star
K_{1,\alpha}
with leaves given by
\alpha\subseteq R
conditional on some fixed, right-sided latent vectors given by
\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}
. For this, we essentially wish to understand how the joint distribution of the edges in
\alpha
deviates from independence. To understand the dependencies that are present, we start from the observation that the inner products

\mathbf{z}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}(\langle\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}\rangle,\langle\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}\rangle,\ldots,\langle\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\mathbf{x}\rangle)^{\top}
for
\alpha=\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
and fixed latent vectors
(\mathbf{x}_{u})_{u\in\alpha}
are jointly Gaussian with covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\alpha}
given as

(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\alpha})_{u,v}=\tfrac{1}{d}\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v}\rangle.

To bound
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\textsc{Sw}(K_{1,\alpha})\mid\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\right]
we would like to integrate the above distribution component-wise up to the connection threshold
\tau
and then study its deviations from a random vector with i.i.d.
\mathsf{Bern}(p)
entries by evaluating the alternating sum

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\textsc{Sw}(K_{1,\alpha})\mid\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\bigg[{\prod_{\ell\in\alpha}(\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\mathbf{x}\rangle)-p)}\bigg]=\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}p^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\bigg[{\prod_{\ell\in\beta}\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\mathbf{x}\rangle)}\bigg].

To this end, we essentially wish to understand the probability
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big[{\prod_{\ell\in\beta}\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\mathbf{x}\rangle)}\big]
for all edge sub-graphs
\beta\subseteq\alpha
. However, to get a good bound on the entire sum, it does not nearly suffice to compute each
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big[{\prod_{\ell\in\beta}\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\mathbf{x}\rangle)}\big]
up to the asymptotically correct order. The reason for this is that
cancellations
occur in the above expression that render the entire sum
much smaller
than the absolute value of its largest term. Because of this, it is actually very important to precisely understand many of the
higher-order terms
that make up
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big[{\prod_{\ell\in\beta}\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\mathbf{x}\rangle)}\big]
. This is even more true if we even wish to get an explicit representation of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big[{\prod_{\ell\in\beta}\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\mathbf{x}\rangle)}\big]
as a polynomial in the fixed latent vectors like in
2.1
.

Expressing perturbations from a ground state.

To start understanding dependencies, we wish to compare
\mathbf{z}_{\alpha}
to an independent ground state.
As the intuition would suggest, the appropriate choice for this ground state is essentially a standard Gaussian vector
\mathbf{z}_{\emptyset}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\alpha|}
. We say ‘essentially’, because we actually need to slightly correct the variance along each dimension, simply to ensure that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{z}_{\emptyset}(j)\leq\tau\right)=p
for all
j
such that we match the ground-truth density
p
after integrating. Therefore, we set our ground state as
\mathbf{z}_{\emptyset}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{I})
where
\widehat{\sigma}
is the
reference variance
chosen as a function of
p
.

Intermediate states.

To better represent every single term in (
2.2
), it is convenient to define some more random vectors with varying levels of dependence in between the very dependent setting given by
\mathbf{z}_{\alpha}
and the independent ground state
\mathbf{z}_{\emptyset}
. Concretely, for every
\beta\subseteq\alpha
, we define an
intermediate state

\mathbf{z}_{\beta}
as a Gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{|\alpha|}
with mean
0
and covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta}
given by

(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta})_{u,v}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{d}\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{u}\rangle&\text{if }u,v\in\beta\\
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}&\text{if }u=v\text{ and }u,v\notin\beta\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Accordingly,
\mathbf{z}_{\beta}
behaves like
\mathbf{z}_{\alpha}
when restricted to the the entries in
\beta
, but has independent components like
\mathbf{z}_{\emptyset}
otherwise.
The big advantage defining these intermediate states is that now,

p^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[{\prod}_{\ell\in\beta}\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\mathbf{x}\rangle)\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{e\in\alpha}\{\mathbf{z}_{\beta}(e)\leq\tau\}\right)
, so our expression for
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\textsc{Sw}(K_{1,\alpha})\mid\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\right]
turns into the succinct expression

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\textsc{Sw}(K_{1,\alpha})\mid\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\right]=\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\ \mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{e\in\alpha}\{\mathbf{z}_{\beta}(e)\leq\tau\}\right).

Switching to Fourier space.

It turns out that it is much easier to understand the cancellations happening in the above sum after applying a Fourier transform all our states
\mathbf{z}_{\beta}
. The reason for this is that the Gaussian distribution has a succinct Fourier representation (a.k.a. characteristic function) that explicitly allows us to express the dependencies between the entries of
\mathbf{z}_{\alpha}
and relate them to the ground state.

The first step towards this is to express
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\textsc{Sw}(K_{1,\alpha})\mid\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\right]
in terms of the
characteristic functions

\varphi_{\beta}(\mathbf{t})
(i.e. the Fourier transforms) of each of the states
\mathbf{z}_{\beta}
. The inversion theorem allows us to express

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\textsc{Sw}(K_{1,\alpha})\mid\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{|\alpha|}}\int_{{\mathcal{T}}}e^{-{i\mathbf{t}}^{\!\top}{\!\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}}\left(\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\hskip 2.84544pt\varphi_{\beta}(\mathbf{t})\right)\prod_{e\in\mathbf{t}}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{h}(\mathbf{t}(e))}{i\mathbf{t}(e)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t},

where
\mathbf{1}_{h}(\mathbf{t}(e))
is a limiting object in
h\rightarrow\infty
that mostly behaves like 1 for our purposes, where
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
is a vector that contains
\tau
in every coordinate, and where
{\mathcal{T}}=[-\infty,\infty]^{|\alpha|}
.

Cancellations after expanding characteristic functions.

Now, we switched from an alternating sum of probabilities to an alternating sum of characteristic functions.
The advantage of this is that it is now much easier to compare every term to the ground state given by
\varphi_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{t})
by considering the ratio
\varphi_{\beta}(\mathbf{t})/\varphi_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{t})
in the sense that

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\hskip 2.84544pt\varphi_{\beta}(\mathbf{t})\right)=\varphi_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{t})\left(\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{\varphi_{\beta}(\mathbf{t})}{\varphi_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{t})}\right)=\varphi_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{t})\left(\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\hskip 2.84544pt\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\top}\!(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{I})\ \mathbf{t}\right)\right).

Then, applying a Taylor expansion to the exponential in the sum, we get a sum of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\hskip 2.84544pt\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\top}\!(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{I})\ \mathbf{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2^{k}k!}\sum_{r_{1},\ldots r_{k}\in\alpha\times\alpha}\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\Phi_{\beta}^{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})}(\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle\text{with }\Phi_{\beta}^{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})}(\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{I})_{r_{j}(1),r_{j}(2)}\mathbf{t}(r_{j}(1))\mathbf{t}(r_{j}(2)).

Now, if we recall the definition of the support
{\textsc{Supp}}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})
as the set of elements
\gamma\subseteq\alpha
covered by at least one of the
2
-tuples
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
, then it turns out that every term corresponding to
{\textsc{Supp}}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})\neq\alpha
cancels out. This phenomenon arises due to the special structure of the
\Phi_{\beta}^{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})}(\mathbf{t})
resulting from our definition of the intermediate states
\mathbf{z}_{\beta}
.

We refer to
Section

6
for the details underlying the above phenomena. The upshot is that the cancellation phenomena add the so-called
coverage constraint

{\textsc{Supp}}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})=\alpha
to the sum over
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
. In particular, this has the consequence that
all terms corresponding to
k<\lceil|\alpha|/2\rceil
vanish
because satisfying the coverage constraint
{\textsc{Supp}}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})=\alpha
is impossible.

Reversing the Fourier transform.

After our cancellations, the alternating sum inside of the integral evaluates as

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\hskip 2.84544pt\varphi_{\beta}(\mathbf{t})\right)=\varphi_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{t})\left(\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{2^{\ell}\ell!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\ldots r_{\ell}\in\alpha\times\alpha\\
{\textsc{Supp}}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})=\alpha\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\Phi_{\alpha}^{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell})}(\mathbf{t})+\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})\right)

for
\ell\coloneqq\lceil|\alpha|/2\rceil
and remainder terms grouped together in
\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})
. Due to the simple expression for
\varphi_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{t})
and the explicit expression of the
\Phi_{\beta}^{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})}(\mathbf{t})
the integral over the first term above evaluates explicitly as a polynomial involving derivatives of the PDF of our ground state
\mathbf{z}_{\emptyset}
, which is equal to
\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
from
2.1
. After carefully controlling the integral over the absolute value of all the remainder terms in
\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})
, we further obtain an explicit bound on
|r_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})|
which is of lower order. For this step, we can avoid complicated sums over Hermite polynomials by exploiting some spectral properties of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{I}
implied by conditioning on
S_{\rho}
.

3.
Outlook

Let us now give some ideas for possible extensions of this work. The most important thing to mention is that our main technical result
2.1
can be extended to the non-bipartite case to give meaningful bounds at least for small
\alpha
of size poly-logarithmic in
n
. On a technical level, this requires some further work in the sense that we cannot limit ourselves to work with characteristic functions of the Gaussian distribution anymore. However, it turns out that similar cancellations in Fourier space as described above occur if we split the characteristic function into the product of a ground state corresponding to independent edges and a remainder, and then apply a series expansions to this remainder but not to the ground state. The terms in these expansions can be expressed in terms of
joint cumulants
whose properties enable similar cancellation phenomena as observed here, while the remainder terms and the resulting errors can be bounded explicitly at the expense of poly-logarithmic factors as long as
|\alpha|\leq\text{polylog}(n)
. The resulting bounds on
\mathbb{E}\left[\textsc{Sw}(\alpha)\right]
decay in powers of
1/\sqrt{d}
while the exponent is proportional to
|\alpha|
instead of
|V(\alpha)|
. This, in particular, improves the bounds given in
[
3
]
and might enable new insights into statistical testing and estimation involving RGGs from the lens of
low-degree polynomials
.
Moreover, the techniques underlying our
2.1
are not limited to Gaussian latent vectors, but can be expected to continue to work on other product spaces like the torus or in case of an anisotropic Gaussian distribution of latent vectors.

Finally, it would be very interesting to see whether the results can be extended to the sparse case where
p=o(1)
. After all, finding the right information-theoretic thresholds for distinguishing RGGs from Erdös-Rényi graphs for all
p=o(1)
is a prominent open problem that has attracted quite some attention over the last years
[
3
,
15
]
. The bipartite setting is thereby an important special case. Since our techniques yield tight testing thresholds for a different notion of sparsity (
q=o(1)
rather than
p=o(1)
), it would be interesting to see whether they also help us understand the case of
p=o(1)
.

Lemma 4.6

5.
Information-Theoretic Hardness

We can use the methods described previously not only for results on low-degree hardness, but also for deriving information-theoretic lower bounds. Concretely,
this section is devoted to proving the following

Theorem 5.1

(Information-theoretic hardness for unknown masks)
.

Consider any fixed
p\in(0,1)
. Then

\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d),\ \mathbb{M}(n,m,p)\right)=o(1)

under one of the following conditions.

(i)

d\gg nmq^{4}\log(n)
and
d\gg m\sqrt{n}q^{2}\log(n)
.

(ii)

d\gg nmq^{4}\log(n)
and
p=1/2
.

Theorem 5.2

(Information-theoretic hardness for known masks)
.

Consider any fixed
p\in(0,1)
. Then with probability
1-o(1)
over the choice of
\ \mathbf{M}\sim\mathbb{M}(n,m,q)

\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{W}_{\textbf{M}}(n,m,q,p,d),\ \mathbb{M}(n,m,p)\right)=o(1)

under one of the following conditions

(i)

d\gg nmq^{2}\log(n)
and
d\gg m\sqrt{n}q\log(n)
.

(ii)

d\gg nmq^{2}\log(n)
and
p=1/2
.

The proof of both theorems uses a conditional second moment method. Let us for now focus on outlining the techniques used for
\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d)
, we will later see how to adapt them to
\mathbb{W}_{\textbf{M}}(n,m,q,p,d)
. To this end, we denote by
\mu
the distribution of
\mathbb{W}(n,m,q,p,d)
, and we denote by
\nu
the distribution of
\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)
. To bound the total variation distance between the two models, we use the triangle inequality to bound

\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mu,\nu\right)\leq\mathbb{P}(\overline{S_{\rho}})+\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid S_{\rho}),\nu\right),

where
\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid S_{\rho})
denotes the distribution
\mu
conditional on the latent information being in some ’good’ event
S_{\rho}\in\sigma(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
to be specified later.
Since
S_{\rho}
will be chosen such that
\mathbb{P}(\overline{S_{\rho}})=o(1)
, it suffices to consider the second term. To this end, we pass from total variation to
\chi^{2}
-divergence, i.e.,

2\mathrm{d}_{\text{TV}}(\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid S_{\rho}),\nu)^{2}\leq\chi^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid S_{\rho}),\nu)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\nu}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid S_{\rho})}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)\right)^{2}\right]-1.

Now, using that
\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid S_{\rho})
is a mixture distribution in terms of some latent information
\phi
, we can express
\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid S_{\rho})=\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\left[\mu_{\phi}\right]
for some distribution
\mu_{\phi}
and then replace the squared expectation by an expectation over two independent copies of the latent randomness
\phi\coloneqq(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{M})
where
\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is the matrix of latent vectors in
R
, and
\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m}
is the mask used to re-randomize the entries in
W
. Concretely, we get

\chi^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid S),\nu)=\underset{\xi\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\underset{\phi\sim\varrho}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\phi}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)\right]\right)^{2}\right]-1=\underset{\phi^{(1)},\phi^{(2)}\sim\varrho\otimes\varrho}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\underset{\xi\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\phi^{(1)}}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\phi^{(2)}}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)\right]\right]-1

where
\varrho
denotes the law of
\phi
conditional on
S_{\rho}
.
Since
\nu
is a sufficiently simple distribution, the inner expectation evaluates explicitly and allows us to simplify the above expression. Concretely, denoting the columns of
\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{X}_{\text{L}}
by
(\mathbf{x}_{u})_{u\in R}
and
(\mathbf{x}_{u})_{u\in L}
, respectively, we use a similar calculation as in
[
16
]
and express the probability of drawing a concrete matrix
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
under the conditional distribution
\mu_{\phi}
as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\phi}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\underset{(\mathbf{x}_{u})_{u\in L}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\ {\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in L\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in R\end{subarray}}\frac{\sigma_{\phi}(\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v}\rangle)^{\xi_{u,v}}\left(1-\sigma_{\phi}(\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v}\rangle)\right)^{1-\xi_{u,v}}}{p^{\xi_{u,v}}(1-p)^{1-\xi_{u,v}}}}\right]\text{ with }\sigma_{\phi}(\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v}\rangle)\coloneqq\begin{cases}p&\text{if }\mathbf{M}_{u,v}=1\\
\sigma(\langle\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v}\rangle)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

To plug this back into our expression for
\chi^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid S_{\rho}),\nu)
, we express our latent randomness as
\phi^{(1)}\coloneqq(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)},\mathbf{M}^{(1)})
and
\phi^{(2)}\coloneqq(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)},\mathbf{M}^{(2)})
, and—in order to simplify notation—we abbreviate
\sigma^{(1)}_{1,v}=\sigma_{\phi^{(1)}}(\langle\mathbf{x}_{u}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}_{v}^{(1)}\rangle)
and
\sigma^{(2)}_{1,v}=\\

5.1.4.

5.2.
The case of
p=1/2

To prove the stronger bounds for
p=1/2
, the methods presented so far would be sufficient, however, they would need some strengthening that would lead to quite some increase in technical complexity. However, it turns out that there is a much simpler and more direct proof, which crucially exploits symmetries arising only in the

Specifically, going back to
Section

5
, instead of switching to an exponential over the sum of conditional signed weights of stars, we expand the expectation into a single sum over squared, conditional signed weights over all subgraphs of

\displaystyle 1+\chi^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mu\mid S_{\rho}),\nu)

\displaystyle=\underset{\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(1)},\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}^{(2)}\sim S_{\rho}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(1+\sum_{\emptyset\neq\alpha\subseteq R}q^{2|\alpha|}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\textsc{Sw}^{(1)}(K_{1,\alpha})\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\textsc{Sw}^{(2)}(K_{1,\alpha})\right]}{(p(1-p))^{|\alpha|}}\right)^{m}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt=1+\sum_{\emptyset\neq\alpha\subseteq K_{n,m}}\left(\frac{q^{2}}{p(1-p)}\right)^{|\alpha|}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{\text{L}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\sim S}\left[\textsc{Sw}(\alpha)\right]\Big)^{2}.

Above, the expression
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{\text{L}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\sim S}\left[\textsc{Sw}(\alpha)\right]
refers to the expected signed weight of
\alpha
such that the left sided latent vectors are drawn unconditionally, while the right sided ones are drawn conditional on
S
. What is very important to take into account when summing over all

if

Observation 5.13

(Noise operator in the conditional probability space)
.

Define the noise operator

\mathbf{T}f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})=\underset{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\sim\{\pm 1\}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(\xi_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n})\right]

,

\underset{\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\sim S}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{T}f(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})\right]=\underset{\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\sim S}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})\right],

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{\emptyset\neq\alpha\subseteq K_{n,m}}\left(\frac{q^{2}}{p(1-p)}\right)^{|\alpha|}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{\text{L}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}\sim S}\left[\textsc{Sw}(\alpha)\right]\Big)^{2}\leq 1+\sum_{a=2}^{n}\sum_{b=2}^{m}\binom{n}{a}\binom{m}{b}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{s}\\

6.2.
Expanding the characteristic function and exploiting cancellations

It is quite convenient for us that the characteristic functions

\displaystyle\varphi_{\beta}(\mathbf{t})=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta}\mathbf{t}\right)

6.2

Concretely, given any

\displaystyle{\textsc{Supp}}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})\coloneqq\{e\in\alpha\mid r_{j}(1)=e\text{ or }r_{j}(2)=e\text{ for some }j\},

Lemma 6.4

Given any

Follows from the fact that

Now, we observe that the number of positive and negative terms in the inner most sum is exactly the same, except when

\overline{\gamma}

Lemma 6.5

Combining all the facts we gathered up to this point, we obtain the following simplification, using in particular that whenever

6.3.
Integrating out

Now, it remains to use the facts established so far together with

6.2

While

6.2

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\textsc{Sw}(K_{1,\alpha})\mid(\mathbf{x}_{u})_{u\in\alpha}\right]

Lemma 6.7

(Leading term)
.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{1}=\lim_{h\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-1}{(2\pi)^{|\alpha|}}\int_{{\mathcal{T}}}e^{-{i\mathbf{t}}^{\!\top}{\!\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\ \widehat{\Lambda}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})\prod_{e\in\mathbf{t}}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{h}(\mathbf{t}(e))}{i\mathbf{t}(e)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}=\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}}).

{\mathcal{I}}_{1}=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{2^{\ell}\ell!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\ldots r_{\ell}\in\alpha\times\alpha\\

\Phi_{\alpha}^{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell})}(\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{(2\pi)^{|\alpha|}}\int_{[-\infty,\infty]^{|\alpha|}}e^{-{i\mathbf{t}}^{\!\top}{\!\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\Phi_{\alpha}^{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell})}(\mathbf{t})\prod_{e\in\mathbf{t}}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{h}(\mathbf{t}(e))}{i\mathbf{t}(e)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}

\widehat{\sigma}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{|\alpha|}}\int_{[-\infty,\infty]^{|\alpha|}}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\prod_{e\in\alpha}(i\mathbf{t}(e))^{s_{e}-1}\left(e^{-i\mathbf{t}(e)\tau}-e^{-i\mathbf{t}(e)(h+\tau)}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt=\prod_{e\in\alpha}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\mathrm{t}^{2}}(i\mathrm{t})^{s_{e}-1}\left(e^{-i\mathrm{t}\tau}-e^{-i\mathrm{t}(h+\tau)}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{t}=\prod_{e\in\alpha}\left(\phi_{\widehat{\sigma}}^{s_{e}-1}(\tau)-\phi_{\widehat{\sigma}}^{s_{e}-1}(\tau+h)\right).

Taking the limit over

, the second term in each factor vanishes and we end up with the term
\Lambda_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}})
, as desired.
∎

Regarding
{\mathcal{I}}_{2}
, we use a different strategy and split the integral into two parts based on
\|\mathbf{t}\|\leq T
and
\|\mathbf{t}\|>T
where
T
is some large enough value (as a function of
d,|\alpha|
) to be chosen later. To split the integral, we set

f(\mathbf{t})\coloneqq\left(\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha\setminus\beta|}\hskip 2.84544pt\varphi_{\beta}(\mathbf{t})\right)-e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\widehat{\Lambda}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})

and bound

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{I}}_{2}|\leq\int_{\|\mathbf{t}\|<T}|f(\mathbf{t})|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}+\int_{\|\mathbf{t}\|\geq T}|f(\mathbf{t})|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}.

Notice that we can omit the limit over
h
, as
\left|\prod_{e\in\mathbf{t}}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{h}(\mathbf{t}(e))}{i\mathbf{t}(e)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}\right|\leq 1
for all

. We bound the two parts above now separately.

Lemma 6.8

(Bound for large
\|\mathbf{t}\|
)
.

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\|\mathbf{t}\|\geq T}|f(\mathbf{t})|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}=0.

Proof.

Bound

\displaystyle\int_{\|\mathbf{t}\|\geq T}|f(\mathbf{t})|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}\leq\int_{\|\mathbf{t}\|\geq T}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\left|\widehat{\Lambda}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}+\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}\int_{\|\mathbf{t}\|\geq T}\left|\varphi_{\beta}(\mathbf{t})\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}.

To see that the limit over
T\rightarrow\infty
for the first term is
0
, it suffices to note that
\widehat{\Lambda}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})
is a polynomial in
\mathbf{t}
, so the integral over all of
{\mathcal{T}}
would be finite and thus tends to zero once we restrict
\|\mathbf{t}\|\geq T
and send
T\rightarrow\infty
. For the second term, we use
Lemma

6.1
to get
\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta}-\mathbf{I}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\delta(\varepsilon)
and thus
\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta}\mathbf{t}=\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}+\mathbf{t}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{t}\geq(1-\delta(\varepsilon))\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}.
Hence for
\varepsilon
small enough, we get

\displaystyle|\varphi_{\beta}(\mathbf{t})|

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta}\mathbf{t}}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\text{ and thus }\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}\int_{\|\mathbf{t}\|\geq T}\left|\varphi_{\beta}(\mathbf{t})\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}=0.\qed

For
\|\mathbf{t}\|\leq T
, we use the fact that
\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})
is equal to a Taylor expansion of
f(\mathbf{t})
, which is uniformly convergent for all
\mathbf{t}\leq T
. Once we restrict
\|\mathbf{t}\|\leq T
, it is therefore not hard to argue that we can exchange the sum and the integral. Each integral in the resulting sum can then be bounded explicitly such that the entire sum is convergent and small enough. We capture this in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.9

(Bound for small
\|\mathbf{t}\|
)
.

There is an absolute constant
C>0
such that for every
T\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\|\mathbf{t}\|<T}|f(\mathbf{t})|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}=\int_{\|\mathbf{t}\|<T}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\left|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}\leq\Bigg(\frac{C|\alpha|\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\Bigg)^{\ell+1}.

Proof.

We apply Cauchy-Schwarz within the definition of
\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})
together with the spectral norm bound from
Lemma

6.1
to get

\displaystyle\int_{\|\mathbf{t}\|<T}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\left|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\beta\subseteq\alpha}\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}k!}\int_{[-\infty,\infty]^{|\alpha|}}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2k}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\beta}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{I}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{k}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{|\alpha|}\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{\infty}\Bigg(\frac{C|\alpha|\rho}{k\sqrt{d}}\Bigg)^{k}\int_{[-\infty,\infty]^{|\alpha|}}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2k}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}

where in the last step, we used Stirling’s approximation to take
k!
into the exponential term. The integral above can now be bounded by exploiting the spherical symmetry, i.e., by rewriting

\displaystyle\int_{[-\infty,\infty]^{|\alpha|}}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2k}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}=\text{Vol}(\mathbb{S}^{|\alpha|-1})\int_{\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}t^{2}}t^{2k+|\alpha|-1}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 91.04872pt\leq C\ \text{Vol}(\mathbb{S}^{|\alpha|-1})\int_{\infty}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{\frac{2k+|\alpha|}{2}-1}\mathrm{d}s=C\ \text{Vol}(\mathbb{S}^{|\alpha|-1})\ \Gamma\left(\frac{2k+|\alpha|}{2}\right).

where
C>0
is a constant and
\text{Vol}(\mathbb{S}^{|\alpha|-1})
is the volume of the
|\alpha|
-dimensional unit sphere, which can be bounded as

\text{Vol}(\mathbb{S}^{|\alpha|-1})\leq(C/|\alpha|)^{|\alpha|/2}
if
C
is a suitable constant. Plugging all this back into our sum over

\displaystyle\int_{\|\mathbf{t}\|<T}e^{-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{t}\|^{2}}\left|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{R}},\mathbf{t})\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{|\alpha|}\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{\infty}\Bigg(\frac{C|\alpha|\rho}{k\sqrt{d}}\Bigg)^{k}\left(\frac{C}{|\alpha|}\right)^{\frac{|\alpha|}{2}}\left(Ck\right)^{\frac{2k+|\alpha|}{2}}

Now, by our assumption that

we get that the base of the first factor in the sum is at most
C^{2}\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. Hence, the ratio of two consecutive terms in the above sum is at most

for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, where we used that

, so the second factor is at most a constant. Therefore, the sum is geometric and dominated by its first term, which provides our explicit upper bound as stated in the lemma.
∎

Using

for both
\mathbb{W}_{\textbf{M}}(n,m,q,p,d)
and
\mathbb{M}(n,m,p)
. These changes then yield the lemma using otherwise the same arguments as above.
