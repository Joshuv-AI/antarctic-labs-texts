A Woman With Generalized Abdominal Pain
Authors: Thiti Tanglitanon, Tanyaporn Nakornchai, Apichaya Monsomboon
Published: 2026

An 81-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with a 4-day history of generalized abdominal pain. She denied fever, vomiting, and diarrhea. Physical examination revealed diffuse abdominal tenderness without guarding or rebound tenderness. Her medical history was significant for multiple comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and stage 3 chronic kidney disease. Chest radiography demonstrated free air beneath both hemidiaphragms (Fig 1). Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), performed using both curvilinear and linear transducers, revealed intra-abdominal reverberation artifacts and the enhanced peritoneal stripe sign.1,2 (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4; Videos 1–3). Axial computed tomography images of the abdomen subsequently confirmed the presence of free intraperitoneal air (Fig 5).

Diagnosis: Gastric Perforation at The Pyloroduodenal Junction

The patient was diagnosed with a gastric perforation at the pyloroduodenal junction. Subsequently, an emergency laparoscopic repair was performed utilizing a primary suture closure technique.

Although chest radiography (CXR) has traditionally served as the initial imaging modality for detecting pneumoperitoneum, recent data show that POCUS, specifically through the identification of the enhanced peritoneal stripe sign, demonstrated superior sensitivity compared with CXR (93% vs. 79%) while maintaining comparable specificity (64%).4 Consequently, POCUS represents a valuable bedside screening tool that facilitates rapid assessment and serial examinations. Nevertheless, positive sonographic findings warrant confirmatory abdominal CT when feasible. Computed tomography remains the diagnostic gold standard, offering a sensitivity of 96% to 100% and accurately localizing the gastrointestinal perforation site in 80% to 90% of cases to guide definitive surgical management.5
