Dietary patterns supportive of gut microbiota and bacterial vaginosis: A cross-sectional analysis from NHANES 2001 to 2004
Authors: Jinhua Zeng, Ziling Ding, Zichang Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is the leading form of vaginal microbiome imbalance in women of reproductive age and has been associated with dietary patterns and gut microbial profiles. This study investigated the association between a literature-based dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM) and BV prevalence in US women. We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 1169 nonpregnant women aged 20 to 49 years from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2001 to 2004 with Nugent score measurements and complete dietary data. BV was defined as a Nugent score ≥ 7. Dietary intake was assessed via two 24-hour recalls, and DI-GM was computed based on 14 food groups classified as beneficial or unbeneficial for gut microbial health. Multivariable survey-weighted logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals. BV prevalence was 31.2%. Higher DI-GM scores were associated with lower odds of BV; women with scores ≥6 had reduced odds compared with those with scores of 0 to 3 (adjusted odds ratio = 0.75; 95% confidence interval: 0.60–0.94; P = .015; P-trend = .013). In conclusion, higher DI-GM scores were inversely associated with BV prevalence in this cross-sectional study. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, and prospective studies are needed to confirm causality.

1. Introduction

Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is a common dysbiosis of the vaginal microbiota in which normally dominant Lactobacillus species are depleted, and mixed anaerobes proliferate. This imbalance, often diagnosed by a Nugent score ≥ 7, is associated with adverse reproductive outcomes (e.g., preterm birth) and increased risk of sexually transmitted infections.[1] Known risk factors include ethnicity, sexual practices, vaginal hygiene, and hormonal fluctuations.[2] Since diet powerfully shapes the gut microbiome, and gut bacteria influence systemic immunity and metabolism, it is plausible that overall diet quality could affect vaginal flora. Recent research has begun to explore this gut–vagina axis. For example, diets rich in whole grains, dietary fiber, and plant-based foods tend to promote a Lactobacillus-dominant vaginal community and lower BV odds,[3] whereas high glycemic load or high-fat diets correlate with increased BV prevalence.[4]

Diet quality can be summarized by composite indices. One such metric is the dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM), a novel index derived from epidemiologic studies linking food components to gut microbial profiles.[5] The DI-GM assigns points for foods reported to favor beneficial gut bacteria (e.g., fiber-rich whole grains, fermented dairy, legumes, fruits, vegetables) and subtracts points for foods detrimental to the gut flora (e.g., red/processed meat, refined grains, a very high-fat diet).[5] Studies have reported that foods that are beneficial for gut microbiota composition correlate positively with gut-health biomarkers.[6]

Several epidemiologic studies have linked specific diet patterns to BV risk. For instance, in a US cohort, Thoma et al found that women consuming diets with higher glycemic load had higher BV odds, whereas a nutrient-rich (naturally nutrient-rich) diet was protective.[4] More recent case-control and review articles have shown that intakes of whole grains, fiber, and plant-derived phytochemicals are inversely associated with BV, while diets high in saturated fat and animal proteins tend to increase BV risk.[2] An Iranian study reported that a higher Dietary Inflammatory Index (reflecting pro-inflammatory foods) was associated with higher BV odds.[7] Together, these findings suggest that overall diet quality and composition influence vaginal microbial balance, but most work has focused on specific nutrients or foods rather than a holistic dietary pattern score.

To our knowledge, however, no prior study has applied a gut-microbiota-focused dietary index to vaginal health outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between DI-GM and BV prevalence using data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2001 to 2004. We hypothesized that higher DI-GM scores would be inversely associated with BV. Prespecified effect modifiers examined in subgroup analyses included age and smoking status, based on prior evidence suggesting differential vulnerability to dysbiosis across these factors.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and population

This cross-sectional study used data from the NHANES 2001 to 2004, a nationally representative survey of the US civilian, noninstitutionalized population conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics. NHANES employs a complex, multistage probability sampling design involving oversampling of certain demographic groups to ensure adequate representation. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about/erb.html).

For this analysis, we included nonpregnant women aged 20 to 49 years who underwent vaginal swab testing for BV and completed two 24-hour dietary recalls. Participants were excluded if they were pregnant, lacked Nugent score measurements, or had incomplete dietary data needed to compute the DI-GM. After applying exclusion criteria, 1169 women were eligible for the final analytic sample (Fig. 1).

2.2. Assessment of BV

BV status was determined using Nugent scoring of Gram-stained vaginal smears. NHANES follows standardized laboratory protocols in which trained technicians score vaginal microbiota on a 0 to 10 scale based on the presence of large Gram-positive rods (Lactobacillus morphotypes), small Gram-variable rods (Gardnerella vaginalis and Bacteroides), and curved Gram-variable rods (Mobiluncus). Consistent with clinical standards, BV was defined as Nugent score ≥ 7, while scores 0 to 6 were classified as negative. Nugent scoring was selected due to its high reliability and clinical validity in epidemiologic research.[8]

2.3. Dietary assessment and calculation of DI-GM

Dietary intake was assessed using 2 interviewer-administered 24-hour dietary recalls conducted by trained NHANES dietary interviewers using the Automated Multiple-Pass Method. Nutrient and food-group intakes were quantified using the United States Department of Agriculture Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies.

DI-GM was derived from dietary components previously reported in the literature to favorably or unfavorably influence gut microbial composition.[5] Following published methodology, we categorized dietary items into beneficial components (e.g., whole grains, avocados, cranberries, chickpeas, broccoli, soybeans, fermented dairy, fiber, and tea/coffee) and unbeneficial components (e.g., refined grains, red/processed meats, high-fat foods, sugary items). For scoring, each dietary component was awarded 1 point when intake aligned with microbiota-supportive patterns. Beneficial components received 1 point when consumption exceeded the sex-specific median, whereas adverse components contributed 1 point when consumption fell below the median (Table S1). All other intakes were scored as 0. Individual component scores were aggregated to produce a total DI-GM ranging from 0 to 14, with higher scores reflecting a diet more compatible with favorable gut microbial characteristics.[5] For descriptive analyses, DI-GM was evaluated both as a continuous variable and in categories (0–3, 4, 5, and ≥6) to account for distributional characteristics within NHANES 2001 to 2004.

2.4. Covariates

Covariate selection was informed by prior scientific literature. We included demographic, behavioral, and clinical characteristics known or suspected to influence both dietary intake and vaginal microbiota composition. The final adjusted model (Model 2) included all of the following covariates. Age, measured in years, was incorporated as a continuous variable. Ethnicity was categorized as Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, or Other. Marital status was classified as married or living with a partner versus all other categories. Education level was grouped as less than high school, high school graduate, or greater than high school. Alcohol consumption was recorded as “yes” if participants reported drinking any alcoholic beverage during the past 12 months and “no” otherwise. Smoking status distinguished smokers, defined as individuals who reported current smoking of cigarettes “every day” or “some days,” from nonsmokers, who reported no current smoking. Body mass index (BMI) was classified using centers for disease control and prevention adult cut-points: normal weight (BMI < 24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obesity (≥30 kg/m2; https://www.cdc.gov/bmi/adult-calculator/bmi-categories.html). Physical activity was classified using NHANES metabolic equivalent (MET)-based criteria as low (<500 MET-minutes per week), moderate (500–1000 MET-minutes per week), or vigorous (≥1000 MET-minutes per week) based on intensity and duration of reported activities.[9] Serum uric acid concentration (mg/dL) was included as a clinical metabolic marker linked to oxidative stress, diet quality, and systemic inflammation. Sexual intercourse status (yes or no) was also included. The poverty-income ratio is a measure used in NHANES to assess socioeconomic status. It is defined as the ratio of a household’s income to the federal poverty threshold, which is adjusted for family size and composition.[10]

2.5. Statistical analysis

All inferential analyses incorporated NHANES’ complex sampling structure, including survey weights, primary sampling units, and strata, to ensure nationally representative estimates and valid variance estimation. Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages for descriptive purposes. Differences in participant characteristics by BV status in Table 1 were assessed using independent sample t tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. All regression analyses evaluating associations between DI-GM and BV were conducted using survey-weighted methods.

2.5.1. Primary analyses

To evaluate the association between DI-GM and BV, multivariable logistic regression models were fitted to obtain odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Analyses began with unadjusted models and subsequently included models adjusted for relevant covariates. DI-GM was evaluated both as a continuous metric and in predefined categories (0–3, 4, 5, ≥6). Tests for linear trend were conducted by entering the categorical variable as an ordinal term in the regression model.

2.5.2. Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted several prespecified sensitivity analyses, including alternative categorizations of DI-GM (tertiles and quartiles) and additional adjustment for total energy intake (kcal/day). Results were materially unchanged after energy adjustment, indicating the robustness of the primary findings.

2.5.3. Exploratory subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses by age (<25, 25–35, >35 years) and smoking status (current smoker vs nonsmoker) were prespecified based on prior evidence suggesting differential BV risk across these factors. In each stratified analysis, all covariates other than the stratifying factor were retained in the model. Interaction was examined by including multiplicative terms, with P-values < .10 considered indicative of possible effect modification. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

All statistical procedures were conducted in Stata 17 using commands appropriate for complex survey data. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < .05 for primary analyses.

3. Results

3.1. Participant characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the 1169 women. BV prevalence varied by ethnicity, marital status, alcohol use, education, smoking, and BMI (all P < .001), but not by sexual activity or uric acid levels. Women with BV had lower DI-GM scores (4.42 ± 1.12 vs 4.55 ± 1.00, P = .002) and lower beneficial components (2.12 ± 0.63 vs 2.24 ± 0.58, P < .001).

3.2. Association between DI-GM and BV

In unadjusted models (Model 1), higher DI-GM scores were significantly associated with lower odds of BV (OR per unit increase = 0.92; 95% CI: 0.87–0.98; P = .006). When categorized, women with DI-GM scores of 5 and ≥6 had significantly lower odds of BV compared with those with scores of 0 to 3 (OR = 0.77; 95% CI: 0.64–0.92, P = .004, and OR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.56–0.85, P = .001, respectively). After adjusting for confounders (Model 2), the association was attenuated but remained significant for higher categories (≥5: OR = 0.81; 95% CI: 0.67–0.98, P = .031; ≥6: OR = 0.75; 95% CI: 0.60–0.94, P = .015), with a significant trend across categories (P for trend = .013). The beneficial component of DI-GM showed a protective association with BV in the unadjusted model (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.67–0.93; P = .004), which was attenuated and became marginally nonsignificant after adjustment (OR = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.72–1.02; P = .076; Table 2).

3.4. Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we performed several sensitivity analyses examining the DI-GM-BV association under alternative specifications (Table S2). When DI-GM was categorized into tertiles, women in the highest tertile had significantly lower odds of BV compared with those in the lowest tertile (adjusted OR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.63–0.96; P = .019). Similarly, using quartile categorization, the highest quartile showed a stronger protective association (adjusted OR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.55–0.92; P = .009; P-trend = .007). In continuous models, the association was directionally consistent but did not reach statistical significance (OR per 1-unit increase = 0.95; 95% CI: 0.89–1.01; P = .09). However, after additional adjustment for total energy intake, the association became statistically significant (OR = 0.94; 95% CI: 0.89–0.99; P = .031). Overall, these findings support the robustness of the primary results.

4. Discussion

In this large cross-sectional analysis of 1169 women, higher diet quality as measured by a novel DI-GM was associated with significantly lower odds of BV. Women with BV had lower mean DI-GM scores and lower scores on the beneficial component than BV-negative women. In unadjusted models, each 1-point increase in DI-GM corresponded to an 8% reduction in BV odds. When DI-GM was categorized, women in the highest categories (scores ≥5 and ≥6) had significantly lower BV odds than those in the lowest category (scores 0–3). For example, after multivariable adjustment, the ORs were about 0.81 for DI-GM ≥ 5 and 0.75 for DI-GM ≥ 6 (vs DI-GM ≤ 3), with a significant dose–response trend. The “beneficial” component score showed a protective association in unadjusted analyses that was attenuated after adjustment. In exploratory subgroup analyses, inverse associations between DI-GM and BV were observed in some strata, including women aged 25 to 35 years and nonsmokers. However, these differences were not supported by statistically significant interaction tests, suggesting no clear evidence of effect modification. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating.

Our findings fit within a growing literature that links overall diet quality with vaginal health. Prior studies have reported that specific dietary factors influence BV risk. For example, Neggers et al found that women consuming more total and saturated fat had significantly higher odds of BV, whereas higher intakes of micronutrients (folate, vitamin A, calcium) were associated with lower risk of severe BV.[11] Thoma et al similarly observed that diets with higher glycemic load were positively correlated with BV prevalence and persistence.[4] Conversely, Shivakoti et al showed that each standard deviation increase in dietary fiber intake was linked to roughly half the odds of molecular-BV.[12] These studies suggest that nutrient-poor, high-fat or high-GI diets elevate BV risk, whereas micronutrient- and fiber-rich diets support a Lactobacillus-dominant flora. Our results are consistent with this pattern. Dall’Asta et al reported that women consuming higher amounts of carbohydrates and sugars before pregnancy tended to have a Lactobacillus-rich vaginal microbiota (lower Nugent scores), whereas higher animal-protein intake predicted a dysbiotic flora.[13] Likewise, a 2025 Frontiers review highlighted that limiting alcohol and animal protein (and increasing omega-3 fats) helps maintain protective Lactobacillus communities in the vagina.[1] In contrast, some studies have not found strong diet–BV links; for instance, Song et al found no significant associations between individual macronutrient intakes and vaginal community state types.[14] Such discrepancies may reflect differences in study populations, BV definitions (clinical Nugent vs molecular community state types), or dietary assessment methods.

Several biological pathways have been hypothesized to explain the inverse association between a gut-friendly diet and BV risk, though these mechanisms could not be directly tested in our study. A leading hypothesis involves the gut–vaginal microbiome axis. Diet strongly shapes gut microbial composition. For example, high-fiber, plant-based diets enrich beneficial gut taxa such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, whereas diets high in animal protein and fat favor taxa such as Bacteroides and Clostridia.[15] Because the gut and vagina can share bacterial species, a healthier gut microbiota may reduce the likelihood of pathogenic bacteria migrating to the vagina. Indeed, prior work suggests that the gut can serve as an extravaginal reservoir for BV-associated bacteria that may ascend into the genital tract.[16] In this way, a high DI-GM diet could indirectly protect the vagina by promoting a balanced gut microbiome. Dietary intake may also alter the vaginal nutrient milieu directly. Lactobacilli in the vagina ferment glycogen-derived substrates into lactic acid, maintaining a low vaginal pH (<4.5) that inhibits BV pathogens. Diets high in complex carbohydrates can raise systemic glucose levels, increasing glycogen deposition in vaginal epithelial cells, which then fuels Lactobacillus growth and lactic acid production.[14,17] Indeed, some studies suggest that high-starch diets correlate with higher vaginal glycogen and lactobacilli. In contrast, BV is characterized by lactobacilli depletion and a shift in vaginal metabolites. Vaginal fluid from BV cases shows markedly reduced lactic acid and elevated levels of biogenic amines (e.g., putrescine, cadaverine) and short-chain fatty acids.[18–20] These metabolic changes raise the pH and promote anaerobic overgrowth. We hypothesize that the foods emphasized in a high DI-GM score help maintain the acidic, lactate-rich environment that deters BV bacteria. Finally, micronutrient-mediated immune and epithelial effects may play a role. Nutrients such as folate, vitamins A, C, E, and minerals (inversely associated with BV in prior studies[11]) are crucial for epithelial integrity and innate immunity in mucosal tissues. For example, vitamin A is necessary for mucosal antibody production and epithelial cell renewal,[21] while folate is needed for rapid cell division and deoxyribonucleic acid repair.[22] Deficiencies in these nutrients could weaken the vaginal barrier or reduce production of antimicrobial peptides, facilitating dysbiosis. Conversely, a nutritious diet delivers ample micronutrients that support host defenses. One specific compound is betaine, a methyl donor found in vegetables. Tuddenham et al reported that women in the lowest quartile of betaine intake had about 9-fold higher odds of molecular-BV.[23] Betaine may stabilize osmotic balance or epigenetic regulation in vaginal tissues.[24] In summary, while these pathways offer plausible explanations, they remain hypothetical in the context of our study given the absence of direct microbiome and metabolic measurements. From a clinical perspective, the observed association, approximately 25% lower odds of BV among women with higher DI-GM scores compared with those with lower scores, represents a difference that could have important population-level implications given the high prevalence and recurrence of BV. However, residual confounding remains possible, particularly from incompletely measured sexual behaviors, antibiotic exposure, and other behavioral factors known to influence BV risk. Because this was a cross-sectional analysis, temporality and causality cannot be established. Prospective cohort studies and randomized dietary interventions incorporating vaginal microbiome profiling would be necessary to determine whether improving overall diet quality causally reduces BV incidence or recurrence.

This study has notable strengths. The large sample size provided ample power to detect associations and allowed adjustment for many confounders. We used a comprehensive, literature-based dietary index (DI-GM) that integrates multiple foods and nutrients known to affect gut microbiota, enabling an overall assessment of diet quality rather than isolated nutrients. We adjusted for key demographic and behavioral factors and performed subgroup analyses to test the robustness of the DI-GM–BV relationship. Despite these strengths, there are limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and we cannot determine whether dietary patterns caused the BV differences, and reverse causation (e.g., women changing diet because of symptoms) cannot be excluded. Although we adjusted for many variables, residual confounding is possible. For instance, we did not have data on intravaginal practices or undiagnosed infections (which may correlate with both diet and BV). Dietary intake was self-reported, which introduces recall error and misclassification. The DI-GM index itself, while evidence-based, is new and has not been externally validated against actual gut microbiome measurements in this or other populations. We did not directly measure the gut or vaginal microbiota or vaginal pH, so the proposed microbial and metabolic mechanisms remain hypothetical. Finally, our cohort, although large, may not be fully representative of all populations (dietary habits and other unmeasured factors could differ elsewhere). Nonetheless, the consistency of our findings with prior research and across sensitivity analyses lends credibility to the observed diet–BV associations.

5. Conclusion

This cross-sectional study identified an inverse association between higher DI-GM scores and BV prevalence. While these findings suggest a potential link between diet quality and vaginal health, causality cannot be inferred. Future prospective and interventional studies are needed to determine whether dietary modification can influence BV risk.
