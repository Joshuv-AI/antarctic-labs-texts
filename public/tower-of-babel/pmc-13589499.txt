Preferential engagement of the anti-inflammatory ATP/adenosine axis by HIV vaccine candidate with V1-deleted envelope in male rhesus macaques

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe evaluate the efficacy and immunogenicity of HIV clade A/E A244 envelope (Env) immunogens with the 23 amino acids of variable region 1 deleted (ΔV1) or retained (wild-type [WT]) in macaques. Only the ΔV1 regimen significantly reduces the risk of mucosal acquisition of clade C simian/human immunodeficiency virus (SHIV)1157(QNE)Y173H versus controls, providing 81% efficacy and leaving 10 of 12 immunized animals uninfected. ΔV1 vaccination induces higher systemic antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) targeting helical-V2 and anti-inflammatory myeloid cells, which, together with IL-17+NKp44+ innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) and systemic PD-1+ helper T cells, correlated with reduced infection risk. By contrast, WT immunization induces higher IL-15, CCR2+pDC, and gp70/V1V2-biased responses, which, along with mucosal IFN-γ+NKG2A−NKp44− ILCs, were associated with increased susceptibility. Ex vivo, ΔV1 gp120 reduced CCR5 expression on CD4+ T cells relative to WT gp120, consistent with the anti-inflammatory mucosal response by ΔV1-vaccine regimens in vivo. Thus, V1 deletion promotes an anti-inflammatory mucosal landscape less permissive to HIV seeding and dissemination following virus exposure.

Introduction

The RV144 clinical vaccine trial showed 31.2% efficacy against human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) acquisition using ALVAC-HIV followed by a gp120 boost formulated in alum.1,2 The primary correlate of reduced risk was the blood IgG level of antibodies binding the gp120 variable region 2 (V2), measured with scaffolds containing V1/V2 or V2 peptides from multiple HIV clades.3,4,5 A protective role for V2-specific antibodies was further supported by mutations at V2 positions 169–184 in transmitted viruses, including the RDK and LDI/V motifs that bind the gut-homing α4β7 integrin receptor,6,7,8,9 consistent with vaccine-induced immune pressure.10,11 Because V2 engagement of α4β7 or the α4β7/CD4 complex promotes virus homing to the gut12 and costimulates CD4+ T cells to upregulate CCR5 expression, a crucial receptor for viral entry,12,13 antibodies that recognize the helical, but not the β strand, conformation of V2 can effectively inhibit α4β7 engagement and V2 costimulation of CD4+ T cells.13 In macaques, vaccine-induced antibodies to helical V2 have likewise been associated with reduced SIVmac251 acquisition.14,15 In RV1444 and in SIVmac251 studies16,17,18,19,20,21 using analogous ALVAC/gp120/alum regimens, both V2-binding antibodies and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) correlated with lower risk of HIV acquisition. Preclinical macaque studies also identified a detrimental role for V1-directed humoral responses: anti-V1 antibodies interfered with V2-specific ADCC and with V2 binding to α4β7.19 Accordingly, removing V1 from simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) immunogens, while preserving the helical V2 conformation, reduced the risk of virus acquisition after vaccination.19 Indeed, in vitro data showed that the monoclonal antibody (mAb) NCI05, which binds V2 in a helical conformation, mediates killing of infected cells in the presence of soluble CD4, suggesting that the helical conformation is transiently exposed and represents a critical site of viral vulnerability.19,22 Additionally, following systemic administration of mAb NCI05 in macaques, NCI05 levels in mucosal secretions correlated with delayed SIVmac251 infection.23

While these findings highlight a central role of antibodies targeting an apical prefusion helical conformation of V2 in lowering the risk of infection, not surprisingly, a plethora of cellular protective innate and adaptive immune responses also contribute, including CD14+ monocytes, Th1/Th2 cells, and mucosal envelope-specific IL-17+NKp44+ innate lymphoid cells (ILCs).15,17,19,21,24 In contrast, CD14−CD16+ non-classical monocytes in blood,15 mucosal IFN-γ+NKG2A−NKp44− cells,14,18,20,21,25 and activated CCR5+CD4+ T cells15 have been linked to increased infection risk. Here, we engineered HIV envelope immunogens that were either V1 deleted (ΔV1) or V1 replete (wild-type [WT]) and compared their immunogenicity and efficacy in macaques. Macaques vaccinated with HIV ΔV1 fared better than those vaccinated with V1-replete immunogens, and key differences between the ΔV1- and WT-vaccinated animals were the level of antibodies to V2 along with a suite of immune cells, including CD73-expressing dendritic cells (DCs) promoting effective ADCC and clearance via efferocytosis. Unexpectedly, we uncovered that V1-containing vaccines are more inflammatory, suggesting that V1 may be an overlooked HIV envelope component evolved to hijack protective innate and adaptive immunity, independently of its ability to induce interfering antibodies. Collectively, the HIV and SIV macaque data support deleting V1 from HIV envelope immunogens, provided that V2 is maintained in its helical conformation.

Results

HIV-based ΔV1 vaccine decreases risk of SHIV-C acquisition

Prior studies with SIV Env immunogens showed that removal of the V1 loop improves vaccine efficacy.19 To determine whether this strategy could be extended to HIV Env immunogens, we engineered V1-deleted derivatives of the HIV-1 A244 D11 Env, a clade A/E immunogen in which 11 amino acids at the amino terminus had previously been removed to enhance V1/V2 antigenicity.3 This backbone was used to generate the ΔV1-A244 gp160 plasmid DNA and ΔV1-A244 gp120 protein vaccines by deleting the V1 region (TKANLTNVNNRTNVSNIIGNITD).19 As expected, ΔV1-A244 gp120 showed reduced binding to PG9,26 a neutralizing mAb that recognizes V2 in a β sheet conformation, compared with WT-A244 gp120 (Figure S1A).27,28 In contrast, ΔV1-A244 and WT-A244 gp120 bound comparably to CH58 and CH59, α-helical28,29 and coil-helical V2 epitopes,28 respectively (Figure S1B). Thus, V1 deletion reduced exposure of the β sheet V2 conformation while preserving helical V2 antigenicity.

Since SIV-based vaccines delivered by gag DNA, gp160 DNA, and ALVAC generate virus-like particles (VLPs), we assessed whether V1 deletion in HIV altered VLP formation. 293T cells were co-transfected with p55gag plasmid DNA and either WT- or ΔV1-A244 gp160 plasmid DNA. Western blot analysis and CH58 immunoprecipitation showed comparable gp120 expression in cell lysates and in VLPs isolated from culture supernatants (Figures S1C and S1D). Electron microscopy confirmed abundant VLP production in both WT- and ΔV1-transfected cultures (Figure 1A). We then evaluated immunogenicity and protective efficacy in male rhesus macaques assigned to ΔV1 vaccination (n = 12), WT vaccination (n = 12), or no vaccination (n = 11). The study was powered to estimate the relative efficacy of each vaccine versus controls but not to directly compare the two vaccine groups. Vaccinated animals received DNA primes at weeks 0 and 4 with p55gag plus either ΔV1-A244 or WT-A244 gp160, followed by ALVAC-HIV vCP2438 expressing gag-pro (clade B) and gp120/TM (clade C)30,31 boosts at weeks 8 and 12 and a matched alum-adjuvanted gp120 protein boost at week 12 (Figure 1B). Beginning at week 21, animals underwent up to 11 weekly intrarectal challenges with clade C-derived SHIV1157(QNE)Y173H, whose V2 region shares 71.4% amino-acid identity with A244 Env (Figure 1C). ΔV1 vaccination significantly reduced SHIV acquisition relative to controls (p = 0.021; Figure 1D), with an estimated vaccine efficacy of 81%. However, the WT vaccine did not show any protection (p = 0.256; Figure 1D). Among infected animals, plasma SHIV RNA levels were comparable across groups during acute and chronic infection (Figures 1E and S1E). These findings replicate the study that used SIVmac251 challenge,19 confirming that deletion of V1 in the HIV envelope also improves protection against virus acquisition without measurably affecting post-infection viral burden in breakthrough infections.

ΔV1 vaccine enhances cross-clade systemic and mucosal Env-specific antibody responses

We next compared humoral responses elicited by ΔV1 and WT regimens. ΔV1 vaccination induced significantly higher gp120-specific plasma IgG against the homologous clade A/E A244 antigen (p < 0.001) and against three heterologous clade C gp120 antigens, 1086C (p = 0.01), 96ZM651 (p < 0.001), and CH505 (p < 0.001) (Figure 1F).32,33 In contrast, WT vaccination elicited higher gp70-scaffolded V1/V2 reactivity to clade A/E CM244 (p = 0.04) and clade C 96ZM651 (p = 0.04) (Figure 1F). Since only two ΔV1-vaccinated animals became infected, within-group correlate analyses lacked statistical power. We therefore combined the two vaccine groups for analyses of immune correlates of acquisition, while also reporting subgroup findings when informative. Interestingly, this latter reactivity, likely toward V1 epitopes, was correlated with earlier acquisition (p = 0.04, R = −0.43; Figure 1G) and was higher in infected animals (p = 0.04; Figure 1H).

ΔV1 vaccination was associated with increased Env-specific IgG-producing B cells in rectal tissue, with a higher fold change (p = 0.01) and a trend toward greater frequency at week 17 (p = 0.052) relative to WT vaccination (Figures 1I, S1F, and S1G), likely related to the ability of the WT regimen to induce more circulating plasmablasts expressing CXCR3+ (p = 0.03; Figures 1J and S1H) that promote B cell recruitment to inflammatory sites rather than the mucosa through CXCL10.34 Accordingly, higher levels of Env-specific IgG against A244 (p = 0.033), 1086C (p = 0.002), 96ZM651 (p < 0.001), and CH505 (p < 0.001) gp120s (Figure 1K) were observed in the ΔV1 group in rectal secretions, and these antibody responses strongly correlated with their matched plasma IgG levels only in the ΔV1 group (Figure 1L), suggesting better systemic-to-mucosal coupling of the antibody level following ΔV1 than WT immunization. Rectal IgG to WT-A244 and ΔV1-A244 also trended higher in ΔV1-vaccinated animals (Figures S1I and S1J), whereas rectal IgA responses were not significantly different (Figures S1K and S1L).

Neither regimen induced neutralization of tier 2 CM244, and only three ΔV1-vaccinated animals neutralized tier 1 TH023.6 (Figures S1M and S1N), precluding outcome correlations. Thus, the ΔV1 regimen was associated primarily with stronger systemic and mucosal Env-specific IgG responses, rather than with broad virus neutralization.

Across all vaccinated animals, total ADCC and both CH58- and CH59-defined V2-specific ADCC correlated with delayed SHIV acquisition (total: R = 0.57, p = 0.003; CH58: R = 0.63, p = 0.001; CH59: R = 0.58, p = 0.003; Figures 2E–2G). Similar associations were observed within the WT group alone (total: R = 0.63, p = 0.03; CH58: R = 0.63, p = 0.03; CH59: R = 0.65, p = 0.02; Figures 2H–2J), supporting prior evidence that ADCC alone is insufficient for complete protection.23 Consistently, animals that remained uninfected had higher total, CH58-defined, and CH59-defined ADCC than animals that became infected (p = 0.009, p = 0.002, and p = 0.01, respectively; Figures 2K–2M). V2-specific (CH58) ADCC correlated positively with Env-specific IgG-producing B cells (R = 0.54, p = 0.009; Figure S1P) and with rectal IgG response (R = 0.50, p = 0.01; Figure 2N) but inversely with antibodies to V1-containing 96ZM652 gp70 (R = −0.46, p = 0.03; Figure 2O), consistent with the interpretation of the data in Figure 1G that anti-V1 antibodies interfere with V2 ADCC and vaccine efficacy, as observed previously.19 Thus, protection was associated with functional V2-directed antibodies, with ΔV1 vaccination preferentially enhancing CH58-like α-helical V2 ADCC.

We next examined CD73+mDCs, because CD73 is a 5′-nucleotidase enzyme that generates adenosine, an anti-inflammatory mediator that can limit T cell activation through IL-10 induction.36 CD73+mDC frequencies were comparable between groups at week 14 (p = 0.13; Figure 3E), but ΔV1 vaccination induced a greater increase from baseline (p = 0.005; Figure 3F). Higher CD73+mDC frequencies were associated with delayed acquisition (R = 0.46, p = 0.03; Figure 3G), trended similarly within WT animals (Figure S2E), and were enriched in uninfected macaques (p = 0.03; Figure 3H). CD73+mDCs also correlated with the frequency and fold change of mucosal Env-specific IgG+ B cells in the combined cohort (R = 0.54, p = 0.008 and R = 0.51, p = 0.01; Figures 3I and 3J) but trended only in the individual groups (Figures S2F–S2I). Thus, protection was associated with a DC profile marked by increased CD73+mDCs, reduced CCR2+pDCs, and enhanced mucosal humoral immunity.

ΔV1 vaccine promotes pro-resolving anti-inflammatory myeloid responses

We next assessed whether WT and ΔV1 HIV immunogens differentially shaped circulating monocytes (Figure S3A). Bulk frequencies of classical (CD14+CD16−), intermediate (CD14+CD16+), and non-classical (CD14−CD16+) monocytes were comparable between groups (Figures S3B–S3D). However, increased fold changes in classical monocytes were associated with SHIV acquisition and reduced V2-specific ADCC (Figures S3E–S3H). WT vaccination preferentially increased CCR7+ classical monocytes (Figure S3I), whereas the ΔV1 regimen favored regulatory and pro-resolving features, including increased CD209+CD14+CD16+ intermediate monocytes (Figure S3J) and higher CD14+ efferocytes at the end of immunization (p = 0.023, Figures 3K, S3K, and S3L). The overall frequency of efferocytic monocytes did not differ by infection outcome (p < 0.999; Figure S3M) and was not correlated with delayed acquisition (R = 0.02, p = 0.93; Figure S3N). Notably, correlation of efferocytosis with vaccine protection was observed with the ΔV1 vaccine group in several studies.16,18,20 Likely, having only two ΔV1-immunized animals that became infected in the current study may limit our power to observe this ΔV1 effect. Nevertheless, CD14+ efferocytes correlated with mucosal Env-specific IgG+ B cell expansion, systemic CXCR3+mDC increases, and reductions in CXCR3+ plasmablasts (Figures S3O–S3Q), signatures enriched in protected ΔV1 vaccinees.

Mucosal CD14+ cells and CD73+DC10 are associated with reduced risk of infection

Protection from rectal challenge is likely related to the local mucosal immune environment. We therefore profiled myeloid, DC, and natural killer (NK)/ILC populations in rectal biopsies collected after the final immunization, at weeks 13 and 15 (Figure S4). In the combined vaccinated cohort, frequencies of CD14+CD16− and CD14+CD16+ monocytes were associated with delayed SHIV acquisition (R = 0.42, p = 0.04 and R = 0.58, p = 0.003; Figures 4C and 4D). This correlation was also observed for CD14+CD16+ cells in the WT group alone (Figure S5B; R = 0.81, p = 0.001; Figure S5C). Contrasting effect of NKG2A+, NKp44+, and NKG2A−NKp44− on the risk of virus acquisition

Since mucosal NK/ILC populations and memory-like NK cells have been linked to reduced SIV/SHIV acquisition,14,18,20,21,25,38 we analyzed these subsets in rectal biopsies. Env-specific mucosal NK cell responses could not be measured directly; therefore, cells were stimulated with PMA/ionomycin to assess functional potential. Total memory NK cell frequencies did not differ between vaccine groups but were higher in animals that remained uninfected (p < 0.001; Figures 4L and S6A) and correlated with delayed acquisition in the combined vaccinated cohort and in WT vaccinees (R = 0.56, p < 0.001; Figures 4M and S6B). ΔV1 vaccination increased PMA/ionomycin-induced CD107+ and TNF-α+ memory NK cells compared with WT (p = 0.02 and p = 0.005, respectively; Figures S6C and S6D), and both subsets trended higher in uninfected animals (Figures S6E and S6F). Although neither subset correlated with delayed acquisition (Figures S6G and S6H), CD107+ memory-like NK cells correlated with V2-specific ADCC (R = 0.50, p = 0.01; Figure 4N).

Total NKp44+ ILCs and IL-17+NKp44+ ILCs were comparable between vaccine groups (Figures S6I and S6J), but both correlated with delayed acquisition in the combined cohort (R = 0.68, p < 0.001; R = 0.70, p < 0.001; Figures 4O and 4P) and within WT vaccinees (Figures S6K and S6L). Both populations were enriched in uninfected animals (p = 0.001 for both; Figures 4Q and S6M), and NKp44+ ILCs correlated with V2-specific ADCC (R = 0.44, p = 0.03; Figure 4R). PMA/ionomycin-induced NKG2A−NKp44− mucosal cells producing IFN-γ also did not differ between the two vaccine groups (Figure S6N) and, as observed previously,14 were associated with earlier acquisition in both groups combined (R = −0.63, p = 0.001; Figure 4S) and in the WT group alone (R = −0.65, p = 0.02; Figure S6O) and were also higher in infected animals (p = 0.001; Figure 4T). PMA/ionomycin-induced CD107+NKG2A−NKp44− mucosal cells followed this same pattern (Figures S6P–S6R). Thus, protection was linked to mucosal innate cell states associated with tissue repair and IL-17 production, whereas susceptibility tracked with IFN-γ-dominant innate inflammation.

ΔV1-gp120 reduces mucosal CCR5 and increases α4β7 expression on CD4+ T cells

Since mucosal CD4+ T cells are primary targets of HIV/SIV, we examined rectal CD4+ phenotypes in fixed biopsies from vaccinated animals, given limited sample availability. Total mucosal CD4+ cell density did not differ by the vaccine group (p = 0.14; Figure S8I) but correlated with delayed acquisition and was higher in animals that remained uninfected (R = 0.44, p = 0.03 and p = 0.03; Figures 5M and 5N). Mucosal CD4+ density also correlated with the frequency of vaccine-induced circulating PD-1+Ki67+CD95+ memory CD4+ T cells (R = 0.46, p = 0.03; Figure 5O), linking systemic helper responses to mucosal homeostasis.

Across vaccinated animals, V2-specific (CH58) ADCC correlated positively with PD-1+Ki67+CD95+CD4+ T cells (R = 0.58, p = 0.004; Figure 5P), PD-1+Ki67+Th1 cells, and PD-1+Ki67+Th2 cells (Figures S8J and S8K). These results are consistent with prior studies in macaques and humans linking circulating PD-1+ helper populations with plasmablast responses43 and improved antibody quality,44 supporting a role for PD-1+ helper T cells in promoting functional antibody responses. In contrast, V2-specific (CH58) ADCC correlated inversely with Ki67+CD95+ Th1 cells (Figure S8L).

High levels of IL-15 associated with decreased vaccine efficacy

To investigate soluble immune mediators that may have orchestrated vaccine immunity, we measured 36 cytokines and chemokines in plasma at week 13. Absolute concentrations did not differ significantly between vaccine groups (Table S1). However, the fold change in IL-15 from baseline to week 13 was lower in the ΔV1 group than in the WT group (p = 0.004; Figure 5Q). Higher plasma IL-15 as well as TNFSF12 at week 13 was associated with earlier virus acquisition in the combined vaccinated cohort (R = −0.53, p = 0.008 and R = −0.49, p = 0.01; Figures 5R and 5S), IL-15 with earlier virus acquisition in the WT group (Figures S9A and S9B), and IL-15 and TNFS12 levels were higher in animals that became infected (Figures S9C and S9D). Furthermore, larger fold changes of IL-15 were associated with increased CCR7+CD14+CD16− monocytes in blood (Figure S9E) and plasma gp70 antibodies (Figures S9F and S9G), along with decreased gp120 antibodies in both plasma and rectal secretions (Figures S9H–S9M), PMA/ionomycin-induced TNF-α+ memory NK cells in the rectal mucosa (Figure S9N), total ADCC (Figure S9O), PD-1+Ki67+Th2, and PD-1+Ki67+ CD4+ T cells in blood (Figures S9P and S9Q) as well as CD4+ cells per square millimeter of rectal mucosa (Figure S9R). These data identify IL-15 as a systemic marker of an inflammatory state linked to susceptibility to SHIV acquisition.

Baseline inflammatory signatures predict SHIV susceptibility, whereas ICOS+ and α4β7+ helper T cell profiles predict protection

We next examined whether pre-vaccination immune states influenced SHIV susceptibility after repeated virus exposure. Animals with higher baseline frequencies of circulating CD86+DC10 or CD206+DC10 in blood, higher plasma CSF1 or CCL13, and higher rectal NKG2A−NKp44− ILC frequencies acquired SHIV earlier (Figures S13A–S13F). Baseline CD86+DC10 or CD206+DC10 frequencies also correlated inversely with subsequent fold changes in gp120-specific IgG+ memory B cells at week 17 or V2-specific (CH58) ADCC at week 21 (Figures S13F–S13H), respectively, suggesting that these pre-existing DC10 phenotypes may limit vaccine-induced humoral effector responses.

In contrast, animals that remained uninfected had higher baseline ICOS+Th1 or ICOS+Th2 cells, as well as CCR5−α4β7+Th1 cells (Figures S13I–S13K). Baseline ICOS+Th2 cells correlated positively with V2-specific (CH58) ADCC, whereas CCR5−α4β7+Th1 cells correlated inversely with IL-15-fold change at week 13 (Figures S13F, S13L, and S13M).

Together, these findings suggest that pre-existing immune states enriched for ICOS+ helper T cells and α4β7+Th1 cells may favor antibody-dependent effector mechanisms and apoptotic clearance of SHIV-infected cells, thereby contributing to protection from infection (Figure S13N).

LASSO modeling confirms the identified immune correlates of acquisition outcome and highlights the myeloid-induced adenosine axis

To identify immune features most strongly associated with acquisition outcome, we applied least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) modeling across measured analytes, with or without prefiltering for association with time to acquisition or infection status. We collected model coefficients for both minimum and first standard error regularization penalties (lambda), using either 10-fold or leave-one-out cross-validation (see STAR Methods). We then compared analytes with non-zero coefficients across this range of parameters to determine the most consistent contributors to protection (Figure S14). This analysis identified several features also detected by univariate analyses. Rectal infiltrating CD14+CD16+ monocytes, total NKp44+ ILCs, IL-17+NKp44+ ILCs, and ADCC contributed most consistently to protection, whereas mucosal IFNγ+NKp44−NKG2A− ILCs, circulating Th1 cells, and plasma TNFSF12 contributed to earlier infection (Figures S14A and S14B). We also attempted similar methods to highlight the strongest contributors to the handful of intriguing analytes elevated in ΔV1-vaccinated animals: B cells (Figure S14C), ADCC (Figure S14D), efferocytosis (Figure S14E), and depleted IL-15 (Figure S14F). While many of the analytes identified by LASSO for these outcomes were not related to either vaccine group or time of acquisition, frequencies of CD73+DC10 in rectal mucosa survived LASSO selection as a strong contributor to both B cell levels and ADCC (Figures S14C and S14D), providing further support for the central role of the myeloid-induced adenosine axis.

Discussion

In this study, deletion of 23 amino acids from V1 (TKANLTNVNNRTNVSNIIGNITD) in clade A/E Env immunogens, while preserving the helical V2 conformation, conferred substantial protection against heterologous mucosal SHIV clade C acquisition. ΔV1 vaccination protected approximately 80% of macaques and reduced average acquisition risk by 81% relative to unvaccinated controls. By contrast, an otherwise identical regimen containing the intact V1 loop was not efficacious. Together with prior studies using similarly engineered SIV ΔV1 Env immunogens,19 these data indicate that V1 deletion reproducibly improves vaccine efficacy when the helical V2 epitope is maintained.

Systems-level profiling showed that ΔV1 and WT immunogens induced distinct systemic and mucosal immune landscapes (Figures 7 and S15). Reduced acquisition risk was paired with a coordinated ΔV1-induced program characterized by higher Env-specific IgG in rectal secretions, increased mucosal Env-specific B cells, broader cross-clade binding to clade C Envs, and enhanced V2-directed ADCC. These responses were accompanied by lower CXCR3 expression on plasmablasts, suggesting improved trafficking or retention of vaccine-induced B cells in the gut. Reduced risk of acquisition was also linked to a mucosal myeloid and innate-effector network. Recruitment of Env-specific B cells to the rectal mucosa was associated with systemic CD73+mDCs, reduced acquisition risk, and increased mucosal tolerogenic CD73+DC10 cells, which correlated with enhanced ADCC and efferocytosis by CD14+ cells. This suggests that ΔV1 vaccination promoted antibody-mediated recognition of infected cells and innate mechanisms for their clearance. Higher frequencies of mucosal CD107+ NK cells and CD14+CD16− infiltrating monocytes further correlated with helical V2-directed ADCC. Systemically, ΔV1 vaccination was associated with increased CD14+CD16+ monocytes, CD28+ICOS+ Th1 cells, and CXCR3+mDCs linked to enhanced efferocytosis. Lower plasma IL-15 was associated with higher gp120-specific antibody levels and stronger CH58 ADCC, identifying restrained inflammatory signaling as an additional feature of protection. Protection was further associated with elevated rectal CD14+CD16+ monocytes, DC10 expressing PDL1 or CD86, memory NK cells, total NKp44+, IL-17+NKp44+ ILCs, and total CD4+ T cells, as well as systemic α4β7+Th1, ICOS+ Th2, and various PD-1+T subsets, many of which correlated with increased CH58 ADCC.

These findings support a model in which ΔV1 vaccination engages the ATP/adenosine axis to promote tissue homeostasis and antibody function. CD73 on myeloid cells can induce IL-10 production and promote efferocytosis,50 whereas adenosine supports mucosal barrier integrity by enhancing epithelial tight junctions, mucus production, and chloride secretion.51 Activation of this pathway may complement IL-17+NKp44+ ILC-mediated maintenance of gut homeostasis, creating a mucosal environment less permissive to viral establishment. Because adenosine also participates in B cell maturation,52,53,54 this pathway may contribute to the increased magnitude and breadth of anti-Env antibodies induced by ΔV1 vaccination. Thus, V1 deletion appears to coordinate B cell, myeloid cell, and barrier programs that enhance ADCC, promote clearance of infected or dying cells, and preserve mucosal homeostasis (Figures 7 and S15).

WT vaccination produced the opposite profile (Figures 7 and S15). The intact V1 regimen was associated with increased rectal CD16+ monocytes; expansion of circulating CCR5+α4β7+ Th2 cells, CCR2+pDCs, and CXCR3+ plasmablasts; and elevated plasma IL-15. CCR2+pDCs and IL-15 were also enriched in infected animals, consistent with prior findings.20,55,56,57,58,59 In WT-vaccinated macaques, circulating CCR7+CD14+ classical monocytes were linked to increased IL-15, which correlated with stronger gp70 antibody responses but weaker V2-specific CH58 ADCC. CXCR3+ plasmablasts also correlated with reduced efferocytosis. Infection was further associated with elevated plasma TNFSF12, increased circulating Th1 cells, and rectal NKG2A−NKp44− ILCs expressing IFN-γ or CD107. Thus, intact V1 promoted an inflammatory, target-cell-permissive immune state associated with impaired ADCC and loss of protection.

The present results suggest that V1 has an immunoregulatory role beyond steric shielding. Located at the Env-trimer apex, V1 can mask V2 and V3 regions,60 undergoes size selection and glycosylation changes during transmission,61 and contributes, together with V2, to viral fitness.62 Our findings extend these observations by indicating that V1 also shapes host immunity at the mucosal portal of entry. This is notable because the helical V2 conformation can increase T cell activation and CCR5 expression, expanding susceptible target cells.13,63 Indeed, V2 has been shown to be under immunological pressure in vaccinated volunteers in the RV144 HIV clinical trial.10,11 Together, these data support the hypothesis that V1 and V2 jointly optimize viral transmission: V1 may suppress tolerogenic and efferocytic pathways, whereas helical V2 can promote target-cell activation and CCR5 expression.

ADCC directed to helical V2 has emerged as a reproducible correlate of reduced acquisition risk in ΔV1 vaccine studies.16,18,19,20,21,25,64,65 Passive-transfer experiments showed that coil-helical V2-specific mAb NCI05, but not β sheet-specific NCI09, delayed SIVmac251 acquisition.23 NCI05, not NCI09, recognizes a transitional CD4-dependent epitope and can kill infected cells, suggesting that the β sheet conformations of V2 may not be sufficiently exposed on the surface of infected cells.22 V2-specific ADCC, revealed by CH58 F(ab')2 antibody binding to α-helical V2,28,29 also correlated with decreased risk of virus acquisition, confirming that helical V2 represents a site of vulnerability also for HIV. However, passive-transfer studies indicate that helical V2 antibodies alone are insufficient for complete protection,23 and prior work showed that V1 deletion did not protect when V2 was forced into a β sheet conformation by GPG insertion.19 Thus, protection appears to require both removal of V1 and preservation of helical V2. In the absence of neutralizing antibodies, ΔV1 immunogens may protect by inducing helical V2 antibodies that limit virion-driven T cell activation,66 eliminate infected cells,67 and engage tolerogenic innate immunity.

Although ALVAC/gp120 regimens1,30 may not themselves be the leading path forward for future efficacy testing, the present findings suggest that V1 deletion is more than an incremental modification of a legacy platform. Rather, it appears to be a mechanistically informed Env-design strategy that redirects vaccine-induced immunity away from inflammatory programs and toward protective responses. Because the protective activity of ΔV1 Env immunogens has now been reproduced across multiple macaque studies,16,18,19 this concept is being advanced to clinical evaluation in the planned phase I C.L.E.A.R. (Combined Long-Term Efferocytosis and ADCC Responses) trial, which will compare WT and V1-deleted immunogens with alum30,31 and will also test the V1-deleted immunogen with ALFQ plus alum.65 If the immune features identified here are reproduced in humans, V1-deleted Env design could inform not only this regimen but also the broader development of next-generation HIV vaccine platforms and become a game changer in the HIV vaccine arena.

Limitations of the study

This study was designed to evaluate vaccine efficacy by comparing vaccinated animals to naive controls. Limitations included the lack of complete infectivity of the SHIV challenge stock, and the small group sizes that precluded direct comparisons between vaccinated groups. Finally, because infection occurred in only a limited number of vaccinated animals, in some cases, the two vaccine groups were combined to enable a more meaningful correlative analysis.

Experimental model details

DNA immunogens and gp120 protein production

The designs of the plasmid DNA expressing gp160 Env and the gp120 protein immunogens were conducted starting from the amino acid (AA) sequence of gp120 of HIV-1 clade A/E strain A244 used for the AIDSVAX B/E administered in the RV144 clinical trial.1

gp120 proteins

To generate the gp120 proteins for boost immunization, we started with the amino acid sequence of the gp120 available from the NIH AIDS reagent program (cat. #12569 AE.A244 D11gp120), which lacks 11 N-terminus amino acids and showed increased immunogenicity to the envelope.3 To design the WT-A244 and ΔV1-A244 gp120 proteins, we first identified the signal peptide of gp120 using an online service (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-5.0/) and removed it. For the ΔV1-A244 gp120 protein, we additionally removed the 23 AA spanning the variable region 1 (TKANLTNVNNRTNVSNIIGNITD). The two protein AA sequences were then provided to GeneArt (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to design codon-optimized DNA sequences biased for expression in Macaca mulatta. A Kozak sequence (GCCGCCACCATGG) and two stop codons were inserted before the start codon and following the end codon, respectively. The WT-A244 gp120 and ΔV1-A244 gp120 DNA sequences were then cloned into the pSWTK plasmid downstream of the CMV promoter and Kozak sequence, and in frame with the signal peptide already embedded in the plasmid vector pSWTK. Plasmids were amplified (Aldevron, Fargo, ND, USA) and used to produce the envelope protein (Advanced BioScience Laboratories, Rockville, MD, USA). Proteins were produced in CHO-S cells, by transiently transfecting the cells using polyethyleneimine (PEI). Proteins were then purified from the conditioned cell culture supernatant using a lectin-affinity chromatography (Galanthus nivalis lectin agarose; Vector Labs, Inc., Newark, CA, USA), capture step, followed by anion exchange chromatography (Q-Sepharose; GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Marlborough, MA, USA) operated in flow-through mode. Proteins were buffer exchanged into Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (DPBS) and filtered with a 0.22 μm filter.

gp160 plasmid DNAs

To design and produce the WT-A244 and ΔV1-A244 gp160 DNA immunogens we used the same amino acids sequence of the A244 gp120 proteins (WT and ΔV1) immunogens described above. The remining C-terminus portion of the gp160 AA sequence was derived from the Env protein of HIV-1 isolate CM244 from Thailand envelope (accession #JQ715397, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/JQ715397.1). The WT-A244 and ΔV1-A244 gp160 amino acid sequences were provided to GeneArt to design codon optimized DNA sequences biased for expression in Macaca mulatta, including the Kozak sequence and two stop codons. The sequences were then cloned into plasmid vector pCLucF downstream of the CMV promoter, replacing the luciferase gene. Following development, the plasmids were amplified by Aldevron, diluted in DPBS and used for prime immunizations.

p55 gag plasmid DNA

The amino acid sequence for the gag DNA immunogen was derived from the HIV clade B gag protein expressed by the canarypox viral vector ALVAC (vCP2438, vCP1521), which expresses the HIV clade BIII LAI gag p55 protein accession (accession #ABQ40365.1, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/ABQ40365.1). The p55 gag AA sequence was provided to GeneArt to design a codon optimized DNA sequence biased for expression in Macaca mulatta, including the Kozak sequence and two stop codons. The sequence was then cloned into plasmid vector pCLucF downstream of the CMV promoter, replacing the luciferase gene. Following development, the plasmid was amplified by Aldevron, diluted in DPBS and used for prime immunizations.

Animals and study design

Ethical statement

The research complies with all relevant ethical regulations. The study was approved by the NCI Animal Care and Use Committee (ACUC) under protocol VB-038 and VB-047. All rhesus macaques were housed at the National Institutes of Health (Bethesda, MD, USA) and handled in accordance with the standards of the Association for the Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) in an AAALAC-accredited facility (OLAW, Animal Welfare Assurance A4149-01). All rhesus macaques care and procedures were conducted under protocols approved by the NCI ACUC prior to study initiation. Rhesus macaques were closely monitored daily for any signs of illness, and appropriate medical care was provided as needed. Rhesus macaques were socially housed per the approved ACUC protocol and social compatibility, except during the viral challenge phase when they were individually housed. All clinical procedures, including biopsy collection, administration of anesthetics and analgesics, and euthanasia, were carried out under the direction of a laboratory animal veterinarian. Steps were taken to ensure the welfare of the animals and minimize discomfort. Animals were fed daily with a fresh diet of primate biscuits, fruit, peanuts, and other food items to maintain body weight or normal growth. Rhesus macaques were monitored for psychological well-being and provided with physical enrichment including sanitized toys, destructible enrichment (cardboard and other paper products), and audio and visual stimulation.

Vaccine study

A total of 35 male Rhesus macaques were randomized by age and weight into three groups. Two vaccinated groups of 12 animals each, the ΔV1-A244 (n = 12) and WT-A244 (n = 12), and a non-vaccinated group of 11 animals kept naive as controls (CT; n = 11).

WT-A244-vaccinated animals received 2 mg of HIV clade A/E WT-A244 gp160 DNA and 1 mg of HIV clade B IIILAI p55gag DNA at week 0 and week 4, whereas ΔV1-A244-vaccinated animals received 2 mg of HIV clade A/E ΔV1-A244 gp160 instead of the WT-A244 gp160 DNA. At weeks 8 and 12, all vaccinated animals were boosted with 108 PFU of ALVAC-HIV (vCP2438) expressing gag-pro Clade B HIV-1 (IIIB), gp120 of clade C HIV-1 (ZM96), and gp120-transmembrane domain of Clade B HIV-1 (LAI) genes. In addition to ALVAC-HIV, at week 12, WT-A244-vaccinated animals were boosted in the contralateral thigh with 0.4 mg of HIV clade A/E WT-A244 gp120 adjuvanted in Alum Alhydrogel 1:1 v/v, whereas ΔV1-A244-vaccinated animals were boosted in the same modality with 0.4 mg of HIV clade A/E ΔV1-A244 gp120.

Nine weeks following the last immunization (week 21), all animals were challenged weekly intrarectally up to 11 consecutive weeks with SHIV1157(QNE)Y173H at a 1:10,000 dilution from stock. The SHIV1157(QNE)Y173H virus stock used in this study was previously grown from the infectious molecular clone in rhesus PBMCs, titrated in rhesus macaques to select the appropriate dilution, and the ideal dilution for challenge via the intrarectal route was determined to be 1:10,000.26

Study for SIV gp120 in vitro stimulation in rectal mucosal cells

Due to lack of naive animals, the study on in vitro stimulation of mucosal cells with SIV gp120 proteins was conducted using rectal biopsies collected from 8 animals that had been vaccinated with HIV-based immunogens. Briefly, the vaccination regimen begun fourteen months prior biopsies collection. All 8 animals received 2 mg of HIV clade A/E ΔV1-A244 gp160 DNA and 1 mg of HIV clade B IIILAI p55gag DNA at week 0 and week 4. At weeks 8 and 12 and 24, all vaccinated animals were boosted with 106-108 TCID50 of ALVAC-HIV (vCP2438) expressing gag-pro Clade B HIV-1 (IIIB), gp120 of clade C HIV-1 (ZM96), and gp120-transmembrane domain of Clade B HIV-1 (LAI) genes. In addition to ALVAC-HIV, at weeks 12 and 24, 4 animals (2 males and 2 females) and 4 animals (1 male and 3 females) were boosted in the contralateral thigh with 0.4 mg of HIV clade A/E ΔV1-A244 gp120 protein adjuvanted in Alum Rehydragel (850 μg aluminum Al3+) or ALFQA (army liposome formulation + QS-21+ Alum; containing 200 μg of 3D-PHAD, 100 μg of QS21 and 850 μg aluminum Al3+), respectively. Following the last vaccination at week 24, all 8 animals were left untreated for 7 months and then collected of the rectal biopsies used in the in vitro experiment.

Sample collection

We selected the sampling time points based on known kinetics of vaccine-induced immune responses, prior studies, and sample availability. Plasma antibody responses (week 14) and rectal secretions (week 14) were assessed 2–3 weeks post-vaccination, corresponding to the expected peak of humoral responses. Circulating plasmablasts (PBMC, week 13) were measured earlier to capture their transient expansion shortly after vaccination. Env-specific mucosal B cells were evaluated at week 17 to allow sufficient time for the development and tissue localization of antigen-specific responses. ADCC activity was measured at week 21, on the day of viral challenge, to assess functional antibody responses at the time of exposure. Innate immune populations were assessed within 1–2 weeks after the final vaccination, consistent with the expected kinetics of innate activation: PBMC monocytes (week 14), efferocytosis (week 13), PBMC dendritic cells (week 14), mucosal NK/ILCs (week 13), and mucosal dendritic cells (week 15). These time points were determined based on prior studies of innate response dynamics14,16,18,20,21,38,65 and the availability of appropriate samples.

Method details

Plasma SHIV viral RNA quantification by droplet PCR

SHIV1157(QNE)Y173H RNA copies per milliliter were determined by a two-step real time qPCR,68 performed by the NHP Core Virology Laboratory at Duke University using −80°C frozen EDTA plasma samples. Briefly, Viral RNA was extracted from cryopreserved plasma, using an automated sample preparation platform QIA symphony SP (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) along with a Virus/Pathogen DSP midi kit and the cellfree500 protocol. The extracted RNA was annealed with a reverse primer designed specifically for the gag gene of SIVmac251 (5′-CAC TAG GTG TCT CTG CAC TAT CTG TTT TG-3′), shared by SIVmac251 and SHIV1157(QNE)Y173H. Subsequently, SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) alongside RNAse Out (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used to reverse transcribe RNA into cDNA. The cDNA was treated with RNase H (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and introduced (in duplicate) into a customized 4× TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which contained primers, and a hydrolysis probe labeled with fluorescence and tailored specifically for the gag gene of SIVmac251. The primer set included a forward primer (5′-GTC TGC GTC ATC TGG TGC ATT C -3′) and a reverse primer identical to the one used for annealing. Additionally, the mix comprised a fluorescently labeled hydrolysis probe (5′-/56-FAM/CTT CCT CAG TGT GTT TCA CTT TCT CTT CTG CG/3BHQ_1/-3′). The qPCR was performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific), applying the subsequent thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 50°C for 2 min, followed by an initial activation step at 95°C for 10 min. The cycling process was then repeated for 50 cycles, 95°C for 15 s, to cool to 60°C for 1 min. Mean copies of SIV gag RNA per reaction were computed by interpolating quantification cycle data against a serial dilution of a characterized custom RNA transcript encompassing a 730 bp segment of the SIV gag gene. Mean RNA copies per milliliter were determined by factoring in the assay dilution factor (DF = 18.72). The assay’s limit of quantification (LOQ) is set at 62 RNA copies per milliliter of plasma.

Binding Antibody Multiplex assay (BAMA) for HIV clades A/E and C specific plasma and mucosal IgG binding antibody

Plasma and mucosal (rectal) IgG levels to HIV-1 Env gp120 proteins were measured by binding antibody multiplex assay, as previously described4,69,70 at baseline and week 14. Rectal mucosal Env HIV-1-specific IgG was measured from snap frozen rectal mucosa swabs collected at baseline and week 14 from vaccinated animals. Samples were processed and visually examined for blood contamination. Total IgG concentration for each mucosal sample was measured by Human/NHP Isotyping Panel 1 Kit (Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD, USA), based on a recombinant human IgG calibrator standard curve. Samples were tested in duplicate at multiple dilutions. Concentration data were analyzed in DISCOVERY WORKBENCH (Meso Scale Discovery) analysis software using a 4-parameter logistic curve-fitting model with a 1/Y2 weighting function.

Antigen-specific antibodies against HIV-1 Env gp120 from different clades were quantified by binding to protein-coated polystyrene beads. Plasma samples were diluted at 1:80, 5-fold for 6 dilutions, and mucosal samples were diluted at 1:2 and 1:20. HIV immunoglobulin (HIVIG) and the HIV-1 V1/V2-specific monoclonal antibody CH58_4A were used as positive controls. Normal HIV-1 seronegative human serum (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA) and blank (no antigen) beads were used as negative controls. IgG to HIV-1 proteins was detected using goat anti-monkey IgG-biotin (Rockland Immunochemicals, Pottstown, PA, USA) followed by PE Streptavidin (BD Pharmingen; BD Biosciences), with antibody binding reported as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). Positive responses in plasma were defined as those that met the following criteria: (1) MFI >100, (2) MFI > antigen-specific cut-off (95th percentile of all baseline sample binding per antigen after high baseline exclusion), and (3) MFI > 3-fold of matched baseline sample binding before and after blank bead subtraction. Positivity calls were made at the 1:80 dilution. Antibody titer is reported as area under the titration curve (AUC).

Mucosal HIV-1 specific BAMA MFI values were normalized to total IgG and computed as specific activity. Binding magnitude is reported as specific activity (SA), calculated as the MFI × dilution divided by total IgG (concentration in μg/mL) for samples in the linear range of the assay. Positive responders were defined by the following criteria: MFI ≥100, SA > antigen-specific cutoff (95th percentile of all baseline sample SA per antigen), and SA > 3-fold that of the matched baseline.

Binding Antibody Multiplex assay (BAMA) for HIV ΔV1-A244 gp120 or WT-A244 gp120 specific mucosal IgG or IgA binding antibody

ΔV1-A244 gp120-or WT-A244 gp120-specific IgG or IgA were measured in rectal secretions eluted from swabs collected at week 15. HIV ΔV1-A244 gp120 or HIV WT-A244 gp120 protein were conjugated to MagPlex C magnetic carboxylated beads (Luminex, Austin, TX, USA) as described.71 Black 96 well microtiter plates (Pierce) were blocked overnight at 4°C with PBS containing 1% BSA, 0.05% Tween 20 and 0.05% azide (bead buffer). Buffer was removed and 25 μL diluted secretions or standard and 25 μL beads were added to each well. The plate was mixed overnight at 4°C and 1100 rpm on an orbital shaker. The following day, the plates were alternatively washed with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 in a Bio-Plex Pro Wash Station (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and mixed at room temperature for 30 min at 1100 rpm with bead buffer containing 2 μg/mL biotinylated goat anti-human IgG (Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL) or anti-monkey IgA antibody (Rockland Immunochemicals), followed by 1/400 phycoerythrin-labeled Neutralite avidin (Southern Biotech) in bead buffer. Prior to performing IgA assays, samples were depleted of IgG using Protein G due to cross-reactivity of the anti-monkey IgA antibody with IgG. After the final wash, beads were suspended in 100 μL of bead buffer and analyzed in a Bioplex 200 using Bioplex Manager software (Bio-Rad). Concentrations of antibodies were adjusted relative to concentrations of total IgG and IgA, which were measured by ELISA as described.71

HIV-1 pseudo virus neutralization assay of plasma samples

Neutralizing antibodies against HIV-1 pseudo viruses were measured as a function of reduction in Tat-regulated luciferase (Luc) reporter gene expression in TZM-bl cells.72,73 Briefly, Test samples were serial-diluted (3-fold dilution in duplicate) and incubated with 200 TCID50 of virus in a total volume of 150 μL for 1 h at 37°C in 96-well flat-bottom culture plates. TZM-bl cells (10,000) were added to each well in 100 μL of growth medium (containing DEAE dextran at concentration 20 μg/mL). Wells with cells only or cells and virus, were used as background control and virus control, respectively. After 48 h incubation, the cells were lysed by the addition of Britelite (PerkinElmer Life Sciences, Waltham, MA), and 3/4 of the cell lysate were transferred to a 96-well black solid plate (Corning Costar) for luminescence measurement. Neutralization titer was defined as the serum dilution at which relative luminescence units (RLUs) were reduced by 50% (ID50) relative to the RLUs in virus control wells after subtraction of background RLUs.

Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity assay

To evaluate the ADCC activity in plasma samples from infected animals, we utilized a previously established protocol incorporating EGFP-CEM-NKr-CCR5-SNAP cells, which constitutively express GFP, as target cells.74 Briefly, one million target cells were incubated with 50 μg of ΔV1-A244 gp120 protein for 2 h at 37°C. Post incubation, the target cells were washed and labeled with SNAP-Surface Alexa Fluor 647 (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for 30 min at room temperature. Heat-inactivated plasma samples (56°C for 30 min) were serially diluted (seven 10-fold dilutions starting at 1:10) and 100 μL of each dilution were added to the wells of a 96-well V-bottom plate (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA). To each well, 50 μL containing 5,000 target cells and 50 μL containing 250,000 human PBMCs were added, achieving an effector-to-target (E/T) ratio of 50:1. The plate was incubated for 2 h at 37°C, followed by two washes with PBS. The cells were then resuspended in 200 μL of a 1% PBS-paraformaldehyde solution and analyzed using a Symphony flow cytometer equipped with a high throughput system (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Specific cytotoxicity was determined by the loss of GFP from the SNAP-Alexa647+ target cells. Background levels were established using target and effector cells cultured in R10 media alone. A positive control was provided by the addition of HG107 monoclonal antibodies (5 μg/mL, NIH AIDS Reagent Program). Normalized percent killing was calculated as: (killing in the presence of plasma - background)/(killing in the presence of positive control-background) ×100.

V2-specific ADCC assay

To evaluate V2 specific ADCC, F(ab')2 fragments were generated from CH58 and CH59 monoclonal antibodies, which target overlapping yet conformationally distinct V2 epitopes, using the Pierce F(ab')2 Micro Preparation Kit (cat. #44688, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. To confirm the purity of the recovered F(ab')2 fragments, an SDS-PAGE gel was performed followed by silver staining using the Silver Quest Staining Kit (cat. #LC6070, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), again following the provided instructions. The V2-specific ADCC activity was subsequently assessed.18,19,20,21,25,64 Target cells, coated with ΔV1-A244 gp120 protein and subsequently labeled with SNAP-Surface Alexa Fluor 647, were incubated with 5 μg/mL of the purified F(ab')2 fragments from CH58 and CH59 monoclonal antibodies for 1 h at 37°C. These prepared target cells were then subjected to the ADCC assay as outlined above. Target cells incubated without F(ab')2 fragments were used as total ADCC response. The presence of F(ab')2 fragments inhibit the binding and ADCC mediated by anti-V2 antibodies present in the plasma of immunized animals. The specific V2 ADCC activity was determined by comparing the percentage of ADCC activity in the presence and absence of the F(ab')2 fragments.

Proximity extension assay (PEA) on plasma samples

Protein quantification was executed employing the Olink Target 48 Cytokine panel∗ (Olink Proteomics AB, Uppsala, Sweden) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. This method leverages the proximity extension assay (PEA) technology, as extensively detailed by Assarsson et al.81 This specific PEA methodology enables the concurrent assessment of 45 distinct analytes. Briefly, we used pairs of oligonucleotide-labeled antibody probes, each tailored to selectively bind to their designated protein targets. Probe pair mix were incubated with 1 μL of plasma. Probes that encountered their cognate proteins are then in close spatial proximity and their respective oligonucleotides engage in pairwise hybridization. A DNA polymerase was used to amplify the polymerized DNA, and to create distinct PCR target sequences. Subsequently, we detected and quantified the newly formed DNA sequences through utilization of a microfluidic real-time PCR platform, specifically the Biomark HD system by Fluidigm (San Francisco, CA, USA; Olink Signature Q100 instrument). Data validation to uphold data integrity was conducted with the Olink NPX Signature software specifically designed for the Olink analysis: the application was used to import data from the Olink Signature Q100 instrument and process the data. Data normalization procedures were executed employing an internal extension control and calibrators, thereby effectively mitigating any inherent intra-run variability. The ultimate assay output was reported in picograms per milliliter (pg/mL), predicated upon a robust 4-parameter logistic (4-PL) fit model, thereby ensuring precise absolute quantification. Comprehensive insights into the assay’s validation parameters, encompassing limits of detection, intra- and inter-assay precision data, and related metrics are available at www.olink.com.

Efferocytosis assay on macaque CD14+ cells

The frequency and engulfment capacity of CD14+ monocyte efferocytes were assessed using the Efferocytosis Assay Kit (Cat. #601770, Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Due to limited cell availability, freshly isolated ex vivo CD14+ monocytes, rather than differentiated macrophages, were utilized as effector cells. Apoptotic neutrophils served as target cells. CD14+ cells were isolated from cryopreserved peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) collected at baseline (10 × 106 cells per sample) using non-human primate CD14 MicroBeads (Cat. #130-091-097, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, thawed PBMCs were incubated with 20 μL of microbeads and 80 μL of MACS buffer at 4°C for 15 min. Cells were then washed with 3 mL buffer, resuspended in 500 μL buffer, and positively selected using the AutoMACS Pro Separator (Miltenyi Biotec) with the “Possel” program. Following isolation, CD14+ cells were labeled with CytoTell Blue according to kit instructions. Cells were resuspended at 1 × 107 cells/mL in buffer and mixed 1:1 with buffer containing 2× CytoTell Blue (stock diluted 1:200 in buffer). The suspension was incubated at 37°C for 30 min, washed three times with R10 medium (RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS), resuspended in R10, and used immediately as effector cell in the efferocytosis assay.

Neutrophils were isolated from whole blood collected from four unrelated macaques using a modified dextran sedimentation method. After PBMC removal by Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation (GE Healthcare), an equal volume of 20% dextran in water was added to the cellular pellet. The mixture was gently inverted and incubated for 1 min, followed by the addition of approximately three volumes of PBS and incubation in the dark for 50–60 min. The upper leukocyte-rich plasma layer, enriched for neutrophils, was collected and centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 10 min. Residual erythrocytes were lysed with ACK buffer (Quality Biological, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) for 5 min at 37°C. Cells were then washed with R10, counted, and labeled with CFSE per the kit instructions. Neutrophils were resuspended at 1 × 107 cells/mL and mixed 1:1 with 2× CFSE (stock diluted 1:200 in buffer), incubated at 37°C for 30 min, washed three times with R10, and resuspended in R10. Apoptosis was induced by resuspending CFSE-labeled neutrophils in R10 medium containing staurosporine (final dilution 1:1000 from kit stock) and incubating at 37°C for 3 h. After incubation, apoptotic cells were washed twice with R10 and pooled from all four animals for use as targets in the efferocytosis assay.

Labeled CD14+ effector cells and apoptotic neutrophils were cocultured at a 1:3 ratio (effector:target) in R10 medium at 37°C for 12 h. Parallel cultures of effector and target cells alone served as negative controls. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Flow cytometry acquisitions were performed on a FACSymphony A5 and examined using FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences) by acquiring all stained cells. Data was further analyzed using FlowJo v10.1 (TreeStar, Inc., Mesa, AZ, USA). The frequency of CD14+ efferocytes was determined as the frequency of positive cells for CFSE in the cells positive for CytoTell Blue. The engulfing capability of CD14+ efferocytes was determined as the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CFSE in the final gate. Gating strategy: FSC/SSC/Singlet cells/CytoTell Blue+/CFSE+.

Assessment of viral like particles (VLPs) formation by Western blot/immune-precipitation and electron microscopy

Transfections of 293 FT cells for VLPs production (HIV A244_D11/gagp55BIII)

293T Cells were transfected with 1 μg of HIV clade A/E WT- or ΔV1-A244 gp160 plasmid and 0.5 μg of HIV BIII p55gag plasmid DNA into 75 μL of serum free DMEM with high glucose, with LipoD293 (Cat# SL100668, SignaGen Laboratories, Frederick, MD, USA) following manufacturer instructions. After 48h of transfection cells were either lysate with RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific) or fixed for electron microscopy. Additionally, cell supernatant was collected and used to obtain VLPs by multistep centrifugations. Briefly, the cell supernatant was initially centrifuged at 2,200 xg for 30 min at 4°C to remove cell debris. The resulting supernatant was then transferred to a new tube and centrifuged again at 16,900 xg for 1 h at 4°C. VLPs pellet was either lysate with RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific) or fixed for electron microscopy.

Western blot of viral like particles

One hundred μg of cell lysate or supernatant containing VLPs were boiled for 5 min at 100°C with 1X sample buffer with 5% β-mercaptoethanol. Approximately 10 μg of each condition was separated by SDS-PAGE (NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris Protein Gels, 1.0 mm, cat. #NP0321PK2, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for approximately 45 min at 120 A and transferred to a 7.0 cm × 8.4 cm, 0.45 μm pore size, hydrophobic PVDF (Immobilon-P PVDF cat. #IPVH07850, Millipore Sigma Millipore), previously activated with methanol for 30 s. Proteins were transferred for 1 h at 140 mA. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with HIV+ plasma in a 1:1000 dilution. Membranes were washed in PBS 0.1% Tween and exposed to a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary anti-human antibody (dilution 1:10,000; Cat# ab6759, Abcam). Immunoreactivity proteins were visualized by chemiluminescence using a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Biorad).

Immunoprecipitation of viral like particles

One μg of monoclonal antibody CH58 and 180 μg of supernatant containing VLPs were added to IP wash buffer (50 mM Tris, 120 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.1% NP40) reaching a final volume of 500 μL, and incubated over night at 4°C in gentle rotation. Antibody-protein complexes were precipitated by incubation with 30 μL of washed Protein G Agarose beads (Roche) on a gentle rotation for 2 h at room temperature. Beads containing the precipitates were washed twice with 1mL of wash buffer and centrifuged at 12,000 xg for 2 min and the supernatant was discarded. Beads were resuspended in 60 μL of 4X sample buffer containing 10% β-mercaptoethanol, and boiled for 5 min at 100°C, following centrifugation at 12,000 xg for 2 min. For each condition, 10 μL were loaded in SDS-PAGE (NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris Protein Gels, 1.0 mm, cat. #NP0321PK2, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and run for approximately 45 min at 120 A and then transferred to a 7.0 cm × 8.4 cm, 0.45 μm pore size, hydrophobic PVDF (Immobilon-P PVDF cat. #IPVH07850, Millipore Sigma Millipore), previously activated with methanol for 30 s. Proteins were transferred for 1 h at 140 mA. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with CH58 monoclonal antibody diluted 1:1,000. Membranes were washed in PBS 0.1% Tween 20 and exposed to a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary anti-human antibody (dilution 1:10,000; Cat# ab6759, Abcam). Immunoreactivity proteins were visualized by chemiluminescence using a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Biorad).

Electron microscopy of viral like particles

Two sets of VLPs samples in the form of adherent cells or VLP pellets were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M Na cacodylate buffer for 1 hat room temperature for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) processing. All samples were washed with 0.1 M Na cacodylate buffer two times for 10 min. After the second buffer wash, the cells were post fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h in the dark. One hour post fixation, the cells were washed with 0.1 M Na cacodylate buffer twice for 10 min and then washed once with 0.1 N Na acetate buffer and stained with 0.5% uranyl acetate for 1 h in the dark. After en block staining, the cells were washed with 0.1N Na acetate buffer twice for 10 min. After the last buffer wash, the cells were subjected to gradual dehydration in the order of 35%, 50%, 70%, and 95% Ethanol twice for each step for 10 min, and three times with 100% Ethanol for 10 min. After the final step of 100% Ethanol rinse, cells were infiltrated in 100% pure epoxy resin at room temperature overnight. The next day, each well was washed with 100% pure epoxy resin twice and transferred to a 55°C oven for 48 h for polymerization. Epoxy resin (Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA) ingredients consisted of a mixture of Poly/Bed 812 embedding media, dodecenylsuccinic anhydride (DDSA), nadic methyl anhydride (NSA), and DMP-30 to solidify the resin. After 48 h, the cells were taken out of the oven, and ultra-thin sectioned with a UC6 Leica Microtome at 75 nm. The ultra-thin sections were picked up on a 150-copper mesh grid and were examined under a Hitachi H7600 transmission electron microscope. The grids were post stained with 1:1 0.5% Uranyl Acetate in ddH2O and 70% Ethanol for 2 min, rinsed with ddH2O four times, then stained with 1:1 lead citrate and H2O for 2 min and rinsed with ddH2O four times. The grids were carbon coated with a Ted Pella Cressington (Redding, CA, USA) evaporator and imaged with a Hitachi H7600 transmission electron microscope at 80 KeV and AMT camera (Advanced Microscopy Techniques, Woburn, MA, USA).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Due to small sample sizes, non-parametric tests were employed. Two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests or two-tailed Mann-Whitney tests were used to compare continuous variables between paired or unpaired groups, respectively. When fold-change ratios vs. Baseline were compared, only animals with non-zero Baseline values were included, after verifying that the number of animals with zeros at Baseline did not differ between groups. Differences between groups in the number of challenges before viral acquisition were assessed using the log rank (Mantel-Cox) test of the discrete-time proportional hazards model. The average per-risk challenge of viral acquisition was estimated by dividing the total number of observed infections by the number of administered challenges. Correlation analyses were conducted using the non-parametric Spearman-rank correlation method, and black trendlines drawn as an ordinary least-squares linear fit to the (log10-transformed, where applicable) data to illustrate the direction of the Spearman correlation rather than to imply a linear model. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) analysis was performed to predict analytes to best describe time of acquisition, binary infection status, or selected analytes of interest. Analytes with non-zero coefficients were compared across the following parameters: prefilter p threshold for relationship with the outcome variable (p < 0.01, p < 0.05 or no prefilter), cross validation (10-fold or leave-one-out), regularization penalty lambda (minimum or first standard error), and scaling of the analytes (scale or no scale). As this research was exploratory in nature, all p-values are reported as nominal values without adjustment for multiple comparisons. Statistical calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2 and R version 4.5.1. Code to produce all data figures can be found at https://github.com/NCI-VB/franchini_silvadecastro_DV1vb038.
