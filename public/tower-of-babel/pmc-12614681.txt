A Randomized Double-blind Placebo-controlled Feasibility Study of IVIG Therapy in Lung Transplantation
Authors: Glen P. Westall, Nobenguni Ndiweni, Kovi Levin, Bronwyn Levvey, Gregory I. Snell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background.IVIG therapy may be clinically beneficial following lung transplantation (LTx), either by correcting hypogammaglobulinemia or by mitigating the humoral response to the lung allograft. However, the evidence base in LTx is limited. We aimed to recruit 40 LTx patients into a pilot placebo-controlled study of adding IVIG therapy to standard immunosuppression. The primary aim of the study was to provide feasibility data to inform the subsequent design of a definitive phase 2/3 randomized controlled trial.Methods.Stable patients were recruited between 3 and 6 mo post-LTx to receive 6 monthly infusions of either IVIG (0.4 g/kg) or placebo. Patients were followed for 12 mo. Primary study outcomes were related to feasibility; namely, the proportion of patients who (1) were screened to be eligible, (2) were eligible and enrolled, (3) lost to follow-up, and (4) received their allocated treatment. Although clinical data were collected, the study was not designed or powered to detect significant differences in clinical or adverse events.Results.Recruitment into the study was significantly impacted by the COVID pandemic. The final study cohort either received IVIG (n = 18) or placebo (n = 19). Four patients on IVIG did not receive the full complement of 6 study infusions because of infusion reactions. Otherwise, there was no significant difference in the safety profile between the 2 study arms. Four patients, all randomized to placebo, were unblinded because of clinical concerns and started on open-label IVIG. Serum IgG levels increased, and the incidence of hypogammaglobulinemia was lower in the IVIG-treated arm.Conclusions.The use of IVIG was relatively well tolerated in LTx recipients. The extracted clinical and safety data support consideration and the design of a definitive multicenter randomized controlled trial of IVIG in LTx.Clinical Trial Registration.Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry: ACTRN12618001394235.

Lung transplantation (LTx) has revolutionized the management of patients with end-stage respiratory failure. Despite improvements in short-term outcomes, the success of LTx continues to be limited by the development of chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD). CLAD represents a progressive and irreversible drop in pulmonary function and has been shown to be associated with acute cellular rejection, antibody-mediated rejection (AMR), and post–infectious (particularly viral) lung damage.1 Therapy for CLAD, once established, is limited, and the current focus is on strategies that minimize the impact of the risk factors that are associated with CLAD.

The use of potent immunosuppressive regimens, consisting of a calcineurin inhibitor, an antimetabolite, and corticosteroids, incompletely protects against both acute and chronic rejection episodes but also predisposes to infectious complications. Immunosuppressive drugs primarily target T-cell function but also reduce B-cell function. Depression of humoral immunity is common, with hypogammaglobulinemia seen in up to 70% of LTx recipients.2 Low IgG levels confer an increased risk of opportunistic bacterial, fungal, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) infections3 and are associated with acute rejection episodes and CLAD.4

IVIG provides replacement therapy and, in the nontransplant setting, decreases the risk of bacterial infections.5 Previous randomized controlled studies of immunoglobulin replacement in cardiac transplantation, although limited by small numbers (n = 23), showed a reduction in CMV infection.6 A more recent crossover study in 11 LTx did not reduce the short-term risk of bacterial infection but was limited both by the small number of participants and the short duration of follow-up.7

IVIG is a standard treatment for episodes of acute AMR.8 Pretransplant, IVIG reduces levels of sensitization, and after renal transplantation, IVIG replacement therapy improves 5-y survival.9 A recent retrospective study in LTx suggested that IVIG replacement therapy had a protective effect on CLAD and survival.10

In immunosuppressed LTx, IVIG reduces the burden of infection and rejection, the 2 main risk factors for CLAD. Replacement IVIG therapy has immunomodulatory effects that limit alloreactivity and corrects IgG levels, thus mitigating infectious episodes. Several retrospective and prospective cohort LTx studies have demonstrated that IVIG therapy is safe and potentially effective. However, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) is needed to determine the optimal strategy to improve outcomes in LTx. Before embarking on a definitive phase III RCT, we performed a pilot feasibility RCT to generate data, mainly related to patient recruitment and retention, to better inform the design of a definitive phase III study. This study was not designed to demonstrate clinical differences associated with IVIG use in LTx.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

An investigator-driven single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study to determine primarily the feasibility of recruiting and retaining patients in a study of IVIG therapy after LTx. Secondary clinical endpoints were related to efficacy and safety. The study was not powered to detect significant differences in clinical endpoints. Clinically stable patients were recruited at 3 mo post-LTx, following completion of their posttransplant education and rehabilitation program, and randomized to receive 6 monthly infusions of either IVIG (0.4 g/kg; Intragam, CSL, King of Prussia, USA) or placebo. Placebo was 4% albumin (Albumex 4) in an equal volume, prepared by the Alfred Hospital pharmacy. All participants were premedicated with paracetamol (650 mg po) and diphenhydramine (25 mg po). Investigators, study personnel, and study participants were blinded to the treatment arms. This investigator-sponsored study was approved by the Alfred Hospital’s Ethics Committee (Alfred Ethics Registration: 53.18) and supported by an unrestricted study grant and provision of investigational product from CSL Bering.

Study Population

All consecutive LTx or heart-lung transplant (HLTx) recipients receiving long-term follow-up at the Alfred were considered for the study. Inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) adults 18 y and older, (2) single or bilateral LTx or HLTx recipients, and (3) provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria are as follow: (1) known allergy to IVIG; (2) hospitalization that requires intubation or mechanical ventilation; (3) active treatment for acute graft rejection; (4) if female, the patient is pregnant, lactating, or breast feeding; (5) any other disease or condition, which in the investigator’s opinion would preclude the patient’s participation in a clinical trial; (6) severe selective IgA deficiency; and (7) history of thrombotic events within 3 mo of transplant.

Sample Size and Randomization

As this is a pilot feasibility RCT to determine recruitment and retention rates, event rates for the clinical variables of interest, and rates of adherence to the protocol, no formal power calculation was performed to determine a sample size. We aimed to recruit 40 patients across both study arms during a 12-mo period. Eligible patients at 3 mo post-LTx were randomized 1:1 to either IVIG or placebo. Patients were stratified according to their pre-LTx sensitization status, as defined by the presence of anti-HLA DSA. Each patient was followed up for 1 y postrecruitment.

Clinical Management of the LTx Recipient

Immunosuppressive therapy was at the discretion of the treating physician and consisted of a calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus), antiproliferative drug (mycophenolate mofetil), and prednisolone, as previously described.11 Induction therapy with an interleukin-2 antagonist was reserved for patients with limited renal reserve. All patients at risk of CMV reactivation received 7 d of intravenous ganciclovir followed by oral valganciclovir during the first 5 mo posttransplant. Primary CMV or Epstein-Barr virus mismatches (recipient CMV seronegative, donor CMV seropositive) additionally received CMV hyperimmune gammaglobulin on days 1, 2, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28. Protocol bronchoscopies with transbronchial biopsy were performed at 2 and 6 wk and at 3, 6, 9, 12, and 18 mo posttransplant or when clinically indicated. Patients were routinely screened pretransplant with Luminex Mixerd Screen (One Lambda Inc, Canoga Park). No specific desensitization was performed pretransplant on sensitized patients. Posttransplant, anti-HLA testing was performed 3 and 12 mo postrandomization. Acute cellular rejection,12 AMR,13 and CLAD14 were defined as per International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation guidelines.

Statistical Analysis

An intention-to-treat analysis was used and included all randomized patients who received an initial study infusion. Descriptive statistics were used for demographic data and study endpoints. Comparative analysis of the primary and secondary endpoints was performed using the Student t test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Effect sizes were analyzed by calculating the odds ratio for proportional data and the standardized mean analysis (Cohen’s d test) for group data. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Primary Endpoint: Feasibility

Study Recruitment

Recruitment into the study was significantly affected by the 2020 COVID pandemic. Between November 2018 and February 2023, 316 lung or multiorgan (including LTx) procedures were performed at the Alfred Hospital. Of these, 50 patients were not screened because of (1) recruitment into other studies (n = 30), (2) COVID restrictions (n = 17), and (3) language barriers (n = 3). Of the 266 patients screened, 172 did not satisfy the inclusion criteria (Figure 1). Failed inclusion criteria included pediatric LTx recipients (n = 19), re-LTx or multiorgan transplants (n = 12), interstate patients with distant post-LTx follow-up (n = 68), and recent or current IVIG treatment (n = 14). Reluctance from the treating clinician, typically related to clinical instability, COVID risk, or competing clinical issues, led to many patients being excluded from the study (n = 59). Clinical comorbidities contributing to study exclusion included active infections (n = 13); frailty, delirium, or anxiety (n = 10); acute kidney injury (n = 9); prolonged intensive care unit admission (n = 8); cardiac events (n = 7); cerebrovascular accidents (n = 6); and other miscellaneous conditions (n = 6). No patient was ineligible for the study because of IVIG allergy, IgA deficiency, or prior thrombotic events.

Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) diagram.

Of the 94 eligible patients, 56 patients elected not to participate in the study. Reasons cited included (1) concerns related to increased risk of COVID exposure in a hospital environment (n = 30) and (2) other unresolved medical and psychological comorbidities (n = 12), and distance from the hospital (n = 4). Ongoing COVID-19 restrictions within the hospital limited enrollment for 10 potential study participants. Given unexpected COVID-related delays in study recruitment, a decision was made to cap recruitment at 38 patients rather than the a priori target of 40 participants.

Study Randomization

Patients were randomized into the IVIG (n = 18) and placebo (n = 20) arms at an average of 58 d (SD 16) and 63 d (SD 17) post-LTx (P = 0.3). One patient randomized to the placebo arm was difficult to cannulate and withdrew from the study without receiving the study infusion. The study cohort for analysis, therefore, consisted of 18 patients in the IVIG arm and 19 patients in the placebo arm. Demographic and baseline LTx characteristics were matched between groups (Table 1), except for a younger age and a larger proportion of bilateral sequential LTx in the placebo arm. One patient in each treatment arm was receiving IVIG before LTx, and only 1 patient with pre-LTx anti-HLA DSA was in the placebo arm. Baseline characteristics collected at recruitment were matched between groups (Table 1), apart from a higher steroid dose in the placebo arm.

Secondary Endpoints: Clinical

Serum IgG levels

Serum levels were matched between treatment arms, both pre-LTx and at study recruitment (Table 1). After the first study infusion, there was a statistically significant change in mean serum IgG level in the IVIG arm (increased by 0.9 g/L) compared with placebo-treated patients (decreased by 0.3 g/L; P < 0.01). Likewise, when comparing pre-first infusion to post-final infusion, there was a statistically significant increase in mean serum IgG level in the IVIG arm (increased by 1.7 g/L) that was not seen in the placebo arm (decreased by 0.4 g/L; P < 0.001). At study end, the incidence of hypogammaglobulinemia was higher in the placebo arm (Table 3).

Clinical

The secondary clinical endpoints for the study are included in Table 3. Notably, the feasibility study was not powered to detect clinical differences between the groups. One patient, in the placebo arm, died 266 d post-LTx, having developed acute lung allograft dysfunction due to fibrinoid organizing pneumonia. The incidence of acute rejection and CMV reactivation was numerically higher in the placebo arm; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance. None of the patients developed CLAD within 12 mo of LTx. Although 4 patients in the placebo arm were unblinded because of clinical concerns and a need to start treatment with IVIG, no patients in the IVIG arm needed to be unblinded because of clinical concerns.

Safety Endpoints

As described earlier, 4 patients receiving IVIG were unblinded from the study as a consequence of developing infusion reactions associated with the study drug (headache n = 2, fever n = 1, nausea/vomiting n = 1). In each case, causality was attributed to the study drug. Other potentially study-related adverse events are listed in Table 4. Most of these adverse events (93/102) were deemed by the study investigators to be unrelated to the study infusion. There was no statistically significant difference in the profile of the adverse events between the 2 arms.

DISCUSSION

This pilot study of IVIG in LTx primarily demonstrated that IVIG given in 6 monthly infusions early posttransplant is both feasible and generally well tolerated. As previously described, hypogammaglobulinemia was common in this LTx population and could be corrected by IVIG. The incidence of study infusion reactions to IVIG was like that previously reported.15 The study was not powered to detect clinical differences; however, favorable trends were seen in patients receiving IVIG, particularly regarding acute rejection, viral infections, and CMV reactivation. Four patients in the placebo arm (and none in the IVIG arm) were unblinded because of clinical concerns and a need to start open-label IVIG. In performing an intention-to-treat analysis, the unblinding and use of IVIG in the placebo arm will have introduced bias into the analysis and further underpowered the study to demonstrate clinical differences. However, as this study was primarily performed to assess feasibility, the identification that some patients needed to be unblinded is an important and meaningful endpoint that would help inform the design of a subsequent phase 3 RCT of IVIG in LTx.

Immunoglobulin, a preparation derived from pooled blood collected from healthy donors, is an accepted treatment for many diseases, including primary and secondary immunodeficiency syndromes, neuromuscular disease, and hematological conditions. In solid organ transplantation, IVIG has been used for desensitization, treatment of AMR, and management of some infectious conditions. The adjunctive use of IVIG in LTx is not dissimilar to that of azithromycin in that it also provides dual antimicrobial and immunomodulatory benefits. Other commonly used adjunctive management approaches include the use of montelukast and extracorporeal photopheresis. In the absence of appropriately powered studies to assess their true efficacy, they are not universally used in all LTx centers. Historically, immunoglobulin was given as an intravenous preparation, but more recently, subcutaneous administration has been incorporated into routine clinical practice,16 although the experience in solid organ transplantation is more limited.

IVIG is generally well tolerated; however, adverse events are well reported and are usually mild and transient. IVIG products that contain higher concentrations of IgA and anti-Rh blood group D antigen have higher incidences of adverse effects17,18 and may be higher in patients who are IgA-deficient. As seen in our study, the most common adverse effects of IVIG are flu-like symptoms, which were reported in 14 of 16 patients (87.5%) in one study.19 These symptoms typically develop within an hour of infusion and can be managed with paracetamol and by slowing down the infusion rate. Delayed events were not seen in our study but can affect 1% of patients, and include thrombotic events, renal impairment, neurological disorders, and transfusion-related infections.15 A weakness of the study is that we did not specifically track blood glucose profiles, as the sugar content of immunoglobulin preparations can vary between products. Four patients receiving IVIG in our study had the infusion ceased because of flu-like symptoms and were unblinded from the study. In routine clinical practice, we would provide symptom relief and continue the infusion, albeit at a slower rate. Before the next infusion, we would premedicate with antihistamines and an increased steroid dose, as has been previously described.20

At study end, the incidence of hypogammaglobulinemia was significantly higher in the placebo arm compared with the IVIG (42.1% versus 11.1%, P = 0.03). Previous studies describe a similar incidence of hypogammaglobulinemia at 1 y post-LTx2,3 and have demonstrated that transplant patients with severe hypogammaglobulinemia are at increased risk of opportunistic infections2,21 and CLAD.4 In our study, the incidence of bacterial isolation and admissions for infections was similar in both arms. This lack of effect is supported by a small RCT that demonstrated that IVIG therapy did not reduce the risk of bacterial infection in 11 LTx recipients.7

The study design whereby patients were recruited between 3 and 6 mo posttransplant reflected 2 competing factors: avoiding the very early posttransplant period, when study recruitment may be limited by the patient’s clinical instability, and avoiding more distant posttransplant periods, when the incidence of hypogammaglobulinemia and humoral rejection is lower. Additionally, we have historically found it very difficult to engage and recruit patients into clinical trials while they are still engaged in our extensive 10-wk posttransplant education and rehabilitation program.

IVIG is commonly used to desensitize patients pre-LTx and to treat AMR post-LTx. However, the use of IVIG to correct hypogammaglobulinemia or to influence other clinical outcomes, including infection, graft function, and mortality, is less clear. A recent systematic review of transplant recipients receiving IVIG for hypogammaglobulinemia described a survival advantage in cardiac transplantation, which was not seen in LTx.22 Additionally, the authors concluded that there was insufficient data to make a recommendation regarding using IVIG for infection prophylaxis. In a large (n = 484 LTx patients) retrospective, observational study, IVIG replacement in patients with hypogammaglobulinemia did not significantly reduce the incidence of CLAD or improve survival.23 The lack of clinical improvement in this study may be related to important differences with our study, namely, IVIG was given at a later point (median 323 d post-LTx), and each patient received a median of 2 doses of IVIG.

Our study was primarily undertaken to provide data that would inform the feasibility and design of a definitive phase 3 RCT of IVIG versus placebo in LTx patients. The main unexpected barrier to patient recruitment was the 2020 COVID pandemic. Based on our pre-COVID annual LTx activity, we expected a priori that it would take 12 mo to recruit 40 patients into this single-center study. The COVID pandemic impacted recruitment in several ways. Hospital restrictions limited our ability to recruit and review patients within the hospital environment, and secondary to this, our LTx patients were reluctant to visit the hospital for additional study visits. As a result, after a prolonged recruitment period (4 y), the study was closed prematurely, having recruited 38 of 40 LTx patients.

Secondary clinical variables were collected to inform the design, clinical end points, and power calculation of a phase 3 RCT. Based on the observed clinical outcomes, we propose that the primary clinical end point for such a study would be the incidence of acute cellular rejection. The incidence of acute cellular rejection in the IVIG arm was 1 of 18 (5.6%) compared with 5 of 19 (26.3%) in the placebo arm.

In conclusion, we have completed the largest RCT of IVIG in LTx, demonstrating that IVIG use is safe and normalizes IgG levels at 1 y post-LTx. There remains a need for a definitive RCT of IVIG in LTx. This study provides feasibility, efficacy, and safety data that could inform the design of such a prospective study.
