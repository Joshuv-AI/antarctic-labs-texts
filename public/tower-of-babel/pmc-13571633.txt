Associations Between Fatigue, Knowledge, Self-Management, and Self-Efficacy Among Community-Dwelling Patients with Stable Coronary Heart Disease in Pingdingshan, China: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Li Liu, Xianguang Bai, Huiping Wang, Pia Vanessa C Basilio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFatigue is a common but underrecognized symptom in community-dwelling Chinese adults with coronary heart disease (CHD), adversely affecting self-care and quality of life. While prior studies have separately examined fatigue, knowledge, and self-management in CHD populations, few have investigated their interrelationships in community-based settings in China. This study aimed to examine the associations among CHD-related fatigue (measured by the Fatigue Scale-14, which assesses both physical and mental dimensions), disease-related knowledge, self-management behaviors, and self-efficacy in community-dwelling patients with stable CHD, and to explore whether knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy are correlated with fatigue severity.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was carried out in two urban communities of a single district in Pingdingshan, China, between March and April of 2024. 136 eligible adults with stable CHD were selected via consecutive sampling. Validated scales were used to measure fatigue, knowledge, self-management behaviors, and self-efficacy. To investigate relationships between these variables, Spearman correlation analyses were carried out.Results92.65% of participants reported feeling fatigued, with a mean score of 8.73 ± 2.15. Only 7.35% of patients had high fatigue-related knowledge, and 6.62% had a high self-management score. Overall, patients showed limited knowledge and low engagement in self-management behaviors. Additionally, self-efficacy was low (mean 32.35 ± 12.18). Fatigue knowledge showed a weak, statistically significant correlation with fatigue (rs = −0.164, p = 0.056), while self-management (rs = −0.181, p < 0.05) and self-efficacy (rs = −0.320, p < 0.001) demonstrated weak and weak-to-moderate negative correlations with fatigue, respectively. Knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy were found to have strong positive correlations with each other (rs = 0.832–0.912).ConclusionCommunity-dwelling CHD patients frequently experience fatigue alongside low disease knowledge, poor self-management, and limited self-efficacy. These findings support the potential value of targeted community-based patient education programs and integrated disease management strategies targeting knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy. Future intervention studies are needed to evaluate whether such approaches can reduce fatigue burden in community-dwelling CHD patients.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are the leading cause of death globally, accounting for approximately 32% of all deaths worldwide, with coronary heart disease (CHD) responsible for nearly half of these fatalities.1 As the most prevalent subtype of CVD, CHD is characterized by atherosclerotic stenosis of the coronary arteries and remains a major cause of disability and premature mortality globally. In China, the number of CHD patients is estimated at 11.39 million, with a steadily rising prevalence driven by population aging and changing lifestyles.2 Henan province bears an even greater disease burden, with approximately 12–16 million affected individuals.3 A case of 13,619 has been reported in Pingdingshan city of central Henan in 2020, assuming the incidence to be 274.61 per 100,000 and estimated mortality to be 110.26 per 100,000 population.4 Despite this substantial regional burden, most epidemiological and intervention research on CHD in China has focused on tertiary hospital settings, with limited data on community-dwelling patients who constitute the majority of the disease burden.

One of the most frequent and troubling symptoms in individuals who have recently suffered from CHD is fatigue. It is described as a chronic state of physical fatigue and an overall mental drain that complicates emotional and psychological weariness, ultimately altering an individual’s ability to complete tasks and diminishing one’s quality of life.5,6 In CHD populations, physical and mental fatigue are recognized as partially distinct dimensions with potentially different clinical correlates, and are commonly assessed as separate subscales using instruments such as the Fatigue Scale-14. Research demonstrates that fatigue is very common in patients with CHD, and it may even be somehow tied to the development and has been associated with disease progression and increased mortality risk.7,8 Reported prevalence of fatigue in CHD populations ranges from 53% to 78%, and moderate to severe symptoms can continue even after the individual has finished rehabilitation.8–10 Many factors contribute to the phenomenon, including social and mental features such as an individual’s historic and current state of emotional as well as psychological health and even to the physical aspects such as age, gender, and disease progression.6,11 Fatigue presentation in CHD is heterogeneous, with known variation by disease severity (NYHA class), comorbidity burden, and treatment approach.5,8 While clinical factors contribute substantially to fatigue, modifiable psychosocial factors such as knowledge, self-efficacy, and self-management may also play an important role across patient subgroups. However, existing studies have predominantly focused on describing fatigue prevalence and its clinical correlates, while paying limited attention to modifiable psychosocial factors such as knowledge and self-management, which represent promising intervention targets for reducing fatigue burden.

Self-management is defined as the ability to collaborating with one’s healthcare providers to control symptoms, follow treatment plans, and make psychosocial adjustments needed to deal with the emotional and social ramifications of the chronic illness.12 For CHD, effective self-management, that is, compliance with medication, suitable dietary and physical activity, as well as early detection of disease exacerbations, is key to mitigating symptoms and suffering and enhancing overall health outcomes.13 Yet, accumulating evidence indicates that self-management remains suboptimal across cardiovascular populations, attributed to factors such as low disease awareness, inadequate self-care support, and diminished self-efficacy.14–17 While these associations have been documented separately, few studies have examined how knowledge, self-efficacy, and self-management operate together as an interconnected system to influence symptom outcomes such as fatigue.

A satisfactory degree of disease-related knowledge is crucial for appropriate self-management. More educated patients about CHD and its management are likely to adopt healthy lifestyles and take an active role in their self-care.18–21 On the other hand though, many patients have considerable knowledge gaps, especially concerning modifiable risk factors, sticking to the prescribed medication, and lifestyles after treatment.21,22 Most research on CHD knowledge has been conducted in Western or hospital-based populations, and the level of fatigue-specific knowledge among community-dwelling Chinese CHD patients remains largely unknown.

Self-efficacy is an individual’s belief in their capabilities to plan and create the necessary actions to reach particular objectives, a concept in Bandura’s social cognitive theory.23 Greater self-efficacy is associated with greater treatment adherence, healthier lifestyle choices and a reduced symptom burden with patients that have chronic disease.24–26 It is especially influential in sustaining motivation and determination over time with patients that have CHD. While self-efficacy has been linked to self-management behaviors, its direct association with fatigue severity in CHD remains underexplored, especially in community settings.

This study is grounded in Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which proposes a reciprocal triadic interaction among personal factors (eg, knowledge, self-efficacy), behavior (eg, self-management practices), and environmental influences.23 Building on this framework, we hypothesized a sequential pathway in which greater fatigue-related knowledge strengthens self-efficacy, which in turn facilitates better self-management behaviors, ultimately associated with lower fatigue severity. In this framework, self-efficacy is a core motivational mediator. Patients with greater confidence in their ability to manage the illness are more likely to maintain positive self-management behaviors, even when face with fatigue. Over time, successful self-management behaviors further build self-efficacy and may reduce fatigue severity, thereby forming a positive cycle. In contrast, severe fatigue can weaken self-efficacy and reduce self-management behaviors, potentially worsening symptoms. Taken together, this model links knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy into an integrated system that collectively affect fatigue in patients with CHD.

Although previous studies have established associations between fatigue, knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy, few studies have examined all four variables simultaneously in the community. Most evidence comes from hospital or structured cardiac rehabilitation programs, and community-based study for Chinese urban populations remain scarce. Based on this gap, the study explored the interrelationships among fatigue, disease knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy in stable CHD patients from community in Pingdingshan, Henan Province. We hypothesize that: H1: Fatigue is negatively associated with knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy; H2: Knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy are positively intercorrelated, forming a coherent psychosocial system consistent with SCT.

H1: Fatigue is negatively associated with knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy;

H2: Knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy are positively intercorrelated, forming a coherent psychosocial system consistent with SCT.

The results offer preliminary descriptive evidence on how these four factors interact in community, providing a foundation for subsequent multivariable investigations and targeted intervention development.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted between March and April 2024 in two urban communities (HB and LX, in the XC District of Pingdingshan City, Henan Province, China). Both communities were selected for the well-established community health infrastructures and community health service center that that maintains electronic medical records for local residents diagnosed with CHD. As a cross-sectional study with data collected over a single two-month time, this study can identify associations among variables but does not establish causal relationships or allow assessment of longitudinal changes over time.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size was calculated using the Raosoft online sample size calculator (Raosoft Inc., Seattle, WA, USA). Based on a total population of 209 registered CHD patients across the two participating community health centers, and applying a 95% confidence level with a 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample was 136 participants. This sample size provides sufficient precision for the study. In Addition, post hoc power calculations in GPower 3.1 indicated over 90% power to detect correlations of rs ≥ 0.30 (two-tailed, α = 0.05), but only approximately 60% power for weaker associations around rs = 0.20. So, 95% confidence intervals was used to convey effect size precision. All 136 eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate, yielding a 100% response rate among eligible individuals.

Participants

Consecutive sampling was adopted in this study. Researchers reviewed electronic medical records from each community health center in chronological order of patients’ latest CHD follow-up visits, and invited all eligible patients to participate in the study between March and April 2024. A total of 136 eligible patients were identified across the two communities (68 per site), and all agreed to participate, yielding a 100% response rate among eligible individuals. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) 18 years of age or older. (2) Clinically stable CHD. (3) Residentially stable within the community for the preceding 6 months of the survey. (4) Participants had to be independent or require minimal assistance to complete the questionnaires. Those who had a psychiatric condition, communication problems, or fatigue due to cancer, auto-immune or rheumatic diseases were excluded. The participants gave informed consent prior to the start of the study.

Measures

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics

The research team documented participant demographics and clinical attributes utilizing a stratified questionnaire created by the team. Some attributes included but were not limited to age, sex, educational attainment, marital status, income, employment, disease duration and history of hospital stays, NYHA functional class, BMI, and blood pressure measurements including MAP.

Fatigue Levels

Fatigue was measured using the Fatigue Scale-14 (FS-14), originally developed by Chalder et al27 The Chinese version of the FS-14, which has good reliability and validity in Chinese chronic disease populations,28 was employed in this study. The items in the FS-14 are based on a binary scale of “yes/no” response format in line with a two-dimensional representation of fatigue step-wise. The dimensions are physical fatigue (items 1–8) and mental fatigue (items 9–14). Total scores on the FS-14 range from 0 to 14, with a score of 7 or above generally considered fatigue.28 In this study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.82 for the FS-14 scale.

Knowledge of Fatigue

Knowledge of fatigue was assessed using the Fatigue Cognition Questionnaire. This scale has been widely used in Chinese cardiovascular disease populations and has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in prior studies of CHD patients in China. There are 15 questions in this questionnaire ranging over 5 domains: fatigue and CHD, classification, etiology and symptomatology, consequences, and management. Correct answers were given 1 point. Overall, these scores were divided into categories: low (<6), moderate (6–10), or high (>11). In the present study sample, the scale exhibited acceptable internal consistency with a Cronbach’s α of 0.76.

Self-Management Behaviors

Participants’ self-management behaviors were assessed using the Coronary Artery Disease Self-Management Scale (CSCMS), a self-report instrument developed and validated specifically for Chinese patients with coronary heart disease. The scale includes 27 items that are organized into 7 behavioral domains and are rated across a five-point Likert scale. The domains include, but are not limited to, symptom management, management of therapy, and emotional control. Increased scores would indicate that self-management is more prevalent among the individuals assessed. The scale has demonstrated excellent internal consistency in previous validation studies (Cronbach’s α = 0.913). Consistent with prior validation findings, the CSCMS demonstrated strong reliability in the current sample, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.89.

Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy was rated using the Cardiac Self-Efficacy Scale (CSES), originally developed by Sullivan et al.29 The Chinese translation of the CSES,30 validated in Chinese patients with CHD, was used in this study. The scale includes 16 items in 2 dimensions: maintenance of function and management of symptoms. A higher score reflects a greater degree of confidence in self to handle the illness. The Chinese version was shown to have good psychometric quality (Cronbach’s alpha=0.82; content validity index= 1.0). In the current sample, internal consistency was satisfactory with a Cronbach’s α of 0.86.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analysis was carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 19.0. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Missing data were examined prior to analysis. Given the face-to-face survey administration with on-site research staff assistance and immediate questionnaire collection upon completion, all 136 participants fully completed the primary study questionnaires with no missing values for fatigue, knowledge, self-management, or self-efficacy scales. Therefore, no data imputation or participant exclusion for missing data was required.

Participant characteristics were described with descriptive statistics (means ± standard deviations for continuous variables or frequencies in percentages for categorical variables). Because the majority of continuous variables were non-normally distributed, Spearman’s rank correlation was used to examine bivariate associations among the four primary total scores (fatigue, fatigue knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy). Subdomain scores were reported descriptively but were not included in the primary correlation analysis. All correlation coefficients are presented with 95% confidence intervals computed via Fisher z-transformation.

Fatigue Level

Participants who received a total score of seven or higher were categorized as being fatigued based on the scoring criteria. There are only 7.35% of participants in this study reported having no fatigue symptoms, compared to 92.65% who reported feeling tired (Figure 1A). This high prevalence highlights that fatigue remains a substantial, underaddressed symptom burden among community-dwelling patients with CHD.

Knowledge of Fatigue

The whole scores of knowledge were categorized into three levels: high (≥11), moderate (6–10), and low (≤6). Of the participants, most participants (62.50%) fell into the low knowledge category, while only 7.35% were classified as having high knowledge (Figure 1B). This pattern points to substantial knowledge gaps regarding fatigue etiology and self-management strategies.

Self-Management Behavior

Self-management scores were categorized as high (≥80%), moderate (60–80%), and low (<60%). Among participants, 50% reported a low level of self-management, and only 6.62% reached a high level (Figure 1C). These results indicate that self-management behavior of community-dwelling CHD patients is at a low level, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to enhance active disease engagement and behavior change.

Fatigue

The average overall fatigue score was 8.73 ± 2.15 (95% CI: 8.37 to 9.10) and therefore the degree of fatigue in the subjects was regarded as the moderate level. Contribution of physical fatigue (mean=6.07 ± 1.13, 95% CI: 5.87 to 6.26) was larger to total fatigue than mental fatigue (2.66 ± 1.39, 95% CI: 2.43 to 2.90), indicating somatic symptoms were more severe.

Knowledge of Fatigue

Among the five subdomains of fatigue cognition (full data shown in Table 3), participants scored highest on Impact of Fatigue on CHD (2.89 ± 0.92, 95% CI: 2.73 to 3.05) and Relationship between Fatigue and CHD (2.06 ± 1.24, 95% CI: 1.85 to 2.27), followed by Classification of Fatigue (0.88 ± 0.55, 95% CI: 0.78 to 0.97). Knowledge of Causes and Manifestations (0.12 ± 0.45, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.20) and Measurement and Relief Methods (0.16 ± 0.47, 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.23) was very limited.

Self-Management Behavior

The average total score for self-management was 80.35 ± 18.59 (95% CI: 77.22 to 83.50), corresponding to an average scoring rate of approximately 59.2%, suggesting overall suboptimal self-management (less than 60% rating). The top two subdomains were bad-habit management (15.23 ± 2.72, 95% CI: 14.78 to 15.69) and general life management (14.32 ± 2.62, 95% CI: 13.87 to 14.76), indicating a stronger commitment to healthy living. The lowest scores were for treatment compliance management (6.14 ± 2.19, 95% CI: 5.77 to 6.51) and first aid preparedness management (7.90 ± 2.76, 95% CI: 7.43 to 8.36), suggesting poor adherence to medication and very limited emergency response capability, separately.

Self-Efficacy

The mean total score of self-efficacy for the participants was 32.35 ± 12.18 (95% CI: 30.29 to 34.40) from which it could be said that participants did not have much confidence to take care of the disease. The score for symptom management (24.02 ± 8.70, 95% CI: 22.55 to 25.49) was substantially higher than that for functional maintenance (8.32 ± 3.49, 95% CI: 7.73 to 8.91). The discrepancy suggested that patients feel more capable of managing acute symptom exacerbations than of sustaining day-to-day functional participation.

Fatigue knowledge showed a weak, marginally significant negative correlation with fatigue (rs = −0.164, 95% CI: −0.323 to 0.004, p = 0.056). Self-management demonstrated a weak but statistically significant negative association with fatigue (rs = −0.181, 95% CI: −0.339 to −0.013, p = 0.035). Though statistically meaningful, the small effect size implies limited standalone clinical predictive value of self-management status for fatigue symptoms. Self-efficacy showed a weak-to-moderate and clinically noteworthy negative correlation with fatigue (rs = −0.320, 95% CI: −0.464 to −0.161, p < 0.001). Among the three indicators, self-efficacy possessed the strongest clinical relevance to fatigue burden and may serve as a practical target for community nursing intervention.

Discussion

The primary contribution of this study lies in providing local baseline data on fatigue, fatigue knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy measured simultaneously in a community-dwelling CHD population in central China. Overall, the observed pattern of associations is broadly consistent with findings from previous studies in other settings. This replication in an understudied community setting supports the transferability of these established relationships to Chinese urban community populations, while also providing context-specific prevalence estimates that can inform local health service planning.

Consistent with previous community-based studies, the present sample showed high fatigue prevalence (92.65%) coupled with low levels of fatigue knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy.8,31 All of these results highlight significant shortcomings in the community’s current approach to managing chronic illnesses. They stress the importance of putting into practice patient-centered strategies that include health education, useful behavioral support, and psychosocial care in order to help patients better manage fatigue and improve overall outcomes in CHD.

The sample was characterized by relatively low income and educational attainment, high rates of unhealthy lifestyles, and frequent comorbidities—factors known to constrain effective chronic disease self-management.32 Effective self-management in chronic diseases is known to be hampered by such behavioral and sociodemographic factors. Our findings are consistent with previous research that shows low health literacy and limited access to trustworthy health information can lower patients’ participation in fatigue-related education and self-care programs.14,20,21 Furthermore, the high prevalence of comorbidities (69.12%) and the scant attention given to fatigue (91.91%) indicate that persistent fatigue is still often disregarded in routine clinical practice.6,8

Community-dwelling CHD patients frequently struggle with fatigue, which is a moderate burden on their daily lives and tends to impact physical functioning more than mental aspects. However, since higher levels of mental fatigue are often linked to emotional distress, decreased motivation, and trouble adhering to treatment, psychological factors should not be disregarded.5,24 Growing awareness that the significant influence of mental fatigue in patients with CHD, which underscores the necessity of integrating psychological evaluations and supportive counseling into routine community-based care.7

Community participants had limited fatigue-related knowledge, particularly especially in the areas of etiology, symptom identification, and self-management strategies. These results align with prior study.21,22 The findings also imply that fatigue education has received little attention from public health and cardiac rehabilitation resources. Knowledge was significantly impacted by education level, with those with only a primary education having especially little awareness. These knowledge gaps may make it more difficult for patients to identify and manage fatigue, underscoring the need for highly illustrated, low literacy, and culturally relevant health education materials.

Community-dwelling patients of CHD had poor self-management, particularly in low adherence to treatment and disease management plans, aligning with previous studies.16,33 Despite some improvements in lifestyle, patients continued to face more complicated problems, such as medication management and disease monitoring.14,19 If lacking of long-term, structured chronic care, older and lower educational people may further reduce self-management behaviors.15,31 Targeted programs such as simplified medication education, simulated emergency response training, and peer-led community workshops may help reduce these gaps.34

In this community study, self-efficacy levels were lower than the study in hospitalized or cardiac rehabilitation cohorts.9,26 The results are consistent with prior study showing that self-efficacy predicts better treatment adherence and self-managment behaviors.29,35 Our cross-sectional findings similarly show a strong positive association between self-efficacy and self-management scores. The strength of the intercorrelations among self-efficacy, self-management, and knowledge lends support to the hypothesis that building patient confidence by motivational counseling, peer support, and graded goal-setting may facilitate improved self‑management and potentially reduce fatigue burden.36

In this cross-sectional study, fatigue was found to be negatively associated with knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy, though the strength of these associations was generally weak to weak-to-moderate (rs ranging from −0.164 to −0.320). Specifically, fatigue knowledge showed only a marginally significant negative correlation with fatigue (rs = −0.164, p = 0.056), while self-management (rs = −0.181, p = 0.035) and self-efficacy (rs = −0.320, p < 0.001) reached statistical significance. Because of the cross-sectional study, these findings only reflect correlational patterns and cannot be interpreted as causal relationships. It remains unclear whether lower self-efficacy leads to greater fatigue, or whether greater fatigue severity undermines patients’ confidence in self-management, or whether both are influenced by unmeasured confounding factors such as disease severity, comorbidity burden, or depressive symptoms.5

Compared with the relatively weak correlations with fatigue, positive interrelationships among knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy were notably substantially stronger (rs = 0.832–0.912), particularly the largest coefficient (0.912) found between self-management and self-efficacy. These results align with Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory, in which knowledge serves as a cognitive foundation, self-efficacy acts as a key motivational mediator, and self-management represents the behavioral output—with bidirectional influences connecting all three constructs.23 These correlations may also partly reflect common method variance from self-report measurement and response-style effects, especially the low educational level in the participants could further magnify such biases.20,31 Meanwhile, the weaker correlations with fatigue, probably stem from the multifactorial characters of fatigue. The fatigue is affected jointly by psychosocial factors and physiological mechanisms, including myocardial dysfunction and inflammatory processes to medication side effects.6,8 Future studies should focus on multi-method assessment approaches to disentangle true variable associations from methodologically induced spurious correlations.

The results of associations can be explained by multiple mechanistic pathways. Grounded in SCT, stronger knowledge enables patients to recognize fatigue triggers and implement self-management behaviors.21,22 Successful experiences can reinforce self-efficacy over time.23 As self-efficacy grows, patients are more likely to maintain self-management behaviors, such as regular exercises and symptom monitoring, all of which may directly contribute to reduced fatigue severity.26,35 In contrast, patients with inadequate knowledge usually fail to recognize fatigue triggers. They rarely make active attempts to engage in self-management behaviors and accumulate limited experiences, which can further reduce their self-efficacy.24 Ultimately, it may lead to a cycle of inadequate self-management and worsening fatigue.8,37 Besides psychosocial mechanisms, physiology and behavior may also play a role. Patients with greater self-efficacy tend to sustain regular activities, which often is associated with to improved cardiorespiratory fitness and reduces fatigue in CHD patients.8,10 Meanwhile, better self-management behaviors in key domains (such as medication adherence, fluid balance management, and comorbidity control) help reduce fatigue severity.11,16 Furthermore, the study did not measure psychological factors such as depression and anxiety, which may explain these associations. Lower self-efficacy and self-management behaviors correlate with heightened depression, which can further increase fatigue severity.5,7 In addition, inadequate knowledge may lead to elevated anxiety levels, which further increase fatigue severity.38

While causal inferences cannot be drawn from this cross-sectional design, the observed associations suggest potential value in integrated community interventions that combine health education, behavior change support, and psychosocial care. On this basis, community interventions could reasonably prioritize self-efficacy enhancement as a central component and should be adapted to meet the needs of patients with low educational attainment and limited health literacy. Future intervention studies are needed to test the effectiveness of such approaches.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study adds to the growing evidence on fatigue and its behavioral determinants among CHD patients living in Chinese communities. It employed validated, outcome-specific measures and achieved adequate representation within a mid-sized urban population. However, several limitations should be noted. First, as a cross-sectional study, only associations can be identified and causal inferences cannot be drawn; longitudinal research is needed to establish directional relationships among the variables. Second, the sample size of 136 provides adequate power to detect moderate-to-strong correlations but may be underpowered for weaker associations. Therefore, marginally significant or nonsignificant findings should be interpreted cautiously, and all correlation coefficients are presented with 95% confidence intervals to better reflect effect size precision. Third, the study was limited to bivariate correlations without multivariable adjustment for potential factors (such as disease severity, comorbidity burden, and depressive symptoms). So the study cannot determine the independent effects among knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy on fatigue. The associations observed may partially explained by shared clinical and demographic characteristics. Fourth, all study variables relied on self-reported, which may lead to common method variance, social desirability bias, and recall error. To a certain degree, the abnormally high correlations observed among the three psychosocial variables (rs up to 0.912) may reflect these methodological bias. Fifth, participants were recruited from two communities within one urban district in central China, and individuals with psychiatric comorbidities or non-CHD-related fatigue were excluded. The narrow sampling limits the generalizability of our findings, which may not apply to rural populations, more diverse urban contexts, more severe CHD subgroups, or regions operating under different healthcare systems. Finally, it is unequal for community residents access fatigue-related knowledge, which may influence participants’ knowledge level. Future studies should focus on prospective longitudinal designs, multivariable modeling, and multi-method assessment to disentangle causal pathways and separate genuine variable associations from methodological bias. In addition, educational interventions targeting populations with low health literacy should be conducted to verify the practical effects on reducing fatigue burden.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional investigation recruited 136 patients with stable CHD across two urban communities in Pingdingshan, China. Overall fatigue prevalence reached 92.65%, and participants generally demonstrated limited fatigue knowledge, poor self-management, and relatively low self-efficacy. Correlation analyses indicated that self-efficacy showed the strongest negative association with fatigue (rs = −0.320), whereas knowledge, self-management, and self-efficacy formed a cluster of strong positive intercorrelations (rs = 0.832–0.912). As expected for a cross-sectional study, these results only reflect the correlation and cannot establish causal directionality.

The study provides baseline evidence for community-based chronic disease management and suggests the practical value of integrated community programs combining health education, behavioral support, and psychosocial care. Given the low education of study participants and the particularly strong association between self-efficacy and fatigue, community nursing interventions may benefit from prioritizing self-efficacy enhancement as a core target, paired with fatigue knowledge education and low literacy adapted educational materials.

Future studies are needed to use prospective intervention trials to evaluate whether integrated community-based programs effectively reduce fatigue burden and improve self-management level. Meanwhile, longitudinal study will be necessary to clarify causal pathways among the variables. The study can serve as a reference baseline for comparable urban community CHD populations in central China; extrapolation to rural settings, more severe CHD subgroups, or other regions should be approached with caution, given the restricted sampling and study exclusion criteria.
