Geographic Analysis of Access to the National Diabetes Prevention Program in New York State
Authors: Elizabeth A. Vrany, Codruta Chiuzan, Eun Jeong Oh, Patti L. Ephraim, Rachael A. Austin, Ian F. Brissette, Susan L. Millstein, Stephanie L. Fitzpatrick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePrediabetes affects 1.7 million adults in New York State (NYS), with higher rates among racial/ethnic minoritized and low socioeconomic status populations. This study examined access to the National Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) – an evidence-based program to prevent/delay diabetes onset – among NYS residents with prediabetes.MethodsSelf-reported prediabetes diagnosis, zip code, and sociodemographic characteristics were obtained from a sample of 39,095 adults from the 2021 NYS Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System. National DPP site location and recognition status were obtained from the CDC Diabetes Prevention Recognition Program Registry. Geospatial analysis defined 30-min drive-time buffers around National DPP sites. The proportion of NYS residents with prediabetes living within a 30-min drive of a site was computed.ResultsPrediabetes prevalence ranged from 5.6%–18.5% across counties; ten counties had rates >15%. There were 117 National DPP sites in NYS. The majority of residents (84.3%) with prediabetes lived within a 30-min drive of a site. Access varied by race and ethnicity, with greater access among Black, Asian, and Hispanic than among White respondents.ConclusionsAlthough most NYS residents with prediabetes have access to the National DPP, areas of unavailability remain. Expansion efforts should focus on counties with high prediabetes rates and limited program availability.

Introduction

In New York State (NYS), the prevalence of prediabetes has doubled from 5.4% in 2011 to 11.5% in 2021, affecting 1.7 million adult New Yorkers (New York State Department of Health, 2024). Among those with prediabetes, 5–10% will develop type 2 diabetes if they do not receive an intervention (Gerstein et al., 2007). This is particularly concerning for racial and ethnic minoritized and low socioeconomic status (SES) adults who have significantly higher prevalence of prediabetes than their counterparts in NYS (New York State Department of Health, 2024). The National Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) is an evidence-based, effective, public health approach to prevent or delay the onset of diabetes (Knowler et al., 2002). There have been numerous translational studies and demonstration projects indicating that National DPP promotes modest, but clinically meaningful, improvements in weight, hemoglobin A1C, and other cardiometabolic risk factors (Whittemore, 2011; Ali et al., 2012; Mudaliar et al., 2016; Dunkley et al., 2014; Ely et al., 2017). Furthermore, access to and engagement in National DPP has been suboptimal, especially among racial and ethnic minoritized, low SES, and rural communities (Chambers et al., 2019; Ritchie et al., 2020; Ariel-Donges et al., 2020).

Previous studies suggest that lack of availability of culturally adapted National DPPs (e.g., program delivered in languages other than English), childcare needs, and health-related social needs (e.g., lack of transportation, nutrition insecurity, housing instability, and poor healthcare access) are barriers to accessing and engaging in National DPP (Ely et al., 2017; Chambers et al., 2019; Ritchie et al., 2020). Furthermore, maintaining Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recognition status as a National DPP site is challenging for many community-based organizations. In addition to the effort needed for program recruitment, delivery, and retention, the CDC requires specific data to be collected (i.e., attendance, weight, and minutes of physical activity per week) and reported in a specific format twice a year. These additional data needs can be burdensome to community-based sites, especially low-resourced sites, and lead to lapses in CDC recognition and program sustainability.

Based on geospatial analysis conducted on data from 2008 to 2010, 80% of NYS's population lived within a 30-min one-way drive to a National DPP site; however, most people living in 10 of the counties with the highest prevalence of prediabetes lived further than a 30-min drive (Ruberto and Brissette, 2014). Thus, in 2010, the number of available National DPP sites in NYS did not meet the need based on the prevalence of prediabetes at the county level. The current study aimed to examine the availability of CDC-recognized National DPP sites 10 years later in NYS at the county level based on the number of NYS residents with prediabetes living within a 30-min drive to a National DPP site. Sociodemographic characteristics of NYS residents that live within versus outside a 30-min drive of a National DPP site were also examined.

Methods

Study design and population

Data for this study was extracted from two sources – the 2021 NYS Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) and the CDC Diabetes Prevention Recognition Program (DPRP) Registry – to compare prediabetes prevalence to National DPP availability in NYS.

BRFSS is an annual telephone surveillance system funded by the CDC, conducted in all 50 states in the United States. The survey collects data on health-related behaviors, preventive health practices, and chronic health conditions. Interviews are conducted by phone in English and Spanish using standardized procedures. More information on BRFSS procedures can be found at: https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/data_documentation/index.htm. Data from the present study came from the 2021 administration of the BRFSS in NYS.

The BRFSS employs a split-sample design (Splits 1, 2, and 3). Participants are randomly assigned to a split, and each split receives a different set of optional modules to shorten the survey. In the 2021 NYS BRFSS, the optional Prediabetes Module was omitted from Split 3. Therefore, from the full sample of 39,095 respondents, analyses excluded 12,946 respondents who were not asked about prediabetes and 4675 respondents who self-reported having diabetes, yielding a final sample of 21,744 respondents in analyses estimating prediabetes prevalence. A subset of 2867 respondents with self-reported prediabetes were included in analyses of geographic access to National DPP sites in NYS. Deidentified data were supplied by the NYS Department of Health; therefore, the study was exempt from institutional review.

Measures

Prediabetes diagnosis

Self-reported prediabetes diagnosis was assessed in the 2021 BRFSS. Respondents were asked, “Have you had a test for high blood sugar or diabetes within the past three years?” Respondents who responded “yes” were additionally asked, “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you have prediabetes?” Those who responded “yes” or “yes, but female told during pregnancy only,” were coded as self-reported prediabetes cases. All others were coded as “no.” Note that adults diagnosed with gestational diabetes during any pregnancy are eligible to participate in National DPP after pregnancy.

Respondent location

Self-reported respondent location data (county and zip code) – used for county-level prediabetes prevalence estimates and geospatial analyses – were assessed in the 2021 BRFSS. Respondents were asked to report, “In what county do you currently live?” and “What is the zip code where you currently live?” The 62 counties in NYS were coded, and the 5-digit zip code was recorded.

National DPP site locations and recognition statuses

Data for National DPP sites came from the NYS Department of Health's 2021 records from the CDC DPRP Registry (https://dprp.cdc.gov/registry), namely the address (street, city, state, and zip code) and recognition status for each site in NYS. Other factors

Respondent characteristics were obtained from the 2021 BRFSS, namely age (years), race and ethnicity (White, Black/African American, Asian, Hispanic, multiracial/other, and refused/don't know/unknown), sex assigned at birth (female, male, intersex, and refused/don't know/unknown), education (did not graduate high school, graduated high school, attended college or technical school, graduated college or technical school, and refused/don't know/unknown), employment (employed for wages/self-employed, homemaker, out of work, retired, student, unable to work, and refused/don't know/unknown), language preference (English or Spanish), health insurance coverage (plan through an employer/union, Medicaid/state sponsored program, Medicare, private, and no coverage), access to health care in the past 12 months (yes, no, and refused/don't know/unknown), nutrition insecurity (always, usually/sometimes, rarely/never, and refused/don't know/unknown), and participation in a health education program in the past 12 months (yes, no, and refused/don't know/unknown). Race and ethnicity were coded using a combined variable. All Hispanic or Latino respondents were coded as Hispanic. Due to low frequencies, American Indian or Alaskan Native, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, other race, or multiracial respondents were coded as multiracial/other.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to estimate prediabetes rates at the NYS county level using the 2021 BRFSS data. To account for the complex sampling design in BRFSS, county-level analyses used appropriate survey weights. For the county-level estimates, a simple survey design used county-level trimmed weights to adjust for variation in respondents' probability of selection, disproportionate selection of population subgroups relative to the state's population distribution, or nonresponse. Details about BFRSS survey methodology (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025a) and weighting (Iachan et al., 2016; Battaglia et al., 2008) are reported elsewhere.

Weighted frequencies and prevalence estimates of prediabetes were computed across counties. Prediabetes rates included the percent of the population at increased risk for developing type 2 diabetes due to reporting a diagnosis of prediabetes or a history of gestational diabetes without a diabetes diagnosis post-pregnancy.

Address geocoding was carried out using the ArcGIS Online North American Geocoding Service (version 10.8.2) (ESRI, 2023). The ArcGIS Network Analyst extension was used in combination with ESRI's (Environmental Systems Research Institute's) StreetMap North America dataset to model drive-time service areas. These models estimated travel time and distance from an individual's home zip code to the nearest National DPP site. Specifically, a 30-min, one-way drive-time buffer was generated around each site to identify areas with reasonable geographic access to program sites.

The prediabetes rates for NYS residents living within versus outside the 30-min drive from a National DPP site were stratified and compared by recognition status using the primary survey weight. To characterize NYS residents with prediabetes by National DPP site proximity, the sample was restricted to individuals reporting prediabetes. Sociodemographic and health-related variables, including race and ethnicity, sex assigned at birth, education, employment, language preference, insurance status, access to care, nutrition insecurity, and prior participation in health education program, were analyzed overall and stratified by living within versus outside the 30-min drive from a National DPP site. Group differences were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test and Pearson's chi-squared test with the Rao and Scott adjustment. Missing data, including cases where individuals refused to respond or selected “don't know,” were grouped together and tabulated as a distinct category to preserve transparency in data reporting. All statistical tests were two-sided, and the significance level α was set at 0.05. Analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2.

Results

Prevalence of prediabetes by NYS county

The prevalence of prediabetes ranged from 5.6% to 18.5% across 62 counties in NYS. Ten counties had prediabetes rates >15%, namely Dutchess (18.5%), Schoharie (17.3%), Madison (16.8%), Franklin (16.2%), Allegany (15.9%), Broome (15.9%), Delaware (15.9%), Queens (15.8%), Bronx (15.7%), and Kings (15.3%) counties. Twenty-seven counties had prediabetes prevalence ranging from 10% to 15%, and 25 counties had rates of prediabetes <10%, with the lowest rates in Onondaga (5.6%), Putnam (5.7%), and Schuyler (5.8%) counties. Characteristics of NYS residents with prediabetes

Among NYS residents with prediabetes, the average age was 56 years. Approximately 21.0% were Black/African American, 11.1% were Asian, 15.7% were Hispanic, 2.8% were multiracial/other race, and 47.7% were White. Slightly more than half were female sex at birth (55.9%). For education and employment, 11.4% did not graduate high school, and 50.5% reported being employed. English language was more common (94.2%) than Spanish language (5.8%). Geographic access to National DPP Sites in NYS

In 2021, there were 117 National DPP sites in NYS with CDC recognition statuses of full plus (n = 19), full (n = 31), preliminary (n = 23), and pending (n = 44). The map in Fig. 1 shows the location and recognition status of National DPP sites in NYS. Sites are located across the state, with clustering of sites near New York City (NYC).

Geospatial analysis was used to calculate 30-min drive time boundaries around National DPP sites, shown in Fig. 1. Based on 2021 BRFSS respondents' zip codes, 84.3% of NYS residents with prediabetes live within a 30-min drive of a National DPP site. Analyses examining 30-min drive boundaries by CDC recognitions status show the following rates of National DPP access for NYS residents with prediabetes: 67.4% for full plus, 70.2% for full, 71.6% for preliminary, and 61.4% for pending recognition. Approximately 15.7% of NYS residents with prediabetes live outside of a 30-min drive of a National DPP site (see Supplementary Table 2). The majority of residents in three counties with a high risk of prediabetes (>15%) live at a distance beyond a 30-min drive to a National DPP site, namely residents of Allegany, Delaware, and Schoharie counties (see Fig. 1).

Differences in prevalence of living within versus outside of a 30-min drive from a National DPP site were observed by race and ethnicity, education, and language (see Table 2). Among NYS residents with prediabetes, proportionally more Black/African American (23.4% within a 30-min drive vs. 8.1% outside a 30-min drive), Asian (13.1 vs. 0.3%), and Hispanic (17.7% vs. 5.2%) relative to White (41.3% vs. 82.8%); college-educated relative (38.4% vs. 20.3%) to non-college-educated (61.2% vs. 79.7%); and Spanish-speaking (6.8% vs. 0.4%) relative to English-speaking (93.2% vs. 99.6%) individuals lived within a 30-min drive of a National DPP site. The prevalence of living within versus outside of a 30-min drive did not vary by age, sex assigned at birth, employment, health insurance coverage, access to care, nutrition insecurity, or prior participation in a health education program.

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence of prediabetes in NYS at the county level and the availability of CDC-recognized National DPP sites across the state. Findings showed that prediabetes prevalence ranged from 5.6% to 18.5% across 62 NYS counties. NYS had a total of 117 National DPP sites, 40 of which had full or full plus recognition status. The majority (84.3%) of NYS residents with prediabetes had access to a National DPP site, with 70.2% having access to a site with full recognition. Access to National DPP sites varied by three demographic factors: race and ethnicity, education, and language, with proportionally greater access among those who were Black/African American, Asian, Hispanic, Spanish-speaking, and college educated relative to their counterparts. Notably, some regions of National DPP unavailability remain in NYS, with 15.7% of residents with prediabetes being outside of a 30-min drive of a site.

A prior report from the NYS Department of Health on prediabetes prevalence in NYS using data from BRFSS reported that prediabetes affects 11.5% of NYS residents (New York State Department of Health, 2024). The present findings are consistent, with the county-level prediabetes prevalence ranging from 5.6% to 18.5%. Although the national prediabetes prevalence is 38% based on measurements of fasting blood glucose or A1C (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024), relatively few people with prediabetes are aware of their diagnosis (19%) (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024). Prediabetes unawareness may explain the lower rates observed in the present study, which used self-reported data. Overall, there is a need increase awareness of prediabetes through routine screening and enhanced communication from the health care team. Being unaware of prediabetes is one barrier to timely engagement in diabetes prevention efforts, including enrolling in the National DPP.

Relative to the prior study examining geographic access to National DPP sites in NYS in 2010 (Ruberto and Brissette, 2014), the number of National DPP sites in NYS increased from 39 to 117 sites. Over this decade, statewide efforts to increase National DPP capacity – led by NYS and NYC Departments of Health – focused on expanding the availability and accessibility of National DPP programs, increasing the number and diversity of the Lifestyle Coaches, providing curriculum language translation and cultural adaptation, enrolling and retaining priority populations, leveraging technology to enhance program delivery, and utilizing health information technology to increase program referrals. Although the percentage of NYS residents with prediabetes residing within a 30-min drive of a National DPP site remained consistent (80.0% in 2010 (Ruberto and Brissette, 2014); 84.3% in 2021), the map displaying 30-min drive boundaries shows that access spans more of the state. Moreover, new sites appeared in counties identified as high-risk in 2010 – namely Chautauqua, Genesee, Seneca, Cortland, and Chenango counties. Three counties with ongoing limited access to National DPP sites have a higher (>15%) prediabetes prevalence – namely Allegany, Delaware, and Schoharie counties. Altogether, National DDP availability has increased over the past decade in NYS, yet continued program expansion would benefit NYS counties with high prediabetes prevalence.

There was a clustering of National DPP sites around NYC, and residents in counties in and near NYC live within a 30-min drive of a site. Although promising, a drive time radius may not adequately reflect accessibility in NYC. It remains unclear whether the capacity of NYC's National DPP sites can serve the full population of people with prediabetes in that region. Furthermore, many NYC residents rely on walking and public transportation, and not having a program in the neighborhood or along a direct transportation line may limit access.

The present study found differences between being within versus outside of a 30-min drive by race and ethnicity, education, and language. Specifically, Black/African American, Hispanic, Asian, college-educated, and Spanish-speaking residents are proportionally more likely than their counterparts to live within a 30-min drive of a National DPP site. This finding may be due to the high concentration of programs in NYC. Importantly, data from the CDC DPRP State Report (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025b) showed that 61% of White participants achieved DPRP outcomes of clinically significant weight loss and/or increased minutes of physical activity by the end of the program, compared to 53% of Black/African American and 52% of Hispanic participants (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025b). These data suggest that National DPP availability is only a part of the picture of reducing diabetes risk. Qualitative research is needed to understand barriers and identify strategies to enhance enrollment, retention, and outcome achievement, particularly among those from racial and ethnic minoritized and low SES backgrounds.

There are three key limitations of the present study. First, this study was conducted in one region, and findings may not be generalizable across the United States. Second, this study examined drive time as an indicator of National DPP accessibility, which may not adequately represent accessibility in a densely populated, urban setting such as NYC. Future studies should investigate alternative parameters of geographical access (walkability/transportation) and the capacity of sites to serve population-dense regions. Third, this study examined the availability of live, in-person delivery of the National DPP and did not include distance learning or asynchronous online delivery, which can improve access to the National DPP, particularly in areas where a DPP site is not within reasonable driving distance. There are two key strengths of this study. First, this study used data from the BRFSS, a large dataset with outcomes generalizable to the noninstitutionalized civilian population of NYS residents. Second, the study employed advanced geospatial analysis to approximate the NYS population residing within a certain distance of National DPP sites. This methodology identified regions to target future National DPP expansion efforts in NYS.

Conclusion

A geospatial analysis of access to the CDC-recognized National DPP in NYS showed that 84.3% of NYS residents have access to a site within a 30-min drive. The number of sites has increased over the past decade, with a clustering of sites in more densely populated, urban areas (i.e., NYC). Differences in National DPP access were observed by race and ethnicity, education, and language, with greater access among Black/African American, Asian, Hispanic, college-educated, and Spanish-speaking residents with prediabetes. Despite this expansion of National DPP access in NYS, other data show suboptimal engagement and outcomes among racial and ethnic minoritized populations, reinforcing the need to increase program awareness and intervene upon barriers to enrollment, retention, and outcome achievement.
