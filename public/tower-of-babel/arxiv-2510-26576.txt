“Show Me You Comply… Without Showing Me Anything”: Zero - Knowledge Software Auditing for AI - Enabled Systems
Filippo Scaramuzza, Renato Cordeiro Ferreira, Tomaz Maia Suller, Giovanni Quattrocchi, Damian Andrew Tamburri, Willem-Jan van den Heuvel

Abstract
. The increasing exploitation of Artificial Intelligence (AI) enabled systems in critical domains has made trustworthiness concerns a paramount showstopper, requiring verifiable accountability, often by regulation (e.g., the EU AI Act). Classical software verification and validation techniques, such as procedural audits, formal methods, or model documentation, are the mechanisms used to achieve this. However, these methods are either expensive or heavily manual and ill-suited for the opaque, “black box” nature of most AI models. An intractable conflict emerges: high auditability and verifiability are required by law, but such transparency conflicts with the need to protect assets being audited—e.g., confidential data and proprietary models—leading to weakened accountability. To address this challenge, this paper introduces ZKMLOps, a novel MLOps verification framework that operationalizes Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs)—cryptographic protocols allowing a prover to convince a verifier that a statement is true without revealing additional information—within Machine-Learning Operations lifecycles. By integrating ZKPs with established software engineering patterns, ZKMLOps provides a modular and repeatable process for generating verifiable cryptographic proof of compliance. We evaluate the framework’s practicality through a study of regulatory compliance in financial risk auditing and assess feasibility through an empirical evaluation of top ZKP protocols, analyzing performance trade-offs for ML models of increasing complexity.

Introduction

AI
-
enabled software systems—i.e., all those that embed components such as deep neural networks (DNNs) or large language models (LLMs)—have moved from academic efforts to mission
-
critical infrastructure in finance, healthcare, transportation, public administration, and more
(
Amodei and Hernandez 2018
;
Lipton 2018
)
.
Decisions made by these systems are increasingly safety
-
, ethics
-
, or money
-
critical, hence legislators worldwide have begun to demand
verifiable accountability
. For example, the EU Artificial Intelligence Act

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, enforced from June 2024
classifies many industrial AI deployments as
high

-

(
Future of Life Institute 2025
)
. Similar requirements appear in sector
-
specific guidance from financial regulators (e.g., the Basel Committee) or in forthcoming U.S. executive orders.

On the one hand, in conventional software engineering, auditors rely on a spectrum of verification and validation (V&V) artifacts, ranging from manual process audits and test suites to formal model-checking
(
Clarke et al. 2018
)
.
On the other hand, the above artifacts are only effective when the implementation logic is explicit and can be modelled deterministically.
Yet modern AI models are opaque “black boxes” whose behaviour emerges from millions (or even billions) of learned parameters
(
Rudin 2019
)
.
Probing those parameters directly may leak intellectual property or personal data, and disclosing training data often conflicts with data
-
protection laws and contracts.
Thus, organizations face an intractable tension: regulators request deep transparency, but disclosing the very artifacts that enable transparency would compromise proprietary or privacy
-
sensitive assets
(
Cooper et al. 2022
;
Cofone and Strandburg 2019
)
. The consequences of this scenario are often dire. For example, let us look a few years back: in 2015, the Volkswagen emissions scandal
(
Jung and Sharon 2019
)
revealed that the company had deliberately programmed its diesel engines with a “defeat device” to circumvent environmental regulations. This software could predict—by way of computational intelligence—when the vehicle was undergoing laboratory testing and alter its performance accordingly, creating a substantial gap between public claims and real-world behavior; needless to say, the whole scenario caused worldwide havoc, financial loss, production hampering, and more. This precedent of engineered deception exemplifies a similar verifiability gap that exists in the domain of artificial intelligence, where the opaque, “black box” nature of many ML models complicates rigorous auditing
(
Mökander 2023
)
.

In response to such scenarios, this paper introduces ZKMLOps, a novel framework for the systematic auditing of Machine Learning (ML) systems. The framework operationalizes Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs)
(
Xing et al. 2023
)
within the Machine Learning Operations (MLOps) lifecycle
(
Kreuzberger et al. 2023
)
, enabling an organization to furnish verifiable cryptographic proof that its systems adhere to specific requirements and regulations. A ZKP is a protocol that allows a prover to convince a verifier that a computational statement is true, without revealing any information beyond the statement’s validity. This property makes ZKPs uniquely suited to resolve the auditing dilemma. However, the theoretical availability of these primitives does not address the practical challenge of their systematic application. This integration allows for the verification of properties, such as the use of an approved model, a fair dataset, or any other arbitrary requirement, without revealing any underlying proprietary information or sensitive data. Consequently, ZKMLOps directly addresses the fundamental conflict between the need for transparency in auditing and for confidentiality in competitive and regulated environments.

The research context in which we operate reflects an emergent
MLOps Engineering
discipline, a new subfield of Software Engineering that studies the challenges of operating and verifying ML-Enabled Systems (MLES)
(
Kumara et al. 2022
;
Kreuzberger et al. 2023
)
. The primary novelty we introduce in this new field is a framework aimed at enforcing the legislation compliance standards in the scope of algorithmic auditing
(
Metaxa et al. 2021
)
with a structured assessment of an AI system against predefined norms. The key technical enabler in our contribution is founded on the cryptographic principles of Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs)
(
Goldreich 2004
)
.
Over the past decade, succinct ZKP schemes such as Groth16
(
Groth 2016
)
, Bulletproofs
(
Bünz et al. 2018
)
, and PLONK
(
Gabizon et al. 2020
)
have reduced proof sizes to the kilobyte scale and verification times to milliseconds, enabling practical deployments in blockchains and confidential computing.
Similarly, the rapidly maturing sub
-
field of
Zero
-
Knowledge Machine Learning
(ZKML) has started to demonstrate how entire AI/ML evaluations can be proven in zero-knowledge facilities
(
Xing et al. 2023
)
.
Nevertheless, there is still a consistent gap between protocol research and disciplined, repeatable
software engineering
lifecycles, principles, and automations that teams can adopt in production, to operationalize such advanced cryptographic tools within their MLOps initiatives. The proposed framework bridges this gap by integrating ZKPs with established software engineering theory and practice, evaluating this integration in action. For example, by leveraging software architectural designs, the framework decouples the core auditing logic from the diverse implementations of ZKP protocols, thereby transforming a complex cryptographic procedure into a modular, repeatable, and maintainable engineering process.

In summary, this paper makes three key contributions. First, we propose the ZKMLOps framework, a novel architecture for integrating ZKP-based verification into the MLOps lifecycle. Second, we demonstrate the framework’s practicality by presenting a detailed use case for regulatory compliance in financial risk auditing, showing how our approach can be applied to solve a real-world problem. Third, we assess its feasibility through an empirical evaluation of various ZKP protocols.

Quantifying these trade-offs confirms that a strategic selection of ZKP protocols makes the framework viable across a range of ML models, especially within asynchronous workflows that can accommodate intensive prover computation.

2.
Background

In this section, we present the necessary background on Trustworthy AI, auditing of AI systems, and ZKP to contextualize our work. We begin by defining Trustworthy AI and its core principles, including fairness, transparency, and accountability. Following this, we outline the practices of AI auditing, explaining its objectives and methodologies as a mechanism for ensuring that AI systems adhere to these principles. Finally, we introduce the cryptographic concept of ZKP, explaining how they function and their growing importance in enabling privacy-preserving verification in machine learning contexts.

2.1.
Trustworthy AI

According to the European Union
(
Smuha 2019
)
, an ML-enabled system should meet four ethical principles: (i) respect for human autonomy, (ii) prevention of harm, (iii) fairness, and (iv) explicability. Based on these four principles, Liu et al.
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
survey the recent research efforts in this domain and identify six dimensions of trustworthy AI: (i)
Safety and Robustness
, i.e., to be robust to the tiny perturbations of inputs and to be able to make secure decisions; (ii)
Non-discrimination & Fairness
, i.e., to avoid unfair bias toward certain groups or individuals; (iii)
Explainability
, i.e., to be able to be explained to stakeholders; (iv)
Privacy
, i.e. to avoid leaking any private information; (v)
Accountability
, i.e., to be assessable by a third-party and assign responsibility for an AI failure; (vi) Environmental Well-Being, i.e., to be sustainable and environmentally friendly.
This work focuses specifically on accountability, as it addresses the regulations on ML-enabled systems to follow these six dimensions. As stated by Li et al.
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, accountability runs through the entire lifecycle of an ML-enabled system and requires that its stakeholders justify their product design, software architecture, implementation, and operation as aligned with human values.

From accountability are also derived the concepts of auditability and traceability, which require an ML-enabled system to be reviewed, assessed, and audited
(
Leslie 2019
)
.

2.2.
Auditing of ML-Enabled Systems

The auditing of an ML-enabled system—AI auditing for simplicity—can be defined along two axes: functionally and methodologically
(
Mökander 2023
)
. Functionally, AI auditing is a governance mechanism that can be managed by various actors. For instance, regulators may assess compliance with legal standards, technology providers can investigate how to mitigate technology-related risks, and stakeholders can make informed decisions about how they engage with specific companies. Methodologically, AI auditing is characterized by a structured process whereby an entity’s past or present behavior is assessed for consistency with predefined standards, regulations, or norms.

The first known auditing procedure was employed during Roman times—the etymology of auditing stems from the Latin
auditus
, “a hearing”—and was used during official examinations of oral accounts
(
Teck-Heang and Md Ali 2008
)
. As the ages passed, auditors began to verify written records as a governance mechanism to secure and enforce accountability, for example, in the form of
financial audits
. The rise of financial audits as we know them today stems from the Joint Stock Companies Act (1844), from the British Parliament, which required finance directors to issue audited financial statements to investors
(
Wright and Sylla 2004
)
. Nowadays, financial auditing and accounting have grown into one of the world’s largest industries, with an estimated market size of over $100 billion
(
Mökander 2023
)
. Another big family of audits is the
safety audits
. While financial audits seek to manage financial risks, safety audits aim at highlighting potential health and safety hazards, while assessing the effectiveness of the prevention mechanisms and countermeasures
(
Allford and Carson 2015
)
. Safety audits have a history dating back to the Industrial Revolution in 19th-century Britain, where workers faced poor conditions and high risks of workplace accidents. As workers formed unions demanding better conditions, workplace safety auditing became a mechanism to hold employers accountable. Nowadays, a lot of these audits exist, spanning from workplace safety audits
(
Gay and New 1999
)
, food safety audits
(
Dillon and Griffith 2001
)
, and operational safety audits
(
Klinect et al. 2003
)
.

Analogously to financial and safety audits, algorithmic auditing has recently emerged as a practice to tackle the novel risks posed by ML-enabled systems. This type of audit provides a mechanism to independently and systematically assess the design, development, and deployment of these systems, ensuring they align with established standards and societal values concerning, for example, fairness, bias, and accountability
(
Metaxa et al. 2021
)
.
Mökander et al.
(
Mökander 2023
)
identify two main drivers for the need for AI auditing, i.e., they serve as (i) a mechanism to implement legislation and (ii) a governance mechanism for private corporations. The first driver is a top-down pressure, as more and more regulations are being released, such as the EU AI Act
(
Future of Life Institute 2025
)
or Canada’s Directive on Automated Decision-Making
(
Secretariat 2024
)
, just to name a few. The latter is, instead, a bottom-up pressure, as organizations that design and deploy autonomous and self-learning technologies have incentives to implement and maintain appropriate governance mechanisms, for example, by subjecting themselves to independent audits, they can assess and improve their software development process and quality management system
(
Vlok 2003
)
.

Per definition, auditing requires a predefined baseline against which the audit’s subject can be evaluated, and depending on the audit’s purpose, this baseline can consist of either technical specifications, legal requirements, or voluntary ethics principles, and therefore three main categories of AI auditing exist: (i) technical, (ii) legal and (iii) ethical
(
Mökander 2023
)
.
Technical approaches include procedures designed to quantify and assess technical properties of ML-enabled systems, including accuracy, robustness, and safety, both before market deployment and after. These procedures include well-known software engineering practices, like model evaluation and system validation & verification
(
Brundage et al. 2020
)
. The term legal approaches, instead, refers to auditing procedures that assess whether the design and use of ML-enabled systems comply with relevant regulations. Lastly, the term ethical approaches refers to auditing procedures for which voluntary ethics principles serve as the normative baseline; for instance, ML-enabled systems may be audited against either a technology provider’s organizational values or ethics principles proposed by well-known institutions like IEEE
(
Chatila and Havens 2019
)
.

In practice, these three types of approaches have loose boundaries. For example, to demonstrate legal compliance, auditors typically rely on technical methods for gathering evidence about the properties and impact ML-enabled systems have
(
Kim 2017
)
. We place our contribution in this last scenario, where the verification of specific properties throughout the MLOps verification lifecycle can serve as a mechanism for legal compliance assessment.

2.3.
Zero-Knowledge Proofs

ZKPs provide a formal mechanism through which a
prover
can convince a
verifier
that a given statement is true, without revealing any information beyond the truth of the statement itself
(
Goldreich 2004
)
.

At the core of modern ZKP systems is the transformation of any arbitrary computations into
arithmetic circuits
defined over finite fields
(
MIT et al. 2019
)
. Any computable function can be rewritten as a sequence of additions and multiplications over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, where
p
is a large prime. The prover’s task is to demonstrate knowledge of a valid assignment to all the variables in the circuit, ensuring that all constraints hold. Formally, the prover proves the existence of a secret witness
w
that satisfies:

C(x,w)=y

where
C
denotes the arithmetic circuit,
x
represents public inputs,
w
is the private witness, and
y
is the public output of the computation. The ZKP convinces the verifier that there exists a secret
w
such that the circuit
C
satisfies
C(x,w)=y=\texttt{true}
, without revealing
w
.

ZKPs were first studied in the setting of
interactive proofs

(
MIT et al. 2019
)
, where the prover and verifier engage in a sequence of challenge-response rounds. These protocols guarantee that a cheating prover cannot convince an honest verifier of a false statement, except with negligible probability. A significant step towards removing interaction was the
Fiat-Shamir heuristic

(
Fiat and Shamir 1987
)
. This technique transforms certain interactive protocols into non-interactive variants by replacing the verifier’s random challenges with the output of a cryptographic hash function applied to the transcript. While widely used and practical, this transformation’s security is typically proven in the idealized Random Oracle Model
(
Bellare and Rogaway 1993
)
. Blum et al.
(
Univ. of Calif. et al. 2019
)
later gave a precise mathematical definition of
Non-Interactive Zero-Knowledge Proofs
(NIZKs) and showed how to build them with provable security guarantees in the standard cryptographic model, typically using a shared reference string that all parties can access. Both approaches result in a self-contained proof that can be verified without further interaction.

To enable efficient proof generation and verification, many systems encode the execution trace of the computation into a polynomial
P(x)
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
:

P(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}x^{i}

The prover commits to this polynomial using a
polynomial commitment scheme

(
Kate et al. 2010
)
, which ensures both
binding
(the committed polynomial cannot be altered later) and optionally
hiding
(its content remains secret). The verifier can then check whether the polynomial satisfies the required properties by querying a few evaluations at selected points. This drastically reduces the size of the proof and the cost of verification, achieving the property of
succinctness
.

A key challenge in applying ZKPs to domains such as ML is handling
non-linear functions
, which are not naturally supported in arithmetic circuits. Neural networks, for example, often include non-linear activation functions like the Rectified Linear Unit (
ReLU(x)=\max(0,x)
)
(
Arora et al. 2018
)
.
To represent such operations in ZKP-friendly form, systems typically use
lookup arguments

(
Kang et al. 2022
)
. In a lookup argument, the prover shows that each non-linear operation maps an input to an output according to a precomputed table
T
:

\exists(x,y)\in T\quad\text{such that}\quad y=f(x)

This allows incorporating non-polynomial logic into ZKPs while preserving succinctness and zero-knowledge. The table
T
encodes valid input-output pairs for the non-linear function, and the verifier only checks that the prover’s values appear in the table.

3.
Related Work

The literature has already investigated the applications of ZKP techniques to ML (ZKML). More specifically, the objective is to employ ZKPs in ML to prove that certain computations or operations within an ML system, such as inference, training, or data preprocessing, have been carried out correctly without disclosing the underlying data, model parameters, or internal workings
(
Xing et al. 2025
;
Scaramuzza et al. 2025
)
.
In particular, the current literature places strong emphasis on inference verification, which is the most extensively explored subfield of ZKML. Notable works include ZEN
(
Feng et al. 2021
)
, vCNN
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
,zkCNN
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
, and
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
. In more recent years, the research community has also focused on other aspects, such as training
(
Abbaszadeh et al. 2024
)
and online metrics verification
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, to name just a few.
The literature also exhibits another trend, concentrating on use cases related to trustworthy AI
(
Scaramuzza et al. 2025
)
, such as ZKAudit
(
Waiwitlikhit et al. 2024
)
and FaaS
(
Toreini et al. 2024
)
. A more in-depth exploration of these approaches can be found in Figure
4
and Table
1

To the best of found knowledge, the existing literature has not addressed the practical application of ZKPs for auditing ML-enabled systems within the MLOps paradigm.
However, a thorough search of the relevant literature yielded two related contributions.

Waiwitlikhit et al.
(
Waiwitlikhit et al. 2024
)
propose
ZKAudit
, a zero-knowledge audit framework enabling trustless verification of model training and data properties without revealing model weights or training data. The system consists of two main phases:
ZKAudit-T
, which proves that a model was trained via stochastic gradient descent on a committed dataset, and
ZKAudit-I
, which allows auditing arbitrary properties over the hidden data and weights through user-defined functions. The framework leverages ZK-SNARKs over AIRs, using the Halo2
(
Electric Coin Company 2020
)
backend with optimizations such as rounded division, variable fixed-point precision, and softmax implementation in finite fields. The framework supports real-world models like MobileNet v2 and DLRM-style recommenders and allows audits such as censorship detection, copyright verification, and counterfactual analysis.
Scaramuzza et al.
(
Scaramuzza et al. 2025
)
first survey the existing literature in ZKP protocols and identify five key properties (non-interactivity, transparent setup, standard representations, succinctness, and post-quantum security) critical for their application in ML validation and verification pipelines. Subsequently, they perform a follow-up systematic survey analyzing ZKP-enhanced ML applications across an adaptation of the Microsoft Machine Learning Workflow model (Data & Preprocessing, Training & Offline Metrics, Inference, and Online Metrics), detailing verification objectives, ML models, and adopted protocols. This contribution, although relevant, misses a practical implementation or any guidelines for implementing such verification systems.

Our approach is grounded on the following
research question
:

RQ:

How can Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs) be operationalized to systematically audit the Machine Learning Operations (MLOps) lifecycle?

To address this question, we propose the ZKMLOps framework, the development of which is structured in four steps. These steps are depicted in Figure
2
. First, we survey practical implementations of ZKP in ML contexts, such as SNARKjs, ezkl, and GKR, to ground our work in available code and concrete cryptographic backends. Second, we analyze representative use cases that highlight real-world needs for such proofs and, most importantly, define the main steps required for verification. These use cases include verifying inference results for proprietary neural networks and auditing datasets for fairness or bias without exposing sensitive data. Building on these first two steps, our main contribution is the design of a modular system architecture that generalizes these code examples into a flexible auditing framework. This architecture employs well-established patterns (e.g., hexagonal architecture, orchestrated saga) to support a variety of ZKP protocols and use cases across the MLOps lifecycle. Finally, we evaluate the framework’s practicality by showcasing a real-world use case and its feasibility by studying the performance of inference verification on different ML models using three different ZKP protocols.

However, the literature may lack a specific method for implementation for a specific audit purpose. If this is the case, the IT Solution Architect needs to consider alternative approaches. The
3

Protocol Selection
, either way, must try to maximize the number of properties that a ZKP protocol should have to be suitable for an ML application. These properties are (i) non-interactivity, (ii) transparent setup, (iii) standard representations, (iv) succinctness, and (v) post-quantum security
(
Scaramuzza et al. 2025
)
. Non-interactive protocols compress proof generation into a single exchange, where the prover submits a self-contained proof that any verifier
can check independently
(
Canetti et al. 2019
)
. This significantly reduces overhead in asynchronous contexts, like auditing scenarios. The setup phase, in a ZKP protocol, refers to the preliminary step in which cryptographic parameters are generated before any proving or verification can occur. In a transparent setup, such parameters are derived solely from publicly available verifiable sources of randomness
(
Sheybani et al. 2025
)
. This is particularly appealing for applications requiring strong auditability and long-term trust guarantees. Zero-knowledge protocols do not operate directly on high-level programs or models; instead, they require computations to be transformed into formal representations that are compatible with their internal proof systems
(
Ernstberger et al. 2024
)
. Having standard and flexible representations is crucial for enabling broader toolchain compatibility, developer accessibility, and seamless integration of ML models into various proof systems. Succinctness is a foundational property of many modern zero-knowledge protocols, particularly those intended for use in bandwidth-limited or resource-constrained environments. A protocol is considered succinct if the size of the proof and the time required for its verification scale only polynomially with the size of the input and output, independent of the complexity of the computation being proven
(
Goldreich and Oren 1994
)
. Lastly, whether a zero-knowledge protocol is considered post-quantum secure depends entirely on the primitives it employs. In general, protocols built solely on collision-resistant hash functions (CRHF
(
Berman et al. 2018
)
) are believed to be more resilient in a quantum context, since no quantum algorithm is currently known to break CRHFs faster than brute force.

After the elicitation of the system requirements and the choice of the ZKP protocol, the IT Solution Architect can now produce the final output of this process. We call this document the
4

ZKP Traceability Specification
. This document contains all the discussed information, namely (i) The
Purpose of the Audit
, the (ii)
Decision Trace
that led to the choice of the specific protocol, consisting of the MLOps verification lifecycle phase and the deployed model, and (iii) the
Selected Protocol
.
As the name suggests, the storage of this protocol must allow the traceability of such a decision. The ZKP Traceability Specification falls into the definition of an
Architecture Decision Record

(
Thoughtworks 2016
)
, and can be stored—following the guidelines from Richards et al.
(
Richards and Ford 2025
)
—in an
ADR Storage
, i.e., a wiki or a shared directory on a shared file server that can be accessed easily with a wiki or other document rendering software from the other actors involved in the process.

5.2.
Trustworthiness-as-a-Service

In this section, we present the building blocks of the application-oriented dimension of the framework. This dimension, named
Trustworthiness as a Service
, represents the implementation of a service-oriented architecture that enables the systematic auditing of trustworthy ML-enabled systems by integrating arbitrary ZKP protocols in its workflow throughout the whole MLOps verification lifecycle.

The architectural pattern employed is the
hexagonal architecture

5

, also known as
onion
or clean architecture
(
Martin et al. 2018
)
. This architectural pattern is designed to enhance the flexibility and maintainability of the codebase by ensuring the core business logic remains independent of external technology stacks, e.g., the diverse ZKP implementations and repositories. The operational logic, on the other hand, employs a
Saga Pattern

(
Richardson 2018
)
, where each audit workflow undergoes decomposition into smaller procedural steps within an
internal state machine
, across which an orchestrator manages the program execution with a
State Pattern

(
Martin et al. 2018
)
.

According to the hexagonal architecture, the system is divided into three main components:
core
,
ports
, and
adapters

(
Martin et al. 2018
)
. As one moves toward the core, the abstraction level increases. Specifically, the inner layer contains higher-level policies and broad business rules, while the outermost layers handle concrete implementation details. The core represents the most general and highest level of abstraction within the system. A detailed visualization of the hexagonal architecture is shown in Figure
5
. Adapters are implementations of ports, and are managed employing a
Dependency Injection Pattern

(
Seemann 2012
)
, where adapters are “injected” at runtime if needed in the specific audit workflow.
The latter is defined through a configuration file and ingested through the
Config
port. In the Figure
5
, two scenarios are shown: neural network inference correctness verification and bias verification over a proprietary dataset. These configuration files are derived from the ZKP Traceability Specifications and contain the scripts needed to perform every step of the verification and the related input and output artifacts.

The inner hexagon, representing the

:
setup
,

,

, and
verification
. This is achieved by implementing the orchestrator with an
Orchestrated Saga Pattern

(
Richardson 2018
)
and a
State Pattern

(
Gamma et al. 1994
)
, where for every step, a set of pre and post conditions are checked, to verify whether the input and output artifacts have been correctly uploaded or generated in the artifact storage

and that the steps are executed in the correct order. In this context, every running audit workflow is represented by an
Audit

Saga

While the implementations and use cases vary considerably, the four steps are the foundational common components in every non-interactive ZKP protocol. Therefore, we abstracted these steps within the business logic, and we made the key-exchange step explicit: despite its conventional inclusion in the setup phase, we believe this separation is more suitable within a service-oriented context, in light of the asynchronous nature of the auditing workflow. Each of these steps is defined by a set of scripts, and each of them ingests and produces artifacts. The scripts and the pre- and post-conditions are defined in the above-mentioned configuration file.

The outer hexagon, representing the

dependencies
, implements an Anti-Corruption Layer against external dependencies
(
Martin et al. 2018
)
where the
Ports and Adapters Pattern
is employed.
More specifically, the system defines one inbound port and three outbound ports. The inbound port,
Router
, allows the business logic to be invoked by a set of REST APIs. This interface is implemented as dedicated adapters for every business logic, i.e., artifacts management and audit management. The outbound ports are the
Interpreter
,
Config
, and
Storage
. The first is the key component of the framework, as it supports the execution of diverse protocols implemented as scripts
7

across varying technology stacks. This is achieved thanks to the
ScriptExecutor
service
(
Senthilvel et al. 2017
)
, which directly runs the scripts and is managed directly from the orchestrator with the
Visitor Pattern

(
Martin et al. 2018
)
and
Strategy Pattern

(
Martin et al. 2018
)
. The other two ports act as interfaces with the storage of the configuration files and the artifacts through a
Repository Pattern

(
Senthilvel et al. 2017
)
.

8.
Discussion

We answer the
RQ
in Section
4
by proposing ZKMLOps. The framework operationalizes ZKPs by first offering a systematic process for selecting an appropriate ZKP protocol based on the audit’s purpose, the specific MLOps stage, and the artifact in question, e.g., a model, a dataset, or any arbitrary component of the AI-enabled system (see Section
5.1
). Second, it provides a concrete, service-oriented architecture that transforms the complex, protocol-specific steps of generating and verifying proofs into a modular, repeatable, and maintainable engineering process (see Section
5.2
and Section
5.3
).

Our approach resolves the fundamental conflict between the need for transparency in auditing and the need for confidentiality in highly-regulated environments. The ZKMLOps framework changes the classical auditing dynamic by enabling verifiable, cryptographic proof of compliance without disclosing the underlying assets. This paradigm allows a financial institution, for example, to prove to an auditor that its deployed credit risk model is the one that was approved, without revealing the model’s parameters (see Section
6
). From a societal perspective, this enables accountability and trust in AI systems by making auditing practical even for opaque "black box" models.

Our empirical evaluation demonstrates the practical feasibility of integrating ZKP-based verification into the MLOps lifecycle for a range of standard ML models. The results, presented in Table
2
through Table
5
, do not point to a single superior ZKP protocol. Instead, they highlight a set of trade-offs between proving time, verification time, and proof size with respect to the different models tested. The existence of these distinct performance profiles provides developers the flexibility to select a protocol that aligns with the specific technical and business constraints of their auditing application, confirming the viability of the ZKMLOps approach. Some ZK frameworks, such as
ezkl

(
EKZL 2023
)
, already leverage GPUs to accelerate both proving and verification, making ZK auditing feasible for larger models.

The most important finding of our work from a Software Engineering perspectives, is how ZKP primitives can be integrated into existing engineering and organizational processes, like MLOps. We show how ingesting selected artifacts, generated in crucial phases of the MLOps lifecycle, within our framework can enable an additional degree of governance, like auditing and accountability.
The development of the ZKMLOps framework was guided by several key design principles and software engineering patterns. The key principle was the decoupling of core business logic from external dependencies, which is essential for building a flexible, maintainable, and extensible system. Among the adopted patterns, the four most relevant were: the
Hexagonal Architecture
, the
Orchestrated Saga Pattern
, the
State Pattern
, and the
Strategy Pattern
. The relationship between these patterns is hierarchical and collaborative. The
Hexagonal Architecture
provides the macro-level system structure. Inside its core, the
Saga Pattern
orchestrates the business process. The
State Pattern
defines the steps of that process, and the
Strategy Pattern
provides the concrete, interchangeable behaviors for each script execution, making the entire framework modular and adaptable to future requirements.

9.
Threats to Validity and Limitations

Following the guidelines from Feldt et al.
(
Feldt and Magazinius 2010
)
on the threats to validity for empirical software engineering, we selected four out of the seven threats proposed that better describe the limitations of the proposed work, namely:
external validity
,
conclusion validity
,
internal validity
, and
construct validity
.

External Validity
.
We demonstrate the framework’s effectiveness through an illustrative use case in a domain with unique regulatory constraints and data sensitivities. The effectiveness of the ZKMLOps architecture may not directly transfer to other regulated fields, such as healthcare or autonomous systems, which present different compliance requirements and operational demands. We mitigated this threat through deliberate architectural choices. The adoption of well-known architectural patterns is intended to decouple the core auditing logic from domain-specific implementations, thereby enabling a high degree of flexibility.

Conclusions Validity
.
The analysis focused exclusively on the inference verification stage, omitting other computationally significant phases like training or data preprocessing. Furthermore, the evaluation employed relatively small machine learning models, which may not reflect the performance characteristics of larger, more complex architectures prevalent in industry.
We addressed this threat by framing the evaluation’s purpose around demonstrating practical feasibility rather than optimizing for absolute speed. The key takeaway is that integrating ZKP verification into a structured MLOps pipeline is achievable for standard models, establishing a baseline for viability. This is particularly relevant in many compliance-driven auditing contexts, where the verification process can be performed asynchronously, prioritizing integrity over real-time speed.

Internal Validity.

The observed performance metrics could reflect the maturity and optimization level of the underlying cryptographic libraries rather than the inherent theoretical efficiency of the protocols themselves. We addressed this threat by executing all benchmarks within a strictly standardized experimental environment, ensuring that hardware and operating system variables remained constant across all tests. Moreover, by averaging results over multiple random initializations, we minimized the impact of transient system noise, thereby providing a robust comparison of the practical performance of the available tools rather than a purely theoretical one.

Construct Validity
.
The theoretical construct of trustworthiness includes multiple attributes, whereas our evaluation is confined to verifying the computational correctness of model inference, a specific aspect of auditing. We mitigate this threat by deliberately and transparently scoping our contribution. After introducing the wider context, the paper focuses specifically on accountability and auditing as a mechanism for legal compliance, rather than claiming to address all dimensions of trustworthiness. Furthermore, the proposed framework is architecturally designed for extensibility, allowing for the future integration of modules that could verify other constructs like fairness or data privacy.

10.
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we introduced ZKMLOps, a novel framework for the systematic auditing of Machine Learning systems. Our work addresses the regulations on ML-enabled systems by focusing on accountability, from which the concepts of auditability and traceability are derived. We demonstrated that Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs) provide the technical foundation to resolve the fundamental conflict between the need for transparency in auditing and the confidentiality required in competitive and regulated environments.
ZKPs give verifiable cryptographic proof of compliance while protecting proprietary models and sensitive data, by convincing a verifier (e.g., an auditing company) that a computational statement is true without revealing information beyond the statement’s validity.

The central contribution of the ZKMLOps framework is to operationalize these advanced cryptographic tools within the MLOps lifecycle. The theoretical availability of ZKP primitives does not address the practical challenge of their systematic application. Our framework bridges this gap by integrating ZKPs with established software engineering patterns, decoupling the core auditing logic from the diverse implementations of ZKP protocols. This transforms a complex cryptographic procedure into a modular, repeatable, and maintainable engineering process. We demonstrated the framework’s practicality by presenting a use case for regulatory compliance in financial risk auditing. Furthermore, we assessed its feasibility through an empirical evaluation of various ZKP protocols, analyzing their performance trade-offs when applied to ML models of increasing complexity. This work thereby establishes a baseline for integrating advanced cryptographic techniques into real-world auditing workflows.

Future work shall proceed in two primary directions. First, we plan to validate the framework in real-world settings through industry case studies to assess its performance and scalability. Second, we shall extend its capabilities by implementing auditing workflows for other trustworthy AI properties, such as verifying dataset fairness or model robustness against adversarial attacks.
