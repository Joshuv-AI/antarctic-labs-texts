Organoid models for T cell-based immunotherapy in hepatobiliary cancers
Authors: Liwei Du, Jiaxun Dong, Shunda Du
Published: 2025

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) are aggressive malignancies associated with poor prognosis (1,2), primarily due to their high heterogeneity, complex immune microenvironment, and challenges in immunotherapy application. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy, and adoptive T cell transfer have demonstrated potential in other cancers, their success in liver cancers remains limited (3,4). The use of organoids, which are three-dimensional (3D) cultures derived from patient tumor tissues, provides novel insights into the interactions between T cells and the hepatobiliary tumor microenvironment. This article examines the application of hepatobiliary tumor organoids in immunotherapy, with particular focus on T cell-based therapies, and discusses the potential of these models to improve therapeutic outcomes.

Organoids are 3D in vitro models derived from stem cells or patient tumor samples that replicate the structure and cellular composition of human tissues (5). Tumor organoids specifically retain the heterogeneity and microenvironmental interactions characteristic of primary tumors, making them valuable tools for cancer research (6). Hepatobiliary tumor organoids generated from primary HCC or iCCA tissues preserve critical features often lost in conventional 2D cultures, including stromal compartments, vascularization, and immune cell interactions (7). These models effectively mimic the complexity of liver cancer, encompassing immune evasion mechanisms and the dynamic interactions between tumor cells and their surrounding microenvironment (8).

T cell-based immunotherapies, including ICIs, adoptive T cell therapy, and CAR-T therapy, have achieved significant progress in treating various malignancies (9). However, their efficacy against hepatobiliary cancers, particularly HCC and iCCA, remains suboptimal (10). A major obstacle is the immunosuppressive liver microenvironment, characterized by the presence of regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and a high proportion of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitors such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab have shown limited clinical efficacy in HCC, with only a subset of patients responding to treatment. Conversely, CAR-T cell therapies targeting tumor antigens such as glypican-3 (GPC3) and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) have shown promise but face limitations due to the difficulty of generating effective T cell responses within the immunosuppressive liver milieu (11).

Organoids provide an excellent platform for investigating T cell interactions in hepatobiliary cancers. These 3D models enable the study of immune responses in an environment that closely resembles the in vivo tumor microenvironment (12). Researchers have successfully utilized tumor organoids to evaluate the efficacy of both ICIs and adoptive T cell therapies (13). In one investigation, organoids derived from HCC patients were employed to assess the effect of anti-PD-1 treatment on T cell activation and tumor cell death. Results demonstrated that although T cells infiltrated the organoids, the presence of immunosuppressive cells such as TAMs diminished the therapeutic response. For example, Dijkstra et al. established a co-culture system combining patient-derived tumor organoids with autologous peripheral blood lymphocytes, which enabled the expansion and functional assessment of tumor-reactive T cells. Their work demonstrated that organoid-based assays can effectively model T cell-tumor interactions and serve as a platform to test adoptive T cell therapies ex vivo (14).

Furthermore, liver cancer organoids have been utilized to test CAR-T cell therapies targeting antigens such as GPC3, which is overexpressed in many HCC cases (15). These findings emphasize the importance of optimizing the tumor microenvironment within organoid models to better simulate the challenges encountered in clinical settings.

Organoids have also been employed to explore combination therapies involving T cells. For instance, a recent study combined PD-1/PD-L1 blockade with CAR-T cell therapy in HCC organoids. This combination enhanced T cell infiltration and tumor cell cytotoxicity, suggesting that such strategic combinations may improve outcomes for patients resistant to monotherapy (16). Additionally, combining ICIs with chemotherapy or targeted therapy in organoid models has provided insights into how multiple treatment modalities might synergistically enhance T cell-mediated tumor control.

Despite the considerable potential of organoid models in immunotherapy research, several challenges persist. A significant limitation involves the difficulty in generating reproducible, high-quality organoid cultures from patient tumors (17). Organoid growth can be variable, and the lack of standardized protocols complicates comparison of results across different studies (18). Moreover, although tumor organoids capture many aspects of the tumor microenvironment, they still lack certain elements present in vivo, such as complete vascularization and complex immune cell networks. Advances in organ-on-a-chip technology that integrate multiple cell types may help address these limitations and create more physiologically relevant models (19).

Another challenge concerns the limited understanding of immune resistance mechanisms in these models. While many organoid studies focus on immune cell infiltration and the direct effects of T cell therapies on tumor cells, the roles of other immune cell populations, including Tregs and myeloid cells, in immune escape remain insufficiently explored.

The ultimate objective of hepatobiliary cancer organoid research is to facilitate personalized treatment strategies (20). Patient-derived organoids can serve as a platform for predicting individual responses to various T cell-based therapies, enabling clinicians to tailor treatments according to specific molecular and immune characteristics of the tumor (21). This approach could significantly improve the therapeutic index while minimizing risks associated with ineffective treatments.
