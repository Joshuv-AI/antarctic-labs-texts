Pharmacodynamics of 2 dosages of orally administered esomeprazole in client‐owned, healthy dogs: A prospective, crossover study
Authors: Abby Ostronic, Christine Gremillion, Song Zhang, Joerg M Steiner, M Katherine Tolbert, Emily N Gould
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsomeprazole use is increasing in dogs, but the gastrointestinal adverse events associated with q12h dosing necessitate pharmacodynamic evaluation of a reduced dose and frequency of administration.ObjectivesTo compare the efficacy of 2 doses of (q24h) esomeprazole in raising intragastric pH in dogs.AnimalsNine healthy, client‐owned dogs, >20 kg.MethodsProspective, randomized, double blinded, crossover study. Esomeprazole (0.5 or 1 mg/kg q24h) was orally administered for up to 5 days per treatment arm, and the mean percentage time intragastric pH was ≥3 (MPT3) and ≥4 (MPT4) for 24 hours periods were compared to pretreatment pH using a continuous pH monitoring system. INTRODUCTION

Administration of a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) is considered standard of care for treatment of gastrointestinal (GI) ulceration, bleeding, and gastroesophageal reflux disorder (GERD) in dogs and cats. 1 In people, a gastric pH ≥3 and ≥4 for a mean percentage time (MPT) of 75% and 67% of the day, respectively, are the pharmacodynamic target goals for effective healing of duodenal ulceration and esophagitis. 2 , 3 These goals are similarly used as benchmarks for acid suppression in dogs. Omeprazole is a widely used PPI, and is comprised of 2 isomers: S‐omeprazole and R‐omeprazole. Esomeprazole, the optical S‐isomer of omeprazole, provides superior acid suppression when administered via both oral and injectable routes in some veterinary 4 , 5 and human populations, 6 , 7 , 8 which might result in greater frequency of administration for dogs and cats. However, esomeprazole prescription is more costly, and as seen with other PPIs, is associated with development of adverse events (AEs). Adverse events that might develop after PPI administration include vomiting, diarrhea, and dysorexia. Esomeprazole at twice daily dosing might be cost‐prohibitive for some owners, especially larger dogs (>20 kg body weight) and can also be associated with GI AEs associated with twice daily administration of a 1 mg/kg dose. 4

Two studies have investigated effectiveness of lower dosage esomeprazole in colony beagles. 9 , 10 While these findings are promising for potential dose or frequency reduction of esomeprazole in clinical practice, all data was from colony dogs with similar epigenetics and the same controlled environment. Thus, studies are lacking looking at a heterogeneous group of larger breed dogs. Also of importance is that GI adverse events are still observed with once daily standard dosing. 9 , 11 The ideal dose and frequency of esomeprazole which simultaneously minimizes cost to the owner, reduces AEs, and achieves pharmacodynamic pH goals in dogs is currently unknown.

Our primary study aim was to evaluate the effect of oral administration of 2 dosages (0.5 vs 1 mg/kg q24h) of esomeprazole in raising intragastric pH in healthy, larger sized dogs. A secondary aim was to identify differences in GI AEs, if any, between doses. We hypothesized that both dosages of esomeprazole would achieve published pH goals for humans, and the lower dosage would result in fewer GI AEs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study animals

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Texas A&M University College of Veterinary Medicine and Biomedical Sciences (IACUC 2022‐0092). The number of dogs was selected based on a sample size calculation from a previous study evaluating the effect of once daily oral esomeprazole administration on intragastric pH in dogs. 10 To detect a 15% change in mean percentage time (MPT) pH is ≥4, using a SD of 7.76 and assuming a moderate correlation of 0.6 and an alpha of 0.05, 8 dogs were needed in each group to have an 85% power of finding significant differences over time, if they existed. One additional dog was enrolled into each group to account for potential study dropout.

All dogs were teaching hospital staff or student owned, and deemed healthy by history, physical examination, and minimum database (ie, complete blood count and serum biochemistry profile). Other inclusion criteria were ≥1 year of age, at least 20 kg in body weight, lack of historical clinical signs of acute or chronic gastrointestinal disease including vomiting, inappetence, and/or diarrhea. Dogs were excluded if receiving any chronic gastroprotectants (ie, acid suppressants, antacids, sucralfate), nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), or corticosteroids. Diet information was recorded for all dogs, and each continued to eat their standard type and amount of diet from start to finish of all treatment arms. Study incentives included either no cost lab work or routine monthly parasite preventatives in exchange for participation. All owners provided informed consent before enrollment of dogs.

Intragastric pH capsule placement

On day 1 (0‐24 hours), the morning meal was withheld, and an intravascular catheter was placed in the lateral saphenous vein. The dogs were sedated using butorphanol (0.2 mg/kg IV; Torbugesic 10 mg/mL injection; Fort Dodge Animal Health, Fort Dodge, Iowa), dexmedetomidine (3‐5 μg/kg IV; Dexdomitor 0.5 mg/mL injection; Orion Pharma, Espoo, Finland), and a subset of dogs received titrating doses of propofol if clinically indicated to facilitate final passage of the pH delivery device (Bravo pH calibration free capsules, Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota) into the gastric lumen (1‐3 mg/kg IV; PropoFlo 10 mg/mL injection; Zoetis, Kalamazoo, MI). One dog in each treatment arm was under general anesthesia for a routine dental procedure so capsule placement was performed during that anesthetic event. The pH capsules were placed within the stomach utilizing digital radiology for assistance. Proper intragastric capsule placement was confirmed by a single board‐certified radiologist (CGM). Intragastric pH capsules were adhered to the gastric mucosa as described previously. 4 Consistent placement location of each pH capsule was done by utilizing the markings on the capsule delivery device to measure the distance from the maxillary canine teeth to the area of capsule placement in the gastric fundus. 4 After the procedure, the sedation was reversed with an equal volume of atipamezole (0.03‐0.05 mg/kg IM; Antisedan 5 mg/mL injection; Orion Pharma, Espoo, Finland).

The same protocol for pH capsule placement was followed in the 2nd treatment arm for each individual dog at a later date. The minimum time between treatment arms was determined as sufficient for drug excretion based on previous esomeprazole PK studies in dogs, 9 , 10 as well as owner, investigator, and support service availability. Dogs were allocated to the treatment regime that they did not receive during the 1st arm, in a crossover fashion. For the subset of dogs which failed to achieve pharmacodynamic pH goals with both dosing regimens from arms 1 and 2, a 3rd treatment arm was then performed to evaluate if these animals had a different response to twice daily, 1 mg/kg dosing. Dogs that met this criteria (n = 3) had pH capsules placed under sedation and continuous pH monitoring performed as described above.

Study design

A prospective, randomized, double blinded, crossover study was performed. Following pH capsule placement on the morning of day 1, the 1st esomeprazole dose was withheld for 24 hours in order to obtain baseline intragastric pH data for that individual dog. Continuous intragastric pH monitoring was recorded throughout PPI administration, which began on day 2, until the capsule was naturally shed from the gastric mucosa into the feces. Dogs were randomized to a treatment schedule via block randomization by a pharmacist so that 5 dogs were randomized into each group for the 1st treatment arm. Esomeprazole 0.5 or 1 mg/kg (Esomeprazole; Teva Pharmaceuticals; Cipla LTD, Kanpur, Uttar Pradesh, India) capsules were compounded by the Texas A&M University College of Veterinary Medicine and Biomedical Sciences small animal pharmacy and administered by mouth (PO) by the dog's owner once daily (q24h) beginning 24 hours following capsule placement (start of day 1 of PPI) and for either up to 144 hours post capsule placement (day 5 of PPI), or until pH capsule detachment was confirmed. Capsules were administered to all dogs approximately 30 minutes before a meal. All dogs were crossed over to the dose they did not receive in arm 1 for treatment arm 2. All dogs were fed their normal diet at their normal interval (once or twice daily) throughout the duration of the study. This protocol was repeated for each treatment arm.

For quantification of secondary study aims in assessing for differences in GI AEs, a daily log was kept by owners that scored the dogs' appetite, fecal score and the presence of any vomiting, regurgitation or ptyalism during the study. Fecal consistency was graded from 1 to 7 on each day of each treatment period using the Purina Fecal Scoring System (Nestle Purina PetCare Company, St. Louis, MO). Diarrhea was defined as a fecal score >4 and the presence of blood within the diarrhea (hematochezia) was recorded when present. Adverse clinical signs, including changes in appetite (characterized by consuming <75%, <50%, or <25% of their meal), presence of vomiting, regurgitation, or ptyalism were recorded during the treatment period. When it occurred, vomitus was evaluated for the presence of medication or the pH capsule by the owner. A minimum of 1 and maximum of 3 months separated treatment arms for all dogs. Investigators and owners were blinded to the treatment group of each arm until all data collection and statistical analyses were completed.

For dogs enrolled in the 3rd treatment arm (n = 3), owners were again instructed to not begin esomeprazole for 24 hours post capsule placement for baseline pH readings. Esomeprazole was orally administered at 1 mg/kg q12h for these dogs.

Intragastric pH monitoring

Intragastric pH was recorded continuously at 6‐second sampling intervals for at least 4 days after capsule placement starting on day 1 and continuing through day 4. The pH capsule naturally detaches from the gastric mucosa within 3 to 6 days after placement. A corresponding recorder remained with the dog throughout the observation period and transferred telemetric data from the capsule continuously. The receiver was worn in a vest and maintained within proximity to the dog (within 1 m) for the entire observation period. Following capsule passage, pH data were uploaded to a computer software system (Reflux Software 6.1, Medtronic). If the pH capsule remained in the stomach longer than day 4, a new receiver was paired with the capsule for the duration until it detached from the stomach. The passage of a capsule from the stomach was defined as a sudden and sustained rise of pH >4.

Statistical analysis

Multivariate linear mixed effects models were constructed to assess differences in MPT intragastric pH ≥3 and MPT intragastric pH ≥4 between treatments across time during days 2 to 4 of treatment. For MPT intragastric pH ≥3, the measurements on days 0, 1 and 2 of PPI therapy were used as longitudinal outcomes for the mixed‐effect model. The fixed effects include treatment, phase of crossover, and time points. Dog was included as a random effect to account for multiple measurements from the same animal. Residual diagnostics were conducted (based on marginal and conditional residuals) and the likelihood ratio test was used to assess the covariance model. Because this is a crossover randomized trial where each dog served as its own control and the order of treatments was randomized, potential confounders such as diet were not considered. Similar analysis was performed for MPT intragastric pH ≥4.

Descriptive analyses were conducted to summarize adverse events (ie, fecal score, vomiting, appetite, regurgitation, and ptyalism). Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Because of the exploratory nature of this study, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed. Statistical analyses were performed using a commercial software package (SAS statistical software, version 9.4, Cary, North Carolina). Figures were created using a commercially available software package (GraphPad Prism 9.5.0).

RESULTS

Study animals

Nine healthy, adult, medium to large breed dogs all belonging to hospital staff were enrolled into the study (5 spayed females, 4 castrated male), all greater than 1 year of age (median 4.9 years; range, 1‐8.5 years) and weighing at least 20 kg (median 26.5 kg; range, 20.1‐33.8 kg). The median body condition score (BCS) of all dogs was 4/9, with a range of 3‐6/9. The following breeds were represented: cattle dog cross (n = 1), Golden retriever (n = 2), hound dog cross (n = 2), Labrador retriever (n = 2), and mixed breed dog (n = 2).

On the day of study enrollment for the twice daily treatment arm, 1 dog (n = 1/3) was newly diagnosed with both a cutaneous mast cell tumor and hypothyroidism based on clinical (ie, weight gain in the face of static daily caloric intake, lethargy), clinicopathologic (ie, hypercholesterolemia, and mild, nonregenerative, normocytic, normochromic, anemia) and endocrine (ie, markedly low total T4 [<0.5 μg/dL; reference interval 1.7‐3.6 μg/dL] and elevated thyroid stimulating hormone [1.86 ng/mL; reference interval 0.05‐0.42 ng/mL]) abnormalities that had developed since the 1st 2 treatment arms.

pH capsule placement

A total of 28 capsules were deployed during the study, with a minimum of 1 and maximum of 3 months in between treatment arms 1 and 2 (the q24h doses). Three dogs do did not achieve the target increases in gastric pH with either of the q24h treatment arms were assessed at a dose of 1 mg/kg q12h 3 to 5 months after completion of the once daily arms. Eight capsules failed and required immediate redeployment (n = 3 in arm 1; n = 2 in arm 2; n = 3 treatment arm 3). Failure of capsule deployment was attributed to inadequate vacuum suction secondary to tubing (n = 6) or patient recumbency (n = 2). A total of 21 capsules were deployed successfully into the gastric mucosa during the study. Twenty of 21 capsules remained in place for the entirety of the 96 hours study period. One capsule detached and was found in the vomitus of a patient on day 4 of the study. For the dog in which the capsule passed before the full 4‐day (96 hours) study period, data beyond 72 hours was excluded from final analyses. During 1 dog's standard treatment arm, the pH capsule detached and exited the stomach on day 3. Therefore, only 48 hours of data was acquired for this dog.

Intragastric pH recordings

Mean intragastric pH, and MPT intragastric pH ≥3 and 4 for all dogs for both treatments arms are depicted in Figure 1. Table S1 provides data from the linear regression analysis. The mixed‐effect models for MPT pH ≥3 and ≥4 both show a significant increase on days 1 and 2 of PPI therapy. The effects of phase and treatment were not statistically significant. While both once‐daily esomeprazole doses significantly increased the MPT intragastric pH ≥3 and ≥4 on days 1 and 2 of esomeprazole administration compared to pretreatment (baseline) pH (P = .0009 and P = .0005, respectively) based on the linear mixed‐effects model, only 6 dogs (66%, [0.36, 0.97]) achieved the MPT efficacy pH goals established for humans for at least 1 of the q24h doses (ie, n = 4 and n = 6 dogs achieved both MPT goals for the 0.5 mg/kg and 1 mg/kg doses, respectively). It took approximately 2 to 3 days for dogs who responded to treatment to achieve the MPT pH goals following initiation of the PPI. The intragastric pH of 3 dogs (33%, [0.03, 0.64]) failed to reach pH goals for humans with both doses of PPI. However, when receiving 1 mg/kg PO q12h in the 3rd treatment arm, the pH goals was achieved in all 3 dogs (see Figure 2).

Adverse events

Baseline scoring of AEs was established for all dogs before treatment administration. All treatments were generally well tolerated. The total number of vomiting episodes during any treatment period was 1 (n = 1 receiving 1 mg/kg q24h). This dog was excluded because of vomition of the capsule on the 1st day of treatment, which occurred within a few hours of drug administration. Five dogs had a decrease in appetite (n = 4, 50% reduction; n = 1, 25% reduction) following capsule placement but before PPI treatment. Four dogs only experienced this reduction for 1 day, whereas 1 dog had 50% reduction for 2 consecutive days. In 4 dogs, hyporexia occurred during esomeprazole administration (n = 3, receiving 1 mg/kg q24h; n = 1, receiving 0.5 mg/kg q24h). Figure 3 depicts fecal scores for all dogs during both treatment arms. There were 12 episodes of fecal scores ≥5 Among 6 dogs (7 episodes in 3 dogs receiving 1 mg/kg q24h, 1 episode in 1 dog the day of sedation and capsule placement, and 4 episodes in 2 dogs receiving 1 mg/kg q12h). Two dogs in the 1 mg/kg q24h group developed severe diarrhea (fecal score 6‐7, 1 with hematochezia) on days 3 to 4 that did not occur with the 0.5 mg/kg q24h dose. Two of the 3 dogs who failed to achieve goals with q24h dosing developed diarrhea with the q12h dosing, and the other dog, who had not developed a change in fecal score for the 1 mg/kg/day initial treatment, developed no adverse events with a 2 mg/kg/day dose. No appreciable differences were identified in appetite or fecal score between treatments.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we compared the efficacy of 2 doses of once daily oral esomeprazole in raising the intragastric pH of healthy medium to large breed dogs. We also assessed for differences in the incidence of AEs between groups. While both once daily PPI doses increased intragastric pH compared to baseline, 33% of dogs failed to reach the guidelines established in human medicine even with a 1 mg/kg dose. Moreover, a large amount of intersubject variation existed with the effect of esomeprazole on intragastric pH. This is clinically important, as more veterinarians might be prescribing esomeprazole at once daily dosing based on 2 previous studies that suggested that once daily dosing was effective in raising intragastric pH in colony beagles. 10

One of the first studies to look at once daily dosing in dogs concluded that oral once daily 1 mg/kg esomeprazole met the therapeutic criteria previously described for humans. 10 It was then established that for the same colony beagles, esomeprazole at 0.5 mg/kg IV q12h was sufficient to increase intragastric pH and pharmacodynamically did not differ from a 1 mg/kg IV q12h dose. 9 While both of these studies found an overall reduction in total daily dose efficacious for raising intragastric pH, the same group of colony housed, research beagles were used for both projects. For this reason, it is hard to extrapolate the results of this homogeneous group of dogs to a more heterogeneous population of client owned animals, which have an increased variability in breed, size, and environment.

The variation observed in individual dogs' pH during esomeprazole treatment might be related to inherent day to day fluctuations in timing of drug administration by the owners, where a single missed dose can significantly lower intragastric pH, 12 or differences in PPI metabolism (eg, cytochrome P450, other epigenetic variability) between dogs. Compared to a research cohort, other uncontrolled variables might include lack of standardization of diet type, administration of treats, breed, daily schedule variation, or housing differences between subjects. For the 3 dogs which failed to achieve MPT pH goals when oral esomeprazole was administered at 1 mg/kg/day, an increase to 1 mg/kg PO every 12 hours resulted in excellent gastric acid suppression on treatment days 2 to 4. Statistical analysis was not conducted on this data set because of the small sample size, but all subjects reached nearly 100% MPT at pH ≥3 or ≥4 by 72 hours of therapy. As 1 dog in this group developed clinical hypothyroidism in between the crossover arms and twice daily dosing, a theory for this dog's poor response to once daily dosing would be subclinical endocrine disease impacting esomeprazole absorption. Veterinarians should assume that there is likely a wide variability in intragastric pH elevation during PPI therapy in their canine patients. While the authors cannot conclude if 1 mg/kg PO q12h is superior to 0.5 mg/kg PO q12h, data from this study would imply that twice daily frequency of administration of a PPI is the most important variable to overcome interindividual variability. This conclusion is supported by work performed with oral omeprazole administration in dogs. 13 , 14 , 15

Significant differences in the MPT3 or MPT4 were not identified between the 0.5 and 1.0 mg/kg/day dose for any time point, confirming that the 0.5 mg/kg q24h was sufficient to raise baseline pH in this group of dogs, despite not fulfilling the human MPT pH goals for efficacy. Because published studies that confirm the optimal intragastric pH in cats and dogs for healing of GI ulceration, bleeding, and esophagitis are lacking, human guidelines have been used as surrogate gold standard measures of acid suppressant efficacy in veterinary medicine. Treatment of duodenal ulceration and GERD in people have aimed at increasing intragastric pH ≥3 for approximately 75% of the day and pH ≥4 for approximately 67% of the day for clinical resolution of ulceration or esophagitis. 2 , 3 Two veterinary case reports show that achievement of these goals resolved naturally occurring ulcers secondary to nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drug (NSAID) administration and neoplasia (gastrinoma) in a dog 12 and cat, 16 respectively. However, the ideal intragastric pH for healing of ulceration or bleeding in dogs might differ from humans, and more studies are necessary to confirm or refute this.

A secondary aim of this study was to look at differences in AEs between doses. Adverse GI effects such as vomiting and diarrhea are reported frequently in dogs after PPI administration. 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 17 No appreciable differences in AEs were identified between groups, with vomiting limited to 1 dog, and severe diarrhea and hyporexia occurring in 2 dogs. These AEs all occurred during the subjects' 1 mg/kg treatment arms, for both the once and twice daily dosing. While this study was likely underpowered to identify a difference for fecal score, more episodes of diarrhea were reported during the 1 mg/kg dosing when compared to the 0.5 mg/kg treatments. Thus, dogs who do not tolerate the 1 mg/kg/day dosage might better tolerate a dose reduction of PPI given at the same frequency.

There are several limitations to this study. Although a crossover design likely helped reduce intersubject variability for outcome measures, our sample size was limited to a small group of dogs and interindividual variability did exist. This is reflective of variability in pH data from different colony animals with the Bravo monitoring system. 14 , 17 We also omitted a placebo treatment arm as that would require 4 arms for these client owned subjects, and 24 hours of baseline pH has been used in similar studies as a surrogate. 9 , 10 Diet was not evaluated as a confounding variable here given the crossover nature of the study and that there were no changes to individual dogs' baseline diet throughout all treatment arms. It is unknown how specific diet differences between dogs might have impacted “responder” versus “nonresponder” phenotypes. All dogs were healthy, and these results are not necessarily reflective of the outcome of treatment for patients with acid‐related disorders. Because only 1 pH capsule was placed per treatment arm, duration of pH monitoring was dependent on the time of gastric mucosal shedding for each dog and varied between subjects. The previous study in beagles which found that once daily dosing did reach human pH goals assessed the dogs' pH on day 5 of esomeprazole administration. 10 It is possible that more time (>96 hours) might be needed to reach the maximal effect of both 0.5 mg/kg and 1 mg/kg/day doses, which was not assessed with this study as it would have required additional pH capsule placements per treatment arm. Lastly, it might be possible that a lower dose (0.5 mg/kg) twice daily could be equally as efficacious as standard dosing. A 1 mg/kg dose was selected for twice daily dosing instead of a 0.5 mg/kg dose given that if any of the dogs failed to reach human goals with the lower dose, we would not be able to perform a 4th treatment arm on these animals to verify if they would or would not have responded to the currently recommended dose for PPI administration according to consensus guidelines. 1 Because the twice daily arm only evaluated if 1 mg/kg twice daily dosing would raise the pH of those failing once daily therapy, it is unknown if these same dogs would have responded to a 0.5 mg/kg given twice daily as has been shown for omeprazole. 13

In conclusion, both 0.5 mg/kg and 1 mg/kg once daily esomeprazole significantly increased the mean percentage time intragastric pH was above 3 or 4 on days 2 and 3 when given orally to healthy, medium to large breed, client owned dogs. Five dogs each for the 0.5 and 1 mg/kg q24h dosing (63% and 56%, respectively) achieved the MPT3 goal by day 3, whereas 50% (n = 4/8) and 33% (n = 3/9) achieved the MPT4 by day 3 of therapy. Thirty‐three percent (n = 3/9) of these dogs failed to reach therapeutic pharmacodynamic guidelines of MPT >3 or >4 for at least 75% or 66% of the day, respectively, with q24h dosing. All 3 of these dogs did reach MPT pH goals with q12h dosing. Large interindividual variability in pH was evident between dogs. Although AEs were uncommon and our study population was small, GI AEs were not observed at the 0.5 mg/kg q24h dosage, whereas 3 dogs experience observable GI AEs at 1.0 mg/kg q24h.
