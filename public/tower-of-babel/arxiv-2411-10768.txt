Abstract
We introduce a framework for developing efficient and interpretable climate emulators (CEs) for economic models of climate change. The paper makes two main contributions. First, we propose a general framework for constructing carbon-cycle emulators (CCEs) for macroeconomic models. The framework is implemented as a generalized linear multi-reservoir (box) model that conserves key physical quantities and can be customized for specific applications. We consider three versions of the CCE, which we evaluate within a simple representative agent economic model: (i) a three-box setting comparable to DICE-2016, (ii) a four-box extension, and (iii) a four-box version that explicitly captures land-use change. While the three-box model reproduces benchmark results well and the fourth reservoir adds little, incorporating the impact of land-use change on the carbon storage capacity of the terrestrial biosphere substantially alters atmospheric carbon stocks, temperature trajectories, and the optimal mitigation path. Second, we investigate pattern-scaling techniques that transform global-mean temperature projections from CEs into spatially heterogeneous warming fields. We show how regional baseline climates, non-uniform warming, and the associated uncertainties propagate into economic damages. Keywords: Climate Change, Social Cost of Carbon, Carbon Taxes, Environmental Policy, Deep Learning, Integrated Assessment Models, DICE-2016 JEL classification: C61, E27, Q5, Q51, Q54, Q58

Motivation, Research Questions, and Key Contributions

There is a rapidly developing literature on the macroeconomics of climate change (see, e.g.,
Dietz, 2024
and
Fernández-Villaverde, Gillingham, and
Scheidegger, 2025

to explore the economic consequences of industrial carbon emissions.
These emulators are reduced‐form models that map emissions into atmospheric
\displaystyle\mathrm{CO}_{2}

Geoffroy et al., 2013b

Folini et al. (2024)
scrutinize the three‐box climate emulator embedded in DICE-2016
(
Nordhaus, 2017
)
and develop a calibration-validation protocol for that specific structure. Their contribution, however, stops short of asking whether the three-box architecture itself is adequate and how emulator uncertainty propagates when regional impacts are required.
In this paper, we address these two questions that are pivotal for contemporary models in climate economics.

(i)

Carbon-cycle resolution.
Does replacing the DICE-2016 three‐box representation with richer multi-reservoir models materially alter carbon stocks, temperatures, and optimal policies?

(ii)

Spatial downscaling.
How robust is standard pattern-scaling when the economic model demands grid‐level or regional climate inputs (cf.

;
Tebaldi and Arblaster, 2014
;
Lynch et al., 2017
)

To answer these questions, we introduce an open-source toolbox that lets economists design physically consistent linear box-model carbon-cycle emulators (CCEs), then auto-calibrate and rigorously test them.
The same framework links to pattern-scaling libraries, keeping emulator, pattern, and baseline uncertainties in a transparent manner.

Carbon Cycle

Integrated assessment models (IAMs) often employ a single linear rule that maps cumulative emissions to global temperature change (see, e.g.,
Dietz and Venmans, 2019
, and references therein).
This cumulative-emissions climate emulator is computationally attractive because it collapses climate dynamics into a single stock of cumulative
\displaystyle\mathrm{CO}_{2}
, but this parsimony masks four critical weaknesses: temperature change in response to changing atmospheric
\displaystyle\mathrm{CO}_{2}
is not instantaneous but takes about 10 years
(
Stjern et al., 2023
)
; forcings from non-
\displaystyle\mathrm{CO}_{2}
greenhouse gases or aerosols are relevant but are ignored
(
Jenkins et al., 2021
)
; the non-distinction between land and ocean carbon reservoirs prevents any study addressing their relative role in climate or economic terms; the assumed linear relation negates a priori the existence of any non-linearities as expected, for example, in the context of negative emissions or climate tipping points
(
Lenton et al., 2008
;
Zickfeld et al., 2021
)
.
Consequently, relying on this linear relationship can lead to misleading temperature projections and, as a direct consequence, flawed policy guidance.

A second strand of literature employs two separate modules to predict the link between emissions and temperature: the former maps emissions into atmospheric concentration by means of a CCE, and the latter maps concentrations to temperature.
The present paper focuses on the link between emissions and concentration. For this, there exist two alternative modeling frameworks. The literature adopts either multi-reservoir box models
(
Nordhaus, 2017
;
Dorheim et al., 2024
)
or impulse-response formulations (IRFs; see, e.g.,
Leach et al., 2021
;
Gasser et al., 2017
;
Meinshausen et al., 2011
). Comparison studies document the strengths and weaknesses of each family
(
Nicholls et al., 2021
;
Melnikova et al., 2023
)
. The two approaches can be shown to be formally equivalent when modeling the decay of a single pulse of carbon emitted to the atmosphere
(
Li, Jarvis, and Leedal, 2009
;
Raupach et al., 2011
;
Raupach, 2013
)
. Both approaches have to be calibrated against properly chosen benchmark data.

In this article, we focus on a box-model CCE, where the global system is partitioned into a handful of well-mixed ‘‘boxes” (reservoirs) between which carbon diffuses.

In a box model, the atmosphere, the ocean layers, the land biosphere, and occasionally additional reservoirs such as permafrost or the shallow ocean are treated as homogeneous pools connected by fluxes that obey mass balance, as illustrated in a stylized fashion in the “climate” bubble of Figure

We argue that the box-model formulation of the CCE is intuitive in its interpretation and adaptation to use-case-specific modifications, including accommodation of non-linearities
(
Hooss et al., 2001
;
Glotter et al., 2014
;
Zhang et al., 2021

. The box approach naturally captures feedbacks that arise because carbon emissions to the atmosphere perturb the equilibrium among the different carbon reservoirs, yet remains computationally light.

The canonical DICE-2016 emulator (
Nordhaus, 2017
and
Folini et al., 2024
), for instance, considers a three-reservoir carbon-cycle box model.
The model contains no explicit land-biosphere reservoir, even though terrestrial carbon stocks and biological carbon dioxide removal (CDR) dominate many low-cost mitigation pathways. Our central question in this paper is how modeling choices, such as how many carbon reservoirs to include, which data sets to fit, and which physical constraints to enforce, shape projected concentrations (and ultimately economic damages).

We present an open-source toolbox that lets economists design, calibrate, and deploy bespoke CCEs that are physically sound and policy-specific.
A key advantage of this modular approach is that it makes modeling choices, how many reservoirs to include, which data sets to fit, and which physical constraints to impose, both transparent and testable.

Three- and four-box emulators (3SR and 4PR), calibrated to both pre-industrial (PI) and present-day (PD) conditions, replicate the historical and long-run evolution of atmospheric
\displaystyle\mathrm{CO}_{2}

\displaystyle 5\%

To illustrate the extent to which the choice of a CCE, the associated calibration targets, and the selected hyperparameters can influence quantitative results, we evaluate the three carbon-cycle models within the standard DICE economy–climate framework under PI conditions and a range of extreme scenarios.
While 3SR and 4PR produce broadly similar policy-relevant trajectories, the 4PR-X variant, which permits the land biosphere to evolve dynamically under deforestation or urbanization, yields markedly higher atmospheric carbon stocks and temperatures: by 2100, atmospheric carbon is almost
\displaystyle 80\,\mathrm{GtC}
(about
\displaystyle 6\%

\displaystyle 0.2^{\circ}\mathrm{C}

\displaystyle\sim 6\%

2

These results underscore the importance of explicitly representing the land-biosphere reservoir when modeling land-use change. Deforestation and urbanization materially alter optimal carbon policy, highlighting the need for coordinated measures that address both industrial emissions and land management. Under an extreme warming scenario, the incremental impact of deforestation is comparable in magnitude, reinforcing its relevance. Overall, our framework provides a robust, interpretable basis for climate-economic analysis, overcoming the limitations of reduced-reservoir models and enabling the study of extreme cases.

Pattern Scaling

Pattern scaling
(
Santer et al., 1990
;
Tebaldi and Arblaster, 2014
;
Lynch et al., 2017
)
remains the workhorse for down-scaling global-mean projections to gridded fields, recently entering economic analyses of spatial damages and insurance
(
Krusell and Smith, 2022
;
Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg, 2024
;
Cruz and Rossi-Hansberg, 2024
;
Kotlikoff et al., 2024
)
.
Using the pattern library of
Lynch et al. (2017)
, our module turns the global mean temperature path from any emulator into a gridded warming map and, when absolute temperatures matter, anchors that map to an observation-based climatology such as ERA5.
4
4

4

ERA5 is the European Center for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) fifth-generation reanalysis, an hourly, global data set that merges observations with a modern forecast model; see
https://www.ecmwf.int/en/forecasts/dataset/ecmwf-reanalysis-v5
.

Because the module keeps the three main uncertainty sources, emulator calibration, ESM pattern choice, and observational baseline, explicit and separate, analysts can see immediately how each factor propagates to regional damages.
Pattern scaling, our computationally efficient bridge from global to local climate, unveils substantial geographic and methodological uncertainty.
Using the pattern library of
Lynch et al. (2017)
, which is derived from the 41 global climate models in the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, Phase 5 (CMIP5), shows that land areas typically heat about 50% faster than the planet as a whole, although the exact amplification varies markedly between models and regions.
Anchoring a given pattern to different present day temperature maps, for example, the ERA5 reanalysis versus the climatology of an individual model, can further shift the projected 2100 regional means by up to 3 °C.
5
5

5

CMIP5 is the international multi-model ensemble that underpinned the IPCC’s Fifth Assessment Report; it provides internally consistent past-to-future climate simulations for dozens of Earth-system models.
See
https://wcrp-cmip.org/cmip-phases/cmip5
for CMIP5 and
https://www.ipcc.ch
for the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC).

Passing these alternative temperature fields through a standard hump-shaped damage function can therefore flip sub-regions from “relative winners” to “relative losers” and vice-versa.

Time-dependent Land Capacity

In principle, the operator
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}
may be either time-invariant (i.e., constant) or time-dependent.
As defined in Equation (
4
), it is time-invariant under the assumptions of (i) a fixed equilibrium partitioning of the total carbon mass across reservoirs, expressed by the ratios
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}_{j}/\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}_{i}
, and (ii) constant inter-reservoir exchange coefficients
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}_{ij}
.
However, land-use-related emissions, primarily permanent deforestation and agricultural expansion, alter the storage capacity of the land biosphere
(
Hurtt et al., 2011
;
Meinshausen et al., 2011
)
.
Within the box-model framework, the equilibrium carbon mass of the land reservoir, therefore, cannot remain constant.

When changes in the equilibrium mass of the land biosphere
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}_{\text{L}}
are taken into account over time, the operator
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}
becomes time-dependent and we denote it as
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}_{t}
.
Given land-use-change emissions
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{e}}^{t}_{\text{L}}
at time
\displaystyle t
, and assuming that a fraction
\displaystyle r
of these emissions results from deforestation, the equilibrium mass of the land biosphere in the next time step is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}_{t+1}^{\text{L}}\;=\;\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}_{t}^{\text{L}}\;-\;r\cdot\bm{\mathbb{e}}_{t}^{\text{L}}.

(7)

This modification yields the time-dependent version of the operator in Equation (
4
).
In our simplified setting we adopt a one-to-one correspondence (
\displaystyle r=1
): every unit of land-use emission decreases the equilibrium mass
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}_{\text{L}}
by the same amount, so that all land-use emissions directly reduce the land-biosphere reservoir.
The equilibrium mass in the box model nonetheless only approximates the true carbon stored on land.
12
12

12

By contrast, the atmospheric carbon stock in the model matches the historical value of
\displaystyle 589
Gt C in
\displaystyle 1750
; cf. Table
Table

1
.

Therefore, a one-to-one correspondence between real-world carbon emissions from land use change and the change in equilibrium land-biosphere mass in the emulator may not be guaranteed. The proportionality factor
\displaystyle r
could be different from
\displaystyle 1
.

The sensitivities of atmospheric and land-biosphere reservoir masses to changes in land-biosphere equilibrium mass can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\mathbb{m}}_{t+1}^{\text{A}}}{\partial\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}_{t}^{\text{L}}}\;\propto\;-\frac{1}{(\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}_{t}^{\text{L}})^{2}},\qquad\frac{\partial\bm{\mathbb{m}}_{t+1}^{\text{L}}}{\partial\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}_{t}^{\text{L}}}\;\propto\;\frac{1}{(\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}_{t}^{\text{L}})^{2}}.

(8)

Hence, a decrease in the land-biosphere equilibrium mass
will (i) increase the carbon remaining in the atmosphere, and (ii) reduce the carbon retained in the land biosphere.
Simulation results confirming this behavior are presented in Appendix
Section

A.2
.
Although the time-stepping formulation in Equation (
1
) remains linear, the above analysis indicates a potentially nonlinear sensitivity of carbon mass exchanges with respect to changes in the land-biosphere equilibrium mass.

2.2
Challenges of Degrees of Freedom

A central challenge in constructing a climate emulator is to determine a parameter set that allows the reduced model to replicate the carbon-cycle behavior of more detailed Earth-system models.
The usual remedy is to solve an optimization problem that minimizes the discrepancy between the emulator’s output and a benchmark simulation.
In practice, however, this calibration task is often ill-posed: different parameter vectors can generate virtually identical carbon-flux trajectories, even though many of those vectors are physically implausible.

The indeterminacy originates partly from Equation (
4
), which shows that only the ratios of the equilibrium masses enter the operator
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}
.
Hence, for any scalar
\displaystyle c>0
, the replacement
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}\!\mapsto\!c\,\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}
leaves the model response unchanged.
In Section
Section

3
we resolve this degeneracy by introducing a physics-informed calibration scheme that (i) enforces additional physical constraints and (ii) exploits the emulator’s linear structure to construct a weighted operator capable of capturing the extreme dynamical regimes present across the admissible model configurations.

3
A Calibrated Climate Emulator

In this section, we introduce a systematic calibration procedure that turns a box-type CCE, configured for a specific integrated-assessment purpose (cf. Section
Section

2
), into a climate-data-constrained component ready for quantitative modeling. Furthermore, we show how the calibrated CCE can be coupled with a temperature module to form a full climate emulator that can be embedded in quantitative IAMs (Figure
Figure

1

Section
Section

3.1

\displaystyle n

Section

2
), encompassing data selection, parameter estimation, and hyperparameter tuning. As an example, we utilize the pulse-decay database of
Joos et al. (2013)

Individual simulations differ, among others, in the climate model used, the amplitude of the pulse, or the background conditions into which the pulse is released. In our work below, we use the so-called multi-model mean (average over more than ten individual models) of the simulated decay of a 100 GtC carbon pulse under pre-industrial (PI) conditions, corresponding to the year 1750. To illustrate that other choices are possible, we provide in Appendix

B

Joos et al. (2013)

(
Folini et al., 2024
)
, and which background state (PI, PD, or another) is most relevant.

Next, in Section
Section

3.2
, we outline the temperature model and its parameterization, which is primarily based on the work of
Geoffroy et al. (2013a)
;
Geoffroy et al. (2013b)
, with slight adjustments to account for external radiative forcing factors.
13
13

13

Radiative forcing factors are constituents or processes that disturb Earth’s energy balance by altering the net flux of incoming solar or outgoing long-wave radiation. Typical examples include long-lived greenhouse gases (e.g. CO
2
, CH
4
, N
2
O), short-lived species such as aerosols or tropospheric ozone, surface-albedo changes from land-use, and variations in solar irradiance or volcanic emissions.

To build a bridge to practical applications, Appendix
appendix

3.1.1
Multi-Model Mean Calibration

Let
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{y}}^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
denote the atmospheric decay trajectory for the
\displaystyle\mu
-benchmark, that is, the multi-model mean, for
\displaystyle T
years after the introduction of the
\displaystyle 100
GtC pulse.
16
16

16

Throughout we use the superscript
\displaystyle\mu
for quantities derived from the benchmark data set, here the multi-model mean. Subsequent sections introduce analogous notation for other data sets.

The fit error, which is measured here based on the deviation between the emulated atmospheric masses and the multi-model mean, that is, our so-called
\displaystyle\mu
-benchmark, is defined as follows:
17
17

17

Note that, whereas
Folini et al. (2024)
employed the maximum (
\displaystyle\ell_{\infty}
) norm, we use the Euclidean (
\displaystyle\ell_{2}
) norm in the present work; both choices are equally defensible. Their choice of the maximum norm was motivated by obtaining a better fit to the early part of the pulse decay, whereas the
\displaystyle\ell_{2}
-norm places relatively greater weight on the tail of the decay.
Our fitting period spans 250 years, compared with the 100 years used in that study.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathbb{a}}^{\mu},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{\mu}):=\frac{1}{T}\Big\|\bm{\mathbb{M}}[\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}]^{\text{A}}-\bm{\mathbb{y}}^{\mu}\Big\|_{2},

(9)

For example, in the
\displaystyle 4
PR model,
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{a}}^{\mu}
is a three-element vector representing carbon mass transfer rates between different reservoirs (see Figure
Figure

2
for details).
Likewise,
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{\mu}
is a four-element vector containing the equilibrium carbon mass of each reservoir.
The atmospheric equilibrium mass is fixed to the estimated preindustrial value
\displaystyle 589
GtC (
Ciais et al., 2014
; see Appendix
appendix

B
for the present-day calibration).

Since the benchmark data only contains atmospheric carbon masses, the models will be over-parameterized relative to the objective.
Consider the
\displaystyle 4
PR model with atmospheric equilibrium fixed: six parameters are fitted to a single output
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{m}}_{t}^{\text{A}}
, while the other carbon reservoirs remain free to take on any value.
This is problematic because it results in a highly under-constrained model, where different parameter sets can yield the same atmospheric reservoir mass, but vastly different carbon distributions across the other reservoirs.
To address this, we introduce three penalty terms,
\displaystyle q_{1}
,
\displaystyle q_{2}
, and
\displaystyle q_{3}
, which penalize deviations from physically motivated behavior observed in comprehensive carbon cycle simulations. For the models considered in this work, we have selected penalty functions to target deviations in dynamic timescales (
\displaystyle q_{1}
), equilibrium mass variability (
\displaystyle q_{2}
), and reservoir absorption ratios (
\displaystyle q_{3}
).
18
18

18

Additional penalty terms may be introduced to capture further physical aspects of the carbon cycle, but they must be chosen carefully to avoid redundancy.
Each penalty should target a specific feature, such as a statistical regularity or a dynamical constraint, identified from observations or large-scale simulations.

Given the non-negative scalar hyperparameters
\displaystyle\rho_{1}
,
\displaystyle\rho_{2}
, and
\displaystyle\rho_{3}
, each corresponding to the respective penalty functions, the
\displaystyle\mu
-benchmark fitted parameters are

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Big\{\bm{\mathbb{a}}^{\mu},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{\mu}\Big\}=\underset{\{\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}\}}{\text{argmin}}\Bigg\{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}})+\rho_{1}q_{1}(\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}})+\rho_{2}q_{2}(\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}})+\rho_{3}q_{3}(\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}})\Bigg\}.

(10)

In our tests, we use
\displaystyle T=250
, aligning with the time scales associated with the carbon exchange between the atmosphere and the Earth’s surface, ranging from decades to centuries.
Below, we provide a detailed description of the penalty functions.

Dynamic Timescales (
\displaystyle q_{1}
):

In addition to surface–atmosphere carbon exchange, we also account for the deep-ocean carbon cycle, which operates on centennial to millennial timescales
(
Ciais et al., 2014
)
.
As such, we penalize model parameters
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{a}}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}
that yield an operator
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}
with short dynamic timescales.
Each eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\leqslant 0
of
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}
defines an exponential decay mode with dynamic timescale
\displaystyle\tau_{i}=1/|\lambda_{i}|
(see, e.g.,
Åström and Murray, 2021
, Chap. 5
); smaller
\displaystyle|\lambda_{i}|
therefore implies slower decay and thus larger values of
\displaystyle\tau_{i}
.
To enforce this characteristic in the model, we propose the following penalty function

\displaystyle\displaystyle q_{1}(\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}):=-\frac{1}{n}\,\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\bm{\mathbb{A}}[\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}]\bigr)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|.

(11)

Notice this penalty function is strictly non-negative because every
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\leqslant 0
.
When included in the objective, this penalty function suppresses the average eigenvalue magnitude of the operator, promoting slower system dynamics and longer dynamic timescales.

Equilibrium Mass Variability (
\displaystyle q_{2}
):

The optimization problem stated in Equation (
10
) is non-convex, as is evident from expression Equation (
4
), which depends only on the ratios of
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}
, making any positive scalar multiple of
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}
equally valid in determining
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}
.
Consequently, the fitted parameters
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}
(and thus
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{a}}
) may vary substantially across equally valid solutions.
To enforce consistency with observational constraints, we add a penalty term for squared deviations between the model’s equilibrium reservoir masses and their target values, where the targets here are the published estimates of PI (or, alternatively, PD) carbon stocks.
Let
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{*}
denote these estimated equilibrium masses.
We penalize the relative difference between
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}
and a reference equilibrium vector
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{*}
using the penalty function:

\displaystyle\displaystyle q_{2}(\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}):=\frac{1}{n}\Big\|(\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}-\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{*})\oslash\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{*}\Big\|_{2},

(12)

where
\displaystyle\oslash
denotes element-wise division.
In our tests,
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{*}
is defined based on the PI estimates from
Ciais et al. (2014)
, where the equilibrium masses of the reservoirs for the atmosphere, upper ocean, lower ocean, and land biosphere are
\displaystyle 589
,
\displaystyle 900
,
\displaystyle 37100
, and
\displaystyle 550
GtC, respectively.
We emphasize that the values of
\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{*}
do not represent the absolute total equilibrium masses of carbon in each reservoir.
Instead, these values estimate the carbon masses most actively involved in the carbon cycle dynamics.
In contrast, carbon stored in fossil fuel reserves, permafrost, and deep soil organic matter is generally sequestered on timescales ranging from centuries to millennia, and is therefore excluded from equilibrium carbon mass estimates.
19
19

19

However, carbon stored in permafrost can rapidly re-enter the active carbon cycle when disturbed, particularly through climate-induced thawing. While this represents a potentially significant perturbation to the carbon cycle, it is not accounted for in the present analysis and warrants further investigation.

Reservoir Absorption Ratios (
\displaystyle q_{3}
):

Under a
\displaystyle 100
GtC pulse, the
\displaystyle 4
PR model may achieve a low fit error while exhibiting negligible flux to the land biosphere, effectively replicating the behavior of the
\displaystyle 3
SR model (cf. Section
Section

3
for further details).
This observation is specific to the
\displaystyle 4
PR configuration, where parallel carbon transfer paths—from the atmosphere to either the ocean or the land biosphere—allow for an arbitrary partitioning of carbon.
To better reflect the behavior of more complex Earth System Models (ESMs), we will aim to mimic the ratio of cumulative ocean to land biosphere fluxes resulting from pulse emissions.
Let
\displaystyle\eta
denote the target ratio of cumulative ocean to land biosphere carbon masses at time
\displaystyle t^{\text{ref}}
; we penalize deviations from this ratio using the following penalty function

\displaystyle\displaystyle q_{3}(\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}):=\left\|\frac{\bm{\mathbb{M}}[\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}]^{\text{O}}_{t^{\text{ref}}}}{\bm{\mathbb{M}}[\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}]^{\text{L}}_{t^{\text{ref}}}}-\eta\right\|_{2},

(13)

where
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{M}}[\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}]_{t^{\text{ref}}}^{\text{O}}
is the total mass of all (
\displaystyle\text{O}_{1}
and
\displaystyle\text{O}_{2}
) ocean reservoirs, and
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{M}}[\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}]_{t^{\text{ref}}}^{\text{L}}
denotes the total mass of all land biosphere reservoirs.
Following the findings of
Joos et al. (2013)
, we set
\displaystyle\eta=1
, corresponding to equal distribution of
\displaystyle 30
GtC between ocean and land biosphere reservoirs at
\displaystyle t^{\text{ref}}=20
years after a
\displaystyle 100
GtC atmospheric pulse.

3.1.2
Calibration For Extreme Scenarios

As demonstrated in Figure
Figure

3
, different ESMs exhibit varying
\displaystyle 100
GtC pulse decay trajectories.
In this section, we aim to capture the extrema, defined as two standard deviations above and below the mean atmospheric response across the trajectories of different ESMs, labeled as the
\displaystyle\mu^{+}
-benchmark and
\displaystyle\mu^{-}
-benchmark, respectively.
We emphasize that the benchmarks used here (and in the previous section, too) do not correspond to any specific ESM but represent plausible upper and lower bounds for atmospheric carbon masses.

A simple way to calibrate the model for the different extrema is to rescale the parameters
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{a}}^{\mu}
obtained in Equation (
10
).
This formulation allows IAM researchers to interpolate smoothly between the mean response and its extremes, eliminating the need to re-calibrate the CCE each time a more or less extreme scenario is examined.

Given the pulse decay trajectories
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{y}}^{\mu^{+}}\!\!\!,\bm{\mathbb{y}}^{\mu^{-}}\!\!\!\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
, we define extreme scaling factors as

\displaystyle\displaystyle c^{\mu^{+}}\!=\underset{c}{\text{argmin}}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{T}\Big\|\bm{\mathbb{M}}[c\cdot\bm{\mathbb{a}}^{\mu},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{\mu}]_{\text{A}}-\bm{\mathbb{y}}^{\mu^{+}}\Big\|_{2}\Bigg\},\;\text{and}\;c^{\mu^{-}}\!=\underset{c}{\text{argmin}}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{T}\Big\|\bm{\mathbb{M}}[c\cdot\bm{\mathbb{a}}^{\mu},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{\mu}]_{\text{A}}-\bm{\mathbb{y}}^{\mu^{-}}\Big\|_{2}\Bigg\}.

(14)

For
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}^{\mu}\!\!:=\bm{\mathbb{A}}[\bm{\mathbb{a}}^{\mu},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}^{\mu}]
, we define the respective extrema operators as
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}^{\mu^{+}}\!\!:=c^{\mu^{+}}\!\!\cdot\bm{\mathbb{A}}^{\mu}
and
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}^{\mu^{-}}\!\!:=c^{\mu^{-}}\!\!\cdot\bm{\mathbb{A}}^{\mu}
.
In our test, we keep
\displaystyle T
consistent with the value used in Equation (
10
).
Note that
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}^{\mu^{+}}
and
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}^{\mu^{-}}
are scaled versions of
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}^{\mu}
, with all eigenvalues multiplied by
\displaystyle c^{\mu^{+}}
and
\displaystyle c^{\mu^{-}}
, respectively.
20
20

20

Specifically,
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}[c\cdot\bm{\mathbb{a}},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}]

\displaystyle c\cdot\bm{\mathbb{A}}_{ij}
; see Equation (
4

The solutions in Equation (

\displaystyle c^{\mu^{+}}<1<c^{\mu^{-}}

Given

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{A}}^{\alpha}:=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha)\bm{\mathbb{A}}^{\!\mu}+\alpha\bm{\mathbb{A}}^{\mu^{+}}&\text{if $\displaystyle\alpha>0$,}\\

Our benchmark comparison reveals no substantive difference between the static three- and four-reservoir emulators: adding a land-biosphere reservoir has no impact on either BAU or optimal-policy outcomes. By contrast, allowing the land-biosphere stock in the four-reservoir model to decline over time raises BAU temperatures relative to the static cases. Explicitly modeling deforestation dynamics further increases the SCC, implying that land-use change materially shifts the optimal mitigation strategy and requires a higher carbon price to curb damages.

The mechanism is straightforward. Deforestation diminishes the land-biosphere sink’s uptake capacity, so carbon that would otherwise have been sequestered remains in the atmosphere, raising temperatures and amplifying damages. This feedback translates into a higher optimal carbon price; omitting land-use change, therefore, leads to a systematic underpricing of carbon.

In the
\displaystyle 4

The underlying reasoning is that, for example, deforestation not only results in carbon emissions, but it also means the forest will no longer be there as a carbon reservoir. \displaystyle 3

\displaystyle 4

This underscores that even with ambitious CCS policies, failing to model biosphere dynamics may lead to overly optimistic projections of climate outcomes and underestimation of required mitigation levels.
Our findings indicate that CCS can succeed only if natural carbon sinks are preserved. Land degradation and deforestation erode the land‐biosphere’s sequestration capacity, undermining the net benefit of engineered carbon removal. Policies that protect or enhance terrestrial sinks are therefore an essential complement to large‐scale CCS deployment.

5
Linking Global to Local Temperatures and Damages

Economic models that resolve climate impacts across space require equally resolved projections of key climatic drivers, in particular regional temperatures (in °C) and their future changes, to quantify local damages and related outcomes (see, e.g.,
Krusell and Smith, 2022
;
Cruz and Rossi-Hansberg, 2024
;
Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg, 2024
;
Kotlikoff et al., 2024
).
In this section, we present a computationally efficient procedure, grounded in state-of-the-art climate science, for deriving regional temperatures and temperature changes from global climate emulators (cf. Section
Section

3
).

A widely used and inexpensive way to obtain such projections is
pattern scaling
, a form of statistical downscaling that expands a change in global mean temperature into a gridded warming pattern (see
Tebaldi and Arblaster, 2014
;
Kravitz et al., 2017
;
Lynch et al., 2017
;
Mathison et al., 2024
;
Mathison et al., 2025
, and references therein).
The resulting pattern can subsequently be aggregated to any geographical units the spatially-resolved models require.

Because both the global-mean temperature trajectory and the warming pattern pertain to an as-yet unobserved future, they must be taken from Earth-system model (ESM) simulations. The pattern is therefore not unique; it inherits the spread of the underlying ESM ensemble, just as an emulator for global mean temperature inherits the calibration choice of its driving ESM(s) (cf. Section
Section

4
). Selecting a particular pattern thus introduces an additional source of uncertainty driven by model choice. When absolute temperatures (rather than anomalies) are needed, the pattern must be anchored to present-day observations, ensuring consistency with the empirical climate record.

In what follows, Section
Section

5.1
formalizes the pattern-scaling method and states the governing equations. Section
Section

5.2
quantifies (i) the spread of temperature-change patterns across an ensemble of ESMs and (ii) the region-specific absolute temperatures projected for 2100. Building on these results, it also outlines a procedure for deriving region-specific temperatures that are consistent with both empirical observations and the ESMs.
Section
Section

5.3
links these local temperature projections to illustrative local impacts and damages in the year 2100.

5.1
Basics of Pattern Scaling

Pattern scaling, introduced by
Santer et al. (1990)
, establishes a relationship between global and local temperature changes using large-scale ESM outputs. Specifically, it relates the change in global mean near-surface air temperature,
\displaystyle\Delta T^{\text{AT}}
, to the local mean near-surface air temperature change,
\displaystyle\Delta T^{\text{z}}
, at a specific location
\displaystyle z
(representing areas potentially as fine as
\displaystyle 1^{\circ}\times 1^{\circ}
). Temperature changes are typically computed after temporal smoothing, often by averaging over 10 or 20 years, to remove high-frequency interannual variability. A linear relationship is generally considered an adequate approximation for temperature, as well as for other variables such as precipitation (
Lynch et al., 2017
;
Pfahl, O’Gorman, and Fischer, 2017
;
Mathison et al., 2025
;
Munday et al., 2025
) and sea level change (
Bilbao, Gregory, and Bouttes, 2015
).
Limitations of the linearity assumption can arise in the presence of highly localized radiative‐forcing agents, such as anthropogenic aerosols, or when multi-century time scales are considered (see, e.g.,
Rugenstein, Sedláček, and
Knutti, 2016
); however, these issues are typically irrelevant for many economic applications.

Formally, the linear pattern-scaling approach used in this article expresses the local temperature change
\displaystyle\Delta T^{\text{z}}
, measured in °C, at location
\displaystyle z
as:

\Delta T^{\text{z}}=\Delta T^{\text{AT}}\beta^{\text{z}},

(27)

where
\displaystyle\beta^{\text{z}}
denotes the spatially resolved temperature change pattern. Because
\displaystyle\beta^{\text{z}}
relates to future climate change, it cannot be derived from observations but must be obtained from model simulations, notably ESMs, thereby inheriting ESM-associated uncertainties.

For the subsequent analysis, this work relies on a publicly accessible library of temperature-change patterns
\displaystyle\beta^{\mathrm{z}}
by
Lynch et al. (2017)
.
28
28

28

https://github.com/JGCRI/CMIP5_patterns
.
They applied least squares regressions to CMIP5 data from future RCP8.5 scenarios across 41 different ESMs. For each model, a least squares regression relates the time series of annual global mean temperature change
\displaystyle\Delta T_{t}^{\text{AT}}
to the time series of the location-specific (
\displaystyle z
), two-dimensional gridded latitude-longitude temperature change
\displaystyle\Delta T_{t}^{\text{z}}
via:

\Delta T_{t}^{\text{z}}=\alpha^{\text{z}}+\beta^{\text{z}}\cdot\Delta T_{t}^{\text{AT}}+\epsilon_{t}^{\text{z}}.

(28)

In this regression, the term
\displaystyle\beta^{\text{z}}
represents the desired temperature change pattern. It is a two-dimensional field of regression slopes quantifying the temperature change at position
\displaystyle z
relative to the global mean temperature change; thus, it has physical dimensions of degrees Celsius (local change) per °C (global change). The other terms in Equation (
28
), the
\displaystyle y
-intercept
\displaystyle\alpha^{\text{z}}
(which
Lynch et al., 2017
assume to be zero) and the residual
\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}^{\text{z}}
, are not directly used in the pattern scaling application itself. The 41 scaling patterns derived from the 41 ESMs are qualitatively similar but quantitatively distinct, reflecting inter-model differences – we provide concrete examples of this below. As there is often no clear basis for selecting one ESM pattern as superior, real-world applications should consider employing multiple patterns to assess the robustness of results against this pattern uncertainty.

Local damage functions, however, often require projections of future
absolute temperature
patterns,
\displaystyle T_{\text{abs}}^{\text{z}}
(see, e.g.,
Krusell and Smith, 2022
;
Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg, 2024
, and references therein). Absolute temperatures are typically more challenging to quantify accurately than temperature changes, both in observations and models. ESMs, for instance, are primarily designed and evaluated based on their ability to simulate temperature changes relative to a baseline (e.g., pre-industrial) rather than absolute temperatures (e.g.,
Mauritsen et al., 2012
).

Obtaining projections of absolute temperatures using pattern scaling requires anchoring the
temperature change
pattern
\displaystyle\beta^{\text{z}}
to an
absolute temperature pattern

\displaystyle T_{\text{abs,c}}^{\text{z}}
from a historical reference period (typically a 30-year climatological average). The future absolute temperature at time
\displaystyle t
is then calculated as:

T_{\text{abs,t}}^{\text{z}}=T_{\text{abs,c}}^{\text{z}}+\Delta T_{t}^{\text{AT}}\cdot\beta^{\text{z}},

(29)

where
\displaystyle\Delta T_{t}^{\text{AT}}
represents the global mean temperature change between the chosen historical reference period and the future time of interest,
\displaystyle t
.

There is no single best choice for the historical anchor pattern
\displaystyle T_{\text{abs,c}}^{\text{z}}
. Since this pattern pertains to the historical period, options include observation-based datasets or model-based climatologies. For economic applications where proximity to real-world conditions is often crucial, using an observation-based historical absolute temperature dataset for
\displaystyle T_{\text{abs,c}}^{\text{z}}
is a common and justifiable choice. Several publicly available gridded datasets of observed absolute surface air temperature exist.
29
29

29

Among them are re-analysis data from ERA5 (
https://www.ecmwf.int/en/forecasts/dataset/ecmwf-reanalysis-v5
), JRA-3Q (
https://jra.kishou.go.jp/JRA-3Q/index_en.html
), MERRA-2 (
https://gmao.gsfc.nasa.gov/reanalysis/merra-2
), and NCEP (
https://psl.noaa.gov/data/gridded/data.ncep.reanalysis2.html
) or for land only temperatures the CRU data (https://crudata.uea.ac.uk/cru/data/hrg).
Among the highest-regarded datasets currently available is the ERA5 reanalysis
(
Hersbach et al., 2020
)
. This dataset is used below to derive the gridded 1991–2020 climatological mean absolute surface air temperature,
\displaystyle T_{\text{abs,c}}^{\text{z}}(ERA5)

\displaystyle T_{\text{abs,c}}^{\text{z}}
, each potentially suitable for different purposes; the choice should be made carefully based on the specific requirements of the application.

Combining pattern scaling, as described, with an emulator for global mean temperature change, such as CDICE
(
Folini et al., 2024
)
, provides a computationally efficient and transparent method for generating spatially resolved temperature fields suitable for input into damage functions. As detailed above, these temperature fields are subject to uncertainties stemming from the choice and calibration of the global temperature emulator, the selection of the scaling pattern
\displaystyle\beta^{\text{z}}
, and the choice of the anchoring pattern
\displaystyle T_{\text{abs,c}}^{\text{z}}
. A key advantage of this component-based approach is that the influence of each source of uncertainty can be explored separately and transparently, as demonstrated below. While other publicly accessible tools for pattern scaling exist (e.g., those presented by
Hernanz et al., 2023
and
Beusch, Gudmundsson, and Seneviratne, 2020

A technical summary of how to do pattern scaling reads as follows.

•

Select a warming pattern.

Choose
\displaystyle\beta^{\text{z}}
from one of the available Earth-system models.

•

Select an absolute temperature pattern for anchoring.

When absolute future temperature fields
\displaystyle T_{\text{abs,t}}
are required, choose
\displaystyle T_{\text{abs,c}}^{\text{z}}
from among observation-based data sets, for example ERA5 re-analysis.

•

Select a baseline period.

Decide on the baseline period relative to which one global mean temperature change can be computed (employing your CE) and over which you average the absolute temperature fields needed for pattern scaling, for example, the 30-year period 1991–2020.

•

Compute future (absolute) temperatures.

Combine the chosen
\displaystyle\beta^{\text{z}}
and
\displaystyle T_{\text{abs,c}}^{\text{z}}
with the projected global-mean warming
\displaystyle\Delta T_{t}^{\text{AT}}
to obtain

T_{\text{abs,t}}^{\text{z}}\;=\;T_{\text{abs,c}}^{\text{z}}+\Delta T_{t}^{\text{AT}}\,\beta^{\text{z}}.

6
Conclusion

This paper presents an adaptable computational framework that enables IAM practitioners to construct transparent, computationally efficient climate box emulators, with up to four reservoirs, tailored to their research questions. By focusing on linear box-model structures, the framework supports rapid calibration while preserving interpretability, physical consistency, and seamless integration into economic and policy analyses.

To illustrate how calibration targets, emulator configuration, and hyperparameter selection affect economic outcomes, we compare three carbon-cycle emulators calibrated to pre-industrial and present-day conditions. Our results show that incorporating a dynamically evolving land-biosphere reservoir materially alters climate-economic projections.
The PI-calibrated
\displaystyle 4
PR model, which treats the land reservoir as static, behaves similarly to the simpler
\displaystyle 3
SR configuration, whereas the
\displaystyle 4
PR-X variant, which endogenizes land-use change, yields markedly higher atmospheric
\displaystyle\mathrm{CO}_{2}
concentrations and temperatures under both RCP and business-as-usual trajectories. These climate differences raise the optimal social cost of carbon and underscore the need for stronger mitigation, highlighting the policy relevance of deforestation and urbanization.

Neglecting land-use dynamics therefore risks systematic underestimation of future atmospheric carbon burdens, temperature rise, and the carbon price required to meet climate targets. Carbon-capture‐and‐storage experiments corroborate that higher carbon taxation (or equivalent measures) becomes necessary whenever land management is imperfect or trends toward net deforestation.

Extending the analysis with linear pattern scaling links global mean warming to regional outcomes, revealing that local temperature responses typically range from 50 % to 250 % of the global mean. Although this spatial resolution enriches impact assessments, it also inherits the structural uncertainty of the underlying Earth system model patterns.

A distinguishing strength of the framework is its transparency. In contrast to many machine-learning emulators that replicate Earth system model output, our constrained least-squares calibration enforces mass balance, non-negativity, and dynamical stability, and is accompanied by automated validation tests that expose how calibration choices propagate to economic indicators.
All model variants are openly available, via a simple Python API, at
https://github.com/ClimateChangeEcon/Building_Interpretable_Climate_Emulators_forEconomics
, enabling effortless exploration of structural and parametric uncertainty.
