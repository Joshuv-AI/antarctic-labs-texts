Recurrent hemorrhagic myospherulosis of the popliteal fossa: A 30-year unrecognized case with implications of fungal involvement
Authors: Imane Eliahiai, Khalid Morabit, Mohammed Eljiar, Jinane Kharmoum, Mariame Chraibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyospherulosis is a rare and poorly understood condition. The condition is initially suspected based on imaging findings, with confirmation requiring anatomopathological analysis. This lesion was first described in the United States in 1977, identified as spherules present in the anterior surgical regions of the nose, paranasal sinuses, and middle ear. The initial hypothesis proposed an infection by an unidentified endo-purulent fungus infection, perhaps due to the use of gauze soaked in antibiotics and non-sterile Vaseline. It was later suggested that these spherules were more likely altered red blood cells affected by lipid substances, leading to a granulomatous inflammatory reaction of the foreign-body type. We present the case of a 60-year-old patient with recurrent small subcutaneous nodules appearing in flare-ups since 2004. These painful purplish nodules evolved into ulcerations and bleeding. The lesions were accompanied by pain and functional weakness of the lower limb, that healed spontaneously. A long remission was reported after the prescription of antifungal treatment, which suggesting that an endosporulating fungal infection may have played a role in the pathophysiology of this lesion, alongside other contributing risk factors. Therefore, initiating antifungal therapy before surgical intervention may help improve the patient’s prognosis.Keywords: Myospherulosis, Spherules, Autoinflammatory disorders, Vascular disorders, Popliteal region, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Myospherulosis is an exceptionally rare, benign condition which clinically presents as subcutaneous and intramuscular inflammatory nodules corresponding histologically to pseudocystic lesions containing saccular formations [1]. These saccules correspond to the accumulation of erythrocytes sequestered by fatty derivatives [2]. It most often occurs in the subcutaneous fat of the paranasal sinuses. However, all the viscera may be involved, including the mammary gland, ovaries, brain, ocular, respiratory, and urinary systems. In certain situations, it may coexist with lipid-enriched neoplasms such as benign teratoma, renal cell carcinoma and others. The authors report a case of myospherulosis unrecognized for 30 years, that developed in the popliteal fossa of the left knee, presenting as subcutaneous nodules with a disfigured appearance of the overlying skin and hemorrhagic complications.

Case presentation

The patient, a garment merchant from a coastal town in northern Morocco in his sixth decade of life, presented to the dermatology department of our University Hospital with an indurated plaque in the left popliteal fossa, consisting of a disfigured hyperpigmented scar with no inflammatory signs and no pus (Fig. 1). A biopsy of the skin lesion was sent to our anatomy and pathological cytology laboratory to examine for fungal infection. Macroscopically, the skin fragment was 7 mm long and showed millimetric cystic formations (1 mm) in the superficial dermis, filled with a gelatinous, homogenous ochre-red material and a blackish center. Microscopic examination revealed regular skin tissue topped by a thin layer of keratosis. Proliferations containing plump, bordered by turgid endothelial cells, and surrounded by saccular conglomerates composed of activated histiocytes, giant cells, and hemosiderin deposits, organized around vacuoles or empty spherules (Fig. 2). Based on the morphological findings, we contacted the patient’s clinicians to further clarify his medical history and understand their arguments in favor of a fungal infection. The attending physician explained that the patient was immunocompetent, with no clinical signs of systemic infection, and had already been hospitalized for 2 months in 2004 in the dermatology department of Rabat University Hospital for profuse bleeding of skin nodules in the left popliteal fossa. After several investigations, the clinical diagnosis was subcutaneous fungal infection based on the anatomopathological evidence. The clinical picture improved following the initiation of an effective antifungal treatment. Considering the clinical data, further levels of sections were requested and re-examined for signs of fungal infection. On re-examination of the histological sections, no eumycetoma grains containing hyphae, single oval or chain yeasts, narrow, wide or septate hyphae, or indirect signs such as pseudoepitheliomatous epidermal hyperplasia or transepidermal elimination of fungal debris were found. In addition, the spherules found in the center of the granulomas were PAS negative. The patient was contacted to request previous hospitalization reports, anatomopathological study, and other previous biological tests, as well as to retake the medical history for better understanding of the disease. Symptoms began in 1994 with the appearance of small, painful, purplish, subcutaneous nodules of sub-centimeter size, which spontaneously ulcerated or developed into a profuse bleeding ulcer that was difficult to stop. The bleeding persisted until the lesion healed spontaneously. Such episodes were recurrent and accompanied by pain and partial functional impairment of the lower limb. In several cases, the bleeding was uncontrollable, prompting an emergency consultation to manage the hemorrhagic attacks. An attack in 2004 was so severe that the patient had to be transferred to University Hospital of our capital city. Furthermore, the patient had never suffered from trauma of the left knee, and had never presented with general signs such as prolonged fever or unexplained chronic respiratory or digestive symptoms.

Unfortunately, the patient did not have documentation illustrating his clinical history, apart from a prescription for an antifungal agent dating from 2004. Since then, the patient did not report recurrence of the disease, apart from intermittent itching and a gradual increase in size at a low rate, until the end of the summer of 2023, when he reported the reappearance of painful, oozing subcutaneous nodules at the same anatomical site.

We re-examined the histological sections to investigate if it was a benign soft tissue swelling of vascular origin. However, the macroscopic cystic nature of the lesion filled with an ointment-like material and the presence of several saccular structures filled with Masson's trichrome-positive empty spherules were pathognomonic signs of myospherulosis (Fig. 2).

The patient was referred to the plastic surgery department for surgical removal of the lesion.

Discussion

Myospherulosis is an extremely rare condition first described by Mc Clatchie et al. [3] who proposed the term myospherulosis for an unexplained intra-muscular and subcutaneous condition showing spherules or saccules on histological sections [4].

It is due to an alteration of extravasated erythrocytes by non-resorbable fatty material [5]. The fat may be of endogenous origin, such as the by-products of necrobiosis of adipose tissue following trauma or rupture of a pre-existing fatty cyst, or more often of exogenous origin, such as exposure to petroleum derivatives and iatrogenic products including ointments, intramuscular injections of antibiotics, vitamins, hormones, etc [4].

The pathophysiology of this rare condition is explained by experimental studies which have shown that non-resorbed oily materials form emulsions with plasma products, leading to the degeneration and sequestration of extravasated red blood cells. Clinically, it presents as subcutaneous nodules accompanied by pain, swelling, and localized disfigurement of the affected sites [5].

The diagnosis of myospherulosis is histological, recognizing the presence of saccules filled with empty spherules surrounded by pseudocyst with lipogranulomatous inflammation nodules and foreign body granulomas. Additionally, cytology is a helpful diagnostic tool, as the individual saccules may be easily aspirated with a fine needle [6]. The differential diagnosis includes endosporulating coccidioidomycosis and rhinosporidiosis [7].

Our observation is both exceptional and instructive for several reasons.

First, to the best of our knowledge, popliteal localization has never been previously described. The clinical history spans nearly three decades, characterized by recurrent hemorrhagic episodes progressing through alternating phases of exacerbation and remission.

Moreover, the prolonged remission—occurring fortuitously after antifungal therapy and lasting for more than twenty years, in the absence of any confirmed fungal infection—suggests the hypothesis of a possible cross-interaction between antifungal agents and certain inflammatory mediators. Such an interaction may have contributed to the modulation, or even inhibition, of the initially deleterious acute inflammatory response.

Finally, erythrocyte degradation, sustained by necrosis of the subcutaneous tissue in the popliteal fossa, appears to have promoted the establishment of a chronic, insidious inflammatory process and the persistence of myospherulosis, clinically manifested by a progressive increase in the mass size and persistent localized oozing.

To conclude, myospherulosis is an exceptional lesion, with little known to clinicians and pathologists. This case reports on myospherulosis of the popliteal fossa of the left knee, unrecognized for 30 years, with hemorrhagic complications, aiming to enrich the literature and to provide description for other anatomo-clinical aspects of this condition.
