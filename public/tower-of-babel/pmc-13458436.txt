Profiling Hydrolysis Rates of αS1‐Casein to αS1‐I‐Casein During Cheese Ripening by Intact‐Protein Liquid Chromatography–High Resolution Mass Spectrometry
Authors: Sebastiaan Dolman, Marjolein van der Eijk, Loes Bevers, Kees Muijlwijk, Mee Loke Lim, Margriet Hendriks, Nicolas Abello, Rob van der Hoeven
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRationaleThe proteolytic behavior of chymosins during cheese ripening impacts cheese texture, flavor, and quality. Especially, the specific hydrolysis of αS1‐casein to αS1‐I‐casein is relevant for early cheese texture development, and this activity varies between chymosin variants. Accurate profiling of αS1‐casein and αS1‐I‐casein during cheese ripening is crucial for understanding these differences between chymosins and the impact on cheese quality.MethodsIntact‐protein liquid chromatography–high‐resolution mass spectrometry (LC‐HRMS) was employed to monitor the hydrolysis of αS1‐casein to αS1‐I‐casein during mozzarella cheese ripening. Cheese samples, produced with both bovine and camel chymosin variants, were collected at multiple ripening stages. Protein extraction, separation, and quantification were performed using optimized LC‐HRMS protocols to assess hydrolysis kinetics and phosphorylation variants.ResultsLC‐HRMS analysis revealed that the tested bovine chymosins displayed significantly higher rates of αS1‐casein hydrolysis to αS1‐I‐casein compared to camel chymosins throughout mozzarella cheese ripening. Distinct phosphorylation variants were confidently detected and quantified. Kinetic profiles demonstrated consistent proteolytic activity differences between coagulants.ConclusionsLC‐HRMS allows tracking of αS1‐casein hydrolysis kinetics throughout mozzarella cheese ripening. This approach not only highlights the pronounced and faster proteolytic activity of bovine chymosins compared to camel chymosins, resulting in more rapid αS1‐I‐casein formation, but also facilitates the monitoring of phosphorylation variants present in the cheese. These findings emphasize the enzyme‐specific effects on cheese texture and quality, demonstrating the importance of advanced protein analysis for comprehensively understanding cheese production and maturation processes.Keywords: αS1‐casein, αS1‐I‐casein, bovine chymosin, camel chymosin, intact protein analysis, LC‐HRMS

Introduction

Enzymatic coagulation of milk to produce cheese is the oldest known enzyme application in food processing, dated back several millennia [1]. This process is initiated by the specific hydrolysis of the Phe105–Met106 bond in bovine κ‐casein by chymosin, which cleaves off the hydrophilic macropeptide, destabilizing casein micelles and triggering their aggregation into a coagulum [2, 3]. Immediately after primary hydrolysis, a firm, stiff curd forms due to strong hydrophobic interactions between caseins and colloidal calcium phosphate (CCP) that shields the negative charges of the caseins and thereby functions as a neutralizing bridge, which further strengthens the formed gel [2, 3]. There are two commercially used types of fermentation‐produced chymosin, originating from Bos taurus (bovine) and Camelus dromedarius (camel) [4]. In addition to κ‐casein cleavage, a second specific hydrolysis site in casein (less frequently discussed because it is not directly involved in milk coagulation) occurs during cheese ripening in bovine αS1‐casein (αS1‐CN) at Phe23–Phe24 producing αS1‐I‐casein (αS1‐I‐CN) and a peptide fragment [5, 6, 7]. This secondary hydrolysis by bovine chymosin is substantially less pronounced for camel chymosin [8, 9, 10]. The specific hydrolysis of αS1‐CN takes place in the first days to weeks of cheese ripening and is hypothesized to soften the cheese structure, enhancing its processability by improved shredding and slicing properties, which are relevant to produce industrial mozzarella. The underlying mechanism for the texture improvement is thought to involve reduced hydrophobicity of αS1‐I‐CN compared to intact αS1‐CN [11] and the increased moisture content because of the hydrophilic N‐ and C‐terminus that are formed. The weakening of the hydrophobic interactions within the protein gel matrix and the increased moisture content will make the texture of the cheese softer and less stiff and brittle. This softening effect is further enhanced by increased solubilization of CCP, reducing its shielding effect [2, 12]. To improve our understanding of the role of this specific casein hydrolysis on cheese texture that varies between coagulants, it is crucial to monitor hydrolysis profiles in cheeses during ripening.

To follow the enzyme hydrolysis, traditional electrophoretic techniques such as sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and Tris‐borate EDTA urea‐PAGE (TBE‐urea‐PAGE) have long been used [9, 11]. However, SDS‐PAGE separates proteins primarily based on molecular weight and offers limited resolution of casein proteoforms with similar molecular weights. In a complex cheese matrix, monitoring the conversion from αS1‐ to αS1‐I‐CN is further complicated by the presence of additional milk protein bands with similar molecular weights. TBE‐urea‐PAGE offers improved resolution due to a secondary charge‐based separation yet remains prone to overlap for some species and lacks definitive identification capability.

To address these limitations, more advanced analytical approaches, including liquid–chromatography high‐resolution mass spectrometry (LC‐HRMS), offer improved sensitivity and specificity for characterizing intact milk proteins and their proteolytic fragments [13, 14, 15, 16]. Recent studies have demonstrated the value of liquid chromatography coupled to high‐resolution MS for protein “fingerprinting” and the quantitative analysis of milk protein genetic variants and posttranslational modifications [14, 17].

In this study, LC‐HRMS was applied to monitor αS1‐CN hydrolysis during mozzarella cheese ripening and to compare the proteolytic activity of bovine and camel chymosin variants for the specific hydrolysis of αS1‐CN into αS1‐I‐CN. The rate of hydrolysis was determined in mozzarella cheese, which was manufactured using two camel and two bovine‐derived commercial chymosins. For both camel and bovine, the wild‐type chymosin was included as well as a variant chymosin. The variants differ in their amino acid sequences, which may influence proteolytic activity and specificity. For each cheese, eight samples were collected during ripening time.

Here, for the first time, intact‐protein LC‐HRMS is applied to quantify αS1‐CN versus αS1‐I‐CN on the intact level in a real cheese matrix to assess proteolytic activity during the ripening process and visualized in kinetic plots.

Beyond monitoring αS1‐CN hydrolysis, LC‐HRMS also allows for characterizing other caseins present in cheese and their posttranslational modifications (PTMs). Phosphorylation variants of αS1‐CN were detected, with two major forms (8P and 9P) consistently observed and tracked throughout ripening.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

α‐Casein standard (≥ 70% αs‐casein basis on electrophoresis), β‐casein standard (purity ≥ 98%), DL‐dithiothreitol (DTT), bis‐tris, and urea were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (the Netherlands). HiperSolv CROMANORM LC‐MS grade water and acetonitrile (ACN) premixed with 0.1% formic acid (FA) were purchased from VWR International (the Netherlands). Hydrochloric acid fuming 37% (HCl), methanol (LC‐MS grade), and acetic acid (glacial) 100% were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Water was purified using a MilliQ Gradient A10 system (Millipore, Molsheim, France).

The coagulants used for the mozzarella cheese manufacture were all commercially available fermentation produced chymosins: camel chymosin (CC, CHY‐MAX M) and a camel chymosin variant (CCv, CHY‐MAX Supreme) manufactured by Novonesis and bovine chymosin (bc, Maxiren DS) and a bovine chymosin variant (BCv, Maxiren EVO) manufactured by dsm‐firmenich.

Cheese Sample Preparation

The ultrafiltration standardized low moisture part skim pasta filata mozzarella cheeses used in this study were produced at the Centre for Dairy Research (CDR) in Wisconsin, United States. The used protocol was representative for a large‐scale production of low moisture part skim mozzarella utilizing UF standardized milk in the United States. In short, milk standardized to a 1.45 protein to fat ratio was concentrated to 4.8% crude protein, pasteurized and preacidified to pH 6.30–6.35 using a CO2 sparger. A commercially available starter culture was used (DelvoCheese CP‐502, dsm‐firmenich), and the coagulants CHY‐MAX M, CHY‐MAX Supreme, Maxiren DS and Maxiren EVO were dosed to obtain similar cutting times (between 20 and 25 min). Cheese samples were collected after 1, 7, 10, 14, 21, 28, 84, and 168 days of ripening and freeze‐dried. Before sample preparation for the analysis described in the next paragraphs, the freeze‐dried cheese was milled for a few seconds to obtain a homogeneous sample.

SDS‐PAGE

Nonreducing SDS‐PAGE was performed on 5 mg of freeze‐dried cheese solubilized in 5‐mL NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (4×, Invitrogen) for 2 h at 21°C in a thermomixer. All samples were heated to 70°C for 10 min prior to gel loading. Casein separation was achieved with a 10% polyacrylamide NuPAGE bis‐tris gel (10‐wells, Invitrogen) and MES SDS running buffer (Invitrogen). Ten‐microliter sample and 5‐μL marker M12 (Invitrogen) were loaded on the gel. Run time was 38 min at 200 V after which the gels were fixed using 50‐mL 50% methanol (containing 7% Acetic Acid) for 1 h under constant shaking. After fixation, gels were rinsed and stained in 50‐mL Sypro Ruby (Invitrogen) overnight. Prior to scanning using the Geldoc GO System, gels were rinsed in MilliQ water for 10 min.

TBE‐Urea‐PAGE

TBE‐urea‐PAGE was performed on 5 mg of freeze‐dried cheese solubilized in 5‐mL 8M Urea (containing 100‐mM bis‐tris and 20‐mM dithiothreitol, pH 7) for 2 h at 21°C. Afterwards, 50‐μL sample and a combined α‐casein and β‐casein standard (0.5 mg/mL) were mixed with 50‐μL 2X TBE urea sample buffer (Invitrogen) and incubated at 70°C for 10 min. Ten‐microliter sample and 10‐μL standard mixture were loaded on a 10% polyacrylamide Novex TBE‐urea gel, and electrophoresis was performed with TBE running buffer (Invitrogen) for 92 min at 180 V. The gels were fixed using 50‐mL 50% methanol (containing 7% Acetic Acid) for 1 h under constant shaking. After fixation, gels were rinsed and stained in 50‐mL Sypro Ruby (Invitrogen) overnight. Prior to scanning using the Geldoc GO System, gels were rinsed in MilliQ water for 10 min.

LC‐HRMS Analysis

Approximately 30 mg of freeze‐dried mozzarella cheese was dissolved in 0.5 mL of 8M urea, 100‐mM bis‐tris, and 20‐mM dithiothreitol (DTT), adjusted to pH 7 with concentrated HCl, and incubated at 30°C for 2 h with continuous shaking. The mixture was diluted tenfold in 0.1% formic acid and centrifuged at 18 213×g for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was subjected to LC‐HRMS analysis. All freeze‐dried samples were processed in triplicate and analyzed individually.

Chromatographic separation was performed using an ExionLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) coupled via an electrospray ionization (ESI) interface to a ZenoTOF 7600 mass spectrometer equipped with a Zeno trap (Sciex, Framingham, MA, United States). Data acquisition and instrument control were performed using SCIEX OS software, and data processing was carried out using the SCIEX OS Analytics module. MS data were Gaussian‐smoothed using a 2‐point window.

Mobile Phase A consisted of LC‐MS–grade water with 0.1% formic acid, and Mobile Phase B consisted of 80% acetonitrile and 20% LC‐MS–grade water with 0.1% formic acid (v/v). Samples and casein standards were injected onto an Acquity UPLC BEH C4 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, United States) equilibrated at 3% Solvent B for optimal focusing on the column. Elution of retained peptides and proteinaceous material was achieved using a linear gradient from 30% to 45% B over 15 min at a flow rate of 400 μL/min, followed by a wash step at 90% B for 2 min. The column was re‐equilibrated at initial conditions (3% B) for 5 min before the next injection. The column temperature was maintained at 50°C, and the injection volume was 2 μL.

Mass spectrometric detection was performed in positive‐ion TOF MS mode with a spray voltage of 5500 V and a declustering potential of 80 V. Ion Source Gasses 1 and 2 were set to 50 psi, curtain gas to 35 psi, and collision‐activated dissociation (CAD) gas to 7. The source temperature was 500°C. Data were acquired from m/z 500–3000 with an accumulation time of 0.5 s.

Detection of αS1‐CN was based on intact‐mass deconvolution using theoretical average masses derived from UniProt (P02662). In addition to the predominant eight‐time phosphorylation state (αS1‐CN‐8P, 23 614 Da19), the nine‐time phosphorylated variants were also observed (αS1‐CN‐9P, 23 694 Da; αS1‐I‐CN‐9P, 20 948 Da). The truncated variant αS1‐I‐CN was identified by subtracting the N‐terminal peptide (Residues 1–23; 2746 Da) from the intact protein mass, resulting in a predicted average mass of 20 868 Da for αS1‐I‐CN‐8P.

Quantitative analysis of αS1‐CN and αS1‐I‐CN was performed on the eight‐time phosphorylation state. The nine‐times phosphorylated variants were evaluated separately for changes in their relative abundance during ripening; no systematic variation was observed, as discussed in more detail in Section 3.4. The +23‐charge state for αS1‐CN and the +19‐charge state for αS1‐I‐CN, as these charge states were consistently the most abundant across all measurements. In addition, because phosphoproteins readily bind metal ions, not only the mature protein but also several iron‐adducted species were detected, each iron adduct adding 53 Da. Because the extent of iron binding varied between analyses, quantitation was performed by summing the signal of the mature form together with its first three iron‐adducted species to ensure a robust and reproducible measurement. Statistical Analysis

The rate of hydrolysis was evaluated kinetically across the 168‐days ripening period for the different chymosin variants used in this study. Differences in hydrolysis rates were statistically evaluated by an exponential decay model fitted to the decline in αS1‐CN levels (decay rate) and by a growth model for the formation of αS1‐I‐CN (growth rate).

Both models were chosen based on their ability to fit all curves of the four enzymes with the same model, to be able to compare respectively decay and growth rates. This resulted in a two‐parameter exponential decay model and a three‐parameter growth model. Models were fitted and rates compared using the statistical software JMP [18].

Results and Discussion

SDS‐PAGE and TBE‐Urea‐PAGE

SDS‐PAGE and TBE‐urea‐PAGE are established techniques for the characterization of milk proteins, including caseins. However, each method exhibits distinct limitations in resolving αS1‐CN and its hydrolyzed form, αS1‐I‐CN. SDS‐PAGE separates denatured proteins primarily by molecular weight due to the uniform negative charge imparted by SDS. Although suitable for estimating protein mass in simpler systems, its resolving power is diminished in complex matrices like cheese, where casein proteoforms with similar molecular weights frequently comigrate. This phenomenon makes it difficult to accurately distinguish and measure different casein variants.

TBE‐urea‐PAGE, on the other hand, employs urea as a denaturant, which preserves the native charge of proteins and allows separation based on both size and charge. This method offers enhanced resolution for intact caseins relative to SDS‐PAGE, yet its effectiveness in completely resolving all casein variants in highly heterogeneous samples remains limited. LC‐HRMS

To address the limited separation power and selectivity of SDS‐PAGE and TBE‐urea‐PAGE observed in this study, intact protein mass spectrometry was employed to achieve high‐resolution mass measurements of casein variants in mozzarella cheese. The chromatographic method demonstrated robust separation capabilities, producing distinct protein peaks that correspond to various casein forms present in the samples. Although this approach does not provide unequivocal protein identification, it is particularly informative for αS1‐CN: The observed average mass aligned closely with the theoretical UniProt value, and the measured mass shift was consistent with the expected cleavage of the N‐terminal fragment (Residues 1–23), resulting in the formation of αS1‐I‐CN. Moreover, Peptide 1–23 was identified by tandem mass spectrometry (data not shown).

Intact αS1‐CN was detected at 23614 Da, in agreement with the full‐length eight‐times phosphorylated form as annotated in UniProt (P02662) and previously reported [17]. The peak corresponding to αS1‐I‐CN exhibited an average mass of 20 868 Da, reflecting the loss of the N‐terminal segment. We indeed observed several iron adducts for each casein type, with varying ratios in time. Chelator‐based approaches were reported to reduce iron binding in phosphopeptide analysis, including the use of EDTA or citric acid [19]. We tried these strategies, but, in our hands, they proved ineffective for the phosphoproteins investigated in our study (data not shown). Further inspection of our analytical system revealed that the most likely source of iron contamination originated from noninert LC‐MS components. To circumvent the issue, we incorporated the first three iron‐adducted species into our data‐processing workflow, enabling reliable quantification despite the unavoidable presence of iron. Overall, the resulting method is robust and does not require additional sample‐preparation steps for iron chelation nor the use of a fully biocompatible LC‐MS system.

In addition to αS1‐CN and αS1‐I‐CN, several other caseins were detected, as well as the 1–23 peptide, identified by tandem mass spectrometry, eluted just before 4 min. However, because the present work focuses specifically on the enzymatic conversion of αS1‐CN to αS1‐I‐CN, neither the additional caseins nor the 1–23 peptide formation are discussed further.

The chromatographic separation achieved for αS1‐CN and its hydrolysis product αS1‐I‐CN allows clear visualization of αS1‐I‐CN formation. After 28 days of ripening, substantial formation of αS1‐I‐CN is observed in cheeses produced with the bovine chymosins Maxiren EVO and Maxiren DS. In contrast, cheeses made with the camel chymosin CHY‐MAX M show lower levels of αS1‐I‐CN formation, and those produced with CHY‐MAX Supreme exhibit almost no detectable formation after 28 days. The high separation efficiency (clearly superior to the urea‐PAGE approach, see again Figure S1), together with differences in protein abundance, enables the enzymatic conversion to be monitored over time and facilitates subsequent kinetic analyses.

Kinetic Profiling of αS1‐Casein Hydrolysis During Cheese Ripening

To evaluate the enzymatic hydrolysis of αS1‐CN into αS1‐I‐CN over time, a relative quantitation strategy was employed, where the rate of hydrolysis was assessed by comparing the summed m/z peak areas, shown in Table 1, of αS1‐CN and αS1‐I‐CN. To account for technical variability, for example, instrumental drift, the hydrolysis rate was expressed in relative abundance where αS1‐CN and αS1‐I‐CN peak areas were divided by the sum of both αS1‐CN and αS1‐I‐CN peak areas. This normalization ensured that the observed changes reflected true biochemical transformations rather than analytical artifacts.

To visualize these differences in proteolytic activity throughout the ripening process, Figure 4 presents a kinetic plot based on the relative abundance of αS1‐CN and αS1‐I‐CN from Days 1 to 168. This representation provides a clear overview of αS1‐CN degradation and αS1‐I‐CN formation, enabling direct comparison of hydrolysis rates and enzymatic efficiency among the different chymosin variants.

αS1‐Casein Phosphorylation Variants

In addition to tracking the hydrolysis of αS1‐CN to αS1‐I‐CN, LC‐HRMS enabled detailed characterization of phosphorylation heterogeneity within the cheese matrix. Caseins are known to exist in multiple phosphorylation states, predominantly at serine residues within recognized consensus motifs (Ser/Thr‐X‐Y). These phosphorylation patterns critically impact the protein's charge, solubility, and calcium‐binding properties, which are essential for casein micelle assembly and the cheese ripening process. Highly phosphorylated αS1‐CN proteoforms tend to bind calcium more effectively, resulting in denser micelle structures that may impede enzymatic hydrolysis. In contrast, less phosphorylated forms are generally more susceptible to proteolytic cleavage. Conventional electrophoretic techniques, such as SDS‐PAGE and TBE‐urea‐PAGE (Figure 1), lacked the resolution necessary to distinguish these phosphorylation variants.

Using mass spectrometry, we identified two predominant αS1‐CN phosphorylation proteoforms in cheese: αS1‐CN 8P (23 614 Da) and αS1‐CN 9P (23 694 Da), consistent with previous reports [17, 20]. Additionally, the corresponding hydrolyzed forms, αS1‐I‐CN 8P (20 868 Da) and αS1‐I‐CN 9P (20 948 Da) were detected. All proteoforms were chromatographically resolved and assigned based on their average molecular masses. Throughout the ripening period, the phosphorylation profile depicted in Figure 5 remained stable for all chymosin variants tested, despite significant differences in overall hydrolysis rates. The persistent 8P:9P ratio observed in both the intact and hydrolyzed casein forms indicates that chymosin‐mediated cleavage occurs independently of phosphorylation state, with both variants hydrolyzed at similar relative rates. These results demonstrate that, although phosphorylation is a fundamental structural attribute of casein, its impact on primary chymosin cleavage is negligible. Instead, observed differences in ripening kinetics are largely attributable to the proteolytic efficiency of the specific chymosin enzyme employed, rather than to substrate phosphorylation heterogeneity.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that LC‐HRMS on the protein level proves to be a superior analytical approach for the detailed characterization of αS1‐CN hydrolysis to αS1‐I‐CN and phosphorylation heterogeneity within mozzarella cheese matrices. Conventional electrophoretic approaches, including SDS‐PAGE and TBE‐urea‐PAGE, offer visualization of αS1‐CN conversion but lack the resolving power required to distinguish closely related casein variants and intermediates. Consequently, gel‐based densitometric analyses may misestimate the relative abundance of αS1‐CN and αS1‐I‐CN and are insufficient for reliable determination of hydrolysis kinetics in complex dairy matrices. In contrast, LC‐HRMS provides enhanced analytical specificity and mass resolution, enabling confident relative quantification of individual αS1‐CN proteoforms, their hydrolysis products, and associated phosphorylation states throughout ripening.

Kinetic analyses revealed differences in proteolytic efficiency between four commercial bovine and camel chymosins, with bovine chymosins catalyzing a more rapid specific hydrolysis of αS1‐CN into αS1‐I‐CN during the early stages of ripening. These findings were statistically confirmed. Importantly, our research shows that the phosphorylation state of αS1‐CN does not significantly affect αS1‐CN chymosin‐mediated cleavage, indicating that enzyme specificity towards αS1‐CN, rather than substrate microheterogeneity, is the principal determinant of ripening kinetics.

Taken together, this work demonstrates a new application of intact‐protein LC‐HRMS for kinetic monitoring of defined casein proteoforms, providing information not accessible through conventional methods and improving our understanding of the molecular processes that influence cheese quality.
