Neural grid control systems with predictive transient stability enhancement enabling 100 percent renewable integration through distributed intelligence and real time topology reconfiguration
Authors: Zhiliang Cui, Yuze Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The galloping movement toward completely renewable power systems means that new control architectures are needed that can cope with increased variability to date whilst maintaining short-lived stability in low-inertia networks. This paper presents a Neural Grid Control System (NGCS) that combines physics-informed graph attention networks with hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning to provide enhanced predictive transient stability and achieve real-time topology reconfiguration. The proposed architecture uses a spatiotemporal graph encoder, which is able to capture the dynamic electrical coupling at grid-forming converter-dominated networks and embeds the physical constraints of swing equation within the learning architecture to ensure physical consistency. An intelligent layer with novel distributed intelligence enables autonomous agents at the autonomous substation level to synchronize topology-switching behavior using consensus-based communication schemes, where a centralized meta-controller coordinates system-wide stability goals. The proposed NGCS demonstrates 94.7% transient stability classification accuracy, achieves inference in 12 ms, and provides a 340 computational speedup compared to conventional time-domain simulation, enabling real-time topology reconfiguration in fully inverter-based grids. These results indicate that NGCS is a promising framework for fast transient-stability assessment and coordinated corrective control in converter-dominated power systems.

Introduction

The universal need to decarbonize the electricity production process has triggered an unprecedented revolution of power systems in the whole world with sources of renewable energy estimated occupying the largest percentage of generating capacity in the next ten years. Although this transition is critical on the mitigation of climate change and the security of energy in the long run, it brings with it groundbreaking troubles to grid stability, which the traditional control paradigms were never intended to address. In contrast to the inherent rotational inertia and damping of synchronous generators, inverter resources have very different dynamic properties that may destabilize frequency response, voltage stability and response to transient events during contingencies1. The physics of power system stability are being fundamentally changed with the ratio of converter interfaced generation to conventional synchronous machine growing and in new operation conditions where the old stability evaluation tools and control strategies no longer suffice2,3. The stable functioning of grids with renewable penetration approaching 100 per cent therefore require novel control structure that can ensure transient stability, real time topology reconfiguration and distributed intelligent agent coordination on large geographical scales4.

The technological history of grid-connected converter control has developed in separate technological generations each of which overcame particular shortcomings of the earlier generation. Early grid-following inverters were phase-locked loop based to synchronize themselves with grid voltage, and only worked with a strong system when there was enough synchronous generation5,6. With greater renewable penetration the weaknesses of grid-following control were revealed especially under weak grid conditions when the instability of phase-locked loops and harmonic resonances undermined performance7,8. Such recognition led to the creation of grid forming control schemes, such as virtual synchronous generators, droop-based techniques, which allow inverters to construct grid voltage and frequency as opposed to following current references9,10. The latest grid-forming converters also feature advanced state-space modelling and hierarchical control structures that offer grid-supportive services and control the dynamics of energy storage11,12. Though these deterministic control methods, though an important development, are essentially based on linearized system models and fixed parameter values which cannot respond to nonlinear, time-changing situations in the high-renewable grids.

Model predictive control has become a potent tool to help manage the computational requirements of converter control in microgrids and distribution systems, and has seen systematic management of constraints and multi-objective optimization in receding horizon models13. Thorough research studies have shown that the predictive control can handle primary-layer dynamics such as controlling voltages, power sharing, and harmonic suppression14. This has been extended to the distributed energy resource coordination so that more complex grid management is now possible, albeit with computational complexity much harder in very large systems with many controllable resources15. The recent developments have combined direct reinforcement learning with model predictive control to obtain adaptive real-time multi-agent control, exhibiting better performance when operating in dynamic conditions16. However, such hybrid strategies have mainly dealt with steady-state optimization and secondary control goals, and have little to do with the dynamics of the milliseconds scale, which characterize the phenomena of transient stability. The inherent conflict between the computational speed needs of a real-time implementation and the fidelity needed to predict the stability correctly is an ongoing inhibiting factor to practical implementation.

Deep learning and reinforcement learning have been applied to control power systems, and neural-network models have proven to be very fast at identifying patterns, approximating functions, as well as making sequential decisions17,18. Zero-inertia microgrid control has also been implemented using deep learning techniques and found to exhibit strong operation when trained on various operating conditions using neural-network controllers19. The use of physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) is one of the directions that are particularly promising, as the differential equations that define the dynamics of a system are directly incorporated into the network architecture to ensure its physical consistency and minimize the data needs20. The combination of model-free reinforcement learning and microgrid control has been widely surveyed, discussing the opportunities of adaptive optimization as well as the issue of sample efficiency, constraint of safety, and the transferability to other system structures21. Trajectory-tracking physics-informed reinforcement learning models have been shown to be able to perform inverter control, but have not been used to apply transient stability enhancement22. Hierarchical deep reinforcement learning has demonstrated the ability to coordinate the work of a networked microgrids, whereas virtual synchronous generator control with state-of-the-art policy-gradient approaches has improved their frequency control performance23 and24. Multi-objective reinforcement learning for grid forming converters has been applied to solve the virtual impedance scheduling problem with transient loads25, but no current framework integrates the predictive transient stability with the distributed intelligent control to achieve the real time topology reconfiguration.

Although these significant contributions have been made in the field of control theory, machine learning, as well as power electronics, there are still significant gaps which hinder the achievement of fully renewable power systems. Although grid forming converters can deliver the desired grid services, they are susceptible to the present saturation and stability margin reduction in times of extreme disturbances26. Transient stability enhancement methods by model prediction have been shown to have positive results but demand important computational resources that cannot be introduced into practice at large scale27. Distribution system reconfiguration based on deep reinforcement learning has been applied to achieve steady-state goals28, but there has not been any attempt to combine topology optimization with dynamic stability objectives and multi-agent decision-making. In addition, new problems related to sector coupling (such as the introduction of the hydrogen infrastructure and electric vehicle charging) also add complexity that the current structures cannot handle satisfactorily29,30. A lack of a single architecture which can be used to predictively measure transient stability, coordination across multiple locations in a distributed manner, and dynamically reconfigure the topology in real-time is a root cause to secure operation of 100 per cent inverter-based grids.

These interrelated issues are addressed in this article by introducing a Neural Grid Control System that is a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning in a synergistic combination with physics-informed graph attention networks. The main contributions of this work are threefold. First, we present a spatiotemporal graph encoder network architecture that simultaneously models dynamic electrical coupling among converter dominated networks, includes swing equation constraints as part of the learning process, and classifies with 94.7% accuracy at 12 ms per fault the stability of pre-fault systems. Second, we implement a decentralized scheme of intelligent control, where autonomous agents at the substation level can coordinate topology switching behaviour using consensus-based schemes, in which a centralized meta-controller organizes system-wide stability goals, and the deviations in post-contingency voltages can be reduced by 67.3%. Third, we report overall validation on modified IEEE 118-bus and 300-bus systems with 100% inverter-based resource penetration, and with 340 times higher execution speed than time-domain simulation and frequency nadir values above 59.5 Hz in N-2 contingencies. The distributed control strategy offers advantages over centralized approaches including reduced communication bandwidth, improved scalability, and enhanced resilience. Event-triggered communication mechanisms represent a promising direction31–33.

Although physics-informed reinforcement learning and distributed multi-agent power system control advance at a blistering pace, existing literature tends to discuss these features separately. Earlier physics-informed learning models are mostly based on stability assessment or trajectory prediction, recent models of multi-agent reinforcement learning use distributed control or steady-state optimization, and do not directly include predictive transient stability constraints. More specifically, this paper presents a combined Neural Grid Control System that integrates predictive transient stability evaluation with a physics-constrained graph attention network with a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning to reconfigure the topology. This integrated architecture makes it feasible to predict real-time stability, achieve physics-consistent learning, and coordinate distributed control in one architecture, Previous grid-control experiments failed to take this into simultaneous consideration.

Motivated by these drawbacks of current methods, the study suggests a combined Neural Grid Control System of the inverter-based power systems comprising of predictive stability evaluation alongside coordinated real-time regulation. Physics-Informed Graph Attention Network (PI-GAT) can be used to predict transient stability quickly, whereas a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning architecture can be used to perform distributed topology reconfiguration. The predictive-control integrated architecture allows providing instability mitigation proactively and enhancing resilience of low-inertia power grids.

Theoretical framework and system architecture

The design of intelligent control over converter dominated power grid needs a rigorous theoretical background that integrates the classical theory of power system with the modern theory of machine learning. The proposed Neural Grid Control System (NGCS) has a mathematical foundation, which is described in this section, and which begins with the reformulation of transient stability constraints of inverter-based resources and ends with the structure of a physics-informed graph attention network. The theoretical progressions in this paper will ensure that the acquired control policies comply with the underlying physical laws and have a computational efficiency comparable to those of real-time stability assessment execution. The proposed framework includes several of the essential characteristics associated with contemporary intelligent power system control. Neural grid control is the combination of neural network models with grid monitoring and control to assist in adaptive stability management. Distributed intelligence is decentralized processing and coordinated decision-making within the network nodes. Predictive transient stability refers to the ability to predict post-disturbance system dynamics with the aid of machine-learning models to determine whether synchronism and the general system stability would be maintained. All these ideas outline the smart, predictive and distributed nature of operations in the proposed NGCS framework.

Transient stability formulation for inverter-dominated grids

Transient stability of power systems is essentially a property that addresses the capability of synchronous machines in preserving the synchronism in the wake of large disturbances. The classical formulation requires significant modification in networks where resource based on inverters prevails to reflect the vastly different dynamic properties of grid forming converters4,5. As opposed to the rotor dynamics of synchronous generators, which are dictated by physical inertia, grid forming inverters create virtual inertia through control algorithms, thus creating opportunities to make them more flexible but also posing difficulties to stability classification analysis9,10.

The resulting coupled system of nonlinear differential algebraic equations comes with significant computational overheads on real-time stability assessment, particularly with the dimensionality of the system being huge13,14. The basin of attraction for a stable equilibrium point cannot be characterized analytically in realistic, large-scale systems. This has led the research community to increasingly promote the development of empirically-grounded, yet physically interpretable, surrogate models20.

An apparent difference between the networks dominated by synchronous-machines and those dominated by inverters can be found in the mechanisms of current limitation due to the fault. The converters that form grids are needed to limit the current output to preserve the life of semiconductor components and thus significantly altering the post-fault dynamics as opposed to synchronous generators which can temporarily deliver a few times their rated current26,27. The saturation of this convergence current adds some more nonlinearities that cannot be well captured through traditional stability classification analysis methods and hence, the learning architecture must explicitly include these constraints.

Graph-based representation of power network topology

Power systems naturally have the graph-based architecture that can be used to provide efficient learning and inference. The transmission network is intuitively represented as a graph in the form of G = (V, E) whereby the network of electrical buses is represented by the vertices V and the transmission lines and transformation devices are represented by the edges E respectively. The connectivity pattern within the network of power is represented by the adjacency matrix A where non-zero values of Aᵢⱼ represent the presence of a transmission element between buses i and j. In the case of power systems, we augment this binary connectivity with electrical characterizing edge weights. The weighted adjacency matrix uses line admittance hence provides a physics rich representation that provides a differentiation between strong and weak electrical associations. the weighted adjacency Laplacian based on this becomes an essential form of structure in the description of network processes and synchronization behavior1,12.

Switching operations can dynamically change the graph information, and as a result, the adjacency pattern, as well as effective electrical distances between nodes can change as well, in a way that provides real-time topology reconfiguration. The repertoire of operational topologies is limited due to operational conditions, such as radial operation conditions in the distribution system, N-1 security conditions, and thermal conditions to post-switching currents. The proposed framework should therefore not only gain an understanding of the state of the current system but also of the implications of possible topology alterations and hence make it possible to predictively estimate the reconfiguration efforts before their implementation takes place.

Physics-informed graph attention network architecture

The Neural Grid Control System comprises two learning modules and one operational safety screen. The predictive module is a supervised physics-informed graph attention network (PI-GAT) that performs graph-based message passing on spatiotemporal network snapshots and then applies a multi-task feedforward prediction head for transient-stability classification and short-horizon post-fault trajectory regression18,20. The control module is a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning controller in which regional agents and a central coordinator are trained using proximal policy optimization under a centralized-training-with-decentralized-execution paradigm. During online operation, the PI-GAT module provides contingency-aware stability predictions and short-horizon dynamic forecasts, and the control module converts these predictions into feasible topology-control actions.

The architecture has three main elements, and they include: a spatiotemporal graph encoder, physics-driven constraint layer and a multi-task prediction head. The spatiotemporal encoder works on snapshots of system states so as to encode both the spatial relations that are defined by the network topology and the temporal dynamics of the dynamic states. The weighting parameter λ significantly affects the trade-off between data-driven learning and physics compliance: low λ values (0.01–0.1) prioritize fitting training data but may produce physically inconsistent predictions, while high λ values (1.0–10.0) enforce strict physics compliance but may reduce prediction flexibility. Through extensive hyperparameter search, λ = 0.5 provides optimal balance, achieving 94.7% accuracy while maintaining physics residuals below 0.012. During real-time operation, λ remains fixed. Hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning framework

Topology reconfiguration in large-scale power systems is a sequential decision problem and is naturally modeled within the reinforcement learning framework16,17. To alleviate the combinatorial growth of the joint action space, a hierarchical multi-agent architecture is adopted that decomposes the overall problem into coordinated regional decision tasks. Independent agents act on a substation scale, whereas a meta-controller that belongs to a central place deal with system-wide goals21,24.

The system is made up of a multi-agent system whose structure consists of K regional agents, who are tasked with the topology control in a specific network partition and a single central coordinator. Regional agent k monitors a local state sk of voltages on buses, line flows as well as converter states, in its region, and picks actions ak in the feasible set of switching actions. The decentralized partially observable Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP) model represents the fact that the agents can only access the local observations but the transition dynamics as well as the reward structure is determined by the global state15,16.

Two different scales of time are included in the hierarchical architecture. The agents on the fast timescale respond to local disturbances by applying pre-trained policies ensuring the stability at the substation level. The central coordinator makes system-wide assessments and issues high-level directives on the slow timescale that are used to moderate the behavior of agents in the region through either reward shaping or policy constraints. This time representation reduces the effective dimension of learning problem yet it preserves the ability to get globally optimal results by means of implicit coordination24,25.

The training process is based on a centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE) model that leaves the agents with access to global state information to conduct training but only local observations to conduct execution16. Research methodology

In this section, the overall approach that will be chosen to create, train, and test the proposed Neural grid control system is outlined. The study design incorporates the use of simulation to generate data, training protocols of neural network, as well as the extensive validation processes to maintain trustworthiness and extrapolation of the findings. The methodology deals with the special issues of power system applications such as the requirement of physically consistent predictions, real-time computational limitation, and resiliency to operating conditions, which are not reflected in the training data. The operational architecture of the proposed NGCS to monitor and control real-time converter-dominated power networks. The framework starts by having a measurement layer that gathers system variables which include bus voltages, phase angles, frequency variations, and power flows. The physics-informed graph attention network (PI-GAT) processes these measurements and finds spatiotemporal features within the topology of the network to predict the conditions of system stability. The expected stability states are then offered to a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning controller, where regional agents figure out the correct switching actions and through a central coordinator, the whole system is guaranteed to maintain stability constraints. This architecture comprises a prediction-control loop of neural control. On detecting instability, topology reconfiguration logic decides on the best switching actions to bring stable grid operations.

The proposed NGCS integrates PI-GAT with hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning to ensure predictive transient stability and real-time topology reconfiguration. The workflow proceeds as follows: system measurements are preprocessed and encoded by the PI-GAT module to predict post-fault stability; regional agents update local topology-switching policies using these predictions; a central meta-controller coordinates system-wide actions; consensus-based communication enables distributed synchronization; and the selected control actions are executed in real time. The resulting system state feeds back into NGCS, forming a closed-loop process that guarantees accurate, physically consistent stability assessment and rapid coordinated control under various contingencies.

Test system set up and benchmark choice

The proposed framework was validated on modified IEEE 118-bus and IEEE 300-bus benchmark transmission systems. In both cases, the underlying power-system model was defined as a transmission-level network with the original benchmark topology retained and all synchronous-generator buses replaced by grid-forming converter-based renewable generation units13,14. The study therefore considered a fully inverter-based renewable operating condition, meaning that no synchronous generation remained online in the tested scenarios and that renewable supply was represented through aggregated grid-forming converter-interfaced resources with scenario-varying dispatch. The detailed system model, converter assumptions, and operating conditions are reported in Sect. “Modified IEEE test systems with 100% inverter-based resources” to make the benchmark configuration explicit and reproducible.

In the case of the 118-bus system, it is a replacement of 54 synchronous generators with grid-forming converters that are distributed amongst the voltage ranges of 138–345 kV. The converter ratings are the same as the original generator capacities, with virtual inertia constants allocated based on a distribution of typical grid-forming converter designs: M ∈ 2,8 s per unit and damping coefficients D ∈ [10, 50] per unit. The 300-bus system uses a similar process which leads to the grid-forming converters (69) that are interconnected with 411 transmission lines. They both contain distributed battery energy storage, which contains frequency-regulation reserves, and the scale of the storage capacity is set to maintain equivalent inertia in the system on a system-wide basis of 3.5 s4,11.

The network partitioning for the multi-agent model decomposes each test system into coherent regions based on electrical-distance metrics. The 118-bus system is subdivided into eight areas, each containing six to eight grid-forming converters distributed across approximately 15 buses. Similarly, the 300-bus system employs twelve regional agents. This partitioning strategy ensures that each regional agent maintains sufficient local observability to assess stability within its designated region while keeping the action-space dimensionality at a manageable level for effective decision-making15,28.

Training data generation

The training data is a set of time domain simulation trajectories, created through the use of electromagnetic transient (EMT) simulation with periodic converter models. The simulation platform has all the order of converter dynamics with inner current-control loops, outer power-control along with imitating the inner synchronous generator, and therefore ensures that the training information appropriately replicate the dynamics of a real grid-forming converters5,8. The simulations are conducted using a step of 50 μs to remove switching transient and the outputs are trimmed down to 1 ms to train the neural-network.

There is operational-point variability in terms of load levels (70–130% nominal), renewable-generation dispatch (diurnal and seasonal characteristics), and network topology (with scheduled outages and maintenance setups). At each operating point, contingencies were modeled, including three-phase faults at different locations with clearing times between 50 and 300 ms, converter-based generation-unit outages, and line outages. The contingency selection allows both stable and unstable outcomes to have been covered, and a more or less equal distribution of classes has been obtained with a specific simulation of near-critical scenarios14,20.

A 150,000-strong training dataset of the 118-bus (200,000 with the 300-bus) system consists of 2 s of post-fault dynamics of every scenario. The labels are determined by maximum deviation of the rotor angle; a trajectory that contains angular separation more than 180 degrees is considered to be unstable. Neural network training protocol

Training was conducted in two coupled stages. First, the PI-GAT predictive module was trained in supervised fashion using EMT-generated trajectories from the modified IEEE 118-bus and IEEE 300-bus systems18,19. Input samples consisted of spatiotemporal network measurements extracted from faulted and post-fault operating windows, and the training targets included binary stability labels together with short-horizon angle and frequency trajectories. The dataset contained 150,000 trajectories for the IEEE 118-bus system and 200,000 trajectories for the IEEE 300-bus system and was partitioned into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing subsets.

The PI-GAT model was optimized using AdamW with a learning rate of 0.001, cosine-annealing learning-rate scheduling, weight-decay regularization, and gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 1.0 over 200 training epochs. The total loss combined binary cross-entropy for stability classification, mean-squared error for trajectory prediction, and the physics residual term introduced in Sect. “Physics-informed graph attention network architecture”. The weighting factor for the physics term was annealed during training so that early epochs emphasized data fitting and later epochs emphasized physics consistency20,22.

Second, the hierarchical control module was trained using proximal policy optimization in two phases. In the first phase, each regional agent was trained independently on isolated subsystem scenarios in order to learn feasible local corrective actions. In the second phase, all regional agents and the central coordinator were jointly trained under centralized training with decentralized execution, using neighbor-to-neighbor coordination and global reward shaping to learn cooperative corrective behavior16,24. This two-stage procedure improved both action feasibility and coordination quality under stressed operating conditions.

The training is implemented on a computing cluster that has eight NVIDIA A100s with 80 GB of memory each. PI-GAT training on 118-bus system will take about 72 h and that of 300-bus system will take about 120 h. Further multi-agent training will require about 96 h because of the computer cost associated with simulating the environment. Validation and performance evaluation

The validation protocol attempts to measure the accuracy of stability classification and the effectiveness of coordinated control behavior. To gauge stability, the metrics to be evaluated include accuracy in classification, the F1-score, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) and, most importantly, the probability of not detecting the unstable scenario as being stable, which is the false-negative rate. Since the functioning of power-system is critical in safety terms, false negativity reduction is of higher priority than overall accuracy13,18.

Transient-stability validation used four primary dynamic indices in addition to classification metrics. Computational implementation

The deployment architecture was designed to satisfy real-time operational latency constraints. The trained PI-GAT model was optimized using TensorRT and mixed-precision execution, and the regional control policies were deployed on edge-computing devices colocated with regional control centers. Under nominal communication conditions, each regional agent transmitted less than 1 kB of compressed coordination data per consensus round, and three consensus rounds were sufficient for convergence in most test cases12,24.

The total measurement-to-control latency consisted of measurement acquisition and preprocessing, PI-GAT inference, multi-agent policy evaluation, consensus communication, and command validation. The resulting end-to-end latency was 47 ms for the IEEE 118-bus system and 62 ms for the IEEE 300-bus system. These values remained well below the 100–200 ms response window available for first-swing corrective action in the tested scenarios26,30. The observed scaling behavior also indicated that the predictive module grew more favorably with system size than conventional EMT simulation, supporting the feasibility of partition-based deployment in larger grids.

Underlying power-system model and assumptions

In this study, 100% renewable integration denotes a fully inverter-based operating condition in which all synchronous generators are replaced by grid-forming converter-interfaced renewable resources and storage-supported fast frequency response, with renewable variability represented through scenario-based dispatch changes rather than plant-level chronological weather series.

The underlying power-system model used in this study consisted of two transmission-network benchmarks derived from the IEEE 118-bus and IEEE 300-bus systems. The original branch connectivity, load distribution, and network areas of the benchmark cases were preserved, while all conventional synchronous-generator units were replaced by grid-forming converter-interfaced renewable generation units with equivalent bus locations and matched active-power ratings. Accordingly, renewable penetration was set to 100% on an online generation basis for all simulated operating conditions, and no synchronous machine was retained for inertia or voltage support.

In the modified IEEE 118-bus case, 54 generator buses were replaced by grid-forming converters over the original transmission network. In the modified IEEE 300-bus case, 69 generator buses were replaced in the same way. Both systems included distributed battery energy storage for fast active-power support and synthetic inertia emulation. The present work focused on system-level transient-stability prediction and corrective control rather than plant-level aerodynamic or photovoltaic conversion modeling. Accordingly, the renewable supply was represented as an aggregated grid-forming converter-interfaced portfolio distributed over the original generator buses, with matched active-power ratings and battery storage support. The study therefore modeled a fully inverter-based renewable operating condition, rather than a fixed technology-specific mix of wind, solar, or hydro units. Renewable intermittency was represented through scenario-based operating-point variation, in which converter dispatch levels were changed across diurnal and seasonal operating envelopes together with load variation between 70 and 130% of nominal demand. This scenario design captured variability in net renewable availability and power-transfer stress without assuming plant-specific weather time series. Contingency studies included three-phase faults, N-1 outages, and N-2 outages under these varying operating conditions, enabling controlled evaluation of transient-stability behavior under fully converter-dominated operation.

The framework was evaluated on two modified benchmark transmission systems: IEEE 118-bus and IEEE 300-bus. The modified IEEE 118-bus system contained 118 buses, 186 transmission lines, and 54 converter-based generation nodes, whereas the modified IEEE 300-bus system contained 300 buses, 411 transmission lines, and 69 converter-based generation nodes. In both systems, the original network topology was retained, but every synchronous-generator bus was replaced by a grid-forming converter-interfaced renewable source. Electromagnetic transient simulation platform

Training data generation is also based on a high-fidelity electromagnetic transient (EMT) simulation platform, which is able to effectively model the fast-changing behavior of power-electronic converters and maintain computational viability to generate large datasets. The simulation engine also supports the switched-mode converter models which give a detailed model of pulse-width modulation process and LC filter dynamics, and the digital control delays present in an actual implementation. The simulation time step of 50 μs ensures that the transient of switching can be resolved correctly thus making the output data decimated into a 1 ms resolution, allowing neural-network training, and thus balancing fidelity with data storage requirements and processing volumes.

Each grid-forming converter was modeled with a hierarchical dynamic control structure consisting of: (i) an inner current-control loop, (ii) an intermediate voltage-control loop, and (iii) an outer virtual synchronous generator control loop that emulated inertia and damping. The virtual inertia constant was varied in the range 2–8 s and the damping coefficient in the range 10–50 pu. A circular current limit of 1.2 pu was enforced during faulted operation, and reactive-current priority was applied under current-limited conditions. These converter dynamics were represented in the EMT simulations used for dataset generation, so the learning model was trained on fault responses that included converter current saturation, control-loop interaction, and post-fault recovery behavior.

The grid-forming converter control is hierarchically organized and is represented by three cascaded loops which can work at different time scales. The innermost current control loop, sampled at 20 kHz, controls the filter-inductor currents with the proportional-resonant controllers filtering out any harmonic disturbance at frequencies characteristic of the circuit. The intermediate voltage control loop provides LC-filter frequency response. The outer loop of power control is a virtual synchronous generator, which calculates the voltage magnitude and angle references depending on active and reactive power deviation against setpoint depending on droop properties. In this stage, additional contingencies are generated by taking into account the effects of the original contingencies. The representative variety of the training data has a significant impact on the extent of generalization of the learned models. The contingency scenario generation systematically alters operating conditions, fault parameters and network structures to provide broad coverage of the stability boundary under balanced class representation. Latin-hypercube sampling of load levels between 70 and 130% of nominal demand, the patterns of renewable-generation dispatch effects of individual diurnal and seasonal variations, and the inter-area-power-transfer configurations between heavy importing and heavy-exporting conditions on an area-by-area basis serve to achieve operating-point diversity.

In the absence of plant-level chronological wind-speed or solar-irradiance traces, renewable intermittency was represented at the system level by varying the active-power dispatch of the aggregated converter-based renewable portfolio across low, medium, and high availability conditions. These dispatch patterns were combined with seasonal load levels and inter-area transfer conditions to create stressed and lightly stressed operating points. As a result, the dataset covered a broad range of net-load and renewable-availability conditions relevant to transient-stability assessment, while remaining consistent with the benchmark-oriented objective of the study.

To train the modules of stability-assessment and control, three contingency categories are simulated. The former type is three phase bolted faults introduced at different points within the network with a uniform distribution of clearing time (having a range between 50 and 300 ms) thus covering the range between protective-relay response speed and slow fault clearance. These second type are the N-1 contingencies that encompass sudden disconnection of the single transmission lines or grid forming converters that do not foresee the faults. The third will consist of N-2 contingencies, involving an outage of two elements of the network being close or directly adjacent to each other, and combine to create the most difficult stability conditions of the control system. The consideration of contingency locations is concluded in regards to the sensitivity analysis aimed at defining that the outage of critical elements causes the most drastic effect on the stability of the system. Results and performance analysis

The following section represents detailed experimental outcomes which prove the effectiveness of the Neural Grid Control System in a broad variety of assessment measures. It consists of the identification of each of them and the performance of the whole system, in which the proposed solution was evaluated against a set of established standards and the improvement rate was measured. All reported findings are based on a test set that is held-out and includes scenarios that have not been observed during training thus validity in assessing ability of generalization.

Transient stability assessment accuracy

PI-GAT stability-assessment module has an outstanding classification rate: stable and unstable cases are recognized with 94.7 percent correlation on IEEE 118-bus test set and 93.2 percent accuracy on the larger IEEE 300-bus system. The smaller average accuracy decrease of the larger network is connected with the fact that inter-area oscillation modes are more complicated; and a large variety of possible instability mechanisms exists. Notably, the level of false-negative rate that is the likelihood of misinterpreting an unstable situation as a stable one is kept under 3.5% of test systems, thus providing both test systems the reliability in providing early warning of potential instability situations to the control system. Post-fault trajectory prediction performance

In addition to binary classification of stability in a system, the PI-GAT module provides continuous predictions of post-fault rotor-angle and frequency evolution to a two-second horizon, facilitating proactive control choices based on predictive system development. The effectivity predicts the success of physics-informed architecture in the prediction of unique specific dynamics by both mean absolute error (MAE) and root-mean-squared error (RMSE). In case of the IEEE 118-bus system, the average MAE of the rotor-angle prediction is 0.023 rad (1.32°) and the average RMSE is 0.041 rad (2.35°). The frequency prediction errors are equally minimal, with the MAE amounting to a negligible 0.018 Hz and RMSE amounting to a negligible 0.031 Hz.

The physics-informed loss functional compares the predicted trajectories to the swing-equation constraints even where the temporal domain being extrapolated is not within the range of the training data. The normalized physics residual indicating the extent to which predictions are incompatible with the governing differential equations stays below 0.012 in 95 per cent of physical scenarios and goes on to confirm that the network has learned to represent physically consistent dynamics and not just adheres to statistical trends. Coordinated control performance

The hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning controller has proven to be outstanding in ensuring a stable system through coordinated actions of system reconfigurations in topology. In 10,000 test contingency cases, the NGCS has a successful rate of mitigation at 96.2% to proactively or reactively manage the case causing instability even though in situations that are normally considered to have the effect of loss of synchronism or cascade failures. The other 3.8 percent failures are mostly in extreme N-2 cases which has essentially inadequate ability to survive as long as possible regardless of topology reconfiguration choices possible.

As observed in Table 6, the NGCS successfully confines the maximum rotor-angle separation well below the 180° synchronism threshold for all stabilized cases. For instance, under a severe 200 ms three-phase fault on the IEEE 118-bus system, the NGCS successfully executes three corrective switching actions, arresting the angular divergence and improving the frequency nadir to 59.58 Hz (compared to an unstable 59.12 Hz baseline without control intervention). Furthermore, even under severe out-of-distribution N-2 contingencies in the heavily loaded IEEE 300-bus system, the coordinated control maintains a frequency nadir above the critical 59.5 Hz limit. Conversely, scenarios where the clearing time exceeds the estimated CCT (e.g., the 280 ms fault) correctly reflect an unmitigable loss of synchronism, highlighting the accuracy of the predictive screening module.

The physics-informed architecture is especially useful when predictions are required out-of-band to extrapolate out-of-band training conditions since the physical constraints implemented in the architecture can place predictions in what are physically consistent paths even in cases where statistical patterns among the training data are few to noisy. The PI-GAT when tested on operating conditions with loads ranging between 50 and 70 not adopted in training condition, still yields a classification accuracy of more than 91% compared to 84% with the standard GCN base when no physics constraints are applied. Equally, in cases of N-3 contingencies with three parallel elements outages, the NGCS has a high accuracy of 82.1 percent, when trained on the N-1 and N-2 cases and this indicates that the trained stability concepts can be successfully compositionally generalized.

Appendage to measurement noise, latency of communication and partial observability cases will be important to real power system environments deployment. The NGCS has been shown to degrade gracefully as noise is increased and be used at signal-to-noise ratios as low as 20 dB with an accuracy of more than 90 percent and achieve an accuracy of more than 85 percent at 15 dB—conditions that are far more difficult than those of today phasor measurement units. Increasing inter-agent communication latency to 50 ms, which is ten times the nominal condition, reduced the control success rate by 4.2%, indicating that the control system remained resilient to network delays in the consensus-based coordination protocol.

Discussion

The experimental findings reported in Section “Results and performance analysis” confirm the main hypothesis according to which physics-informed machine learning when used with hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning can be as accurate as fast and reliable as needed to control stability in converter-dominated power systems on-the-fly. In this section, the practical implications of these findings are mentioned, the limitations and possible extensions discussed, and the contributions addressed in the context of the central theme of the power system research and industry practice.

Considerations of implementation in practice

When implementing the Neural Grid Control System in the real world, the interconnection between learned models and available control infrastructure is to be observed very closely. The architecture proposed in the design is aimed at being integrated with the wide-area monitoring systems based on the use of phasor measurement units, and the PI-GAT input layer accepts the standard format of synchro phasor data. Current PMUs report at 60 samples per second which is sufficient to offer proper temporal resolution in stability assessment however when equipment is available, higher report rates can be utilized. This natural structure of the regional agent deployment shapes to the current area boundaries of utility control, and regional control centers contain edge computing devices that can host agents and reduce total communication latency to controlled substations.

Practical deployment requires synchronized voltage phasors, bus-frequency estimates, breaker-status signals, regional line-flow measurements, and converter active/reactive power telemetry at the regional boundaries. The PI-GAT module consumes time-synchronized measurement streams, whereas the topology controller issues supervisory switching decisions at discrete decision intervals. Online operation uses fixed trained model weights, while tuning and retraining are performed offline. The communication layer assumes time-synchronized monitoring and low-latency supervisory messaging compatible with modern wide-area monitoring and substation automation practice.

The computation power needed to deploy is also low as opposed to the common control center installations. The approximate amount of GPU memory needed by each regional agent is 2 GB and runs on mid-range accelerators (NVIDIA T4 or similar) that consume less than 70 W. A single server grade GPU can be used to host the central coordinator and PI-GAT module, which has greater computational needs. The estimated hardware cost of a system that is similar to the IEEE 118-bus will be less than $50,000, a modest portion of the investment in infrastructure of a remediation action scheme. The co-ordination with the old protection and control systems should be conducted with due care that the NGCS acts do not contradict the traditional protective relays, and remedial action programmes. The suggested solution is advisory in the early stages of implementation, whereby suggested measures are taken and handed to the operators to be accepted by them before implementation. With usage experience in showing that the system performs satisfactorily, the level of automation can be gradually intensified along the operator-in-the-loop, operator-on-the-loop, and finally the fully autonomous operation in case of time-related contingency involving timing.

Continuous learning and model updating

Power systems are not fixed; and continued adjustments of the system networks, changes in the generation fleet generation, and changing operating practices can eventually alter the statistical characteristics of the working data to be no longer in the training conditions. The NGCS also handles this challenge by using a continuous learning framework where components are retrained periodically with the requisite accumulated operational data with the underlying physics-informed architecture kept unchanged.

The fact that the architecture is physics informed gives it intrinsic resistance to distribution shift as any prediction is limited to physics-plausible trajectories even outside training data conditions of operation. Still, it periodically compares with time-domain simulations of the existing network configuration and holds the accuracy of prediction according to acceptable limits. Recommended update period of every monthly fine-tuning with annual total retraining, the balance between adaptation requirement and the cost of computation and the validation cost of model updates has been achieved.

Limitations and future research

Although these findings indicate that the gains compared to current methods are significant, there are several constraints that should be mentioned and indicate possible perspective of future studies. To begin with, the existing framework presupposes trusted and consistent measurements of all the buses under monitoring, the further development of the framework to include situations of partial observability, measurements loss, or data corruption due to cyber-attacks is an interesting direction. Early experiments with deliberately masked inputs indicate that the graph attention reduced some inherent tolerance to missing data since the weights on attention bands can redistribute themselves to highlight available measurements, although observable acting constraints should be treated systematically.

Second, the training process presupposes the access to high-fidelity simulation environment, which is a sufficient model of real system dynamics. In addition, the renewable supply was represented as an aggregated inverter-based portfolio with scenario-driven dispatch variability rather than plant-level weather-coupled wind and photovoltaic generation models; therefore, the present results should be interpreted as benchmark-level system-control findings rather than source-specific renewable integration studies. Renewable utilization rate was not reported because the present framework did not include explicit renewable curtailment, economic dispatch, or market-based redispatch variables. Although the electromagnetic transient simulation is a satisfactory way to get a decent approximation of the phenomena of interest, the model-plant mismatch can cause a performance decline in case of an implementation of the policies learnt into physical systems. The methods of sim-to-real transfer, such as domain randomization and system identification with operational data have been a promising way out of this gap.

Third, the existing action space only allows topology switching; continuous control actions like converter setpoint adjustments and coordinated voltage regulation would allow a larger range of corrective actions which can be taken by the controller. In addition, although the present benchmarking covers RAS, MPC, and SA-RL, future studies should include direct comparisons with OPF-based corrective topology control and adaptive islanding strategies in order to broaden the system-level baseline set.

Fourth, scaling to systems well beyond the IEEE 300-bus network, and proposed by good trends of computational scaling, is not proven by extensive experiments on realistic models of continental-scale interconnections with thousands of buses. The hierarchical structure also gives a natural structure of scaling by further partitioning of the architecture, though additional research is needed into the complexities associated with interregional coordination by decreasing the complexity of communication topology. Furthermore, because the distributed-intelligence layer depends on timely and trustworthy communication, practical deployment requires authenticated measurements, time-stamped control messages, message-integrity checks, and timeout monitoring. The present framework already includes a fail-safe fallback mode in which a regional agent reverts to local-only control when consensus is not reached within the allowed communication window. The experiments reported in this study evaluate delay tolerance and packet-loss tolerance, but they do not model adversarial false-data injection, spoofed breaker-status signals, replay attacks, or denial-of-service conditions. These attack models remain important directions for future work on cyber-resilient distributed grid intelligence.

Broader implications for power system operations

The ability to control stability on a real-time basis and coordinate its evaluation has effects that are not only limited to the direct project application but also applied to the transient stability. The physics informed graph attention architecture offers a flexible system that has the potential of solving associated issues in the stability of voltages, small-signal stability, and frequency control, where the physics loss expression can easily be modified to encode various governing equations. Multi-agent coordination framework provides a broad methodology of distributed decision-making in a networked infrastructural system, and it can be applied to gas and water distribution systems, as well as other areas, which can be characterized by physical interconnection of the components and the necessity to react promptly and in a coordinated manner to disturbance.

Scalability follows from the regional partitioning used by the hierarchical controller. The observation size and action space of each regional agent grow with partition size rather than with the full network, while the predictive module showed more favorable scaling with system size than conventional EMT simulation. From an interoperability perspective, NGCS is positioned above local converter control and below operator-level dispatch, using time-synchronized wide-area measurements as inputs and issuing supervisory corrective switching recommendations compatible with existing protection, remedial-action, and control-center workflows.

Above all, what the results have shown is that machine learning is not an attempt without a future, but quite on the contrary, it can be used together with conventional methods of power system engineering and not in their place. The physics-informed architecture takes decades of gained experience in the dynamics of power systems, and implements this knowledge into the learning process instead of having neural networks recreate the basic physics of the system by analyzing data. This integration approach of not touching the domain knowledge, exploiting the pattern recognition and optimization abilities of modern machine learning implies a productive paradigm of addressing other, more complicated engineering problems in which physical insight and data-driven adaptation are both useful. The NGCS enables real-time topology reconfiguration and transient stability control, supporting higher renewable penetration without compromising reliability. Its distributed architecture allows gradual integration into existing control centers, while the low computational footprint makes edge deployment feasible for rapid contingency response. By combining predictive accuracy with modular design, NGCS offers a practical pathway for modernizing low-inertia grids, enhancing operational flexibility, and improving system resilience, all while remaining compatible with legacy protection and monitoring infrastructure.

Conclusion

Furthermore, this paper has described the Neural Grid Control System which is a new machine to combine the physics-informed graph attention networks with hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning to enhance predictive transient stability in power systems with a high proportion of inverter-based renewable resources. The paper tackles the underlying problem of stability in low inertia grids where traditional synchronous generation can no longer offer instances of automatically stabilizing the grid, and works out a smart control architecture that can assess the threat of stability at milliseconds, and orchestrate distributed topology reconfiguration responses to avoid cascades of failures.

This research has made three major contributions. First, using the physics-informed graph attention network architecture, transient stability assessment has a classification accuracy of 94.7 percent at inference times of less than 12 ms, which is 340 times faster than traditional time-domain simulation. Physics determined loss term is used to guarantee that the predictions meet the swing-equation constraints, significantly lower data requirements, and the generalization to operating conditions outside the training range. Second, the hierarchical multi-agent system which is based on reinforcement learning allows to coordinate topology control across network partitions where regional agents are running local policies influenced by consensus-based communication and central coordinator instructions. The commanding mitigation success rate of 96.2 percent in various types of contingencies indicates a successfulness of the acquired coordination measures. Third, tests on modified IEEE 118-bus and IEEE 300-bus systems showing 100 percent inverter-based generation prove that the integrated system can achieve frequency nadir above 59.5 Hz during N-2 contingencies with within 47 ms response time, which shows favorable performance relative to the benchmark methods considered in this study.

These findings have many practical implications beyond these technical achievements. The illustration of real time intelligent stability classification control makes a big hurdle of the penetration of high renewables, it is now possible to operate a system with either minimal or no synchronous generation whilst maintaining a standard of reliability. The modular structure division, which is the separation of stability tests and regional control and system-wide coordination enables incremental deployment and accommodation to a variety of utility scenarios. The physics-informed method, which makes sure that the learnt models comply with the basic physical restrictions, deals with issues regarding the reliability of machine learning in safety-related infrastructure projects.

Future efforts will expand the framework to overcome limitations noted in Section “Discussion” such as strong performance with partial observability, sim-to-real transfer to physical system implementation and scaling to continental-scale interconnections. The ability to combine the basic steps of continuous control measures, coordinate with the settings of protective relays, and reflect on the risks associated with cybersecurity is more of the priorities towards the effective deployment. In addition to these short-term extensions, the physics-informed multi-agent structure is provided as a model of intelligent management of other networked infrastructure systems that meet similar challenges of distributed decision-making, physical limits, and response needs in the real time.

With the world on the accelerated track into using renewable generation over power systems to substitute the current food resources, there is an ever-increasing urgency to have sophisticated control functions which will take care of the dynamics which accompany the shift. As shown by the Neural Grid Control System, this is a problem which can be addressed by carefully blending machine learning with the principles of power systems engineering and is a way towards reliable, resilient and sustainable electricity infrastructure in the next few decades.
