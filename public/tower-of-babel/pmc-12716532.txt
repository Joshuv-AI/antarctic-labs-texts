The local food system and dietary diversity among children aged 6–23 months in Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional survey
Authors: Tamene Asayehu, Alemtsehay Sergawi, Wubshet Asnake Metekia, Mulugeta Teamir, Muhajir Abdela, Shibabaw Yirsaw, Yoseph Halala Handiso, Namukolo Covic, Dessalegn Tamiru, Tefera Belachew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe objective of this study was to generate evidence for strengthening the local food system to contribute to improved dietary diversity among children in Ethiopia.DesignA community-based cross-sectional survey.SettingThe study was conducted in the Amhara, Oromia and Somali regions of Ethiopia.ParticipantsA total of 1475 children aged 6–23 months were included. Children requiring special care or not permanently residing in the study areas were excluded.Primary outcome measureThe primary outcome measure was the adequate Minimum Dietary Diversity, defined as the consumption of at least five of the eight defined food groups in the previous 24 hours.ResultsOnly 25.6% of children met the adequate minimum dietary diversity requirement. Children from households with backyard gardening (Adjusted odds ratio (AOR)=1.43, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.88), access to communication devices (AOR=1.99, 95% CI 1.51 to 2.64), attendance at cooking demonstrations (AOR=1.99, 95% CI 1.44 to 2.74), use of labour-saving and time-saving technologies (AOR=1.55, 95% CI 1.15 to 2.09) and irrigation use for crop production (AOR=1.78, 95% CI 1.26 to 2.52) had higher odds of meeting adequate minimum dietary diversity. Residence was strongly associated with dietary outcomes: children in Amhara had more than seven times the odds of achieving adequate minimum dietary diversity of children compared with Somali (AOR=7.56, 95% CI 4.92 to 11.62), while those in Oromia had nearly twice the odds (AOR=1.74, 95% CI 1.17 to 2.60).ConclusionDietary diversity among children was low in the study areas. Strengthening local food systems through promotion of backyard gardening, cooking demonstrations, irrigation use, communication access and appropriate technologies could improve children’s dietary diversity. Regional disparities highlight the need for context-specific strategies, particularly to address the low dietary diversity in children.

Introduction

Significant advances have been made in recent years to address poverty and undernutrition in developing countries, following the initiatives outlined in the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs).1 However, a substantial gap persists in meeting the food and nutrient needs of populations in these geographical areas. Globally, an estimated 178 million children suffer from stunting, with a staggering 90% residing in Africa.1 Ethiopia is no exception, as child undernutrition remains a major public health challenge. According to recent reports, 39% of children under 5 years old are stunted, 22% are underweight and 11% are wasted, indicating persistence of undernutrition in the country with an even greater prevalence for stunting (43%) in rural areas.2 3

Dietary diversity, characterised by the consumption of a variety of foods across different food groups, is a crucial factor influencing children’s health and growth.46 These practices not only ensure adequate nutrient intake but are also critical for promoting optimal health, physical growth and cognitive development in children aged 6–23 months averting the risk of stunting and other forms of malnutrition.3 6 7 However, according to the Ethiopian Food and Nutrition Strategy baseline survey, only 8% of children aged 6–23 months meet the adequate minimum dietary diversity requirement.3 Adequate minimum dietary diversity of children (MDD-C) is an indicator of dietary quality that measures the proportion of children aged 6–23 months who consumed foods from at least five out of eight defined food groups a day.6 Food insecurity, a widespread problem in developing countries, including Ethiopia, often overshadows the importance of dietary quality.8 Children may not receive adequate or frequent meals, and the food itself may lack essential nutrients.911

Among the important approaches to improve nutrition in the rural setting is to produce and consume diverse and nutrient-dense food varieties from the local food system. The local food system is a terminology that states the practice of producing, processing, distributing and consuming food that occurs within a defined geographic area.12 It emphasises short supply chains, direct relationships between producers and consumers, and the use of locally available resources to enhance food security, environmental sustainability and community well-being.12

In address to the widespread undernutrition challenge, the Ministry of Agriculture of Ethiopia has taken a proactive approach through its National Nutrition Sensitive Agriculture Strategy, aiming to improve household nutrition.9 Recent studies, both published and unpublished, suggest that agricultural and other related nutrition-sensitive interventions in women’s empowerment can significantly contribute to reducing stunting by 30%–50%, highlighting the crucial role the agricultural sector could play in prioritising effective interventions.9

This study aims to generate evidence to support the agricultural sector in tailoring interventions that contribute to improved local food system for improved dietary diversity and a subsequent reduction in undernutrition rates in Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and setting

A community-based cross-sectional survey was conducted from 6 May to 31 May 2024, to assess the dietary diversity of children aged 6–23 months in selected rural woredas of the Amhara, Somali and Oromia regions of Ethiopia. Households with children aged 6–23 months were included in the study. Children with any special care needs and children who were not permanent residents of the study settings were excluded. Permanent residence refers to children who lived in the study area for an extended period, rather than those who are temporarily visiting or residing there.

The reporting of this study was guided by Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) (online supplemental file 1) and Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) (online supplemental file 2) checklists to ensure completeness and transparency.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

Sample size was calculated using Epidemiological Information version 7.2.5.0 to ensure adequate precision for dietary indicators among children aged 6–23 months. The sample size was determined based on the prevalence of a key indicator, adequate MDD-C, with a 5% margin of error, a 95% confidence level and a design effect of 2. Assuming a 50% anticipated proportion of children meeting adequate minimum dietary diversity and incorporating a 10% non-response rate, the total sample size was calculated to be 1475. This sample size was considered sufficient to be representative of the source population.

A multistage sampling technique was employed. First, Oromia, Amhara and Somali regional states were selected. Within each region, four woredas were randomly selected, followed by the random selection of six kebeles from each selected woreda. The total sample size of 1475 was then allocated proportionally to the size of households containing women of reproductive age (15–49 years) with children aged 6–23 months across the 49 selected kebeles from the region. This proportional allocation aimed to ensure that the sample accurately reflected the distribution of the target population within each kebele, enhancing the representativeness of the study findings.

Household listings of eligible children were compiled in each selected kebele with the assistance of local government administration, woreda agricultural development agents and health extension workers. From these compiled lists, 1475 households were selected using simple random sampling. Households with children who do not meet the study eligibility criteria were excluded.

Variables

The study examined several independent variables selected based on their established influence on child dietary diversity in rural settings. These included demographic factors (child age, place of residence, maternal education, religion), socioeconomic factors (household income, family size, ownership of communication devices), community engagement (participation in village gatherings), agricultural practices (food production, use of irrigation, use of biofortified seeds) and gender-related factors (income-generating activities, women’s participation in household decision-making, use of labour-saving and time-saving technologies, women’s attendance at cooking demonstrations). The dependent variable was adequate MDD-C.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews from all the 1475 sampled households conducted at respondents’ residences using a pretested, structured questionnaire. The questionnaire had three sections with a total of 49 questions supplemented by a consent form, 24 hours dietary recall guide and the eight-food group list of WHO (online supplemental file 3). The questionnaire was prepared in English and then translated to Amharic, Afaan Oromo and Somali language, and then rendered back to English to keep the consistency of the questions. Data collectors and supervisors were trained for 2 days about the proper filling of the questionnaire. Data collectors were selected from each region that can communicate by local language fluently and can understand the sociocultural practice of the community. Pretesting of the questionnaire was done on 5% of women and children in a similar area to the study sites to ensure the validity and reliability of the questionnaire. Feedback from the pretest was incorporated into the final questionnaire design. Principal investigators and supervisors checked on the spot and reviewed all the filled-out questionnaires to ensure completeness and consistency of the information. In case of missing information, the sampled households with the missing data were revisited the second day to fill in the missing information.

A structured 24 hours dietary recall interview was conducted to capture detailed information about all foods and beverages consumed by the respondent in the past 24 hours, from midnight to midnight of the previous day. We used the Eight Food Groups tool6 to categorise dietary intake data from 24 hours recalls, allowing us to compute the adequate MDD-C. The adequate MDD-C refers to the consumption of at least five out of the eight recommended food groups by children aged 6–23 months.6

The minimum dietary diversity score was calculated by summing the number of food groups consumed by the individual respondents over the 24 hours recall period. First, a new food group variable was created for those food groups that need to be aggregated, then a new MDD-C variable was formed. Finally, we computed values for the dietary diversity variable by summing all food groups included in the minimum dietary diversity.

Statistical analysis

First, the data were checked for completeness and consistency for data entry and cleaning. Then, data were entered into the computer using EPI-data version 4.4.2 and exported to SPSS version 26.0 for further analysis. For a multivariable analysis, we began by including all potential sociodemographic, economic and other relevant factors in initial bivariate analyses. Variables demonstrating statistical significance (p<0.25) at this stage were then advanced to the multivariable model, employing a less stringent p value to avoid prematurely excluding potentially important factors or confounders.

Following this, variables significant from the bivariate screening were entered into an initial multivariable regression model. A backward elimination strategy was then applied, retaining variables that remained statistically significant at p<0.05. To ensure robustness of the final model, we also performed an additional check for potential confounding by re-evaluating the sociodemographic and economic variables that had been excluded during the bivariate screening step. These variables were reintroduced one at a time while controlling for the already significant predictors, to confirm that their exclusion did not alter the associations observed. OR for logistic regression along with 95% CI was estimated. The level of statistical significance was set at α=0.05. All standard errors were adjusted for the survey design.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

All study participants completed the interview process successfully, yielding a 100% response rate. More than half of the women (57.3%) were aged between 20 and 30, followed by 32.8% in the 30 to 40 age range. Most participants were married (97.4%). A significant portion identified with the major religions in the country, with 48.3% being Orthodox Christians and 48.0% being Muslims. Over half of the participants (51.4%) were illiterate, while 18.9% were literate and 17.2% had received primary education. In the study area, 56.1% of households had a family size of fewer than five members. Most participants (70.8%) were engaged in farming activities, and a notable percentage of women (57.8%) were housewives. Only a small fraction (4.1%) participated in additional income-generating activities, such as petty trade. Most households (93.7%) were male-headed (table 1).

Child feeding practices

More than half of the children (52%) were aged between 6 and 12 months, while 48% were aged 12 to 24 months. Most mothers initiated breastfeeding within 1 hour of delivery (71.5%), followed by 18.2% who began feeding within 2 hours. A significant majority of participants (97.6%) were breastfeeding. About 36.2% of mothers fed their children breast milk three times a day, while 23.4% fed them four times daily. Only 12.6% of mothers provided breast milk five times a day. Most mothers (69%) in the study area began complementary feeding at 6 months, while 12.6% started before that age (table 2).

Women’s involvement in developments and social affairs

Nearly half of the participants (54.0%) did not have communication devices like radio, television and mobile phones, while 46% did. Among those with devices, 62% owned a radio, 40.4% had a mobile phone, 22.7% had a television and 2.2% had a wired phone for communication. More than half of the participants (66.3%) did not attend village gathering events related to agriculture extension service, gender-related events and other important matters, whereas 33.7% did. Almost three-quarters (70.8%) of the participants did not attend cooking demonstrations. Only 26.7% of women engaged in income-generating activities. 81% of women had decision-making power in their households, either independently or jointly with their husbands. The study found that 66.2% of women lacked access to time-saving and labour-saving technologies. In all three regions, most women did not engage in income-generating activities like petty trade or other local small businesses (table 3).

Local food system related to production of crops and rearing small ruminants in the study areas

Cereals are the dominant crop cultivated across all regions, with over 96% of farms reporting cereal production. Root crops (tubers) are more prevalent in Oromia (39.2%) compared with Amhara (21.5%) and Somali (13.3%). Pulse production (lentils, beans, peas) is highest in Amhara (58%) and lowest in Somali (12.2%). Vegetable production is more consistent across regions, with 31.4% to 58.1% of farms reporting cultivation. Fruit production was extremely low in all regions, with over 90% of farms not cultivating fruits. Like fruit production, oil crops were grown on a small percentage of farms in all regions. Cattle ownership was most common practice in Amhara (73.5%) and Oromia (67.9%), with a significant decrease in Somali (14.1%). Sheep ownership was highest in Somali (50.4%) followed by Amhara (31.2%) and Oromia (22.8%). Goat ownership was most prevalent in Somali (76.7%), followed by Oromia (17.2%) and Amhara (11.5%). Chicken production is higher in Oromia (54.9%) compared with Amhara (45.7%) and Somali (28.1%). Camel ownership was almost non-existent in Amhara and Oromia regions, but it was found to be owned by a significant portion of farms in Somali (27.4%) (table 4).

Foods production and consumption from own farmland in the local food system

Cereals and roots/tubers are the dominant dietary staples with over 90% consumption rate across all regions. However, the prevalence is significantly lower in Somali compared with Amhara and Oromia (68.9% for cereals and roots/tubers). Pulse consumption (lentils, beans, peas) is highest in Amhara (62.3%) and lowest in Somali (9.3%). Vegetable consumption is relatively consistent across regions, with around 40% of the population reporting regular intake. Fruit consumption is generally low in all regions, with over 89% rarely consuming fruits. Regarding consumption of animal-based foods, milk and meat consumption varies considerably. Cattle milk/meat consumption is most prevalent in Somali (53.7%) followed by Oromia (24.8%) and Amhara (6.8%). Sheep and goat milk/meat consumption is highest in Somali (39.6% for sheep and 49.3% for goat) compared with Amhara and Oromia. Chicken consumption is more frequent in Oromia (31.2%) compared with Amhara (14.5%) and Somali (17.4%). Egg consumption follows a similar pattern to chicken, with higher intake in Oromia (33.6%) compared with Amhara (14.2%) and Somali (12.2%) (table 5).

Dietary diversity and the corresponding food groups consumed by children in the past 24 hours

Most of the children across all regions consume breast milk (81.1% to 88.8%). However, Amhara has a slightly lower breastfeeding rate compared with Oromia and Somali. Grains and roots/tubers are the most consumed food group, with over 70.7% of children in all regions having them in their diet. Consumption of legumes and nuts varies significantly. Oromia has the lowest intake (34.6%) while Amhara and Somali have higher consumption rates (64.2% and 39.6% respectively). Dairy consumption was very low (9.8%) for the Amhara region with the highest prevalence reported for Somali (86.7%). Meat, poultry and fish consumption was very low in all regions, with over 94% of children not consuming them within a 24-hour period. Somali has the relatively better intake (19.3%) compared with Amhara and Oromia. Egg consumption is more prevalent in Oromia (41.2%) compared with Amhara (16.2%) and Somali (34.9%). Vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables were consumed by less than a third of children in all regions. Oromia has the highest intake (35.5%) followed by Somali (32.2%) and Amhara (12.5%). Modest consumption (45.6%) of other fruits and vegetables was reported from Somali, while the other two regions had a lower consumption.

Out of the total number of studied children, only 25.6% of children were consuming diversified diets per day or fulfilled the dietary diversity requirement for their age. Regionally, Oromia (33.7%) and Somali (37.8%) show higher percentages of children with adequate dietary diversity compared with Amhara, where only 11.8% meet this standard (table 6).

The multivariable logistic regression analysis identified several predictors of achieving adequate MDD-C. After adjusting for other covariates, region of residence showed a strong association with adequate MDD-C. Children residing in Amhara region had more than seven times higher odds of meeting adequate MDD-C compared with those in Somali region (Adjusted odds ratio (AOR)=7.56, 95% CI 4.92 to 11.62, p<0.001). Similarly, children from the Oromia region had 75% higher odds of achieving adequate MDD-C than those in Somali (AOR=1.74, 95% CI 1.17 to 2.60, p=0.007).

Backyard gardening was positively associated with dietary diversity; children from households practising backyard gardening had 1.43 times higher odds of attaining adequate MDD-C compared with those who did not (AOR=1.43, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.88, p=0.012). Ownership of communication devices also demonstrated a strong relationship, with children in such households having twice the odds of meeting MDD-C (AOR=1.99, 95% CI 1.51 to 2.64, p<0.001).

Participation in cooking demonstrations significantly improved dietary diversity outcomes, with participating households showing nearly two times higher odds of their children achieving MDD-C compared with non-participants (AOR=1.99, 95% CI 1.44 to 2.74, p<0.001). Similarly, households using labour-saving and time-saving technologies had 1.55 times higher odds of attaining MDD-C (AOR=1.55, 95% CI 1.15 to 2.09, p=0.004). The use of irrigation for production was also significant, with irrigating households showing 1.78 times higher odds of achieving MDD-C compared with non-users (AOR=1.78, 95% CI 1.26 to 2.74, p=0.001).

Other variables, including production of biofortified crops, larger household size, participation in village gatherings and women’s participation in household decision-making, did not show significant association with adequate MDD-C in the adjusted analysis (table 7).

Discussion

Dietary diversity refers to the practice of consuming a wide range of different foods from various food groups.1315 This approach to nutrition aims to ensure that individuals obtain a balanced and comprehensive array of essential nutrients, vitamins and minerals necessary for maintaining optimal health.13 16 By embracing dietary diversity, one can avoid the pitfalls of a monotonous diet that may lead to nutrient deficiencies or imbalances.

This study revealed that just over a quarter of the children who participated in the study were consuming a diversified diet that included at least five of the eight food groups, providing them with essential nutrients. The remaining percentage of children in the study was likely not consuming a diversified diet, which may indicate a higher risk of nutritional deficiencies or inadequate nutrient intake.57 Consistent with our findings, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Temesgen et al17 reported that only one-quarter of Ethiopian children aged 6–23 months consumed a diversified diet that meets WHO recommendations.6 Notably, the dietary diversity of our target communities is higher than what was reported in the Food and Nutrition Strategy baseline survey conducted by Ethiopian Public Health Institute.3 This variation might be because our study was conducted only in three regions where most of the woredas were situated in the good agro-ecological area.

Regional variations in child dietary diversity were evident across the study regions, with descriptive findings suggesting that diversity was lowest in Amhara compared with Somali and Oromia. However, multivariable analysis revealed a more nuanced pattern. After adjusting for key socioeconomic and demographic factors, including maternal education, household wealth and child age, children in Amhara had significantly higher odds of achieving adequate dietary diversity than those in Somali. This shift between unadjusted and adjusted results underscores the importance of accounting for confounding factors, indicating that the apparent regional disparities likely reflect underlying socioeconomic differences rather than inherent contextual barriers. Religion, being closely related to region, was therefore excluded as an independent covariate to avoid collinearity.

Comparable regional disparities in child nutrition have been documented in other Ethiopian studies, often linked to differences in agro-ecology, livelihood strategies and cultural feeding practices.3 18 19 Despite Amhara’s higher adjusted odds, its persistently low baseline dietary diversity may still be influenced by the widespread fasting culture associated with Orthodox Christianity20 and the predominance of grain-based diets centred on teff and pulses.21 Conversely, the stronger associations observed in Amhara and Oromia may reflect contextual advantages such as more diverse agricultural systems, better infrastructure and greater exposure to agricultural extension and nutrition-sensitive interventions. Cultural practices around complementary feeding and broader behaviour change communication may further reinforce these advantages, explaining why children in these regions are more likely to meet dietary diversity requirements compared with those in Somali.22

Nutrition-sensitive agriculture (NSA) interventions that incorporate behaviour change communication (BCC) have been shown to significantly improve the likelihood of meeting adequate minimum dietary diversity among children aged 6–23 months.23 Evidence from rural Rwanda supports this, demonstrating increased dietary diversity because of NSA practices.24 Similarly, a study from Ghana highlighted a strong association between household dietary diversity and the combined effect of cooking demonstrations and homestead gardening,25 reinforcing the importance of integrating practical, behaviour-oriented components into NSA interventions. Literatures also clearly stated the decision-making power of women in household resource to impact child dietary diversity.26 27 Accordingly, this study depicted a remarkable association between the dietary diversity of children and various nutrition-sensitive agricultural practices and activities of their households. These practices encompass backyard gardening, access to communication materials/devices, and participation in cooking demonstrations, and the active participation of women in household decision-making.

In contrast, women’s participation in household decision-making was not significantly associated with dietary diversity after adjusting for other covariates. This may reflect cultural norms and intra-household power dynamics, where reported decision-making does not always translate into control over resources. It may also suggest that the stronger influence of agricultural and community-based practices may outweigh the independent effect of decision-making autonomy. Strengthening food systems and nutrition-sensitive practices, alongside efforts to enhance women’s empowerment, therefore remains essential for improving child feeding outcomes.

The backyard gardening is believed to enhance the availability and access to diverse foods,28 while the use of mass media communication devices and attendance in cooking demonstrations serve as important social behavioural change communication means to bring the desired behavioural changes.29 30 Furthermore, the use of improved agricultural technologies has been shown to positively impact children’s dietary diversity at the household level. Studies from Ethiopia and Zimbabwe indicate that households using or adopting improved agricultural practices experience higher food security and consume a more diverse diet.31 32 In alignment with previous studies, this research has found that the utilisation of irrigation water for crop production positively impacts child dietary diversity. This association may stem from the fact that households adopting improved agricultural technologies are likely to translate their learning into practice. As a result, these households benefit from enhanced availability and access to a more diverse diet, contributing to improved dietary outcomes for their children.

While methodologically robust, this study has inherent methodological limitations primarily due to its cross-sectional design. Due to the observational and cross-sectional nature of the study, the omission of potential confounders cannot be completely ruled out, and the associations should not be interpreted causally. Readers should consider these methodological limitations, and the geographic scope of the study, when interpreting the findings and generalising them to broader contexts. This design provides only a snapshot of dietary diversity at a single point in time, thereby restricting the establishment of cause-and-effect relationships and the ability to track changes in dietary practices over time. Furthermore, the reliance on 24 hours dietary recall introduces potential recall bias, as caregivers may not accurately remember or report all foods consumed. Social desirability bias could also influence responses during face-to-face interviews, potentially leading to an overestimation of favourable feeding practices.

Although the study employed multistage sampling across three regions, its findings may not be generalisable to the entire country, as Ethiopia’s diverse agro-ecological zones and socioeconomic conditions could influence dietary practices in ways not captured by this research. Finally, while the MDD-C is a useful metric, it reflects only the number of food groups consumed, rather than the quantity, nutrient density or overall caloric intake, thus providing an incomplete picture of dietary adequacy.

Conclusion and recommendations

The findings of this study indicate that the local food environment in the study areas was not adequately programmed to ensure the consumption of diverse and nutritious foods among children. Overall, dietary diversity among children 6–23 months remains low, with only about one in four meeting the minimum requirement. Several household and agricultural practices were positively associated with improved dietary diversity. Specifically, backyard gardening, attendance at cooking demonstrations, use of irrigation for crop production and access to communication devices were significantly associated with higher odds of achieving adequate MDD-C. Biofortified crop production showed a positive but non-significant association. Regional differences were also notable with children in Amhara and Oromia having substantially higher odds of meeting dietary diversity compared with those in Somali, likely reflecting differences in agricultural production patterns, food availability and market access.

To address these gaps, interventions should focus on strengthening nutrition-sensitive agricultural practices and supportive systems. Promoting backyard gardening can ensure year-round access to diverse foods. Creating greater access to cooking demonstrations is also essential, as these practical sessions can empower caregivers with the knowledge of diverse foods and skills and knowledge to prepare nutritious and diverse meals. Enhancing access to irrigation infrastructure will enable more consistent crop production throughout the year, further contributing to dietary diversity. Finally, improving access to media and communication devices, along with ensuring the availability of relevant food and nutrition information, is crucial for raising awareness and promoting healthy dietary practices. Implementing these recommendations will be vital for improving the dietary diversity of children in the study areas and addressing the prevailing nutritional challenges.

Future studies should investigate how market components of local food systems such as supply chains, pricing, accessibility and food safety standards interact with farming practices and sociocultural contexts to shape adequate MDD-C across diverse regions. While this study focused on adequate MDD-C as a proxy for diet quality, subsequent research should also assess how local food systems influence actual nutrient intake to inform targeted interventions to strengthen food systems for improved child nutrition outcomes.
