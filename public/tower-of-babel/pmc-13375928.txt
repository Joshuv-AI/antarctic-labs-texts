High-throughput screening of heat stress response in Chinese cabbage (Brassica rapa L. ssp. pekinensis) seedlings using integrated 3D multispectral phenotyping and time-series analysis
Authors: Yoonah Jang, Roland Schafleitner, Ya-ping Lin, Jinhee Kim, Me Hea Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change threatens global Chinese cabbage (Brassica rapa L. ssp. pekinensis) production, a cool-season crop essential for Asian markets. With optimal growth at 18-20 °C and severe disruption above 25 °C, developing heat-resilient varieties is critical. This study integrated high-throughput 3D multispectral phenotyping with multivariate analysis to characterize temporal heat stress responses in 18 Chinese cabbage genotypes. Seedlings were subjected to heat stress (setpoint 40/35 °C day/night; measured 35.7/31.5 °C day/night air temperature) or controls (setpoint 25/20 °C day/night; measured 25.0/17.7 °C day/night air temperature) for 14 days, with continuous non-destructive monitoring of 14 morphological and spectral parameters using PlantEye F600 multispectral 3D scanner. Principal component analysis of temporal phenotyping data explained 62-68% of variance, enabling quantitative assessment of phenotypic stability through Euclidean distance measurements in PC space. Temporal analysis revealed crop-specific response patterns with maximum treatment separation at 3 days after treatment (DAT) (ΔC = 3.27), reflecting Chinese cabbage's rapid heat sensitivity as a cool-season crop, followed by progressive acclimation by 14 DAT (ΔC = 1.41). Early responses (3-5 DAT) were dominated by morphological parameters, transitioning to physiological adjustments (10-14 DAT) characterized by spectral indices. Under heat stress, plants prioritized evaporative cooling through increased transpiration (four-fold increase) over carbon assimilation. A critical finding was the disproportionately greater reduction in root biomass relative to shoot biomass under heat stress, with root biomass declining 38-47% versus 20% in shoots. Strong correlations (r > 0.8) between 3D imaging parameters and destructive biomass measurements validated the non-destructive approach's reliability. Notably, image-based root surface area analysis correlated strongly with actual root biomass (R2 = 0.698, p < 0.001), enabling practical assessment of root area without conventional destructive processing. Based on integration of phenotypic stability (Euclidean distances in PC space) and biomass production under heat stress, this approach identified four distinct heat tolerance strategies: stable-productive genotypes (ideal breeding targets combining phenotypic stability with high heat-stress biomass production), stable-conservative genotypes (phenotypic stability with lower production), plastic-productive genotypes (substantial phenotypic changes yet high biomass production), and plastic-sensitive genotypes (phenotypically unstable and poor biomass production). This validated framework accelerates heat-tolerant Chinese cabbage breeding through efficient high-throughput phenotyping, enabling targeted genotype selection for diverse production environments facing climate warming.Keywords: 3D multispectral imaging, High-throughput phenotyping, Chinese cabbage, Heat stress tolerance, Phenotypic stability

Introduction

Climate change poses a severe threat to global agricultural production, with rising temperatures and increasingly frequent heat waves disrupting crop growth and threatening food security worldwide. Among the vegetables most vulnerable to these changes is Chinese cabbage (Brassica rapa L. ssp. pekinensis), a cool-season crop that serves as both a staple fresh vegetable and the essential ingredient for kimchi [1,2]. As a major component of the global Brassica vegetable production that reached 73.8 million tons in 2023, Chinese cabbage represents a critical component of Asian food systems [3]. However, its narrow optimal temperature range of 18-20 °C and severe growth disruption above 25 °C make it vulnerable to warming climates, creating urgent demand for heat-resilient varieties [4].

Heat stress triggers complex physiological cascades in Chinese cabbage that fundamentally alter plant function and productivity. At the cellular level, elevated temperatures disrupt photosynthetic machinery, particularly affecting Rubisco activase and Calvin cycle enzymes, leading to non-stomatal limitations where carbon fixation declines despite adequate CO2 availability [5,6]. Plants respond by prioritizing thermoregulation over carbon assimilation, increasing transpiration to facilitate evaporative cooling even at the cost of water use efficiency [7,8]. This physiological trade-off becomes particularly critical in Brassica species, where heat stress differentially impacts root and shoot systems. Recent evidence across multiple crop species demonstrates that root systems exhibit disproportionate vulnerability to elevated temperatures compared to aboveground tissues, with root biomass reductions often exceeding shoot losses by 20-30 percentage points [9,10]. This root sensitivity creates cascading effects on water and nutrient acquisition, potentially explaining why field-grown Chinese cabbage shows severe yield penalties under heat stress despite maintaining relatively intact shoot architecture [11].

Molecular and physiological studies have revealed substantial genetic variation in heat stress responses among Chinese cabbage genotypes. Heat-tolerant lines demonstrate coordinated stress mitigation strategies, including heat shock proteins (HSPs), accumulation of compatible solutes such as proline and soluble sugars, and upregulation of antioxidant enzyme systems, including superoxide dismutase [12,13]. Transcriptomic analyses have identified key regulatory pathways involving plant hormone signaling, particularly abscisic acid-mediated responses, alongside alterations in carbon metabolism and glycolytic pathways [1,13]. However, a critical gap remains in understanding how these molecular responses translate into integrated whole-plant phenotypes across temporal scales, and how shoot and root systems coordinate their responses to maintain functionality under sustained heat exposure. Furthermore, the relationship between phenotypic stability under stress and productive capacity remains poorly characterized, yet understanding this relationship is essential for breeding programs seeking to balance stress tolerance with yield maintenance.

Traditional field-based evaluation of heat tolerance faces fundamental methodological constraints. Environmental heterogeneity, unpredictable weather patterns, and complex genotype-by-environment interactions create confounding effects that obscure true genetic differences [14,15]. Conventional destructive sampling, while providing accurate biomass data, captures only endpoint measurements and cannot reveal the temporal dynamics of stress responses and acclimation processes. High-throughput phenotyping technologies offer transformative solutions to these limitations. Modern phenotyping platforms integrating 3D structural imaging via LiDAR with multispectral reflectance measurements enable continuous, non-destructive monitoring of both morphological changes and physiological status throughout stress progression [16,17]. Recent applications to heat stress evaluation have demonstrated the power of this approach. Studies in wheat documented temporal shifts from initial morphological responses to later physiological adjustments, revealing distinct phases of heat adaptation [18]. In maize, combined analysis of chlorophyll fluorescence parameters and hyperspectral indices revealed complementary insights into drought responses, enabling differentiation of genotypes under stress and showing consistent associations with plant vigor and performance [19]. Pepper heat stress screening using similar 3D multispectral approaches validated the utility of temporal phenotyping for capturing genotype-specific acclimation dynamics [20]. However, comprehensive application to Chinese cabbage remains limited, particularly regarding integration of root system assessments and validation of image-based parameters against traditional destructive measurements under heat stress conditions.

Heat stress responses are complex, involving multiple simultaneous changes in morphological, structural, and physiological aspects. Principal component analysis (PCA) has emerged as a powerful framework for integrating diverse phenotypic measurements into quantitative stress tolerance metrics [21]. By calculating Euclidean distance in principal component space, researchers can objectively quantify phenotypic stability under stress and identify genotypes with distinct adaptive strategies. Recent applications have demonstrated these conditions. In tomato, a PCA-based clustering approach using Euclidean distances successfully classified heat stress tolerance among genotypes under controlled greenhouse conditions, demonstrating that tolerant genotypes maintained more stable phenotypes across multiple physiological and morphological traits [22]. Similarly, PCA combined with Euclidean distance calculations effectively differentiated drought-tolerant and drought-sensitive maize genotypes in a diversity panel of inbred lines, integrating chlorophyll fluorescence and hyperspectral imaging data to capture temporal dynamics of stress responses [19]. Additionally, unsupervised multivariate analysis of morphometric and spectral parameters enabled detection of subtle stress responses before visual symptoms appeared, demonstrating the power of integrated phenotyping approaches [23]. The PCA-based distance approach offers particular advantages for breeding programs by enabling classification of genotypes into tolerance categories. However, application of this methodology to Chinese cabbage heat tolerance screening, particularly with validation against traditional physiological measurements, remains unexplored.

Controlled environment phenotyping provides essential advantages for dissecting heat stress responses. By eliminating confounding environmental variation, controlled conditions enable precise characterization of genotype-specific responses to defined temperature treatments [24]. This approach has successfully identified heat-tolerant genotypes with seedling-stage screening, offering rapid generation turnover and efficient resource utilization [25]. However, comprehensive integration of temporal response dynamics and root system assessment remains limited. Root phenotyping traditionally requires destructive sampling and labor-intensive measurements, creating a bottleneck for high-throughput applications. Recent advances in image-based root phenotyping offer potential solutions, with studies demonstrating that root surface area measurements from digital images can effectively predict root biomass [[26], [27], [28]]. Non-destructive root assessment methods that can be integrated into high-throughput screening pipelines would significantly advance breeding program capabilities for developing varieties with improved root system resilience.

The objectives of this study were to develop a standardized methodology for temporal heat stress phenotyping in Chinese cabbage seedlings using non-destructive 3D multispectral imaging under controlled conditions and to comprehensively characterize morphological and physiological responses to heat stress in both shoot and root systems, while establishing quantitative relationships between non-destructive imaging parameters and traditional destructive measurements. We also aimed to establish PCA-based quantitative criteria for identifying heat-tolerant genotypes based on multiple trait analyses. Through these integrated approaches, this research seeks to contribute to the development of heat-tolerant Chinese cabbage varieties by providing robust screening methods and enhancing our understanding of heat stress responses.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

This experiment was conducted at the World Vegetable Center (WorldVeg), Taiwan, from July to August 2024. Twenty Chinese cabbage (Brassica rapa L. ssp. pekinensis) genotypes were initially selected, including 12 inbred lines developed by the Rural Development Administration (RDA), four Korean commercial F1 hybrids, and four Taiwanese commercial F1 hybrids (Table 1). However, due to pest infestation, two genotypes (Ccab11 and Ccab12) were damaged and excluded from the heat stress treatment experiment. Thus, a total of 18 genotypes were evaluated for heat stress response. The experimental design, including timeline, measurement schedule, treatment conditions, and phenotyping workflow is presented in Fig. 1.

Seeds of each genotype were sown in 70-cell plug trays (L 550 × W 353 × H 48 mm, 7 × 10 cells, 360 plants·m−2) filled with commercial growing medium (King Root, Dayi Agritech Co., Ltd., Taiwan). For each genotype, 35 seeds were sown and grown in a controlled growth chamber (L 1.5 × W 0.9 × H 1.5 m) equipped with LED lighting systems for 28 days after sowing. The chamber was set to 25/20 °C (day/night) with a 12/12 h photoperiod. Actual growth chamber conditions were continuously monitored, with day/night air temperature of 25.8 ± 0.2/20.8 ± 0.2 °C, relative humidity of 77.0 ± 1.9/77.0 ± 1.3%, and photosynthetic photon flux (PPF) averaging 195.3 ± 14.4 μmol m−2 s−1 (Fig. 1A). Irrigation was provided twice weekly through bottom watering, with approximately 2 L of solution per tray. Beginning from the second week after sowing, a nutrient solution (Autopot power nutrients, Autopot Australia Pty., Ltd., Australia, EC 1.2 dS m−1) was applied. Additional irrigation was done as needed based on growing medium moisture and plant condition.

Heat treatment and measurements

Heat treatment

At 28 days after sowing, seedlings with 4-5 true leaves were transplanted into new trays with doubled spacing (180 plants·m−2) (Fig. 1A). Plants were randomly assigned to control or heat treatment groups with 17 replicates per genotype per treatment (n = 306 plants per treatment). Both treatments were established in separate walk-in chambers (W 4.0 × L 3.6 × H 3.0 m, Model H-6400, Taiwan Hipoint Corporation, Taiwan; operating temperature range 15-40 °C; temperature control accuracy ±1.5 °C at ≥20 °C) maintained at 50% relative humidity, approximately 200 μmol m−2 s−1 PPF, and 12/12h photoperiod (Fig. 1C). Control plants were maintained at a setpoint of 25/20 °C (day/night) while heat-treated plants were exposed to a setpoint of 40/35 °C (day/night) for 14 consecutive days.

The 40/35 °C setpoint was selected to simulate acute summer heat stress increasingly documented in East Asian Chinese cabbage production regions. Prolonged heatwaves have become more frequent and severe across East Asia [29], directly disrupting vegetable supply chains. Consecutive heatwaves in 2023 and 2024 reduced wholesale arrival volumes and elevated prices of major vegetables, including Chinese cabbage, in Japanese markets [30]. A setpoint of 40 °C has also been applied in previous controlled-environment screening studies for Chinese cabbage heat tolerance to achieve sufficient genotypic differentiation [25], consistent with the approach adopted here. To confirm the thermal equivalency of the two walk-in chambers before the main experiment, both chambers were operated under identical conditions (35 °C day/18 °C night; 12/12 h photoperiod, lights on 08:30-20:30) for four consecutive days (June 3-7, 2024) without any plant material. Environmental conditions were logged continuously at 15-min intervals using dataloggers. Analysis was restricted to stable-phase readings (daytime: 10:00-20:30; nighttime: 21:00-07:00) to exclude temperature ramp-up and ramp-down transitions. During the stable daytime period, the first walk-in chamber recorded a mean air temperature of 35.04 ± 0.14 °C, and the second one recorded 35.00 ± 0.08 °C, yielding a between-chamber difference of 0.04 °C. During the stable nighttime period, the first walk-in chamber recorded 18.48 ± 0.12 °C, and the second one recorded 18.87 ± 0.16 °C, a difference of 0.39 °C. Both differences are well within the manufacturer-specified temperature control accuracy of ±1.5 °C (at ≥20 °C), confirming near-identical thermal performance of the two chambers (Fig. S2).

Environmental parameters, including air temperature, root-zone temperature, relative humidity, and PPF were collected at 30-min intervals using calibrated sensors (LightScout Quantum Light sensor and external soil temperature sensor, Spectrum Technologies, Inc., USA) and data loggers with integrated temperature and humidity sensors (WatchDog 1000 Series Micro Stations, Spectrum Technologies, Inc., USA).

During the experiment, control chamber day/night air temperature averaged 25.0 ± 0.3/17.7 ± 0.3 °C, while the heat treatment chamber averaged 35.7 ± 0.3/31.5 ± 0.1 °C. Root-zone temperatures consistently measured 2-3 °C lower than air temperatures, with average day/night values of 22.3 ± 0.3/14.8 ± 0.4 °C in the control and 33.5 ± 0.6/29.9 ± 0.4 °C in the heat treatment environment. Relative humidity levels were recorded at 70.0 ± 1.5/73.2 ± 1.6% (day/night) in the control and 59.5 ± 2.0/65.3 ± 0.8% in the heat treatment chamber. Average PPF measurements were 195.1 ± 5.7 μmol m−2 s−1 in the control and 226.8 ± 9.9 μmol m−2 s−1 in the heat treatment chamber [20].

The deviations between the nominal setpoints and the measured temperatures - 4.3 °C below setpoint for the heat treatment chamber (daytime) and 2.3 °C below setpoint for the control chamber (nighttime) - are attributed to setpoint-dependent chamber performance. Pre-experiment monitoring conducted under an intermediate setpoint of 35 °C confirmed near-exact temperature achievement (35.04 ± 0.14 °C and 35.00 ± 0.08 °C in each chamber), indicating that the chambers perform with high accuracy at mid-range operating conditions. The larger deviations observed in the main experiment are consistent with reduced heating and cooling capacity as setpoints approach the upper (40 °C) and lower ends of the manufacturer-specified operating range (15-40 °C; accuracy ±1.5 °C at ≥20 °C). All results are therefore interpreted based on the measured temperatures throughout this study.

Non-destructive phenotyping using multispectral 3D scanner

For non-destructive phenotypic data acquisition, a phenotyping workstation (TraitFinder, W 2.5 × L 0.6 × H 2.0 m, Phenospex, The Netherlands) equipped with dual multispectral 3D laser scanners (PlantEye F600, Phenospex, The Netherlands) was employed. This station was placed in the same research facility as the growth chambers, with ambient temperature maintained at approximately 25 °C during measurement periods. For each phenotyping session, plants were transferred directly from the growth chamber to the adjacent workstation, and scanning was initiated within approximately 5 min of removal, minimizing the duration of exposure to the measurement environment prior to data acquisition.

High-throughput phenotyping was performed at 2-day intervals (Days 3, 5, 7, 10, 12, and 14 after treatment initiation) (Fig. 1B). The system simultaneously captured 3D structural information via LiDAR (central wavelength range 935-945 nm) and multispectral reflectance across four wavebands: red (R; 624-634 nm), lime green (G; 530-540 nm), blue (B; 465-485 nm), and near-infrared (NIR; 720-750 nm). Scans were conducted at a fixed distance of 0.7-0.8 m with a scanning speed of 6.7 cm/s, within the manufacturer-specified valid measurement range of 0.4-1.5 m. Scanning sessions were managed using HortControl software (Phenospex, The Netherlands).

The total scan area was subdivided into individual units using the block splitting function, configured as 2 rows × 4 columns per block, with the spatial dimensions of each unit and the starting position defined relative to the top-left corner of the scan block. This configuration ensured consistent spatial correspondence between each unit and a single plant across all scan sessions. To exclude plug tray substrate from parameter calculation, a plug tray height threshold (defined as ‘pot height' in HortControl) was applied to remove all 3D points below the base of the plant canopy, and a cut-off height was set to exclude points above the canopy boundary.

All scans were performed at similar times of day throughout the experimental period. The PlantEye F600 system employs 3D line scanning and multispectral imaging designed to operate independently of ambient lighting conditions, with in-built R, G, B, and NIR LED light sources providing direct illumination at a fixed working distance during each scan pass (Phenospex, The Netherlands). The system is factory-calibrated and equipped with internal temperature control. The manufacturer reports a sunlight resistance of 0.96% RMSPE (Root Mean Square Percentage Error) between measurements under complete darkness and 2530 μmol·m-2·s-1 direct sunlight, indicating negligible sensitivity to ambient light variation (Phenospex, The Netherlands). As reported for the same platform by Agarwal et al. [31], this design eliminates the need for further spectral calibrations between sessions, supporting cross-temporal comparability of spectral parameters throughout the study. Point-cloud quality was assessed by the operator through visual inspection of the rendered 3D output immediately after each scan acquisition. Scans judged to be inadequate due to incomplete canopy coverage or apparent sensor occlusion were repeated before proceeding to the next plant, ensuring that only scans of acceptable quality were retained for analysis.

Raw point cloud data were subsequently processed using Phena software (Phenospex, The Netherlands) through a six-stage pipeline comprising coordinate transformation, plant segmentation, mesh triangulation, canopy splitting, data merging, and parameter calculation. Phena automatically computed morpho-spectral parameters, including 3D leaf area, projected leaf area, digital biomass, plant height (maximum and average), canopy light penetration depth, normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI, (NIR - R)/(NIR + R)), normalized pigment chlorophyll index (NPCI, (R - B)/(R + B)), plant senescence reflectance index (PSRI, (R - G)/NIR), and green leaf index (GLI, (2 × G - R - B)/(2 × G + R + B)) (Fig. 1D). Processed data were exported as comma-separated value (.csv) files, with one row per plant per scan time point, and subsequently imported into R software (version 4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) for statistical analysis. Full definitions and calculation methods for parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Gas exchange measurements

Photosynthetic gas exchange parameters were measured at 1 week (Days 6-7) and 2 weeks (Days 13-14) using a LI-6800 Portable Photosynthesis System (Li-Cor Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA) (Fig. 1B). All measurements were conducted between 09:00 and 16:00. The youngest fully expanded leaves (third or fourth leaf) of three plants per genotype in both control and heat treatments were used for measurement. Measurement conditions were carefully controlled during gas exchange analysis. Leaf chamber temperature was matched to the respective growth conditions (25 °C for control, 40 °C for heat-treated plants), while other parameters were standardized as follows: light intensity of 300 μmol m−2 s−1 (built-in LED light source), reference CO2 concentration of 460 μmol mol−1, relative humidity of 60-65%, and airflow rate of 500 μmol s−1. After 3-5 min of stabilization, net assimilation rate (A), transpiration rate (Tr), intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci), and stomatal conductance (gsw) were recorded.

Destructive measurements

At Day 10, three plants per genotype from each treatment group were harvested for destructive analysis (Fig. 1B). Shoots and root systems were separated at the root-hypocotyl junction, and shoot fresh weights were immediately recorded. Individual leaves and intact root balls were carefully extracted and photographed in a standardized imaging environment using a photobox (L 63 × W 63 × H 63 cm, Z60 LED, ZENITH, Taiwan) with overhead LED illumination. High-resolution digital images were captured under standardized lighting conditions using a digital camera mounted at a fixed position. Image processing and analysis utilized ImageJ Fiji software (version 1.54f, a calibration ratio of 3025 pixels:1 cm2) to quantify total leaf area and root surface area. Root fresh weights were measured after washing.

Samples were then oven-dried at 80 °C for 48 h in a forced-air oven (Double-Doors Oven, Chaoli Technology Corp.) and weighed to determine dry weights. Non-destructive estimation of leaf chlorophyll content utilized a SPAD meter (SPAD-502 Plus, Konica Minolta, Japan). Measurements were taken at three positions on three fully expanded leaves per plant, with values averaged to obtain a representative SPAD reading for each plant.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-way ANOVA tested the effects of genotype, treatment, and their interaction for each parameter. PCA was applied to centered and scaled data from all measured parameters. For the PCA analysis, all morphological and spectral parameters were incorporated, with the first three principal components (PC1, PC2, PC3) retained for subsequent analysis as they consistently explained over 75% of the total variance across all time points. Results

Visual assessment of heat stress damage

Visual scores were evaluated throughout the 14-day treatment period using a 5-point scale (Fig. 2), where scores ranged from 5 (no visible heat damage) to 1 (>2/3 of leaves damaged or plant death). Heat stress significantly reduced visual scores compared to control plants at all time points (p < 0.001, Fig. 3A). Control plants maintained scores between 4.30 and 4.96 throughout the experiment, while heat-stressed plants declined from 4.55 ± 0.04 (at 3 days after treatment (DAT)) to 3.96 (14 DAT).

Visual and morphological responses to heat stress (A) Temporal dynamics of visual scores under control and heat treatments, (B) Morphological responses of four genotypes. ∗∗∗ indicates significance at p < 0.001.

Heat stress caused rapid yellowing and abscission of lower leaves during the initial 3-5 days, with scores declining to the 4 range (mild stress: <1/3 of leaves affected). However, no plant mortality occurred and all genotypes maintained scores above 3, indicating damage remained within mild to moderate levels. Individual genotype trajectories revealed substantial variation, with some maintaining scores near 4.5 while others declined below 4.0 by 14 DAT, suggesting differential heat tolerance among genotypes (Fig. 3B).

Representative genotypes demonstrated distinct visual responses. Ccab10 (Wongyo20048ho) maintained a relatively compact architecture with better leaf color retention despite lower leaf yellowing. Ccab13 (Bulam3ho) exhibited lower leaf chlorosis with partial abscission, confirming biomass reduction. Ccab17 (HV-472) showed the most pronounced lower leaf yellowing and wilting. Ccab03 (Wongyo20036ho) displayed chlorosis symptoms even under control conditions but maintained biomass under heat stress (Fig. 3B).

Growth and biomass responses to heat stress

Analysis of variance for growth parameters

Two-way ANOVA revealed highly significant genotype effects on all measured parameters (p < 0.001, Table 2), indicating substantial genetic variation among the 18 Chinese cabbage genotypes. Heat treatment significantly affected shoot fresh weight (M_SFW), root fresh weight (M_RFW), total fresh weight (M_FW), shoot dry weight (M_SDW), root dry weight (M_RDW), total dry weight (M_DW), root area (P_RA), and leaf mass per area (LMA) (p < 0.001), as well as dry matter content (DM) (p < 0.05). Notably, leaf number (M_NL) and leaf area (P_LA) were not significantly affected by heat stress. Significant genotype × heat treatment interactions occurred for M_SFW, M_FW, LMA (p < 0.01), and DM (p < 0.05), confirming genotype-specific responses.

Biomass reduction patterns under heat stress

Heat stress significantly reduced most growth parameters at 10 DAT (Fig. 4A). Shoot fresh weight declined from 5.59 g (control) to 4.47 g (heat), representing a 20% reduction. Root fresh weight showed a more pronounced reduction from 1.03 g to 0.55 g (47% decrease). Similarly, shoot dry weight decreased from 0.71 g to 0.57 g (20% reduction), while root dry weight declined from 0.08 g to 0.05 g (38% reduction). Total fresh and dry weights followed similar patterns.

Growth of Chinese cabbage genotypes under heat stress at 10 days after treatment. (A) Treatment effect, (B) Genotype comparison. Values are means ± SE.

Leaf number remained stable at 8-9 leaves regardless of treatment, while leaf area showed minimal change (147-152 cm2), confirming that heat stress primarily affected biomass accumulation rather than leaf initiation or expansion. Root area exhibited a significant reduction from 64.0 cm2 to 41.8 cm2 (35% decrease).

Genotypic comparison revealed substantial variation in stress responses (Fig. 4B). Some genotypes (e.g., Ccab03) maintained relatively higher biomass under heat stress, while others (e.g., Ccab17) showed more pronounced reductions, suggesting varying heat tolerance mechanisms.

A critical finding was the disproportionately greater reduction in root biomass relative to shoot biomass. Root biomass reductions (38-47%) substantially exceeded shoot biomass reductions (20%), suggesting that root systems may be particularly sensitive to the experimental heat stress conditions, although the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully characterized. Root-zone temperatures (33.5/29.9 °C day/night) were 2 to 3 °C lower than air temperatures (35.7/31.5 °C), meaning that the greater relative reduction in root biomass cannot be attributed solely to direct thermal exposure, and may instead reflect indirect effects such as differential carbon allocation patterns or heat-induced disruption of hormonal signaling between shoot and root systems. This differential response has important implications for plant water and nutrient acquisition under heat stress conditions.

Photosynthetic responses to heat stress

Gas exchange measurement at 14 DAT revealed significant physiological responses to heat stress across all genotypes (Fig. 5). Temporal phenotyping of representative genotypes revealed distinct response patterns (Fig. S3). Morphological parameters showed progressive changes. 3D leaf area in control plants increased to 10,000–30,000 mm2 (14 DAT) across genotypes, with Ccab17 showing the highest values. Under heat stress, most genotypes exhibited suppressed leaf expansion. However, Ccab03 displayed a paradoxical response, with heat-stressed plants achieving comparable or greater leaf area than controls by 14 DAT (∼25,000 mm2), consistent with its anomalous growth response.

Spectral indices provided complementary physiological information. NDVI patterns differed among genotypes. Ccab10 maintained high, stable values (∼0.6) across both treatments, indicating sustained chlorophyll content. Ccab03 showed increasing NDVI under heat stress during days 3-7 before stabilizing at 0.4-0.45. Surface angle measurements indicated altered leaf architecture under heat stress. Ccab17 exhibited a pronounced decline in surface angle, reflecting a shift toward more erect leaf posture, while other genotypes maintained relatively stable surface angle values, suggesting less pronounced architectural reorganization under the applied heat conditions. The temporal phenotyping approach revealed that genotypic differences became increasingly apparent over time, with maximal divergence at 10-14 DAT, indicating that screening for heat tolerance requires extended evaluation periods to capture cumulative stress effects. To comprehensively evaluate heat stress responses and tolerance levels across all 18 genotypes, we employed PCA to integrate multiple phenotypic dimensions across the entire treatment period.

PCA of temporal responses

PCA of 14 morphological and spectral traits demonstrated clear differentiation between control and heat-treated Chinese cabbage seedlings throughout the treatment period (Fig. 6A). The separation between treatment groups was most pronounced at 3 DAT (ΔC = 3.27) and remained distinct until 12 DAT (ΔC = 2.82), before showing potential acclimation by 14 DAT (ΔC = 1.41). Shift in parameter contributions over time

Analysis of parameter contributions revealed two distinct phases of heat stress response (Fig. 6B). During the early response phase (3-5 DAT), morphological parameters dominated the separation between treatment groups, with digital biomass, Hue value, and NDVI being the primary contributors (>10% each). This morphological dominance explained the maximum ΔC value of 3.27 observed at 3 DAT, indicating that initial stress responses were primarily manifested through changes in plant morphology.

As the treatment period progressed, the relative importance of traits shifted. By the later phase (10-14 DAT), spectral indices became more influential, with NDVI, Hue, and PSRI emerging as the dominant contributors (>11% each).

Validation of PCA-based classification through destructive measurements

To validate the PCA-based classification approach, we compared reduction percentages in growth and phenotyping parameters across the three stability groups (Fig. S6). This analysis tests whether phenotypic stability (measured by PC distance) correlates with actual stress tolerance (measured by biomass retention).

Growth and biomass parameter validation

Clear relationships emerged between phenotypic stability and biomass retention (Fig. S6A). Group 1 (most stable, low PC distance) showed minimal reductions, with shoot fresh weight declining by 17.9%, shoot dry weight by 15.9%, and LMA by 17.3%. Group 3 (least stable, high PC distance) exhibited substantially greater reductions, with shoot fresh weight declining by 30.4%, shoot dry weight by 31.2%, and LMA by 27.5%. The progressive increase in reduction rates demonstrates strong concordance between phenotypic stability metrics and actual growth performance.

Root biomass showed severe reductions across all groups with distinctive patterns. Root fresh weight reductions increased progressively with Group 1 (37.4%), Group 2 (43.2%), and Group 3 (47.4%). Root dry weight showed similarly high reductions with 45.6%, 45.0%, 56.1% for Groups 1, 2, 3, respectively. Notably, even the most stable genotypes (Group 1) experienced root fresh weight reductions exceeding 37%, confirming that root systems are universally vulnerable regardless of overall tolerance classification. The disproportionate impact on roots versus shoots (37-56% vs. 16-31%) highlights a fundamental physiological constraint.

Phenotyping parameter validation

Phenotyping parameters validated the classification while revealing parameter-specific sensitivities (Fig. S6B). 3D leaf area reduction increased systematically with 10.2% (Group 1), 15.8% (Group 2), 20.5% (Group 3), demonstrating that stable genotypes maintained leaf expansion better. Digital biomass showed the smallest reductions (3.6%, 7.4%, 14.5%), though following expected patterns. The relatively small digital biomass reductions compared to actual biomass suggest 3D imaging captures canopy volume rather than tissue density.

Surface angle exhibited the largest reductions (21.7%, 29.3%, 43.7% for Groups 1, 2, 3, respectively), representing the most sensitive phenotypic indicator. The dramatic 43.7% reduction in Group 3 indicates substantial canopy architectural modifications in sensitive genotypes.

Spectral indices showed progressive deterioration across the stability groups. NDVI reduction increased from 7.6% in Group 1 to 24.7% in Group 3. GLI reduction scaled dramatically, with Group 1 showing 17.2%, Group 2 showing 24.1%, and Group 3 showing 36.5%, meaning Group 3 exhibited more than double the reduction of Group 1, confirming severe green tissue loss in sensitive genotypes.

This validation demonstrates that PC distance effectively stratifies genotypes by heat tolerance, as stability groups derived from phenotypic data strongly predict biomass performance. The approach successfully integrates multiple phenotypic dimensions, capturing both morphological and spectral responses.

Discussion

Heat stress responses in Chinese cabbage seedlings: traditional approaches

Visual assessment and biomass reduction patterns

Traditional destructive sampling and visual assessment revealed severe impacts on Chinese cabbage seedling growth throughout the 14-day heat treatment period. Visual scoring showed a consistent decline in plant condition under the measured heat treatment conditions of 35.7/31.5 °C (day/night) (p < 0.001), with control plants maintaining scores of 4.30-4.96 compared to heat-stressed plants that declined to 3.96 by 14 DAT. This visual decline was accompanied by characteristic symptoms, including progressive leaf yellowing, abscission in the lower canopy, and senescence responses consistent with thermal stress responses documented in previous studies [32,33].

The biomass measurements revealed a critical distinction in heat stress impacts between shoot and root systems, consistent with observations across multiple crop species [11]. While shoot fresh weight declined by approximately 20% under heat stress, root fresh weight exhibited far more severe reductions of 47%, representing a disproportionately greater reduction in root biomass relative to shoot biomass. Root area also showed substantial reductions (35% decrease from 64.0 to 41.8 cm2), indicating that heat-induced damage encompasses not only root biomass reductions but also modifications to root system architecture and absorptive capacity.

This differential response between root and shoot systems reflects multiple interconnected physiological processes. Although root-zone temperatures (33.5/29.9 °C day/night) were 2 to 3 °C lower than air temperatures (35.7/31.5 °C), the greater relative reduction in root biomass suggests that indirect mechanisms may contribute substantially to the observed root response. These include reduced carbon supply to roots resulting from heat-impaired photosynthesis coupled with preferential carbon allocation to shoots under stress, as well as heat-induced disruption of hormonal signaling pathways that regulate root development. The resulting compromised root function further restricts shoot water and nutrient supply, creating a cycle wherein degraded roots become progressively less capable of supporting aboveground demands [9,[10], [11]].

Photosynthetic responses and non-stomatal limitations

Gas exchange measurements at 14 DAT revealed a complex and physiologically contradictory response pattern under the measured heat treatment conditions of 35.7/31.5 °C (day/night). Net photosynthesis declined by approximately 32% under heat stress (from 7.11 to 4.84 μmol m−2 s−1), indicating substantial impairment of carbon assimilation capacity. However, this photosynthetic decline occurred while transpiration rate increased four-fold (from 0.001 to 0.004 mol m−2 s−1) and intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci) increased from 305.8 to 356.7 μmol mol−1.

This pattern is consistent with thermoregulatory responses expected under well-watered heat stress conditions. The measured treatment temperature of 35.7 °C substantially exceeds the crop's growth disruption threshold of 25 °C, and the observed transpiration response differs from the stomatal closure responses typically observed under combined drought-heat stress. Under well-watered conditions, Chinese cabbage appears to prioritize leaf cooling through increased transpiration over maintaining photosynthetic efficiency, a thermoregulatory strategy documented previously in heat-tolerant crops [8,32].

The elevated intercellular CO2 concentration despite greater stomatal opening reveals the critical role of non-stomatal limitations in heat stress responses. This pattern demonstrates that CO2 availability is not the limiting factor, but rather the photosynthetic apparatus itself, particularly Rubisco activase and Calvin cycle enzymes, is damaged under elevated temperatures [5]. The accumulated intercellular CO2 likely results from both reduced carbon fixation capacity and increased photorespiration and respiration under high temperatures [6].

Water use efficiency was severely impaired by heat stress, declining by 84% (from 8760 to 1388 μmol CO2·mmol−1 H2O), reflecting the cost of utilizing transpiration as a thermoregulatory mechanism. This substantial trade-off highlights the physiological constraints Chinese cabbage seedlings face when adapting to heat stress under well-watered conditions. Genotypic variation in photosynthetic responses was evident, with some commercial varieties (Ccab13-20) showing particularly pronounced increases in Ci under heat stress.

Phenotyping technology as a complementary tool: correlation analysis and validation

Validation of non-destructive parameters through correlation analysis

Our correlation analysis between non-destructive phenotyping parameters and destructive measurements provides strong evidence that image-based phenotyping can effectively replace or complement traditional screening methods [16,34,35]. The 3D leaf area measurements derived from the PlantEye F600 scanner showed remarkably strong positive correlations (r > 0.8) with shoot fresh weight across both control and heat-stressed conditions, validating the utility of image-based morphological assessment. Similarly, digital biomass demonstrated strong correlations with actual biomass measurements.

Particularly noteworthy was the strong correlation between image-based root surface area estimates and actual root fresh weight (R2 = 0.698, p < 0.001) and root dry weight (R2 = 0.501, p < 0.001) across treatments. This finding has important implications for high-throughput breeding applications, as it demonstrates that root development can be rapidly quantified from intact root ball surface images without the labor-intensive excavation and measurement procedures traditionally required.

Visual scoring demonstrated strong positive correlations with spectral parameters including P_GLI, P_Hue, and P_NDVI. Interestingly, visual scoring of aboveground symptoms also correlated strongly with image-based root surface area estimates (P_RA), suggesting that visible shoot stress symptoms reflect underlying root health patterns. This finding suggests that aboveground stress symptoms captured by visual scoring are indicative of the overall plant stress status, including root system condition.

Phenotyping parameters as stress indicators: advantages over traditional methods

The integration of morphological and spectral phenotyping parameters offers several critical advantages over traditional destructive measurement approaches. Image-based phenotyping enables rapid, non-destructive assessment of large plant populations without requiring harvest and destructive analysis, fundamentally accelerating breeding cycle efficiency. A single 3D multispectral scan captures multiple morphological and spectral parameters simultaneously, including 3D leaf area, plant height, surface angle, and multiple spectral indices (NDVI, PSRI, GLI, Hue, Saturation, Lightness), in less than 1 min per plant, compared to hours of labor required for destructive sampling and analysis [36,37].

The strong correlations between these non-destructive parameters and destructive biomass measurements validate their use as reliable indicators for screening heat tolerance. Moreover, the combination of morphological and spectral data captures complementary physiological information. While morphological parameters reflect growth and architectural responses, spectral indices reveal information about pigmentation status and senescence processes. This dual-perspective assessment provides more comprehensive phenotypic characterization than any single measurement approach [38,39].

Among phenotyping parameters, surface angle showed the largest response to heat stress, with reductions ranging from 21.7% in stable genotypes to 43.7% in sensitive genotypes, confirming this parameter as the most sensitive indicator of heat stress severity. In Chinese cabbage, heat stress induces rapid wilting and abscission of lower leaves while upper leaves adopt a more vertical orientation, both of which contribute to a net reduction in mean surface angle. This architectural reorganization reflects a combination of heat-induced lower leaf deterioration and adaptive upper leaf reorientation to reduce direct radiation interception, integrating multiple physiological responses including water status, cellular turgor maintenance, and radiation management, and can be captured rapidly through non-destructive 3D scanning.

Spectral indices, particularly GLI, showed progressive deterioration across stability groups (from 17.2% reduction in Group 1 to 36.5% in Group 3), with Group 3 exhibiting more than double the reduction of Group 1, demonstrating that spectral parameters effectively stratify genotypes by heat tolerance severity [40].

The validation of high-throughput phenotyping methods for assessing heat tolerance represents an important methodological advancement with direct applications to breeding programs. The consistency of correlations between image-derived traits and traditional destructive measurements aligns with recent findings in other crop species, confirming the broad applicability of this approach [23]. This enables screening of much larger populations than traditional methods allow, while maintaining measurement accuracy [17,41,42].

Spectral indices as physiological indicators: NDVI, GLI, and PSRI under heat stress

While NDVI is frequently used as a proxy for photosynthetic capacity [43], our results demonstrate that under heat stress conditions, the relationship between NDVI and actual growth performance is more complex than commonly assumed. NDVI showed strong correlations with chlorophyll content (SPAD values, r = 0.72) and visual scores (r = 0.60), confirming that NDVI accurately reflects pigment status and overall plant health appearance. However, NDVI demonstrated limited direct relationships with biomass parameters, indicating its primary role as an indicator of pigment content rather than direct biomass production capacity under heat stress.

This apparent disconnect aligns with recent findings [23,44], suggesting that photosynthetic apparatus damage can occur without immediate changes in chlorophyll content. The discrepancy may reflect energy allocation to stress protection mechanisms rather than growth, or impaired carbon partitioning despite maintained photosynthetic machinery [45,46]. This highlights the importance of integrating multiple complementary spectral and morphological parameters rather than relying on any single index for comprehensive heat tolerance assessment.

Comprehensive assessment of heat tolerance: PCA-based classification

PCA as an integrative framework for multidimensional phenotypic assessment

The application of PCA to 14 morphological and spectral traits demonstrated that PCA effectively integrates the complex, multidimensional nature of heat stress responses that would be impossible to characterize through any single trait alone [20,21,23,47,48]. The first two principal components consistently explained 62-68% of total variance, with PC1 explaining 36.2-45.7% (primarily associated with morphological traits) and PC2 accounting for 20.4-30.8% (more closely correlated with spectral parameters).

Notably, Chinese cabbage demonstrated substantially greater phenotypic divergence under heat stress compared to pepper seedlings evaluated under the same conditions [20]. In the PCA space, control and heat-treated Chinese cabbage plants showed maximum separation at 3 DAT (ΔC = 3.27), which progressively decreased through 12 DAT (ΔC = 2.82) and 14 DAT (ΔC = 1.41), indicating rapid initial stress response and subsequent acclimation. In contrast, pepper seedlings exhibited considerably smaller separation distances, with maximum separation occurring later at 10 DAT (ΔC = 2.05).

This differential response pattern reflects physiological differences in their thermal tolerance. Pepper's higher temperature optimum (25-30 °C) provides a broader thermal buffer against elevated temperatures. Chinese cabbage, with its optimum at considerably lower temperatures, lacks this thermal plasticity, resulting in rapid phenotypic divergence when exposed to 40/35 °C. This difference illustrates how species-specific thermal preferences fundamentally shape the intensity and kinetics of stress response patterns detected through multidimensional phenotypic analysis.

Euclidean Distance as a quantitative measure of phenotypic stability

Euclidean distance in three-dimensional principal component space (PC1-PC3) provided a reliable, quantitative metric for assessing heat stress response severity across genotypes. K-means clustering identified three distinct phenotypic stability groups: Group 1 (Low stress response, n = 9, mean PC distances 2.2-3.2), Group 2 (Moderate stress response, n = 7, distances 3.8-4.7), and Group 3 (High stress response, n = 2, distances 5.0-5.4).

The PC distance-based classification framework showed clear correspondence with actual growth performance. Genotypes with shorter PC distances (Group 1) exhibited superior biomass retention under heat stress, with shoot fresh weight declining by only 17.9% versus 30.4% in Group 3. However, the analysis revealed that phenotypic stability and productivity under heat stress represent largely independent traits. Root biomass reductions were severe across all groups (37-56% for fresh weight), but did not differ between stability groups as clearly as shoot responses.

This independence of stability and productivity represents a critical biological insight. Rather than a simple linear relationship where greater stability invariably correlates with superior growth performance, our results reveal distinct tolerance strategies. The Euclidean distance metric effectively captures genotype-level differences in stress response magnitude without directly predicting productivity outcomes.

Heat stress response patterns based on phenotypic stability and growth performance

The four response groups identified by combining phenotypic stability (PC distance) and growth performance (biomass maintenance) represent distinct phenotypic patterns. Phenotypic stability and growth performance do not consistently co-vary across genotypes, suggesting they may reflect partially dissociable regulatory mechanisms. This observation aligns with extensive literature demonstrating that stress tolerance frequently comes with trade-offs in growth rates and productivity [13,[49], [50], [51]], although natural variations exist in the magnitude of these trade-offs across genotypes [52]. The distinct response patterns identified enable targeted genotype selection aligned with specific production goals and environmental conditions.

Response Pattern 1 genotypes, combining phenotypic stability with high biomass retention, represent ideal candidates for conventional breeding programs targeting heat-resilient, productive cultivars. The maintenance of both phenotypic stability and productivity under heat stress may reflect the coordinated upregulation of protective mechanisms, including HSPs that stabilize cellular proteins and membranes, and antioxidant enzyme systems (SOD, CAT, APX) that limit reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, enabling sustained photosynthetic activity and carbon assimilation under stress [32,53]. Accumulation of compatible solutes such as proline and soluble sugars may further contribute to osmotic adjustment and membrane stabilization in these genotypes [13].

Response Pattern 2 genotypes, while demonstrating excellent phenotypic stability, show limited biomass production under heat stress. These materials could be valuable for environments where survival during temporary heat stress is prioritized, or for fundamental studies investigating stability mechanisms independent of growth responses. The combination of phenotypic stability with reduced productivity may reflect a strategy of prioritizing cellular protection at the expense of growth, with resources redirected toward stress defense mechanisms rather than biomass accumulation. This resource allocation trade-off under heat stress has been documented across multiple species [49,50].

Response Pattern 3 genotypes, which tolerate substantial phenotypic change while maintaining productivity, suggest an alternative adaptive strategy based on phenotypic plasticity rather than homeostasis. The pronounced surface angle reduction observed in these genotypes reflects a shift toward more erect upper leaf posture following heat-induced lower leaf abscission, which may serve as an adaptive mechanism to reduce direct radiation interception and limit heat accumulation in remaining photosynthetically active tissue [32]. This morphological adjustment, combined with the early transient NDVI increase observed in representative genotypes such as Ccab03, suggests that canopy restructuring enabled the preservation of photosynthetic capacity in surviving leaves despite overall phenotypic instability. The ability to sustain biomass production through rapid morphological reorganization represents a distinct tolerance strategy that may be particularly valuable in warming climates characterized by sustained moderate temperatures rather than acute heat events. This reorganization may be facilitated by ABA-mediated hormonal reprogramming that accelerates stress acclimation [13,32].

Response Pattern 4 genotypes, showing neither phenotypic stability nor productivity under heat stress, represent the least favorable phenotypic combination for heat tolerance breeding.

The classification approach employed here integrates multiple phenotypic measures rather than relying on single-trait evaluation, revealing genotypic diversity that would be obscured by conventional univariate selection criteria. The stability-growth trade-off evident between Response Patterns 1, 2, and 3 emphasizes that heat tolerance evaluation must incorporate multiple relevant endpoints to fully characterize genotype responses and identify materials suitable for diverse agricultural applications. It should be noted that the mechanistic interpretations proposed above are inferred from phenotypic observations and existing literature and remain speculative in the absence of direct biochemical measurements. Integration of oxidative stress markers (e.g., MDA, SOD activity) and compatible solute quantification (e.g., proline, soluble sugars) in future studies would provide direct biochemical validation of these hypothesized mechanisms for each tolerance strategy.

Integration of temporal and phenotypic dimensions

The combination of temporal analysis and PCA-based classification revealed important patterns in how genotypes navigate the transition from acute heat shock to acclimation. Genotypes maintaining consistently short PC distances throughout the treatment period (e.g., Ccab10, Ccab09, Ccab08) demonstrated inherent heat stability with minimal stress-induced phenotypic change. In contrast, other genotypes displayed dynamic temporal patterns. Ccab20 exhibited initially long distances that shortened considerably by 14 DAT, indicating substantial acclimation capacity, while Ccab05 and Ccab17 showed progressively increasing distances, suggesting cumulative stress damage without effective adaptation.

This temporal heterogeneity in PC distance trajectories demonstrates that Euclidean distance captures not only the magnitude of stress response but also genotype-specific temporal patterns of response and adaptation. The integration of temporal dynamics with phenotypic stability classification provides a more complete picture of heat tolerance mechanisms than cross-sectional analysis at fixed time points [54,55].

Temporal dynamics of heat stress responses and adaptation mechanisms

Distinct adaptation phases revealed by temporal analysis

The temporal phenotyping approach enabled the identification of distinct physiological phases in heat stress response that traditional endpoint analyses would miss [33,56]. The maximum separation between control and heat-treated plants occurred at 3 DAT (ΔC = 3.27), indicating this represents an optimal time point for early screening of heat tolerance. The subsequent gradual decrease in separation through 12 DAT (ΔC = 2.82) to 14 DAT (ΔC = 1.41) reflects progressive acclimation in many genotypes.

This pattern aligns with established heat stress response theory describing transitions from acute shock responses to longer-term acclimation [53,[57], [58], [59]]. According to Wahid et al. [29], short-term heat stress primarily triggers immediate protective responses involving stomatal closure, osmolyte accumulation, and emergency protein synthesis, while prolonged heat exposure activates more sustainable acclimation processes involving altered gene expression, metabolic reorganization, and structural modifications.

Shift from morphological to physiological parameters during adaptation

The temporal phenotyping pattern was accompanied by a shift in the dominant parameters characterizing heat stress responses. Early responses (3-5 DAT) were primarily characterized by morphological parameters (digital biomass, Hue value, and NDVI, each contributing >10% to PC variance), reflecting rapid growth suppression and initial pigment changes. By the later phase (10-14 DAT), spectral indices became more influential, with NDVI, Hue, and PSRI emerging as dominant contributors (>11% each).

This transition suggests that initial heat stress responses manifest primarily through altered growth and canopy architecture, representing emergency reactions to minimize heat exposure through modified structure [18,29]. The subsequent dominance of spectral indices indicates that later adaptation involves more complex physiological adjustments to photosynthetic machinery, pigment composition, and cellular structure [60,61].

Visual observation documented this transition during the heat treatment period. Lower leaves showed progressive yellowing, wilting, and abscission during initial treatment (3-5 DAT), while new leaves developing later in the treatment period (10-14 DAT) exhibited fewer stress symptoms, suggesting successful acclimation in at least some genotypes.

Crop-specific temporal patterns and breeding implications

Our findings in Chinese cabbage, where maximum heat stress response separation occurred at 3 DAT, differ from those reported for pepper, where maximum separation occurred at 10 DAT [20]. This temporal difference may reflect distinct crop life strategies. Chinese cabbage, as a cool-season leafy vegetable, exhibits faster initial responses to heat stress, potentially reflecting evolutionary adaptation to cooler climates where rapid stress recognition and response confer adaptive value. These crop-specific temporal patterns have important implications for breeding strategies. For Chinese cabbage, early screening at 3 DAT may be most efficient for identifying heat-tolerant genotypes, while pepper breeding programs might benefit from extended evaluation periods to properly capture adaptive potential. The distinct timelines of heat response between these crops demonstrate that breeding programs must carefully consider these temporal differences and target the specific physiological adaptations underlying each crop's stress response.

Implications for breeding and screening methodologies

Multi-stage breeding framework incorporating phenotyping technology

Our findings establish a practical, phased breeding strategy that leverages high-throughput phenotyping at early developmental stages while maintaining confirmation through traditional physiological measurements. We recommend a multi-stage selection approach beginning with large-scale seedling screening utilizing indoor phenotyping and PC distance-based classification. Selected promising candidates would then progress to secondary evaluation through destructive measurements and gas exchange analysis to confirm biomass maintenance and photosynthetic efficiency under heat stress. The final validation stage would involve field evaluation under realistic growing conditions to ensure mature plant performance across varying environmental scenarios.

This stepwise approach optimizes selection efficiency by progressively narrowing the candidate pool while ensuring that selected genotypes maintain superior performance across different developmental stages and environmental conditions. The integration of PC distance-based phenotypic assessment at early stages enables rapid screening of large populations with minimal resource investment, while later-stage validation confirms that laboratory-identified tolerance mechanisms translate to field performance.

Optimized temporal sampling for practical implementation

For optimal breeding program implementation, we recommend focusing on three key time points. 3 DAT captures maximum separation between stress responses and provides an opportunity for rapid tolerance identification. 7 DAT allows intermediate assessment that captures the transition between acute and acclimation responses. 14 DAT enables evaluation of acclimation capacity and longer-term stress tolerance. This targeted approach captures the essential dynamics of heat stress response while minimizing resource requirements.

The combination of early (3 DAT) and late (14 DAT) measurements is particularly valuable, as it enables identification of genotypes with both immediate heat resistance and effective acclimation potential. This two-time-point evaluation strategy efficiently identifies genotypes suitable for diverse thermal environments, encompassing both acute and chronic heat stress scenarios.

Methodological limitations and future research directions

While this study demonstrates the utility of PC distance-based classification and phenotyping validation, several experimental limitations require consideration. First, the use of two separate walk-in chambers - one for each treatment - without chamber rotation or independent experimental repeats introduces a potential confounding between temperature treatment and chamber identity. However, pre-experiment monitoring conducted under identical setpoint conditions (35 °C day/18 °C night) for four days before plant introduction confirmed near-identical thermal performance between the two chambers, with between-chamber differences of 0.04 °C during the daytime and 0.39 °C during the nighttime, both well within the manufacturer-specified control accuracy of ±1.5 °C. These data substantially mitigate chamber confounding concerns, though effects arising from differential plant heat loads under the distinct operating conditions of the actual experiment cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies should incorporate chamber rotation designs to fully eliminate this potential source of bias.

Second, a pre-treatment phenotypic baseline scan at 0 DAT was not conducted in this study, meaning that the ΔC value observed at 3 DAT cannot be fully partitioned between treatment-induced responses and any pre-existing phenotypic variation among genotypes. To minimize this risk, plants were assigned to treatments following uniform seedling selection and tray randomization. Nonetheless, the 3 DAT data point should be interpreted as the earliest observed response rather than as a change from a confirmed identical baseline. Future studies should incorporate a 0 DAT scan as standard practice to enable rigorous quantification of early stress-induced phenotypic change.

Third, while the in-built LED illumination of the PlantEye F600 system provides consistent lighting conditions across scan sessions and the ratio-based computation of vegetation indices reduces sensitivity to absolute intensity variation, inter-session spectral comparability of single-band absolute values cannot be fully guaranteed in the absence of external calibration targets. Interpretations of spectral parameters in this study should therefore be made in the context of relative differences between treatments and time points rather than as absolute reflectance values. Future studies employing this platform could consider additional validation using external spectral reference panels to further strengthen the inter-session comparability of spectral data.

Fourth, the effective sensor-to-canopy scanning distance decreased marginally over the 14-day treatment period as plant canopy height increased. The scanning distance of 0.7-0.8 m remained within the manufacturer-specified valid measurement range of 0.4-1.5 m throughout the experiment (Phenospex, The Netherlands). Spatial resolution is known to vary with scanning distance; however, given the small canopy height increments at the seedling stage, the associated resolution changes are unlikely to introduce systematic biases in between-genotype or between-treatment comparisons. Interpretations of absolute structural trait values across time points should nonetheless account for this potential source of variation.

Fifth, although plants were transferred from the growth chamber to the phenotyping workstation and scanned within approximately 5 min, brief exposure to the cooler workstation environment (approximately 25 °C) prior to scanning may have introduced transient physiological changes. Structural and morphological traits are unlikely to change meaningfully within this timeframe, and spectral indices reflecting chlorophyll content and pigmentation (NDVI, GLI, PSRI) are governed by biochemical states that change on timescales of hours to days. Nonetheless, the surface angle parameter, which reflects leaf orientation and turgor status, may be susceptible to partial recovery of leaf posture during the transfer period, and values for this parameter should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Sixth, biochemical markers of oxidative stress, including malondialdehyde (MDA) content and antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, APX), and compatible solutes such as proline and soluble sugars, were not measured in this study. These parameters are widely used to characterize genotype-specific heat stress responses at the cellular level [13,32] and would have provided direct biochemical validation for the four tolerance strategies identified through phenotypic classification. Their inclusion in future studies would substantially strengthen the mechanistic interpretation of the response patterns described here.

Our controlled laboratory environment cannot replicate field conditions where heat stress occurs in combination with variable water availability, radiation intensity, and atmospheric humidity [62,63]. Additionally, the seedling-scale constraints limited detailed examination of root system architecture and functionality, though recent advances in root phenotyping systems offer opportunities for more comprehensive below-ground analysis [26,28]. Regarding the 3D phenotyping platform, potential measurement errors from reflective or transparent leaf surfaces remain an inherent consideration of overhead LiDAR-based scanning. A further technical limitation is the potential for leaf occlusion, whereby upper canopy leaves partially obstruct the scanner's detection of lower canopy layers. At the seedling stage evaluated in the present study, canopy closure was limited and multi-layered leaf overlap was minimal, reducing the practical impact of occlusion on parameter estimation. However, as plants develop denser canopies at later growth stages, occlusion-related underestimation of 3D leaf area and digital biomass may become more pronounced, and this should be considered when applying the present screening framework to more advanced developmental stages or to genotypes with characteristically dense rosette architectures. Technological advances in 3D imaging resolution and accuracy, or complementary measurement approaches such as thermal imaging, could further enhance future phenotyping evaluations.

While the present findings suggest the presence of diverse heat stress response patterns among Chinese cabbage genotypes, the underlying genetic and molecular mechanisms remain to be elucidated. The clear physiological divergence among genotypes, particularly the independence of phenotypic stability and biomass production, provides a foundation for integrated genomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses. Comparative analysis between genotypes showing distinct adaptation patterns could reveal the underlying genetic and molecular mechanisms controlling heat tolerance. Recent multi-omics studies have identified key heat tolerance modules warranting investigation in the context of our phenotypic classification [64,65].

Translational considerations and breeding application

The controlled environment design of this study provides methodological advantages for rigorous genotypic comparison by eliminating confounding from variable solar radiation, vapor pressure deficit, soil water content, and co-occurring stresses. However, field heat stress in East Asian summer production environments is typically accompanied by elevated solar radiation and soil water deficits, which may amplify or modify the stress responses observed under the well-watered seedling conditions applied here [62,63]. Additionally, seedling-stage tolerance does not necessarily predict performance at later developmental stages, as distinct growth stages may engage different heat response mechanisms. These considerations should be borne in mind when translating the genotypic rankings reported here to field performance predictions.

To contextualize the present findings within a practical breeding pipeline, we propose a four-stage heat tolerance breeding framework (Fig. S9). Stage 1 encompasses crossing and germplasm introduction to generate segregating populations. Stage 2 (Indoor Phenotyping; F2-F5/R1-R4) applies controlled environment heat stress treatment at 35-40 °C and uses high-throughput 3D multispectral phenotyping, image-based root surface area analysis, and complementary tools such as thermal imaging and chlorophyll fluorescence measurement for early heat tolerance selection. The PC distance-based classification framework developed in this study is directly applicable at this stage, enabling rapid and objective stratification of large segregating populations. Stage 3 bridges indoor and field evaluations, providing an intermediate assessment of genotype performance under semi-controlled or multi-environment conditions. Stage 4 (Field Phenotyping; F5-F9/R4-R7) encompasses destructive evaluation and uniformity assessment (F5/R4), yield, quality, and pest and disease resistance evaluation (F8/R6), and multi-location adaptation trials (F9/R7) to confirm agronomic performance under realistic and diverse growing conditions.

Within this framework, Response Pattern 1 genotypes identified in the present study, which combined phenotypic stability with high biomass retention under heat stress, represent the most promising candidates for advancement through the pipeline. These genotypes warrant prioritized evaluation in Stage 3 bridging trials and subsequent field-level validation to confirm whether seedling-stage stability and productivity translate to mature plant performance and agronomic yield under summer production conditions.

Conclusion

This integrated study demonstrates that traditional destructive measurements and advanced non-destructive phenotyping technology provide complementary information for comprehensive heat tolerance assessment in Chinese cabbage. Visual assessment, biomass measurement, and gas exchange analysis revealed severe heat stress impacts with distinctive root vulnerability. Phenotyping technology, validated through strong correlation analysis, offers high-throughput alternatives to labor-intensive destructive methods. PCA-based Euclidean distance analysis integrated these multiple dimensions into a quantitative classification system, identifying four distinct heat tolerance strategies. Temporal dynamics analysis revealed crop-specific response patterns with distinct acute and acclimation phases. These findings establish practical frameworks for accelerating heat-tolerant Chinese cabbage breeding while advancing a broader understanding of stress tolerance mechanisms in cool-season crops adapting to warming climates.
