Contextual Search in Principal-Agent Games: The Curse of Degeneracy
Yiding Feng, Mengfan Ma, Bo Peng, Zongqi Wan

Abstract
In this work, we introduce and study contextual search in general principal-agent games, where a principal repeatedly interacts with agents by offering contracts based on contextual information and historical feedback, without knowing the agents’ true costs or rewards. Our model generalizes classical contextual pricing by accommodating richer agent action spaces. Over T T rounds with d d -dimensional contexts, we establish an asymptotically tight exponential T 1 − Θ ⁡ ( 1 / d ) T^{1-\Theta(1/d)} bound in terms of the pessimistic Stackelberg regret , benchmarked against the best utility for the principal that is consistent with the observed feedback. We also establish a lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( T 1 2 − 1 2 ​ d ) \Omega(T^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2d}}) on the classic Stackelberg regret for principal-agent games, demonstrating a surprising double-exponential hardness separation from the contextual pricing problem (a.k.a, the principal-agent game with two actions), which is known to admit a near-optimal O ⁡ ( d ​ log ⁡ log ​ T ) O(d\log\log T) regret bound ( Kleinberg and Leighton 2003 ; Leme and Schneider 2018 ; Liu et al. 2021 ) . In particular, this double-exponential hardness separation occurs even in the special case with three actions and two-dimensional context. We identify that this significant increase in learning difficulty arises from a structural phenomenon that we call contextual action degeneracy , where adversarially chosen contexts can make some actions strictly dominated (and hence unincentivizable), blocking the principal’s ability to explore or learn about them, and fundamentally limiting learning progress.

Introduction

The
contextual pricing problem

(
Lobel et al. 2018
;
Leme and Schneider 2018
;
Cohen et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2021
)

provides a quintessential example of learning under partial feedback in a market setting. In this model, a seller (learner) repeatedly offers prices for products described by observed context vectors, and only
binary feedback
is received in each round – the buyer either purchases the product at the offered price or not. The buyer’s valuation is assumed to be an unknown linear function of the context, so the seller faces the challenge of
learning the buyer’s valuation
while setting prices. Regret is measured as the revenue loss relative to a clairvoyant seller who knows the buyer’s valuation in advance. A sequence of works culminating in
Liu et al. 2021
established that this pricing problem admits an exceedingly small optimal regret of order
O(d\log\log T+d\log d)
over
T

d
is the dimension of the context vector. In other words, with an optimal pricing strategy, the seller’s regret grows only double-logarithmically in
T
, a nearly negligible rate.

In this paper, we introduce a new model of
contextual search in principal-agent games
that extends the classical framework of contextual pricing to accommodate richer action spaces and information structure. In this setting, a principal interacts with a sequence of agents over
T
rounds. In each round, the principal observes contextual information associated with each available action of the arriving agent and offers a
linear contract
, specifying a fraction of the reward to be transferred from the principal to the agent.

Based on this contract, the agent selects one of the available actions to maximize their own utility, which depends on the payment (i.e., the fraction of reward transferred) from the principal and the action’s cost. The agent receives the payment minus the incurred cost, while the principal gains the reward associated with the chosen action, net of the payment made to the agent.
In our contextual search model, the principal does not observe the actions’ costs or rewards directly. Instead, each action is associated with a context vector, and the principal observes these contexts. We consider two canonical settings: the
cost-context setting

reward-context setting

Conversely, in the reward-context setting, the reward is the inner product of the context and the hidden vector, and the cost is known.

It is not hard to observe that the pricing problem is a special case of our principal-agent model with two actions (‘‘purchase’’ vs. ‘‘no purchase’’), and that the contextual pricing problem corresponds to a special case of our proposed contextual search problem under the principal-agent model with two actions and cost context.

Does the (double-)logarithmic regret guarantee established for contextual pricing with binary actions extend to contextual search in general principal-agent games with multiple actions? Can the algorithmic ideas and analytical techniques developed for contextual pricing be generalized to this broader contextual search setting?

1.1
Our Contributions and Techniques

In this paper, we provide compelling answers to both of these questions. In a nutshell, we show that the (double-)logarithmic regret guarantee breaks down as soon as we move beyond the two-action setting (i.e., contextual pricing). However, several algorithmic ideas and technical tools developed in the literature can still be adapted to our more general setting. Below, we summarize our main results and techniques. Model setups.

In the first part of the paper, we focus on the
cost-context setting
. In each round, when a new agent arrives, the principal (online algorithm) observes the action set and the reward associated with each action. Both the action set and the reward profile can vary across rounds. However, the cost associated with each action is not directly observed. Instead, each action is linked to a
d
-dimensional cost context, which is observable by the principal. The cost of each available action for a given agent follows a linear structure, i.e., it is the inner product of the corresponding context and an unknown cost hidden vector
\hat{\theta}
. We assume that all actions from all agents follow the same linear structure, meaning that
\hat{\theta}
is both agent-independent and action-independent.
Although the cost hidden vector is not directly known to the principal (as implied by its name), it can be learned through interaction with the agents over multiple rounds. The principal’s goal is to design an online algorithm that maximizes her long-term utility. Following the contextual search literature, we impose no stochastic assumptions on the arrival pattern. Specifically, both the agents’ action set, reward profiles, and cost context profiles are designed by an adaptive adversary.

In the second part of the paper, we study the
reward-context setting
, which is symmetric to the cost-context setting. In this case, the cost profile of each agent is directly observed, while the reward for a given agent and action follows a linear structure: it is the inner product of an observed reward context and an unknown reward hidden vector. A key difference in the reward-context setting is that, once an action is selected by the agent, the principal not only observes the action but also the induced reward, i.e., the value of the inner product between the reward context and the unknown reward. In contrast, in the cost-context setting, the cost of the selected action is not observable.
4
4

4

Although the reward-context setting may initially appear to be an easier learning problem, we obtain the same regret upper and lower bounds in both settings.

Double-exponential separation from contextual pricing.

Our problem is closely related to several classic problems in the literature. As mentioned earlier, contextual dynamic pricing
(
Kleinberg and Leighton 2003
;
Liu et al. 2021
)

When the contexts remain identical (i.e., the online algorithm faces the same principal-agent game) across all rounds (or equivalently, the non-contextual version of our model), our problem reduces to the task of optimizing a one-sided Lipschitz function with a domain between
[0,1]
, which was recently studied in
Dütting et al. 2023

\log\log T

T

As the first (conceptual) contribution of this work, we show that the (double-)logarithmic regret observed in the prior models is fragile and quickly breaks down in our more general model. Even in the setting with three actions and two-dimensional contexts, we show that the Stackelberg regret of every online algorithm is at least
\Omega(T^{\frac{1}{4}})
(

Adversary construction with contextual action degeneracy.

The primary driving force behind this double-exponential hardness separation arises from a structural phenomenon in our model, which we refer to as
contextual action degeneracy
. In the principal-agent game, an action is considered degenerated if there is no contract that can incentivize the agent to choose this action, meaning its payoff for the agent is dominated by other actions in all linear contracts. The contextual action degeneracy occurs when a key unknown action remains degenerated for most possible values of the hidden vector
\hat{\theta}
. This structure creates a
“needle in a haystack” challenge
: an exploration is only informative if it targets one of the very few non-degenerated hidden vectors. Consequently, the principal is forced to explore exhaustively, with each exploration action only capable of eliminating a single, small “spherical cap” of the uncertainty space (aka., hypothesis set), all while incurring regret. Loosely speaking, this provides the adversary with sufficient leverage to prevent online algorithms from achieving (double-)logarithmic regret.

For readers familiar with the contextual pricing literature, recall that a Stackelberg regret of
O(d\log T)
can be easily achieved using the Ellipsoid method. By incorporating the asymmetric structure of the pricing loss function, the optimal regret of
O(d\log\log T)
can be achieved. In both of these results, a crucial algorithmic idea is ensuring the desired shrinkage of the hypothesis set for the hidden vector. For instance, to achieve
O(d\log T)
, it suffices to shrink the hypothesis set by half with each round. To achieve
O(d\log\log T)
, a more aggressive shrinkage is required when the agent does not purchase (leading to constant immediate regret), and a less aggressive shrinkage when the agent purchases (resulting in immediate regret proportional to the “width” of the hypothesis set). We refer to this adaptive search strategy as
KL search

(
Kleinberg and Leighton 2003
)
; see

However, in our more general model, the adversary can limit the online algorithm’s ability to shrink the hypothesis set by making some actions degenerated for most points in the hypothesis set. This creates an
insufficient learning challenge
for the algorithm. Concretely, in
Section

3
, we construct an adversary (
) with three actions:
\{\blacklozenge,\clubsuit,\heartsuit\}
. Here,
\blacklozenge

\heartsuit
correspond respectively to the known “no purchase” and unknown “purchase” actions from contextual pricing, while
\clubsuit
—which we call the
dominating action
—is a known, high-performing action introduced by the adversary to induce contextual action degeneracy. By carefully choosing the context, the adversary ensures that, for a large fraction of the hypothesis set, the dominating action
\clubsuit
strictly outperforms the unknown “purchase” action
\heartsuit
.
Consequently, if the online algorithm employs a linear contract corresponded to KL search, it fails to achieve the same shrinkage guarantees as in contextual pricing. In standard pricing, one can always observe whether the “purchase” action
\heartsuit
is preferable to the no purchase action
\blacklozenge
, enabling informative feedback for hypothesis elimination. In our setting, however, the presence of the dominating action
\clubsuit
masks this comparison: for many parameter values, the algorithm observes
\clubsuit
being chosen instead, yielding no information about the relative ordering of
\heartsuit
and
\blacklozenge
. As a result, the hypothesis set cannot be effectively refined. See
Figure

1(b)
for an illustration of this phenomenon.

Nonetheless, the constructed adversary cannot make the shrinkage of the hypothesis set arbitrarily small. In fact, our adversarial construction inherently embodies a tradeoff. At a high level, while the dominating action
\clubsuit
obscures information about the hidden parameter (by masking comparisons between “purchase” action
\heartsuit
and “no purchase” action
\blacklozenge
), it simultaneously provides the algorithm with a “safe” contract: since
\clubsuit
is known and consistently high-performing, the algorithm can always incentivize this action without incurring large regret. More precisely, in the presence of
\clubsuit
, the algorithm may deploy a contract that incentivizes either the dominating action
\clubsuit
or the unknown “purchase” action
\heartsuit
—whichever yields higher utility.
Quantitatively, there is an inverse relationship between the fraction of the hypothesis set masked by
\clubsuit
and the regret achievable by such a safe contract: the larger the masked region, the lower the regret the algorithm can guarantee by defaulting to
\clubsuit
. See
Figure

1(c)
for an illustration. Our final adversarial construction (
in

\Omega(T^{1/4})
for this three-action setting with two-dimensional contexts.

Finally, to achieve our targeted dependence on the context dimension
d
in the regret lower bounds across all settings (see
Table

1
), our adversarial constructions for these more general results introduce a fourth action and incorporate a key technical ingredient from coding theory:
spherical codes
(
Definition

2.8
). The core idea is to leverage the geometric properties of these codewords to force any algorithm into a prolonged and costly exploration phase. Specifically, the exponentially large number of codewords defines a correspondingly large number of uncertainty directions in the hypothesis set. To learn the true hidden vector, the principal must explore along each of these directions, which ensures that the overall hypothesis set shrinks at an exceedingly slow rate. This slow shrinkage results in a significant regret dependence on the context dimension
d
.

Benchmark-optimal contextual search algorithm.

Given the challenges posed by contextual action degeneracy, it seems unlikely to naively apply the algorithmic ideas and the regret analysis framework from the literature to our settings. Specifically, we can not apply the KL search approach used by previous works
(
Kleinberg and Leighton 2003
;
Leme and Schneider 2018
;
Liu et al. 2021
)
to our problem. Instead, we can only expect to cut a small region from the current hypothesis set by carefully selecting the contract.
Beyond the failure of KL-search, other natural approaches also face significant obstacles. For instance, one might view our problem as a contextual continuous bandit problem, where contracts constitute a continuous action space. However, a straightforward discretization of this space followed by the application of classical online learning algorithms—such as the multiplicative weights update (MWU)—fails to yield meaningful guarantees. The primary difficulty lies in controlling the discretization error. Crucially, neither the optimal payoff nor the optimal contract exhibits a clear Lipschitz dependence on the hidden parameter vector, which undermines standard discretization arguments.
5
5

5

In the online contract design literature, some works establish “weaker-than-Lipschitz” regularity properties; however, these are defined over the contract space rather than the hidden vector space that governs the contextual structure of our setting
(
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Dütting et al. 2023
)
. It remains unclear whether such techniques can be extended or adapted to our framework.

We design our online algorithms following the
principle of optimism
. In the cost-context setting, at each round, our proposed algorithm (Algorithm
2
) first identifies the
optimistic hidden vector
—that is, the value of the hidden vector (consistent with the observed history) that maximizes the principal’s payoff given the current context. It then computes the optimal contract under this optimistic hidden vector and perturbs it slightly by sharing a bit more revenue with the agent. Our proposed algorithm (Algorithm
4
) for the reward-context setting follows a similar but more complicated construction. To understand the rationale behind this procedure, consider the three-action adversary described earlier. If the adversary selects a context such that dominating action
\clubsuit
masks the majority of the hypothesis set, the resulting contract closely approximates the “safe” contract, thereby guaranteeing low regret. Conversely, when dominating action
\clubsuit
does not mask most of the hypothesis set, the slight revenue-sharing perturbation ensures a carefully controlled shrinkage of the hypothesis set—enabling effective learning over time.

One key technical ingredient of our algorithms is a new notion we introduce: the
information width
(
Definition

4.3
), defined as the width of the portion of the hypothesis set excluded by the algorithm in the contextual direction. Informally, the information width governs the immediate regret in a round; in the worst case (e.g., against our adversary), it reflects the degree of contextual action degeneracy—namely, the fraction of the hypothesis set not masked by dominating action
\clubsuit
. (Also see
Figure

1(c)
for an illustration.)
As in prior work, we use the
intrinsic volume
(
Definition

2.7
) to define a potential function over the hidden vector space, enabling a regret analysis via the potential method. However, unlike previous approaches that rely on multiplicative volume reduction, we establish a direct relationship between the decay of intrinsic volume and the information width of the contract selected by the algorithm. Through this relationship, we build our regret upper bounds for both cost-context and reward-context settings using a potential argument.

To evaluate the performance of our proposed online algorithms, we consider the classic
Stackelberg regret
(
Definition

2.4
), which is defined against the
optimum-in-hindsight benchmark
, where the hidden vector is known and the optimal contract is designed for each agent individually. Additionally, we introduce a stronger regret measure, termed the
pessimistic Stackelberg regret
(
Definition

2.5
), which provides a more stringent comparison. This stronger notion evaluates the long-term utility of the principal under the algorithm, relative to an “optimistic benchmark”. Specifically, for each round, the optimistic benchmark is defined as the maximum achievable principal utility over all possible contracts, considering all potential values of the hidden vector that are consistent with the algorithm’s observed history (i.e., agents’ information, proposed contracts, and the agents’ best responses in all previous rounds). By construction, since the true hidden vector is always consistent with the observed history, the optimistic benchmark serves as an upper bound to the optimum-in-hindsight benchmark. Consequently, an upper bound on the pessimistic Stackelberg regret implies the same upper bound on the classic Stackelberg regret. Notably, the analysis framework used in many prior works
(
Kleinberg and Leighton 2003
;
Lobel et al. 2018
;
Leme and Schneider 2018
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Krishnamurthy et al. 2021
;
Feng et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
in contextual pricing and related problems implicitly compares the algorithm’s utility against the optimistic benchmark’s gain in each round. Therefore, although not explicitly stated, the regret guarantees in these prior works are directly applicable to the pessimistic Stackelberg regret definition.

6

6

Proving the optimality of our proposed algorithms under the classic Stackelberg regret or designing improved algorithms is an interesting future direction. Since most prior work essentially computes the pessimistic Stackelberg regret, achieving an improved upper bound for the classic Stackelberg regret in our model would likely require additional algorithmic and analytical approaches.

In summary, our work reveals that contextual search in principal-agent games is fundamentally more complex than the well-studied contextual pricing problem. The paper establishes both the possibility of dramatically larger regret in the general model (even under mild extensions of action space and context dimension) and provides matching or complementary bounds that characterize the growth of regret in both cost-context and reward-context settings. These findings yield new insights for contextual learning in principal-agent environments, highlighting how additional actions and hidden parameters can sharply increase the learning difficulty.

Related Work

Our work connects to several lines of research, including contextual search and pricing, learning in principal-agent settings, and incentive exploration. Each of these areas has developed distinct approaches to learning and decision-making under uncertainty and strategic interaction. We review the most relevant developments below.

Contextual search and pricing.

Kleinberg and Leighton 2003
initiated the study of dynamic pricing in non-contextual settings, showing that doubly logarithmic regret can be attained and establishing a foundational
\Omega(\log\log T)
lower bound for regret in adversarial posted-price auctions. Extending this result to contextual scenarios yields a
\Omega(d\log\log T)
lower bound
(
Leme and Schneider 2018
)
.
A sequence of works
(
Cohen et al. 2020
;
Cohen et al. 2017
;
Lobel et al. 2018
;
Leme and Schneider 2018
)
studied the contextual pricing problem and progressively improved this to
O_{d}(\log T)
and eventually to
O_{d}(\log\log T)
using tools from integral geometry, particularly intrinsic volumes. These advances, culminating in the near-optimal regret bound of
O(d\log\log T+d\log d)
by
Liu et al. 2021
, confirm that the
\Omega(d\log\log T)
lower bound is tight up to additive and polynomial factors in
d
. Other variants of this problem have been explored—for example, contextual search with irrational agents
(
Krishnamurthy et al. 2021
)
, contextual pricing with noise
(
Luo et al. 2022
;
Tullii et al. 2024
)
, stochastic context
(
Amin et al. 2014
;
Javanmard and Nazerzadeh 2019
;
Luo et al. 2022
)
, and non-myopic agents
(
Golrezaei et al. 2019
)
.

Learning in principal-agent game.

Learning in contract design has drawn growing interest, particularly under hidden-action models.

Ho et al. 2014
initiated the study of bandit algorithms for repeated contract design with bounded, monotone contracts, later extended to risk-averse agents by
Cohen et al. 2022
.
Zhu et al. 2023
showed that removing the monotonicity assumption can lead to exponential sample complexity, motivating efficient algorithms under structural assumptions by
Chen et al. 2024
and under small action spaces by
Bacchiocchi et al. 2024
.
Dütting et al. 2023
reduced optimal linear contract learning to one-sided Lipschitz optimization, achieving optimal
\Theta(\log\log T)
regret under deterministic feedback. Several studies consider learning under more complex agent behaviors where actions remain unobservable:
Scheid et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Guruganesh et al. 2024
;
Bollini et al. 2024
investigate contract learning with reinforcement-learning agents,
Collina et al. 2024
accounts for repeated interactions with non-myopic agents, and
Zuo 2024
addresses multitask settings with unobserved individual efforts.
Dogan et al. 2023
considers agents with perfect knowledge of rewards under hidden principal information. In contrast, other works consider settings where the agent’s action is either directly observed or deterministically inferred by the principal, as in
Liu and Ratliff 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Scheid et al. 2024
;
Dogan et al. 2023
;
Han et al. 2024
;
Ben-Porat et al. 2024

Finally, our work is conceptually related to the incentivized exploration literature
(
Frazier et al. 2014
;
Kremer et al. 2014
;
Mansour et al. 2015
;
Sellke and Slivkins 2021
;
Mansour et al. 2022

. In both models, the online algorithm must incentivize strategic participants to take desired actions. However, while in the incentivized exploration setting the algorithm is focused on learning the true state of nature, in our model the algorithm is learning the participants’ types (i.e., their preferences).

The Principal-Agent Game

The principal-agent model describes the interaction between a
principal
,

agent
, who performs the task on the principal’s behalf.
The agent’s set of possible actions is denoted by

A\triangleq\left\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\right\}
,
where

Each action

Remark 2.1

Our model can be viewed as a simplification of the classic binary-outcome hidden-action principal–agent setting, where each action induces a successful outcome with some probability, and the action’s reward equals this success probability. 2.2
Contextual Search in Principal-Agent Games

In this section, we introduce the
contextual search
setting for the principal-agent game. Unlike the classical setting, where the principal has full knowledge of the agent’s cost profile over all actions,
9
9

9

In
Section

5
, we introduce and study a variant where the principal is instead uncertain about the reward profile over actions.
the principal in this variant has no prior information about costs and must learn through repeated interactions with a sequence of possibly heterogeneous agents.

There are
T\in\mathbb{N}
sequential and discrete rounds. In each round
t\in[T]
, a new agent indexed by
t
arrives. For this agent, both the action set
A_{t}
and the reward profile
\{r^{(a)}_{t}\}_{a\in A_{t}}
are observed by the principal. However, the cost profile
\{c^{(a)}_{t}\}_{a\in A_{t}}
is not directly observable. Instead, the principal observes a
cost context profile

\{\mu_{t}^{(a)}\}_{a\in A_{t}}
, where
\mu_{t}^{(a)}\in\mathbb{B}_{d}
is a
d
-dimensional vector lying in the unit ball. For each action
a\in A_{t}
, the corresponding cost satisfies the following linear structure:

\displaystyle c^{(a)}_{t}=\langle\mu_{t}^{(a)},\hat{\theta}\rangle,

(linear cost structure)

where
\hat{\theta}\in\mathbb{B}_{d}
is the
cost hidden vector
, an unknown vector in the
d
-dimensional unit ball.

We assume that the action sets
\{A_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
, reward profiles
\{r^{(a)}_{t}\}_{a\in A_{t},t\in[T]}
, cost context profiles
\{\mu_{t}^{(a)}\}_{a\in A_{t},t\in[T]}
, and the hidden cost vector
\hat{\theta}
are all chosen by an
adaptive
adversary.
Moreover, we assume that the linear cost structure ensures
c^{(a)}_{t}=\langle\mu_{t}^{(a)},\hat{\theta}\rangle\geq 0
for all actions
a\in A_{t}
, with
r^{(0)}_{t}=c^{(0)}_{t}=0
for the trivial action
0
.

In each round
t\in[T]
, the principal proposes a contract
x_{t}\in[0,1]
, and the agent selects a best-response action

\displaystyle a_{t}\in\argmax\nolimits_{a\in A_{t}}~x_{t}\cdot r^{(a)}_{t}-\langle\mu_{t}^{(a)},\hat{\theta}\rangle.

The principal receives utility
{\Pi}_{t}(x_{t})=(1-x_{t})\cdot r^{(a_{t})}_{t}
, and the agent receives utility
U_{t}(x_{t})=x_{t}\cdot r^{(a_{t})}_{t}-\langle\mu_{t}^{(a_{t})},\hat{\theta}\rangle
. The principal then observes the chosen best-responding action
a_{t}
. The goal of the principal is to design an online algorithm that proposes contracts
\{x_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
to maximize her long-term utility
\sum_{t\in[T]}{\Pi}_{t}(x_{t})
.

Benchmark and regret definitions.
We evaluate the performance of an online policy by its Stackelberg regret
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
against the optimum-in-hindsight benchmark. The
optimum-in-hindsight benchmark
knows the cost hidden vector
\hat{\theta}
and proposes the optimal contracts

\displaystyle x_{t}^{*}\in\argmax\nolimits_{x\in[0,1]}~{\Pi}_{t}(x)

for each arriving agent
t\in[T]
separately.

Definition 2.4

(Stackelberg regret)
.

Given cost hidden vector
\hat{\theta}
and a sequence of
T
agents with action sets
\{A_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
, reward profiles
\{r^{(a)}_{t}\}_{a\in A_{t},t\in[T]}
, and cost context profiles
\{\mu_{t}^{(a)}\}_{a\in A_{t},t\in[T]}
, the
Stackelberg regret
of an online algorithm
ALG
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\texttt{ALG})\triangleq\sum\nolimits_{t\in[T]}{\Pi}_{t}(x_{t}^{*})-{\Pi}_{t}(x_{t}),

where
x_{t}^{*}
and
x_{t}
are the contracts proposed in the optimum-in-hindsight benchmark and algorithm
ALG
for each agent
t\in[T]
.

In addition to the standard optimum-in-hindsight benchmark and its corresponding Stackelberg regret, we are also interested in the following
optimistic benchmark
and the corresponding
pessimistic Stackelberg regret
.

Fix an online algorithm
ALG
. For each round
t\in[T]
, define the
hypothesis set

\hat{\Theta}_{t}\subseteq\mathbb{B}_{d}
as the set of vectors consistent with the contract history
\left\{x_{\tau}\right\}_{\tau\in[t-1]}
and observed best-response actions history
\{a_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[t-1]}
.
10
10

10

That is, a vector
\theta\in\mathbb{B}_{d}
belongs to the hypothesis set
\hat{\Theta}_{t}
if and only if the first
t-1
agents’ best-response actions are
\{a_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[t-1]}
when the hidden cost vector is
\hat{\theta}=\theta
.

The optimistic benchmark is defined with respect to the execution of the algorithm, and in particular, the hypothesis sets
\{\hat{\Theta}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
it induces. In each round
t\in[T]
, the benchmark receives a payoff
\tilde{\Pi}_{t}(\hat{\Theta}_{t})
equal to the maximum achievable principal utility over all vectors in the hypothesis set
\hat{\Theta}_{t}
, i.e.,
11
11

11

Throughout the paper, we use tilde notation (e.g.,
\tilde{\cdot}
) to denote concepts related to the optimistic benchmark and pessimistic Stackelberg regret.

\tilde{\Pi}_{t}(\hat{\Theta}_{t})\triangleq\max\limits_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{t}}\max\limits_{x\in[0,1]}~{\Pi}_{t}(x|\theta),

where
{\Pi}_{t}(x|\theta)
is principal’s utility when proposing a contract
x
under the hidden vector
\theta
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\Pi}_{t}(x|\theta)\triangleq(1-x)\cdot r^{(a_{t,\theta})}_{t}\;\;\mbox{s.t.}\;\;a_{t,\theta}\in\argmax\limits_{a\in A_{t}}~x\cdot r^{(a)}_{t}-\langle\mu_{t}^{(a)},\theta\rangle.

Definition 2.5

(Pessimistic Stackelberg regret)
.

Given cost hidden vector
\hat{\theta}
and a sequence of
T
agents with action sets
\{A_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
, reward profiles
\{r^{(a)}_{t}\}_{a\in A_{t},t\in[T]}
, and cost context profiles
\{\mu_{t}^{(a)}\}_{a\in A_{t},t\in[T]}
, the
pessimistic Stackelberg regret
of an online algorithm
ALG
is

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}(\texttt{ALG})\triangleq\sum\nolimits_{t\in[T]}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{t}(\texttt{ALG})\triangleq\sum\nolimits_{t\in[T]}\tilde{\Pi}_{t}(\hat{\Theta}_{t})-{\Pi}_{t}(x_{t}),

where
\tilde{\Pi}_{t}(\hat{\Theta}_{t})
is the payoff received in the optimistic benchmark, and
x_{t}
is the contracts proposed in algorithm
ALG
for each agent

.

By construction, the optimistic benchmark upper bounds the optimum-in-hindsight benchmark, and thus the pessimistic Stackelberg regret upper bounds the classic Stackelberg regret. Since it depends only on revealed history, the pessimistic Stackelberg regret also serves as a more robust measure of algorithmic performance.

Geometric Concepts for Regret Analysis

In this section, we introduce two geometric concepts—
intrinsic volume
and
spherical code
—that will be used in our subsequent regret analysis.

Intrinsic volumes.

For any convex body
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
intrinsic volumes

(V_{0}\left(\Theta\right),V_{1}\left(\Theta\right),\dots,V_{d}\left(\Theta\right))

are a sequence of geometric quantities that generalize volume, surface area, and other notions of size. For example,

V_{d}\left(\Theta\right)
equals the standard
d
-dimensional volume of
\Theta
, while
V_{d-1}\left(\Theta\right)
corresponds (up to scaling) to its surface area. The formal definition is as follows.

Definition 2.7

(Intrinsic volumes)
.

Let
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex body, and let
\mathbb{B}_{d}
denote the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Given any convex body
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, its
intrinsic volumes

\{V_{j}\left(\Theta\right)\}_{j\in[0:d]}
are defined as the coefficients in the
Steiner formula
:
12
12

12

It has been shown that
\mathrm{Vol}\left(\Theta+\epsilon\mathbb{B}_{d}\right)
is a polynomial in
\epsilon

(
Schanuel 1986
)
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}\left(\Theta+\epsilon\mathbb{B}_{d}\right)=\sum\nolimits_{j\in[0:d]}\kappa_{d-j}\,V_{j}\left(\Theta\right)\,\epsilon^{d-j},

where
\mathrm{Vol}\left(\!\cdot\!\right)
is the
d
-dimensional volume and
\kappa_{d-j}
is the volume of the
(d\!-\!j)
-dimensional unit ball.

Our regret analysis utilizes the following properties of the intrinsic volumes.

Lemma 2.2

(
Leme and Schneider 2018
;
Schneider and Weil 2008
)
.

The intrinsic volumes satisfy the following properties:

•

(Monotonicity):
For any two convex bodies
S,T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and index
j\in[0:d]
, if
S\subseteq T
, then

V_{j}\left(S\right)\leq V_{j}\left(T\right).

•

(Additivity):
For any two convex bodies
S,T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and index
j\in[0:d]
,

V_{j}\left(S\right)+V_{j}\left(T\right)=V_{j}\left(S\cup T\right)+V_{j}\left(S\cap T\right).

•

(Cone inequality):
For any convex cone
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and index
j\in[0:d]
,

V_{j}\left(C\right)\geq\frac{h}{j}\cdot V_{j-1}\left(B\right),

where
B
and
h
are the base and height of convex cone
C
, respectively.

•

(Isoperimetric inequality):
For any convex body
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and index
j\in[0:d]
,

\displaystyle\left({(j+1)}!\cdot V_{j+1}\left(\Theta\right)\right)^{1/(j+1)}\leq\left({j}!\cdot V_{j}\left(\Theta\right)\right)^{1/j}.

•

(Intrinsic volume of unit ball):
For the
d
-dimensional unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
,

V_{1}\left(\mathbb{B}_{d}\right)\leq\sqrt{2\pi d}
.

3
Double Exponential Separation from Contextual Pricing

In this warm-up section, we establish the connection between our model and the classical contextual pricing model
(
Lobel et al. 2018
;
Leme and Schneider 2018
)
. We begin by showing that a special case of our model with only two actions is equivalent to contextual pricing. As a result, the optimal regret bound of
O(d\log\log T+d\log d)
in this setting can be directly implied by existing result
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
from the contextual pricing literature.
We then highlight a fundamental difference between the two models. Specifically, we demonstrate that even with just three actions in a two-dimensional setting, the optimal regret in our model becomes
\Omega(T^{1/4})
, representing a double-exponential gap compared to the regret achieved in classical contextual pricing.

3.1
Contextual Search in Principal-Agent Games with Two Actions

The pricing problem can be viewed as a special case of the principal-agent game with two actions. The principal is the seller, and the agent is the buyer. Without loss of generality, we assume that the valuation of the buyer is in
[0,1]
. There is only one non-trivial action in pricing, which represents “purchase”. The reward of “purchase” is
r=1
and the cost
c
of the “purchase” action is
c=1-v
where
v
is the value of the buyer. The trivial action represents “no purchase” with reward and cost both being
0
. Posting a price
p
is equivalent to proposing a linear contract
x=1-p
.

To understand why the above correspondence is reasonable, let us further investigate the utility functions of the principal (seller) and the agent (buyer). In the pricing view, the buyer purchases the item only when the price
p\leq v
, in which case their utility is
v-p
. When
p>v
, the buyer does not purchase the item, and their utility is zero.
This corresponds to the principal-agent view, where the agent will choose the action “purchase” only when
x\cdot r-c\geq 0
, which is equivalent to the condition
p\leq v
according to our definition of actions.
As for the principal’s utility, if the agent chooses “purchase”, the principal receives a utility of
(1-x)\cdot r=p
, which aligns with the pricing view.

By the above discussion, it is not surprising to see that the contextual principal-agent game with two actions (i.e., a single non-trivial action) has an
O(d\log\log T+d\log d)
regret since contextual pricing has such a regret bound by
Liu et al. 2021
.
In fact, we prove the following theorem by giving a reduction to the contextual pricing problem.
Its formal analysis
is deferred to
Section

C.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

In the setting where agents have at most two actions, there exists an online algorithm
ALG
with a pessimistic Stackelberg regret of

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}(\texttt{ALG})=\Theta(d\log\log T+d\log d).

Moreover, it matches the lower bound of the (classic) Stackelberg regret, and thus is regret-optimal.

To better illustrate how this reduction works, we next outline the key idea behind the proof. Let action
\heartsuit
denote the “purchase” action in the contextual pricing problem, and let
\mu_{t}^{(\heartsuit)}
be the only context. Then, we note that the value
v_{t}=\langle\mu_{t}^{(\heartsuit)},\hat{\theta}\rangle
follows a linear structure.
By invoking the correspondence mentioned earlier, we obtain a variant of the contextual search in the principal-agent game, where the context is defined based on the welfare
r^{(\heartsuit)}_{t}-c^{(\heartsuit)}_{t}
, rather than the cost
c^{(\heartsuit)}_{t}
. Nevertheless, it is straightforward to construct a reduction to contextual pricing even for the cost context, which can be achieved by increasing the dimension of the context by one to encode
r^{(\heartsuit)}_{t}
.

3.2
Regret Lower Bound for Contextual Search with Three Actions

In this section, we illustrate the difference between our model and the contextual pricing model. As we will demonstrate, the optimal regret in our model (even with three actions) grows double exponentially worse compared to the optimal regret in the contextual pricing model.

One important feature of the contextual pricing model is the implementability of the “purchase” action. Namely, the “purchase” action is never degenerated (
Definition

2.2
).
Although not explicitly stated, this simple feature allows the contextual pricing algorithms
(
Leme and Schneider 2018
;
Liu et al. 2021
)
to identify an ideal price for each buyer. As a result, whenever a high immediate regret occurs, the hypothesis set for the hidden vector
\hat{\theta}
shrinks significantly.

Unfortunately, in our principal-agent model with three or more actions, the adversary can exploit certain actions to intentionally degenerate a targeted action under the true cost hidden vector
\hat{\theta}
. Since the algorithm does not know the hidden vector
\hat{\theta}
but only has a hypothesis set for it, it cannot determine whether such an action is degenerated. This gives the adversary sufficient leverage, leading to the following lower bound on the regret.

Theorem 3.2
.

In the setting where agents have at most three actions and
2
-dimensional cost contexts, no online algorithm
ALG
can achieve a Stackelberg regret of

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\texttt{ALG})\leq\frac{1}{32}\cdot T^{\frac{1}{4}}.

In the remainder of this section, we first sketch the high-level intuition by describing a concrete scenario in which no online algorithm can achieve the ideal balance between immediate regret and the shrinkage of the hypothesis set, as is possible with contextual pricing algorithms with double logarithmic regret. We then extend the insights from this scenario to formally construct an adversary and prove
Theorem

3.2
.

Insufficient learning due to contextual action degeneracy.

Consider action set
A=\left\{\blacklozenge,\clubsuit,\heartsuit\right\}
, where
\blacklozenge
is the trivial action with zero cost and zero reward,
\clubsuit
and
\heartsuit
is associated with a
2
-dimensional context
\mu^{(\clubsuit)}
and
\mu^{(\heartsuit)}
. The reward of actions
\clubsuit
and
\heartsuit
are
r^{(\clubsuit)}=\frac{1}{2}
and
r^{(\heartsuit)}=\frac{1}{2}+\Delta
, respectively. Here
\Delta
is a small parameter to be determined by the adversary.
The cost hidden vector
\hat{\theta}
is promised to take the form of
\hat{\theta}=(\frac{1}{2},\hat{\theta}_{2})
, where
\hat{\theta}_{2}
is in
[\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}]
.

Suppose the cost contexts for actions
\clubsuit
and
\heartsuit
are given by
\mu^{(\clubsuit)}=\left(\frac{1}{2},0\right)
and
\mu^{(\heartsuit)}=\left(0,\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\right)
, respectively. By construction, the cost of action
\clubsuit

, while the cost

of action

.
It can be verified that action

is fully dominated by action
\clubsuit
and becomes

\frac{1}{4}
. As a result, the principal cannot determine whether the cost of action
\heartsuit
exceeds any given threshold greater than
\frac{1}{4}+\Delta
.
Even when proposing the contract
x=1
, the algorithm can only decide whether
c^{(\heartsuit)}\leq\frac{1}{4}+\Delta
or not. Under this contract, the algorithm incurs
\Omega(1)
immediate regret, while only shrinking the hypothesis set by a small
O(\Delta)
region.

This illustrates a key difference compared with contextual pricing, where the contextual pricing algorithm can determine whether the valuation exceeds any threshold in the range
[0,1]
. In this case, the algorithm can shrink a much larger region while suffering
O(1)
immediate regret, leading to a low cumulative regret.

[Part (i)] Cost context construction.

Algorithm.
Our algorithm employs an optimistic approach. In each round

Given this optimistic hypothesis

To mitigate this risk, our algorithm proposes a more conservative contract,

•

\Theta_{t+1}\leftarrow\Theta_{t}\cap\left\{\theta\mid\langle\mu_{t}^{(a_{t},a^{*}_{t})},\theta\rangle\geq x_{t}\right\}

a^{*}_{t},x_{t}^{*}
can be efficiently calculated by solving
n
convex programs, where
n
is the number of actions. Concretely, for each action
a
, we solve the following programming problem:

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\theta\in\Theta_{t},x\geq 0}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle\theta\in\Theta_{t},x\geq 0}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\quad x

(3a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\theta\in\Theta_{t},x\geq 0}}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject~to}}}\quad

\displaystyle x\cdot r^{(a)}_{t}-\langle\mu_{t}^{(a)},\theta\rangle

\displaystyle\geq x\cdot r^{(a^{\dagger})}_{t}-\langle\mu_{t}^{(a^{\dagger})},\theta\rangle,\quad\quad\forall a^{\dagger}\in A_{t}

(3b)

In our algorithm,
\Theta_{t}
is always the intersection of a unit ball and a polytope. Thus,
\Theta_{t}
is convex for any
t
. Therefore, the above program is a convex program and can be solved efficiently.
The convex program calculates the “min-pay” contract to implement
a
under any possible
\theta
. We then select the action
a^{*}_{t}
with best principal’s utility under the corresponding “min-pay” contract. Then we can obtain
x_{t}^{*}
and
\theta_{t}^{*}
from its “min-pay” program.

Our analysis of Algorithm
2
use a potential argument. In
Section

4.1.1
, we introduce the potential function and a concept of information width to quantify the knowledge obtained by cutting the hypothesis set using a hyperplane.

4.1.1
Information Width and Potential Function

Definition 4.3

(Information width)
.

Given a convex body
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a context vector
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a real number
r\in[\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\langle\mu,\theta\rangle,\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\langle\mu,\theta\rangle]
, we define the
information width
as

\mathtt{InfoWidth}\left(\mu,r;\Theta\right)\triangleq\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\langle\mu,\theta\rangle-r.

(4)

Definition 4.4

(Potential function)
.

Let
\phi_{j}\left(\Theta\right)\triangleq j!V_{j}\left(\Theta\right)
where
V_{j}\left(\Theta\right)
is
j
-th intrinsic volume of
\Theta
. The potential function of
\Theta
is defined as
\Phi\left(\Theta\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_{j}\left(\Theta\right)
.

Lemma 4.2

(Initial potential)
.

\Phi\left(\mathbb{B}_{d}\right)\leq d(2\pi d)^{d/2}
.

Proof.

Lemma

2.2
.(4) and
Lemma

2.2
.(5) directly give
\phi_{j}\left(\mathbb{B}_{d}\right)\leq\left(\phi_{1}\left(\mathbb{B}_{d}\right)\right)^{j}\leq(2\pi d)^{j/2},\forall j\geq 1
.
Lemma follows from
(2\pi d)^{j/2}\leq(2\pi d)^{d}
and
\Phi\left(\mathbb{B}_{d}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_{j}\left(\mathbb{B}_{d}\right)
.
∎

The following two lemmas build the connection between information width and the decay of the potential function.

Lemma 4.3
.

Given a vector
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a real number
r\in\mathbb{R}
, define

\Theta^{+}\triangleq\Theta\cap\left\{\theta\,\mid\,\langle\theta,\mu\rangle\leq r\right\}.

Then there exist
1\leq j\leq d
such that,

\phi_{j}\left(\Theta\right)-\phi_{j}\left(\Theta^{+}\right)\geq\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{1}{\|\mu\|}\mathtt{InfoWidth}\left(\mu,r;\Theta\right)\right)^{j}.

Proof.

Firstly, we define a convex cone. Let its apex be
\theta_{p}\in\argmax_{\theta\in\Theta}\langle\theta,\mu\rangle
. Let its base be

B\triangleq\Theta\cap\left\{\theta\,\mid\,\langle\theta,\mu\rangle=r\right\}.

Then the cone is defined as

C\triangleq\bigcup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\left(\lambda\theta_{p}+(1-\lambda)B\right).

Since
\theta_{p}\in\Theta
,
B\subseteq\Theta
and
\Theta
is convex, we have
C\subseteq\Theta
. By the modularity of intrinsic volume, we have

V_{j}\left(C\right)+V_{j}\left(\Theta^{+}\right)=V_{j}\left(C\cup\Theta^{+}\right)+V_{j}\left(C\cap\Theta^{+}\right).

Note that
C\cup\Theta^{+}\subseteq\Theta
and
C\cap\Theta^{+}=B
, we have

V_{j}\left(C\right)+V_{j}\left(\Theta^{+}\right)\leq V_{j}\left(\Theta\right)+V_{j}\left(B\right).

The inequality is due to the monotonicity of the intrinsic volume.

Note that the height of
C
is
H\triangleq\frac{1}{\|\mu\|}\mathtt{InfoWidth}\left(\mu,r;\Theta\right)\leq 1
.
By Lemma
2.2
.(3),
V_{j}\left(C\right)\geq\frac{1}{j}H\cdot V_{j-1}\left(B\right)
and we have

\frac{1}{j}H\cdot V_{j-1}\left(B\right)+V_{j}\left(\Theta^{+}\right)\leq V_{j}\left(\Theta\right)+V_{j}\left(B\right).

By rearranging,

V_{j}\left(\Theta\right)-V_{j}\left(\Theta^{+}\right)\geq\frac{1}{j}H\cdot V_{j-1}\left(B\right)-V_{j}\left(B\right).

(5)

Note that inequality (
5
) holds for all
j\geq 1
.

Firstly, we show that, there exist a
1\leq j\leq d
such that the following condition holds:

V_{j-1}\left(B\right)\geq\frac{1}{{(j-1)}!}\left(1-\frac{j-1}{d}\right)H^{j-1}\text{ and }V_{j}\left(B\right)\leq\frac{1}{{j}!}\left(1-\frac{j}{d}\right)H^{j}.

We will show this by contradiction. Suppose for all
1\leq j\leq d
, the above condition does not hold, then
\forall 1\leq j\leq d
,

V_{j-1}\left(B\right)<\frac{1}{{(j-1)}!}\left(1-\frac{j-1}{d}\right)H^{j-1}\text{ or }V_{j}\left(B\right)>\frac{1}{{j}!}\left(1-\frac{j}{d}\right)H^{j}.

Note that
V_{0}\left(B\right)=1\geq H
, so it must holds that
V_{1}\left(B\right)>\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)H
since the the condition does not hold for
j=1
. Using the reduction argument, suppose we have

V_{j-1}\left(B\right)>\frac{1}{{j-1}!}\left(1-\frac{j-1}{d}\right)H^{j-1},

then it must hold that
V_{j}\left(B\right)>\frac{1}{{j}!}\left(1-\frac{j}{d}\right)H^{j}
since the condition does not hold for
j
. By reduction,

V_{d}\left(B\right)>\frac{1}{{d}!}\left(1-\frac{d}{d}\right)H^{d}=0.

However,
B
can be embodied in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
so
V_{d}\left(B\right)=0
which leads to a contradiction.

Let
1\leq j\leq d
be the integer satisfying

V_{j-1}\left(B\right)\geq\frac{1}{{(j-1)}!}\left(1-\frac{j-1}{d}\right)H^{j-1}\text{ and }V_{j}\left(B\right)\leq\frac{1}{{j}!}\left(1-\frac{j}{d}\right)H^{j}.

(6)

Then we have

\displaystyle\phi_{j}\left(\Theta\right)-\phi_{j}\left(\Theta^{+}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}{j}!\left(V_{j}\left(\Theta\right)-V_{j}\left(\Theta^{+}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\geq}{j}!\left(\frac{1}{j}H\cdot V_{j-1}\left(B\right)-V_{j}\left(B\right)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\geq}{j}!\left(\frac{1}{{j}!}\left(1-\frac{j-1}{d}\right)H^{j}-\frac{1}{{j}!}\left(1-\frac{j}{d}\right)H^{j}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(d)}{=}\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{1}{\|\mu\|}\mathtt{InfoWidth}\left(\mu,r;\Theta\right)\right)^{j}.

(a) is by the definition of
\phi_{j}\left(\Theta\right)
and
\phi_{j}\left(\Theta^{+}\right)
; (b) is due to Lemma
2.2
.(3); (c) is due to (
6
); (d) is by identical transformation and
H=\frac{1}{\|\mu\|}\mathtt{InfoWidth}\left(\mu,r;\Theta\right)
.

∎

[Part (i)] Benchmark relaxing

Definition 5.5

(Restricted instance)
.

If the action set of
\mathcal{I}
contains the action set of
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
(with the same cost and reward), we say
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is a
restricted instance
of
\mathcal{I}
, denoted by
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\prec\mathcal{I}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
A
and
A^{\prime}
be the action set of
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, respectively.
If
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\prec\mathcal{I}
, then
\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a\right)\geq\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I},a\right)\ \forall a\in A^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For any action
a\in A
, recall
c^{(a)}
is its cost and
r^{(a)}
is its reward. Let
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I},a}
be the set of all linear contracts that action
a
is induced in instance
\mathcal{I}
. Thus, we have

\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I},a}=\left\{x\in[0,1]\,\mid\,x\cdot r^{(a)}-c^{(a)}\geq x\cdot r^{(a^{\prime})}-c^{(a^{\prime})},\forall a^{\prime}\in A\right\},

and

\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a}=\left\{x\in[0,1]\,\mid\,x\cdot r^{(a)}-c^{(a)}\geq x\cdot r^{(a^{\prime})}-c^{(a^{\prime})},\forall a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}\right\}.

Since
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
, we have
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I},a}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a}
for any
a\in A^{\prime}
.

Note that

\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I},a\right)=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I},a}}(1-x)r^{(a)}\mbox{ and }\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a\right)=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a}}(1-x)r^{(a)}.

Since
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I},a}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a}
, we have
\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a\right)\geq\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I},a\right)
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
A=\bar{A}\cup\left\{a\right\},A^{\prime}=\bar{A}\cup\left\{a^{\prime}\right\}
be the action set of
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
respectively. Assume that
c^{(a)}=c^{(a^{\prime})}
and
r^{(a^{\prime})}\geq r^{(a)}
, than
\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a^{\prime}\right)\geq\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I},a\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I},a}
be defined as in the proof of Lemma
5.2
. Specifically,

\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I},a}=\left\{x\in[0,1]\,\mid\,x\cdot r^{(a)}-c^{(a)}\geq x\cdot r^{(\bar{a})}-c^{(\bar{a})},\forall\bar{a}\in\bar{A}\right\}

and

\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a^{\prime}}=\left\{x\in[0,1]\,\mid\,x\cdot r^{(a^{\prime})}-c^{(a^{\prime})}\geq x\cdot r^{(\bar{a})}-c^{(\bar{a})},\forall\bar{a}\in\bar{A}\right\}.

Since
c^{(a)}=c^{(a^{\prime})}
and
r^{(a^{\prime})}\geq r^{(a)}
we have
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I},a}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a^{\prime}}
. Then

\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a^{\prime}\right)=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a^{\prime}}}(1-x)r^{(a^{\prime})}\overset{(a)}{\geq}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a^{\prime}}}(1-x)r^{(a)}\overset{(b)}{\geq}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I},a}}(1-x)r^{(a)}=\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I},a\right),

where inequality (a) is due to
r^{(a^{\prime})}\geq r^{(a)}
and inequality (b) is due to
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I},a}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},a^{\prime}}
.
∎

Lemma 5.5

(Benchmark relaxation)
.

Let
ALG
be Algorithm
4

Proof.

Recall that
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{t}(\texttt{ALG})=\tilde{\Pi}_{t}(\hat{\Theta}_{t})-{\Pi}_{t}(x_{t})=\max_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{t}}\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A_{t},\theta\right)\right)-{\Pi}_{t}(x_{t})

\max_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{t}}\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A_{t},\theta\right)\right)\leq\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A_{t},\theta\right)\right)
.

Let
\theta^{\dagger}_{t}\in\argmax_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A_{t},\theta\right)\right)
and
a^{\dagger}_{t}
be the agent’s action when the principal proposed the optimal contract in

By

5.2
, we have

\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A_{t},\theta^{\dagger}_{t}\right),a^{\dagger}_{t}\right)\leq\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{\dagger}_{t}\right\},\theta^{\dagger}_{t}\right),a^{\dagger}_{t}\right),

\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{\dagger}_{t}\right\},\theta^{\dagger}_{t}\right)\prec\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A_{t},\theta^{\dagger}_{t}\right)

where

has the largest reward. We have

\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{\dagger}_{t}\right\},\theta^{\dagger}_{t}\right),a^{\dagger}_{t}\right)\overset{(a)}{\leq}\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{\dagger}_{t}\right\},\theta_{t}^{(a^{\dagger}_{t})}\right),a^{\dagger}_{t}\right)\overset{(b)}{\leq}\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},\theta_{t}^{*}\right),a^{*}_{t}\right),

and

which completes the proof of

5.5
.

\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},r\right)

a^{*}_{t}
is set to
r
.

At each round, our analysis considers four cases and analyzes the immediate regret respectively:

1.

a_{t}\in A^{+}_{t}
;

2.

a^{*}_{t}\in A^{-}_{t}
and
a_{t}\in A^{-}_{t}
;

3.

a^{*}_{t}\in A^{+}_{t}
,
a_{t}\in A^{-}_{t}
and
\Delta\left(a^{*}_{t};A^{-}_{t}\right)\geq\Delta
;

4.

a^{*}_{t}\in A^{+}_{t}
,
a_{t}\in A^{-}_{t}
and
\Delta\left(a^{*}_{t};A^{-}_{t}\right)<\Delta
.

Case I:
a_{t}\in A^{+}_{t}
.

In this case, our algorithm adds a context vector
\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})}
into
\mathcal{S}
and makes the dimension of
\mathrm{Span}(\mathcal{S})
added by one. Since the dimension of context vectors is
d
, this case happens at most
d
times. Therefore, the cumulative regret incurred by this case is at most
d
.

Case II:
a_{t}\in A^{-}_{t}
and
a^{*}_{t}\in A^{-}_{t}
.

In this case, the principal’s utility is
{\Pi}_{t}(x_{t})=\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})\right)
. We show that the immediate regret is
0
in this case.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
ALG
denote Algorithm
4
.
If round
t
of
ALG
falls into Case II, we have

\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{t}(\texttt{ALG})=0.

Proof.

By
Lemma

5.5
,

\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{t}(\texttt{ALG})\leq\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},\theta_{t}^{*}\right),a^{*}_{t}\right)-{\Pi}_{t}(x_{t})
.
By the condition of Case II, we have
\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},\theta_{t}^{*}\right),a^{*}_{t}\right)\leq\max_{a\in A^{-}_{t}}\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a\right\},\theta_{t}^{(a)}\right),a\right)
. Since action in
A^{-}_{t}
is independent of the hidden vector
\theta\in\Theta_{t}
, we have

\max_{a\in A^{-}_{t}}\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a\right\},\theta_{t}^{(a)}\right),a\right)=\max_{a\in A^{-}_{t}}\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t}),a\right)=\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})\right).

Overall, we have

\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},\theta_{t}^{*}\right),a^{*}_{t}\right)=\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})\right).

Therefore,
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{t}(\texttt{ALG})=0.
since
{\Pi}_{t}(x_{t})=\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})\right)
.
∎

Case III:
a^{*}_{t}\in A^{+}_{t}
,
a_{t}\in A^{-}_{t}
and
\Delta\left(a^{*}_{t};A^{-}_{t}\right)\geq\Delta
.

In this case, Algorithm
4
proposes non-degenerating contract
x_{t}\triangleq\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})
(
Definition

5.3
).

Let
\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})
be the instance equipped with action set
A^{-}_{t}
, then this instance is independent of
\theta
. Let the linear contract
x
vary from
0
to
1
in
\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})
, then different actions in
A^{-}_{t}
will be induced. We temporarily index the actions in
A^{-}_{t}
by this order. That is, we index the actions as
A^{-}_{t}\triangleq\left\{a_{t}^{(0)},a_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,a_{t}^{(m-1)}\right\}
where
m\triangleq\left|A^{-}_{t}\right|
. The index ensures that there exists
0=x_{t}^{(0)}\leq x_{t}^{(1)}\leq\ldots\leq x_{t}^{(m)}=1
such that
a_{t}^{(j)}
is induced if the principal issues contract
x\in[x_{t}^{(j)},x_{t}^{(j+1)})
. Moreover,
0=c^{(a_{t}^{(0)})}_{t}\leq c^{(a_{t}^{(1)})}_{t}\leq\ldots\leq c^{(a_{t}^{(m-1)})}_{t}
and
0=r^{(a_{t}^{(0)})}_{t}\leq r^{(a_{t}^{(1)})}_{t}\leq\ldots\leq r^{(a_{t}^{(m-1)})}_{t}
. Here, the action
a_{t}^{(0)}
is the null action.

Lemma 5.7
.

If round
t
in Algorithm
4
falls into Case III, then

\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})-x\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\Delta}\mathtt{InfoWidth}\left(\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t});\Theta_{t}\right).

Proof.

There exists
j\geq 1
such that
\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})=x_{t}^{(j)}
.
Moreover, there exists
k\leq j-1
such that
x\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\right)\in[x_{t}^{(k)},x_{t}^{(k+1)}]
since
x\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\right)\in[0,\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})]
. The above claim is easy to see on the
Figure

3
.

Let
U_{\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})}(x)
denote the agent’s utility in instance
\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})
when proposing the contract
x
. Namely,

U_{\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})}(x)=x\cdot r^{(a_{t}^{(i)})}_{t}-c^{(a_{t}^{(i)})}_{t},\mbox{ if }x\in[x_{t}^{(i)},x_{t}^{(i+1)}].

Let
f(x)
be the reward of
a^{*}_{t}
such that the min-pay contract that implements
a^{*}_{t}
is
x
. That is, it is the solution to

x\cdot f(x)-c^{(a^{*}_{t})}_{t}=U_{\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})}(x),\quad x\in(0,\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})].

By identical transformation,

f(x)=\frac{U_{\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})}(x)+c^{(a^{*}_{t})}_{t}}{x},\quad x\in(0,\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})].

Note that
U_{\mathcal{I}(A^{-}_{t})}(x)
is a continuous piecewise linear function. Thus,
f(x)
can also be written as a piecewise function. Moreover,
f(x)
is differentiable on
[x_{t}^{(i)},x_{t}^{(i+1)}]
and its derivative is

f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{r^{(a_{t}^{(i)})}_{t}}{x}-\frac{x\cdot r^{(a_{t}^{(i)})}_{t}+c^{(a^{*}_{t})}_{t}-c^{(a_{t}^{(i)})}_{t}}{x^{2}}=-\frac{c^{(a^{*}_{t})}_{t}-c^{(a_{t}^{(i)})}_{t}}{x^{2}},\quad\forall x\in[x_{t}^{(i)},x_{t}^{(i+1)}]

by simply applying derivative rules.

Note that
c^{(a^{*}_{t})}_{t}-c^{(a_{t}^{(i)})}_{t}\geq\Delta
by the condition of Case I and
x\leq 1
, we have
f^{\prime}(x)\leq-\Delta
almost everywhere on
[x\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\right),\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})]
. Since
f(x\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\right))=\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\langle\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\theta\rangle
and
f(\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t}))=\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})
, we have

\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})-\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\langle\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\theta\rangle\leq-\Delta\left(\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})-x\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\right)\right).

By identical transformation and the definition of information width, we have

\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})-x\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\Delta}\mathtt{InfoWidth}\left(\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t});\Theta_{t}\right).

This completes the proof of
Lemma

5.7
.

∎

Lemma 5.8

Let
ALG
denote Algorithm
4

of

Proof.

By the definition of our notations,
\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t},a^{*}_{t}\right)=\left(1-x\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\right)\right)\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\langle\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\theta\rangle

If

Therefore, whatever

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{t}(\texttt{ALG})

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{I}_{t},a^{*}_{t}\right)-{\Pi}_{t}(x_{t})

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\left(1-x\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\right)\right)\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\langle\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\theta\rangle-\left(1-\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})\right)\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})

\displaystyle=\left(1-x\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\right)\right)\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\langle\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\theta\rangle-\left(1-\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})\right)\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\langle\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\theta\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\left(1-\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})\right)\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\langle\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\theta\rangle-\left(1-\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})\right)\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})

\displaystyle=\left(\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})-x\left(\mathcal{I}_{t}\right)\right)\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\langle\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\theta\rangle+\left(1-\bar{x}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})\right)\left(\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\langle\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\theta\rangle-\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\leq}\frac{1}{\Delta}\mathtt{InfoWidth}\left(\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t});\Theta_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{\Delta}\mathtt{InfoWidth}\left(\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t});\Theta_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\Delta}\mathtt{InfoWidth}\left(\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t});\Theta_{t}\right),

where inequality (a) is because of

we have just proved, and inequality (c) is by

\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{t}}\langle\mu_{t}^{(a^{*}_{t})},\theta\rangle\leq 1

6
Conclusion and Future Directions

This paper pioneers the study of contextual learning in the general principal-agent game. Our central finding is that the degeneracy of the agent’s action set fundamentally increases the complexity of the learning problem, rendering
O(\log\log T)
-type regret bounds impossible. This work opens up several promising avenues for future research:

Optimal classic Stackelberg regret bound.
Though our algorithm is asymptotically optimal in the sense of pessimistic Stackelberg regret, there still exists a gap in the classic Stackelberg regret bound. It is not clear whether one could prove a classic Stackelberg regret lower bound similar to the pessimistic regret lower bound in this paper.
On the other hand, since the regret bound in most of the previous works is essentially the pessimistic Stackelberg regret bound, achieving an
O(\sqrt{T})
upper bound for the classic regret will likely require novel algorithmic and analytical approaches that do not rely on immediate, round-by-round regret estimation. In addition, it would be interesting to explore whether our analysis framework with information width can be applied to the classic contextual pricing model and obtain the optimal regret of
O_{d}(\log\log T)
.

Stochastic contexts.
We suggest an inquiry into stochastic contexts. Our current lower bound construction heavily relies on adversarially selecting the context each round. Notably, to the best of our knowledge, the adversarial context is considered in all prior work in the contextual pricing literature
(
Lobel et al. 2018
;
Leme and Schneider 2018
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Krishnamurthy et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. A compelling open problem is to determine if assuming an i.i.d. context sampled from an unknown distribution would circumvent this hardness result, potentially permitting the achievement of more favorable
O(\log T)
or
O(\log\log T)
regret bounds.

Stochastic outcomes and hidden action model.
Our work follows the line of works on contextual pricing, assuming deterministic feedback. Another significant direction for future work is to consider the contextual learning problem of more general principal-agent models incorporating stochastic outcomes and hidden actions, as seen in settings like contract design. As this model is a superset of our own, our lower bound holds directly, highlighting the inherent challenges in this broader class of problems.

Appendix A
Discussion on Practical Applications

Our model provides a unified foundation for studying sequential decision-making and learning in principal–agent interactions under contextual uncertainty. The cost-context setting captures scenarios in which the principal faces uncertainty about agents’ effort costs while action rewards are known. This includes crowdsourcing markets: the platform repeatedly offers payment contracts over multiple tasks to sequentially arriving workers characterized by cost-related context features; each worker chooses a task based on private, task-specific effort cost. The platform observes only the chosen task and the realized outcome, and learns over time how the observed context predicts hidden costs.

The reward-context setting, by contrast, captures situations in which the principal can estimate the cost for an agent to perform each action—such as required time, resources, or effort—yet remains uncertain about the resulting benefit. For example, in product development or R&D, the sponsor (a government agency, firm, or university) issues repeated funding calls across multiple projects and offers outcome-contingent contracts. Teams arrive over time with contextual features on execution capacity, risk management, and impact potential; each submits a proposal and bids for a project based on private, project-specific success assessments, and the sponsor learns from outcomes how context predicts rewards.

x_{0}\cdot r_{0}-c^{(a^{*}_{t})}\geq x_{0}\cdot r^{(a^{\dagger})}-c^{(a^{\dagger})},\forall a^{\dagger}\in A^{-},

which directly leads
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},r_{0}\right),a^{*}_{t}}
. Thus
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},r_{0}\right),a^{*}_{t}}\neq\emptyset
and
r_{0}\in S
. Since our argument holds for any converging sequence
\left\{r_{n}\right\}_{n}
,
S
is closed. Furthermore,
S\subseteq[0,1]
, so
S
is compact.
∎

Lemma E.2
.

We have
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})\right),a^{*}_{t}}\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, it is a closed interval in
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

E.1
,
\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})\in\left\{r\in[0,1]\,\mid\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},r\right),a^{*}_{t}}\neq\emptyset\right\}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},\bar{r}_{t}(a^{*}_{t})\right),a^{*}_{t}}\neq\emptyset
. By definition,
\left\{r\in[0,1]\,\mid\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},r\right),a^{*}_{t}}\neq\emptyset\right\}
is closed and convex. Since

\left\{r\in[0,1]\,\mid\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{t}\left(A^{-}_{t}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{t}\right\},r\right),a^{*}_{t}}\neq\emptyset\right\}\subseteq[0,1],

it is a closed interval (or singleton).
∎
