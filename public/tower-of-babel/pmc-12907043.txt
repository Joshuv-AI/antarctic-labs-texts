From coordination to function: Inorganic insights into metalloproteins and metallopeptides in biosystems
Authors: Jiyeon Han
Published: 2025

Metal ions are often framed in terms of toxicity, yet this standpoint inversely emphasizes the underlying chemical principle of how cells regulate metal homeostasis and why biological systems highly depend on them. Inside cells, trace amounts of metal ions such as Fe(II/III), Cu(I/II), and Zn(II) exist not as free ions but as components of highly buffered, precisely coordinated labile pools [1–4]. Mislocalized redox-active metal ions can exacerbate oxidative stress by overproducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) through Fenton chemistry [5,6]. In particular, ROS can induce oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA, all of which potentially contribute to neuronal death and progression of neurodegenerative diseases and cancer [7–9]. On the other hand, when non-native heavy metal ions, e.g., Cd(II), Pb(II), or Hg(II), enter the system, they compete with endogenous metal centers of static pools, frequently prevailing through higher binding affinity and slower off-rates [10,11]. In detail, heavy metals with larger ionic radii exhibit enhanced affinity for Cys, His, Asp, and Glu residues, enabling them to adopt flexible coordination geometry. As a representative example, lead poisoning is related to Pb(II) substitution for Zn(II) center in 5-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase [12]. Although Pb(II) can replace Zn(II) with minimal perturbation to the overall protein folding, its enzymatic function is lost, subsequently leading to microcytic or normocytic anemia due to impaired hemoglobin synthesis, along with decreased heme-dependent mitochondrial cytochromes that compromise ATP synthesis. Taken together, such mis-metalation in labile and static pools disrupts the metal coordination sphere, accompanied by the alteration in geometry, ligand-field stabilization, protonation states, and redox properties. These cytotoxic profiles arising from metal dyshomeostasis underscore how precisely tuned metal–protein interactions are indispensable for maintaining proper biological functions. In this context, this commentary covers the coordination-function relationships of metalloproteins and metallopeptides with comprehensive examples and outlines future directions where inorganic chemistry can provide critical insights.

Myoglobin and hemoglobin, responsible for dioxygen (O2) transport and delivery in muscle tissues, represent the first example of metalloproteins whose three-dimensional structure was elucidated by X-ray crystallography. The Fe(II) center is embedded within an organic macrocycle, the porphyrin, composed of four pyrrole units that coordinate Fe(II) in a nearly square-planar arrangement (Figure 1a). In addition to these four nitrogen donors, the axial His provides the fifth ligand, while the sixth coordination site is open for O2. As expected from the highest occupied molecular orbital of O2, the Fe(II)–O2 adduct adopts a bent geometry where the Fe–O–O bond angle is 156° as illustrated in Figure 1b [13]. Although carbon monoxide (CO) can bind more strongly to the metal center via π-backbonding, its linear geometry is disfavored within the protein matrix. This structural constraint highlights that the globular architecture of metalloproteins also enforces selectivity against desired ligands to achieve their precise biological function. More interestingly, despite the redox properties of Fe(II/III) couple, neither oxidation of Fe(II) nor dimerization occurs for myoglobin and hemoglobin, reflecting that its primary role is focused on reversible O2 binding rather than O2 activation [14]. In contrast to hemoproteins specialized for O2 transport, heme enzymes such as cytochrome P450 or catalase employ distinct axial ligands that tune the electronic structure of the Fe center for oxidative catalysis [15]. For example, cytochrome P450 features a thiolate ligand from an axial Cys residue that strongly donates electron density and stabilizes high-valent Fe(IV)=O species. The Fe(IV)-oxo porphyrin π-cation radical intermediate, called compound I, is required for C–H bond activation of substrates in the catalytic cycle (Figure 2a). Beyond the O-atom transfer chemistry, certain heme proteins can participate in rapid electron transfer with the minimal reorganization energy, as exemplified by mitochondrial cytochromes in the electron transport chain that drive oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis.

Whereas heme proteins derive their reactivity from a rigid porphyrin macrocycle, non-heme metalloenzymes construct their ligand environment from side chains of amino acids, e.g., His, Glu, Asp, or Cys [16]. The active site of mononuclear non-heme enzymes like taurine dioxygenase or α-ketoglutarate-dependent oxygenases typically shares a conserved structural motif where the octahedral Fe(II) center is coordinated by a facial triad composed of two His residues and one Asp (or Glu) residue, as shown in Figure 2b. The remaining three coordination sites are occupied by readily displaceable water molecules, providing open positions for O2 or other substrates, ultimately forming Fe(IV)=O intermediates for catalytic reactions. Interestingly, a similar principle underlying coordination-function relationships applies to redox-active Cu(I/II) metalloenzymes (Figure 2c), where the coordination sphere governs both the geometric and electronic structure of the active site [17]: Cu(I) typically adopts a tetrahedral geometry, whereas Cu(II) favors square-planar or square-pyramidal. Copper enzymes are commonly categorized into type 1 (blue copper), type 2 (non-blue copper), and type 3 (binuclear copper) centers based on their spectroscopic and coordination features [18]. These distinct coordination environments allow Cu(I/II) to carry out a wide range of redox transformations. Specifically, type 1 centers mediate long-range electron transfer, as exemplified by azurin and plastocyanin; type 2 centers catalyze substrate oxidation in enzymes such as dopamine β-monooxygenase and peptidylglycine α-hydroxylating monooxygenase; and type 3 binuclear Cu sites activate O2 in tyrosinase and laccase.

Unlike redox-active metals, divalent Zn(II) operates mainly as a strong Lewis acid throughout catalytic reactions, attributed to its relatively small ionic radius and highly concentrated charge density [19]. Its d10 closed-shell configuration renders the metal strictly redox-inert, eliminating the risk of generating free radicals as well as removing ligand-field stabilization effects that would otherwise restrict coordination geometry. Consequently, Zn(II) center with flexible coordination numbers and geometries is dictated primarily by ligand size or charge. Its borderline hardness enables coordination to oxygen donors (Asp, Glu, and H2O), nitrogen donors (His), and sulfur donors (Cys), allowing Zn(II)-dependent proteins to achieve precise catalytic roles without electron-transfer reactions. Indeed, the mechanism of most Zn(II) metalloenzymes, e.g., hydrolases, peptidases, and alcohol dehydrogenases, originates from the Lewis acidity of Zn(II) to generate nucleophilic hydroxide or facilitate the deprotonation of the substrate. In addition to the catalytic roles, Zn(II) contributes to the structural stabilization of proteins, most notably in zinc-finger domains [20], adopting a tetrahedral geometry typically coordinated by a combination of Cys and His residues. A classical Zn(II) finger features two Cys from a β-hairpin and two His from an α-helix, forming a characteristic ββα fold. This structural scaffold is responsible for DNA recognition, thereby mediating transcriptional regulation, DNA repair, or epigenetics.

Different from the aforementioned metalloproteins typically larger than 5 kDa, the metal binding of smaller-sized peptides relies on backbone amide groups [21]. Although metal coordination through amide bonds with pKa values in the range of 15 to 18 is apparently considered challenging, chelate effects arising from continuous amide bonds can generate thermodynamically favorable 5- or 6-membered rings in metal–peptide complexes even in the absence of classical side chains (Figure 3a). For example, Cu(II) can bind to the native neuropeptide, e.g., oxytocin or somatostatin, through the backbone amide bonds where an N-terminal free amine serves as the initial anchoring site, followed by sequential involvement of amide bond(s) depending on pH [22,23]. On the other hand, some peptides containing the ATCUN motif such as histatin-5 exhibit a far more defined and high-affinity Cu(II) coordination mode, in which the N-terminal amine, two adjacent deprotonated backbone amides, and an imidazole from His form a rigid square-planar geometry (Figure 3b) [24]. Although the catalytic roles of these metallopeptides are less understood, recent studies have begun to suggest how metal ions influence downstream cellular signaling pathways of hormonal peptides or antimicrobial effects [25–27].

Unlike large, well-folded metalloproteins, structural characterization of metallopeptides by X-ray crystallography is often impractical due to their small size and conformational flexibility, underscoring the importance of spectroscopic and computational approaches to define their coordination spheres [28–30]. Electronic absorption and CD spectroscopy provide useful information on d-d transitions, charge-transfer bands, and metal-induced changes in secondary structures. NMR spectroscopy enables the identification of coordinating residues in diamagnetic systems, whereas EPR spectroscopy is invaluable for analyzing paramagnetic metal centers, allowing detailed analysis of ligand fields, coordination geometry, and hyperfine coupling. X-ray absorption spectroscopy with XANES and EXAFS analysis offers direct insight into oxidation states, coordination numbers, and metal–ligand bond distances. Metal-to-peptide stoichiometry and binding thermodynamics can be deduced by isothermal titration calorimetry, together with mass spectrometry or fluorescence quenching experiments. Complementary computational approaches, including DFT calculations and MD simulations, will further assist in predicting preferred geometries, evaluating electronic structures, and integrating experimental observations into coherent coordination models of metallopeptides.

To summarize, a clear understanding of the coordination environment around a metal center provides the fundamental basis for interpreting diverse functions of metalloproteins and enables the rational prediction of their reactivity. The inherent ability of transition metal ions to recruit and align amino acid residues within peptide sequences makes coordination chemistry to serve as a powerful design principle for engineering peptide-based catalysts, nanomaterials, and supramolecular assemblies with programmable structures and functions [31,32]. Taking advantage of the ease of synthesis and the capacity to modify sequences with precisely positioned donor groups [33], metalloproteins and metallopeptides offer chemically tunable platforms for catalysis, electron transfer, molecular recognition, and the development of functional biomaterials. These insights from inorganic chemistry underscore the broader potential of de novo design of proteins and/or peptides beyond their traditional biological roles, opening new opportunities in molecular engineering and applications in the near future.
