Abstract
. In this article, we study how to compute the number of K K -rational points with a given j j -invariant on an arbitrary modular curve. As an application, for each positive integer n n , we determine the list of possible numbers of cyclic n n -isogenies an elliptic curve over some number field can admit. Similarly, for an odd prime power p k p^{k} , we calculate the possible values for the number of points above some j j -invariant on Cartan modular curves X s ​ ( p k ) X_{\mathrm{s}}(p^{k}) , X ns ​ ( p k ) X_{\mathrm{ns}}(p^{k}) and their normalizers. Combining known results about images of Galois representations of CM elliptic curves with our work, we also devise a simple algorithm to determine the number of rational CM points on any modular curve.

Introduction

Let
H
be a subgroup of
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
and let
X_{H}
be the corresponding modular curve. The aim of this article is to explore the possible cardinalities of the sets

\{x\in X_{H}(K)\mid j(x)=j_{0}\},

where
K
is any number field and
j_{0}
is any element of
K
. In our work, we do not attempt to pin down the number fields or elliptic curves for which each cardinality is achieved. In that sense, our work is purely group-theoretic.

In particular, setting
X_{H}
to be the modular curve
X_{0}(n)
, we determine the list of possible values of the number of
n
-isogenies of an elliptic curve over a number field. Recall that an
n
-isogeny is a cyclic isogeny of degree
n
.

Isogenies of elliptic curves over number fields are an interesting and well-studied topic. There is a lot of interest in classifying possible degrees of isogenies over number fields. Mazur
[
11
]
and Kenku
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
have determined all possible degrees of cyclic isogenies of elliptic curves defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. This amounts to finding all positive integers
n
for which there exists a non-cuspidal rational point on the modular curve
X_{0}(n)
. A generalization of this problem is the problem of classifying all points of some degree
d
on
X_{0}(n)
. For
d>1
, this is much more difficult than for
d=1
. There is no complete classification of all degree
d
points on all
X_{0}(n)
for no value of
d>1
. However, there has been a lot of recent progress in the case
d=2
. One of the hurdles which makes the problem of finding all quadratic points on
X_{0}(n)
difficult is the fact that for infinitely many
n
there exist quadratic points on
X_{0}(n)
corresponding to elliptic curves with complex multiplication (CM). This complicates the problem, as it is usually easier to prove that a curve has no points than to prove that a non-empty list of points which were found is complete. When determining quadratic points on
X_{0}(n)
, it is often useful to count the points on
X_{0}(n)
with a given CM
j
-invariant, or the total number of quadratic CM points. For example, in
[
1
]
, Adžaga, Keller, Michaud-Jacobs, Najman, Ozman and Vukorepa developed various techniques for finding quadratic points on
X_{0}(n)
. In some cases, it is neccessary to count the number of CM points. The methods they use to count the CM points are often computationally difficult.

One may also fix a
j
-invariant
j_{0}
and classify all possible degrees of points on
X_{0}(n)
whose

-invariant equals
j_{0}
. This question was explored by Terao in
[
15

for modular curves

and

, and solved in the case of rational

-invariants. Similarly, in
[
4

, Clark, Genao, Pollack and Saia consider the problem of determining the least degree of a CM point on modular curves

In this paper, we discuss questions of a similar type. As mentioned, we determine all possible values of the number of cyclic

2.
Number of
K
-rational points on a modular curve with a given
j
-invariant

We start by recalling general definitions regarding modular curves. Let
K
be a number field and
j\in K
. Let
H\leq\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
be a subgroup. The non-cuspidal points on the modular curve
X_{H}
are classes
[(E,\alpha)]
, where
E
is an elliptic curve and
\alpha
is an
H
-level structure on
E
. More precisely, define the following equivalence relation on the set of all ordered bases for
E[N]
. We say
(P,Q)
and
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
are equivalent mod
H
if there is some
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\in H
such that
P^{\prime}=aP+cQ
and
Q^{\prime}=bP+dQ
. An
H
-level structure
\alpha
is an equivalence class with respect to this relation.

Furthermore, we say
(E,\alpha)
and
(E^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
are isomorphic if there is an isomorphism of elliptic curves
\phi:E\to E^{\prime}
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
such that if
(P,Q)
is a representative for the level structure
\alpha
, then
(\phi(P),\phi(Q))
is a representative for the level structure
\alpha^{\prime}
. A point
x
on
X_{H}
is an ordered pair
(E,\alpha)
, up to the mentioned isomorphism.

A point
x
on
X_{H}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
is
K
-rational if
\sigma(x)=x
for all
\sigma\in G_{K}
, where we define
\sigma([(E,\alpha)])
to be
[(E^{\sigma},\alpha^{\sigma})]
, where if
\alpha
is
(P,Q)\pmod{H}
, then
\alpha^{\sigma}
is
(P^{\sigma},Q^{\sigma})\pmod{H}
. This means that
E
and
E^{\sigma}
are isomorphic over the algebraic closure
\overline{K}
, and the isomorphism sends
(P,Q)\pmod{H}
to
(P^{\sigma},Q^{\sigma})\pmod{H}
. Since isomorphic elliptic curves have the same
j
-invariant, we have
j(E)^{\sigma}=j(E)
, hence
j(E)\in K
.

This allows us to pick
E^{\prime}
isomorphic to
E
such that
E^{\prime}
is defined over
K
, since all elliptic curves with the same
j
-invariant are isomorphic over the algebraic closure. Thus, it is not a loss of generality to assume that
E
is defined over
K
and
E=E^{\sigma}
. This means that the isomorphism is in fact an automorphism
\phi:E\to E
, and a rational point on
X_{H}
can be represented as
(E,(P,Q)\pmod{H})
, where

(P^{\sigma},Q^{\sigma})\pmod{H}=(\phi(P),\phi(Q))\pmod{H}.

In other words, for every
\sigma\in G_{K}
, there exists an automorphism
\phi
of
E
such that
(P^{\sigma},Q^{\sigma})
equals
h(\phi(P),\phi(Q))
for some
h\in H
.

Denote by
\rho_{E,N}
the mod
N
Galois representation of an elliptic curve
E
. The images of
\rho_{E,N}(G_{K})
and
\Aut(E)
inside
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
depend on the choice of basis for
E[N]
. Let
R_{(P,Q)}
and
A_{(P,Q)}
respectively denote the images inside
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
of
\rho_{E,N}(G_{K})
and
\Aut(E)
with respect to the basis
(P,Q)
for
E[N]
. The point
(E,(P,Q)\pmod{H})
is
K
-rational if and only for all
r\in R_{(P,Q)}
there exists some
h\in H
and
a\in A_{(P,Q)}
such that
r=ha
. To determine the number of
K
-rational points on
X_{H}
with a fixed
j
-invariant, we need to take any
E/K
with that
j
-invariant and count the number of bases
(P,Q)
such that
R_{(P,Q)}\subseteq H\cdot A_{(P,Q)}
, up to the action of
H
and up to the action of
\Aut(E)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
E/K
be an elliptic curve and
(P_{0},Q_{0})
a fixed basis for
E[N]
. Denote by
A
the image of the action of
\Aut(E)
on
E[N]
, written in basis
(P_{0},Q_{0})
. Let
(P,Q)=g(P_{0},Q_{0})
be another basis for
E[N]
, where
g\in\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
. The number of bases
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
such that
(E,(P,Q)\pmod{H})
and
(E,(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\pmod{H})
define the same point on
X_{H}
is equal to the cardinality of the double coset
HgA
.

Proof.

Any basis
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
can be uniquely written as
g(P_{0},Q_{0})
for some
g\in\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
. Then
(E,g_{1}(P_{0},Q_{0})\pmod{H})
and
(E,g_{2}(P_{0},Q_{0})\pmod{H})
define the same point on
X_{H}
if and only if
g_{1}(P_{0},Q_{0})\pmod{H}=g_{2}(\phi(P),\phi(Q))\pmod{H}
for some
\phi\in\Aut(E)
, which happens if and only if there are some
h\in H
and
\phi\in\Aut(E)
such that

g_{1}(P_{0},Q_{0})=h(g_{2}(a(P_{0},Q_{0}))),

where
a
denotes the matrix of action by the automorphism
\phi
in basis
(P_{0},Q_{0})
. This is equivalent to
g_{1}=hg_{2}a
.

\Box

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
E/K
be any elliptic curve with
j
-invariant
j_{0}
. Fix a basis
(P,Q)
for
E[N]
. Denote by
R
the image
\rho_{E,N}(G_{K})
in this basis and by
A
the image of action of
\Aut(E)
on
E[N]
in this basis. The number of
K
-rational points
x
on

with

equals

\sum_{g\in\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{gR\subseteq HgA\}}}{\#(HgA)}.

Proof.

Note that the change of basis is equivalent to conjugating the groups
R
and
A
by some element
g\in\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
. This means that the corresponding basis
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
yields a rational point on
X_{H}
if and only if

gRg^{-1}\subseteq H\cdot gAg^{-1}.

By
Lemma

2.1
, there are
\#(HgA)
bases which yield the same point on
X_{H}
, so we need to divide the corresponding summand by this number.

\Box

Remark 2.3
.

Note that the condition
gR\subseteq HgA
in fact only depends on the double coset of
g
, so the sum can be written to only span over double cosets, and equals

\#\{HgA\in H\backslash G/A\mid gR\subseteq HgA\}.

Namely, if
gR\subseteq HgA
and
h\in H
, then
hgR\subseteq hHgA=HhgA
.

Similarly, suppose that
gR\subseteq HgA
and
a\in A
. Let
gar\in gaR
. Then note that
ra=a^{\prime}r
for some
a^{\prime}\in A
, since if
\phi\in\Aut(E)
and
\sigma\in G_{K}
, then
\sigma(\phi(P))=\phi^{\sigma}(\sigma(P))
, and
\phi^{\sigma}\in\Aut(E)
. In other words, each

normalizes
A
, so

, and
gar\in gRa^{\prime}\subseteq HgAa^{\prime}=HgA
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
j_{0}\neq 0,1728
. With the notation from the previous proposition, the number of
K
-rational points

X_{H}
with

equals

\frac{\#\{g\in\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})\mid gRg^{-1}\subseteq\pm H\}}{\#(\pm H)}.

Note that the number of points on
X_{H}
and

is the same. The following well-known lemma explains this in a different way.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
\rho_{E,N}(G_{K})
is a subgroup of
\pm H
in some basis for
E[N]
and
-I\not\in H
, there is a quadratic twist
E^{\prime}
of

defined over
K
such that
\rho_{E^{\prime},N}(G_{K})
is conjugate to a subgroup of

.

Proof.

Take a basis in which the Galois image
\rho_{E,N}(G_{K})
is a subgroup of
\pm H
. Now, for each
\sigma\in G_{K}
, we either have
\rho(\sigma)\in H
or
\rho(\sigma)\in-H
, which defines a homomorphism

. The kernel of this homomorphism is an index
2

for some

, and the mentioned homomorphism is the corresponding quadratic character

\rho_{E^{\prime},N}(\sigma)=\chi_{d}(\sigma)\rho_{E,N}(\sigma)\in H.

If the point
x=(E,(P,Q)\pmod{H})
on

is not defined over

for

, one might be interested in determining the degree of

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
E/K
be an elliptic curve, and fix a basis
(P_{0},Q_{0})
for
E[N]
. Let
R\leq\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
be the Galois image
\rho_{E,N}(G_{K})
in that basis, and let

be the image of the action of

on

in that basis. Let
(P,Q)=g(P_{0},Q_{0})
be a different basis, with

. The degree over

of the point
(E,(P,Q)\pmod{H})
on the modular curve

is equal to the index
[R:(R\cap(g^{-1}HgA))]
.

Remark 2.7
.

Note that
g^{-1}HgA
is not neccessarily a group if
A
is larger than
\pm I
. However, in this setting,
R\cap(g^{-1}HgA)
turns out to be a group.

Proof.

The action of the Galois group

on bases for

modulo the action of
H
and

is realised as the action of the group
R
on double cosets
HgA
for

, and is given by
\sigma(HgA)=HgrA
, where

is the matrix determined by the action of
\sigma
on
(P_{0},Q_{0})
.

The degree of
(E,g(P_{0},Q_{0})\pmod{H})
is equal to the size of the orbit of
HgA
under the action of

, which is equal to the index of the stabilizer of
HgA
inside

. The coset

is stabilized if and only if

, which is equivalent with

, since

normalizes

.

Now note that

holds for some

if and only if

. Namely, if
grg^{-1}=hgag^{-1}
for some
h\in H
,

, then

Conversely, if
HgA=HgrA
, then for each

, there are some

and

with

, which implies

for some

,

. Thus, the stabilizer of

.

\Box

\frac{\#\{g\in\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})\mid gRg^{-1}\subseteq B_{0}(p^{k})\}}{\#B_{0}(p^{k})},

where
B_{0}(p^{k})
is the group of uppertriangular invertible
2\times 2
matrices modulo
p^{k}
.
Our goal is to determine all possible values that this expression can obtain, as
R
varies over all subgroups of
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
.

More generally, let
N
be a positive integer and
H
a subgroup of
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
. Denote by
S(H)
the set of all values of the expression

\frac{\#\{g\in\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})\mid gRg^{-1}\subseteq H\}}{\#H},

as
R
ranges over all subgroups of
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
k
a positive integer. The set
S(B_{0}(p^{k}))
is contained in the set

\{0\}\cup\{p^{j}\mid 0\leq j<k\}\cup\{2p^{j}\mid 0\leq j<k\}\cup\{p^{k}+p^{k-1}\}.

Proof.

Let
R
be a subgroup of
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
. If
R
fixes no subgroups of
\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}
of order
p^{k}
, the number is
0
. Otherwise,
R
is conjugate to a subgroup of
B_{0}(p^{k})
, and it is not a loss of generality to assume that
R
itself is a subgroup of
B_{0}(p^{k})
.

Now suppose that
gRg^{-1}\subseteq B_{0}(p^{k})
. If
g=\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
w&z\end{bmatrix}
, that means that for every
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\in R
, the bottom-left entry of
\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
w&z\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
w&z\end{bmatrix}^{-1}
is equal to
0
. Explicitly calculating the bottom-left entry, we obtain the equality

(1)

w(z(a-c)-bw)=0,\ \text{for all }\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\in R.

We now need to calculate the number of pairs
(w,z)
for which this holds. Note that since
g\in\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
, at least one among
w
and
z
is not divisible by
p
.

Suppose that
w\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. Dividing the equality (
1
) by
w^{2}
and setting
x=\frac{-z}{w}
, we obtain

(c-a)x=b,\text{ for all }\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\in R.

This is a system of equations in one variable over
\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}
. We will call solutions of this system “type A solutions”.

Suppose that
w\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. Then
z\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. Dividing
eq.

1
by
z^{2}
and setting
y=\frac{-w}{z}
, we obtain

(c-a)y=by^{2},\text{ for all }\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\in R.

Note that there is an additional constraint that
y\in p\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}
. We will call solutions of this system “type B solutions”.

Now note that, for every solution of any type, there are
\#B_{0}(p^{k})
different matrices
\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
w&z\end{bmatrix}
above it. Namely, if
x
is a solution of type A, then we can choose
w
to be any number coprime to
p
in
\varphi(p^{k})
ways, we then have
z=-wx
, and we can choose
u
and
v
in
p^{k}\cdot\varphi(p^{k})
ways in such a way that
(u,v)
and
(z,w)
are coprime. Similarly, if
y
is a solution of type B, we choose
z
to be any number coprime to
p
, which uniquely determines
w
, and we get
p^{k}\cdot\varphi(p^{k})
ways to choose the entire matrix. Since
\#B_{0}(p^{k})=p^{k}\cdot\varphi(p^{k})^{2}
, this proves our claim.

Let
A
and
B
respectively denote the number of solutions of type A and type B. We now need to determine all possible values of
A+B
.

Denote by
\alpha
the least possible value of
v_{p}(c-a)
and by
\beta
the least possible value of
v_{p}(b)
, for
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\in R
.

Suppose that there exists a type A solution
x
. This means that there exists some
x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}
such that
b=(c-a)x
for all
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\in R
. Note that
x
is a solution of type A if and only if
x+p^{k-\alpha}
is a solution, and there is a unique solution modulo
p^{\alpha}
. Thus, there are
p^{\alpha}
solutions of type A.

Since
b=(c-a)x
, any equation of the system for type B solutions can be written as

(c-a)y=(c-a)xy^{2}.

Note that any
y
such that
v_{p}(y)\geq k-\alpha
is a solution of type B. There are
p^{\alpha}
such solutions, unless
\alpha=k
since
y
has to be divisible by
p
. In that case, there are
p^{k-1}
such solutions.

Suppose that some other
y
is a solution. Take
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}
such that
v_{p}(c-a)=\alpha
. Then
v_{p}((c-a)y)<v_{p}((c-a)by^{2})
, and we reach a contradiction. Thus, there are
p^{\alpha}
solutions of type B, unless
\alpha=k
in which case there are
p^{k-1}
solutions. In total, there are
2p^{\alpha}
fixed subgroups for
\alpha<k
and
p^{k}+p^{k-1}
fixed subgroups for
\alpha=k
.

Now suppose that there are no type A solutions. We say a type B solution
y
is
proper
if
v_{p}(y)<k-\alpha
, so that
(c-a)y\neq 0
for some
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\in R
.

If there are no proper solutions, then all solutions
y
satisfy
(c-a)y=0
and
by^{2}=0
for all
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\in\rho
. This is equivalent to
v_{p}(y)\geq k-\alpha
and
v_{p}(y)\geq\frac{k-\beta}{2}
.

In total, there are
p^{\min\left(\alpha,\left\lfloor\frac{k+\beta}{2}\right\rfloor\right)}
solutions.

Now suppose that there is a proper solution. We first prove that there exists an element
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\in R
with
v_{p}(c-a)=\alpha
and
v_{p}(b)=\beta
.

Suppose that there is no such element. Take
\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}\\
0&c_{1}\end{bmatrix}
with
v_{p}(a_{1}-c_{1})=\alpha
and
\begin{bmatrix}a_{2}&b_{2}\\
0&c_{2}\end{bmatrix}
with
v_{p}(b_{2})=\beta
. We claim that the product
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}
of these elements has
v_{p}(c-a)=\alpha
and
v_{p}(b)=\beta
. Namely, we have

\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}\\
0&c_{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a_{2}&b_{2}\\
0&c_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}a_{2}&a_{1}b_{2}+b_{1}c_{2}\\
0&c_{1}c_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Then

c-a=c_{1}c_{2}-a_{1}a_{2}=c_{1}(c_{2}-a_{2})+a_{2}(c_{1}-a_{1})

and

b=a_{1}b_{2}+b_{1}c_{2}.

Since
a_{i}
and
c_{i}
are invertible, the claim follows from the elementary fact that
v_{p}(x+y)=v_{p}(x)
whenever
v_{p}(x)<v_{p}(y)
.

If
y
is a proper solution, taking
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}
which minimises
v_{p}(c-a)
and
v_{p}(b)
, we obtain

\alpha+v_{p}(y)=\beta+2v_{p}(y)<k,

which implies
v_{p}(y)=\alpha-\beta>0
. Thus, any proper solution has fixed
v_{p}
. This means that any other proper solution is of the form
ry
for some invertible
r\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{k-\alpha+\beta}\mathbb{Z}
, and one has

(c-a)ry=br^{2}y^{2},

which is equivalent with
r\equiv 1\pmod{p^{k-2\alpha+\beta}}
, which has
p^{\alpha}
solutions in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{k-\alpha+\beta}\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, there are
p^{\alpha}
proper solutions.

We have already seen that the number of improper solutions is
p^{\min\left(\alpha,\left\lfloor\frac{k+\beta}{2}\right\rfloor\right)}
. We now need to prove
\alpha<\frac{k+\beta}{2}
. However, this is equivalent with
2\alpha-\beta<k
, which holds since for a proper solution
y
, one has
v_{p}((c-a)y)=2\alpha-\beta<k
. Thus, there are also
p^{\alpha}
improper solutions, and
2p^{\alpha}
solutions in total, which completes the proof.

Note that there cannot be
p^{k}
solutions, since we would then need to have
\alpha=k
and
\lfloor(k+\beta)/2\rfloor=k
, which would imply
\beta=k
and all matrices in
R
would be scalar matrices, which would give us
p^{k}+p^{k-1}
solutions.

\Box

Remark 3.2
.

Note that one can also arrive to the systems of equations of type A and type B directly. Namely, each cyclic subgroup of order
p^{k}
either has a generator of the form
(x,1)
or of the form
(1,y)
for
y
divisible by
p
. In the first case, the subgroup is fixed if and only if
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}sx\\
s\end{bmatrix},
which is equivalent with
(c-a)x=b
, and this yields the system of type A. In the second case, the subgroup is fixed if and only if
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}s\\
sy\end{bmatrix},
which is equivalent with
(c-a)y=by^{2}
, and this yields the system of type B.

Proposition 3.3
.

Each number listed in
Proposition

3.1
is achieved for some number field
K
and some elliptic curve
E/K
.

Proof.
We keep the same notation from
Proposition

3.1
. It suffices to find a subgroup
R
which fixes a given number of subgroups, and then we can reconstruct a field and an elliptic curve as explained before. If
R
is a subgroup consisting only of scalar matrices, then the number
p^{k}+p^{k-1}
is achieved, and vice versa. Note that it is enough to check that all generators of
R
fix a subgroup in order for the entire
R
to fix it.

If
R
is not conjugate to a subgroup of the group of all uppertriangular matrices, for example if
R=\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
, then
0
is achieved.

Now fix
j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
, and take
R
to be the cyclic subgroup generated by
\begin{bmatrix}1&p^{j}\\
0&p^{j}+1\end{bmatrix}
. The type A equation is
p^{j}x=p^{j}
, which has
p^{j}
solutions. The type B equation is
p^{j}y=p^{j}y^{2}
, which also has
p^{j}
solutions and we get
2p^{j}
solutions in total. This settles the case of
2p^{j}
for
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
.

It remains to prove that each power
p^{j}
for
j=0,\ldots,k-1
can appear. To do this, we must ensure there are no solutions of type A and no proper solutions of type B, and
\min(\alpha,\lfloor(k+\beta)/2\rfloor)
should be
j
.

For the case
j=0
, take
R
to be the group of all uppertriangular matrices. Then
\alpha=0
and there are no solutions of type A, no proper solutions of type B and only
y=0
is an improper solution of type B.

Now let
0<j<k
. Take
R
to be the subgroup generated by
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1+p^{j}\end{bmatrix}
and
\begin{bmatrix}1&p^{j}\\
0&1+p^{j}\end{bmatrix}
. There are no type A solutions, and the type B equations are
p^{j}y=p^{j}y^{2}
and
p^{j}y=0
. There are
p^{j}
solutions. This completes the proof.

\Box

The case
p=2
is similar for the most part but the answer is slightly different, so we handle it separately.

Proposition 3.4
.

We have
S(B_{0}(2))=\{0,1,3\}
. For
k>1
,

S(B_{0}(2^{k}))=\{0\}\cup\{2^{m}\mid m=1,\ldots,k\}\cup\{2^{k}+2^{k-1}\}.

Proof.

The entire proof of
Proposition

3.1
is also valid for
p=2
. The only difference is that one cannot have
\alpha=0
, as
c-a
is always divisible by
2
.

If there exists a type A solution and
\alpha<k
, then there are
2^{\alpha}
type A solutions, and
2^{\alpha}
type B solutions, which gives
2^{\alpha+1}
solutions in total.

If there are no type A solutions and no proper solutions, then there are
2^{\min\left(\alpha,\left\lfloor\frac{k+\beta}{2}\right\rfloor\right)}
solutions in total. If
k>1
, this number is one of
2^{1},2^{2},\ldots,2^{k}
.

If
k=1
, this number is one of
2^{0},2^{1}
. However, if
k=1
, then
\beta=1
implies that there is a proper solution, so
2^{0}
is impossible in that case.

Finally, if there are no type A solutions and there is a proper type B solutions, then there are
2^{\alpha+1}
solutions in total, unless
\alpha=k
in which case there are
2^{k}+2^{k-1}
solutions in total.

Now let us comment why each number can be achieved. For
k=1
, this is well known. Let
k>1
. We wish to prove that each of
0,2^{1},2^{2},2^{3},\ldots,2^{k},2^{k}+2^{k-1}
can be achieved. The numbers
0
and
2^{k}+2^{k-1}
are obviously achieved by taking
R
to be
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/2^{k}\mathbb{Z})
and the trivial group respectively.

Taking
R
to be the entire
B_{0}(2^{k})
, the type A system is
(c-a)x=b
for all odd
a,c
and for all
b
, which has no solution. The type B system is
(c-a)y=by^{2}
for all odd
a,c
and for all
b
. In particular, for
b=0
, one has
(c-a)y=0
, which has
2
solutions:
0
and
2^{k-1}
. Both of these numbers are solutions of the entire system, so
2^{1}
can be achieved.

Now let
1\leq j<k
, and take
R
to be generated by
\begin{bmatrix}1&2^{j}\\
0&1+2^{j}\end{bmatrix}
. The type A equation is
2^{j}x=2^{j}
, which has
2^{j}
solutions, and the type B equation is
2^{j}y=2^{j}y^{2}
, which also has
2^{j}
solutions (remember,
y
has to be divisible by
2
to be a solution of type B). In total, we obtain
2^{j+1}
solutions, as claimed.

\Box

As a consequence, it is now easyto determine the possible number of
n
-isogenies of an elliptic curve for any
n
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
n=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}^{e_{i}}
be a positive integer. Then
S(B_{0}(n))
is equal to the set of all products of the form

x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{r},\ \text{ where }x_{i}\in S(B_{0}(p_{i}^{e_{i}})).

Proof.

This follows immediately from the fact that the number of
n
-isogenies is the product of the number of
p_{i}^{e_{i}}
-isogenies for
i=1,\ldots,r
and the fact that for given subgroups
H_{i}\leq\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p_{i}^{e_{i}}\mathbb{Z})
, there is a subgroup
H\leq\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
such that the reduction of
H
modulo
p_{i}^{e_{i}}
equals
H_{i}
. The second fact follows from the Chinese remainder theorem.

\Box

Now suppose that we are given an elliptic curve
E
with a fixed number of
p^{k}
-isogenies. It is natural to ask how many
p^{k+1}
-isogenies
E
can have. If we go through the proof of
Proposition

3.1
more carefully, we can recover this information as well.

For
m\in S(B_{0}(p^{k}))
, denote by
S_{k\to k+1}(m)
the set of possible values for the number of
p^{k+1}
-isogenies of an elliptic curve whose number of
p^{k}
-isogenies is
m
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
k
a positive integer. Then

•

S_{k\to k+1}(p^{k}+p^{k-1})=\{0,p^{k},2p^{k},p^{k+1}+p^{k}\}
,
S_{k\to k+1}(0)=\{0\}
.

•

For
0\leq j<k
,
S_{k\to k+1}(2p^{j})=\{0,p^{j},2p^{j}\}
.

•

For
0\leq j<\frac{k-1}{2}
,
S_{k\to k+1}(p^{j})=\{0,p^{j}\}
.

•

For
j=\frac{k-1}{2}
,
S_{k\to k+1}(p^{j})=\{0,p^{j},p^{j+1}\}
.

•

For
\frac{k}{2}\leq j<k
,
S_{k\to k+1}(p^{j})=\{0,p^{j},p^{j+1},2p^{j}\}
.

Proof.

Let us fix some notation. For a subgroup
R
of
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k+1}\mathbb{Z})
, denote by
R^{\prime}
the image of
R
under the projection
\pi:\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k+1}\mathbb{Z})\to\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
.

Note that the number of
p^{k+1}
-isogenies can always be
0
, if we take
R=\pi^{-1}(R^{\prime})
, so that
R
contains all matrices congruent to the identity modulo
p^{k}
. Now suppose that the number of
p^{k+1}
-isogenies is at least
1
, and let
R\leq B_{0}(p^{k+1})
. Again, let
\alpha=\min_{R}(v_{p}(c-a))
and
\beta=\min_{R}(v_{p}(b)).

If the number of
p^{k}
-isogenies is
p^{k}+p^{k-1}
, then by the proof of
Proposition

3.1
, it follows that
\alpha
and
\beta
are at least
k
and every matrix in
R^{\prime}
is scalar. If
\alpha=k+1
, and
\beta=k
, there are only improper solutions of type B equations, and hence
p^{k}
solutions in total. If
\alpha=\beta=k+1
, there are
p^{k+1}+p^{k}
solutions. If
\alpha=k
and
\beta=k+1
, there are
p^{k}
solutions of type A and hence
2p^{k}
solutions in total. If
\alpha=\beta=k
, there are either
p^{k}
solutions of type A and
p^{k}
solutions of type B, or there are
p^{k}
improper solutions of type B.

To prove that each possibility arises, take
R
to be trivial to obtain
p^{k+1}+p^{k}
, generated by
\begin{bmatrix}1&p^{k}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
to obtain
p^{k}
, generated by
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1+p^{k}\end{bmatrix}
to obtain
2p^{k}
.

Suppose the number of
p^{k}
-isogenies is
2p^{j}
for
0\leq j<k
. Then there are either solutions of type A or proper solutions of type B. In any case, one has
\alpha=j
,
\beta\leq\alpha
and it follows from the proof of
Proposition

3.1
that the number of
p^{k+1}
-isogenies is either
p^{j}
or
2p^{j}
. The number
2p^{j}
is achieved by taking
R
generated by
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1+p^{j}\end{bmatrix}
. The number
p^{j}
is achieved by taking
R
generated by
\begin{bmatrix}1&p^{k}\\
0&1+p^{j}\end{bmatrix}
and
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1+p^{j}\end{bmatrix}
.

Finally, suppose that the number of
p^{k}
-isogenies is
p^{j}
for
j<k
. Then there are no type A solutions mod
p^{k}
, so there are also no type A solutions mod
p^{k+1}
. Furthermore, all solutions mod
p^{k}
satisfy
(c-a)y=by^{2}=0\pmod{p^{k}}
.

Suppose that all solutions mod
p^{k+1}
are improper. Then, there are
p^{\min(\alpha,\lfloor(k+1+\beta)/2\rfloor)}
solutions. If
j\geq\frac{k-1}{2}
, taking
\alpha=k+1
, and
\beta=2j+1-k
so that
\lfloor(k+\beta)/2\rfloor=j
and
\lfloor(k+\beta+1)/2\rfloor=j+1
gives
p^{j+1}
solutions. This can be achieved if
R
is generated by
\begin{bmatrix}1&p^{2j+1-k}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}.

If
\alpha\leq\frac{k+\beta}{2}
, then
j=\alpha
, and we get
p^{j}
solutions. This can be achieved if
R
is generated by
\begin{bmatrix}1&p^{k-1}\\
0&p^{j}+1\end{bmatrix}
and
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&p^{j}+1\end{bmatrix}
.

Now suppose that there exist proper solutions mod
p^{k+1}
. Then
\alpha<\frac{\beta+k+1}{2}
, and hence
\alpha=j
. Any proper solution satisfies
v_{p}(y)=\alpha-\beta
. Since there are no proper solutions mod
p^{k}
, we must have
(c-a)y=by^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p^{k}}
for any solution, and taking
v_{p}
yields
2\alpha-\beta=k
, so
j=\alpha=\frac{k+\beta}{2}\geq\frac{k}{2}
. This can be achieved by taking
R
generated by
\begin{bmatrix}1&p^{2j-k}\\
0&1+p^{j}\end{bmatrix}
. In this case, we get
2p^{j}
solutions.

\Box

Remark 3.7
.

Note that one can also think about the problem of
p^{k}
-isogenies geometrically. There is a bijection between cyclic subgroups of
(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{2}
of order
p^{k}
and points on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
. The group
\mathbb{P}\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
acts on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
by

\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}(x:y)=(ax+by:cx+dy).

For
k=1
, these are projective linear transformations on the projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
, and any such transformation that fixes three points fixes all points, which is the reason for there only being
1,2
or
p+1
possible
p
-isogenies. As we saw in our results, this is not true for
k>1
. However, there is a sense in which this generalizes, as shown by the next proposition.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
p
be an odd prime. Suppose that a matrix
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{P}\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
fixes three points on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
whose reductions modulo
p
on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
are distinct. Then
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}
fixes all points on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

Note that the reduction mod
p
map
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is well defined: any point
(x:y)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
can be written either as
(x^{\prime}:1)
or
(1:y^{\prime})
. In that case, the reduction mod
p
is defined as
(x^{\prime}\pmod{p}:1)
or
(1:y^{\prime}\pmod{p})
.

Since
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}
fixes at least one point, we can conjugate it to a matrix of the form
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{bmatrix}
, so that it fixes the point
(1:0)
. Then, by the condition, it also fixes two points of the form
(x:1)
and
(y:1)
with
x\not\equiv y\pmod{p}
. We then have
(ax+b:c)=(x:1)
and
(ay+b:c)=(y:1)
, so
(c-a)y=(c-a)x=b
, from where it follows that
a=c
and
b=0
, and hence the matrix is scalar, so it fixes all points.

\Box

Rephrasing in terms of isogenies, we obtain the following.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
k
a positive integer. Let
E
be an elliptic curve over a number field
K
, and suppose that
E
has three cyclic
p^{k}
-isogenies over
K
, with kernels
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
. If
C_{i}\cap C_{j}
is trivial for

, then
E
has all
p^{k}+p^{k-1}
isogenies defined over

.

Proof.

Consider the correspondence between cyclic subgroups of
E[p^{k}]
of order
p^{k}
and
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
. Let
P_{i}
be the point on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
corresponding to
C_{i}
. The condition
C_{i}\cap C_{j}=\{\mathcal{O}\}
is equivalent to
P_{i}
and
P_{j}
being distinct modulo

. The claim now follows from
Proposition

3.8
.

\Box

4.
Possible number of points above a
j
-invariant on some classes of modular curves

Having determined the possible values for the number of
p^{k}
-isogenies of an elliptic curve over a number field, we aim to do analogous work for the number of points on some other families of modular curves. We will discuss modular curves corresponding to Cartan subgroups and their normalisers.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
k
a positive integer.

The
split Cartan subgroup
of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
is the subgroup of all diagonal matrices,

C_{\mathrm{s}}(p^{k})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\[4.0pt]
0&b\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\right\}.

Let
\epsilon\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
be a non-square element. The
nonsplit Cartan subgroup
of
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
is

C_{\mathrm{ns}}(p^{k})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&\epsilon b\\
b&a\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z},\ a^{2}-\epsilon b^{2}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\right\}.

Remark 4.2
.

Any conjugate of the split/nonsplit Cartan subgroups defined above is also referred to as split/nonsplit Cartan. The different choices of
\epsilon
in the definition of
C_{\mathrm{ns}}
yield conjugate groups. Since our results do not depend on conjugation, we will simply work with the above realisations of Cartan subgroups.

The description of normalisers of the Cartan subgroups is well-known.

Lemma 4.3
.

a

(a)

The normaliser
N_{\mathrm{s}}(p^{k})
of
C_{\mathrm{s}}(p^{k})
in
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
is generated by
\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\

and

. The group

has index

inside

.

(b)

The normaliser

of

inside

.

Proof.

Part (a) follows from direct calculation. For part (b), see

.

\Box

We now introduce some notation to simplify the statements of the results that follow.

For a group
H\leq\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
, let
S(H)
denote the set of possible values that

\frac{\#\{g\in\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})\mid gRg^{-1}\subseteq H\}}{\#H}

obtains as
R
ranges over all subgroups of
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
k
a positive integer. Then

S(C_{\mathrm{s}}(p^{k}))=\{0,p^{2k}+p^{2k-1}\}\cup\{2p^{2j}\mid j\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}\}.

Proof.

Let
H=C_{\mathrm{s}}(p^{k})
. Let
R
be a subgroup of
H
. Let
g=\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
w&z\end{bmatrix}
. Then
gRg^{-1}\subseteq H
if and only if

\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
w&z\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}z&-v\\
-w&u\end{bmatrix}

is a diagonal matrix for every
\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}\in R
. Calculating the top right and bottom left entry in the product and equating them with
0
gives us the following two equations:

\displaystyle uv(a-b)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle wz(a-b)

\displaystyle=0.

Let
\alpha
denote
\min_{g\in R}v_{p}(a-b)
. If
\alpha=k
,
H
consists only of scalar matrices and is hence normal in
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
, so
gRg^{-1}
is always contained in
R
. Suppose
\alpha<k
. Then the equations imply that
gRg^{-1}
is contained in
H
if and only if
uv
and
wz
are divisible by
p^{k-\alpha}
. Since
(u,v)
is a row of an invertible matrix, at most one among
u
and
v
is divisible by
p
, and analogously for
(w,z)
.

If
u
is divisible by
p^{k-\alpha}
, then
w
and
v
are invertible modulo
p
and
z
is divisible by
p^{k-\alpha}
. There are
p^{\alpha}
choices for
u
,
p^{\alpha}
choices for
z
,
\varphi(p^{k})
choices for
v
and
\varphi(p^{k})
choices for
w
. We get the same number in total if
v
is divisible by
p^{k-\alpha}
.

In total, the number of elements
g
such that
gRg^{-1}\subseteq H
is
2p^{2\alpha}\cdot\varphi(p^{k})^{2}
. Dividing by the size of
H
, which is
\varphi(p^{k})^{2}
, we obtain
2p^{2\alpha}
.

To complete the proof, for each
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
we must construct a group
R
such that
\min_{g\in R}v_{p}(a-b)=j
. To achieve this, simply take
R
to be a cyclic group generated by
\begin{bmatrix}p^{j}+1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
.

\Box

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
k
a positive integer. Let

\displaystyle A_{k}(p)

\displaystyle=\{0\}\cup\{p^{2j}\mid 0\leq j<k\}\cup\left\{\frac{p^{2k}+p^{2k-1}}{2}\right\},

\displaystyle B_{k}(p)

\displaystyle=\{p^{j}\mid 0\leq j<k\},

\displaystyle C_{k}(p)

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{p^{k}+p^{k-1}}{2},\ \frac{p^{k}-p^{k-1}}{2}+1\right\},

\displaystyle D_{k}(p)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\{2\},&\text{if }p\equiv 3\pmod{4},\\[4.0pt]
\{3\},&\text{if }p\equiv 1\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

Then

S(N_{\mathrm{s}}(p^{k}))=A_{k}(p)\cup B_{k}(p)\cup C_{k}(p)\cup D_{k}(p).

Proof.

Let
H=N_{\mathrm{s}}(p^{k})
. Note that any matrix in
H
is either of the form
\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}0&c\\
d&0\end{bmatrix}
. Let
R
be a subgroup of
H
.
Let
g=\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
w&z\end{bmatrix}
. We now explicitly calculate the matrices
g\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}g^{-1}
and
g\begin{bmatrix}0&c\\
d&0\end{bmatrix}g^{-1}
.

We have

g\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\[4.0pt]
0&b\end{pmatrix}g^{-1}=\frac{1}{uz-vw}\begin{pmatrix}uza-vwb&uv(b-a)\\[4.0pt]
wz(a-b)&uzb-vwa\end{pmatrix},

and similarly

g\begin{pmatrix}0&c\\[4.0pt]
d&0\end{pmatrix}g^{-1}=\frac{1}{uz-vw}\begin{pmatrix}uzd-vwc&u^{2}c-v^{2}d\\[4.0pt]
z^{2}d-w^{2}c&vwc-uzd\end{pmatrix}.

Now consider the following four systems of equations:

System A1:

\begin{cases}auz=bvw,\\[4.0pt]
buz=avw.\end{cases}

System A2:

\begin{cases}wz(b-a)=0,\\[4.0pt]
uv(b-a)=0.\end{cases}

System B1:

\begin{cases}vzd=uwc.\end{cases}

System B2:

\begin{cases}u^{2}c=v^{2}d,\\[4.0pt]
w^{2}c=z^{2}d.\end{cases}

A matrix
g=\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
w&z\end{bmatrix}\in\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
satisfies
gRg^{-1}\subseteq H
if and only if for each
\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}\in R
,
g
is a solution of system A1 or system A2, and for each
\begin{bmatrix}0&c\\
d&0\end{bmatrix}\in R
,
g
is a solution of system B1 or system B2. We will call any such matrix
g
a solution. We say a solution is trivial if
g\in H
. Note that
g
cannot simultaneously be a solution of systems A1 and A2, or B1 and B2. However, for some
\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}
it may be a solution of A1, while for some other
\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}
it is a solution of A2.

Case I.
There is a solution
g
which is a solution of some system A1. Subtracting the two equalities, we obtain
(a+b)(uz-vw)=0
, so
a+b=0
since
uz-vw=\det(g)
is invertible. It follows that
b=-a
. Furthermore, we obtain
uz+vw=0
, from where it follows that
u,z,v,w
are all invertible. Thus, if
g
is a solution of system A2 for some other
\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}
, it follows that
a=b
.

In conclusion, if there is a solution which is a solution of system A1, then
a/b\in\{-1,1\}
for all
\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}\in R
, and
-1
is achieved at least once. Also, any solution for which some of
u,v,w,z
is not invertible has to be an element of
H
, i.e. a trivial solution.

Case I.a
There are no matrices of the form
\begin{bmatrix}0&c\\
d&0\end{bmatrix}
in
R
. We need to count the number of solutions to
uz+vw=0
among invertible matrices
\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
w&z\end{bmatrix}.
We can pick
u
,
z
and
w
to be any invertible elements of
\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}
, and
v
is then uniquely determined. This gives us
\varphi(p^{k})^{3}
solutions in total. The solutions which are not solutions of system A1 are always solutions of system A2, which turns into
wz=uv=0
for
b=-a
. The solutions to this system are the elements of
H
, so we get
2\varphi(p^{k})^{2}
solutions.

Dividing by
\#H
gives us the number
\frac{1}{2}\varphi(p^{k})+1=\frac{p^{k}-p^{k-1}}{2}+1
.

Case I.b
There are matrices of the form
\begin{bmatrix}0&c\\
d&0\end{bmatrix}
in
R
. If
\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
z&w\end{bmatrix}
is a nontrivial solution, then
uz+vw=0
, so
uzd=vwc
holds only if

Note that the systems B1 and B2 only depend on the value of
\frac{d}{c}
. Furthermore, note that if
t
is achieved as
\frac{d}{c}
for some

d&0\end{bmatrix}\in R

-t
is achieved for
\begin{bmatrix}0&c\\
d&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&-a\end{bmatrix}

is closed under multiplication by
-1
. We now claim that there are exactly two values of
\frac{d}{c}
which are achieved. Namely, since
g
is a solution with
u,v,w,z
invertible, one has
\frac{d}{c}\in\{u^{2}/v^{2},uw/vz\}
, depending on whether
g
is a solution of B1 or B2. Furthermore, if
g
is a solution of system B1 for some
\begin{bmatrix}0&c\\
d&0\end{bmatrix}

\begin{bmatrix}0&c\\
d&0\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&-a\end{bmatrix}
. Let

be the set of those values. Note that at least one among

-t
is a square (this follows from system B2 having a solution). Without loss of generality, we may assume that

is a square.

Case I.b.(i)

-1
is not a square.
Let
\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
w&z\end{bmatrix}
be any nontrivial solution. We have

\begin{cases}u^{2}=tv^{2},\\[4.0pt]
w^{2}=tz^{2},\\[4.0pt]
-vzt=uw,\\[4.0pt]

We have

and

. To satisfy the third equation, the sign choices need to be distinct. In this way, the fourth equation is automaticaly true. Thus, we have

choices for
v
and
z
, and two choices for a combination of

and
w
with distinct signs. In total, there are
2\varphi(p^{k})^{2}
nontrivial solutions. Dividing by
\#H
and adding the trivial solution, we obtain the number

.

-1
is a square. In this case, there is an extra choice, as

.

Case II.a.

. In this case, any matrix

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
k
a positive integer. Then

S(C_{\mathrm{ns}}(p^{k}))=\{0\}\cup\{2p^{2j}\mid 0\leq j<k\}\cup\{p^{2k}-p^{2k-1}\}.

Proof.

Let
H=C_{\mathrm{ns}}(p^{k})
. We start by calculating a suitable set of coset representatives for
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})/H
.

Lemma 4.7
.

The matrices of the form
\begin{bmatrix}1&v\\
0&z\end{bmatrix}
for
v\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}
and
u\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
form a full list of coset representatives of
C_{\mathrm{ns}}(p^{k})
inside
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

The number of listed matrices coincides with the index
[\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}):C_{\mathrm{ns}}(p^{k})]
, so it suffices to prove that each matrix can be written in the form
h\cdot r
, where
r
is a matrix from the list and
h\in C_{\mathrm{ns}}(p^{k})
.

Start with any matrix
\begin{bmatrix}u&v\\
w&z\end{bmatrix}\in\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})
. If
u\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, then
w
is invertible, so if we multiply from the left by
\begin{bmatrix}0&\epsilon\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
, we get a matrix with top left entry
\epsilon w
, which is invertible. Thus, we may assume
u
is invertible.

Furthermore, take
a
and
b
such that
a
is invertible and
bu+aw=0
. Then multiplying on the left by
\begin{bmatrix}a&\epsilon b\\
b&a\end{bmatrix}
gives us a matrix whose bottom left entry equals
0
. Finally, multiplying by a suitable scalar matrix yields a matrix whose top left entry is
1
and bottom left entry is
0
, which proves the claim.

\Box

Thus, for a subgroup
R
of
H

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
p>11
be a prime, and let
f(x)
and
g(x)
be irreducible quadratic polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
with no common factors. Then there is some
A\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
such that
f(A)
and
g(A)
are both squares in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
.

Proof.

Write
\chi
for the quadratic character modulo
p
.

For signs
s,t\in\{+1,-1\}
let

N_{s,t}=\#\{A\in\mathbb{F}_{p}:\ \chi(f(A))=s,\ \chi(g(A))=t\}.

Using the standard identity
\mathbf{1}_{\chi(f(x))=s}=(1+s\chi(f(x)))/2
(and similarly for
\chi_{g}
) we obtain

N_{s,t}=\sum_{A\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\frac{1+s\chi(f(A))}{2}\cdot\frac{1+t\chi(g(A))}{2}=\frac{1}{4}\Big(p+s\Sigma_{f}+t\Sigma_{g}+st\Sigma_{fg}\Big),

where

\Sigma_{f}=\sum_{A\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\chi(f(A)),\qquad\Sigma_{g}=\sum_{A\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\chi(g(A)),\qquad\Sigma_{fg}=\sum_{A\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\chi(f(A)g(A)).

For an irreducible quadratic polynomial
q(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c
one has
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\chi(q(x))=-\chi(a).
In particular,
|\Sigma_{f}|=|\Sigma_{g}|=1
.
The product
f(x)g(x)
is a quartic polynomial with no repeated roots, so the Weil bound gives us

|\Sigma_{fg}|\leq 3\sqrt{p}.

Combining these estimates we obtain, for every sign pair
(s,t)
,

N_{s,t}\geq\frac{1}{4}\Big(p-\big(|\Sigma_{f}|+|\Sigma_{g}|+|\Sigma_{fg}|\big)\Big)\geq\frac{1}{4}\big(p-2-3\sqrt{p}\big).

The right-hand side is positive whenever
p-2-3\sqrt{p}>0
, which holds for every prime
p\geq 13
. Hence for
p\geq 13
each
N_{s,t}
is positive. In particular, there exists
A
with
\chi_{f}(A)=\chi_{g}(A)=+1
, i.e. both
f(A)
and
g(A)
are nonzero squares.

\Box

Applying this for polynomials

and

In conclusion, adding the trivial solution, we obtain

5.
Number of rational CM points on a modular curve

The goal of this section is to provide an algorithm for the number of rational CM points on a given modular curve
X_{H}
. We start by recalling known results about images of Galois representations of elliptic curves with complex multiplication, due to Lozano-Robledo
[
10
]
. In what follows,
K
is an imaginary quadratic field,
\mathcal{O}_{K,f}
is the order of
K
of conductor
f
, and
j_{K,f}
is any value of
j
-invariant of an elliptic curve with CM by
\mathcal{O}_{K,f}
. Note that for rational
j
-invariants,
j_{K,f}
is uniquely determined.

Theorem 5.1
.

[
10
, Theorem 1.1]

Let
E/\mathbb{Q}(j_{K,f})
be an elliptic curve with CM by
\mathcal{O}_{K,f}
, let
N\geq 3
, and let

\rho_{E,N}:\Gal(\mathbb{Q}(j_{K,f})/\mathbb{Q}(j_{K,f}))\to\Aut(E[N])\cong\textnormal{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}).

We define groups of
\textnormal{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
as follows:

•

If
\Delta_{K}f^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, let
\delta=\Delta_{K}f^{2}/4
and
\phi=0
.

•

If
\Delta_{K}f^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, let
\delta=(\Delta_{K}-1)f^{2}/4
and
\phi=f
.

Define the Cartan subgroup
\mathcal{C}_{\delta,\phi}(N)\subset\textnormal{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
by

\mathcal{C}_{\delta,\phi}(N)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a+b\phi&b\\[4.0pt]
\delta b&a\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z},\ a^{2}+ab\phi-\delta b^{2}\in(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\right\}.

Let

\mathcal{N}_{\delta,\phi}(N)=\langle\mathcal{C}_{\delta,\phi}(N),\ c_{\phi}\rangle,\qquad c_{\phi}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\[4.0pt]
\phi&1\end{pmatrix}.

Then there is a
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
E[N]
such that the image of
\rho_{E,N}
is contained in
\mathcal{N}_{\delta,\phi}(N)
. Moreover,

(1)

\mathcal{C}_{\delta,\phi}(N)\cong(\mathcal{O}_{K,f}/N\mathcal{O}_{K,f})^{\times}
is a subgroup of index
2
in
\mathcal{N}_{\delta,\phi}(N)
, and

(2)

the index of the image of
\rho_{E,N}
in
\mathcal{N}_{\delta,\phi}(N)
coincides with the order of the Galois group

\Gal(K(j_{K,f},E[N])/K(j_{K,f},h(E[N])))
for a Weber function
h
, and it divides the order of

\mathcal{O}_{K,f}^{\times}/\mathcal{O}_{K,f,N}^{\times}
, where

\mathcal{O}_{K,f,N}^{\times}=\{u\in\mathcal{O}_{K,f}^{\times}:u\equiv 1\pmod{N\mathcal{O}_{K,f}}\}
.

Remark 5.2
.

Note that the choices of the parameters
\delta
and
\phi
(and consequently, the group
\mathcal{C}_{\delta,\phi}
) in our statement of the theorem differs from the statement in the original paper by Lozano-Robledo. However, those are conjugate subgroups of
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
, so the claim still holds. In fact, our choice of parameters was used in the theorem that follows.

Theorem 5.3
.

[
10
, Theorem 1.2.(1)]

Using notation from
Theorem

5.1
, for every
K
and
f\geq 1
, and a fixed
N\geq 3
, there is an elliptic curve
E/\mathbb{Q}(j_{K,f})
such that the image of
\rho_{E,N}
is precisely
\mathcal{N}_{\delta,\phi}(N)
.

These two theorems tell us what the Galois image looks like in some basis. In order to apply our results from Section 2, we must also know what the automorphisms of
E
look like in this basis. From the proof of
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
, it follows that the basis in which the image of the Galois representation is of the above format is the one corresponding to
(f\tau,1)
, where
\tau=\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{K}}}{2}
or
\tau=\frac{1+\sqrt{\Delta_{K}}}{2}
, depending on
\Delta_{K}\pmod{4}
. One has
\mathcal{O}_{K,f}=\mathbb{Z}+f\mathbb{Z}[\tau]
. It is now easy to deduce the action of automorphisms. Let
A
denote the image of the action of
\Aut(E)
in this basis.

•

j=1728
. There are
4
automorphisms, corresponding to multiplication by powers of
i
. One has
f=1
and
\tau=i
. Moreover, the action of multiplication by
i
in the basis
(i,1)
is given by the matrix
\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}
, so
A=\langle\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}\rangle
.

•

j=0
. There are
6
automorphisms, corresponding to multiplication by powers of
\frac{-1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}
. One has
f=1
and
\tau=\frac{1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}
. Moreover, the action of multiplication by
\tau
in the basis
(\tau,1)
is given by the matrix
\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}
.

•

j\not\in\{0,1728\}
. One simply has
A=\pm 1
.

Fix a rational CM
j
-invariant. One can now easily deduce an algorithm for the number of rational points
x
on a modular curve
X_{H}
such that
j(x)=j_{0}
.

Suppose that the input is the
j
-invariant with the corresponding quadratic order which has minimal discriminant
\Delta_{K}
and conductor
f
, the level
N
and a group
H\leq\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
which contains
-I
(if it does not contain it, one can simply consider
\langle H,-I\rangle
instead).

•

Calculate the conductor
f
and the discriminant
\Delta_{K}
of the corresponding quadratic order
\mathcal{O}_{K,f}.

•

If
\Delta_{K}f^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, set
\delta=\Delta_{K}f^{2}/4
and
\phi=0
. Else, set
\delta=\frac{\Delta_{K}-1}{4}f^{2}
and
\phi=f
.

•

Let

\mathcal{C}_{\delta,\phi}(N)=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}a+b\phi&b\\
\delta b&a\end{bmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z},\ a^{2}+ab\phi-\delta b^{2}\in(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\right\}

and

\mathcal{N}_{\delta,\phi}(N)=\left\langle\mathcal{C}_{\delta,\phi},\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\
\phi&1\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle.

•

Then there exists an elliptic curve with the given
j
-invariant such that the mod
N
Galois image
R
is equal to
\mathcal{N}_{\delta,\phi}(N)
in some basis, and the image of the action of automorphisms is
A
.

•

If
A
is
0
or
1728
, calculate the number of double cosets
HgA
with
gR\subseteq HgA
.

•

Otherwise, calculate the number of cosets
Hg
with
gRg^{-1}\subseteq H
.

Remark 5.4
.

The described algorithm works for rational CM points on modular curves. It is often of interest to determine
K
-rational points on
X_{H}
above a fixed
j
-invariant
j_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}
for some larger number field
K
. In that case,
\rho_{E,N}(G_{K})
is a subgroup of
\mathcal{N}_{\delta,\phi}(N)
, and in general we do not know which subgroup it is. However, if
K
is exactly the quadratic CM field of
j_{0}
, then one can choose
E
with
j(E)=j_{0}
so that
\rho_{E,N}(G_{K})
equals
\mathcal{C}_{\delta,\phi}(N)
(this follows from the proof of
Theorem

5.1
) and one can apply the same algorithm, just replacing
\mathcal{N}_{\delta,\phi}(N)
with
\mathcal{C}_{\delta,\phi}(N)
. This is often useful, since quadratic CM points on modular curves are often defined exactly over CM fields of rational elliptic curves.

Example 5.5
.

In
[
12
, Example 4.2]
, the number of quadratic points on
X_{0}(88)
above
j
-invariants
-3375
and
16581375
is determined. The corresponding imaginary quadratic orders are
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1+\sqrt{-7}}{2}\right]
and
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-7}]
. In that article, the number of points was found using properties of isogenies of CM curves and the splitting behaviour of prime
2
and its powers inside the quadratic orders. In the special case of
X_{0}(n)
, the point count can be done using these tricks, but for general modular curves
X_{H}
this may not work.

Using our code, we quickly find that there are
8
points on
X_{0}(88)
above each of the mentioned
j
-invariants over their CM field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})
. Magma’s
time
function returns 0.440 and 0.660s of CPU time for each of the
j
-invariants. For comparison, the code used in
[
12
]
as a sanity check, based on methods developed in
[
1
]
, takes much longer. There, the method is to compute the
j
-map
X_{0}(88)\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
and then determine its preimages.
