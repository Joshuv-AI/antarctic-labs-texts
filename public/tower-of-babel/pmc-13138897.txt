Efficacy Comparison of Open Reduction and Robot‐Aided Infra‐Acetabular and Posterior Column Screws Fixation in Complex Acetabular Fractures Surgery: Union Plate Versus Reconstruction Plate
Authors: Jianan Chen, Yiran Feng, Runlin Zhu, Pengqing Zhang, Zhihua Zhao, Ziming Zhang, Xiaodong Guo, Lulu Tang, Kaifang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAn anatomical supra‐pectineal quadrilateral surface buttress plate (Union Plate) has been designed and shown promising mechanical strength in vivo. Robot‐aided technique has shown promising results in navigation of infra‐acetabular and posterior column screws placement. This study sought to further examine whether the emerging anatomical plate provided advantages over the reconstruction plate in robot‐aided complex acetabular fracture surgery.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis comparing two internal fixation methods was conducted between September 2023 and January 2025. Specifically, 23 patients with complex acetabular fractures received robot‐aided Union Plate fixation (Union Plate group), while another 20 patients underwent robot‐aided reconstruction plate fixation (Reconstruction plate group). Intra‐operative plate contouring time, robot‐aided manipulation time, the number of channel adjustments per periarticular long screws, total operation time, blood loss, periarticular long screw placement quality, fracture reduction quality, hip joint function, and postoperative complications were compared between the two groups.ResultsWith identical robotic assistance, the Union Plate group experienced significantly shorter times for robot‐aided manipulation (−12 min), intraoperative plate contouring (−9 min), and total operation (−23 min), along with reduced blood loss (−128 mL) and fewer channel adjustments of the periarticular long screws, compared to the reconstruction plate group. Both groups achieved satisfactory periarticular long screws placement, with a 95.7% perfect rate in the Union Plate group and 90.4% in the reconstruction plate group, showing no significant difference. Neither group experienced iatrogenic neurovascular injuries or screw penetration. There were also no significant differences in postoperative complications, fracture reduction quality, or hip joint function between the groups.ConclusionIn surgery for complex acetabular fractures, the Union Plate showed superior operability and safety compared to traditional reconstruction plates. It required less time for robot manipulation, intraoperative plate contouring, and total operation, while also resulting in reduced blood loss and fewer adjustments of the periarticular long screws.Keywords: acetabular fracture, infra‐acetabular, periarticular, posterior column, robot, screw

Background

Acetabular fractures are serious orthopedic injuries with a 20% fatality rate and 36.6% disability rate, particularly when both anterior and posterior columns are affected [1, 2]. The traditional combined anteroposterior surgical approach is risky, often resulting in complications such as excessive bleeding, infections, and prolonged surgery time [3, 4, 5]. The periarticular long screws technique enables simultaneous fixation of the anterior and posterior acetabular columns through a single anterior approach, and when used with a supra‐pectineal reconstruction plate, it has become the gold standard for mechanical fixation [6]. However, the non‐anatomical shape of the reconstruction plate and the risky application of the long screws impose high surgical demands in clinical practice [7], especially for periarticular long screws placement through the standard screw hole.

To simplify the challenging operation, we have designed the Union Plate based on a big data‐driven standardized acetabular model, an anatomically shaped supra‐pectineal quadrilateral surface buttress plate that has demonstrated promising biomechanical results [6, 8, 9, 10]. It anatomically matches the complex acetabular anatomy and features two elongated elliptical holes in the placement areas for the infra‐acetabular and posterior column screws, aiding in fracture reduction, fixation, and easier placement of periarticular long screws.

Robot‐aided technology enables precise, minimally invasive percutaneous screw placement in closed surgery for simple acetabular fractures [11, 12, 13], but its application in open surgery for complex acetabular fractures is rarely documented. We recently reported promising results from using robotic‐assisted periarticular long screws placement in complex acetabular fracture surgery, compared with the traditional freehand technique [14]. However, it remains unclear whether the Union Plate offers advantages over the reconstruction plate in robot‐aided complex acetabular fractures surgery. Thus, this study aimed to further compare the benefits of the Union Plate against the reconstruction plate under identical robotic assistance. Therefore, the aims of the study were as follows: (i) to introduce the design purpose of the Union Plate; (ii) to explore the advantages of robot‐aided Union Plate fixation for complex acetabular fractures; and (iii) to discuss critical operational aspects of robot‐aided Union Plate fixation.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study, conducted at the Orthopedics Department of Tongji Medical College of HUST, examined surgically treated patients with complex acetabular fractures from September 2023 to January 2025. The Institutional Research Ethics Committee approved the study, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants, adhering to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion criteria: (i) complex acetabular fractures affecting both anterior and posterior columns; (ii) treatment via a single supra‐ilioinguinal approach; and (iii) age ≥ 18 years; Exclusion criteria: (i) open or old fractures; (ii) severe preinjury hip arthritis; (iii) combined posterior acetabular wall fractures with dislocation needing an anterior–posterior approach; (iv) incomplete imaging data and follow‐up under 6 months.

According to the criteria, 43 patients were included in this study. Among them, 23 cases adopt robot‐aided Union Plate fixation (Union Plate group), while 20 cases employed robot‐aided reconstruction plate fixation (reconstruction plate group). Before surgery, all patients received pelvic X‐rays and 3D‐CT scans. Patients with central hip dislocation received closed reduction and supracondylar femoral traction under general anesthesia in the emergency room within 12 h of injury. Sterilized 3D‐printed mirror hemipelvis models were routinely prepared for intraoperative plate contouring.

Structure of the Union Plate

Based on a big data‐driven standardized acetabular model, the novel supra‐pectineal quadrilateral surface buttress plate (ZL201820491496.0, Union Plate, Dabo Medical Technology Co. LTD., China) is crafted from pure titanium (TA3) and features a main plate with an arcuate edge and a triangular buttress plate, connected by two rods as shown in Figure 1.

The Union Plate features a main plate with an arched edge and a triangular buttress plate. Screw holes 1–2 and 8–9 provide fixation for the anterior column, while holes 3 and 7 enable periarticular long screws fixation for the posterior column. The triangular buttress plate aids in reducing and fixing the quadrilateral surface and posterior column (Appendix 1: Union Plate fixation animation.mp4).

The main plate above the pectineal line features 9 holes. Holes 1–2, 4–6, and 8–9 are standard round holes for 3.5 mm screws. Hole 3 is an elliptical shape for a 3.5 mm infra‐acetabular screw, while hole 7 is an oblique elliptical shape for a 6.5 mm posterior column cannulated screw. The entry point of the posterior column screw could be adjusted during surgery to implant either a long posterior column screw toward the ischial tubercle or a short one toward the ischial spine. The triangular buttress plate offers reduction and fixation for the quadrilateral surface and posterior column, with three specialized screw holes (11–13) toward posterior‐inferiorly to ensure safe screw placement. Surgical Procedure

All the operations were carried out by the same orthopedic trauma team. All patients were under general anesthesia and positioned supine on a radiolucent table. The lumbosacral region was elevated to enhance pelvic anteversion, preventing posterior column screws placement from being obstructed by the abdomen. A 10 cm pad was placed under the ipsilateral popliteal fossa to flex the hip and knee, relaxing the iliopsoas muscle, femoral nerve, and iliac vessels to aid traction during the surgery.

Fractures were routinely exposed using the single supra‐ilioinguinal approach. Reduction began from the back to the front and from the periphery to the center, starting with the iliac bone and anterior column fractures, followed by the posterior column and quadrilateral surface fractures. The 3D‐printed mirror hemipelvis models guided the intra‐operative contouring of the Union Plate and reconstruction plate, ensuring optimal placement of the infra‐acetabular and posterior column screw holes.

After achieving satisfactory anterior column fracture reduction, 3.5 mm screws were used to fix the caudal and cranial screw holes of the supra‐pectineal plate, establishing the basic anatomical frame structure. Next, a push rod or pelvic reduction forcep was used to reduce the posterior column and quadrilateral surface fractures, maintaining the fracture with 2.5 mm Kirschner wires. Especially, when using the Union Plate for fixation, its anatomical buttress plate design was leveraged to aid in reduction and provide preliminary fixation. Targeted guide pin placement: The operator used a handheld tracker‐integrated sleeve toward the optical camera for recognition. The sleeve was then adjusted so its projection, shown in green from yellow on the screen, aligned with the center of the static screw channel, indicating correct positioning (Figure 3). The guide pin was drilled through the sleeve, and then confirmed whether it passed through the planned screw hole. If misaligned, the channel would be slightly adjusted through the plate or further replanned outside the plate. For each patient, the number of channel adjustments per periarticular long screws was derived by dividing total adjustments by the number of screws implanted per patient. Although the robot could effectively aid in finding guide pin direction, careful attention was still needed. For example, when accessing the infra‐acetabular screw channel, it was important to touch the posterior margin of the obturator ring to monitor for vibrations and heat, confirming the guide pin in the correct osseous channel. Postoperative Treatment

Antibiotics were given for three days post‐operation to prevent infection, and Rivaroxaban (10 mg/d) was administered daily. The drainage tube was removed after 48 h once the volume was under 50 mL/day. Elastic stockings were used to prevent deep vein thrombosis. The affected limb was non‐weight‐bearing for four weeks, then allowed partial weight‐bearing with crutches, progressing to a single crutch at eight weeks, and full weight‐bearing at 12 weeks. X‐rays and 3D‐CT scans were conducted within five days post‐operation. Regular postoperative outpatient follow‐ups and imaging exams were conducted to assess fracture healing and hip joint function.

Outcome Assessment

Surgical outcomes: Intra‐operative plate contouring time, robot‐aided manipulation time, total operation time, and blood loss of both groups were recorded. Periarticular long screws placement quality was assessed using the Lonstein standard [15], where grade 0 indicates complete screw placement within the osseous corridors, grade I indicates ≤ 2 mm protrusion, grade II indicates 2–4 mm protrusion, and grade III indicates ≥ 4 mm protrusion from the cortical bone.

Clinical outcomes: Fracture reduction quality was assessed using the Matta criteria [16] within a week post‐surgery. Fracture healing time and postoperative complications were recorded during outpatient follow‐up, and hip joint function was evaluated with the modified Merle d'Aubigné‐Postel score system [17] at the final follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 19.0 software (IBM, USA). Continuous data were compared using independent sample t‐tests, while categorical data were analyzed with the chi‐square test or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test as appropriate. All tests were two‐sided with a significance level of 0.05.

In the Union Plate group, 46 long screws were all placed through the plate, including 23 infra‐acetabular 3.5 mm screws and 23 posterior column 6.5 mm cannulated screws; of these, 44 were grade 0 and two were grade I, resulting in a 95.7% (44/46) perfect placement rate. Neither group experienced iatrogenic injuries like screw penetration, or damage to the sciatic nerve, obturator nerve, or iliac vessels.

Fracture reduction quality was assessed within a week post‐surgery using Matta criteria. The Union Plate group had 16 excellent, four good, and three poor outcomes, while the reconstruction plate group had 14 excellent, three good, and three poor outcomes, with no significant difference (p = 0.988). The excellent‐good fracture reduction rate in the Union Plate group was 87% and 85% of the reconstruction plate group. Hip joint function was evaluated using the modified Merle d'Aubigne‐Postel score at final follow‐up. The Union Plate group reported 14 excellent, 6 good, and 3 fair cases, while the reconstruction plate group had 11 excellent, six good, and three fair cases, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.710).

In the Union Plate group, there was one case each of wound fat liquefaction and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) injury. The reconstruction plate group experienced one case of incision fat liquefaction, two LFCN injuries, and one case of heterotopic ossification. All incisions healed with regular dressing changes. Nutritional nerve drugs were applied in cases of nerve injuries, while heterotopic ossification was managed with NSAIDs and physical therapy, with regular outpatient follow‐up. There was no significant difference in postoperative complication rates between the groups (8.7% vs. 20.0%, p = 0.393).

Discussion

Main Findings of the Study

In surgery for complex acetabular fractures using open reduction and robot‐assisted fixation with periarticular long screws, the Union Plate demonstrated better operability and safety than the traditional reconstruction plate. It required less time for robot manipulation, intraoperative plate contouring, and total operation, while also resulting in less blood loss and fewer channel adjustments of the periarticular long screws.

The Design Purpose of Union Plate

The combination of a supra‐pectineal reconstruction plate with periarticular long screws could provide rigid fixation for complex acetabular fractures, facilitating early postoperative rehabilitation [6, 8]. However, its clinical application presents certain challenges and issues that need to be addressed. First, the reconstruction plate requires repeated contouring, which is time‐consuming and might weaken the fixation strength [7]. Moreover, the lack of an ideal anatomical match with the anterior column curve might hinder stabilization maintenance after reduction, potentially leading to reduction loss [18]. Additionally, the standard hole for periarticular long screw fixation is too small, making the location of the ideal entry point difficult.

Thus, we designed a supra‐pectineal quadrilateral surface buttress plate based on the acetabular fractures database. Its curved design aligns with the anterior column, reducing the need for recontouring during surgery. The specialized, elongated elliptical holes were ideally positioned for infra‐acetabular and posterior column screws. Additionally, the anatomical triangular buttress plate could aid in fracture reduction, particularly in elderly patients with quadrilateral surface displacement, and act as a barrier to prevent posterior column screw protrusion into the pelvic cavity.

Advantages of Robot‐Aided Union Plate Fixation

Traditional freehand fixation with periarticular long screws necessitates repeated fluoroscopy, increasing the risk of screw penetration and neurovascular injuries [5, 19, 20]. Robot‐aided complex acetabular fractures fixation using either Union Plate or reconstruction plate fixation yielded a satisfactory periarticular long screws quality, with a perfect rate of 92.9% (91/98) and no intraoperative complications. This slightly surpassed the 91.7% success rate (22/24) and 8.3% complication rate (2/24) reported in a cadaver study by Lehmann et al. [21], likely due to the lesser accuracy of electromagnetic navigation compared to optical navigation.

However, the Union Plate offered better operability and safety than the reconstruction plate, even with the same robotic support. First, attributed to the specialized design with elongated holes that facilitate easier channel plan of infra‐acetabular and posterior column screw placement through the plate, the Union Plate group had a significantly reduced manipulation time by 12 min compared to the reconstruction plate group. In the Union Plate group, all long screws were placed through the plate, whereas in the reconstruction plate group, only 76.9% (40/52) were. Specifically, the Union Plate group needed fewer channel adjustments per long screw compared to the reconstruction plate group, with 82.6% (19/23) requiring no adjustments, whereas in the reconstruction plate group, 20% (4/20) needed one adjustment, 40% (8/20) needed two, and another 40% (8/20) required three adjustments. Second, due to the anatomical configuration of the Union Plate, the Union Plate group exhibited a significantly shorter average intraoperative plate contouring time, with a reduction of 9 min compared to the reconstruction plate group. In general, robot‐aided Union Plate fixation offered similar reduction quality and hip function recovery as reconstruction plate fixation, but it reduced operation time and blood loss, making the robotic navigation of long screw placement more efficient.

Critical Operational Aspects of Robot‐Aided Union Plate Fixation

There are several caveats to this new method. First, the quality of periarticular long screws placement depends on clear fluoroscopic images and a visible bony corridor, making effective bowel preparation and fluoroscopic skills essential. Second, the optical camera and patient positioning are vital. The robot tracks the pelvis and acetabulum's spatial position using infrared emissions from the tracker. During surgery, it's essential to avoid occlusion of any tracker or camera. Specifically, when locating the posterior column screw entry point, the angle between the screw head and the coronal plane is about 20°. To prevent blockage of the handheld tracker‐integrated sleeve by the abdominal wall, we recommend elevating the patient's lumbosacral region to increase pelvic anteversion.

Limitations and Prospect

Based on our previous mechanical study [6], we found that a single 6.5 mm posterior column cannulated screw is comparable to two 3.5 mm screws. Consequently, to ensure the fixation strength, the reconstruction plate group inevitably used longer screws than the Union plate group, potentially impacting operation time and bleeding. Nevertheless, our statistics showed that the Union plate group required fewer channel adjustments per long screws during robotic navigation, which might help reduce both operation time and bleeding. Besides, this study was limited by its retrospective design, potential patient selection bias, single‐center focus, small sample size, and the need for further longitudinal follow‐up to evaluate long‐term efficacy.

Therefore, considering that robot‐aided Union Plate fixation for complex acetabular fractures is more minimally invasive and precise, it may represent a favorable option within the current field of laparoscopic minimally invasive treatment for acetabular fractures. Given that laparoscopic procedures demand higher safety and precision in fixation, future efforts will be directed toward this promising approach.

Conclusions

In summary, robot‐aided technology allowed for precise and minimally invasive placement of periarticular long screws in open surgery for complex acetabular fractures, while using the Union Plate could decrease operation difficulty (fewer channel adjustments, less time for robotic manipulation and intraoperative plate contouring), operation time, and bleeding. The method of robot‐aided Union Plate fixation might be expected to reduce the already challenging surgical difficulty of complex acetabular fractures.
