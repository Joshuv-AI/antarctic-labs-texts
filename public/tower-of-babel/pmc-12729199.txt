Folic acid ameliorates placental structure and function in fetal growth restriction via epigenetic modifications
Authors: Juanmei Gao, Yingya Lou, Weiwu He, Kaixing Xu, Xin Zhan, Jinyi Tong, Hongbo Zhai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFetal growth restriction (FGR) is associated with perinatal complications and potential neurodevelopmental risks, yet mechanistic understanding of metabolic dysregulation remains incomplete. We investigated amniotic fluid metabolites as potential functional biomarkers of fetal-placental dysfunction and explored the possibility of folic acid-mediated epigenetic remediation of metabolic imbalances in FGR.ResultsIn FGR amniotic fluid, hypoxanthine was significantly upregulated, valproic acid (VPA) was significantly downregulated, and arginine/proline metabolism pathways were markedly enriched. Folic acid intervention significantly improved fetal growth parameters (crown–rump length, body weight, and placental weight; all P < 0.001). Compensatory labyrinth zone (Lb) hyperplasia was observed in FGR placenta, with a 40% increase in Lb/Jz ratio, while folic acid supplementation reduced Lb proportion by 10% and appeared to restore placental architecture. Epigenetically, folic acid supplementation suppressed histone deacetylases1 (HDAC1) expression, elevated H3K9ac levels and enhanced trophoblast proliferation.ConclusionsOur research suggests that dysregulation of the VPA-hypoxanthine axis may serve as a hallmark of FGR-associated metabolic stress. Folic acid may exerts potential dual therapeutic effects by correcting placental architecture through Lb normalization and initiating epigenetic reprogramming via HDAC1 inhibition and acetylation at lysine 9 of histone H3 (H3K9ac). These findings indicate that folate metabolism could play a regulatory role in placental gene transcription and fetal growth trajectories.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-01985-y.

Introduction

Fetal growth restriction (FGR) is one of the primary causes of adverse perinatal outcomes, with a global incidence rate of approximately 5–10% [1]. Diagnostic criteria for FGR vary internationally, the Delphi consensus recommends ultrasonographic biometry and colour Doppler for diagnosis [2], while many countries define FGR as an estimated fetal weight (EFW) or abdominal circumference below the 10th percentile for the corresponding gestational week [3–5]. The placenta serves as the pivotal organ for fetal nutrition and metabolic regulation, with its dysfunction directly implicated in FGR. Epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs, critically regulate trophoblast dynamics and fetal adaptation to environmental stressors [6, 7]. Nevertheless, the epigenetic underpinnings of placental dysfunction remain poorly understood, hindering the development of effective clinical interventions.

Amniotic fluid, a dynamic medium reflecting placental-fetal metabolic interactions, has emerged as a valuable source of biomarkers for FGR. Metabolomic studies have identified significant dysregulation in pathways associated with folic acid (FA) metabolism in FGR amniotic fluid, highlighting its potential for early diagnosis and intervention [8, 9]. Despite these advances, current research is deficient in a systematic correlation analysis between placental and amniotic fluid metabolites. Although DNA methylation abnormalities [10, 11] and metabolomic changes have been documented in FGR placentas[12–15], global fortification programs have significantly lowered the incidence of neural tube defects [16, 17]. While higher periconceptional FA doses 4.0 mg/day show no additional benefit over lower doses 0.4 mg/day [18], excessive supplementation in animal models leads to metabolic disturbances. 2.5-folds folic acid leads to dyslipidemic-diabetic slightly in male offspring, while 5-folds folic acid aggravates the disorder and prominent hepatic lipid accumulations [19]. In addition, our previous work indicates that folate deficiency increases the risk of SGA without causing preeclampsia [20]. These findings highlight the need to explore the spatiotemporal regulation of FA metabolism in placental development and its implications for FGR.

The placental labyrinthine architecture, comprising the maternal decidual zone (De), the fetal junctional zone (Jz), and the labyrinth zone (Lb), undergoes precisely orchestrated developmental transitions [21–23]. Imaging studies show that anomalies in labyrinth zone vascularization correlate with metabolic disturbance [24], with epigenetic modifications playing a central role in placental function. Notably, valproic acid (VPA), a histone deacetylases (HDACs) inhibitor creates a unique epigenetic nexus, has been shown to disrupt placental function and impair fetal development through both epigenetic mechanisms and metabolic interference [25–28]. Our study aims to explore the potential of amniotic fluid metabolite profiling as a biomarker screening tool and to elucidate the role of folic acid in placental epigenetic regulation, with a focus on their contributions to the molecular mechanisms underlying fetal growth restriction.

Materials and methods

Research object collection

From January 2024 to December 2024, amniotic fluid samples were collected from pregnant women undergoing invasive prenatal diagnosis at our hospital, with all specimens stored at − 80 °C prior to testing. After excluding pregnancies with fetal anomalies, cases lacking first-trimester crown–rump length (CRL) measurements, and those with incomplete clinical data, 48 non-smoking, non-alcohol-consuming pregnant women were ultimately enrolled. The study group comprised 19 women aged 24–32 years, while the control group included 29 women aged 24–33 years.

The inclusion criteria for the study group were as follows: According to the Chinese Expert Consensus on Fetal Growth Restriction, participants demonstrated ultrasound-estimated fetal weight or abdominal circumference below the 10th percentile for gestational age, indicating failure of growth-restricted fetuses to achieve their genetically programmed growth potential.

Pregnant women diagnosed with fetal growth restriction by ultrasound underwent invasive prenatal diagnosis including fetal karyotyping and chromosomal microarray analysis. We collected 10–15 mL of amniotic fluid from each participant. All participants received routine genetic counseling and provided written informed consent prior to the procedure. All human subject research protocols were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Hangzhou First People’s Hospital Ethics Committee (ID: [2021] Medical Review No. (011) –01) and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration with its subsequent amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Sample preparation

Amniotic fluid was collected by amniocentesis at gestational weeks 20–30. The collected amniotic fluid was immediately centrifuged (1750 × g, 4 °C, 10 min), and the supernatant was stored at − 80 °C. Samples were divided into the FGR group, diagnosed by B-ultrasound, and the control group. A volume of 100 μL of each sample was transferred to an EP tube, followed by the addition of 400 μL of extraction solvent (methanol: acetonitrile = 1:1 (v/v)) containing isotopically labeled internal standards. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, ultrasonicated for 10 min (in an ice water bath), and then left to stand at − 40 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 4 °C, 12,000 rpm (centrifugal force of 13,800 × g, radius of 8.6 cm) for 15 min. The supernatant was collected into the injection vial for subsequent analysis.

Mass spectrometry detection

For polar metabolites, LC–MS/MS analyses were performed using an UHPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide (2.1 × 50 mm,1.7 μm) coupled to Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Orbitrap MS, Thermo). The mobile phase consisted of 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 ammonia hydroxide in water (pH = 9.75) (A) and acetonitrile (B). The auto-sampler temperature was 4 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer was used for its ability to acquire MS/MS spectra on information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode in the control of the acquisition software (Xcalibur, Thermo). In this mode, the acquisition software continuously evaluates the full scan MS spectrum. The raw data were converted to the mzXML format using ProteoWizard and processed with an in-house program, which was developed using R and based on XCMS, for peak detection, extraction, alignment, and integration.

Animal model

All rat experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial Laboratory Animal Center (Approval No.: ZJCLA-IACUC-20011117). Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were purchased from Zhejiang Provincial Laboratory Animal Center and housed at the Laboratory Animal Research Center of Hangzhou Medical College. Forty female rats (250–300 g) and 20 male rats (300–450 g) were used. Animal feeding, interventions, and breeding were conducted in an SPF-grade laboratory under controlled conditions (humidity: 60–70%; temperature: 21–25 °C; 12-h light/dark cycle). After acclimation, female and male rats were co-housed at a 2:1 ratio. Vaginal smears were examined microscopically the following morning, and the presence of sperm was designated as gestational day (GD) 0.5. Pregnancy was confirmed by the detection of vaginal plugs (embryonic day [E] 0.5). Pregnant rats were randomized into groups using a digital allocation method.

Pregnant rats were divided into three groups (n = 6–10 per group): control, FGR, and folic acid supplementation (FA) groups. Dietary adjustments began upon confirmed pregnancy: Control group: Standard diet (20% protein, 67% carbohydrates, 13% fat; 2 mg folic acid/kg). FGR group: 40% of standard diet intake (10 g/day) throughout gestation to establish the FGR model. FA group: 40% of a folic acid-supplemented diet (5 mg folic acid/kg, 2.5 times the standard diet) throughout gestation. Note: Standard feed: Item No. M2011; Folic acid feed: Item No. M2011 + FA. The formula is based on M2011 with folic acid supplemented to 5 mg/kg. All feeds were procured from Wuxi Fanbo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

On E17.5, rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (45 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection). Maternal weight was recorded at GD0, E3.5, E9.5, E13.5, and E17.5. Blood samples were collected from the inferior vena cava, centrifuged, and stored at − 80 °C. Fetuses and placentas were dissected from the uterus, weighed, and measured for crown–rump length using vernier calipers. Note: FGR was defined as fetal weight two standard deviations (SD) below the control group’s average.

Morphology and histology

Placentas from rats were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, they were rinsed with PBS, transferred to 70% ethanol, and processed into paraffin-embedded tissue blocks. Placental tissue sections of 4 μm thickness were selected for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and analyzed under a microscope. The labyrinth structure was analyzed as previously described [29]. Placental tissues were washed with sterile PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (v/v) for 48 h, dehydrated, embedded, sectioned, and stained with H&E to observe placental structure and inflammatory responses. The structural integrity of the placental implantation site was examined, the ratio between the two functional zones (labyrinth and junctional zone) was calculated.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum samples from rats were analyzed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Rat IL-6 ELISA Kit, Cat No. KE20024, Proteintech; Rat TNF-alpha ELISA Kit, Cat No. KE20001, Proteintech) to measure the pro-inflammatory markers interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescent staining analysis

The placenta tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, with the fixation time dependent on the size of the tissue. After fixation, the tissues were dehydrated, with the dehydration time also dependent on tissue size, followed by embedding in paraffin. Tissue sections with a thickness of 5 μm was used for immunofluorescence. After dewaxing and rehydration of the sections, high-pressure antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer, followed by blocking with 0.5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. The primary antibody Ki67 (1:2000, Rabbit, Proteintech, 28,074–1-AP), HDAC1 (1:200, Rabbit, Abcam, ab109411) and Histone H3 (acetyl K9) (1:400, Rabbit, Abcam, ab10812) were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Sections were rinsed with phosphate buffer and incubated with secondary antibody (1:500, Goat Anti-Rabbit, FITC, Proteintech, SA00003-2) for 1 h at room temperature. After sections were counterstained with DAPI, immunofluorescence images were collected using microscope (﻿BX53, Olympus) and 3D HISTECH (﻿Pannoramic SCAN II).

Western blot

Animals were anesthetized, and the placenta was dissected. The placenta was homogenized in radioimmune precipitation assay (RIPA) buffer with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma Aldrich) and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Solarbio) at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were measured by BCA assay using a Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific, 2000c). Protein homogenates were separated by SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad). PVDF membranes were blocked in 4% nonfat milk for 1 h with TBS buffer containing 0.1% Tween-20 (Sigma Aldrich) (TBST) and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Antibodies were diluted in 1% nonfat milk. We used the following antibodies: anti-HDAC1 (1:2000, Rabbit, Abcam, ab109411), anti-acetylation H3K9 (1:2000, Rabbit, Abcam, ab10812), and anti-GAPDH (1:10,000, Rabbit, Millipore, ABS16). Blots were rinsed with TBST and incubated with horserace dish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies [goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000, Proteintech, Cat No. SA00001-2) for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescent (1:1, Pierce). Grayscale analysis was performed using ImageJ. To ensure accurate and reproducible western blot results, the data were normalized.

Apoptosis Assay

Cell apoptosis was assayed by a TUNEL (in-situ terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase deoxyuridyl triphosphate nick-end labeling) method (Beyotime, C1089). The placenta was fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C. Cryosections of the entire placenta were cut from the OCT-embedded tissue. Slices were washed three times with PB (0.1 M) every 10 min and treated with 0.3% TritonX-100 in PBS for 30 min. After rinsing in PBS, the sections were incubated with a TUNEL reaction mixture (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase plus nucleotide mixture in reaction buffer) in dark for 60 min at 37℃. Sections were rinsed with PBS three times and counterstained with DAPI (1:10,000). The fluorescent images were photographed using confocal microscopy (LSM710, Zeiss, Germany). All apoptotic cells were counted by ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

Paired data were tested with Student’s t-test. Multiple group data were tested with ANOVA followed by post hoc Tukey’s test unless noted. Shapiro–Wilk test was used to carry out the normality test in the behavioral studies. Data are represented as the mean ± SEM. Experiments and analysis were performed blind to the experimental conditions unless noted.

On day E17.5, pregnant rats from each group underwent cesarean section to retrieve the fetuses, and the schematic diagram of the uterine horn is visible (Supplementary Figure 1). There was no significant difference in the number of pups per litter among the groups (Fig. 5C). The results showed that compared with fetuses in the Control and FA groups, fetuses in the fetal growth restriction group had significantly decreased crown–rump length, while there was no difference between the Control and FA groups (Fig. 5B, D). Notably, both fetal weight and placental weight were significantly lower in the FGR group compared with the Control and FA groups, but folic acid supplementation significantly increased both fetal weight and placental weight (Fig. 5E, F). In addition, the fetal-to-placental weight ratio was increased in the FGR group, with no difference observed between the Control and FA groups (Fig. 5G). This suggests an increase in net nutrient flux per gram of placenta, indicating adaptive or compensatory molecular regulatory mechanisms in FGR placental efficiency. However, these changes may not be sufficient to overcome the negative impacts of fetal growth restriction, including placental functional changes and metabolic adjustments. Notably, ELISA analysis revealed significant changes in serum inflammatory cytokine levels in FGR rats compared with the Control group. Interestingly, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels were significantly decreased by 48% in FGR rats, and FA supplementation significantly reversed TNF-α expression levels (Fig. 5H). However, interleukin-6 (IL-6) was overactivated and increased 3.4-fold, and folic acid supplementation significantly reduced IL-6 expression (Fig. 5I). During normal pregnancy, there is a physiological imbalance between Th1 (TNF-α, etc.) and Th2 (IL-6, etc.) cells in pregnant women, with Th2 cells predominating to maintain pregnancy through immune protection and altering placental efficiency [33–35]. This may also be a reason for enhanced placental efficiency in FGR.

After folic acid supplementation, the total placental area increased significantly compared with FGR group, but there was no significant difference compared with control group (Fig. 6B). Additionally, our study uncovered massive perivillous fibrin deposition specifically in the FGR group, indicating that folic acid supplementation significantly reversed the morphological and structural inflicted by FGR on the placenta. Discussion

This study utilized robust metabolomic methodologies to identify amniotic fluid biomarkers associated with fetal growth restriction, employing stringent modeling approaches to minimize confounding variables. Our findings highlight a profound dysregulation of lipid metabolism and folate-dependent pathways in FGR, with 24.24% of differentially expressed metabolites classified as lipids or lipid-like molecules. Notably, perturbations in hypoxanthine and valproic acid (VPA) levels, the key folate associated metabolites and significant enrichment of arginine/proline metabolism and TCA cycle intermediates underscore the metabolic complexity of FGR pathogenesis. Accumulating evidence from both clinical and preclinical studies supports the pivotal role of folate supplementation in mitigating pregnancy complications and fostering fetal development [36–40]. These studies indicated that appropriate periconceptional and prenatal folate supplementation can effectively reduce risks of pregnancy complications and promote fetal growth. Folic acid, as a cofactor in nucleotide synthesis, drives cellular proliferation and metabolic homeostasis [41–43]. An emerging insight suggests that folic acid-mediated metabolic remodeling may stabilize folate cycles via 5-methyltetrahydrofolate-homocysteine methyltransferase (MTR) [44]. Building on this foundation, our findings suggest a potential role of folic acid in modulating epigenetic markers such as histone acetylation in placental tissues, which may contribute to the amelioration of FGR. However, further mechanistic studies are needed to establish a causal relationship.

The downregulation of VPA metabolites in amniotic fluid is associated with fetal growth restriction

Metabolomics mainly uses targeted or non-targeted technologies to analyze metabolites. Leveraging LC–MS/MS-based metabolomics and multivariate models (PCA for intrinsic metabolic variance; OPLS-DA for group discrimination [45, 46], we identified 197 differentially expressed metabolites in FGR amniotic fluid, including 8 upregulated and 2 downregulated folic acid dependent species. These metabolites are present in 15 different metabolic pathways. Amniotic fluid metabolic disturbances participate in the pathophysiological processes of FGR through multiple pathways, including abnormalities in amino acid metabolism, elevated inflammatory factors, exposure to environmental pollutants, and alterations in the microbial environment [14, 47, 48]. Future studies should collect amniotic fluid samples at different gestational periods and expand the sample size to better characterize the temporal dynamics of metabolic changes. Interestingly, our metabolite analysis identified synthetic compounds VPA and allopurinol in amniotic fluid, suggesting placental processing of exogenous substrates or dynamic maternal-fetal substance transfer, potentially impacting offspring development and metabolism [49]. VPA, as a histone deacetylase inhibitor and an antiepileptic drug, was markedly suppressed in FGR, reflecting compromised fetal nutrient availability [50]. This aligns with clinical observations linking maternal VPA exposure to heightened risks of preeclampsia, placental insufficiency, and severe FGR [25, 51, 52]. VPA exposure induces specific neurotoxic perturbations in developing zebrafish embryos, and that FA reversed most of the identified defects [53]. VPA, known to cross the placenta and modulate fetal epigenetic programs in animal models [47, 54, 55], exhibited reduced levels in FGR AF, potentially indicating impaired placental transport or altered maternal metabolism. The reduction may intersect with the epigenetic dysregulation observed in our cohort. Similarly, allopurinol, a xanthine oxidase inhibitor linked to oxidative stress pathways, was elevated in FGR AF. This aligns with prior reports of increased xanthine, a metabolite of purine metabolism in FGR placentas, where oxidative stress is a hallmark of placental insufficiency [56–58]. However, it is important to note that VPA and allopurinol are not endogenous metabolites, and their physiological relevance in FGR requires further validation in larger cohorts with detailed maternal exposure histories. Family history of major congenital malformation, VPA use, topiramate use, and not taking FA were strong predictors of major congenital malformation in infants born to women with epilepsy [59]. FA is the synthetic form of natural folate. Supplementation with FA prior to and during early pregnancy has been recommended to reduce the risks of several neurodevelopmental diseases, including neural tube defects in newborns [60–63]. Mechanistically, VPA modulates GABAergic neurodevelopment through HDAC inhibition, and its deficiency disrupts histone acetylation patterns critical for synaptic plasticity and neurobehavioral outcomes [54, 64]. Notably, high-dose folic acid supplementation (4 mg/kg) rescues neurodevelopmental deficits in VPA-induced models [65], underscoring the interplay between folic acid sufficiency and VPA-mediated epigenetic regulation. Our findings suggest that changes in amniotic fluid VPA levels may be associated with FGR, potentially reflecting underlying metabolic and epigenetic dysregulation. However, further studies are needed to determine whether VPA levels can serve as a reliable diagnostic marker or therapeutic target in clinical practice.

Folic acid supplementation alters placental structure and function in FGR

Using a starvation-induced FGR rat model, we demonstrated that folate intervention reverses placental maladaptation. Consistent with prior studies [66–71], FGR fetuses exhibited significant reductions in body weight, crown–rump length, and placental weight. However, FA supplementation can effectively promote fetal growth. During pregnancy, folic acid supplementation reprograms maternal metabolism, potentially mitigating energy deficits from caloric restriction [72], may influence epigenetic marks that regulate metabolic gene expression. It modulates oxidative stress enzymes, such as cytochrome P450, to reduce energy loss, and upregulates placental amino acid transporters to prioritize nutrient allocation to critical maternal tissues over fat storage [73]. Each standard deviation increase in maternal folic acid levels correlates with a 71-gram rise in fetal birth weight, highlighting its role in nutrient efficiency [74]. Folic acid also maintains tissue integrity by preventing DNA damage and cell apoptosis, while promoting probiotic growth, which synthes B vitamins and inhibits obesity-related bacteria [75–77]. Folic acid may enhance energy acquisition efficiency in FGR through similar mechanisms, potentially mediated by epigenetic modifications that promote fetal growth and development. Paradoxically, FGR placental Lb/Jz ratio increased by 40%, indicative of compensatory hypervascularization in the Lb to augment nutrient exchange a response ultimately sufficient to normalize fetal growth. Folate supplementation restored placental architecture, reducing Lb hyperplasia by 10% and normalizing the Lb:Jz ratio. Histopathological and cytokine analyses revealed that folic acid rebalances Th1/Th2 immunity (normalizing TNF-α/IL-6 ratios) [33, 78, 79] and enhances decidual zone thickness, which could potentially enhance nutrient transport while alleviating metabolic stress. However, the effects of nutrient transport are needed to confirm this hypothesis. Our findings suggest that increased placental efficiency in FGR may be a compensatory response to functional challenges, such as potential limitations in nutrient or oxygen supply. However, further studies are needed to directly assess placental perfusion and its relationship to placental efficiency.

Folic acid supplementation restores the Lb:Jz ratio to normal by targeted regulation to thicken the De area, inhibit the expansion of the Jz area, and maintain the functional homeostasis of the Lb area. Folic acid demonstrates pleiotropic efficacy in enhancing placental structural–functional equilibrium and mitigating FGR-induced fetal growth impairment. Notably, spatial genomic analyses have revealed compartmentalized imprinting patterns, with paternal genes dominating placental tissue and maternal genes enriched in labyrinthine and decidual regions [80]. This spatial functional partitioning provides a contextual basis for investigating cell-specific responses to FGR and folate supplementation. Indeed, apoptosis assays confirmed that, compared with the control group, the number of apoptotic cells in the FGR group was significantly reduced in placental Lb regions, and this FGR-associated apoptotic aberration was markedly reversed by folic acid supplementation (Supplementary Figure 2). Together, these observations suggest a potential interplay between folic acid and placental cellular dynamics. This interplay may inform future investigations into folic acid’s therapeutic potential in developmental reprogramming for FGR.

Folic acid supplementation ameliorates fetal growth restriction by modulating epigenetic modification levels

﻿FGR is a complex condition involving placental dysfunction at multiple levels. Folic acid plays a crucial role in epigenetic. Substantial epigenetic resetting during early embryo development from fertilization to blastocyst formation ensures zygotic genome activation and leads to progressive cellular heterogeneities, which may, epigenetically, program molecular pathways related to fetal growth and stress response [81–84]. Inadequate or excessive FA intake may elevate DNA mutation rates and contribute to abnormal epigenetic modifications [85–87]. A comprehensive understanding of genomic and epigenomic alterations induced by high-FA or low-FA dietary patterns has yet to be conclusively established through systematic research, particularly in terms of precise quantification of damage mechanisms and identification of critical thresholds for FA homeostasis. Folic acid is active in one-carbon metabolism with the impact on epigenetic modification of gene expression and maternal folic acid supplementation during pregnancy may help to counteract the effects of folate depletion [88, 89]. Placental dysfunction in FGR is intricately linked to epigenetic dysregulation [90–92]. Affected placentae have malformed villi with evidence of impaired exchange capacity [93, 94]. Trophoblasts in FGR placentae exhibit reduced proliferation, increased apoptotic death, altered metabolism, senescence and impaired invasive capacity. However, there are also studies that have confirmed that mTOR regulates amino acid transporter activity in placental trophoblast cells by sensing folate levels, thereby influencing fetal growth [95]. This process depends on the proton-coupled folate transporter rather than homocysteine accumulation. As a folate sensor, mTOR links folate availability to placental nutrient transport and fetal growth through the regulation of mTORC1 and mTORC2 signaling pathways, providing insights into the mechanisms of folate-related disorders such as fetal growth restriction and cancer [96, 97]. Interestingly, research has found that the loss of maternal H3K27me3-dependent imprinting status in the amino acid transporter Slc38a4 leads to the overactivation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway, which in turn causes excessive placental growth [98]. This suggests that epigenetic modifications can regulate function by influencing mTOR signaling.

We identified elevated HDAC1 in FGR placentas, concomitant with reduced H3K9ac, a histone mark governing transcriptional activation. FA supplementation normalized HDAC1 activity and restored histone acetylation modification levels, directly linking folate sufficiency to histone acetylation-driven cell proliferation. Intriguingly, our complementary experiments using immunofluorescence assays revealed that HDAC1 and H3K9ac were predominantly co-localized in large quantities within the Jz region of the placenta, whereas this co-localization was relatively diminished in the FGR group (Supplementary Figure 3). Excessive activation of HDAC1 in FGR may inhibit the transcription of H3K9ac-dependent key genes in placental cells. However, the direct association between the placental Jz and Lb region remains to be further investigated. As VPA inhibits HDACs to promote histone hyperacetylation [99–101], its downregulation in FGR amniotic fluid likely exacerbates HDAC1 overexpression, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of epigenetic imbalance. Our results suggest that folic acid supplementation may influence HDAC1 activity and H3K9ac levels, potentially contributing to the regulation of gene expression and acetylation homeostasis. Future studies should examine mTOR signaling as a convergent pathway, given its role as a folate sensor. This dual mechanism not only clarifies folic acid role in FGR reversal but also positions the HDAC1 and H3K9ac as a nexus integrating metabolism, epigenetics, and placental function.

Conclusion

This study integrates metabolomic profiling, placental histopathology, and epigenetic analyses to explore potential associations between folic acid supplementation and the amelioration of FGR. Further mechanistic studies are needed to establish causal relationships. Under FGR conditions, our observations suggest that changes in epigenetic markers may be associated with functional adaptations in the placenta. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these changes remain to be elucidated. Our study suggest that epigenetic regulation may play a role in placental adaptation during FGR, potentially contributing to fetal viability. However, further studies are needed to establish the functional significance of these epigenetic changes. Folic acid may influence acetylation homeostasis through HDAC1 suppression, highlighting a potential link between nutrient availability and epigenetic regulation in developmental disorders. Further mechanistic studies are needed to validate this hypothesis.
