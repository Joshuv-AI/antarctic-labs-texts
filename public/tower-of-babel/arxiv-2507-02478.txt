Prior approaches to associating randomized MAC addresses with devices primarily focus on probe requests, overlooking the broader set of management frames and their transition dynamics. This narrow focus limits their robustness in dense, real-world environments with high device mobility, where probe activity alone fails to yield stable and distinctive signatures. In this paper, we present a novel framework for fingerprinting Wi-Fi devices based on behavioral dynamics extracted from passively observed management frames. We model each device’s behavior as a finite state machine and introduce matrix-based representations that encode both structural (state transition frequencies) and temporal (inter-state delays) characteristics. Through extensive evaluation in diverse real-world settings, our method achieves over 86% identification accuracy for non-randomized devices using only Wi-Fi management frames, with further improvements observed through temporal burst aggregation. Our findings are sufficient to uniquely and consistently characterize devices at scale, outperforming the state-of-the-art.



1.
Introduction

The increasing ubiquity of Wi-Fi-enabled devices fuels a growing need to understand and protect user privacy at the wireless edge. While Wi-Fi protocols enable seamless connectivity, they also expose unencrypted management frames—used for device discovery, authentication, and association—that leak structured behavioral patterns. These patterns allow observers to track and fingerprint devices, even in the absence of explicit identifiers
(
Koh et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2021
;
Mishra et al. 2021
)
. Device fingerprinting, originally intended for security and diagnostics, now raises serious privacy concerns as adversaries can leverage it to re-identify and track users across sessions and locations
(
Vanhoef and Piessens 2016
;
Mishra and Cunche 2024b
;
Tan and Chan 2021
;
Mishra et al. 2024b
)
.

MAC address randomization
(
Vanhoef et al. 2016
)
emerges as a popular countermeasure to combat long-term tracking. Modern devices periodically rotate their MAC addresses to obfuscate persistent identifiers during Wi-Fi interactions. However, randomization alone does not eliminate all sources of device uniqueness. The sequence, timing, and structure of emitted management frames remain consistent and often reflect implementation-specific logic within the device’s network stack. As a result, devices still exhibit distinct behavioral signatures that persist across randomized sessions
(
Tan and Chan 2021
;
Mishra et al. 2024b
)
.

Existing fingerprinting approaches largely rely on analyzing probe requests in isolation
(
Tan and Chan 2021
;
Vanhoef et al. 2016
;
Mishra et al. 2024b
)
. While effective in controlled environments, this narrow focus limits their applicability in dense, real-world settings with high device mobility and diverse access point (AP) deployments. Probe-based signatures alone often fail to provide stable or distinctive identifiers due to variability in probe frequency and content
(
Mishra et al. 2023
)
.

We present a novel framework for fingerprinting Wi-Fi devices based on their full behavioral dynamics, captured through passively observed management frames. We model each device as a
finite state machine
(FSM) that captures its state transitions during Wi-Fi interactions, from scanning for access points via probe requests to establishing connections through authentication and association. Our FSM-based abstraction captures both structural properties (transition frequencies) and temporal characteristics (inter-state delays) of device behavior.

Through extensive real-world evaluations, we show that FSM-based fingerprints remain consistent across randomized MAC sessions and outperform existing methods. Our approach achieves over 86% identification accuracy for devices using only management frames, and exhibits enhanced robustness when identifiers (MAC addresses) change after a short number of temporal bursts.

This paper makes the following contributions:

•

We introduce the first FSM-based fingerprinting framework that captures both structural and temporal properties of Wi-Fi device behavior using passively observed management frames.

•

We propose an efficient representation to characterize FSMs that supports efficient and scalable similarity comparisons across devices.

•

We evaluate our approach on real-world traces in multiple contextual scenarios, demonstrating that behavioral fingerprints persist even under MAC address randomization and significantly outperform probe-based methods in accuracy and robustness.

2.
Related Work

Early work on Wi-Fi device fingerprinting focuses heavily on information element (IE) analysis within probe requests. Techniques such as those proposed in
(
Vanhoef and Piessens 2016
;
Tan and Chan 2021
;
Mishra and Cunche 2024a
;
Mishra et al. 2024a
;
Robyns et al. 2017
;
Pang et al. 2007
;
Cunche et al. 2014
)
extract specific IE fields - transmission rates, SSIDs, and vendor tags—to construct device signatures. Vanhoef et al.
(
Vanhoef and Piessens 2016
)
evaluate the stability and discriminability of various IE fields and propose selection strategies for robust fingerprint construction. However, IE-based methods suffer from limited resilience: devices from the same manufacturer often share identical IEs, and firmware or OS updates can significantly alter emitted fields. Our approach avoids such volatile metadata and instead focuses on persistent behavioral signals embedded in management frame sequences.

Overview of FSM-based Wi-Fi device fingerprinting framework

Beyond content features, several studies explore timing-based fingerprinting. Clock skew techniques
(
Jana and Kasera 2008
;
Lanze et al. 2012
)
exploit low-level hardware clock variations but require long-term observation and high-precision hardware, limiting practicality. Other approaches
(
Mishra and Cunche 2021
;
Vanhoef et al. 2016
)
extract statistical timing features between frames. However, these techniques generally depend on high probe activity, making them unreliable in dense environments. In contrast, we capture behavioral consistency across the full management lifecycle—not just probe traffic—resulting in more robust and generalizable fingerprints.

Some prior works use sequence numbers to correlate frames over time
(
Vanhoef and Piessens 2016
;
Tan and Chan 2021
)
, often extending to probabilistic association techniques. For example, the framework by
(
Tan and Chan 2021
)
integrates sequence number continuity with multi-frame correlation using a minimum-cost flow formulation. Other lines of research aim to defeat MAC randomization through protocol-level attacks. Techniques such as UUID-E reversal
(
Vanhoef and Piessens 2016
)
bait target specific vulnerabilities to expose a device’s real identity.

Our approach distinguishes itself from prior work by modeling the full range of Wi-Fi management interactions using finite state machines (FSMs). Unlike methods that focus on specific frame types or isolated features, we capture the complete sequence and timing of events—including authentication, association, and control exchanges—at the management layer.

The process (see Figure
1
) begins by passively capturing management frames emitted by Wi-Fi-enabled devices (cf. Section
3
). From this data, we construct FSMs that represent each device’s behavioral patterns (Section
4
). These FSMs are then encoded into feature vectors and compared using both distance-based metrics (Section
5.2
) and machine learning classifiers (Section
5.3
). Finally, we perform fingerprinting by identifying stable, device-specific FSM signatures (Section
6
).

By capturing both the structural logic and temporal dynamics of device behavior, our FSM-based framework offers a robust and generalizable foundation for behavioral fingerprinting in diverse environments.

3.
Data Collection and Preprocessing

To capture realistic and diverse Wi-Fi behaviors, we collect packet traces across six distinct environments: a residential apartment, café, laboratory, classroom, public square, and restaurant. Each session lasts between 30 and 95 minutes, with a total collection exceeding 2.6 million packets. We extract management frames across scenarios, resulting in a focused subset of 234,852 frames.

3.2.
Privacy consideration and processing

All captures follow a privacy-compliant sanitization procedure aligned with local data collection regulations. We remove all non-essential information, including MAC addresses, SSIDs, and absolute timestamps.

After collection, we discard malformed or unrecognized frames and retain only valid management, control, and data frames. To focus on client behavior, we apply a multi-stage filtering pipeline. First, we flag frames with randomized MAC addresses. Out of 32,310 observed MAC addresses, 20,615 (
63.8\%
) are flagged as randomized. Additionally, 30,529 (
94.5\%
) lack known vendor prefixes, reinforcing their classification. We also exclude MACs that send
Beacon
or
Association Response
frames, which likely represent access points. This process removes 265 MACs and ensures our analysis focuses exclusively on client-side behavior.

3.3.
Isolating bursts and ground truth

We define a
burst
as a sequence of packets from the same MAC address where inter-packet intervals do not exceed one second
(
Tan and Chan 2021
)
. This models a typical probing session for Wi-Fi clients
(
Mishra et al. 2024b
)
.

Since device identities are not directly observable, we treat persistent MAC addresses as proxies for ground truth. For each such MAC, we treat every burst as an independent behavioral instance of the same device. We assign unique identifiers to a collection of consecutive bursts from a device. This enables supervised evaluation of fingerprint stability across different time windows.

4.
FSM-Based Device Characterization

Wi-Fi communication is governed by the IEEE 802.11 standard
(
Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers 2020
)
, which defines a structured exchange of frame types across three categories: management, control, and data. Our analysis focuses exclusively on
management frames
, which coordinate key operations such as device discovery, authentication, and association. These frames are typically unencrypted and observable in passive captures, making them ideal for fingerprinting without requiring active network participation.

When a device attempts to join a network, it initiates a series of well-defined interactions—beginning with
Probe Request
, followed by
Authentication
, and culminating in
Association
. While the protocol dictates the expected sequence of these exchanges, device manufacturers often introduce implementation-specific variations for performance optimization, power savings, or compliance extensions
(
Vanhoef and Piessens 2016
)
. These deviations manifest in observable differences in frame orderings, timing, and repetition patterns, offering a rich source of behavioral fingerprints.

4.1.
Modeling FSM

To systematically capture these patterns, we model each device’s behavior as a
Finite State Machine (FSM)
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of all management frame subtypes observed across the dataset. For a given device
d
, we define its FSM as a tuple:

\mathcal{F}_{d}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0})

where:

•

Q
is the set of states, each representing a unique management frame subtype (e.g.,
Probe Request
,
Association Response
),

•

\Sigma
is the sequence of frame arrivals ordered by time,

•

\delta:Q\times\Sigma\rightarrow Q
is the transition function, encoding observed transitions between frame subtypes,

•

q_{0}
is the initial state, typically set to
Probe Request
.

We populate FSM transitions based on observed sequences within the captured dataset. A transition
q_{i}\rightarrow q_{j}
is created when frame subtype
j
immediately follows subtype
i
for the same MAC address during/after a burst of probe requests. To support bidirectional modeling—such as client-to-AP interactions—we include frame directionality by incorporating destination MAC addresses into the state context. Frequent self-transitions (e.g., repeated
Probe Requests
) naturally emerge in this structure, often signaling retries or active scanning phases.

Empirical transition statistics reveal distinct patterns across vendors. Let
\mu_{v}
denote the average number of transitions per FSM for vendor
v
. We observe significant variation in
\mu_{v}
, indicating vendor-specific transmission logic and protocol adherence. These structural differences remain stable even under MAC randomization, supporting their use as robust fingerprints.

4.2.
FSM construction from bursts

We construct one FSM per burst, as defined in Section
3.3
. Each burst encapsulates a short-lived, coherent exchange of management frames (burst of probe requests followed by potential subsequent interactions with nearby APs) from a single MAC address, enabling fine-grained behavioral modeling. This per-burst construction is essential under MAC randomization, where devices frequently change their identifiers between bursts. By focusing on burst-local behavior, we capture stable transmission logic while remaining agnostic to long-term identifiers.

For each burst, we parse the ordered list of management frame subtypes. Each unique subtype becomes a state in the FSM, and adjacent frame pairs define directed transitions. Despite adhering to a common protocol, devices produce structurally distinct FSMs due to differences in retry behavior, vendor-specific optimizations, and response timing strategies. These nuanced variations provide the foundation for behavior-based fingerprinting that operates independently of MAC addresses or volatile metadata.

5.
FSM Comparison Metrics

To compare behavioral patterns across devices, we transform each FSM into a structured numerical vector. While FSMs are inherently graph-based, direct comparison between FSM graphs is computationally intensive and not scalable to large datasets. Instead, we extract statistical and temporal features from each FSM and embed them into a real-valued vector space. This transformation enables efficient and scalable similarity computation using standard distance metrics.

where

5.2.
Similarity Computation

We compute similarity between FSMs using three distance metrics, each capturing different aspects of behavioral similarity:

Euclidean Distance.

Captures absolute differences in feature magnitudes:

d_{\text{euclidean}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}(x_{i}-y_{i})^{2}}

Manhattan Distance.

Measures cumulative absolute differences:

d_{\text{manhattan}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|

Cosine Similarity.

Captures structural similarity, invariant to scale:

d_{\text{cosine}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=1-\frac{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|\cdot\|\mathbf{y}\|}

While Euclidean and Manhattan distances emphasize magnitude differences, cosine similarity captures the direction of behavior. We combine these perspectives by averaging the pairwise distance matrices computed from all three metrics. We then perform nearest-neighbor matching using the minimum combined distance. Empirically, this hybrid approach consistently outperforms any single metric across diverse environments and device groups.

This vector embedding enables the computation of a full
n\times n
similarity matrix using efficient matrix operations. For our dataset of over 32,000 FSMs, we compute all pairwise distances and nearest neighbors in under five minutes on a standard workstation. This scalability makes our approach viable for large-scale deployments.

5.3.
Supervised Learning-Based Fingerprinting

In addition to nearest-neighbor search, we explore a supervised learning approach to predict whether two FSMs originate from the same device. For each pair
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
, we construct a pairwise feature vector:

\mathbf{f}_{ij}=\left[\text{Euclidean}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}),\ \text{Cosine}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}),\ \text{Manhattan}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})\right]

We label each pair as 1 if both FSMs are from the same persistent MAC address, and 0 otherwise. Only non-randomized (persistent) MACs are used for supervised learning, as they provide ground-truth continuity across bursts (See Section
3.3
).

We evaluate three standard binary classifiers: i)
Random Forest (RF)
, ii)
Support Vector Machine (SVM)
with RBF kernel, and iii)
Logistic Regression (LR)
.

These classifiers operate on the pairwise distance features and output the likelihood that two FSMs belong to the same device. In later sections, we present classification results across environments and burst frequency tiers, demonstrating strong generalization even in dense or noisy settings.

6.4.
Comparison with state-of-the-art

We evaluate the robustness of our frameworks by revisiting prior literature in MAC address association (associating randomized MAC addresses to a single device). In particular, we analyze the
Infocom’21
framework
(
Tan and Chan 2021
)
under diverse capture scenarios. This framework encompasses multiple association strategies, including the use of sequence numbers (SEQ), information elements (IEs), and RSSI-based signatures. Notably,
Infocom’21
outperforms earlier approaches that relied solely on packet reception timings.

Building on this, we examine two key components of the signature proposed in
(
Tan and Chan 2021
)
: the information elements (IEs) and the sequence number patterns (SEQ). We exclude the RSSI component due to its dependency on geographically distributed sniffers, which is impractical in many real-world deployments and contributes only marginally to performance (up to a
10\%
drop when removed, as shown in Fig. 13 of
(
Tan and Chan 2021
)
).

We adopt the evaluation methodology introduced in
Infocom’21

(
Tan and Chan 2021
)
, which measures the effectiveness of probe-to-device associations using
discrimination accuracy
. This metric reflects the proportion of correctly associated probe requests among 1000 randomly selected samples, each matched to previous probes within a time window defined as the
Period
(
[t_{i}-\tau,t_{i}]
). We fix
\tau
to 600 seconds.

Using this setup, we compare our FSM-based fingerprinting approach against the IE- and SEQ-based signatures from
Infocom’21
. Consistent with findings in
(
Mishra et al. 2023
)
, the
Infocom’21
method achieves no more than 63%
discrimination accuracy
with IEs and no more than 24% with SEQ—under ideal conditions using only long and persistent MACs and no pseudo-randomization. When we simulate pseudo-randomized identifiers using the ground-truth methodology from Section
3.3
and vary the
grouping size
from
P=1
to
P=10
, accuracy for these baseline methods becomes negligible.

This highlights the superior robustness of our FSM framework in dynamic and dense environments. Unlike earlier approaches that depend on static identifiers or sequence counters sensitive to noise and burst boundaries, our method captures stable, implementation-level behavioral transitions. As a result, it delivers significantly higher accuracy across a wide range of real-world conditions.

7.
Discussion

Our results show that finite state machine (FSM) modeling provides a powerful and scalable foundation for fingerprinting Wi-Fi devices, even in the presence of MAC address randomization. In this section, we discuss the behavioral principles behind this success, the value of burst aggregation and multi-metric comparison, and the broader implications for privacy and defenses.

Structural and Temporal Uniqueness:
Unlike traditional approaches that rely on static identifiers, our method focuses on behavioral traits—specifically, how devices transition between protocol states and the timing of those transitions. These characteristics emerge from a combination of vendor-specific implementation choices, hardware-level constraints, and environment-triggered behaviors.

Firmware and driver logic influence the order and structure of management frames, while hardware tuning affects retry rates and scanning intervals. Additionally, contextual factors—such as user actions or access point availability—contribute to subtle variations in device behavior. These aspects are difficult to standardize or randomize without impacting device performance. By embedding both transition frequencies and timing data into structured feature vectors, FSMs offer a rich representation of this behavioral uniqueness.

Value of Burst Aggregation:
Individual bursts often reflect only a partial view of a device’s communication logic. Aggregating multiple bursts, using the grouping parameter
P
, enables the model to capture more complete and representative behaviors. As shown in our evaluation, even modest aggregation (
P=4
) leads to significant improvements in fingerprinting accuracy, particularly in structured environments. This aggregation smooths out short-term variability and reinforces persistent patterns across time.

Limitations and Countermeasures:
Despite its robustness, FSM-based fingerprinting can be challenged through deliberate protocol obfuscation. For instance, vendors could randomize frame sequences, introduce artificial timing jitter, or adopt uniform scanning profiles across devices. Additionally, encrypting management frames—as partially addressed in WPA3—could reduce visibility into frame content and sequence.

However, such defenses are not without cost. Obfuscation may degrade network performance or increase power consumption, while aggressive frame suppression can limit support for essential features like roaming or diagnostics. The tension between privacy and performance complicates the deployment of effective countermeasures.

Implications for Privacy and Future Design:
Our findings underscore a key vulnerability in current wireless privacy strategies: even when identifiers are randomized, device behavior often remains distinctive. FSM-based modeling reveals this hidden layer of identifiability, highlighting the need for privacy-preserving designs that account for both metadata and behavioral signals.

Future protocol updates and mitigation efforts must address not only what devices transmit, but how they interact over time. Designing for behavioral uniformity—without sacrificing performance or utility—remains an open challenge in the wireless ecosystem.

8.
Conclusion

We present a fingerprinting framework based on finite state machine (FSM) modeling of Wi-Fi management frame behavior. By encoding both structural transitions and temporal dynamics, our method captures persistent device-specific patterns that remain identifiable even under MAC address randomization. Evaluated on real-world traces across diverse environments, the approach consistently outperforms content- and timing-based baselines. Burst aggregation and combined distance metrics further improve accuracy without requiring intrusive assumptions. FSM-based fingerprinting offers a scalable, protocol-compliant, and effective tool for behavior-based identification. In the future, we plan to propose adaptive defenses against FSM-based fingerprinting strategies.
