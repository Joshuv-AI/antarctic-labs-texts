Brief Report: Impact of PLCOm2012 Ethnicity Model Variations on Eligibility for the Alberta Lung Cancer Screening Program
Authors: Matthew T Warkentin, Kirk Graff, Darren R Brenner, Gary Teare, Monica L Mullin, Laura Hampton, Huiming Yang, Alain Tremblay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionScreening with low-dose computed tomography reduces lung cancer mortality among individuals at high risk of lung cancer. Three PLCOm2012 model variations for predicting lung cancer risk have been developed: the original model with six race or ethnicity categories (6Race), a model with three race or ethnicity categories (3Race), and a model excluding race and ethnicity (NoRace). We evaluated the impact of model selection on screening eligibility for the Alberta Lung Cancer Screening Program.MethodsRetrospective analysis of 5961 individuals contacting the Alberta Lung Cancer Screening Program. For each participant, we calculated their 6-year risk of lung cancer using the three PLCOm2012 models and compared their eligibility using a 1.5% 6-year lung cancer risk threshold.ResultsOf the participants, 70% identified as White, 9% Asian, 4% Indigenous, 0.5% Black, 0.4% Hispanic, and 17% not reported. Furthermore, 67% had a risk more than or equal to 1.5% risk across all three models, whereas 30% had less than 1.5% risk across all models. Using the NoRace model, 31% of Indigenous and 25% of Black individuals were classified below the 1.5% threshold but would have been eligible using 3Race or 6Race. Using the 6Race model, 18% of Asian and 22% of Hispanic individuals were below 1.5% but were above 1.5% in either the NoRace or 3Race model.ConclusionsChoice of PLCOm2012 model can have a considerable impact on screening eligibility, particularly for ethnic minorities. Using the 3Race model increases screening eligibility for Indigenous, Black, Hispanic, and Asian individuals. Canadian-based data validating and quantifying the impact of race and ethnicity on lung cancer risk are urgently needed.Keywords: Screening guidelines, Screening eligibility, Risk-based approaches, Race, Ethnicity

Introduction

The PLCOm2012 model has become widely used for lung cancer risk assessment for screening program eligibility. A risk-based approach to lung cancer screening eligibility may have advantages over a National Lung Screening Trial (NLST)–like criteria, including increased sensitivity and cost-effectiveness. This has been demonstrated in several retrospective studies1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and in the large prospective International Lung Screening Trial (ILST) where 15.8% more participants selected using the PLCOm2012 model developed lung cancer compared with an equal number of participants meeting the U.S. Preventative Services Task Force 2013 (USPSTF2013) criteria, with no difference in stage distribution.6 More recently, the SUMMIT trial in the United Kingdom also found that USPSTF2013 would have missed 20.7% of screen-detected lung cancers compared with 3.4% missed using a 1.3% threshold based on PLCOm2012.7

A risk-based approach may be more inclusive, as NLST-like criteria do not account for different cancer risks experienced by some races or ethnicities for a given age and tobacco exposure history.8 NLST-like criteria result in higher effective risk eligibility thresholds for Black and Indigenous peoples, which are accounted for in risk-based approaches.9

As the interest for implementing PLCOm2012 into screening programs continues, several model variations have been developed, in part, to address concerns around the collection and use of race and ethnicity in health care systems and that race or ethnicity coefficients in the PLCOm2012 model based on U.S. data that may not be generalizable to other populations. Two variations of the base PLCOm2012 model4 (with six race or ethnicity categories) are the PLCOm2012-NoRace (excluding race and ethnicity)10 and the PLCOm2012-3Race (with the following three race or ethnicity categories: Indigenous, Black, and other) (M.C. Tammemägi, Brock University, written communication, 2020).11

The additional workload to collect the required information and perform the calculations outside of a research setting has been viewed as a potential barrier to implementation of risk-based approaches. However, based on our own experiences in Alberta, and those observed in Ontario12 and the United Kingdom,13 clinical programs have successfully implemented this approach. In this study, we evaluated the potential impact of PLCOm2012 model selection on recruitment into the Alberta Lung Cancer Screening Program (ALCSP).

Methods

Data Source

This study was a retrospective analysis of participants who contacted the ALCSP. The ALCSP is a publicly funded pilot-phase lung cancer screening program designed to enroll 3800 participants from September 2022 to September 2024 through self-referrals and primary care providers. The ALCSP used routinely-collected program data and focused on quality improvement assessment of the risk prediction process implemented as part of the ALCSP. The data collection for the ALCSP was reviewed by the Information and Privacy Advisor (Privacy Impact Assessment, Legal & Privacy, Alberta Health Services) who oversees the clinical lung cancer screening program. The Alberta Innovates Ethics Screening Tool (i.e., ARECCI) was applied (score = 3), and it was determined that formal ethics review of this study was not required.14 Individual patient consent was not required as this study used aggregated administrative data and was conducted as part of quality improvement for the ALCSP.

At program onset, the PLCOm2012-3Race model was used and a 1.5% lung cancer risk threshold, age 50 to 74 years, and absence of severe co-morbidities were the main criteria for screening eligibility. Trained nursing staff with access to scripted questions and approaches to obtaining the relevant information completed the risk assessment by phone for all participants who contacted the program and entered the data into a separate risk calculator built as a flowsheet in the program's electronic medical records (EPIC - Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, WI). Race and ethnicity were self-identified (and voluntary), recorded according to the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) guidelines for race-based and indigenous identity data,15 and then adapted to the PLCOm2012 categories (Supplementary Table 1). Participants who did not provide race or ethnicity data were coded as the reference group for the given PLCOm2012 model (6Race or 3Race). All variables for the PLCOm2012 model were required by participants (other than race or ethnicity), so there were no other missing data. Eligible individuals were consented and enrolled in the program, and a baseline low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) examination was scheduled.

Statistical Analysis

For this evaluation, we included information from all individuals contacting the program staff by phone and completing the risk assessment. The following data were extracted from electronic medical records (and anonymized): discrete PLCOm2012 risk components, gender, program eligibility, date of baseline LDCT scan, lung cancer diagnosis (yes/no), stage, and primary treatment as of March 4, 2025. We recalculated all three versions of the PLCOm2012 and evaluated the impact of using each version applied to the study population using the same 1.5% lung cancer risk threshold. The multivariable logistic regression beta coefficients for all three PLCOm2012 models are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

To evaluate the potential impact of race and ethnicity in the PLCOm2012 models, we constructed pseudo-populations for each race or ethnicity group. Each participant was assigned, in turn, to every race or ethnicity category, and their risk score was recalculated using all three versions of the PLCOm2012 model using their self-reported data for all other model inputs. This approach created hypothetical populations that retained realistic smoking histories and other risk factors, enabling us to isolate and evaluate the influence of model choice on race or ethnicity-specific screening eligibility. The benefit of the analysis based on pseudo-populations is to address the small sample sizes for some of the race or ethnicity groups observed in our study. Those small subpopulations may not be representative of the range of smoking histories and clinical characteristics in the population. This approach allows us to observe how risk distributions vary across race or ethnic groups using a larger distribution of observed smoking and clinical data.

Results

Risk evaluation was completed for 5961 individuals contacting the ALCSP. Demographic information required for the calculation of PLCOm2012 risks are outlined in Supplementary Table 3. Participants had a mean age of 62.7 years and were more frequently male (55%). Most participants self-identified as White (70%), 9% as Asian, 4% as Indigenous, and 17% were unknown or unreported.

A total of 4189 (70.3%) of participants met the PLCOm2012-3Race 1.5% threshold and were invited for screening if no other exclusions were present (Table 1). Fewer individuals were selected with the PLCOm2012 (4088; 68.6%) and PLCOm2012-NoRace (4126; 69.2%) models. The impact of model choice on selection rates was more important for certain race or ethnicity groups. PLCOm2012-NoRace led to 33.7% and 40.0% fewer Indigenous and Black participants eligible for screening, respectively. PLCOm2012 led to 30.2% and 55.6% fewer Asian and Hispanic participants eligible for screening, respectively. The distribution of PLCOm2012 risk scores by each model according to race or ethnicity is presented in Supplementary Figure 1.

Selecting the PLCOm2012-NoRace rather than PLCOm2012-3Race led to a 23.4% decrease in the number of eligible Indigenous participants and 11.9% fewer eligible Black participants. Selecting PLCOm2012 than PLCOm2012-3Race reduced eligible Asian and Hispanic participants by 29.5%. Discussion

We report the potential impact of PLCOm2012 model variations among individuals considered for lung cancer screening eligibility in Alberta, Canada. Although the directionality of impact on eligibility for races and ethnicities can be assessed based on model parameter coefficients, the actual impact in a program catchment area and within its racial and ethnic subpopulations depends on the size of each ethnic group, the distribution of other risk parameters included in the models, and the eligibility risk threshold.

Given the predominance of White individuals in our cohorts and the almost identical risk coefficients for this group in all three models, the overall impact of model selection on eligibility was modest and the PLCOm2012-3Race included the highest proportion of individuals. Conversely, model selection had much greater impact on eligibility for racial and ethnic minorities in this study. PLCOm2012 reduces risk for Asian and Hispanic participants (compared with White), which are not considered in the other two models. As such, we found a decrease in eligible Asian and Hispanic individuals in our actual and synthetic cohorts, respectively. Conversely, the PLCOm2012 and m2012-3Race models increase risk for Indigenous and Black participants not included in the PLCOm2012-NoRace model. As such, the PLCOm2012-NoRace model led to a decrease in Black and Indigenous individuals eligible in the actual and hypothetical cohorts, respectively.

Although the PLCOm2012-3Race model was the most inclusive, this does not necessarily imply that it is the optimal model to use. The aim of risk prediction is to enroll those most at risk for lung cancer, and inappropriately enrolling or excluding individuals could be considered inequitable. To date, lung cancer detection rates and accrual of follow-up are limited in this study, so the potential impacts of moving away from the PLCOm2012-3Race on cancer detection rates cannot yet be determined from our data. Value decisions on screening which consider program efficiency, cost, number needed to screen, and overall impact on lung cancer mortality in the entire population can best be managed by increasing or reducing the risk threshold for enrollment rather than removing validated risk parameters.

A recent study based on U.S. data similarly evaluated the impact of the various PLCOm2012 models on lung screen eligibility.16 This study used a U.S. screening cohort and found differential eligibility by race or ethnicity in their data.16 Our study uses a different methodologic approach, and, to our knowledge, it is the first study to evaluate the impact of race or ethnicity based on PLCOm2012 model variations within a Canadian screening cohort.

There remains a lack of validation of the impact of race and ethnicities on lung cancer risk outside of the PLCO (U.S. based) population. As an example, it is unclear whether the higher risk coefficients associated with being African American in the PLCO study are transferable to Black Canadians who have relatively lower lung cancer rates. Indigenous Canadians do appear to have adverse outcomes from lung cancer, so although the direction of risk adjustment in both the base PLCOm2012 and m2012-3Race model may be reasonable, little is known about the magnitude of the coefficients among Canadians and even less if these apply homogenously across the country and its diverse indigenous communities. In part, this lack of information results from a reticence to collect race and ethnicity data as part of many healthcare data sets, which, fortunately, is being addressed in many lung cancer screening programs.

Impact estimates within our cohort may differ considerably in populations with different racial and ethnic distributions. Model selection will have greater impact in populations with higher proportions of people identifying into certain race or ethnicity groups. The proportion of Black and Hispanic individuals in our screening cohort were low, and no individual identified as Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander. The synthetic population estimates for each race and ethnicity may be more broadly applicable if other risk factors are similar to those observed in our cohort. The inclusion of race or ethnicity in risk prediction models has been controversial, as self-reported race or ethnicity may conflate social, environmental, and structural determinants of health with biological differences. In this context, we view race or ethnicity not only as a biological construct but as a proxy for unmeasured social and structural factors that contribute to disparities in lung cancer risk. Risk models are designed to predict risk rather than to explain it.

Although risk-based approaches that incorporate race and ethnicity into LCS eligibility may reduce disparities resulting from the application of NLST-like criteria, more research is required to ensure the suitability of such tools to the local populations where these models will be applied.
