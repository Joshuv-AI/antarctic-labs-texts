Global, Regional, and National Burden of Self‐Harm and Projections to 2030: A Systematic Analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Jiaying Li, Yushen Dai, Junyan Xi, Stefanos Tyrovolas, Yin Karis Ngai, Yufei Lu, Janelle Yorke, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSelf‐harm is a global public health problem, yet comprehensive analyses of its trends and the impact of sociodemographic factors remain insufficient. This study aimed to systematically examine the spatial and temporal trends of self‐harm burden from 1990 to 2021 and forecast trends through 2030.MethodsWe analyzed data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, examining age‐standardized incidence, mortality, and disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) for self‐harm by region, age, gender, and sociodemographic index (SDI) from 1990 to 2021. Attributable factors were assessed, with decomposition analysis identifying drivers of change and frontier analysis highlighting the minimum achievable burden relative to national development. Projections for 2030 were also estimated.ResultsIn 2021, global age‐standardized rates per 100,000 population were 67.89 (95% UI: 56.60, 80.76) for incidence, 8.99 (95% UI: 8.34, 9.64) for mortality, and 410.19 (95% UI: 383.38, 438.42) for DALYs. Alcohol use, drug use, and temperature‐related exposures were associated with self‐harm burden. In high, low‐middle, and low SDI regions, increases in DALYs were driven predominantly by population growth. Higher‐SDI settings showed greater potential for burden reduction relative to the estimated frontier. Projections suggest continued declines in mortality and DALY rates by 2030.ConclusionThis study enhances understanding of the evolving patterns of self‐harm, providing evidence to inform policy and resource allocation. Continued progress may require implementing adaptable, evidence‐based strategies that consider local healthcare and cultural contexts.Keywords: epidemiology, global burden of disease, projection, self‐harm, suicide

Introduction

Self‐harm is a major global public health challenge and contributes substantially to the overall disease burden. In 2021, self‐harm ranked as the 21st leading Level 3 cause of death globally in terms of age‐standardized death rate [1]. Its high incidence and severe outcomes impose substantial strain on affected individuals, their families, and the wider community. Suicide, a fatal outcome of self‐harm, accounted for more than 1 in 100 deaths (1.1%) in 2021 and was the third leading cause of mortality among youth aged 15–29 years [2]. Despite decades of prevention efforts and the implementation of various strategies aimed at reducing its impact, the burden of self‐harm remains substantial, underscoring the complexity of its underlying causes and the challenges inherent in addressing this pressing issue on a global scale [3].

However, high‐quality and comprehensive evidence tracking the burden of self‐harm across different periods and regions is still lacking, leaving critical gaps in understanding its global and national trends. Existing studies mainly focus on specific populations, age groups, or regions, offering a fragmented view of this pressing issue. For instance, while recent research has summarized the global burden of self‐harm and its risk factors, it primarily relies on broad sociodemographic regional classifications rather than detailed analyses at the regional or country level [4]. Other studies have examined particular groups, such as young Europeans [5], young Asians [6], adolescents [7], or populations in the Middle East and North Africa [8].

Demographic changes, including increases in population and an aging society, have further impacted the burden of self‐harm [9]. However, most existing studies remain largely descriptive [4, 10], offering limited insight into the underlying drivers of self‐harm burden. This lack of deeper analysis hinders the ability to optimize resource allocation and design targeted interventions. Moreover, socioeconomic challenges and resource constraints are key factors shaping self‐harm trends, while cultural, policy, and healthcare system differences across regions further influence its occurrence and management [3]. Understanding how countries can minimize this burden relative to their development level is therefore crucial for identifying gaps in health system performance and guiding more effective policy responses.

Aligned with Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) Target 3.4 and its 2015–2030 monitoring framework [11, 12], this study focuses on projecting future trends in self‐harm and analyzing its key drivers. Specifically, it seeks to [1]: examine the burden of self‐harm at global, regional, and national levels from 1990 to 2021, along with GBD‐attributable estimates for available risk factors stratified by age and sex, utilizing the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 dataset [2]; decompose changes in disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) during this period into the contributions of population aging, population growth, and evolving epidemiological trends [3]; assess each country's proximity to the efficiency frontier in 2021; and [4] forecast global trends in self‐harm mortality, DALYs, and incidence through 2030.

Methods

Data Sources and Definitions

This is an observational modeling study based on secondary data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. Data on self‐harm from 1990 to 2021 were extracted from the GBD 2021 results using the GBD Compare (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-compare) tool provided by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation. The GBD framework uses standardized methods to quantify the health burden of 371 diseases and injuries across global, regional, and national levels, drawing on multiple data sources (e.g., vital registration and cause‐of‐death databases, health surveys, hospital/clinical records, censuses, registries, and published studies). The GBD estimation framework applies systematic bias correction and modeling to generate internally consistent estimates, and uncertainty is propagated via draw‐level calculations. Non‐fatal estimates are primarily modeled using Disease Modeling Meta‐Regression version 2.1, whereas cause‐specific mortality is estimated using the Cause of Death Ensemble model. Detailed methods are available in prior GBD publications [13].

GBD‐attributable burden estimates were derived using the Comparative Risk Assessment framework, as outlined in previous studies [14]. This study included all risk factors for self‐harm available in the GBD 2021 database. In cases of overlapping risk factors at multiple levels, data from the more specific, lower‐level factors were used.

Self‐harm was defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, utilizing codes X60–X64.9, X66–X84.9, and Y87.0, which capture intentional self‐inflicted injuries resulting in death or nonfatal harm [1].

The GBD study received ethical approval, including a waiver of informed consent, from the Institutional Review Board of the University of Washington. This secondary analysis used de‑identified, aggregate GBD data and therefore did not require additional Institutional Review Board approval. To maintain rigor and transparency, the study follows the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting [15].

GBD Measures

This study drew on GBD 2021 estimates to assess self‐harm incidence, mortality, and DALYs across different levels. Incidence estimates were emphasized, as the majority of the self‐harm burden resulted from fatal cases [5]. DALYs are calculated as the combined total of years lived with disability and years of life lost from premature death. Additionally, the Sociodemographic Index (SDI) was incorporated to assess development levels across the 204 countries and territories. The SDI is an index that combines data on income, educational attainment, and fertility rates [16].

Data Presentation and Statistical Analysis

This study assessed the burden of self‐harm within five SDI groups, 21 regions, and 204 countries and territories. The analysis covered age groups including children under five and subsequent 5‐year intervals from ages 5–9 up to 95 years and older. Incidence, mortality, and DALYs were reported as both absolute numbers and age‐standardized rates (ASRs) per 100,000 population, along with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). All analyses were pre‐specified and were conducted in R (version 4.5.0) using the packages ggplot2, dplyr, ggmap, BAPC, and INLA.

Temporal trends in incidence, mortality, and DALYs were evaluated using the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), a standard epidemiological metric for quantifying changes in ASRs over time. Trends were classified according to the EAPC and its 95% confidence interval (CI). An upward trend was defined when both the EAPC value and the lower limit of its 95% CI were above zero, whereas a downward trend was determined when both the EAPC and the upper limit of its 95% CI were below zero [17, 18]. To determine the influence of population growth, aging, and epidemiological transitions on variations in self‐harm burden, decomposition analyses were conducted [19, 20]. Frontier analysis was utilized to estimate the minimum achievable age‐standardized DALYs corresponding to each SDI level, allowing assessment of the relationship between self‐harm burden and sociodemographic development. The effective distance from the frontier was determined by measuring the vertical gap between a country's actual DALY value and the estimated frontier benchmark [21, 22]. The Bayesian Age–Period–Cohort (BAPC) model, utilizing the integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) framework, was employed to project self‐harm incidence, mortality, and DALYs through 2030 [23, 24]. No formal hypothesis tests or p values were used; statistical inference relied on 95% two‐sided uncertainty or confidence intervals around the estimated rates and EAPCs, consistent with the GBD analytical framework.

Results

Global and SDI Regional Trends

In 2021, the global age‐standardized rates per 100,000 population for self‐harm included an incidence rate of 67.89 (95% UI: 56.60, 80.76), a mortality rate of 8.99 (95% UI: 8.34, 9.64), and a DALY rate of 410.19 (95% UI: 383.38, 438.42). From 1990 to 2021, all three indicators declined: the EAPC was −1.42 (95% CI: −1.52, −1.32) for incidence, −1.97 (95% CI: −2.12, −1.82) for mortality, and −1.96 (95% CI: −2.10, −1.81) for DALYs. Table 1 shows that the burden of self‐harm varies by sex and age group. While the EAPC showed a downward trend in both sexes, the reduction was less pronounced among males. Although females exhibited higher levels of self‐harming behavior, males experienced greater mortality and DALY burdens. Age group analysis revealed that the incidence and DALY rates for self‐harm initially rose with age before declining. In 2021, rates peaked in individuals aged 20–24 years, with an incidence of 140.95 (95% UI: 94.77, 202.78) and a DALY rate of 724.36 (95% UI: 666.85–782.46). In contrast, mortality rates consistently increased with advancing age, reaching their highest point at 37.79 (95% UI: 31.80, 42.42) in individuals aged 85–89 years.

Regional and National Trends

Supporting Information S1: Table S1 indicates that, of the 21 regions, high‐income North America recorded the highest age‐standardized incidence rate of self‐harm, with a value of 176.40 (95% UI: 145.0, 210.77). Eastern Europe recorded the highest age‐standardized mortality rate (19.18; 95% UI: 17.55, 20.83) and DALY rate (913.97; 95% UI: 835.2, 994.98), with the Russian Federation and Ukraine bearing the greatest burden within this region. Central Latin America experienced the fastest growth in incidence (EAPC: 0.99; 95% CI: 0.8, 1.09), mortality (EAPC: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.59, 1.02), and DALY rates (EAPC: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.7, 1.21), with Mexico showing the most rapid rise in this region.

In 2021, Greenland showed the highest age‐standardized self‐harm rates among all countries: an incidence rate of 456.61 (95% UI: 410.02, 500.45; Supporting Information S1: Table S1, Figure 1A), a mortality rate of 53.46 (95% UI: 42.96, 63.80; Supporting Information S1: Table S1, Figure 1B), and a DALY rate of 2687.61 (95% UI: 2112.87, 3211.29; Supporting Information S1: Table S1, Figure 1C). Lesotho showed the most rapid growth in mortality (EAPC: 2.68; 95% CI: 2.14, 3.23; Supporting Information S1: Table S1, Figure S1B) and DALY rates (EAPC: 2.97; 95% CI: 2.40, 3.54; Supporting Information S1: Table S1, Figure S1C).

Burden Associated With Available Risk Factors

The analysis identified positive GBD‐attributable estimates for high alcohol use, drug use, and high temperature, and negative estimates for low temperature. Among individuals aged 20 years and above, high alcohol use showed the largest positive GBD‐attributable DALY rates, particularly among men, although the estimates decreased progressively with age. Drug use estimates were most prominent among individuals aged 20–45 years. The positive GBD‐attributable DALY rates for high temperature were greater in men than in women. In contrast, low temperature showed negative age‐standardized GBD‐attributable DALY rates, with estimates of −24.78 and −10.78 per 100,000 population in men and women, respectively (Figure 2).

Decomposition Analysis of Change in Age‐Standardized DALYs

Over the past 30 years, self‐harm DALYs have decreased globally and in regions with middle and high‐middle SDI, primarily driven by improvements in epidemiological factors. In contrast, while epidemiological advancements helped mitigate the burden in high, low, and low‐middle SDI regions, the pronounced influence of population growth in these areas led to a net increase in DALYs (Figure 3). At the national level, population growth was the main driver of the rising DALYs in countries with the largest increases, including India, the United States of America, Pakistan, and Brazil. Additionally, population aging played a disproportionately large role in the DALY increases in certain countries, such as the Northern Mariana Islands, Cyprus, Nauru, and Morocco. Although epidemiological changes contributed to the increase in DALYs in some countries, such as Guyana, Greece, and Uruguay, they largely offset the upward trends in most other countries. Frontier Analysis of the Relationship Between Optimal DALYs and SDI

In 2021, the effective distances from the efficiency frontier, which represent the gap between observed and minimum achievable burdens for countries with similar SDI levels, varied significantly. For DALYs attributable to self‐harm, the countries with the largest distances from the frontier were Greenland (2646.73), Guyana (1444.05), Nauru (1295.82), Lesotho (1063.56), and the Marshall Islands (1050.18). As SDI increased, these gaps tended to become more pronounced, suggesting greater potential for burden reduction relative to the estimated frontier among higher‐SDI settings (Figure 4 and Supporting Information S1: Table S3).

Projections of Self‐Harm up to 2030

Using the INLA framework and BAPC method, we projected trends in the global self‐harm burden through 2030 (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). Under a continuation‐of‐recent‐trends scenario, the age‐standardized incidence rate was projected to increase by 32.4% from 2021 to 2030, reaching 89.88 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 78.79, 100.97) in 2030. In contrast, the age‐standardized DALY rate was projected to decrease by 7.9% over the same period, reaching 377.64 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 337.94, 417.34) in 2030. For SDG monitoring, the age‐standardized mortality rate was compared with the 2015 baseline and was projected to decline by 11.8% by 2030, reaching 8.77 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 7.83, 9.71).

Discussion

This study presents an in‐depth analysis of global, regional, and national trends in the burden of self‐harm from 1990 to 2021, with projections extending to 2030. By offering location‐, sex‐, and age‐specific benchmarks, the findings support monitoring progress toward SDG Target 3.4.2 and provide a broader, more in‐depth estimate of the self‐harm burden to date.

Global and Regional Trends in Context

The global burden of self‐harm, measured by incidence, mortality, and DALYs, has steadily decreased over the past three decades, likely due to improved mental health awareness and progress in intervention approaches [25]. However, the pace of reduction has varied significantly across regions with different sociodemographic levels. High‐SDI regions have shown the highest burden and the slowest declines in age‐standardized incidence, mortality, and DALY rates, potentially driven by factors such as intense social competition, work‐related stress, and substance abuse [4]. Meanwhile, low‐SDI regions also show high mortality and DALY burdens, and the pace of decline is slower than in other regions. The limited progress observed may be due to barriers such as underdeveloped healthcare systems and a lack of mental health education, both of which diminish the effectiveness of interventions [7].

Our findings highlight distinct regional challenges contributing to the self‐harm burden. Eastern Europe experiences persistently high mortality and DALY rates, likely driven by historical socio‐political instability and high alcohol consumption [4, 26]. In Latin America, rising self‐harm rates are associated with socio‐economic inequalities, limited access to healthcare, and increased availability of means of self‐harm [27]. Greenland, which had the highest self‐harm burden, faces challenges from alcohol abuse, rapid societal transitions, and geographical inequalities [28]. Similarly, Lesotho has seen a sharp rise in self‐harm mortality and DALYs due to poverty, unemployment, social isolation, and gender‐based violence [29]. These findings emphasize the necessity for tailored strategies that address regional differences. For example, in Greenland, culturally relevant, community‐based interventions that strengthen intergenerational bonds and foster cultural identity may help reduce self‐harm [30].

Burden by Sex, Age, and Risk Factors

Sex differences in self‐harm prevalence and outcomes have remained consistent over decades. Females report higher rates of suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts, likely related to higher rates of affective disorders and psychological distress [31, 32]. However, males exhibit significantly higher suicide mortality, partly due to the use of more lethal means and the influence of societal norms discouraging emotional vulnerability and help‐seeking behavior [33]. Addressing these disparities requires a cultural and societal shift to reduce stigma, promote emotional expression, and improve access to mental health services for males [34]. Young people aged 20–29 bear the highest burden of self‐harm incidence and DALYs, likely due to transitional life stages, heightened vulnerability to stressors, and physiological and psychological changes [7, 35]. Digital therapeutics, tailored to their needs and interests, may offer innovative solutions to address mental health challenges in this group [36, 37]. For older adults aged 70 years and above, high self‐harm mortality is associated with psychiatric disorders, chronic pain, social isolation, and bereavement [38]. Integrating geriatric mental health screening into chronic disease management and addressing physical‐pain comorbidities may help reduce their burden [39].

Consistent with previous studies, alcohol and drug use showed positive GBD‐attributable DALY estimates for self‐harm, particularly among young and middle‐aged males [4]. This pattern may be related to the effects of substance use on neurological and psychological functioning, such as impaired executive function and decision‐making, which may increase impulsivity and the likelihood of self‐harm [40, 41]. Consequently, reducing alcohol and drug use may be an effective strategy for self‐harm prevention [42]. Temperature‐related associations were also observed. High temperature showed a positive association with self‐harm DALYs in the GBD comparative risk assessment, consistent with epidemiological evidence that suicide risk tends to increase with ambient temperature [43, 44]. Conversely, the negative GBD‐attributable DALY estimates for low temperature should be interpreted as a model‐derived inverse association within the same GBD framework, rather than definitive evidence of a causal protective effect [4, 14]. As climate change intensifies temperature extremes, clarifying temperature‐mental health pathways is important for prevention and resource planning [44].

Drivers of Change: Decomposition and Frontier Analysis

Decomposition analysis identified population aging and growth as major contributors to increases in DALYs, reflecting a demographic transition consistent with global trends, where these factors play an increasingly significant role in shaping disease burdens [45]. For countries where population growth predominantly drives the increasing burden, including India, the United States, Pakistan, and Brazil, targeted investments are crucial. These nations could focus on strengthening health‐system capacity and implementing age‐responsive prevention and support strategies, particularly for younger populations [46, 47]. In contrast, countries burdened by aging must adopt a comprehensive approach that includes social isolation prevention and psychological health support to meet the complex and growing needs of elderly individuals [9]. In countries such as Guyana, Greece, and Uruguay, where increases in DALYs were predominantly driven by epidemiological factors, prioritizing the reduction of health inequities and targeting modifiable risks is essential.

As sociodemographic development increases, differences in the self‐harm burden tend to widen. High‐SDI countries, such as the Republic of Korea, Japan, Canada, Lithuania, and the United States, showed large effective distances from the frontier, indicating potential to reduce the burden relative to countries with similar SDI levels. However, these gaps should not be interpreted solely as health‐system underperformance, as self‐harm burden is shaped by cultural attitudes, societal expectations, social stressors, and gaps in mental health care policies [48]. For instance, persistent stigma, work‐related stress, and inadequate mental health support may contribute to high suicide rates in Korea and Japan [49, 50]. In contrast, countries near the efficiency frontier, such as Somalia and Niger, may reflect the role of community‐led interventions and informal support systems, even in the absence of robust formal healthcare infrastructure [51, 52]. These findings suggest that proximity to the frontier can help identify potential room for improvement, but frontier analysis is a descriptive benchmarking approach and cannot determine whether gaps are driven by health‐system, cultural, social, or measurement‐related factors.

Projected Trends and Incidence‐Mortality Divergence

The projection analysis showed a divergence between age‐standardized incidence and mortality trends. Although the age‐standardized incidence rate was projected to increase from 2021 to 2030, the age‐standardized mortality rate was projected to decline from the 2015 SDG baseline to 2030. This pattern may reflect a shift in the balance between self‐harm occurrence and fatality, rather than a uniform worsening across all outcomes. Epidemiologically, such divergence can occur when recorded non‐fatal self‐harm increases while deaths decline, potentially because of changes in the case‐fatality profile, including reduced access to highly lethal methods [53]. However, because BAPC projections extrapolate recent age‐period‐cohort patterns, the projected incidence increase may also be influenced by recent‐period fluctuations and pandemic‐related changes in help‐seeking thresholds or health‐service presentations following self‐harm [24, 54]. Therefore, this dissociation should be interpreted cautiously, but it highlights that monitoring suicide mortality alone may underestimate the future service burden of self‐harm.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several key strengths. First, it presents a comprehensive and in‐depth analysis of the self‐harm disease burden from 1990 to 2021, highlighting differences among regions, age groups, and genders, as well as examining GBD‐attributable estimates for available factors. Second, the burden was analyzed by attributing changes to population growth, aging, and epidemiological transitions, providing insight into the drivers of change. Third, the study establishes efficiency frontiers, allowing for benchmarking of country‐level burden relative to sociodemographic development and identifying areas for potential improvement. The findings are valuable for regional authorities to develop targeted action plans, allocate resources effectively, and monitor progress toward achieving the 2030 SDG goals.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, self‐harm burden may be underestimated, particularly for non‐fatal events, because routine health and vital registration systems can miss cases, stigma may discourage disclosure and care seeking [55], and injuries may be misclassified, especially between self‐harm and undetermined intent in vital registration systems. Although GBD estimates include uncertainty intervals, our BAPC projections did not additionally quantify uncertainty from underreporting, stigma‐related nondisclosure, or misclassification. Therefore, projected incidence should be interpreted as GBD‐modeled incidence under current assumptions, rather than a fully bias‐corrected estimate of true incidence. Second, analyses of available GBD‐attributable factors are limited by the exposures currently quantified within the GBD comparative risk assessment framework. Some established determinants of self‐harm, such as childhood adversity, access to means, social isolation, and a broader spectrum of mental health conditions, may not be fully captured, which may lead to an incomplete characterization of self‐harm determinants in our study. Moreover, as these estimates are based on population‐level attributable fractions, they should not be interpreted as individual‐level causal effects or used for individual risk prediction. Finally, BAPC projections assume that recent trends in demography and health patterns will continue. As such, they cannot anticipate abrupt changes driven by future pandemics, economic shocks, conflicts, or major policy shifts that could alter self‐harm trajectories. Therefore, the projections should be interpreted as scenario‐based forecasts under current trends rather than definitive predictions, while still providing useful guidance for planning.

Clinical Implications and Future Direction

Our findings have important implications for both policy and practice. Achieving SDG Target 3.4‐related goals for suicide mortality reduction by 2030 may require urgent and accelerated action, as current projections suggest that progress remains insufficient. High‐SDI regions could prioritize optimizing resource allocation, enforcing means restriction, and addressing societal pressures [56, 57]. Meanwhile, low‐ and low‐middle SDI regions may benefit from strengthening mental health services, expanding social support systems, and scaling up community‐based interventions [3]. Tailored strategies for different sexes and age groups, such as promoting emotional expression and mental health access for males, implementing digital therapeutics for young people, and preventing social isolation for older adults, may help reduce the self‐harm burden. Addressing substance misuse and the mental health impacts of climate change should also be considered in prevention planning. Furthermore, population growth and aging underscore the importance of targeted investments in healthcare infrastructure and support systems to meet the changing needs of various regions.

Conclusion

To conclude, this study provides important perspectives on both current trends and future projections related to the burden of self‐harm, deepening our understanding of its changing global and local patterns. These findings can support informed policy‐making and resource allocation for this significant yet often neglected issue. Achieving meaningful reductions in the self‐harm burden and advancing global health goals may require tailored, evidence‐based, and scalable strategies that take into account local healthcare systems and cultural contexts.
