Tilia Tomentosa promotes sleep, preserving sleep structure
Authors: Antonio Di Soccio, Martina Canova, Nicola Rizzo, Claudia Lodovichi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTilia extracts have been used for centuries for their relaxing and hypnotic properties; however, their impact on the sleep-wake cycle and sleep architecture remained elusive. In this study, we investigated the effects of a chronic treatment with Tilia tomentosa extracts on the sleep–wake cycle and the associated neuronal activity, using chronic electrophysiological recordings in freely moving mice. We found that Tilia treatment induced a significant increase in NREM sleep and a concomitant reduction of wakefulness during the nocturnal (active) hours, in treated mice compared to controls. Furthermore, the treatment significantly reduced brief awakenings during light (resting) hours. Tilia did not cause alterations of the essential electrophysiological features of the EEG signal. Moreover, Tilia administration did not impair either the homeostasis or the structure of the sleep-wake cycle. Spectral analysis reported mild changes in high gamma and theta bands in Tilia-treated mice compared to controls. Altogether, our data provide preliminary evidence that Tilia favours sleep and reduces sleep fragmentation, without interfering with the physiological structure of sleep and the associated neuronal activity.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Physiology, Zoology

Introduction

Sleep and anxiety disorders are among the most frequent symptoms in several psychiatric and neurological disorders, affecting a large portion of the population. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that 970 million individuals worldwide present anxiety–related symptoms and experience disturbances in sleep (WHO, 2022). These symptoms are bidirectionally connected as anxiety can disrupt sleep, and poor sleep exacerbates anxiety1. From a broader perspective, sleep is a key physiological process required for the regulation of overall health and well-being2–5. Impaired sleep is accompanied by cognitive dysfunctions, increased irritability and anxiety, along with negative emotional states that compromise decision-making ability, reduce work productivity and increase the risk of accidents6,7. The considerable burden of symptoms associated with the lack of sleep has encouraged the use of drugs which favour relaxation and sleep, despite their potential adverse effects, especially with long -term use8,9. In this context, to promote complementary and alternative therapeutic approaches, the WHO has, since 2002, developed and launched the Traditional Medicine Strategy to support the use of medicinal plants and plant-derived compounds in maintaining and enhancing human health and well-being.

Among the plants employed as remedies for human ailments, Tilia extracts (from different Tilia species) present a wide range of properties10,11, and have been employed for centuries in traditional medicine for their relaxing and somnogenic effects. Their therapeutic efficacy and widespread use have prompted numerous studies in mice aimed at elucidating the underlying mechanisms of action.

Behavioural studies showed that mice treated with Tilia maintained normal locomotor activity but exhibited a significant increase in immobility time, reduced rearing and other behavioural features consistent with sedative effects12–14. In addition, extracts and isolated flavonoid fractions from different species of Tilia produced anxiolytic-like effects on mice tested with the elevated plus maze. In this behavioural task, treated mice spent more time in, and made more frequent crossings of the open arms—an area that typically induces fear or discomfort in mice under normal circumstances15. In addition, Tilia extracts induced an increase in head-digging in the hole board test and exploration time in the open field test16. Altogether, these findings suggest that treated mice exhibited reduced levels of anxiety, which resulted in increased exploratory behaviour compared to control, non-treated animals.

Regarding the mechanisms underlying the effects of Tilia extracts, it has been reported that they can exert at least part of their action by modulating gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) transmission17. GABA, the major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the mature brain, operates via GABAA receptors to trigger fast inhibition, which has a key role in modulating the inhibitory tone, the excitation-inhibition balance and correlated activity among neurons18. Importantly, GABA is also essential in regulating the sleep-wake cycle and sleep architecture19.

The molecular mechanism underlying the anxiolytic-like effects of Tilia has been investigated using electrophysiological recordings20. In their study, Allio et al. showed that acute application of Tilia extracts to hippocampal neurons in culture activated a chloride influx comparable to that elicited by the activation of GABAA receptors. This effect was largely blocked by bicuculline and picrotoxin, while a minor component was prevented by the benzodiazepine antagonist flumazenil, suggesting that Tilia extracts may act via GABAA receptor-activation20. In line with these observations, several studies have proposed that flavonoids present in numerous plants, including Tilia, may act as ligands of GABAA receptors13,17,21.

Despite the evidence of the relaxing and somnogenic effects attributed to Tilia extracts, their influence on the sleep–wake cycle, sleep structure, and the underlying effect on EEG spectral composition remains poorly understood.

Here, we analysed the impact of a diet enriched with Tilia tomentosa extracts on the sleep–wake cycle and on the neuronal correlates of the distinct behavioural states (wakefulness, rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, and non-REM (NREM) sleep). By combining chronic electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings in freely moving mice treated with Tilia tomentosa extracts, with quantitative analyses of sleep–wake architecture and EEG spectral composition, we found that Tilia treatment resulted in a significant increase in NREM sleep, mostly during the dark (active) hours, along with a reduced incidence of brief awakenings in the light (resting) phase. Spectral analysis of the EEG signal of treated mice revealed a decrease in gamma power during wakefulness and a slight, not significant, increase in spectral power in the theta band during REM sleep.

Results

Tilia treatment increased the amount of NREM sleep, during the nocturnal phase

To investigate the effects of Tilia extracts on sleep parameters, mice were fed a Tilia-enriched diet for 3 weeks, followed by continuous EEG recordings over 5 days of treatment. Two chronic electrodes were placed in the frontal and parietal cortex and the signal was recorded for 5 days in an open arena in a quiet environment and subjected to a regular daily cycle with light on at 6 AM and off at 6 PM. The treatment did not alter the key electrophysiological features of wake, NREM or REM sleep (Fig. 1a, b), allowing for accurate scoring of sleep-wake episodes (also indicated as behavioural states). Wakefulness was characterised by low-amplitude, high-frequency activity and irregular EEG signal; NREM sleep presented slow wave activity (SWA) characterised by high-amplitude and slow-frequency waves, while REM sleep exhibited a considerable reduction of SWA and an increase in low-amplitude activity typically dominated by theta waves (Fig. 1a–d).

We first analysed the effects of Tilia on the sleep-wake cycle across the 24 h. We observed that during the light phase (indicated as Zeitgeber Time ZT 0-11), that is the resting period for nocturnal animals such as mice, the time course and percentage of wakefulness and NREM sleep were comparable in treated and control mice. In the period surrounding lights off (ZT 12), wakefulness increased rapidly in controls and persisted throughout the dark phase (ZT 12-23), as expected in nocturnal animals. Concomitantly, NREM sleep was reduced (Fig. 2a). Treated animals exhibited a similar temporal profile of these behavioural states; however, they showed a smaller increase in wakefulness and a less pronounced reduction in NREM sleep during the same period compared to controls (Figs. 2a and S1). Notably, around ZT 20, treated mice displayed a reversal of this pattern, with a higher proportion of NREM sleep relative to wakefulness (Figs. 2a and S1). The time course and percentage of REM sleep did not differ between the two groups of mice (Fig. 2a, c, see below).

Quantitative analysis of the percentage of time spent in each behavioural state over a 24 h period revealed a significant reduction in wakefulness accompanied by a corresponding increase in NREM sleep in treated mice relative to controls (Wake Control mice (Cnt) median = 52.8, Tilia-treated mice (Tilia), median = 47.2, p = 0.01, r = 0.83; NREM Cnt median = 40.1; Tilia median = 46.5, p = 0.01, r = 0.83 Fig. 2b). The amount of REM sleep was not significantly affected by the treatment (REM Cnt median = 6.8, Tilia median = 6.6, Fig. 2b). To assess whether Tilia’s effects varied across phases of the circadian cycle, we analysed the percentage of the different behavioural states during the light period (ZT 0-11) and dark period (ZT 12-23), separately (Fig. 2c). During the light phase (ZT 0-11) no differences were observed in any behavioural states between control and Tilia treated mice (Wake Cnt median = 46.2, Tilia median=46.4, p = 0.90; NREM Cnt median = 45.2, Tilia median = 47.3, p = 0.90; REM Cnt median = 9.4; Tilia median = 8.3, p = 0.35; Fig. 2c). However, during the dark phase (ZT 12-23) Tilia-treated mice exhibited a statistically significant reduction in wakefulness and a corresponding increase in NREM sleep (Wake Cnt median = 59.8; Tilia median = 54.8, p = 0.02, r = 0.72; NREM Cnt median = 34.3; Tilia=43.6, p = 0.01, r = 0.83; Fig. 2c). No significant differences were observed in REM sleep between the two groups (REM Cnt median = 4.6; Tilia median = 4.8, p = 0.60; Fig. 2c).

Tilia treatment reduced sleep fragmentation during the light (resting) phase

The quality of sleep depends not only on the total amount of sleep but also on its structure, i.e. if and how it is fragmented. To further characterise the sleep-wake structure, we computed the bout length of each behavioural state. A bout was defined as a sequence of at least four consecutive 4-s epochs in the same behavioural state. Therefore, the bout length refers to the number of consecutive epochs within a given state. Analysis of this parameter showed no statistically significant differences in bout length for any behavioural state between control and treated mice (Bout length expressed as the number of 4 s episodes ZT 0-11, Wake Cnt median = 25.9; Tilia median = 26.4, p = 0.38; NREM Cnt median=18.5, Tilia median = 25.3, p = 0.23; REM Cnt median = 11.1; Tilia median = 16.3, p = 0.13; ZT 12-23, Wake Cnt median = 66.1, Tilia median = 41.7, p = 0.13; NREM Cnt median = 34.5; NREM Tilia median = 33.9, p = 0.46; REM Cnt median = 14.9, REM Tilia = 17.5, p = 0.06, Fig. 2d, e).

Another key contributor to sleep fragmentation is the occurrence of brief awakenings, defined as episodes of wakefulness lasting less than three consecutive 4-s epochs and occurring within NREM sleep bouts of at least 1 min in duration. Such episodes are often accompanied by small body movements. Analysis of brief awakenings revealed that Tilia-treated mice showed a significantly lower number of such events across the light phase (ZT 0-11) compared to control mice (number of brief awakenings per minute ZT 0-11, Cnt median = 0.26, Tilia median = 0.095, p = 0.03, Fig. 2f, g). During the dark phase (ZT 12-23), the number of brief awakenings was very low (approximately half of that observed during the light phase, in controls) and it remained low in Tilia-treated mice, with no significant differences between the two groups (Cnt median = 0.12, Tilia median = 0.10, p = 0.46 Fig. 2f, g). These data suggested that Tilia treatment favoured less fragmented sleep during the resting phase.

Decreased gamma power in wakefulness in Tilia treated mice

To assess the impact of Tilia treatment on the spectral composition of the electrophysiological signals associated to the sleep-wake states, we recorded the EEG from the frontal and the parietal lobe of each mouse. At first, we calculated the Power Spectral Densities (PSDs) in each channel for each behavioural state, across the 24 h. We observed a mild but significant decrease in the high gamma band power during wakefulness in animals treated with Tilia compared to controls, in both parietal and frontal lobes (Wake, frontal EEG, p ≤ 0.05; parietal EEG,, p ≤ 0.05, Figs. 3 and S2). No differences in normalized spectral power were observed between groups during NREM and REM sleep (p > 0.05, Figs. 3 and S2).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effects of a Tilia tomentosa extract administered in the diet on the sleep–wake cycle and on the EEG recorded during wakefulness, NREM and REM sleep. By using chronic EEG recordings in freely moving mice combined with behavioural scoring and spectral EEG analysis, we found that Tilia treatment resulted in a significant increase in NREM sleep and a concomitant reduction in wakefulness during the nocturnal (active) phase (ZT 12-23), along with reduced incidence of brief awakenings during the light (resting) phase (ZT 0-11).

Spectral analysis revealed only modest state-dependent changes in power distribution. Tilia treatment was associated with a decrease in gamma band power during wakefulness and a slight, not significant increase in theta band power during REM sleep. These spectral modifications were more consistently observed during the dark phase (ZT 12-23). Importantly, these data show that Tilia does not negatively impact the main electrophysiological signatures of deep sleep since Slow Wave Activity (SWA, 0.5–4 Hz) is not affected by the treatment.

Rodents, unlike humans, display a polyphasic sleep pattern characterised by recurrent alternations between sleep and wakefulness across the 24-h cycle, with predominant activity during the dark hours and rest during the light hours. Within this physiological framework, we found that the structure of wake, NREM and REM sleep bouts remained unaffected, supporting the idea that Tilia promotes sleep without altering the intrinsic sleep architecture. The reduced incidence of brief awakenings during the resting period contributed to improved sleep continuity. The observed effects are consistent with the action of Tilia extracts on GABA receptors, as previously reported in cultured hippocampal neurons20, which may result in an enhanced inhibitory tone and decreased neuronal excitability. Flavonoids, contained in several plants, including Tilia, are reported to favour sleep and relaxation by activating GABAA receptors17,22. Flavonoids could also promote sleep via their antioxidant properties. Indeed, a bidirectional relationship between sleep and oxidative stress has recently emerged23,24. Further investigations are warranted to clarify the circuit- and cellular-level mechanisms through which Tilia extracts promote sleep.

It is worth noticing that most of the Tilia effects we observed on the sleep – wake cycle (Fig. 2) and on the EEG signal associated with the different behavioural states, i.e. wakefulness, NREM and REM sleep (Figs. 3 and 4), occurred mostly during the dark (active) phase, suggesting that the effects of Tilia are time-of-day dependent. Further investigations are required to determine whether these effects reflect a phase-dependent modulation of Tilia’s action and /or are related to the level of activity of the animals.

The GABAergic inhibitory tone is critically regulated by the intracellular Chloride concentration, that has been shown to be modulated along the 24 h25,26, in the occipital cortex of mice. Intracellular Chloride concentration was shown to be low at midday (resulting in high inhibition) and high at midnight, resulting in a lower inhibitory drive25. Although it remains to be demonstrated whether the circadian regulation of intracellular Chloride concentration applies to other brain areas, it is tempting to speculate that the predominantly nocturnal effects of Tilia may be consistent with the diurnal regulation of chloride, potentially increasing inhibitory drive during the phase of the day (ZT11-23) when inhibition is dampened.

The predominant nocturnal effects of Tilia extracts may offer translational advantages, particularly in contexts where reducing hyperactivity and promoting relaxation during the active phase is desirable.

Remarkably, this selective action is accompanied by a minimal impact on the electrophysiological signature of the wake and sleep phases.

Human beings frequently encounter situations in which elevated arousal interferes with cognitive performance27,28. Therefore, interventions that reduce arousal and enhance relaxation could potentially improve attention and cognitive functions. Another advantage of Tilia extracts is their apparent lack of side effects, in contrast to conventional drugs with tranquilizing and sleep-inducing action, such as benzodiazepine (BDZ), which also act on GABA receptors but are often associated with side effects, such as cognitive impairment, impaired coordination, tolerance, dependence, and withdrawal symptoms29–31. Numerous studies, conducted both in humans and animals, have shown that BDZs facilitate sleep but also alter EEG activity and sleep structure.

Studies conducted in humans have shown that BDZs reduce sleep latency32, but also suppress SWA during NREM sleep and increase fast-frequency activity, such as sleep spindles33,34. The strong impact of these drugs is underscored by the observation that for some compounds of the BDZ family, the suppression of SWA persisted into the subsequent drug-free night, whereas spindle effects were over the day after33. It has also been reported that BDZs decreased REM sleep35.

In mice and rats, BDZs such as diazepam decreased SWA in NREM sleep, increased high-frequency activity36–40 and suppressed REM sleep36,37. A recent study in mice reported that diazepam not only decreased slow wave amplitude, but it also reduced the incidence and duration of OFF periods, which indicate recurrent periods in which neurons are silent40. In addition, suppression of SWA in NREM sleep following diazepam administration appears to be particularly strong, since it is insensitive to the photoperiod, which has been shown to affect sleep and the associated EEG signal41.

Diazepam was shown to slow oscillatory activity across all frequency domains in freely moving mice42, and exerted distinct effects on specific EEG frequency domains43.

Altogether, these findings suggest that while BDZs facilitate sleep onset, they significantly alter the network activity underlying NREM and REM sleep, resulting in a sleep state that differs from the physiological one. These alterations may also contribute to the cognitive impairments often reported by individuals with insomnia who rely on BDZs to manage sleep disturbances44–46. Moreover, BDZs are highly addictive, and patients using them regularly, often develop tolerance and significant withdrawal symptoms47–50, which can lead to misuse. Because of these limitations, their use in the management of chronic sleep disturbances is generally discouraged.

In this context, Tilia offers a valid alternative as a relaxing and somnogenic agent. In our study, chronic Tilia treatment promoted sleep without major alterations of the EEG rhythms. Spectral analysis revealed a significant reduction in gamma-band power during wakefulness (Fig. 4) in Tilia-treated mice, likely reflecting decreased correlated neuronal activity. This is consistent with Allio et al., who reported suppression of spontaneous synchronous activity in cultured hippocampal neurons following Tilia exposure. More broadly, reduced neuronal synchrony has been linked to decreased gamma power across multiple brain regions51–54. Additionally, a slight increase in theta-band activity during REM sleep was observed, although it did not reach statistical significance. This trend may be consistent with greater synchronized neuronal activity during this important sleep phase. Indeed, theta bursts during REM sleep have been linked to facilitation and consolidation of cognitive processes55,56 related to enhanced cortico-hippocampal communication57. Noteworthy, SWA during NREM sleep remained stable, further indicating that Tilia does not impair the restorative features of deep sleep, unlike BDZs, which reduced, or even suppressed, slow-wave power during NREM sleep in humans32–34 and rodents36–40 (see above). Furthermore, up to now, no evidence has been reported regarding addiction associated with Tilia extracts.

Several plants with reported relaxing and hypnotic effects, including chamomile, passionflower, and valerian, have been primarily investigated in clinical studies, where they appear to improve sleep quality. However, their mechanisms of action, as well as their effects on EEG activity and sleep architecture, remain poorly understood58,59, largely due to the lack of preclinical studies that would allow the dissection of their effects at the circuit and cellular levels.

Findings similar to those observed in the present study have been reported following treatment with other plant extracts, namely Centella asiatica extracts60, administered through the diet in aging mice (18 months old). EEG recordings in aged mice showed that C. Asiatica treatment increased REM theta power and reduced arousal during sleep60. In contrast to our findings, Dovek et al.60 also reported an increase in REM sleep and a sex bias toward aged female mice. These differences may be attributed to variations in age and in the compounds used.

The present study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the relatively small sample size limits the statistical power to detect subtle effects and precludes a systematic assessment of sex-specific differences. Accordingly, the present findings should be regarded as exploratory and aimed at identifying coherent patterns of behavioural and electrophysiological changes associated with Tilia supplementation.

Collectively, our findings suggest that Tilia acts as a relaxing and somnogenic agent that—overall—preserves the physiological architecture of sleep and EEG spectral profiles. These results extend and corroborate previous behavioural and in vitro studies and provide a contribution in support of the potential use of Tilia for managing conditions such as restlessness, hyperactivity and insomnia. By performing chronic EEG recordings in freely moving mice, we reported the effects of Tilia extracts on the sleep-wake cycle architecture and on the associated EEG signal, but we had limited possibilities to dissect the underlying mechanisms. Further studies are needed to elucidate the neuronal mechanisms underlying the observed status-dependent changes in power across different frequency bands. Such studies will require combining recordings of single-cell activity (including synaptic activity and action potentials) with population-level recordings, such as local field potentials.

Methods

Animals

Male and female C57BL/6J mice (control, n = 5; Tilia treated mice, n = 5, about 8 weeks of age) were housed in filtered cages in a temperature-controlled room with a 12/12-h dark/light cycle (light on at 6 AM, which corresponds to 0 ZT). All procedures conformed to the EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments and to the ARRIVE guidelines. Experimental protocols were approved by the Italian Ministry of Health. All analyses were performed blind to the experimental condition.

Dietary regimen

Mice were divided into 2 groups: (1) one group had access to 4 g/day of standard food; (2) the other group was fed 4 g/day food enriched in Tilia’s extracts. The enriched diet was prepared by supplementing standard food with fluid extracts of Tilia tomentosa at a concentration of 0.78 ml of extract per gram of food, corresponding to 3.12 ml of fluid extract per daily ration (4 g). Animals were fed the Tilia-enriched diet for 3 consecutive weeks. Both groups had access to ad libitum water.

Surgical procedure

After 16 days of treatment, animals were anesthetized with a mixture of Zoletil 100 (a combination of Zolazepam and Tiletamin, 1:1, 10 mg/kg; Virbac, Carros, Cedex, France) and Rompun (Xylazine 2%, 0.06 mL/kg; Bio98, Milan, Italy) and placed into a custom stereotaxic frame. Two small holes (~1.2 mm diameter) were made in the skull to allow the insertion of two iron-plated, round-tipped miniature screws that served as EEG electrodes. Electrodes were placed over the parietal lobe (3 mm lateral to midline, 2 mm posterior to bregma) and the frontal lobe (1.5 mm lateral to midline, 1.5 mm anterior to bregma). A third screw, used as a reference electrode, was fixed on the skull above the cerebellum (2 mm posterior to lambda, on the midline). Two small stainless steel wires (Advent, Stock No: FE635011) were inserted into the neck muscles and served to record the electromyogram (EMG). Electrodes were soldered to stainless steel wires and secured to the skull with dental cement (Paladur, AgnTho’s, Lidingö, Sweden). Mice were immediately treated with post-operative analgesia (Tramadol 10 mg/kg, Formevet, Milan, Italy). Upon waking up from the anaesthesia, animals were placed in individual cages for 5 days to allow complete recovery before initiation of EEG recordings.

EEG acquisition and analysis

The electroencephalographic signal was recorded using the Micromed Brain Quick LTM Holter EEG system (Micromed, Mogliano Veneto, Italy) for 5 days. Data were sampled at 256 Hz. Mice were free to move in a plexiglass arena (35 cm × 35 cm × 40 cm) placed within a Faraday cage. Before the beginning of the recording session, mice were habituated to the apparatus for about 30 min. The three behavioural states - wake, NREM and REM sleep - were determined for 4-s epochs by visual inspection of the EEG and EMG signals using the Sirenia Sleep software (Pinnacle Technology). Epochs containing artifacts in any one of the recorded channels were identified by visual inspection and excluded from subsequent analyses. After the manual scoring, the recorded signal, together with the information about the behavioural states, was imported into MATLAB (MathWorks Inc.) and further analysed. We considered ‘bouts’ as periods of at least three consequent 4-s epochs that mice spent in a single behavioural state. PSD of EEG was computed over each epoch time interval with Welch’s method, choosing a Hann window with a length of 0.5 s and with 0.25 s overlap between windows. To enable normalization across mice, the PSD of all states was normalized to the mean of the first five frequency points of the awake state.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB. Given the relatively small sample size, group comparisons were conducted using a non-parametric two-sided Mann–Whitney U test, and significance was set at p < 0.05. Effect size (r) was calculated from the z statistic, and 0.3 ≤ r < 0.5 = medium effect; r ≥ 0.5 = large effect.

EEG spectral analysis was performed across all frequencies, and the p values returned by the Mann–Whitney U test is a function of frequency. Spectral analysis was performed in distinct behavioural states and different day periods, namely dark hours (zeitgeber, ZT 12-23) and light hours (ZT 0-11). Spectral power values were averaged within animals prior to group-level statistical comparisons. All statistical tests were two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests, and significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

The study was designed as an exploratory investigation; therefore, no formal a priori power calculations were performed.
