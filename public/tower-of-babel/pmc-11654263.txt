Serial multiple mediation of psychological capital and self-directed learning ability in the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity among undergraduate nursing students in China: a cross-sectional questionnaire survey
Authors: Xiu-Juan Feng, Xiao-Hui Chai, Huan Liu, Xiang-Xiang Li, Chun-Ni Heng, Jing-Hui Tong, Wen-Kai Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProfessional identity has a positive impact on the career development, job satisfaction, and job performance of undergraduate nursing students, and it is also an important foundation for their future nursing careers. Therefore, it is necessary to explore the influencing factors of professional identity and the inherent relationships between these factors.ObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the serial multiple mediating effect of psychological capital and self-directed learning ability in the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity among undergraduate nursing students.MethodsAll participants (n = 596) were collected using an online survey between October 2023 to January 2024 through cluster sampling. Measures of perceived stress, psychological capital, self-directed learning ability, and professional identity were assessed. The serial multiple mediation of psychological capital and self-directed learning ability in the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity was examined using the SPSS PROCESS 3.4 macro by Hayes. Data analysis included descriptive analysis, independent samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA, Pearson's correlation, ordinary least-squares regression, and the bootstrap method.ResultsProfessional identity was negatively correlated with perceived stress (r = -0.414, p < 0.001), and was positively correlated with psychological capital (r = 0.495, p < 0.001) and self-directed learning ability (r = 0.443, p < 0.001). In the model of perceived stress → psychological capital → self-directed learning ability → professional identity, the overall mediated effect was -0.567 (95% CI: -0.707 ~ -0.443), and the mediating effect of psychological capital was -0.365 (95% CI: -0.501 ~ -0.237), accounting for 46.09% of total effect. The mediating effect of self-directed learning ability was -0.083 (95% CI: -0.150 ~ -0.028), accounting for 10.48% of total effect. The serial mediating effect of psychological capital and self-directed learning ability was -0.120 (95% CI: -0.184 ~ -0.065), accounting for 15.15% of total effect.ConclusionsPsychological capital and self-directed learning ability serve as chain mediators between perceived stress and professional identity among undergraduate nursing students. This indicates that nursing educators can enhance the psychological capital and self-directed learning ability of nursing students to reduce the adverse effects of perceived stress on their professional identity.Keywords: Psychological capital, Self-directed learning ability, Perceived stress, Nursing, Students, Professional identity, Serial-multiple mediation model

Introduction

With the accelerated process of population aging, the shortage of nurses has become one of the pressing issues that the whole country needs to face and solve [1]. As a populous country, China experiences significant pressure regarding nurse demand, highlighting the nurse shortage. Undergraduate nursing students are a crucial reserve in the nursing workforce, and their career choices play a vital role in addressing this shortage. In recent years, researchers have conducted in-depth studies on the factors influencing the career choices of nursing students. Research has found that professional identity plays a crucial role in the career choices of nursing students in the future [2, 3]. Professional identity of nursing students refers to their degree of identification with their professional identity and role, while also having profound impacts on career satisfaction, work motivation, and career development path choices [4, 5]. Zhang et al. found that domestic nursing students have a moderate level of professional identity, with stress being a major factor contributing to its decline [6].

Perceived stress refers to the subjective perception and evaluation of individuals towards stressors in the environment and the threat or challenge they pose to oneself [7]. According to the stress-adaptation model, an individual's perception of pressure has a significant impact on their emotional and cognitive responses, which further influences their adaptive capacity [8]. Undergraduate nursing students encounter various pressures in campus life, including academics, environmental adaptation, interpersonal and romantic relationships, further education, employment, and transitioning to clinical nursing. The intertwining of these multiple tasks brings a great burden of stress to students. If we perceive the pressures encountered during this period as stressors and feel threatened by them, it may lead to the generation of negative emotions and cognitive reactions, thereby reducing the sense of professional identity towards nursing. Researchers have found a correlation between stress and the decrease in nursing students' professional identity [9]. Although research abroad has confirmed the relationship between stress and professional identity among undergraduate nursing students, related studies are still insufficient in China. There is limited understanding of the mechanisms linking perceived stress and professional identity. This study aims to explore their relationship and underlying mechanisms to provide insights for enhancing the professional identity of undergraduate nursing students.

Psychological capital is the positive psychological state exhibited by individuals during their growth and development, including self-efficacy, hope, optimism, and resilience, which plays an important role in individual adaptation to the environment and emotional regulation [10]. According to the Job Demands-Resources Model, the pressures and resources individuals face have an impact on their mental health [11]. According to this model, the higher the individual's perception of stress, the more it may deplete their psychological capital, leading to a decrease in psychological resources. Liu et al. found that a negative correlation between perceived stress and psychological capital among college students [12]. At the same time, researchers have found a positive correlation between psychological capital and professional identity [13]. Individuals with higher psychological capital tend to adopt a positive coping style in their daily lives and studies. When faced with challenges, they mobilize social resources effectively and maintain strong interpersonal relationships, often receiving encouragement from teachers and friends, which boosts their self-efficacy [12]. The higher an individual's self-efficacy, the more likely they are to identify with their value and abilities in the professional field, thereby promoting the formation of professional identity [14]. Furthermore, The psychological capital can mitigate the negative impact of work stress on PI [12, 15, 16]. Therefore, psychological capital may mediate the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity.

Self-directed learning ability refers to the ability of individuals to actively and independently think and act during the learning process [17]. Research shows that there is a positive correlation between self-directed learning ability and professional identity [18]. Social identity theory suggests that an individual's perception of the profession they engage in is influenced by several factors, including their mastery of knowledge and skills related to that profession [19]. In undergraduate nursing students, self-directed learning ability enables them to better grasp the knowledge and skills in the nursing profession, thereby enhancing their professional identity in the field of nursing [17]. Research shows that there is a negative correlation between stress and self-directed learning ability [20]. According to the general strain theory, stress can lead to problem behaviors [21]. Students with high perceived stress often exhibit lower self-control, increased academic fatigue, and are more prone to problem behaviors, such as excessive internet or cellphone use, to cope with stress, potentially leading to a decline in self-directed learning ability [22, 23]. Therefore, self-directed learning ability may mediate the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity.

Psychological capital and self-directed learning ability play important roles in cultivating professional identity, and there is a close relationship between the two. Psychological capital refers to the psychological resources possessed by individuals, including self-efficacy, hope, optimism, and resilience as the four dimensions [10]. According to social cognitive theory, self-efficacy is an important factor influencing self-directed learning ability [24]. Research has shown that an individual's self-efficacy affects their motivation level and behavioral goals in subsequent learning activities [25]. Compared to students with low self-efficacy, students with higher self-efficacy are more inclined to use self-directed learning strategies, therefore, their self-directed learning ability is stronger [26]. College students often face stress from academic, employment, further education, and life-related issues, though perceived stress levels can vary at different academic stages. According to conservation of resources theory, psychological resources are limited, and high stress levels can deplete these resources. If these resources are not replenished in a timely manner, individuals may experience feelings of confusion and avoidance, which can lead to academic fatigue and potentially decrease their self-directed learning ability [22, 23, 27]. Therefore, psychological capital and self-directed learning ability may serve as a mediating chain between perceived stress and professional identity.

Research indicates a negative relationship between perceived stress and professional identity, and a positive relationship between psychological capital, self-directed learning ability, and professional identity. However, studies on the pathways among these four factors are lacking. Cultivating professional identity in undergraduate nursing students remains a key goal of nursing education. Therefore, this study focuses on undergraduate nursing students, with perceived stress as the independent variable, psychological capital and self-directed learning ability as the mediating variables, and professional identity as the dependent variable, aiming to explore the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity, as well as the independent and chain mediating effects of psychological capital and self-directed learning ability, in order to provide a theoretical foundation for nursing educators to enhance professional identity in undergraduate nursing students. Based on existing relevant research, this study proposes the following hypotheses: H1) Perceived stress would be negatively correlated with professional identity; H2) Psychological capital would play a mediating role in the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity; H3) Self-directed learning ability would play a mediating role the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity; H4) Psychological capital and self-directed learning ability act as chain mediators between perceived stress and professional identity. Method

Study design and participants

This study is a cross-sectional study that was conducted in Xi'an, Shaanxi Province, China. There were ten undergraduate nursing schools in Xi'an subsequently labeled 1 to 10, five of which were randomly selected for the study via a random number table. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) full-time undergraduate nursing students enrolled in a four-year program; (2) undergraduate nursing students who voluntarily signed an informed consent form. Students who were not enrolled due to reasons such as taking a leave of absence or joining the military were excluded. The sample size was calculated with the formula N = 4Uα2S2/δ2 [28]. S = 0.54 was determined based on the preliminary experimental results. The allowable error δ is set to 0.1, and α is set to 0.05; therefore, N = 4 × 1.962 × 0.542/0.12≈448. Considering the 15% invalid questionnaire rate, I have decided to set the minimum sample size as 516. Participants were recruited through cluster sampling from the aforementioned randomly selected 5 undergraduate nursing schools, resulting in a total of 623 recruited undergraduate nursing students for this study. A questionnaire was distributed to each participant involved in this study, and a total of 596 valid questionnaires were successfully collected (effective response rate of 95.67%).

Measurements

Demographic characteristics

Demographic characteristics involved gender, grade, one-child, type of residence, and family finances.

Measurement of perceived stress

The perceived stress scale (PSS), originally complied by Cohen et al. and revised by Yang et al. was used to measure the perceived stress of undergraduate nursing students [7, 29]. The PSS consist of 14 items that includes two dimensions with each dimension consist of seven items. The two dimensions included: a sense of loss of control and a sense of tension. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The PSS is scored on a range of 14 to 70, with higher scores indicating a higher level of perceived stress. Previous studies have shown that the scale exhibits robust reliability and validity [30]. In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient for the scale was 0.753.

Measurement of psychological capital

The positive psychological capital questionnaire (PPCQ), developed by Zhang et al. was used to measure the psychological capital of undergraduate nursing students [31]. The PPCQ consists of 26 items that includes four dimensions. The four dimensions included: self-efficacy (7 items), hope (6 items), optimism (6 items), and resilience (7 items). Each item was rated on a 7point Likert scale, from 1 (completely disagree) to 7 (completely agree). The PPCQ is scored on a range of 26 to 182, with higher scores indicating higher levels of psychological capital in the participants. Previous studies have demonstrated that the scale exhibits good reliability and validity [32]. In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient for the scale was 0.931.

Measurement of self-directed learning ability

The self-directed learning ability scale (SLAC), developed by Lin et al. was used to measure the self-directed learning ability of undergraduate nursing students [33]. The SLAC consists of 28 items that includes four dimensions. The four dimensions included: self-management ability (10 items), information ability (11 items), and collaborative learning ability (7 items). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, from 1 (completely matches) to 5 (completely does not match). The SLAC is scored on a range of 28 to 140, with higher scores indicating a higher level of self-directed learning ability. Previous studies have demonstrated that the scale exhibits good reliability and validity [34]. In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient for the scale was 0.826.

Measurement of professional identity

The professional identity questionnaire (PIQ), developed by Hao. was used to measure the professional identity of undergraduate nursing students [35]. The PIQ consists of 17 items that includes five dimensions. The five dimensions included: professional self-concept (6 items), retention benefits and turnover risks (4 items), social comparison and self-reflection (3 items), autonomy in career choices (2 items), and social persuasion (2 items). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The PIQ is scored on a range of 17 to 85, with higher scores indicating a higher level of professional identity. Previous studies have demonstrated that the scale exhibits good reliability and validity [9]. In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient for the scale was 0.944.

Statistical Methods

The data were analyzed utilizing SPSS 27.0 and the PROCESS 3.4 Macro. (1) In this study, the data were assessed through the Harman single-factor test [36]. The variance explained by the first common factor was 24.74%, which falls below the critical threshold of 40% [36]. This indicates that the potential issue of common method bias is not a concern in this study. (2) Descriptive analysis was conducted, and the results were presented in terms of frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. (3) Independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed to compare the general demographic characteristics (gender, grade, one-child status, type of residence, and family finances) with regards to their influence on perceived stress, psychological capital, self-directed learning ability, and professional identity among the participants. (4) Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to explore the relationships among four variables: perceived stress, psychological capital, self-directed learning ability and professional identity. (5) To evaluate the significance of the multiple-mediator model in this study, we utilized model 6 of the PROCESS macro for SPSS, which was developed by Hayes [37]. This approach was based on ordinary least-squares regression and the bootstrap method. In comparison to the causal steps approach or the Sobel test, Hayes regarded bootstrapping as the most robust method for evaluating indirect effects due to its independence from assumptions about the shape of the sampling distribution and its superior control over type I errors [37–39]. Hayes suggested utilizing 10,000 bootstrap bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (BC CI) for conducting mediation analyses in the Serial-Multiple Mediation Model 6 test, considering them significant if they did not include zero [37]. The threshold for statistical significance was established at a p-value below 0.05. The central hypothesis model investigated in this study focused on the influence of perceived stress on professional identity through psychological capital and self-directed learning ability. Additionally, in accordance with the guidelines of Mathieu and Taylor, we examined both the direct and total effects [40]. Lastly, gender, grade, one-child status, type of residence, and family finances were included as covariates in the analysis.

Mediation analyses

The study employed the bootstrap method to investigate the serial mediation effects of psychological capital and self-directed learning ability in the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity. Gender, grade, one-child status, type of residence, and family finances were included as covariates in the analysis. In this approach, we computed the unstandardized path coefficients to reduce type 1 error resulting from distribution [37]. The findings from the mediation analysis are displayed in Fig. 2.

The total effect (c = −0.792, SE = 0.072, t = −10.956, p < 0.001) of perceived stress on professional identity was found to be statistically significant (Step 1). Additionally, perceived stress had a negative direct effect on psychological capital (B = −1.787, SE = 0.088, t = −20.341, p < 0.001), as well as on self-directed learning ability (B = −0.296, SE = 0.072, t = −4.128, p < 0.001). The direct effect of psychological capital, serving as the first mediating variable, on the second mediating variable of self-directed learning ability (B = 0.239, SE = 0.026, t = 9.293, p < 0.001), was also found to be statistically significant (Step 2). An examination of direct effects of mediating variables on professional identity revealed that both psychological capital (B = 0.204, SE = 0.034, t = 6.098, p < 0.001) and self-directed learning ability (B = 0.280, SE = 0.050, t = 5.579, p < 0.001) showed statistically significant (Step 3). When perceived stress and the two mediating variables were simultaneously included in the model (Step 4), it was observed that the direct effect of perceived stress on professional identity was also statistically significant (B = −0.225, SE = 0.088, t = −2.555, p = 0.011). In summary, these findings indicate the occurrence of serial-multiple mediation. Discussion

The primary objective of this study is to investigate the association between perceived stress and professional identity among undergraduate nursing students, while examining the potential roles of psychological capital and self-directed learning ability as independent and serial mediators in this relationship. By expanding the understanding of the underlying mechanism through which perceived stress influences professional identity among undergraduate nursing students, this study not only contributes to the theoretical foundation but also offers new avenues for enhancing their professional identity. Additionally, the findings of this study revealed a significant negative correlation between perceived stress and psychological capital, self-directed learning ability, as well as professional identity. Furthermore, a positive correlation was observed between psychological capital and self-directed learning ability, as well as professional identity. Similarly, self-directed learning ability was positively associated with professional identity. Of particular significance, the results of this study demonstrate that perceived stress exerts a direct impact on the professional identity of undergraduate nursing students, while also exerting an indirect influence through the distinct mediating effects and serial mediating effects of psychological capital and self-directed learning ability. The analysis revealed that the mediating effects accounted for 46.09%, 10.48%, and 15.15%, respectively, underscoring the pivotal role of psychological capital as the most influential mediator between perceived stress and professional identity.

After controlling for demographic variables, this study revealed that perceived stress exerted an independent predictive effect, accounting for 17.61% of the variability in professional identity among undergraduate nursing students. This finding underscores the substantial influence of perceived stress on the development of their professional identity. This finding not only verifies Hypothesis 1 posited in this study but also aligns with the congruent findings reported in previous research [9, 41]. Previous studies have indicated that stress is perceived to pervade the entire process of undergraduate nursing education, and students with heightened stress perception are inclined to exhibit diminished levels of professional identity [20]. On one hand, undergraduate nursing students may face numerous learning tasks and demands during their academic tenure, and academic pressure can lead to anxiety and unease, thereby diminishing their sense of professional identity in nursing. On the other hand, undergraduate nursing students may exhibit inadequate confidence in their competencies and practical skills, which can engender doubts regarding their professional identity and role. Concerns about their proficiency in fulfilling nursing responsibilities may consequently undermine their sense of professional identity. Furthermore, the findings of this study indirectly lend support to the stress-adaptation model, positing that excessive stress may engender negative emotions and maladaptive cognitive responses in undergraduate nursing students, consequently impeding the development and nurturing of their professional identity [16]. Therefore, in interventions aimed at enhancing professional identity among undergraduate nursing students, school administrators and educators can initiate actions to mitigate stress levels in these students.

This study found that psychological capital plays a mediating role in the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity among undergraduate nursing students, confirming Hypothesis 2. This suggests that higher levels of perceived stress among undergraduate nursing students are associated with lower levels of psychological capital, leading to a decrease in professional identity. Several studies had confirmed that high levels of stress may lead to a decrease in psychological capital [26, 32]. Additionally, the psychological capital can effectively mitigate the negative impact of stress on medical students' professional identity, which in agreement with previous research [12]. Perceived stress has the potential to deplete psychological capital, thus exerting an influence on professional identity. This further implies that the enhancement of professional identity can be pursued through the reduction of perceived stress and the augmentation of psychological capital. More specifically, the provision of mental health education and training, along with assistance in cultivating undergraduate nursing students comprehension of the significance of psychological capital, and the provision of coping skills and strategies to address stress and setbacks, can contribute to their effective stress management and promotion of mental well-being. Stimulating self-reflection among undergraduate nursing students to gain insight into their strengths and abilities, fostering a positive self-perception, and bolstering self-efficacy. Providing psychological counseling to afford undergraduate nursing students the chance to articulate their emotions and concerns, thus alleviating stress.

This study found that self-directed learning ability plays a mediating role in the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity among undergraduate nursing students, confirming Hypothesis 3. This suggests that higher levels of perceived stress among undergraduate nursing students are more likely to be associated with a decrease in self-directed learning ability, which hinders the cultivation of professional identity. Perceived stress negatively predicts self-directed learning ability, as individuals with higher levels of perceived stress tend to have lower levels of self-directed learning ability, which is consistent with previous research findings [20, 22, 23] and supports the general theory of stress [21]. Furthermore, Several studies had confirmed that a close relationship between the enhancement of students' professional identity and the increase in self-directed learning ability [17, 18]. The impact of perceived stress on self-directed learning ability can impede the nurturing of professional identity. This further implies that the enhancement of professional identity can be pursued by bolstering self-directed learning ability. Various strategies can be employed to amplify the self-directed learning ability of undergraduate nursing students, encompassing: 1) furnishing explicit learning goals and guidance; 2) fostering time management aptitude and study planning proficiency; 3) incentivizing proactive learning; 4) nurturing students' evaluative thinking abilities; 5) furnishing feedback and evaluation mechanisms; 6) providing an array of learning resources and tools.

This study found that psychological capital and self-directed learning ability play a chained mediating role in the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity among undergraduate nursing students, confirming Hypothesis 4. This suggests that perceived stress is initially related to a decrease in psychological capital, which is then associated with a decrease in self-directed learning ability, and the decrease in self-directed learning ability is further related to a decrease in professional identity. Numerous studies have shown that perceived stress can negatively predict psychological capital [26, 42]. As a positive psychological resource for individuals, psychological capital has been found to effectively mitigate the impact of stress on individuals' mental health [43]. Wang et al. discovered that psychological capital and self-directed learning ability are positively correlated, and by enhancing individuals' psychological capital, not only can their self-confidence be improved, but their self-directed learning ability can also be enhanced [44]. Compared to students with lower levels of psychological capital, students with higher levels of psychological capital may find it easier to establish self-confidence in the learning process, thus making them more likely to engage in active learning and possess higher levels of self-directed learning ability. Xu found a positive correlation between psychological capital and self-directed learning ability [45]. Finally, self-directed learning ability is positively correlated with professional identity, with individuals who possess higher levels of self-directed learning ability often having higher levels of professional identity [17, 18].

Therefore, the study's findings have significant implications for the enhancement and improvement of professional identity among undergraduate nursing students. Firstly, nursing educators should prioritize students who face high levels of perceived stress, possess low levels of psychological capital, and exhibit low levels of self-directed learning ability. Secondly, there should be a focus on fostering psychological capital and augmenting self-directed learning ability among undergraduate nursing students, guiding them to objectively assess stress-inducing events in their everyday learning through a positive lens, consequently mitigating the influence of PS on professional identity and ultimately enhancing professional identity among undergraduate nursing students.

Limitations

This study is not without its limitations. Firstly, it selectively focused on a limited number of universities in Xi'an, located in Shaanxi Province, China, which raises concerns about the representativeness of the sample and introduces potential biases to the research findings. To further validate the results, future studies should expand the scope of investigation. Nonetheless, the findings of this study bear significant implications for nursing education in the local area. Secondly, this study employed a cross-sectional research design, thereby precluding the establishment of causal relationships and allowing for only suggestive evidence. To confirm the findings, future research should consider adopting longitudinal designs or conducting experimental studies. Thirdly, the relationship between perceived stress and professional identity is highly intricate. This study solely examined the mediating role of psychological capital and self-directed learning ability, which fails to fully elucidate the underlying factor model of professional identity. Therefore, future research should incorporate additional relevant variables to construct a more comprehensive and systematic model. Moreover, this study did not investigate the relationship between undergraduate nursing students' professional practice during internships and key variables such as perceived stress, psychological capital, self-directed learning ability, and professional identity. Future research could examine this relationship by focusing on the role of professional practice during internships in shaping the variables of interest. Lastly, the data collection in this study relied on online self-reporting, which inevitably entails the risk of recall bias and selection bias, potentially compromising the accuracy of the results.

Conclusion

This study is the first to adopt a chain mediation model to explore the chain mediation effect of psychological capital and self-directed learning ability between perceived stress and professional identity among Chinese undergraduate nursing students. The results of this study demonstrate that psychological capital and self-directed learning ability play a chain mediating role between perceived stress and professional identity. Specifically, the experience of perceived stress is initially associated with a decline in psychological capital, which subsequently contributes to a reduction in self-directed learning ability, ultimately leading to a diminution in professional identity. Hence, the reduction of perceived stress, cultivation of psychological capital, and enhancement of self-directed learning ability hold significant potential for fostering the development of professional identity among undergraduate nursing students.
