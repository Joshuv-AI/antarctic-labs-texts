1-Lipschitz Network Initialization for Certifiably Robust Classification Applications: A Decay Problem
Marius F. R. Juston, Ramavarapu S. Sreenivas, William R. Norris, Ahmet Soylemezoglu, Dustin Nottage

Abstract
This paper discusses the weight parametrization of two standard 1-Lipschitz network architectures, the Almost-Orthogonal-Layers (AOL) and the SDP-based Lipschitz Layers (SLL). It examines their impact on initialization for deep 1-Lipschitz feedforward networks, and discusses underlying issues surrounding this initialization. These networks are mainly used in certifiably robust classification applications to combat adversarial attacks by limiting the impact of perturbations on the classification output. Exact and upper bounds for the parameterized weight variance were calculated assuming a standard Normal distribution initialization; additionally, an upper bound was computed assuming a Generalized Normal Distribution, generalizing the proof for Uniform, Laplace, and Normal distribution weight initializations. It is demonstrated that the weight variance holds no bearing on the output variance distribution and that only the dimension of the weight matrices matters. Additionally, this paper demonstrates that the weight initialization always causes deep 1-Lipschitz networks to decay to zero.

1
Introduction

The robustness of deep neural networks, primarily against adversarial attacks, has been a significant challenge in the field of modern applications of machine learning
Nguyen et al. (2015)
;
Szegedy et al. (2013)
;
Biggio et al. (2013)
by manipulating the input so that the model produces incorrect output. The problem of network robustness in deep networks stems mainly from the fact that large network weight magnitudes have an exponential impact on the output, the deeper it goes. The significant weight magnitudes thus enable a small perturbation to the input to cause a drastic change in the classification output
Costa et al. (2024)
.

The design of the 1-Lipschitz neural network has provided a reliable solution to certifying the network to be robust, such that the decision output remains the same within a sphere of perturbation
Tsuzuku et al. (2018)
. For the design, multiple approaches have been proposed, ranging from utilizing Spectral Normalization (SN)
Miyato et al. (2018)
;
Roth et al. (2020)
, Orthogonal Parametrization
Trockman and Kolter (2021)
, Convex Potential Layers (CPL)
Meunier et al. (2022)
, Almost-Orthogonal-Layers (AOL)
Prach and Lampert (2022)
, Sandwich layers
Wang and Manchester (2023)
and the recent SDP-based Lipschitz Layers (SLL)
Araujo et al. (2023)
. All these previous techniques assume standard Kaiming initialization scheme for its parameterized layered weights, which might or might not be appropriate for these networks. This remains under-studied for these networks; paper aims to further the theoretical initialization dynamics understanding of AOL and SLL networks.

This paper explores the impact of the weight parameterization of 1-Lipschitz networks employing Almost-Orthogonal-Layers and SDP-based Lipschitz Layers on the initialization of deep neural networks. Exploring the challenges in applying certifiably robust neural networks, such as 1-Lipschitz neural networks, is crucial, especially as neural network attacks become more frequent and robust classification results become more important. As such, discussing ways to improve training for deeper neural network architectures is important to understand and address. This article aims to illuminate some of the issues underlying these weight-normalizing networks.

•

An extended derivation for the network layer variance while accounting for the bias term and its recursive definition using the ReLU activation function is provided.

•

Given the structure for the Almost-Orthogonal-Layers and SDP-based Lipschitz Layers feedforward network structure and weight parameterization, an upper bound and exact network weight variance is derived assuming a normal distribution initialization

•

A general upper bound based on the Generalized Normal Distribution for the parameterized network weight variance is derived.

•

Based on the calculated weight variance, insights for the 1-Lipschitz network are discussed as to potential issues in this network’s initialization.

The initial work for the initialization analysis is inspired by the works of Kaiming
He et al. (2015)
and Xavier
Glorot and Bengio (2010)
, while the 1-Lipschitz network structure is derived from
Araujo et al. (2023)
.

Understanding the initialization characteristics of these networks is extremely important as it allows researchers to better understand how to generate deeper Lipschitz networks, which enable robust certification guarantees, thus the bounds derived in the paper can help with researchers to develop more novel initialization or parameterization methods to help mitigate the issues that arise from the current state of the art methods.

2
Related Work

The starting work from Xavier
Glorot and Bengio (2010)
was a pivotal moment for deep neural networks with the methodology to properly initialize deep neural networks such that they would converge, assuming hyperbolic tangent activation functions; however, their work posed simplifying assumptions on the activation functions which caused issues when transition to more modern activation functions such as the commonly used ReLU
Nair and Hinton (2010)
.

The works by Kaiming expanded on this concept and developed a method to generate weight initializations for deep networks using the Parameterized ReLU family
He et al. (2015)
; this work demonstrated the ability to ensure that the network would converge and train properly, regardless of depth. Since then, all modern machine learning has used Kaiming initialization for its networks, and modifications to the initialization gain have been activation-specific to ensure the stability criteria derived by Kaiming remain satisfied, as in the work on SELUs
Klambauer et al. (2017)
. An issue with the works above is the assumption of a bias term initialized to zero. From these works the connection to the correct initialization choice to enabling gradient stability establishes a direct link to impact the training accuracy and thus the robustness of networks.

In conjunction with the works for network initialization,
Araujo et al. (2023)
developed a unifying methodology to combine multiple existing 1-Lipschitz network structures into a unifying framework. This framework provides a guideline for creating a new, certifiably robust neural network. The authors achieve this by formulating feedforward networks as a nonlinear robust control Lur’e system
Lur’e and Postnikov (1944)
and enforcing conditions on the generalized residual network’s weights via SDP constraints. From this work, they can demonstrate general conditions for enforcing a multilayered 1-Lipschitz network and combine previous works from Spectral Normalization (SN)
Miyato et al. (2018)
;
Roth et al. (2020)
, Orthogonal Parameterization
Trockman and Kolter (2021)
, Convex Potential Layers (CPL)
Meunier et al. (2022)
, and Almost-Orthogonal-Layers (AOL)
Prach and Lampert (2022)
into a single constraint. From the framework, they generate an augmented version of the AOL with additional parameterization, called SDP-based Lipschitz Layers, which improves the network’s generalizability. However, previous work on robust networks also uses standard Kaiming initialization for network weights. In addition, because 1-Lipschitz activation functions are required, the ReLU activation function is commonly used and will also be used in the proofs below. This article explores the impact of using such an initialization scheme on networks. While the authors of
Araujo et al. (2023)
use the residual network, which, due to the additional interdependence, will be explored in future work, as convolution layers can be represented as a similar feedforward structure, the proof for the feedforward network generalizes to convolution layers
Chetlur et al. (2014)
.

3
Feed Forward Variance With Bias

This article starts with a definition similar to the Kaiming
He et al. (2015)
and Xavier
Glorot and Bengio (2010)
initialization schemes; however, unlike their implementations, which set the bias to zero, this assumption is not made. The bias term is assumed to be a normally distributed, IID variable, similar to the weight matrix. The activation function is assumed to be ReLU for this derivation, as it is used in SLL and AOL networks. The desired end goal was to find
\operatorname{Var}\left[y_{l}\right]
. The variable
y_{l}
was defined as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y_{l}}

\displaystyle=W_{l}T_{l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{x_{l}}+\boldsymbol{b_{l}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x_{l}}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\boldsymbol{y_{l-1}}).

(1)

Where
\sigma(x)=\max(0,x)
, which was the ReLU activation function. The matrix
T_{l}
is a positive definite diagonal matrix as defined by the SLL 1-Lipschitz function definition
Araujo et al. (2023)
:

\displaystyle T_{l}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{W_l^T W_l }_{ij}\frac{q_{j}}{q_{i}}\right),q_{i}>0.

(2)

In this article, the parameter
q_{i}
is initialized to the constant
1
; when set to the unit vector, the SLL
T_{l}
derivation also encapsulates the AOL parameterization
Prach and Lampert (2022)
. The vectors were defined such that
x_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times 1}
,
W_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}\times n_{l}}
,
b_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}\times 1}
with the following assumptions:

•

The initialized elements in
W_{l}
were mutually independent and shared the same distribution
\forall l,j
,
\operatorname{Cov}\left[W_{l},W_{j}\right]=0
with
l\neq j
, and that
\operatorname{Var}[W_{1}]=\cdots=\operatorname{Var}\left[W_{l}\right]

•

Likewise, the elements in
x_{l}
were mutually independent and shared the same distribution.
\forall l,j
,
\operatorname{Cov}\left[x_{l},x_{j}\right]=0
with
l\neq j
, and that
\operatorname{Var}\left[x_{1}\right]=\cdots=\operatorname{Var}\left[x_{l}\right]

•

Additionally, the elements in
b_{l}
were mutually independent and shared the same distribution.
\forall l,j
,
\operatorname{Cov}\left[b_{l},b_{j}\right]=0
with
l\neq j
, and that
\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{1}\right]=\cdots=\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{l}\right]

•

The vectors
x_{l}
,
W_{l}
and,
b_{l}
were independent of each other,
\operatorname{Cov}\left[W_{l},x_{l}\right]=\operatorname{Cov}\left[W_{l},b_{l}\right]=\operatorname{Cov}\left[b_{l},x_{l}\right]=0

Under these assumptions, it could be determined that:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left[\boldsymbol{y_{l}}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}\left[W_{l}x_{l}+b_{l}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}\left[\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{l}}w_{1,j}x_{j}+b_{1}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n_{l}}w_{2,j}x_{j}+b_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n_{l}}w_{d_{j},j}x_{j}+b_{d_{j}}\end{bmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{l}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{l}}\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{l}x_{l}\right]+\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{l}\right]\right)

\displaystyle d_{l}\times\operatorname{Var}\left[y_{l}\right]

\displaystyle=d_{l}\times\left(n_{l}\times\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{l}x_{l}\right]+\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{l}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left[y_{l}\right]

\displaystyle=n_{l}\times\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{l}x_{l}\right]+\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{l}\right].

(3)

The results were the same as those of Kaiming and Xavier, except for the addition of a bias term. Given the independence between the terms, the layer’s variance could be expanded as:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left[y_{l}\right]

\displaystyle=n_{l}\times\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{l}x_{l}\right]+\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{l}\right]

\displaystyle=n_{l}\times\left(\underbrace{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{l}^{2}\right]}_{\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{l}\right]}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[x_{l}^{2}\right]-\underbrace{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{l}\right]^{2}}_{=0}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[x_{l}\right]^{2}\right)+\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{l}\right]

\displaystyle=n_{l}\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{l}\right]\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[x_{l}^{2}\right]+\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{l}\right].

(4)

The
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[x_{l}\right]
does not have zero mean because the previous layer is
x_{l}=\max(0,y_{l-1})
and thus does not have zero mean. As such,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[x_{l}^{2}\right]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max(0,y_{l-1})^{2}\right]
needed to be handled.

3.1
ReLU Expected Value

In Kaiming’s work, the expected value of the ReLU was derived; however, the bias term was set to zero. In contrast, the following expected value derivation includes bias in its computation:

Given that
b_{l-1},w_{l-1},x_{l-1}
were independent.

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[y_{l-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}+b_{l-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{l-1}\right]}_{=0}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[x_{l-1}\right]+\underbrace{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[b_{l-1}\right]}_{=0}=0.

(5)

Theorem 1
.

Given an ReLU activation function,
\sigma(\cdot)
the variance of the linear layer
y_{l}=\sigma(w_{l-1}y_{l-1}+b_{l-1})
, where
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{l-1}\right]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[b_{l-1}\right]=0
and
y_{l-1}
is an unknown random variable has the following output variance,
\operatorname{Var}\left[y_{l-1}\right]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[y_{l-1}^{2}\right]
and mean
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[y_{l}\right]=0
.

Proof.

Because
w_{l-1}
and
b_{l-1}
have zero mean and were distributed symmetrically around zero:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[b_{l-1}>0\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2},

(6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[y_{l-1}>0\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}+b_{l-1}>0\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}>-b_{l-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}>\left(b_{l-1}>0\text{ or }b_{l-1}<0\right)\right].

(7)

Using conditional probability:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[y_{l-1}>0\right]=

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}>-b_{l-1}\rvert b_{l-1}>0\right]\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[b_{l-1}>0\right]

\displaystyle+\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}>-b_{l-1}\rvert b_{l-1}<0\right]\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[b_{l-1}<0\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}>-b_{l-1}\rvert b_{l-1}>0\right]\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}>-b_{l-1}\rvert b_{l-1}<0\right]\right).

(8)

Using the property of symmetry around zero :

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}>t\right]=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}<-t\right],\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

(9)

Given this:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}>-b_{l-1}\rvert b_{l-1}<0\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}>b_{l-1}\rvert b_{l-1}>0\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}<-b_{l-1}\rvert b_{l-1}>0\right].

(10)

As such:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[y_{l-1}>0\right]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}>-b_{l-1}\rvert b_{l-1}>0\right]\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{l-1}x_{l-1}<-b_{l-1}\rvert b_{l-1}>0\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(1)=\frac{1}{2}.

(11)

As concluded,
y_{l-1}
was indeed centered on zero and symmetric around the mean. The expectation of
x_{l}^{2}
could now be computed:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[x_{l}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max(0,y_{l-1})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[y_{l-1}<0\right]\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[0\right]+\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[y_{l-1}>0\right]\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[y_{l-1}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[y_{l-1}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Var}\left[y_{l-1}\right].

(12)

∎

Plugging this back into
4
, it was computed that:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left[y_{l}\right]

\displaystyle=n_{l}\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{l}\right]\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[x_{l}^{2}\right]+\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{l}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{l}}{2}\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{l}\right]\operatorname{Var}\left[y_{l-1}\right]+\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{l}\right].

(13)

A recursive equation between the actions at layer
l
and the activations at layer
l-1
was evaluated. Starting from the first layer,
2
, the following product was formed:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left[y_{L}\right]=

\displaystyle\prod_{l=2}^{L}\left(\frac{n_{l}}{2}\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{l}\right]\right)\operatorname{Var}\left[y_{1}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=2}^{L-1}\left(\prod_{d=1}^{L-l}\left(\frac{n_{L-d+1}}{2}\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{L-d+1}\right]\right)\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{l}\right]\right)

\displaystyle+\operatorname{Var}\left[b_{L}\right].

(14)

Thus, this was a similar implementation to the network variance derivation determined by Kaiming, but with the bias term included.

4
Transformed Weight Variance

The next step was to better understand what
\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{l}\right]
was, given the network structure of the
WT^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. To see how the weight matrix was transformed, with the vector
q_{i}=1
as previously stated, an example weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}
was looked at. Following this weight dimension, the following transformation was acquired:

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}a&c&e&g\\
b&d&f&h\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

(15)

Where the transformed matrix was thus:

\displaystyle\bar{W}=WT^{-\frac{1}{2}}=W\text{diag}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{W^T W}_{ij}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}=

(16)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{a}{\sqrt{\left|a^{2}+c^{2}+e^{2}+g^{2}\right|+|ab+cd+ef+gh|}}&\frac{b}{\sqrt{|ab+cd+ef+gh|+\left|b^{2}+d^{2}+f^{2}+h^{2}\right|}}\\
\frac{c}{\sqrt{\left|a^{2}+c^{2}+e^{2}+g^{2}\right|+|ab+cd+ef+gh|}}&\frac{d}{\sqrt{|ab+cd+ef+gh|+\left|b^{2}+d^{2}+f^{2}+h^{2}\right|}}\\
\frac{e}{\sqrt{\left|a^{2}+c^{2}+e^{2}+g^{2}\right|+|ab+cd+ef+gh|}}&\frac{f}{\sqrt{|ab+cd+ef+gh|+\left|b^{2}+d^{2}+f^{2}+h^{2}\right|}}\\
\frac{g}{\sqrt{\left|a^{2}+c^{2}+e^{2}+g^{2}\right|+|ab+cd+ef+gh|}}&\frac{h}{\sqrt{|ab+cd+ef+gh|+\left|b^{2}+d^{2}+f^{2}+h^{2}\right|}}\end{bmatrix}.

4.1
Upper bound

Given the highly complex distribution generated from this output, the system’s complexity was reduced by looking at the upper bound approximation of
4
. This could be done by simply removing the off-diagonal terms in the normalization denominator as such:

\displaystyle WT^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\begin{bmatrix}\frac{a}{\sqrt{\left|a^{2}+c^{2}+e^{2}+g^{2}\right|}}&\frac{b}{\sqrt{\left|b^{2}+d^{2}+f^{2}+h^{2}\right|}}\\
\frac{c}{\sqrt{\left|a^{2}+c^{2}+e^{2}+g^{2}\right|}}&\frac{d}{\sqrt{\left|b^{2}+d^{2}+f^{2}+h^{2}\right|}}\\
\frac{e}{\sqrt{\left|a^{2}+c^{2}+e^{2}+g^{2}\right|}}&\frac{f}{\sqrt{\left|b^{2}+d^{2}+f^{2}+h^{2}\right|}}\\
\frac{g}{\sqrt{\left|a^{2}+c^{2}+e^{2}+g^{2}\right|}}&\frac{h}{\sqrt{\left|b^{2}+d^{2}+f^{2}+h^{2}\right|}}\end{bmatrix}.

(17)

More generally, for this upper bound, each element was thus represented as:

\displaystyle\bar{w}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}}}.

(18)

Given that
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{i}\right]=0
, it was expected that the normalized expected value of
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bar{w}_{i}\right]=0
as well. As such, the variance for this system was defined as
\operatorname{Var}\left[\bar{w}_{i}\right]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bar{w}_{i}^{2}\right]
.

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bar{w}_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{w_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}}\right]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{w_{i}^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}}\right].

(19)

Given that the distribution
w_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
the distribution
w_{i}^{2}
represented a scaled Chi-Squared distribution defined as
w_{i}^{2}\sim\sigma^{2}\chi^{2}(1)
which will be presented as
X
. The additional independent term
\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}
was thus represented as a Chi-Squared distribution of the form
\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}\sim\sigma^{2}\chi^{2}(d_{l}-1)
, represented as
Y
. The distribution thus followed the form:

\displaystyle\bar{w}_{i}^{2}=\frac{X}{X+Y}.

(20)

Theorem 2
.

If
X
and
Y
are independent, with
X\sim\Gamma(\alpha,\theta)
and
Y\sim\Gamma(\beta,\theta)
then
(
198, 1982
, Theorem 3.15.)
:

\displaystyle\frac{X}{X+Y}\sim\boldsymbol{B}(\alpha,\beta),

(21)

where
\boldsymbol{B}(\alpha,\beta)
represents a Beta distribution and
\Gamma(\alpha,\theta)
represents a Gamma distribution.

Given that Chi-Squared distributions can be represented as gamma distributions,
\Gamma(\alpha,\beta)
, with distribution parameters defined as
\sigma^{2}\chi^{2}(n)\sim\Gamma(\frac{n}{2},\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}})
, the resultant Beta distribution and its expected value is thus:

\displaystyle\bar{w}_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\boldsymbol{B}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d_{l}-1}{2}\right),

(22)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bar{w}_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{d_{l}-1}{2}}=\frac{1}{d_{l}}.

(23)

This demonstrated that the resultant distribution variance between the layers depended only on the dimension
d_{l}
of the weight matrix
W
, and that the initial distribution variance,
\sigma^{2}
, had no impact. This made creating an initialization scheme complicated, as no matter the initial variance of the weight matrix
W
, the output distribution would not be affected—only the matrix’s dimension mattered, which was predetermined.

4.2
Exact bound

In addition to deriving the upper bound approximation of the output distribution, the exact distribution’s variance was computed by considering the off-diagonal terms. Similarly to the generalized distribution, the generalized form of each element was examined. Represented by the following formula:

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}}{\sqrt{w_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n_{l}}\left|w_{i}w_{a}+\sum_{k=1}^{d_{l}-1}w_{b}w_{c}\right|}}.

(24)

The weights
w_{a},w_{b},w_{c}
were random elements from
W
; since the actual indexing does not affect the bound, the exact indexing is ignored. Similarly to the upper bound
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\hat{w}_{i}\right]=0
and thus
\operatorname{Var}\left[\hat{w}_{i}\right]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\hat{w}_{i}^{2}\right]
:

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n_{l}}\left|w_{i}w_{a}+\sum_{k=1}^{d_{l}-1}w_{b}w_{c}\right|}.

(25)

As with the upper bound, the distribution
w_{i}^{2}=X\sim\sigma^{2}\chi^{2}(1)
and
\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}=Y\sim\sigma^{2}\chi^{2}(d_{l}-1)
were present; however, the additional term
\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n_{l}}\left|w_{i}w_{a}+\sum_{k=1}^{d_{l}-1}w_{b}w_{c}\right|

=Z
provided a challenge as to what kind of distribution it would be. The
Z
variable thus needed to be analyzed.

The product of two IID Gaussian samples,
w_{b}w_{c}
, denoted as a Normal Product Distribution,
Weisstein (2003)
has the following probability density function (PDF) distribution:

\displaystyle p(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{0}\left(\frac{|w|}{\sigma^{2}}\right)}{\pi\sigma^{2}}

(26)

Where
K_{n}(z)
was the modified Bessel function of the second kind
Olver et al. (2010)
,

\displaystyle K_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}\frac{e^{-z}}{(n-\frac{1}{2})!}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}t^{n-\frac{1}{2}}\left(1-\frac{t}{2z}\right)^{n-\frac{1}{2}}dt,

(27)

To then derive the generalized sum of the Normal Product Distribution
\sum^{d_{l}}_{k=1}w_{k,b}w_{k,c}
, this involves taking the convolution of the continuous probability distributions
d_{l}
times; however, due to the modified Bessel function inside the PDF, this makes it difficult. Instead, the Fourier transform of the PDF can be taken,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(p(w))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}+t^{2}}},

(28)

and then the convolution can be represented as taking the transformed function to the
n
-th power and inverting the transformed Fourier function,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}(p(w))^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{n}{2}}\sigma^{-n-1}|w|^{\frac{n-1}{2}}K_{\frac{n-1}{2}}\left(\frac{|w|}{\sigma^{2}}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)},

(29)

where
\Gamma(z)
represents the Euler gamma function
Abramowitz and Stegun (1964)
,

\displaystyle\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}e^{-t}dt.

(30)

The expected value needed to be computed only when
w\geq 0
. The function is symmetric
p_{w}(w)=p_{w}(-w)
and thus centered around zero, resulting in
P(w>0)=\frac{1}{2}
. The original PDF function only needed to normalize a single side to generate a valid PDF. This thus resulted in the following output distribution.

\displaystyle p_{\absolutevalue{w}}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{P_{w}(w\geq 0)}p_{w}(w\geq 0)

(31)

\displaystyle=2p_{w}(w\geq 0)

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{n}{2}}\sigma^{-n-1}w^{\frac{n-1}{2}}K_{\frac{n-1}{2}}\left(\frac{w}{\sigma^{2}}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}.

(33)

Given this, the expected value could be computed as:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[p_{\absolutevalue{w}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}wp_{\absolutevalue{w}}(w)dw=\frac{2\sigma^{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}.

(34)

Sadly, this distribution could not be presented as a Gamma or Beta distribution. The trick for the upper bound cannot be used, as the distribution of
p_{\absolutevalue{w}}(w)
depends on
w_{i}
; however, this explicit dependence was removed to simplify the computation and approximation. The expected value of the system is thus:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\hat{w}_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{w_{i}^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n_{l}}\left|w_{i}w_{a}+\sum_{k=1}^{d_{l}-1}w_{b}w_{c}\right|}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{i}^{2}\right]}{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n_{l}}\left|w_{i}w_{a}+\sum_{k=1}^{d_{l}-1}w_{b}w_{c}\right|\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{i}^{2}\right]}{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{i}^{2}\right]+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}\right]+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n_{l}}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d_{l}}w_{b}w_{c}\right|\right]}

(35)

Substituting the expectation of each of the components, we get that,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\hat{w}_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\sigma(d_{l}-1)+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n_{l}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d_{l}}w_{b}w_{c}\right|\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{2}(d_{l}-1)+(n_{l}-1)\frac{2\sigma^{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{d_{l}+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{d_{l}}{2}\right)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{l}+(n_{l}-1)\frac{2\Gamma\left(\frac{d_{l}+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{d_{l}}{2}\right)}}.

(36)

To make it more computationally stable, the logarithm of the
\Gamma(\cdot)
function is usually used, as the factorial can become exceedingly large. This could be replaced with:

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d_{l}+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d_{l}}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=e^{\ln{\Gamma\left(\frac{d_l+1}{2}\right)}-\ln{ \Gamma\left(\frac{d_l}{2}\right)}}.

5
Generalized Normal Initialization

Interestingly, using a uniform distribution to initialize the system yields the same result as using the normal distribution with output weight variance. To demonstrate this result, the transformed weight variance was formulated using a Generalized Normal Distribution (GND)
Nadarajah (2005)
for the weight matrix initialization instead of the normal distribution. This was because, from the Generalized Normal Distribution, it is possible to extract multiple other distributions, including but not limited to the Laplace distribution when the shape parameter
\alpha
,
\alpha=1
, the normal distributions when
\alpha=2
, and the uniform distribution when
\alpha=\infty
. The PDF and the cumulative distribution function (CDF) are described below
Soury et al. (2012)
;
Soury and Alouini (2015)
:

\displaystyle p(x;\mu,\sigma,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\Lambda}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}e^{-\Lambda^{\alpha}|x-\mu|^{\alpha}},

(46)

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{\Lambda_{0}}{\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(3/\alpha)}{\Gamma(1/\alpha)}},

(47)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}(x;\mu,\sigma,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{x-\mu\geq 0}-\frac{\text{sign}(x-\mu)}{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad Q\left(\frac{1}{\alpha},\Lambda_{0}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{\absolutevalue{x-\mu}}{\sigma}\right)^{\alpha}\right),

(48)

where
Q(a,z)=\frac{\Gamma(a,z)}{\Gamma(a)}
is the regularized incomplete upper gamma function, where
\Gamma(a,z)
is the upper incomplete gamma function,

\displaystyle\Gamma(a,z)

\displaystyle=\int_{z}^{\infty}t^{a-1}e^{-t}dt,

(49)

and
\mathbbm{1}_{x>0}
is the indicator function. Given that it was previously assumed that
\mu=0
, this assumption will carry through in the following derivations.

The inverse CDF of this distribution, useful when performing efficient sampling of the distribution, is defined as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}(x;\mu,\sigma,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mu+\frac{\text{sign}(x-\frac{1}{2})}{\Lambda}Q^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha},1-\left|2x-1\right|\right)

(50)

where
Q^{-1}(a,s)
represents the inverse of the regularized incomplete gamma function
DiDonato and Morris (1986)
.

5.1
Squared Generalized Normal Distribution

Given the previous distribution in Eq.
25
,
w_{i}^{2}
needed to be derived. To derive this
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
was denoted as the CDF of
w_{i}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(z)=P(Z<z)=F_{Z}(z)
, the CDF of
Y=Z^{2}
first needed to be calculated, in terms of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
.

\displaystyle F_{Y}(y)=P(Y\leq y)

\displaystyle=P(Z^{2}\leq y)

\displaystyle=P(-\sqrt{y}\leq Z\leq\sqrt{y}),\text{for y $\geq 0$}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\left(\sqrt{y}\right)-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\left(-\sqrt{y}\right).

(51)

To compute the PDF, the CDF’s derivative needed to be calculated.

\displaystyle f_{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\delta y}\left[F_{Y}(y)\right]=\frac{\delta}{\delta y}\left[\boldsymbol{\Phi}\left(\sqrt{y}\right)-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\left(-\sqrt{y}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\delta y}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\left(\sqrt{y}\right)-\frac{\delta}{\delta y}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\left(-\sqrt{y}\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi\left(\sqrt{y}\right)\frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}+\varphi\left(-\sqrt{y}\right)\frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}\left(\frac{\alpha\Lambda e^{-\Lambda^{\alpha}|\sqrt{y}|^{\alpha}}}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}+\frac{\alpha\Lambda e^{-\Lambda^{\alpha}|-\sqrt{y}|^{\alpha}}}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}\frac{\alpha\Lambda e^{-\Lambda^{\alpha}|\sqrt{y}|^{\alpha}}}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}.

(52)

The moment-generating function can be derived as:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{n}f_{Y}(y)dy

(53)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2n}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{n-1}\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{\alpha}\right)^{-n}\Gamma\left(\frac{2n+1}{\alpha}\right),

(54)

which generated the mean of
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{i}^{2}\right]=\sigma^{2}
and variance
\operatorname{Var}\left[w_{i}^{2}\right]=\sigma^{4}\left(\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{5}{\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{\alpha}\right)^{2}}-1\right)
.

Given that the distribution
w_{i}^{2}
has thus been determined, it was also wished to determine what the distribution
\sum_{i=1,j\neq i}^{d_{l}}w_{j}^{2}
represented.

To find this distribution, the characteristic function (CF) of the Squared Generalized Normal Distribution (SGND) is used, as it is easier to perform the summation in the characteristic equation, which represents the product of characteristic functions, rather than convolving the PDFs.

5.2
SGND Characteristic Function

Let
\sigma,\alpha>0
, and
Y
be a random variable (RV) following a
SGND(0,\sigma,\mu)

Theorem 4
.

The CF of Y,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{itY}\right]
, is given by

\displaystyle\varphi(t;\sigma,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{\alpha})}{\mathrm{H}}_{1,1}^{1,1}\left[-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\Lambda^{2}t\left|\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(1-\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{2}{\alpha})}{(0, 1)}\right.\right],

(55)

where
\mathrm{H}^{\cdot,\cdot}_{\cdot,\cdot}[\cdot]
is the Fox H function (FHF)
(
Kilbas, 2004
, Eq. (1.1.1))
.

Proof.

Starting with the definition of the CF and the PDF of the SGND, the CF is defined as

\displaystyle\varphi(t;\sigma,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{itY}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{{i\mkern 1.0mu}ty}f_{Y}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\Lambda}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}\int_{\mathbb{R_{+}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}e^{-\Lambda^{\alpha}y^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}e^{{i\mkern 1.0mu}ty}dy.

(56)

We can find alternative expressions to the exponentials in terms of FHF as
(
Kilbas, 2004
, Eq. (2.9.4))
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}y^{\frac{b}{\beta}}e^{-y^{\frac{1}{\beta}}}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{H}}_{0,1}^{1,0}\left[y\left|\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\emptyline}{(b, \beta)}\right.\right],

(57)

which allows us to rewrite the CF integral as the product of two FHF

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}e^{-\Lambda^{\alpha}y^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\Lambda}{\alpha}{\mathrm{H}}_{0,1}^{1,0}\left[\Lambda^{2}y\left|\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\emptyline}{(-\frac{1}{\alpha}, \frac{2}{\alpha})}\right.\right],

(58)

\displaystyle e^{{i\mkern 1.0mu}ty}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{H}}_{0,1}^{1,0}\left[-{i\mkern 1.0mu}ty\left|\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\emptyline}{(0, 1)}\right.\right],

(59)

over the positive real numbers, which enables the use of the integral identity defined in
(
Kilbas, 2004
, Eq. (2.8.4))
. As a result, the CF is rewritten as

\displaystyle\varphi(t;\sigma,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\Lambda}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}\frac{2\Lambda}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{H}}_{0,1}^{1,0}\left[\Lambda^{2}y\left|\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\emptyline}{(-\frac{1}{\alpha}, \frac{2}{\alpha})}\right.\right]\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad{\mathrm{H}}_{0,1}^{1,0}\left[-{i\mkern 1.0mu}ty\left|\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\emptyline}{(0, 1)}\right.\right]dy,

\displaystyle=\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2}}{\mathrm{H}}_{1,1}^{1,1}\left[-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\Lambda^{2}t\left|\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(1-\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{2}{\alpha})}{(0, 1)}\right.\right].

(60)

which completes the derivation of the CF.
∎

5.3
Moment Generating Function

The moment generating function (MGF) can be directly concluded from the Cf by the relation
M(t;\sigma,\alpha)=\varphi(-{i\mkern 1.0mu}t;\sigma,\alpha)
such that,

\displaystyle M(t;\sigma,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}{\mathrm{H}}_{1,1}^{1,1}\left[\Lambda^{2}t\left|\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(1-\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{2}{\alpha})}{(0, 1)}\right.\right].

(61)

5.4
Sum of independent SGND random variables

While it would be interesting to be able to compute the PDF of the generalized sum of
n
independent SGND random variables and its different characteristics, this would involve an
n
-dimensional Mellin-Barnes integration
(
Kilbas, 2004
, Eq. 1.1.2)
. These integrations do not have an explicit solution, except for a very limited parameterization of the FHF. Given the CF of a function, the sum of independent SGND random variables (with equal parameters) would be defined as

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(t;\sigma,\alpha):=\varphi(t;\sigma,\alpha)^{n},

(62)

with the
n
-dimensional Mellin-Barnes integral being represented as,

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(t;\sigma,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi{i\mkern 1.0mu})^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{L}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(s_{j})\Gamma(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{2}{\alpha}s_{j})}{\Gamma(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{2}{\alpha}s_{j})\Gamma(1-s_{j})}\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad(-i\Lambda^{2}t)^{-s_{j}}d{s_{j}}.

(63)

Given that the derivation of a simplification of is not feasible, taking the inverse Laplace transform of the CF to retrieve the PDF is also not feasible. Instead, given that this paper is only interested in the expectation of this distribution, this would be the
n
-sum of the expectation of a single SGND (Eq.
54
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum^{n}_{j=1}w_{j}^{2}\right]=\sum^{n}_{j=1}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[w_{j}^{2}\right]=n\sigma^{2}

(64)

5.5
Sum of the absolute value of the product of independent GND

Finally, the last term that is required to be computed is the sum of the absolute value of the product-independent GND
\sum_{j=1}^{n_{l}}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d_{l}-1}w_{b,k,j}w_{c,k,j}\right|
, where
w_{b,k,j}w_{c,k,j}\sim GND(0,\sigma,\alpha)
.

Theorem 5
.

The PDF of the product of two independent IID random variables
Z=XY
, where
X,Y\sim GND(0,\sigma,\alpha)
is annotated as the PGND distribution, is defined as,

\displaystyle f_{Z}(z)=\frac{\alpha\Lambda^{2}}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{\alpha})^{2}}K_{0}(2\Lambda^{\alpha}|z|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})

(65)

Proof.

For two independent random variables with PDF
f_{X}(x)
, the PDF of the product can be defined as:

\displaystyle f_{Z}(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X}(x)f_{X}\left(\frac{z}{x}\right)\frac{1}{|x|}dx.

(66)

Given that in this case the
f_{X}(x)
is even
p(x)=p(-x)
, it is possible to rewrite the integral as

\displaystyle f_{Z}(z)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{X}(x)f_{X}\left(\frac{z}{x}\right)\frac{1}{|x|}dx.

(67)

The definition of
f_{X}(x)
from Eq.
46
is substituted in
f_{Z}(z)
and since
x>0
,
|\frac{z}{x}|=\frac{|z|}{x}
as such

\displaystyle f_{Z}(z)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{\alpha\Lambda}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\Lambda^{\alpha}x^{\alpha}}e^{-\Lambda^{\alpha}\left(\frac{|z|}{x}\right)^{\alpha}}\frac{dx}{x},

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{\alpha\Lambda}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x}e^{-\Lambda^{\alpha}\left(x^{\alpha}+|z|^{\alpha}x^{-\alpha}\right)}dx.

(68)

Which has a known solution given the standard integral formula
(
Zwillinger et al., 2015
, Eq. 3.478.4)
which states that for
\Re{\beta}>0,\real\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\nu-1}e^{-\beta x^{p}-\gamma x^{-p}}=\frac{2}{p}\left(\frac{\gamma}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{\nu}{2p}}K_{\frac{\nu}{p}}\left(2\sqrt{\beta\gamma}\right).

(69)

In this case we set
\nu=0,\beta=\Lambda^{\alpha},p=\alpha
and
\gamma=\Lambda^{\alpha}|z|^{\alpha}
and get the final answer that,

\displaystyle f_{Z}(z)=\frac{\alpha\Lambda^{2}}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{\alpha})^{2}}K_{0}(2\Lambda^{\alpha}|z|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})

(70)

∎

Proof.

The Mellin transform of

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{s-1}f(z)fz

Given that

\displaystyle f_{Z}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\Lambda^{2}}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{\alpha})^{2}}K_{0}(2\Lambda^{\alpha}z^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}).

7
Conclusion

This article has demonstrated that the variance of feedforward layers decays at a superlinear rate, which causes issues when using deep 1-Lipschitz feedforward networks. The problem is that the network’s output and gradient variances decay to zero, which halts training of the deep network.

The decay issue was noted when assuming a weight initialization from the generalized normal distributions (Normal, Uniform, and Laplace); as such, initializing with the standard Kaiming methodology causes a problem. While a solution to forward propagation was demonstrated by setting the bias term to an appropriate level, the vanishing backward propagation variance remains to be addressed and will be addressed in future work.

In addition, the work of
Araujo et al. (2023)
demonstrates the architecture of a 1-Lipschitz network implemented with a residual network structure; however, due to the more complicated interdependence between components, the layer variance for this type of general structure will also be the focus of future work.
