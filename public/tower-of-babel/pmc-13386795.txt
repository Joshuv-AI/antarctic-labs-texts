Examining facilitators, barriers, and social support in PrEP uptake and utilization for young African women: implications for HIV prevention
Authors: Ndidiamaka Amutah-Onukagha, Yoann Sophie Antoine Jean-Felix, Nomhle Khoza, Audrey E. Pettifor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent girls and young women (AGYW) in sub-Saharan Africa experience disproportionately high HIV incidence, making pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) a critical prevention strategy. While perceived HIV risk is known to influence PrEP initiation and continuation, the role of social support in shaping PrEP use among AGYW remains insufficiently understood.MethodsThis study presents a secondary analysis of qualitative interview data collected as part of HPTN 082, an open-label PrEP trial conducted between 2016 and 2018 in Harare, Zimbabwe, and Cape Town and Johannesburg, South Africa. Participants were offered daily oral PrEP (tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine). Sixty-seven AGYW aged 16–24 were purposively sampled from the parent cohort of 426 participants, all of whom were identified as being at high risk for HIV using an empiric risk score. Participants completed in-depth interviews at 13 and/or 26 weeks following study enrollment and PrEP initiation. Interviews explored motivations for PrEP use, experiences of adherence, and social influences. Analysts conducted thematic analysis to examine how social support and perceived HIV risk shaped PrEP initiation, adherence, and discontinuation.ResultsThree interconnected themes emerged: (1) multidimensional social support influencing PrEP adherence, (2) stigma, privacy constraints, and constrained adherence, and (3) perceived HIV risk as a key motivator for PrEP initiation and continuation. Emotional, instrumental, informational, and normative forms of support facilitated adherence in some contexts, while stigma, anticipated judgment, and limited privacy within households led to concealment, missed doses, and discontinuation in others. Perceived HIV risk, particularly related to partner infidelity or uncertainty, motivated PrEP initiation and persistence despite social barriers.ConclusionsSocial support operates as a dynamic, double-edged influence on PrEP use among AGYW, simultaneously enabling and constraining adherence, depending on relational and structural contexts. Interventions that integrate stigma reduction, household- and community-level education, and strategies supporting autonomy and disclosure decision-making may improve sustained PrEP use. Addressing both social dynamics and risk perception remains critical to strengthening HIV prevention efforts.

Introduction

Adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) in sub-Saharan Africa continue to experience a disproportionate burden of HIV infection, particularly during the transition from adolescence to young adulthood. Across southern Africa, HIV prevalence and incidence rise sharply between ages 15–19 and 20–24, with young women consistently experiencing higher rates of infection than their male peers [1, 2]. These disparities are driven by intersecting social, behavioral, and structural factors, including gendered power dynamics, age-disparate partnerships, economic vulnerability, and limited negotiating power within sexual relationships [2, 3].

Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a highly effective biomedical HIV prevention strategy when taken consistently. In this study context, PrEP refers to daily oral tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine, the standard regimen evaluated in HPTN 082. Daily oral PrEP has demonstrated strong efficacy across diverse populations, including young African women, and remains a cornerstone of HIV prevention efforts for AGYW at high risk of acquisition [1, 4]. Despite this effectiveness, real-world uptake and continuation of PrEP among AGYW remain suboptimal. Compared to other populations, AGYW face distinct barriers to sustained use, including stigma, concerns about disclosure, medication side effects, limited privacy, and competing social expectations [5, 6].

While newer PrEP modalities such as long-acting injectable formulations show promise in improving biomedical adherence, emerging evidence suggests that social and relational barriers, including stigma, disclosure concerns, and relationship dynamics, continue to shape prevention behaviors regardless of delivery method [7, 8].

Social support has emerged as a potentially influential, but inconsistently characterized, determinant of PrEP uptake and adherence. For the purposes of this analysis, social support is conceptualized as a multidimensional construct encompassing emotional support (encouragement), instrumental support (reminders or assistance), informational support (understanding of PrEP), and normative support (approval or acceptance of PrEP use). These dimensions may operate differently across relationships and contexts and may simultaneously facilitate or constrain health behaviors.

Social relationships may facilitate PrEP use through encouragement, reminders, and normalization of preventive behaviors. However, they may also function as sources of constraint when PrEP use is associated with sexual activity or HIV infection. These dynamics reflect multiple forms of stigma, including anticipated stigma (fear of judgment), enacted stigma (experienced discrimination), and internalized stigma (internal acceptance of negative beliefs), all of which may influence PrEP-related behaviors.

Existing literature often treats social support as a binary construct, rather than as a relational process that may function as a “double-edged” influence, simultaneously enabling and constraining PrEP use. These dual effects may occur within the same relationships over time or across different actors within an individual’s social network.

Perceived HIV risk is a critical driver of PrEP initiation and persistence among AGYW. Prior research indicates that partner-related factors such as concurrency, geographic separation, and uncertainty about partner fidelity heighten perceived vulnerability and motivate interest in PrEP [9]. However, high perceived risk alone does not ensure sustained adherence when social and household environments are unsupportive. This study draws on qualitative data from HPTN 082 to examine how social support and perceived HIV risk jointly shape PrEP initiation, adherence, and discontinuation among AGYW.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is a secondary analysis of qualitative interview data collected as part of HPTN 082, an open-label trial evaluating AGYW acceptance of and adherence to daily oral PrEP in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. The parent study enrolled 426 AGYW aged 16–24 from Cape Town and Johannesburg, South Africa, and Harare, Zimbabwe between 2016 and 2018.

All participants were identified as being at high risk for HIV acquisition using an empiric HIV risk scoring tool incorporating factors such as age, sexual behavior, partner characteristics (including age disparity and suspected concurrency), and history of sexually transmitted infections [10]. HPTN 082 was designed to evaluate PrEP uptake, adherence, and persistence, including behavioral support components that may have influenced participant experiences.

Qualitative recruitment

Participants were purposively sampled to capture variation in PrEP experiences, including initiation, adherence, discontinuation, and non-initiation. A total of 67 AGYW participated in in-depth interviews conducted at approximately 13 and/or 26 weeks following enrollment.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted by trained, in-country research assistants with experience in qualitative research and familiarity with local contexts. Interviewers received training in ethical research practices, confidentiality, and conducting interviews on sensitive topics.

Interviews were conducted in English, isiZulu, or Shona based on participant preference and lasted approximately 45–60 min. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and translated into English where necessary. Translations were reviewed for accuracy and preservation of meaning.

Interviewers were not involved in clinical care and did not have access to participants’ adherence data, which may have reduced social desirability bias. However, interviewer positionality may have influenced disclosure.

Participants received modest compensation and transportation reimbursement. All data were de-identified and securely stored.

Data analysis

We conducted reflexive thematic analysis, combining inductive coding with sensitizing concepts related to social support and HIV risk perception. Multiple analysts independently coded transcripts and met regularly to refine the codebook and resolve discrepancies.

Themes were developed through iterative comparison across transcripts, with attention to patterns across adherence trajectories and social contexts. Analytic rigor was enhanced through team-based coding, reflexivity, and documentation of analytic decisions.

Thematic saturation was achieved when no new themes or insights emerged from additional transcripts, indicating sufficient depth and variation in the sample.

Ethical statement

The parent HPTN 082 study received ethical approval from institutional review boards at the University of California, San Francisco, and the University of Washington. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, with parental consent procedures followed for minors in accordance with local regulations. The secondary analysis of de-identified qualitative data received approval from the Tufts University School of Medicine Institutional Review Board. The parent study protocol was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02732730).

Results

Three interrelated themes illustrate how social support and perceived HIV risk shaped PrEP initiation, adherence, and discontinuation among AGYW: (1) multidimensional social support as a facilitator of adherence, (2) stigma, household surveillance, and constrained adherence, and (3) perceived HIV risk as a motivator for PrEP use.

Theme 1: multidimensional social support facilitating adherence

Participants described multiple forms of social support, emotional, instrumental, informational, and normative, that facilitated PrEP adherence. Emotional encouragement from family members and partners reinforced participants’ motivation to remain adherent, while instrumental support, such as reminders, helped integrate PrEP into daily routines.

Several participants described how family members became supportive once they understood the purpose of PrEP:“She did not understand what we are taking the pills for and I then explained to her… It’s my father and my mother, the people who motivate me to take it.”

“She did not understand what we are taking the pills for and I then explained to her… It’s my father and my mother, the people who motivate me to take it.”

This shift highlights the importance of informational support in transforming initial skepticism into acceptance. Normative support, expressed through approval and encouragement, contributed to the normalization of PrEP use within households.

Partner support also played a significant role, particularly through reminders and ongoing engagement:“He asks me if I have taken my pill or not… if I have it with me I drink it.”

“He asks me if I have taken my pill or not… if I have it with me I drink it.”

These findings suggest that when social relationships are informed and supportive, they can create enabling environments that reinforce adherence behaviors and reduce stigma.

Theme 2: stigma, privacy constraints, and constrained adherence

In contrast, many participants described social environments that constrained PrEP use. Stigma, both anticipated and enacted, was a central concern, particularly the fear that PrEP would be mistaken for HIV treatment or interpreted as evidence of sexual activity.

Limited privacy within households, described here as “household surveillance,” further exacerbated these concerns. Participants living in shared or crowded environments reported that personal belongings were frequently accessed or monitored, making discreet medication use difficult:“Everyone will just go through each other’s stuff… I realized that if I take the tablet I may miss them.”

“Everyone will just go through each other’s stuff… I realized that if I take the tablet I may miss them.”

In these contexts, participants often engaged in concealment strategies, including hiding medication or skipping doses to avoid disclosure. These behaviors sometimes disrupted adherence routines and contributed to discontinuation.

Concerns about side effects also intersected with these challenges, particularly when adherence was inconsistent:“When you return you won’t just get home and start taking… you will be hesitant.”

“When you return you won’t just get home and start taking… you will be hesitant.”

These findings illustrate how social environments characterized by surveillance and stigma can actively undermine adherence, even in the absence of direct opposition. The lack of privacy and fear of judgment created structural and psychological barriers that constrained consistent PrEP use.

Theme 3: perceived HIV risk as a motivator

Across interviews, perceived HIV risk emerged as a central motivator for PrEP initiation and continuation. Participants frequently referenced the high prevalence of HIV in their communities and concerns about partner behavior, particularly infidelity and concurrency.“HIV is spreading a lot amongst young people so I thought if I drink the pill I will be safer.”

“HIV is spreading a lot amongst young people so I thought if I drink the pill I will be safer.”

Perceived risk was often relationally constructed, shaped by uncertainty about partners’ fidelity:“I don’t only think of my cheating, I think about his cheating… that’s what motivated me most.”

“I don’t only think of my cheating, I think about his cheating… that’s what motivated me most.”

These accounts demonstrate that participants actively assessed their vulnerability within the context of their relationships and used PrEP as a means of maintaining control over their health.

However, high perceived risk did not consistently translate into sustained adherence. In environments where stigma and lack of privacy were present, social constraints often outweighed individual motivation. This highlights a critical tension: while perceived risk drives initiation, social context determines sustainability.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that PrEP use among AGYW is shaped by the interaction of perceived HIV risk and multidimensional social support within relational and structural contexts. Social support functions as a dynamic, double-edged influence, simultaneously enabling and constraining adherence.

Consistent with prior research, supportive relationships improved adherence by providing emotional reinforcement and practical assistance [11–13]. However, this study extends existing literature by illustrating how supportive and constraining dynamics coexist within the same social environments.

Stigma and constrained household environments emerged as key barriers. Even in the absence of overt opposition, the lack of privacy and fear of disclosure created conditions that were incompatible with consistent PrEP use. These findings underscore the importance of addressing not only attitudes but also structural features of living environments.

The persistence of social barriers across PrEP modalities suggests that biomedical innovation alone is insufficient. Recent evidence on long-acting PrEP similarly highlights the continued importance of social and relational determinants [7, 8].

Limitations

Participants were enrolled in a clinical trial and were required to be English literate, which may limit generalizability. Trial-related support may have influenced experiences. Interviewer positionality may have affected disclosure.

Conclusion

PrEP adherence among AGYW is not solely an individual behavior but is deeply embedded in social and relational contexts. Recognizing social support as a multidimensional and dynamic influence provides a critical pathway for designing more effective, contextually grounded HIV prevention interventions.
