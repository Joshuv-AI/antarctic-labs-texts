The application of novel ultrathin iron sulphide nanosheets in the nanotherapy of post-traumatic osteoarthritis
Authors: Jiajun Xu, Wenbo Wang, Li Liu, Chenglong Wang, Nan Liu, Xiaomei Wang, Lianxin Li, Jinlei Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) is a progressive joint disease characterised by cartilage degeneration and subchondral bone remodelling, in which persistent oxidative stress and inflammation jointly drive structural deterioration and functional loss. Most therapeutic strategies primarily alleviate symptoms or target single tissues, with limited efficacy in modifying disease progression. Here, we developed degradable ultrathin iron sulphide nanosheets (FeSNSs) and prepared a PEGylated therapeutic formulation (FeS-PEG) that concurrently protects articular cartilage and stabilises subchondral bone by alleviating oxidative-stress-associated joint damage in PTOA. The resulting FeS-PEG formulation exhibited favourable colloidal stability and pronounced ROS-scavenging activity. In vitro, FeS-PEG reduced intracellular ROS and lipid peroxidation in H2O2- and IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes, restored antioxidant enzyme activity, attenuated COX-2/iNOS and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, and preserved chondrocyte phenotype by promoting COL II expression while suppressing MMP3- and MMP13-mediated matrix degradation. Transcriptomic profiling further revealed that FeS-PEG reprogrammed IL-1β-induced PTOA-like gene expression, rebalancing extracellular matrix organisation, inflammatory signalling, and redox homeostasis. In an anterior cruciate ligament transection-induced PTOA rat model, intra-articular FeS-PEG administration significantly reduced cartilage erosion, OARSI histological scores, and local inflammation. Micro-CT revealed preserved subchondral bone microarchitecture with increased bone volume fraction and decreased trabecular separation, indicating inhibition of early maladaptive bone remodelling. Collectively, the findings establish FeS-PEG as a dual-functional, ROS-targeting nanotherapeutic platform that couples cartilage protection with subchondral bone preservation, offering a promising disease-modifying strategy for PTOA and broadening the potential applications of FeS-based nanomedicine in orthopaedics.Keywords: Iron sulphide nanosheets, Osteoarthritis, Cartilage, Subchondral bone, Reactive oxygen species

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a whole-joint degenerative disease and a leading global cause of disability. Recent epidemiological studies indicate that the global burden of OA increases continuously, with increasing prevalence, incidence, and projected case numbers driven by population ageing, obesity, and other risk factors [1,2]. Among various OA subtypes, post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) develops following joint injury, accounts for a substantial proportion of OA cases in young and active populations, and provides a well-defined clinical scenario for mechanism-based intervention. Despite incremental advances in symptomatic care, there are no approved disease-modifying OA drugs that demonstrably halt or reverse structural damage, underscoring a pressing need for mechanism-based interventions [3,4]. In parallel with advances in therapeutic strategies, in vitro models that more closely recapitulate the physiological conditions of OA are also emerging [5]. Pathobiology studies now view OA as a biomechanical-inflammatory-metabolic disorder of the entire joint unit, in which oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation act as central drivers of chondrocyte dysfunction and matrix loss. Although increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels may also occur in other inflammatory, traumatic, and tissue-repair processes, oxidative stress remains an important pathological contributor to cartilage degeneration and subchondral bone remodelling in PTOA. Excess ROS, including H2O2, O2•-, and peroxynitrite, accumulate in articular cartilage while endogenous antioxidant defences decline, tipping redox homeostasis toward catabolism [6,7]. Converging evidence further implicates mitochondrial dysfunction in PTOA-induced chondrocytes as a prominent ROS source that promotes senescence and apoptosis, linking bioenergetic failure to matrix degradation [[8], [9], [10]]. In parallel, subchondral bone remodelling (trabecular rarefaction followed by sclerosis) emerges early and feeds back to cartilage architecture via mechanical and biochemical crosstalk at the bone-cartilage unit, necessitating therapies that address both compartments [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

Given this biology, redox-active nanotherapies that quench excessive ROS and restore the joint microenvironment have gained increasing attention. Prior studies showed that black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs) scavenge ROS, normalise chondrocyte anabolism-catabolism, and promote subchondral osteogenesis in PTOA models, demonstrating dual cartilage-bone benefits [15]. Other nano-antioxidant systems, including cerium oxide nanoparticles, MnO2 nanomaterials, polydopamine-based nanozymes, and Mo-based clusters/MoS2 platforms, have similarly demonstrated ROS-quenching and chondroprotective effects after intra-articular delivery [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Beyond ROS-scavenging nanomaterials, targeted nanocarriers can enhance drug delivery to diseased chondrocytes and alleviate OA, further highlighting the translational potential of nanomedicine-based therapy [24]. These findings support ROS scavenging as a rational therapeutic strategy for mitigating oxidative-stress-associated joint damage in PTOA and motivate exploration of new 2D materials with complementary mechanisms and degradability.

The present study developed ultrathin iron sulphide nanosheets (FeSNSs) as a distinct, biodegradable two-dimensional (2D) platform engineered for co-targeting cartilage and subchondral bone. FeSNSs are layered 2D materials composed of iron and sulphur with a structure analogous to black phosphorus [25]. Owing to their fundamental physicochemical properties, 2D FeSNSs have shown promise across biomedical areas, including drug delivery [26], photothermal therapy [27], anti-tumour applications [28,29], and tissue engineering [30]. In addition, FeSNSs possess antioxidant capability that enables efficient scavenging of ROS, thereby mitigating both acute and chronic inflammation and demonstrating potential for treating inflammation-related diseases such as arthritis. Their biocompatibility stems from the roles of iron and sulphur as essential elements in the human body, with iron serving as a key component of erythrocytes. The 2D morphology confers a large specific surface area, further enhancing ROS-scavenging efficiency and therapeutic performance in inflammatory settings. Upon degradation, FeSNSs primarily yield iron sulphide species at non-toxic levels in vivo. Furthermore, FeSNSs comprise van der Waals-stacked FeS layers with redox-active surfaces that support rapid interfacial electron transfer; in aqueous environments they readily interact with O2/H2O2, endowing broad ROS-regulatory behaviour [31,32]. Recently developed iron-sulphide-based nanozymes exhibited superoxide dismutase (SOD)-/catalase (CAT)-like activities and hydrogen polysulfide–releasing properties, together producing broad-spectrum ROS scavenging and anti-inflammatory effects in vivo [33]. To enhance colloidal stability and biocompatibility, PEGylation was adopted in the current study, an approach in clinical nanomedicine [34].

We investigated the performance of the biodegradable 2D FeSNSs platform in the treatment of PTOA. Our findings showed that FeSNSs act as effective, biocompatible ROS scavengers in chondrocytes, thereby reducing joint inflammation. More importantly, FeSNSs attenuated abnormal subchondral bone remodelling and preserved subchondral bone architecture during the early stages of PTOA. In addition to characterising the therapeutic effects of FeSNSs, we performed a direct comparison with BPNSs to clarify the advantages of the proposed platform in terms of ROS removal efficiency and degradation behaviour. Collectively, these results highlighted FeSNSs as a highly efficient nanoplatform capable of modifying PTOA progression by neutralising ROS and supporting subchondral bone repair in early disease (Scheme 1).

ROS play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of PTOA, where their excess drives cartilage breakdown and resorption of the subchondral bone; FeS-PEG exerts therapeutic effects on PTOA by scavenging ROS in chondrocytes and promoting subchondral bone repair.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fe (NH4)2·(SO4)2·6H2O, polyethylenimine (PEI), polyethylene glycol (PEG), trisodium citrate, thioacetamide and bisBenzimide H33342 trihydrochloride (Hoechst 33342), FITC, BCA protein assay kit, cell lysis buffer for western blotting, and immunoprecipitation, primary antibody dilution buffer and other commonly used buffers were purchased from Aladdin Chemical Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Black phosphorus nanoplates dispersion (BPNSs) were purchased from Nanjing XFNANO Materials Tech Co. Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Foetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from Lonsera (USA). Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and penicillin-streptomycin were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). An •OH scavenging assay kit was obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) were sourced from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Cy5.5 and WSP-1 were purchased from Shanghai Maokang Biotechnology Co. Ltd. GAPDH monoclonal antibody was purchased from Servicebio. Calcein-acetoxymethyl ester (calcein-AM), propidium iodide (PI), 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), 4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), superoxide dismutase (SOD) assay kit, lipid peroxidation malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit, dexamethasone, 4% paraformaldehyde fix solution, and bovine serum albumin (BSA) were procured from Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co.

Measurements

The morphology and elemental composition of the FeSNSs were characterised using TEM (Talos F200i) and AFM (Dimension Icon, Bruker). UV-visible absorption spectra were recorded on a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were acquired with a Magna-560 spectrometer. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (STA 200, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) was used to characterise the chemical composition. Metal contents were determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (G8018A, Agilent, USA). Intracellular ROS were visualised using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; DMi8, Leica, Germany). Elemental composition was analysed via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB 250 Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA). The diffraction characteristics of FeSNSs were examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku SmartLab SE, Japan).

Cell culture

Primary chondrocytes and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were harvested from Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats obtained from Huafukang Bioscience Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). BMSCs and chondrocytes were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. For harvesting, cells were detached using 0.25% (w/v) trypsin in PBS and subsequently resuspended in fresh medium for downstream experiments.

Synthesis of FeSNSs

Fe (NH4)2·(SO4)2 (0.6 mmol) and trisodium citrate (0.2 mmol) were first dissolved in 15 mL ethylene glycol (EG). A PEI solution (500 mg in 5 mL EG) was then added, and the mixture was stirred for 120 min. Thioacetamide and triethanolamine (2 mL) were subsequently introduced and stirring continued for an additional 5 min. The resulting precursor was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 200 °C for 24 h. The final product was recovered using centrifugation.

Synthesis of FeS-PEG

FeSNSs (1 mg) and PEG (50 mg) were dissolved in 5 mL of anhydrous ethanol and stirred under dark conditions for 6 h at room temperature. The product was centrifuged, washed, and finally dispersed in anhydrous ethanol or deionized water. PEGylated FeSNSs (FeS-PEG) were used as the therapeutic formulation in subsequent cellular and animal experiments.

Degradation behaviour of FeSNSs

FeSNSs were dispersed in simulated body fluid (SBF) and incubated at room temperature, during which colour changes and UV-vis absorbance were recorded at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 h.

In vitro cytotoxicity study

Cytotoxicity of FeS-PEG toward rat primary chondrocytes and BMSCs was assessed using a CCK-8 assay. Cells were seeded onto 96-well plates at 1 × 105 cells·mL−1 and pre-cultured in DMEM at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. The cells were then treated with different concentrations of FeS-PEG for 24 h or 48 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Antioxidant activity detection

Standard antioxidant assays (DPPH•, ABTS•+ and •OH) were monitored using UV–vis spectroscopy; H2O2-FeSNSs interactions using Raman; and •OH, 1O2, and O2•- scavenging using electron spin resonance (ESR). For DPPH•, ethanolic DPPH (0.04 mg mL−1) was mixed 1:1 with FeSNSs (2 mL each), incubated 30 min, and read at 519 nm. ABTS•+ was generated by incubating ABTS (7.4 mmol L−1, 0.2 mL) with K2S2O8 (2.6 mmol L−1, 0.2 mL) for 24 h in the dark, diluted 50× with PBS, then reacted with FeSNSs (0.6 mL) in ABTS•+ (2.4 mL) for 10 min and measured at 734 nm. •OH scavenging followed the kit protocol (A550). Before spectroscopy, all samples were centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 10 min) to remove residual FeSNSs. Each test was run in triplicate. H2O2 scavenging was assessed by mixing 10% H2O2 (2.9 M) with FeSNSs (50 μg mL−1), depositing the mixture on a glass slide, and collecting Raman spectra for 6 min at room temperature. ESR assays used FeSO4/H2O2 (•OH), TiO2 photocatalysis (1O2), and xanthine/xanthine oxidase (O2•-); TEMP trapping for 1O2 and DMPO trapping for •OH and O2•-.

Determination of ROS under different pH and H2O2 conditions

The oxidation of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) in the presence of FeSNSs was evaluated under different pH and H2O2 conditions. For the H2O2 concentration-dependent assay, FeSNSs were incubated with TMB in the presence of H2O2 at concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 μM. For the pH-dependent assay, FeSNSs were incubated with TMB and H2O2 in buffer solutions at pH 4.5, 5.5, 6.5, and 7.4. The absorbance spectra of the reaction products generated from the FeSNSs were recorded over 550–750 nm, and the absorbance signal around 650 nm was used to compare ROS generation under different experimental conditions.

Live/dead cell staining

In vitro therapeutic efficacy was assessed using a live/dead viability and cytotoxicity assay kit, in which viable cells are stained with calcein AM and nonviable cells are stained with PI. Chondrocytes were seeded onto six-well plates at 4 × 105 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cells were then subjected to the indicated treatments for 24 h, and subsequently incubated in DMEM containing calcein AM (2 μM) and PI (4.5 μM) for 30 min in the dark, followed by PBS rinsing. Fluorescence staining patterns were subsequently imaged using CLSM.

Intracellular ROS detection

Chondrocytes were exposed to H2O2 (400 μM) or IL-1β (10 ng·mL-1) for 24 h to establish oxidative stress and OA-like cell models, respectively [15,35,36]. FeS-PEG was co-administered with H2O2 or IL-1β. Intracellular ROS were assessed using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA, following incubation with the treated cells for 30 min, rinsing with PBS, and fluorescence imaging under a microscope. In H2O2-challenged chondrocytes, total SOD activity was measured using a WST-8-based SOD assay kit. Lipid peroxidation in the various treatment groups was evaluated with an MDA assay kit. Flow cytometry (Beckman CytoFLEX SRT, Beckman Coulter, USA) was performed to evaluate intracellular ROS levels. After the indicated treatments, chondrocytes were washed with PBS and incubated with DCFH-DA working solution at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The cells were then washed three times with PBS, harvested, and resuspended in PBS. DCF fluorescence was detected using the FITC channel. Mitochondrial ROS were further examined using the mitochondria-targeted fluorescent probe MitoNeoD. Chondrocytes were treated with IL-1β (10 ng·mL-1) alone or co-treated with FeS-PEG at concentrations of 10, 20, or 50 μg·mL-1 for 24 h. After treatment, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with MitoNeoD at 37 °C in the dark. Cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342. Representative fluorescence images were acquired using CLSM under identical acquisition settings.

RT-qPCR analysis of oxidative stress-treated and osteoarthritic chondrocytes

To assess intracellular oxidative stress in both normal and OA chondrocytes following FeS-PEG exposure, RT-qPCR was performed for key antioxidant enzymes (SOD3, GPX, CAT), and inflammatory mediators (iNOS, COX-2). Chondrocytes were seeded and co-incubated for 24 h with FeS-PEG at graded concentrations together with H2O2 or IL-1β, as described in Section 2.11. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol, and cDNA was synthesized with a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit. SYBR Green-based RT-qPCR was then conducted on a StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (ABI, USA) using gene-specific primers and FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (ROX; Roche, Switzerland). Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed using a buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors according to the manufacturer's protocol, and total protein was extracted. Total protein was collected, and concentrations were determined using the BCA assay kit (Aladdin, China). After normalisation to the same protein concentration, 5 μL of each sample was loaded per lane for SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk or 5% BSA, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against inflammatory cytokines and matrix-degradation markers, including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-17, MMP3, and MMP13, as well as signalling pathway-related proteins, including Nrf2, HO-1, p-p65, p65, p-Akt, Akt, p-p38, p38, p-ERK1/2, ERK1/2, p-JNK, JNK, p53, p21, and GAPDH. The membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and protein bands were visualised using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system. All Western blot experiments were independently repeated three times. Protein band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. Antibody information is provided in Table S2.

RNA sequencing of chondrocytes

Chondrocytes were seeded and divided into two experimental groups. The IL-1β group comprised cells stimulated with IL-1β (10 ng mL−1) to induce a PTOA-like phenotype (n = 3). The FeS-PEG group received a co-treatment of IL-1β (10 ng mL−1) and FeS-PEG (10 μg mL−1) (n = 3). After 24 h, cells were collected and total RNA was isolated using TRIzol. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) libraries were generated and sequenced on a BGI platform. Transcript abundance was quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads. Differential expression was evaluated with the DESeq algorithm, with significance based on set P-values and false discovery rate (FDR) thresholds (FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1). To interpret functional implications, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was performed using the R package clusterProfiler [37,38], with terms obtained from the GO resource (http://www.geneontology.org/) and tested using Fisher's exact test. Pathway enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was assessed against the KEGG database with clusterProfiler using Fisher's exact test, considering a set P-value threshold [38,39]. Associations between representative GO terms and corresponding DEGs were depicted via a GOChord plot. Visual outputs—including a circular heatmap of selected DEGs, KEGG pathway classification summary, and the GOChord plot were produced using the online bioinformatics platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn).

To validate the DEGs identified in the RNA-seq analysis, the levels of representative cartilage matrix-related genes (COL2A1 and ACAN), matrix-degradation genes (MMP13 and MMP3), and an inflammatory cytokine-related gene (IL-6) were analysed as described under Section 2.12. The primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Construction of PTOA models

SD rats (6 weeks old, weighing approximately 200 g) were obtained from Huafukang Bioscience Co. (Beijing, China). All animal experiments were fully authorised and approved by the Experimental Animal Care Ethics Committee of Shandong First Medical University (approval no. LS2024006).

To assess the therapeutic efficacy of FeS-PEG in treating PTOA in vivo, the animals were randomly assigned to one of four groups: sham, control, FeS-PEG (10 μg/mL), and FeS-PEG (50 μg/mL). Briefly, under isoflurane anaesthesia, the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) of the right hind limb was carefully exposed and transected under direct visualisation in the control and FeS-PEG treatment groups. In the sham group, the ACL was exposed but left intact. Two weeks after surgery, FeS-PEG (10 μg/mL or 50 μg/mL) was intra-articularly injected every 2 d over the course of 2 weeks. The control group (PTOA knees) and sham group (normal knees) received normal saline injections at the same time intervals. The rats were euthanised at 4 and 8 weeks post-ACLT surgery for further analysis [[40], [41], [42]].

Histological assessment

After receiving the scheduled treatments, whole knee joints from ACLT-induced PTOA rats were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 3 d. The samples were then decalcified, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), toluidine blue (TB), and safranin O/fast green (SO-FG) staining were performed for histological evaluation. Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scores were used for the quantitative analysis of histological changes in cartilage. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was used to evaluate osteoclast activity in the subchondral bone region. In addition, immunohistochemical staining of COL II, RUNX2, OCN, RANKL, OPG, and phosphorylated NF-κB p65 (p-p65) was performed to evaluate cartilage matrix preservation, osteogenesis-related changes, osteoclastogenic regulation, and inflammatory signalling. TRAP and immunohistochemical staining were quantitatively analysed using ImageJ.

Micro-CT analysis

The dissected knee joints were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 3 d before being imaged using a micro-CT scanner (Nemo NMC-100, PINGSENG Healthcare Inc., China) under the following conditions: 45 kV tube voltage, 145 μA tube current, 400 ms integration time, and 15 μm voxel/pixel resolution. The acquired scanning data were processed and analysed with Avatar version 1.6.6, enabling the generation of three-dimensional reconstructions of the knee joints. The trabecular bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) were quantified from the 3D images.

Biosafety evaluation

To assess in vivo biosafety, rats were sacrificed after the treatment to collect the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) and blood. The tissue samples were stained with H&E. The blood was used for serum biochemistry assay of representative indicators.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments (n = 3 biological replicates per group, unless otherwise stated in the figure legends). For in vivo studies, each group contained 6 rats. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-hoc test was performed. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student's t-test was used when variances were equal (tested using F-test); otherwise, Welch's t-test was applied. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Design, synthesis, and characterisation of FeS-PEG

Ammonium iron (II) sulfate hexahydrate and thioacetamide were reacted under solvothermal conditions to obtain iron sulphide nanosheets (FeSNSs). To further improve colloidal stability and biocompatibility, the nanosheet surface was subsequently modified with polyethylene glycol (PEG), yielding PEGylated FeS (FeS-PEG).

TEM revealed that the FeSNSs predominantly exhibited a uniform 2D sheet-like morphology with sharp lateral edges and relatively low contrast, consistent with an ultrathin architecture; the lateral size was approximately 100 nm, and the dispersion was homogeneous, confirming successful formation of nanoscale sheets (Fig. 1A). AFM analysis showed an average height of about 2 nm, further corroborating their ultrathin 2D nature (Fig. 1B). Elemental mapping demonstrated co-localised signals of Fe and S across the entire flake area, indicating that the nanosheets are mainly composed of iron and sulphur and validating the successful synthesis of FeSNSs (Fig. 1C).

(C) STEM–EDS elemental maps showing the distributions of Fe, S, and N. (D) High-resolution XPS of S 2p with S-Fe (∼162-163 eV). (E) High-resolution XPS of Fe 2p indicating Fe2+/Fe3+ components with shake-up satellites. (F) Powder XRD pattern showing broadened diffraction peaks associated with the nanoscale features of FeSNSs. (G) DLS size distributions: ∼112 nm (FeSNSs) vs ∼183 nm (FeS-PEG). (H) Zeta-potential analysis of FeSNSs and FeS-PEG. (I) FTIR spectra of FeSNSs and FeS-PEG.

XPS survey analysis confirmed the presence of Fe and S, together with surface-associated C, O, and N signals (Fig. S1). The characteristic peaks of S 2p1/2, S 2p3/2, Fe 2p1/2, and Fe 2p3/2 are consistent with the expected chemical states of FeS (Fig. 1D and E). Powder XRD showed broadened diffraction peaks consistent with the ultrathin nanoscale morphology of FeSNSs (Fig. 1F). Dynamic light scattering and zeta potential measurements further supported successful PEGylation. Unmodified FeSNSs displayed a unimodal hydrodynamic size distribution in aqueous dispersion, whereas the hydrodynamic diameter of FeS-PEG increased compared to FeSNSs, indicating the formation of a hydrated PEG corona (Fig. 1G). Simultaneously, the surface potential of FeSNSs decreased after PEG modification (Fig. 1H), reflecting electrostatic shielding by the neutral PEG chains and suggesting improved stability and biocompatibility under physiological conditions.

FTIR spectra provided additional evidence for both the FeSNS framework and surface functionalization (Fig. 1I). Spectral analysis showed a characteristic FeS stretching vibration band at approximately 600 cm−1, while FeS-PEG retained this band and exhibited new absorption peaks corresponding to the PEG backbone, including C-O-C stretching around 1100 cm−1, as well as C-N (∼1350 cm−1) and N-H (∼1610 cm−1) vibrations. These features collectively indicate that PEG was successfully associated with the surface of FeSNSs without disrupting the underlying iron sulphide lattice. TGA showed different thermal decomposition profiles for FeSNSs and FeS-PEG (Fig. S2). The marked weight loss of FeS-PEG at approximately 350-420 °C was attributed to the decomposition of surface organic components, further supporting successful PEG modification.

Collectively, these results demonstrate the successful preparation of FeSNSs and their PEGylated counterpart FeS-PEG. The synthesized FeSNSs are ultrathin, structurally well-defined, and, after PEG modification, exhibit enhanced colloidal stability, providing a robust physicochemical basis for subsequent studies on ROS regulation and biological performance in the intra-articular environment.

In vitro degradability and ROS-scavenging capabilities of FeSNSs

Degradability is a major bottleneck for the clinical translation of 2D nanomaterials, because their long-term fate in vivo, including degradation pathways, degradation products, and clearance routes, is tightly coupled to safety. Accordingly, nanosystems should exhibit controllable degradation behaviour to be considered for clinical application [43,44]. In this context, we first examined the degradability of FeSNSs dispersed in SBF, and incubated at room temperature for 6 h in terms of their optical/spectroscopic properties and morphological changes at different time points (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 h). The colour intensity of the FeSNSs suspension gradually weakened over time, attributed to its inherent strong reduction and degradation behaviour (Fig. 2A). Consistently, UV-vis spectra displayed a significant decrease in absorbance intensity as the dispersion time increased (Fig. 2B). The biodegradability of FeSNSs was also compared with that of BPNSs. BPNSs also showed gradual changes in colour and absorbance over the same period, although these changes appeared less pronounced than those observed for FeSNSs (Fig. S3). These observations suggest that FeSNSs underwent more pronounced degradation-related optical changes under the present experimental conditions. Time-resolved Raman spectroscopy of FeSNSs-H2O2 mixtures (Fig. S4) showed a synchronous attenuation of FeS-related vibrational bands and H2O2 characteristic peaks, indicating that H2O2 is rapidly consumed during the redox interaction with FeSNSs, consistent with previous observations [45,46].

(B) UV-vis absorbance spectra of FeSNSs (100 μg/mL) at different degradation times, (C-E) ESR spectra of •OH, O2−, and 1O2 in the presence or absence of FeSNSs. (F-I) The scavenging activity of the FeSNSs toward three typical free radicals was monitored via UV-vis spectroscopy and quantitatively analysed: (F, G) ABTS and (H, I) DPPH. (J) •OH-scavenging ability of the FeSNSs monitored via UV-vis spectroscopy and (K) its corresponding quantitative analysis. All data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Building on this, we conducted a systematic assessment of the radical-scavenging performance of FeSNSs. To benchmark their antioxidant capacity in chemical assays, we employed ABTS•+ and DPPH• decolorisation tests, which are classical electron-transfer-based methods for evaluating radical-scavenging activity [47]. FeSNSs induced a distinct concentration-dependent decrease in ABTS•+ absorbance (Fig. 2F), with a scavenging efficiency of 78.7% at 5 μg mL−1 (Fig. 2G). In the DPPH• system, increasing the FeSNS concentration from 3 to 15 μg mL−1 led to a significant and continuous reduction in the DPPH• signal (Fig. 2H and I), indicating strong scavenging ability toward organic nitrogen radicals as well. According to the commercial hydroxyl radical (•OH) assay kit, FeSNSs achieved •OH-scavenging efficiencies of 58.3% and 65.9% at 30 and 40 μg mL−1, respectively (Fig. 2J and K). The ROS-scavenging ability of FeSNSs was also compared with that of BPNSs. When compared at the same concentrations, FeSNSs showed higher scavenging efficiencies for ABTS•+ and •OH than BPNSs, and slightly stronger DPPH• scavenging activity (Fig. S5). Under our experimental conditions, FeSNSs exhibited broad-spectrum and efficient quenching activity toward multiple representative ROS [48]. Furthermore, using a well-established DMPO/TEMP spin-trapping protocol [49], clear characteristic ESR signals of •OH, O2•- and 1O2 were detected in the control systems, whereas the corresponding signals were markedly attenuated upon addition of FeSNSs (Fig. 2C–E). The high degree of concordance between ESR spin-trapping and multiple in vitro radical-scavenging assays consistently supports the function of FeSNSs as efficient ROS nano-scavengers with a clear dose–response relationship.

FeS-based nanozymes can exhibit enzyme-mimetic antioxidant activities, whose relative contributions are regulated by microenvironmental factors such as pH, oxygen tension, and substrate type [33]. Given that FeSNSs contain iron, we clarified their redox behaviour under different microenvironmental conditions. Classical homogeneous Fenton reactions generally occur under strongly acidic conditions and are mediated by the reaction between free Fe2+ and H2O2 [50,51]. However, the iron in FeSNSs is incorporated within the FeS nanostructure and should not be simply regarded as equivalent to freely dissolved and highly reactive Fe2+ systems. Therefore, to evaluate the ROS-regulatory behaviour and safety profile of FeSNSs under different pH values and H2O2 concentrations, we examined changes in the corresponding absorbance signals. The absorbance signal around 650 nm was highest under strongly acidic conditions (pH 4.5), whereas the signal was overall much lower under near-physiological or mildly acidic conditions (pH 5.5–7.5). Meanwhile, the absorbance signal progressively increased as the H2O2 concentration increased from 0.5 to 4.0 μM, suggesting that stronger acidity and a higher peroxide load may favour FeSNS-mediated Fenton-like ROS generation (Fig. S6). This is consistent with recent studies showing that acidic and H2O2-rich microenvironments can promote FeS decomposition and ·OH generation, whereas neutral or low-H2O2 conditions are more compatible with ROS-scavenging behaviour [52]. The ROS-scavenging ability of FeSNSs may be attributed to their ultrathin two-dimensional structure and redox-active FeS surface, providing abundant active sites for interfacial electron-transfer reactions with ROS. The SOD-like and CAT-like activities of FeS-based nanomaterials may further contribute to ROS elimination under PTOA-relevant conditions. Overall, under the near-physiological or mildly acidic conditions more relevant to PTOA [53], FeSNSs predominantly exhibit ROS-scavenging rather than ROS-generating activity. These results support the safety and functional validity of FeSNSs as an intra-articular nanomaterial.

In vitro protective effect of FeS-PEG on chondrocytes against ROS

Before probing the antioxidant properties, we first evaluated the cytocompatibility of FeS-PEG using a CCK-8 assay. Primary rat chondrocytes and BMSCs were exposed to a range of FeS-PEG concentrations for 24 h and 48 h. No obvious decline in cell viability was observed in either cell type at any tested concentration or time point (Fig. 3A and B); overall, mean viability remained close to, or slightly above, that of the untreated controls. These data indicate that FeS-PEG exhibits negligible cytotoxicity within this dose window, providing a safe concentration range for subsequent studies on ROS scavenging and cytoprotective effects in chondrocytes. The cytotoxicity of FeS-PEG was also compared with that of BPNSs. Both nanoplatforms exhibited good in vitro cytocompatibility under the present experimental conditions (Fig. S7). On this basis, we examined whether FeS-PEG could protect chondrocytes from oxidative damage. Exposure of chondrocytes to 400 μM H2O2 for 24 h markedly reduced cell viability, whereas co-treatment with increasing concentrations of FeS-PEG led to a gradual recovery of viability toward baseline, as quantified using the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 3C). This suggests that under oxidative stress, FeS-PEG attenuates H2O2-induced cell death in a concentration-dependent manner.

To visualise the impact of FeS-PEG on cell survival more directly, calcein AM/propidium iodide (PI) live/dead staining was performed. In the untreated control group, most cells were viable and exhibited strong green fluorescence, whereas H2O2 alone induced a large population of PI-positive (red) cells, indicative of extensive cell death. In contrast, addition of FeS-PEG in the presence of H2O2 substantially increased the proportion of calcein-positive live cells, with a clear dose-dependent trend with rising FeS-PEG concentration (Fig. 3D), further confirming the robust protective effect of FeS-PEG against oxidative injury in chondrocytes. We validated this protective effect from the perspective of intracellular ROS burden and oxidative stress markers. To mimic an inflammatory PTOA-like environment in vitro, chondrocytes were stimulated with IL-1β (10 ng mL−1) or H2O2 (400 μM) [36,54]. Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA probe [55,56]. Both stimuli markedly enhanced DCF fluorescence, indicating a pronounced increase in intracellular ROS. Notably, treatment with FeS-PEG visibly reduced DCF fluorescence in both models (Fig. 3E), demonstrating that FeS-PEG effectively suppresses excessive ROS accumulation in chondrocytes under both inflammatory and oxidative stress conditions. Flow cytometry was used to evaluate intracellular ROS levels. IL-1β stimulation markedly increased DCF fluorescence intensity, as shown by a rightward shift of the fluorescence peak. In contrast, FeS-PEG treatment reduced DCF fluorescence intensity and shifted the peak toward the control level, consistent with the fluorescence imaging results (Fig. S8). MitoNeoD staining similarly revealed increased mitochondrial ROS after IL-1β stimulation, which was visibly attenuated by FeS-PEG treatment (Fig. S9). These data further confirm that FeS-PEG effectively suppresses IL-1β-induced ROS accumulation in chondrocytes and help restore intracellular redox homeostasis.

We assessed lipid peroxidation based on MDA levels [57,58]. Compared with the control group, H2O2 stimulation caused a sharp increase in MDA content in chondrocytes, whereas FeS-PEG treatment reduced MDA levels in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3F), suggesting that FeS-PEG alleviates membrane lipid peroxidation. In parallel, the endogenous antioxidant defence was evaluated based on SOD activity. The WST-8-based SOD activity assay showed that H2O2 significantly inhibited SOD activity, while FeS-PEG restored SOD activity toward near-normal levels in a dose-responsive fashion (Fig. 3G). These findings indicate that FeS-PEG not only directly scavenges ROS but also helps maintain or re-establish the intrinsic antioxidant capacity of chondrocytes. We examined the mRNA expression of antioxidant enzymes and inflammatory markers using RT-qPCR. Relative to the injury/inflammation groups, FeS-PEG treatment markedly upregulated key antioxidant enzymes including CAT, GPX and SOD3 in chondrocytes (Fig. 3H–J), indicating the recovery and reinforcement of endogenous antioxidant defences. Therefore, the chemical ROS-scavenging activity of FeS-PEG translates into intracellular redox regulation, thereby reducing oxidative damage in stressed chondrocytes. At the inflammatory level, COX-2 and iNOS are well-recognised mediators of catabolic responses and cartilage erosion in PTOA; IL-1β robustly induces their expression in chondrocytes, and inhibition of these pathways can ameliorate PTOA-like phenotypes [[59], [60], [61]]. In our model, both IL-1β and H2O2 upregulated COX-2 and iNOS expression, whereas FeS-PEG treatment significantly reduced their mRNA levels (Fig. 3K and S10).

Collectively, these results demonstrated that FeS-PEG is not only highly cytocompatible in vitro but also capable of re-establishing redox homeostasis in stressed chondrocytes and dampening downstream inflammatory signalling, thereby providing a coherent mechanistic basis for its cytoprotective effects.

Transcriptomic analysis of FeS-PEG activity in chondrocytes under IL-1β-induced PTOA

The loss of COL II (COL2A1) is a hallmark of cartilage degeneration during the progression of PTOA [62]. As the principal fibrillar component that maintains the structural integrity of hyaline cartilage and a classical phenotypic marker of chondrocyte homeostasis, a reduction in COL II expression directly reflects impaired matrix synthesis and enhanced catabolic activity. Thus, changes in COL II serve as a sensitive indicator of the restoration of the anabolic capacity of cartilage. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that IL-1β markedly diminished COL II signals, whereas treatment with FeS-PEG visibly restored COL II fluorescence in a dose-dependent manner, with fluorescence intensity progressively approaching that of the unstimulated control (Fig. 4A). These data suggest that FeS-PEG effectively corrects the impairment of matrix synthesis in chondrocytes under inflammatory stress. Cartilage destruction in PTOA is largely driven by MMPs. Among them, MMP13 (collagenase-3) is the key enzyme responsible for COL II degradation in PTOA cartilage, while MMP3 (stromelysin-1) can cleave proteoglycans and activate pro-collagenases, thereby amplifying matrix breakdown [[63], [64], [65]]. Western blot analysis showed that IL-1β stimulation upregulated MMP3 and MMP13 levels, whereas FeS-PEG treatment markedly reduced the levels of both enzymes and tended to normalise them toward baseline (Fig. 4B), indicating that FeS-PEG exerts a clear anti-catabolic effect that helps limit excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation. To further explore the anti-inflammatory mechanism of FeS-PEG, we examined the expression of the key pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-17. Under IL-1β stimulation, all three cytokines were robustly upregulated, a response typically mediated by canonical inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB, which in turn drive catabolic gene expression and accelerate cartilage erosion [[66], [67], [68]]. Notably, FeS-PEG treatment suppressed the protein levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-17. Together with the previously observed downregulation of COX-2/iNOS and upregulation of antioxidant enzymes, these findings support the notion that FeS-PEG restores ECM homeostasis in inflamed chondrocytes through a combination of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions.

(B) Western blots of IL-6, IL-17, MMP3, MMP13, TNF-α (GAPDH loading control) in chondrocytes ± IL-1β and ± FeS-PEG. (C) GO enrichment bubble plot of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). (D) KEGG pathway enrichment bar plot. (E) Volcano plot of DEGs. (F) Hierarchical clustering heatmap of differentially expressed genes.

On this basis, we performed RNA-seq on primary chondrocytes under different treatment conditions to obtain a systems-level view of FeS-PEG-mediated modulation of the IL-1β-induced PTOA-like phenotype. GO enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs were mainly associated with biological processes highly relevant to PTOA pathology, including regulation of inflammatory responses, ECM organisation, collagen-containing ECM, glycosaminoglycan/integrin binding, chondrocyte differentiation and endochondral ossification, as well as antioxidant activity (Fig. 4C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the DEGs were significantly enriched in ECM–receptor interaction, focal adhesion, the TNF signalling pathway, IL-17 signalling pathway, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, MAPK signalling pathway, PI3K-Akt signalling pathway, and p53 signalling pathway (Fig. 4D). Therefore, the protective effects of FeS-PEG against IL-1β-induced chondrocyte injury may involve coordinated regulation of ECM homeostasis, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and cell stress-related signalling.

The volcano plot showed that, compared with the IL-1β group, FeS-PEG treatment resulted in numerous significant DEGs in chondrocytes, including both upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 4E), indicating that FeS-PEG markedly altered the IL-1β-induced PTOA-like transcriptional state. We generated a heatmap to illustrate the key functional modules associated with the DEGs (Fig. 4F). Compared with the IL-1β group, FeS-PEG treatment altered the expression of genes associated with cartilage matrix synthesis, matrix degradation, inflammatory responses, and antioxidant defence. The expression patterns of several IL-1β-associated catabolic and inflammatory genes were partially reversed after FeS-PEG treatment. Among the DEGs, COL2A1 and ACAN represent cartilage matrix synthesis and chondrocyte phenotype maintenance; MMP3 and MMP13 represent matrix degradation and catabolic remodelling, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-17, PTGS2/COX-2, and NOS2/iNOS represent inflammatory responses, and CAT, GPX, and SOD3 represent antioxidant defence.

We performed RT-qPCR validation of the DEGs (Fig. S11). Compared with the IL-1β group, FeS-PEG treatment significantly restored the mRNA levels of COL2A1 and ACAN, while downregulating the expression of MMP13, MMP3, and IL6, consistent with the RNA-seq heatmap results. These findings indicate that FeS-PEG promotes the expression of cartilage matrix-related genes and suppresses the expression of matrix degradation- and inflammation-related genes at the transcriptional level. Specifically, the restoration of COL2A1 and ACAN corresponds to the enrichment of ECM organisation, collagen-containing ECM, ECM–receptor interaction, and focal adhesion in the GO/KEGG analyses. The downregulation of MMP13 and MMP3 levels suggests inhibition of ECM degradation and catabolic remodelling, whereas the reduced expression of IL-6 is consistent with the suppression of cytokine-mediated inflammatory signalling, including TNF, IL-17, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction-related pathways.

To further verify whether the key pathways enriched in the RNA-seq analysis were regulated by FeS-PEG at the protein level, we performed western blot analysis of proteins related to antioxidant defence, inflammation, and stress responses (Fig. S12). IL-1β stimulation reduced the expression of Nrf2 and HO-1, while increasing the levels of p-p65, p-Akt, p-p38, p-ERK1/2, p-JNK, p53, and p21, suggesting that IL-1β impaired antioxidant defence and activated NF-κB, PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and p53/p21-related inflammatory and stress signalling. After FeS-PEG treatment, Nrf2/HO-1 expression was restored, whereas the inflammation-, catabolism-, and stress-related protein levels or phosphorylation signals were decreased. These results further support the restoration of antioxidant defence and suppression of IL-1β-induced inflammatory and stress-related signalling in chondrocytes by FeS-PEG.

Collectively, the transcriptomic data, RT-qPCR validation, western blot results, and functional assays indicate that FeS-PEG re-establishes redox and ECM homeostasis and dampens cytokine-driven inflammatory and catabolic signalling in IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes (Fig. S13). Mechanistically, these effects may be associated with direct ROS scavenging by the redox-active FeS surface of FeS-PEG, restoration of Nrf2/HO-1-related antioxidant defence, and suppression of NF-κB, MAPK, PI3K-Akt, and p53/p21 related inflammatory and stress signalling, thereby providing a mechanistic basis for the chondroprotective and anti-PTOA effects of FeS-PEG.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy and biosafety of intra-articularly administered FeS-PEG in PTOA

PTOA was induced using a rat ACLT model, which reliably reproduces progressive articular cartilage degeneration accompanied by early subchondral bone remodelling [69]. Beginning 2 weeks after surgery, ACLT joints received intra-articular injections of FeS-PEG (10 μg/mL or 50 μg/mL) every other day for 2 weeks, whereas the sham-operated and ACLT control groups were administered equal volumes of normal saline. This dosing schedule was selected because nanoparticles and small molecules are cleared from the rat knee joint within hours to a few days [70]; therefore, relatively frequent administration is necessary to maintain local exposure of FeS-PEG in the synovial cavity. The 2-week treatment period during the early post-traumatic phase (weeks 2-4 after ACLT) allowed us to assess whether sustained ROS scavenging can interrupt the pathological cascade of cartilage degradation and subchondral bone remodelling before irreversible structural damage occurs. Similar intermediate-frequency, short-duration intra-articular dosing protocols have been used in other rodent OA studies [41,71]. At 8 weeks post-ACLT, knee joints were harvested for histological and immunohistochemical evaluation, while micro-CT analysis was performed at both 4 and 8 weeks. Safranin O/fast green, toluidine blue, and H&E staining revealed typical PTOA-like lesions in the ACLT control group, including surface fibrillation of articular cartilage, marked loss or attenuation of proteoglycan staining, chondrocyte clustering, and disruption of the tidemark. In contrast, these degenerative features were substantially alleviated in FeS-PEG-treated joints in a dose-dependent manner, with the 50 μg/mL group exhibiting cartilage morphology closely resembling that of the sham group (Fig. 5A). Blinded semi-quantitative evaluation using the OARSI scoring system [72], recommended for histological assessment of experimental PTOA, showed significantly reduced scores in both FeS-PEG-treated groups compared with the ACLT controls (Fig. S14), indicating that FeS-PEG effectively attenuates PTOA-related cartilage structural damage in vivo. COL II immunohistochemical staining showed stronger positive signals in the FeS-PEG-treated groups than in the ACLT control group. Quantitative analysis further showed an increased COL II-positive staining area following FeS-PEG treatment, indicating improved preservation of the cartilage matrix (Fig. 5B, S17). To further evaluate the anti-inflammatory effects of FeS-PEG in situ, immunohistochemical staining for p-p65 was performed on knee joint sections. NF-κB signalling is a central inflammatory pathway involved in IL-1β-induced chondrocyte activation and the transcriptional regulation of inflammatory mediators [73]. Compared with the sham group, the ACLT control group showed a higher percentage of p-p65-positive chondrocytes in articular cartilage, indicating activation of NF-κB-related inflammatory signalling during PTOA progression. FeS-PEG treatment reduced the percentage of p-p65-positive chondrocytes, suggesting that FeS-PEG suppressed NF-κB-associated inflammatory signalling in vivo (Fig. S15). These tissue-level findings are consistent with our in vitro data, collectively indicating that FeS-PEG downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines and exerts coordinated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects within the joint microenvironment.

To evaluate the impact of FeS-PEG on subchondral bone remodelling, high-resolution micro-CT scans were performed at 4 and 8 weeks after ACLT. 3D reconstructions revealed prominent osteophyte formation, altered joint contour, and a sparse trabecular architecture beneath the tibial plateau in the ACLT control group. By contrast, FeS-PEG treatment mitigated osteophyte development and trabecular rarefaction in a dose-dependent fashion, with overall joint morphology more closely resembling that of sham-operated animals (Fig. 5C and D). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that, at both time points, FeS-PEG significantly increased the subchondral BV/TV and reduced Tb.Sp (Fig. 5E–H), indicating that FeS-PEG helps preserve and stabilise the microarchitecture of subchondral bone, which undergoes early remodelling in PTOA and is closely coupled to cartilage degeneration. Previous bone regeneration studies showed that bioactive ionic cues released from degradable biomaterials can regulate the local inflammatory and osteogenic microenvironment, providing conceptual support for the involvement of FeS-PEG in subchondral bone protection [74].

To further clarify whether the improvement in subchondral bone microarchitecture observed by micro-CT was associated with changes in bone remodelling activity, we performed TRAP staining and immunohistochemical analysis of bone remodelling-related markers. TRAP staining showed that ACLT-induced PTOA markedly increased TRAP-positive staining along the subchondral bone surface, indicating enhanced osteoclast-associated bone remodelling. Quantitative analysis showed that FeS-PEG treatment reduced the TRAP-positive staining area, suggesting that FeS-PEG attenuated abnormal osteoclast-associated remodelling in the subchondral bone region (Fig. S16). The expression of osteogenesis- and osteoclastogenesis-related markers in the subchondral bone region was further examined. Compared with the sham group, the ACLT control group showed weaker staining for the osteogenic markers RUNX2 and OCN, whereas the pro-osteoclastogenic factor RANKL showed increased staining while that of its decoy receptor OPG was decreased, resulting in a staining pattern consistent with an increased osteoclastogenic tendency. Quantitative analysis of the positive staining areas supported these changes (Fig. S17). This expression pattern indicated an imbalance between bone formation and bone resorption in the PTOA subchondral bone microenvironment. After FeS-PEG treatment, RUNX2 and OCN expression was partially restored, whereas RANKL expression decreased and OPG expression increased, thereby producing a staining pattern consistent with reduced osteoclastogenic activity. These results provide histological and molecular support for the subchondral bone protection observed using micro-CT, suggesting that FeS-PEG may improve early subchondral bone remodelling in PTOA by suppressing abnormal osteoclast activity, restoring osteogenesis-related marker expression, and regulating the RANKL/OPG balance.

The nanosheet exhibited excellent degradability and favourable biosafety characteristics essential for an ideal 2D nanomaterial. At the indicated time points, all rats were euthanised, and blood along with major organs were collected. H&E staining of the organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney; Fig. S18) revealed no obvious histopathological abnormalities in the FeS-PEG-treated groups compared with the sham groups. Consistently, haematological and serum biochemical indices, including WBC, RBC, ALT, AST, UREA, and CREA showed no significant abnormal changes under the present dosing regimen and observation period (Fig. S19). These data support a favourable short-term biosafety profile for FeS-PEG in vivo. However, longer-term studies together with more comprehensive toxicological, pharmacokinetic, and clearance evaluations will be required.

Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrate that FeS-PEG exerts structure-protective effects in the PTOA model. At the joint level, FeS-PEG treatment was associated with improved cartilage matrix preservation, reduced osteochondral inflammatory signalling, and better maintenance of subchondral bone microarchitecture. Combined with the in vitro findings, these observations suggest that FeS-PEG alleviates oxidative-stress-associated joint injury and modulates catabolic and inflammatory responses. Importantly, by targeting oxidative stress, a central upstream driver in PTOA pathogenesis, FeS-PEG exerts its effects by modulating pathological mechanisms rather than merely alleviating pain or inflammation, highlighting its potential as a disease-modifying therapeutic platform.

Limitations

Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study mainly evaluated the short-term therapeutic effects of FeS-PEG in early-to-intermediate PTOA, and its long-term efficacy in delaying disease progression requires further verification. Second, although FeS-PEG showed favourable biocompatibility and short-term biosafety under the present experimental conditions, their long-term intra-articular retention, degradation, clearance, and potential delayed toxicity require systematic evaluation. In addition, this study was limited to a small-animal ACLT model. Further long-term animal studies, large-animal validation, and clinical trials are required to clarify the translational potential of FeS-PEG.

Conclusion

Based on the rational design of ultrathin 2D FeSNSs, this study developed a dual-functional nanotherapeutic platform for PTOA treatment, integrating cartilage protection with subchondral bone stabilisation. The resulting FeS-PEG exhibited favourable stability, biocompatibility, and ROS-scavenging activity. As efficient ROS-targeting nano-scavengers, FeS-PEG effectively reduced excessive intracellular ROS accumulation under inflammatory or oxidative stress conditions, alleviated lipid peroxidation and inflammatory responses, inhibited matrix degradation, and maintained the chondrocyte phenotype and ECM homeostasis. Importantly, FeS-PEG not only protected articular cartilage but also attenuated abnormal subchondral bone remodelling during early PTOA. In the ACLT-induced rat PTOA model, intra-articular administration of FeS-PEG effectively attenuated cartilage degeneration, preserved cartilage matrix, and maintained the stability of subchondral bone microarchitecture. With dual functions in cartilage protection and subchondral bone stabilisation, as well as good biocompatibility, the FeS-PEG nanoplatform shows great potential for early-to-intermediate PTOA intervention. This study provides new insight into disease-modifying therapeutic strategies that simultaneously target cartilage degeneration and subchondral bone lesions, and provides a basis for the further orthopaedic development of FeS-based nanomaterials in redox-targeted therapy.
