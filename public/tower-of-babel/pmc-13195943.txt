Comparison of prognosis between patients undergoing surgical resection and stereotactic ablative radiotherapy for early-stage lung cancer
Authors: Pelin Erdizci, Elçin Ersöz Köse, Mustafa Akyıl, Cansel Atinkaya Baytemir, Rashad Rzazade, Selami Volkan Baysungur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical resection is the standard curative treatment for stage I non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), whereas stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) is an established alternative for patients who are medically inoperable or decline surgery. However, comparative long-term oncologic outcomes remain debated. This study compared outcomes of surgery and SBRT in patients with clinical stage I NSCLC.MethodsThis retrospective two-center cohort study included 157 patients treated between 2010 and 2022 (93 surgery, 64 SBRT). The surgical cohort included a substantial proportion of thoracotomy procedures, reflecting routine clinical practice. Overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), local control (LC), and locoregional control (LRC) were estimated using Kaplan–Meier analysis. Multivariable Cox regression was used to adjust for baseline differences. Because histological confirmation was unavailable in a subset of SBRT patients, a sensitivity analysis restricted to histologically confirmed NSCLC cases (n = 143) was performed.ResultsMedian follow-up was 57 months in the surgical group and 48 months in the SBRT group. Median OS, DFS, LC, and LRC numerically favored surgery, although no statistically significant differences were observed (p > 0.05). Five-year OS was 58% after surgery and 49% after SBRT. The SBRT cohort had a higher burden of comorbidities (93% vs. 56%). Distant metastasis occurred more frequently after SBRT (28.1% vs. 15.1%, p = 0.006). Sensitivity analysis of histologically confirmed cases yielded results consistent with the primary analysis.ConclusionsSurgical resection and SBRT demonstrated comparable oncologic outcomes in this real-world cohort of stage I NSCLC. SBRT remains an effective option for medically inoperable patients, whereas surgery offers the advantage of pathological staging in eligible individuals. Consistent findings in the histologically confirmed cohort support the robustness of these results. Prospective randomized trials are needed to further define optimal management.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Stereotactic body radiotherapy, Surgical resection, Early-stage NSCLC, Survival

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among men and the third leading cause among women worldwide, representing a substantial global health burden [1]. For patients with early-stage disease, lobectomy with systematic lymph node dissection continues to be regarded as the standard curative treatment. Reported five-year survival rates approaching 92% in stage I disease highlight the effectiveness of surgical management [2].

Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), however, has emerged as an increasingly established therapeutic alternative, particularly for patients who are not suitable candidates for surgery due to advanced age, significant comorbidities, or personal preference. SBRT provides high local control rates—approaching 90% in many series—while maintaining a toxicity profile that is generally considered manageable in appropriately selected patients [3, 4]. Several prospective and retrospective studies have reported comparable survival outcomes between surgical resection and SBRT [5, 6]. Conversely, other reports suggest that surgical approaches, particularly sublobar resection, may yield superior long-term survival compared with SBRT [7, 8].

Despite several advantages—including short treatment duration, delivery of high biologically effective radiation doses, and its minimally invasive nature—the role of SBRT in operable patients remains controversial. Questions persist regarding its safety in central tumors and the appropriateness of its use without histological confirmation. Furthermore, post-SBRT radiologic changes may mimic tumor recurrence, standardized follow-up strategies remain insufficiently defined, and the risk of toxicity increases when tumors are located near bronchial structures. These factors complicate clinical management and interpretation of treatment outcomes [9–11].

Although SBRT provides high local tumor control, uncertainty remains regarding broader oncologic endpoints such as disease-free survival and patterns of distant progression, which continue to support surgery in medically operable patients [12].

Given these uncertainties, further clarification of the relative clinical value of surgery and SBRT in early-stage NSCLC remains necessary, particularly in real-world clinical settings where treatment allocation is strongly influenced by patient comorbidities, functional status, and tumor characteristics. Comparative interpretation of these treatment strategies is further complicated by heterogeneity in patient selection, the inclusion of cases without histological confirmation in some SBRT cohorts, and variability in reported oncologic endpoints [4].

In this context, real-world comparative data with long-term follow-up and clearly defined oncologic endpoints remain important for clarifying the relative clinical roles of surgery and SBRT. This study aimed to compare the oncologic outcomes of surgical resection and SBRT in patients with clinical stage I (T1–T2aN0M0) NSCLC. The primary objective was to evaluate overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), local control (LC), and locoregional control (LRC) between the two treatment modalities, while secondary objectives included analyzing patterns of disease progression and treatment-related complications. Additionally, to address potential misclassification related to the absence of tissue diagnosis in a subset of SBRT patients, a sensitivity analysis restricted to histologically confirmed NSCLC cases was performed.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was designed as a retrospective cohort analysis comparing oncologic outcomes between surgical resection and SBRT in patients with clinical stage I NSCLC. The study was conducted at two tertiary referral centers in Turkey.

The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Protocol No:105; April 30, 2021), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective design of the study, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Clinical, radiological, pathological, treatment-related, and follow-up data were retrospectively obtained from institutional electronic medical records. The study design and reporting adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations.

Patient selection

Patients treated between January 2010 and April 2022 were screened for eligibility. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had clinical stage I NSCLC (T1–T2aN0M0) according to the 9th edition of the TNM classification and were treated with either surgical resection or SBRT.

Pretreatment evaluation included thoracic computed tomography (CT), 18-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–computed tomography (18 F-FDG PET/CT), and pulmonary function testing. All pretreatment CT and 18 F-FDG PET/CT images were reviewed to confirm clinical stage I disease. All patients were discussed in a multidisciplinary tumor board involving thoracic surgeons, radiation oncologists, and radiologists, and treatment decisions were made accordingly. Patients were excluded if they had neuroendocrine tumors or small cell lung cancer, a previous malignancy that could interfere with staging, incomplete clinical documentation, or insufficient follow-up information. A total of 214 patients with clinically suspected stage I NSCLC were screened. After exclusion of 57 patients because of missing staging data, loss to follow-up before treatment, or histology inconsistent with primary NSCLC, the primary cohort consisted of 157 patients (93 surgical resection and 64 SBRT). A sensitivity analysis restricted to histologically confirmed NSCLC was subsequently performed after excluding 14 SBRT patients without tissue diagnosis, resulting in a cohort of 143 patients (93 surgery and 50 SBRT).

Treatment allocation reflected real-world clinical decision making. Surgical resection was generally recommended for medically operable patients, whereas SBRT was offered to patients considered medically inoperable because of advanced age, comorbidities, or impaired functional status, or to patients who declined surgical treatment.

Histological confirmation and presumed malignancy criteria

Histopathological confirmation was available for all patients in the surgical cohort. In the SBRT cohort, tissue diagnosis was obtained whenever feasible; however, 14 patients (21.9%) underwent SBRT without histological confirmation because biopsy was considered unsafe due to significant medical comorbidities or because the patient declined invasive diagnostic procedures. In accordance with national radiotherapy guidelines, SBRT without tissue confirmation was permitted when all of the following criteria were met: (1) a new or enlarging pulmonary lesion on CT with radiologic features suggestive of malignancy; (2) high clinical risk for lung cancer based on age and smoking history; and (3) increased metabolic activity on 18 F-FDG PET/CT, with no evidence of metastatic lymphadenopathy. In such cases, the diagnosis of presumed malignancy was established through multidisciplinary tumor board evaluation based on combined clinical and radiological findings. Supporting criteria included progressive enlargement of the pulmonary nodule on serial CT imaging, high metabolic activity on 18 F-FDG PET/CT (typically SUVmax > 2.5), radiologic features suggestive of malignancy such as spiculated margins or pleural indentation, and the absence of an alternative benign etiology following clinical evaluation. Multimodality imaging -particularly 18 F-FDG PET/CT has demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy in the evaluation of suspicious pulmonary nodules, and previous studies have shown that SBRT may be safely administered in medically inoperable patients with clinically diagnosed early-stage lung cancer when tissue confirmation cannot be obtained and imaging findings strongly support malignancy [13–15].

Surgical treatment

Patients in the surgical cohort underwent anatomical lung resection with curative intent. Surgical procedures included lobectomy or pneumonectomy, performed either via thoracotomy or video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) according to surgeon preference and patient characteristics. Systematic mediastinal lymph node sampling or dissection was performed in accordance with international thoracic surgery guidelines. Resected specimens were evaluated for tumor histology, tumor size, margin status, and pathological stage.

Image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) was performed before each treatment fraction to ensure accurate target localization.

Tumor location was categorized as central or peripheral based on the relationship to the proximal bronchial tree. Peripheral tumors were defined as lesions located more than 2 cm away from the proximal bronchial tree, whereas central tumors were defined as lesions within 2 cm of the proximal bronchial tree, consistent with established SBRT consensus guidelines.

SBRT was delivered as hypofractionated high-dose external beam radiotherapy. Total doses ranged from 45 to 60 Gy (Gray), delivered in 3–8 fractions. Fractionation schedules were individualized according to tumor size, tumor location, proximity to critical structures, and dose constraints for organs at risk. Peripheral tumors were generally treated using regimens delivering higher doses per fraction in fewer fractions, whereas central tumors were treated using a greater number of fractions with lower dose per fraction in order to reduce toxicity to mediastinal structures. Biologically effective dose values (BED10) were calculated assuming an α/β ratio of 10 Gy. Treatment plans were generated using conformal or intensity-modulated radiotherapy techniques, and dose calculations were performed using standard treatment planning algorithms in accordance with institutional protocols. Patients were immobilized using a dedicated stereotactic body frame with vacuum cushions to minimize setup variability. Radiation was typically prescribed to the planning target volume with coverage of at least 95% of the PTV by the prescription isodose line. Dose constraints for organs at risk, including the proximal bronchial tree, esophagus, spinal cord, heart, and major vessels, were applied according to established SBRT guidelines. Follow-up and recurrence assessment

Patients in both groups were followed with chest CT and clinical examination at 3–6 month intervals. 18 F-FDG PET/CT imaging was performed when clinically indicated. Follow-up duration was calculated from the date of treatment initiation to the date of the last clinical contact or death.

Because post-SBRT radiographic changes such as radiation pneumonitis or fibrosis may mimic tumor recurrence, suspected local recurrence was defined based on progressive enlargement of the treated lesion on serial imaging, new or increasing metabolic activity on 18 F-FDG PET/CT, or histopathological confirmation when biopsy was feasible. In cases with equivocal imaging findings, recurrence status was determined through multidisciplinary tumor board evaluation and longitudinal imaging follow-up. 18 F-FDG PET/CT evaluation was typically performed at least three months after completion of SBRT to minimize false-positive inflammatory uptake.

Outcome definitions

Time-to-event outcomes were calculated from the date of treatment initiation (time zero), defined as the date of surgery for patients undergoing resection and the date of the first SBRT fraction for patients treated with SBRT.

Patterns of disease progression were categorized as local recurrence (recurrence within the treated lung lesion or surgical resection margin including the stapling line and bronchial/vascular stumps, and in the same lobe), regional recurrence (involvement of ipsilateral hilar or mediastinal lymph nodes and adjacent lobes), and distant metastasis (metastasis occurring in N3 lymph nodes, the contralateral lung, outside of the thorax, and pleural or pericardial seeding).

OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause. DFS was defined as the time to local recurrence, regional lymph node recurrence, distant metastasis, or death. LC was defined as the time to recurrence at the treated primary tumor site or death, whereas LRC was defined as the time to recurrence at the primary tumor site or regional lymph nodes, or death. For composite endpoints, the first occurring event was considered the defining event. Patients alive without evidence of recurrence at the last follow-up were censored at the date of their most recent clinical evaluation.

Treatment-related complications

Treatment-related complications were assessed separately for each treatment modality using established grading systems. Postoperative complications were classified according to the Clavien–Dindo classification. In the SBRT cohort, treatment-related toxicities were evaluated using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. Because these grading systems differ in structure and clinical context, complications associated with surgery and SBRT were analyzed descriptively rather than through direct numerical comparison.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges depending on distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Patients with missing key variables required for survival analysis were excluded from the respective analyses.

Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Differences between groups were primarily assessed using the log-rank test, which was selected as the primary comparison test because it provides optimal statistical power when proportional hazards assumptions are satisfied. The Breslow and Tarone–Ware tests were used as supplementary analyses to explore potential differences in early survival patterns.

To account for baseline differences between treatment groups, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed, including age, sex, tumor size, tumor location, histological subtype, and comorbidity status as covariates. Because treatment allocation was not randomized and reflected clinical decision making, these adjusted analyses were performed to address potential confounding by indication. Effect estimates were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using log–minus–log survival plots.

To address potential bias related to the absence of histological confirmation in a subset of SBRT patients, a sensitivity analysis restricted to histologically confirmed NSCLC cases was performed to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 157 Stage I NSCLC patients were included in the study, of whom 93 underwent surgical resection and 64 received SBRT. The proportion of female patients was 24.7% in the surgical group and 20.3% in the SBRT group, with no significant sex distribution difference (p = 0.262). Median age was 66 years in the surgical cohort and 72 years in the SBRT cohort (p = 0.136). Smoking exposure was similar between groups, with a mean smoking history of 38 pack-years in surgical patients and 33.2 pack-years in SBRT patients. Comorbidities were present in 56% of surgical patients compared with 93% of those treated with SBRT. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) was the most common comorbidity among SBRT patients (51.5%).

Tumor localization was central in 47.3% and peripheral in 52.7% of surgical patients; corresponding values for the SBRT cohort were 31.2% and 68.7% (p = 0.045). Mean tumor diameter was 28.2 mm in the surgical group and 22 mm in the SBRT group.

Histopathological evaluation in the surgical group demonstrated adenocarcinoma in 48.4% and squamous cell carcinoma in 51.6% of patients. In the SBRT cohort, adenocarcinoma was observed in 32.8%, squamous cell carcinoma in 29.7%, NSCLC not otherwise specified (NSCLC-NOS) in 15.6%, and no tissue diagnosis was available in 21.9% of patients.

In the surgical cohort, most procedures were performed using open surgical approaches. VATS was performed in 18 patients (19.4%), whereas thoracotomy was used in 75 patients (80.6%). Among surgical patients, lobectomy was performed in 96.7%, whereas 3.2% underwent pneumonectomy.

In the SBRT cohort (n = 64), fractionation schedules were selected according to tumor location and institutional protocols. Peripheral lesions were predominantly treated with hypofractionated regimens of 54–60 Gy in 3 fractions or 55 Gy in 5 fractions, whereas centrally located tumors were generally treated with more fractionated schedules, most commonly 60 Gy in 8 fractions, in order to reduce toxicity to adjacent critical structures. Less frequently, 45 Gy in 3 fractions was applied in selected peripheral tumors with small target volumes. Overall, the median prescribed dose was 55 Gy (range, 45–60 Gy) delivered in a median of 5 fractions (range, 3–8 fractions), corresponding to a median BED10 of approximately 120 Gy (range, 105–180 Gy).

Median follow-up duration was 57 months in the surgical cohort and 48 months in the SBRT cohort. Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered to 25.8% of surgical patients and 3.1% of SBRT patients. Chemotherapy in the surgical cohort was guided by pathologic risk factors (EGFR mutation, ALK rearrangement, visceral pleural invasion, grade 3 tumors), whereas in SBRT patients it was offered selectively based on clinical judgment.

Overall survival

The median OS was 64 months in the surgical group and 51.6 months in the SBRT group. The hazard ratio for death associated with SBRT compared with surgical resection was 1.32 (95% CI 0.84–2.06; p = 0.226). The estimated OS rates at 12, 24, and 60 months were 94%, 84%, and 58% in the surgical group and 98%, 80%, and 49% in the SBRT group, respectively. After adjustment for baseline differences (age, sex, tumor size, tumor location, histological subtype, and comorbidity status) treatment modality remained not independently associated with OS: adjusted HR 1.27 (95% CI 0.75–2.17; p = 0.374) (Fig. 2).

Disease-free survival

Median DFS was 64.2 months in the surgical group and 42 months in the SBRT group. The unadjusted hazard ratio for DFS associated with SBRT compared with surgical resection was 0.99 (95% CI 0.57–1.71; p = 0.964), with estimated DFS rates at 12, 24, and 60 months of 85%, 78%, and 63% in the surgical group and 93%, 90%, and 58% in the SBRT group, respectively. In multivariable Cox regression analysis, treatment modality was not independently associated with DFS (adjusted HR 1.15; 95% CI 0.6–2.2; p = 0.672) (Fig. 3).

Local control

Median LC time was 64 months in the surgical cohort and 48.9 months in the SBRT cohort. The estimated LC rates at 12, 24, and 60 months were 95%, 94%, and 87% in the surgical group and 95%, 87%, and 80% in the SBRT group, respectively. Kaplan–Meier analysis of LC demonstrated no statistically significant difference between the SBRT and surgical resection groups. The unadjusted Cox proportional hazards model showed a hazard ratio for local recurrence of 0.27 for SBRT compared with surgery (95% CI 0.06–1.23; p = 0.092). After adjustment for baseline differences treatment modality remained not independently associated with LC (adjusted HR 0.46; 95% CI 0.08–2.58; p = 0.375) (Fig. 4).

Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrating LC in patients treated with surgery or SBRT. Event definition: local recurrence or death

Locoregional control

Median LRC was 51.2 months in the surgical cohort and 46 months in the SBRT cohort. Kaplan–Meier analysis of LRC demonstrated no statistically significant difference between the SBRT and surgical resection groups. The unadjusted Cox proportional hazards model showed a hazard ratio for locoregional recurrence of 1.1 for SBRT compared with surgery (95% CI 0.52–2.32; p = 0.803). After adjustment for baseline covariates treatment modality remained not independently associated with LRC (adjusted HR 1.61; 95% CI 0.62–4.17; p = 0.331) (Fig. 5).

Patterns of progression

Local recurrence occurred in 9.6% of surgical patients and 2.1% of SBRT patients. Mediastinal lymph node metastasis was observed in 5.3% of surgical patients and 7.8% of SBRT patients. Ipsilateral lung metastases developed in 6.2% of SBRT patients. Distant metastasis occurred in 18 patients (28.1%) in the SBRT group and in 14 patients (15.1%) in the surgical group, representing a significantly higher incidence in the SBRT cohort (p = 0.006). In contrast, differences in the rates of local recurrence and mediastinal lymph node metastasis between the two groups were not statistically significant. Because some patients experienced multiple types of recurrence during follow-up, only the first event was considered in the Kaplan–Meier disease-free survival analysis.

Survival analysis stratified by histological subtype (adenocarcinoma vs. squamous cell carcinoma) demonstrated no significant difference within or between treatment groups (p > 0.05).

Treatment-related complications

In the early postoperative period (within 30 days), pneumonia occurred in 4.3% of surgical patients, including two deaths (2.1%) due to postoperative pneumonia, while prolonged air leak was observed in 1% of cases. SBRT was not associated with early mortality, and late treatment-related toxicities included grade 2 radiation pneumonitis in 6.25% of patients, grade 3 radiation pneumonitis in 1.5%, and rib fractures in 3% of patients. Because surgical and radiation toxicities are graded using different classification systems (Clavien–Dindo and CTCAE), direct numerical comparison between treatment modalities was not performed.

Sensitivity analysis: histologically confirmed cohort

Because 14 patients in the SBRT cohort lacked pathological confirmation, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed excluding these cases.

This analysis included 143 patients with histologically confirmed NSCLC (93 surgery, 50 SBRT). Baseline characteristics of this cohort are presented in Table 2. In this restricted analysis, SBRT patients remained significantly older (72.5 ± 8.1 vs. 64.9 ± 11.4 years, p < 0.001) and more comorbid than surgical patients.

Kaplan–Meier analyses of this histologically confirmed cohort yielded results consistent with the primary analysis. No statistically significant differences were observed between treatment modalities for OS, LC, or DFS.

For OS, the hazard ratio for SBRT compared with surgery was 1.21 (95% CI 0.74–1.96; Cox p = 0.45; log-rank p = 0.449). The estimated 12, 24, and 60 month OS rates were 100%, 92%, and 54.7% in the SBRT group and 94%, 84%, and 58% in the surgical group, respectively.

Similarly, LC did not differ significantly between groups (HR 0.35; 95% CI 0.08–1.60; Cox p = 0.177; log-rank p = 0.158), with 12, 24, and 60 month LC rates of 100%, 98%, and 95.5% in the SBRT cohort and 95%, 94%, and 87% in the surgical cohort.

DFS analysis also demonstrated no statistically significant difference (HR 1.24; 95% CI 0.77–2; Cox p = 0.38; log-rank p = 0.377). The estimated 12, 24, and 60 month DFS rates were 94%, 80%, and 41% in the SBRT group and 85%, 78%, and 63% in the surgical group.

Although survival curves showed a numerical trend favoring surgical resection in long-term outcomes, treatment modality was not independently associated with survival after adjustment for baseline covariates. Kaplan–Meier curves for this sensitivity analysis are presented in Figs. 6, 7 and 8.

Discussion

In our study, the oncologic outcomes of surgical resection and SBRT in clinical stage I NSCLC were compared. No statistically significant differences were observed in OS, DFS, or LC. However, the numerically longer median LC duration in the surgical group and the trend toward improved OS among patients with histopathological diagnosis may reflect the potential advantages of comprehensive pathological staging and more accurate assessment of tumor biology achieved through surgery. These findings suggest that, in appropriately selected patients, surgery may still offer a potential advantage in long-term outcomes for early-stage NSCLC.

Previous studies have reported heterogeneous results regarding survival outcomes following surgery or SBRT. Several retrospective studies and pooled analyses have suggested that OS may be comparable between the two treatment strategies in carefully selected patients [6, 16]. For example, the pooled analysis of the STARS and ROSEL randomized trials reported favorable survival outcomes for SBRT compared with surgery in operable patients [5]. However, the limited sample size and early termination of these trials restrict the generalizability of their conclusions, and therefore these findings should be interpreted with caution.

In the present study, DFS did not differ significantly between patients treated with surgical resection and those receiving SBRT. Although the median DFS was numerically longer in the surgical cohort, treatment modality was not independently associated with DFS in multivariable analysis. This pattern may also reflect differences in the timing of events between treatment groups, with relatively fewer early events observed after SBRT and a gradual divergence of survival curves favoring surgery during longer follow-up. These findings are consistent with previous comparative studies reporting similar recurrence-free survival between SBRT and surgery in selected early-stage NSCLC populations [17]. Differences in DFS may reflect multiple factors beyond the local treatment itself, including patient selection, comorbidity burden, and the accuracy of tumor staging. In particular, the absence of pathological nodal staging in SBRT-treated patients may lead to undetected occult nodal disease and influence recurrence patterns [5]. In our cohort, the higher incidence of distant metastasis observed in the SBRT group further highlights the multifactorial determinants of DFS in early-stage NSCLC.

The numerically longer median LC duration observed in the surgical group in our study is consistent with previous reports indicating favorable LC outcomes with anatomic resection in early-stage NSCLC [18]. Although some studies have demonstrated comparable survival outcomes between SBRT and surgery in selected patient populations [6, 16], both treatment modalities play important roles depending on patient suitability and clinical context. Surgery offers the advantages of complete tumor removal and pathological staging [1, 2], whereas SBRT provides a minimally invasive treatment option that is particularly relevant for medically inoperable patients, despite potential limitations such as the absence of tissue confirmation and the risk of increased toxicity in centrally located tumors [9–11]. The absence of a statistically significant difference in LC in our cohort suggests that both modalities can achieve effective disease control in appropriately selected patients, which is consistent with previous reports [19].

Although the local recurrence rate was numerically higher in the surgical group in our study, this difference may partially reflect variations in cohort composition and baseline characteristics rather than a true treatment-related effect. Previous reports have suggested that intraoperative tumor cell dissemination may contribute to postoperative recurrence in selected cases [5, 20]. In contrast, LC following SBRT is known to depend strongly on dose and fractionation, with higher BED10 values associated with improved tumor control [21]. In addition, the literature indicates that centrally located tumors may carry an increased risk of recurrence with both treatment modalities [22]. Taken together, these findings highlight the multifactorial nature of recurrence patterns in early-stage NSCLC and suggest that both surgery and SBRT can achieve effective local disease control when treatment selection is guided by appropriate patient and tumor characteristics.

Surgical intervention is associated with early postoperative complications; however, advances in minimally invasive techniques have substantially reduced these risks over time [23, 24]. SBRT-related complications, in contrast, typically occur later and most commonly include radiation pneumonitis and rib fractures [9, 10, 25]. These differences highlight the distinct complication profiles and timing associated with each treatment modality. While SBRT offers advantages such as a shorter, noninvasive treatment course and is generally associated with lower treatment-related mortality, surgery provides comprehensive pathological staging information, including tumor histology and lymph node status, which is essential for accurate staging and for guiding adjuvant therapy decisions [5].

In many contemporary comparative studies evaluating surgery and SBRT for early-stage NSCLC, the surgical cohort predominantly consists of minimally invasive procedures such as VATS or robotic-assisted thoracic surgery [26]. The increasing adoption of these techniques has led to a declining proportion of thoracotomy procedures reported in recent surgical series [27].

Nevertheless, thoracotomy remains an important surgical approach in routine clinical practice, particularly in patients with complex anatomy, centrally located tumors, dense pleural adhesions, or intraoperative circumstances that preclude minimally invasive access [28]. Consequently, comparative studies in which the surgical cohort is composed predominantly of minimally invasive procedures may not fully reflect the broader spectrum of surgical practice when evaluating oncologic outcomes of surgery versus SBRT in early-stage NSCLC [29]. In addition, previous studies comparing SBRT and surgery have highlighted substantial heterogeneity in the surgical techniques used in these analyses, which may influence survival outcomes and limit the generalizability of comparisons between treatment modalities [30]. Surgical resection enables comprehensive pathological evaluation of tumor characteristics, including visceral pleural invasion, lymphovascular invasion, tumor grade, and lymph node involvement, all of which are important for accurate staging and prognostic assessment [1, 2, 18]. In contrast, patients treated with SBRT generally do not undergo pathological lymph node sampling, which may result in undetected occult nodal disease and introduce uncertainty in systemic therapy planning [9–11, 25]. In our cohort, the higher rate of adjuvant therapy observed in the surgical group likely reflects the additional prognostic information obtained through pathological staging and lymph node evaluation.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces the potential for selection bias, as patients undergoing surgery generally had better baseline functional status, whereas SBRT was preferentially offered to medically inoperable individuals. Second, the unequal group sizes and the higher burden of comorbid conditions in the SBRT group may influence comparisons of recurrence patterns and long-term survival outcomes. In addition, a subset of SBRT patients lacked histological confirmation of malignancy, which may introduce diagnostic uncertainty. However, to address this issue, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to histologically confirmed NSCLC cases, which yielded results consistent with the primary analysis and supported the robustness of our findings.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study, surgical resection and SBRT demonstrated comparable oncologic outcomes in patients with clinical stage I NSCLC, with no statistically significant differences observed in OS, DFS, LC, or LRC. Although survival curves showed a numerical trend favoring surgery, SBRT remained an effective treatment option, particularly for patients who were medically inoperable or declined surgical treatment. The sensitivity analysis restricted to histologically confirmed NSCLC cases yielded results consistent with the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of the findings despite the absence of tissue diagnosis in a subset of SBRT patients. These results provide additional real-world evidence supporting the use of both treatment strategies in appropriately selected patients. Prospective randomized trials are warranted to further clarify the optimal management of early-stage NSCLC.
