Exploring smartphone use and its applicability in academic training of medical students in Latin America: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Juan S. Izquierdo-Condoy, Marlon Arias-Intriago, H. A. Nati-Castillo, R. Gollini-Mihalopoulos, C. D. Cardozo-Espínola, Valentina Loaiza-Guevara, Widad Dalel-Gómez, Juan Villamil, Isaac A. Suárez-Sangucho, Estefania Morales-Lapo, Andrea Tello-De-la-Torre, Cougar Hall, Esteba Ortiz-Prado
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMobile phones are essential tools worldwide, including for the academic training of medical students. However, their role in Latin America (LATAM) remains underexplored. This study aimed to evaluate cell phone use and its applicability in the academic training of medical students in LATAM.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted among undergraduate medical students from seven Latin American countries between December 2023 and February 2024. The study assessed academic smartphone use across five types of applications: medical apps, knowledge updating apps, academic podcast apps, video streaming apps, and social media apps.ResultsA total of 1,590 medical students participated, with 63.6% being women, 21.2% third-year students, and 55.0% from private universities. Nearly all participants (98.4%) owned a mobile phone, and 88.2% used them for academic purposes. The most common academic use was for viewing academic content videos (82.9%). A significant association was found between academic cell phone use and progression through medical studies, with fifth-year students being the most likely to use their phones for academic purposes (OR = 4.517, 95% CI = 1.843–11.075). Students in the clinical stage of education were also more likely to rely on smart phones for academic purposes (OR = 2.090, 95% CI = 1.500–2.911). Conversely, public university students were less likely to use their phones for academic purposes compared to private university students (OR = 0.714, 95% CI = 0.522–0.977). Additionally, students who perceived that their academic preparation would be more difficult without a cell phone were more likely to use it for academic purposes (OR = 2.157, 95% CI = 1.463–3.181).ConclusionsThese results suggest the importance of incorporating smartphones into the educational frameworks of medical schools and universities in Latin America, with a particular emphasis on academic video content applications, which already capture the attention and preference of medical students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06334-w.

Background

In recent years, the widespread integration of smartphones into everyday life has been particularly pronounced within the medical field, ushering in transformative changes in the way healthcare professionals, including medical students, access and use information [1, 2]. Evidence shows an increase in the use of smartphones among physicians worldwide with substantial adoption rates in developed countries [3, 4]. For instance, in 2010, approximately 70% of US physicians and 80% of Chinese physicians were already using smartphones as indispensable tools in their professional activities [5, 6]. More recently, medical students have also recognized the positive impact of smartphones on medical education [7, 8]. However, as Ramos-Galarza C. et al. pointed out, in Latin America, the full implementation of technology in educational institutions, both private and public, remains a challenge due to significant disparities in access and resources, leaving many students with unequal opportunities [9].

The evolution of smartphones from mere communication devices to sophisticated mobile “computers” has revolutionized the landscape of information management and access, with its effects evident in medical education for both professionals and undergraduate students [10, 11]. This transformation was highlighted, in 2014 at Canadian universities, where a staggering 93 to 98% of medical students were found to own smartphones [12, 13].

These portable devices, equipped with web browsing capabilities, serve as conduits for seamless peer-to-peer interactions, communication, and collaborative learning opportunities. The flexibility offered by smartphones allows knowledge acquisition to transcend physical boundaries, a fundamental factor for their widespread adoption among medical students [14]. Moreover, this revolution has been accompanied by the development of mobile applications specifically focused on medical education, which has become a hallmark of contemporary medical student life.

A growing body of literature highlights the increasing reliance on smartphones in medical education and the positive perceptions of students regarding their impact on training [15]. These devices also play a pivotal role in bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application in clinical settings [3]. As medical students transition from years of study in preclinical disciplines to clinical disciplines, the prevalence and utility of smartphones further intensify, with breaks between clinical activities effectively leveraged for continuous learning opportunities [15]. This evolution has led to a tangible reduction in medical errors by improving communication and understanding between healthcare professionals, ultimately enhancing patient care [16].

Traditionally, medical education in Latin America has heavily relied on conventional lecture-based methods. However, recent years have seen a gradual shift toward the adoption of online educational tools, although progress has been slow and largely dependent on available resources. This transition was significantly accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, which underscored the need to expand and enhance online education across the region. Among the most widely adopted tools in Latin American medical education are videoconferencing platforms such as Zoom, Microsoft Teams, and Google Meet, along with virtual classroom environments like Google Classroom and virtual labs. Additionally, live-streaming platforms (e.g., Discord) and academic search engines (e.g., Google Scholar, PubMed, and LILACS) have played pivotal roles in supporting this digital transformation [17, 18].

Despite the growing use of online platforms, the impact of mobile applications on academic training among Latin American medical students remains largely underexplored, leaving a gap in our understanding of how these innovations affect learning outcomes in the region. In this context, this article aims to comprehensively assess the use of smartphones in academic training and knowledge acquisition among medical students in seven Latin American countries, providing insights into the overall importance of this technological shift in medical education across the region.

Materials and methods

Study design

A descriptive, cross-sectional, multicenter study was conducted over a three-month period from December 2023 to February 2024.

Setting and participants

The study employed an online self-administered questionnaire targeting medical students from universities in seven Latin American countries: Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Panama, Paraguay, and Peru. The universities were selected based on their proximity to the research team, as part of a research network that facilitated the distribution of questionnaires.

According to data from recent years, the development of medical schools in these countries has been uneven. Bolivia has approximately 10 medical schools with around 4942 medical students; Chile has 37 schools with about 19,655 students; Colombia has 63 schools with approximately 90,979 students; Ecuador has 22 schools with around 11,392 students; Panama has 7 schools with 1772 students as of 2021; Paraguay has 44 medical schools; and Peru has 42 institutions with around 52,234 students [19–23]. In the region, medical curricula typically span six years, with the first three years focused on basic sciences and the final three on clinical and surgical training. However, some countries, like Colombia, Peru, and Chile, offer extended programs lasting up to 13 semesters. The last year usually consists of a hospital-based internship.

Participants in the study were undergraduate medical students officially enrolled in medical programs, ranging from the first to the sixth or seventh year. A non-probability convenience sampling method was employed to select participants accessible to the research team through an online survey platform. All responses were obtained from voluntary participants who consented to take part in the research.

Survey development and measures

The study employed an anonymous, 13-question online survey specifically designed by the research team to gather data on the types of cell phones, usage characteristics, and academic applications of cell phones among medical students in Latin America.

The initial version of the questionnaire was reviewed by two public health experts to ensure relevance, accuracy, and to identify any potential errors. Subsequently, the questionnaire underwent pilot testing with 30 medical students from Ecuador to address any comprehension or design issues, particularly regarding the types of cell phones and the classification of applications used for academic purposes. Responses from the pilot phase were excluded from the final dataset.

Based on feedback from the pilot test, the questionnaire was refined to produce the final version, initially drafted in Spanish. A translated English version of the final survey instrument is included in Supplementary File 1.

Questionnaire and variables

The questionnaire gathered diverse variables to address the research objective. Demographic variables included sex, age, country of residence, year of study, and type of university (public or private).

Additionally, the characteristics of cell phone use were assessed through variables such as the type of cell phone (basic cell phone and smartphone), mobile internet access, and the most used social media platforms. Reasons for cell phone use were also examined, encompassing interaction on social networks, communication, gaming, listening to music, watching streaming platforms or movies, staying updated on the news, and academic purposes.

Furthermore, several variables were evaluated regarding the potential academic use of the cell phone. These included the applications used for learning through the cell phone, such as medical apps (including calculators, drugs formulation, diagnosis SCORE, anthropometric indices), knowledge updating apps (such as UpToDate, Dynamed, Medscape), academic podcast apps (such as Spotify, Apple podcasts, Google podcasts), academic content video playing apps (such as YouTube), and social networks apps (including Instagram, Facebook, Twitter, TikTok, among others). The percentage of doubts resolved via the cell phone was also examined.

Data collection and management

Within the demographic variables, the type of university was classified as public or private, depending on the source of funding for the university. The year of study was categorized from first to sixth year, with each year comprising two semesters. A seventh-year category was included for students from countries where the medical degree extends to thirteen semesters, such as Colombia and Panama. Additionally, students were grouped into two academic stages: basic (first to third year) and clinical (fourth to sixth or seventh year).

Cell phone types were classified into two categories: basic cell phones for devices that can only make calls and send short message service (SMS), and smartphones for devices that can perform many functions of a computer, such as internet access and application usage.

Data collection was facilitated through the online platform Google Forms, with participants accessing the survey via a unique link. This link was strategically disseminated through medical student groups on social networks, including Facebook and WhatsApp, to which research team members had direct access or connections. The survey preamble provided a brief overview of the study’s objectives, emphasized confidentiality, and included a request for informed consent. Participants were required to agree to the informed consent and participation terms before they could proceed, which helped reinforce the anonymity of their responses.

To maintain data integrity, all responses were meticulously scrutinized for potential errors or inconsistencies, such as implausible age ranges or respondents choosing all available answers. Resulting implausible responses were excluded from the final sample dropping the original sample of 1607 survey responses to a final 1590 responses deemed valid.

Study size

The sample size was determined using a formula for estimating sample sizes in populations of unknown size [24]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n=\:\frac{{Z}^{2}\cdot\:(p\cdot\:q)}{{e}^{2}}$$\end{document}

With a 99% confidence level (Z = 2.576), a 4% margin of error (e = 4%), and an assumed 50% distribution for both positive (p) and negative (q) responses, the required sample size was calculated to be n = 1,041 completed questionnaires, which were subsequently distributed among medical students across the seven participating countries.

Bias

Several strategies designed to mitigate potential bias during data collection and management were employed. To prevent duplicate submissions, the “Google Forms” platform was configured to limit one response per IP device. Additionally, the questionnaire was designed to avoid collecting identifiable information, including IP addresses. In the analysis phase, further steps were taken to minimize bias. One member of the research team meticulously reviewed the results, and any discrepancies that arose were collaboratively resolved. This approach ensured that only valid and genuine responses were included in the final results. These measures substantially enhanced the reliability and credibility of the research outcomes.

Statistical analysis

This study predominantly employed descriptive statistics to analyze responses for each categorical variable, encompassing the calculation of frequencies and percentages. For quantitative variables, measures of central tendency (mean) and dispersion (± standard deviation) were assessed.

To examine distribution differences between qualitative variables and perceptions of cell phone use in academic training, a chi-square test was applied. Additionally, a t-test with Bonferroni correction was used to detect mean differences between quantitative variables and academic cell phone use. A logistic regression model was utilized to estimate the association between participant characteristics and the academic use of cellphone, expressed by Odds Ratio and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). In all statistical analyses, a p -value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For both our descriptive and inferential analyses, IBM SPSS Statistics software for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Company, Chicago, IL, USA) were utilized.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 1,590 medical students participated in this study. Among the seven countries, Ecuador and Panama represented the highest number of participants with 21.1% and 18.9%, respectively. In addition, 63.6% were women, with a mean age of 22.0 years (± 3.6). Regarding education, the largest groups of students were in their third year (21.1%) and second year (19.6%). Additionally, 55.7% of participants were in the basic academic stage, and 55.0% were enrolled in private universities (Table 1).

Device characteristics and perceptions

Nearly all participants use a smartphone (98.4%). A majority have access to mobile internet (88.0%), and/or accessed Wi-Fi provided by the university (61.6%). Participants mainly use cell phones for communication (94.7%), social network interaction (91.3%), academic purposes (88.2%), and listening to music (85.2%). Most participants report academic training would be more difficult without a cell phone (61.8%). Nearly four in ten participants (39.3%) report increased self-efficacy for academic problem solving with a cell phone, noting a belief that they can resolve academic doubts on 75–90% of occasions with their phone. Academic use of smartphone

Within the academic purpose use of the cell phone, the most common were for academic content videos (82.9%), social networks (56.9%), and knowledge update applications (55.3%) (Fig. 1).

In all seven countries the leading academic purpose for cell phone use was academic videos (Fig. 2). Social networking applications were the second most reported academic purpose for cell phone use in four countries including Bolivia (21.1%), Chile (20.9%), Colombia (19.9%) and Paraguay (20.2%), while in Ecuador, Panama and Peru knowledge update applications were the second most reported academic purpose of use (Fig. 2).

Participant characteristics and perceptions of cell phone use

A clear trend was observed in the perceptions of academic cell phone use, with students placing increasing importance on their cell phones as they advanced in their undergraduate studies. Students in the clinical academic stage (fourth to seventh year) were the most likely to believe that their academic preparation would be significantly harder without their cell phones (P < 0.001). Furthermore, a positive correlation was identified between the cell phone’s ability to resolve academic doubts and its perceived importance. Students who reported successfully using their cell phones to solve academic queries were also more likely to believe that their academic preparation would be harder without access to their phones (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Participant characteristics association with academic cell phone use

Significant regional variations were observed in academic cell phone use among participants. Students from Colombia (OR: 2.410; 95% CI: 1.410–4.121; P = 0.001), Panama (OR: 1.606; 95% CI: 1.027–2.509; P = 0.037), Paraguay (OR: 3.312; 95% CI: 1.693–6.479; P < 0.001), and Peru (OR: 3.636; 95% CI: 1.905–6.937; P < 0.001) were significantly more likely to use cell phones for academic purposes compared to their peers in Ecuador. A trend toward increased cell phone use for academic purposes was noted as students advanced in their studies, with statistical significance achieved specifically among fifth-year students (OR: 4.517, 95% CI: 1.843–11.075, P = 0.001). Moreover, students in the clinical stage of their education were more likely to rely on cell phones for academic purposes (OR = 2.090, 95% CI: 1.500–2.911; P < 0.001). On the other hand, attending a public university was associated with lower academic cell phone use compared to private university students (OR = 0.714, 95% CI: 0.522–0.977; P = 0.035) (Table 4).

Additionally, the perceived impact of cell phone use on academic training was evident, as students who believed that their academic preparation would be harder without a cell phone were more likely to use it for academic purposes (OR = 2.157, 95% CI 1.463 to 3.181; P = < 0.001) and in those who believe that using the cell phone reduces the stress caused by academic training (OR = 1.826, 95% CI 1.080 to 3.087; P = 0.025) (Table 4).

In terms of practical application, we found a positive association between the actual use of the cell phone for resolving academic doubts and academic performance. Students who reported that their cell phone helped resolve academic questions between 50% and 75% of the time were more likely to use it for academic purposes (OR = 2. 526 95% CI 1.480 to 4.313; P = < 0.001), between 75 and 90% capacity (OR = 2.651, 95% CI 1.615 to 4.350; P = < 0.001) and between 90 and 100% capacity (OR = 5.260, 95% CI 3.021 to 9.159; P = < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

Smartphones have become an integral part of human lifestyle, and medical students are no exception [25, 26]. While their usage spans various disciplines, their pivotal role in facilitating medical education is undeniable [15]. This study aims to describe the current state of smartphone use among medical students in seven Latin American countries and to evaluate its applicability in academic training, covering all levels of medical schools and including students from public and private universities. A predominance of female respondents was observed, reflecting recent trends in medical school demographics in the region, as highlighted by multicenter studies on Latin American medical students [27, 28].

Smartphone access and use has become increasingly ubiquitous across the globe. The current study found that nearly all (98.4%) participants had a smartphone. These findings are similar to a recent study conducted in the Philippines where 93% of medical students had a smartphone [29]. Additionally, the majority (88.0%) of medical students in LATAM already have access to mobile internet and do not depend on Wi-Fi networks. This finding is, nearly identical to results among medical students in Egypt, where 88.6% have access to mobile internet [30], yet falls short of both India and the Philippines, where approximately 98% of medical students enjoy mobile internet access [25, 29, 31].

A key challenge identified in this study is the lack of Wi-Fi access in academic institutions in Latin America. Findings from the current study indicating that just over 60% of medical students have access to Wi-Fi networks demonstrates a lack of progress in this area over the past decade. A 2009 study among medical students in Lima, Peru, similarly exposed deficiencies in progress within regard to Wi-Fi access and detailed the importance of improvements in this area [32]. The disadvantages experienced by medical students in LATAM are further evidence by the nearly universal access to Wi-Fi provided at universities in the United States and other developed nations. Indeed a significant digital divide remains based upon findings from the American Community Survey (ACS) which has shown access to the Internet through Wi-Fi networks among university students in the U.S., including medical students, is practically universal [33].

Although the sample size was not homogeneously distributed across the seven countries, a consistent trend of high academic smartphone use (over 80%) was observed across all regions. Interestingly, students from Colombia, Panama, Paraguay, and Peru exhibited higher academic smartphone usage than their Ecuadorian counterparts. These differences may be attributed to varying levels of technological integration across these countries. For instance, Colombia and Panama rank among the most prepared Latin American countries for adopting advanced technologies, while infrastructure disparities may be more pronounced in certain countries [34, 35].

While academic smartphone use was widespread among medical students at all stages of study, a significant association was only identified among fifth-year students. However, when the data were stratified by academic stage, a clear correlation emerged between academic smartphone use and students in the clinical stage (fourth to seventh years) (OR = 2.090, 95% CI: 1.500–2.911). This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Pakistan, where medical students in more advanced stages reported higher use of medical apps [36], though it contrasts with Egyptian students, where medical app usage was more common among those in the basic academic stage after the COVID-19 pandemic [30]. The association identified in our study may reflect a temporal effect, as more advanced students increasingly recognize the value of smartphones as learning tools. This is further supported by the observation that students using smartphones for academic purposes were, on average, older (22.1 ± 3.6 years; P = 0.009).

Concerning the type of university (education), the current study differences in Wi-Fi access between public and private universities, finding significant disparities. Participants in private universities reported 70.6% access to Wi-Fi, while in public universities, only 29.4% reported having access (p < 0.001). These differences can be explained by the differences in economic resources that characterize public versus private universities in Latin America [37, 38]. Additionally, these findings expose the scenario of Latin American medical students from public universities in terms of limitations in academic activities dependent on the Internet or enhanced by it. Disturbingly, even though students from private universities have greater opportunities for internet access in the academic environment, this group was associated with a lower probability of using cell phones for academic purposes compared to students from public universities (OR = 0.714, 95% CI: 0.522–0.977).

Medical training must keep up with the rapid advancement of science and technology. New tools for knowledge acquisition are a necessity for the training of a new generation of doctors. Modern education and training demand the integration of technological resources, including the Internet, where both access, and the quality of dependable services are essential in meeting learning needs. This has been reflected in the exploration by De La Mora et al. in exposing the reality of medical students in Cuba who cannot link to social networks for academic purposes given that the internet networks provided by universities are limited (Web 1.0) and typical of an “earlier era” [39]. This is important because Wi-Fi Web 2.0 networks that allow users to interact and collaborate as content creators, instead of providing a unidirectional flow of information on websites, are considered necessary [40].

Previous explorations have shown that medical students hold favorable opinions toward the positive impact of smartphones on their medical education [41]. In our study, 61.8% of medical students reported that their academic training would be more difficult without access to a cell phone. Notably, perceptions of the importance of smartphones for academic preparation were closely tied to students’ year of study and stage of undergraduate training. This likely reflects increased familiarity with the academic applications of smartphones as students’ progress through their medical education. Moreover, a positive correlation was found between students’ perceptions and their actual use of smartphones for academic purposes, indicating that both perceptions and practices evolve together in medical students across Latin America. Such perspectives appear common based upon previous studies. most Barbadian medical students believe that smartphones enhance their educational experience [16], while 76.9% of Egyptian medical students express confidence in medical applications, with over 80% recognizing them as flexible learning tools [30]. In the Americas, Quant et al. demonstrated in the United States that 90% of participants think that medical applications support clinical knowledge and 61% think that the information in them is as reliable as that which is found in books [42].

Clearly the Internet and smartphones have become widely accepted tools in medical education and training. Most (88.2%) of the current study sample claimed to use the phone for academic purposes, indicating a paradigm typical of the new generation of doctors in training. This change is part of a global wave that has been evidenced in different corners of the world. For example, in Europe, nearly half (53%) of medical students stated they were familiar with the academic use of the Internet, and 84.9% of medical students in Europe stated that the increased academic use of the Internet in university curricula was a necessity [43]. Similarly, a 2019 study conducted in India, has shown that between 32.9% and 86.8% of medical students have used their cell phone for academic purposes [25, 44]. Likewise, 92% of students in Barbados have been shown to use cell phones for this purpose [16].

An abundance of smartphone applications has been specifically developed for use in medical education. However, the current study’s findings indicate that applications designed to deliver video formats are the most commonly used by medical students. Indeed, YouTube, an application where users can consume and produce original video content, was the most used application for academic purposes in this study. These findings demonstrate medical students’ preferences for less traditional methodologies with the ability to use infographics and videos to organize and present difficult information in a more learning-friendly way, making the learning process more manageable and less stressful [45–47].

In the context of medical education, social networking appears to have the capacity to help medical educators, librarians, and students alike, enhancing teaching-learning experiences through personalization in a rich networking and collaborative environment [43]. Social networking provides a forum for contacting colleagues and teachers from wherever they are, offering the flexibility of “extended work hours.” Selwyn comments that the use of social networking in university education is driven by the learning environment that for students today is based on collective and creative connection; and that social networking in university environments enables a degree of student-driven education in social networking allows students to take a more active role in what they learn, as well as how and when they learn it [36, 48].

While previous investigations into the academic use of mobile phones have primarily focused on medical applications, they often overlook other apps not specifically designed for medical education. In our study, participants were asked to evaluate a broader range of applications, revealing that video platforms with academic content, such as YouTube, were the most frequently used for academic purposes (82.9%), followed by medical update apps like UpToDate (55.3%). Additionally, medical apps like calculators or SCORE were used by only 48.1% of our participants. In this context, research conducted in other countries has shown a preference for medical applications aimed at knowledge updating. For example, in the United States, UpToDate was the most used application among medical students, with a usage rate of 97%, followed by Epocrates with 61% [42]. In contrast, in Pakistan, the most used application was Wikipedia Medical, with a usage rate of 36.2%, followed by Medscape with 31.7% [36]. In Barbados, UpToDate and Medscape were both popular choices [16]. In contrast, Egyptian medical students showed a preference for medical dictionary apps [30].

These results of our study suggest a particular inclination among LATAM medical students towards a preference for academic videos, a tendency that may be supported by a survey conducted in 2016 which found that the majority (85%) of first-semester medical students in Venezuela utilized YouTube videos related to anatomy [49]. The inclination toward, and acceptance of video-based learning may pose risks regarding the quality of such educational content. While specialized users may evaluate their reliability, there is also a likelihood that some videos may disseminate unreliable information, potentially miseducating medical students.

This research aims to clarify the panorama surrounding the academic use of smartphones in medical students. Although the current trend strongly favors the integral and permanent inclusion of these devices in the knowledge acquisition process, we cannot overlook the potential negative effects that these devices may have on learning. Phones can present a significant distraction for some students, which could adversely affect their medical training. A significant portion of the participants (91.3%) showed a tendency to dedicate their cell phone usage to interacting on social networks. Social networking has the potential to affect academic preparation in various ways, including procrastination and a lack of attention towards studies and knowledge acquisition [39]. Similarly, previous research by Paul JA et al. revealed a small but significant negative correlation (r = -0.119, p = 0.048) between time spent on social networks and academic performance, defined by course grade point average and cumulative grades [50].

Our study had certain limitations. Firstly, our survey was conducted online, which could bias our sample toward medical students with easier access to technology or greater familiarity with online platforms. Additionally, our data were based on participant self-reports. This introduces the possibility of recall bias or social desirability bias, wherein participants may exaggerate their academic smartphone usage or provide responses they believe are socially acceptable.

Also, due to the cross-sectional design of our study, we cannot establish causality between smartphone use and academic performance. It is not yet clear whether high smartphone use leads to better academic outcomes or whether successful students simply use smartphones more frequently. Moreover, while our study included participants from seven Latin American countries, the findings may not be universally applicable across the region. Variations in internet accessibility, smartphone ownership, and educational resources could affect the relevance of our findings for other settings.

Conclusions

Medical students in Latin America are part of the global shift and exhibit a marked inclination towards using smartphones as a significant tool for academic learning and medical training. To maximize the utility of smartphones for the service of learning, academic institutions should make broad student access to Wi-Fi a resource priority.

Despite the existence of many medical applications currently available, the preference of Latin American medical students lies in video content platforms such as YouTube and TikTok. Medical training should consider developing content for use and distribution on these platforms.
