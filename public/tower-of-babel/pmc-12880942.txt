Psychological Impact of Pulmonary Embolism: A Narrative Review
Authors: Farooq Hyder, Hina Pervaiz, Mubeena Javid, Raveena B Bansari, FNU Karishma, Daya Kumari, Poonam Kumari, Ali Karim, Hari V Nenwani, Allah Warayo, FNU Dropati, Kishor Kumari, Muhammad Salman, Abida Parveen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a potentially life-threatening thromboembolic condition that demands urgent medical attention. While significant strides have been made in the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of PE, its long-term psychological consequences remain underexplored. Survivors often face considerable emotional and psychological challenges that can impair recovery and reduce quality of life. This narrative review aims to explore the psychological impact of PE, including common mental health outcomes such as anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and health-related fears. It also examines contributing factors such as persistent physical symptoms, anticoagulation therapy, social and occupational disruptions, and gaps in mental health care within current clinical practice. PE is not solely a physical health event but a psychologically impactful experience with lasting consequences. Holistic management strategies that address both physical and emotional recovery are vital to improving long-term outcomes and quality of life in PE survivors.Keywords: Pulmonary embolism, Psychological impact, Post-traumatic stress disorder, Anxiety, Depression, Mental health, Thromboembolism, Quality of life, Post-PE syndrome, Holistic care

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a potentially life-threatening condition characterized by the obstruction of pulmonary arteries by thrombotic material, most commonly originating from deep veins of the lower extremities.1 While the physical consequences of PE, such as respiratory compromise, right heart strain, and risk of recurrence, are well documented, its psychological impact remains an often underrecognized aspect of patient care.2 Individuals who experience PE frequently confront significant emotional distress, including anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress symptoms, driven by the sudden onset, severity, and potential fatality of the event.3 The unpredictable nature of PE and the prolonged treatment course, which often involves anticoagulation therapy and lifestyle modifications, may further contribute to psychological burden.4 This narrative review aims to explore the psychological sequelae of PE, examine contributing factors, and highlight the importance of integrating mental health support into the long-term management of affected patients.

Methods

This narrative review was conducted to synthesize current evidence on the psychological impact of pulmonary embolism (PE) and its implications for patient care. The methodology followed a structured approach, though it did not adopt the systematic review framework, in keeping with the aims of a narrative review.

A comprehensive literature search was performed across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, covering the period from 2000 to June 2025. The following keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were used in various combinations: “pulmonary embolism,” “psychological impact,” “mental health,” “depression,” “anxiety,” “post-traumatic stress disorder,” “quality of life,” and “anticoagulation therapy.” Boolean operators (AND/OR) were applied to refine search results.

Studies were considered eligible if they included adult patients with confirmed pulmonary embolism, reported psychological outcomes such as anxiety, depression, PTSD, health-related quality of life, or treatment-related emotional burden, were published in English, and consisted of observational studies, randomized controlled trials, qualitative studies, reviews, or guideline papers relevant to the topic. Studies focusing solely on acute hemodynamic or surgical management of PE without psychological outcomes, case reports, editorials, or conference abstracts lacking sufficient detail were excluded.

Titles and abstracts of retrieved articles were screened for relevance, and full-text articles meeting inclusion criteria were reviewed in detail. Data extracted from the selected studies included study design, sample size, patient population, psychological outcomes assessed, instruments used for assessment, and key findings. Additional references were identified by reviewing bibliographies of included articles.

Although this is not a systematic review, efforts were made to ensure credibility and transparency by including studies from peer-reviewed journals, prioritizing larger and methodologically sound investigations, and acknowledging limitations such as variability in psychological assessment tools and small sample sizes in some studies.

Discussion

3.1. Overview of pulmonary embolism and its broader impact

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a serious and potentially fatal condition that occurs when a blood clot, usually originating from the deep veins of the lower extremities, becomes lodged in the pulmonary arteries, obstructing blood flow to the lungs.5,6 While the immediate concern in PE management revolves around hemodynamic stabilization and prevention of recurrence, increasing attention is being drawn to the long-term consequences that extend beyond physical health.7 Survivors of PE may continue to experience physical symptoms for months or even years after the acute episode.8 However, the emotional and psychological ramifications are frequently overlooked in both clinical research and practice.9 Psychological distress following PE is common and can significantly impact a patient's recovery trajectory, quality of life, and functional status10 as described in Table 1.

3.2. Psychological responses to the acute PE event

The acute presentation of PE is often sudden, dramatic, and life-threatening. Patients frequently present with chest pain, dyspnea, tachycardia, and in some cases, syncope or cardiac arrest.11 These symptoms can lead to profound fear, confusion, and emotional shock. For many individuals, this initial experience leaves a lasting psychological imprint.12

The sense of immediate danger, loss of bodily control, and uncertainty about survival are comparable to those seen in patients experiencing myocardial infarction or stroke.13 Studies have reported that a substantial number of PE survivors describe their experience as traumatic as shown in Table 2. This acute psychological response may initially be adaptive, enabling patients to cope with the crisis.14,15 However, for many, these symptoms do not resolve completely and may evolve into longer-term mental health conditions.16,17

3.3. Post-traumatic stress disorder and anxiety after PE

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is increasingly recognized in patients following PE, especially in those who experienced a severe or near-fatal episode.18 The criteria for PTSD, including re-experiencing the trauma, avoidance behavior, hyperarousal, and negative mood alterations, can manifest in individuals long after hospital discharge. Flashbacks, intrusive thoughts, and nightmares related to the PE episode are not uncommon.19,20

Furthermore, generalized anxiety is prevalent among PE survivors. Patients often describe a persistent fear of recurrence, known colloquially as “thrombo-phobia.” This fear can lead to heightened awareness of bodily sensations―such as palpitations or minor chest discomfort―and misinterpretation of these signals as early signs of another PE.21 This hypervigilance not only impairs emotional well-being but can result in frequent healthcare utilization, repeated emergency visits, and unnecessary diagnostic testing.22

3.4. Depression and emotional numbing in PE survivors

Depression is another major psychological sequela in patients recovering from PE. While some depressive symptoms may be reactive to the traumatic nature of the event, others may stem from long-term disability, uncertainty about health, or disruption in social and occupational roles.23,24 The psychological toll of ongoing symptoms such as fatigue and dyspnea―especially when they persist without clear explanation―can foster feelings of helplessness and despair.25

Patients may also experience a phenomenon known as emotional numbing, where they feel detached from their surroundings, unable to experience pleasure or connect emotionally with others.26 This can lead to strained relationships, social withdrawal, and reduced participation in daily life. Without recognition and treatment, depression following PE can become chronic and severely impair a patient's psychosocial functioning.27

3.5. Health anxiety and somatization

Health anxiety, a preoccupation with the belief of having or acquiring a serious illness, is frequently reported in PE survivors.28 The unpredictable nature of the initial PE episode, combined with limited public awareness about the condition, can create an environment of constant fear. Patients may become obsessed with monitoring their symptoms, excessively researching PE-related content, or repeatedly seeking reassurance from physicians.29

In some cases, this anxiety may manifest as somatization―when emotional distress is expressed through physical symptoms. Patients may report ongoing pain, fatigue, or breathlessness without clear physiological cause. These symptoms are often difficult to manage, especially in the absence of objective findings, and may lead to frustration for both the patient and healthcare provider. Left unaddressed, health anxiety can significantly diminish the patient's quality of life.30

3.6. Role of anticoagulation and its psychological impact

Long-term anticoagulation is a cornerstone of PE management, particularly in patients with unprovoked or recurrent embolic events. While medically necessary, anticoagulation therapy presents unique challenges that can contribute to psychological stress.31 Patients often worry about bleeding risks, dietary restrictions (especially with vitamin K antagonists like warfarin), and potential interactions with other medications. These concerns may foster fear around engaging in physical activities or traveling, further limiting lifestyle choices.32

Moreover, the need for regular monitoring (such as INR checks) and frequent doctor visits can reinforce the perception of being chronically ill. For some, the treatment itself becomes a source of anxiety, a constant reminder of their vulnerability. In cases where direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are prescribed, the absence of a monitoring requirement may paradoxically cause anxiety in patients who equate frequent testing with safety and control.33

3.7. Persistent symptoms and Post-PE syndrome

Post-PE syndrome is a relatively recently defined condition characterized by ongoing symptoms such as breathlessness, fatigue, and exercise intolerance, in the absence of identifiable pulmonary hypertension. These symptoms can significantly impair daily functioning, especially when they persist despite normal imaging and laboratory results.34

This mismatch between subjective symptoms and objective findings can be particularly distressing for patients. They may feel dismissed by healthcare professionals, misunderstood by family and friends, and uncertain about the future. This disconnect can intensify psychological symptoms such as frustration, hopelessness, and irritability. Addressing post-PE syndrome requires a nuanced approach that recognizes the interplay between physical and psychological health.35

3.8. Social and occupational implications

The psychosocial consequences of PE extend into occupational and social domains. Patients recovering from PE may face prolonged work absences, reduced productivity, and fear of re-integrating into physically demanding roles. These challenges can trigger financial strain, reduced self-esteem, and fear of job loss, especially in those without supportive work environments.36

Socially, individuals may experience isolation, particularly if they feel others do not understand the seriousness or lingering impact of their condition. The invisible nature of many post-PE symptoms can lead to a lack of empathy, which further discourages patients from sharing their struggles.37 Support groups and patient education initiatives can help mitigate this isolation, providing a platform for shared experiences and emotional validation.

3.9. Gaps in clinical practice and need for integrated mental health care

Despite the growing body of evidence highlighting the psychological toll of PE, mental health assessment is rarely a routine component of post-PE care. Clinical guidelines primarily emphasize anticoagulation, risk stratification, and recurrence prevention, with little focus on emotional or psychological recovery. Mental health screening tools―such as the Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7), Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), or PTSD Checklist (PCL-5)―are underutilized in this population.38

Furthermore, the siloed nature of healthcare delivery often separates physical and mental health care. As a result, patients with emotional symptoms may be bounced between providers without receiving appropriate treatment. There is a pressing need for a multidisciplinary approach in PE follow-up that includes mental health professionals, social workers, rehabilitation specialists, and primary care providers.38,39

3.10. Strategies for addressing psychological impact

Effective strategies for addressing the psychological impact of PE should involve early identification, timely intervention, and sustained support. Routine mental health screening during follow-up appointments can help identify at-risk individuals. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), mindfulness techniques, and trauma-informed counseling have all shown benefit in managing anxiety and PTSD symptoms.40

Patient education is also crucial. Providing clear, consistent information about PE, its treatment, and prognosis can reduce uncertainty and empower patients to engage actively in their recovery. Peer support groups, whether in-person or virtual, can offer emotional solidarity and practical advice from others who have gone through similar experiences.41

Conclusion

Pulmonary embolism is not merely an acute vascular event―it is a life-altering experience with complex psychological consequences that often go unrecognized. Survivors may grapple with anxiety, depression, PTSD, health-related fears, and ongoing emotional distress related to persistent symptoms, social challenges, and treatment burdens. 43 These psychological responses can be as debilitating as the physical aftermath and can significantly impair quality of life and functional recovery.44

Unfortunately, mental health remains a neglected component of PE management. The current clinical approach tends to prioritize physical recovery, leaving patients to cope with emotional struggles in silence.45 There is a critical need for comprehensive, multidisciplinary care models that integrate mental health support into routine PE follow-up. By acknowledging and addressing the psychological impact of PE, clinicians can offer more compassionate, holistic care that promotes full recovery and long-term well-being.46–48
