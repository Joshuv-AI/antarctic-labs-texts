Introduction

Anastomotic pseudoaneurysms are rare complications of any vascular reconstruction and require prompt management to prevent life-threatening complications, such as rupture and thromboembolism [1]. The available literature mainly describes anastomotic pseudoaneurysms in the inguinal region or involving reconstructed visceral arteries [1, 2]. Although considered relatively safe, carotid endarterectomy can be complicated by pseudoaneurysm formation, which is potentially life-threatening [3]. Pseudoaneurysms occurrence is mainly attributed to arterial wall degeneration, intraoperative arterial damage, suture line disruption, and infection [4, 5]. This case report presents the successful management of anastomotic pseudoaneurysms of both the distal and proximal anastomoses of an interposition vein graft between the CCA and the ICA. The original interposition graft was performed as a bailout procedure after the patient developed a complication of carotid surgery. We performed revisional surgery, which involved en bloc excision of the vein graft with the pseudoaneurysms and subsequent interposition of a new extra-anatomical vein graft from the subclavian artery to the remnant extracranial ICA. Owing to the lack of data in published literature and the rarity of this complication, we believe that this case report can be helpful in guiding the decision-making process in similar circumstances.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old Ukrainian male presented to our hospital with false aneurysms of both the distal and proximal anastomoses of an interposition vein graft between the common carotid artery (CCA) and the internal carotid artery (ICA) 3 months after the index operation.

The patient initially presented to a military hospital with an ischemic stroke that occurred during military action. The preoperative imaging showed an ischemic area in the territory of the middle cerebral artery, a 90% stenosis of the left ICA according to NASCET criteria, and a 65% stenosis of the right ICA. He underwent an emergency endarterectomy and bovine pericardial patch. On the postoperative day (POD) 18, the patient experienced sudden pain and swelling at the surgical site associated with leaking blood through the site of a prior drain. He was urgently returned to the theater where active bleeding was identified from a total disruption of the patch suture line. Ligation of the external carotid artery and interposition vein grafting from the CCA to the ICA was performed. On POD 8 after the relook surgery, the patient sustained temporary loss of consciousness and raised temperature. An urgent magnetic resonance image (MRI) of the brain was performed but no new changes were reported. The patient was subsequently transferred to another medical facility where a carotid ultrasound demonstrated a false aneurysm of both distal and proximal anastomoses of the interposition vein graft.

After admission to our facility, the patient underwent a thorough preoperative workup. Pseudoaneurysms of interposition vein graft were confirmed on computed tomography (CT) scan, which is demonstrated in Fig. 1. The operation was performed under general anesthesia. The first stage was an extra-anatomical interposition of vein grafting from the subclavian artery to the 15 mm long remaining extracranial internal carotid artery (Fig. 2). The second stage was excision of the pre-existing vein graft and the false aneurysm and wound drainage (Figs. 3, 4).

The patient was started on linezolid and cefepine and the surgery was performed as planned. As a postoperative anticoagulation patient was prescribed cilostazol 200 mg daily and triflusal 600 mg daily.

On POD 7, once the microbiology results of the debrids of the pseudoaneurism culture showed Pseudomonas aureginosa sensitive to piperacillin and tazobactam, the antibiotic treatment was changed accordingly. Unfortunately on POD 9, the patient developed pseudomembranous colitis, and oral vancomycin with intravenous metronidazole was prescribed for the next 5 days until resolution of symptoms. The patient developed a fever and a CT scan of the head and neck with contrast was performed to exclude surgical complications. This revealed good graft patency and only a fluid collection at the site of the resected pseudoaneurysms (Fig. 5), which was successfully drained. Staphylococcus haemolyticus sensitive to piperacillin and tazobactam was demonstrated on the culture of the fluid drained. Antibiotic therapy was continued for 5 days.

Discussion

Anastomotic pseudoaneurysm formation, though uncommon, occurs in 2–5% of patients undergoing arterial reconstruction [1]. Whilst literature mainly focuses on pseudoaneurysms in the femoral region, sporadic cases involving reconstructed visceral arteries are also reported [2].

Pseudoaneurysms (PAs) following carotid endarterectomy (CEA) are rare but potentially life-threatening complications, with a very low incidence ranging from 0.3% to 0.6% [3].

Factors contributing to pseudoaneurysm formation include arterial wall degeneration, intraoperative arterial damage, suture line disruption and infection [4]. Infections complicate approximately one-third of all post-CEA pseudoaneurysms [5]. This diagnosis should be considered early when patients present with persistent hematoma, recurrent bleeding from the wound, or late wound infection [6].

Despite an unestablished rate of rupture, surgical intervention is recommended to prevent rupture or thromboembolism [7]. Open surgical repair remains the gold standard approach, offering several advantages over endovascular techniques. In a study by El-Sabrout et al., the authors retrospectively reviewed a series of infected post-carotid endarterectomy pseudoaneurysms and concluded that open surgical repair resulted in satisfactory outcomes [4]. Similarly a further case series, Ahuja and Tefera reported successful outcomes with open surgical repair [6].

Open surgical repair provides several benefits: it allows for direct visualization of the pseudoaneurysm, thorough debridement of infected tissues, and meticulous reconstruction of the arterial wall with autologous or synthetic grafts [8]. Moreover, it reduces the risk of recurrent infection and embolic complications associated with endovascular techniques [9]. Ocal et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of hybrid vascular techniques in managing complex cervical carotid artery aneurysms, highlighting the importance of personalized treatments [7].

In conclusion, mycotic anastomotic pseudoaneurysms following carotid endarterectomy are rare but serious complications that require expedited treatment. Open surgical repair remains the cornerstone of their management, offering superior long-term outcomes and minimizing the risk of postoperative complications [1].

Conclusions

Mycotic anastomotic pseudoaneurysms following carotid endarterectomy represent rare yet grave complications that demand urgent attention. While endovascular techniques could be considered as an alternative approach, open surgical repair remains the gold standard of management, offering unparalleled advantages in preventing postoperative ischemic complications and ensuring optimal long-term outcomes. The efficacy of open repair is granted by the direct visualization of the pseudoaneurysm, meticulous debridement of infected tissues, and precise reconstruction of the arterial wall with autologous or synthetic grafts. In light of the substantial evidence supporting its superiority, open surgical repair should remain the preferred approach in addressing mycotic anastomotic pseudoaneurysms following carotid endarterectomy. Future research should continue to explore advancements in surgical techniques and refine treatment strategies to further enhance patient outcomes in this challenging clinical scenario.
