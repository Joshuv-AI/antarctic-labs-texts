A Schwannoma in the Anterior Orbit: Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Bassam Alobaid, Rahaf Alok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOrbital schwannoma is a rare tumor that can occur in any orbital compartment, posing a diagnostic challenge for clinicians.Case PresentationWe report a 35‐year‐old male who presented with a visible schwannoma in the inferomedial orbital angle. Our patient was treated with en bloc transconjunctival surgical resection without complications.DiscussionOnly 35 cases of anterior orbital schwannomas have been reported in the literature. The majority of these masses were superior or superomedial. The nerve of origin was able to be identified in 42.9% of cases only, and the schwannoma arose from the frontal nerve or its branches in about half of these cases. Surgery was the standard of care, although in a few cases small masses were just observed. None of the reported cases has neurofibromatosis. This case highlights the importance of considering schwannoma in the differential diagnosis of anterior orbital masses in any of its anatomical compartments.

1. Introduction

Schwannoma is a benign peripheral nerve sheath tumor (PNST) that can arise from any peripheral nerve. It generally affects adults between 20 and 60 years old with a male‐female ratio of 1:1. Orbital schwannoma is a rare tumor, accounting for about 0.7%–2.3% of all orbital tumors [1, 2]. Schwannoma can arise in any anatomical compartment of the orbit, but it is usually encountered in a superior, extraconal location [2]. Symptoms vary according to the site, size, and involved nerve [3]. This diversity in clinical presentation makes it a significant masquerader in the orbit. Complete surgical resection is the treatment of choice with a low rate of complications [4]. Here, the authors highlight the importance of considering schwannoma in the differential diagnosis for any orbital lesion, and present a comprehensive review of the literature on anterior orbital schwannomas.

2. Case Presentation

A 35‐year‐old male with no prior medical history presented with a painless and visible mass in the medial canthus, which had been growing progressively over the last year. On physical examination, his best corrected visual acuity was (20/20) in both eyes. A regular nontender mass was noted between the right globe and the medial canthus (Figure 1). It caused mild limitation in adduction and anterior displacement of the lower eyelid. Ocular movements were otherwise normal. The lacrimal drainage system, the pupillary reaction, and the fundus examination were normal in both eyes.

Preoperative photograph showing the mass.

Radiographic assessment with computed tomography (CT) without contrast revealed a round well‐circumscribed mass in the anterior inferomedial compartment of the right orbit, below the insertion of the medial rectus muscle. The lesion exhibits iso‐ to hyperdense attenuation, measuring 20 × 15 × 15 mm. The adjacent medial orbital wall was intact without signs of bone erosion. Due to the radiological shape of the lesion, the most probable diagnosis was dermoid cyst (Figure 2).

Axial CT without contrast showing a well‐circumscribed round mass in the inferomedial orbital compartment (the arrow); there is no bone erosion (A, B). Note its location near the insertion of the medial rectus muscle (A).

The patient was scheduled for an excisional biopsy. A transconjunctival incision was performed inferomedially above the mass. The surrounding tissues (conjunctiva and periorbital fat) were bluntly dissected using Westcott scissors, taking special care to preserve the lacrimal sac. After that, a smooth, rubbery, and well‐encapsulated mass was carefully removed en bloc (Figure 3). A relationship between the mass and a specific nerve could not be identified. The incision was closed with absorbable sutures (6/0 Vicryl). The patient had an uneventful recovery.

Intraoperative photograph showing the mass after dissecting the surrounding tissues.

Histopathologic examination revealed a well‐defined spindle cell tumor, without evidence of mitotic activity or necrosis. The tumor had a biphasic pattern of closely packed spindle cells (Antoni A) and loosely arranged cells (Antoni B). Some tumor cells were arranged in palisades forming Verocay bodies. These findings were consistent with benign orbital schwannoma (Figure 4). The patient remains recurrence free 1 year after resection. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case.

Photomicrograph of the tumor specimen (H&E) ×10 showing Antoni A areas (A) with Verocay body (star), and Antoni B areas (B), consistent with schwannoma.

3. Discussion

This case highlights the importance of considering schwannoma in the differential diagnosis of anterior orbital masses. A MEDLINE database search identified a total of 35 anterior orbital schwannomas cases published between January 1990 and September 2025. Cases describing schwannomas with posterior orbital compartment extension, if the site of the tumor was not specified or published in a language other than English, were excluded. The majority of the 35 cases were females (19, 54.3%) (Table 1). The patients ranged in age between 5–75 years, with an average of 41.2 years (Table 1). The most common presenting symptom was a palpable painless eyelid mass (65.7%), followed by proptosis (22.9%), dystopia (11.4%), diplopia (17.1%), ptosis (8.6%), and pain (5.7%). Abnormal head posture, decreased visual acuity, hypesthesia, and epiphora were reported in one case each (2.9%). The mass was discovered incidentally in two cases (5.7%), and symptoms were not reported in four of the cases (Table 1).

The distribution of the masses′ location was as follows: superior (10 masses, 28.6%), superomedial (9 masses, 25.7%), medial (5 masses, 14.3%), inferomedial (2 masses, 5.7%), inferior (9 masses, 25.7%), inferolateral (2 masses, 5.7%), lateral (1 masses, 2.9%), and superolateral (1 mass, 2.9%). Only one schwannoma, besides the one presented in this case, was found in an inferomedial location (Table 1).

Surgery was the treatment method of choice in the majority of cases (77.1%), whereas three cases were observed without intervention (8.6%). One of them required surgery 1.5 years later due to rapid tumor growth. The management was not reported in the remaining cases. Although small, asymptomatic orbital schwannoma can be observed, the patient in our case was treated surgically as the lesion had demonstrated growth over the preceding year (Table 1).

The nerve of origin was specified in 15 patients (42.9%) (Table 1). In eight of these (22.9%), the tumor originated from the frontal nerve or one of its branches (supraorbital or supratrochlear nerves). Three cases reported a schwannoma arising from the infraorbital nerve (8.6%). Origin from the nerve of the inferior oblique muscle, the lacrimal, and the zygomaticotemporal nerves was each reported once (2.9%). One case reported an equivocal origin, between the nasal branch of the anterior ethmoidal nerve or the infraorbital nerve [19]. The nerve of origin was not identified in more than half of the published cases of anterior orbital schwannomas, including the present case. This could be explained by the fact that anterior orbital schwannomas often originate from small, unnamed terminal branches that are challenging to delineate. Indeed, it could be due to the large volume of orbital fat and the great number of nerves crossing it [9] (Table 1).

Surgical complications were minor: Three patients reported numbness of forehead (8.6%), three developed diplopia (8.6%), and two developed ptosis (5.7%). These findings tend to improve over the reported follow‐up periods. Tumor recurrence is rare when gross total resection is achieved. In contrast, two cases of incomplete tumor resection were complicated by tumor regrowth [11, 17] (Table 1).

Out of 35 reported cases, the majority of patients had a solitary tumor. Multiple orbital schwannomas were found in four patients; one of them had a mass in each orbit [14]. Two patients were diagnosed with orbital schwannomatosis (5.7%) [6, 11]. Neurofibromatosis was not diagnosed in any patient (Table 1).

Schwannomas can be missed due to the rarity of the diagnosis. The most frequently reported preliminary differential diagnosis included dermoid cyst and cavernous hemangioma. Other differential diagnoses are shown in the table (Table 1).

Typically, schwannomas present as a smooth, oval mass with its long axis oriented in the anterior–posterior direction. On CT imaging, it is usually isodense or hypodense to the gray matter [20]. On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), schwannomas tend to be isointense or hypointense on T1‐weighted (T1W) images and mildly hyperintense on T2‐weighted (T2W) images compared with extraocular muscles, exhibiting either homogenous or heterogenous contrast enhancement [2, 21]. In contrast, a dermoid cyst is the most common orbital tumor in children. It typically occurs near the lateral brow adjacent to the suture. It is a well‐circumscribed and heterogeneous mass that has a visible wall and produces bone changes [22]. On MRI, it is hyperintense on T1‐weighted images and lacks gadolinium enhancement, differentiating it from a schwannoma [23]. Cavernous hemangioma is the most common primary orbital tumor in adults, particularly in middle‐aged women, and it is often seen in the intraconal space. It shows significant homogenous hyperintensity on T2‐weighted images and a progressive enhancement pattern on MRI. Both are key features that distinguish it from a schwannoma [24]. Preoperative MRI was not obtained in our case, due to financial restraints in a resources‐limited setting, and because it would not have changed the surgical goal of gross total resection. However, MRI images can offer valuable information regarding the anatomical location of the tumor and its relationship with surrounding important structures. This case illustrates an anterior orbital schwannoma in an unusual inferomedial location. Schwannoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of a circumscribed, slow‐growing anterior orbital mass. Surgical resection is safe and almost always curative, with low risk for complications.
