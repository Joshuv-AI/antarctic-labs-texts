Exploring the interrelationships among physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depression symptoms in university students
Authors: Xiaoling Zhu, Feng Ding, Shuqi Jia, Sen Li, Xing Wang, Xingze Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to investigate the interrelationship between physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depression symptoms among university students experiencing depression, while elucidating the potential mediating pathways through which resting-state EEG may modulate these relationships, ultimately providing neural regulation targets for behavioral interventions. This study utilized a case-control design involving 76 university students with depression symptoms and 64 healthy controls. Standardized questionnaires were employed to evaluate behavioral characteristics, and performance on visuospatial working memory tasks was integrated with multidimensional analyses of resting-state EEG to formulate a structural equation model that elucidates the mediating pathways. The analysis revealed that there were no statistically significant differences in demographic variables between the case and control groups (p > 0.05). Notably, significant differences emerged in visuospatial working memory, physical exercise, and depression symptoms when comparing university students with depression to their healthy counterparts (p < 0.05). Furthermore, physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depression symptoms exhibited significant correlations among students with depression (p < 0.05). The δ (C3 + C4) was established as a common EEG-specific indicator for all three variables, mediating 26.7% of the total effect [B = -0.031, 95% CI (-0.069, -0.006)], while the direct effect constituted 73.28% of the total effect [B = -0.085, 95% CI (-0.149, -0.019)]. This study demonstrates a significant correlation among physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depression symptoms in university students with depression. The δ (C3 + C4) emerges as a potential shared target for these three factors. It is recommended that universities integrate structured physical activity into their mental health programs.

Introduction

Depression is a prevalent mental health issue among university students and is broadly acknowledged as a major threat to both individuals and society1. According to the World Health Organization, more than 280 million people globally experience varying degrees of depressive symptoms, resulting in over 700,000 annual deaths by suicide, a trend that is especially pronounced in the university student population2,3. Visuospatial working memory is defined as the cognitive capacity individuals utilize to process, store, and manipulate visual and spatial information4. Studies indicate a notable correlation between depressive symptoms and visuospatial working memory, suggesting that university students experiencing depressive symptoms frequently demonstrate a reduction in their visuospatial working memory capabilities5,6. Prior studies have demonstrated that an exacerbation of depressive symptoms can result in approximately a 20% decline in working memory performance, and that the adverse effects of depressive symptoms on visuospatial working memory are both progressive and cumulative7. Visuospatial working memory influences the efficiency of information processing, the choice of learning strategies, and the ability to execute complex tasks, all of which are vital for university students’ academic performance, social interactions, and decision-making in everyday life4.

Exercise, recognized as a form of neurofeedback therapy, is distinguished by its natural, healthy attributes and absence of side effects8,9. Research demonstrates that regular physical exercise can enhance cognitive function by stimulating the release of endogenous neurotransmitters (such as endorphins and serotonin), improving cerebral blood circulation, and fostering neural plasticity10. On a microscopic scale, physical exercise enhances the nutritional supply and energy metabolism of brain cells while promoting synaptogenesis and neuronal survival. On a macroscopic scale, physical exercise not only increases the volume of brain structures such as the cerebellum and hippocampus but also affects the activation levels of brain regions and their functional connectivity11. These biological mechanisms offer a theoretical foundation for the enhancement of visuospatial working memory among university students experiencing depressive symptoms through physical exercise. Consequently, investigating the effects of physical exercise on boosting visuospatial working memory in university students with depressive symptoms not only possesses substantial theoretical significance but also provides innovative strategies for practical interventions.

Electroencephalography (EEG), a non-invasive bioelectrical monitoring technique, has garnered extensive application in psychology and neuroscience research in recent years12. EEG possesses several advantages, including non-invasiveness, low examination costs, portability (making it suitable for community screening), and high temporal resolution, which have been substantiated by various studies13–15. EEG enables real-time recording of brain electrical activity, uncovering the brainwave patterns of individuals in a resting state, which provides critical evidence for elucidating the complex relationship between depressive symptoms and cognitive function16. By analyzing EEG data, researchers can identify specific EEG markers that are common to both physical exercise and cognitive function, thereby providing potential pathways for enhancing working memory in university students experiencing depressive symptoms through physical exercise17. Current research has predominantly concentrated on the impact of physical exercise on depressive symptoms and overall cognitive function, with limited focus on the role of EEG in the interplay between physical exercise and visuospatial working memory among university students experiencing depressive symptoms.

Over the past decade, our team has been dedicated to exploring the dose-response relationship between physical exercise and cognition. Through previous studies, we have discovered a close association between physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depressive symptoms. Physical exercise may serve as an effective means to improve visuospatial working memory, sleep, and depressive symptoms, with the delta frequency band potentially being a key target18–20. Based on prior findings, this study aims to utilize a case-control research design to investigate the potential pathways connecting physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depressive symptoms, specifically aiming to address the following questions: Do physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depressive symptoms exhibit any common EEG-specific markers? If such markers exist, which electrode sites are primarily associated with these common EEG-specific indicators? Can these common EEG-specific markers serve as mediators in the relationship between physical exercise and depressive symptoms?

Methods

Subjects

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software, utilizing t-tests: Fixed effects, omnibus, one-way. A medium effect size of 0.5 was selected21, with an alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.8, leading to a total sample size of 126 participants, ensuring that each group comprises at least 64 individuals.

Seventy-six university students with depressive symptoms were selected as the case group from seven universities in the Songjiang University Town of Shanghai through mental health screenings. The control group was composed of 64 healthy university students, matched based on differences in depressive symptoms among the students, recruited in an approximately 1:1 ratio through online advertisements and offline posters. This research was carried out at the Sports and Health Promotion Research Center of Shanghai University of Sport and has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Sport (Ethics No.: 102772021RT004). This research strictly adhered to the Helsinki Declaration. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for both the case and control groups are presented in Table 1.

Testing procedure

The testing took place between September and December 2023 at the Research Center for Exercise and Health Promotion, Shanghai University of Sport, from 2 PM to 5 PM. All participants were instructed not to engage in vigorous exercise or consume coffee, alcohol, or other stimulants within 12 h before the experiment. It is important to note that another sleep-related study19 conducted by our team during the same period utilized the same EEG equipment; however, there are substantial differences between the two studies in terms of research subjects, core variables, and statistical models. Each study specifically explores different dimensions of the pathological mechanisms of depression. The experimental procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Measurement tools and indicators

(1) Basic Information Questionnaire.

This questionnaire collected essential demographic and background information from participants, including gender, age, height, weight, smoking habits, drinking habits, place of household registration, only child status, single-parent family status, parents’ marital status, family relationships, academic difficulty, and frequency of social activities.

(2) Behavioral Assessment of Visuospatial Working Memory.

Taking into account the cognitive capabilities of university students, the aims of the research, and the task’s difficulty and feasibility (the 2-back task effectively provokes cognitive challenges, contrasting with the lower burden of the 1-back and the higher burden of the 3-back, while preventing a “floor effect” in individuals with depression caused by overly challenging tasks22, this study employs a 2-back task for testing. The task includes a total of 55 trials, comprising 5 practice trials and 50 formal trials. Following the completion of the 5 practice trials, participants were asked if they were familiar with the experimental task and procedure. If they replied negatively, they were instructed to press the “Q” key for additional practice; if they confirmed their understanding, they could enter the formal experiment by pressing the “Enter” key. To ensure a balance between task duration and signal-to-noise ratio while maintaining an adequate number of trials, we selected a total of 50 trials. The detailed experimental procedure begins with the presentation of a fixation point “+” for 3000 ms, followed by images displaying a single letter for 2000 ms, with a stimulus interval of 1000 ms. The task materials and stimuli were displayed on a computer with a resolution of 1920 pixels × 1080 pixels, and the viewing distance was kept at around 40 cm.

To distinguish between university students with depressive symptoms and healthy university students, this experiment selected eight uppercase letters as stimuli for assessing visuospatial working memory. 为To minimize the potential for confusion, the study chose the uppercase letters B, D, H, K, M, P, S, and Y, thus preventing any confusion among participants. The background of the screen was black, and the eight uppercase letters were randomly displayed at eight positions across the screen. This experiment employed eight equally probable spatial positions, segmenting the screen into eight evenly distributed virtual quadrants. Each stimulus was positioned at a central point defined in a polar coordinate system, with the center at the midpoint of the screen. The radius was set at one-third of the screen height (360 pixels), and the angular intervals were 45° (0°, 45°, 90°, …, up to 315°). Within the 50 formal trials, each spatial position theoretically appeared 50/8 = 6.25 times (rounded to either 6 or 7 occurrences), with the actual presentation frequency managed through a complete randomization algorithm. In the 2-back task, participants were required to memorize the spatial positions of the letters while ignoring the letters themselves. If the position of the currently presented letter matched that of the letter presented two trials prior, participants were to press the “J” key; if it was different, they were to press the “F” key. The ratio of stimulus matches to non-matches was set at 1:1. The statistical results comprised reaction times and accuracy rates for the 2-back task. In the phase of behavioral data analysis, incorrect trials (including erroneous key presses of “F/J” or lack of response) were eliminated, as were reaction times that were considered anomalous (RT < 200 ms indicating anticipatory responses, and RT > 2000 ms indicating responses exceeding the stimulus interval). (3) Physical Activity Rating Scale-3 (PARS-3).

The PARS-3 scale, developed by Hashimoto Kinn, and adapted into Chinese by Liang Deqing and colleagues, has an internal consistency reliability of 0.80–0.86 and a test-retest reliability of 0.8223. This scale primarily reflects the physical activity of non-physical education majors over the past month. It evaluates three dimensions: intensity, duration, and frequency, where intensity and frequency are scored from 1 to 5, and duration from 0 to 4. Higher scores in these dimensions indicate greater physical activity. The formula for calculating physical activity is: Intensity × Duration × Frequency, with a score range of 0–100. Scores ≤ 19 indicate low activity (insufficient physical activity), 20–42 indicate moderate activity, and ≥ 43 indicate high activity levels.

(4) Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II).

The second edition of the Beck Depression Inventory, developed by psychologist Aaron Beck and colleagues, was adapted into Chinese by Wang Zhen and others. The Chinese version of the BDI-II has an internal consistency reliability of 0.94 and good test-retest reliability24. This scale assesses the severity of various depressive symptoms and consists of 21 items, each scored from 0 to 3. The total score is the sum of the 21 items, where 0–13 indicates no depression, 14–19 indicates mild depression, 20–28 indicates moderate depression, and 29–63 indicates severe depression.

(5) EEG Signal Acquisition and Preprocessing.

EEG signals were acquired using an electroencephalogram (EEG) device (NCERP-190012) manufactured by Shanghai Nuo Cheng Electric Co., Ltd., which included a preamplifier. The setup utilized 16 unipolar channels, with a sampling frequency of 500 Hz, a notch filter set at 50 Hz, a low-pass filter at 30 Hz, and a high-pass filter at 0.3 Hz. The device classifies EEG signals into various frequency bands: delta band [1–4 Hz), theta band [4–8 Hz), alpha1 band [8–10.5 Hz), alpha2 band [10.5–13 Hz), beta1 band (13–20 Hz), and beta2 band (20–30 Hz), with critical values categorized as high bands, and the divisions of the bands are defined using left-closed, right-open intervals. In light of prior experimental findings, this study chose the delta frequency band as the primary area of interest20,25. The testing was conducted in a quiet, ventilated dark room, prohibiting the presence of electronic devices. Once participants entered the dark room, they were asked to sit quietly to familiarize themselves with the environment. After achieving emotional stability, they wore the EEG cap and connected it to the EEG device. Test personnel adjusted the impedance to below 5 kΩ, adhering to the electrode placement method of the 10/20 system as recommended by the International Federation of Clinical Neurophysiology. Electrodes were positioned at Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, F7, F8, C3, C4, P3, P4, O1, O2, T3, T4, T5, and T6, with a ground electrode set to GND and reference electrodes placed at the bilateral earlobe positions (A1, A2). Participants were asked to relax their bodies, position their hands naturally at their sides, close their eyes, stay alert, maintain steady breathing, and refrain from swallowing or clenching their teeth. After achieving stability, EEG signals were recorded for 5 min. EEG signals were gathered solely from the group of university students displaying depressive symptoms. (See Fig. 2b)

Data preprocessing was conducted using EEGLAB in MATLAB 2023a. EEGLAB is an open-source toolbox operating within MATLAB, which includes various MATLAB functions. The specific preprocessing steps included: (1) Importing continuous EEG data; (2) Electrode localization; (3) Baseline correction; (4) Removal of unnecessary electrodes; (5) Reducing the sampling rate to 256 Hz; (6) Setting high-pass filtering at 0.1 Hz and low-pass filtering at 30 Hz; (7) Implementing average reference electrodes; (8) Checking the waveform for defective electrodes and interpolating as necessary; (9) Segmenting the continuous EEG data into 2-second epochs; (10) Applying Independent Component Analysis (ICA) to adjust for eye movements, blinking, electromyography (EMG), electrocardiography (ECG), or any sections affected by non-physiological artifacts; (11) Identifying features of principal components and eliminating interfering components; (12) Discarding electrode values with amplitudes exceeding ± 100 µV; (13) Manually removing segments with significant drift; (14) Saving the processed continuous EEG data.

Statistical analysis

Data computations and analyses were carried out using SPSS 29.0, and structural equation modeling for path analysis was performed using Amos 24.0. The EEG power values for each brain region were determined by summing P left and P right. Continuous data conforming to normal or approximately normal distribution were described as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD), with intergroup comparisons analyzed using independent samples t-tests. For data exhibiting significant skewness, medians (along with interquartile ranges) were reported, and intergroup comparisons utilized the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as n (%), with intergroup comparisons analyzed using the χ² test. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to investigate the relationships among physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and specific EEG indicators, with effect sizes indicated by the coefficient of determination (R²). To mitigate Type I error inflation, we employed the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction. The adjusted threshold for specific EEG indicators associated with physical exercise was set at 0.00625, the same threshold applied to the visuospatial working memory task, while the threshold for EEG indicators related to depressive symptoms was set at 0.04375. The evaluation metrics for the structural equation model included RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation), RMR (Root Mean Square Residual), NFI (Normed Fit Index), GFI (Goodness of Fit Index), and CFI (Comparative Fit Index)33. Parameter estimation for path analysis was conducted using a non-parametric percentile bootstrap method (which does not impose strict distributional assumptions on the variables), with a total of 5000 resampling iterations. A 95% confidence interval (95% CI) that excludes zero was used to assess the statistical significance of mediating effects. Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001, with an alpha level set at 0.05, and all parameter statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed tests.

The mediating role of common EEG specific indicators among physical Exercise, visuospatial working Memory, and depressive symptoms in university students

To explore the potential mediating effects of common EEG specific indicators among physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depressive symptoms in university students, physical exercise was designated as the independent variable. The common EEG specific indicators encompassing physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depressive symptoms served as the mediating variables, while the BDI-II score functioned as the dependent variable. This led to the construction of the hypothetical model for this research, as depicted in Fig. 4.

The structural equation model results showed the following: CMIN/DF = 1.33, RMSEA = 0.07, GFI = 0.96, IFI = 0.99, CFI = 0.99, and TLI = 0.99, indicating a good fit for the model. The variable δ (C3 + C4) mediated 26.7% of the total effect [B = −0.031, 95% CI (−0.069, −0.006)]. (See Table 6)

These findings indicate that variations in the activity of C3 + C4 may be closely linked to an individual’s emotional and cognitive functions. Physical exercise not only directly contributes to the improvement of depressive symptoms but also indirectly affects an individual’s emotional state and cognitive abilities by modulating the electrical activity in specific brain regions.

Discussion

This research discovered a common EEG specific indicator—δ (C3 + C4)—shared among physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depressive symptoms in university students. Furthermore, this indicator mediated 26.72% of the total effect between physical exercise and depressive symptoms. Prior research indicates that the right parietal lobe is essential for processing spatial information and attention26–28, while the frontal lobe mainly oversees the maintenance and manipulation of information. Notably, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is particularly active during working memory tasks29–31. The brain regions associated with the EEG specific indicator (C3 + C4) imply that physical exercise not only has a direct impact on the improvement of depressive symptoms but also indirectly influences an individual’s depressive symptoms and visuospatial working memory by modulating specific brain regions. Additionally, the frequency bands related to the EEG specific indicators indicate that physical exercise may improve sleep quality and thereby alleviate depressive symptoms and enhance visuospatial working memory by modulating the electrical activity in specific brain regions such as C3 + C4, particularly within the delta band32. Collectively, these EEG specific indicators offer potential pathways through which physical exercise can enhance both depressive symptoms and visuospatial working memory in university students. In summary, improving depressive symptoms can occur through two pathways: a direct pathway (exercise → depressive symptoms) and an indirect pathway (exercise → specific brain regions and frequency bands → depressive symptoms).

This study found significant differences in physical exercise, depressive symptoms, and visuospatial working memory between university students with depressive symptoms and their healthy counterparts. Furthermore, correlations were identified among physical exercise, depressive symptoms, and visuospatial working memory in students with depressive symptoms, a finding supported by previous studies18,33–35. This may be attributed to regular physical exercise, which promotes the release of endogenous neurotransmitters (such as endorphins and serotonin), enhances cerebral blood circulation, and improves neural plasticity, ultimately boosting cognitive function and alleviating depressive symptoms34,36,37. Furthermore, this study revealed no significant difference in the response times of visuospatial working memory between university students with depressive symptoms and their healthy peers, a finding that contradicts previous research38. This could indicate that students experiencing depressive symptoms are still capable of maintaining a level of alertness and responsiveness while performing tasks. This insight offers guidance for clinical interventions, recommending that strategies designed for university students with depressive symptoms should emphasize improving the accuracy of their visuospatial working memory, rather than focusing exclusively on response speed.

In recent years, numerous researchers have employed EEG to seek specific indicators39–41, which has offered valuable guidance for enhancing visuospatial working memory. However, a consensus on the different frequency bands across various brain regions remains elusive. Future studies should aim to delve deeper into specific electrophysiological indicators and enhance the examination of how different exercise protocols impact these indicators. Identifying specific electrophysiological indicators and constructing targeted exercise protocols will necessitate large sample sizes, prolonged study durations, and collaborative research endeavors.

Conclusion

A significant correlation exists among physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depressive symptoms in university students experiencing depressive symptoms. The δ (C3 + C4) wave may serve as a common underlying pathway connecting these three factors. Consequently, universities should contemplate the integration of structured physical activity into their mental health initiatives.

Limitations and future perspectives

(1) This study utilized a case-control design, which imposes limitations on causal inference. Future research should carry out longitudinal studies to explore the effects of physical exercise on enhancing visuospatial working memory in university students with depressive symptoms and to confirm the proposed pathway involving δ (C3 + C4).

(2) The assessment of physical exercise in this study relied on self-reported scales, which may introduce a degree of subjectivity. Future studies should consider employing objective measurement tools, such as accelerometers and Polar heart rate monitors.

(3) Future studies should involve larger sample sizes to further clarify the relationship among physical exercise, visuospatial working memory, and depressive symptoms. Additionally, further research should assess the efficacy of enhancing visuospatial working memory through EEG specific indicators and explore the potential of these indicators as outcome measures for designing targeted exercise prescriptions. Furthermore, in the future, further research needs to be conducted on the midline of the brain (Fz, Cz, Pz, Oz).

(4) The participants in this study were predominantly university students in China. Future research should broaden its scope to include diverse age groups, cultures, and populations across different regions. The participants in this study were assessed for depressive symptoms using the Beck Depression Inventory, but no clinical diagnosis was conducted. Future research could validate the conclusions of this study through clinical diagnosis of depression.
