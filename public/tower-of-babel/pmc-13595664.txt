Balloon-assisted radial release for retrieval of a surgically suture-fixed inferior vena cava filter: a case report
Authors: Peng Gao, HuanLiang Liu, Jindi Wei, Xuanfeng Zhu, Xiquan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe retrieval of an inferior vena cava (IVC) filter is essential for preventing complications related to the filter. Surgically induced suture fixation is an extremely rare cause of filter entrapment and presents unique technical challenges. This case describes a balloon-assisted radial release within the filter frame followed by snare retrieval to successfully address this condition.Case presentationWe report a rare case of successful retrieval of a surgically suture-fixed inferior vena cava filter via femoral venous access using a balloon-assisted radial release technique combined with snare retrieval, which has not been previously reported. An endovascular strategy of “release first, then retrieval” was adopted. Sequential balloon dilations were performed to achieve controlled radial expansion and mechanical release at the fixation site. Following controlled strut fracture and resolution of the external constraint, the filter was completely retrieved using a snare technique under large-sheath protection.ConclusionThe balloon-assisted radial release technique may provide a minimally invasive solution for the retrieval of inferior vena cava filters fixed by surgical sutures.

Background

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is a common and potentially fatal disease [1, 2]. Inferior vena cava (IVC) filters are clinically important tools for preventing pulmonary embolism (PE) in patients with contraindications to anticoagulation or at high risk of venous thromboembolism [3–5]. With the widespread application of retrievable filters, there is consensus that filters should be retrieved in a timely manner to reduce the risk of long-term complications associated with the device [6–8].

Within the recommended retrieval window, most retrievable filters can be successfully retrieved using standard snare techniques [5]. However, when the filter becomes embedded, penetrates the caval wall, or develops significant endothelial overgrowth, retrieval may become extremely difficult, thus requiring the use of more advanced techniques [9]. These include wire-loop (loop-snare) techniques, rigid endobronchial forceps, balloon-assisted retrieval, and excimer laser sheath–assisted retrieval [10]. Notably, balloon-assisted techniques have been reported to achieve high success rates in terms of their application and possess good safety features [11].

This article reports a rare case of mechanical fixation of an inferior vena cava filter caused by accidental entanglement of filter struts with surgical sutures placed in an adjacent operative field. A mechanism-oriented strategy of balloon-assisted radial release within the filter frame followed by snare retrieval was employed. The entrapped filter was successfully removed without complications.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old woman was diagnosed with a right renal vascular lesion accompanied by thrombosis. Imaging revealed a vascular mass located at the upper pole of the right kidney with associated thrombosis formation, as well as bilateral renal cysts (Fig. 1A). Given the high risk of pulmonary embolism, a spindle-shaped retrievable inferior vena cava filter (Weigao, China) was implanted via the right femoral vein and positioned above the level of the right renal vein.

On the same day, the patient underwent laparoscopic-assisted right nephrectomy. Due to the presence of severe and tight adhesions around the right renal vein and the difficulty in performing vascular separation, the procedure was converted to open surgery. During the operation, a right renal arteriovenous fistula was discovered. After the lesion was removed, since the remaining kidney tissue could not be saved, a kidney removal surgery was performed. The anastomosis site of the inferior vena cava was reinforced with 4-0 vascular sutures to prevent postoperative bleeding. Completion venography showed no extravasation of contrast agent, confirming the integrity of the blood vessels.

Postoperative imaging examinations revealed an abnormal structure of the filter and signs of mechanical compression. Axial abdominal computed tomography (CT) showed the filter struts were unevenly distributed, with narrower spacing between adjacent struts and close apposition to the vena caval wall (Fig. 1B). Coronal computed tomography venography (CTV) reconstruction confirmed the filter was in the inferior vena cava, showing that struts were closely opposed to the caval wall with subtle tilting (Fig. 1C). Three-dimensional CT reconstruction further revealed asymmetric strut orientation and reduced spacing of struts, reinforcing the presence of abnormal filter configuration (Fig. 1D). Based on these imaging findings and surgical records, it was suspected that filter struts had accidentally been sutured to the surgical sutures. A prior attempt at retrieval using a conventional snare technique at another hospital was unsuccessful. Subsequently, the patient was transferred to our center.

After a comprehensive review of the imaging and surgical records, a strategy of “balloon-assisted radial release followed by snare retrieval” was formulated. The procedure was performed under local anesthesia. The Seldinger technique was used to obtain right common femoral venous access. All subsequent balloon-assisted radial expansion and filter retrieval procedures were performed through this access. No transradial arterial or venous access was used during the procedure. An 8 F catheter sheath was first introduced to pre-expand the puncture tract, followed by the insertion of an 18 F catheter sheath. Venography confirmed that the inferior vena cava was patent without stenosis or contrast extravasation (Fig. 2A).

A 5 F single-curve catheter and a 1.5-meter rigid hydrophilic guidewire were advanced through the large catheter towards the suspected fixation site. During the advancement process, resistance was encountered along the right side of the filter near the tip, which supported the suspicion of suture fixation. After careful manipulation, the catheter was successfully positioned above the filter (Fig. 2B). The guidewire was then exchanged for a 2.6-meter stiff guidewire. Sequential balloon dilations (4 mm, 8 mm, 12 mm, and 16 mm) were performed within the filter frame to achieve controlled radial expansion at the fixation site. Repeated venography during balloon inflation demonstrated no evidence of vascular injury (Fig. 2C). During inflation of the 16 mm balloon, fluoroscopy revealed fracture of one upper filter strut with opening of the filter configuration, indicating release of the mechanical constraint (Fig. 2D).

Following confirmation of mechanical release, a trimmed and flattened sheath core and a memory snare were introduced through the 18 F catheter sheath. The filter’s lower end retrieval hook was successfully captured. The sheath core was gradually pushed upwards, allowing most of the filter to be inserted into the sheath. The snare was tightened to firmly fix the filter and the sheath together. The large sheath was slowly pushed upwards by the left hand, successfully completely inserting the filter into the sheath and pulling it out of the body (Fig. 2E). No free metallic filter strut fragments were observed. Post-retrieval venography confirmed a patent inferior vena cava without stenosis or contrast extravasation (Fig. 2F).

Extracorporeal examination showed an intact filter structure with fracture of one upper filter strut (Fig. 3A, red arrow). A blue surgical suture knot was found wrapped around the strut (Fig. 3A, white arrow), confirming the suspected inadvertent suture fixation of the filter. Postoperative imaging, including abdominal CT and venography, demonstrated a patent inferior vena cava without residual filter fragments or complications (Fig. 3B and C). Three-dimensional CT reconstruction at the same vertebral level as the preoperative images confirmed complete filter removal (Fig. 3D).

Discussion

The rarity of this case lies in the fact that the filter entrapment was not caused by its intrinsic device characteristics or prolonged indwelling time, but rather by iatrogenic fixation from surgical sutures placed during adjacent vascular surgery. This mechanical fixation renders conventional longitudinal traction with a standard snare technique highly prone to failure and significantly increases the risk of vascular injury or filter strut fracture. This case underscores the importance of mechanism-oriented planning in the management of complex inferior vena cava filter retrieval. Careful preprocedural interpretation of CT and CTV images, particularly findings such as asymmetric strut distribution and focal abnormal wall apposition, can suggest inadvertent suture entrapment of the filter [12].

In the present case, the occurrence of filter entrapment was closely related to the complex operative conditions. The patient underwent laparoscopic-assisted right nephrectomy on the same day as filter placement. However, due to severe dense adhesions surrounding the right renal vein and difficulty in vascular dissection, the procedure was converted to an open approach. During surgery, a right renal arteriovenous fistula was identified. Renal arteriovenous fistula (RAVF) is a rare vascular anomaly characterized by abnormal high-flow communication between the renal artery and vein, which can lead to significant hemodynamic disturbances and an increased risk of thrombosis, providing a rationale for inferior vena cava filter placement [13, 14]. In complex renal vascular surgeries, distortion of the hilar anatomy and limited surgical exposure often make rapid hemostasis essential. Reinforcement suturing of the inferior vena cava anastomotic site is therefore a necessary surgical measure to prevent postoperative hemorrhage. Under such circumstances, surgical sutures may inadvertently involve filter struts. In this case, a filter strut was unintentionally incorporated into the reinforcing suture, resulting in unexpected mechanical fixation of the filter. This event should be considered a rare vascular surgery complication rather than a consequence of subjective operator error.

To improve the success rate of inferior vena cava filter retrieval, various advanced retrieval techniques have been developed. Advanced retrieval techniques are defined as any method other than routine venous access with a snare for the filter hook or the use of a dedicated retrieval device. The three most employed advanced techniques are forceps-assisted, modified loop-snare, and laser sheath approaches [15–17]. Travis Merritt et al. compared the retrieval success rates and adverse event incidences between standard and advanced IVC filter retrieval techniques [18]. The results demonstrated that advanced techniques achieved a markedly higher success rate of 90%, compared to 76% for standard methods.

Balloon-assisted retrieval has been described as an effective technique for managing complex IVC filter cases, particularly those with significant tilt or embedded struts. In an early series by Vink et al., severely tilted OptEase filters with apices embedded into the caval wall were successfully removed using a balloon-assisted retrieval approach, demonstrating its feasibility with commonly available interventional tools and without procedure-related complications [19]. Similarly, Burke et al. further described a balloon-assisted retrieval technique for Greenfield filters with strut perforation of the cava wall, achieving successful retrieval in all six cases without complications [20]. Collectively, these studies support balloon-assisted retrieval as a useful adjunct in complex filter extraction, particularly when tilt and wall embedding impede standard snare techniques.

For balloon-assisted retrieval of IVC filters, the primary complication is vascular rupture. If the operator overinflates or keeps inflating the balloon for a long time on the blood vessel, it may cause the vessel to rupture [9]. The success of balloon-assisted retrieval is highly dependent on filter position and the degree of embedment. For cases with severe intimal hyperplasia or unfavorable filter angles, balloon dilation may not be sufficient to achieve complete retrieval [15]. Furthermore, there are currently no evidence-based guidelines to determine the size of the balloon, the inflation pressure, or the placement angle required to maximize retrieval efficacy. Therefore, these parameters are usually determined by the operator’s experience and the availability of the equipment.

Our balloon-assisted radial expansion technique differs fundamentally from conventional balloon-assisted retrieval methods used for inferior vena cava filter removal. Traditional balloon-assisted techniques are primarily designed to correct filter tilt or to separate the retrieval hook from the caval wall to facilitate snare capture. In contrast, the present strategy was specifically developed to address suture-induced mechanical fixation of filter struts. The key principle of this technique is controlled radial expansion within the filter frame rather than forceful longitudinal traction. By generating outward radial force, the balloon progressively loosens the constricting effect of the surgical suture around the filter struts, thereby relieving the mechanical constraint that prevents filter mobilization. This mechanism allows gradual restoration of filter mobility before retrieval, reducing the need for excessive traction forces that may increase the risk of caval injury. Another important aspect of this approach is the stepwise dilation strategy combined with continuous angiographic monitoring. Gradual balloon expansion allows progressive release of the fixation while maintaining control over the degree of radial force applied to the filter and the caval wall. Once sufficient mobility of the filter is restored, standard snare-based retrieval can be performed under large-sheath protection, minimizing the risk of fragment embolization. Therefore, this mechanism-oriented strategy of radial expansion followed by conventional retrieval represents a minimally invasive alternative for managing rare cases of mechanically fixed filters caused by surgical sutures.

The choice of venous access represents an important consideration in complex IVC filter retrieval. In the present case, “radial release” should be clearly distinguished from a venous access route, as it refers to balloon-assisted radial expansion within the filter frame to relieve the mechanical constraint caused by surgical suture fixation. Conventional IVC filter retrieval is commonly performed through internal jugular or femoral venous access using a snare-and-sheath technique [21]. Femoral venous access provides a relatively direct pathway to the inferior vena cava, stable mechanical support, and compatibility with large-bore sheaths, making it suitable for complex retrieval procedures requiring controlled manipulation and complete filter capture. In contrast, upper-extremity venous approaches may be considered in selected patients, with potential advantages including easier access-site management and earlier mobilization; however, they may be limited by longer access distance, smaller vessel caliber, reduced mechanical support, and restricted compatibility with large-bore devices [22]. In this case, femoral venous access was selected because the surgically suture-fixed filter required balloon-assisted radial expansion followed by retrieval under 18 F sheath protection. The large-bore sheath was used to facilitate controlled capture of the mobilized and potentially deformed filter after release, rather than as a routine requirement for standard filter retrieval. In complex retrieval procedures involving embedded or long-dwelling filters, advanced techniques and additional device support may be required [23, 24]. Comparative studies have suggested that access site may influence filter angulation and access-related outcomes, although available evidence has not demonstrated clear superiority of one access route in terms of retrieval success [25, 26]. Therefore, access selection should consider patient-related factors, including age and comorbidities, vascular anatomy and vessel condition, filter dwell time, filter orientation, degree of tilt, caval penetration, thrombus burden, fixation mechanism, and anticipated procedural complexity. At present, evidence is insufficient to support one access route as universally superior; femoral venous, radial venous, and alternative venous approaches should be considered complementary options according to clinical circumstances.

The primary potential risk of this balloon-assisted radial expansion technique is vascular injury caused by excessive radial force or uncontrolled fracture of filter struts during the dilation process. Previous studies have reported that intense mechanical operations during the complex removal of inferior vena cava filters may lead to complications such as injury to the vena cava wall, bleeding, or embolization of fractured filter fragments, and fracture of filter struts is not uncommon in challenging retrieval procedures [27].

Therefore, the size of the balloon was carefully selected, and sequential dilation was performed under continuous fluoroscopic guidance, while repeatedly evaluating the vascular wall with angiography. In this case, a strut fracture did occur during the balloon dilation process. However, considering the uncertain degree of suture fixation with the vena cava wall, this possibility had been anticipated in advance. Under these preventive measures, the observed strut fracture should be regarded as a controllable operational event rather than a sign of surgical failure.

This case also highlights the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration in dealing with rare complications involving inferior vena cava filters. In some cases, surgical sutures may accidentally get stuck on the struts of the filter. In such situations, careful preoperative evaluation of the spatial relationship between the filter and the surgical area is essential. The location of the filter and the distribution of its struts should be clearly identified to minimize the risk of entanglement with sutures or surgical instruments during the operation. Endovascular techniques may provide a minimally invasive solution. However, open surgery remains a reliable definitive salvage option when endovascular retrieval fails. If balloon-assisted expansion fails to release the fixation, or if uncontrolled structural damage to the filter occurs during the procedure, endovascular manipulation should be promptly discontinued. Early conversion to open surgery is recommended to prevent severe vascular injury and ensure safe removal of the filter [28].

Conclusion

This study presents a rare case of inferior vena cava filter incarceration caused by inadvertent suture fixation of the filter struts, a mechanism that has not been previously described. Based on precise preoperative imaging analysis of the entrapment mechanism, a modified technique combining balloon-assisted radial expansion with conventional retrieval was successfully applied. This approach enabled safe and effective removal of the filter and demonstrated the feasibility of this minimally invasive strategy. This technique broadens the range of advanced filter retrieval strategies and may provide a valuable approach for managing similar complex cases in clinical practice.
