Emergency bronchial artery embolization using n-2-butyl-cyanoacrylate: a safe and effective solution for massive hemoptysis
Authors: Mehmet Beyazal, Merve Solak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rationale and objectivesMassive hemoptysis is a serious respiratory emergency. In most cases, the bleeding source is the bronchial circulation, and bronchial artery embolization (BAE) is considered the primary approach for treating massive hemoptysis. However, there is no standardized protocol regarding embolic agents. The aim of this study is to evaluate the efficacy and safety of BAE performed with n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA) in the treatment of massive hemoptysis.Materials and methodsThis single-center, retrospective study included patients who underwent BAE with NBCA for massive hemoptysis between January 2021 and December 2024. Demographic data and etiological factors were documented. BAE procedural details were thoroughly analyzed and recorded. Postprocedural complications, technical success, and clinical success rates (within 24 h and 1 month) were evaluated. Recurrence rates beyond 1 month were assessed.ResultsThe study included 41 patients (33 male, 8 female). The most frequently identified etiologies were tuberculosis (28.5%), bronchiectasis (20%). The mean 24-hour hemoptysis volume was 535 ± 448.2 mL (range: 240–1200 mL). The most common DSA findings were bronchial artery hypertrophy/tortuosity (78.2%), hypervascularization (67.4%). BAE procedures most frequently targeted the right bronchial artery (57.14%). Embolization involved a single vessel in 75% of cases, two vessels in 17.5%, and three vessels in 7.5%. The mean NBCA/Lipiodol volume per vessel was 1.625 ± 0.66 mL (range: 0.5–2 mL). Technical success was achieved in 97.14% of cases. Clinical success rates were 100% within 24 h and 97.5% at 1-month follow-up. Over a median follow-up period of 13 months, no recurrence requiring repeat embolization was observed.ConclusionOur study demonstrates that BAE using NBCA/Lipiodol is an effective and safe method for treating massive hemoptysis, with high technical and clinical success rates. When carefully administered by experienced specialists, NBCA/Lipiodol may be considered as a potential primary embolic agent for BAE.

Introduction

Massive hemoptysis, which is defined as the expectoration of blood from the respiratory tract, is one of the most feared emergencies in pulmonary medicine. Although it is associated with high mortality rates, prompt diagnosis and treatment can reduce the mortality rate to below 30% [1–3]. Therefore, accurately identifying the underlying pathology and selecting appropriate therapeutic interventions is critical in managing massive hemoptysis [4].

Bronchial artery embolisation (BAE) has emerged as a minimally invasive therapeutic alternative that serves as a bridge to definitive medical or emergency surgical interventions, which are tailored to the cause of the bleeding [5–7]. Over time, BAE has become the primary treatment for hemorrhage control, including cases deemed inoperable. Recent large-scale studies demonstrate that this minimally invasive procedure is feasible even in high-risk patients [6, 8–12]. Reported immediate success rates of BAE range from 70% to 99%, solidifying its global acceptance as a safe and effective intervention [6, 8, 9].

As the importance of BAE has increased, procedural techniques have also advanced. The evolution of UAE can be traced back to 1973, when it was first introduced. Since then, it has progressed from the use of agents such as gelatin sponges and hypertonic saline to more effective materials such as polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) [9, 13, 14]. Liquid embolic agents such as N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA) are utilised less frequently due to their technical complexity and increased risk of complications [9, 13]. However, the seminal study by Woo et al. found no significant difference between PVA and NBCA embolisation in terms of technical success, clinical outcomes or complication rates, reviving interest in liquid embolics [15]. However, several studies in the literature have raised concerns about the use of adhesive embolisation in cases of massive haemoptysis. These relate to potentially serious complications, such as off-target embolisation, premature polymerisation, and adhesion of the microcatheter tip to the adhesive [13–15]. As with all liquid embolic agents, optimum mixture concentration and injection rate are required for the use of NBCA, as well as expertise in this field [2, 15].

The aim of this study is to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy, procedural safety, and clinical outcomes of bronchial artery embolization (BAE) using n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA) in patients presenting with massive hemoptysis.

Materials and methods

Ethic: This single-center retrospective study was conducted in the interventional radiology clinic. Consent was obtained or waived from all participants in this study. It was approved by the local Ethical Review Committee (Decision number: 2024/316).

Patient selection and methods

A retrospective analysis was conducted of consecutive patients who underwent endovascular treatment for massive hemoptysis in the Interventional Radiology Clinic of our institution between January 2021 and December 2024. Patients with unavailable radiological images or follow-up data were excluded. Based on these criteria, 41 patients (33 male, 8 female) who underwent bronchial BAE using NBCA were included in the study.

Demographic data, comorbidities, hemoptysis volume, and flexible bronchoscopy findings (when available) were recorded. Thoracic computed tomography (CT) and CT angiography (CTA) images were reviewed, and findings were documented. The etiology and lateralization of hemoptysis were determined by evaluating CT/CTA and bronchoscopy findings together. Additionally, potential vascular sources of bleeding were identified.

Thoracic CTA was performed using a 16-slice CT scanner (Toshiba Alexion Advance Edition 16, Japan) with a slice thickness of 0.625–1 mm.

Hemoptysis volume

Massive hemoptysis was defined as > 300 mL within 24 h or, regardless of volume, any hemoptysis compromising airway patency/ventilation or causing hemodynamic instability [14, 16].

Bronchial artery embolization procedure

BAE was performed within the first 24 h of admission in patients presenting with massive hemoptysis. During the endovascular procedure, the vascular structures suspected to be the source of bleeding, and their abnormal features were evaluated. Our embolization strategy aims to target all bronchial and non-bronchial systemic arteries that are potentially associated with ipsilateral parenchymal lesions. All embolization procedures were performed by an interventional radiologist with 10 years of experience in an angiography suite under local anesthesia or conscious sedation. The initial step involved obtaining standard vascular access through the common femoral artery using a 5-French introducer under ultrasound guidance.

The bronchial arteries suspected as the source of massive hemoptysis on CTA were selectively catheterized, and diagnostic angiographic images were obtained. Based on CT and CTA findings, when necessary, the subclavian arteries, costocervical trunk, thyrocervical trunk, and intercostal arteries were also selectively catheterized for diagnostic angiography. Diagnostic angiography was performed using 5-French catheters (Simmons 1, Cobra, Vertebral catheter; Boston Scientific, Natick, Massachusetts). These images were carefully evaluated for vascular pathologies and the presence of spinal arteries. Angiographic findings were assessed for contrast extravasation, bronchial artery hypertrophy and tortuosity, hypervascularity, bronchial artery aneurysms, and shunting to pulmonary arteries or veins.

Target arteries for embolization were superselectively catheterized using a coaxial microcatheter (130 cm 2.7 F Progreat; Terumo Corporation, Shibuya, Tokyo, Japan) and microguidewire. The microcatheter was advanced to an optimal distal position, aiming to target diseased vasculature while avoiding potential spinal feeders and maintaining a safe distance to prevent NBCA reflux.

For bronchial artery embolization, n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA, e.g., TruFill® n-BCA Liquid Embolic System, Johnson & Johnson/DePuy, Raynham, MA) was used. NBCA kits do not contain radiopaque material. Lipiodol (Lipiodol Ultra Fluide, Guerbet, France) was used as the standard opacifying agent. NBCA was prepared by mixing with Lipiodol. The NBCA/Lipiodol ratio (ranging from 1:1 to 1:5) was adjusted based on vessel diameter and diseased vascularity, with 1:4 (0.5 mL NBCA/2 mL Lipiodol) being the most frequently used ratio. Before injection, the microcatheter position was reconfirmed, and contrast injection verified the absence of vasospasm or dissection. NBCA was supplied in sterile 0.5 mL containers and aspirated into a 5 mL syringe, then mixed with Lipiodol to the desired ratio. Before embolization, the microcatheter was flushed with 5% dextrose. Subsequently, embolization is performed using a 2.5 mL syringe. Initially, 0.2 mL of mixture was injected for small vessels and 0.4 mL for large vessels, followed by flushing with 5% dextrose to advance the mixture into the vascular bed. Injections were terminated once the distal vascular bed was completely opacified, maintaining approximately a 2-cm safety margin from the microcatheter tip. Throughout the procedure, real-time fluoroscopy was used to prevent reflux or catheter adherence. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (minimum–maximum). Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Group comparisons for categorical variables were performed using Fisher’s exact test due to small subgroup sizes. Continuous variables were compared using independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate, based on the results of the normality test.

Recurrence-free survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier estimates. 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated for recurrence and survival outcomes. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study cohort comprised 41 patients with a mean age of 61.05 ± 17.03 years (range: 44–80 years). The population was predominantly male (80.5%, n = 33) versus female (19.5%, n = 8). Comorbid conditions were present in the majority of patients (87.8%), with hypertension (68.6%) and diabetes mellitus (53.7%) being the most prevalent.

Massive hemoptysis represented the initial presentation in 74.4% of cases, while 25.6% had prior episodes. Etiological evaluation revealed tuberculosis (28.5%), bronchiectasis (20%), and lung cancer (11.4%) as the predominant underlying pathologies. One female patient was diagnosed with alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency. Notably, no definitive etiology could be established in a substantial proportion of cases (22.8%).

The mean 24-hour hemoptysis volume was 535 ± 448.2 mL (range: 240–1200 mL). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) revealed the following characteristic findings: bronchial artery hypertrophy and tortuosity (78.2%), hypervascularity (67.4%), and arterio-arterial shunting (34.8%). Notably, no cases demonstrated contrast extravasation.

Technical success was defined as successful catheterization and complete embolization of the target vessel [2]. The procedure could not be performed in only one case (2.4%) due to failure to achieve superselective catheterization, yielding a technical success rate of 97.5%.

The NBCA/Lipiodol mixture was used in varying ratios for embolization: a 1:4 ratio in 31 cases (75.6%), 1:3 ratio in 3 cases (7.3%), 1:2 ratio in 4 cases (9.8%), 1:1 ratio in 2 cases (4.9%), and 1:5 ratio in 1 case (2.4%). The volume of NBCA/Lipiodol mixture administered per embolized vessel ranged from 0.5 to 2 mL, with a mean volume of 1.625 ± 0.66 mL used.

Among the 41 patients who underwent BAE, spinal arteries were identified in two cases. In both instances, successful embolization was achieved while preserving the spinal arteries (Fig. 2).

No major complications associated with significant morbidity or mortality were observed following endovascular treatment, including spinal cord infarction, bronchial infarction, bronchial artery dissection, oesophagobronchial fistula, myocardial infarction, ischemic colitis, transient cortical blindness, or stroke.

During and after the procedure, chest or back pain was reported however, analgesic administration was not required. The second most frequently reported symptom was dry cough. In the cohort of 41 patients, chest and back pain was reported in 28 individuals, corresponding to an overall prevalence of 67.1%. Dry cough was documented in 10 patients (23.3% overall).

The median follow-up duration was 13 months (range: 60 days to 48 months). During follow-up, recurrent hemoptysis developed in one patient (2.5%) on day 14, requiring repeat BAE. While the initial procedure had successfully embolized the bronchial arteries, the second intervention identified and embolized a contralateral ectopic bronchial artery supplying the same bleeding source (Fig. 3). No further recurrence was observed in this patient during 2-year follow-up.

Clinical success was defined as complete cessation of hemoptysis or a reduction sufficient to obviate the need for additional intervention within the specified time frame (first 24 h and first 30 days) following bronchial artery embolization [2]. The clinical success rate within the first 24 h was 100%. The clinical success rate within the first 30 days remained high, at 97.5%.

At late follow-up (beyond 1 month), recurrent hemoptysis was observed in 7 cases (17.5%), all of which were minimal and clinically insignificant. Detailed results are given in Table 1.

Discussion

Massive haemoptysis can lead to life-threatening consequences if not promptly diagnosed and managed [17]. Due to the various potential causes, including inflammatory, neoplastic and vascular pathologies, accurately assessing and managing haemoptysis poses a significant challenge for clinicians.

BAE, first described by Remy et al. in 1973, has since become the primary treatment for massive haemoptysis [16]. In cases where conservative measures fail, BAE offers an effective approach to bleeding control [18–20].

In this study, we evaluated the efficacy of BAE using NBCA/Lipiodol to treat massive haemoptysis. NBCA, used as an embolic agent, was mixed with Lipiodol to adjust its radiopacity and polymerisation rate. Safe use of NBCA requires operators to fully understand the behaviour and polymerisation properties of this embolic agent. The injection volume and dilution ratio (typically 1:2 to 1:4 NBCA/Lipiodol) must be carefully optimised to ensure proper vessel penetration and embolisation efficiency [2].

Several crucial technical aspects require meticulous attention to minimize complication risks during NBCA-based BAE. Aggressive guidewire manipulation should be strictly avoided as it may cause bronchial artery spasm or dissection [2, 21]. Optimal microcatheter positioning is paramount for procedural safety, ensuring that the microcatheter is not advanced too far and that there is adequate free flow. NBCA should be injected at a low rate, carefully avoiding reflux. This approach greatly reduces the risk of off-target embolisation while improving treatment efficacy [2]. A thorough angiographic evaluation should include a careful assessment of collateral vessels, bronchopulmonary shunts, and vessels supplying critical anatomical structures, as these factors are essential for preventing complications [22].

Spinal cord ischemia is a potential risk during bronchial artery embolization; special attention should be given to identifying the anterior spinal artery (Adamkiewicz artery), typically originating between T9 and T12 and showing a characteristic ‘hairpin turn’ on angiography [2, 23, 24]. A comprehensive neurological examination before and after the procedure, along with careful intra-procedural monitoring and preservation of the anterior spinal artery, is essential to prevent serious complications such as spinal cord ischemia [23–25]. In our study, the spinal artery was identified in two cases, and successful embolization was performed while preserving these arteries. Nevertheless, the limited sample size does not allow for definitive conclusions to be drawn regarding the absolute safety profile of NBCA in bronchial artery embolization.

BAE for massive haemoptysis requires effective airway management and a multidisciplinary approach. Anaesthesia support is particularly recommended for patients with chronic lung disease or dyspnoea, since the supine position can cause clinical deterioration [5, 22]. Proper upper body positioning is essential for non-intubated patients, along with continuous monitoring of vital parameters (pulse, SpO₂, respiration and blood pressure), and the judicious use of antitussive medications [2, 5, 9].

In this study, the technical and clinical success of BAE with NBCA/Lipiodol in the treatment of massive hemoptysis was also evaluated. Technical success was defined as the successful catheterization and embolization of diseased bronchial or non-bronchial arteries, and in our study, this rate was recorded as 97.5% [2, 13, 26]. Clinical success was defined as the absence of recurrent hemoptysis after BAE [2, 5, 13]. In a study by Shamseldin et al. where BAE was performed using NBCA in 31 patients, technical success was achieved in all cases. In this study, the most commonly used NBCA/Lipiodol ratio was reported to be 1:4. The clinical success rate was reported as 94.3%. In this study, malignant lung diseases constituted the most common underlying condition among the cases [13]. In a study by Baltacioğlu et al., where BAE was performed with a 12.5% NBCA/Lipiodol mixture, immediate hemostasis was achieved in the patients. Twenty-five patients were enrolled in this study. During the 14-month follow-up in this study, recurrent hemoptysis was observed in 16% of cases [27]. In a study by Mazıcan et al., where particles or NBCA/Lipiodol were used for BAE, the technical and clinical success rates were reported as 100% for both groups. In this study, 28 patients treated with particulate embolic agents and 30 patients treated with an NBCA/Lipiodol mixture were included. In addition, in this study, the NBCA/Lipiodol ratio used in the NBCA group ranged from 12.5% to 25%. This study demonstrated that the use of NBCA/Lipiodol is a effective method, comparable to the use of particulate embolic agents [6]. A total of 41 patients were included in our study, representing a relatively large sample size for this clinical setting. In our study, the most frequently used NBCA/Lipiodol mixture ratio was 1:4. The clinical success rate within the first 30 days was 97.5%, indicating a notably high level of effectiveness. In our study, the etiological evaluation of hemoptysis has been found to reveal underlying pathologies such as tuberculosis (28.5%), bronchiectasis (20%), and lung cancer (11.4%). No underlying cause has been identified in 22.8% of the cases. Unlike studies focusing on specific patient subgroups such as bronchiectasis or malignancy, the heterogeneous etiological distribution observed in our study can be considered a positive distinguishing feature in terms of generalizability. This, in turn, strengthens the clinical relevance of our findings.

Recurrence of hemoptysis is generally associated with the progression of the underlying disease and the development of new collateral circulation. In the literature, recurrence rates can reach up to 60% [2, 22, 28, 29]. In a study by Kolu et al. where BAE was performed using NBCA, a recurrence rate of 8.9% was reported [28]. However, it was noted that the vessel responsible for recurrent hemoptysis was not the same one previously embolized with NBCA. In these patients, the cause of recurrent hemoptysis was found to be non-embolized intercostal arteries. In our study, the recurrence rate within the first month was recorded as 2.5%. During follow-up after the first month, no cases requiring re-embolization for massive hemoptysis were observed. This low rate suggests an association with the achievement of superselective and complete embolization using NBCA/Lipiodol.

This study demonstrates that, when applied by experienced operators following standardised protocols, NBCA can be an effective and safe embolic agent in the treatment of massive hemoptysis. Our findings are consistent with the existing literature and suggest that NBCA could be considered a primary embolic agent [2, 15, 22]. However, these results require confirmation through larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up studies.

Limitations

This study has limitations that should be acknowledged. These include the relatively small sample size, which restricted multivariate analysis, and the predominance of specific aetiologies (particularly tuberculosis), which may limit generalisability to other patient populations. The single-centre design and the absence of direct comparisons with other embolic agents (e.g. particles or coils) are also important considerations. Furthermore, the study lacks a sample size sufficient to draw conclusions about safety concerning major complications such as spinal cord ischemia. While larger, multicentre studies with extended follow-up would further validate these findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the efficacy and reliability of BAE with NBCA in the management of massive hemoptysis, a respiratory emergency. The recurrence rate observed at one-month follow-up was 2.5%, which appears to be lower than the 10–20% recurrence rates reported in previous literature. This may be associated with procedural standardisation, encompassing superselective catheterisation and controlled injection techniques performed by experienced operators. However, further validation through larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up studies will strengthen the evidence for NBCA’s use and contribute to establishing new standards in this field.
