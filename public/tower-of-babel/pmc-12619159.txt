RNA-sequencing-based elucidation of the mechanism underlying aFGF mediated regulation of BMSCs via the PI3K-AKT pathway and its implications for rotator cuff injury repair
Authors: Haonan Liu, Zhichuan Jiang, Xin Guo, Xiaowei Liu, Chaoxiang Lu, Daifeng Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRotator cuff injury is a common clinical musculoskeletal issue with poor tendon-bone healing and high re-tear rates, posing therapeutic challenges. Acidic fibroblast growth factor (aFGF) has drawn wide attention for promoting angiogenesis, wound healing, and metabolic regulation. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) enhance rotator cuff tendon-bone healing, making them ideal for tissue engineering repair. However, how aFGF regulates BMSCs and the involved signaling pathways in this repair process remain unclear.MethodsRabbit BMSCs were isolated and identified via flow cytometry for surface markers. Their osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation capacities were evaluated using Alizarin Red S (ARS) and Oil Red O staining, respectively. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was utilized to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between aFGF-treated and control groups. DAVID database was used for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses, while String database combined with Cytoscape software was employed to construct the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. Finally, protein levels, cell proliferation, migration, angiogenic capacity, and osteogenic differentiation along with expression of osteogenic factors and chondrogenic genes were detected by Western blot, CCK-8, wound healing assay, tube formation assay, and alkaline phosphatase (ALP)/ARS staining and qRT-PCR, respectively.ResultsBMSCs were identified by high CD44/CD90 expression, low CD34/CD45 expression, and osteogenic/adipogenic differentiation potential. RNA-seq revealed 370 DEGs (158 upregulated, 212 downregulated). GO and KEGG pathway analyses identified significant terms, with prominent enrichment of the PI3K-AKT pathway. Furthermore, a PPI network illustrated DEG interactions. Mechanistically, aFGF treatment increased PI3K/AKT phosphorylation, enhanced BMSC proliferation, migration, angiogenesis and osteogenic differentiation, and upregulated osteogenic factors and chondrogenic genes, while these effects were attenuated by the PI3K inhibitor LY294002.ConclusionaFGF modulated the differential gene expression in BMSCs by activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, thereby promoting BMSCs’ proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and osteogenic differentiation. This mechanism provides a novel theoretical basis and potential therapeutic targets for rotator cuff injury management.Keywords: Rotator cuff injury, Acidic fibroblast growth factor, Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, RNA sequencing, PI3K-AKT signaling pathway

Introduction

Rotator cuff injury is a prevalent tendinous degenerative disorder within orthopedics and sports medicine, with its incidence rising markedly as age increases [1–3]. Reports indicate a prevalence of 13% among individuals aged 50–59 years, 20%–30% for those 60–80 years, and over 50% in the population aged 80 and above [4, 5]. As a critical structure for maintaining shoulder joint function, the rotator cuff tendon, when injured, not only induces chronic pain and mobility limitations but also poses persistent clinical challenges due to slow tendon-bone interface healing, scar tissue formation, and a high re-tear rate [6–9]. While surgical repair remains the mainstay of treatment, achieving physiological tendon-bone healing is often elusive, particularly in patients with chronic injuries or large-sized tears, where suboptimal postoperative functional recovery is common [10–14]. Thus, developing effective strategies to enhance tendon-bone interface regenerative repair is an urgent clinical imperative.

Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), renowned for their multipotent differentiation potential (osteogenic, chondrogenic, adipogenic), low immunogenicity, and paracrine regulatory capabilities, have emerged as a focal point in tissue repair research [15–17]. Preclinical studies using animal models have demonstrated that transplanting BMSCs into injured sites augments the regenerative potential of bone, cartilage, muscle, ligaments, and tendons in vivo, positioning BMSCs as ideal candidates for tissue-engineering-based repair [18–20]. Specifically, BMSCs can participate in tendon-bone interface regeneration by differentiating into osteoblasts and chondrocytes and secreting cytokines to promote local angiogenesis and tissue remodeling, thereby improving rotator cuff repair outcomes [21, 22]. However, the functional regulation of BMSCs is tightly controlled by multiple microenvironmental factors, and the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated.

As members of the fibroblast growth factor (FGF) family, acidic FGF (aFGF) and basic FGF (bFGF) potently stimulate osteoblast and chondrocyte mitosis and neovascularization [23]. aFGF, a key growth-promoting factor, has been demonstrated to boost cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, playing pivotal roles in bone defect repair and wound healing [24, 25]. Recent evidence suggests aFGF may modulate tendon repair through regulating stem cell functions; however, its specific effects on BMSC functions (such as osteogenic differentiation and migration ability) and downstream molecular mechanisms in the context of rotator cuff injury have not been systematically studied.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), a cornerstone of high-throughput transcriptome analysis, enables unbiased and systematic screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across experimental groups [26]. Integrating functional enrichment analyses allows exploration of potential regulatory pathways, offering a systems-level perspective to decode complex biological processes. Unlike traditional candidate gene approaches, RNA-seq can uncover novel regulatory networks beyond known pathways [27, 28]. To date, RNA-seq-based investigations of aFGF-mediated BMSC regulation are absent, and the functional pathways governing rotator cuff injury repair remain to be elucidated.

To address these gaps, this study uses rabbit BMSCs as a model system. First, RNA-seq is used to identify DEGs in aFGF-treated BMSCs, and bioinformatics analyses are performed to predict key regulatory pathways. Subsequent cell-based functional assays and molecular validation elucidate aFGF’s effects on BMSC functions and the underlying signaling pathways. Ultimately, this work aims to provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of rotator cuff injury repair and lay a foundation for targeted therapeutic strategies. Materials and methods

Rabbit BMSCs isolation and culture

The experimental protocol for this work was examined and sanctioned by the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University, adhering to procedures detailed in previous research [29]. BMSCs were isolated from the femoral bone marrow of 2-week-old New Zealand rabbits. Under aseptic conditions, the femoral medullary cavities were rinsed with low-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The resulting cell suspension was diluted 1:2 with 0.9% physiological saline, then layered onto a Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient solution and centrifuged at 1300 × g for 15 min. Nucleated cells located at the sample-medium interface were collected and resuspended in complete medium (CM), which consisted of low-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockville, MD, USA) and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen). Cells were plated in Petri dishes at a density of 3 × 104 cells/cm2 and maintained in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2.

Identification of rabbit BMSCs

For the characterization of BMSCs, surface marker profiles were analyzed via flow cytometry. Briefly, BMSCs were harvested, centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in PBS at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated antibodies against CD44 (Cat No. ab30405, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and CD90 (positive markers; Cat No. ab226, Abcam), as well as CD34 (Cat No. ab131589, Abcam), and CD45 (negative markers; Cat No. ab27287, Abcam), were added, and cells were incubated at 4°C for 30 min under dark conditions. Following two washes with PBS, the stained cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer.

To evaluate the multi-lineage differentiation potential, assays for osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation were carried out. For osteogenic induction: BMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 104 cells/cm2 and cultured in osteogenic induction medium supplemented with dexamethasone (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), β-glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and ascorbic acid (MCE) for 21 days. The medium was renewed every 3 days. Upon completion of induction, cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and calcium deposits were detected via Alizarin Red S staining (ARS, Beyotime). For adipogenic differentiation: BMSCs were cultured in adipogenic induction medium containing insulin (MCE), isobutylmethylxanthine (MCE), and dexamethasone (MCE) for 14 days, with medium changes every 3 days. After induction, cells were fixed, and intracellular lipid droplets were stained with Oil Red O (Beyotime). Stained samples were observed and imaged under a light microscope.

Cell treatment

Rabbit BMSCs were cultured in complete growth medium until reaching 80% confluency, and then divided into three groups for treatment: the Control group received regular medium without intervention; the aFGF group was treated with 50 ng/mL aFGF, with medium refreshed every 24 h for 72 h (for RNA-seq and subsequent assays); and the aFGF + LY294002 group, where cells were first pretreated with 30 μmol/L PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 h before the addition of 50 ng/mL aFGF [30], with co-treatment medium refreshed daily over 72 h to maintain stable concentrations.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), fragmented, then reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and subjected to end repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification to construct sequencing libraries. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (≥ 20 million paired-end reads per sample). Raw data were trimmed for adapters and low-quality reads, aligned to the rabbit reference genome (OryCun2.0) using STAR, and gene expression was quantified with featureCounts.

Bioinformatic analysis

Differential gene analysis was performed using Sangerbox. RNA-seq data from rabbit BMSCs of the aFGF-treated group and the control group were analyzed, with genes filtered as DEGs based on the criteria of FDR < 0.01 and fold change > 2. For functional annotation, DEGs were uploaded to the DAVID database (target species: Oryctolagus cuniculus) (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/), and Gene Ontology (GO, including biological process, BP, cellular component, CC, and molecular function, MF) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted, with terms and pathways screened by p < 0.05. Visualizations for GO (top 10 terms in each category) and KEGG (top 20 pathways) were generated. For protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction, DEGs were submitted to the STRING database (https://string-db.org/), and genes were filtered by high-confidence interactions (minimum score = 0.700). The network was visualized in Cytoscape, with node font size and color mapped to gene degree.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from treated cells using a protein extraction kit (Beyotime). Then, protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). 5% skim milk was used for blocking. After 2 h of incubation at 37°C, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against target proteins (Anti-PI3Kinase p85 alpha, 1:1000, Cat No. ab191606, Abcam; Phospho-PI3 Kinase p85 (Tyr458)/p55 (Tyr199) Antibody, 1:1000, Cat No. #4228 Cell Signaling, Danvers, Massachusetts, USA; Anti-pan-AKT, 1:1000, Cat No. ab8805, Abcam; Anti-AKT, 1:1000, Cat No. ab38449, Abcam; anti-β-actin, 1:1000, Cat No. ab8226, Abcam). After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L, 1:5000, Cat No. ab6721, Abcam; Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L, 1:5000, Cat No. ab205719, Abcam) for 1 h, followed by visualization of protein bands using BeyoECL Plus Kit (Beyotime). Band intensities were quantified using Image J software.

Cell proliferation assessment

Cell proliferation was evaluated using Enhanced Cell Counting Kit 8 (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Cells were harvested and seeded into 96-well plates with 100 μL cell suspension (approximately 2000 cells) per well. Following intervention, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added, and cells were further incubated for 4 h under the same conditions as cell culture. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Wound healing assay

Rabbit BMSCs in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates. Once the cells had confluently covered the wells, a sterile 200 μL pipette tip was used to vertically scratch the cell monolayer to create wounds (with at least 3 parallel scratches per well). The medium was discarded, and the wells were rinsed 3 times with pre-warmed PBS. Images of the same scratch areas were captured under a light microscope immediately after treatment (0 h) and at 24 h. The scratch widths were measured using ImageJ software.

Tube formation assay

Matrigel matrix (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) was diluted and added to 96-well plates at 50 μL per well, then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow gelation. BMSCs from each treatment group were cultured to collect conditioned medium. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were harvested, resuspended in BMSC-conditioned medium at appropriate density, and 100 μL of the suspension was seeded onto the solidified Matrigel in each well. Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 6 h. Tube-like structures formed by HUVECs were imaged under a light microscope, and parameters including tube number, total length, and branching points were quantified using Image J software to assess the effect of BMSCs on HUVEC tube formation capacity.

Assessment of osteogenic differentiation

Following osteogenic induction, BMSCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for alkaline phosphatase (ALP) evaluation. They were stained using a commercial ALP staining kit (Beyotime) per the manufacturer’s protocol, observed under a light microscope; ALP activity was then quantified by eluting the stained dye with the kit-provided reagent, with absorbance measured at 405 nm via a microplate reader. For ARS assessment of mineralized nodules, quantification was performed by eluting ARS-stained nodules (following prior staining procedures) with cetylpyridinium chloride solution (Sigma-Aldrich), and absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a microplate reader.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells, and cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcription using Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche, Vilvoord, Brussel, Belgium). qPCR reactions were conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA) with specific primers (listed in Table 1) synthesized by RiBoBio (Guangzhou, China). Amplification was performed on a real-time PCR instrument, followed by melting curve analysis to confirm product specificity. Relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. For comparisons among multiple groups with a single independent variable, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. When experimental design involved two independent variables, two-way ANOVA was used to assess main effects of each variable and their interaction, with Bonferroni’s test for post-hoc comparisons. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of BMSCs

To characterize the rabbit BMSCs, flow cytometry (Fig. 2A) showed that the cells were highly positive for mesenchymal stem cell markers CD44 and CD90, while negative for hematopoietic lineage markers CD34 and CD45, confirming their mesenchymal phenotype. For multipotent differentiation assessment (Fig. 2B), after osteogenic induction, mineralized nodules were visualized by ARS staining (left panel, red precipitates), indicative of osteogenic differentiation. Following adipogenic induction, lipid droplets were clearly visualized by Oil Red O staining (right panel, red puncta), demonstrating adipogenic differentiation capacity. Collectively, these results verified that the isolated cells possessed the typical phenotypic and multipotent differentiation properties of BMSCs.

Phenotypic and multipotent differentiation characterization of rabbit BMSCs. A Flow cytometry profiles demonstrating BMSCs express mesenchymal stem cell markers CD44 and CD90, but lack hematopoietic lineage markers CD34 and CD45. B ARS staining of mineralized nodules following osteogenic induction (scale bar = 100 μm). Oil Red O staining of lipid droplets after adipogenic induction (scale bar = 100 μm)

RNA-seq revealed aFGF-induced transcriptomic remodeling and top DEGs in BMSCs

RNA-seq was performed to decipher the transcriptional responses of BMSCs to aFGF. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a distinct separation between control and aFGF-treated samples along the first principal component (PC1, accounting for 84.5% of variance), indicating aFGF had induced profound global transcriptomic remodeling (Fig. 3A). The volcano plot identified hundreds of DEGs, with substantial numbers of genes upregulated (red) and downregulated (green) by aFGF, reflecting widespread transcriptional reprogramming (Fig. 3B). Hierarchical clustering heatmaps showed that all DEGs clustered sharply by experimental group, highlighting consistent transcriptional patterns within each condition (Fig. 3C). Additionally, a focused heatmap further showcased the top 10 most significantly upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 3D).

GO and KEGG pathway analysis of the DEGs

To decipher the functional implications of aFGF-regulated DEGs, GO and KEGG pathway analyses were conducted. GO enrichment revealed: in BP (Fig. 4A), DEGs clustered in cell–matrix adhesion, melanocyte differentiation, axon guidance, cell adhesion, extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, and cell migration; in CC (Fig. 4B), top hits included external side of plasma membrane, centrosome, extracellular region, nucleoplasm, and extracellular; in MF (Fig. 4C), terms included protein binding, identical protein binding, and protein homodimerization activity. KEGG analysis (Fig. 4D) further identified PI3K-AKT as the most significant pathway.

Construction of PPI network

To dissect core regulatory hubs in aFGF-responsive networks, DEGs were submitted to the STRING database. The network was visualized in Cytoscape, with node font size and color mapped to gene degree (number of interacting partners) (Fig. 5). This revealed a modular structure: genes with higher degree formed central hubs, visually emphasized by larger fonts and distinct colors.

PPI networks of the DEGs

aFGF promoted proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs via the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway

To investigate whether the PI3K-AKT pathway mediated aFGF’s effects on BMSCs, functional assays were performed. Western blot (Fig. 6A) showed aFGF significantly increased phosphorylation of PI3K (p-PI3K) and AKT (p-AKT), while LY294002 co-treatment abolished this phosphorylation, confirming pathway inhibition. CCK-8 and wound healing assays revealed that aFGF enhanced BMSC proliferation and migration, with these effects being reversed by LY294002 (Fig. 6B–D). Tube formation assay indicated that aFGF promoted angiogenic-like behavior, which was suppressed by the inhibitor (Fig. 6E). For osteogenic differentiation: ALP staining and ARS staining showed aFGF stimulated ALP activity and mineralized nodule formation, while LY294002 attenuated these effects (Fig. 6F–G). qRT-PCR further demonstrated that aFGF upregulated osteogenic markers including runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), osteocalcin (OCN), and type I collagen (COL1A1), and chondrogenic genes such as aggrecan (ACAN), SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9), and type II collagen (COL2A1), with LY294002 blocking this induction (Fig. 6H–I). Collectively, aFGF promoted BMSC proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation via activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway.

IGF1 and ITGA8 serve as critical mediators in aFGF-induced PI3K-AKT signaling in BMSCs

To identify key downstream mediators of aFGF in the PI3K-AKT pathway, the top 10 hub genes were selected based on Degree values (connectivity) from the PPI network, and overlap analysis with PI3K-AKT pathway-related genes (from KEGG enrichment results) revealed IGF1 and ITGA8 as common nodes (Fig. 7A). qRT-PCR validation in Control, aFGF-treated, and aFGF + LY294002 groups showed that aFGF significantly increased IGF1 and ITGA8 mRNA levels compared to controls, while these upregulations were reversed by LY294002 co-treatment (Fig. 7B), confirming that aFGF upregulated IGF1 and ITGA8 expression in a PI3K-AKT-dependent manner.

Discussion

Rotator cuff injury represents a prevalent musculoskeletal disorder, frequently associated with persistent pain and functional limitations [31, 32]. A critical challenge in post-surgical repair lies in the high re-tear rates, primarily attributed to the inherently insufficient healing capacity of the tendon-bone interface, specifically, deficient bone formation and inadequate fibrocartilage regeneration [33–35]. Elucidating strategies to augment endogenous repair mechanisms thus holds substantial clinical significance. This study focused on aFGF and employed RNA-seq technology to investigate its molecular mechanism underlying the regulation of BMSCs.

First, this study performed phenotypic identification and functional verification of the isolated rabbit BMSCs. The cells highly expressed mesenchymal stem cell surface markers CD44 and CD90, while being negative for hematopoietic lineage markers CD34 and CD45, confirming their phenotypic characteristics as mesenchymal stem cells. The multipotent differentiation potential assay further verified that these cells could form mineralized nodules (positive for ARS staining) after osteogenic induction and generate lipid droplets (positive for Oil Red O staining) after adipogenic induction. These findings align well with the classic biological properties of BMSCs [36, 37], indicating the isolated cells are typical BMSCs, thus providing a reliable cellular model for subsequent studies. PCA analysis indicated that samples from the two groups were completely separated along the first principal component, indicating that aFGF induces significant remodeling of the global transcriptome in BMSCs. Volcano plot and heatmap analyses further confirmed the presence of a large number of DEGs following aFGF treatment, with a marked number of both upregulated and downregulated genes. Notably, all DEGs were distinctly clustered by treatment group, suggesting that aFGF-induced transcriptional changes exhibit consistency and specificity.

GO results showed that DEGs were enriched in ECM organization, cell adhesion, and cell migration. These findings imply that aFGF may optimize the healing microenvironment of the tendon-bone interface by enhancing BMSCs’ migratory capacity, homing efficiency, and ECM remodeling functions, aligning well with the core requirement for cellular involvement in matrix reconstruction during tissue repair [38, 39]. Notably, KEGG analysis further identified the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway as the most significantly enriched. Additionally, the MAPK signaling pathway was also enriched. As another key downstream pathway of FGF receptors, MAPK often collaborates with PI3K-AKT to regulate stem cell functions [40]. For example, FGF2 has been reported to promote BMSC osteogenic differentiation by co-activating PI3K-AKT and ERK-MAPK pathways, while FGF18 modulates chondrocyte proliferation and matrix synthesis via MAPK-dependent mechanisms [41–43]. The co-enrichment of these two pathways here suggests potential crosstalk between them in mediating aFGF’s effects on BMSCs, which deserves further investigation. As a central hub regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival, the PI3K-AKT pathway has been validated to play critical roles in BMSC osteogenic differentiation and angiogenesis [44], consistent with prior reports that FGF family members modulate cell survival and proliferation via PI3K-AKT activation. For instance, Wen et al. demonstrated that FGF17 protects the blood–brain barrier from damage caused by cerebral ischemia–reperfusion via PI3K-AKT signaling mediated by the FGF receptor (FGFR) [45], while FGF18 exerts anti-osteoarthritis effects via PI3K-AKT-dependent regulation of mitochondrial dynamics [46]. To explore the core nodes of the aFGF regulatory network, this study constructed a PPI network of DEGs. The highly connected hub genes in its modular structure may act as key regulatory factors to coordinate downstream effects, providing a direction for in-depth research on potential targets in the future.

Functional experiments demonstrated that aFGF enhances BMSC proliferation, migration, angiogenic potential, and osteogenic differentiation by activating the PI3K-AKT pathway, while upregulating osteogenic (RUNX2, OCN, COL1A1) and chondrogenic (ACAN, COL2A1, SOX9) marker genes. Importantly, further screening (via PPI hub genes and PI3K-AKT pathway overlap) and qRT-PCR validation confirmed that aFGF upregulates the expression of IGF1 and ITGA8 in a PI3K-AKT-dependent manner. This finding is supported by existing literature: IGF1 has been shown to reverse the osteogenic inhibitory effect of dexamethasone on BMP9-induced osteogenic differentiation in mouse embryonic fibroblasts through the PI3K/AKT/COX-2 pathway [47], indicating it may serve as a pro-osteogenic effector downstream of aFGF. For ITGA8, a gene associated with focal adhesion, integrated bioinformatics analyses have suggested it can regulate integrin expression to promote chondrogenesis [48]—this chondro-inductive property may indirectly support tendon-bone interface repair by facilitating the transition between cartilage and bone tissues. Mechanistically, RUNX2, recognized as the “master switch” of osteogenic differentiation, drives BMSC commitment to the osteoblast lineage upon upregulation; concurrently, elevated expression of OCN and COL1A1 (hallmark molecules of osteoblast maturation and bone matrix formation) further confirms aFGF’s capacity to promote the terminal stage of osteogenic differentiation [49–51]. In chondrogenesis, SOX9, a key transcription factor, regulates cartilage matrix synthesis by activating COL2A1 and ACAN expression [52, 53]. The coordinated upregulation of these genes indicates that aFGF not only facilitates lineage-specific differentiation but also balances osteogenic and chondrogenic programs. Zhu et al. reported that adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) with stable aFGF overexpression promote wound repair via PI3K-AKT upregulation [54]. Extending this mechanism to BMSCs, our findings suggest a potential cell-type universality in aFGF-mediated regulation of stem cell functions through the PI3K-AKT pathway. While prior studies confirmed PI3K-AKT activation induces RUNX2 upregulation [55, 56], the current work further revealeds that aFGF synchronously enhances both osteogenic and chondrogenic gene expression via this pathway, a critical feature for tendon-bone interface regeneration, wherein the repair demands orderly transition and integration of bone and cartilage tissues. Additionally, the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 abrogated aFGF’s effects, directly validating PI3K-AKT as an essential pathway for aFGF-mediated BMSC regulation and providing a definitive target for clinical targeted interventions.

However, this study has limitations: First, it relied solely on in vitro rabbit BMSCs, lacking in vivo validation. The static in vitro setting differs from the complex in vivo tendon-bone microenvironment, and rabbit-human species differences may affect clinical translatability. Notably, the in vivo application of aFGF also faces practical challenges, including dosage optimization, local retention, and safety: it exhibits thermal instability, is easily hydrolyzed by proteases, and has a short in vivo half-life, necessitating long-term, high-frequency administration—factors that limit its clinical application [24]. In contrast, localized delivery via biomaterial carriers (e.g., hydrogels, nanofibers) can improve its retention at the repair site and reduce off-target effects [57]. Additionally, comparisons with other FGF family members help highlight aFGF’s unique advantages: unlike many counterparts, aFGF balances osteogenesis, chondrogenesis, and angiogenesis, rendering it more suitable for tendon-bone interface repair. Second, while PI3K-AKT’s core role is confirmed, key downstream molecules and synergistic pathways remain underexplored, leaving mechanistic gaps. Third, RNA-seq-identified DEGs and PPI hub genes need deeper analysis regarding their roles in cellular phenotypes. Future work should validate aFGF’s efficacy and mechanisms in large animal rotator cuff injury models in vivo; develop biomaterial carriers for localized, sustained aFGF release at repair sites; explore synergies with other growth factors or physical stimuli; and clarify PI3K-AKT downstream effectors and hub gene functions to deepen mechanistic understanding.

In summary, integrating RNA-seq analysis with functional validation, this study systematically demonstrates that aFGF enhances BMSC proliferation, migration, and osteogenic/chondrogenic differentiation primarily by activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. These findings lay a robust theoretical foundation for aFGF’s application in rotator cuff repair. By targeting and boosting BMSCs’ osteogenic differentiation and tissue remodeling capacities at the tendon-bone interface, aFGF-based therapies hold promise for improving repair outcomes and functional prognosis in patients with rotator cuff injuries.
