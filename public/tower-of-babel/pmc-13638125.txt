Enhancement of Dissymmetry Factors Through Axial Elongation of Hetero[n]helicenes
Authors: Jeppe S. Mogensen, Christoffer Bræstrup, Christoffer Warming, Georg I. Junker, Yann Lie, Mathias Hermann, Kurt V. Mikkelsen, Michael Pittelkow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHelicenes are attractive emitters of circularly polarized light, yet strategies to enhance their luminescence dissymmetry factor (g lum) remain underdeveloped. Herein, we report a scalable, facile synthetic route to hetero[9]helicenes utilizing a benzofuran scaffolding strategy. This efficient one‐pot protocol provides access to the hetero[9]helicenes at gram scale, enabling progressive axial elongation to hetero[12]‐ and hetero[15]helicenes. Structural characterization by NMR and single‐crystal X‐ray diffraction confirms increasing helical distortion and reduced helical pitch upon elongation. Chiroptical investigations reveal a pronounced enhancement of both absorption (g abs) and emission (g lum) dissymmetry factors with increasing ring count. Notably, annulation with a three‐ring extension via a naphtofuran unit yields a twofold increase in g lum, reaching 1.95 × 10−2 at 475 nm for the [15]helicene. TD‐DFT calculations reproduce these trends, revealing that the enhancement originates from a systematic decrease in the electric transition dipole moment while the magnetic transition dipole moment remains constant, thereby increasing the relative magnetic contribution to the S1 →S0 transition as well as the angle between the dipole moments. These findings establish axial elongation as a robust design principle for amplifying chiroptical responses in helicenes.

Introduction

Helicenes are a unique class of molecules consisting of ortho‐annulated aromatic rings forming a chiral, helical‐shaped molecular structure [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Their helical chirality coupled with their π‐conjugated aromatic system makes them strong candidates as intense emitters of circularly polarized light with applications within optoelectronics, CPL‐OLEDs (circularly polarized luminescence‐organic light‐emitting diodes), and bioimaging [6, 7, 8]. Despite their potential, utilization of helicenes in chiral optical applications requires enhancement of their CPL output [9, 10]. Therefore, a central challenge in the field of helicenes is enhancing the luminescence dissymmetry factor (g lum), which quantifies the emission of circularly polarized light. The highest reported g lum values for helicenes are in the order of ∼10−2 [11, 12]. Studies correlating g lum with systematic structural modification remain scarce. Consequently, no general structural design principles for g lum optimization have been formulated, further complicating the enhancement of g lum [13, 14]. Axial elongation has emerged as a promising pathway toward the enhancement of g lum [15, 16]. For an electronically allowed transition, the g lum depends on three parameters: the electronic (μ⃗), magnetic (m⃗) transition dipole moments for the S1 → S0 transition, and the angle between them θ. These parameters are related by the formula: g lum ≈ 4(|m⃗||μ⃗|)·cosθ [6]. Theoretical studies have indicated that helicenes behave similarly to a nano‐solenoid in which increasing the number of turns in the coil leads to an increase in the magnetic moment and therefore an increase in g lum [11, 17]. It is still uncertain whether this correlation between turn numbers and increased g lum holds. Several studies support the correlation (Figure 1), such as the tetraaza[n]helicenes (n = 7, 9, 11, 13, 15) reported by Shibata [18], the expanded‐aza[n]helicenes (n = 11, 19, 27, 35, 43) reported by Wu [19], PDI‐fused helicenes NP[n]H (n = 2, 3, 4, 5) reported by Nuckolls [20, 21] or the extended [n]helicenes (n = 7, 9) reported by Narita [22]. However, several other studies [23, 24, 25] such as the fully ortho‐annulated aza[n]helicenes (n = 9, 11, 13, 15, 17, 19) reported by Tanaka [26] show no direct correlation between helical length and g lum, highlighting the need for studies which correlate ring count with predictable changes in chiral optical properties. Further, understanding the effect of axial elongation requires effective scalable protocols toward axially elongated helicenes [27]. Axial elongation of helicenes is associated with an increase in steric strain, rendering it synthetically challenging [28, 29, 30, 31], making the development of effective scalable protocols for axial elongation of particular interest.

Selected examples of homologous series of helicenes reported in literature: extended[n]helicenes, PDI‐fused helicenes, expanded[n]helicenes, aza[n]helicenes, and tetraaza[n]helicenes.

We have previously utilized the inherent chemistry of phenols in a benzofuran scaffolding approach [32] to obtain a series of hetero[n]helicenes (n = 7, 10, 13) [33].

This approach relies on an initial oxidative C–C coupling of the ortho‐position relative to the phenol followed by an intramolecular acid‐mediated dehydration. We have also established a facile synthetic route to hetero[9]helicenes, albeit with significant oxidative byproduct formation [34]. In this study, we describe a scalable and optimized synthetic route to hetero[9]helicenes. We further report the synthesis of hetero[n]helicenes (n = 12, 15) via a systematic elongation of the [9]helicenes using a benzofuran scaffolding approach. We showcase significant enhancement of g lum and g abs upon elongation, providing insight into possible design principles for chiroptical enhancement of helicenes. We also report quantum chemical simulations of the absorption and emission spectra, along with calculations of the electronic and magnetic transition dipole moments for the presented hetero[n]helicenes.

Results and Discussions

We began our investigations by reexamining our previously described synthesis of [9]helicenes [34]. The benzofuran scaffolding strategy is a 2‐step‐1‐pot protocol, where an oxidative C–C coupling is followed by an acid‐mediated dehydration to form a furan. The oxidative coupling between carbazole 1 and naphthol 2 using chloranil in chlorobenzene quantitatively yielded compound A within 8 h (Scheme 1). In previous work, the combination of BF3•OEt2 and chloranil promoted the desired dehydration but also produced oxidized byproducts. Therefore, a non‐oxidizing acid was chosen in the furan‐forming dehydration step. Without work‐up, direct addition of 4 equiv of p‐toluene sulfonic acid monohydrate (TsOH•H2O) into the reaction mixture and boiling under reflux resulted in the formation of helicene 3a (67% yield). This protocol also led to the loss of tert‐butyl groups on the carbazole moieties. The loss of the tert‐butyl groups did not result in significant reduction of solubility, likely due to the non‐planar structure of the molecules. Increasing the amount of TsOH•H2O to 6 equiv promoted O‐demethylation, in addition to ring closure and removal of tert‐butyl groups, yielding helicene 3b (24%) and helicene 3c (5%) along with 3a (40%). Demethylation of helicene 3a was desired as the hydroxy‐[9]helicenes 3b and 3c were targeted for further axial elongation. These unanticipated demethylation and de‐tert‐butylation reactions resulted in a synthetic procedure with double oxidative coupling, dehydration, reverse‐Friedel–Crafts alkylation, and demethylation taking place within a single reaction vessel. The reaction proved reliable at the gram scale, providing a very efficient synthetic route to hetero[9]helicenes.

Lewis acid mediated demethylation of 3a provided another straightforward pathway to helicenes 3b and 3c. While previously reported [9]helicenes carrying both tert‐butyl groups on the [9]helicene underwent demethylation with BBr3 within 3 h, demethylation of helicene 3a was significantly slower and required the combination of BCl3 and TBAI for full demethylation within 3 h [35]. Further, axial elongation from the [9]helicenes required the use of an oxidant which could selectively oxidize the terminal naphthol at the 1‐position adjacent to the phenol. Chloranil in PhCl did not show any reactivity, and the focus was therefore turned toward first row transition metal catalysts, which have typically been used in the analogous formation of BINOLs [36]. Treating [9]helicene 3b and naphthol 2 with CuIICl(OH)‐TMEDA (TMEDA = N,N,N’ ,N’ ‐tetramethylethylenediamine) resulted in complete consumption of both starting materials. Subsequent addition of TsOH•H2O and refluxing resulted in the formation of [12]helicene 4. Subjecting helicene 3c to analogous synthetic conditions resulted in the formation of [15]helicene 5, consisting of 15 ortho‐annulated fully conjugated aromatic rings. Although the synthesis of helicenes 4 and 5 proceeded in low yield, the reaction procedure was robust and reproducible. Curiously, O‐demethylation, as observed for 3 under treatment with TsOH•H2O, was not observed for either 4 or 5 (Scheme 1).

All synthesized helicenes (3a, 3b, 3c, 4, 5) were fully characterized by 1H‐NMR, 13C‐NMR spectroscopy, HRMS, and X‐ray crystallography. Notably, the CH2 propyl protons (closest to the nitrogen) of helicenes 4 and 5 display diastereotropic behavior and fully separate into two distinct signals. The methoxy groups of helicenes 4 and 5 at 2.71, 2.81, and 2.33 ppm, respectively, in CD2Cl2 are significantly upshifted compared to the methoxy of naphthol 2 at 3.90 ppm in CDCl3 [37], due to the shielding effect of the aromatic ring positioned directly above and below the methoxy groups. The X‐ray crystal structures confirmed the helical structure of all the helicenes. Hydrogen bonding from the ─OH to solvent molecules was observed for helicenes 3b and 3c. Crystals suitable for X‐ray diffraction were obtained through slow evaporation of mixtures of i‐PrOH and CH2Cl2 for both helicenes 4 and 5. The helical pitch of 3a, 4, and 5 measured from centroid to centroid of overlapping benzene rings (Figure 2) decreases with increased helicene length, revealing that the helical framework becomes more compact as it extends. The increase in helical distortion is also observed from the torsion angles of the central pyrrole ring. The torsion angles of the central pyrrole ring increase along the series 3a (16.8°), 4 (20.8°), 5 (24.5°) (Table S2), while the inner furan of helicenes 4 (4.18°) and 5 (5.06°) remain almost planar. This is similar to our previously reported hetero[n]helicene (n = 7, 10, 13) series in which the central heterocyclic ring also accommodates most of the bend.

The absorption and fluorescence spectra of helicenes 3a, 4, and 5 revealed a small redshift (18 nm) upon increasing the size of the helical system. Despite increasing the π‐system, a decrease in molar extinction coefficient (ε) was also observed upon increased helical length. This decrease is likely caused by the non‐planar helical structure, which leads to a decrease in the effective conjugation of the helicenes. The Stokes shift increases from 1338 cm−1 (3a) to 1778 cm−1 (5) (Table 1) with increased length of the helicenes, possibly due to more non‐radiative decay pathways with increased flexibility in the helicenes. The quantum yields (Φ) for helicenes 3a, 4, and 5 were determined to be 0.51, 0.18, and 0.10, respectively, showing a decrease with more annulated rings as observed for other series of helicenes [18, 26, 33]. The decrease in quantum yield is also reflected in the increase in the non‐radiative rate constant, which more than doubles from 3a (0.118 ns−1) to 5 (0.259 ns−1), possibly also due to increased flexibility in the longer helicenes. The enantiomers of helicenes 3a, 4, and 5 were separated using semi‐preparative recycling HPLC on a chiral stationary phase. The ECD and CPL of helicenes 3a, 4, and 5 measured in CH2Cl2 (Figure 3) revealed symmetrical absorption and emission peaks for the enantiomers, confirming the enantiomeric purity. The ECD revealed a negative Cotton effect for helicene 3a in the highest wavelength absorption. TD‐DFT enabled us to assign the absolute configuration of the helicenes 3a, 4, and 5 (Figures S50 and S51). The P‐enantiomers for helicenes 3a, 4, and 5 give rise to a positive lobe in the most redshifted absorption. Here, the effect of elongation is pronounced as the g abs increases substantially with length for almost all wavelengths. A similar trend is observed for the g lum values (Table 1). Remarkably, annulation with a single naphthol unit leads to almost twofold enhancement of g lum. The [15]helicene (5) exhibits g lum of 1.95 × 10−2 at 475 nm. It should be noted that even though axial elongation for this series of helicenes increases g lum across the series, the overall CPL‐brightness (Table S3) is optimal for the [12]helicene (4), highlighting the delicate balance required when optimizing chiroptical properties.

Having established a clear increase in g lum upon annulation with naphthalene units, we wondered whether helical length is the primary factor governing g lum for these molecules. To examine this, we compared the g lum as a function of helical length [n] for our newly synthesized series of helicenes and previously synthesized helicenes from our group, which also consist of naphthol and carbazole motifs with diverse functionalization at both the terminal oxygens and the aromatic core (Figure 4). For these molecules, elongation of the helical framework consistently results in enhanced g lum values. Notably, despite the electronic differences, the functionalization of the helicene core exerts only a marginal impact on the dissymmetry factor. Compound 4 differs from compounds 3a and 5. It has the carbazole unit positioned closer to one end of the helicene rather than near the center of the helical framework. Despite this structural difference, compound 4 follows the same monotonic increase in both g abs and g lum. This showcases axial elongation of the helicene core as the main structural determinant of g lum for this series of molecules. We further compared the g lum trajectory with helical length for our newly synthesized helicenes and the homologous series of helicenes shown in Figure 1. Excluding the aza[n]helicenes, an increase in g lum is observed with increased helical length. The longest member of each series displays the highest g lum value, resulting in g lum around 10−2 (Figure S49). Our series of helicenes displays an outstanding trajectory in g lum, which has only been observed in the tetraaza[n]helicenes reported by Shibata and coworkers [18]. To understand the origin of g lum enhancement in this series of helicenes, we undertook investigations into the transition dipole moments using TD‐DFT calculations with the functional CAM‐B3LYP‐D4 [38, 39] and the def2‐SVP [40] basis set as implemented in ORCA [41, 42]. Solvation of the helicenes was modeled using C‐PCM [43] (see Section 6 in Supporting Information for further computational details).

We note that the calculated CPL and ECD transitions match the experimentally recorded spectra, with a slight blueshift of the absorbance and emission maxima (Figures S50 and S51). Two effects are responsible for the increase in g lum values upon elongation (Figure 5). First, the magnitude of the electronic transition dipole moment decreases along the series, while the magnitude of the magnetic transition dipole moment remains constant, leading to an increase in the |m⃗|/|μ⃗| ratio. Secondly, the angle between the transition dipole vectors increases upon elongation. This results in the transition dipole moments becoming more antiparallel, contributing to a larger dissymmetry factor. Although compound 4 possesses a different placement of the carbazole unit compared with compounds 3a and 5, the calculated transition dipole moments exhibit the same systematic evolution observed across the homologous series. This indicates that the global electronic structure of the extended helicene framework dominates the optical transition, while the different carbazole position introduces only a local perturbation that does not alter the overall mechanism responsible for the enhanced g lum.

The direction of the magnetic transition dipole vector for all helicenes is aligned along the helical axis (Figure 6, red vectors). This alignment indicates that the helicenes are behaving as a nano‐solenoid. However, the magnetic transition dipole moment does not increase across the series, suggesting the electron behavior of the helicenes to be more complex.

Conclusion

We have developed a very efficient and scalable synthetic protocol for the synthesis of hetero[9]helicenes via benzofuran scaffolding. We further annulated the [9]helicenes systematically with one and two naphthol units, yielding hetero[n]helicenes (n = 12, 15). We obtained single x‐ray crystal structures of all helicenes and fully characterized them. We demonstrated the effect of axial elongation on the absorbance, fluorescence, and chiral optical properties, revealing a substantial increase in g abs and g lum as a consequence of axial elongation for this series of helicenes. TD‐DFT calculations revealed that a decrease in the magnitude of the electronic transition dipole moment is the primary contributor to the increase in chiral optical response. We have exemplified a promising path to increasing g abs and g lum for helicenes. This work further builds toward the formulation of design principles for optimization of chiral optical properties of helicenes, holding promise for the utility of multilayered helicenes in optical devices.
