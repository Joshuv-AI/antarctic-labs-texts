Edge centrality and the total variation of graph distributional signals
Feng Ji

Abstract
This short note is a supplement to [ 1 ] , in which the total variation of graph distributional signals is introduced and studied. We introduce a different formulation of total variation and relate it to the notion of edge centrality. The relation provides a different perspective of total variation and may facilitate its computation.

Graph distributional signals and notions total variation

The concept of graph distributional signals is introduced and studied in
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. In
[
2
]
, signal processing aspects such as Fourier transform and filtering are studied in detail. On the other hand, the notion of total variation of graph distributional signals is defined in
[
1
]
, which we recall as follows.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite, connected and unweighted simple graph. Let
{\bm{x}}=({\bm{x}}(i))_{1\leq i\leq n}
be a graph signal, where
{\bm{x}}(i)
belongs to a signal space such as
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{S}
(a finite set with discrete metric). Its (squared)
total variation
(
[
4
]
) is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{G}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in E}({\bm{x}}(i)-{\bm{x}}(j))^{2}.

(1)

To generalize, for a metric space
{\mathbb{M}}
(e.g.,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbb{S}^{n}
), let
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{M}})
be the space of probability measures on
{\mathbb{M}}
(
with respect to (w.r.t.)
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra) having finite second moments.

Definition 1
.

Given a graph
G
with
n
nodes and graph signal space
{\mathbb{M}}
such as
\mathbb{S}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, an element
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{M}})
is called a
graph distributional signal
. The marginals of a graph distributional signal
\mu
are the marginal distributions
\mathscr{N}=\{\mu_{i}\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}1\leq i\leq n\}

w.r.t.
the
n
coordinates of
{\mathbb{M}}
.

Given a graph distributional signal
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{M}})
for
{\mathbb{M}}={\mathbb{S}}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, its total variation can be modified directly from (
1
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mu}\mathcal{T}_{G}({\bm{x}})=\int\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in E}d({\bm{x}}(i),{\bm{x}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu({\bm{x}}).

(2)

where
d
is the metric on the signal space. Modeled on the Wasserstein metric
W(\cdot,\cdot)

[
5
]
, the total variation of a set of graph distributional marginals
[
1
]
is as follows.

Definition 2
.

Given the marginals
\mathscr{N}=\{\mu_{i}\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}1\leq i\leq n\}
of a graph distributional signal in
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{M}})
, the total variation of
\mathscr{N}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})=\inf_{\mu\in\Gamma(\mathscr{N})}\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mu),

(3)

where
\Gamma(\mathscr{N})
is the collection of all graph distributional signals in
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{M}})
whose marginals agree with
\mathscr{N}
.

For marginal distributions
\mathscr{N}=(\nu_{v})_{v\in V}
, we have introduced the total variation
\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
. However, computing the total variation often requires solving an optimization problem that is usually hard or even impossible to solve. It does not have a closed-form formula. In GSP, a useful strategy to understand a general graph
G
is to study the spanning trees of
G

[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, when the estimation of a quantity defined on
G
is intractable. This inspires us to aggregate the total variation of
\mathscr{N}
on subtrees of
G
. Naturally, we may consider expectation as a way of aggregation, which leads to the following framework.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathbb{T}_{G}
be the finite set of all subtrees of
G
. We use
\mathfrak{M}_{G}
to denote the space of probability distributions on
\mathbb{T}_{G}
. For
\eta\in\mathfrak{M}_{G}
, define the total variation of
\mathscr{N}
w.r.t.
\eta
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\eta}(\mathscr{N})=\mathbb{E}_{T\sim\eta}\mathcal{T}_{T}(\mathscr{N}).

(4)

Here, if
T
does not contain all the nodes of
G
, then
\mathcal{T}_{T}(\mathscr{N})
considers
\mathscr{N}
as the subset restricted to nodes of
T
.

Unlike
[
1
]
,
\eta
is fixed and independent of
\mathscr{N}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{T}_{\eta}
is a proxy of
\mathcal{T}_{G}
that is different from that in
[
1
]
. We relate it to the notion of “centrality” in the next section, which may facilitate its computation.

Connection to edge centralities

Centrality
is a well-studied topic in network analysis. An
edge centrality
is a function:
C:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. As
E
is a finite set,
C
can also be arranged as a vector
C=\big(C(e)\big)_{e\in E}
. This means we can take the inner product. Let
\mathfrak{C}_{G}
be the set of edge centralities on
G
. We have the following result.

Theorem 1
.

There is a map
\phi_{G}:\mathfrak{M}_{G}\to\mathfrak{C}_{G}
such that the following identity holds for any
\eta\in\mathfrak{M}_{G}
and marginal
\mathscr{N}=(\mu_{i})_{v_{i}\in V}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\eta}(\mathscr{N})=\langle\phi_{G}(\eta),W_{\mathscr{N}}\rangle,

(5)

where
W_{\mathscr{N}}=\big(W_{\mathscr{N}}(e)\big)_{e\in E}
is the (edge) vector
W_{\mathscr{N}}(e)=W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})^{2}
with
e=(v_{i},v_{j})
, where
W(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Wasserstein metric. Moreover,
\phi_{G}
is unique if
\mathbb{R}^{n}\subset\mathbb{M}
.

Proof.

We first claim that for any tree
T=(V_{T},E_{T})
, we have
\mathcal{T}_{T}(\mathscr{N})=\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in E_{T}}W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})^{2}
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there is a
\mu_{0}\in\Gamma(\mathscr{N})
such that
\mathcal{T}_{T}(\mathcal{N})\geq\mathcal{T}_{T}(\mu_{0})-\epsilon
. For each edge
(v_{i},v_{j})\in E
, let
\mu_{0,i,j}
be the marginal distribution of the pair
(v_{i},v_{j})
. The marginals of
\mu_{0,i,j}
are
\mu_{i}
and
\mu_{j}
at
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
respectively. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{T}(\mathscr{N})-\epsilon\geq\mathcal{T}_{T}(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in E_{T}}d({\bm{x}}(i),{\bm{x}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in E_{T}}\int d({\bm{x}}(i),{\bm{x}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in E_{T}}\int d({\bm{y}}(i),{\bm{y}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0,i,j}({\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in E_{T}}W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})^{2}.

Letting
\epsilon\to 0
, we see
\mathcal{T}_{T}(\mathscr{N})\geq\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in E_{T}}W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})^{2}
.

In the opposite direction, fix a node
v_{0}\in T
as the source and orient all the edges away from
v_{0}
. For each edge
e=(v_{i},v_{j})
, let
\mu_{e}\in\Gamma(\{\mu_{i},\mu_{j}\})
that realizes
W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})^{2}
. Using the orientation
T
and Bayesian network
[
9
]
Section 8.1, there is a joint distribution
\mu_{0}
on all the nodes whose marginal of each
e=(v_{i},v_{j})
is
\mu_{e}
. Therefore, similar to the estimations above, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in E_{T}}W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=(v_{i},v_{j})\in E_{T}}\int d({\bm{y}}(i),{\bm{y}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{e}({\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{T}(\mu_{0})\geq\mathcal{T}_{T}(\mathscr{N}).

The claim is proved.

For a general
G
and
\eta=\big(p(T)\big)_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{G}}\in\mathfrak{M}_{G}
, we may compute that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\eta}(\mathscr{N})=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{G}}p(T)\mathcal{T}_{T}(\mathscr{N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{G}}p(T)\sum_{e=(v_{i},v_{j})\in T}W_{\mathscr{N}}(e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=(v_{i},v_{j})\in E}W_{\mathscr{N}}(e)\sum_{e\in T}p(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=(v_{i},v_{j})\in E}W_{\mathscr{N}}(e)\cdot\big(\mathbb{E}_{T\sim\eta}\chi_{e}\big),

where
\chi_{e}:\mathbb{T}_{G}\to\{0,1\}
is the indicator function, i.e,
\chi_{e}(T)=1
if
e\in T
and
0
otherwise. Hence, to satisfy (
5
), it suffices to let the centrality
C_{\eta}=\phi_{G}(\eta)
to be defined by

\displaystyle C_{\eta}(e)=\mathbb{E}_{T\sim\eta}\chi_{e}.

(6)

Assuming
\mathbb{R}^{n}\subset\mathbb{M}
, to show
\phi_{G}
is unique, it suffices to construct
m=|E|
ordinary graph signals
{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{m}
such that the following holds. Write
{\bm{x}}_{i}=(x_{i,j})_{1\leq j\leq n}
as a vector and we form

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{i}=\big((x_{i,k}-x_{i,l})^{2}\big)_{(v_{k},v_{l})\in E}.

It suffices to require that the
m
-dimensional vectors
({\bm{y}}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
are linearly independent, i.e., the
m\times m
matrix
\mathbf{Y}
with rows
({\bm{y}}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
has non-zero determinant. However, if we view
X=(x_{i,j})_{\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n}
as independent variables, then
\det(\mathbf{Y})
is a nonzero polynomial in
X
. Therefore, by Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz, we can always find numbers to substitute the variables in
X
such that the resulting
\det(\mathbf{Y})
is nonzero. The claim is proved.
∎

The result essentially claims that there is a correspondence between edge centralities and subtree distributions. Therefore, to study such a distribution and the associated total variation, one may consider the corresponding edge centrality, and vice versa.

Examples

There are many choices of
\eta
that give rise to familiar centralities
\phi_{G}(\eta)
(see below), which can always be computed efficiently. This means that, by the theorem, we can compute the corresponding total variation efficiently.

Suppose two edge centralities
C_{1},C_{2}
differ by scaling, i.e., there is
r>0
such that
C_{1}=rC_{2}
. We usually treat them as the same in providing information such as edge importance. We may thus define an equivalence relation “
\sim
” on
\mathfrak{C}_{G}
as
C_{1}\sim C_{2}
if they differ by scaling. From the expression (
6
), we see that
\phi_{G}
is linear. Since
\mathfrak{M}_{G}
is compact, so is
\ima\phi_{G}
. However, the equivalence class of
\ima\phi
can contain many more edge centralities. For any graph
|\mathbb{T}_{G}|
is usually much larger than
|E|
. Hence,
\phi_{G}
is rarely injective. Hence, given an edge centrality
C
in
\ima\phi
, it usually requires additional prior information to recover or estimate the distribution that gives rise to
C
, such as the support and smoothness.

We next give some examples. In all the examples, we write
C_{\eta}
for
\phi_{G}(\eta)
.

Example 1
.

1.

Constant centrality:
\eta\in\mathfrak{M}_{G}
is supported on all the edges with equal probability for each edge. In this case,
C_{\eta}(e)=1/|E|
and the resulting total variation is equivalent to the ordinary total variation (for ordinary graph signals) in GSP.

2.

Betweenness centrality:
\eta\in\mathfrak{M}_{G}
is supported on the set of geodesic paths
G
constructed in the following two-step sampling procedure. We uniformly sample a pair of distinct nodes
v_{i},v_{j}
. A geodesic
P_{v_{i},v_{j}}
between
v_{i},v_{j}
is uniformly sampled among all the geodesic between
v_{i},v_{j}
. Hence,

\displaystyle C_{\eta}(e)=\frac{1}{c}\sum_{v_{i}\neq v_{j}\in V}\frac{\#P_{v_{i},v_{j},e}}{\#P_{v_{i},v_{j}}},

where
P_{v_{i},v_{j},e}
is a geodesic path between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
containing
e
. The constant
c
is the number of distinct pairs of nodes. It is apparent that
C_{\eta}
is equivalent to the betweenness centrality
[
10
]
.

3.

Spanning tree centrality:
\eta\in\mathfrak{M}_{G}
is the uniform distribution supported on the set of
spanning trees
of
G
. Then for each
e
,
C_{\eta}(e)
is the percentage of spanning trees containing
e
. This is nothing but the spanning tree centrality
[
11
]
.

By Theorem
1
, we have closed form formula for
\mathcal{T}_{\eta}(\mathscr{N})
if we have that for
W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})
. We conclude this note by showing examples for which the above holds.

Example 2
.

1.

Suppose
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mu_{i}
is the normal distribution on
\mathbb{R}
with mean
m_{i}
and standard deviation
s_{i}
. Then
W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})^{2}=(m_{i}-m_{j})^{2}+(s_{i}-s_{j})^{2}
.

2.

Suppose
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mu_{i}
is the empirical distribution supported on the (increasingly) ordered set
\{x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,N}\}
, where
N
is independent of
i
. Then

\displaystyle W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq k\leq N}(x_{i,k}-x_{j,k})^{2}.

3.

Suppose
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{S}^{n}
, where
\mathbb{S}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}\}
is a finite discrete metric space. For
1\leq k\leq N
, let
p_{i,k}
be the probability weight of
s_{k}
for
\mu_{i}
. Then

\displaystyle W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq k\leq N}|p_{i,k}-p_{j,k}|.
