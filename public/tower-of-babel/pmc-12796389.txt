A global perspective on cold wave disasters
Authors: Mustafa Öcal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCold-wave disasters cause substantial loss of life and property, yet their global patterns remain poorly understood in a warming climate. Using EM-DAT records (1951–2023), we quantified cold-wave disaster frequency, mortality, and damages worldwide. Linear-regression models were applied to assess temporal trends (annual and decadal), and “deaths per disaster” was calculated as a vulnerability indicator. A total of 312 cold-wave disasters occurred across 70 countries, causing 17 933 deaths and US $52.48 billion (inflation-adjusted) in damages. Europe recorded the most events, while Asia accounted for more than half of global deaths. India experienced the highest number of disasters and fatalities, and Peru had the greatest deaths per event. Disaster frequency rose significantly over time (β = 0.14 events year−1; p = 0.017; R2 = 0.11). Mortality showed no significant annual trend (p = 0.27) but increased significantly on a decadal scale (p = 0.007). Contrary to expectations of fewer cold extremes in a warming world, cold-wave disasters have increased globally. Mortality remains concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, underscoring the need for strengthened early-warning systems, equitable adaptation strategies, and international collaboration.Keywords: Cold-wave disaster, Climate change, Extreme weather events, Vulnerability, EM-DATSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Natural hazards, Health care

Introduction

Cold waves are periods of abnormally low temperatures that may persist for days to weeks, typically driven by large-scale atmospheric circulation anomalies such as polar-vortex breakdowns or jet-stream meanders1. They rank among the most destructive natural hazards, capable of causing substantial mortality even in high-income countries2. Low temperatures can trigger a wide range of cold-related illnesses—including pneumonia, influenza, asthma exacerbations, and cardiovascular events—posing particular risks to infants, older adults, and unhoused or socially vulnerable populations3.

Although a warming climate would traditionally be expected to reduce the occurrence of extreme cold events, recent evidence suggests otherwise. Arctic amplification may destabilize the polar vortex and increase the likelihood of severe cold outbreaks across mid-latitudes, presenting a paradox wherein global warming does not uniformly decrease cold extremes4–6.

Cold-wave–related mortality varies substantially across regions and is strongly influenced by socioeconomic status, energy access, housing quality, population density, and health-system preparedness7–9. Rapid urbanization and population growth further heighten exposure, particularly in low- and middle-income countries that lack adequate adaptive capacity10,11. As a result, cold waves remain an under-recognized but persistent threat to public health and socioeconomic stability.

Although numerous regional studies have examined cold-related mortality or temperature–health associations, these investigations are generally limited in geographic scope and do not provide a unified global perspective7,12. Prior research has also largely focused on health outcomes without jointly evaluating long-term patterns in event frequency, mortality, and economic losses. Consequently, the global, multi-decadal evolution of cold-wave disasters remains insufficiently characterized, and the distribution of mortality burden across different socioeconomic contexts has not been systematically assessed.

This study addresses these critical gaps by analyzing all cold-wave disasters recorded in the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT) from 1951 to 2023. We quantify temporal trends in disaster frequency, mortality, and economic losses; assess geographic distributions across continents and countries; and evaluate vulnerability using deaths-per-event and income-group comparisons. The findings provide a comprehensive global perspective on the evolving burden of cold-wave disasters in the context of climate change.

Results

The analysis encompassed all cold wave disasters occurring between 1951 and 2023, which marks the inaugural entry in the international disaster database. A total of 312 cold-wave disasters were recorded across 70 countries and five continents, resulting in 17,933 deaths. The aggregate damage caused by all disasters was calculated to be 22,150,284,000 USD, with an inflation-adjusted total damage (Adjusted Total Damage) of 52,476,908,000 USD. Europe recorded the highest number of cold-wave disasters, followed by Asia, South America, North America, and Africa. Approximately half of all fatalities occurred in Asia, while Europe and South America accounted for most of the remainder (Table 1; Fig. 1).

The average number of deaths per disaster can be used as an indicator of a country’s capacity to combat disasters, its preparedness for disasters, and its vulnerability to disasters. A review of the data reveals that Peru, India, Spain, Afghanistan, and Bangladesh have the highest rates of deaths per disaster, with an average of 215, 177, 147, 123, and 112 deaths per disaster, respectively. The mean number of deaths per disaster varied substantially across income groups, with low- and lower-middle-income countries showing higher averages than upper-middle- and high-income countries. Trend analysis revealed a significant long-term increase in the number of cold-wave disasters globally. The annual rate of increase was modest but statistically significant (β = 0.142; p = 0.017; R2 = 0.109), indicating a steady rise in the recorded frequency of events since 1951. In contrast, the annual mortality trend was not statistically significant (p = 0.273).

When aggregated by decade, both disaster frequency and mortality exhibited strong and statistically significant upward trends (p = 0.0016 and p = 0.007, respectively). The decadal slope coefficients (β = 1.43 for event counts; β = 210 for deaths) indicate that cold-wave disasters and related fatalities have both increased markedly over the past seven decades(Table 2; Figs. 5 and 6).

The regression analysis revealed a statistically significant upward trend in the annual frequency of cold-wave disasters (β = 0.142; p = 0.017; R2 = 0.109), indicating that approximately 10.9% of the variation in event frequency can be explained by time. This corresponds to an average annual increase of about 0.14 events per year over the 1951–2023 period.

In contrast, the annual trend in cold-wave–related mortality was not statistically significant (β = 3.2; p = 0.273; R2 = 0.024), suggesting that only 2.4% of the observed variation in global deaths is attributable to time. This indicates that improvements in early-warning systems, housing, and medical response may have stabilized mortality despite increasing event frequency.

When aggregated by decade, the regression results revealed much stronger relationships. Decadal models explained 88.4% of the variance in disaster frequency (p = 0.0016) and 79.4% of the variance in mortality (p = 0.007), both indicating significant upward trends. On average, cold-wave events increased by 1.43 per decade, while associated deaths rose by approximately 210 per decade. These findings demonstrate that although short-term annual variability is high, long-term decadal patterns reveal a clear and statistically significant intensification of cold-wave disasters globally(Figs. 7 and 8).

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This study provides the first global, multi-decadal assessment of cold-wave disasters using EM-DAT records from 1951 to 2023. A total of 312 disasters were recorded across 70 countries, resulting in 17 933 deaths and an inflation-adjusted total damage of US $ 52.48 billion. A statistically significant increase in disaster frequency was observed (β = 0.14 events yr−1, p = 0.017; R2 = 0.11), while annual mortality exhibited no significant trend (p = 0.27). However, decadal aggregation revealed a clear and significant rise in both events and deaths (p = 0.0016 and 0.007, respectively).

These results demonstrate that, despite advancements in early warning, health care, and infrastructure, the overall exposure to cold-related disasters continues to expand globally. The observed patterns reflect an interplay between meteorological dynamics and social vulnerability, challenging the assumption that global warming uniformly reduces cold extremes.

Climatic and atmospheric mechanisms

The persistence and even increase of cold-wave disasters in a warming climate appear paradoxical but can be explained by emerging evidence on large-scale atmospheric circulation shifts. Arctic amplification—accelerated warming of high latitudes relative to the global mean—reduces the equator-to-pole temperature gradient, thereby weakening the polar vortex and enhancing Rossby wave meandering4–6,13,14. Recent high-resolution reanalyses further support this linkage, showing that Arctic amplification is associated with more frequent stratospheric warming and increased mid-latitude cold outbreaks. These distortions allow intrusions of polar air into mid-latitudes, producing intense and prolonged cold episodes over Eurasia and North America.

This mechanism is supported by Coumou and Rahmstorf15, who demonstrated that amplified planetary waves are linked to more persistent weather extremes, including cold outbreaks. Internal climate variability such as ENSO and the North Atlantic Oscillation further modulate these anomalies. Nevertheless, the consistent increase in decadal disaster counts suggests that the phenomenon extends beyond natural variability alone.

An additional factor is the improvement in observational coverage and reporting accuracy after the 1990s, when EM-DAT adopted standardized inclusion criteria. Yet even accounting for this, the magnitude and spatial consistency of the observed rise suggest a real increase in exposure and impact rather than an artifact of reporting.

Regional and socioeconomic vulnerability

Marked regional disparities were evident. Europe recorded the greatest number of cold-wave events, but mortality rates were comparatively low, reflecting robust housing, heating systems, and public health preparedness. Conversely, Asia and South America accounted for the majority of deaths, with India, Peru, and Bangladesh exhibiting the highest deaths-per-event ratios.

This heterogeneity underscores the decisive role of socioeconomic and infrastructural vulnerability in shaping disaster outcomes. The findings align with previous studies7,12,16,17, which demonstrated that low- and middle-income countries experience disproportionate temperature-related mortality due to inadequate housing insulation, energy poverty, and limited early-warning systems.

The relationship between vulnerability and mortality observed here embodies the principle of “climate inequity”: the same meteorological hazard produces vastly different human impacts depending on socioeconomic resilience. While mortality in Europe and North America has decreased due to robust adaptation measures, developing nations remain disproportionately affected, emphasizing the need for equitable adaptation funding and technical support.

Reconciling climate models and observational evidence

Most climate projections, including those in IPCC AR618,19, predict a gradual reduction in cold extremes under continuous warming. However, observational data—including the present analysis—suggest regional intensification of cold events. Recent European projections indicate that while heat-related deaths are expected to rise, cold-related mortality will remain substantial in northern regions20. This apparent contradiction can be reconciled by recognizing spatial heterogeneity in climate feedbacks.

Arctic amplification weakens the jet stream, increasing the amplitude and persistence of planetary waves, which can prolong cold anomalies over continents21,22. Meanwhile, population growth, urban sprawl, and exposure expansion amplify human and economic losses even if the meteorological frequency remains stable. Furthermore, improved detection and communication technologies have heightened event documentation.

The absence of a significant global mortality trend, despite rising event frequency, suggests that improvements in healthcare, energy infrastructure, and early-warning dissemination have mitigated per-event fatality. Nevertheless, persistent high mortality in certain countries highlights that adaptive progress is uneven and globally inequitable. These results underline that cold extremes remain a relevant public-health challenge even in a warming world.

Policy and adaptation implications

The findings carry several implications for climate adaptation and disaster risk management.

First, cold extremes must remain on the global adaptation agenda, which is currently dominated by heatwaves and hydrometeorological hazards. Many national adaptation plans under the Paris Agreement neglect low-temperature risks. Yet the EM-DAT data demonstrate that cold-related mortality still rivals that from other weather disasters in several regions.

Second, investments in energy security and housing quality are vital adaptation strategies. In countries with recurrent cold-wave fatalities, such as India and Bangladesh, energy poverty directly translates to higher mortality. Expanding access to affordable heating, electricity, and insulation can substantially reduce risk.

Third, early-warning and health-response systems should be strengthened through local meteorological services and community outreach. Integrating these programs within the frameworks of the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030) and Sustainable Development Goal 1323,24 will enhance resilience at both national and regional scales.

Finally, international cooperation—particularly between high-income and vulnerable countries—is essential. Technology transfer, data sharing, and financial mechanisms under the UNFCCC Adaptation Fund can help bridge resilience gaps. These priorities are consistent with the findings of the Sendai Framework Midterm Review25, which emphasizes mortality reduction and inclusive resilience as core disaster-risk goals.Europe’s Cold Weather Plan and Japan’s Disaster Resilience Strategy26 serve as effective models for integrated governance that could inform developing regions.

Strengths, Limitations, and future research

The principal strength of this study lies in its comprehensive temporal coverage and standardized dataset, enabling consistent comparison across decades and continents. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. EM-DAT relies on secondary reporting; thus, underreporting or inconsistent definitions may bias results. The 2024 CRED Annual Disaster Report similarly noted underestimation of indirect cold-related losses and reporting gaps in low-income regions27.The database lacks detailed meteorological parameters—such as minimum temperature, duration, and spatial extent—that would allow direct linkage between physical hazard intensity and health outcomes.

Future research should integrate EM-DAT records with gridded reanalysis products28,29 to quantify temperature anomalies and exposure duration. Incorporating socioeconomic indicators such as the Human Development Index, energy access, and healthcare expenditure would enable multivariate modeling of vulnerability. Interdisciplinary studies combining climatology, epidemiology, and economics will be critical to refine global risk assessment frameworks and to guide evidence-based adaptation policies.

Conclusion

Despite rapid global warming, cold-wave disasters persist and are increasing in frequency. The paradox of rising cold hazards amid climate change highlights that global warming does not uniformly translate into reduced cold risk. The persistence of cold-related mortality—particularly in low- and middle-income countries—demonstrates that adaptation remains incomplete and uneven. Addressing these inequities through targeted investments, robust governance, and international cooperation is imperative for reducing future cold-wave disaster impacts.

Methods

Data source

All data were obtained from the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT; www.emdat.be), which compiles standardized information on natural disasters reported by national authorities, United Nations agencies, non-governmental and humanitarian organizations. EM-DAT is one of the most comprehensive and methodologically transparent global disaster databases, systematically integrating information from multiple authoritative sources and applying standardized inclusion criteria, multi-source validation, and internal quality-control procedures before events are released30.

Records categorized under the “Cold wave” disaster type were extracted for the period 1951–2023. EM-DAT applies predefined inclusion criteria for disaster registration: (1) ≥ 10 deaths, (2) ≥ 100 people affected, (3) declaration of a state of emergency, or (4) international assistance requested. All records meeting these criteria and labeled as “Cold wave” were included in this analysis without additional filtering. Events with incomplete temporal or geographic information (missing year or country) were excluded.

EM-DAT has been widely used in climate-related disaster research because it provides globally consistent, multi-decadal data on disaster occurrence, mortality, and economic losses, allowing reliable comparisons across countries and decades31. No other global dataset offers comparable temporal depth, standardized reporting, or hazard-specific classification for cold-wave events. Therefore, EM-DAT represents the most appropriate and internationally recognized source for conducting long-term global analyses in this study.

All monetary loss values were converted to constant 2023 USD using the World Bank GDP deflator index.

Operational definitions and derived variables

A cold-wave disaster was defined as a sustained period of abnormally low temperature resulting in human casualties or economic losses. For each event, EM-DAT provides the year of occurrence, country, continent, number of deaths, and reported economic damage (USD). From these records, annual and decadal totals for event counts and deaths were generated directly based on the reported event year. “Deaths per disaster” was calculated by dividing total deaths by total events for each country and for each World Bank income group (2023 classification).

Data processing and quality control

All EM-DAT records were screened for duplicate entries and geographic inconsistencies using ISO-standard country codes and the United Nations geographic classification. Events missing death data were excluded from mortality analyses, while those lacking economic-loss information were excluded only from financial assessments. No additional filtering, merging, or modification of EM-DAT values was performed. Outliers were retained unless clear evidence indicated a reporting error. Economic losses were converted to constant 2023 USD using the World Bank GDP deflator index.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean, median, frequency, and percentage) were computed to summarize the dataset.Temporal trends in annual and decadal disaster counts and deaths were analyzed using simple linear regression in Jamovi v2.6.17 (The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia). Model outputs included the slope (β), coefficient of determination (R2), and two-tailed p-values, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05 (α = 0.05).Non-parametric comparisons across continents and income groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc pairwise analysis when significant.

All analyses were cross-validated in IBM SPSS Statistics v29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) to ensure reproducibility.
