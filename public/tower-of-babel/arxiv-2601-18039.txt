1.2
Sonnet equations.

They are a refined version of tetrahedron equations. To describe them we need some combinatorics
which is a particular case of
[
MS
]
.

Categories
{\cal A}(3,2)
and
{\cal A}(4,2)
.
Consider the symmetric group
S_{3}

with Coxeter generators
s_{1},s_{2}
. The relations are

s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}=s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}

and
s_{1}^{2}=s_{2}^{2}=e
. The set
A(3,2)
of reduced decompositions of the longest element
in
S_{3}
consists of two elements
s_{1}s_{2}s_{1},s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}
, to be denoted
(121),(212)
.

The category
{\cal A}(3,2)
has two objects
(121),(212)
and one arrow

R:\ (121)\longrightarrow(212)
.

Consider the symmetric group
S_{4}
with Coxeter generators
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}
. The relations are

s_{i}s_{i+1}s_{i}=s_{i+1}s_{i}s_{i+1},\ i=1,2,

and
s_{i}^{2}=e,\ 1\leq i\leq 2
. As before, products
s_{i_{1}}\ldots s_{i_{k}}
will be denoted
(i_{1}\ldots i_{k})
. Let
A(4,2)
denote the set of all reduced decompositions
of the longest element in
S_{4}
. For example

(121321),(321323)\in A(4,2).

By definition
Ob{\cal A}(4,2)=A(4,2)
.
Altogether
A(4,2)
consists of
14
elements; let us describe them together with
the morphisms in
{\cal A}(4,2)
. By definition morphisms in
{\cal A}(4,2)
are compositions of
elementary arrows. Elementary arrows are of two types:

(a)
R
-type
: these are arrows of the form

R(k):\ (...i_{k}i_{k+1}i_{k+2}...)\longrightarrow(...i^{\prime}_{k}i^{\prime}_{k+1}i^{\prime}_{k+2}...),\ 1\leq k\leq 4,

where
(i_{k},i_{k+1},i_{k+2})=(j,j+1,j)
and

(i^{\prime}_{k},i^{\prime}_{k+1},i^{\prime}_{k+2})=(j+1,j,j+1)
for
j=1
or
2
, the other elements
not being changed.

For example

R(1):\ (121321)\longrightarrow(212321).

(b)
L
-type
: these are arrows of the form

L(k):\ (...i_{k}i_{k+1}...)\longrightarrow(...i_{k+1}i_{k}...),\ 1\leq k\leq 5,

identical on other elements, where
(i_{k},i_{k+1})=(1,3)
or
(3,1)
.

For example

L(3):\ (121321)\longrightarrow(123121).

Now we can draw all objects in
{\cal A}(4,2)
, and almost all arrows.

Namely, there are two chains of elementary arrows: the first (upper) one is

(121321)\overset{R(1)}{\longrightarrow}(212321)\overset{R(3)}{\longrightarrow}(213231)\overset{L(2)}{\longrightarrow}(231231)\overset{L(5)}{\longrightarrow}(231213)

\overset{R(3)}{\longrightarrow}(232123)\overset{R(1)}{\longrightarrow}(323123)\overset{L(3)}{\longrightarrow}(321323)

Let
C_{+}
denote the composition

C_{+}=L(3)R(1)R(3)L(5)L(2)R(3)R(1):\ (121321)\longrightarrow(321323).

The second (lower) chain is

(121321)\overset{L(3)}{\longrightarrow}(123121)\overset{R(4)}{\longrightarrow}(123212)\overset{R(2)}{\longrightarrow}(132312)\overset{L(4)}{\longrightarrow}(132132)

\overset{L(1)}{\longrightarrow}(312132)\overset{R(2)}{\longrightarrow}(321232)\overset{R(4)}{\longrightarrow}(321323)

Let
C_{-}
denote the composition

C_{-}=R(4)R(2)L(1)L(4)R(2)R(4)L(3):\ (121321)\longrightarrow(321323).

We impose the relations

L(i)^{2}=\text{Id},\ 1\leq i\leq 5,

L(i)L(j)=L(j)L(i)\ \text{if}\ |i-j|\geq 2,

and

C_{+}=C_{-}

(the sonnet relation), which may be written symbolically in the form

LRRLLRR=RRLLRRL.

If we forget the letter
L
we get an equation of the form

RRRR=RRRR,

the tetrahedron relation.

Let
{\cal C}
be a category. Let us call a
sonnet equation in
{\cal C}
a functor

{\mathbb{A}}:\ {\cal A}(4,2)\longrightarrow{\cal C}
. So it is a collection of
14

and
14

given for each elementary arrow

f:\ {\bf a}\longrightarrow{\bf a}^{\prime}
, i.e.
f=R(i)
or
f=L(j)
, such that
{\mathbb{A}}(L(j))^{2}=\text{Id}
,

{\mathbb{A}}(L(j)){\mathbb{A}}(L(j^{\prime}))={\mathbb{A}}(L(j^{\prime})){\mathbb{A}}(L(j))
for
|j-j^{\prime}|>1
, and

L(3)R(1)R(3)L(5)L(2)R(3)R(1)=R(4)R(2)L(1)L(4)R(2)R(4)L(3):\

{\mathbb{A}}(121321)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{A}}(321323).

where we have denoted for brevity

by

.

More generally, for any

we can define in a similar manner the sets

1.3
Category of rational correspondences.

In the examples below the target category
{\cal C}
will be the category whose objects are
varieties over a fixed base field, or, more generally, schemes, and morphisms are rational correspondences.

Namely, let
X
,
Y
be two varieties.
Let us call a rational map
f:X\longrightarrow Y
a map of varieties
f_{0}:U\longrightarrow Y
where

U\subset X

is a dense Zarisky open in
X
, where two maps
f_{0}:\ U\longrightarrow Y
and
f_{0}^{\prime}:\ U^{\prime}\longrightarrow Y
are identified if they coincide on
U\cap U^{\prime}
.

Let us say that
f
is
good
if

f_{0}(U)
contains a Zarisky dense open in
Y
. Good rational maps may be composed.

A rational correspondence between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
is a rational map

P\longrightarrow X_{1}\times X_{2}

whose components
p_{i}:P\longrightarrow X_{i},\ i=1,2
are good.

Rational correspondences may be composed in the usual way. Namely, given another correspondence

(q_{2},q_{3}):\ Q\longrightarrow X_{2}\times X_{3},

let
R=P\times_{X_{2}}Q
; let
r_{1}:R\longrightarrow P
,
r_{3}:R\longrightarrow Q
be the projections.
The composition
QP:R\longrightarrow X_{1}\times X_{3}
is defined as
(p_{1}r_{1},q_{3}r_{3})
.

The composition is associative, and
\text{Id}_{X}
is the diagonal

X\longrightarrow X\times X
. This way we get the category
{\cal V}
of varieties and good rational
correspondences. It will be our target category.

1.4
Categories
{\cal B}(n,2)
.

More generally, for each
n\geq 2
our examples give rise to functors

{\cal A}(n,2)\longrightarrow{\cal V}
.
In
[
MS
]
the sets
B(n,2)
are introduced as certain quotients of
A(n,2)
. For example
B(4,2)
is obtained from
A(4,2)
by identifying elements related by
L
transformations. It contains
8
elements. We can define the category

{\cal B}(n,2)
with
Ob{\cal B}(n,2)=B(n,2)
similarly to
{\cal A}(n,2)
.

A
tetrahedron equation
with values in a category
{\cal C}
is a functor

{\cal B}(4,2)\longrightarrow{\cal C}

It will have a form

RRRR=RRRR

We have the canonical projection functor

{\cal A}(4,2)\longrightarrow{\cal B}(4,2),

and one sees immediately that it is an equivalence of categories. Therefore
tetrahedron equations are equivalent to sonnet equations.

2.1
Flags and bases.

Let
V=V_{r+1}={\mathbb{C}}[x]_{\leq r}
denote the space of complex polynomials of degree
\leq r

denote the variety of flags of linear subspaces

L_{\bullet}:\ L_{1}\subset L_{2}\subset\ldots\subset L_{r}\subset L_{r+1}=V_{r+1},\ \dim L_{i}=i.

Let
U\subset Fl_{r}
be the Zarisky dense open subvariety
of flags in general position. The meaning of the words ”in general position” will be
as follows:

(1) if

is a base vector then we demand that
u_{1}(0)\neq 0
;

(2) pick
u_{1}(x)
as above; we demand that there exists
u_{2}(x)\in L_{2}
such that

u_{1},u_{2}
form a base of
L_{2}

u_{2}(0)=0
, and

(3) pick

, and

We will also call flags in general position

Let

3
Wronskians and transition maps

3.1
Wronskians.

For a sequence
u=(u_{1}(x),\ldots,u_{i}(x)),\ u_{j}(x)\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]
, define the Wronskian matrix

\mathcal{W}r(u_{1},\ldots,u_{i})=\left(\begin{matrix}u_{1}&u_{2}&\ldots&u_{i}\\
u^{\prime}_{1}&u^{\prime}_{2}&\ldots&u^{\prime}_{i}\\
.&.&\ldots&.\\
u^{(i-1)}_{1}&u^{(i-1)}_{2}&\ldots&u^{(i-1)}_{i}\\
\end{matrix}\right),

and let
Wr(u)=Wr(u_{1},\ldots,u_{i})
denote its determinant.

For example

\mathcal{W}r(1,x,x^{2}/2,\ldots,x^{i-1}/(i-1)!)

is an upper triangular matrix with
1
’s on the diagonal,
whence

Wr(1,x,x^{2}/2,\ldots,x^{i-1}/(i-1)!)=1.

On the other hand

\mathcal{W}r\left(1,x,\frac{x^{2}}{2},\ldots,\frac{x^{i-1}}{(i-1)!},\frac{x^{i+1}}{(i+1)!}\right)

is an upper triangular matrix whose diagonal part is
Diag(1,\ldots,1,x)
,
whence

Wr\left(1,x,\frac{x^{2}}{2},\ldots,\frac{x^{i-1}}{(i-1)!},\frac{x^{i+1}}{(i+1)!}\right)=x.

3.2
Wronskian of a normalized collection.

Let
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r+1})
be a normalized collection with
parameters
\{c_{i}\}
. Denote

f_{i}(x):=Wr(u_{1},\ldots,u_{i})

and

Wr(u):=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r+1}).

We have

f_{i}(x)=c_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot c_{i}+O(x).

Denote

a_{i}:=\prod_{j=1}^{i}c_{j}.

We have

\frac{a_{i-1}a_{i+1}}{a_{i}}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}c_{j}\right)c_{i+1}.

3.3
Proposition.

We have

Wr(A_{i}(a)u)=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{i-1},a^{-1}f_{i}+\frac{a_{i-1}a_{i+1}}{a_{i}}x+O(x^{2}),af_{i+1},f_{i+2},\ldots,f_{r+1}).

3.4
Wronskian evolution

We can go in the opposite direction. Let us consider a
variety

W=W_{r+1}=\{f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r+1}),\ f_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}[x],f_{j}(0)\neq 0,\ 1\leq j\leq r+1\}.

Let

There exists a unique solution

\widehat{f}_{i}=\frac{a_{i-1}a_{i+1}}{a_{i}}x+O(x^{2}).

For
a\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
denote

\widetilde{f}_{i}=a^{-1}f_{i}+\widehat{f}_{i}

and define

A_{i}(a)f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{i-1},\widetilde{f}_{i},af_{i+1},f_{i+2},\ldots,f_{t+1}).

The space of

A square matrix

Let

complementary diagonal

We have an injective group homomorphism
