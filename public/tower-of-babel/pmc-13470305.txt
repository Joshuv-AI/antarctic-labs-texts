Exploring delta/alpha and theta/alpha ratios as longitudinal treatment-response biomarkers in post-stroke rehabilitation
Authors: Itay Inbar, Batsheva Weisinger, Dharam P. Pandey, Jeffrey L. Saver, Steven C. Cramer, Kevin N. Sheth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPost-stroke motor recovery relies on rebalancing of network connectivity, yet objective biomarkers for longitudinal monitoring remain limited. The delta/alpha ratio (DAR) is an established quantitative electroencephalography (qEEG) index of pathological slow-wave activity associated with stroke outcomes; the theta/alpha ratio (TAR) is a related but less-established measure. In this exploratory analysis, we investigated whether these biomarkers can serve as longitudinal treatment-response markers during neurorehabilitation, using electromagnetic network targeting field (ENTF) stimulation.Patients and methodsWe conducted a longitudinal analysis of 21 subacute ischemic stroke patients from a double-blind, sham-controlled randomized trial. Participants received either active ENTF stimulation or sham treatment over 15 sessions spanning 2 months. Resting-state EEG was recorded from 8 scalp electrodes. DAR and TAR were computed per session. Linear mixed-effects models (LMM) assessed session-by-treatment interactions. Secondary exploratory analyses examined coherence-based functional connectivity.ResultsLMM analysis revealed significant session-by-treatment interactions for both DAR (β = −0.29, p = 0.016) and TAR (β = −0.034, p < 0.001). The active group was associated with steeper biomarker declines in DAR and TAR than sham. Secondary connectivity analyses showed no robust effects.DiscussionIn this analysis, DAR and TAR showed treatment-associated longitudinal changes, and were associated with functional improvement trajectories, consistent with their behavior as candidate treatment-response markers.ConclusionThese exploratory findings are consistent with DAR and TAR behaving as longitudinal treatment-response markers in post-stroke rehabilitation. The results are hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in a trial with pre-specified endpoints.

Introduction

Post-stroke motor recovery depends on reorganization of cortical networks (Murphy and Corbett, 2009; Cramer, 2008). While standardized functional assessments such as the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA) remain the clinical standard for evaluating outcomes, they may lack sensitivity to early neurophysiological changes that precede or accompany functional improvement. Quantitative electroencephalography (qEEG) offers a complementary, high-resolution window into the neural network reorganization underlying recovery.

Stroke causes widespread neuronal damage leading to disruptions in neural network connectivity and oscillatory dynamics. Impaired neuronal function post-stroke is characterized electrophysiologically by an increase in low-frequency oscillations (delta and theta bands) and a decrease in higher-frequency activity (alpha band) (Finnigan et al., 2016; Sheorajpanday et al., 2011). This pathological shift manifests as an elevated delta/alpha ratio (DAR) and theta/alpha ratio (TAR). DAR has an extensive literature associating it with stroke severity and functional outcome, while TAR is slightly less established in comparison (Finnigan et al., 2016; Sheorajpanday et al., 2011; Lacerda et al., 2024; Schleiger et al., 2014; Leon-Carrion et al., 2009; Milani et al., 2022; Trujillo et al., 2017; Bentes et al., 2018; Dimyan and Cohen, 2011).

The rebalancing of these spectral ratios, reflecting a shift away from slow-wave dominance, has been proposed as a proxy for neurophysiological recovery (Finnigan and van Putten, 2013; Giaquinto et al., 1994; Boyd et al., 2017), although longitudinal treatment-referenced evidence remains limited. Prior studies have demonstrated correlations between DAR/TAR reduction and clinical improvement, yet few have examined these biomarkers longitudinally across an intensive rehabilitation period with the temporal density required to characterize treatment-specific trajectories, especially in the subacute phase.

Electromagnetic network targeting field (ENTF) stimulation represents a novel neuromodulatory approach designed to facilitate motor recovery by delivering frequency-tuned electromagnetic fields that may promote neural network reorganization (Dromerick et al., 2021; Hummel and Cohen, 2006). A pilot randomized controlled trial demonstrated improved motor outcomes in patients receiving ENTF stimulation compared to sham (Weisinger et al., 2022). However, the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying this improvement remain incompletely characterized.

The present study is an exploratory analysis of a uniquely dense longitudinal EEG dataset, derived from a randomized sham controlled trial. This study aims to examine whether DAR and TAR behave as longitudinal treatment-response markers during ENTF-augmented neurorehabilitation. We examined whether patients receiving active treatment showed steeper declines in these spectral ratios than sham-treated controls. Additionally, we conducted exploratory analyses of functional connectivity metrics to assess whether network-level changes accompany the spectral findings.

Patients and methods

Study design and participants

This study relied on data from a randomized double-blind, sham-controlled pilot trial (NCT04039178) of ENTF stimulation in subacute ischemic stroke (Weisinger et al., 2022). Twenty-one participants were randomized to receive either active ENTF stimulation (N = 13) or sham treatment (N = 8). Data were collected over 15 rehabilitation sessions spanning approximately 2 months, beginning 10–15 days post-stroke onset. EEG spectral ratios were not pre-specified endpoints of the trial; all EEG analyses reported here are post-hoc.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) ischemic stroke within prior 28 days, (2) upper extremity motor impairment, and (3) ability to participate in rehabilitation sessions. Exclusion criteria included hemorrhagic stroke, severe cognitive impairment precluding protocol adherence, and contraindications to electromagnetic stimulation. The study was approved by the relevant institutional review boards (see Ethical Approval), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Baseline characteristics

Groups did not differ significantly on demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, hand dominance, time from stroke onset, and baseline functional scores (FMA-UE, modified Rankin Scale [mRS]). Baseline FMA-UE scores were 26.8 ± [11.5] in the active group and 18.8 ± [8.7] in the sham group (p = n.s.) (Fugl-Meyer et al., 1975). Although not statistically significant, the numerical imbalances in baseline FMA-UE and DAR were addressed in the sensitivity analyses. These characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics.

Data generation and processing pipeline. EEG data were collected from 21 participants across 15 sessions over 2 months. For each session, resting-state periods were concatenated, preprocessed, and analyzed using Welch's method to obtain power spectral density estimates. Delta/alpha ratio (DAR) and theta/alpha ratio (TAR) biomarkers were then extracted, yielding one DAR and one TAR value per hemisphere per session for each participant.

EEG acquisition and processing

Resting-state EEG was acquired from an 8-electrode montage positioned according to the 10–20 international system at F3, F4, Fz, C3, C4, Cz, P3, and P4, with dedicated ground and reference electrodes [19]. Recordings were acquired using a portable EEG system with standardized protocols administered by trained technicians. Signals were digitized at 512 Hz and resampled to 256 Hz. The acquisition paradigm employed a block design of 160-s recordings consisting of 20-s task/rest alternations. Four recordings were obtained per session (2 tasks × 2 hemispheres). For resting-state spectral estimation, only the interleaved 20-s rest blocks were used; the first and last two seconds of each rest block were discarded to remove task-to-rest transition activity. The retained rest segments were concatenated to yield cumulative resting-state epochs of four minutes per session, a duration sufficient to stabilize resting-state network measures. The task blocks were recorded but not used in this analysis, and consisted of (1) a continuous hand grip and (2) a hand raise.

Signal quality

Electrode impedance was maintained below 5 kΩ, raw amplitudes remained within ±100 μV, and power spectra exhibited the expected 1/f distribution characteristic of biological neural signals (Welch, 1967). Preprocessing included bandpass filtering (1–40 Hz), a 50-Hz notch filter to suppress powerline interference, and artifact removal using the autoreject algorithm (Jas et al., 2017). Independent component analysis (ICA) was considered but not used, as autoreject provided sufficient artifact suppression in this dataset.

Spectral power ratios

Power spectral density was estimated by Welch's method (Hann window, 2-s segments, 50% overlap) and integrated over the canonical bands: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), and beta (13–30 Hz). For each channel and recording, band power was obtained per segment and averaged; DAR and TAR were then formed from these averaged band powers, so ratios were computed after averaging. Ratios were summarized by hemisphere relative to the lesion (ipsilesional/contralesional) and analyzed per session.

Exploratory functional connectivity

As a secondary analysis, functional connectivity was estimated from the same concatenated resting-state epochs used for the spectral ratios. Magnitude-squared coherence was computed by Welch's method between all pairs of six of the eight channels (excluding the midline Fz and Cz) and averaged within the alpha and beta bands. For each band the six-channel coherence values were assembled into a weighted, fully connected graph; edge weights equal to the magnitude-squared coherence, without thresholding. From this graph we derived standard weighted graph-theoretic summaries: global efficiency, mean coherence, clustering coefficient, characteristic path length, network density, and node betweenness centrality. In addition, we examined primary motor cortex (M1) connectivity using C3 and C4 as seeds, quantifying the interhemispheric C3–C4 coherence and the mean coherence of each M1 electrode with the remaining channels. All connectivity measures were summarized per session and compared between groups. Magnitude-squared coherence estimated on a low-density scalp montage is influenced by volume conduction and reference choice, which we did not correct for; these analyses are therefore descriptive.

Statistical analysis

Model description

To assess the effect of treatment on biomarker trajectories over time, we employed linear mixed-effects models (LMM) (Laird and Ware, 1982). The model tested the interaction between treatment group and time (sessions). The model specification was as follows:yij=β0+β1·Sessionij+β2·Treatmenti+β3·Sessionij×Treatmenti+μi+εijwhere y_ij represents the biomarker value for subject i at session j, Session_ij is the session number, Treatment_i is a binary indicator for treatment group (1 = active, 0 = sham), and epsilon_ij is the residual error term. A subject-specific random intercept was included to absorb between-subject differences in baseline level. The treatment-by-time contrast is estimated from within-subject change over sessions. The parameter of interest was beta_3, the differential rate of change between groups over time; session number was grand-mean-centered to improve interpretability and reduce collinearity.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess whether the interaction reflected trajectory differences rather than the baseline imbalance, we ran four sensitivity analyses, all retaining the random-intercept structure of the primary model. (1) We refitted each model adding baseline Fugl-Meyer Assessment Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) as a covariate. (2) We refitted each model adding the subject's baseline (session-0) DAR or TAR as a covariate, with the baseline session removed from the outcome to avoid a mechanical baseline-to-outcome dependence. (3) We conducted a change-score ANCOVA defined as the endpoint-minus-baseline ratio regressed on group, baseline ratio and baseline FMA-UE, in which the baseline-ratio coefficient directly indexes regression to the mean. (4) As a check on the random-effects specification, we refitted the interaction model with a subject-specific random slope for the session in addition to the random intercept. All models were additionally refitted on log-transformed ratios, and correlational analyses were repeated with Spearman rank statistics.

Outliers

Subjects whose entire session-mean trajectory deviated by more than 3 standard deviations from their treatment-group mean were to be excluded. This rule was not pre-specified, and applying it flagged no subjects; all 21 participants were retained in the analysis. Multiple comparisons were controlled with the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). The primary family comprised the full-cohort DAR and TAR session-by-treatment interactions in the ipsilesional and contralesional hemispheres; FDR was applied within this family and statistical significance was defined as FDR-corrected p < 0.05. Correlational analyses were FDR-corrected separately across the correlation family. Interaction effect sizes were quantified using Cohen's d approximation (β / residual standard deviation).

Correlations with functional outcomes

To validate the clinical relevance of the spectral biomarkers, correlations between biomarker trajectory characteristics and clinical motor outcomes were examined. For each biomarker, we computed baseline values, endpoint values, mean values across sessions, trajectory slopes (rate of change), and absolute change from baseline to endpoint. These trajectory metrics were correlated with clinical outcomes including the FMA-UE score and change in FMA-UE from baseline to end of study. Both Pearson product-moment correlations and Spearman rank correlations were calculated to assess linear and monotonic relationships, respectively. Correlations were computed separately for the combined cohort and stratified by treatment group.

Results

Delta-to-alpha ratio

LMM analysis revealed a statistically significant session-by-treatment interaction for ipsilesional DAR (β = −0.29, 95% CI [−0.51, −0.08]; p = 0.016). The active group demonstrated a steeper decline in DAR over the 15 sessions compared to the sham group (Fig. 2). This steeper decline is consistent with a shift away from pathological slow-wave dominance. Theta-to-alpha ratio

A statistically significant session-by-treatment interaction was also observed for ipsilesional TAR (β = −0.034, CI [−0.052, −0.016]; p < 0.001). The active group's TAR values decreased over time in a manner distinct from the sham group (Fig. 3). Contralesional ratios

In the contralesional hemisphere the interaction was significant for TAR (β = −0.024, 95% CI −0.043 to −0.004; FDR-corrected p = 0.022) but not for DAR (β = −0.12, 95% CI −0.35 to 0.11; p = 0.30). The ipsilesional hemisphere therefore carried the more consistent DAR effect, whereas TAR changes were present bilaterally.

Sensitivity analysis

Baseline DAR values differed between groups (active: 9.5; sham: 3.7), a substantial imbalance given the sample size. The mixed model separates subject-specific intercepts from slopes, but this does not rule out confounding by baseline severity. We therefore tested the interaction under baseline adjustment. The Session × Treatment interaction for DAR and TAR was preserved after (i) adjustment for baseline FMA-UE (DAR β = −0.28, p = 0.003; TAR β = −0.032, p < 0.001), (ii) additionally adjusting for each subject's baseline DAR or TAR (DAR β = −0.26, p = 0.016; TAR p < 0.001), and (iii) log-transformation of the ratios (DAR β = −0.05, p < 0.001; TAR β = −0.022, p = 0.001); the last reducing ratio skewness from 1.2 to 1.8 to near zero. Adding a subject-specific random slope for the session left the point estimates unchanged but inflated their standard errors: the DAR interaction became non-significant (p = 0.81) whereas the TAR interaction remained significant (p = 0.004). A cruder endpoint-minus-baseline analysis was, as expected, less powerful: after baseline adjustment it showed no independent group effect on total change (treatment p = 0.21) and a regression-to-the-mean effect (baseline-DAR coefficient p = 0.010), indicating that the baseline imbalance inflates the group difference but does not create it. In summary, the longitudinal group difference is robust to adjustment for baseline clinical severity, baseline DAR, and to distributional assumptions under the primary random-intercept model, while remaining modestly inflated by regression to the mean and sensitive to the random-effects specification.

Correlation with functional outcomes

A steeper DAR trajectory was associated with greater FMA-UE gain (Pearson r = −0.55, p = 0.010; Spearman r = −0.66, p = 0.001). This concurrent association attenuated to a non-significant trend after partialling out baseline DAR and baseline FMA-UE (partial r = −0.38, p = 0.087), indicating that it is partly accounted for by baseline severity. The active group achieved a mean FMA-UE improvement of 31.5 ± [10.7] points over the study period compared to 23.1 ± [14.1] points in the sham group; both gains substantially exceed the FMA-UE minimal clinically important difference for subacute stroke of 9–10 points (Arya et al., 2011), indicating clinically meaningful recovery in both arms.

Connectivity analysis

Exploratory coherence-based connectivity analyses (see Methods) did not reveal robust treatment-related effects. Across the alpha and beta bands and across the graph-theoretic and motor-cortex seed measures, both groups showed stable or declining coherence trajectories, and apparent baseline group differences were not robust to the removal of individual subjects.

Discussion

This exploratory analysis provides longitudinal evidence that DAR and TAR follow distinct trajectories by treatment assignment across an intensive rehabilitation period. The findings are consistent with both DAR and TAR acting as candidate treatment-response markers in post-stroke rehabilitation (Cramer et al., 2011; Grefkes and Ward, 2014). We note that although DAR is the better-established index historically, here the TAR interaction was the more robust to model specification. TAR survived the addition of a subject-specific random slope and reached significance in both hemispheres, whereas the DAR interaction, though larger in absolute magnitude, was more sensitive to these choices. The significant session-by-treatment interactions indicate that active ENTF stimulation was associated with a steeper longitudinal decline in both ratios than sham.

The simplicity, reproducibility, and interpretability of DAR and TAR are practically attractive (Delcamp et al., 2024; Erani et al., 2020): unlike connectivity analyses, spectral ratios can be computed from minimal electrode configurations with straightforward signal processing and yield intuitive metrics (Ajčević et al., 2021). These properties motivate their further evaluation as candidate monitoring markers. The present exploratory data cannot, however, establish clinical utility, and any role in routine monitoring or in personalising therapy intensity would require prospective, adequately powered validation (Cervera et al., 2018; Pichiorri et al., 2015).

The observed reduction in DAR and TAR in the active group aligns with established theories of neurological recovery post-stroke (Saes et al., 2020). Following stroke, cortical activity shifts toward low-frequency oscillations, reflecting diaschisis and impaired network function (Carrera and Tononi, 2014). Recovery is characterized by rebalancing toward higher-frequency activity, indicating restoration of normal cortical dynamics (Finnigan and van Putten, 2013; Giaquinto et al., 1994; Boyd et al., 2017). The association between biomarker change and FMA-UE improvement in both treatment groups is consistent with DAR, and less strongly TAR, indexing processes related to motor recovery, though these associations rely on small-N. Their presence in both arms is compatible with DAR tracking recovery irrespective of its driver (Blum et al., 2022). The group difference manifested as a steeper longitudinal decline in the active arm, an association we interpret cautiously in light of the baseline DAR imbalance.

The exploratory coherence analyses did not show treatment-related increases in functional connectivity (Nicoló et al., 2015; Cassidy et al., 2022). Given the low-density montage and the absence of volume-conduction correction, we do not draw mechanistic conclusions from these analyses. Whether network-level changes accompany the spectral findings is a question for larger cohorts using high-density, source-localized EEG stratified by lesion location (Cassidy et al., 2021; Michel and Brunet, 2019; Luu et al., 2001).

The longitudinal density of our dataset, 15 sessions over 2 months, represents a significant strength, enabling characterization of treatment-specific trajectories with temporal resolution rarely published in prior stroke biomarker studies. This density supports the use of these biomarkers for ongoing treatment monitoring rather than single-timepoint assessments.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. The sample size (N = 21) limits statistical power for detecting smaller effects and precludes robust subgroup analyses. Moreover, the active group had substantially higher baseline DAR and higher baseline FMA-UE than the sham group. Because more abnormal baseline values leave more room to change, regression to the mean is a plausible partial contributor to the group difference in DAR trajectory. However, the longitudinal Session×Treatment difference remained significant after explicit adjustment for baseline DAR and baseline FMA-UE and after log-transformation, indicating that the baseline imbalance inflates, but does not create the effect.

The EEG baseline was not recorded before randomization; rather, data derived from sessions that began after treatment assignment; this design prevents assessment of pre-treatment connectivity states. The limited electrode montage (8 channels) may have missed relevant network changes detectable with high-density EEG (Michel and Brunet, 2019; Luu et al., 2001). Finally, the absence of structural imaging data precluded lesion-based analyses that could help explain interindividual variability. The exploratory connectivity analyses should be interpreted cautiously given the low-density scalp montage utilized and the lack of pre-specified hypotheses.

Conclusion

This study shows that DAR and TAR follow longitudinal changes that differ by treatment assignment and are concurrently associated with motor improvement, making them candidate treatment-response markers in subacute stroke rehabilitation. Confirmation requires a prospectively powered trial with pre-specified EEG endpoints.
