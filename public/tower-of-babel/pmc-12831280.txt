Evaluation of the bond strength of glass ionomer cement modified with fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles to caries-affected dentin
Authors: Hanife Altınışık, Merve Nezir, Hülya Erten Can, Necibe Başaran Mutlu Ağardan, Aysel Berkkan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the shear bond strength (SBS) of glass ionomer cement (GIC) modified with fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles when applied to both sound dentin and caries-affected dentin (CAD).MethodsSixty human molar teeth were randomly allocated into two main groups based on dentin condition: sound dentin and CAD. Each group was further divided into three subgroups, containing 10 specimens each, as follows: conventional GIC, chitosan-modified GIC (CH-GIC), and fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticle-modified GIC (NCH-GIC). The occlusal surfaces of the teeth were ground to expose dentin, creating a 3 × 3 mm dentin window. For subgroups 1, 2, and 3, the dentin surfaces remained untreated. In subgroups 4, 5, and 6, the dentin was subjected to a demineralization solution for 66 h to simulate artificial carious lesions. Subsequently, the conventional and modified GICs were applied to the dentin surfaces, and SBS measurements were conducted. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA, Tukey’s post hoc test, and Paired samples t-test.ResultsNo significant differences in bond strength to sound dentin were found among the groups (p > 0.05). However, bond strengths to CAD varied significantly (p < 0.05) and the NCH-GIC showed notably higher SBS than conventional GIC (p < 0.05). Conventional GIC had significantly lower shear bond to CAD compared to sound dentin (p < 0.05) while the modified GICs showed no significant bond strength difference between the two dentin types (p > 0.05).ConclusionsIncorporating fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles into glass ionomer cement significantly improved its bond strength to caries-affected dentin while maintaining comparable adhesion to sound dentin.

Background

Tooth caries is one of the most prevalent infectious diseases worldwide [1]. In recent years, advancements in restorative materials and a deeper understanding of caries pathogenesis have supported the adoption of minimally invasive dental approaches. This philosophy emphasizes the preservation of sound tooth structure by removing as little tissue as possible while avoiding unnecessary damage to adjacent healthy areas [2]. Carious dentin lesions are composed of two distinct layers: the outer infected dentin and the inner affected dentin. These layers exhibit significant differences in their ultramicroscopic, morphological, biochemical, bacteriological, and physiological characteristics. The outer caries-infected layer is heavily contaminated with bacteria, structurally disorganized, lacks remineralization potential, and therefore must be completely removed during cavity preparation. In contrast, caries-affected dentin (CAD) is partially demineralized, exhibits increased porosity compared to sound dentin, and contains limited collagen degradation. Importantly, this layer retains the ability to undergo remineralization [3]. Contemporary preventive and minimally invasive dentistry encourages the selective removal of only the outer infected dentin while preserving the remineralizable affected dentin layer [4, 5]. As a result, the demand for biomimetic remineralizing agents and adhesive restorative materials capable of effectively bonding to the remaining CAD has increased substantially [2].

CAD presents a challenging substrate for adhesion because of the substantial biochemical and structural alterations induced by the caries process [6]. Previous studies have demonstrated that CAD exhibits markedly lower bond strength compared with sound dentin, largely because its dentinal tubules become partially or completely obstructed by acid-resistant mineral crystals. In addition, CAD possesses approximately half the hardness of sound dentin and displays increased porosity resulting from extensive mineral loss. These changes weaken the micromechanical interlocking potential of adhesive systems and compromise the durability of the resin–dentin interface. For these reasons, it is thought that bonding can be increased by remineralizing the CAD [2, 7–9].

Glass ionomer cements (GICs) are commonly used restorative materials, particularly for patients at high risk of caries [10]. It has advantages such as chemical bonding to tooth structures [6, 11], reducing demineralization by releasing fluoride ions, increasing remineralization, and inhibiting microbial growth [12–14]. However, some studies indicate that the antibacterial efficacy of fluoride released from GICs is limited [15] and insufficient to prevent secondary caries development [16]. For this reason, many laboratory studies have focused on enhancing the fluoride-releasing capacity of GICs by incorporating polymeric biomaterials—such as chitosan—without affecting the clinical advantages of GIC [17–20].

Chitosan is a natural polymer obtained by alkaline N-deacetylation of chitin, which is the main component of the shells of crustaceans such as lobsters, crabs, shrimps, and the cell walls of some fungi [17, 21]. It is biodegradable, non-toxic, and biocompatible, and exhibits antifungal, antibacterial, anti-plaque, anti-protozoal, anti-tartar, anti-cancer, and hemostatic properties [18, 19]. It is biodegradable, non-toxic, and biocompatible, and exhibits antifungal, antibacterial, anti-plaque, anti-protozoal, anti-tartar, anti-cancer, and hemostatic properties [19]. Chitosan particles may reduce interfacial surface stress and improve interaction among components within the GIC matrix [20].

With the combined properties of chitosan and nanomaterials, chitosan nanoparticles offer additional advantages due to their increased surface area and load density, enabling better interaction with their environment [18]. Nanosized chitosan formulations are widely used as drug delivery agents in the field of pharmacology [22]. In dentistry, chitosan nanoparticles can be utilized to achieve controlled, low-concentration yet sustained fluoride release [23].

Previous studies conducted by our research group demonstrated that fluoride-enriched chitosan nanoparticles significantly increase the fluoride release capacity of glass ionomer cements (GICs), thereby enhancing their potential to protect against secondary caries [24]. Given these promising findings, it is reasonable to hypothesize that incorporating fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles into GICs may also improve their adhesion to caries-affected dentin (CAD), a substrate known for its compromised bonding characteristics. Accordingly, the present study aimed to investigate the shear bond strength of GICs modified with fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles when applied to CAD. The null hypothesis tested was that the incorporation of chitosan or fluoride-loaded nanochitosan particles into GIC powder would not significantly influence the shear bond strength to CAD compared with conventional GIC.

Materials and methods

Formulation of glass ionomer powder modified with chitosan and fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles

The preparation, characterization, and fluoride-release behavior of fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles were thoroughly described in our previous study [24]. Low–molecular-weight chitosan with an 85% degree of deacetylation and sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). Chitosan solution was prepared by dissolving chitosan in 0.5% acetic acid to obtain a final concentration of 0.5% (w/v). The mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature at 500 rpm to ensure complete dissolution [25].

Chitosan nanoparticles were synthesized via the ionic gelation technique using TPP as the anionic crosslinker [26]. Based on our previous work, a chitosan: TPP mass ratio of 2:1 was selected [25]. For the preparation of fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles, 5 mg NaF was added to the chitosan solution and stirred for 10 min prior to TPP addition. After this period, the TPP solution was added dropwise under continuous stirring for 30 min, maintaining a constant stirring speed of 900 rpm throughout nanoparticle formation. The resulting suspensions were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min to isolate the nanoparticles. To prevent fluoride adsorption onto glass surfaces, all procedures were conducted using polypropylene labware [27]. The collected nanoparticles were then dried at room temperature and ground into a fine powder for further use.

Petri et al. [20] demonstrated that the incorporation of 10% (w/w) chitosan into glass ionomer cement (GIC) enhances its flexural strength. Guided by this finding, the present study incorporated either chitosan or fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles into GIC powder at a concentration of 10% (w/w). The modified GIC powders were blended using a vibratory mixer for 10 min to ensure homogeneous distribution of the additives. Subsequently, the powders were mixed with the liquid component using a plastic spatula, following the manufacturer’s recommended powder-to-liquid ratio (3.6/1.0 g/ml).

Specimen preparation and shear bond strength test

Power analysis performed using G*Power software (Version 3.1, Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) indicated that a total sample size of 60 teeth was required for six experimental groups (effect size = 0.50; α = 0.05; power = 0.95). Consistent with previous study by Elizabeth et al. [28] 10 specimens were assigned to each group.

Sixty non-carious human third molars were collected in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Gazi University Faculty of Dentistry Clinical Research Ethics Committee (ethical protocol no. 2023.05/1). Residual connective tissues were removed from the tooth surfaces using hand scalers, and the teeth were stored in 0.1% thymol solution at 4 °C until preparation. The occlusal enamel was removed just below the dentino-enamel junction using a precision cutting machine (Micracut 201, Metkon, Bursa, Turkey) under continuous water cooling to obtain a flat dentin surface. The roots were then embedded in self-curing acrylic resin (ProBase Cold, Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Liechtenstein) within polyvinyl chloride cylinders. The exposed dentin surfaces were polished with microfine 2000-grit silicon carbide sandpaper under running water to obtain a smooth and standardized surface. Subsequently, nail varnish was applied to the specimen surfaces, leaving a standardized dentin window measuring 3 × 3 mm. Flow chart of the study

The specimens were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 10). No surface treatment was applied to the dentin in Groups 1, 2, and 3. In Groups 4, 5, and 6, artificial caries lesions were created by immersing the dentin surfaces in a demineralization solution for 66 h. The demineralization solution was prepared as previously described by buffering a solution containing 2.2 mM calcium and 2.2 mM phosphate with 0.05 M acetic acid to achieve a pH of 5 [29]. According to one research, this procedure produces an artificial lesion that is about 140 microns deep and yields a repeatable smooth demineralized zone that satisfies the requirements needed to quantify SBS [29]. Therefore, we followed this established protocol to obtain standardized artificial lesions in this study.

Cavity Conditioner (GC Corp., Tokyo, Japan) was applied to the 3 mm x 3 mm windows created on the dentin surfaces with a micro brush for 10 s and rinsed thoroughly. Then, the conventional and modified GICs were placed on the dentin surface using a Tygon tubes (2 mm internal diameter × 3 mm height). All specimens were stored at 37 °C and 100% humidity for 24 h to ensure complete setting before shear bond strength (SBS) testing. After removal of the Tygon tubes with a scalpel, SBS testing was performed at a speed of 1 mm/min using a universal testing device (Shimadzu IG-IS, Kyoto, Japan). The obtained Newton values were converted to megapascal (MPa) values by calculating the surface area of the samples and the obtained data were recorded for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of the data in each group was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data exhibited a normal distribution, comparisons among the groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Paired samples t-tests were used to compare bond strength values between sound dentin and caries-affected dentin within each group. A significance level of P < 0.05 was adopted for all statistical evaluations.

Results

The SBS values (MPa) of the tested groups are summarized in Table 2. The SBS values of the groups to sound dentin are 5.80 ± 1.72 Mega Pascals (MPa), 5.09 ± 2.72 MPa, and 6.14 ± 2.32 MPa for the C-GIC, CH-GIC, and NCH-GIC groups, respectively. There was no statistically significant difference among the groups in terms of bond strength to sound dentin (p = 0.587).

In contrast, the SBS values for CAD differed significantly among the groups (p = 0.013, p < 0.05). NCH-GIC demonstrated a significantly higher SBS compared with the conventional GIC (p < 0.05). Although the SBS values of CH-GIC and NCH-GIC did not differ statistically, NCH-GIC showed slightly higher mean values (p > 0.05).

There was no statistically significant difference between the SBS values of CH-GIC and NCH-GIC when bonded to sound dentin versus CAD (p = 0.535 and p = 0.563, respectively). Only the conventional GIC exhibited significantly lower bond strength to CAD compared with sound dentin (p = 0.003, p < 0.05).

Discussion

In the present study, only the conventional GIC exhibited significantly lower bond strength to CAD compared with sound dentin. Therefore, the first null hypothesis—predicting no difference in bond strength among the materials tested on CAD—was rejected. This finding is compatible with the findings of previous studies in which reduced SBS values were observed in the CAD compared to sound dentin [2, 29, 30]. Because GIC adhesion relies on ionic and polar interactions between polycarboxylate chains and hydroxyapatite, the presence of acid-resistant mineral deposits and reduced mechanical integrity in CAD directly weaken this chemical interaction [29, 31]. These substrate limitations highlight the importance of biomodification strategies to stabilize the demineralized dentin matrix and improve bonding durability [32]. In this study, modifications of GIC with chitosan and fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles were used to provide dentin biomodification.

Chitosan and chitosan nanoparticles play an important mechanistic role in modifying the chemical and structural behavior of GICs. These additives may enhance fluoride release by exerting a catalytic effect on the acid–base reaction within the GIC matrix [33]. Consistent with this mechanism, our previous study demonstrated that both CH-GIC and NCH-GIC released significantly higher cumulative fluoride levels than the conventional GIC [24], a finding in agreement with prior research [18, 33]. Petri et al. [20] found that the addition of chitosan to GIC significantly enhanced its flexural strength while also catalyzes the release of fluoride ions from the GIC material. This accelerated diffusion process is believed to be due to the formation of polyacrylic acid networks surrounding the inorganic particles, which helps reduce the interfacial tension between the various components of the GIC [33].

A study using 10% carboxymethyl chitosan (CMC) to modify GIC provides additional evidence supporting the benefits of chitosan derivatives. The CMC-modified GIC demonstrated greater calcium deposition and a higher Ca/P ratio, indicating enhanced hydroxyapatite formation [34]. These findings suggest that chitosan-based additives promote dentin biomineralization by altering chemical interactions within the GIC matrix, supporting our interpretation that increased fluoride release from CH-GIC—and particularly from NCH-GIC—may enhance bonding to CAD through interfacial remineralization.

Chitosan nanoparticles further enhance these effects due to their high surface area, improved solubility, and capacity for sustained ion release—properties that have made them widely used in drug-delivery systems [35]. Nyugen et al. [23] showed that fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles release fluoride continuously at low concentrations, and our previous findings similarly demonstrated that NCH-GIC provided the highest sustained fluoride release over 28 days [24].

These mechanisms help explain the improved bonding behavior of the CH-GIC and NCH-GIC groups to CAD. While bond strength values to sound dentin did not differ significantly among materials, CH-GIC exhibited slightly higher—but statistically nonsignificant—bond strength to CAD compared with conventional GIC, and NCH-GIC demonstrated a statistically significant improvement. This enhancement may stem from sustained fluoride release from NCH-GIC, which may promote remineralization at the demineralized dentin–GIC interface and improving mechanical and ionic bonding capacity. This study is the first to evaluate the bonding performance of nanochitosan-modified GIC on both sound and caries-affected dentin [24]. This study is the first to evaluate the bonding performance of nanochitosan-modified GIC on both sound and CAD.

There are limited studies evaluating the bond strength of CH-GIC to enamel and sound dentin, while there are no studies reporting the bond strength to CAD. Debnath et al. [19] reported improved enamel adhesion when chitosan was added to the liquid phase of a conventional GIC, whereas Khudhair et al. [36] found reduced dentin adhesion for RMGIC modified with 10% v/v chitosan. Such variability likely results from differences in study designs, GIC formulations, chitosan concentrations, and whether chitosan is incorporated into the powder or liquid phase. The present study contributes novel evidence regarding CAD-specific bonding behavior for both chitosan and nanochitosan modifications.

A limitation of the present study is that it was conducted in a controlled laboratory environment in which dentin was artificially demineralized to simulate CAD. However, natural CAD exhibits a more complex and heterogeneous microstructure, with residual mineral crystals that influence dentinal permeability and fluid movement within the tubules [37]. Therefore, artificial and natural CAD substrates may not behave identically, potentially affecting adhesive performance. Additionally, the lesion depth of the artificially demineralized dentin was not directly measured for each specimen; instead, a previously validated protocol known to produce lesions of approximately 140 μm was used. Future studies should incorporate direct lesion-depth measurements—such as transverse microradiography or cross-sectional microhardness—to verify lesion uniformity and better reflect clinical conditions.

Another limitation of the present study is that bond durability and fracture strength were not evaluated. The investigation focused solely on immediate shear bond strength, whereas long-term performance under aging conditions (e.g., thermocycling or prolonged water storage) may differ significantly. Future studies should include durability testing and fracture strength assessments to provide a more clinically relevant understanding of the long-term behavior of chitosan-modified GICs.

Furthermore, fracture type (failure mode) analysis after debonding was not performed. Because adhesive, cohesive, and mixed failures were not examined using stereomicroscopy or SEM, the underlying failure mechanisms could not be fully interpreted. Incorporating failure mode evaluation in future studies would yield a deeper understanding of how chitosan-based modifications influence interfacial failure patterns.

Moreover, the distribution of nano-chitosan particles within the GIC matrix was not evaluated using transmission electron microscopy. Although the nanoparticles had been previously characterized, direct TEM imaging of the modified GIC would provide clearer insight into nanoparticle–matrix interactions and confirm homogeneous dispersion.

A further limitation is that the setting and working times of the experimental GIC formulations were not quantitatively measured. Although polymeric additives may alter the acid–base reaction between fluoroaluminosilicate glass and poly(acrylic acid) [34], no observable change in handling characteristics was noted during this study. Nevertheless, standardized setting-time measurements and early mechanical testing are recommended for future investigations. Finally, as laboratory conditions differ substantially from the oral environment, it remains uncertain how modified GICs would perform after prolonged intraoral exposure; therefore, caution is advised when extrapolating these results to clinical practice.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this laboratory study, incorporating fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles into glass ionomer cement significantly improved its bond strength to caries-affected dentin while maintaining comparable adhesion to sound dentin.
