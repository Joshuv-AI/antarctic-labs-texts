Conditioned medium from the inflammatory microenvironment of human dental pulp stem cells regulated functional pulp regeneration
Authors: Qian Zhang, Hengwei Zhang, Linting Wei, Bin Yang, Xiaofan Bai, Jianping Ruan, Ning Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study explored the effects of inflammatory microenvironment on stem cells from the apical papilla (SCAP) for cell homing strategy-based pulp regeneration.MethodsDental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 48 h, creating a conditioned medium (LPS-CM). The influence of LPS-CM on SCAP proliferation, migration, odontogenic and neurogenic differentiation, pro-angiogenetic effects, cell apoptosis and senescence were assessed. Following construction of the ectopic pulp regeneration model, treated dentin matrix (TDM) specimens were harvested after a 2-month implantation period and subjected to histological examination to assess changes in the regenerated tissues.ResultsWe found that a moderate inflammatory microenvironment (LPS-5 CM) significantly enhanced SCAP proliferation, migration, odontogenic differentiation, and the formation of neuron-like cells. In contrast, a high-inflammatory microenvironment (LPS-10 CM) exerted inhibitory effects on these processes and concurrently induced cellular apoptosis and senescence. All LPS-CM groups promoted angiogenesis in vitro. Critically, only the LPS-5 CM group successfully facilitated the regeneration of well-vascularized pulp-like tissue in vivo. ConclusionsInflammatory microenvironment performed a dual role in pulp regeneration. A moderate inflammatory stimulus enhances the regenerative functions of SCAP, while excessive inflammation is detrimental. This underscores the importance of inflammatory signals for successful cell homing-based pulp regeneration.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08563-5.Keywords: Pulp regeneration, Inflammatory microenvironment, Conditioned medium, SCAP, Cell homing

Introduction

Regenerative endodontic therapy (RET) is a biologically-based treatment aimed at restoring damaged dental structures [1]. According to this definition, the ultimate goal of RET is to achieve “developmental” regeneration of the pulp-dentin complex [2]. Its primary objective is to regenerate functional pulp tissue that closely resembles natural structures, thereby ensuring the pulp can perform its defensive functions. The regenerated tissue should contain abundant blood vessels and exhibit cell density and nerve distribution similar to that of natural pulp, providing essential nutrition and sensory functions [3, 4]. RET also facilitates the formation of new odontoblasts, which help secrete dentin and enable dentin deposition or resorption within the tooth. Especially the function of the pulp and guide the continued development of the root of young permanent teeth [4]. Regenerative endodontic therapy, a paradigm shift from conventional root canal therapy, relies on the foundational triad of tissue engineering: seed cells, bioactive factors, and biomaterial scaffolds [5, 6]. The strategy employs either the transplantation of relevant seed cells, such as DPSCs or SCAP, or the recruitment of endogenous cells via cell homing [7]. These cells are then orchestrated by a cocktail of bioactive factors, including angiogenic VEGF and osteo/odontogenic BMPs, to direct cell fate towards pulp-dentin complex formation. These components are delivered within a three-dimensional biomaterial scaffold, often a natural polymer (e.g., collagen, fibrin) or an injectable synthetic hydrogel [5], which provides structural support for cell proliferation and tissue ingrowth while enabling the controlled release of signaling molecules. The integration of these three elements is critical for regenerating a functional, vascularized, and innervated tissue, with current research focused on optimizing their synergy to overcome challenges in clinical translation, particularly in mature teeth with closed apices.

The process of tissue regeneration is primarily driven by the interplay between mesenchymal stem cells and their surrounding microenvironment [8]. The microenvironment of dental pulp plays a critical role in preserving the stem cell phenotype and its differentiation potential [9, 10]. This microenvironment encompasses soluble substances, extracellular matrix components, and neighboring cells, each of which significantly influences cell behavior [9, 11]. Thus, strategies that replicate this natural environment are essential for advancing research in dental pulp regeneration. Inflammation serves as a crucial precursor to the healing process [12, 13]. Abuarqoub [14] emphasized that inflammation is a necessary precursor for the activation of DPSCs, which are vital for the regeneration of the dentin-pulp complex. Similarly, Goldberg [15] pointed out that moderate inflammation is not only associated with the presence of bacteria but also integral to the body’s natural repair mechanisms, facilitating the activation and differentiation of stem cells. Crucially, the regenerative response of DPSCs is deeply intertwined with their immunomodulatory capacity. As members of the mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) family, DPSCs possess remarkable functional plasticity that is ‘licensed’ by the inflammatory microenvironment [16]. The nature and intensity of inflammatory signals can steer DPSCs towards either a pro-regenerative or an immunosuppressive phenotype, thereby finely tuning the repair process. Recent evidence further demonstrates that DPSCs actively modulate the local immune environment; for instance, in a pro-inflammatory setting, they can influence inflammasome pathway activation and extracellular matrix remodeling in neighboring fibroblasts [17, 18]. This crosstalk underscores that DPSCs are not merely passive targets of inflammation but are active regulators that help resolve inflammation and create a conducive environment for pulp regeneration.

Therefore, this study aims to explore the effect of inflammatory microenvironment on pulp regeneration. We will collect conditioned supernatant to simulate this environment and utilize SCAP for dental pulp regeneration. Our focus will be on the essential components of RET, specifically vascularization, innervation, and dentin deposition, to better understand the relationship between inflammation and dental pulp regeneration through in vitro experiments.

Materials and methods

Cell isolation, culture, and identification

All human procedures complied with guidelines of the Ethics Committee, College of Stomatology, Xi’an Jiaotong University (No. 2024-XJKQIEC-GXB-0003). DPSCs were isolated from orthodontically extracted premolars (12–15 years, n = 6), SCAP were collected from normal human impacted third molars with immature roots (15–20 years, n = 6). Pulp/apical papilla tissues were separated and digested with 3 mg/ml collagenase type I (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C for 45 min, then cultured in α-MEM (HyClone, USA) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 100 U/ml penicillin–streptomycin (Solarbio, China) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Nonadherent cells were removed after 3–4 days. DPSCs and SCAP from passages P3–P5 were used. Cell surface markers (CD34, CD45, CD90, CD105; Biolegend, China) were analyzed by flow cytometry (Novocyte, USA). Cells were induced for 14 days in osteogenic medium (containing 50 µM ascorbic acid, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 100 nM dexamethasone; all Sigma-Aldrich) and assessed by Alizarin red staining (Beyotime, China). Cells were induced for 21 days using adipogenic medium (Haixing Biosciences, China) and lipid droplets visualized by Oil Red O staining (Haixing Biosciences, China).

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) and EdU staining

P3 to P5 passage logarithmic growth phase DPSCs were selected, trypsin routinely digested and resuspended with appropriate α-MEM, DPSCs were seeded into 96-well plates at 7000 cells/well. The culture medium was replaced with 1.0 µg/mL, 5.0 µg/mL, and 10 µg/mL LPS (Solarbio, China) for 48 h. 10 µL CCK-8 (FD biology, China) was added to each well and incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h. The absorbance at the wavelength of 450 nm was detected by a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). According to the instructions of the EdU staining kit, cells were pulsed with 10 µM EdU and incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-azide Click Additive Solution for 30 min respectively. Images were fixed and analyzed under confocal microscopy (Olympus, FV3000). Quantification of EdU-positive cells was performed using Image J software (Version 1.53, USA) with thresholds set based on negative controls.

qRT-PCR

RNA isolation was conducted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The quality and quantity of the isolated RNA were tested using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (DENOVIX, USA). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed on 1000 ng of total RNA. qRT-PCR amplifications were carried out in 20 µL reaction mixture using 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA) with an initial denaturation of 10 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s. The relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH. The primers were used in this study: GAPDH: 5’-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3’ and 5’- GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3’; IL-6: 5’- GTCTTGGCCGAGGACTAAGG-3’ and 5’- TACTCGGCAAACCTAGTGCG-3’; TNF-α: 5’- GTGTCCCAACATTCATATTGTCAGT-3’ and 5’- TGGGAAGAGAAACCAGGGAGA-3’. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with the control group as the calibrator.

Preparation of LPS Conditioned-medium (LPS-CM)

DPSCs were grown until 70–80% confluence. The culture medium was replaced with 1.0 µg/mL, 5.0 µg/mL, and 10 µg/mL LPS and incubated at 37 ℃ in 5% CO2 for 48 h. Then washed with PBS and incubated with freshly α-MEM for at least 48 h. The inflamed DPSCs-cultured media was collected and centrifuged at 2000 g for 5 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant was filtered through 0.2 μm filters (Millipore, USA), and conditioned media groups were defined according to LPS concentration as LPS-0 CM, LPS-1 CM, LPS-5 CM, and LPS-10 CM, stored at -20 ℃.

Wound healing assay and cell migration assay

Cells were seeded into 12-well culture plates (Corning, USA) at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. A sterile 200 µL pipette tip was vertically pressed against the cell monolayer, and three parallel linear scratches were created using a ruler as a guide. Dislodged cells were removed by gently washing the monolayer three times with PBS (Service, China), replaced media with LPS-CM. Wound closure dynamics were monitored at defined intervals (0, 24 h post-scratching). Images were captured at identical coordinates for each time point. Image J software (Version 1.53, USA) used to calculate scratch healing rate. The migration of SCAP was assessed utilizing Transwell inserts (Corning, USA) featuring 8 μm pores. A suspension of 1 × 10⁴ cells in 200 µL of α-MEM was introduced into the upper chamber, while LPS-CM was added to the lower chamber. The Transwell inserts were fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, China) after 24 h, Stained cell images were captured and analyzed via Image J software (Version 1.53, USA).

Tube formation assay

Matrigel matrix substrate gel (Corning, USA) was thawed at 4°C overnight, then 50 µL Matrigel was added to 96-well plates, which were then placed in a 37 ℃ incubator for 30 min to solidify the gel. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) were pre-treated with LPS-CM, then HUVEC was seeded at a density of 1.2 × 104 cells/well and incubated in a serum-free medium with LPS-CM. Tube formation was monitored at 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, and 8 h using a Cytation™5 Cell Imaging Multi-Mode Reader (Biotech, Winooski, VT, USA). The data was determined with Image J software (Version 1.53, USA) via the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin. Key parameters included: total length, branches, meshes, junctions.

Alkaline phosphatase and alizarin red staining

SCAP was seeded in a 12-well plate and cultured in an osteogenic medium (100 nM dexamethasone, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 5 mM I-glycerophosphate) with LPS-CM. The medium was changed every three days. Then, the cells were stained with ALP (Beyotime, China) and ARS (Beyotime, China), followed by a final PBS wash. Images were captured by the camera in natural light. The stained areas were quantitatively analyzed using Image J software (Version 1.53, USA) through standardized image processing protocols.

Neurogenic differentiation

The neurogenic differentiation method was referred to in Rajabi’s review [19]. Briefly, SCAP was cultured in induction media containing 10 ng/mL FGF (MCE, China) for 24 h, then cells were cultured in neurogenic medium containing LPS-CM, 200 µM butylated hydroxyanisole (Aladdin, China), 25 mM KCL (Aladdin, China), 2.0 mM valproic acid (Aladdin, China), 10 µM forskolin (Aladdin, China), 1.0 µM hydrocortisone (Aladdin, China), 5 µg/mL insulin (Beyotime, China), and 2.0 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells grown on glass-bottom dishes (NEST, China) were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min at 25 °C, followed by permeabilization using 0.3% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, China) for 10 min. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 5% BSA (Solarbio, China) in PBS for 1 h at 25 °C. Primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer were applied overnight at 4 °C: Nestin (Abways, China), P53(CST, USA). Species-matched secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Proteintech, China) were incubated for 2 h at 25 °C under light-protected conditions. Images were fixed and analyzed under confocal microscopy (Olympus, FV3000).

Western blot

Cellular proteins were isolated using ice-cold RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China), lysates were collected and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were quantified via BCA assay (BOSTER, China). Equal protein aliquots (20 µg) underwent electrophoretic separation through 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), followed by semi-dry transfer onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST (Solarbio, China) for 2 h at 25 °C, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: GAPDH (diluted 1:10000, Proteintech, China), DSPP (diluted 1:500, Santa Cruz, USA), DMP1 (diluted 1:500, Santa Cruz, USA), RUNX2 (diluted 1:500, Santa Cruz, USA), p21(diluted 1:1000, CST, USA), p53(diluted 1:1000, CST, USA). After TBST washes, membranes were probed with species-matched HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted 1:10000, BOSTER, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using ECL substrate (FD Bio, China) and quantified via Image Lab™ Software (Bio-Rad, USA).

Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase (SA β-gal) staining

Cell senescence was assessed using SA β-gal staining kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, SCAP was seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well, the culture media was changed into LPS-CM for 24 h. Then, the cells were fixed and incubated with SA-β-gal working solution (staining solution A 10 µL, staining solution B 10 µL, X-Gal solution 50 µL, staining solution C 930 µL) at 37 °C overnight. Senescent cells exhibiting blue cytoplasmic precipitates were quantified using bright-field microscopy (Leica, Japan).

Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis

Cellular apoptosis was quantified using an Annexin V-FITC/Propidium Iodide (PI) dual-staining kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, adherent cells were detached with non-enzymatic cell dissociation buffer (Gibco, USA). Cell pellets (1 × 10⁶ cells/sample) were resuspended in 100 µL binding buffer, followed by sequential incubation with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC (Beyotime, China) and 10 µg/mL PI (Beyotime, China) for 15 min at 25 °C under light-protected conditions. The flow cytometry (Novocyte, USA) was used to analyze samples.

In vivo ectopic model for pulp regeneration

Treated dentin matrix (TDM) was prepared as Li’s research [20]. TDM was maintained in sterile PBS with 100 U/ml penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and finally stored in α-MEM at 4 ℃ (Fig. 6A, B). Six-week-old Nude mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Xian Jiaotong University. Experimental procedures were approved by the Health Science Centre of Xian Jiaotong University (NO. XJTUAE2024-1979). The nude mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 3): blank (collagen + PBS), LPS-5 CM (collagen + LPS-5 CM). PBS or LPS-5 CM was encapsulated with collagen (Corning, USA) and injected into the TDM. The samples were subcutaneously injected into the left and right sides of the mice back. After eight weeks, the grafts were removed, fixed with 4% PFA (Biosharp, China), decalcified with 0.5 M EDTA (Biosharp, China), and paraffin-embedded. Sections of 5 μm were made for histological examinations.

LPS-5 CM promoted pulp-like tissue regeneration in vivo ectopic model. A, B. Schematic diagram of the TDM preparation process; C. A nude mouse model of ectopic pulp regeneration was established by subcutaneous transplantation; D. HE staining of TDM regenerated tissue. Scale bar 500μm and 50 μm, n=3

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) was utilized for statistical analyses. Continuous data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significant differences between groups were assessed through a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), the pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Fisher’s LSD test for statistical analysis between two groups. P < 0.05 *, P < 0.01 **, P < 0.001 ***, and P < 0.0001 **** were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Cell culture and identification

Human DPSCs and SCAP were successfully isolated and cultured, followed by comprehensive characterization. Both cells exhibited characteristic spindle-shaped to fibroblast-like morphology (Fig. S1A, E). Multipotent differentiation capacity was demonstrated. Positive staining for calcium deposits (Alizarin Red S; Fig. S1B, F) and lipid vacuoles (Oil Red O; Fig. S1C, G) confirmed osteogenic and adipogenic potential, respectively. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that CD90 and CD105 were positive for mesenchymal stem cell markers, whereas CD34 and CD45 were negative in DPSCs and SCAP (Fig. S1D, H). These results collectively confirm that isolated cells are mesenchymal stem cells with active proliferation and multiple differentiation potential.

To create an in vitro model of inflammatory DPSCs, DPSCs were stimulated with different doses of LPS (0, 1.0, 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 9.0, and 10.0 µg/mL) for 48 h. When the LPS concentration was lower than 5.0 µg/mL, there was no significant impact on cell proliferation. However, once it exceeded 5.0 µg/mL, the cell viability significantly decreased (Fig. S1I). The expression of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α, was also evaluated. The results showed that adding LPS greatly increased the expression levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in DPSCs (Fig. S1J). Considering both findings, the inflammatory dental pulp stem cell model was constructed using LPS concentrations of 1 µg/mL, 5 µg/mL, and 10 µg/mL.

LPS-5 CM promoted SCAP proliferation

LPS-CM at various concentrations were applied to SCAP to evaluate their effects. LPS-1 CM and LPS-5 CM significantly enhanced SCAP proliferation, while LPS-5 CM having the strongest effect (Fig. 1A). For a more precise evaluation of DNA replication and cell proliferation, the EdU-555 S kit was used. Results (Fig. 1B, C) demonstrated that the number of EdU-positive cells significantly increased with inflammatory conditioned media, peaking at 60% in the LPS-5 CM group, while the LPS-10 CM group showed a decrease in positive cell proportion compared to LPS-5 CM. In conclusion, LPS-CM significantly promotes SCAP proliferation, although excessive inflammation may inhibit this process.

LPS-5 CM promoted migration of SCAP

SCAP are odontogenic mesenchymal stem cells with notable migratory abilities, moving from the apical region to damaged areas in response to specific signals for tissue remodeling and repair. In a scratch assay after 24 h, SCAP cells migrated significantly toward the scratch center, with the LPS-5 CM group showing the highest scratch healing rate and cell migration efficiency compared to LPS-0 CM. The LPS-1 CM and LPS-10 CM groups showed no significant differences from the control group (Fig. 1D, E). Further migration experiments (Fig. 1F, G) revealed that LPS-5 CM greatly enhanced SCAP migration, while LPS-10 CM resulted in fewer migrated cells than the control group, indicating inhibition. In summary, LPS-5 CM significantly boosts SCAP migratory capacity, whereas excessive inflammation can hinder migration.

LPS-5 CM promoted odontogenic differentiation of SCAP

To investigate the effects of the inflammatory microenvironment on SCAP, we prepared various LPS-CM for dentinogenesis. The LPS-5 CM group demonstrated significantly increased protein expression levels of DSPP, DMP1, and RUNX2 compared to the control group, while no significant changes were observed in the LPS-1 CM and LPS-10 CM group (Fig. 2A-D). Furthermore, the protein levels of DSPP and RUNX2 in the LPS-10 CM group exhibited a downward trend relative to those in the LPS-5 CM group (Fig. 2A-D). The results of alkaline phosphatase and alizarin red staining revealed that the LPS-5 CM group had the highest levels of ALP blue deposits and red mineralized nodules (Fig. 2E-H), indicating its significant ability to enhance the mineralization capacity of SCAP. In summary, LPS-5 CM effectively promoted odontoblastic differentiation and mineralized tissue formation in SCAP, while LPS-10 CM appeared to inhibit these processes, particularly during the later stages of maturation.

LPS-CM promoted angiogenesis of HUVEC

To investigate the effect of the inflammatory microenvironment on angiogenesis, a tube formation assay was conducted to simulate vascularization in dental pulp. The results showed that all experimental groups (LPS-1 CM, LPS-5 CM, and LPS-10 CM) significantly enhanced tube formation compared to the LPS-0 CM group, with increases in mesh-like lumens in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3A). Statistical analysis using ImageJ revealed significant increases in the total length of tubes (Fig. 3B), number of branches (Fig. 3C), number of meshes (Fig. 3D), and number of nodes (Fig. 3E). These findings indicated that LPS-CM promotes tube formation by HUVEC.

LPS-5 CM promoted neurogenic differentiation of SCAP

SCAP were subjected to a neurochemical induction protocol to evaluate their neural differentiation potential. Following induction, noticeable morphological changes were observed. Cells in the induced groups transitioned from a fibroblast-like appearance to a neuronal-like phenotype, characterized by a rounded cell body and extended, slender processes (Fig. 4A). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed neural commitment, as evidenced by a marked upregulation of Nestin, a key neural stem cell marker. Induced cells exhibited strong Nestin expression, with positive staining localized in the cytoplasmic and perinuclear regions (Fig. 4B). Quantitative assessment using ImageJ software revealed a significant increase in both the percentage of neuron-like Nestin-positive cells (Fig. 4C) and the fluorescence intensity of Nestin (Fig. 4D) in the induced groups compared to the non-induced control (NC). Among the LPS-CM-treated groups, LPS-1 CM and LPS-5 CM enhanced neural differentiation more effectively than LPS-0 CM, whereas LPS-10 CM resulted in lower expression levels compared to LPS-5 CM. Collectively, these results demonstrate that LPS-CM, particularly at a concentration of 5 µg/mL, effectively promotes the neural differentiation of SCAP.

LPS-10 CM induced cell apoptosis and senescence

After different concentrations of LPS-CM stimulated in SCAP for 48 h, the apoptosis rate was detected by flow cytometry, and it was found that the apoptosis level of the LPS-1 CM and LPS-5 CM groups was not significantly different from that of the LPS-0 CM group, and the apoptosis rate of the LPS-10 CM group was significantly increased (Fig. 5A, B). The expression of cell senescence-related proteins P53 and P21 was significantly up-regulated in the LPS-10 CM group (Fig. 5C, D), and SA β-gal staining showed an increase in the number of blue-stained positive cells in the LPS-10 CM group (Fig. 5E, F). Immunofluorescence staining showed that P53 was expressed in the nucleus, and its fluorescence intensity was significantly increased in the LPS-10 CM group (Fig. 5G, H). Representative images and subsequent quantification (Fig. 5F-H) confirmed that the inflammatory microenvironment represented by LPS-10 CM significantly induced both cellular apoptosis and senescence.

LPS-5 CM promoted cellular recruitment and tissue ingrowth in vivo ectopic model

The transplanted tooth slices were examined for new tissue formation after two months’ transplantation. H&E staining (Fig. 6D) revealed distinct outcomes between the groups. The blank control group exhibited an essentially empty root canal space, with minimal evidence of organized pulp-like tissue. In stark contrast, the LPS-5 CM group demonstrated robust regeneration, characterized by newly formed, vascularized pulp-like tissue that filled the entire root canal. This tissue displayed a rich network of blood vessels within a loose, fibrous connective tissue matrix and showed evidence of dentin-like tissue formation, indicating successful ectopic pulp regeneration.

Discussion

The use of LSP to establish an inflammatory cell model is predicated on its ability to recapitulate key aspects of the pulpitis microenvironment. Based on several considerations supported by the existing literature on MSC immunomodulation, 48 h time point is considered sufficient for the cells to respond to inflammatory stimuli, undergo transcriptional and translational changes, and release a substantial and stable concentration of paracrine factors into the medium, while avoiding the depletion of nutrients or the accumulation of excessive cellular debris [21]. This in vitro chemokine-induced model offers a significant advantage over primary cells isolated from inflamed pulp: it allows for precise control over the inflammatory “dose,” thereby enhancing experimental reproducibility and reliability [22]. Extensive studies demonstrate that the inflammatory microenvironment is vital for pulp tissue regeneration, with inflammation serving as a key facilitator for stem cell mobilization, angiogenesis, and the formation of reparative dentin [12, 23]. The choice of working concentration of LPS depends on numbers of previous studies [24–26]. Mendes-Soares et al. [24] reported that a seven-day exposure to 10 µg/mL LPS induced an inflammatory microenvironment that significantly diminishes the odontogenic differentiation capacity of human dental pulp cells (HDPCs) by over 50% in vitro, thereby mimicking pathophysiological conditions characteristic of pulpitis. Therefore, our study introduced a novel nuance by utilizing conditioned medium from LPS-primed DPSCs. This approach shifts the focus from the direct effects of LPS on stem cells to the paracrine orchestration within the inflammatory niche.

LPS-CM serves as a bioactive repository of secretory proteins, extracellular vesicles, and metabolites, effectively mirroring the parent cell’s functional state [27, 28]. Our data revealed that LPS-5 CM acted as a potent instructing cue, enhancing the proliferation, migration, and odontogenic differentiation of SCAP. Furthermore, its pro-angiogenic effect on HUVECs and neurogenic differentiation capacity on SCAP highlighted its role as a multi-lineage regenerative booster. This aligns with reports that CM from inflamed DPSCs retains key inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) while simultaneously upregulating regenerative factors like VEGF and BDNF [13, 29]. The therapeutic implications of inflammation’s dual role were profound. The application of CM represented a paradigm shift towards cell-free regenerative endodontics, leveraging the secretome’s power while circumventing the challenges of cell transplantation [7]. However, the variability of CM, dictated by the specific inflammatory cues used to prime the parent cells, presents both a challenge and an opportunity. The finding that CM from periodontitis-affected teeth (P-CM) exhibits enhanced pro-angiogenic capacity, which is critically dependent on extracellular vesicle secretion, points to the central role of vesicles as key mediators [30]. This suggests that future therapeutic strategies may not merely rely on crude CM but on engineered or purified vesicle fractions derived from optimally primed cells.

While our ectopic model demonstrated the efficacy of LPS-5 CM in cell homing-based pulp regeneration, we acknowledged the limitations of such systems, including the natural variation in scaffold dimensions. Future orthotopic studies in large animals, employing scaffolds with controlled biophysical properties [5, 31], would be crucial to validate these findings. With the in-depth study of pulp regeneration, the current model of ectopic pulp regeneration, in which cell migration is transplanted into animals with the aid of dental root slices, preserves certain anatomical and microenvironmental features for better study of tissue regeneration and grafting [32], and thus this model is most widely used in studies related to pulp regeneration. A potential variable in our ectopic model is the natural size variation of the TDM chambers derived from clinical samples [33]. While effort was made to standardize the dentin slices, precise control over the apical foramen size presented a practical challenge. The absolute volume of the pulp cavity could be a confounding factor in studies quantifying the total volume of regenerated tissue. However, we aimed to investigate the initial cell homing and recruitment—the presence of a patent apical foramen, rather than its exact diameter, was the fundamental prerequisite enabling cell migration. To mitigate the potential influence of cavity size variability on our quantitative assessments, we normalized cell counts to the area or unit length of the canal space, thereby focusing on the efficiency of cell recruitment rather than the total number. Future studies designed to evaluate the complete regeneration of pulp-dentin complexes would benefit from utilizing scaffolds or matrices with precisely engineered dimensions to control for this variable.

Moreover, a deeper mechanistic understanding is warranted. Mesenchymal stem cells release various growth factors into this environment, collectively referred to as secretome, which include both conditioned media and extracellular vesicles that facilitate the transport of proteins and genetic materials [11, 34].Future work must dissect the relative contributions of soluble factors versus extracellular vesicles within the CM and delineate the signaling networks that drive the observed “priming” effect. Employing a comprehensive panel of markers for stem cells (e.g., c-Kit, Sox10), vascular (e.g., CD31), and neural (e.g., neurofilament, S100) lineages will be essential to fully characterize the regenerated tissue [35, 36]. While the present study delineates the role of the soluble inflammatory microenvironment, future strategies for functional pulp regeneration should integrate such biochemical conditioning with tailored biomaterials that provide critical biophysical cues, such as the fluid shear stress within scaffold pores as investigated by Salerno [37].

Conclusion

We obtained inflammatory conditioned media for co-culture studies involving SCAP and HUVEC. Our findings indicated that moderate inflammatory conditioned media enhance the proliferation, migration, differentiation, and tube formation in vitro, as well as promoted pulp-like tissue regeneration in vivo ectopic model, while excessive inflammation could induce apoptosis and senescence of stem cells, reducing their regenerative potential. This suggested that a moderate inflammatory microenvironment supports dental pulp regeneration. Nevertheless, the precise components of the inflammatory conditioned media mediating the effects remain unclear, and the exclusive reliance on an ectopic model warrants further investigation using an orthotopic pulp regeneration model for validation.
