Introduction

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
be a set system with ground set
X
. Given a subset
S\subseteq X
, we say that
\mathcal{F}

shatters

S
if
\{F\cap S:F\in\mathcal{F}\}=2^{S}
, that is, every subset of
S
can be realized as the intersection of
S
with some member of
\mathcal{F}
. The
Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension
(
VC-dimension
for short) of
\mathcal{F}
is the largest integer
d
such that there exists
S\subseteq X
with
|S|=d
that is shattered by
\mathcal{F}
. The VC-dimension is a key measure of the complexity of
\mathcal{F}
, with wide applications in combinatorics, learning theory, and discrete geometry.

As early as the 1970s, three groups of researchers
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
independently established a fundamental result, which states that any set system
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
with VC-dimension at most
d
satisfies
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{n}{i}
.
This bound is tight and can be achieved by several distinct extremal constructions. Despite this classical result, the following fundamental question concerning uniform set systems remains open.

Question 1.1
.

For given integers
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 2d+2
, what is the maximum size of
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
with VC-dimension at most
d
?

An elegant upper bound for this problem is
\binom{n}{d}
, established by Frankl and Pach
[
7
]
using the so-called linear algebra methods in 1984. Over the past four decades, this bound has been improved in various settings. In particular, Ge, Xu, Yip, Zhang, and Zhao
[
8
]
recently proved that
\binom{n}{d}
is not tight for every
d
and every
n\geq 2d+2
. When
n
is sufficiently large and
d
is a prime power, Mubayi and Zhao
[
9
]
showed that the Frankl–Pach bound can be improved by an additive term of order
\Omega_{d}(\log n)
. The above upper bounds are all based on algebraic methods. Very recently, Chao, Xu, Yip, and Zhang
[
3
]
improved the upper bound via a purely combinatorial argument, showing that for any
d\geq 2
and sufficiently large
n
, the size of a
(d+1)
-uniform set system with VC-dimension
d
is at most

\binom{n-1}{d}+O_{d}\left(n^{d-1-\frac{1}{4d-2}}\right).

On the other hand, a natural lower bound is
\binom{n-1}{d}
, given by the canonical star construction. Moreover, Erdős
[
4
]
, Frankl and Pach
[
7
]
conjectured that
\binom{n-1}{d}
is the maximum size when
n
is large. However, in 1997, Ahlswede and Khachatrian
[
1
]
provided a surprising counterexample, proving a stronger lower bound
\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}.

This construction was later generalized by Mubayi and Zhao
[
9
]
, who showed that there exist many non-isomorphic extremal constructions achieving this lower bound. Based on these findings, Mubayi and Zhao
[
9
]
conjectured that the lower bound

\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}

is in fact the solution to
Question

1.1
when
n\geq 2(d+1)
.

The main purpose of this paper is to completely determine the maximum size of
3
-uniform set systems on
[n]
with VC-dimension

2.1
Notations and preliminary structural results

Note that when
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[7]}{3}
has VC-dimension at most
2
, by
Lemma

2.1
, its size satisfies

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{7-1}{2}+1=16.

Our proof proceeds by induction on
n
. Assume that for all integers
7\leq n^{\prime}\leq n-1
, any set system
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\binom{[n^{\prime}]}{3}
with VC-dimension at most
2
satisfies

|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\leq\binom{n^{\prime}-1}{2}+1.

Now consider
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{3}
with VC-dimension at most
2
. Our goal is to show that

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{2}+1.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{m}\}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{3}
be a set system with VC-dimension at most
2
. In remainder of the proof, we always assume that

m\geq\binom{n-1}{2}+2

and aim to derive a contraction.

By the definition of VC-dimension, for each
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
(with
i\in[m]
) there exists at least one proper subset
B_{i}\subsetneq F_{i}
such that
F\cap F_{i}\neq B_{i}
for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
. Henceforth, we assume that for each
i\in[m]
,
B_{i}
is chosen to have the largest cardinality among all available options. Usually we refer to this set
B_{i}
as the
witness
of
F_{i}
. In
[
3
]
, the following auxiliary result was established using the sunflower lemma
[
2
,
5
,
6
]
, and the proof was inspired by
[
9
, Lemma 3]

Lemma 2.3

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{m}\}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d}
be a set system with VC-dimension at most
d
and
\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}\}
be selected as above. Then the followings hold.

(1)

For each subset
A\subseteq[n]
, the number of indices
i\in[m]
with
B_{i}=A
is at most
C_{d}
, where
C_{d}
is a constant. Consequently, for each integer
0\leq s\leq d
, the number of indices
i\in[m]
with
|B_{i}|=s
is at most
C_{d}\cdot\binom{n}{s}
.

(2)

In particular, when
|A|=d
, the number of indices
i\in[m]
with
B_{i}=A
is at most one.

For each
x\in[n]
, define

\mathcal{F}(x):=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:x\in F\}

and

\mathcal{F}(\bar{x}):=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:x\notin F\}.

We also define

\mathcal{G}(x):=\{F\setminus\{x\}:F\in\mathcal{F}(x)\}.

Clearly,
|\mathcal{F}(x)|=|\mathcal{G}(x)|
for any
x\in[n]
. Similarly, for distinct
x,y\in[n]
, we define

\mathcal{F}(x,y)=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:\{x,y\}\subseteq F\}.

We begin with a simple observation derived from our assumptions, which serves as a key density condition throughout the proof.

Claim 2.4
.

For any
x\in[n]
,
|\mathcal{F}(x)|\geq n-1
.

Proof of claim.

Suppose that
|\mathcal{F}(x)|\leq n-2
for some
x\in[n]
. Since
\mathcal{F}(\bar{x})\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus\{x\}}{3}
has VC-dimension at most
2
, the inductive hypothesis gives

|\mathcal{F}(\bar{x})|\leq\binom{n-2}{2}+1.

Therefore,

|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{F}(x)|+|\mathcal{F}(\bar{x})|\leq(n-2)+\left(\binom{n-2}{2}+1\right)=\binom{n-1}{2}+1,

a contradiction.
∎

Our proof makes essential use of the following three structures.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{m}\}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{3}
be a set system with VC-dimension at most
2
and
\{B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{m}\}
be selected as above. We define the following set families.

(1)

Define
\mathcal{B}
as the collection of witness sets of size two:

\mathcal{B}:=\{B_{i}:i\in[m],\ |B_{i}|=2\}.

(2)

Define
L
as the set of elements in
[n]
which serves as singleton witness:

L=\bigg\{x\in[n]:\textup{there\ exists\ some\ }i\in[m]\ \textup{with\ }B_{i}=\{x\}\bigg\}.

(3)

Define
\mathcal{C}
as the collection of sets in
\mathcal{F}
whose witness is empty set:

\mathcal{C}:=\{F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}:B_{i}=\emptyset\}.

Since
\mathcal{B}
is
2
-uniform, we can regard it as a graph, whose vertex set is
[n]
and edge set is
\mathcal{B}
. Moreover by
Lemma

2.3
(2),
\mathcal{B}
is a simple graph. As is commonly used in graph theory, we denote
N_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
as the set of neighbors of the vertex
x
in the graph
\mathcal{B}
.

Based on
Claim

2.4
, we have the following observation.

Claim 2.6
.

For any
x\in L
,
|N_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|\leq n-4
.

Proof of claim.

Recall that
\mathcal{G}(x):=\{F\setminus\{x\}:F\in\mathcal{F}(x)\}
, we first observe that the transversal number of
\mathcal{G}(x)
is at most
2
. Indeed, let
F_{i}=\{x,y,z\}
correspond to
B_{i}=\{x\}
. By the definition of
B_{i}
, the set
\{y,z\}
is a transversal of
\mathcal{G}(x)
. Since
|\mathcal{G}(x)|=|\mathcal{F}(x)|\geq n-1
, there exists some
w\in[n]\setminus\{x,y,z\}
such that both
\{w,y\}
and
\{w,z\}
belong to
\mathcal{G}(x)
. In other words,

\{x,y,z\},\quad\{x,y,w\},\quad\text{and}\quad\{x,z,w\}\in\mathcal{F}.

Therefore,

\{x,y\},\quad\{x,z\},\quad\text{and}\quad\{x,w\}\notin\mathcal{B},

which implies

|N_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|\leq n-4.

∎

2.2
Technical overview

The proof of the main theorem proceeds by induction on
n
, with the base case
n=7
verified by explicit computation. The inductive step (see
Theorem

2.2
) assumes the upper bound holds for
n-1
, and aims to derive a contradiction if a 3-uniform set system
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{3}
with VC-dimension at most 2 satisfies
|\mathcal{F}|\geq\binom{n-1}{2}+2
. While the proof is self-contained and elementary, it involves a considerable amount of detailed case analysis. To help the reader better understand the proof, we provide here an overview of the entire argument, and highlight several auxiliary results that we consider important.

The main strategy relies on a sophisticated structural analysis. For each
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
, a witness subset
B_{i}\subsetneq F_{i}
is chosen as large as possible such that no other set in
\mathcal{F}
intersects
F_{i}
exactly in
B_{i}
. By
Lemma

2.3
(based on prior work
[
3
]
using the sunflower lemma), the number of times a given
B_{i}
can appear is bounded. In particular, the collection of 2-element witnesses
\{B_{i}:|B_{i}|=2\}
forms a simple graph
\mathcal{B}
.

The first key technical insight is
Claim

2.4
, which asserts that every element must appear in at least
n-1
sets of
\mathcal{F}
, enforcing a strong density condition. This motivates the introduction of several structural tools: the auxiliary graph
\mathcal{B}
, the set
L
of vertices appearing as singleton witnesses
B_{i}=\{x\}
, along with the collection
\mathcal{C}
of sets
F_{i}
with empty witnesses
B_{i}=\emptyset
.

A central component of the proof is
Lemma

2.8
, which shows that no element can occur in more than one singleton witness
B_{i}=\{x\}
. Its proof is intricate and builds on lots of observations. A key outcome of this lemma is the concise identity given in
Corollary

2.12
:

|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{B}|+|L|+|\mathcal{C}|,

which gives us tighter control on the size of
\mathcal{F}
. The remainder of the proof is divided into two main cases according to whether
\mathcal{C}
is empty.

In
Section

2.4
, we analyze the case
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
. Here we want to understand the structural of the graph
\mathcal{B}
formed by witness sets of size two. Using the size constraint on the links
\mathcal{F}(x)
, we show that
\mathcal{B}
cannot be too dense. We approach this by examining the maximum degree
\Delta(\mathcal{B})
and consider the cases
\Delta=n-2
,
n-3
, and
n-4
. Each of these cases requires tailored arguments based on neighborhood properties, set intersections, and auxiliary counting estimates. At the end of this analysis we can already prove that

\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\leq\binom{n-1}{2}+1+\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\leq\binom{n-1}{2}+O(1).

However, lots of work remain to reduce the
O(1)
to
1
. To achieve this, we perform a sophisticated structural analysis on
\mathcal{C}
.

In
Section

2.5
, we analyze interactions among
\mathcal{B}
,
L
, and
\mathcal{C}
when
\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset
. Key claims like
Claim

2.14
and
Claim

2.15
quantify precisely how
\mathcal{C}
restricts the structure and size of
\mathcal{B}
and
L
. We also systematically identify and exclude forbidden substructures in
\mathcal{C}
, such as 2-intersecting families and linear triangle in
Claim

2.16
and
Claim

2.17
, thus significantly restricting the structure of
\mathcal{C}
.

In
Section

2.6
, we analyze the structure of
\mathcal{C}
based on its transversal number
\tau(\mathcal{C})
. Noting that
\mathcal{C}
is intersecting, its transversal number is at most 3. We first quickly eliminate the case where the transversal number is exactly
3
, as this implies
|\mathcal{F}|\leq 19
.

The case
\tau(\mathcal{C})=2
is involved. Here, two earlier structural properties in
Claim

2.16
and
Claim

2.17
become essential. In particular, we leverage the density condition from
Claim

2.4
and the greedy construction of linear triangles to derive contradictions. These tools allow us to rule out this scenario by consistently constructing forbidden substructures under the given constraints.

The most intricate case arises when
\tau(\mathcal{C})=1
. In this case,
\mathcal{C}
must share a common element. However, its structure remains highly complex. To handle this, we draw on the density condition and the interplay among
\mathcal{B}
,
L
, and
\mathcal{C}
, as developed in
Section

2.5
. We also exploit structural features of
\mathcal{C}
itself. Crucially, we shift focus to the link graph
\mathcal{Z}=\{F\setminus\{z\}:F\in\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\}
for the center
z
of the star. By analyzing several combinatorial properties of
\mathcal{Z}
in
Claim

2.20
, including its matching number, maximum degree, and the absence of
K_{2,2}
subgraphs, we efficiently complete the argument, avoiding exhaustive enumeration of all possible
\mathcal{Z}
’s.

2.3
Key lemma: Structural insights of
L

By
Lemma

2.3
(1), we know that for any element
x\in L
, there are at most
C
sets in
\mathcal{F}
whose witness is exactly
\{x\}
, for some constant
C>1
. The main result of this section is
Lemma

2.8
, where we improve this bound and show that the constant
C
can be strengthened to 1. Before proving this, we begin with a preliminary step showing
C\leq 2
.

Claim 2.7
.

For each
x\in[n]
,
|\{\ell\in[m]:B_{\ell}=\{x\}\}|\leq 2
. Moreover, the equality holds if and only if

\mathcal{F}(x)=\big\{\{x,y,z\},\{x,y,w\},\{x,z,w\}\big\}\cup\bigg(\bigcup_{v\in[n]\setminus\{x,y,z,w\}}\{x,y,v\}\bigg),

for some distinct
y,z,w\in[n]\backslash\{x\}
.

Proof of claim.

Suppose there exist
i,j,k\in[m]
such that

B_{i}=B_{j}=B_{k}=\{x\}.
Let
F_{i}=\{x,y,z\}
, then the set
\{y,z\}
is a transversal of
\mathcal{G}(x)
. By
Claim

2.4
,
|\mathcal{G}(x)|=|\mathcal{F}(x)|\geq n-1
, there exists some
w\in[n]\setminus\{x,y,z\}
such that
\{y,w\},\{z,w\}\in\mathcal{G}(x)
, which also yields that

\{x,y,w\},\ \{x,z,w\}\in\mathcal{F}(x).

Then
F_{j}
and
F_{k}
must be exactly
\{x,y,w\}
and
\{x,z,w\}
, respectively. Otherwise,
F_{j}\cap\{x,y,w\}\neq\{x\}
or
F_{k}\cap\{x,z,w\}\neq\{x\}
, contradicting the assumption that
B_{j}=B_{k}=\{x\}
. Thus, any set in
F\in\mathcal{F}(x)
must satisfies
|F\cap\{y,z,w\}|\geq 2
, which implies that
|\mathcal{F}(x)|=3,
contradicting the assumption that
|\mathcal{F}(x)|\geq n-1
. Therefore,
\left|\{i\in[m]:B_{i}=\{x\}\}\right|\leq 2.

Furthermore, suppose that
\left|\{\ell\in[m]:B_{\ell}=\{x\}\}\right|=2
for some
x\in[n]
. Let
F_{i}=\{x,y,z\}\in\mathcal{F}
be one of the sets with witness
B_{i}=\{x\}
. By the preceding argument, we may assume without loss of generality that the other such set is
F_{j}=\{x,y,w\}
with
B_{j}=\{x\}
. Then every set
F\in\mathcal{F}(x)\setminus\{\{x,y,z\},\{x,y,w\},\{x,z,w\}\}
must contain both
x
and
y
; otherwise, its intersection with either
\{x,y,z\}
or
\{x,y,w\}
would be exactly
\{x\}
, contradicting the definition of a witness.

Moreover, since
|\mathcal{F}(x)|\geq n-1
by
Claim

2.4
, we conclude that
\mathcal{F}(x)
must be exactly

\big\{\{x,y,z\},\{x,y,w\},\{x,z,w\}\big\}\cup\bigg(\bigcup_{v\in[n]\setminus\{x,y,z,w\}}\{x,y,v\}\bigg).

∎

We now state the central lemma of this subsection. It asserts that, under the assumption
|\mathcal{F}|\geq\binom{n-1}{2}+2
and the inductive hypothesis, for any fixed
x\in[n]
, there is at most one index
i\in[m]
such that
B_{i}=\{x\}
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{m}\}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{3}
be a set system with VC-dimension at most
2
and
\{B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be selected based on the above rules. If
m\geq\binom{n-1}{2}+2
, then for any
x\in[n]
, we have
\left|\{i\in[m]:B_{i}=\{x\}\}\right|\leq 1.

Proof of
Lemma

2.8
.

Suppose there is some
x\in[n]
such that
\left|\{i\in[m]:B_{i}=\{x\}\}\right|=2.
By
Claim

2.7
, there exist elements
y,z,w\in[n]\setminus\{x\}
such that
F_{i}=\{x,y,z\}
and
F_{j}=\{x,y,w\}
satisfy
B_{i}=B_{j}=\{x\}
, and

\mathcal{F}(x)=\big\{\{x,y,z\},\{x,y,w\},\{x,z,w\}\big\}\cup\bigg\{\{x,y,v\}:v\in[n]\setminus\{x,y,z,w\}\bigg\}.

We now prove a key claim.

Claim 2.9
.

\{y,z,w\}
belongs to
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof of claim.

Suppose
\{y,z,w\}\notin\mathcal{F}
, we claim that the set system

\mathcal{H}:=\big(\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}(x)\big)\cup\{y,z,w\}\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus\{x\}}{3}

has VC-dimension at most
2
. We verify this as follows.

1.

Suppose there exists some
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}(x)
such that
F_{k}\cap\{y,z,w\}=B_{k}.
Then
B_{k}
must be contained in some
C\in\big\{\{y,z\},\{y,w\},\{z,w\}\big\}
. On the other hand, we already know that

\{x,y,z\},\quad\{x,y,w\},\quad\text{and}\quad\{x,z,w\}\in\mathcal{F}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{F}.

Therefore,

F_{k}\cap(C\cup\{x\})=F_{k}\cap C=B_{k},

which contradicts the choice of
B_{k}
. This shows that any set in
\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}(x)
is not shattered by
\mathcal{H}
.

2.

It remains to show that
\{y,z,w\}
is not a shattered set in
\mathcal{H}
. Let

F_{\ell}=\{x,z,w\}\in\mathcal{F}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{F}.

Moreover, observe that

\{x\}=\{x,z,w\}\cap\{x,y,v\},\quad\text{for some }v\in[n]\setminus\{x,y,z,w\},

\{x,z\}=\{x,z,w\}\cap\{x,y,z\},

and

\{x,w\}=\{x,z,w\}\cap\{x,y,w\}.

These intersections imply that

B_{\ell}\subseteq\{z,w\}.

(1)

Now suppose there exists
F\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}(x)
such that

F\cap\{y,z,w\}=B_{\ell}.

Then we would have

F\cap\{x,z,w\}=B_{\ell},

contradicting the choice of
B_{\ell}
.

Since
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus\{x\}}{3}
has VC-dimension at most
2
, by inductive hypothesis, we conclude that

a contradiction to

.
∎

By

\big\{\{x,y,z\},\{x,y,w\},\{x,z,w\},\{y,z,w\}\big\}\cup\bigg(\bigcup_{v\in[n]\setminus\{x,y,z,w\}}\{x,y,v\}\bigg)\subseteq\mathcal{F}.

Recall that

\mathcal{B}:=\{B_{i}:i\in[m],\ |B_{i}|=2\},

L=\big\{x\in[n]:\textup{there\ exists\ some\ }i\in[m]\ \textup{with\ }B_{i}=\{x\}\big\},

and

\mathcal{C}:=\{F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}:B_{i}=\emptyset\}.

By
Lemma

2.8
, we then can assume that for any
x\in L
,
\left|\{i\in[m]:B_{i}=\{x\}\}\right|\leq 1.

Let us summarize the most important consequence of
Lemma

2.8
here.

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{m}\}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{3}
be a set system with VC-dimension at most
2
. If
|\mathcal{F}|\geq\binom{n-1}{2}+2
, then we have

|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{B}|+|L|+|\mathcal{C}|.

2.4
When
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset

When
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
, we mainly take advantage of
Corollary

2.12
and focus on the auxiliary graph
\mathcal{B}
and the set
L
. Our key claim is the following one.

Claim 2.13
.

If
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
, then
|L|\leq n-2
.

Proof of claim.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{B}}
be the complement graph of
\mathcal{B}
. By the definition of
L
, for any
x\in L
, there exists some set
F_{i}=\{x,y,z\}
such that
B_{i}=\{x\}
. Thus,
\{y,z\}
serves as a transversal set of
\mathcal{G}(x)=\{F\setminus\{x\}:F\in\mathcal{F}(x)\}.
For convenience, we can regard
\mathcal{G}(x)
as a graph on vertex set
[n]\setminus\{x\}
. By
Claim

2.4
, the vertices
y
and
z
share at least
|\mathcal{F}(x)|-(n-2)\geq 1
common neighbors in
\mathcal{G}(x)
. Observe that for any such common neighbor
v\in[n]\setminus\{x,y,z\}
, we have
\{x,v\}\notin\mathcal{B}
. Additionally, it is clear that
\{x,y\},\{x,z\}\notin\mathcal{B}
. Consequently, we obtain

|N_{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}(x)|\geq 2+|\mathcal{F}(x)|-(n-2),

which further implies that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}(x)|\leq n-4+|N_{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}(x)|.

(2)

If
|\mathcal{B}|\leq\binom{n-1}{2}-n+1
, then by
Corollary

2.12
, we have

|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{B}|+|L|\leq\binom{n-1}{2}+1,

which contradicts our assumption. Therefore, we proceed under the assumption that

|\mathcal{B}|\geq\binom{n-1}{2}-n+2,

which further implies

|\overline{\mathcal{B}}|\leq\binom{n}{2}-\binom{n-1}{2}+n-2=2n-3.

Equivalently, we have

\sum_{a\in L}|N_{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}(a)|\leq 2|\overline{\mathcal{B}}|\leq 4n-6.

Suppose that
L=[n]
, then we have

\sum\limits_{a\in[n]}|\mathcal{F}(a)|\leq n(n-4)+\sum\limits_{a\in L}|N_{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}(a)|\leq n^{2}-6,

which further yields that when
n\geq 8
,

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\frac{1}{3}\sum\limits_{a\in[n]}|\mathcal{F}(a)|\leq\frac{n^{2}}{3}-2<\binom{n-1}{2}+1,

a contradiction. Therefore
|L|\leq n-1
. We then claim that for any element
b\in[n]\setminus L
, we have

|N_{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}(b)|\leq n-3.

(3)

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists some
b\in[n]\setminus L
such that
|N_{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}(b)|\geq n-2
. Let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
denote the subgraph of
\mathcal{B}
induced by
[n]\setminus\{b\}
. Observe that for each
c\in[n]\setminus\{b\}
, we have
|N_{{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}}(c)|\leq n-2
. By
Claim

2.6
, it follows that

|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c\in[n]\setminus\{b\}}|N_{{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}}(c)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigg(|L|\cdot(n-4)+(n-1-|L|)\cdot(n-2)\bigg)\leq\binom{n-1}{2}-|L|.

Thus, we have

|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{B}|+|L|\leq|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|+1+|L|\leq\binom{n-1}{2}+1,

which is a contradiction.

Then suppose
|L|=n-1
, without loss of generality, we assume that
L=[n]\setminus\{1\}
, and by the above argument we have
|N_{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}(1)|\leq n-3
and hence by (
2
),

|\mathcal{F}(1)|\leq n-4+|N_{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}(1)|\leq 2n-7.

Since
\sum\limits_{a\in L}|N_{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}(a)|\leq 4n-6
and

, we then have

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\frac{1}{3}\sum\limits_{a\in[n]}|\mathcal{F}(a)|\leq\frac{1}{3}\bigg(2n-7+\sum\limits_{a\in L}(n-4+|N_{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}(a)|)\bigg)\leq\frac{(n-1)(n-4)}{3}+2n-\frac{13}{3}\leq\binom{n-1}{2}+1,

a contradiction to our assumption. Therefore

, completing the proof.
∎

Based on

2.13
, we complete the proof by examining the maximum degree of the graph
\mathcal{B}
, denoted by
\Delta(\mathcal{B})
. Obviously

, since if

, then there exists some corresponding set

, which yields that

. Moreover, when

, applying

, we obtain

which leads to a contradiction. Therefore, we proceed by dividing the final argument into three parts, each aimed at ruling out the case where
\Delta(\mathcal{B})=n-2
,
n-3
, or
n-4
.

2.4.1
\Delta(\mathcal{B})=n-2

Let
x\in[n]
satisfy
|N_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|=n-2
, and let

. Then, for any
v\in[n]\setminus\{x,y\}
, the set in
\mathcal{F}
corresponding to the witness

must be
\{x,y,v\}
. This implies
N_{\mathcal{B}}(y)=\emptyset
.

We now claim that

. Suppose, for contradiction, that

. Then, there exists some
F_{i}=\{a,b,y\}
such that
B_{i}=\{y\}
. In this case,
x\notin F_{i}
. Since

, we can find

such that

\{x,y,w\}\cap\{a,b,y\}=\{y\}.

However, this contradicts the assumption that
B_{i}=\{y\}
. Thus,
y\notin L
. Finally, by (
3
), we have
|N_{\mathcal{B}}(y)|\geq 2
, which is a contradiction to the fact
N_{\mathcal{B}}(y)=\emptyset
. Therefore, it is impossible that
\Delta(\mathcal{B})=n-2
.

2.4.2
\Delta(\mathcal{B})=n-3

Let
x\in[n]
satisfy
|N_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|=n-3
, and let

. For each
v\in[n]\setminus\{x,y,z\}
, the set in
\mathcal{F}
corresponding to

must contain either
y
or

. Therefore, at least one of
\{v,y\}
and

does not belong to
\mathcal{B}
, which further implies that

|N_{\mathcal{B}}(y)|+|N_{\mathcal{B}}(z)|\leq 2(n-1)-(n-3)-2=n-1.

Moreover, we set

. Note that
|L|-2\leq t\leq|L|
by
Claim

2.6
and
\Delta(\mathcal{B})=n-3
, we then have

\sum\limits_{v\in[n]\setminus\{y,z\}}|N_{\mathcal{B}}(v)|=\sum\limits_{v\in[n]\setminus(\{y,z\}\cup L)}|N_{\mathcal{B}}(v)|+\sum\limits_{v\in([n]\setminus\{y,z\})\cap L}|N_{\mathcal{B}}(v)|\leq(n-2-t)\cdot(n-3)+t(n-4).

Therefore, we have

|\mathcal{B}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\big(n-2\big)\big(n-3\big)-t+n-1\bigg)\leq\binom{n-2}{2}-\frac{|L|}{2}+\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{2}.

Furthermore, by
Corollary

2.12
and the fact that
|L|\leq n-2
from
Claim

2.13
, we have

|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{B}|+|L|\leq\binom{n-2}{2}+\frac{|L|}{2}+\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\leq\binom{n-1}{2}+\frac{3}{2},

which is a contradiction to the assumption that
|\mathcal{F}|\geq\binom{n-1}{2}+2
. Therefore, it is impossible that
\Delta(\mathcal{B})=n-3
.

2.4.3
\Delta(\mathcal{B})=n-4

Let
x\in[n]
satisfy
|N_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|=n-4
, and let
y,z,w\notin N_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
. For each
v\in[n]\setminus\{x,y,z,w\}
, the set in
\mathcal{F}
corresponding to
\{x,v\}\in\mathcal{B}
must contain exactly one of
y
,
z
, or
w
. Therefore, at least one of
\{v,y\}
,
\{v,z\}
, and
\{v,w\}
does not belong to
\mathcal{B}
. Moreover,
\{x,y\},\{x,z\},\{x,w\}\notin\mathcal{B}
. This observation implies that

|N_{\mathcal{B}}(y)|+|N_{\mathcal{B}}(z)|+|N_{\mathcal{B}}(w)|\leq 3(n-1)-(n-4)-3=2n-2.

(4)

Since
\Delta(\mathcal{B})=n-4
, we have

|\mathcal{B}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\big((n-4)(n-3)+2n-2\big)=\binom{n-3}{2}+n-1.

(5)

Applying
Corollary

2.12
, it follows that

|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{B}|+|L|\leq\binom{n-3}{2}+n-1+n-2=\binom{n-1}{2}+2.

(6)

Now, suppose the equality in (
6
) holds, that is,
|\mathcal{F}|=\binom{n-1}{2}+2
. Then all of the equalities in
Claim

2.13
, (
4
) and (
5
) should hold. Therefore, we have
|L|=n-2
, and for each
v\in[n]\setminus\{y,z,w\}
, we have
|N_{\mathcal{B}}(v)|=n-4
. Moreover,
\{y,z\},\{y,w\},\{z,w\}\in\mathcal{B}
since
|N_{\mathcal{B}}(y)|+|N_{\mathcal{B}}(z)|+|N_{\mathcal{B}}(w)|=2n-2.

We then claim that
x\notin L
. By symmetry, showing this for
x
will imply the same for all
v\in[n]
with
|N_{\mathcal{B}}(v)|=n-4
, leading to a contradiction since
|L|=n-2
.

Consider
F_{i}=\{x,a,b\}
with witness
B_{i}=\{x\}
. Since
\{x,a\},\{x,b\}\notin\mathcal{B}
, we infer that
\{a,b\}\subseteq\{y,z,w\}
. However, it implies that
\{a,b\}\in\mathcal{B}
, contradicting our choice of
B_{i}=\{x\}
. Thus
x\notin L
and we cannot have equality in (
6
).

Combining the above analysis with the inductive hypothesis, we conclude that when
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
, it holds that
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{2}+1
.

Building on the structural properties established in

We first simply show that the transversal number of

Proof of claim.

Suppose that

We further claim that

Concluding remarks and future work

In this paper, we investigate a fundamental open problem in extremal set theory: determining the maximum size of a
(d+1)
-uniform set system on
[n]
with VC-dimension at most
d
, for any given
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 2d+2
. Our main contribution is a complete determination of the maximum size of 3-uniform set systems with VC-dimension at most 2 for all values of
n
. From the perspective of extremal results, this represents a significant first step toward resolving a central problem that has attracted attention from Erdős
[
4
]
, Frankl and Pach
[
7
]
since the 1980s. In particular, our result answers the specific case of 3-uniform set systems posed by Mubayi and Zhao
[
9
]
in 2007. We believe that the following research directions are interesting for further exploration.

3.1
Characterization of extremal configurations for
3
-uniform set systems

In 2007, Mubayi and Zhao
[
9
]
extended earlier constructions from
[
1
]
and established that there exist infinitely many
(d+1)
-uniform set systems with VC-dimension
d
and maximum size
\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}
. They conjectured that this is the correct answer for
Question

1.1
.

We completely resolve the case for
3
-uniform set systems with VC-dimension
2
, proving that for
n\geq 7
, the maximum size is precisely
\binom{n-1}{2}+1
, thereby confirming their conjecture when
d=2
. An intriguing question that naturally follows is whether the Mubayi-Zhao constructions are the only extremal configurations achieving this bound. Note that the transversal number of their constructions is always 2. However, consider the following 3-uniform set system on
[7]
with VC-dimension 2:

\displaystyle\big\{

\displaystyle\{1,2,3\},\{1,2,4\},\{1,3,4\},\{2,3,4\},\{1,2,5\},\{1,3,5\},\{2,3,5\},\{1,4,5\},

\displaystyle\{1,2,6\},\{1,3,6\},\{2,3,6\},\{2,4,6\},\{3,5,6\},\{1,2,7\},\{1,3,7\},\{2,3,7\}\big\}.

It is straightforward to verify that the transversal number of this system is
3
, demonstrating that even for
n=7
, the Mubayi-Zhao constructions are not the only extremal configurations achieving the upper bound of
16
. While it is possible to explicitly list all extremal constructions for
n=7
, it remains an open question whether for larger
n
, there are infinitely many such constructions beyond those provided by Mubayi and Zhao.

3.2
Improved lower bound when
n=2d

Interestingly, we also discovered that the construction previously believed to be optimal fails when
n=6
; it only becomes optimal for
n\geq 7
. This leads to the rather surprising conclusion that the solution to
Question

1.1
might naturally divide into three or even more distinct regimes. More precisely, we suspect that such a phenomenon may occur more generally for all
(d+1)
-uniform set systems with
d\geq 2
. As supporting evidence, we present a 4-uniform set system
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[8]}{4}
with VC-dimension 3 and size 45, which exceeds the size
\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}=39
achieved by the previously known constructions in
[
1
,
9
]
. The system
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[8]}{4}
is given explicitly by:

\displaystyle\big\{

\displaystyle\{1,2,3,4\},\{1,2,3,5\},\{1,2,4,5\},\{1,3,4,5\},\{2,3,4,5\},\{1,2,3,6\},\{1,2,4,6\},\{1,3,4,6\},\{2,3,4,6\},

\displaystyle\{1,2,5,6\},\{1,3,5,6\},\{2,3,5,6\},\{1,4,5,6\},\{2,4,5,6\},\{3,4,5,6\},\{1,2,3,7\},\{1,2,4,7\},\{1,3,4,7\},

\displaystyle\{2,3,4,7\},\{1,2,5,7\},\{1,3,5,7\},\{2,3,5,7\},\{1,4,5,7\},\{2,4,5,7\},\{3,4,5,7\},\{1,2,6,7\},\{1,3,6,7\},

\displaystyle\{2,3,6,7\},\{1,2,3,8\},\{1,2,4,8\},\{1,3,4,8\},\{2,3,4,8\},\{1,2,5,8\},\{1,3,5,8\},\{2,3,5,8\},\{1,4,5,8\},

\displaystyle\{3,4,5,8\},\{1,4,6,8\},\{2,4,6,8\},\{3,4,6,8\},\{1,5,6,8\},\{2,5,7,8\},\{3,5,7,8\},\{4,5,7,8\},\{2,4,5,8\}\big\}.

This example further highlights the subtleties in understanding the extremal behavior of set systems under VC-dimension constraints, even for small parameters. In particular, it will be interesting to find a systematical construction for
Question

1.1
when
n=2d+2
that beats Ahlswede-Khachatrian’s lower bound. Note that for this particular
n
, the lower bound given by Ahlswede-Khachatrian is

\binom{2d+1}{d}+\binom{2d-2}{d-2}=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{16}+o(1)\right)\binom{2d+2}{d+1}.

We suspect the coefficient
\frac{9}{16}
can be further improved.

3.3
Improved upper bound for general
d

At present, we think that fully resolving
Question

1.1
for all
d\geq 3
and all
n\geq 2d+2
is extremely challenging. A potentially attainable goal is to determine whether, for
d\geq 3
and sufficiently large
n
, every set system
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
with VC-dimension at most
d
has maximum size
\binom{n-1}{d}+C_{d}n^{d-2}
for some constant
C_{d}>0
.
