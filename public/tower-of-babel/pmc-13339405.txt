Determinants of dental anxiety in adults attending tertiary care dental centres in Karachi, Pakistan
Authors: Ammarah Muhammad Nauman, Yousra Altaf, Sidra Mohiuddin, Maryam Panhwar, Aahad Rasul, Umaiya Altaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental anxiety is a multifactorial condition that negatively affects oral health behaviours, yet evidence on modifiable determinants in developing settings remains limited. This study assessed associations between dental anxiety and sociodemographic, dental attendance, environmental, experiential, and patient-dentist interaction factors among adults in Karachi, Pakistan.MethodologyA multi-centre cross-sectional study included 400 adults aged ≥ 18 years. Participants completed a structured questionnaire covering sociodemographics, dental attendance, prior dental experiences, environmental triggers, and patient-dentist interactions. Dental anxiety was measured using the Corah Dental Anxiety Scale (DAS). Data were analysed using SPSS v27. Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for group comparisons, and hierarchical multiple linear regression was performed to identify independent predictors (p < 0.05).ResultsMost participants reported low anxiety (81.8%), with a mean DAS score of 8.57 ± 3.39. Higher anxiety was significantly associated with females (U = 15790, p = 0.009) and longer time since last dental visit (H = 11.19, p = 0.011). The regression model was significant (F(28,371) = 10.02, p < 0.001), explaining 43.1% of variance (R² = 0.431; adjusted R² = 0.388). Significant predictors of increased anxiety included clinic environment anxiety (B = 1.48, p < 0.001), exposure to others’ expressions of pain (B = 1.54, p < 0.001), drill sound (B = 1.35, p < 0.001), and drill smell (B = 0.73, p = 0.021), while clear explanations (B = − 1.83, p = 0.001) and involvement in decision-making (B = − 1.00, p = 0.031) reduced DAS scores.ConclusionDental anxiety in this population was more strongly associated with modifiable environmental and communication-related factors than with sociodemographic characteristics. Improvements in patient-centred communication and clinical environments may help reduce anxiety and enhance care experiences; however, causal inferences cannot be drawn from the cross-sectional design.Keywords: Dental anxiety, Corah dental anxiety scale DAS , Oral Health, Dental care, Dental care utilisation, Patient-centred care, Patient-dentist interaction, Health behaviour

Introduction

Background

Dental anxiety is a multifactorial psychological condition characterised by intense negative emotional responses associated with dental treatment, ranging from mild apprehension to severe phobic reactions, and it significantly affects both oral health outcomes and overall well-being [1, 2]. It exists on a continuum of severity and is widely recognised as a global health concern not restricted to any specific population or country [2, 3].

Multiple biological, psychological, and socio-environmental factors contribute to the development of dental anxiety [1, 3]. These include previous traumatic or negative dental experiences, fear of pain, perceived lack of control, and inadequate communication between the patient and dentist [4–6]. Environmental triggers also play a significant role, as dental settings are often associated with invasive procedures involving sharp instruments, exposure to characteristic clinical odours, supine positioning, isolation within clinical environments, and anticipation of pain or judgment [7]. In addition, patient-related factors such as age, gender, educational level, financial status, and cultural influences further modify anxiety levels [3, 8, 9].

Dental anxiety has important physiological and behavioural consequences [6, 10]. Anxiety activates the autonomic nervous system, resulting in increased blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, gastrointestinal activity, and cardiac output [10]. Clinically, anxious patients are more likely to avoid or delay dental visits, leading to disease progression and poorer oral health outcomes [1, 6]. This avoidance behaviour often reinforces a vicious cycle of fear, avoidance, and worsening oral health status [5, 11]. Furthermore, managing anxious patients can be challenging for dental practitioners, often increasing stress levels among clinicians and potentially compromising the quality of care delivered, thereby reinforcing negative patient-dentist interactions [7].

The prevalence of dental anxiety varies widely across populations, ranging from approximately 4% to over 20% globally. In the United Kingdom, nearly 48% of adults report dental anxiety, underscoring its relevance even in high-income healthcare settings [12]. Similarly, high prevalence rates have been documented in Asia and other regions, including 83.1% in China [6], 51.8% in India [13], and 31.5% in Lebanon among adult dental patients [14]. In the United Arab Emirates, 72.3% of patients attending dental outpatient clinics reported moderate to severe anxiety [9]. Comparable findings have been observed in South Asia, where a tertiary care study in Kathmandu, Nepal, reported a prevalence of 47.82% [15]. In contrast, in Sri Lanka, 70% of participants were moderately anxious and 19% severely anxious in clinical settings [16].

Despite the well-established global burden of dental anxiety, most existing literature remains largely descriptive, with limited analytical focus on how and why it develops within clinical encounters [1, 12]. In particular, evidence is scarce regarding the contribution of modifiable factors such as clinical environment, sensory stimuli, and patient-dentist interaction dynamics, especially in low- and middle-income countries where healthcare systems and patient experiences may differ substantially [12, 17]. These factors constitute an important knowledge gap, as understanding these contextual and interpersonal determinants is essential for developing targeted, patient-centred interventions rather than relying primarily on demographic or historical predictors [12, 18].

In Pakistan, dental anxiety remains an under-recognised barrier to oral healthcare access, where utilisation patterns are frequently shaped by socioeconomic constraints, limited preventive awareness, and irregular dental attendance [19, 20]. However, empirical data from the Pakistani context remain scarce, particularly regarding the influence of environmental and communication-related factors on anxiety during dental care [19, 21]. Addressing this gap is essential to inform context-specific strategies to improve patient engagement, reduce avoidance behaviour, and enhance long-term oral health outcomes [22, 23].

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the association between dental anxiety and sociodemographic characteristics, dental visit patterns, environmental factors, previous dental experiences, and patient-dentist interaction variables among adult patients attending tertiary care dental centres in Karachi, Pakistan.

Objective

To determine the association between dental anxiety and sociodemographic characteristics, dental visit patterns, environmental factors, prior dental experiences, and patient-dentist interaction variables.

Methodology

This multi-centre cross-sectional study was conducted over six months at multiple tertiary-care dental institutions in Karachi, Pakistan. A total of 400 adult participants attending outpatient dental clinics were recruited using non-probability convenience sampling. Inclusion criteria comprised adults aged 18 years or older who presented for dental care at the participating centres and provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria included individuals with previously diagnosed psychological disorders, those currently using anxiolytic medications and patients with no prior dental experience.

The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi, assuming an anticipated prevalence of dental anxiety of 22.2% [5], a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error, which yielded a minimum of 266 participants. To account for non-response, 30% was added [24], yielding a final sample of 346 participants. The sample was further increased to 400 to enhance precision and analytical robustness.

The data collection instrument comprised a questionnaire adapted from a previously published study by Kheir et al. (2019), which evaluated the patient-dentist relationship and dental anxiety among young Sudanese adults. It consisted of four sections. The first section collected sociodemographic information, while the second section assessed participants’ dental experiences and attendance patterns. The third section evaluated environmental and situational factors influencing dental anxiety, including the dental clinic environment, drill sound and smell, exposure to other patients’ expressions of pain, time spent in the waiting area, the effect of soft music, and aspects of patient-dentist interaction. The fourth section utilised the validated and widely used Corah Dental Anxiety Scale (DAS) [25], with scores ranging from 4 to 20 and standard cut-offs of < 12 (low anxiety), 12–14 (moderate anxiety), and > 14 (high anxiety). Minor modifications were made to align the questionnaire with the local clinical context. The instrument was translated into Urdu using forward and backward translation by bilingual experts to ensure linguistic validity. Content validity was established through expert review by specialists in dental public health and clinical dentistry. The patient-dentist interaction and environmental and sensory trigger variables were measured as individual items reflecting distinct communication, behavioural, and contextual aspects of dental care. These items were conceptually independent and were not intended to represent a single latent construct; therefore, internal consistency reliability (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) was not calculated. Each item was analysed separately to examine its specific association with dental anxiety. This item-level analytical approach has been used in prior studies examining contextual and interaction-related determinants of dental anxiety.

Ethical approval (reference code: 7220523YACPD) was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee (ERC) at Ziauddin University.

Data collection was conducted at each participating tertiary-care centre by designated members of the research team. After confirming eligibility, participants received a detailed explanation of the study objectives, procedures, and confidentiality measures. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. The questionnaire was self-administered, allowing participants to complete it independently in a quiet, designated area within the clinic. The research team was present only to provide clarification if requested, ensuring that data collectors did not influence participants’ responses. All responses were collected anonymously and handled with strict confidentiality. PI reviewed questionnaires to ensure completeness and stored them securely. This standardised approach across all centres ensured procedural consistency, reduced the risk of bias, and safeguarded participant privacy.

Data were entered and analysed using SPSS version 27. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of the histogram. Descriptive statistics of qualitative and quantitative variables were calculated. Inferential statistics were conducted to examine associations and differences between groups. Differences in DAS scores between the two groups were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test, while comparisons across three or more groups were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Where significant differences were identified, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Mann-Whitney U tests with Bonferroni adjustment.

A hierarchical multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to determine independent predictors of DAS score. This approach was used to evaluate the additional variance explained by environmental and patient-dentist interaction factors after controlling for potential confounding from sociodemographic and dental visit-related variables using sequential block entry.

In Block 1, sociodemographic and dental visit variables were entered, including gender (male, female), education (Undergraduate, Postgraduate; reference category: ≤ Secondary), insurance status (insured, not insured), economic status (Within Standard, Above Standard; reference category: Below Standard), interval since last dental visit (7–12 months, 1–5 years, ≥ 6 years; reference category: <6 months), and reason for visit (Occasionally, Problem; reference category: Check-up).

In Block 2, environmental, experiential, and patient-dentist interaction variables were added, including anxiety related to the dental clinic environment, anxiety due to exposure to other patients expressing pain, history of negative dental experiences, preference for a specific dentist, sensory factors (disruption due to soft music, drill sound, and drill smell), waiting time (15–30 min, > 30 min; reference category: <15 min), and patient-dentist interaction factors (clear explanation of treatment reasons, respectful and dignified treatment, confidence in the patient-dentist relationship, absence of negative responses, attentiveness to patient concerns, sufficient time for discussion, patient involvement in decision-making, and provision of understandable answers).

The change in explained variance between Block 1 and Block 2 was assessed using the change in R² (ΔR²) to determine the additional contribution of environmental and patient-dentist interaction variables beyond sociodemographic and dental visit-related factors.

All categorical predictors with more than two categories were dummy-coded using the specified reference categories. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Socio-demographics

A total of 400 participants were included in the study. The sample was predominantly female (62.7%, n = 251), and most participants had completed university-level education (78.3%, n = 313). More than half of the participants reported a standard economic status (62.5%, n = 250), while a minority had dental insurance coverage (13.8%, n = 55).

Regarding dental attendance patterns, approximately half of the participants reported visiting a dentist due to a dental problem (49.8%, n = 199), whereas nearly one-third attended for routine check-ups (32.8%, n = 131). Most participants had visited a dentist within the past 6 months (39.5%, n = 158). Detailed sociodemographic characteristics and dental attendance characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Factors associated with dental anxiety

Bivariate analysis

Bivariate analyses were conducted to examine differences in DAS scores across sociodemographic, dental visit, environmental, and patient-dentist interaction variables.

A significant difference in DAS scores was observed by gender, with females reporting higher anxiety levels than males (Mann-Whitney U = 15790, Z = -2.62, p = 0.009). No significant differences were found across levels of education (Kruskal-Wallis H [2] = 1.18, p = 0.556), economic status (H [2] = 2.66, p = 0.265), or dental insurance status (U = 8198, Z = -1.63, p = 0.104).

DAS scores differed significantly according to the interval since the last dental visit (H [3] = 11.19, p = 0.011). Post hoc pairwise comparisons with the Bonferroni adjustment showed that participants who had not visited a dentist for 1–5 years had significantly higher anxiety scores than those who had visited within ≤ 6 months (adjusted p = 0.008). No other pairwise comparisons were significant.

Similarly, DAS scores varied significantly by reason for the last dental visit (H [2] = 15.36, p < 0.001). Participants who visited due to dental problems had higher anxiety scores compared to those attending for routine check-ups (adjusted p = 0.001) and occasional visits (adjusted p = 0.033), with no difference between routine and occasional visits (adjusted p = 1.000).

Environmental and sensory factors showed strong associations with dental anxiety. Participants reporting anxiety related to the dental clinic environment (U = 7882, Z = -7.92, p < 0.001) and those distressed by exposure to other patients expressing pain (U = 9783.5, Z = -8.70, p < 0.001) had significantly higher DAS scores. Similarly, disturbance due to the sound (U = 10095, Z = -8.27, p < 0.001) and smell (U = 12877.5, Z = -5.52, p < 0.001) of the drill was associated with higher anxiety, whereas disturbance due to soft music was not (p = 0.106).

Participants with a history of negative dental experiences had higher DAS scores (U = 14770.5, Z = -2.20, p = 0.028), while preference for a specific dentist was not associated with anxiety levels (p = 0.528).

Waiting time was significantly associated with DAS scores (H [2] = 13.38, p = 0.001). Post hoc analysis indicated that participants who waited 15–30 min reported higher anxiety than those waiting less than 15 min (adjusted p = 0.001), with no other significant differences.

Regarding patient-dentist interaction, several factors were associated with lower DAS scores, including clear explanation of treatment (U = 4757.5, Z = -2.94, p = 0.003), confidence in the dentist (U = 4086, Z = -2.66, p = 0.008), absence of negative responses (U = 9967.5, Z = -2.47, p = 0.013), being listened to (U = 2421.5, Z = -2.13, p = 0.033), sufficient discussion time (U = 6315, Z = -3.53, p < 0.001), involvement in decision-making (U = 6672.5, Z = -2.56, p = 0.011), and receiving understandable answers (U = 3837.5, Z = -3.29, p = 0.001). Being treated with respect and dignity was not significantly associated with DAS scores (p = 0.728). Detailed results are presented in Table 3.

Hierarchical multiple linear regression analysis

A hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of dental anxiety. Model 1, which included sociodemographic and dental visit variables, was statistically significant (F(11, 388) = 3.48, p < 0.001) and explained 9.0% of the variance in dental anxiety (R² = 0.09; Adjusted R² = 0.06).

When environmental, sensory, experiential, and patient-dentist interaction variables were added in Model 2, the model showed a significant improvement in fit (ΔR² = 0.34, F change (17, 371) = 13.06, p < 0.001).

This expanded model accounted for 43.1% of the variance in DAS scores (R² = 0.431; Adjusted R² = 0.388) and was statistically significant (F(28, 371) = 10.02, p < 0.001), demonstrating a substantial increase in explanatory power. The Durbin-Watson statistic was 2.03. Given the number of predictors included in the final model, the possibility of overfitting should be considered when interpreting the regression estimates.

In the final model, female patients had 0.846 units higher DAS scores than male patients (p = 0.003), indicating that they were more likely to experience higher dental anxiety. Educational attainment was not significantly associated with DAS scores (p > 0.05). Patients with dental insurance had DAS scores 1.315 units lower than those without insurance (p = 0.001), indicating lower levels of dental anxiety. Economic status was not significantly associated with DAS scores.

Regarding dental visit characteristics, patients whose previous dental visit was 1–5 years ago had DAS scores 0.681 units higher than those who visited within the past 6 months; however, this association did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.058). Other visit interval categories and reasons for visit were also not statistically significant.

Environmental and sensory factors were significantly associated with higher DAS scores. Patients who reported anxiety due to the dental clinic environment had 1.483 units higher DAS scores (p < 0.001), those who were anxious due to hearing other patients express pain had 1.539 units higher DAS scores (p < 0.001), and those disturbed by the sound of the drill had 1.345 units higher DAS scores (p < 0.001). Those disturbed by the smell of the drill had DAS scores 0.725 units higher (p = 0.021) than those not reporting these triggers. Waiting 15–30 min in the clinic was associated with 0.712-unit higher DAS scores than waiting less than 15 min (p = 0.019), indicating higher anxiety levels. Waiting more than 30 min was not statistically significant (p = 0.434). A history of negative dental experiences was not significantly associated with DAS scores in the adjusted model (p = 0.981).

Regarding patient-dentist interaction, patients whose dentist understandably explained dental care had 1.834-unit lower DAS scores (p = 0.001), and those who were involved in decision-making had 0.999-unit lower DAS scores (p = 0.031), indicating lower levels of dental anxiety. Other interaction variables, including being treated with respect and dignity (p = 0.051), having confidence in the dentist (p = 0.570), receiving negative responses (p = 0.415), being listened to when explaining problems (p = 0.117), being given sufficient time for discussion (p = 0.298), and receiving understandable answers (p = 0.644), were not statistically significant. Discussion

This study aimed to assess the association between dental anxiety and sociodemographic characteristics, dental attendance patterns, environmental and sensory factors, prior dental experiences, and patient-dentist interaction variables among adults attending tertiary care dental centres.

The findings indicate that the majority of participants reported low levels of dental anxiety; however, a clinically relevant proportion experienced moderate to high anxiety, highlighting that dental anxiety remains an important concern even within treatment-seeking populations [6, 26].

Female participants demonstrated significantly higher levels of dental anxiety compared to males, consistent with findings reported in previous literature [1, 16]. This difference may reflect a combination of biological, psychological, and sociocultural influences, as well as potential variations in the reporting and expression of fear [3, 11]. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as reporting bias may contribute to the observed gender differences [27].

In the present study, sociodemographic variables, including educational attainment and economic status, were not significantly associated with dental anxiety. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which have similarly reported a lack of significant association between dental anxiety and socioeconomic indicators after adjustment for other variables [1, 22]. This suggests that dental anxiety may be more strongly influenced by psychological, environmental, and experiential factors rather than purely socioeconomic characteristics [3, 12]. Comparable findings have also been reported in studies conducted in Iran and Pakistan [19, 28].

Dental attendance patterns showed a significant association with anxiety levels in the bivariate analysis, with individuals reporting longer intervals since their last dental visit demonstrating higher anxiety scores. This relationship may be explained by avoidance behaviour, whereby fear of dental procedures discourages regular attendance, potentially leading to delayed care and more invasive treatments, thereby reinforcing a cycle of fear and avoidance [29, 30]. However, this association did not remain statistically significant after adjustment in the multivariable model, suggesting that its effect may be mediated by other factors, particularly environmental and interaction-related variables.

Environmental and sensory factors emerged as strong and independent predictors of dental anxiety. Participants who reported anxiety related to the clinical environment, exposure to other patients expressing pain, and sensitivity to auditory and olfactory stimuli, particularly the sound and smell of dental drills, demonstrated significantly higher anxiety levels. These findings are consistent with existing literature highlighting the role of sensory stimuli as potent triggers of dental anxiety [12, 31]. In contrast, soft background music was not associated with increased anxiety in the present study, aligning with previous research suggesting that calming auditory stimuli may help reduce physiological stress responses during dental procedures [23].

Waiting time was also significantly associated with dental anxiety, with participants who waited 15–30 min reporting higher anxiety levels compared to those with shorter waiting times. A plausible explanation is that prolonged waiting may increase anticipatory anxiety and fear of impending procedures. While similar findings have been reported in previous studies, it has also been suggested that improvements in waiting area environments may help mitigate this effect [21].

Although participants with a history of negative dental experiences exhibited higher anxiety levels in the bivariate analysis, this association was not statistically significant in the adjusted model. This suggests that the impact of prior experiences may be mediated by current environmental conditions and the quality of patient-dentist interactions [5, 12]. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in the United States, India, and Pakistan, where the influence of past experiences diminished after controlling for other variables [17, 20, 32].

The present study further highlights the importance of patient-dentist interaction in influencing dental anxiety. Specifically, patients who received clear and understandable explanations of their treatment and those who were actively involved in decision-making demonstrated significantly lower anxiety levels. These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that effective communication enhances patient trust, improves treatment adherence, and reduces anxiety [5, 18, 33]. While other aspects of interaction, such as perceived respect, listening, and consultation time, were associated with anxiety in unadjusted analyses, they did not retain statistical significance in the final model, suggesting that clarity of communication and patient involvement may be the most critical components.

The relatively lower prevalence of high dental anxiety observed in this study may be partly attributable to the inclusion of patients already attending tertiary care dental centres, as individuals with severe anxiety may be more likely to avoid seeking care altogether [6, 29]. Nonetheless, the findings highlight that modifiable factors within the clinical environment and communication practices play a substantial role in shaping patient anxiety.

This study acknowledges several limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the observed associations. The use of non-probability convenience sampling from tertiary care centres may limit the generalisability of findings, particularly to individuals who avoid dental care or attend primary or rural healthcare settings. Additionally, data on dental experiences and patient–dentist interactions were self-reported and may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Although individuals with diagnosed psychological disorders were excluded, several potential confounders, such as baseline trait anxiety, pain sensitivity, type and invasiveness of dental procedures, oral health status, and health literacy, were not assessed and may have influenced the findings. The inclusion of multiple predictors in the regression model, despite an adequate sample size, raises the possibility of overfitting, which may affect the stability and generalisability of the estimates. Validation of these findings in independent samples is therefore warranted. Moreover, several environmental and patient-dentist interaction variables were assessed using single-item measures rather than validated composite scales, and some degree of measurement error cannot be excluded. Future research should incorporate formal psychometric validation of these constructs to enhance measurement precision and robustness.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths, including its multi-centre design, adequate sample size, and use of a validated measure of dental anxiety [25]. Importantly, the study provides context-specific evidence regarding modifiable factors associated with dental anxiety in tertiary care settings, which may inform targeted interventions to improve patient experience and treatment outcomes.

Conclusion

Dental anxiety in this population was more strongly associated with modifiable environmental, sensory, and patient-dentist interaction factors than with sociodemographic characteristics. Clinical surroundings, exposure to distressing stimuli, and the quality of communication, particularly clear explanations and patient involvement in decision-making, were significantly associated with DAS scores.

These findings support considering patient-centred communication practices and optimising the clinical environment as potentially relevant strategies for addressing dental anxiety. However, the observed relationships represent statistical associations rather than causal effects.

Future longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to determine whether targeted environmental and communication-based strategies can effectively reduce dental anxiety, particularly in resource-constrained settings.
