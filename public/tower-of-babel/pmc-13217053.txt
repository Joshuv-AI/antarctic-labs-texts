Improving childhood vaccination among minority populations in middle- and high-income countries: a realist review of health system interventions
Authors: Jumanah Essa-Hadad, Yanay Gorelik, Johanna Vervoort, Danielle Jansen, Michael Edelstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost minority populations in Europe generally exhibit lower childhood vaccine uptake compared with the general population. Improving uptake in these populations requires contextually tailored interventions. We conducted a realist review to identify interventions effective at improving measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) and human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine uptake among underserved communities.MethodsWe searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, Cochrane and ProQuest for English language publications from 2005 to 2022. Following title and abstract screening, full texts were assessed for relevance. Grey literature and reference lists were also examined. Data extraction and analysis were performed independently by two reviewers. Programme theories were generated from included articles and data extraction focusing on context-mechanism-outcome configurations.ResultsOf 1942 screened titles, 87 studies underwent full-text review of which 34 met inclusion criteria. 10 primary intervention categories were identified: parental and youth education; clinical outreach; data infrastructure/quality improvement; health provider training; school-based education; digital technology for patients; cash incentives; home visits; comic books; community leaders’ education; and consent policy changes. Analysis highlighting contextual factors enabling or hindering each intervention category’s success was conducted.ConclusionsMulticomponent strategies proved the most effective, with strongest evidence supporting home visits, parental and youth education, school-based programmes, data infrastructure and quality improvement and healthcare provider training. Moderate evidence supported reminders/recall strategies, comic books and consent policy changes, while digital technology for patients and cash incentives showed limited effectiveness. Importantly, there is no one-size-fits-all solution. Policymakers and practitioners should tailor and adapt interventions to the unique cultural, social and economic contexts of each group to ensure success.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42021268068.Keywords: VACCINATION, CHILD HEALTH, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Childhood vaccination is among the most cost-effective public health interventions, drastically reducing the spread of communicable diseases1 and health inequality worldwide.2 Between 2010 and 2018, measles vaccination alone prevented 23 million deaths.3 Human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines have shown promising results in decreasing HPV infection and related deaths.4

Despite the availability of measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) and HPV vaccines through national immunisation programmes across all European countries,5 some groups remain fully or partially unvaccinated (‘undervaccinated’), with disadvantaged groups frequently involved in outbreaks.2 As defined by the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), these disadvantaged populations include ethnic minorities, Traveller communities, religious minority communities, new migrants and asylum seekers, individuals living in high deprivation areas and homeless populations.6

Childhood vaccine uptake among minority and ethnic communities in Europe is generally lower than the general population, with studies reporting reduced rates in England (MMR),7 Greece (measles and routine childhood vaccines)8 and the Netherlands (HPV and routine vaccines among Turkish and Moroccan immigrants).9,11 Ethnic, religious or cultural minorities often face health system barriers, including restricted access, lack of information, digital exclusion, exclusion from population registers and culturally inappropriate vaccination, which contribute to lower vaccine uptake.12,15 This increases the vulnerability of these populations to outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases like measles and diphtheria.16,18

Interventions promoting childhood vaccination in disadvantaged and underserved populations can reduce health inequalities. A 2017 systematic review found educational programmes, telephone and post reminders and home visits can increase vaccine uptake among ethnic minorities, with varying degrees of effectiveness.19 Multicomponent, context-specific approaches are most successful. Another review found limited evidence that education and reminders improve HPV vaccine initiation and completion in minority populations.20 Other studies emphasised the need for additional research on intervention effectiveness across diverse populations and reducing missed HPV vaccination opportunities.21 Despite existing evidence, it remains unclear why certain interventions are effective in certain contexts but not others.

To maximise impact and resource efficiency, interventions should be tailored to the needs of specific disadvantaged, minority populations.19 As part of RIVER-EU (Reducing Inequalities in Vaccine uptake in the European Region – Engaging Underserved communities), a European Commission funded project aiming to improve access to vaccine services for children, reducing inequality and improving vaccine uptake of HPV and MMR vaccination in underserved communities in Europe, we conducted a realist review to identify effective interventions and factors influencing outcomes. Our realist review addressed two key questions: (1) What health system interventions improve HPV and MMR vaccine uptake in disadvantaged European communities with low vaccine uptake and (2) What mechanisms drive their success or failure?

Methods

Realist synthesis systematically reviews primary studies to identify, test and refine programme theories, offering insights into transferability of interventions across communities.22 Unlike traditional systematic reviews (SR), which aggregate quantitative evidence to assess effectiveness, realist reviews examine mechanisms driving outcomes through context-mechanism-outcome (CMO) configurations.23 This approach examines how and why interventions work (or not) within specific settings by analysing CMO patterns unique to realist analysis. It offers a deeper understanding of contextual influences that SRs often overlook. While SRs focus on quantitative aggregation, realist reviews integrate theoretical perspectives to explore causative pathways, combining qualitative and quantitative evidence to explain how interventions achieve effects, considering contextual factors such as cultural, organisational and socio-political environments.23

This realist review followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines24 and was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42021268068).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included health system intervention studies aimed at increasing routine MMR and HPV childhood vaccinations in underserved, disadvantaged populations in middle-to-high-income countries. Health systems were defined broadly per the WHO’s definition as organisations, people and actions intended to promote, restore or maintain target populations’ health.25 Disadvantaged groups, as defined by NICE, included minorities, migrants, refugees and isolated communities due to religious, ethnic or geographical reasons.6 Target populations in the interventions included: parents of children 0–18 years and adolescents (9–18 years) from disadvantaged populations, healthcare providers (HCPs) or clinics serving these communities. We searched English-language articles published between January 2010 and December 2021. Interventions implemented in low-income countries were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

We searched MEDLINE, Cochrane Collaboration, CINAHL, ProQuest and EMBASE databases. Reference and citation searches of all included references were performed using Google Scholar. To identify grey literature (government reports, dissertations, conference proceedings, policy reports or reports published by international organisations like WHO, UNICEF, European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control), repeated snowball searches were conducted using Google until the cut-off date of 23 December 2021.

A search strategy was developed in consultation with a medical librarian, combining Medical Subject Headings and keywords based on four key concepts: interventions, vaccinations (MMR and HPV), uptake and underserved/disadvantaged populations. Online supplemental appendix 1 presents the full search strategy. All retrieved publications were imported into Rayyan review manager, a free web tool for systematic reviews,26 for screening against the eligibility criteria. Duplicates were removed. An assessment of titles and abstracts was conducted for the remaining publications. For those records that passed the abstract stage, full-text reviews were conducted. Reference lists and related systematic reviews were also checked for relevant studies.

Study selection process

The literature search was conducted by JE-H. Two reviewers (JE-H and YG) independently screened titles and abstracts; studies meeting eligibility criteria proceeded to full-text review. Full texts of all potentially relevant articles were retrieved and assessed for relevance, with disagreements resolved through discussion with the lead principal investigator (ME). Following article selection, JE-H and YG completed data extraction and quality assessments.

Data extraction and analysis

Data were extracted from eligible studies into Microsoft Excel, including: author(s), year, country, target population, child age, study design, sample size, intervention type and setting, vaccine and outcomes. We also extracted data on underlying programme theories and CMOs, checked by the second author. Data extraction was conducted by two reviewers, JE-H and YG.

We analysed the included studies based on specific contextual factors and mechanisms. In line with realist methodology, we defined mechanisms as the underlying processes (trust, perceived access, norms) that are triggered by specific intervention strategies and shape vaccination decisions, rather than the intervention activities themselves. The outcomes of interest were childhood vaccine uptake rates (both initiation and completion), vaccine knowledge and awareness and intention to vaccinate. To synthesise the findings, we used an iterative process consistent with realist synthesis methodology. Interventions were grouped by type coded into CMO configurations. Each reviewer independently identified mechanisms and contextual factors explaining intervention success or failure in different settings. CMOs were then compared, discussed and refined through team meetings until agreement was reached; any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer. Coding decisions and refinements were documented to enhance transparency and reliability. The resulting programme theories were iteratively refined and tested against the full data set, producing a coherent explanatory model linking intervention strategies with the contextual conditions under which they are most effective.

We ascertained the level of evidence as low, moderate and high based on: (1) study design and quality; (2) number of studies and their agreement and (3) context of the evidence. The certainty of evidence rating of high, moderate and low was a subjective estimation based on these constructs.

Intervention categories

Parental and youth education

Most interventions to increase vaccine uptake in disadvantaged populations included education, targeting populations with similar contextual factors: low income, low education, lack of health insurance and limited English proficiency. These interventions improved vaccine uptake or completion,27,30 boosted vaccine intention2629 31,33 and raised knowledge among parents and youth.27 29 32 34 35

For minority populations, parent education programmes were more effective when delivered in native languages, as seen among Somali, Ethiopian, Hispanic, Chinese, American Indian and Asian-American minorities.33,37 Effectiveness increased when programmes were delivered by HCPs from the same cultural background, such as Promotoras (A Promotora is a lay Hispanic/Latino community member who receives specialised training to provide basic health education in the community, without being a professional healthcare worker)38 for Hispanic communities.34 37 Using trusted community figures and culturally relevant methods was key. Programmes combining education with referral and navigation support also showed strong potential.34

Multicomponent interventions involving multimedia campaigns and reminders increased HPV-vaccine knowledge in African American, Somali, Ethiopian and Hispanic populations without consistently boosting uptake.27 36 Storytelling by community members raised awareness among Hispanics and Cambodians in the USA,28 33 but this did not always improve vaccination rates.

A key finding was the necessity of using simplified educational materials, ideally at a fifth grade reading level, even in native languages.33 34

Reminders/recalls: clinical outreach

Vaccine reminders and recall messages have effectively increased childhood vaccination rates,19 targeting missed doses due to forgotten appointments. Common characteristics of target populations include minority status, low income, lack of insurance and Spanish-speaking backgrounds, with studies equally split between urban and rural settings.

Effectiveness of reminders for HPV vaccine uptake among minorities yielded mixed results. Rand and colleagues found text reminders more effective than phone calls among Hispanic urban populations in the USA.39 In contrast, email and text reminders in rural North Carolina had little impact on HPV uptake among low-income black and Hispanic youth. Another randomised controlled trial (RCT) showed success with Hispanics but not African Americans.40 Text reminders improved MMR vaccination rates in high-risk urban Hispanic children.37 Message framing mattered: emphasising Latina mothers’ protective role boosted vaccination intention.41

Since many interventions combined reminders with education, isolating the effect of reminders alone is difficult. One study using electronic HPV education plus reminder messages found no significant knowledge differences between intervention and control, suggesting that reminders alone drove increased vaccine completion.42

Data infrastructure and quality-improvement

Data infrastructure and quality-improvement strategies strengthened HCPs’ ability to efficiently deliver childhood vaccines to minority, low-income populations in rural and urban areas. Effective interventions included modernising electronic medical records, introducing automatic reminder systems, establishing in-clinic vaccine quality-improvement teams and developing screening tools.43 Computerised databases reduced provider workloads.44 45 Combining in-clinic teams with electronic alerts significantly boosted HPV vaccine initiation and uptake.46

HCP training

HCP training consistently increased vaccination rates across diverse contexts. Seven studies strengthened knowledge and communication skills among HCPs serving low-income, minority populations, mainly in urban areas.47,53 Training formats varied: 4-hour workshops,50 seven-session multicomponent courses,54 2-day courses,53 online modules,51 two half-day sessions,52 periodic staff training47 and weekly meetings.48 All improved providers’ knowledge and communication, several emphasising cultural awareness to increase confidence in addressing sensitive issues.50 51 Multiple studies reported significant increases in vaccination recommendations post-training.49 50

One US study emphasised a ‘shared concerns’ strategy to build trust, rapport and enhance communication with disadvantaged populations. This approach improved providers’ understanding of parental concerns and developed effective talking points, making discussions around vaccinations more comfortable.50

A seven-session multicomponent intervention combining interprofessional education, communication training, data feedback and systemic change, improved vaccination rates, with sustained gains over 6–18 months.55 Providers were encouraged to share personal stories, leverage long-term parent relationships and use simple language. Post-intervention audio recordings of patient encounters showed a 20% rise in ‘indicated’ vaccine recommendations, reflecting self-reported improvements in communication.54

School-based education

Delivering translated vaccine education in schools increases minority adolescents’ knowledge and parental informed consent. Schools provide an ideal setting to reach large groups of teens quickly. In Scotland, a translated Polish pamphlet boosted vaccine consent form returns, with 7.4% higher uptake in three pilot schools versus controls without translated materials. On-site school-based vaccination programmes, paired with physician-led HPV education, show greater potential for increasing vaccine uptake among underserved minority populations compared with education-only approaches.56

Patient-focused digital technology

Patient-focused digital technology, including smartphones, tablets, interactive online tools and mobile applications, is feasible for raising vaccine awareness in low-income, minority populations in disadvantaged urban areas. These interventions were interactive and often provided tailored feedback. However, results were mixed. For instance, a customised web-based intervention for Latino teens in the USA increased vaccination intentions but did not significantly increase HPV vaccine uptake.57 Similarly, a computer-based intervention led to higher HPV uptake among minority, disadvantaged teens, though not statistically significant.58

Cash incentives

Cash incentives have been used to boost vaccination uptake, with mixed results.53 Adolescents, parents and healthcare providers have all been targeted. One study found cash incentives positively impacted HPV vaccine initiation and series completion among disadvantaged, low-income minority adolescents in urban areas.59 However, financial incentives were viewed negatively by parents and HCPs, sometimes as coercive or unethical.60 These perceptions highlight the need to consider context and target audience before implementing such interventions.

Home visits

Home visit interventions consistently demonstrated effectiveness in minority, low-income populations.54 61 62 A programme targeting First Nations parents in Canada increased vaccination rates and participation in parenting support groups by connecting families to community resources.61 Similarly, a home visit programme for low-income Hispanic mothers, delivered by a Spanish-speaking lay health educator, boosted HPV vaccination among teens.54 In Sweden, a six-session programme for immigrant, low-educated mothers significantly improved MMR vaccination rates.62

Home visits foster trust by building strong relationships between HCPs and parents, using a family-centred approach that enhances parenting confidence, knowledge and awareness, especially valuable for minority populations facing systemic barriers to healthcare access. Hispanic participants, for instance, preferred visits from providers of the same cultural background. Extended home visit programmes, when resources allow, further strengthen trust, reduce emergency room visits and enhance overall care access.62

Comic books

Comic books effectively increased HPV vaccine knowledge and awareness among minority Somali and Ethiopian teens in the USA.63 Key improvements included comfort discussing vaccination with parents (58% vs 90%), willingness to vaccinate (38% vs 83%) and vaccination intention (34% vs 86%), with larger effects among male adolescents.

The English-language comic book featured a culturally relevant Somali female character who learns about HPV vaccination at school health and discusses it with peers and family. Despite its effectiveness in improving knowledge and intention, this approach was only tested in a single study with a small sample size, limiting generalisability.

Consent policy changes

Among minority populations where parents may face language or literacy barriers, changing consent requirements can support HPV vaccination for young women in disadvantaged families, particularly when offered as part of the school system. One study found that allowing parent verbal rather than written consent reduced the number of unvaccinated teens from ethnic groups in the most deprived areas.30

Discussion

This realist review analysed interventions designed to increase HPV and MMR vaccine uptake among disadvantaged, minority populations. From 36 included studies, 10 categories of interventions were identified: parental/youth education, clinical outreach, data infrastructure and quality improvement, health provider/community leader training, school-based education, patient-focused digital technology interventions, cash incentives, home visits, comic books and consent policy changes. A key contribution of this review is the identification of contextual factors and mechanisms that drive intervention success.

Despite heterogeneity in the populations, disadvantaged minority groups often shared characteristics such as rurality, low education, low socio-economic status and discrimination, all of which impact childhood vaccination.64 The findings highlight that some elements are universally important for intervention success to improve childhood vaccine uptake, including trust in the system, simplified language and access to services. However, population identity is layered and complex, meaning interventions are not always transferable across groups. For example, African Americans in the USA differ significantly from Somali immigrants in Sweden, who may not share language or cultural experiences, especially if they are first-generation immigrants. A one-size-fits-all approach is inadequate for addressing the unique barriers faced by these communities. Instead, context-specific interventions tailored to the needs, preferences and cultural characteristics of the target populations were found to be more successful in achieving improved vaccine coverage. Universal versus context-specific factors in vaccine uptake interventions

The highest level of evidence was found for interventions combining at least two strategies, regardless of their components and contextual elements. These interventions were more likely to increase vaccine uptake rather than just knowledge levels or intention to vaccinate. For instance, one intervention incorporated parental education and reminders,36 while another combined reminders, routine recommendations, patient reminders, educational materials, incentives and training, with similarly positive results.47 Comprehensive, multilevel strategies targeting both parents/adolescents and HCPs appear to have the greatest impact on uptake, consistent with previous studies.19 20 Extended interventions also proved more effective than brief, one-off activities, regardless of contextual factors. Longer follow-up periods may better capture changes in vaccination rates, especially for series completion.61

The most promising interventions included parental and youth education, school-based programmes, community leaders’ education, comic books, HCPs training and home visits. These interventions were effective across various contexts and often interlinked. Parent/adolescent education was most impactful when led by a local HCP or community leader, in the group’s native language, culturally tailored and conducted over multiple sessions. Parental knowledge gaps remain a barrier to vaccination.53,5564 Although education on safety, efficacy and side effects can reduce hesitancy, increased knowledge alone often does not translate into higher uptake. Thus, combining strategies in multicomponent interventions is usually necessary to achieve vaccine uptake. HCP training has improved vaccination rates across contexts.68 A key barrier to vaccination in disadvantaged populations is the lack of HCP recommendations.6769,72 Provider-directed strategies increase knowledge and awareness, providing professional orientation to help HCPs communicate more effectively and confidently with their patients in a culturally sensitive way, particularly regarding HPV vaccine which can be sensitive in some communities.73 74 Data infrastructure and quality improvement strategies improved HCPs and clinics’ ability to provide childhood vaccines in a timely manner. The strategies that were successful in increasing child vaccine rates among minority populations included updated and working electronic medical records and automatic reminder systems, establishing vaccine quality improvement teams within the clinics and developing screening tools within the clinics. Computerising databases eased the workload of providers.43 46 In-clinic quality improvement teams increased initiation of the HPV vaccine. A significant increase in HPV vaccine rates was seen.

Interventions focusing on reminder/recall messages, written consent and comic books showed moderate promise. The effectiveness of reminder and recall messages was highly context-dependent and stronger combined as part of a multicomponent intervention than used alone. The type of message received and the language is also important. Hence, such interventions may not necessarily be transferrable to other disadvantaged populations. Further research is required to ascertain benefits. Written parent consent requirements can be a barrier. Local policy changes allowing verbal consent from parents and adolescents to self-consent overcame some of the barriers to vaccination of adolescent girls belonging to families less likely to respond to paper-based consent methods and at greater risk of developing cervical cancer. Since only one study examined this intervention among disadvantaged, minority populations,30 more research is needed to determine level of effectiveness.

Low evidence

Was found as to the effectiveness of patient-focused digital technology interventions among disadvantaged, minority population. Although feasible and acceptable, they did not increase vaccine uptake, consistent with prior findings that computer programmes show no effect on vaccine behaviour.19 75 Similarly, evidence for cash incentives was limited for parents and HCPs, though moderate for adolescent minorities. However, sustainability is a concern for long-term use of cash incentives, and the limited number of studies highlights the need for further research to assess their effectiveness.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, most studies targeted minority and disadvantaged populations in the USA, whose cultural, ethnic and religious backgrounds differ significantly from disadvantaged groups in Europe. Although the US healthcare system differs from Europe’s, child vaccinations are offered at no cost to disadvantaged groups, such as the uninsured, underinsured, Medicaid-eligible and American Indian or Alaska Native populations, through the Center for Disease Control’s (CDC) Vaccines for Children programme. Therefore, while healthcare system differences may influence vaccination, their impact is likely less significant than cultural and systemic factors.

Second, there is substantial heterogeneity within these minority groups. Effective interventions must account for the intersection of racial, ethnic, cultural and socio-economic factors, including urban/rural context, integration level and language barriers. For instance, second-generation US-born Hispanics may differ from first-generation immigrants facing language challenges. A one-size-fits-all approach is unlikely to succeed given these diverse needs, requiring tailored interventions.

Another limitation of the available evidence is the lack of cost-effectiveness data, which is very important for health policymakers when prioritising interventions.

Lastly, although based on specific criteria, the certainty of evidence level rating of high, moderate and low was a subjective estimation based on these constructs.

Conclusions

Improving vaccination in underserved minority populations requires identifying effective interventions and addressing context-specific barriers and enablers. Multicomponent strategies proved the most effective, with the strongest evidence supporting home visits, parental and youth education, school-based programmes, data infrastructure and quality improvement initiatives and healthcare provider training. Moderate evidence was found for reminders/recall strategies, comic books and consent policy changes. Patient-focused digital technology interventions and cash incentives showed limited effectiveness in increasing vaccine uptake. Given the limited research on certain interventions, further studies are needed. Importantly, there is no one-size-fits-all solution. Interventions must be tailored and adapted to the unique cultural, social and economic contexts of each group to ensure success.
