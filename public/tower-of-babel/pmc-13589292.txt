Adolescents’ Experiences of Online Harms by Platform
Authors: Angela Y. Lee, Carmen H. J. Lim, Sunny Xun Liu, R. Kang Xing Jin, Anneke Buffone, Ravi Iyer, Zachary Rausch, Jonathan Haidt, Nathanael J. Fast, Jeffrey T. Hancock, Eleni Linos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow often do US adolescents report having negative experiences online, and does prevalence vary across different online platforms?FindingsThis cross-sectional pilot survey of 472 US adolescents aged 13 to 17 years found that reported negative online experiences varied substantially across platforms.MeaningThese findings provide baseline, platform-level prevalence estimates that may inform future measurement and monitoring of adolescents’ online experiences.

Introduction

Concerns about children’s online safety have spurred efforts among parents, policymakers, and technology companies to reduce risks. A growing literature has examined adolescents’ exposure to online harms, including probability-based, age-stratified work that compares online and offline victimization (eg, Pew Research Center, EU Kids Online, and Common Sense Media). Several opportunities remain to strengthen measurement in this area. First, existing studies tend to focus on one category of harm at a time (eg, cyberbullying and sexual solicitation) rather than the full range of harms young people may encounter. Assessing multiple harm types in a single survey may facilitate comparisons across harm categories. Second, harm prevalence estimates rarely differentiate between specific platforms (ie, comparing cyberbullying on TikTok to Instagram). Platform-specific estimates may inform targeted prevention efforts. Studies including younger adolescents (aged 13 to 14 years) rarely combine age-stratified reporting with broad, cross-platform harm assessment; doing so may clarify how reported harms relate to youth development.

To help address these gaps, we conducted a national survey estimating the prevalence of reported negative experiences among US adolescents online, offline, and by platform in a representative probability-based survey of US youth aged 13 to 17 years. The goal of this study was to demonstrate that such assessment is feasible, enabling the detection of platform-specific patterns.

Methods

We conducted a nationally representative, probability-based cross-sectional survey of US adolescents aged 13 to 17 years between February and December 2025. This was fielded through the Understanding America Study (UAS) panel, which is maintained by the Center for Economic and Social Research at the University of Southern California. The panel was weighted to be nationally representative for adolescents aged 13 to 17 years (Table 1). Participants were recruited from UAS household panel members with parental permission and teen assent. All study protocols were approved by the University of Southern California institutional review board.

The survey collected information on demographic characteristics, use of online platforms, and negative experiences online and offline (eg, bullying and sexual solicitation). These were assessed for offline experiences and across 20 online communication platforms, including Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok, YouTube, Discord, and Pinterest (up to 102 total items, conditional on skip logic). Platforms with fewer than 100 users were not reported. Survey items were adapted from existing instruments including the University of Southern California Neely Indices and Meta’s Bad Experiences and Encounters Framework survey, standardized to a 4-week recall window. The adapted instrument was cognitively tested with 6 in-person interviews and piloted online with approximately 500 respondents aged 18 to 25 years. Household income, education, and state of residence were obtained from UAS.

The study was not preregistered and no a priori hypotheses were made; all analyses were exploratory. No a priori sample size was calculated; the achieved sample reflects the full set of eligible UAS Teen panel members during the study period. Analyses used UAS survey-specific poststratification weights, correcting for unequal selection probabilities, panel nonresponse, and deviations from the US population on demographic characteristics, including age, sex, race, and ethnicity. To identify demographic correlates of reported harm exposure, we fit separate survey-weighted logistic regression models, regressed on demographic characteristics, platforms used daily, and parental-control status, for each harm outcome using the survey package in R version 4.4 (R Foundation). See eMethods in Supplement 1 for details. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Results

The final weighted sample included 472 participants (mean [SE] age, 14.9 [0.1] years; 46.8% female; 1.7% American Indian or Alaska Native; 5.3% Asian; 14.6% Black; 1.5% Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, 65.4% White, and 10.9% multiple races; 24.5% Hispanic) from 47 states (response rate, 96.3%; completion rate, 98.7%; median completion time, 13.8 minutes). Table 1 shows weighted demographic characteristics overall and by users of each platform. The mean (SE) age at first acquiring a personal smartphone was 11.1 (0.1) years; 91.5% of adolescents had their own smartphone, 26.9% reported parental controls on their smartphone, and 96.1% used at least 1 of the social media platforms. The most commonly used social media platforms were YouTube (77.1%), Instagram (55.1%), TikTok (54.4%), and Snapchat (51.2%). Adolescents from families with highest socioeconomic status (>$150 000 annual income) were more likely to have parental controls (46.2%).

Table 2 shows weighted percentages of adolescents that reported experiencing each specific harm in the last 4 weeks: overall, online, offline, and on each platform. Across both online and offline contexts, 44.7% witnessed harassment, and 29.8% reported experiencing harassment directly. Online exposure exceeded offline exposure for several harms, including seeing violent imagery (10.7% vs 1.6%), unwanted sexual images (13.4% vs 2.0%), and content encouraging self-harm (9.7% vs 5.2%). More than half (51.5%) reported sleep disruption due to technology use. Parental education, income, and parental controls on smartphones were not significantly associated with online harm experiences.

Between-platform variation was substantial, and harms concentrated on specific platforms. For instance, 9.1% of individuals using Instagram reported seeing violent imagery, compared to 1.9% of individuals using YouTube. Feeling worse about oneself due to other people’s posts was most common on Instagram (16.6%) and TikTok (13.4%) compared to YouTube (4.1%) and text messaging (5.6%). A total of 42.2% of individuals using TikTok and 27.8% of individuals using Instagram attributed disrupted sleep to those platforms, compared with 10.2% of individuals using text messaging. Some subgroups were more likely to experience certain harms online (Table 3), with sex and age correlated with different harms (see the eMethods in Supplement 1 for details).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study found that reports of negative online experiences were common among US adolescents and varied by platform. Reported exposure to negative content was more common online than offline for some harms: more than 6 times more common for both violent imagery and unwanted sexual images, and 2 times more for content encouraging self-harm. Notably, more than half of adolescents reported that their use of technology interfered with their sleep, 1 in 10 had seen someone harm themselves online, and 1 in 8 had seen unwanted sexual images online in the last 4 weeks. However, direct interpersonal harassment was reported at comparable rates offline and online, consistent with prior literature showing that online and offline victimization often occur within the same social relationships. Reported harm exposure also differed by adolescent demographics. Together, these findings highlight the importance of considering heterogeneity by different subpopulations and across different online spaces.

In addition, we demonstrated that cross-platform, multiharm assessment is achievable in a nationally representative adolescent sample. Strengths include detailed platform-specific assessments and a nationally representative survey that can be replicated at scale.

Limitations

This study should be interpreted with respect to its limitations. First, this was a modest sample that, while weighted to the US adolescent population, limits precision for platform-specific and subgroup analyses. Estimates should be treated as preliminary, particularly for less commonly used platforms and low-prevalence outcomes where subgroup sample sizes are small. Second, as this is an observational study, the differences in prevalence across platforms may partly reflect differences in the user populations rather than platform design alone. For example, exposure to content that makes one feel bad about oneself was more prevalent on Pinterest than on Discord. While this could reflect stronger content moderation policies or safer design decisions on Discord, it could also reflect Pinterest’s greater popularity among girls, who may be more susceptible to social comparison than boys. Third, the study’s reliance on self-report is subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, and the limitations of asking individuals to retrospectively recall online experiences over a 4-week window. Online interactions are frequent and often ambient; more emotionally salient events may be disproportionately likely to be remembered, which may mean estimates reflect memorable rather than representative experiences. Availability heuristics may also lead adolescents to attribute experiences to platforms they use most rather than where the harm occurred, which may inflate estimates for high-use platforms. Stigmatized experiences (eg, sending explicit images and unwanted sexual contact) may also be underreported. Selection through parental UAS participation and rapid shifts in platform design and policy may limit generalizability over time. Harms reported may vary based on device or account safety settings and whether the individual registered with their true age. Larger studies, akin to other public health monitoring programs, are needed to examine these trends as policies and norms change.

Conclusions

The results of this study suggest that among approximately 22 million US adolescents aged 13 to 17 years, roughly 3 million reported seeing unwanted sexual content in a 4-week period and more than 10 million reported experiencing sleep disruption. This study provides baseline prevalence estimates of negative online experiences across platforms and demographic groups. Similar measurement is already in place for other public health risks for youth, such as the Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System for substance use, sexual behavior, and violence. Although stakeholders may disagree on how to reduce these harms, we still need a consistent way to measure them to evaluate what is working. Understanding the evolving experiences of US adolescents is essential to support evidence-based interventions to protect youth in an increasingly digital world.
