Determinants of food insecurity among slum households of Khulna Division, Bangladesh
Authors: Suvasish Das Shuvo, Ambika Charan Mandal, Asma Khatun, Abu Bashar, Deepa Roy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
​Food insecurity remains a significant challenge in Bangladesh’s urban slums, which are still considered neglected communities. This study aimed to explore the factors associated with food insecurity and dietary diversity status among slum households of Khulna Division, Bangladesh. This study employed a cross-sectional design, incorporating a household survey and key informant face-to-face interviews. A multi-stage sampling technique was used to select Bangladeshi slum dwellers (n = 749) ≥ 18 years old from five districts residing in the Khulna Division. Data were collected using a pretested structured questionnaire. The household vulnerability to food insecurity indicator and dietary diversity status were used to assess the level of vulnerability to food insecurity. A logit model was used to identify the determinants of vulnerability to food insecurity in slum households. In this study, about 74.6% of the households were mild-to-moderate (MMFI) food insecure while 22.1% experienced severe food insecurity (SFI). Similarly, above 42% of the household respondents had low household dietary diversity (HDDS) and moderate HDDS. We revealed that monthly income, occupation, household size, anxiety and uncertainty, inadequate quality, and insufficient food intake were significantly associated with household dietary diversity and food insecurity status. Additionally, household respondents with MMFI and SFI status have significantly more experience with potential risk indicators of low and moderate HDDS. The study highlighted that in slum areas, households have higher levels of food insecurity and low household dietary diversity status, which may create a negative impact on their mental health and well-being. Therefore, the ongoing and future governments should prioritize supporting vulnerable communities to ensure food security by providing food packages and reducing food prices.

Introduction

The global urban landscape is transforming rapidly, with nearly 68% of the world’s population expected to live in urban areas by 20501. This swift and large-scale urbanization is leading to increased demand for affordable housing, infrastructure, employment, and essential services2. Bangladesh is also undergoing rapid urbanization, with 40% of its population currently living in cities, many of whom settle in slums (‘bastes’), which lack proper infrastructure and basic amenities3,4. According to the National Food Policy of Bangladesh, urban slum dwellers are the most vulnerable group to ensure food security5. According to the United Nations Habitat and the Global Food Security Index, one-third of people in urban areas in slower-middle-income countries live in slums6, and Bangladesh is 80th out of 113 nations worldwide7. In LMICs like Bangladesh, about 51 million people experienced moderate to severe food insecurity8.The recent COVID-19 outbreak affected approximately 60 million poor urban residents across Bangladesh, causing widespread distress and unprecedented challenges to food security9. Surprisingly, more than half of slum households in Dhaka, Bangladesh, experience food insecurity due to their failure to meet the minimum energy requirement per day5. These findings indicate that the food security status of slum households in Bangladesh has not significantly improved over time. Food security is inherently multi-dimensional, encompassing food availability, accessibility, and utilization, which can help identify households at risk of food insecurity4,10. People living in urban slums are particularly vulnerable to severe socio-economic challenges, including limited income opportunities, poor living conditions, and inadequate access to essential services11. Additionally, urban slum inhabitants frequently have to pay more for housing, transportation, and medical care, which makes it harder for them to maintain a healthy, varied diet12. The economic downturn and the resulting rise in poverty, along with the dramatic increase in food prices, indicate that there is a significant food gap among slum dwellers13. The human capital of slum household members is impacted by several other factors linked to household food security (HFS), including socioeconomic position, family income, and family size; these factors can also be linked to worse economic access to food14,15. A study conducted in India revealed that rapid urbanization, overcrowding, and a lack of job prospects are the main causes of food insecurity for households in urban slums16. Among these challenges, household food insecurity and dietary diversity have emerged as pressing issues, significantly impacting the health and well-being of slum dwellers12,17. Thus, FI during this period results in not only food-related health outcomes, such as micronutrient deficiency and poor health outcomes, but also consists of anxiety, feelings of distress and deprivation, as well as adverse changes in family and social interactions18,19. This contextual concern has led most studies to focus on household food insecurity in the slum areas of the megacity of Dhaka, Bangladesh5,19–22. To the best of our knowledge, very few studies have examined household food insecurity in slum regions of other divisions in Bangladesh17,23. However, in Khulna Division, only a limited number of studies have been conducted, primarily focusing on household food insecurity among children, women, and climate migrants, who face unique socio-economic and environmental challenges24–26. Urban slums, marked by complex socio-economic disparities, environmental challenges, and inadequate access to essential resources, are especially prone to food insecurity and child malnutrition. These conditions highlight the necessitating close monitoring27. Therefore, this study aims to assess the determinants of food insecurity and dietary diversity status among slum households in Khulna Division, Bangladesh. By integrating food security (HFIAS) with dietary outcomes (HDDS) and employing sensitivity analysis to validate self-reported food insecurity, this study will enhance the credibility of food security assessments in marginalized urban slum communities. On top of that, in recent years, the government of Bangladesh has been trying to change the National Food Policy (NFP) and concentrating on these vulnerable urban slum dwellers to safeguard their fundamental right to food to meet the Sustainable Development Goal of zero hunger, sustainable cities and communities5. This study will be useful for policymakers to effectively implement food policy-based interventions and provide diversified food options among underprivileged slum dwellers, which may assist in improving mental and physical health among low-income slum communities in Bangladesh.

Methods

Study settings and data collection techniques

This study was conducted in the slum areas of Khulna Division, one of the eight administrative divisions in Bangladesh. Khulna division is a representative area with significant urban poverty and a large population (130,863) living in slums28,29. This division was chosen due to their diverse socio-economic conditions, environment challenges, varying levels of urbanization and limited access to resources, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of food insecurity among low-income urban populations30. A multistage sampling method was used to select households for the study from November to December 2023. In the first stage, five districts (Jashore, Khulna, Narail, Jhenaidaha, and Kusthia) from the Khulna division (Fig. 1) were listed using a random sampling method31. Within each district, specific slum areas were identified and selected using a stratified random sampling method to ensure representation across geographical and socio-economic strata. The selected slum areas included Rupsha Nutan Bazar and Railway slum in Khulna district; Railway slum of Arabpur and Churamonkati in Jashore; Lohagara and Narail Sadar Upazila slums in Narail; Lahinipara (near the Gorai River) and Railway slum in Kushtia; and Mohishakundu Sardarpara slum and Municipality slum area in Jhenaidah. The diversity in location and local contexts provides a broader perspective on regional disparities and challenges affecting slum dwellers in the Khulna division. Lastly, a simple random sampling technique was applied to select households from each slum.

The sample size was determined using Cochran’s formula for cross-sectional studies (Cochran, 1977): n = Z2×P (1 - P)/e2 where n = sample size, Z = Z-value (1.96 for 95% confidence level), P = estimated prevalence of food insecurity (assumed to be 50% due to lack of precise data), e = margin of error (4%). Using this formula, the sample size is approximately 600 households. To account for possible non-responses, 10% was added, resulting in a final sample size of 749 households.

Eligible participants included household heads (male or female) and primary caregivers responsible for food procurement and preparation. The inclusion criteria were households residing in the slum for at least 6 months, aged 18 years and above, and willing to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were households not involved in food procurement or preparation and households unwilling to participate.

A pretested structured questionnaire was used to assess household food insecurity and dietary diversity, incorporating standardized tools such as the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) and the Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS). The pretest was conducted in the Sankarpur slum area of Jashore district, which was not included in the final sample. Expert review ensured content relevance and clarity, followed by a pretest among 30 randomly selected slum households excluded from the final study. The pilot testing evaluated reliability and comprehensibility, leading to minor modifications for clarity and cultural appropriateness. Cronbach’s alpha (> 0.7) confirmed the internal consistency of key sections before full-scale data collection32. Data collection was conducted by trained enumerators with prior experience in field surveys. Enumerators visited selected households and conducted face-to-face interviews with household heads or primary caregivers. Enumerators were provided with detailed training on administering the survey instruments, ethical considerations, and handling sensitive questions about food insecurity. Each interview was expected to last about 30–45 min.

Measures

Outcome measures

Household food insecurity access scale (HFIAS)

Food insecurity was measured using the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) and Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS). The HFIAS was developed by the Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance (FANTA) project, United States Agency for International Development (USAID). The HFIAS, a perception-based is a widely used tool for assessing the access component of food insecurity and consists of nine standardized questions that capture experiences of food insecurity over the past 30 days, categorized into mild, moderate, and severe food insecurity33. Each household was assigned a score based on the frequency of food insecurity experiences.

The total score for the nine items, ranging from 0 to 27, represents the household’s degree of food insecurity, with higher scores indicating greater food insecurity and lower scores reflecting food security. The HFIAS indicator guide provides a detailed methodology for calculating these scores. In this analysis, households experiencing mild and moderate food insecurity were grouped into a single category labeled mild-to-moderate food insecurity. As a result, the final dataset comprised three distinct household types: food security, mild-to-moderate food insecurity, and severe food insecurity households18,34. To assess the validity of this perception-based measure, we conducted a sensitivity analysis, examining the internal consistency of HFIAS scores and their association with dietary diversity. This helped ensure the robustness of food security classification in our study population.

Household dietary diversity score (HDDS)

Household food access to and consumption were assessed using the Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS), which records the number of different food groups consumed by the household in the previous 24 hours35. The HDDS includes 12 food groups (cereals, white tubers and roots, vegetables, fruits, meat, eggs, fish and other seafood, legumes, nuts and seeds, milk and milk products, oils and fats, sweets, spices, condiments, and beverages), and households will be scored based on their diversity of food consumption as recommended by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)36. The HDDS was calculated using a minimum consumption of at least half a serving of one food from each mentioned food groups. A score of 1 was given for each food group consumed, and the maximum score was 12. Cut-off points for household dietary diversity categories are lowest dietary diversity (≤ 3 food groups), medium dietary diversity (4 to 5 food groups), and high dietary diversity (≥ 6 food groups)36. While diet diversity is often used as a supplemental indicator of food security, we did not incorporate a calorie-based approach due to the study’s scope and methodological constraints. Instead, HDD was selected for its practicality, relevance in low-resource settings, and established link with food security and nutritional adequacy. To increase the credibility of dietary diversity measurement, extreme or implausible 24-hour dietary recall data were carefully reviewed. If a household reported an unusually high or low diversity score, the data were cross-checked with socio-economic conditions and food security status. In cases where discrepancies suggested a reporting error or temporary food consumption anomaly (e.g., due to religious festivals, social events, or crisis situations), the data were flagged and sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess their impact on results.

Explanatory variables

Socio-demographic information

The socio-demographic status such as geographic location, gender (male/female), age (20–30, > 30–40, and > 40 years), education level (illiterate, primary, and secondary and above), occupation of the household head (farmer, day laborer, serviceman/maid, small businessman, and others), Family income per month (< 10000 BDT, 10000–15000 BDT, > 15000–20000 BDT, and > 20000 BDT), household size (≤ 4/>4), marital status (unmarried/married), duration in slums (< 5 years, 5–15 years, and > 15 years), anxiety and uncertainty about the household food supply, inadequate quality of food, and insufficient food intake were collected through pre-coded questionnaire. This questionnaire was adapted from prior studies on food insecurity in Bangladesh37.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for STATA (Version 14.0) was used for statistical analysis. Qualitative variables were described using frequency and percentage. Additionally, separate multinomial logit models were employed to examine the factors associated with food insecurity and household dietary diversity (HDD), following the standard approach for analyzing unordered discrete outcome models. The primary outcome variables included food insecurity status (FS, MMFI, and SFI) and HDDS status (high HDDS, medium HDDS, and low HDDS). For food insecurity (HFIAS categories), the reference category was food secure (FS) and dietary diversity (HDDS categories), the reference category was high dietary diversity (high HDDS). These models allow for the inclusion of categorical dependent variables, enabling a comprehensive analysis of associated factors. All potential determinants of food insecurity and HDDS status were included in the models, which were adjusted for geographic area, gender, age, education level, occupation, monthly family income, household size, marital status, and duration of residence in slums, all of which could influence household food security and dietary diversity. Independent variables were selected based on their theoretical relevance, prior literature, and potential confounding effects on the relationship between food insecurity and dietary diversity. The strength of associations was reported as relative risk ratios (RRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), along with corresponding p-values for each variable. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, while the Wald test was applied to evaluate the significance of individual variables. A two-sided statistical significance test was conducted, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in the multinomial logistic regression models.

Results

Socio-economic characteristics of the study sample

A total of 749 low-income households participated in this study; most of the respondents were from Jashore (22.4%), Khulna (21.2%), and Jhenaidha (20.9%) districts. Similarly, those aged between 20 and 30 years and 31–40 years were 55.4% and 22.6%, respectively. The male and female respondents were 47.3% and 52.7%. Most respondents were illiterate (49.1%) and completed the primary level of education (33.4%). Furthermore, in the occupation status, most of the participants were day laborers (38.2%) and had low-income levels ranging between < 10000 (42.2%) and 10000–15000 (42.5%) BDT/month, respectively. Regarding marital status, the largest proportion of the respondents were married (98%) and 65.4% of household respondents had ≤ 4 family members in each household. A majority of the participants living duration in slums was > 15 years (67.8%), and 44.5% had low HDDS, whereas only 13.2% had high HDDS (Table 1).

The proportion of food insecurity status and household dietary diversity score status in the study area

In the bivariate analyses, levels of food security were significantly associated with geographic location (p = < 0.001), occupation (p = 0.015), and monthly family income (p = 0.002) (Table 1). The proportion of households experiencing moderate food insecurity (MMFI) was significantly higher in Jashore (85.1%) and Narail (80.6%). In contrast, the prevalence of severe food insecurity was greater in Jhenaidha (31.8%) and Khulna (29.6%). In terms of occupation, the highest rates of MMFI were observed among respondents working as servants/maids (80.4%) and day laborers (74.5%), while the highest rates of severe food insecurity (SFI) were found among those who were businessmen (32.8%) and farmers (26.9%). Households with a monthly income of less than 10,000 Taka and between 10,000 and 15,000 Taka were predominantly MMFI (76.3% and 73.3%) and SFI (21.5% and 22.9%), respectively.

Table 2 outlines the individual factors and their relationship with different levels of household dietary diversity status. This study found that the highest rates of LHDDS were observed in the Jashore and Kushtia districts, with 55.9% and 53.5%, respectively. In contrast, the highest prevalence of MHDDS was recorded in Jhenaidah (45.2%), followed by Jashore (43.5%) and Khulna (47.2%) (p < 0.001). In terms of family income, those who had an overall income between 10000 and 15000 BDT had a lower household dietary diversity score of 49.1% whereas people having income less than 10,000 BDT had LHDDs 45.2% (p < 0.001). Among the slum households with more than four family members, 50.6% were classified as having a low household dietary diversity score (LHDDS) and 40.1% as having a MHDDS, with the difference being statistically significant (p < 0.05). Among the study participants, those who had been living in the slums for 5–15 years and more than 15 years had LHDDS of 47.2% and 44.9%, respectively. Additionally, respondents who had lived in the slums for 5–15 years had a MHDDS of 47.2% (p < 0.05).

Table 3 displays the level of household food insecurity among slum dwellers as well as the conditions of the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS). The results of our study indicate that just 3.3% of households had food security (FS), while roughly 74.6% and 22.1% of households experienced mild-to-moderate food insecurity (MMFI) and severe food insecurity (SFI), respectively. Table 3 also revealed that the largest proportion, in a total of 94.6% of households had experienced anxiety and uncertainty about their food supply, more than 94% had inadequate food quality and 88% had insufficient food intake among slum dwellers respectively.

Factors associated with the household’s food insecurity and dietary diversity status among slum dwellers

In the multivariate logistic regression model, location, age, occupation, monthly income, household size, duration of slum residence, anxiety and uncertainty about food supply, inadequate food quality, and insufficient food intake were significantly associated with mild to moderate and severe food insecurity compared to food security, as well as moderate and low HDDS compared to high HDDS, as shown in (Table 4). Respondents who lived in slum areas of Jashore and Kushtia districts were (1.7 and 2.6 times) and (2.8 and 1.6 times) more likely to be mild-to-moderately food insecure (MMFI) and severely food insecure (SFI) compared to the respondents from Khulna district. Similarly, the participants in the Narail and Jhenaidha districts were 1.8 times and 1.7 times more likely to be MMFI than respondents from the Khulna district slum areas. Those aged between 20–30 years was 1.7 times more likely to be only MMFI, but aged > 40 years were 1.9 and 2.5 times more likely to be MMFI and SFI than < 20 years aged respondents.

In terms of occupation, households that rely on day labor jobs and servant or maids were (2.4 and 2.8) times and (2.5 and 3.5) times more likely to be MMFI and SFI compared to farmers’ households. Furthermore, slum households’ monthly income < 10,000 BDT was 1.4 times and 1.3 times more likely to experience MMFI and SFI, but respondents whose income was between 10,000–15,000 takas were 0.6 times more likely to experience severe food insecurity (SFI) than those whose income was > 15,000 BDT. Regarding household size, households with > 4 family members were 1.1 times more likely to be MMFI than those with ≤ 4 members. Moreover, participants whose life duration in slums was > 15 years were 2.5 times and 0.8 times more likely to experience MMFI and SFI than their counterparts with slum duration of 5–15 years and < 5 years.

Regarding the anxiety and uncertainty about the household food supply, the odds of being MMFI and SFI were 3.2 times and 3.8 times more likely to be feeling the anxiety of household respondents than their anxiety-absent counterparts in slum areas. Respondents whose family members consumed inadequate quality of food were 2.4 times and 3.7 times more likely to be MMFI and SFI than those who didn’t consume adequate quality food in these slum regions. Similarly, households’ participants who intake insufficient food were 3.8 times and 3.2 times more likely to be MMFI and SFI compared to their counterparts who intake sufficient food.

In Table 4, similar factors are also significantly associated with household dietary diversity scores among slum dwellers. In the slum regions of Jashore and Kushtia districts, household respondents were (2.7 and 2.2) times and (3.5 and 2.6) times more likely to have low household dietary diversity score (HDDS) and moderate household dietary diversity score (HDDS) than respondents from the Khulna district. On the other hand, slum households of Jhenaidha had 1.2 times higher odds of having low HDDS compared with peers in Khulna district’s slum households. Respondents aged between 20–30 years were 1.7 times more likely to have low HDDS, but those aged > 40 years were 1.6 times and 2.2 times low HDDS and moderate HDDS than reference household heads aged < 20 years.

The regression analysis further elucidates a significant association between the occupation of household heads with low HDDS and moderate HDDS. Day laborer household heads were 1.8 times more likely to have low HDDS and moderate HDDS than farmers. Again, servant/ maids were 1.7 times more likely to have a low risk of HDDS and moderate HDDS than reference households’ heads job (farmers). The study results show that monthly income < 10,000 BDT were 2.8 times more likely to have low HDDS and moderate HDDS than those with a monthly income > 15,000 BDT, respectively. On the other hand, the household’s income level between (10000–15000) BDT/month was 1.7 times more likely to have low HDDS than their peers with an income level of > 15,000 BDT/month.

Moreover, the risk of low HDDS and moderate HDDS was 2.8 times and 1.7 times higher in slum households that had > 4 members than in < 4 or 4 family member counterparts. Additionally, participants living duration of > 15 years in slum areas were 1.7 times and 1.8 times more likely to have low HDDS and moderate HDDS compared to slum dwellers who had a slum duration of < 5 years. In terms of anxiety and uncertainty about the household food supply, slum households that had suffered from anxiety had 2.7 times and 1.9 times higher odds of having low HDDS and moderate HDDS in comparison with peers with anxiety absent. Respondents who consumed inadequate quality food were 3.5 times and 2.9 times more likely to have moderate and low HDDS than their counterparts who didn’t consume inadequate quality food in the slum areas. Slum households with insufficient food intake were found to be 2.8 times more likely to have a low Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS) and 3.3 times more likely to have a moderate HDDS compared to their peers with sufficient food intake. Lastly, regarding food security status, household respondents who experienced MMFI and SFI were (2.3 and 2.3 times) and (2.3 and 2.5 times) more likely to have experienced both low HDDS and moderate HDDS than the counterparts of food-secured households in slum areas.

Discussion

In developing nations, maintaining household food security and dietary diversity is a important challenge for public health aspects among both rural and urban people. Concerning this issue, our study highlights the prevalence of household food insecurity, dietary diversity status, and its correlated factors among more vulnerable urban slum households in Bangladesh. We found that various factors, including household respondents’ age, monthly family income, occupation, living duration in the slum, presence of anxiety and uncertainty, inadequate quality, and insufficient food intake, were significantly associated with higher levels of household food insecurity and low/moderate level HDDS in the slum areas of Khulna division.

In our study, 74.6% of the households experienced mild-to-moderate food insecurity (MMFI) and 22.1% had severe food insecurity (SFI), while 44.5% and 42.3% of respondents had a low HDDS and moderate HDDS, respectively. The findings are more consistent across different settings34,38–40. This could be due to limited access to land, low-income levels, high food prices, and lack of acceptability, which are universal experiences among slum households and food insecurity41. Geographical location plays a significant role in food security and dietary diversity, particularly in urban slum settings where access to affordable, nutritious food is often limited. We found that slum households of Jashore and Kushtia districts were more likely to be MMFI and SFI, as well as moderate and low Household Dietary Diversity Scores, than respondents from Khulna district slum areas—similar results evidence found in the study among slum children in Bangladesh and India24,42. The southern slum region of Bangladesh is experiencing climate change, struggling with unemployment, poverty, and health problems43. As a result, restricted availability of purchasing power may make it difficult to address these concerns. Slums often lack infrastructure and services that support food security, including safe food markets, employment opportunities, and social safety nets44. Age influences food security through its impact on employment, health status, and household roles. Our study also found that slum household respondents aged 20–30 years were more likely to experience MMFI and have low HDDS, while those aged over 40 years were more likely to experience both MMFI and SFI, as well as low and moderate HDDS, compared to those under 20 years. The study observation is more equivalent to studies conducted in Bangladesh and Nepal18,45. This may be because older adults in low-income settings experience food insecurity due to financial instability, limited income-generating capacity, financial dependence, declining health, and chronic illnesses that affect their ability to work, as well as a lack of nutritional awareness and dietary conservatism, which restrict their food choices46. A study in Nigeria found that household heads aged 60 and above were less likely to diversify their diets compared to younger household heads, a trend echoed in similar socio-economic settings47. Age also intersects with dependency burdens and caregiving responsibilities, which may lead to compromised dietary quality within the household45. In this study, household respondents dependent on day labor jobs and servant or maids were more likely to be MMFI and SFI and have low and moderate HDDS than farmers in slum areas. This observation is more consistent with studies conducted in different countries48,49. Concerning that even when work is available, day laborers often get low wages and unstable income, and poor market access, and poor financial planning capacity, where residents may rely on tiny, local shops that might not be enough to provide a variety of diets for their families20,50. In our study, lower-income (< 10000 BDT) households were significantly more likely to experience mild to severe food insecurity and low to moderate HDDS, which aligns with findings from global studies emphasizing income as a key determinant of food5,51. A plausible rationale could be because of their limited income sources and rising food prices, which hurt their food security and consumption12. Moreover, they frequently rely on calorie-dense but nutrient-poor foods due to cost constraints, putting them at risk of consuming less diversified diets52. Low-income households allocate a significant proportion of their earnings to food, making them highly susceptible to price fluctuations and economic shocks44. Furthermore, larger household size was significantly associated with MMFI as well as low to moderate HDDS and in our study, consistent with findings from similar studies in South Asia and Africa51,53. As household size increases, along with the demand for food rises, the cost of health care, education, travel, and clothes increases, often exceeding income capacity, leading to inadequate food distribution within families40. Additionally, the study revealed that participants who had lived in slums for more than 15 years were more likely to experience MMFI as well as low and moderate HDDS and less likely to be SFI than their counterparts who had lived in slums for < 5 years. A similar study was conducted in Nairobi, Kenya52. Over time, residents in slums experience persistent poverty, inadequate social support, and limited upward mobility, making it difficult to break the cycle of food insecurity. Moreover, slum dwellers often lack formal land tenure and government assistance, exacerbating their vulnerability to food shortages54,55. Furthermore, the present study identified that household respondents experiencing moderate to severe food insecurity (MMFI and SFI), as well as those with low and moderate HDDS, were more likely to report feelings of anxiety and uncertainty about the food supply compared to their counterparts who did not experience such anxiety and uncertainty in the slum areas. This finding is supported by studies indicating that households experiencing food-related stress often engage in negative coping strategies, such as reducing meal frequency or consuming cheaper, less nutritious food56.Research shows that mental distress is both a cause and consequence of food insecurity, as economic stressors contribute to poor dietary choices and disordered eating patterns57. Poor food quality and inadequate food intake were strongly associated with food insecurity and low HDDS in our study. Households experiencing severe food insecurity often consume monotonous diets lacking essential micronutrients, leading to poor health outcomes44. Research in Bangladesh and Ethiopia highlights that food-insecure households rely heavily on low-cost staple foods, such as rice and wheat, while reducing the intake of protein, dairy, and vegetables58,59. The association between food insecurity and HDDS in this study is consistent with findings from several global contexts52. For instance, food-insecure households were significantly less likely to meet the minimum dietary diversity standards because of limited purchasing power52,60.

Policy implications

The results indicate that households in Bangladeshi slum areas experience high levels of food insecurity and low dietary diversity, increasing their risk of nutritional deficiencies and associated health complications. To address this issue, the government and policymakers should implement targeted interventions based on the study’s findings. First, introducing subsidized food markets within slum areas can improve access to affordable, diverse foods. Second, conditional cash transfer programs linked to nutrition education can help households make healthier food choices. Third, expanding urban agriculture initiatives, such as community gardens, can enhance local food availability. Since longer slum residency duration was linked to dietary diversity status, improving urban resettlement policies and ensuring access to stable food markets could mitigate food insecurity among newly migrated families. Lastly, integrating nutritional support into existing healthcare programs can help address deficiencies and reduce the disease burden among vulnerable slum populations. By informing targeted policy interventions, this research contributes to promoting sustainable urban development and improving the resilience of vulnerable populations in comparable settings. These measures, informed by the study’s findings, would contribute to long-term improvements in food security and dietary diversity.

Our study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships between the identified factors and food insecurity and dietary diversity. Second, self-reported data on food security and dietary diversity may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias. Third, while the study adjusts for key confounders, there may still be unmeasured variables, such as cultural food preferences or seasonal variations in food availability, that influence household dietary patterns. Additionally, the survey was also unable to determine the food purchasing patterns of the respondents while on their settings, which markets they frequented, or the types of food on offer at each market, and these will be important considerations for future work. Finally, the study used the Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS) as a proxy for diet quality, which does not capture individual-level dietary intake, portion sizes, or nutrient adequacy.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant determinants of food insecurity and household dietary diversity (HDD) status among slum households in Khulna Division, Bangladesh. The findings reveal that geographic location, age, low monthly income, length of time spent in the slum, anxiety and uncertainty, poor quality, and inadequate food consumption are key risk factors for food insecurity and dietary diversity. These insights underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions, such as social protection programs, affordable food policies, and nutrition education, to enhance food security in urban slums. Beyond Khulna, these insights hold relevance for other rapidly urbanizing cities in the developing nations globally, where slum populations face similar vulnerabilities. By addressing food insecurity at the household level, this research contributes to broader discussions on urban sustainability, public health, and poverty reduction. Furthermore, this research contributes to urban sustainable development by advocating for integrated food security policies that align with SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) and SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities). Future research should explore longitudinal trends, climate change, policy impacts, and community-driven solutions to enhance resilience against food insecurity. Integrating these findings into urban planning frameworks can help build sustainable and food-secure cities worldwide.
