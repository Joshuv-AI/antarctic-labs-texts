Estimation of glomerular filtration rate by standard renal CT imaging

Introduction

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) describes the volume of fluid filtered through the kidneys per unit of time [1]. Accurate assessment of GFR is critical in a wide range of clinical settings, including the staging of chronic kidney disease, selection for nephrotoxic treatments, and organ transplantation planning.

Absolute GFR can be directly measured by evaluating the clearance of exogenous filtration markers. Although inulin clearance has historically been regarded as the reference standard for measured GFR (mGFR), its complexity has limited routine clinical use [2, 3]. Iohexol plasma clearance has therefore emerged as a practical and well-validated alternative. This test is regarded as the gold standard due to its independence from confounding factors such as muscle mass, inflammation, and protein intake [1]. However, it is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and costly, limiting its widespread use, particularly in time-sensitive settings [3].

Most clinical assessments rely on estimated GFR (eGFR) derived from endogenous markers, mainly serum creatinine or cystatin C [1]. These estimations are practical and highly accessible but suffer from considerable interindividual variability. Creatinine-based equations, such as Cockcroft–Gault and the Revised Lund–Malmö formula, are prone to inaccuracies from factors like muscle mass, protein intake, and physical activity [4]. Cystatin C–based estimates may improve precision but are influenced by systemic inflammation, thyroid function, adiposity, and steroid use [4]. Across commonly used methods, an error margin of up to ± 30% has been documented, with GFR and eGFR differing by more than 30% in approximately 20% of individuals, particularly at the extremes of renal function [5, 6].

Iohexol is also employed as a contrast medium (CM) during routine computed tomography (CT) scans [3]. This presents an opportunity to measure GFR non-invasively from CT imaging. An image-based method to extract GFR data from contrast-enhanced CT would offer a significant clinical advantage. A potential barrier to clinical implementation is the time required for manual image segmentation. However, automated segmentation methods based on artificial intelligence may overcome this limitation and enable rapid calculation of GFR in clinical practice. Unlike iohexol plasma clearance testing, this methodology relies on the full iodinated contrast dose required for multiphasic CT, which may limit its applicability in patients with severe renal impairment.

There are several approaches to estimate renal function from imaging. The “Gates’ method” using radioisotopes provides reliable results but requires radiotracers and gamma camera equipment [7]. The “Patlak method” allows for GFR estimation using triphasic CT scans and dynamic contrast tracking but is mathematically complex [8, 9].

This study aimed to develop and evaluate a method to predict GFR from clinical renal CT by quantifying radiodensity and volume changes in the renal pelvis and bladder during unenhanced and excretory contrast phases and integrating them with age and body surface area (BSA).

Materials and methods

Patients

After ethical approval (Swedish Ethical Review Authority, Gothenburg, Sweden (Ref. 2023-00881-01)), candidates for living kidney donation between 2013 and 2022 or patients with muscle-invasive urinary bladder carcinoma who were candidates for neoadjuvant cisplatin prior to cystectomy between 2019 and 2024 were screened retrospectively. Both these groups routinely undergo renal CT and iohexol clearance testing as part of standard clinical workup. Informed consent was waived after IRB approval. Information was obtained from the electronic medical records and images extracted from the hospital’s Picture Archiving Communication System.

Body surface area (BSA) was calculated according to Du Bois [10]. mGFR (mL/min) was collected through iohexol clearance testing (mGFRiohexol) performed on the same day as the cystatin C and creatinine measurements. eGFR (mL/min) was derived from cystatin C (eGFRcystatin C) as well as creatinine using the Cockcroft–Gault formula (eGFRCockcroft-Gault) and using the Revised Lund–Malmö formula (eGFRLMR18), which is based on both creatinine and cystatin C [11–13].

Inclusion criteria were (1) a renal CT obtained at 120 or 100 kVp visualizing the renal pelvis and bladder before and after contrast administration, (2) an excretory phase CT image between 5–15 min after contrast administration, and (3) an iohexol clearance test performed within 2 months of the imaging and before surgery. Exclusion criteria were failure to meet inclusion criteria and improperly recorded amount of contrast (Fig. 1). Subjects examined using CT acquisition parameters outside the specified 100 and 120 kVp protocols were excluded.

CT measurements

Renal pelvis and bladder were manually segmented from 5 mm thick axial images obtained in the unenhanced and excretory phases using ImageJ (National Institute of Health, version 1.53e) by a medical intern trained by a senior radiologist [14]. Volume and radiodensity (mean Hounsfield units (HU)) were recorded (Fig. 2). The CTmeasurement was designed as an imaging surrogate of the renal contrast excretion over the excretory period. The mean attenuation (HU) in contrast-enhanced urine is approximately proportional to iodine concentration. The difference in volume multiplied by respective HU yields an index proportional to the amount of excreted iodine.

We quantified net excreted contrast by the change in (HU × volume) from unenhanced to excretory phase (renal pelvis and bladder), normalized by the administered iodine dose (grams) and by the time from injection to excretory acquisition (minutes), yielding an index of excreted contrast per unit dose per unit time. To account for variation in excretory phase timing, CTmeasurement was normalized by the time from contrast injection to excretory phase acquisition. The natural logarithm was applied to reduce right-skew and to linearize the association with mGFRiohexol in linear regression.

At lower tube voltages, attenuation is more strongly influenced by the photoelectric effect. Because our cohort included acquisitions at both 100 and 120 kVp, we applied a normalization for HU values across the two protocols. HU values from 100 kVp examinations were scaled to an approximate 120 kVp equivalent, using a factor derived from published data, where the mean attenuation of iodinated fluid increased by 23% at 100 kVp compared with 120 kVp [15]. This represents an approximation rather than a physics-calibrated correction. To evaluate whether results were dependent on this assumption, we repeated the model development using only the 120 kVp subgroup.

Automatic segmentation

The renal pelvis and urinary bladder were segmented in both the nonenhanced and excretory phases using TotalSegmentator (version 2.5.0), an open-weight U-Net-based neural network trained on CT scans from 1228 patients. All segmentations were visually inspected, corrected where necessary, and confirmed by a senior radiologist with 13 years of experience. The full automatic segmentation pipeline is described in the Supplementary Material.

Radiodensity values and volumes were then used to compute CTmeasurement, and a linear regression model was developed to predict mGFRiohexol, following the same procedure as for manual segmentation.

Split renal function calculation (SRF)

Following segmentation of the left and right kidney using TotalSegmentator in both nonenhanced and excretory phases, the mean contrast accumulation in each kidney was calculated as the difference in mean radiodensity between the two phases, normalized by the average kidney volume across phases [16, 17].

SRF for the right kidney was then defined as the proportion of right kidney accumulation relative to total renal accumulation [16]. A lower threshold of −20 HU was applied to exclude fat surrounding the kidney and fat within the renal pelvis.

Statistical analysis

To determine GFR from renal CT scans (eGFRCT), multivariate linear regression analysis comparing mGFRiohexol to age, BSA and the combined variable using CTmeasurement was performed.

Because low mGFRiohexol values were underrepresented in this retrospective cohort, reflecting the lower frequency of contrast-enhanced CT among patients with reduced renal function, we used weighted least squares to improve performance in the clinically important low-GFR range. Observations with mGFRiohexol < 60 mL/min were assigned a higher weight (n = 7, weight = 10) representing a pragmatic compromise to increase their influence on model fitting without allowing this small subgroup to dominate the model. To evaluate the robustness of this choice, sensitivity analyses with alternative weights (weight = 5 and 15) were calculated for comparisons.

Agreement between mGFRiohexol and eGFRCT was assessed using Bland–Altman analysis by plotting the difference (mGFRiohexol − eGFRCT) against the mean of the two methods. To evaluate proportional bias, we assessed the association between the differences and the mean values using linear regression and visually inspected the plot for non-constant variance. Agreement between manual and automatic segmentation was evaluated using a Bland–Altman analysis of the residuals and a paired Welch’s t-test to assess differences between residuals.

The mean absolute error (MAE) of eGFRCT, eGFRcystatin C, eGFRCockcroft-Gault, and eGFRLMR18 to the mGFRiohexol was calculated separately. Statistical analyses were conducted using R Core Team (2013. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, version 4.3.3). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The standard deviation (SD) is provided, if not otherwise stated.

Model performance was evaluated using repeated random split-sample validation. In each iteration, the dataset was split into a training group (80%) and a test group (20%) using stratified random sampling to preserve the proportion of donor and bladder-cancer patients in both sets. Performance metrics were computed on the test set and averaged across all iterations.

Results

Study population characteristics

139 patients out of 345 screened met the study criteria and none of the exclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Mean age was 52 years (± 13), and 60% were women (n = 83). Mean mGFRiohexol was 99 mL/min (± 22), mean eGFRcystatin C 100 mL/min (± 26), mean eGFRCockcroft-Gault 106 mL/min (± 30) and mean eGFRLMR18 91 mL/min (± 17) (Table 1). The mean time between CT imaging and iohexol clearance test was 0.89 (± 0.57) months and between iohexol clearance test and cystatin C and creatinine was 0.1 (± 0.4) months. On average, 35.6 grams (IQR 32–40) iodine was administered at CT.

CT-derived eGFR

Multivariate linear regression analysis with mGFRiohexol as a dependent variable and CTmeasurement, BSA and age as independent variables gave an adjusted R-squared of 0.77 (p < 0.001) with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.77.

The CT-derived estimate had a P10 of 40%, P20 of 80%, and P30 of 93%. Corresponding values were 45%, 73%, and 87% for eGFRCockcroft-Gault, 40%, 77%, and 92% for eGFRcystatin C, and 45%, 81%, and 94% for eGFRLMR18.

The underestimation of mGFRiohexol by eGFRCT was −4.4 mL/min (95% limits of agreement −34.7 to 25.8), without any evidence of proportional bias (Fig. 4).

Validation cohort and sensitivity analyses

The final full-cohort model showed better performance in living kidney donors compared to patients planned for cystectomy with RMSE 14.6 mL/min versus 19.6 mL/min, MAE 11.9 mL/min versus 15.4 mL/min, and P20 of 84% versus 67%.

There was little impact of the interval between CT and iohexol clearance testing, where patients with less than 30 days (n = 79) between the two examinations, instead of 60 days, showed an RMSE of 15.3 mL/min, MAE of 12.6 mL/min, and adjusted R² of 0.72.

To internally validate the model, we performed five iterations of stratified random split-sample validation. In each iteration, 80% of participants were randomly assigned to the training set and 20% to the test set. Across five runs, the mean RMSE was 17.1 mL/min and the mean MAE was 12.9 mL/min. The average Pearson correlation coefficient across the test sets was 0.73.

When model development and evaluation were repeated and restricted to examinations acquired at 120 kVp (n = 100), the model demonstrated similar performance compared with the full cohort, with an RMSE of 15.9 mL/min and MAE of 12.6 mL/min. Model fit remained high (R² = 0.798, adjusted R² = 0.792), and all predictors remained statistically significant.

Automatic segmentation model

Automatic segmentations required manual correction in 3 patients following visual review. Multivariate regression analysis using CTmeasurement values from the automatic segmentations gave an adjusted R-squared value of 0.80 (p < 0.001), with an RMSE and MAE of 15.4 mL/min and 12.4 mL/min, respectively. The Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated similar limits of agreement and spread for the automatic and manual methods (Fig. 6).

The variability in SRF was substantially greater in the patients planned for cystectomy (0.577 ± 0.272) compared to the living kidney donors (0.487 ± 0.048), p < 0.0001, Supplementary Fig. 1.

Discussion

Renal function could be estimated from routine, clinical renal CT with high precision by estimation of volume and radiodensity of the bladder and renal pelvis before and after contrast administration with adjustment for age and body surface area. Additionally, split renal function could be derived simultaneously without the need for additional imaging [17, 18], although not verified by renal scintigraphy in our study.

This method may be useful when both a precise estimate of kidney function and imaging of the kidneys are required, e.g., at living donor kidney assessments. More importantly, another potential use is the evaluation of deceased kidney donors. Currently, it is often impractical to perform an mGFR test, like an iohexol clearance test, due to time constraints. In clinical practice, risk-scoring systems and the donor’s most recent pre-donation serum creatinine are used to gauge organ quality [19]. However, elevated creatinine is frequently seen in deceased donors [20]. This can be attributed to reduced renal function, but also to factors such as muscle breakdown, liver disease, sepsis, and the use of medications that interfere with tubular secretion of creatinine [21].

Creatinine-based equations of eGFR have been shown to be subpar in patients with advanced liver disease, elevated bilirubin, or refractory ascites [22]. In the pretransplant evaluation for liver transplantation, the Model for End-stage Liver Disease (MELD) score is employed, incorporating renal function [23]. The CT-based GFR estimation could then be applied, both pre- and postoperatively, using their perioperative contrast-enhanced CT examinations. Likewise, patients undergoing nephrotoxic treatments, such as platinum-containing chemotherapy, would benefit from precise CT-based GFR measurements. The CT studies used to evaluate treatment effect can then be used to calculate the optimal treatment dose of renally excreted drugs such as carboplatin [24]. A similar rationale may apply to acute care settings in which rapidly changing renal function complicates drug dosing [25].

In 2023, Stehlé et al reported a method to calculate CT-GFR using iomeprol excretion rate change between arterial and excretory phases, normalized by aortic contrast HU [26]. While similar, our method uses the actual administered contrast dose rather than an indirect surrogate, reducing variability from vascular dynamics. Also, we did not segment according to thresholds, possibly better reflecting the excreted contrast. Additionally, by incorporating age and BSA, our model accounts for differences that influence renal clearance and contrast distribution. Finally, we achieved a lower mean absolute error (12.8 mL/min) compared to their ± 16 mL/min interquartile bias range.

GFR declines with age, independently of comorbidities [27]. Age not only correlates with declining GFR but also leads to a decrease in muscle mass accompanied by an increase in fat mass [28]. BSA was incorporated not only as a proxy for body habitus, which influences both GFR and contrast distribution, but also to account for technical artifacts such as beam hardening and photon starvation, which disproportionately affect image quality in individuals with higher body mass [29, 30].

As manual segmentation is time-consuming and exhibits limited reproducibility in routine clinical practice, automatic segmentation was investigated as an additional approach for predicting GFR. Similar predictive performance to the manual approach was achieved. Although three cases required manual correction, this likely reflects the use of a general-purpose open-weight model. Segmentation might be improved by task-specific neural network segmentation. The automatic kidney segmentation enables an approximate calculation of SRF. Patients planned for cystectomy had substantially greater variation in SRF compared to living kidney donors, probably due to asymmetric functional impairment associated with urinary bladder disease.

This study has several limitations. Renal function may have changed between CT and iohexol clearance testing. However, when restricting the censoring interval from 2 months to 1 month, no significant difference in model prediction was observed. The cohort also consisted of two highly selected clinical groups, with an underrepresentation of patients with moderate chronic kidney disease, resulting in a bimodal distribution of renal function. That may inflate correlation-based metrics such as R². We therefore emphasize absolute error metrics, and future studies should validate the method in larger cohorts with intermediate renal function ranges. In addition, no direct comparison with established imaging-based renal function methods, such as renal scintigraphy, was performed.

Our method assumes relatively normal urinary drainage and anatomy. Performance may therefore be affected in patients with urinary obstruction, marked anatomical anomalies, or neurogenic bladder. The correction applied for differences between 100 and 120 kVp is also approximate. Yet, even though kVp attenuation differences are influenced by patient size and beam-hardening effects and cannot be perfectly corrected with a single factor, similar results in the 120 kVp subgroup suggest that the main findings were not driven by this normalization. Phantom-based calibration would allow more precise comparison of HU values between 100 kVp and 120 kVp acquisitions. However, the predictive performance of our model across both settings underscores its potential applicability in clinical environments with varying imaging protocols. Finally, our method depends on the amount of contrast dose required for multiphasic renal CT, potentially limiting its use when reduced amounts are administered.

In conclusion, renal function can be estimated from standard renal CT with high precision. This model may be of special relevance in the evaluation of living and deceased kidney donors and for cancer patients who are candidates for nephrotoxic systemic therapies. In a larger perspective, eGFRCT could be used in any clinical setting where a precise estimate of kidney function and multiphase contrast-enhanced abdominal CT is required.
