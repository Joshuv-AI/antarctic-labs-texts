List Decoding Expander-Based Codes up to Capacity in Near-Linear Time
Shashank Srivastava, Madhur Tulsiani

Abstract
We give a new framework based on graph regularity lemmas, for list decoding and list recovery of codes based on spectral expanders. Using existing algorithms for computing regularity decompositions of sparse graphs in (randomized) near-linear time, and appropriate choices for the constant-sized inner/base codes, we prove the following: - Expander-based codes constructed using the distance amplification technique of Alon, Edmonds and Luby [FOCS 1995] with rate ρ \rho , can be list decoded to a radius 1 − ρ − ε 1-\rho-\varepsilon in near-linear time. By known results, the output list has size O ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) O(1/\varepsilon) . - The above codes of Alon, Edmonds and Luby, with rate ρ \rho , can also be list recovered to radius 1 − ρ − ε 1-\rho-\varepsilon in near-linear time, with constant-sized output lists. - The Tanner code construction of Sipser and Spielman [IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory 1996] with distance δ \delta , can be list decoded to radius δ − ε \delta-\varepsilon in near-linear time, with constant-sized output lists. Our results imply novel combinatorial as well as algorithmic bounds for each of the above explicit constructions. All of these bounds are obtained via combinatorial rigidity phenomena, proved using (weak) graph regularity. The regularity framework allows us to lift the list decoding and list recovery properties for the local base codes, to the global codes obtained via the above constructions.



1
Introduction

Expander graphs have been a powerful and versatile tool for the construction of codes with several interesting
properties.
The simple combinatorial structure of codes based on expander graphs, often makes them adaptable for a variety of applications, leading to several elegant constructions.
A (very) small sample of the list of applications already includes the seminal
constructions of expander codes
[
SS96
]
, widely used distance amplification
constructions
[
ABN+92
,
AEL95
]
, as well as recent breakthrough constructions of

\varepsilon
-balanced codes
[
Ta-17
]
, locally testable codes
[
DEL+22
]
, and quantum LDPC
codes
[
PK22
,
LZ22
]
.
A detailed account of the rich interactions between coding theory and expander graphs, and
pseudorandom objects in general, can be found in several excellent surveys and
textbooks on these areas
[
Gur04
,
Vad12
,
HLW06
,
GRS23
]
.

One particular advantage of codes based on expanders, has been the amenability of their combinatorial and spectral structure, to techniques for highly efficient encoding and decoding algorithms.
However, while several expander-based codes admit linear time (and even parallelizable) algorithms for unique decoding, this has not necessarily been the case for
list decoding
algorithms.
Although some constructions of expander-based codes
[
GI03
,
Ta-17
]
do allow for fast list decoding, we know of few general algorithmic techniques to exploit expansion for list decoding. Moreover, even the known algorithms do not extend to the optimal tradeoffs between the the code parameters such as rate and distance, and the fraction of correctible errors.

For an error-correcting code
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
over a finite alphabet
\Sigma
, the distance
\delta({\mathcal{C}})
and the rate
\rho({\mathcal{C}})
are fundamental parameters, which measure the error-correction capacity and redundancy of a code

\delta({\mathcal{C}})~:=~\min_{\underset{g\neq h}{g,h\in{\mathcal{C}}}}\frac{1}{n}\cdot\lvert\left\{i\in[n]~|~g_{i}\neq h_{i}\right\}\rvert\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho({\mathcal{C}})~:=~\frac{1}{n}\cdot\log_{\lvert\Sigma\rvert}\lvert{\mathcal{C}}\rvert\,.

The task of list decoding, defined by Elias
[
Eli57
]
and Wozencraft
[
Woz58
]
, requires finding the list of all codewords within a given distance (say)
\beta
of a given
g\in\Sigma^{n}
, denoted as
{\mathcal{L}}(g,\beta)
, where

{\mathcal{L}}(g,\beta)~:=~\left\{~h\in{\mathcal{C}}~\mid~\Delta(g,h)\leq\beta~\right\}\,.

Understanding list decoding then presents two challenges: the
combinatorial
problem of finding the largest
\beta
(as a function of other parameters of the code) for which the list size remains bounded, and the
algorithmic
problem of efficiently recovering the list even when the size is known to be bounded.

Combinatorial bounds for list decoding.

Any code
{\mathcal{C}}
with distance
\delta
can be unique decoded from
\delta/2
errors, and it is known that the list size remains bounded for a threshold
{\mathcal{J}}(\delta)\in(\delta/2,\delta)
known as the Johnson bound.
Decoding beyond the Johnson bound requires relying on the structure of the code to obtain bounds on the list size.
This has often been achieved by incorporating additional algebraic structure in the code construction, to take advantage of the significant machinery for list decoding using polynomials
[
Gur07
]
.
For a code
{\mathcal{C}}
with rate
\rho
, the Singleton bound implies that
\delta\leq 1-\rho
, and a generalization by Shangguan and Tamo
[
ST20
]
shows that the list size at radius
\beta=1-\rho-\varepsilon
must be
\Omega(1/\varepsilon)
.
Algebraic code families such as folded Reed-Solomon codes and multiplicity codes, are indeed known to be list decodable close to the optimal radius of
1-\rho-\varepsilon
(known as the list decoding capacity)
[
GR08
,
Kop15
]
, with asymptotically optimal list size
[
CZ25
]
.
However, algebraic structure can be incompatible with other desirable properties such as the presence of low-density parity checks (LDPC), or may require an alphabet size growing with
n
.
Also, this line of work does not yield list size bounds for several of the expander-based code constructions mentioned above, which may not posses such algebraic structure.

Recently, list-size bounds for codes constructed via the expander-based distance amplification technique of Alon, Edmonds and Luby
[
AEL95
]
(which we will refer to as “AEL codes”) were also obtained using “local-to-global" phenomena for such codes
[
JMST25
]
.
These results, which also yield list-size bounds at near optimal radii of
\beta\geq 1-\rho-\varepsilon
(with an optimal list size of
O(1/\varepsilon)
), also have algorithmic versions based on the Sum-of-Squares (SoS) hierarchy of semidefinite programs.
However, while the algorithms based on the SoS hierarchy run in polynomial time, the exponents of these polynomials are quite large (and grow with
1/\varepsilon
).
Moreover, these techniques still do not yield list-size bounds (beyond the Johnson bound) for other expander-based codes of interest, nor for generalizations of list decoding like list recovery.
Indeed, it is not known if even the long-studied families of expander codes, such the Tanner code constructions of by Sipser and Spielman
[
SS96
]
, have polynomial (in
n
) list sizes beyond the Johnson bound.

Algorithms for list decoding.

While the task of bounding the list size is easy below the Johnson bound, the algorithmic question still remains nontrivial even if the list is known to be of small size.
In the case of algebraic codes, such algorithms were obtained in the foundational work of Guruswami and Sudan
[
Sud97
,
GS98
]
.
Since then, a number of beautiful algorithms have been developed for these and related algebraic codes, and we refer the reader to excellent textbooks and surveys on the subject
[
Gur04
,
GRS23
]
for a more detailed account.
Broadly speaking, these algorithms can be seen as characterizing the list via certain “algebraic rigidity” phenomena. They show that certain low-degree polynomials satisfying conditions also satisfied by the list codewords, must in fact
contain
the list of codewords as roots.
However, as mentioned earlier, such algebraic structure is not always available for codes based on expanders.

The use of expansion for list decoding was explored in an early work of Guruswami and Indyk
[
GI03
]
who used expanders to construct linear time list decodable codes (for very small rates).
For the case AEL codes, the study of list decoding algorithms relying on (high-dimensional) expansion was initiated by Dinur et al.
[
DHK+19
]
, who used semidefinite programs to list decode a special case of AEL codes (to a radius smaller than the Johnson bound).
Recent works have also led to list decoding algorithms up to the Johnson bound for AEL and Tanner codes
[
JST23
]
, and also for Ta-Shma’s expander-based constructions of
\varepsilon
-balanced codes
[
RR23
]
, using the Sum-of-Squares (SoS) hierarchy.
These algorithms can be seen as the application of a general "proofs to algorithms" framework for designing SoS algorithms
[
FKP19
]
which shows that the spectral proofs used for obtaining distance and list-size bounds for expander codes, can generically be translated to list decoding algorithms.
However, the runtime of algorithms based on the SoS hierarchy involve large polynomial exponents, growing with the proximity to the Johnson bound in the above cases.
As mentioned earlier, for the case of AEL codes, the SoS hierarchy also yields algorithms beyond the Johnson bound, and arbitrarily close to the list decoding capacity
[
JMST25
]
, but again with similarly large exponents.

For the
\varepsilon
-balanced code construction of Ta-Shma
[
Ta-17
]
, a faster list decoding algorithm was obtained by
[
JST21
]
using structural characterizations for expanding structures, known as “regularity lemmas”.
This algorithm runs in near-linear time, but is only known to achieve list decoding up to a threshold significantly
below
the Johnson bound for code.
Moreover, it also requires the development of new regularity lemmas specially adapted to the code construction.
In this work, we consider if such techniques can be applied to obtain new combinatorial and algorithmic results for a broader class of codes.

1.1
Our results

We develop a new framework based on graph regularity lemmas, to obtain near-linear time algorithms for list decoding Tanner codes and AEL codes.
We show that the regularity lemmas lead to “combinatorial rigidity” phenomena, which can be used to design fast algorithms for list decoding various codes up to optimal list decoding radii.
Our results also imply new combinatorial bounds on list sizes, for codes which were not previously known to be list decodable beyond the Johnson bound.

We describe the relevant code constructions and results below.
For simplicity, we restrict the discussion in this paper to versions based on balanced bipartite expanding graphs, although the techniques can be generalized to other variants.
In the discussion below, we always take
G=(L,R,E)
to be a
d
-regular bipartite graph with
\lvert L\rvert=\lvert R\rvert=n
. It is said to be an
(n,d,\lambda)
-expander if the second singular value of the biadjacency matrix is at most
\lambda\cdot d
.

List decoding.

It was recently proved by Jeronimo et al.
[
JMST25
]
that AEL codes also yield “local-to-global" phenomena for list decodability.
They proved that if the inner code
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{in}}
satisfies a (slight strengthening of) the generalized Singleton bound, then
{{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{AEL}}}
also satisfies such a bound (for an outer code
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{out}}
is chosen as above).
In particular, their result gives codes of rate
\rho
with asymptotically optimal list size of
O(1/\varepsilon)
at radius
\beta=1-\rho-\varepsilon

We give a near-linear time
algorithm for recovering such a list, at any radius
\beta\leq 1-\rho-\varepsilon
(assuming near-linear time unique decoding for
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{out}}
).

1.2
Technical overview

The starting point for our argument is the simple observation that the proofs of distance and other
local-to-global phenomena for expander-based codes essentially rely on estimating the number of
edges between subsets of vertices in the graph, via the expander mixing lemma.
For carefully chosen subsets
S,T
, the proofs rely on relating the number of edges with some
property (say where two codewords differ) to sizes
\lvert S\rvert,\lvert T\rvert
.
To develop an algorithmic analogue of such proofs, we consider regularity
lemmas, which provide a framework to capture any such “edge counting” proofs for subgraphs
H
of an expanding graph
G
.

Regularity lemmas.

The well-known weak regularity lemma of Frieze and Kannan
[
FK96
]
gives an approximate
decomposition of the adjacency matrix of any (dense) graph as a linear combination of few cut matrices of the form
{\mathbf{1}}_{S_{t}}{\mathbf{1}}_{T_{t}}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Formally, given any graph
H
on
n
vertices and
\gamma\in(0,1)
, there exist
\left\{(c_{t},S_{t},T_{t})\right\}_{t\in[p]}
for
p\leq 1/\gamma^{2}
, where
c_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
S_{t},T_{t}\subseteq[n]
and
\sum_{t}\lvert c_{t}\rvert\leq 1/\gamma
,
such that for any
S,T\subseteq[n]

\left\lvert\lvert E_{H}(S,T)\rvert-\sum_{t\in[p]}c_{t}\cdot\lvert S_{t}\cap S\rvert\lvert T_{t}\cap T\rvert\right\rvert~\leq~\gamma n^{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\left\lvert{\mathbf{1}}_{S}^{\mathsf{T}}{\left\lparen A_{H}-\sum_{t\in[p]}c_{t}{\mathbf{1}}_{S_{t}}{\mathbf{1}}_{T_{t}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rparen{\mathbf{1}}_{T}}\right\rvert~\leq~\gamma n^{2}\,.

Several known statements generalize the above to subgraphs of an expanding
graph
[
RTTV08
,
TTV09
,
CCF10
,
OT15
,
BV22
]
. A version for subgraph
H
of an

(n,d,\lambda)
-expander
G
, would give (see
Section

3
)

\left\lvert\lvert E_{H}(S,T)\rvert-\sum_{t\in[p]}c_{t}\cdot\frac{d}{n}\cdot\lvert S_{t}\cap S\rvert\lvert T_{t}\cap T\rvert\right\rvert~\leq~\left\lparen\gamma+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}\right\rparen\cdot nd~\leq~2\gamma\cdot nd\qquad(\text{for}~\lambda\leq\gamma^{2})\,.

Thus, any proof or statement regarding edge counts between any sets
S,T
can equivalently be
phrased as a statement about their intersections with
fixed
sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{p}
and
T_{1},\ldots,T_{p}
.
Moreover there exist near-linear time algorithms to compute the decomposition
\left\{(c_{t},S_{t},T_{t})\right\}_{t\in[p]}
, which allow us to efficiently search for sets
S,T
with given intersection
sizes (up to a small error) and thus, also a given number of edges between them.
We will next show how (for example) the list decoding problem for AEL codes can be characterized in
terms of this search.

\mathchoice{\underset{\ell\in L}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\Delta}(g_{\ell},h_{\ell})\leq\beta+\varepsilon\right]}{{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell\in L}\left[{\Delta}(g_{\ell},h_{\ell})\leq\beta+\varepsilon\right]}{{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell\in L}\left[{\Delta}(g_{\ell},h_{\ell})\leq\beta+\varepsilon\right]}{{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell\in L}\left[{\Delta}(g_{\ell},h_{\ell})\leq\beta+\varepsilon\right]}~~\geq~~1-\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{\varepsilon}}~~\geq~~1-\gamma\,.

The task is then find the right

Agreement graphs.

Fix a codeword

in the global list, and for

be the

We will also ignore the

Note that picking

Rigidity.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be the family of all the sets appearing the regularity decompositions of all
the agreement graphs
H_{i}
.
For a set
S
, we refer to the intersection sizes of
S
with all sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
(normalized to be between 0 and 1, or correlations when viewed as functions) as the
signature

\sigma(S)
of
S
with respect to the family
{\mathcal{F}}
.
We will show the signatures essentially characterize the sets

\left\{A_{i}\right\}_{i\in K}
i.e., any collection of sets
\left\{A_{i}^{\prime}\right\}_{i\in[K]}
which have (approximately) the same signatures, must actually be close to the sets
\left\{A_{i}\right\}_{i\in K}
.
This “combinatorial rigidity” will allow us to search for the sets
\left\{A_{i}\right\}_{i\in K}
by
simply searching for their
signatures
.
By choosing an appropriate level of discretization
\eta
for the densities, it will be sufficient to
enumerate over a net of size at most
(1/\eta)^{\lvert{\mathcal{F}}\rvert}\leq(1/\eta)^{{2pK}}
, where the
parameters
\eta,p,K
are all constants independent of

.

The rigidity property follows from the fact that being close in their signatures
implies that
E_{H_{i}}(A_{i}^{\prime},B)\approx E_{H_{i}}(A_{i},B)=E_{G}(A_{i},B)
for all
i\in[K]
, using the regularity
lemma. Formally, we get

\sum_{i\in[K]}\lvert E_{H_{i}}(A_{i}^{\prime},B)\rvert~~\geq~~\lvert E_{G}(\cup_{i}A_{i},B)\rvert-K\cdot\gamma\cdot dn~~\geq~~d\cdot\lvert B\rvert-K\cdot\gamma\cdot dn\,,

for sufficiently close densities of
A_{i}^{\prime}
and
A_{i}
, and error parameter
\gamma
from the
regularity lemma. Thus, a typical vertex in
L
, contributes
\approx d\cdot\lvert B\rvert/n=d\cdot(1-\beta)
edges to the above sum.
On the other hand, any vertex in
A_{i}^{\prime}\setminus A_{i}
can contribute at most
d\cdot(1-\delta({\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{in}}))

edges in
E_{H_{i}}(A_{i}^{\prime},B)

{\mathcal{L}}_{{\ell}}[i]
and
h_{\ell}
must differ on such a vertex on at least
\delta({\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{in}})\cdot d
edges, and these cannot go to
B
(where
g
and
h
agree) in the graph
H_{i}
(where
{\mathcal{L}}[i]
and
g
agree).
Edge counting using the expander mixing lemma yields

\sum_{i\in[K]}\lvert E_{H_{i}}(A_{i}^{\prime},B)\rvert~~\leq~~\left\lparen\sum_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{\prime}\cap A_{i}\rvert\right\rparen\cdot\lvert B\rvert\cdot\frac{d}{n}+\lambda\cdot dn+\left\lparen\sum_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{\prime}\setminus A_{i}\rvert\right\rparen\cdot(1-\delta({\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{in}}))\cdot d\,.

Comparing the two bounds and using

gives
\sum_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{\prime}\setminus A_{i}\rvert\leq((K\gamma+\lambda)/(\delta({\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{in}})-\beta))\cdot n

choosing the regularity error
\gamma
and
\lambda

\delta({\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{in}})-\beta\geq 2\varepsilon
.
Note that we only needed rigidity for the left sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}
, and it will actually suffice for our applications to only consider signatures with respect to families of subsets of the left set
L
.

List decoding via enumeration.

Given the above rigidity property, we just need to (approximately) find the correct signatures for the sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}
corresponding to each global codeword
h\in{\mathcal{L}}
.
Enumerating with a discretization
\eta
, such that the error in the edge densities (relative to
\lvert E(G)\rvert=nd
) is bounded by
\gamma
(which is chosen to be small relative to
\varepsilon
), we are guaranteed to find sets
A_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,A_{K}^{\prime}
sufficiently close to
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}
. We can then recover
h
by unique decoding in
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{out}}
to correct for the small differences.
Note that the enumeration argument also implies a combinatorial bound of
(1/\eta)^{\lvert{\mathcal{F}}\rvert}
on the size of the list, since we proved that every list codeword must be found by searching this discrete net.

One subtle issue here is that we do need to produce sets
A_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,A_{K}^{\prime}
corresponding to each point (signature) in the discrete net we search over, for the decoding algorithm to proceed.
However, not every signature
\sigma
(collection of intersection sizes with sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
) might actually be
realizable
as
\sigma(S)
for some set
S
, due to possibly complex overlaps between the sets in the family
{\mathcal{F}}
which constrain the intersection patterns.
But we look at the partition
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{F}})
generated by all the sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
, then the density of
S
in any cell of this partition is unconstrained by densities in other cells.
We test the realizability of the signatures in our enumeration algorithm by expressing this as a simple linear program in densities corresponding to the cells of the partition.

It will be more convenient to work in the language of “factors” instead of partitions for the enumeration argument, since it facilitates a cleaner error analysis. This is described in
Section

2
. The error estimates for set (function) families arising from regularity lemma are developed in
Section

3

List recovery.

An argument similar to the list decoding of Tanner codes, with two-sided local lists, can also be used for the list recovery of AEL codes. In this case, the lists for the right vertices

(with

The local lists

Remarks.

We collect a few additional observations regarding our techniques and results below.

-

The expander based constructions typically lift a property such as distance
[
AEL95
,
GI05
]
or average-radius list decodability
[
JMST25
]
from local code to the global code. Our results can be seen as lifting, both combinatorially and algorithmically, list size bounds from the local code to the global code. Given a collection of local lists, the regularity decomposition allows us to stitch together these lists in a globally consistent manner, as was done by
[
HW18
,
RWZ21
]
in the erasures-only setting.

-

The only component of our algorithm that taken near-linear instead of linear time (or uses
randomization), is the computation of the regularity decomposition. All other parts of our
algorithm can be implemented in deterministic linear time. The regularity decomposition can also
be computed in deterministic
O(n^{3.5})
which also gives the same deterministic runtime for all
our results. The randomized near-linear time computation can also be implemented in time
O(n\cdot(\log n)^{2}\cdot(1/\varepsilon)^{O(1)})
using the approximate primal-dual SDP solvers of Arora and Kale
[
AK07
]
. Their solvers require
O(\log n)
iterations of matrix multiplicative updates, with each step projecting SDP solutions to
O(\log n)
random directions.

-

The only step which depends on the alphabet size of the code, is the computation of the local lists for the code
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{in}}
in AEL, and the base code
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
in Tanner codes. Given the local lists, the rest of the algorithm only depends on the size of these lists and is independent of the alphabet size.

-

Using standard concatenation methods, our list recovery algorithms can also be used to obtain binary codes which are list decodable up to the Zyablov bound in near-linear time.

-

Given a list recovery instance for the Tanner code, one can perform list recovery for the local code to obtain local lists, and then extract the list codewords from them in a way similar to list decoding. We omit the details of this list recovery algorithm in the Tanner code’s case since it follows by a relatively straightforward extension of the list decoder.

Algebraic vs. combinatorial rigidity.

As discussed above, regularity lemmas lead to rigidity properties for the sets corresponding to list codewords.
These can be compared to algebraic rigidity phenomena which underlie decoding algorithms for codes based on polynomials, such as Reed-Solomon codes.
In the algebraic setting, one often sets up a multivariate polynomial interpolation problem, which can be solved by solving linear equations depending on the input. One then needs to argue that the list codewords must always be roots of such a multivariate polynomial, using the fact that low-degree polynomials have some algebraic rigidity (once they vanish at sufficiently many points, they must be zero everywhere).

The arguments using regularity can be considered analogues of the above interpolation-based list decoding arguments. Instead of linear equations, one considers edge density conditions that must be satisfied by the sets corresponding to the true codewords. We consider a strengthening of this condition by matching not only edge densities, but all densities in the regularity partition. At this point, the regularity conditions and the local distances of the codes are sufficient to imply rigidity, saying that sets matching in densities according to the partition, should in fact match almost everywhere.

1.3
Related work

The rigidity argument used in our work is also similar in spirit to the rigidity argument used by
Bhowmick and Lovett
[
BL18
]
for obtaining bounds on the list size for Reed-Muller codes, at
radii arbitrarily close to distance.
They develop (much stronger) regularity lemmas for polynomials using tools from higher order Fourier analysis, and show that any list codeword must be “measurable” in a factor (partition) determined by a constant number of polynomials (independent of the block length). Counting the number of functions that are measurable in the factor then yields the list size bound in their setting.
However, the regularity lemmas in their setting require significantly stronger properties, and yield
tower-type bounds on the list size, while our argument only relies on the weak regularity lemma and
yields exponential bounds.

Our results can also be seen as sparse analogues of the results by Gopalan, Guruswami and Raghavendra
[
GGR09
]
, for list decoding tensor codes and interleaved codes.
When
G
is taken to be the complete bipartite graph, the resulting Tanner code is simply the tensor
code
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\otimes{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
, and more generally
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
when the left and right
codes are different.
Their algorithm for tensor codes chooses from the local (row and column) lists by randomly sampling rows and columns, while our algorithm extends it to sparse expanders via regularity.
Thinking of consistently picking from local lists as a constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) this
can be seen as analogous to literature on approximation of CSPs, where sampling-based algorithms for dense
instances
[
AKK95
,
FK96
]
, can be extended to sparse expanding ones via regularity
[
OT15
]
.

As mentioned earlier, Guruswami and Indyk
[
GI03
]
also gave the first explicit construction of
expander-based codes, which are list decodable from
1-\varepsilon
fraction of errors in (randomized)

linear
time.
Their approach also relies on consistently choosing from
n
local lists corresponding to vertices
of an expander, in time
O(n\log n)
, independent of the alphabet for the local codes.
They then achieve linear run-time by using an alphabet of size
n^{O(1)}
and concatenating, so that
the block size becomes
N=O(n\log n)
. However, their constructions use a variant of AEL with the
repetition code as the inner code
[
ABN+92
]
, which causes the rate to decay as

\exp(-\exp(1/\varepsilon^{3}))
.
A similar concatenation with our AEL construction may also lead to linear-time list decodable codes
with improved rates, using the fact that our algorithm’s runtime does not directly on the alphabet size (see remark above).

Another closely related work is that of
[
JST21
]
, which uses analogues of the weak regularity lemma for expanding (ordered) hypergraphs, to obtain a list decoding algorithms for Ta-Shma
\varepsilon
-balanced code.
Similar ideas were also used by Blanc and Doron
[
BD22
]
for near-linear time list decoding of a different (probabilistic) construction of an
\varepsilon
-balanced code.
The key challenge in these applications is actually developing algorithmic versions of the
hypergraph regularity lemmas to do any nontrivial list decoding (both above results are
significantly below the Johnson bound), while the focus in our work is to using known graph
regularity lemmas to list decode from an
optimal
fraction of errors.

There is also a very rich literature of algebraic code constructions with efficient list decoding
near capacity, and other interesting properties, that we do not exhaustively list here.
It was shown by Goyal et al.
[
GHKS24

Efficient regularity lemma

To make use of the regularity lemma algorithmically, we use a simple modification of

]

[
AK07
]

in the local lists

A_{i}~=~\left\{\ell\in L~|~{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}[i]=h_{\ell}\right\}\,.

The sets
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in[K]}
are disjoint, and
4.10
implies that
\sum_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert\geq(1-\gamma)\cdot\lvert L\rvert
.
As before, if we can recover the sets
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in[K]}
up to a small fraction of errors, then we correctly know
h_{\ell}
for most
\ell\in L
, and can find
h
by unique decoding the outer code
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
.

In the case of list decoding, we recovered the sets
A_{i}
by understanding their interaction with some unknown set
B
that was defined to be the set of
{r}\in R
where
h_{{r}}
agreed with the received word. For list recovery, we will need to study the interaction between sets
A_{1},A_{2},\cdots,A_{K}
with a
collection
of disjoint sets
B_{1},B_{2},\cdots,B_{{k}}
. For
j\in[{k}]
, we define

B_{j}=\left\{{r}\in R~|~h_{{r}}={{\mathcal{L}}_{{r}}}[j]\right\}

The condition
\mathchoice{\underset{{r}\in R}{\mathbb{P}}\left[h_{{r}}\not\in{{\mathcal{L}}_{{r}}}\right]}{{\mathbb{P}}_{{r}\in R}\left[h_{{r}}\not\in{{\mathcal{L}}_{{r}}}\right]}{{\mathbb{P}}_{{r}\in R}\left[h_{{r}}\not\in{{\mathcal{L}}_{{r}}}\right]}{{\mathbb{P}}_{{r}\in R}\left[h_{{r}}\not\in{{\mathcal{L}}_{{r}}}\right]}\leq\beta
on
h
can now be written in terms of the sets
B_{1},B_{2},\cdots,B_{{k}}
as
\sum_{j=1}^{{k}}|B_{i}|\geq(1-\beta)\cdot|R|
.

Consider the graph
H_{ij}=(L,R,E(H_{ij}))
constructed by deleting all edges of
G
that are inconsistent between assignments picked according to index
i\in[K]
on the left and index
j\in[{k}]
on the right,

E(H_{ij})~=~\{e=({\ell},{r})\in E(G)~|~({\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}[i])_{e}=({{\mathcal{L}}_{{r}}}[j])_{e}\}\,.

Note that the graphs
H_{ij}
can be easily computed based on the local lists
\{{{\mathcal{L}}_{{\ell}}}\}_{{\ell}\in L}
and
\{{{\mathcal{L}}_{{r}}}\}_{{r}\in R}
.
Also, we must have
E_{H_{ij}}(A_{i},B_{j})=E_{G}(A_{i},B_{j})
since for an edge
e=(\ell,r)\in E_{G}(A_{i},B_{j})
, we have

({{\mathcal{L}}_{{\ell}}}[i])_{e}=h_{e}=({{\mathcal{L}}_{{r}}}[j])_{e}
, and therefore such an edge will never be deleted when constructing
H_{ij}
.

Finally, we prove an analog of the rigidity lemma in the setting of list recovery. This lemma shows that a sufficiently regular family
{\mathcal{F}}
, which is
simultaneously

(\eta,\gamma)
-regular with respect to
\{H_{ij}\}_{i\in[K],j\in[{k}]}
, allows us to identify the sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}
.
As before, we can obtain such a family by applying the regularity lemma for each of the graphs
H_{ij}
for
i\in[K]
and
j\in[{k}]
, and then taking the union.

4.11
Rigidity.

Let the family
{\mathcal{F}}
be simultaneously
(\eta,\gamma)
-regular for the graphs
\{H_{ij}\}_{i\in[K],j\in[{k}]}
. Let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{K}\subseteq L
be any collection of disjoint sets satisfying
\max_{i\in[K]}~\lVert{\mathbf{1}}_{A_{i}}-{\mathbf{1}}_{S_{i}}\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}~\leq~\eta
.
Also, let the list recovery radius
\beta
satisfy
\beta\leq\delta_{\mathrm{in}}-\varepsilon
, and assume
\lambda\leq\gamma
. Then,
\sum_{i}\lvert S_{i}\setminus A_{i}\rvert\leq(3{k}K\gamma/\varepsilon)\cdot n
.

Proof.

By the
\eta
-closeness of the sets
A_{i}
and
S_{i}
with respect to
{\mathcal{F}}
, we have

\forall i\in[K],j\in[{k}]\qquad\lvert E_{H_{ij}}(S_{i},B_{j})\rvert~\geq~\lvert E_{H_{ij}}(A_{i},B_{j})\rvert-\gamma\cdot dn~=~\lvert E_{G}(A_{i},B_{j})\rvert-\gamma\cdot dn~\geq\lvert E_{G}(S_{i},B_{j})\rvert-2\gamma\cdot dn\,.

Summing the above and using the bound
\sum_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert\geq(1-\gamma)\cdot n
from
4.10
, we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{H_{ij}}(S_{i},B_{j})\rvert~~\geq~~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{G}(S_{i},B_{j})\rvert-2{k}K\cdot\gamma\cdot dn

Define the sets
W_{i}=S_{i}\setminus A_{i}
.
For each
\ell\in W_{i}
, we have
({\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}[i])_{e}\neq h_{e}
for at least
\delta_{\mathrm{in}}
fraction of the incident edges on
{\ell}
since
{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}[i]
and
h_{\ell}
must be distinct codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{in}}
.
Also, such an edge
e=({\ell},{r})
cannot be in
E_{H_{ij}}(S_{i},B_{j})
since we have
({\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}[i])_{e}\neq h_{e}=({{\mathcal{L}}_{{r}}}[j])_{e}
. Using this, and the fact that
S_{i}\setminus W_{i}\subseteq A_{i}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{H_{ij}}(S_{i},B_{j})\rvert

\displaystyle~=~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{H_{ij}}(S_{i}\setminus W_{i},B_{j})\rvert+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{H_{ij}}(W_{i},B_{j})\rvert

\displaystyle~\leq~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{G}(S_{i}\setminus W_{i},B_{j})\rvert+\sum_{i\in[K]}\lvert W_{i}\rvert\cdot(1-\delta)\cdot d

\displaystyle~=~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{G}(S_{i},B_{j})\rvert-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{G}(W_{i},B_{j})\rvert+\sum_{i\in[K]}\lvert W_{i}\rvert\cdot(1-\delta)\cdot d\,.

We can now use expander mixing lemma to bound the number of edges in
G
.

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{G}(W_{i},B_{j})\rvert~\geq~\frac{d}{n}\cdot\left\lparen\sum_{i}\lvert W_{i}\rvert\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen\sum_{j}\lvert B_{j}\rvert\right\rparen-{k}K\cdot\lambda\cdot dn~\geq~(1-\beta)\cdot d\cdot\left\lparen\sum_{i}\lvert W_{i}\rvert\right\rparen-{k}K\cdot\lambda\cdot dn

Combining the above inequalities, and using
w
to denote
\sum_{i}\lvert W_{i}\rvert/n
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{G}(S_{i},B_{j})\rvert-2{k}K\cdot\gamma\cdot dn

\displaystyle~\leq~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{H_{ij}}(S_{i},B_{j})\rvert~\leq~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{G}(S_{i},B_{j})\rvert-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[K]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\lvert E_{G}(W_{i},B_{j})\rvert+\sum_{i\in[K]}\lvert W_{i}\rvert\cdot(1-\delta)\cdot d

\displaystyle\Rightarrow~

\displaystyle-2{k}K\cdot\gamma\cdot dn\leq-(1-\beta)w\cdot dn+{k}K\cdot\lambda\cdot dn+w(1-\delta)\cdot dn

\displaystyle\Rightarrow~

\displaystyle w(\delta-\beta)\leq 2{k}K\cdot\gamma+{k}K\cdot\lambda

\displaystyle\Rightarrow~

\displaystyle w\leq\frac{2{k}K\cdot\gamma+{k}K\cdot\lambda}{\delta-\beta}\,.

Using
\lambda\leq\gamma
and
\beta\leq\delta-\varepsilon
then proves the claim.

4.2.2
The list recovery algorithm

Let
{{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{AEL}}}

For each

.

We note that the computation of the local lists

and

, and the graphs

2.17

4.2.3
Instantiating AEL codes for list recovery

As in the case of list decoding, we instantiate

5
Algorithms for Tanner Codes

Let

be a bipartite
(n,d,\lambda)
-expander. Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
be two codes over a common alphabet

5.1
Distance of Tanner codes.

The distance of the code
{{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{Tan}}}
is at least
\sqrt{{\delta_{L}}{\delta_{R}}}\left\lparen\sqrt{{\delta_{L}}{\delta_{R}}}-\lambda\right\rparen\geq{\delta_{L}}{\delta_{R}}-\lambda
.

That is, by making

small enough, the distance of Tanner codes approaches the product of distance
{\delta_{L}}{\delta_{R}}
. We will not be much concerned with the rate of this code, but we mention that if the codes
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
are linear codes with rates
\rho_{L}
and
\rho_{R}
respectively, then the rate of
{{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{Tan}}}
is at least
\rho_{L}+\rho_{R}-1
. In particular, if both
\rho_{L}
and
\rho_{R}
are close to 1, so is the rate of

, and this makes Tanner codes particularly suitable as an outer code in the AEL code construction.

The following lemma is well-known as unique decodability of Tanner codes and goes back to
[
SS96

Zém01
]
, but we need it with two minor modifications: (1) we need an error-and-erasure version
[
SR03
]
, and (2) we will think of decoding from a

instead of from

. For this reason, we adapt the proof of
[

to this setting in
Appendix

5.2
Unique-decodability of Tanner codes.

Let
{{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{Tan}}}
be a Tanner code defined as in
Lemma

5.1
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
such that
\lambda\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\cdot\min({\delta_{L}},{\delta_{R}})
. Given a

with

many erasures, we have the following:

-

there is at most one

that satisfies
2{\Delta}_{L}(g,h)+s\leq{\delta_{R}}-4\varepsilon
.

-

If such an

exists, then there is an algorithm that given
g
, runs in linear time
O(n\cdot|\Sigma|^{d})
, and outputs

.

Tanner codes are well known for this linear time unique decodability, and were the first explicit construction of LDPC codes. Beyond their practical uses, they were used as the base (outer) code in an application of the AEL procedure by Guruswami and Indyk
[
GI05
]
to obtain linear-time unique decodable near the Singleton bound. However, not much was known about the list decoding of Tanner codes until the work of
[
JST23
]
, who used the Sum-of-Squares hierarchy to decode them up to their Johnson bound.

In this section, we show how Tanner codes can be list decoded even beyond the Johnson bound, using simple extensions of the techniques we already saw
Section

4
for AEL codes. Suppose the code
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
is decodable with constant-sized lists (independent of its blocklength

) up to a radius
{\delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_{L}}
, and the code
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
is decodable with constant-sized lists up to a radius
{\delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_{R}}
. We will design an algorithm that can decode arbitrarily close to the radius
{\delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_{\mathrm{Tan}}}
(by making
\lambda
small enough), where

{\delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_{\mathrm{Tan}}}~:=\min\left\lparen{\delta_{L}}{\delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_{R}},{\delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_{L}}{\delta_{R}}\right\rparen

This radius matches the decoding radius obtained for tensor codes, which are dense analogs of Tanner codes, by
[

]
. Note that in the worst case, the decoding radius (with constant sized lists) for
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
is just
{\delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_{L}}={\mathcal{J}}({\delta_{L}})
, where

denotes the Johnson bound corresponding to distance

.

Suppose the codes

and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
are same (this was the setting addressed by
[

]
), and

Main result for Tanner codes

We instantiate the
Theorem

5.6

and

5.7
.

Suppose the code
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
is list decodable up to radius
{\delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_{L}}
with list size
{K_{1}}
and the code
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
is list decodable up to radius
{\delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_{R}}
with list size
{K_{2}}

in
g_{2i}
. Therefore, we only need to count how many right vertices send at least
\frac{{\delta_{R}}}{2}\cdot d
edges to
L^{\prime}
. Let
R^{\prime}=\{{r}\in R~\big|~|N({r})\cap L^{\prime}|\geq\frac{{\delta_{R}}}{2}\cdot d\}
.

\displaystyle|R^{\prime}|\cdot\frac{{\delta_{R}}}{2}\cdot d\leq E(L^{\prime},R^{\prime})\leq\frac{d}{n}\cdot|L^{\prime}|\cdot|R^{\prime}|+\lambda d\cdot\sqrt{|L^{\prime}|\cdot|R^{\prime}|}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{|L^{\prime}|}{|R^{\prime}|}\geq\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\left\lparen\frac{{\delta_{R}}}{2}-\frac{|L^{\prime}|}{n}\right\rparen^{2}\geq\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\frac{\delta_{R}^{2}}{16}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\qquad|R^{\prime}|\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\delta_{R}^{2}/16}\cdot|L^{\prime}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\cdot|R^{\prime}|\,.

It is now easy to see that the distances
\Delta_{R}(g_{2i},h)
are monotonically decreasing with

\Delta_{R}(g_{2i+2},h)\leq\left\lparen\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\right\rparen^{2}\cdot\Delta_{R}(g_{2i},h)\,.

Thus for some
i=\Theta(\log n)
,
g_{2i}
will converge to
h
.

This immediately implies a runtime bound of
O(\log n\cdot|\Sigma|\cdot n)
, since there are
O(\log n)
iterations, and each iteration only involves locally decoding for each left or right vertex. This decoding takes time
|\Sigma|^{d}
per vertex in the worst case, so the total cost per iteration is
O(|\Sigma|^{d}\cdot n)
. For our purposes, this near-linear running time suffices.

To get a linear time implementation, observe that to obtain
g_{2i+2}
from
g_{2i}
, it suffices to change only the neighborhoods of vertices where
g_{2i+1}
changed from
g_{2i-1}
. This difference is at most

\Delta_{L}(g_{2i-1},g_{2i+1})\cdot n\leq\Delta_{L}(g_{2i-1},h)\cdot n+\Delta_{L}(g_{2i+1},h)\cdot n\leq 2\Delta_{L}(g_{2i-1},h)\cdot n\,.

A similar claim also holds true for computing the odd indices
g_{5},g_{7},\ldots
. Thus the total amount of work done to compute
g_{6},g_{8},\ldots
is

\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}|\Sigma|^{d}\cdot 2\Delta_{L}(g_{2i-1},h)n\leq 2|\Sigma|^{d}n\cdot\Delta_{L}(g_{3},h)\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left\lparen\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\right\rparen^{i}=\frac{2|\Sigma|^{d}n\Delta_{L}(g_{3},h)}{1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}}=O(n\cdot|\Sigma|^{d})\quad\qquad\hbox{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}

Note that runtime analysis assumed a worst case bound of
|\Sigma|^{d}
for the inner code, which is constant if the alphabet
\Sigma
is of constant size. In the case when faster errors-and-erasures decoders are available for the inner code, such as in the case of polynomial codes like Reed-Solomon, we can get rid of the exponential dependence on
d
and replace it with a polynomial one, as done in
[
RWZ21
]
, for instance.
