Abstract
This paper presents a safety-critical control framework to maintain bounded lateral motions for vehicles braking on asymmetric surfaces. We synthesize a brake controller that assists drivers and guarantees safety against excessive lateral motions (i.e., prevents the vehicle from spinning out) while minimizing the stopping distance. We address this safety-critical control problem in the presence of input constraints, since braking forces are limited by the available friction on the road. We use backup control barrier functions for safe control design. As this approach requires the construction of a backup set and a backup controller, we propose a novel, systematic method to creating valid backup set-backup controller pairs based on feedback linearization and continuous-time Lyapunov equations. We use simple examples to demonstrate our proposed safety-critical control method. Finally, we implement our approach on a four-wheel vehicle model for braking on asymmetric surfaces and present simulation results.



I
Introduction

Vehicle control systems have been designed to improve safety in a wide variety of driving conditions.
One of the most dangerous and intricate situations is braking on asymmetric, so-called split-
\mu
surfaces, where the left and right wheels of road vehicles experience different coefficients of friction.
This scenario, illustrated in Fig.
1
, typically occurs for wet or frozen road sections, or when miscellaneous substances are present on the road.
The resulting difference in left and right braking forces generates an unexpected yaw moment which may find most drivers unprepared and may cause accidents if drivers react with inappropriate steering movements.

The challenge of safe split-
\mu
braking arises from two contradictory control objectives: stopping over the shortest possible distance while maintaining lateral stability
[
1
]
.
Minimizing the stopping distance requires maximizing the braking forces, up to the available road friction.
As the friction differs on the left and right sides, maximal braking forces would generate maximal yaw moment.
On the other hand, to eliminate the yaw moment and ensure lateral stability, both sides of the vehicle must brake equally.
This requires reducing the braking forces on one side, which increases the stopping distance.
Both of these approaches may endanger the vehicle and its driver.

Fig. 1:
A vehicle braking on an asymmetric (split-
\mu
) surface. The lateral kinematics (side slip angle and yaw rate) are kept within safe bounds with limited braking forces using the proposed safety-critical controller.

Vehicle control methods have the potential to mitigate the trade-off between the stopping distance and lateral stability.
To this end, brake controllers may exploit three fundamental quantities: brake pressure, brake pressure difference between left and right sides, and steering angle. Anti-lock braking systems (ABS) adjust the brake pressure in order to maximize the braking forces. Several studies
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
draw upon this idea and adopt ABS to split-
\mu
braking.
Other methods
[
5
,
6
]
use active steering control alongside ABS to ensure safe braking.
Alternatively, one may also regulate the brake pressure difference which is proportional to the yaw moment.
The work
[
1
]
optimizes the yaw moment to reduce the yaw rate to a desired value, whereas
[
7
]
determines a desired yaw moment for ABS
while using active steering control.
To allocate the yaw moment among the wheels, the former reference developed its own algorithm, while the latter applied a fuzzy controller.

In this paper, we take a novel approach to split-
\mu
braking: we develop
safety-critical controllers
.
In contrast to previous works which aim to
stabilize
lateral motions via a specific yaw moment, we seek to
constrain
the vehicle’s lateral kinematics.
Our goal is to prevent the yaw rate and side slip angle from rising to extreme values, instead of driving them to zero which would lead to equal braking on both sides and a drastic increase in the stopping distance.
We use safety-critical control techniques to keep
the yaw rate and side slip angle below safe bounds.
The proposed controllers provide the required braking forces, which may be applied to the wheels using an ABS.

In particular, we use the concept of control barrier functions (CBFs)
[
8
]
to design safe controllers.
CBFs provide a state-of-the-art solution to a wide range of safety-critical control problems by ensuring that the state of the system is maintained inside a safe set in the state space.
This approach has demonstrated success in applications ranging from robotics

[
9
,
10
,
11
]

to aerospace systems
[
12
]
.
Vehicle control has also leveraged CBFs to ensure safety for both longitudinal and lateral motions, including adaptive cruise control
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
with experiments on connected automated vehicles
[
16
]
, obstacle avoidance scenarios
[
17
]
, and lane keeping
[
14
,
18
]
.

In split-
\mu
braking, a key aspect of designing a safe controller is friction, which limits the available braking forces and thus leads to input constraints in addition to the lateral safety constraints.
The literature offers several CBF-based approaches to input-constrained safety-critical control.
These include input-constrained CBFs
[
19
]
and sum-of-squares programming
[
20
]
that reduce the size of safe sets to account for input bounds,
and integral CBFs
[
21
]
that use forward integration to define safe sets for states and inputs separately.
A core challenge in these approaches is to prove the recursive feasibility of safe controllers.
To address this, the
backup set method

[
22
]
(or also called backup CBF method) predicts the future evolution of the system under a predefined backup controller and uses this information to calculate a safe and feasible control input.
This approach has been leveraged in a variety of applications, such as robotic systems
[
23
]
,
including rovers
[
24
]

and drones
[
25
]
,
spacecraft
[
26
,
27
]
,
and kinematic vehicle models
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
.

Thanks to its formal safety guarantees and feasibility under input constraints, the backup set method is an ideal candidate for tackling split-
\mu
braking.
However, a prerequisite to this method is the synthesis of a backup controller and a corresponding backup set, which may be challenging for complex systems.
Most of the above-mentioned works used physical intuition to design backup sets and controllers, while
[
31
]
elaborated a formal construction for the special case of second-order systems.
These prior works, however, do not necessarily verify that the resulting backup set-backup controller pairs satisfy the properties required for safety and feasibility.
An algorithmic approach to the construction of
valid
backup set-backup controller pairs is missing from the literature.

In this paper, we develop a safety-critical control framework for vehicles braking on asymmetric surfaces.
We use the backup set method to keep the lateral kinematics (yaw rate and side slip angle) within safe bounds while satisfying input constraints arising from the physical limits of braking forces.
Our contributions are twofold.
First, we introduce a systematic method to generate backup set-backup controller pairs via feedback linearization and continuous-time Lyapunov equations.
This advances control theory for input-constrained safety-critical systems
by solving a key challenge for backup CBFs.
Second, we use this method to develop a safe controller for split-
\mu
braking that prevents excessive lateral motions while bringing vehicles to a stop within a short distance.
We establish the controller for a four-wheel planar vehicle model with tire dynamics,
and we demonstrate the efficacy of our approach in simulation.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of CBF theory to split-
\mu
braking.

II-B

Backup set method

The

[
22

has been proposed for addressing safety-critical control under input constraints. Its core idea is to predict the future state of the system under an input-constrained controller, called the backup controller, and ensure that the safe control task remains feasible with this controller.
A detailed proof of safety guarantees is given in
[

32

The first step of this method is the construction of a valid

That is, one must define a subset of the constraint set

III
Synthesis of Backup Sets and Controllers

Our main contribution is a systematic method for constructing valid backup set-backup controller pairs that rigorously handle input constraints. Our approach begins with defining the desired geometry of the backup set using the continuous-time Lyapunov equation
[
35
]
. This allows us to generate ellipsoid-shaped sets that are provably forward invariant, but for a simplified, ”target” linear system. To bridge the gap to our original nonlinear system, we synthesize a backup controller via feedback linearization. The crucial insight for handling input constraints is that the linearization is only required inside the backup set. Thus, by choosing the backup set to be sufficiently small to lie within the region where the controller does not saturate, we can safely apply saturation outside the set. This helps to satisfy the input limits globally without invalidating the set’s guaranteed forward invariance.

Our construction can be represented in the following form for feedback linearizable systems with invertible input matrix:

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=c-V(\mathbf{x})\,,

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sat}\Big(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})^{-1}\big(-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\big)\Big)\,,

where
{c>0}
is a constant,
{V:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}}
is a Lyapunov function,

\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a system matrix,
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is an equilibrium point, and
\mathrm{sat}(\cdot)
saturates the backup controller to satisfy (
10
).

In the following subsections, we revisit Lyapunov’s method and feedback linearization, and we explain how to choose

V

\mathbf{A}

, as well as how to extend this idea to more general systems. Finally, we provide illustrative examples.

III-A

Lyapunov equations for backup sets

First we discuss the construction of backup sets for linear systems.
An important observation is that the shape of the backup set is irrelevant for the applicability of the method. This allows us to focus on finding a forward invariant backup set for a given backup controller—opposite to the QPs above that seek controllers ensuring forward invariance for given sets.

We use Lyapunov’s method for the construction of forward invariant sets for linear systems in the form:

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\,,

(18)

{\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

Lemma
1
states that invariant sets exist for the stable system (
18
). To construct them, we use a quadratic Lyapunov function:

V(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\,

(21)

with a positive definite matrix
{\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}
. This form of
V
satisfies the positivity conditions in Lemma
1
.
The time derivative of
V
along the linear system (
18
) is:

\dot{V}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{PA}\big)(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\,.

(22)

If the matrix in the middle is negative semidefinite, then
{\dot{V}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0}
holds for all
{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, and
\Omega_{c}
in (
20
) is forward invariant per Lemma
1
. This condition is enforced by satisfying the
continuous-time Lyapunov equation
(CTLE):

\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{PA}=-\mathbf{Q}\,,

(23)

where
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is chosen to be a symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrix.
The following lemma connects the stability of
\mathbf{A}
to the solvability of the CTLE.

Lemma 2

(​​
[
35
]
)
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is Hurwitz and
\mathbf{Q}
is symmetric positive definite, the CTLE (
23
) has a unique symmetric positive definite solution for
\mathbf{P}
. With this solution,
V
in (
21
) satisfies (
19
), thus

\Omega_{c}
in (
20
) is forward invariant along (
18
).

With Lemma
2
, we can construct forward invariant backup sets for (
18
) by setting
{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}\triangleq\Omega_{c}}
.
Thus
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
is given by (
9
) with:

h_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})=c-V(\mathbf{x})=c-(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\,,

(24)

where one must choose a symmetric positive definite
\mathbf{Q}
(such as
{\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}}
) and solve the CTLE (
23
) to obtain
\mathbf{P}
.
This gives an ellipsoid-shaped set as shown with blue in Fig.
2
.
The center of this set is the point
{\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}}
.
The size of the set depends on
c
whereas its shape and orientation are determined by
\mathbf{P}
.

To obtain a
valid
backup set, as in Definition
3
,
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
must be forward invariant.
Therefore, based on Lemma
2
,
\mathbf{A}
must be Hurwitz (while
\mathbf{x}^{*}
and
c
do not affect invariance).
Additionally, the backup set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
must be a subset of the constraint set
\mathcal{S}
in (
4
).
Thus, its center
\mathbf{x}^{*}
must be in the interior of
\mathcal{S}
:

h(\mathbf{x}^{*})>0,

(25)

while its size
c
must be small enough.
We provide the suitable choices of
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, and
c
in the context of the backup controller.

III-B

Feedback linearization for backup controllers

Having established the form of the backup set, now the backup controller needs to be developed. Evidently, the objective is to transform the original nonlinear system in (
1
) into the linear one in (
18
). This is achieved via
feedback linearization
, that stabilizes the system around the equilibrium point
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

We first present feedback linearization under the following strong assumption which will be relaxed in the next subsection.

Assumption 1
.

The system (
1
) is fully actuated (
{n=m}
) and the input matrix
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})
is invertible for all
{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

With this assumption, the system (

\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{FL}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})^{-1}\big(-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\big).

(26)

Note that this controller may violate the input bounds, i.e., there may exist
{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
such that

The states where controller (

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ns}}=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{FL}}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{U}\big\}\,.

(27)

As outlined in (
17

4
4

4

To ensure the smoothness of the backup controller

\begin{split}\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})&=\mathrm{sat}\big(\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{FL}}(\mathbf{x})\big)\,,\\

k_{\mathrm{FL},i}(\mathbf{x})&\mathrm{if}\,\,u_{\min,i}\leq k_{\mathrm{FL},i}(\mathbf{x})\leq u_{\max,i}\,,\\

for all

Note that

III-C

Design considerations and practical guidelines

The overall procedure of input-constrained safety-critical control is summarized in Fig.
3
.
First, we design the proposed backup set, as in (
9
) and (
24
), and backup controller, as in (
26
) and (
28
), through the following steps.

1.

Choose an equilibrium
\mathbf{x}^{*}
satisfying (
25
) and (
29
).

2.

Choose a Hurwitz
\mathbf{A}
.

3.

Choose a SPD
\mathbf{Q}
and solve the CTLE (
23
) for
\mathbf{P}
.

4.

Choose
c
so that
{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ns}}}
.

These steps are performed only once (offline).
Then, at each time moment during safety-critical control (online), we proceed with the steps of the backup set method as in
[
22
]
.

5.

Solve the initial value problem (
16
).

6.

Solve the backup CBF-QP in (
14
).

Fig. 3:
Flow chart of the proposed method, summarizing the steps to generate valid backup set-backup controller pairs for use in the backup set method, which ultimately enables input-constrained safety-critical control.

While these steps provide a constructive framework for synthesizing a valid
(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}},\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{b}})
pair, they also offer considerable design freedom. The choice of the parameters
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Q},c)
collectively determine the final invariant set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{I}}
.
The parameter selection is guided by the following principles and trade-offs.

Typically, the practical goal is to select these parameters so that they yield the largest possible set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{I}}
, as this translates into a less conservative controller.
The size and shape of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{I}}
is determined by the interplay of the backup controller
\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{b}}
, backup set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
, and prediction horizon
T
. Since a long horizon is computationally expensive and sensitive to model errors, it is practically advantageous to construct the largest possible
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
and obtain a large
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{I}}
with a short and robust prediction.

To enlarge
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
, the equilibrium point
\mathbf{x}^{*}
should be chosen strategically to center the backup set. A choice deep within the intersection of the constraint set
\mathcal{S}
and the no-saturation region
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ns}}
maximizes the available space for
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
to be expanded.
A minimal-effort steady state is often a good candidate.

The system matrix
\mathbf{A}
dictates the desired closed-loop dynamics and presents a key trade-off. While an ”aggressive”
\mathbf{A}
(with fast poles) promises rapid stabilization, the total control effort of
\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{FL}}
in (
26
)
may become large. This shrinks the no-saturation region
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ns}}
, thus limiting the maximum possible size of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
. The optimal choice of
\mathbf{A}
is often system-dependent, balancing the desired convergence rate with having low total control effort over a large region.
The matrix
\mathbf{Q}
then shapes the geometry of the resulting ellipsoid
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
via the CTLE in (
23
).

Finally, the choice of the scalar
c
can maximize the size of the backup set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
while satisfying the conditions
{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ns}}}
. The maximal
c
is found where the boundary of the ellipsoid
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
becomes tangent to the boundary of either
\mathcal{S}
or
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ns}}
. This geometric condition can be formulated as a constrained optimization problem and solved using the method of Lagrange multipliers
[
36
]
. The core idea, that the gradients of the respective surfaces are parallel at the point of tangency, leads to a system of nonlinear algebraic equations. A similar principle of finding the largest invariant set via tangency conditions was applied to systems with time delays in
[
37
]
.

To illustrate the full control synthesis procedure, we present a simple example: a fully-actuated scalar system.

(31)

III-D

Generalization to output coordinates

The backup set-backup controller pair above is established for a certain class of feedback linearizable systems satisfying Assumption
1
, where the number of states and inputs is the same (
{n=m}
) and the input matrix
{\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})}
is invertible.
Since this assumption is restrictive, now we relax it and extend our method to a wider class of systems.

In particular, we perform feedback linearization for a set of output coordinates
\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})
rather than for the full state
\mathbf{x}
.
We construct backup controllers that make the output dynamics linear and we create forward invariant backup sets based on the output.
For this construction, the notion of relative degree needs to be introduced for output coordinates.

Definition 4
.

(​​
[
38
]
) The smooth function
\mathbf{y}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is said to have
relative degree

r\in\mathbb{N}
with respect to (
1
) if:

\displaystyle(\mathrm{i})

\displaystyle L_{\mathbf{g}}L_{\mathbf{f}}^{i}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}\,,\quad\forall i\in\{0,\dots,r-2\}\,,

(36)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{ii})

\displaystyle\mathrm{rank}\big(L_{\mathbf{g}}L_{\mathbf{f}}^{r-1}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})\big)=p\,,

hold
5
5

5

The Lie derivative of
\mathbf{y}
along
\mathbf{f}
is
L_{\mathbf{f}}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\nabla\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
, while higher-order Lie derivatives are defined recursively:
L_{\mathbf{f}}^{i}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\nabla\big(L_{\mathbf{f}}^{i-1}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})\big)\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
.
for all
{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.

With this definition, we replace Assumption
1
with a milder assumption that requires an output with a valid relative degree.

Assumption 3
.

For the system (
1
), there exists a smooth output
{\mathbf{y}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}}
with relative degree
r
, so that the number of outputs and inputs is the same (
{p=m}
).

This means that
L_{\mathbf{g}}L_{\mathbf{f}}^{r-1}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})
is an invertible square matrix, as opposed to Assumption
1
where
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})
is invertible.
Later we demonstrate in an example that this is a much milder condition.

.
This results in the output dynamics:

\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\\
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{3}\\
\vdots\\
L_{\mathbf{f}}^{r}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})+L_{\mathbf{g}}L_{\mathbf{f}}^{r-1}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(38)

The feedback linearization controller for (
38
) becomes
[
38
]
:

\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{FL}}(\mathbf{x})=\big(L_{\mathbf{g}}L_{\mathbf{f}}^{r-1}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})\big)^{-1}\bigg(-L_{\mathbf{f}}^{r}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{K}_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}\bigg)\,,

(39)

where
\mathbf{K}_{i}
are control gains.
Note that the decoupling matrix
L_{\mathbf{g}}L_{\mathbf{f}}^{r-1}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})
is invertible based on Assumption
3
.
With this controller, the closed-loop output dynamics are:

\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}&\dots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}&\dots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\dots&\mathbf{I}\\
-\mathbf{K}_{1}&-\mathbf{K}_{2}&-\mathbf{K}_{3}&\dots&-\mathbf{K}_{r}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\eta}\,,

(40)

where the matrix
\mathbf{A}
can be made Hurwitz by designing the gains
\mathbf{K}_{i}
, for example, using pole placement.

Then, we propose to use the backup controller in (
28
) by saturating the feedback linearization controller in (
39
).
The corresponding backup set becomes:

h_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})=c-\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\eta}\,,

(41)

cf. (
24
).
This construction recovers the backup controller and backup set in Section
III-B
as a special case where the output is
{\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}}
and it has relative degree one (i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{\eta}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}}
).

To make this backup set-backup controller pair valid, one shall proceed with the following steps:

1.

Find an
m
-dimensional output
\mathbf{y}
with a relative degree.

2.

Choose an equilibrium
\mathbf{x}^{*}
satisfying (
25
) and (
29
).

3.

Design the gains
\mathbf{K}_{i}
so that
\mathbf{A}
in (
40
) is Hurwitz.

4.

Choose a SPD
\mathbf{Q}
and solve the CTLE (
23
) for
\mathbf{P}
.

5.

Choose
c
so that
{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ns}}}
.

In the following example we demonstrate this procedure with emphasis on the choice of the output coordinate.

Example 2
.

Consider the model of an inverted pendulum:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\dot{x}_{1}\\
\dot{x}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{2}\\
\sin(x_{1})\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}u\,,

(42)

where the state
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},\,x_{2}]^{\top}
contains the angle
x_{1}
measured from the upright position and the angular velocity
x_{2}
, while the control input
u
is proportional to a torque generated by a motor.
Our goal is to keep the inverted pendulum above the horizontal position by
satisfying
{-\pi/2\leq x_{1}\leq\pi/2}
. We consider the following constraint function from
[
39
,
40
]
:

h(\mathbf{x})=\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(x_{2}+Kx_{1}\right)^{2}\,,

(43)

with parameters
{\mu>0}
,
{K>0}
, resulting in a rotated ellipse around the origin as constraint set.
Note that
h
is a valid CBF without input bounds (for an appropriate
{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}}
)
[
39
,
40
]
.
The difficulty in realizing a safety-critical controller is, however, the saturation of the input at
{u_{\min}\leq u\leq u_{\max}}
.
We address this challenge for
{u_{\min}=-0.75}
and
{u_{\max}=1.25}
.
We design a safe controller by modifying the desired controller
k_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{x})=0
that would let the pendulum fall.

We construct a backup set-backup controller pair using the proposed method.
Note that the inverted pendulum model (
42
) does not satisfy Assumption
1
as it has more states than inputs.
Thus, we shall select an output coordinate with a valid relative degree for feedback linearization, according to Section
III-D
.
While choosing
{y(\mathbf{x})=x_{2}}
would yield relative degree one, the resulting backup set would depend only on
x_{2}
and could not to be a subset of the constraint set given by (
43
).
Instead, the right choice is
{y(\mathbf{x})=x_{1}}
, which has relative degree two with

{L_{\mathbf{f}}y(\mathbf{x})=x_{2}}
,

{L_{\mathbf{g}}y(\mathbf{x})=0}
,

{L_{\mathbf{f}}^{2}y(\mathbf{x})=\sin(x_{1})}
, and

{L_{\mathbf{g}}L_{\mathbf{f}}y(\mathbf{x})=1}
.
This choice satisfies Assumption
3
.

Then, with
{\boldsymbol{\eta}=[x_{1}-x_{1}^{*},\,x_{2}]^{\top}}
, we construct the backup set in (
41
).
This is associted with the backup controller:

k_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{sat}\big(-\sin(x_{1})-K_{1}(x_{1}-x_{1}^{*})-K_{2}x_{2}\big)\,,

(44)

and the corresponding linear dynamics:

\begin{bmatrix}\dot{x}_{1}\\
\dot{x}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-K_{1}&-K_{2}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}-x_{1}^{*}\\
x_{2}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(45)

Next, we choose the parameters
c
,
\mathbf{Q}
,
\mathbf{x}^{*}
,
K_{1}
, and
K_{2}
.
We look for the equilibrium point
\mathbf{x}^{*}
by setting the right-hand side of (
42
) equal to zero. Solving the algebraic equations we get:

u^{*}=-\sin(x_{1}^{*})\,,\quad x_{2}^{*}=0\,.

(46)

The equilibrium point
\mathbf{x}^{*}
must lie inside the safe set, while the corresponding input must not saturate:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}-\big(x_{1}^{*}\big)^{2}-\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(x_{2}^{*}+Kx_{1}^{*}\right)^{2}>0\,,

(47)

\displaystyle u_{\min}<u^{*}=-\sin(x_{1}^{*})<u_{\max}.

This can be satisfied, for example, by
{x_{1}^{*}=0}
,
{x_{2}^{*}=0}
,
{u^{*}=0}
.
Then we design the gains
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
so that matrix
\mathbf{A}
in (
45
) is Hurwitz.
This holds for any
{K_{1},K_{2}>0}
.
The matrix
\mathbf{P}
can be constructed by solving the CTLE in (

) with
{\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}}
:

\mathbf{P}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{K_{1}(K_{1}+1)+K_{2}^{2}}{2K_{1}K_{2}}&\frac{1}{2K_{1}}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\frac{1}{2K_{1}}&\frac{K_{1}+1}{2K_{1}K_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(48)

Finally, parameter
c
is tuned so that the backup controller does not sature over the backup set that is inside the constraint set.

IV
Vehicle Braking on Asymmetric Surfaces

We now proceed to apply the proposed method to safe vehicle braking on asymmetric (split-
\mu
) surfaces.
We focus on the realization of a safe vehicle controller which can prevent the vehicle from spinning out while the braking forces – which are viewed as control inputs – are kept feasible.

IV-A

Modeling

Control design and numerical simulation require a suitable model.
This involves
vehicle, tire, and driver modeling.

Fig. 8:
Free-body diagram of the four-wheel planar vehicle model utilized for control design and numerical simulations.

IV-A
1
Vehicle model

To capture a braking on a split-
\mu
surface, we use a four-wheel vehicle model
[
41
]
, including left and right sides with different braking forces, and front and rear axles for considering front axle steerability.
The free-body diagram of the selected model is shown in Fig.
8
.
The vehicle has mass
m
and mass moment of inertia
I_{z}
about the vertical axis at the center of mass C.
The wheels are at distance
w
from C in the lateral direction, while the front and rear axles are at distance
a_{\rm f}
and
a_{\rm r}
from C, respectively.
The vehicle’s position in the Earth frame is given by
x_{\rm E}
and
y_{\rm E}
, whereas the longitudinal and lateral directions are indicated by
x
and
y
, respectively.
The yaw angle is
\psi
and the yaw rate is
\omega
.
The velocity of the center of mass is
\mathbf{v}
, its longitudinal and lateral components are
v_{x}
and
v_{y}
, and the side slip angle between the velocity vector and the longitudinal axis is
\beta
.
Each wheel is associated with a velocity vector
\mathbf{v}_{ij}
, a side slip angle
\alpha_{ij}
between the velocity and the longitudinal axis of the wheels, as well as longitudinal forces
F_{ij}^{x}
and lateral forces
F_{ij}^{y}
acting on the wheels.
The index
{i\in\{{\rm f},{\rm r}\}}
denotes the front or rear axle while the index
{j\in\{{\rm l},{\rm r}\}}
refers to the left or right wheel.
We assume parallel steering with small angles, so the front wheels are at the steering angle
\delta

relative to the longitudinal axis.

The kinematics of the vehicle are described by:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\rm E}

\displaystyle=v_{x}\cos\psi-v_{x}\tan\beta\sin\psi\,,

(49)

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{\rm E}

\displaystyle=v_{x}\sin\psi+v_{x}\tan\beta\cos\psi\,,

\displaystyle\dot{\psi}

\displaystyle=\omega\,,

which are used to calculate the trajectory of the vehicle during simulations.
The equations of motion can be derived using Lagrange’s equations of the second kind
[
41
, Ch. 10.2]
:

\begin{split}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\dot{v}_{x}\\
\dot{\beta}\\
\dot{\omega}\end{bmatrix}}_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}f_{v}\\
f_{\beta}\\
f_{\omega}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\cos\delta}{m}\!&\!\frac{\cos\delta}{m}\!&\!\frac{1}{m}\!&\!\frac{1}{m}\\
g_{1}\!&\!g_{1}\!&\!g_{2}\!&\!g_{2}\\
g_{3}\!&\!g_{4}\!&\!-\frac{w}{I_{z}}\!&\!\frac{w}{I_{z}}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}F_{\rm fl}^{x}\\
F_{\rm fr}^{x}\\
F_{\rm rl}^{x}\\
F_{\rm rr}^{x}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{u}}\,,\end{split}

(50)

which are used for control design considering
{v_{x}>0}
with:

\displaystyle f_{v}

\displaystyle=\omega v_{x}\tan\beta-\frac{\sin\delta}{m}\big(F_{\rm fl}^{y}+F_{\rm fr}^{y}\big)\,,

(51)

\displaystyle f_{\beta}

\displaystyle=-\omega\!+\!\frac{\cos\beta}{mv_{x}}\big((F_{\rm fl}^{y}\!+\!F_{\rm fr}^{y})\cos(\delta\!-\!\beta)\!+\!(F_{\rm rl}^{y}\!+\!F_{\rm rr}^{y})\cos\beta\big)\,,

\displaystyle f_{\omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{I_{z}}\!\big(\!(F_{\rm fl}^{y}\!-\!F_{\rm fr}^{y})w\sin\delta\!+\!(F_{\rm fl}^{y}\!+\!F_{\rm fr}^{y})a_{\rm f}\cos\delta\!-\!(F_{\rm rl}^{y}\!+\!F_{\rm rr}^{y})a_{\rm r}\big),

\displaystyle g_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos\beta}{mv_{x}}\sin(\delta-\beta)\,,\qquad\quad g_{2}=-\frac{\cos\beta}{mv_{x}}\sin\beta\,,

\displaystyle g_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{I_{z}}(a_{\rm f}\sin\delta-w\cos\delta)\,,\quad g_{4}=\frac{1}{I_{z}}(a_{\rm f}\sin\delta+w\cos\delta)\,.

IV-A
2
Tire model

The tire model determines the forces acting on the wheels.
We consider the wheels in plane with forces at the wheel’s contact point.
The longitudinal force takes the role of the control input.
For the lateral force, there exists a wide range of models in the literature, ranging from simple expressions to sophisticated models like Pacejka’s magic formula
[
42
]
.
To keep the system control affine and to simplify the control design, we assume that the lateral force is not affected by the longitudinal force, and we use a linear tire model:

F_{ij}^{y}=-C_{i}\alpha_{ij}\,,

(52)

{i\in\{{\rm f},{\rm r}\}}
,
{j\in\{{\rm l},{\rm r}\}}
, where
the wheel’s cornering stiffness
C_{i}
is considered to be the same for the left and right wheels.
The side slip angles
\alpha_{ij}
are calculated based on kinematics
[
41
]
:

\alpha_{{\rm f}j}=\tan^{-1}\!\!\left(\!\frac{v_{y}\!+\!a_{\rm f}\omega}{v_{x}\!\mp\!w\omega}\!\right)\!-\!\delta\,,\;\;\alpha_{{\rm r}j}=\tan^{-1}\!\!\left(\!\frac{v_{y}\!-\!a_{\rm r}\omega}{v_{x}\!\mp\!w\omega}\!\right),

(53)

with negative sign for left, positive for right, and
{v_{y}\!=\!v_{x}\tan\beta}
.

IV-A
3
Driver model

The driver model assigns the steering angle
\delta
based on the vehicle’s position and orientation.
Note that the driver model is required only for numerical simulations and it is not used by the control design.
Instead, the braking controller may use steering angle
\delta
measurements directly.
As the emphasis is not on the driver but on the controller, we choose a simple driver model on a straight road that
responds to the lateral position
y_{\rm E}
and yaw angle
\psi

[
43
]
:

\delta=-K_{y}y_{\rm E}-K_{\psi}\psi\,,

(54)

with driver parameters
{K_{y},K_{\psi}>0}
that affect lateral stability.

IV-B

Safety-critical control design

Next, we use the proposed method to design a safety-critical controller that determines the longitudinal forces
F_{ij}^{x}
for the system (
50
).
The controller seeks to prevent the vehicle from spinning out by keeping the side slip angle
\beta
and yaw rate
\omega
within safe bounds.
Thus, we define the constraint function:

h(\mathbf{x})=1-\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta_{\mathrm{cr}}}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{\mathrm{cr}}}\right)^{2}\,,

(55)

where
\beta_{\mathrm{cr}}
,
\omega_{\mathrm{cr}}
are critical side slip angle and yaw rate values,
leading to an ellipse-shaped safe region in the
(\beta,\omega)
plane.
We assume that the wheels cannot be accelerated during braking, so the longitudinal forces
F_{ij}^{x}
cannot be positive, while there is limited friction, hence the braking forces cannot exceed a maximum value
\bar{F}_{ij}
.
This leads to the input constraints:

-\bar{F}_{ij}\leq F_{ij}^{x}\leq 0\,.

(56)

Importantly, the maximum braking force
\bar{F}_{ij}
is different for the left and right wheels on asymmetric surfaces.
To minimize the stopping distance, the desired controller gives the maximum braking force,

{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{x})=[-\bar{F}_{\rm fl},\,-\bar{F}_{\rm fr},\,-\bar{F}_{\rm rl},\,-\bar{F}_{\rm rr}]^{\top}}
.

To ensure safety with input bounds, we construct a backup set-backup controller pair.
In this process, we view the steering angle
\delta
as a parameter.
First, we choose output coordinates:

\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}v_{x}\\
\omega\end{bmatrix}\,.

(57)

Notice that we excluded the side slip angle
\beta
from the output, because if
{\delta=\beta=0}
, the second row of
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})
in (
50
) is zero, thus
{L_{\mathbf{g}}\beta(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}}
, and
\beta
does not have a valid relative degree.
As opposed, the output
\mathbf{y}
has relative degree one.

Then, we establish a feedback linearization controller.
Based on Assumption
3
, the number of outputs and inputs must be the same.
Thus, we design only two inputs instead of four
and we assign the remaining two inputs proportionally:

\begin{bmatrix}F_{\rm fl}^{x}\\
F_{\rm fr}^{x}\end{bmatrix}=\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})\,,\quad\begin{bmatrix}F_{\rm rl}^{x}\\
F_{\rm rr}^{x}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}r_{\rm l}F_{\rm fl}^{x}\\
r_{\rm r}F_{\rm fr}^{x}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}r_{\rm l}\!&\!0\\
0\!&\!r_{\rm r}\end{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})\,,

(58)

where
{r_{j}=\bar{F}_{{\rm r}j}/\bar{F}_{{\rm f}j}}
,
{j\in\{{\rm l},{\rm r}\}}
, are the ratios of the maximal rear and front braking forces on the left and right. This gives:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x})\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x}),

(59)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}\\
\mathbf{r}\end{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})\,,\quad\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}\\
\mathbf{r}\end{bmatrix}\,,

where
\mathbf{I}
is the
{2\times 2}
identity matrix and
{\mathbf{r}={\rm diag}\{r_{\rm l},r_{\rm r}\}}
.
As the output in (
57
) has relative degree one, the backup controller can be computed using (
39
):

\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{sat}\bigg(\!\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\cos\delta+r_{\rm l}}{m}\!&\!\frac{\cos\delta+r_{\rm r}}{m}\\
g_{3}\!-\!\frac{r_{\rm l}w}{I_{z}}\!&\!g_{4}\!+\!\frac{r_{\rm r}w}{I_{z}}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\!\!\begin{bmatrix}-f_{v}-a_{x}^{*}\\
-f_{\omega}\!-\!K_{\omega}(\omega\!-\!\omega^{*})\end{bmatrix}\!\bigg),

(60)

where

a_{x}^{*}
is a desired deceleration to be selected later.
This yields the closed-loop output dynamics with the gain
{K_{\omega}>0}
:

\begin{bmatrix}\dot{v}_{x}\\
\dot{\omega}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-a_{x}^{*}\\
-K_{\omega}(\omega-\omega^{*})\end{bmatrix}\,.

(61)

Next, we construct the backup set based on (
41
):

h_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{x})=c-p_{\beta}\big(\beta-\beta^{*}\big)^{2}-p_{\omega}(\omega-\omega^{*})^{2}\,.

(62)

This formula is a modified version of (
41
), as we incorporate the state
\beta
instead of the output
v_{x}
when constructing the backup set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
.
While this modification allows us to ensure that
{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}}
, it may affect the forward invariance of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
under
\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{b}}
.
Therefore, forward invariance will be verified later for the appropriate choice of
{c>0}
,
\beta^{*}
,
\omega^{*}
, and
{p_{\beta},p_{\omega}>0}
.

Next, we select the parameters
c
,
p_{\beta}
,
p_{\omega}
,
\beta^{*}
,
\omega^{*}
,
a_{x}^{*}
, and
K_{\omega}
.
To select the equilibrium point
\beta^{*}
and
\omega^{*}
, we observe that constant-speed rectilinear motion is described by
{\beta=0}
,
{\omega=0}
,
{\delta=0}
, and zero braking force.
However, this cannot be chosen directly as equilibrium, because the driver may set nonzero
\delta
and also the zero force violates (
29
) as it is equal to the maximum input.
Instead, we choose
{\omega^{*}=0}
at the center of the constraint set and we seek for a nonzero
\beta^{*}
that satisfies
{\dot{\beta}=0}
in (
50
).
For
{\omega=0}
, we have
{\alpha_{{\rm f}j}=\beta-\delta}
,
{\alpha_{{\rm r}j}=\beta}
, and with small angle approximations for
\beta
and
\delta
we obtain:

\displaystyle f_{v}

\displaystyle\approx 0\,,\;\;f_{\beta}\approx\frac{-2}{mv_{x}}\big(C_{\rm f}(\beta-\delta)+C_{\rm r}\beta\big)\,,\;\;g_{1}\approx 0\,,\;\;g_{2}\approx 0\,,

(63)

\displaystyle f_{\omega}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{2}{I_{z}}\big(C_{\rm f}(\beta-\delta)a_{\rm f}-C_{\rm r}\beta a_{\rm r}\big),\;\;g_{3}\approx-\frac{w}{I_{z}}\,,\;\;g_{4}\approx\frac{w}{I_{z}}\,.

With this approximation,
{\dot{\beta}=0}
yields the choice:

\beta^{*}=\frac{C_{\rm f}}{C_{\rm f}+C_{\rm r}}\delta\,.

(64)

As
\beta^{*}
depends on
\delta
, the backup set in (
62
) moves in the state space if
\delta
changes over time.
Since the center of the backup set must be inside the constraint set according to (
25
), this choice of
\beta^{*}
is valid if
{-\beta_{\mathrm{cr}}<\beta^{*}<\beta_{\mathrm{cr}}}
.
Thus, our method is applicable in a narrower range of steering angles.

Now we determine the parameter
a_{x}^{*}
that affects the evolution of the velocity
v_{x}
in (
61
).
We notice that, while the backup set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
must be forward invariant, the choice of
a_{x}^{*}
does not need to render
v_{x}
exponentially stable, because the definition (
62
) of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
does not involve
v_{x}
.
Instead, we can just ensure
{\dot{v}_{x}\leq 0}
for braking by choosing
{a_{x}^{*}\geq 0}
.
We must select
a_{x}^{*}
so that the braking forces in (
60
) do not saturate inside the backup set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
.
We choose
a_{x}^{*}
such that the saturation curves at zero force move together with
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{b}}
as
\delta
changes, by setting:

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{FL},1}(v_{x},\beta^{*}+\beta_{\mathrm{d}},0)=0\,,\quad\mathrm{if}\,\delta\geq 0\,,

(65)

V
Conclusions

In this paper, we developed a safety-critical control framework for road vehicles to ensure bounded lateral motions with minimal stopping distance during split-
\mu
braking.
The fact that braking forces are limited by friction, and that vehicles cannot be accelerated during heavy braking, led to input constraints in addition to lateral safety constraints.
We employed the backup set method to synthesize controllers that guarantee the satisfaction of these constraints.
We introduced a novel, systematic approach to generating the required valid backup set-backup controller pairs, using a combination of feedback linearization and continuous time Lyapunov equations, along with meticulously chosen parameters.
We demonstrated our method on simple examples and highlighted its advantages over traditional CBF formulations.
Finally, we adapted the method to split-
\mu
braking and showcased that the proposed controller achieves safety with limited braking forces while maintaining a short stopping distance.
