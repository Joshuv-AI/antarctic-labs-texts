Longitudinal associations between exercise duration and sleep quality and poor sleep outcomes among college students: a latent growth curve modeling study
Authors: Qian Zhang, Ruichen Sun, Hanqing Zhao, Jiakun Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the effects of regular exercise on sleep quality and poor sleep outcomes, including bedtime procrastination, insomnia, and daytime sleepiness, among college students, and to examine the dynamic relationships between these outcomes and cumulative exercise duration over time.MethodsA total of 687 college students were followed longitudinally at baseline and at 4 and 8 weeks after the intervention. Sleep-related outcomes were assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Bedtime Procrastination Scale (BPS), Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), and Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS). Latent growth curve modeling (LGCM) was used to examine the developmental trajectories of sleep indicators as exercise duration accumulated over time, and cross-lagged panel modeling was further used to test the longitudinal predictive relationships among these variables.ResultsPSQI scores decreased linearly with increasing exercise duration (β = -0.88, P < 0.001), indicating progressive improvement in sleep quality. Bedtime procrastination (β = -0.72, P < 0.001), daytime sleepiness (β = -0.55, P < 0.001), and insomnia severity (β = -0.17, P < 0.05) also declined linearly over time. Baseline sleep quality and bedtime procrastination positively predicted subsequent changes in the later stages of the intervention (β = 0.41 and 0.65, respectively; P < 0.001). In addition, baseline bedtime procrastination positively predicted sleep quality at 4 weeks after the intervention (β = 0.20, P < 0.05).ConclusionsAccumulated exercise duration may effectively attenuate the deterioration of sleep quality among college students. Regular exercise may promote sleep health through multidimensional physiological and behavioral pathways and exert beneficial effects on bedtime procrastination, insomnia, and daytime sleepiness. For students with more severe sleep problems, individualized exercise programs combined with behavioral interventions may help sustain improvements in sleep outcomes.

Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental biological process that is essential for maintaining physical health, cognitive performance, and emotional regulation [1]. Sleep quality is a multidimensional construct that typically includes sleep duration, sleep latency, nocturnal awakenings, subjective restorativeness, and daytime functioning [2]. International consensus has emphasized that sufficient and regular sleep is necessary for optimal health, whereas poor sleep outcomes refer to a range of problems that impair sleep quality or duration and are associated with adverse cognitive, emotional, and health consequences [3].

College students are particularly vulnerable to sleep problems because they are in a transitional developmental stage between late adolescence and early adulthood and are frequently exposed to academic pressure, changing social roles, and irregular daily routines. Recent evidence indicates that poor sleep outcomes are highly prevalent in this population, including difficulty falling asleep, insufficient nocturnal sleep, daytime sleepiness, and bedtime procrastination. In particular, problematic smartphone use has been linked to poorer sleep quality and increased risks of depression and anxiety, whereas bedtime procrastination has been identified as a distinct behavioral factor associated with shorter sleep duration, poorer sleep quality, and greater sleep-related impairment [4–7]. These sleep-related problems may further impair academic performance, daily functioning, and psychological well-being [8, 9].

Among the most common poor sleep outcomes in college students are bedtime procrastination, daytime sleepiness, and insomnia. Bedtime procrastination refers to the voluntary delay of bedtime in the absence of external constraints and is generally considered a form of self-regulation failure [5]. Previous studies have shown that bedtime procrastination is associated with insufficient sleep, poorer subjective sleep quality, delayed sleep onset, and greater daytime impairment [6, 7]. In Chinese undergraduate students, bedtime procrastination has also been reported to independently predict both the prevalence and severity of poor sleep quality [7]. Daytime sleepiness, another common problem, is characterized by difficulty maintaining wakefulness and alertness during the day and is associated with inattention, reduced academic performance, increased negative affect, and lower life satisfaction [8]. Insomnia is also highly prevalent among college students and young adults and is typically characterized by difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, early morning awakening, or nonrestorative sleep despite adequate opportunity for sleep, together with impaired daytime functioning. Existing research has further shown that insomnia is closely associated with depression, anxiety, attentional difficulties, and elevated overall health risks. From a public health perspective, chronic sleep insufficiency and insomnia have also been linked to obesity, metabolic dysregulation, and increased cardiovascular risk [9]. These poor sleep outcomes should therefore not be regarded as isolated problems; rather, they represent interconnected manifestations of impaired sleep health that may jointly compromise cognitive functioning, emotional stability, and daytime functioning. Examining their dynamic relationships with sleep quality may therefore help identify developmental patterns and potential intervention targets in college students [10].

Regular physical activity is widely recognized as an important behavioral factor for improving sleep and emotional well-being. A systematic review and meta-analysis focusing on university students showed that sleep and physical activity are closely related in this population, although the available evidence remains heterogeneous in study design, measurement approaches, and intervention characteristics [11]. Previous studies have shown that college students who engage in regular exercise report fewer sleep problems than those who do not [12]. Exercise may also enhance psychological adaptation and improve the ability to cope with stress and social challenges [13]. In contrast, physical inactivity and sedentary behavior have been associated with poorer mental health, lower life satisfaction, and impaired behavioral adaptation among college students [14]. In addition, insufficient physical activity may adversely affect physiological and psychological functioning through multiple biological pathways. For example, physical inactivity may impair mitochondrial energy metabolism, reduce prefrontal executive functioning, and alter the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), thereby weakening emotional regulation and stress-coping capacity [15–18]. From a behavioral medicine perspective, promoting regular physical activity may therefore be of substantial value for improving both physical and mental health in college students. Exercise-based behavioral interventions may also help reduce the risk of poor sleep behaviors and enhance overall adaptation in this population [14].

Although previous studies have reported beneficial associations between physical activity and sleep quality, several important gaps remain. First, much of the existing evidence in college students is cross-sectional, which limits the ability to describe how sleep-related outcomes change over time as exercise exposure accumulates [11]. Second, recent meta-analytic evidence suggests that exercise type, intensity, dose, and intervention duration may influence the magnitude of sleep improvement, but these findings have mainly been derived from broader adult or older adult populations rather than from college students specifically [19, 20]. Third, few studies have simultaneously examined multiple sleep-related outcomes, including sleep quality, bedtime procrastination, daytime sleepiness, and insomnia, within the same longitudinal framework. Therefore, although the sleep-promoting effects of exercise are supported by previous research, the dynamic trajectories of multiple poor sleep outcomes during an exercise intervention among college students remain insufficiently understood.

To address these gaps, the present study examined the longitudinal relationships between exercise duration and multiple sleep-related indicators in college students using latent growth curve modeling. Specifically, this study aimed to investigate whether sleep quality improves and whether bedtime procrastination, daytime sleepiness, and insomnia decrease over time as exercise duration accumulates, and to further test the predictive relationships among their developmental trajectories. The findings may provide evidence for the prevention and intervention of poor sleep outcomes and contribute to the promotion of sleep health and psychological well-being among college students.

The hypotheses of this study were developed based on behavioral medicine and self-regulation perspectives. Exercise may improve sleep quality through both physiological pathways, such as thermoregulation, neuroendocrine modulation, and enhanced sleep homeostasis, and psychological pathways, such as stress reduction, improved mood, and strengthened self-regulation [21–25]. Therefore, sleep quality, daytime sleepiness, and insomnia symptoms were expected to improve progressively as exercise duration accumulated. In addition, bedtime procrastination is considered a behavioral manifestation of self-regulation failure and has been associated with poor sleep quality and insufficient sleep [5–7]. It is therefore reasonable to expect that changes in bedtime procrastination and sleep quality may be dynamically related over time. Finally, participants with poorer baseline sleep status may have greater room for improvement, whereas they may also show more persistent sleep-related vulnerability. Accordingly, baseline sleep status was examined as a predictor of subsequent rates of change in sleep-related outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a longitudinal follow-up design to examine the dynamic relationships between changes in exercise duration and sleep quality and poor sleep outcomes among college students. Repeated measurements were conducted over one academic semester at three time points: before the exercise intervention, 4 weeks after the intervention, and 8 weeks after the intervention. By continuously tracking changes in sleep-related indicators in the same group of participants, the study evaluated the effects of exercise intervention on sleep health in college students. Latent growth curve modeling and cross-lagged panel modeling were further used to examine the longitudinal predictive relationships among the study variables.

Participants

A longitudinal survey was conducted between September and November 2024 in two universities in Beijing, China. The participants were undergraduate students recruited through an online survey platform. All participants took part voluntarily and completed the questionnaires independently. All participants were at least 18 years old at the time of enrollment. Data were collected across three waves. The first survey was administered in September 2024, followed by two follow-up surveys at 4-week intervals. To ensure longitudinal matching, each participant was assigned a unique identification code that remained consistent across all three waves.

A total of 984 valid questionnaires were obtained in the first wave. Responses were considered valid if participants (1) completed all questionnaire items, (2) provided a consistent identification code that could be matched across waves, and (3) passed embedded attention-check items with regularity of response patterns and reasonable completion time of > 3 min. Of these, 186 participants completed only Wave 1 and were lost to follow-up at Wave 2; 94 participants completed Waves 1 and 2 but did not complete Wave 3; and 17 participants completed Waves 1 and 3 but missed Wave 2. After the second and third waves of follow-up, 687 participants who completed all three surveys were included in the final analysis. The final sample comprised 258 males and 429 females, with a mean age of 18.3 ± 0.5 years(range:18–20). The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (Approval No. Z2023SY050).

Measures

Pittsburgh sleep quality index

Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [26]. The PSQI consists of 18 items covering seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored from 0 to 3, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for the PSQI was 0.81.

Epworth sleepiness scale

Daytime sleepiness was assessed using the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) [27]. The ESS includes eight items, each rated on a 4-point scale from 0 to 3, where 0 indicates no chance of dozing and 3 indicates a high chance of dozing. Total scores range from 0 to 24. A score of 11 or higher is generally considered indicative of excessive daytime sleepiness, with higher scores reflecting greater severity. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha for the ESS was 0.84.

Insomnia severity index

Insomnia symptoms were assessed using the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) [28]. The ISI contains seven items and evaluates insomnia-related symptoms and their impact on daily life over the previous two weeks. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale from 0 to 4, and the total score is calculated by summing all item scores. Based on the total score, insomnia severity can be categorized as no clinically significant insomnia, mild insomnia, moderate insomnia, or severe insomnia. Higher scores indicate more severe insomnia symptoms. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha for the ISI was 0.76.

Bedtime procrastination scale

Bedtime procrastination was assessed using the Bedtime Procrastination Scale (BPS) [29]. The BPS consists of nine items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always).The total score is calculated as the mean of the nine items, ranging from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating more severe bedtime procrastination.In this study, Cronbach’s alpha for the BPS was 0.76.

Exercise intervention

A three-stage progressive training program was implemented as the exercise intervention. Each session lasted 90 min and was conducted one to two times per week. Each training session consisted of three approximately 30-minute phases: aerobic exercise, skill-based exercise, and flexibility stretching. This progressive structure was designed to gradually increase physical activity levels while promoting post-exercise recovery.

The aerobic phase included steady jogging, basic aerobic exercise step combinations, and intermittent rope skipping, with the aim of improving cardiopulmonary fitness and activating major muscle groups. The skill-based phase included basic aerobics movements, simplified Tai Chi routines, and fundamental ball-sport techniques to improve coordination and motor skills. The flexibility phase primarily involved static stretching, supplemented by proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation stretching, to enhance joint range of motion and facilitate muscular recovery.

Exercise intensity was regulated mainly by heart rate and perceived exertion. During the aerobic phase, the target heart rate was set at approximately 50% of age-predicted maximum heart rate. Maximum heart rate (HRmax) was estimated using the formula proposed by Tanaka et al.: HRmax = 208 − 0.7 × age [30]. Exercise intensity was also monitored using the talk test. During the stretching phase, a mild sensation of stretch was used as the standard, with emphasis placed on breathing rhythm and movement stability. The exercise intervention was conducted in a group-based format on the university campuses, supervised by qualified physical education instructors. The frequency of one to two sessions per week was designed to accommodate students’ academic schedules; sessions were typically held twice weekly, with frequency reduced to once weekly during periods of academic examinations or public holidays to ensure adherence and safety. The average attendance rate across the 8-week intervention was 98%, with participants completing on average 13.7 out of the 14 out of the total scheduled sessions.

The intervention parameters (frequency, duration, and intensity) were designed in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines on physical activity for adults, which recommend at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week [31]. The selection of intervention components was further informed by previous studies demonstrating that multimodal exercise programs combining aerobic, skill-based, and flexibility training are effective in improving sleep quality among young adults [21, 22].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t tests. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and between-group differences were examined using chi-square tests. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Latent growth curve modeling was conducted using AMOS version 23.0 to examine the developmental trajectories of sleep quality and poor sleep outcomes over time. Two latent factors were specified in each model: an intercept factor representing the initial level of the variable and a slope factor representing the rate of change over time. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the chi-square/degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR).

Cross-lagged panel models were further constructed using the three-wave follow-up data to examine the dynamic relationships between sleep quality and poor sleep outcomes. Both autoregressive paths and cross-lagged paths were specified to test reciprocal predictive relationships among variables across time. All model parameters were estimated using maximum likelihood estimation.

Specifically, Hypotheses 1–4, which proposed linear changes in sleep quality, bedtime procrastination, daytime sleepiness, and insomnia over time, were tested using unconditional linear latent growth curve models. Hypotheses 5–7, which proposed predictive relationships between baseline levels and subsequent rates of change among sleep quality and bedtime procrastination, were examined using cross-lagged panel models based on the three-wave data. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d for paired comparisons; standardized β for path analyses) were reported alongside significance tests.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 687 college students were included in the final analysis, comprising 258 males (37.6%) and 429 females (62.4%), with a mean age of 18.4 ± 0.7 years (range: 18–20 years). At baseline, the mean global PSQI score of the total sample was 7.44 ± 4.10, indicating that overall sleep quality among first-year university students was sub-optimal. Using the commonly applied cut-off of PSQI > 5, a total of 426 participants (62.0%) were classified as poor sleepers at baseline, including 168 males (65.1%) and 258 females (60.1%). Male students reported significantly higher PSQI total scores than female students (8.08 ± 4.61 vs. 7.06 ± 3.72), suggesting poorer baseline sleep quality in males.

With respect to the seven PSQI components, male participants showed higher scores than females in subjective sleep quality (1.25 ± 0.94 vs. 0.84 ± 0.63), sleep latency (2.29 ± 1.92 vs. 1.73 ± 2.46), habitual sleep efficiency (0.42 ± 0.78 vs. 0.16 ± 0.59), and use of sleep medication (0.13 ± 0.61 vs. 0.05 ± 0.29), whereas female participants scored slightly higher on daytime dysfunction (1.67 ± 1.07 vs. 1.38 ± 0.97). Scores for sleep duration (0.71 ± 0.75 vs. 0.67 ± 0.68) and sleep disturbance (1.92 ± 0.58 vs. 1.95 ± 0.45) were comparable between sexes. Detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table 2.

Overall, sleep quality showed a gradual improvement as the duration of the exercise intervention increased. PSQI total scores decreased from baseline to week 4 and week 8, indicating progressive improvement in sleep quality over time. A similar trend was observed in both male and female students.

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis

The descriptive statistics of the study variables are shown in Table 4. The mean scores of the PSQI, ESS, BPS, and ISI were 4.98 ± 2.75, 7.62 ± 3.62, 25.04 ± 3.55, and 7.73 ± 3.25, respectively.

Pearson correlation analysis showed that the PSQI total score was significantly positively correlated with several of its sub-dimensions, including subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, sleep medication use, and daytime dysfunction (all P < 0.01). The ESS score was weakly but significantly negatively correlated with sleep efficiency (r = − 0.10, P < 0.05) and sleep medication use (r = − 0.12, P < 0.05). The BPS score was not significantly correlated with the PSQI total score (r = − 0.08, P > 0.05) or the ESS score (r = 0.03, P > 0.05) at the cross-sectional baseline level. Sensitivity analysis

To test the robustness of the model results, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by including age and sex as covariates in the original latent growth curve model and estimating a conditional LGCM. The results showed that, after controlling for age and sex, the model still demonstrated good fit (CFI > 0.90, RMSEA < 0.08), indicating that the model structure remained stable.

Path analysis further showed that the linear improvement trend in sleep quality over time was not significantly affected by age (β = -0.04) or sex (β = 0.09), suggesting that the beneficial effect of exercise intervention on sleep quality was generally consistent across age and sex groups.

In addition, the intercept factor and slope factor were significantly negatively correlated (r = -0.97), indicating that participants with poorer sleep quality at baseline, as reflected by higher PSQI scores, exhibited more pronounced improvement during the intervention (Fig. 7). Further visualization showed that both male and female participants exhibited similar downward trajectories in PSQI scores during the intervention period, with largely parallel patterns of change. Taken together, the sensitivity analysis supported the main conclusions of the original model, indicating that cumulative exercise duration is an important factor in improving sleep quality among college students and that this effect remains stable after adjustment for demographic variables (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Main findings

Using latent growth curve modeling and cross-lagged panel modeling, this study examined the dynamic trajectories of sleep quality and poor sleep outcomes among college students during the exercise intervention. The findings showed that as exercise duration accumulated, sleep quality improved progressively, whereas bedtime procrastination, daytime sleepiness, and insomnia symptoms all declined significantly. Overall, these results were broadly consistent with the proposed hypotheses and suggest that regular exercise may exert a stable and beneficial effect on sleep health in college students.

Further analyses indicated that participants with poorer baseline sleep status showed greater improvement during the intervention, although their overall sleep levels remained worse than those of participants with better baseline sleep. This finding suggests that exercise may improve sleep effectively, but exercise alone may be insufficient to fully reverse pre-existing sleep problems in higher-risk individuals.

In addition, bedtime procrastination appeared to improve more slowly than the other sleep-related indicators. This pattern suggests that bedtime procrastination may represent a relatively stable behavioral risk factor that is less responsive to short-term exercise intervention alone.

Possible mechanisms

College students face multiple challenges, including academic stress, role transition, and emotional adaptation, all of which may compromise sleep stability. The present findings further support the positive association between exercise and sleep quality and highlight the potential value of regular exercise for promoting sleep health in this population. Notably, the beneficial effects of exercise were reflected not only in cumulative improvements over time but also in measurable short-term changes, providing support for exercise-based approaches to sleep promotion [31].

Several physiological mechanisms may underlie these associations. Exercise-induced fluctuations in core body temperature may mimic physiological processes that precede sleep onset. In addition, acute exercise may regulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis; previous studies have shown reductions in salivary cortisol and increases in beta-endorphin following exercise. Such neuroendocrine responses may facilitate sleep by reducing arousal and enhancing sleep drive [21]. Regular exercise may also improve sleep through enhanced hippocampal neuroplasticity [22] and increased expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), thereby strengthening prefrontal regulation of sleep-wake rhythms [23]. These mechanisms may partly explain why individuals with more severe baseline sleep symptoms showed greater improvement during the intervention [24].

Psychological pathways may also be important. Acute exercise has been shown to reduce state anxiety and emotional fluctuation, which may in turn decrease sleep-onset delay and nocturnal awakening associated with negative affect. This interpretation is consistent with the dual-mode framework of exercise-related affect regulation [25]. In addition, regular exercise may generate a positive feedback cycle through behavioral reinforcement and improved self-regulation. Individuals with more severe sleep problems often exhibit lower self-efficacy and greater behavioral inertia, [32] whereas sustained aerobic exercise may enhance perceived control over health behaviors and improve the ability to regulate bedtime routines, thereby reducing delays in sleep onset related to bedtime procrastination.

The greater improvement observed among participants with more severe baseline symptoms may also be interpreted through the stress-buffering hypothesis. Individuals with higher levels of insomnia or daytime sleepiness may be exposed to greater academic stress and social anxiety, and regular exercise may reduce these burdens through both physiological and psychological pathways. When baseline symptom burden is greater, the buffering effect of exercise may become more apparent [33]. For example, moderate-intensity exercise may improve thermoregulation and increase the proportion of deep sleep, thereby reducing daytime sleepiness. Similarly, individuals with higher baseline daytime sleepiness may present greater metabolic or neuroregulatory dysregulation, making the restorative effect of exercise on sleep homeostasis easier to detect.

Comparison with previous studies

The present findings are consistent with previous studies reporting a beneficial association between exercise and sleep quality. Earlier research has shown that college students who regularly engage in physical activity experience fewer sleep-related problems than those with limited exercise habits. Although cumulative improvements were observed over the 8-week intervention period, the early-stage effects at week 4 were not uniform across all outcomes. Improvements at week 4 were limited to selected sleep-related indicators, whereas several outcomes showed minimal or non-significant changes. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of partial early improvement and more consistent cumulative benefits over time, rather than as a uniform early intervention effect across all sleep outcomes.

The present study also found that bedtime procrastination improved more slowly than daytime sleepiness and insomnia. This finding is consistent with previous evidence suggesting that bedtime procrastination is strongly shaped by contextual and habitual factors. Compared with daytime sleepiness and insomnia, bedtime procrastination may be more susceptible to external influences such as academic demands, social activities, and irregular daily schedules. As a result, exercise alone may have limited short-term effects on this behavior unless combined with more targeted behavioral strategies.

Implications

These findings have several practical implications. First, university health promotion programs may benefit from incorporating structured exercise interventions into broader sleep health management strategies and encouraging students to accumulate at least 150 min of moderate-intensity physical activity per week [34]. Such efforts may help improve sleep quality while also reducing multiple poor sleep outcomes among college students [35].

Second, for students with high levels of bedtime procrastination, exercise alone may be insufficient to produce sustained behavioral change. In such cases, exercise-based interventions may need to be combined with sleep hygiene education and behavioral approaches, including cognitive-behavioral strategies, to enhance the durability of intervention effects.

Third, the finding that students with more severe baseline sleep problems showed greater improvement suggests that exercise intervention may be particularly valuable for higher-risk individuals. Because these students may carry a greater physiological and psychological stress burden, reductions in stress load following regular exercise may result in more pronounced improvements in sleep-related outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, sleep quality, bedtime procrastination, daytime sleepiness, and insomnia were assessed primarily through self-report measures, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Future studies should combine self-report data with objective measures such as actigraphy, wearable devices, or polysomnography to improve measurement accuracy. Second, the sample was drawn from universities in a single geographic region, which may limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Third, although the sensitivity analyses showed that the model fit and the main path estimates remained stable after adjustment for age and sex, suggesting reasonable robustness, several potentially important confounding variables were not fully controlled, including diet, caffeine intake, academic stress, psychological status, social support, and exercise adherence. Accordingly, causal inferences should be made with caution. Finally, this study primarily focused on the relationship between exercise duration and sleep-related indicators and did not distinguish the potentially differential effects of exercise type or exercise intensity. Future studies should adopt multicenter, longer-term, or randomized controlled designs and incorporate additional behavioral, psychological, and environmental variables to further validate and extend the present findings.

Strength

Despite these limitations, the present study has several notable strengths. The relatively large sample size and longitudinal three-wave design strengthened the reliability of the findings and allowed us to examine changes in sleep-related outcomes over time. In addition, the exercise intervention incorporated multiple forms of physical activity, including aerobic exercise, skill-based exercise, and flexibility stretching, which enhanced the ecological validity of the intervention in a university setting. This study also collected diverse sleep-related data, including sleep quality, bedtime procrastination, daytime sleepiness, and insomnia symptoms, providing a multidimensional assessment of sleep health among college students. Moreover, the use of latent growth curve modeling and cross-lagged panel modeling enabled a more detailed examination of both developmental trajectories and longitudinal predictive relationships. These strengths support the contribution of the present study to understanding how structured exercise may promote sleep health in college students.

Conclusions

Accumulated exercise duration was associated with gradual improvements in sleep quality and reductions in bedtime procrastination, daytime sleepiness, and insomnia among college students. These findings support regular exercise as a potentially useful strategy for promoting sleep health in this population. However, because bedtime procrastination improved more slowly than other sleep-related indicators, exercise alone may be insufficient for some students, and combined behavioral approaches may be needed to achieve more sustained benefits.
