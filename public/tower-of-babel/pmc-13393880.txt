Breastfeeding in the shadow of war: a qualitative study on the experiences of Palestinian women
Authors: Seda Serhatlioğlu, Shaymaa S. J. Abdullatif, Hıdır Apak, Yeşim Yeşil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArmed conflict and protracted humanitarian crises profoundly affect maternal and infant health, rendering breastfeeding practices particularly vulnerable. While the existing literature largely focuses on breastfeeding rates and nutritional outcomes in conflict settings, limited attention has been given to how women experience, sustain, and make sense of breastfeeding under such conditions of violence, deprivation and displacement. This study aimed to investigate mothers’ breastfeeding experiences during the Gaza War (2023–2025) within the framework of the Infant and Young Child Feeding in Emergencies (IYCF-E) standards.MethodsThis qualitative study employed a phenomenological design. Sixteen forcibly displaced mothers who were actively breastfeeding during the 2023–2025 Gaza War were included using purposive sampling. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews conducted in Arabic via online platforms. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis approach, with independent coding by three researchers and consensus agreement.ResultsFive main themes were identified: (1) structural conditions of war, (2) psychological reflections of breastfeeding under war conditions, (3) physical health problems and living conditions, (4) coping and resistance mechanisms, and (5) lack of psychological and humanitarian aid. Mothers have experienced breastfeeding more intensely as a multidimensional tool for nutrition, soothing the baby, establishing physical closeness, providing emotional regulation, and maintaining the mother-baby attachment.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that protecting and supporting breastfeeding is not an option for assistance, but a non-negotiable humanitarian obligation. The example of Gaza highlights the urgency of implementing standards, integrating breastfeeding services with mental health and psychosocial support systems, and treating breastfeeding as a priority life-saving intervention in conflict settings.Trial registrationNot applicable. Background

Armed conflict and protracted humanitarian crises profoundly affect maternal and infant health and render breastfeeding practices structurally vulnerable. Restricted access to food, destruction of agricultural resources, forced displacement, and disruption of market mechanisms substantially increase the risk of malnutrition in conflict-affected settings, as documented in regions such as Sudan, Ethiopia, and Nigeria [1–4]. These structural conditions place women, particularly mothers of young children, at heightened risk of compromised health, infant survival, and caregiving capacity [5].

One of the most acute ongoing examples of this crisis today is the Gaza Strip, where violence has intensified since October 7, 2023 [6]. Ongoing conflict has created conditions of severe humanitarian devastation, markedly restricting humanitarian access and directly threatening maternal and child health [7]. Recent reports indicate that families in northern Gaza have been forced to consume animal feed and even wild plants to survive, with large segments of the population facing imminent risk of starvation [8]. Approximately two-thirds of households with children under five are experiencing food insecurity, with widespread reports of weakness, stunting, and acute malnutrition in Gaza [9].

The assessment of infant feeding practices in the context of emergencies and humanitarian crises should be addressed within the Infant and Young Child Feeding in Emergencies (IYCF-E) framework. The Operational Guidance on Infant and Young Child Feeding in Emergencies (OG-IFE) [10], endorsed by the World Health Organization (WHO), the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), and the World Food Programme (WFP) and published under the coordination of the IFE Core Group, defines the protection, support, and continuation of breastfeeding as a fundamental humanitarian standard in emergencies. Similarly, the Sphere Handbook [11] classifies breastfeeding as an essential life-saving intervention to prevent infant morbidity and mortality. In this regard, maternal undernutrition does not generally constitute a contraindication for breastfeeding; on the contrary, continuing to breastfeed is recommended, particularly in humanitarian crisis settings where access to safe water and suitable alternatives is limited [12]. The recommendations of the WHO and UNICEF regarding optimal infant and young child feeding practices include early initiation of breastfeeding; exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of life; age-appropriate, nutritionally adequate, and safe complementary foods starting at 6 months; and continued breastfeeding for 24 months or longer. [10, 13, 14]. In humanitarian emergencies, breastfeeding is not merely a recommended infant feeding practice but is recognized as a life-saving public health and humanitarian intervention. In conflict situations, the sustainability of these standards depends on systemic protection mechanisms rather than individual efforts. Beyond its nutritional and immunological functions, breastfeeding is a foundational relational and psychosocial practice that supports infant mental health, emotional regulation, early bonding, and maternal well-being [15–18]. Close physical contact, responsive feeding, and skin-to-skin interaction inherent to breastfeeding are central to stress regulation and early neurodevelopment, particularly under conditions of threat and instability [16, 19].

Recently, the literature on IYCF-E has been expanding. Reviews in the field of IYCF-E indicate that infant feeding practices are shaped by structural factors such as displacement, food insecurity, and gaps in access to health services [5]. Furthermore, qualitative studies conducted in the context of conflict and forced migration reveal that breastfeeding is not solely a feeding practice; it is also a multifaceted experience for mothers related to survival, caregiving, and the maintenance of maternal role identity [12, 20].

Despite its recognized life-saving role, armed conflicts severely disrupt breastfeeding practices. As in the case of the conflict settings, including the Lebanese war, demonstrates early cessation, increased mixed feeding and reduced breastfeeding frequency during periods of violence [21, 22]. Systematic reviews examining interventions related to infant feeding in crisis settings show that uncontrolled formula distribution and the absence of structured breastfeeding support services weaken breastfeeding practices. They also emphasize the importance of training health workers, providing breastfeeding counseling, and creating mother-and-baby-friendly spaces [23].

However, the existing literature has largely focused on breastfeeding rates, program outcomes, and nutritional indicators; qualitative evidence on how mothers experience, sustain, and make sense of breastfeeding under conditions of active bombardment, siege, and widespread hunger remains limited [15, 24]. Yet the WHO, UNICEF, and IYCF-E operational guidance emphasizes that breastfeeding is a holistic care practice associated with feeding, protection, bonding, and psychosocial well-being. In such settings, breastfeeding becomes a site where violations of the rights to food, health, child survival, and maternal dignity are embodied [10, 13, 14].

The evaluation of infant feeding practices during emergencies and humanitarian crises must be conducted within the framework of IYCF-E. In this study, IYCF-E was adopted as the primary analytical framework. Building on this framework, the study aims to examine the breastfeeding experiences of mothers in Gaza not through the lens of individual maternal behaviors, but rather in the context of the applicability and violations of humanitarian standards regarding the protection and support of breastfeeding in the field.

Methods

Design and participants

This study is a qualitative, phenomenological study conducted to gain an in-depth understanding of the experiences of breastfeeding mothers during the war in the Gaza Strip. Using a phenomenological approach, the study reveals the individual experiences, emotions, and meaning-making processes of mothers who were internal displaced persons within the Gaza Strip who continue to live in the region and were actively breastfeeding. Within this context, the study focused on understanding the psychosocial and existential dimensions of breastfeeding in a war environment, beyond it being a biological process. The study group consisted of 16 breastfeeding mothers living in the Gaza Strip who were forcibly displaced during the 2023–2025 war. Participant selection was carried out using purposive sampling. Only mothers who were currently breastfeeding, had experienced war and displacement, had access to communication tools to participate in the online interview, and voluntarily agreed to participate in the study were included. Although all participants were breastfeeding, some described temporary use of unsafe alternatives during periods of extreme deprivation, reflecting systemic failure rather than maternal choice.

Data collection

In this study, data were collected through in-depth interviews using online platforms (Zoom, WhatsApp, Google Meet) to enable breastfeeding mothers to express their experiences, feelings, and thoughts, using a descriptive information form and a semi-structured individual interview form. A semi-structured interview guide was used covering: breastfeeding experiences under war conditions; access to food and water; physical and psychological impacts; coping strategies; and access to health and humanitarian support (see Additional file 1). The research team is based in Turkey, while participants are living in Gaza under active war environment. Due to ongoing bombardment, security risks, movement restrictions, and the impossibility of physical access to the field, interviews were conducted exclusively via online platforms. This method was deemed an ethical and feasible means of data collection for both researchers and participants. However, this situation only allowed mothers with internet access to participate in the study and was therefore considered a limitation that could potentially introduce selection bias into the study.

Both the descriptive information form and the interview form were developed based on a literature review and question patterns from similar qualitative studies [25, 26]. The interview form consists of 4 main headings and 16 sub-questions aimed at revealing the experiences, feelings, and thoughts of mothers during the breastfeeding process in depth. To ensure the validity and applicability of the form, two pilot interview sessions were conducted on two cases before proceeding with the data collection process. These pilot applications were conducted to assess the clarity of the questions, the appropriateness of the content, and whether the interview flow progressed naturally, and were not included in the study [27].

Additionally, the content validity of the form was assessed using the Lawshe [28] method based on six expert opinions, and CVI values ranged from 0.833 to 0.889. Values above 0.80 across all dimensions indicated that the form had high content validity. This result supports the form as a valid and reliable data collection tool for examining breastfeeding experiences in a war environment.

The research team consisted of three female and one male researcher, all of whom had prior experience in qualitative research. Interviews were conducted by female researcher who a Palestinian academic working in the field of psychology who currently works at a university in Türkiye and speaks Arabic. The researcher’s knowledge of the cultural context and professional experience in the field of trauma facilitated the establishment of a relationship of trust during the remote interviews. Participant access was facilitated through the Palestinian researcher’s existing social and professional networks. The researcher reached out to potential participants through individuals with whom they had previously communicated in health and academic contexts, provided information about the study’s objectives, and invited participants on a voluntary basis. During this process, a relationship of trust was established with the participants through a common language (Arabic), cultural affinity, and the researcher’s familiarity with the region. No video recordings were made during the interview process. It was anticipated that the data collection process could be emotionally challenging for participants due to its inclusion of experiences of war and loss. Therefore, interviews were conducted using a trauma-sensitive approach, and it was clearly stated that participants could end the interview at any time or if bombing occurred in their area.

During the interviews, the emotional boundaries of the participants were respected. When intense emotional reactions arose, the interview was paused, the participant was asked if they wished to continue, and it was emphasized that control remained with the participant. Participants were informed that they could be referred for free online psychological support in cases of potential psychological distress. Interviews lasted an average of 30–45 min. The data collection process was terminated when data saturation was reached [27]. To preserve the accuracy and integrity of the interview content, each interview was audio-recorded with the mothers’ permission and transcribed verbatim by the researcher after the interview. Transcribed data were first translated from Arabic to English and then to Turkish by a researcher fluent in both languages to minimize linguistic and cultural meaning loss during the analysis process. Researchers performed coding and thematic analysis on the texts. Findings were transferred back into the publication language, English. To prevent loss of meaning, translations were cross-checked by the research team.

To protect participant privacy, online platforms were used solely for conducting the interviews, and cloud-based recording features were not utilized. Interview recordings were stored on password-protected local devices; the data was de-identified and kept in secure digital files accessible only to the research team. Any residual data that might have remained on the platforms following the analysis process was deleted.

Ethical process

The necessary ethical approval for conducting the study was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Mardin Artuklu University (Date: 07.10.2025, Decision No: 2025\10–31). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the purpose of the study, confidentiality principles, and the basis of voluntary participation; verbal informed consent was obtained. Participants’ identities were kept confidential, and interview recordings were coded and stored in a manner accessible only to the research team. The findings regarding the use of infant formula in this study do not in any way imply that formula feeding is endorsed or supported in emergency situations. On the contrary, the use of infant formula is presented not as a “solution,” but as an indication of the failure of the humanitarian aid system to support breastfeeding.

Data analysis

This research report is based on the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) guidelines for reporting qualitative research [29]. Quantitative data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software. The descriptive characteristics of the participants were evaluated using frequency (n, %), mean, and standard deviation for categorical variables. Qualitative components were analyzed using thematic analysis. The analysis process was conducted according to the six-step thematic analysis approach proposed by Braun and Clarke [30]: (1) Familiarization with the data, (2) Initial coding, (3) Identification of themes, (4) Review of themes, (5) Definition and naming of themes, (6) Reporting of findings. The data analysis was conducted by synthesizing the principles of reflective thematic analysis with the standards of the IYCF-E [10, 31]. The interview transcripts obtained from the audio recordings were transcribed verbatim by the researcher (SA) and anonymized by assigning a code number (Case 1, Case 2, Case 3…) to each participant. During the analysis process, the themes identified through inductive reasoning were interpreted in comparison with humanitarian aid standards established by IYCF-E, such as “protection of breastfeeding, provision of safe water and sanitation, regulation of formula donations, and mother-baby friendly spaces.” This analytical approach positioned mothers’ experiences not as individual behaviors, but as indicators of the current humanitarian aid system’s level of compliance with standards. During the thematic analysis process, researchers continuously compared their findings to ensure the consistency of the themes [30, 31]. The Max Qualitative Data Analysis (MAXQDA) Analytics Pro 2024 program was used as a supporting software tool for data analysis. The software facilitated the systematic coding of data, grouping under themes, and visualization of patterns. Direct quotations were used without any changes to the participants’ statements during the analysis process.

Methodological rigor and reflectivity

In this study, methodological rigor was maintained in accordance with the principles of validity and reliability in qualitative data analysis. Data were collected systematically, and the analysis process was documented transparently. Coding decisions and analysis steps were recorded in writing to ensure the auditability of the study. Interviews were conducted in Arabic, the participants’ native language; a second translator evaluated whether the quotations retained their meaning after translation [32]. Throughout the analysis process, the principle of reflexivity was adopted to minimize the influence of researchers’ own positions and preconceptions on the data. The research team considered how shared language, cultural familiarity, professional background, and researcher positioning might influence data collection, interpretation, and theme development [33]. The study analyzed women’s experiences not only from a clinical perspective but also at the intersection of factors such as displacement and gender [34] and within the framework of global public health dynamics [35].

Results

Sixteen breastfeeding mothers who experienced war-related displacement and famine in the Gaza Strip participated in the study. The ages of the mothers participating in the study ranged from 22 to 38, and all had been forcibly displaced due to the conflicts that lasted from 2023 to 2025. Half of the mothers live in tents, and one-third live in partially destroyed homes. Although most of the mothers’ husbands are alive, some are widowed or their husbands are missing.

The analysis of the data revealed five main themes reflecting the breastfeeding experience under war conditions in Gaza, with a total of 16 sub-themes identified under these themes. The themes identified in this study are not independent areas; they are interrelated processes that reflect the multi-layered nature of breastfeeding in war settings. The first three themes reveal the effects of structural conditions on mothers’ psychological worlds and physical experiences; “coping and resistance mechanisms” show mothers’ efforts to continue breastfeeding in this environment, while “lack of psychological and humanitarian aid” reveals the systemic void that encompasses the entire experience. When considered together, the themes reveal that breastfeeding under war conditions is experienced as a multidimensional practice shaped by hunger, trauma, faith, motherhood, and coping processes. Each main theme was presented with sub-themes that deeply reflected the experiences of breastfeeding mothers and was supported by the mothers’ statements. The frequencies (f) reported for themes reflect their occurrence within this qualitative sample and are presented for descriptive purposes only, without implying population-level inference (Table 1).

Themes and subthemes

Theme 1. Structural conditions of war

This theme encompasses the structural restrictions, severe food insecurity, and severe difficulties in accessing basic resources that shaped mothers’ breastfeeding experiences during the war. Mothers reported living under conditions of severe hunger and chronic deprivation and noted that these circumstances directly affected their breastfeeding processes. Under this theme, four sub-themes have been identified that reflect how hunger, nutritional deprivation, and lack of basic resources shape breastfeeding practices and maternal care.

Subtheme 1.1. Maternal hunger and perceived disruption of milk supply (f = 15)

Almost all mothers stated that the prolonged hunger and food deprivation caused by the war directly affected the breastfeeding process. Mothers frequently reported perceiving a decrease in milk production or fearing that their milk had dried up. Mothers’ narratives indicated a perceived lack of milk, which they associated with hunger, intense stress, and trauma, with no evidence from their reports suggesting a primary physiological insufficiency in milk production.We were displaced, and my baby is only one month old. There is a famine in Gaza, and the situation is extremely difficult. Due to my malnutrition, my baby is not getting enough milk from me. I lack the vitamins and healthy foods necessary for milk production. (Case 1)

We were displaced, and my baby is only one month old. There is a famine in Gaza, and the situation is extremely difficult. Due to my malnutrition, my baby is not getting enough milk from me. I lack the vitamins and healthy foods necessary for milk production. (Case 1)

Some mothers reported that they continued to breastfeed as a primary survival strategy, noting that they felt compelled to do so because the humanitarian aid system failed to provide any safe or accessible alternatives.I had no choice; I couldn’t wean my son because there was no other food. If I stopped breastfeeding, he would die. So I told myself, even a little breast milk is better than none at all. (Case 2)

I had no choice; I couldn’t wean my son because there was no other food. If I stopped breastfeeding, he would die. So I told myself, even a little breast milk is better than none at all. (Case 2)

Subtheme 1.2. Lack of infant formula, water, and sterilization (f = 11)

Many mothers (n = 11) the absence of formula and safe water, even when breastfeeding, as these conditions directly shaped mothers’ feeding decisions and emergency coping practices. They reported that they were often unable to access potable water to prepare infant formula and were therefore compelled to use unsafe water sources.There was no water or gas. I couldn’t boil the water. I used water that was not safe to drink when preparing formula. Sometimes, when there was no fresh water, I used salty water” (Case 3).

We give lentil soup and rice. (2-month-old baby) (Case 4).Because I gave him food that was not age-appropriate, he developed constipation, vomiting, and infection. I had to give him inappropriate food to save him from starvation and death, but in the end we wfwaent to the hospital. He became very ill, and I was exhausted along with him. He was my first child, my first joy, but unfortunately we were never happy together. (Case 10)

Subtheme 1.4. Non-nutritive breastfeeding as regulation and comfort (f = 5)

The analyzed data revealed that breastfeeding was experienced by participants not only as a nutritional act but also as an integrated process of infant care, aligning with the concepts of emotional regulation and secure attachment found in the literature. Women described putting their babies to the breast not only for nutrition, but also for comfort, calming, emotional regulation, and physical closeness.My child is only sucking to calm down, no milk is coming. Theme 2. Psychological reflections of breastfeeding under war conditions

This theme captures the psychological dimensions of breastfeeding under war conditions, highlighting how breastfeeding already a relational and emotionally regulating care practice became embedded in mothers’ experiences of fear, trauma, and survival. Mothers stated that they lived with constant fear, anxiety, and uncertainty during this process; breastfeeding was experienced as an effort to keep their babies alive. Under this theme, four sub-themes were identified that reveal the reflections of breastfeeding on mothers’ emotional experiences.

Subtheme 2.1. Fear of loss and traumatic motherhood experience (f = 16)

All mothers described motherhood under war conditions as an experience shaped by the fear of losing their children. Mothers stated that breastfeeding was experienced primarily through the lens of survival, safety, and protection, rather than as a routine feeding practice. Some participants mentioned giving birth under bombardment and direct threat, noting that this situation profoundly affected their experience of motherhood.I gave birth during the war, under bombardment. I was very afraid my child would die. (Case 12)Fourteen members of my family were martyred. My husband is missing; we don’t know if he’s alive or dead. I am alone with my child. The hardest thing… Really… Suffering… The fear that my child might die of hunger or disease. When I held him in my arms, he was so light. There was no milk due to malnutrition. It was truly terrifying. (Case 2)

The hardest thing… Really… Suffering… The fear that my child might die of hunger or disease. When I held him in my arms, he was so light. There was no milk due to malnutrition. It was truly terrifying. (Case 2)

Theme 3. Physical health problems and living conditions

All women stated that they had been displaced due to the war and were forced to move frequently. Mothers expressed that they were physically very weak, that moving or traveling was difficult, and that the hardest part was moving with their babies to unsuitable places such as tents. This theme reflects how maternal and infant health and daily living conditions intersected with breastfeeding under conditions of structural deprivation.

Subtheme 3.1. Deterioration of maternal health (f = 13)

Mothers reported feeling physically weak due to prolonged hunger and malnutrition. Participants stated that fatigue, weight loss, and a general feeling of weakness made it difficult to continue breastfeeding.I can’t even hold my baby in my arms; I’m hungry and very weak. Theme 5. Lack of psychological and humanitarian aid

All participants emphasized that basic humanitarian aid and psychological support were completely inadequate under ongoing bombardment and siege conditions. Under this theme, three sub-themes reflecting the humanitarian and psychosocial support needs expressed by the mothers were identified.

Subtheme 5.1. Need for access to food, infant formula, and hygiene supplies (f = 16)

All mothers stated that they faced serious difficulties in accessing basic necessities such as food, safe water, infant formula, diapers, and safe shelter, and that this situation threatened both their own health and their babies’ lives, and as fundamentally undermining breastfeeding and infant care.Stop the war, bring food, formula, and diapers. Discussion

The findings of this study reveal that breastfeeding under war conditions has become an extremely strained and intensified form of breastfeeding, which is already a relational, physical, and protective care practice. In the context of Gaza, breastfeeding has been shaped by severe hunger, forced displacement, psychosocial trauma, and the absence of structural support mechanisms; the daily practice of breastfeeding has become embedded in a living environment characterized by constant threat, deprivation, and uncertainty. Mothers’ narratives show that breastfeeding functions as a multidimensional caregiving practice encompassing nourishment, emotional regulation, physical closeness, and infant–mother connection, while supporting infant survival amid the collapse of humanitarian systems and basic living conditions.

These findings reveal that war-related hunger and food deprivation structurally undermine breastfeeding by eroding the physical, psychosocial, and environmental conditions required to sustain it. The existing literature indicates that maternal food insecurity is closely associated with breastfeeding duration and sustainability; mothers experiencing food insecurity are less likely to continue breastfeeding exclusively [10, 36–40]. However, in the context of Gaza, this situation is not just an economic deficiency; it represents a systemic breakdown on the ground of the obligations regarding “safe environments and nutritional support” for breastfeeding women, as committed to by the IYCF-E (2017) and the Sphere Standards [11]. The widespread food insecurity is weakening not only the physical resilience of mothers but also their ability to breastfeed [8]. As emphasized in the IYCF-E guidelines, it is a scientific fact that breast milk is biologically sufficient for undernourished women; however, the system’s failure to provide psychosocial support and food security to the mother triggers “perceived milk insufficiency” [10, 36, 41]. The unsafe breast milk substitutes or inappropriate complementary foods resorted to as a result of this perception should not be defined as a “maternal preference” or “lack of knowledge” but rather as a structural protection gap arising from humanitarian aid mechanisms’ failure to meet breastfeeding protection standards [8, 20, 42].

Despite severe physical deprivation and psychological distress, mothers reported that they continued to use breastfeeding as their primary care strategy to soothe their babies, promote emotional regulation, and foster bonding. Non-nutritive breastfeeding, a prominent finding in this study, is a normal, developmentally expected, and indispensable component of breastfeeding, as defined in the literature and WHO guidelines. Mothers’ accounts demonstrate how the regulatory and bonding functions of breastfeeding assume a central role in an environment where other sources of security are absent. This practice is not merely a crisis-specific or “compensatory” behavior; rather, it is a holistic care practice inherent to breastfeeding under all conditions [8, 13]. In this context, the act of breastfeeding by mothers in Gaza should be interpreted not merely as a transfer of nutrients, but as a “secure attachment” intervention aligned with Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) standards.

The findings reveal that safe infant feeding environments in Gaza have been severely disrupted. The breakdown food systems, lack of breastfeeding support, and absence of safe, age-appropriate foods have left families without viable options to meet infants’ basic nutritional needs. The fact that mothers are unable to access professional support confirms the absence of “expert breastfeeding counseling” and “mother-baby friendly safe spaces”, which are required to be provided to breastfeeding women under IYCF-E (2017) Standard 5. Mothers’ narratives show that the collapse of humanitarian systems and widespread food insec urity sometimes necessitate the use of unsafe or age-inappropriate foods. The fact that mothers are forced to prepare baby formula using contaminated water constitutes a clear violation of the obligations regarding “daily safe water per person” and “hygienic preparation conditions for infants” as committed to under the Sphere Standards (WASH). The failure of infant feeding systems indicates that the IYCF-E operational guidance is not being implemented in the field and that humanitarian actors are unable to fulfill their “duty of standard care.” This situation increases the risks associated with the early replacement of breast milk with inadequate alternatives (e.g., infection, malnutrition, mortality) [8, 9]; it confirms that these risky practices are not individual failures but rather structural and systemic failures in ensuring minimum humanitarian standards [10, 11]. Breastfeeding women described profound psychological distress associated with the war, including fear, anxiety, depressive symptoms, exhaustion, and hopelessness. Some mothers emphasized the inadequacy of words, using silence itself as an expression of the depth of psychological devastation. Ongoing violence and destruction in Gaza have created conditions of chronic trauma that significantly impair maternal mental health. Existing evidence consistently shows that conflict-related psychological distress directly and indirectly affects breastfeeding by disrupting maternal well-being, emotional regulation, and caregiving capacity [42–45, 47]. War-related stress, depression, and anxiety have been associated with delayed initiation of breastfeeding, early cessation, and increased reliance on mixed feeding [46, 48–50]. These findings underscore the need to integrate MHPSS with IYCF-E interventions, as emphasized in WHO, UNICEF, and IFE Core Group guidance. Protecting breastfeeding in the context of Gaza is not just a matter of food safety but a holistic protection strategy that supports the mother’s emotional well-being, creates a “safe spaces” in a violent environment, and aims to reduce the intergenerational transmission of trauma.

The findings reveal how war makes women’s bodies and motherhood vulnerable, while also undermining the fundamental foundations of the mother-infant relationship. Forced displacement, repeated relocations, hunger, and illness severely compromised mothers’ physical resilience and breastfeeding capacity, while mothers’ accounts of infant weakness, weight loss, diarrhea, and infections indicate profound threats to infant health. Barriers to food, clean water, and safe living conditions in temporary shelters emerged as critical contextual factors disrupting breastfeeding continuity [42–44, 51]. These findings clearly reflect conditions where humanitarian standards for infant and young child feeding in emergencies are not being implemented. The collapse of these systems in Gaza has resulted in breastfeeding being sustained almost entirely through the efforts of mothers, in the absence of structural protection. Furthermore, the findings highlight the need for integrated MHPSS and IYCF-E interventions. The widespread psychological distress reported by mothers indicates that breastfeeding in a war environment cannot be protected by nutritional interventions alone but rather requires trauma-informed, system-level support that addresses both maternal mental health and infant care.

The experiences of mothers in Gaza demonstrate that a combination of inadequate shelter, hygiene, and loss of privacy, coupled with malnutrition, significantly complicates breastfeeding. Ongoing conflict and forced displacement have confined women to crowded and unhygienic living conditions, negatively impacting both maternal health and the continuity of breastfeeding. Living in tents and the collapse of infrastructure constitute a violation of the “minimum hygienic environment” requirement for infant feeding as stipulated by the Sphere Standards [11]. Women’s loss of privacy in crowded areas confirms that “Mother-Baby Friendly Spaces” mandated for breastfeeding women in the OG-IFE [10] Standard 5. and IYCF-E protocols have not been provided. In line with the literature [20, 52–55], the fact that physical exhaustion and psychological stress make breastfeeding difficult is not an individual vulnerability in the context of Gaza; rather, it is a consequence of humanitarian actors failing to prioritize breastfeeding as a “protection intervention.” This situation transforms breastfeeding from a natural act into a fragile care practice surrounded by structural barriers [56].

Our findings reveal that, in the context of a devastating war, mothers’ efforts to breastfeed represent a necessary response to structural deprivation rather than an act of individual heroism. However, this necessity does not imply that mothers are passive victims. In line with the concept of “restlessly eager to breastfeed” defined by Palmquist et al., [20] in the Palestinian context, mothers in Gaza continue to breastfeed in an environment where all support mechanisms have collapsed as both a survival strategy and an active act of resistance and care aimed at protecting their infants. This situation represents not only individual resilience but also a structural struggle waged under the failure of humanitarian systems [45, 51, 57]. Thus, the determination to continue breastfeeding is a way for mothers to defend their children’s right to life under a siege where no other safe option remains.

In war conditions, mothers’ coping mechanisms have emerged not only as physical survival efforts but also as forms of fragile resilience based on emotional, spiritual, and social resilience. Mothers have stated that they find inner strength through the desire to keep their children alive, faith, and prayer; these practices help them generate hope and meaning in an environment of intense trauma and uncertainty. Similarly, the literature shows that mothers turn to spirituality in conditions of high stress and life-threatening circumstances, and that this is associated with psychological resilience [25, 58–60]. However, while community and spiritual resources are important in mothers’ coping processes, they serve as a limited and intermittent buffer under conditions of widespread trauma and resource scarcity. Studies with displaced Palestinian women reveal that social support networks and spiritual practices support daily survival but are insufficient in promoting lasting well-being [61–63]. These findings indicate that individual resilience may not be sufficient to protect breastfeeding and maternal-infant health in the face of systemic deprivations. Therefore, our findings support the integration of breastfeeding support with maternal mental health services (MHPSS–IYCF integration) in humanitarian interventions, as emphasized by WHO, UNICEF, and the IFE Core Group.

This study demonstrates that the devastating effects of war on the health of women and children are not limited to individual suffering, but stem from structural neglect and the systematic collapse of support mechanisms. In Gaza, the severe shortage of food, clean water, infant formula, and hygiene supplies constitutes fundamental humanitarian failures that directly undermine efforts to protect and support breastfeeding. Since October 7, 2023, Gaza has reportedly entered an unprecedented famine meeting the criteria for famine, with a large portion of the population becoming dependent on humanitarian food aid [8, 39]. Chronic food insecurity has been linked to serious maternal and child health problems such as malnutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, anemia, and weakened immune systems [64]. The siege conditions have severely restricted humanitarian aid operations, delayed the delivery of aid, and rendered health and nutrition interventions inadequate [47]. Ongoing military attacks and large-scale destruction of infrastructure have further weakened the already fragile health system, with shortages of facilities and medical supplies seriously endangering the health of mothers and infants [65, 66]. Under these conditions, thousands of children are reported to be at risk of starvation, making unrestricted access to humanitarian aid and the implementation of established emergency nutrition and breastfeeding standards an urgent necessity [39, 67].

The lack of psychological support is one of the less visible but extremely important effects of war. Even before the current conflict, Gaza suffered from a severe shortage of mental health professionals, a situation that has worsened due to the destruction of health infrastructure and forced displacement [68–70]. Damage to hospitals and clinics severely limited access to the few remaining services, forcing many mothers to bear the burden of trauma without professional care. At the same time, the disruption of health services that normally support breastfeeding has removed an essential layer of protection, intensifying mothers’ isolation and emotional distress and further destabilizing breastfeeding practices [50, 71]. These findings also emphasize the critical need for integrated MHPSS and IYCF-E services, as highlighted in humanitarian aid standards.

In conclusion, our results from Gaza clearly show that breastfeeding protection and support cannot be viewed as a secondary intervention in humanitarian aid operations. In accordance with IYCF-E (2017) and the Sphere Standards [11], supporting breastfeeding is not an “optional” activity during crises; it is a non-negotiable humanitarian obligation aimed at protecting the health of mothers and infants. Therefore, humanitarian aid budgets and operational plans must define breastfeeding counseling and the establishment of safe spaces as a “life saving” essential service on the same priority level as shelter and water supply.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the 2023 Gaza war created an extremely vulnerable context for breastfeeding through chronic hunger, forced displacement, the collapse of living conditions, and widespread psychological trauma. The findings reveal that breastfeeding in these conditions has transformed into a multidimensional care practice encompassing nutrition, survival, emotional regulation, physical closeness, and the maintenance of the mother–infant bond.

Mothers’ narratives show that breastfeeding has become a central arena where infant life, maternal well-being, and human dignity are negotiated under conditions of systematic deprivation. Non-nutritive breastfeeding has emerged as a normal and significant component of infant care, particularly in contexts where security, comfort, and support resources are severely compromised. Mothers’ resorting to age-inappropriate alternative foods is not a choice; it is a consequence of structural constraints, food insecurity, and the absence of breastfeeding support systems. These findings demonstrate that breastfeeding in war environments is a deeply relational and ethical dimension of care shaped by humanitarian conditions. The resilience of mothers is evident; however, this resilience cannot replace structural protection and professional support. In fact, positive outcomes in infant feeding are not merely a matter of individual determination; they depend on the rigorous implementation of international humanitarian aid standards such as IYCF-E and Sphere in the field.

In the context of conflict and humanitarian emergencies, these findings demonstrate that the protection and promotion of breastfeeding should not be limited to nutritional interventions alone; comprehensive approaches encompassing mother-infant bonding, emotional regulation, and psychosocial well-being are crucial. In line with OG-IFE and Sphere standards, integrating breastfeeding services with MHPSS services, establishing mother-baby friendly spaces, and empowering healthcare workers in trauma-sensitive breastfeeding counseling should be key priorities. In this context, the following three operational steps are critical to the success of IYCF-E programs in Gaza:

Deploying specialized personnel to the field who can provide not only food assistance but also relactation and trauma-sensitive breastfeeding support.The urgent creation of dedicated physical spaces (mother-baby friendly spaces) within shelters that provide privacy, safety, and psychosocial support.Preventing donations and distributions of inappropriate breastmilk substitutes that violate international codes and put breastfeeding at risk by subjecting them to strict oversight.
