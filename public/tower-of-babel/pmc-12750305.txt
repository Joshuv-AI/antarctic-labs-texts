Editorial: 2nd collection of special issue in innovations, advances, and challenges in precision radiation oncology physics
Authors: Fada Guan, Sabrina Campelo, Zhuoran Jiang, Lawrence Bronk
Published: 2025

This editorial introduces the 2nd Collection of Special Issue in Innovations, Advances, and Challenges in Precision Radiation Oncology Physics. Following the success of our first collection published in 2023, 1 which featured eight manuscripts covering topics from Monte Carlo simulations to clinical outcomes in various radiotherapy modalities, this second collection continues to highlight significant developments in radiation oncology physics. The manuscripts in this collection, published between March and November 2024, span diverse areas including stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), brachytherapy dosimetry, quality assurance methodologies, emerging imaging techniques, ultra‐high dose rate proton therapy, and preclinical radiotherapy research.

Nakamura et al. presented a retrospective analysis examining the importance of accurate CT interpretation by expert chest radiologists in determining SBRT indications for clinically diagnosed early‐stage non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). 2 The study compared outcomes between 48 pathologically diagnosed and 47 clinically diagnosed patients receiving SBRT. While median overall survival showed no significant difference between groups (6.0 years vs. 5.4 years, P = 0.57), the investigators found that within the clinically diagnosed cohort, patients assessed as having low probability of malignancy by expert radiologists demonstrated notably lower lung cancer mortality (25%) compared to those with high probability assessments (47.4%). These findings underscore the critical role of expert CT interpretation in optimizing patient selection for SBRT when pathological diagnosis is not available.

Poorbaygi et al. investigated dose distributions around eye plaques containing 125I seeds for choroidal melanoma treatment using Monte Carlo N‐Particle eXtended (MCNPX) simulation and Plaque Simulator (PS) treatment planning software. 3 The study validated MCNPX calculations against published literature, finding maximum relative differences of approximately 9–10% for Collaborative Ocular Melanoma Study (COMS) plaques. Notably, the dose distributions calculated by MCNPX were consistently lower than those from PS, with relative differences ranging from 27.7% to 35.4%. The authors attribute these discrepancies to differences in the 125I source spectra and seed design implementations between the two computational methods, highlighting the importance of understanding dosimetric variations in eye plaque brachytherapy planning.

Pandu et al. analyzed the effect of gantry angle variations on portal imaging accuracy and subsequent dosimetric impact for head and neck cancer patients receiving intensity‐modulated radiation therapy (IMRT). 4 In this prospective study of 30 patients, portal images were acquired at gantry angles of 0°, 0.5°, 1°, 1.5°, and 2.0° relative to the reference digitally reconstructed radiograph. The investigators found that average isocenter shifts in the lateral direction progressively increased from 0.7 mm to 2.5 mm as gantry angle deviation increased, while longitudinal direction shifts remained within ±0.1 mm. The dosimetric analysis demonstrated that gamma pass percentages decreased as isocenter position deviated from the planned location, with shifts of 1.0 mm producing significant treatment errors. These results emphasize the importance of ensuring gantry angle consistency during electronic portal imaging device acquisition.

Raj et al. investigated calibration methodologies for EBT‐XD radiochromic film dosimetry, 5 specifically examining three approaches for selecting background pixel intensity values: Film‐by‐Film (FBF), where each film serves as its own control; Batch‐by‐Film (BBF), using a single film as control for all calibration films; and Generic (GEN), employing a fixed value of 65,535 as the background for all films. Calibration curves were established for red, green, blue, and combined RGB channels using the NIH Rodbard curve‐fitting model. The study revealed that the GEN method exhibited dose prediction differences of up to 6% below 2 Gy and showed statistically significant differences in optical density changes compared to FBF and BBF methods (P<0.0001). These findings provide practical guidance for clinical physicists implementing EBT‐XD film dosimetry, particularly for high‐dose radiotherapy applications.

Jiang et al. presented a deep learning‐based method for enhancing electroacoustic tomography (EAT) to enable real‐time monitoring of nanosecond pulsed electric field (nsPEF) electroporation therapy. 6 The investigators developed a modified U‐Net architecture trained on 56 experimental datasets to correct distortions inherent in linear array‐based EAT systems. A custom rotating platform enabled acquisition of paired full‐view and single‐view signals for supervised learning validation. The enhanced images achieved excellent quantitative metrics including low root mean square error (0.018), high peak signal‐to‐noise ratio (35.15), and high structural similarity index (0.942) compared to ground truth. Importantly, the enhancement processing time of only 0.016 seconds per 256×256 image demonstrates feasibility for real‐time clinical applications. This work represents the first demonstration of high‐quality EAT from a single linear array in an experimental setting.

Guan et al. described the development of a robotic motion platform to mimick spot‐scanning radiation fields for proton FLASH preclinical studies on a synchrotron‐based horizontal beamline without steering magnets. 7 Operating at 87.2 MeV, the platform utilizes G‐code motion patterns with 9‐mm spacing for 96‐well plate irradiation using a 7‐mm circular collimator and 10.6‐mm spacing for mouse irradiation using a 1‐cm2 square collimator. Validation studies demonstrated excellent agreement between film‐measured dose profiles and Monte Carlo calculations, achieving one‐dimensional gamma pass rates exceeding 95% using 2%/1mm criteria. The system achieved FLASH‐relevant ultra‐high dose rates of 113.7 Gy/s for cell and organoid irradiation and 191.3 Gy/s for mouse studies. This innovative approach provides a practical solution for institutions seeking to conduct proton FLASH research on existing synchrotron‐based beam delivery systems.

Wang et al. presented a methodology for evaluating radiation‐induced liver injury (RILI) using longitudinal computed tomography in image‐guided precision murine radiotherapy. 8 Eighteen mice were divided into control, 15 Gy, and 30 Gy whole‐liver irradiation groups, with radiation delivered using volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) following CT simulation and cone‐beam CT guidance on a medical linear accelerator. Weekly CT imaging enabled quantitative assessment including body weight monitoring, mean Hounsfield unit (HU) reduction measurements, and HU histogram analysis. This work establishes a framework for conducting well‐controlled, dose‐escalated preclinical studies of hepatic radiation toxicity that closely mimics clinical radiotherapy workflows, potentially advancing our understanding of radiation‐induced liver disease pathophysiology.

The manuscripts in this 2nd Collection demonstrate the continued evolution and diversification of radiation oncology physics research. From Monte Carlo‐based dosimetry investigations to deep learning applications, from quality assurance methodology refinements to novel preclinical platforms, these works collectively address fundamental challenges while exploring innovative approaches to improve precision in radiation therapy delivery. The emergence of ultra‐high dose rate FLASH radiotherapy research, the integration of artificial intelligence for real‐time imaging guidance, and the development of sophisticated preclinical models all point toward an exciting future for the field. We anticipate that subsequent collections will continue to capture the dynamic landscape of innovations, advances, and challenges in precision radiation oncology physics.
