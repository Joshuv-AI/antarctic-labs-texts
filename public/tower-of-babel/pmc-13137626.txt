Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome in drug-induced syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion: a case report
Authors: Clemens A. Aden, Axel Meissner, Katharina S. Kuchenbecker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a condition with multiple possible causes, leading to hypotonic overhydration and reduced serum osmolality. Complications include seizures, headaches and cerebral edema resulting from rapid and severe hyponatremia. Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is a largely reversible, neurological condition typically associated with arterial hypertension, autoimmune diseases or infections. Complications include visual disturbances, seizures and increased intracranial pressure. While concurrent occurrence of both entities has been documented, such cases are often confounded by malignancies or antineoplastic treatment. Co-occurrence in an otherwise relatively healthy individual is rare and offers new insights into pathophysiology.Case presentationWe report on a case of concurrent SIADH and PRES in a 54-year-old Ukrainian woman with minimal confounding factors (pre-existing medical conditions or recent medical interventions). The patient developed acute-onset SIADH following high-dose antihypertensive drug intake and subsequently presented with PRES, characterized by typical clinical findings and characteristic imaging results. She required intensive care treatment, fully recovered within days and showed no long-term deficits.ConclusionsThe concurrent presentation of SIADH and PRES has rarely been described in the absence of clinical confounders. This report supports a direct, pathophysiological link between vasopressin/SIADH activity and the pathogenesis of PRES. Furthermore, it reinforces the role of cerebral hyperperfusion as a risk factor.

Background

The syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion has multiple etiologies including paraneoplastic, infectious, metabolic or drug-induced causes. Common to these is an increased secretion of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) or ADH-like peptides, leading to the reabsorption of free water via aquaporin-2 channels. This results in hemodilution, with decreased serum osmolality and urine concentration. In cases of hypotonic overhydration, dilutional hyponatremia can trigger reactive natriuresis due to inhibition of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) and increased release of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) [1–3]. Therapeutic strategies focus on treating the underlying cause, along with fluid restriction, administration of vasopressin-2 receptor antagonists and intravenous correction of hyponatremia [1–5]. In comparison, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome typically presents with subcortical and cortical edema, predominantly in the posterior regions. Causes include hypertensive crises, autoimmune disorders, immunosuppression or (cytotoxic) medication [6]. Pathophysiologically, cerebral hypo- and hyperperfusion, loss of cerebral autoregulation and endothelial dysfunction are thought to contribute. There is uncertainty as to whether the edema represent ischemic–toxic or hypertensive–vasogenic processes [7–10]. Although edema most commonly appear temporo-occipitally and symmetrically, frontal or atypical localizations or even normal brain imaging findings have been reported [11–15]. Treatment involves elimination of the underlying trigger. In addition, antihypertensive and possibly seizure-suppressive therapy should be initiated if indicated [16, 17].

Case presentation

A 54-year-old, Ukrainian woman presented with hypertensive crisis, blurred vision and headaches. She had a known history of Hashimoto's thyroiditis and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). On physical examination, she appeared distressed and suffering, with floating eyes, blurred vision and headaches. Blood pressure was at 160/90 mmHg, initial serum sodium levels at 121 mmol/L. 5 h after admission, the patient developed generalized tonic–clonic seizures, followed by a comatose state. Repeat serum sodium had dropped to 113 mmol/L, despite 500–1000 mL of balanced isotonic intravenous fluids given since admission, presumably due to onset of SIADH as well as high captopril and hydrochlorothiazide dosages. A postictal cranial computed tomography CT (cCT) scan diffuse cerebral edema, including effacement of the cortical sulci and narrowing of the parapontine cisterns (Figs. 1 and 2). The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit with acute, symptomatic hyponatremia and suspected PRES. In the following hours, the patient developed increased muscle tone with pronounced bruxism. Serum creatine kinase was reported at 27,700 U/L. Despite persistent fevers of 39.1–39.5 °C, lumbar puncture and blood cultures ruled out infectious causes. An insufficient response to paracetamol raised suspicion of a central origin of the fever. Endocrinological testing excluded adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism. The patient demonstrated clinical euvolemia, normal renal function, serum osmolality of 249 mosmol/kg, urine osmolality of 881 mosmol/kg and urine sodium of 34 mmol/L—findings consistent with drug-induced SIADH (Table 1). Based on clinical presentation, imaging, risk factors and the exclusion of alternative diagnoses, neurologic consultancy confirmed the diagnosis of PRES in the context of metabolic encephalopathy (Table 2). Particularly suggestive were the visual disturbances, arterial hypertension and imaging evidence of brain stem edema. Following admission, continuous intravenous therapy with 3% hypertonic sodium chlorid solution at an initial rate of 20 mL/h and an initial short-term infusion of 150 mL over a 15 min period were initiated. Despite hourly monitoring, serum sodium levels did not initially rise, prompting a gradual increase in perfusor flow rates up to 40 mL/h. After 9–10 h, an increase in serum sodium levels was observed reaching 118 mmol/L. The slow increase can be attributed to a pronounced SIADH activity and a prolonged effect of captopril and hydrochlorothiazide counteracting infusion therapy leading to a “steady state” level with no further drop but also no increase in sodium levels. Sodium levels continued to rise steadily thereafter, allowing for reduction of flow rates and eventual termination. However, despite correction in sodium levels, the patient remained comatose. Verbal communication only became possible when levels of approximately 130 mmol/L were reached (approx. 36 h after admission). Normal sodium levels (> 135 mmol/L) were achieved 72 h day post-admission, at which point the patient was transferred to the general ward. Antihypertensive therapy was adjusted. Neurological follow-up showed no abnormalities and subsequent cranial MRI detected no residual signs of PRES (Table 3). Three months after the patient was discharged from the hospital, she presented for a follow-up examination. Clinically, she remained stable and stated to have not experienced any long-term side effects. Discussion

This case illustrates the fulminant course of acute drug-induced SIADH and PRES. The likelihood that captopril and hydrochlorothiazide triggered SIADH is high, given the patient’s minimal comorbidities and absence of recently introduced, potentially confounding medications. The diagnosis of PRES was based on typical clinical findings, risk factors, characteristic imaging and the exclusion of alternative diagnoses [12]. Although posterior edema is both, a typical and eponymous feature in PRES, atypical or even normal imaging has been reported [11–15]. The follow-up MRI showed no evidence of PRES, indicating rapid resolution consistent with the favorable prognosis in timely treatment [6, 20].

Interestingly, associations of SIADH and PRES have been well-described. However, they are typically observed in the context of antineoplastic therapy or the presence of malignancies. Therefore, this report is novel in its findings, as it describes PRES being closely linked to SIADH in an otherwise relatively healthy individual. A shared underlying pathophysiological mechanism is often proposed in neoplastic or treatment-related cases [21–23]. Wong et al. [22] suggest that overactivation of the arginine vasopressin (AVP) axis—and consequent V1A and V2 receptor stimulation in cerebral vessels and renal collecting duct principal cells—represents a common pathway. In malignancy, this mechanism may result from chronic, ectopic vasopressin production, as seen in small cell lung cancer [24]. However, ectopic vasopressin production is unlikely in this case. Instead, overactivation of AVP axis seems more plausible due to an acute and excessive release of vasopressin in consequence of drug-induced SIADH. Notably, desmopressin-induced PRES has been reported, supporting a link between vasopressin excess and PRES [25].

Furthermore, this case supports the hypothesis of cerebral hyperperfusion serving as a risk factor of PRES development. Although some patients present with blood pressure values below the thresholds for impaired cerebral autoregulation (mean arterial pressure ~ 60–150 mmHg), the presence of pre-existing arterial hypertension in this patient suggests that sustained cerebral hypoperfusion was unlikely [11, 12, 17, 20, 26–28]. The main limitation of this report lies in its single-patient sample (n = 1) and the limited literature available regarding the co-occurrence of both entities in otherwise healthy individuals. While it is likely that simultaneous occurrence reflects the underlying mechanism mentioned above, it may be due to an unidentified mechanism or have occurred by chance.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare constellation of findings: simultaneous occurrence of SIADH and PRES in an otherwise healthy individual. Although both conditions have been reported together, most documented cases involve patients with neoplastic diseases or ongoing antineoplastic therapy. A common pathophysiological mechanism may involve overactivation of the arginine vasopressin axis, resulting in effects on both the kidneys and the brain. In oncology patients, this mechanism is linked to chronic ectopic vasopressin production. In contrast, the mechanism in this case involves an acute-onset, drug-induced SIADH. A sudden and excessive surge in vasopressin likely impaired cerebral autoregulation and resulted in typical findings of PRES.
