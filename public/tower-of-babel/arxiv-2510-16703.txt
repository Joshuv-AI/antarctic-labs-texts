On the Granularity of Causal Effect Identifiability
Yizuo Chen, Adnan Darwiche

Abstract
The classical notion of causal effect identifiability is defined in terms of treatment and outcome variables. In this note, we consider the identifiability of state-based causal effects: how an intervention on a particular state of treatment variables affects a particular state of outcome variables. We demonstrate that state-based causal effects may be identifiable even when variable-based causal effects may not. Moreover, we show that this separation occurs only when additional knowledge — such as context-specific independencies and conditional functional dependencies — is available. We further examine knowledge that constrains the states of variables, and show that such knowledge does not improve identifiability on its own but can improve both variable-based and state-based identifiability when combined with other knowledge such as context-specific independencies. Our findings highlight situations where causal effects of interest may be estimable from observational data and this identifiability may be missed by existing variable-based frameworks.

Introduction

The causal effect is a distribution on some outcome variables that materializes as a result of an intervention on a set of treatment variables;
e.g., the probability that a patient will recover if the doctor instructs them to take a drug. In general, a causal effect cannot be estimated based on observational data only, therefore requiring experimental studies
[
Pearl and Mackenzie 2018
]
.
However,
given a causal graph that encodes the causal relations among variables, a causal effect may in some cases be uniquely determined (i.e., estimable) using observational data only. In such cases, the causal effect is said to be
identifiable
and a formula may be derived to estimate it based on observational data.

Both complete and incomplete methods have been proposed in the past for testing the identifiability of causal effects based on causal graphs; see, e.g.,
[
Pearl 2009
;
Hernán and Robins 2020
]
for some overviews. Some early methods for testing identifiability include the back-door criterion
[
Pearl 1993
;
Pearl 2009
]
and front-door criterion
[
Pearl 1995
;
Pearl 2009
]
. These methods are sound yet not complete and hence may fail to identify causal effects that are indeed identifiable. Complete methods for identifying causal effects include the do-calculus
[
Pearl 2009
]
, the identification algorithm in
[
Huang and Valtorta 2006
]
, and the ID algorithm in
[
Tian and Pearl 2003
;
Shpitser and Pearl 2006
;
Shpitser and Pearl 2008
]
.
All these methods, however, are
variable-based

in that they test
whether the causal effect is identifiable for
every
possible intervention on the treatment variables and
every
possible state of the outcome variables (for the same set of treatment and outcome variables).
As we argue in this note, variable-based identifiability is appropriate when the
available information consists only of a causal graph and some observational
data but can be too coarse-grained when additional information
is available. In particular, given additional information, it is conceivable that the causal effect of
some
treatment on
some
outcome may be identifiable, while the causal effect of some other treatment on some other outcome may not be identifiable. This highlights the need for more refined (state-based) approaches to identifiability as we discuss later.

This distinction becomes particularly relevant in light of
recent works that study the identifiability problem
while assuming additional knowledge beyond causal graphs and observational data. This includes knowledge of functional dependencies
[
Chen and Darwiche 2024
]
, context-specific independencies (CSIs)
[
Tikka et al. 2019
;
Mokhtarian et al. 2022
;
Chen and Darwiche 2025
]
, and known parameters
[
Chen and Darwiche 2025
]
.
Such additional knowledge can be formulated as constraints, leading to the notion of
constrained-identifiability

[
Chen and Darwiche 2024
;
Chen and Darwiche 2025
]
. The main use of such additional knowledge has been to show that it may render some (variable-based) causal effects identifiable which would otherwise be unidentifiable without the additional knowledge. In this note, we show that such knowledge has an additional value: it renders the distinction between variable-based causal effects and state-based causal effects meaningful and worthy of study
as it highlights some new directions for improving identifiability.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}
be the treatment variables and
{\mathbf{Y}}
be the outcome variables. We start by formulating state-based identifiability which is concerned with whether the causal effect of a particular treatment (
{\mathbf{X}}\!\!=\!\!{\mathbf{x}}
) on a particular outcome (
{\mathbf{Y}}\!\!=\!\!{\mathbf{y}}
) can be uniquely determined based on available information. We then illustrate the distinction between state-based and variable-based identifiability given knowledge in the form of context-specific independencies (CSIs) which are effectively constraints on model parameters that cannot be captured by causal graphs. We further formulate another type of knowledge called conditional functional dependencies (CFDs), which was introduced in the database literature but has not been investigated in the domain of causal inference, and show that certain causal effects become state-based identifiability but remain variable-based unidentifiable under this knowledge.
We conclude by considering state constraints as an additional
type of knowledge, showing that they are not useful for identifiability on their own but they do widen the gap between state-based and variable-based identifiability when coupled with other knowledge such as CSIs and CFDs.

Two Levels of Identifiability

Let

also yield the same

For example, the probability that a major accident will occur if a particular speed limit is enforced,

\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{\mathsf{speed}=65}(\mathsf{accident}\!=\!\textit{major}).

Definition 2

(State-Based Identifiability)
.

The causal effect of
{\mathbf{x}}
on
{\mathbf{y}},

\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{y}}),
is identifiable wrt
\langle G,{\mathbf{V}}\rangle
if
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{y}})=\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{y}})
for any pair of models that induce
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits^{1},\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits^{2}

\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits^{1}({\mathbf{V}})=\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits^{2}({\mathbf{V}}).

State-based identifiability refers to a particular treatment
{\mathbf{x}}
and a particular outcome

which are states of the treatment and outcome variables.
The distinction between variable-based and state-based identifiability is not interesting if the only information available consists of a causal graph
G
and an observational distribution
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits({\mathbf{V}}).
In fact, the two notions are equivalent in this case, which we formulate and prove as Proposition
1
in Appendix

The distinction between the two notions materializes though if we have additional knowledge beyond

and
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits({\mathbf{V}}).
We elaborate on this next.

The Impact of CSI Constraints on Identifiability

We next show how CSI constraints

We consider CSIs in the form of (
Y\!\perp\!\!\!\perp{\mathbf{X}}|{\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{S}}

{\mathbf{X}},\mathbf{C},{\mathbf{S}}
partition the parents of
Y

interpreted as follows:
Y

For example, the CSI (
\mathsf{Light}\!\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathsf{People}\ |\ \mathsf{Outage}\texttt{=}\textit{yes},\mathsf{Time}

considered when deciding identifiability.

The Impact of Conditional Functional Dependencies on Identifiability

Functional dependency
is another type of knowledge that can be leveraged to improve the identifiability of variable-based causal effects
[
Chen and Darwiche 2024
]
. We next consider a more refined type of functional dependencies, called
conditional functional dependencies
, which allow us to draw a distinction between variable-based and state-based identifiability.

By definition, a variable
X
exhibits functional dependency if it is functionally determined by its parents
{\mathbf{P}}
in the causal graph, i.e.,
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(x|{\mathbf{p}})\in\{0,1\}
for all instantiations
(x,{\mathbf{p}})
. In this case, variable
X
is called
functional
. Note that we only assume the
existence
of functional dependencies without the need to know the
specific
functions. To illustrate,
\mathsf{Legal\ Driving\ Age}
functionally depends on
\mathsf{Country}
; Students’
\mathsf{Letter\ Grade}
from a course functionally depends on their
\mathsf{Weighted\ Average}
; and
\mathsf{Passenger\ Capacity}
functionally depends on
\mathsf{Aircraft\ Model}
.

Conditional functional dependencies (CFDs)
are a refined version of functional dependencies that have been studied in the database literature
[
Bohannon et al. 2007
;
Fan et al. 2008
]
but have not been examined in the context of causal inference as far as we know. Specifically, the knowledge partitions parents
{\mathbf{P}}
into
{\mathbf{P}}_{1},{\mathbf{P}}_{2}
then declares
X
as functionally determined by
{\mathbf{P}}_{1}
when
{\mathbf{P}}_{2}
is set to a particular instantiation
{\mathbf{p}}_{2}
— i.e.,
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(x|{\mathbf{p}}_{1},{\mathbf{p}}_{2})\in\{0,1\}
for all
x,{\mathbf{p}}_{1}.

Following
[
Bohannon et al. 2007
]
,
we denote this CFD as
[{\mathbf{P}}_{1},{\mathbf{P}}_{2}\texttt{=}{\mathbf{p}}_{2}]\rightarrow X.
Similar to functional dependencies, we do not need to know the specific function between
X
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{1}
conditioned on
{\mathbf{p}}_{2}.
For a concrete example of CFDs, consider the following graduate school admission scenario at institution
I
. Applicants who earned their undergraduate degree from the institution
I
are guaranteed admission if they achieved high undergraduate grades. Others will be evaluated based on their grades and the standing of their undergraduate school. In other words, admission decisions functionally depend on grades only when applicants earned their undergraduate degree from institution
I,
which is denoted as
[\mathsf{Grades},\mathsf{Institution}\texttt{=}I]\rightarrow\mathsf{Admission}.
We next consider CFDs as additional constraints on the identifiability problem, which enables more (state-based) causal effects to become identifiable.

The Impact of State Constraints on Identifiability

We showed earlier that CSIs and CFDs can improve state-based identifiability.
We next show that these constraints can further improve state-based identifiability when coupled with
state constraints,
which specify the possible states of some variables.
Note that CSI and CFD constraints do not in principle require the specification of variable states (i.e., set of all states) even for variables that participate as contexts in such constraints. For example, the CSI (
\mathsf{Light}\!\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathsf{People}\ |\ \mathsf{Outage}=\textit{yes}

\mathsf{Outage}
but does not require specifying the other states of
\mathsf{Outage}

in

6

Conclusion

We considered how the identifiability of causal effects can be improved by focusing on a particular treatment and outcome and by including additional knowledge beyond causal graphs. This led us to
suggest a more granular notion of identifiability which is state-based. It also led to some findings on the extent
to which various types of knowledge and their combinations can draw distinctions between state-based and variable-based identifiability.

Functional Elimination Under Conditional Functional Dependencies

We first review an operation called

We first define Causal Bayesian Networks (CBNs). A CBN is a pair

{\mathbf{P}}

.

The theorem allows us to remove variables from a causal graph while preserving state-based identifiability. Since

f(X|U_{1},U_{3})=\begin{cases}(0.05,0.95,0)&\text{if $U_{1}=0$}\\
(0.05,0,0.95)&\text{if $U_{1}=1$}\\
\end{cases}

f(Y|U_{2},U_{3},A,B)=\begin{cases}0.99&\text{if $A=Y$}\\
0.01&\text{if $A\neq Y$}\\
\end{cases}

We assign different CPTs for
A
in
{\cal F}_{1}
and
{\cal F}_{2}
:

f_{1}(A|U_{1},U_{2},X)=\begin{cases}0.5&\text{if $X=0$}\\
0.99&\text{if $X\neq 0$ and $(X-1)\oplus U_{1}=A$}\\
0.01&\text{if $X\neq 0$ and $(X-1)\oplus U_{1}\neq A$}\\
\end{cases}

f_{2}(A|U_{1},U_{2},X)=\begin{cases}0.5&\text{if $X=0$}\\
0.99&\text{if $X\neq 0$ and $A=0$}\\
0.01&\text{if $X\neq 0$ and $A=1$}\\
\end{cases}

The parameterizations
{\cal F}_{1},{\cal F}_{2}
induce a same
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits({\mathbf{V}})
but different values for
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X=1}(Y=1).

∎

Proposition 8
.

There exist a causal graph
G
, observed variables
{\mathbf{V}}
, CFD constraints
{\cal C}_{1}
, and state constraints
{\cal C}_{2}
for which
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{\mathbf{X}}({\mathbf{Y}})
is unidentifiable wrt
\langle G,{\mathbf{V}},{\cal C}_{1}\rangle
but is identifiable wrt
\langle G,{\mathbf{V}},{\cal C}_{1}\cup{\cal C}_{2}\rangle.

Proof.

Consider the causal graph in Section
5
for the hospital example. We first show that the causal effect
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y)
is identifiable, assuming that
B
is binary with states
\{0,1\}
and CFDs
[A,B=0]\rightarrow D
,
[C,B=1]\rightarrow E.
Since
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y)=\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y,B=0)+\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y,B=1)
, it suffices to identify
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y,B=0)
and
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y,B=1)
separately.

We start with the identification of
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y,B=0)
:

\begin{split}&\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y,B=0)\\
&=\sum_{A,C}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C)\sum_{F}\sum_{E}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(E|B=0,C)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|X,F,E)\sum_{D}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(D|A,B=0)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|D,E)\\
&=\sum_{A,C}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C)\sum_{F}\sum_{E}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(E|B=0,C)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|X,F,E)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|A,E,B=0)\;\;\;\text{(Corollary\penalty\ \ref{cor:f-cpts})}\\
&=\sum_{A,C}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C)\sum_{F}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|A,B=0,C)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|A,B=0,C,F,X)\;\;\;\text{(c-component ID)}\end{split}

We next identify
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y,B=1)
:

\begin{split}&\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y,B=1)\\
&=\sum_{A,C}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C)\sum_{F}\sum_{D}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(D|A,B=1)\sum_{E}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(E|B=1,C)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|D,E)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|X,F,E)\\
&=\sum_{A,C}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C)\sum_{F}\sum_{D}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(D|A,B=1)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|B=1,C,D)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|B=1,C,X,F)\;\;\;\text{(Corollary\penalty\ \ref{cor:f-cpts})}\\
&=\sum_{A,C}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C)\sum_{F}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|B=1,C,X,F)\sum_{X}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|A,B=1,C,X)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(X|A,B=1)\\
\end{split}

The last step is by the c-component identification method introduced in
[
Tian and Pearl 2003
]
.

To show that
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y)
is unidentifiable without constraining the states of
B,
we present two parameterizations
{\cal F}_{1},{\cal F}_{2}
that induce a same
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits({\mathbf{V}})
yet different values for
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{X}(Y).

In both parameterizations,
B
has three states
\{0,1,2\},

D
and
E
have four states
\{0,1,2,3\}
, and all other variables have two states
\{0,1\}.
We now define the parameterizations below.

f_{1}(A)=f_{2}(A)=0.5,\;\;\;f_{1}(C)=f_{2}(C)=0.5\;\;\;f_{1}(B)=f_{2}(B)=\frac{1}{3}

f_{1}(D|A,B)=f_{2}(D|A,B)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $B=0$ and $D=A$}\\
0&\text{if $B=0$ and $D\neq A$}\\
0.5&\text{if $B=1$ and $D<2$}\\
0&\text{if $B=1$ and $D\geq 2$}\\
0&\text{if $B=2$ and $D<2$}\\
0.5&\text{if $B=2$ and $D\geq 2$}\\
\end{cases}

f_{1}(E|B,C)=f_{2}(E|B,C)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $B=1$ and $E=C$}\\
0&\text{if $B=1$ and $E\neq C$}\\
0.5&\text{if $B=0$ and $E<2$}\\
0&\text{if $B=0$ and $E\geq 2$}\\
0&\text{if $B=2$ and $E<2$}\\
0.5&\text{if $B=2$ and $E\geq 2$}\\
\end{cases}

f_{1}(X|D)=f_{2}(X|D)=\begin{cases}0.5&\text{if $D<2$}\\
1&\text{if $D\geq 2$ and $X=D-2$}\\
0&\text{if $D\geq 2$ and $X\neq D-2$}\\
\end{cases}

f_{1}(F|D,E)=f_{2}(F|D,E)=\begin{cases}0.5&\text{if $D<2$ or $E<2$}\\
1&\text{if $D\geq 2$ and $E\geq 2$ and $F=(D-2)\oplus(E-2)$}\\
0&\text{if $D\geq 2$ and $E\geq 2$ and $F\neq(D-2)\oplus(E-2)$}\\
\end{cases}

f_{1}(Y|X,E,F)=\begin{cases}0.5&\text{if $E<2$}\\
0.99&\text{if $E\geq 2$ and $Y=(E-2)\oplus F\oplus X$}\\
0.01&\text{if $E\geq 2$ and $Y\neq(E-2)\oplus F\oplus X$}\\
\end{cases}

f_{2}(Y|X,E,F)=\begin{cases}0.5&\text{if $E<2$}\\
0.99&\text{if $E\geq 2$ and $Y=0$}\\
0.01&\text{if $E\geq 2$ and $Y=1$}\\
\end{cases}\qed

Proposition 9
.

There exist a causal graph
G
, observed variables
{\mathbf{V}}
, CFD constraints
{\cal C}_{1}
, state constraints
{\cal C}_{2}
, and particular states
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}
where
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{y}})
is unidentifiable wrt
\langle G,{\mathbf{V}},{\cal C}_{1}\rangle
but is identifiable wrt
\langle G,

{\mathbf{V}},

{\cal C}_{1}\cup{\cal C}_{2}\rangle
; and
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{\mathbf{X}}({\mathbf{Y}})
is unidentifiable wrt
\langle G,{\mathbf{V}},{\cal C}_{1}\cup{\cal C}_{2}\rangle
.

Proof.

We consider the last example in Section
5
. First, we adapt the proof of Proposition
8
to show that the causal effect
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{c,x}(y)
is unidentifiable for all instantiations
(c,x,y)
. This is because we only introduce a new hidden variable to the causal graph without modifying the CFDs. Moreover, intervening on
C
is equivalent to conditioning on
C
since it is a root node.

We next show that the causal effect
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{C=0,X=0}(Y=0)
becomes identifiable if the states of
B
are
\{0,1\}
, assuming the CFDs
[A,B=0]\rightarrow D,

[C,B=1]\rightarrow E
, and
[A,C=0]\rightarrow G.
It suffices to identify
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{C=0,X=0}(Y=0,B=0)
and
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{C=0,X=0}(Y=0,B=1)
separately.

We start by identifying
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{C=0,X=0}(Y=0,B=0)
:

\begin{split}&\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{C=0,X=0}(Y=0,B=0)\\
&=\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C=0)\sum_{A}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\sum_{F}\sum_{E}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(E|B=0,C=0)[\sum_{G}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(G|A,C=0)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|F,E,X=0,G)]\\
&\ \ \ [\sum_{D}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(D|A,B=0)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|D,E)]\\
&=\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C=0)\sum_{A}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\sum_{F}\sum_{E}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(E|B=0,C=0)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|A,C=0,F,E,X=0)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|A,B=0,E)\\
&\hskip 100.00015pt\text{(Corollary\penalty\ \ref{cor:f-cpts})}\\
&=\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C=0)\sum_{A}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\sum_{F}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|A,B=0,C=0)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|A,B=0,C,F,X)\;\;\text{(c-component ID)}\\
\end{split}

We now identify
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{C=0,X=0}(Y=0,B=1)
:

\begin{split}&\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{C=0,X=0}(Y=0,B=1)\\
&=\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C=0)\sum_{A}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\sum_{F}\sum_{D}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(D|A,B=1)\sum_{G}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(G|A,C=0)\\
&\;\;\;\sum_{E}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(E|B=1,C=0)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|D,E)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|X=0,E,F,G)\\
&=\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C=0)\sum_{A}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\sum_{F}\sum_{D}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(D|A,B=1)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|D,B=1,C=0)\sum_{G}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(G|A,C=0)\\
&\;\;\;\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|X=0,B=1,C=0,F,G)\hskip 50.00008pt\text{(Corollary\penalty\ \ref{cor:f-cpts})}\\
&=\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C=0)\sum_{A}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\sum_{F}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|A,B=1,C=0,F,X=0)\\
&\;\;\;\sum_{D}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(D|A,B=1)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|D,B=1,C=0)\hskip 50.00008pt\text{(Corollary\penalty\ \ref{cor:f-cpts})}\\
&=\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(C=0)\sum_{A}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(A)\sum_{F}\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y|A,B=1,C=0,F,X=0)\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(F|A,B=1,C=0)\\
&\hskip 100.00015pt\text{(c-component ID)}\\
\end{split}

We next construct two parameterizations
{\cal F}_{1},{\cal F}_{2}
that induce a same
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits({\mathbf{V}})
yet different
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{C=1,X=0}(Y=0)
. We assume variable
G
has four states
\{0,1,2,3\}
and all other variables have two states
\{0,1\}.
We now construct the parameterizations
{\cal F}_{1},{\cal F}_{2}
as follows.

f_{1}(A)=f_{2}(A)=0.5,\;\;\;f_{1}(C)=f_{2}(C)=0.5\;\;\;f_{1}(B)=f_{2}(B)=0.5

f_{1}(D|A,B)=f_{2}(D|A,B)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $B=0$ and $D=A$}\\
0&\text{if $B=0$ and $D\neq A$}\\
0.5&\text{if $B=1$}\\
\end{cases}

f_{1}(E|B,C)=f_{2}(E|B,C)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $B=1$ and $E=C$}\\
0&\text{if $B=1$ and $E\neq C$}\\
0.5&\text{if $B=0$}\\
\end{cases}

f_{1}(G|A,C)=f_{2}(G|A,C)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $C=0$ and $G=A$}\\
0&\text{if $C=0$ and $G\neq A$}\\
0&\text{if $C=1$ and $G<2$}\\
0.5&\text{if $C=1$ and $G\geq 2$}\\
\end{cases}

f_{1}(X|D,G)=f_{2}(X|D,G)=\begin{cases}0.5&\text{if $G<2$}\\
1&\text{if $G\geq 2$ and $X=G-2$}\\
0&\text{if $G\geq 2$ and $X\neq G-2$}\\
\end{cases}

f_{1}(F|D,E)=f_{2}(F|D,E)=0.5

f_{1}(Y|X,E,F,G)=\begin{cases}0.5&\text{if $G<2$}\\
0.99&\text{if $G\geq 2$ and $Y=(G-2)\oplus X$}\\
0.01&\text{if $G\geq 2$ and $Y\neq(G-2)\oplus X$}\\
\end{cases}

f_{2}(Y|X,E,F,G)=\begin{cases}0.5&\text{if $G<2$}\\
0.99&\text{if $G\geq 2$ and $Y=0$}\\
0.01&\text{if $G\geq 2$ and $Y=1$}\\
\end{cases}\qed
