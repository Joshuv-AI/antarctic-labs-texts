CCDC120 phase separation contributes to desmosomal integrity and cardiac function
Authors: Hui Meng, Wei Zhao, Yangyingzi Xi, Donghui Zhang, Xipan Han, Rixu Cong, Tianning Wang, Yingchun Hu, Yonglu Tian, Linwen Gong, Xiaopeng Zou, Junlin Teng, Jianguo Chen, Ning Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesmosomes are cell-cell adhesive junctions that provide structural integrity and mechanical resistance to tissues. Disruptions in desmosome organization lead to severe cardiac and dermatological disorders. Emerging evidence reveals that desmosomes exhibit dynamic behaviors during remodeling. However, the mechanisms that initiate and regulate these dynamics under physiological conditions, and their correlation with desmosome-related disorders remain elusive. Here, we uncover the role of coiled-coil domain-containing 120 (CCDC120) in linking desmosomal organization to cardiac function via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). CCDC120 localizes to desmosomes and is required for desmosomal integrity. CCDC120 exhibits LLPS properties and co-condenses with the desmosomal component plakophilin-2 (PKP2) during desmosomal assembly, forming dynamic condensates crucial for preserving desmosomal structure and junction stability. Moreover, CCDC120 condensates are modulated by PKCα phosphorylation. Loss of CCDC120 or altered CCDC120 phase separation properties leads to intercalated disc structure impairment and cardiac dysfunction in mice. Our findings propose a model wherein CCDC120 phase separation orchestrates desmosomal integrity, thereby establishing connections between desmosomal dynamics and the molecular etiology of cardiac dysfunction.Subject terms: Desmosomes, Cardiac hypertrophy, Mechanisms of disease

Introduction

Desmosomes are button-like intercellular junctions that anchor stress-bearing intermediate filaments (IFs) to the plasma membrane and are critical for tissues that experience high mechanical stress, such as cardiac muscle and the epidermis1,2. Desmosomes are precisely organized complexes assembled by a series of desmosomal components. Desmogleins (DSGs) and desmocollins (DSCs) are desmosomal cadherins that mediate cell-cell contact through their extracellular domains, while cytoplasmic plaque proteins link the intracellular regions of cadherins to IF networks. Plakophilins (PKPs) and plakoglobin (PG) are the main components of the outer dense plaque (ODP), whereas the inner dense plaque (IDP) marks the region where IF bundles attach to the C-terminus of desmoplakin (DP)3,4. Assembly of desmosomal components is a dynamic process, particularly during development, morphogenesis, and wound healing5–7. During desmosome assembly, both desmosomal cadherins traffic to the plasma membrane through microtubule-based transport8. DP forms cytoplasmic particles that partially colocalize with PKP2 and exhibit fusion and transport events toward newly generated desmosomes5. Several signaling molecules, including protein kinase C α (PKCα) and Src kinase, modulate desmosomal dynamics6,9–11. Disruption of desmosomal structure impairs tissue integrity and physiological functions12,13. Emerging evidence suggests that impaired desmosomal organization is strongly associated with arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy and skin disorders14. These findings raise a fundamental question of how desmosomal dynamics are regulated under physiological and pathological conditions.

Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) is an emerging mechanism for macromolecular organization that concentrates biomolecules into compartments and promotes biochemical reactions15. The spherical condensates formed by LLPS are characterized by fusion events and rapid molecular exchange with the surrounding solutions16. Mechanistically, phase separation is typically driven by proteins with intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), where multivalent interactions among amino acid residues commonly occur17–21. Recent studies have shown that some scaffold proteins recruit client proteins into their condensates formed via phase separation to facilitate structural organization22–25. Condensation of the zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) scaffolding protein is associated with the partitioning of other tight junction (TJ) proteins and local actin assembly, a process that is implicated in maintaining TJ integrity and epithelial barrier function25,26. Furthermore, microtubule-induced LLPS of the actin nucleator Cordon-bleu (Cobl) is required for epithelial morphogenesis and the formation of paracellular barriers27. However, whether phase separation-related mechanisms contribute to desmosomal dynamics and cardiac function remains unclear.

We previously reported that coiled-coil domain-containing 120 (CCDC120) is a centrosomal subdistal appendage (SDA) component. Loss of CCDC120 disrupts SDA assembly and microtubule anchoring in HeLa cells during interphase28. Here, we identified an unexpected role of CCDC120 in crosslinking desmosomal structure and maintaining cardiac function through phase separation. CCDC120 is enriched at the desmosomal plaque and is crucial for desmosomal integrity. CCDC120 undergoes LLPS and co-condenses with PKP2 to preserve functional desmosomal organization. Furthermore, CCDC120 condensation is regulated by PKCα phosphorylation. Loss of CCDC120 or altered CCDC120 phase separation properties impair intercalated disc structure and leads to cardiac dysfunction.

Results

CCDC120 is a desmosomal component that maintains desmosomal integrity

Coiled-coil domain-containing 120 (CCDC120) was previously identified as a subdistal appendage (SDA) component required for SDA assembly and microtubule anchoring at centrosomes in HeLa cells28. Unexpectedly, robust localization of GFP-tagged CCDC120 was observed at cell-cell junctions in Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells (Fig. 1a), a widely used model for studying cell junctions29. To characterize the localization of endogenous CCDC120, we performed immunofluorescence assays and found that it localized at cell junctions in wild-type (WT) MDCK cells. As expected, this junctional localization was absent in CCDC120 knockout (KO) cells (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c). Then we co-labeled the CCDC120 with the desmosomal adhesion markers PKP2 and DP I/II or the adhesion junction marker β-catenin in MDCK cells. To minimize junction heterogeneity in colocalization analysis, MDCK cells were maintained and imaged at a consistent density ( ~ 80% confluency). CCDC120 exhibited greater colocalization with PKP2 and DP I/II at cell borders when compared to that with β-catenin (Fig. 1b–d and Supplementary Fig. 1d). In addition, CCDC120 colocalized with PKP2 and DP at sites where Keratin 19 filaments in adjacent cells anchor (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1e), suggesting that CCDC120 may localize to desmosomes. Moreover, immunoprecipitation assay results showed that CCDC120 interacts with the desmosomal component PKP2 (Fig. 1f). Three-dimensional structured illumination microscopy (3D-SIM) was performed to identify the detailed localization of CCDC120 at desmosomes. Two DP I/II antibodies respectively targeting its Rod domain (Fig. 1g, h) and N-terminus (Fig. 1i, j) were utilized to co-label with CCDC120. The results revealed that CCDC120 localizes closer to the N-terminal, rather than middle part of DP I/II (Fig. 1g–j). Furthermore, CCDC120 also displayed better colocalization with ODP protein PKP2 (Fig. 1k, l). Together, these findings suggest that CCDC120 localizes near the ODP of desmosomes in MDCK cells (Fig. 1m).

To determine the role of CCDC120 in desmosomes, we first examined the localization of different desmosomal proteins in both WT and CCDC120 KO MDCK cells. Depleting CCDC120 significantly disrupted the continuous distribution of DP I/II and PKP2 (Fig. 2a–d) as well as that of desmosomal cadherin protein DSG1/2 and DSC2 (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d), as quantified by image entropy analysis. Furthermore, the width of DP I/II localization at cell junctions was increased in CCDC120 KO cells (Supplementary Fig. 2e), and an abnormal double-band phenotype was observed at cell junctions from a subset of CCDC120-depleted cells (3/60 cell junctions in KO#1 cells, 2/60 cell junctions in KO#2 cells, Supplementary Fig. 2f–h). By contrast, β-catenin distribution at cell junctions remained unchanged after CCDC120 depletion (Supplementary Fig. 2i, j), confirming that CCDC120 specifically participates in desmosomal component assembly. A previous study showed that most desmosomal components are insoluble in lysis buffer containing nonionic detergents, such as Triton X-100, because of their ability to associate with the desmosomal plaque30. Analysis of solubility properties of desmosomal components revealed that depleting CCDC120 shifted a portion of DP I/II and PKP2 from the insoluble to the soluble pool (Fig. 2e), without altering their total expression levels (Fig. 2f), further suggesting the impaired desmosomal organization after CCDC120 depletion. In addition, the loss of PKP2, but not DP I/II, disrupted the proper localization of CCDC120 at desmosomes (Fig. 2g, h and Supplementary Fig. 2k), revealing a strong functional correlation between CCDC120 and PKP2.

We then investigated whether CCDC120 loss alters desmosomal adhesion function in MDCK cells. Given that desmosomal structure and IFs contribute to the mechanical properties of cells31,32, we used atomic force microscopy (AFM) to measure the elasticity of the apical surface of the MDCK monolayers, from which the Young’s modulus was obtained (Fig. 2i). First, we measured WT cells and DSP or PKP2 KO cells to verify this system. AFM results showed that both DSP KO cells (Young’s modulus: 2.247 ± 0.06 kPa) and PKP2 KO cells (Young’s modulus: 2.486 ± 0.06 kPa) exhibited a significant decline in cellular elasticity than WT cells (Young’s modulus: 4.385 ± 0.09 kPa, Supplementary Fig. 2l). Then we measured WT and CCDC120 KO cells, and also found a remarkable decrease in the value of Young’s modulus (WT: 4.186 ± 0.06 kPa; KO: 2.415 ± 0.027 kPa, Fig. 2j, k). Notably, this reduction in stiffness was not accompanied by significant alterations in IFs organization (Supplementary Fig. 2m, n), suggesting that decreased cell elasticity after CCDC120 depletion is mainly caused by adhesion impairment.

To further examine the role of CCDC120 in intercellular adhesion, we performed dispase-based dissociation assays8,9. Dispase II treatment was applied to lift the intact cell monolayer off the culture plate, then monolayers were transferred into tubes to undergo mechanical perturbation through inversion. Adhesion strength was determined by measuring the number of monolayer fragments after inversion. CCDC120 KO cells exhibited a greater number of fragments than WT cells, suggesting reduced intercellular adhesion following CCDC120 deletion (Fig. 2l, m). In contrast, focal adhesion size remained unchanged in CCDC120 KO cells (Supplementary Fig. 2o, p), suggesting that CCDC120 depletion-induced adhesion defect is specific to cell-cell junctions rather than cell-matrix interactions. Together, our results suggest that CCDC120 is enriched at desmosomes and is essential for maintaining desmosomal structural integrity and adhesive function.

CCDC120 dynamics during desmosome assembly

To characterize CCDC120 dynamics in cells, we generated MDCK cell lines stably expressing GFP-tagged CCDC120. At low expression levels, GFP-CCDC120 localized predominantly to desmosomal adhesions (Fig. 3a, upper panel). Surprisingly, at high expression levels, round-shaped particles with varied sizes appeared in the cytoplasm of MDCK cells (Fig. 3a, lower panel). To validate the dynamics of GFP-CCDC120 signal within cytoplasmic particles and cell junctions, the fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assays were performed. Compared to the dynamics of GFP-CCDC120-formed particles (55.69 ± 3.56% recovery at 180 s post-bleaching, Fig. 3b, c), GFP-CCDC120 signal at cell borders displayed restricted mobility (41.95 ± 3.35% recovery at 180 s post-bleaching, Fig. 3d, e). In addition, we transiently expressed GFP-CCDC120 in HEK293T cells, a cell line with high transfection efficiency but weakened desmosomal adhesion25. GFP-CCDC120 primarily formed one large particle in the cytoplasm (Supplementary Fig. 3a) and exhibited exchange dynamics, as confirmed by FRAP assays (38.52 ± 2.70% recovery at 144 s post-bleaching, Supplementary Fig. 3b, c).

DP-GFP expression leads to the formation of cytoplasmic particles that colocalizes with exogenous PKP2, and a series of DP-GFP dynamic events near the cell borders are triggered by cell-cell contact5. Building on this, we next examined the dynamic behaviors of CCDC120 during desmosome assembly in MDCK cells. Live-cell imaging revealed that at the beginning of spontaneous cell-cell contact, GFP-CCDC120 puncta were observed along the forming border (Supplementary Fig. 3d, 0-30 min). After that, some GFP-CCDC120 particles near the contacting borders appeared to fuse together, then moved towards the borders, and finally coalesced with assembled desmosomes at cell contact sites (Supplementary Fig. 3d, 204-258 min). Consistently, similar fusion events of GFP-CCDC120-formed particles were observed at cell borders in HeLa cells (Supplementary Fig. 3e). Given that the similar dynamic behavior to DP during cell-cell contact, we next investigated whether GFP-CCDC120 co-assembles with mApple-DP into pre-existing cell-cell contacts during calcium switch-induced desmosome assembly in MDCK cells. Similar with the dynamics observed under spontaneous cell-cell contact (Supplementary Fig. 3d), some particles containing both GFP-CCDC120 and mApple-DP fused into assembled desmosomes at cell contact sites (Fig. 3f), Supporting the involvement of CCDC120 particles in the dynamic process of desmosome formation.

CCDC120 undergoes LLPS in vitro

Given that cytoplasmic CCDC120 particles exhibit exchange dynamics, and perform fusion capacity during desmosomal assembly, we hypothesized that CCDC120 may form biomolecular condensates through phase separation. Since IDRs commonly play a key role in mediating phase separation18,19,21,33, we first analyzed the CCDC120 protein sequence and identified two continuous IDRs on its C-terminus (IDR1: 210-430 aa and IDR2: 431-629 aa; Fig. 3g). To test whether CCDC120 generates droplets via its IDRs alone or in conjunction with other cellular components, we purified His-GFP-tagged CCDC120 IDR1 or IDR1 + 2 (Supplementary Fig. 3f), and performed a series of in vitro experiments. Both purified CCDC120 IDR1 or IDR1 + 2 formed spherical droplets, and droplet size changed in a protein concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 3g). A FRAP assay revealed that IDR1-formed droplets could exchange molecules with the surrounding solutions (63.95 ± 2.37% recovery at 120 s post-bleaching, Fig. 3i, j). Furthermore, dynamic fusion events of the droplets formed by IDR1 were observed (Fig. 3k), suggesting that these droplets possess liquid-like properties in vitro.

Enriched D/E residues in IDR1 are essential for CCDC120 phase separation

To decipher the molecular grammar encoded in the IDRs of CCDC120 that govern its phase separation, we first investigated whether both IDRs are required for condensates formation in cells. The results showed that depleting IDR1, but not IDR2, severely reduced GFP-CCDC120 condensates in MDCK cells (Supplementary Fig. 3h, i), suggesting that CCDC120 condensation in cells is primarily mediated by IDR1. By generating and expressing a series of truncation mutations within CCDC120 IDR1, we found that the 210-250 aa. region of CCDC120 is critical for CCDC120 condensation in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c). We then analyzed this protein sequence, and identified several evolutionary conserved acidic amino acid residues clustered within or near the 210–250 aa. region of CCDC120 (Fig. 4a). Electrostatic interactions are a type of multivalent interactions that mediate LLPS34,35, and clusters of negatively charged residues contribute to charge-driven phase separation formation36,37. To test this, we next neutralized these negative-charge residues by mutating ten aspartic acid and glutamate residues to alanine in IDR1 (DE10A), and examined its droplet formation in vitro under various protein and salt concentrations (Fig. 4b). The IDR1-DE10A mutant displayed attenuated LLPS properties compared with IDR1-WT, forming only small droplets at low salt concentrations (Fig. 4b–d). Finally, we assessed condensate formation and dynamics of CCDC120 DE10A mutant in cells. The CCDC120 DE10A mutant exhibited fewer cytoplasmic condensates in both MDCK and HEK293T cells when compared to those in CCDC120 FL group (Supplementary Fig. 4d–g). In FRAP assays, the condensates formed by the CCDC120-DE10A mutant exhibited a lower fluorescence recovery rate compared to those formed by the CCDC120-FL (21.05 ± 1.07% vs. 39.57 ± 3.16% recovery at 153 s post-bleaching, Supplementary Fig. 4h, i). Taken together, we conclude that the enriched aspartic acid and glutamate residues in IDR1 are essential for CCDC120 to undergo LLPS.

CCDC120 phase separation is required for desmosomal organization and function

To investigate the functional relevance of CCDC120 phase separation, GFP-tagged CCDC120 FL or the DE10A mutant were stably expressed in CCDC120 KO MDCK cells to evaluate their ability to support desmosomal assembly and function. First, we examined endogenous PKP2 and DP I/II localization at cell junctions. In CCDC120 FL-rescued cells, a continuous distribution of PKP2 and DP I/II was detected at cell-cell borders (Fig. 4e–h), which is similar with the localization patterns shown in WT cells. However, rescuing by the CCDC120 DE10A mutant resulted in a disorganized, bead-like localization of PKP2 and DP I/II around junction sites (Fig. 4e–h). To further investigate the role of CCDC120 phase separation in desmosome maintenance, we then performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to analyze changes in desmosomal structure across each group. Notably, shortened desmosomes were observed in CCDC120 DE10A mutant-rescued cells, in contrast to the WT and CCDC120 FL-rescued groups (Fig. 4i, j), suggesting that CCDC120 with defective LLPS properties affects desmosome assembly. Finally, we examined the correlation between CCDC120 LLPS properties and desmosomal function. Dispase assays showed that CCDC120-FL, but not the DE10A mutant, restored adhesion loss in CCDC120 KO cells (Fig. 4k, l), supporting the role of CCDC120 phase separation in desmosomal adhesion. In addition, CCDC120 KO cells rescued by CCDC120 FL, but not the DE10A mutant, exhibited a partial increase in Young’s modulus, as measured by AFM (FL: 3.288 ± 0.045; DE10A: 2.600 ± 0.036; Fig. 4m, n). Together, these findings suggest that phase separation property of CCDC120 is essential for desmosomal integrity, thus ensuring intercellular adhesion and cell stiffness.

CCDC120 interacts and co-condenses with PKP2

Phase separation drives cellular compartmentalization38. Condensates of ZO proteins selectively partition TJ proteins during TJ assembly25,26. We asked whether CCDC120 LLPS similarly facilitates the selective enrichment of desmosomal components during desmosomal assembly. Due to impaired phase separation of CCDC120 affects the continuous localization of PKP2 at desmosomes, we first examined whether CCDC120 co-condenses with PKP2. Overexpressed PKP2 associated with CCDC120 condensates in the cytoplasm of HeLa cells (Fig. 5a). Furthermore, fusion events of condensates containing both CCDC120 and PKP2 were observed at cell borders after spontaneous cell-cell contact (Fig. 5b). We then investigated the role of CCDC120 in PKP2 particle formation in MDCK cells. A robust decline in the percentage of cells with PKP2 particles was detected after CCDC120 depletion (Fig. 5c, d). This phenotype was rescued by expressing CCDC120 FL, but not the DE10A mutant (Fig. 5c, d), further demonstrating that CCDC120 LLPS properties facilitate PKP2 particle formation. Interestingly, some cytoplasmic particles containing both PKP2 and CCDC120 were located along keratin filaments (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b), which is similar with the distribution of DP-GFP particles5. We then examined the relationship between CCDC120 and DP in cytoplasmic particle formation. Although some DP-GFP signal was associated with CCDC120 condensates in HeLa cells (Supplementary Fig. 5c), there was no significant change in DP particle formation between WT and CCDC120 KO cells (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e), suggesting that DP may generate cytoplasmic particles in a CCDC120-independent manner.

To further investigate the relationship between CCDC120 and PKP2, we mapped the domains responsible for their interaction. Immunoprecipitation assays revealed that the N-terminal region (1-330 aa.) of PKP2, which mediates interactions with several desmosomal proteins such as DP and PG39, also binds to the 522-629 aa. region of CCDC120 (Fig. 5e–g). In addition, neither cytoplasmic colocalization nor the interaction with PKP2 was impaired in the CCDC120 DE10A mutant (Supplementary Fig. 5f–h). Given that the altered residues in the CCDC120 DE10A mutant are located within IDR1 (Fig. 4a), these results indicate that the LLPS capacity of CCDC120 may not directly influence its interaction with PKP2.

At last, we characterized whether CCDC120 and PKP2 co-condense in vitro. An IDR domain (80–240 aa.) within the PKP2 N-terminus was predicted (Supplementary Fig. 5i), and purified PKP2-IDR could also form droplets in a concentration-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 5j, k). Moreover, Co-incubation with CCDC120 IDR1 + 2 not only increased the size of PKP2-IDR droplets compared to those formed by PKP2-IDR alone (Fig. 5h, i), but also decreased the condensation threshold of PKP2 IDR (Supplementary Fig. 5l). Together, these results suggest that CCDC120 co-condenses with PKP2, and promotes PKP2 phase separation.

PKCα-mediated phosphorylation promotes CCDC120 condensation

PKC signaling regulates various types of intercellular junction assembly40,41. In particular, PKCα governs desmosomal dynamics by forming a DP-PKP2-PKCα complex11. To test whether PKCα regulates the phase separation properties of CCDC120, we performed an in vitro condensation assay by incubating purified CCDC120-IDR1 with PKCα. In the presence of ATP, liquid droplets formed by CCDC120-IDR1 were larger than those in the non-ATP groups (Fig. 6a, b), suggesting that PKCα activation increases CCDC120 droplet formation in vitro. Since phosphorylation may regulate LLPS via electrostatic modulation25,42,43, we asked whether PKCα modulates CCDC120 phase separation by phosphorylating IDR1. Using mass spectrometry analysis, we first screened phosphorylation sites of CCDC120 that could be targeted specifically by PKCα. Ten evolutionary conserved serine and threonine residues within IDR1 were identified (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 6a). We then constructed a non-phosphorylatable CCDC120 mutant by mutating all serine and threonine residues to alanine (ST10A), and performed an in vitro kinase assay. A strong thiophosphate-ester signal was detected after incubating PKCα with WT but not ST10A mutant CCDC120-IDR1 proteins (Fig. 6d), further confirming the specificity of these phosphorylation sites. We next examined the role of PKCα in CCDC120 condensation formation in cells. Treatment with the PKC activator PMA significantly enhanced CCDC120-FL condensation in CCDC120 KO MDCK (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c) and WT HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e). However, no significant change of CCDC120-ST10A condensates was observed after PMA treatment (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c), further suggesting that PKCα promotes CCDC120 condensation through phosphorylating these residues. Finally, we investigated the role of CCDC120 phosphorylation in desmosome assembly by expressing the ST10A mutant in CCDC120 KO MDCK cells. Compared to WT or CCDC120 FL-rescued MDCK cells, the endogenous PKP2 and DP I/II signal exhibited a disorganized distribution around the junction sites in CCDC120 ST10A-rescued MDCK cells (Fig. 6e–h). Additionally, the CCDC120 ST10A mutant did not impair the interaction or cytoplasmic colocalization with PKP2 (Supplementary Fig. 6f–h). Furthermore, similar to the phenotypes observed in CCDC120 DE10A-rescued cells, TEM analysis also revealed shortened desmosomes in CCDC120 ST10A-rescued cells (Fig. 6i, j). Taken together, these results suggest that PKCα directly phosphorylates CCDC120-IDR1 and promotes its condensate formation, thus maintaining desmosomal assembly.

CCDC120 condensation maintains cardiac function and ICD structure

Recent studies show that mutations in genes encoding desmosomal proteins, such as DP and PKP2, lead to an inherited heart disease known as arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC)44,45. Since CCDC120 contributes to desmosome assembly and function (Figs. 1 and 2), we examined its role in cardiac function. We first generated Ccdc120 KO mice using CRISPR/Cas9. A 50-bp deletion in exon 4 of Ccdc120 was detected (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Immunoblotting further confirmed the loss of CCDC120 protein in KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Using echocardiography, we assessed left ventricular (LV) function in WT and Ccdc120 KO mice at 2 months of age (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 1). We detected a significant decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and fraction shortening (FS) in Ccdc120 KO mice (Fig. 7b). To explore whether CCDC120 maintains cardiac function through phase separation, we generated Ccdc120 DE10A mutant mice (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e). Importantly, Ccdc120 DE10A mutant mice phenocopied the cardiac dysfunction observed in Ccdc120 KO mice (Fig. 7a, b, Supplementary Table 1). Together, these results demonstrate that CCDC120 condensation is essential for maintaining cardiac function in mice.

Intercalated discs (ICDs) are specialized structures that connect adjacent cardiomyocytes. The area composita, the predominant junction type within ICDs, contains desmosomal and fascia adherens components46,47. To investigate the cause of cardiac dysfunction in Ccdc120 KO and DE10A mutant mice, we first examined the localization of desmosomal components in ICDs. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant decline in DP and PKP2 signal length on ICDs in Ccdc120 KO and DE10A mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 7c, d). This mislocalization pattern was similar to the disrupted DP and PKP2 localization observed in CCDC120 KO and DE10A-rescued MDCK cells (Figs. 2 and 4). TEM was performed to examine the ultra-structural changes in ICDs further. The length of the desmosomal structure was reduced in Ccdc120 KO and DE10A mice (Fig. 7e, f). In addition, the area composita exhibited a widened gap in Ccdc120 KO mice, which was even more pronounced in Ccdc120 DE10A mice (Fig. 7e, f).

Finally, we analyzed the solubility properties of desmosomal components in the heart tissues of different mice. The insoluble fraction of CCDC120 in Ccdc120 DE10A was lower (Fig. 7g, h) than that in WT heart tissue, which is consistent with its irregular desmosomal localization in MDCK cells (Fig. 4). Moreover, the insoluble DP I/II and PKP2 protein levels were also decreased in Ccdc120 KO and DE10A mice (Fig. 7g, h), further supporting the role of CCDC120 LLPS in the proper localization of desmosomal components within ICDs. These results suggest that CCDC120 phase separation property is important for maintaining cardiac function by stabilizing ICD structure.

Discussion

In this study, we uncovered the role of CCDC120 in desmosome assembly and function. CCDC120 exhibits LLPS properties, which are further enhanced by PKCα phosphorylation. Moreover, CCDC120 specifically interacts with PKP2 and co-condenses with it during desmosomal formation. Impaired CCDC120 condensation causes desmosomal protein mislocalization, weakened intercellular adhesion, and cell stiffness, ultimately resulting in cardiac dysfunction in mice.

Our results demonstrate that CCDC120 colocalizes and interacts with PKP2. Loss of CCDC120 impaired both DP and PKP2 assembly at desmosomes. However, only PKP2 depletion reciprocally alters the desmosomal localization of CCDC120, suggesting that CCDC120 and PKP2 may function upstream of DP in desmosomal assembly. Since IFs also contribute to the mechanical properties of cells, whether CCDC120 depletion induces the change in IF-desmosome anchoring that further contribute to the decline of cellular elasticity needs to be further investigated.

Although PKP2 alone is able to form condensates in vitro via its IDR, interacting with CCDC120 further promotes its phase separation properties. Since immunoprecipitation assays indicated that the 1-330 aa. region of PKP2, which includes the IDR (80-240 aa.), interacts with CCDC120, we propose that the binding between CCDC120 and PKP2 may enhance the multivalency of the PKP2-IDR. This increased multivalency could facilitate the formation of PKP2 condensates, potentially contributing to the organization and function of desmosomal complexes. During desmosomal assembly, DP-GFP displays multiple dynamic phases triggered by cell-cell contact5. Some DP-GFP particles are co-transported with PKP2 and subsequently integrate into newly formed junctions5,11, a pattern similar to GFP-CCDC120 condensates formed via LLPS (Fig. 3). In addition, some CCDC120/PKP2 double-positive particles localized along the keratin filaments, we speculate that this colocalization might be indirectly mediated by endogenous DP I/II. Since DP is predicted to exhibit LLPS properties (www.predict.phasep.pro), it would be interesting to determine whether cell contact-triggered DP-GFP dynamics are also dependent on phase separation. Some DP-GFP particles at perinuclear sites colocalize with engulfed desmosomal cadherins5,48. Whether GFP-CCDC120 condensates also partition with desmosomal cadherins or PG requires further investigation.

In our study, we found that PKCα directly phosphorylates multiple serine and threonine residues on CCDC120-IDR1, enhancing its LLPS properties (Fig. 6). Phosphorylation modulates phase separation in both positive and negative manners25,49,50. A recent study revealed that phosphorylation modification modulates LLPS by altering residue charges rather than by attaching phosphate groups at specific sites49. Since a negative charge block on IDR1 is essential for CCDC120 LLPS (Fig. 4), we speculate that PKCα-mediated phosphorylation further increases the negative charge on IDR1, thus enhancing CCDC120 phase separation via charge interactions. Furthermore, PKCα regulates desmosomal protein dynamics. PKCα-mediated phosphorylation of DP at Ser2849 weakens its binding with IFs and is involved in desmosomal remodeling during both desmosome formation5,11,51 and disassembly52,53. PKCα inhibition can also switch desmosomes from a calcium-dependent state to a more static conformation known as hyperadhesion52,54, thus reducing desmosomal protein turnover55. Future studies will focus on how PKCα modulates the dynamics of CCDC120 condensates in cells.

ARVD/C is a complex myocardial disorder that leads to heart failure and sudden cardiac death56. Mutations in four genes encoding desmosome proteins (DP, PKP2, JUP, and DSG2) have been identified in ARVC/D patients57–62. Transgenic mouse models carrying these mutations exhibit cardiac dysfunction and even embryonic lethality44,63. This highlights the importance of desmosome proteins in maintaining the mechanical resilience of contracting cardiomyocytes. In our study, we showed that loss of CCDC120 impairs the distribution of desmosomal components at ICDs and causes cardiac dysfunction in mice (Fig. 7), further supporting the concept that ARVD/C is a desmosomal disease64. However, whether CCDC120 abnormalities contribute to other ARVD/C phenotypes, such as arrhythmias and fibrosis64, as well as the correlation between CCDC120 mutations and ARVD/C, remains to be determined. Recent studies have highlighted the potential role of LLPS in cardiovascular diseases such as heart failure65,66, and cardiomyopathy67. Our findings demonstrate that CCDC120 phase separation is required for desmosomal assembly and cardiac function. Moreover, ZO-1, which is involved in ICD composition and atrioventricular node conduction at gap junctions68,69, also displays LLPS properties25,26. These findings suggest that LLPS properties and LLPS-related mutations in junctional proteins may provide new insights into the regulation of cardiovascular functions. Interestingly, neither Ccdc120 KO nor DE10A mutant mice display structural impairment in the skin, indicating the CCDC120 may maintain desmosomal integrity in a tissue-specific manner. Our future work will focus on the role of CCDC120 in other desmosomal-enriched tissues such as intestinal epithelium.

In summary, we show that CCDC120 LLPS is critical for regulating desmosomal integrity, which is necessary for maintaining cardiac function. These findings shed light on the mechanistic interplay between LLPS-mediated desmosomal dynamics and cardiac function and may contribute to the understanding and diagnosis of desmosome-related cardiovascular disorders.

Methods

Animal models

All animals were handled following the Principles for the Utilization and Care of Vertebrate Animals and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free environment with regulated temperature (20-26 °C), relative humidity (40-70%), and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Animal studies were approved by the IACUC of the Center for Experimental Animal Research (China) and Peking University Laboratory Animal Center (IACUC No. LSC-ChenJG-3). Ccdc120 knockout mice were generated using a CRISPR/Cas9 approach on a C57BL/6 J background by Shanghai BioModel Organisms Center, Inc. An sgRNA (5’-GTGAAGTCAGACCGTCTTCG-3’) was designed to target exon 4 of the Ccdc120 gene. To generate Ccdc120 DE10A mutant mice, two sgRNAs targeting intron 6 and intron 9 (5’-TCTGTTTGCTGCATGCATAC-3’ and 5’-ACTATCGATTAGCAGAC-3’), respectively, along with one vector containing donor template to perform homologous-dependent-repair (Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc.). Detailed information on genotyping, sequencing primers, and donor templates is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Plasmid construction

The full-length complementary DNAs (cDNAs) of Ccdc120 and Pkp2 were amplified from a mouse cDNA library by PCR. The DSP cDNA was a gift from Kathleen Green (Addgene plasmid #32227). The full-length and mutant cDNAs were subcloned into pEGFP-C2, pCMV7.1-3×Flag, pmApple-N1, or pET-28a. All primers were generated by Sangon Biotech. The cDNA sequences of CCDC120 full-length and truncation mutants were listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Cell culture, transfection, and treatment

MDCK-II, HeLa and HEK293T cells were purchased from ATCC and cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (CellMax) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. For cell transfection, Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) and jetPRIME (Polyplus-Sartorius) were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To activate PKCα kinase, MDCK-II cells were treated with PMA (15 nM, MCE) for 30 min.

Generation of knockout and stable expressing MDCK cell lines

All knockout cell lines were generated using a CRISPR/Cas9 approach. The sgRNAs targeting CCDC120, PKP2, and DSP were inserted into the LentiCRISPR-V2 vector. The vectors were then transfected into MDCK cells, and single-cell colonies were selected by puromycin treatment (3 μg/mL, Solarbio). The colonies were subjected to immunoblotting and genome sequencing to verify the knockout efficiency. Detailed information on the PCR primers used is provided in Supplementary Table 2. Full-length and mutant CCDC120 cDNAs were cloned into pEGFP-C2 vectors and transfected into wild-type (WT) or CCDC120 knockout (KO) MDCK cells for stable cell line selection. Single-cell colonies were selected by treatment with G418 (200 μg/mL, Invitrogen).

Immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation

Total protein from cultured cells was extracted using SDS loading buffer and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. For mouse tissue protein, Triton X-100 soluble and insoluble fractions were extracted as previously described30. Briefly, tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA) with protease inhibitor cocktail (Epizyme) and PMSF (1 mM, Solarbio). Triton X-100 soluble and insoluble fractions were separated by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The pellets were then solubilized for 30 min at room temperature in lysis buffer containing 8 M urea and 50 mM DTT. All protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Primary and secondary antibodies used for immunoblotting are listed in Supplementary Table 3. The gray values of the immunoblot bands were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH).

For immunoprecipitation, cultured cells or tissues were lysed on ice in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, and 10% glycerol) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Epizyme) for 30 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were incubated with the indicated antibodies for 2 h at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads (Selleck) for an additional hour. Finally, the immunoprecipitated samples were mixed with SDS loading buffer, heated at 100 °C for 10 min, and analyzed by immunoblotting.

Protein expression and purification

All recombinant proteins were expressed in E. coli (BL21-DE3, Tsingke) and induced with 0.5 mM IPTG (Solarbio) for 16–18 h at 16 °C. To purify His-tagged recombinant proteins, E. coli cells were resuspended in binding buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 30 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol and 0.5% Triton X-100) containing protease inhibitor cocktail and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). The cells were lysed by sonication on ice, and the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was incubated with Ni-NTA agarose beads (CWBIO) and eluted using elution buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 300 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol and 0.5% Triton X-100). Finally, purified proteins were concentrated in P buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol) using centrifugal filtration (Millipore), aliquoted and stored at -80 °C. In the droplet formation assays of CCDC120 with different concentrations of protein and salt, both CCDC120 IDR1 WT and DE10A proteins were eluted and stored with indicated buffers that containing 750 mM NaCl.

In vitro droplet assays

For most experiments, phase separation was induced by diluting purified proteins into phase separation buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol, and 5% PEG 4000) at the indicated concentration, followed by incubation for 5-20 min at 25 °C. Droplet formation was observed by adding 4 μL protein mixture to a glass-bottom cell culture dish (NEST). This was followed by imaging using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope equipped with a 100×/1.45 NA oil immersion objective lens. To identify the role of PKC in CCDC120 droplet formation, 260 μM His-GFP-CCDC120-IDR1 proteins were diluted in kinase buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 10 mM MgCl2, 500 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT and 0.01 mg/ml PKCα) with or without 200 μM ATP (Solarbio). After incubation at 30 °C for 1 h, the protein mixture was incubated with LLPS buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM KCl, and 5% PEG 4000), followed by imaging. For FRAP experiments, CCDC120 droplets were photobleached using a 488 nm laser at 100% laser power, followed by imaging at 5-s intervals for 2.5 min.

In the phase separation diagram of CCDC120, 10 images in each condition were analyzed from three independent experiments. Conditions were clarified as LLPS if spherical droplets with diameter exceeding 300 nm (corresponding to an approximate area of 0.07 μm2) were detected in ≥ 2 out of 3 repeats, while conditions with irregular protein aggregates or sub-300 nm particles across all repeats were clarified as No LLPS. The 300 nm threshold was determined based on the detection limit of imaging system, and the liquid-like properties of droplets above this size were verified by FRAP assay.

Droplet quantifications

All droplet confocal images in each experiment were captured in the same parameter. The droplet size was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH) as follows: Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale. The droplets were defined using the Auto threshold with the Otsu method in Threshold tool. Subsequently, the number and size of individual droplets was counted using the Analyze Particles tool, the droplets meet the following criteria were included in the quantification: Circularity between 0.50-1.00, and Size greater than 0.07 μm2. To validate the robustness of the selected threshold for droplet size, we adjusted the baseline Auto threshold (Otsu method) was adjusted by ±10% and droplet size was quantified as mentioned above. The results showed that the significance trend of inter-group differences remained consistent (Supplementary Table 5), confirming that the selected threshold is robust and the core conclusion is reliable.

To validate the reliability of the Otsu threshold segmentation algorithm for droplet quantification, synthetic droplet images were constructed matched to the statistical characteristics of real experimental data, and a quantitative validation of segmentation accuracy was performed as described below.

First, key statistical features were extracted from two experimental conditions of the droplet formation assay, with 5 real confocal images randomly selected for each condition. The density, size distribution of droplets, as well as background noise levels (baseline noise intensity and shot noise variance) were included. Subsequently, based on these extracted parameters, simulated droplets were generated with radii following a normal distribution at random positions. The idealized images were then convolved with a PSF derived from system-specific parameters, so as to simulate the optical diffraction effects under a 100 × oil immersion objective (NA = 1.45). Finally, to recapitulate the authentic imaging environment, Gaussian noise, and Poisson noise consistent with experimental datasets were introduced into the images; the noise intensity and variance were strictly calibrated to match the measured values of real confocal images (Supplementary Fig. 8a, c, left panel).

For the quantitative evaluation of segmentation performance, the binary masks generated by Otsu segmentation were compared against the simulated ground truth to calculate the Intersection over Union (IoU), a metric that enables precise quantification of the spatial overlap between segmentation outputs and ground truth, and the relative error on droplet size. The results revealed that in both conditions, IoU values yielded by Otsu segmentation exceeded 0.9, and the relative error values on droplet diameter was <5% (Supplementary Fig. 8b, d), indicating that Otsu algorithm exhibits high segmentation accuracy and reliability under current imaging settings. In addition, error maps were generated to illustrate the distribution of false negative (green) and false positive (magenta) regions recognized by Otsu segmentation, further validating the efficacy of the algorithm in resolving edge details of droplets (Supplementary Fig. 8a, c, right panel). All image simulation, error map generation and loU value analyses were implemented in Matlab (R2024b, MathWorks), the droplet size measurement was performed in ImageJ software (NIH) as described above.

Immunofluorescence and live-cell imaging

Immunofluorescence staining of cell samples was performed as previously described70. Briefly, MDCK cells were fixed and permeabilized in cold methanol for 10 min at 4 °C. HEK293T cells were fixed in PBS containing 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and 4% sucrose for 20 min at RT, then permeabilized in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking in PBS containing 4% BSA, samples were incubated with the indicated primary and secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table 3), and mounted for imaging. For confocal imaging, an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscopy equipped with a 100×/1.45 NA objective lens was used at RT. For desmosome super-resolution imaging, cell samples were imaged using a Zeiss Elyra 7 3D-SIM microscope equipped with a 63×/1.4 NA objective lens at RT. All images were processed by ImageJ software (NIH).

For quantification of the full width at half maximum (FWHM) representing DP I/II signal width, line scan analysis was performed on immunofluorescent images using ImageJ software (NIH). For each cell junction with double-band phenotype, peak-to-peak distance was measured in triplicate.

To quantify average focal adhesion size in WT and CCDC120 KO MDCK cells, ImageJ software (NIH) was utilized. Images were first converted to 8-bit, then each cell was outlined by manually tracing its boundary with the Freehand selections tool, and focal adhesions within the cells were delineated using the Auto threshold function with the Otsu method in Threshold tool, and Watershed tool was applied to resolve confluent/fused focal adhesions into individual structures. At last, the Analyze Particles tool was utilized to measure the size of individual focal adhesions with Circularity between 0.10-0.80, and Size between 0.1-10 μm2. The average focal adhesion size per cell was computed, and normalized to that in WT cells. To validate the robustness of the selected threshold for focal adhesion size, the baseline Auto threshold (Otsu method) was adjusted by ±10% and focal adhesion size was quantified as mentioned above. The results showed that the significance trend of inter-group differences remained consistent (Supplementary Table 6), confirming that the selected threshold is robust and the core conclusion is reliable.

For immunostaining of tissue samples, mice were first anesthetized with tribromoethanol and perfused with 4% PFA. Heart tissues were then isolated and post-fixed in freshly prepared 4% PFA overnight. After dehydration in a graded series of alcohol and xylene, samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated in water, heated in antigen retrieval buffer (10 mM citrate, pH 6.0) for 20 min in a microwave oven, and permeabilized in 0.25% Triton X-100. Samples were blocked, incubated with indicated primary and secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table 3), and then mounted for imaging. To quantify the localization of DP and PKP2 in the heart tissue sections, the length of DP and PKP2 signal at the intercalated disc was measured using ImageJ software (NIH).

To validate the expression level of GFP-CCDC120 in MDCK cells, ImageJ software (NIH) was utilized. All images were first captured with identical parameters. Subsequently, a 20 × 20 μm2 region was selected within the cytoplasm of MDCK cells expressing GFP-CCDC120, along with a non-fluorescent region. The integrated density was measured for both regions, and the corrected fluorescence intensity was calculated by subtracting the integrated density of the background from that within the cells. GFP-CCDC120 expression was categorized as high when the corrected fluorescence value exceeded 2800 arbitrary units (a.u.), and as low when the value was less than 2800 a.u. For experiments analyzing the fluidity and colocalization of GFP-CCDC120 condensates, MDCK cells with high GFP-CCDC120 expression were selected; conversely, cells with low GFP-CCDC120 expression were chosen for experiments measuring GFP-CCDC120 signal at cell borders.

For live-cell imaging, transfected cells were seeded in a glass-bottom dish and imaged using a spinning-disk confocal microscope (UltraView VoX, PerkinElmer) equipped with a 60×/1.4 NA objective lens or a Zeiss LSM 880 Airyscan confocal microscope equipped with a 40×/1.3 NA objective lens at 37 °C. Images were analyzed using Volocity software (PerkinElmer) or ZEN 3.8 (Zeiss).

For FRAP assays, MDCK cells were transfected with a GFP-CCDC120 vector. FRAP assays were performed 24 h after transfection using a Zeiss LSM 880 Airyscan confocal microscope. GFP-CCDC120 droplets or desmosome-localized GFP-CCDC120 were photobleached using a 488 nm laser at 100% laser power, followed by imaging at 9-s intervals for 3 min.

In vitro kinase assay

The in vitro kinase assay was performed as described in a previous study43. Recombinant His-GFP-CCDC120-IDR1 WT or ST10A mutant protein, purified from E. coli, was used as a substrate and incubated with 0.05 μg PKCα (Abcam) for 1 h at 30 °C in kinase buffer (40 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, and 500 μM ATP-γS (Solarbio)) with protease inhibitor cocktail. A final concentration of 2.5 mM p-nitrobenzyl mesylate (PNBM, Abcam) was added to the mixture for an alkylation reaction and incubated for 1 h at 25 °C. Reaction products were analyzed by immunoblotting using an anti-thiophosphate-ester antibody (SD2020, HUABIO).

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

The AFM (BioScope Resolve, Bruker) indentation method was used to measure cell stiffness71. A pre-calibrated probe (MLCT-SPH-5UM, Bruker) with a hemispherical tip was used. Cantilever D was typically used with a spring constant of 0.028 N/m. The deflection sensitivity was measured before each experiment to ensure complete probe calibration.

Cells were plated in a 35-mm cell-culture dish to form monolayers with greater than 80% confluency. AFM measurements were performed in cell culture medium at RT. The cantilever was brought into contact with the cell layer at a constant speed of 4 µm/s until reaching the force setpoint of 3 nN, at which point the maximum indentation depth of cells ranged from 0.5 to 1.0 µm. A force volume scan containing 256 spots in a 100 × 100 μm region was set, and 12-15 regions were selected in one group of the cells from three independent experiments. For each experiment, the bottom of cell-culture dish was partitioned into a 5 × 5 grid of subregions, and 3-4 subregions were randomly selected for the following measurement.

Force-indentation curves were fit to the Hertz model for spherical tips using NanoScope Analysis software 1.8, with a Poisson’s ratio of 0.5 for all calculations, to determine Young’s modulus.

Dispase assay

The dispase assay was performed as described in a previous study9. MDCK cells were plated in a 6-well plate, and each group of cells were seeded in one well per experiment. After 36 h, cells at 90% confluence were treated with culture medium containing dispase II (2.4 U/mL) for 30 min at 37 °C. After that, intact monolayer cells per well were washed with PBS and carefully detached via gentle pipetting, and then transferred into a 15-mL centrifuge tube using a wide-bore pipette in 4 mL of PBS, followed by 50 inversion cycles at 30 rpm at RT. Finally, the contents were transferred into one well of a fresh 6-well plate, and imaged under stereomicroscope (Stemi 305, Zeiss). The number of monolayer fragments per well was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH) as follows: Images were converted to 8-bit. The fragment areas within a well were defined using the Auto threshold with the Triangle method in Threshold tool. Subsequently, the number of fragments per well was counted using the Analyze Particles tool, with only those fragments having a Circularity between 0.20-0.80, and Size greater than 10 pixel2 were quantified. Four independent experiments were performed. To validate the robustness of the selected threshold for cell fragment counting in the dispase assay, we adjusted the baseline Auto threshold (Triangle method) was adjusted by ±10% and cell fragments was quantified as mentioned above. The results showed that the significance trend of inter-group differences remained consistent (Supplementary Table 7), confirming that the selected threshold is robust and the core conclusion is reliable.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a Vevo 3100 Ultrasound Imaging System (VisualSonics Inc.) equipped with a 40-MHz probe. After anesthesia in an induction chamber containing 3% isoflurane in oxygen, the mouse was placed in a supine position on a warm table to maintain body temperature at approximate 37 °C. Anesthesia was maintained using 1% isoflurane in 500 mL O2/min via a nose mask. Images were acquired in parasternal short-axis views (M-mode), and cardiac function parameters were measured and calculated using Vevo3100 analytical software. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and fractional shortening (FS) were calculated by at least five measurements of LV anterior (LVAW), LV internal diameter (LVID) and LV posterior wall (LVPW) in diastole and systole respectively.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For heart tissue samples, two-month-old mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol and perfused with 2% PFA and 1% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB) at RT. Heart tissues were isolated and fixed in 2% PFA and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M PB at RT for 1 h, then stained with 2% uranyl acetate. Samples were washed four times with 0.1 M PB and post-fixed in 1% OsO4 and 0.8% K4Fe(CN)6 for 1 h at RT. Following three washes with double-distilled H2O (ddH2O), cells were stained with 1% tannic acid for 1 h at RT, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in Embed 812 resin.

For cell samples, MDCK cells were fixed for 2 h at RT using 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M PB. Subsequently, post-fixation was carried out for 1 h in a mixture of 1% OsO4 and 0.8% K4Fe(CN)6 at RT. The samples were then washed three times with ddH₂O and stained with 1% tannic acid for 1 h. Following another three washes with ddH₂O, the cells were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series and transitioned to pure acetone through two 6-minute exchanges, and then embedded in Embed 812 resin.

Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were obtained using a Leica Microsystems UC7 ultramicrotome and mounted on single-slot copper grids. After counterstaining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, samples were imaged using Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTWIN (FEI) transmission electron microscope with a CCD camera (Orius 832, Gatan), or Tecnai G2 20 TWIN (FEI) transmission electron microscope. At least 20 images per sample were acquired for measurements. The lengths and widths of desmosome-like segments of the area composita were measured in different groups, as described in a previous study72.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software). Data are presented as mean ± SEM or Box & Whisker plot showing the median (central line), first and third quartiles (box bounds), and minimum to maximum values (whiskers). An unpaired or paired two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied for comparisons between two groups. One-way ANOVA was applied for multiple group comparisons.
