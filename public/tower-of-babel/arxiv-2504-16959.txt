On Validating Angular Power Spectral Models for the Stochastic Gravitational-Wave Background Without Distributional Assumptions
Xiangyu Zhang, Erik Floden, Hongru Zhao, Sara Algeri, Galin Jones, Vuk Mandic, Jesse Miller

Abstract
It is demonstrated that estimators of the angular power spectrum commonly used for the stochastic gravitational-wave background (SGWB) lack a closed-form analytical expression for the likelihood function and, typically, cannot be accurately approximated by a Gaussian likelihood. Nevertheless, a robust statistical analysis can be performed by extending the framework outlined in Algeri et al. 2025 to enable the estimation and testing of angular power spectral models for the SGWB without specifying distributional assumptions. Here, the technical aspects of the method are discussed in detail. Moreover, a new, consistent estimator for the covariance of the angular power spectrum is derived. The proposed approach is applied to data from the third observing run (O3) of Advanced LIGO and Advanced Virgo.



I
Introduction

Estimating and testing models for the angular power spectrum is required in many astrophysical and cosmological settings, including the cosmic microwave background
Collaboration et al. 2020
, galaxy sky surveys
Ahumada et al. 2020
, and weak gravitational lensing surveys
Jeffrey et al. 2021
. The focus here is on the stochastic gravitational-wave background (SGWB) resulting from the superposition of gravitational-wave (GW) signals that are too weak or too numerous to be resolved individually. While the SGWB and its anisotropies have not yet been detected by second-generation GW detectors such as the Advanced LIGO
Aasi et al. 2015
and Advanced Virgo
Acernese et al. 2014
, upper limits have been placed on the GW energy density of the background as well as its angular power spectra
Abbott et al. 2021a
,
Abbott et al. 2021b
.

Anisotropies in the SGWB are expected to arise from a variety of cosmological and astrophysical phenomena
Contaldi 2017
,
Jenkins et al. 2019
,
Jenkins and Sakellariadou 2019
,
Pitrou et al. 2020
. They can provide information on the astrophysical distribution of GW sources within galaxies, formation of large-scale structure in the universe, and different GW production mechanisms
Cusin et al. 2017
,
Cusin et al. 2018a
,
Cusin et al. 2018b
,
Cusin et al. 2019
. GWs generated by cosmological phase transitions necessarily have anisotropies analogous to those of the cosmic microwave background and may provide information regarding primordial inhomogeneities during an early inflationary era
Geller et al. 2018
,
Bertacca et al. 2020
. GW signals emitted by neutron stars within our galaxy can trace out the galactic plane
Talukder et al. 2014
,
Mazumder et al. 2014
, while cosmic strings may cause anisotropies at small angular scales
Jenkins and Sakellariadou 2018
.

Theoretical models of SGWB anisotropy typically do not predict the realization of the anisotropy on the sky; instead, they predict the distribution of the SGWB power across different angular scales described by the SGWB angular power spectrum. It is, therefore, critical to measure the SGWB angular power spectrum in order to establish a comparison with theoretical models. In practice, one starts by cross-correlating the strain time-series data measured by two GW detectors located at two different locations on Earth. Under the assumption of a stationary SGWB, one can then leverage the rotating antenna pattern of the detector pair (induced by the rotation of the Earth) to estimate the SGWB anisotropy across the sky from the GW data
Allen and Romano 1999
,
Thrane et al. 2009
,
Abbott et al. 2021a
. Expanding the estimated anisotropy in terms of spherical harmonics, and then squaring and summing the expansion coefficients, results in estimators of the SGWB angular power spectrum (see Sec.
II
). These estimators can then be directly compared with their theoretical counterparts.

Estimators of the SGWB anisotropy, such as coefficients in the spherical harmonic expansion, are obtained by averaging over many time segments of the strain data and are, therefore, asymptotically multivariate Gaussian distributed. This feature has been used in past parameter estimation analyses whose objective was to estimate individual spherical harmonic modes
Tsukada et al. 2023
or to estimate a specific anisotropy realization on the sky, such as the galactic plane
Agarwal et al. 2022
. Estimators of the angular power spectrum are obtained by squaring the SGWB anisotropy estimators; hence, they are not Gaussian distributed. Nevertheless, some studies have assumed Gaussianity of the angular power spectrum
Agarwal et al. 2023
, but such an assumption has been shown to lead to biases.

As shown in Appendix
A
, the estimators of the angular power spectrum follow a generalized
\chi^{2}
distribution, which cannot be accurately approximated by the Gaussian distribution. Moreover, using the generalized
\chi^{2}
likelihood is nontrivial because, in general, the density does not have a closed-form expression. Alternatively, one could devise a scheme that does not require any assumptions regarding the angular power spectrum’s underlying distribution. The latter approach is used here to implement and adapt the statistical procedure outlined in our companion paper
Algeri et al. 2025
to the context of the SGWB.

The remainder of the manuscript is organized as follows. Sec.
II
describes the search methods used to estimate the angular power spectra from GW detector data. Sec.
III
presents a comprehensive explanation of how the distribution-free statistical framework outlined in our companion paper
Algeri et al. 2025
can be tailored here to the specifics of an SGWB analysis. Sec.
III.3
investigates the statistical properties of the proposed procedure via simulation studies. In Sec.
IV
, the method is applied to GW data from Advanced LIGO and Advanced Virgo’s third observing run (O3), comparing it with a theoretical model for the angular power spectrum arising from the superposition of compact binary coalescences
Cusin et al. 2018a
,
Cusin et al. 2019
,
Yang et al. 2023
. Our results demonstrate that this framework can reliably estimate the unknown parameters of the hypothesized models for the angular power spectrum and provides a powerful and computationally efficient goodness-of-fit test to assess the validity of such models.

II
Spherical Harmonics Search Methods for the SGWB

GW data from the Advanced LIGO and Advanced Virgo detectors may be used to generate estimators of the SGWB angular power spectrum. Each GW detector, labeled by
I=1,2
, produces a strain time series given by

\displaystyle s_{I}(t)=h_{I}(t)+n_{I}(t),

with
n_{I}(t)
denoting the detector noise and
h_{I}(t)
is the detector response to a GW signal. The GW signal can be decomposed in terms of GW modes
h_{A}(f,\hat{\Omega})
defined by their polarization
A
, frequency
f
, and propagation direction
\hat{\Omega}
:

\displaystyle h_{I}(t)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\mathrm{d}f\int_{S^{2}}\mathrm{d}\hat{\Omega}h_{A}(f,\hat{\Omega})F_{I}^{A}(\hat{\Omega},t)e^{i2\pi f(t-\hat{\Omega}\cdot\vec{x}_{I}(t)/c)}.

Here,
F_{I}^{A}(\hat{\Omega},t)
is the time-dependent detector response function,
\vec{x}_{I}(t)
gives the time-dependent location of interferometer
I
, and
S^{2}
denotes integration over the 2-sphere. If
\hat{X}_{I}(t)
and
\hat{Y}_{I}(t)
are unit vectors pointing along the arms of detector
I
, the detector response function is

\displaystyle F_{I}^{A}(\hat{\Omega},t)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{X}^{a}_{I}(t)\hat{X}^{b}_{I}(t)-\hat{Y}^{a}_{I}(t)\hat{Y}^{b}_{I}(t)\right]e^{A}_{ab}(\hat{\Omega}),

in which we assume Einstein summation over the spatial indices
a,b
. The gravitational-wave polarization tensors
e^{A}_{ab}(\hat{\Omega})
are defined as in
Thrane et al. 2009
.
We assume the SGWB is unpolarized, Gaussian, and stationary. Additionally, by assuming perturbations to the spacetime metric are real, the Fourier coefficients
h_{A}(f,\hat{\Omega})
satisfy
h_{A}(-f,\hat{\Omega})=h_{A}^{*}(f,\hat{\Omega})
, where
∗
denotes complex conjugation. They also have zero means,
\langle h_{A}(f,\hat{\Omega})\rangle=0
, and the expected value of their two-point correlation is

\displaystyle\langle h_{A}^{*}(f,\hat{\Omega})\,h_{A^{\prime}}(f^{\prime},\hat{\Omega}^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{1}{4}\,\mathcal{P}(f,\hat{\Omega})\,\delta_{AA^{\prime}}\,\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,\delta^{2}(\hat{\Omega},\hat{\Omega}^{\prime}),\,

where
{\mathcal{P}}(f,\hat{\Omega})
gives the spectral and angular distribution of the SGWB
Thrane et al. 2009
,
\delta_{AA^{\prime}}
denotes the Kronecker delta function in polarization, and the remaining
\delta
’s denote Dirac delta functions in frequency and angular position on the sky. We also
assume that
{\mathcal{P}}(f,\hat{\Omega})
can be factored into its separate spectral and angular components:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(f,\hat{\Omega})=H(f)\,\mathcal{P}(\hat{\Omega}),

where
H(f)
is a dimensionless quantity assumed to be a power law both for its simplicity and its ability to approximate most interesting SGWB models
Abbott et al. 2017a
,
Allen and Romano 1999
,
Abbott et al. 2007
,

\displaystyle H(f)=\left(\frac{f}{f_{\rm ref}}\right)^{\alpha-3}.

We set the reference frequency
f_{\rm ref}=25
Hz while the spectral index
\alpha
may be assigned a value depending on the type of SGWB model being considered. For the astrophysical SGWB resulting from merger events between compact objects such as black holes and neutron stars,
\alpha=2/3

Abbott et al. 2021a
.

Next, decompose the angular distribution of the background
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\Omega})
into its spherical harmonic components
Y_{\ell m}
and coefficients
a_{\ell m}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\hat{\Omega})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\ell_{\rm max}}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\Omega}).

Note that these definitions assume that each GW frequency bin has the same anisotropy. This assumption can be relaxed, and the analysis can be pursued in separate frequency bins (or bands)
{f_{b}}
. In this case, the spherical harmonic coefficients will become frequency dependent and we denote them as
a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
.

The value of
\ell_{max}
determines the highest-order spherical harmonic modes that are considered in the analysis. This value may be chosen differently for different spectral models of the SGWB, and while
\ell_{max}=4
is typically used when
\alpha=2/3

Abbott et al. 2021a
,
\ell_{max}=8
is used here. This choice of
\ell_{max}
is more appropriate for the frequency range over which we will evaluate the angular power spectrum. It has been shown that while
\ell_{max}=4
is an optimal choice for making a GW detection of the chosen SGWB model, it corresponds to an angular resolution that is lower than what is resolvable by the Advanced LIGO-Virgo detectors
Floden et al. 2022
, which justifies considering spherical harmonic modes corresponding to a higher angular resolution.

Next, apply a short-term Fourier transform of the time series output
s_{I}(t)
to obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{s}_{I}(f,t)\equiv\int^{t+\tau/2}_{t-\tau/2}dt^{\prime}e^{-i2\pi ft^{\prime}}s_{I}(t^{\prime}).

Here,
\tau
is chosen to be 192 seconds, which is long enough to be much greater than the light travel time between detectors but short enough so as to minimize effects due to changes in the detectors’ response functions induced by the Earth rotatation
Thrane et al. 2009
,
Abbott et al. 2021a
. The cross-correlation between detectors
I
and
J
is

\displaystyle C(t;f)=\frac{2}{\tau}\tilde{s}_{I}^{*}(t;f)\tilde{s}_{J}(t;f),

where, to simplify the notation, the
I,J
indices corresponding to the two detectors from the cross-spectra terms have been dropped. Assuming that the noise of the two detectors is uncorrelated
Thrane et al. 2009
, the expectation value of the cross-correlation spectrum can be expressed as

\displaystyle\langle C(t;f)\rangle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\ell_{\rm max}}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}H(f)\gamma_{\ell m}(t;f){a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m},

where frequency
f
belongs to the band
f_{b}
.
The overlap reduction function,
\gamma_{\ell m}(t;f)
, is a geometric factor that accounts for the relative orientation and separation of the two detectors in a given pair
Thrane et al. 2009
. The covariance matrix of the cross-correlation spectra is given by

\displaystyle N_{ft,f^{\prime}t^{\prime}}=\langle C(t;f)C^{*}(t^{\prime};f^{\prime})\rangle-\langle C(t;f)\rangle\langle C^{*}(t^{\prime};f^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta(f-f^{\prime})P_{I}(t;f)P_{J}(t;f),

(1)

Here,
P_{I}(t;f)
and
P_{J}(t;f)
correspond to the one-sided power spectral densities of the two GW detectors’ outputs. Assuming the present GW signal powers (both cross- and auto-correlated) are much less than the detector noise power, we obtain the approximation in Eq. (
1
). For a given set of
a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
, the likelihood function for the cross-correlation spectra in the frequency band
f_{b}
is proportional to

\displaystyle\exp(-\sum_{{f},t}[C(t;{f})-H({f})\,\sum_{\ell m}\gamma_{\ell m}(t;{f})\,{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}]^{*}

(2)

\displaystyle N^{-1}_{{f}t,{f}t}[C(t;{f})-H({f})\,\sum_{\ell m}\gamma_{\ell m}(t;{f})\,{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}])

and the summation is over
{f}\in{f_{b}}
and
t\in[0,T)
. The maximizers of this likelihood function have the form

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\big(\Gamma_{{f_{b}}}^{-1}\big)_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\hat{X}_{{f_{b}},\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}

(3)

where

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}=\sum_{{f}\in{f_{b}},t\in[0,T)}\gamma_{\ell m}^{*}(t;{f})\frac{H({f})}{P_{I}(t;{f})P_{J}(t;{f})}C(t;{f})

(4)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{{f_{b}},\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}=\sum_{{f}\in{f_{b}},t\in[0,T)}\gamma_{\ell m}^{*}(t;{f})\frac{H^{2}({f})}{P_{I}(t;{f})P_{J}(t;{f})}\gamma_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t;{f}).

(5)

Here,
\Gamma
is the Fisher information matrix
Thrane et al. 2009
. Note that if Eq. (
2
) holds, each
\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
is a linear combination of complex Gaussian variables; thus, they also
follow a complex Gaussian distribution with mean

\langle\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}\rangle={a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
(discussed further below)
and covariance
\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m},\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}})=(\Gamma^{-1}_{{f_{b}}})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}.
Indeed the same is true, at least approximately, even if Eq. (
2
) does not hold, but the summation over
t
in Eq. (
4
) is taken over a large number of segments (as is the case with the Advanced LIGO and Advanced Virgo observing runs).

Eq. (
4
) defines a map of the gravitational-wave background convolved with the antenna pattern of the detector pair, the so-called dirty map. The covariance matrix of the dirty map is given in Eq. (
5
). Since Eq. (
3
) represents the deconvolution of the GW signal from the detector response, it is the so-called clean map. The covariance matrix of the clean map is obtained by inverting the covariance matrix of the dirty map.

The estimates
\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
are evaluated by summing over discrete bins in the frequency band
{f_{b}}
. Broadband analyses perform this sum over the entire available range of frequencies to which the detectors are most sensitive
Abbott et al. 2021a
. However, in the analysis presented here, we obtain multiple samples of
\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
corresponding to sums over different frequency bands. The subscript
{f_{b}}
used in Eq. (
2
) and onwards denotes the frequency range over which each set of
\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
,
\Gamma_{{f_{b}},\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}
, and
\hat{X}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
is evaluated. Similar notation is employed throughout the manuscript whenever a quantity is evaluated over some discrete range of frequencies rather than considering
{f_{b}}
to be a continuous variable.

From a statistical perspective, maximum likelihood is fundamentally a method for estimating deterministic quantities. This means the
\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
in Eq. (
3
) serve as maximum likelihood estimators of the spherical harmonic coefficients,
a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
, that uniquely characterize the SGWB of the only realization of the universe available. Therefore, similarly to what is implicitly done in many statistical analyses, the
a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
are not treated as random variables
[
Thrane et al. 2009
,
Abbott et al. 2017b
,
Abbott et al. 2019
,
Abbott et al. 2021a
, e.g.,]
. This is a reasonable assumption in practical scenarios, and treating the
a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
as fixed avoids the risk of incorrectly specifying their underlying distribution, which can substantially bias the resulting inference.

The proposed statistical methodology requires that multiple replicates of
\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
are available. To ensure that is the case, we partition the total observation time
T
into
S
segments, each of duration
\Delta T
, so that
T=\Delta T\cdot S.
The third observing run (O3) includes data from both Advanced LIGO detectors as well as the Advanced Virgo detector, collected from April 1, 2019, to March 27, 2020, with a month-long pause in October 2019. Aside from this pause, the duty cycles of the three detectors were 77%, 75%, and 76% for LIGO Livingston, LIGO Hanford, and Virgo, respectively
Davis et al. 2021
. For this study, we subdivide the O3 data set into
S=
15 segments, each corresponding to approximately 10 days of detector live time. We also divide the frequency range 20-180 Hz into
B=8
discrete frequency bins, each 20 Hz wide. We assume the same time-domain and frequency-domain data quality cuts that were applied during other O3 anisotropic SGWB search analyses
Abbott et al. 2021a
.

For each
s=1,\dots,S
, define
\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell m}
,
\hat{X}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell m}
, and
\Gamma_{{f_{b}},s,\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}
using Eqs. (
3
)-(
5
), but sum over
t
in the interval

\bigl[(s-1)\Delta T,\,s\Delta T\bigr)
instead of
[0,T)
. It can be easily verified that
\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell m}
are independent (across
s
) complex Gaussian variables, each with mean
\langle\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell m}\rangle=a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
and covariance

{\rm Cov}(\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell m},\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}})=(\Gamma_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}
. Because the detector sensitivity varies over time – due to factors such as transient environmental noise, detector maintenance, and modifications – we do not assume that
{\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1}
is identical across different time segments
s.

Since the detector network consists of two Advanced LIGO detectors and one Advanced Virgo detector, there are three pairs of detectors to consider, or three ‘baselines’. Each baseline has its own dirty map and Fisher matrix, and these may be summed together to obtain one overall dirty map and one overall Fisher matrix corresponding to the entire detector network
Thrane et al. 2009
.

Now, consider the estimator of the squared angular power in each mode
\ell

Thrane et al. 2009
,
Romano and Cornish 2017
,

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell}=\frac{1}{1+2\ell}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\left[|\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell m}|^{2}-(\Gamma^{-1}_{{f_{b}},s})_{\ell m,\ell m}\right].

(6)

Denote with
\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}
the
\ell_{\max}\times 1
-dimensional vector with components
\hat{A}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell}
for all
\ell=1,\ldots,\ell_{\rm max}
. For each
s=1,\dots,S,
the estimator
\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}
has mean
\langle\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}\rangle
and covariance matrix
\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}
, whose
(\ell,\ell^{\prime})
-th element is

\displaystyle\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s,\ell\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+2\ell)(1+2\ell^{\prime})}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\Big(|({\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}|^{2}

(7)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}[a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}^{*}\;({\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\;a_{{f_{b}},\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}]\Big).

The derivation of Eq. (
7
) is given in Appendix
B
.
Previous works
[
Agarwal et al. 2023
, e.g.,]
have omitted the second term in the numerator of Eq. (
7
), implicitly making the assumption that
a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
’s are complex Gaussian random variables with zero mean. However, this assumption is not consistent with estimating
the coefficients
a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
(as realized in our universe) via the maximum likelihood technique – that is, likelihood maximization implicitly assumes the coefficients to be the (deterministic) limit to which the maximum likelihood estimates converge. In this manuscript, this inconsistency is avoided by treating
a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
as fixed.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that one could always replace
{\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1}
in Eq. (
7
) with its regularized counterpart if a regularization scheme were applied to invert the Fisher matrix
[
Thrane et al. 2009
,
Agarwal et al. 2023
,
Panda et al. 2019
, Cf.]
. Such regularization is typically implemented via singular value decomposition to mitigate small singular values arising from directions in the sky to which the detector network is insensitive
[
Thrane et al. 2009
,
Abbott et al. 2021a
]
. However, this procedure introduces a bias that propagates into
\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}
in nontrivial ways
[
Yang et al. 2023
]
. To avoid this bias, the Fisher matrix is not regularized here.

III
Statistical Methods

The main goal is to develop an inferential strategy for estimating angular power spectral models and propose a statistical test to assess whether the proposed model is consistent with the data derived from the estimator in Eq. (
6
).

Let
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be an unknown parameter vector, and denote with
\bm{A}_{f_{b}}(\bm{\theta})
the proposed model for the angular power spectrum. Now, assume that the
\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}
vectors are independent across all frequency bins and data segments. Hence, in this context, ‘large sample’ asymptotic results refer to the situation in which
N=\ell_{\rm max}\cdot B\cdot S\rightarrow\infty
, but, since typically the
\ell_{\rm max}
elements of the vectors
\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}
are not independent for different values of
\ell=1,\dots,\ell_{\rm max}
, the effective sample size is (at least)
B\cdot S
. Formally, we aim to estimate
\bm{\theta}
and test the hypotheses

\displaystyle

\displaystyle H_{0}:\langle\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}\rangle=\bm{A}_{f_{b}}(\bm{\theta})\quad\hbox{vs.}

\displaystyle H_{a}:\langle\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}\rangle\neq\bm{A}_{f_{b}}(\bm{\theta})

(8)

without assuming
\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}
follows any specific distribution.

III.1
Parameter and covariance estimation

Begin by estimating the true value of the parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
, hereinafter denoted by
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, using the vectors
\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}
. For the moment, assume that the covariance matrices
\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}
are known, and define the
sphered errors
as

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta})=\tilde{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}-\tilde{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta}),

(9)

with

\tilde{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}=\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1/2}\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta})=\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1/2}\bm{A}_{{f_{b}}}(\bm{\theta}),

(10)

where
\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1/2}
is the inverse square-root matrix of
\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}
and can be obtained via standard procedures
[
Higham 2008
,
Horn and Johnson 2012
]
.
The transformation in Eq. (
10
) is known as
whitening
or
sphering
and enables the construction of a statistically orthonormalized version of the angular power spectrum
\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}
. In other words, under
H_{0}
in Eq. (
8
),
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}
has mean given by
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta})
, its components are uncorrelated, and each has a unit variance.

The true value of the unknown parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}
can be estimated by solving the
generalized nonlinear least squares
problem:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\bm{\theta}}\sum_{b,s}\bm{\epsilon}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta})^{T}\bm{\epsilon}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta}).

(11)

The optimization in Eq. (
11
) is the same as a maximization of the likelihood
of
\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}
under the assumption of Gaussianity. Yet, its underlying rationale differs in that it aims to minimize the squared differences of the sphered errors, regardless of their distribution.
In general, such a minimization does not enjoy a closed-form solution and is typically solved using iterative numerical methods
Nocedal and Wright 1999
.

The solution of Eq. (
11
), can be shown to be consistent under
H_{0}
. That is, if the model
\bm{A}_{f_{b}}(\bm{\theta})
is correctly specified, and if it is identifiable – that is,
\bm{A}_{f_{b}}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{A}_{f_{b}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
implies
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}^{\prime}

[Cf.
Davidson and MacKinnon 2004
, Ch. 7]
– then,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
converges in probability
[for a definition see
van der Vaart 2000
, Ch. 2]
to
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. Furthermore, under additional classical regularity conditions
[Cf.
Davidson and MacKinnon 2004
, Ch. 7]
,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
is approximately Gaussian distributed with mean
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and its variance approaches zero as
N\rightarrow\infty
. This can be seen by taking the derivative of the objective function in Eq. (
11
) with respect to
\bm{\theta}
, which shows that the generalized least squares estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
is a solution to the system of
p
estimating equations:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{b,s}\dot{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})^{T}\bm{\epsilon}_{{f_{b}},s}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})=\bm{0},

(12)

where
\dot{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})
denotes the
\ell_{\rm max}\times p
matrix with columns corresponding to the partial derivatives of
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta})
evaluated at
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
, i.e.,

\dot{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})=\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1/2}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{1}}\bm{A}_{f_{b}}(\bm{\theta}),\ldots,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{p}}\bm{A}_{f_{b}}(\bm{\theta})\right]\biggl|_{\bm{\theta}=\hat{\bm{\theta}}},

in which
\theta_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,p
are the elements of
\bm{\theta}
. A first-order Taylor expansion of the left-hand side of Eq. (
12
) around
\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{\theta}_{0}

III.2
Distribution-free testing of the angular power spectum

In its essence, the statistical formulation of the problem provided in Sec.
III.1
is that of a regression problem in which the estimated angular power spectrum,
\hat{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}
, plays the role of the outcome whose mean we aim to describe through the model
\bm{A}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta})
. We can, therefore, employ constructs from the theory of goodness-of-fit for regression to assess the validity of such a model.

The main building blocks needed to devise a test for the hypotheses in Eq. (
8
) are the
sphered residuals
. They can be obtained by replacing
\bm{\theta}
in Eq. (
9
) with the solution of Eq. (
11
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{{f_{b}},s}=\tilde{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}-\tilde{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}).

(17)

In principle, one could test the hypotheses in Eq. (
8
) using classical goodness-of-fit statistics constructed as the sum of some function of the sphered residuals – with Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
being possibly the most prominent example among those – and derive their distribution under the hypothesis
H_{0}
using the parametric bootstrap
[
Freedman 1981
, Cf.]
based on the generalized
\chi^{2}
distribution derived in Appendix
A
. Such statistics, however, are known to exhibit no statistical power
2
2

2

In statistics, the concept of ‘power’ refers to the sensitivity of a test in detecting departures from the model hypothesized under the null hypothesis.
toward infinitely many possible ‘weak’ deviations from the postulated model
[
Khmaladze 1984
,
Algeri and Khmaladze 2024
,
Acharya and Kashyap 2024
, e.g.,]
. That is, there are infinite possible ways in which the hypothesized model could be wrong, but such statistics would not be able to identify them.
Test statistics based on the process of partial sums of the residuals
[
Loynes 1980
,
Stute 1997
, e.g.,]
, however, can restore statistical power
[
Khmaladze 1984
,
Algeri and Khmaladze 2024
,
Acharya and Kashyap 2024
, e.g.,]
and are thus recommended.

III.2.1
Model testing via partial sums of the residuals

Let
\bm{\epsilon}
and
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}
denote, respectively, the
N\times 1
stacked vectors of errors
\bm{\epsilon}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
and residuals
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{{f_{b}},s}
for all frequency bins and data segments, that is,

\bm{\epsilon}=\left[\bm{\epsilon}_{f_{1},1}^{T}(\bm{\theta}_{0}),\dots,\bm{\epsilon}_{f_{B},1}^{T}(\bm{\theta}_{0}),\dots,\bm{\epsilon}_{f_{1},S}^{T}(\bm{\theta}_{0}),\dots,\bm{\epsilon}_{f_{B},S}^{T}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\right]^{T},

and

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}=\left[\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{f_{1},1}^{T},\dots,\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{f_{B},1}^{T},\dots,\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{f_{1},S}^{T},\dots,\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{f_{B},S}^{T}\right]^{T}.

The process of partial sums of the sphered residuals is

\begin{split}w_{k,N}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}^{T}\mathbb{I}_{k,N},\\
\text{with}\quad&\mathbb{I}_{k,N}=[\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k},\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{N-k}]^{T}.\end{split}

(18)

It consists of normalized cumulative sums of the first
k
elements of
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}
with
k=1,\dots,N
.

Following the same argument of
Khmaladze 2021
, it is possible to show that
w_{k,N}
is asymptotically equal to a projection of the process of partial sums of the sphered errors given by

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{\epsilon}^{T}\mathbb{I}_{k,N}.

(19)

As shown below, such a projection plays a fundamental role in deriving the limiting distribution of
w_{k,N}
.

Consider a first-order Taylor expansion of the residuals in Eq. (
17
) evaluated at
\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{\theta}_{0}
, that is,

\begin{split}\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{{f_{b}},s}&=\bm{\epsilon}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta}_{0})-\dot{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta}_{0})(\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}_{0})+o_{p}(1).\end{split}

(20)

Substituting the right-hand side of Eq. (
15

\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
in Eq. (
20
) leads to

\begin{split}\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{{f_{b}},s}=&\bm{\epsilon}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\\
&-\frac{1}{N}\dot{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\Bigl[\bm{R}_{p}^{-1}\sum_{{f_{b}},s}\dot{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{T}\bm{\epsilon}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\Bigl]+o_{p}(1).\\
\end{split}

(21)

Let
\dot{\bm{A}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
be a
N\times p
matrix denoting the
stacked matrix of
\dot{\bm{A}}_{{f_{b}},s}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
across all frequency bins and time segments, i.e.,

\dot{\bm{A}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\bm{A}}^{T}_{f_{1},1}(\bm{\theta}_{0}),\cdots,\dot{\bm{A}}^{T}_{f_{B},1}(\bm{\theta}_{0}),\cdots,\dot{\bm{A}}^{T}_{f_{B},S}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\end{bmatrix}^{T},

then, the vector
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}
, with elements given by Eq. (
21

\begin{split}\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}&=\bm{\epsilon}-\frac{1}{N}\dot{\bm{A}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\bm{R}_{p}^{-1}\dot{\bm{A}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{T}\bm{\epsilon}+o_{p}(1)\\
&=\bm{\epsilon}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}^{T}\bm{\epsilon}+o_{p}(1),\end{split}

(22)

where
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}
corresponds to the
jth
column of the matrix

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\dot{\bm{A}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\bm{R}_{p}^{-1/2}.

From Eq. (
22

\{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{p}
. Similarly, the process
w_{k,N}
can be written as:

w_{k,N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{\epsilon}^{T}\Bigl(\bm{I}_{N}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}^{T}\Bigl)\mathbb{I}_{k,N}+o_{p}(1),

(23)

where
\bm{I}_{N}
denotes the
N\times N
identity matrix. It follows that, in the limit,

is a projection of the process in Eq. (
19

\{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{p}
.

The process in Eq. (
19

H_{0}
in Eq. (
8

3
3

3

Intuitively, a sequence of random variables is said to be ‘weakly dependent’ if the dependence among the elements of the sequence reduces and eventually ceases to exist for elements that are sufficiently far away from one another within the sequence.
quantities
[
Philipp and Stout 1975

Bradley 2005
, Cf.]
with mean zero and finite variance. The condition of weak dependence is satisfied since the subvectors

are all independent from one another.
It follows that since

is a projection of the process in Eq. (

Its mean and covariance are

\begin{split}\langle w_{k,N}\rangle&\simeq 0\qquad\text{and}\\
\langle w_{k,N}w_{k^{\prime},N}\rangle&\simeq\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{I}_{k,N}^{T}\Bigl(\bm{I}_{N}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}^{T}\Bigl)\mathbb{I}_{k^{\prime},N}.\end{split}

(24)

where the notation

III.2.2
Construction of distribution-free test statistics

Since the null distribution of the test statistics in Eq. (
25
) depends on the model being tested, it can only be derived on a case-by-case basis for each specific choice of the postulated angular power spectrum model
\bm{A}_{f_{b}}(\bm{\theta})
. Unfortunately, this process can be computationally burdensome when testing several different and possibly complicated models. Nevertheless, it is possible to overcome this limitation by relying on the
Khmaladze-2 (K2) transform

Khmaladze 2021
.

Given an arbitrary orthonormal set of vectors
\{\bm{r}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the K2 transformation allows us to map
w_{N,k}

into a process of partial sums, hereafter denoted by
v_{k,N}
with ‘standard’ limiting distribution under the null hypothesis. Specifically, for sufficiently large
N
, its null distribution is well-approximated by that of a projected Brownian motion with covariance

\begin{split}\langle v_{k,N}v_{k^{\prime},N}\rangle\simeq\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{I}_{k,N}^{T}\Bigl(\bm{I}_{N}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{r}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}^{T}\Bigl)\mathbb{I}_{k^{\prime},N}.\end{split}

(28)

Thus, for any choice of
\{\bm{r}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
independent from
\bm{A}_{f}(\theta)
and such that
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{r}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}^{T}\neq\bm{I}_{N}
,
the distribution of
v_{k,N}
is also independent from such a model. One can then rely on the functionals of the newly constructed process
v_{k,N}

such as

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{N}=\max\limits_{k}|v_{k,N}|,\quad\widetilde{C}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|v_{k,N}|^{2},

(29)

to test the hypotheses in Eq. (
8
) in a distribution-free manner. As demonstrated in Section
III.3
, this implies one can test different postulated models for the angular power spectrum by relying on a single simulation of the distribution of the statistics in Eq. (
29
).

There are many possible ways to choose the orthonormal set
\{\bm{r}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
. For example, as proposed by
Khmaladze 2021
, one could consider

\bm{r}_{1}=(1/\sqrt{N},\dots,1/\sqrt{N})^{T},

letting
\bm{r}_{2}
be the vector of elements

\bm{r}_{2,n}=\sqrt{\frac{12N}{N^{2}-1}}\left(\frac{n}{N}-\frac{N+1}{2N}\right),\ \ n=1,\ldots,N;

(30)

the remaining vectors
\bm{r}_{3},\ldots,\bm{r}_{p}
can be obtained by the Gram–Schmidt process applied to the powers of
\bm{r}_{2}
. Alternatively, one could construct the orthonormal set
\{\bm{r}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
by considering a set of
p
orthonormal functions with a given inner product. For example, the Hermite polynomials
\{H_{j}(x)\}_{j=1}^{p}
satisfy the condition:

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}H_{i}(x)H_{j}(x)e^{-x^{2}}dx=\delta_{ij}.

We can then specify the elements of the vectors
\bm{r}_{j}
as

r_{j,n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{j}}}H_{j}(x_{n})\sqrt{e^{-x_{n}^{2}}},

where
c_{j}=\sum_{n}H^{2}_{j}(x_{n})e^{-x_{n}^{2}}
are normalizing constants and
x_{n}=-\zeta+\frac{2\zeta(n-1)}{N-1},

n=1,\dots,N
, represent
N
equally spaced points over the interval
[-\zeta,\zeta]
for some appropriately chosen constant
\zeta>0
.

The main steps involved in the construction of the process
v_{k,N}
, for any given orthonormal set
\{\bm{r}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
, are outlined in
Algeri et al. 2025
. Below, we provide a thorough exposition of the technical aspects of such a transformation.

To ease the intuition, let us first consider the simple scenario where the unknown parameter
\bm{\theta}
is one-dimensional. This situation is encountered, for example, when the interest is solely in estimating the amplitude of the astrophysical kernel used to generate theoretical values of the angular power spectrum (see Sec.
IV
).
Define the operator
U_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
as

U_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}=\bm{I}-\frac{\langle\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}-\bm{r}_{1},\cdot\rangle}{1-\langle\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}\rangle}(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}-\bm{r}_{1}),

(31)

where
\bm{I}
is the identity operator. One can easily verify that
U_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}

maps
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}
to
\bm{r}_{1}
,
\bm{r}_{1}
to
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}
, and leaves vectors orthogonal to both
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}
and
\bm{r}_{1}
unchanged.
As shown in Appendix
D
, such an operator is unitary and self-adjoint. The
K2-transformed errors
are given by

\bm{e}=U_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}\bm{\epsilon}

and we define the
K2-transformed residuals
as

\hat{\bm{e}}=U_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}.

Similarly to Eq. (
22
), the K2-transformed residuals are asymptotically equal to a projection of the K2-transformed errors. Specifically,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{e}}

\displaystyle=U_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}\bm{\epsilon}-U_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}^{T}\bm{\epsilon}+o_{p}(1)

(32)

\displaystyle=\bm{e}-\bm{r}_{1}\bm{r}_{1}^{T}\bm{e}+o_{p}(1),

where the second
equality arises from the fact that

\displaystyle U_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}=\bm{r}_{1}

\displaystyle\text{ and }\ \ \bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}^{T}\bm{\epsilon}=\langle U_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}},U_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}\bm{\epsilon}\rangle=\bm{r}_{1}^{T}\bm{e}.

The process of partial sums of the K2-transformed residuals is

\displaystyle v_{k,N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\hat{\bm{e}}^{T}\mathbb{I}_{k,N}

(33)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{e}^{T}\Bigl(I_{N}-\bm{r}_{1}\bm{r}_{1}^{T}\Bigl)\mathbb{I}_{k,N}+o_{p}(1);

its limiting distribution is that of a projected Brownian motion with zero mean and covariance approximately equal to the right-hand side of Eq. (
28
) for
p=1
.

Let us now consider the situation in which
\bm{\theta}
is multidimensional – as would be the case when estimating the amplitude, mean, and standard deviation of a Gaussian astrophysical kernel (see Sec.
IV
, Eq. (
47
)).
Similarly to the one-dimensional case, the goal is to identify an operator, hereafter denoted by
\bm{U}^{T}_{p}
, such the resulting K2-transformed vector of residuals
\hat{\bm{e}}=\bm{U}^{T}_{p}\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}
is asymptotically equal to a projection of the vector of K2-transformed errors
\bm{e}=\bm{U}^{T}_{p}\bm{\epsilon}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{e}}

\displaystyle=\bm{U}^{T}_{p}\bm{\epsilon}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{U}^{T}_{p}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}^{T}\bm{\epsilon}+o_{p}(1)

(34)

\displaystyle=\bm{e}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{r}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}+o_{p}(1).

To serve this purpose, we aim to construct
\bm{U}^{T}_{p}
so that it enables mapping the vectors
\{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{p}
to the arbitrarily chosen orthonormal set
\{\bm{r}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
.
A – somewhat naive – first attempt, could be that of combining operators of the form
\bm{U}_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}},\bm{r}_{j}}
, constructed as in Eq. (
31
). Such an approach, however, does not yield the desired result. To see this, consider

\bm{U}_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}\bm{U}_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{2}},\bm{r}_{2}}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}=\bm{U}_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\bm{r}_{1}}\bm{r}_{2}\neq\bm{r}_{2}.

To overcome this difficulty, consider a set of vectors
\{\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
constructed so that each
\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{j}
is orthogonal to
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}
, for all
k<j
.
Specifically, we choose
\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{1}=\bm{r}_{1}

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{j}=\bm{U}_{j-1}\bm{r}_{j}

(35)

for
j=2,\ldots,p
, with
\bm{U}_{j}
denoting the product operator:

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{j}=\bm{U}_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}},\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{j}}\ldots\bm{U}_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{1}}

(36)

with each operator
\bm{U}_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}},\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{j}}
acting on everything on its right.
Since the operator
\bm{U}_{j}
involves a product of unitary operators, it is also unitary. Its adjoint operator is given by its transpose:

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{T}_{j}=\bm{U}_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{1}}\ldots\bm{U}_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}},\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{j}}.

(37)

One can verify that the vectors
(\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{j})_{j=1}^{p}
are orthogonal to
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}
, for all
k<j
by noticing that

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{2},\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{r}_{2},U_{\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{1}}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\rangle=\langle\bm{r}_{2},\bm{r}_{1}\rangle=0,

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{3},\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\rangle

Testing spectral models for the SGWB

We now apply the methodology described in Sec.

Let us turn our attention to the theoretical model we wish to test. The energy density of the stochastic gravitational-wave background is defined as

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{GW}}(\hat{\Omega},f)=\frac{f}{\rho_{c}}\frac{d^{3}\rho_{\text{GW}}}{d^{2}\hat{\Omega}df}(\hat{\Omega},f)=\frac{\bar{\Omega}_{\text{GW}}(f)}{4\pi}+\delta\Omega_{\text{GW}}(\hat{\Omega},f),

(43)

\bar{{f_{b}}}

Cusin et al. 2018a
,
Cusin et al. 2019

\displaystyle\bar{\Omega}_{\text{GW}}(f)=\int\mathrm{d}r\,\partial_{r}\bar{\Omega}_{\text{GW}}(f,r),

(44)

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\bar{\Omega}_{\text{GW}}(f,r)=\frac{f}{\rho_{c}}\,\mathcal{B}(f,r;\bm{\theta})\,,

where
r
denotes the conformal distance
and the function
\mathcal{B}(f,r;\bm{\theta})
is an astrophysical kernel with free parameters
\bm{\theta}
that contains information on the local production of GWs at galaxy scales (we set the speed of light

). Following
Cusin et al. 2017

\displaystyle A_{\ell}(f;\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int dkk^{2}\,|\delta\Omega_{\text{GW}\,,{\ell}}(k,f;\bm{\theta})|^{2},\hbox{ and}

(45)

\displaystyle\delta\Omega_{\text{GW}\,,\ell}(k,f;\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f}{4\pi\rho_{c}}\int dr\,\mathcal{B}(f,r;\bm{\theta})\,\big[b(r)\,\delta_{m,k}(r)j_{\ell}(kr)\big],

where

is the wavenumber,

if we replace the conformal distance
r
with redshift
z

Yang et al. 2023

The astrophysical kernel can be computed from the galaxy distribution and galaxy GW luminosity; for our purposes, we also replace the conformal distance

Cusin et al. 2018a
,
Cusin et al. 2019

A direct detection of the SGWB has not yet been achieved, so the data from O3 are consistent with noise

V
Summary and Discussion

This paper extends the distribution-free statistical framework presented in a companion paper
Algeri et al. 2025
to account for the unique challenges arising when estimating and testing models for the angular power spectrum of the SGWB.

Framing the problem in the context of regression avoids the need to specify a likelihood function for the angular power spectrum. The procedure only requires a model for its mean and covariance matrix. To ensure consistency in our analysis, a new closed-form expression for the latter is derived to account for the variability associated with the maximum likelihood estimation of the spherical harmonic coefficients of the SGWB sky map.

Simulation studies and an application to data from the third observing run of Advanced LIGO and Advanced Virgo demonstrated that the proposed testing procedure can detect departures from the hypothesized angular power spectral models when misspecified while adequately controlling for the probability of false discoveries. We note that the current sensitivity of the Advanced LIGO and Advanced Virgo detectors has not allowed for direct detection of the SGWB. To circumvent this limitation, we have used the O3 dataset as a noise realization, and a simulated signal has been injected into it. Given the good performance in such a setting, we expect the proposed framework to be valuable when applied to data collected by more sensitive detectors.

Finally, we note that inconsistencies in the statistical formalism can be found in the literature concerning the statistical analysis of the SGWB. In particular, when the spherical harmonic coefficients of the SGWB sky map are estimated via maximum likelihood
[
Thrane et al. 2009
, cf.]
the user implicitly assumes such quantities – as well as the corresponding sky maps– are deterministic. Therefore, they should not be simultaneously treated as zero-mean complex Gaussian random variables. A statistical analysis that enables the estimation and testing of angular power spectral models while also allowing the spherical harmonic coefficients to be random can be established. It requires, however, a revision of the modeling framework at a fundamental level. While this is beyond the scope of this manuscript, it is the subject of ongoing work.

. Therefore,
\{\hat{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}}
is distributed as a multivariate complex Gaussian random variable with mean
\{{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}
. That is,

\{\hat{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\{{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}},\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}),

where
\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}=\{\Gamma^{-1}_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\}_{(\ell,m),(\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}
.
Define

\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}=\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}|\hat{a}_{\ell m}|^{2}.

The goal is to show that
\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}
has a generalized
\chi^{2}
distribution.

Let
\bm{X}
be the random vector whose components are the real and imaginary parts of
\{\hat{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}}
. Because the density of
\{\hat{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}}
is proportional to

\displaystyle\exp\{-(\hat{a}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}-{a}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}})^{*}\Gamma_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime},\ell m}(\hat{a}_{\ell m}-{a}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu})^{T}\bm{\Omega}^{-1}(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu})\},

where

\bm{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(\{\hat{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}})\\
\operatorname{Im}(\{\hat{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}})\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(\{{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}})\\
\operatorname{Im}(\{{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}})\\
\end{pmatrix}

and

\bm{\Omega}^{-1}={2}\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Gamma})&-\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Gamma})\\
\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Gamma})&\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Gamma})\end{pmatrix},

it follows that
\bm{X}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Omega}\right)
, i.e., a real multivariate normal with mean
\bm{\mu}
and covariance
\bm{\Omega}
.
Because
\bm{\Gamma}=\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Gamma})+i\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Gamma})
and
\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}=\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})+i\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})
are inverse of each other, we have

\bm{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})&-\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})\\
\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})&\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})\end{pmatrix}.

Subvectors of
\bm{X}
also follow a (real) multivariate Gaussian distribution. In particular,

\bm{X}_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(\{\hat{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}_{\ell}})\\
\operatorname{Im}(\{\hat{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}_{\ell}})\\
\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\mu}_{\ell},\bm{\Omega}_{\ell}\right),

with mean

\bm{\mu}_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(\{{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}_{\ell}})\\
\operatorname{Im}(\{{a}_{\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}_{\ell}})\\
\end{pmatrix},

and covariance matrix

\bm{\Omega}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell,\ell}&-\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell,\ell}\\
\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell,\ell}&\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell,\ell}\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}=\|\bm{X}_{\ell}\|^{2}
.
Since
\bm{\Gamma}
is a positive definite matrix,
(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell,\ell}
is also positive definite.
Therefore, if, for any non-zero vector
(\bm{x}^{T},\bm{y}^{T})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2l+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{2l+1}
,
\bm{z}=\bm{x}+i\bm{y}
and let
\bm{z}^{\dagger}
be its conjugate transpose, then

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}(\bm{x}^{T},\bm{y}^{T})\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell,\ell}&-\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell,\ell}\\
\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell,\ell}&\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell,\ell}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}\\
\bm{y}\end{pmatrix}\\
\quad&=\frac{1}{2}\bm{z}^{\dagger}(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell,\ell}\bm{z}>0,\end{split}

(54)

which implies that
\bm{\Omega}_{\ell}
is also positive definite.

Consider the eigendecomposition of
\bm{\Omega}_{\ell}
,

\bm{\Omega}_{\ell}=\bm{C}_{\ell}\bm{\Lambda}_{\ell}\bm{C}_{\ell}^{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}\bm{C}_{\ell,i}\bm{C}_{\ell,i}^{T},

where
\bm{C}_{\ell}=[\bm{C}_{\ell,1},\cdots,\bm{C}_{\ell,r_{\ell}}]
is an orthogonal matrix, and
\bm{\Lambda}_{\ell}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{\ell,1}\bm{I}_{k_{\ell,1}},\cdots,\lambda_{\ell,r_{\ell}}\bm{I}_{k_{\ell,r_{\ell}}})
is a diagonal matrix. Here,
r_{\ell}
represents the number of distinct eigenvalues of the matrix
\bm{\Omega}_{\ell}
, and
\{\lambda_{\ell,1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell,r_{\ell}}\}
is the set of these distinct eigenvalues. The values
k_{\ell,1},\ldots,k_{\ell,r_{\ell}}
represent the algebraic multiplicities of the corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{\ell,1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell,r_{\ell}}
for the matrix
\bm{\Omega}_{\ell}
.

Set

\begin{split}\bm{U}_{\ell}&=\bm{\Lambda}_{\ell}^{-1/2}\bm{C}_{\ell}^{T}(\bm{X}_{\ell}-\bm{\mu}_{\ell})=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}_{\ell,1}\\
\bm{U}_{\ell,2}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{U}_{\ell,r}\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

with
\bm{U}_{\ell,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\ell,i}}
and

\begin{split}\bm{\mu}_{\ell}^{\prime}&=\bm{\Lambda}_{\ell}^{-1/2}\bm{C}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{\mu}_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mu}^{\prime}_{\ell,1}\\
\bm{\mu}^{\prime}_{\ell,2}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\mu}^{\prime}_{\ell,r}\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

where

\bm{\mu}^{\prime}_{\ell,i}=\lambda_{\ell,i}^{-1/2}\bm{C}_{\ell,i}^{T}\bm{\mu}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\ell,i}},

for any
1\leq i\leq r_{\ell}
.
It is easy to verify that

\bm{U}_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{4\ell+2})\text{ and }\bm{X}_{\ell}=\bm{C}_{\ell}\bm{\Lambda}_{\ell}^{1/2}(\bm{U}_{\ell}+\bm{\mu}^{\prime}_{\ell}).

Therefore,

\begin{split}\|\bm{X}_{\ell}\|^{2}=&\|\bm{C}_{\ell}\bm{\Lambda}_{\ell}^{1/2}(\bm{U}_{\ell}+\bm{\mu}^{\prime}_{\ell})\|^{2}\\
=&\|\bm{\Lambda}_{\ell}^{1/2}(\bm{U}_{\ell}+\bm{\mu}^{\prime}_{\ell})\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}\|\bm{U}_{\ell,i}+\bm{\mu}_{\ell,i}^{\prime}\|^{2},\end{split}

and thus, due to the correspondence between
\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}
and
\|\bm{X}_{\ell}\|^{2}
, it follows that
\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}\|\bm{U}_{\ell,i}+\bm{\mu}_{\ell,i}^{\prime}\|^{2}
have the same distribution. Furthermore,
\|\bm{U}_{\ell,i}+\bm{\mu}_{\ell,i}^{\prime}\|^{2}
follows a noncentral chi-squared distribution, denoted by
\chi^{2}(\theta_{\ell,i},k_{\ell,i})
, with noncentrality parameter

\theta_{\ell,i}=\|\bm{\mu}^{\prime}_{\ell,i}\|^{2}=\lambda_{\ell,i}^{-1}\bm{\mu}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{C}_{\ell,i}\bm{C}_{\ell,i}^{T}\bm{\mu}_{\ell}

and
k_{\ell,i}
degrees of freedom.

It follows that the random variable
\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}
can be represented as a sum of independent noncentral chi-squared random variables

\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}\cdot\chi^{2}(\theta_{\ell,i},k_{\ell,i}).

(55)

In general, the density of Eq. (
55
) does not have a closed-form expression since it involves modified Bessel functions of the first kind
[
Kotz et al. 2000
]
.
Because integrating the modified Bessel functions in the convolution lacks a simple closed-form solution, we cannot derive the density of the generalized chi-squared distribution unless

r_{\ell}=1
, that is, when no convolution and integration are needed. This situation occurs only when

\bm{\Omega}_{\ell}=c\bm{I}_{4\ell+2}
for some constant
c>0
, which is equivalent to

(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell,\ell}=c^{\prime}\bm{I}_{2\ell+1}
for some constant
c^{\prime}>0
.

Appendix B
The covariance of the angular power spectrum

The goal of this section is to prove the following result about the covariance of the angular power spectrum.

Proposition 1
.

At a given frequency bin
{f_{b}}
, and time segment
s
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{A}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell},\hat{A}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell^{\prime}})

(56)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\frac{|({\Gamma^{-1}_{{f_{b}},s}})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}|^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}[a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}^{*}({\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}a_{{f_{b}},\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}]}{(1+2\ell)(1+2\ell^{\prime})}.

The proof of Proposition
1
is deferred until two preliminary lemmas have been established.

Lemma 1
.

If the random variables
U
and
V
satisfy

\begin{pmatrix}U\\
V\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}{\mu}_{U}\\
{\mu}_{V}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{11}&\sigma_{12}\\
\sigma_{12}&\sigma_{22}\end{pmatrix}\right),

then

{\rm Cov}(U^{2},V^{2})=2\sigma_{12}(2\mu_{U}\mu_{V}+\sigma_{12}).

(57)

Proof.

Notice that
{\rm Cov}(U^{2},V^{2})=\langle U^{2}V^{2}\rangle-\langle U^{2}\rangle\langle V^{2}\rangle
and

\langle U^{2}\rangle=\mu_{U}^{2}+\sigma_{11},\text{ and }\langle V^{2}\rangle=\mu_{V}^{2}+\sigma_{22}.

(58)

All that is left is to calculate
\langle U^{2}V^{2}\rangle
. The moment generating function of
(U,V)
, denoted as
M_{U,V}(a,b)
, is

\begin{split}M_{U,V}(a,b)&=\langle{e^{aU+bV}\rangle}\\
&=\exp\left(\mu_{U}a+\mu_{V}b+\frac{1}{2}a^{2}\sigma_{11}+ab\sigma_{12}+\frac{1}{2}b^{2}\sigma_{22}\right)\end{split}

and direct calculation yields

\begin{split}&\langle U^{2}V^{2}\rangle=\frac{\partial^{4}}{\partial a^{2}\partial b^{2}}M_{U,V}(0,0)\\
=&\mu_{U}^{2}\mu_{V}^{2}+\mu_{V}^{2}\sigma_{11}+4\mu_{U}\mu_{V}\sigma_{12}+2\sigma_{12}^{2}+\mu_{U}^{2}\sigma_{22}+\sigma_{11}\sigma_{22}.\end{split}

(59)

Combining Eqs. (
58
) and (
59
)
yields the result.
∎

Consider a random variable
\mathbf{Z}
that follows a multivariate complex Gaussian distribution with mean vector
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Xi}
, which is Hermitian and non-negative definite, that is,

\mathbf{Z}=[Z_{1},Z_{2},\cdots,Z_{n}]^{T}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Xi}).

The real and imaginary parts of
\mathbf{Z}
satisfy
[Cf.
Andersen et al. 1995
, for details]

\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf{Z})\\
\operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{Z})\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\mu})\\
\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\mu})\end{pmatrix},\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Xi})&-\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Xi})\\
\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Xi})&\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Xi})\end{pmatrix}\right).

(60)

Lemma 2
.

If
\mathbf{Z}=[Z_{1},Z_{2},\cdots,Z_{n}]^{T}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Xi})
, then, for any indices
i,j=1,\dots,n
,

{\rm Cov}(|{Z}_{i}|^{2},|{Z}_{j}|^{2})=|{\Xi}_{ij}|^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}(\mu_{Z_{i}}^{*}{\Xi}_{ij}\mu_{Z_{j}}),

(61)

where
\mu_{Z_{i}}
denotes the
i
-th element of
\bm{\mu}
and
{\Xi}_{ij}
denotes the
(i,j)
-th element of the covariance matrix
\bm{\Xi}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality consider
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
. Let
Z_{1}=X_{1}+iY_{1}
and
Z_{2}=X_{2}+iY_{2}
, where
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,
Y_{1}
, and
Y_{2}
are real-valued Gaussian random variables. From Eq. (
60
), if
j,k\in\{1,2\}
, then
(X_{j},Y_{k})^{T}
follows a bivariate Gaussian distribution. Let the means of
Z_{j}
,
X_{j}
, and
Y_{j}
be denoted by
\mu_{Z_{j}}
,
\mu_{X_{j}}
, and
\mu_{Y_{j}}
, respectively. Then
\mu_{Z_{j}}=\mu_{X_{j}}+i\mu_{Y_{j}}
. If
\sigma_{X_{j},Y_{k}}
represents the covariance between random variables
X_{j}
and
Y_{k}
, then, using Lemma
1
, obtain

\begin{split}&{\rm Cov}(|{Z}_{1}|^{2},|{Z}_{2}|^{2})\\
=&{\rm Cov}({X}_{1}^{2},{X}_{2}^{2})+{\rm Cov}({X}_{1}^{2},{Y}_{2}^{2})+{\rm Cov}({Y}_{1}^{2},{X}_{2}^{2})+{\rm Cov}({Y}_{1}^{2},{Y}_{2}^{2})\\
=&2\sigma_{X_{1},X_{2}}(2\mu_{X_{1}}\mu_{X_{2}}+\sigma_{X_{1},X_{2}})+2\sigma_{X_{1},Y_{2}}(2\mu_{X_{1}}\mu_{Y_{2}}+\sigma_{X_{1},Y_{2}})\\
&+2\sigma_{Y_{1},X_{2}}(2\mu_{Y_{1}}\mu_{X_{2}}+\sigma_{Y_{1},X_{2}})+2\sigma_{Y_{1},Y_{2}}(2\mu_{Y_{1}}\mu_{Y_{2}}+\sigma_{Y_{1},Y_{2}}).\end{split}

(62)

If
\bm{\Xi}^{\prime}
is the covariance matrix between
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
, that is,

\begin{split}\bm{\Xi}^{\prime}&=\begin{pmatrix}\Xi_{11}&\Xi_{12}\\
\Xi_{21}&\Xi_{22}\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

then

\quad\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Xi}^{\prime})=\begin{pmatrix}\Xi_{11}&\operatorname{Re}(\Xi_{12})\\
\operatorname{Re}(\Xi_{21})&\Xi_{22}\end{pmatrix},

and

\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Xi}^{\prime})=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{12})\\
\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{21})&0\end{pmatrix}.

From Eq. (
60
), the distribution of
(X_{1},X_{2},Y_{1},Y_{2})
is

\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}\\
X_{2}\\
Y_{1}\\
Y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{X_{1}}\\
\mu_{X_{2}}\\
\mu_{Y_{1}}\\
\mu_{Y_{2}}\\
\end{pmatrix},\bm{\Omega}\right),

(63)

where

\bm{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}\Xi_{11}&\operatorname{Re}(\Xi_{12})\\
\operatorname{Re}(\Xi_{21})&\Xi_{22}\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}0&-\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{12})\\
-\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{21})&0\end{matrix}\\
\begin{matrix}0&\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{12})\\
\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{21})&0\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}\Xi_{11}&\operatorname{Re}(\Xi_{12})\\
\operatorname{Re}(\Xi_{21})&\Xi_{22}\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix}.

(64)

Furthermore, since the covariance matrix
\bm{\Xi}
is Hermitian,

\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Xi})=\operatorname{Re}(\bm{\Xi})^{T}
,
\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Xi})=-\operatorname{Im}(\bm{\Xi})^{T}
. Thus,

\begin{split}\sigma_{X_{1},X_{2}}&=\sigma_{Y_{1},Y_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}(\Xi_{12})=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}(\Xi_{21}),\\
\sigma_{X_{1},Y_{2}}&=-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{12})=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{21}),~~~\text{and}\\
\sigma_{Y_{1},X_{2}}&=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{12})=-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{21}).\end{split}

(65)

By combining Eqs. (
62
) and (
65
) obtain

\begin{split}&{\rm Cov}(|{Z}_{1}|^{2},|{Z}_{2}|^{2})\\
=&(\operatorname{Re}(\Xi_{21}))^{2}+(\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{21}))^{2}+2(\mu_{X_{1}}\mu_{Y_{2}}-\mu_{X_{2}}\mu_{Y_{1}})\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{21})\\
&+2(\mu_{X_{1}}\mu_{X_{2}}+\mu_{Y_{1}}\mu_{Y_{2}})\operatorname{Re}(\Xi_{21})\\
=&|\Xi_{21}|^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}(\Xi_{21})\operatorname{Re}(\mu^{*}_{Z_{1}}\mu_{Z_{2}})+2\operatorname{Im}(\Xi_{21})\operatorname{Im}(\mu^{*}_{Z_{1}}\mu_{Z_{2}})\\
=&|\Xi_{12}|^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}(\mu_{Z_{1}}^{*}\Xi_{12}\mu_{Z_{2}}).\end{split}

(66)

The only remaining case to consider is when the indices coincide. Specifically,

{\rm Cov}(|{Z}_{1}|^{2},|{Z}_{1}|^{2})={\rm Var}(|{Z}_{1}|^{2})={\rm Var}(X_{1}^{2})+{\rm Var}(Y_{1}^{2}).

The last equality follows since, by Eqs. (
63
) and (
64
), the random variables
X_{1}
and
Y_{1}
are independent. Also, note that from Eq. (
64
),
{\rm Var}(X_{1})={\rm Var}(Y_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\Xi_{11}
. Using standard results about the moments of Gaussian distributions, direct calculation yields

\begin{split}{\rm Var}(X_{1}^{2})=&\left\langle X_{1}^{4}\right\rangle-\left\langle X_{1}^{2}\right\rangle^{2}\\
=&\frac{3}{4}\Xi_{11}^{2}+3\mu_{X_{1}}^{2}\Xi_{11}+\mu_{X_{1}}^{4}-\left(\frac{1}{2}\Xi_{11}+\mu_{X_{1}}^{2}\right)^{2}\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\Xi_{11}^{2}+2\mu^{2}_{X_{1}}\Xi_{11}\end{split}

and, similarly,

{\rm Var}(Y_{1}^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\Xi_{11}^{2}+2\mu^{2}_{Y_{1}}\Xi_{11}.

It follows that

\begin{split}{\rm Var}(|Z_{1}|^{2})=&{\Xi}_{11}^{2}+2|\mu_{Z_{1}}|^{2}{\Xi}_{11}\\
=&\Xi_{11}^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}(\mu_{Z_{1}}^{*}\Xi_{11}\mu_{Z_{1}}).\end{split}

(67)

∎

We are now in position to prove Proposition
1
.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Since

\{\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\{{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}},\bm{\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1}),

where the index set
\mathcal{I}=\{(\ell,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2};1\leq\ell\leq\ell_{max},-\ell\leq m\leq\ell\}
, and
\bm{\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1}=\{(\Gamma_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\}_{(\ell,m),(\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}
, applying Lemma
2
yields

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Cov}(|\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell m}|^{2},|\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}|^{2})

\displaystyle=|({\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}|^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}[a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}^{*}({\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}a_{{f_{b}},\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}].

Recalling the definition of
\hat{A}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell}
in Eq. (
6
) obtain

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Cov}(\hat{A}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell},\hat{A}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+2\ell)(1+2\ell^{\prime})}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}{\rm Cov}(|\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell m}|^{2},|\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}|^{2}),

and Eq. (
56
) follows.
∎

Appendix C
Properties of the plug-in estimator of the covariance of angular power spectrum based on Eq. (
7
)

Recall that at a given frequency band
{f_{b}}
, the unknown covariance function between
\hat{A}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell}
and
\hat{A}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell^{\prime}}
is

(\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s})_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}={\rm Cov}(\hat{A}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell},\hat{A}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell^{\prime}}).

Assume that
\{\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},s,\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}},\,s\geq 1
, is a sequence of independent complex Gaussian random variables, with distribution
\mathcal{CN}(\{{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}\}_{\mathcal{I}},\bm{\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1})
, where
\bm{\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}
and
\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}_{{f_{b}},s}
are deterministic and inverses of each other. The index set
\mathcal{I}
is defined similarly as in Appendix
A
.

As described in Section
III.1
, the elements of the plug-in estimator of
(\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s})_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
are given by

\begin{split}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\frac{|({\Gamma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}|^{2}+2\,\operatorname{Re}\left[\hat{a}^{*}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}(\Gamma_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\right]}{(1+2\ell)(1+2\ell^{\prime})}.\end{split}

Recall from Eq.
(
16
),
\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
depends on
S
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of random variables defined on the same probability space, and let
X
be another random variable on this space. Say that
X_{n}
converges in probability to
X
as
n\to\infty
, denoted

X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}X.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{op}
denote the operator norm, which is equal to the largest singular value of the matrix.

Theorem 3
.

The following statements hold.

1.

Both
\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}
and
\hat{\Sigma}_{{f_{b}},s}
are positive definite matrices.

2.

If there exists
\zeta>0
such that, for all
s\geq 1
and
{f_{b}}
,

\|\Gamma^{-1}_{{f_{b}},s}\|_{op}\leq\zeta,

(68)

then
\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
, as
S\to\infty
.

3.

If
\hat{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}{a}_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
, then
\hat{\Sigma}_{{f_{b}},s}
is a consistent estimator of
\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}
, for any
s
.

4.

If condition (
68
) holds, then, as
S\to\infty
,

\max_{{f_{b}}}\max_{1\leq s\leq S}\bigl\|\hat{\Sigma}_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1}-\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}^{-1}\bigr\|_{op}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}0.

Proof.

Proof of statement 1:

This proof will be the same for all frequency bins
{f_{b}}
and segments
s
considered. Therefore, for notational simplicity, let us denote
\Sigma=\Sigma_{{f_{b}},s}
,
\bm{\Gamma}=\Gamma_{{f_{b}},s}
and
a_{\ell m}=a_{{f_{b}},\ell m}
.

Let
d_{1},\cdots,d_{\ell_{\max}}
be complex numbers such that
|d_{1}|^{2}+\cdots+|d_{\ell_{\max}}|^{2}>0
. Let
\bm{d}=\{d_{\ell}/(2\ell+1)\}_{(\ell,m)\in\mathcal{I}}
. A direct calculation shows that

\begin{split}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}d^{*}_{\ell}&(\Sigma)_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}d_{\ell^{\prime}}=\sum_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\frac{d_{\ell}^{*}}{1+2\ell}({\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime},\ell m}({\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\frac{d_{\ell^{\prime}}}{1+2\ell^{\prime}}\\
&+2\sum_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\frac{d_{\ell}^{*}}{1+2\ell}\operatorname{Re}[a_{\ell m}^{*}({\Gamma}^{-1})_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}a_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}]\frac{d_{\ell^{\prime}}}{1+2\ell^{\prime}}\\
=&\operatorname{tr}\Big((\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}))^{*}\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d})\Big)\\
&+2\operatorname{Re}\left(\sum_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\frac{d_{\ell^{\prime}}}{1+2\ell^{\prime}}a_{\ell m})^{*}\big({\Gamma}^{-1}\big)_{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\big(a_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\frac{d_{\ell}}{1+2\ell}\big)\right)\\
\geq&\operatorname{tr}\Big((\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}))^{*}\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d})\Big)>0.\end{split}

(69)

Thus,
\Sigma
is positive definite. The proof for
\hat{\Sigma}_{{f_{b}},s}
is similar.

Proof of statement 2:

Recall that, as described in Section
III
,
\Gamma_{{f_{b}}}=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Gamma_{{f_{b}},s}

Appendix D
Properties of the operator in Eq. (
31
)

Consider the operator
U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
introduced by
Khmaladze 2013
and defined as

U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}=\bm{I}-\frac{\langle\bm{a}-\bm{b},\cdot\rangle}{1-\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle}(\bm{a}-\bm{b}),

(70)

where
\bm{a},\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfy

||\bm{a}||=||\bm{b}||=1,\quad\langle{\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle}\neq 1.

The operator
U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}
maps
\bm{a}
to
\bm{b}
, maps
\bm{b}
to
\bm{a}
, and keep the vectors that are orthogonal to both
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
unchanged, i.e.,

\begin{split}U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\bm{a}&=\bm{b},\ U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\bm{b}=\bm{a},\\
\text{and}\quad U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\bm{x}&=\bm{x},\quad\text{for}\quad\bm{x}\perp\bm{a},\bm{b}.\end{split}

(71)

Such an operator
U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}
is unitary – that is, it is surjective and preserves the inner product.

The surjectivity can be easily shown from Eq. (
71
). Moreover,
for any vectors
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
:

\displaystyle\langle U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\bm{x},U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\bm{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x}-\frac{\langle\bm{a}-\bm{b},\bm{x}\rangle}{1-\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle}(\bm{a}-\bm{b}),\bm{y}-\frac{\langle\bm{a}-\bm{b},\bm{y}\rangle}{1-\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle}(\bm{a}-\bm{b})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle-\frac{\langle\bm{a}-\bm{b},\bm{x}\rangle\langle\bm{a}-\bm{b},\bm{y}\rangle}{(1-\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle)^{2}}\left(||\bm{a}-\bm{b}||^{2}-2-2\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle,

therefore,
U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}
preserves the inner product. Moreover,
U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}
is a self-adjoint operator because it satisfies the condition

\displaystyle\langle U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle-\frac{\langle\bm{a}-\bm{b},\bm{x}\rangle}{1-\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle}\langle\bm{a}-\bm{b},\bm{y}\rangle

(72)

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}-\frac{\langle\bm{a}-\bm{b},\bm{y}\rangle}{1-\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle}(\bm{a}-\bm{b})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x},U_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\bm{y}\rangle.
