Mechanistic study of inspiratory training in childhood Asthma: Rationale and methods of a pediatric clinical trial
Authors: Dharini M. Bhammar, Harrison N. Jones, Tarig M. Ali-Dinar, Melissa C. Orenduff, Angel Davalos, Benjamin A. Goldstein, Jason E. Lang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Asthma is a common pediatric disease of the airways. Obesity among children increases the risk of asthma and is associated with a unique non-allergic asthma phenotype that is highly symptomatic and often unresponsive to conventional drugs. However, the mechanisms linking obesity with incident asthma, more frequent and severe symptoms, and resistance to conventional drugs remain unknown. Excess fat on the chest wall and abdomen forces children with obesity to breathe at lower lung volumes where small airway dysfunction is more likely to occur and where the normal length-tension relationship of the diaphragm is suboptimal; this leads to altered breathing mechanics and inspiratory muscle fatigue, which could lead to exertional dyspnea. While inspiratory training (IT) increases inspiratory muscle strength and endurance, improves exercise tolerance, and reduces exertional dyspnea, most research has focused on adults. The Mechanistic Study of Inspiratory Training in Childhood Asthma (MICA) study is designed with 2 objectives: (1) compare measures of small airway dysfunction and inspiratory muscle functioning between obese (n = 38) versus non-obese (n = 38) 6-17-year-olds with asthma and (2) measure small airway disease and inspiratory muscle function in response to 8 weeks of inspiratory training. We will also assess physical fitness, activity levels, and exertional dyspnea. This study will lay the foundation for dose selection and optimal target population of a larger trial. IT could represent a novel therapy to help children with asthma and obesity improve quality of life through reduced exertional symptoms and enhanced activity levels.

Introduction

Obesity in childhood asthma is associated with a non-allergic phenotype that is often highly symptomatic and poorly responsive to conventional drugs [1,2]. Pediatric obesity has repeatedly been associated with more frequent and severe asthma symptoms [1,3], greater airflow obstruction [[4], [5], [6], [7]], the need for more frequent albuterol use [1], and more frequent and severe exacerbations [8]. However, the mechanisms linking obesity with incident asthma, excess symptoms, and resistance to conventional drugs remain unknown. A consistent finding across most pediatric studies investigating the link between obesity and asthma has been an increase in the frequency of chronic asthma symptoms, specifically dyspnea [1] and asthma-related activity limitation [1,4,9,10]; it is imperative that the research community investigate the mechanisms underlying the ‘obese asthma’ phenotype to improve care.

Excessive fat tissue worsens respiratory compliance in adults, which impedes normal contraction of the diaphragm and restricts healthy chest wall expansion [4,11,12]. These disadvantages impair the inspiratory phase of respiration, weaken the inspiratory muscles and impair their endurance, and contribute to excess dyspnea with activity in healthy adults [13]. The mechanical effects of pediatric obesity on the complexities of respiratory function are generally understudied. Compared to adults, children breathe at lower lung volumes and have more compliant chest walls [14]. The excess weight of obesity produces even greater changes in lung volumes and airway function (i.e., more compliant and collapsible airways) [15,16], which could make children with obesity more susceptible to inspiratory muscle dysfunction (IMD) and dyspnea.

An under-recognized component of airflow limitation in asthma is small airway dysfunction (SAD) [17,18], which causes peripheral small airway closure/collapse [19,20]. Obesity during childhood is linked to abnormal lung growth (dysanapsis) [6,7], which in combination with breathing at lower lung volumes could promote SAD. This is important because small airway closure leading to lung hyperinflation is known to impair breathing efficiency by flattening and shortening the diaphragm [21]. Lung hyperinflation could further explain the heightened dyspnea seen in many patients with obesity and asthma [22]. Numerous studies demonstrate the sensitivity of oscillometry in detecting SAD, with X5, R5-20, and AX being most specific [23]. Plethysmography, specifically residual lung volume (RV) (individually and as a portion of total lung capacity), also offers important information on small airway closure and hyperinflation [17,20].

Pulmonary rehabilitation in its various forms has become a cornerstone for chronic lung disease treatment in adult medicine [24]. Inspiratory training (IT) increases inspiratory muscle strength and endurance and improves peak cardiopulmonary endurance [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Perhaps most significant for children with obesity and asthma, IT consistently reduces dyspnea with activity [27]. Studies examining the effects of IT in obese adults have reported improved exercise performance [[29], [30], [31], [32]] increased maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP) [[30], [31], [33]] and respiratory muscle endurance [29,32], and reduced exertional dyspnea [29,32]. Three studies have examined the effects of 3 weeks of IT in otherwise healthy adolescents with obesity [[34], [35], [36]]. These studies showed that IT combined with counseling resulted in significantly improved exercise performance [34,36], lung and chest wall volume recruitment [34], improved forced vital capacity (FVC) [34], and reduced dyspnea [34,35] and oxygen cost of breathing [35,36]. Two additional IT studies in children with asthma demonstrated improved inspiratory muscle strength and asthma control [37,38]. These findings suggest that IT has the potential to improve symptom burden among children with obesity and asthma; however, the mechanisms underlying these benefits are not fully understood. Improvements in inspiratory muscle strength are consistently reported in IT trials [39], yet only one study has demonstrated improvements in forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and FEV1/FVC following 12 weeks of IT in children with asthma [38]. SAD, an underrecognized contributor to asthma morbidity, promotes small airway closure/collapse and hyperinflation, which increases the resistive and elastic load of breathing and contributes to dyspnea [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. To date, no studies have evaluated whether IT influences SAD.

The objectives of the Mechanistic Study of Inspiratory Training in Childhood Asthma (MICA) study are to assess key respiratory mechanisms (small airway function and inspiratory muscle function) in obese versus non-obese children with asthma and to determine how each of these mechanisms is affected by 3 IT intensities. Our central hypothesis is that obesity promotes dyspnea and worse asthma control via inspiratory muscle fatigue/dysfunction and SAD, and IT will improve inspiratory muscle function and small airway measures.

Methods

Study design

The MICA study is a cross-sectional analytic study of pulmonary mechanics comparing lean and obese children with asthma followed by a randomized, masked, sham-controlled 3-arm intervention trial of IT on SAD and inspiratory muscle function. The 3 study arms will be: 1) high-dose: IT at 75% of MIP; 2) low-dose: IT at 40% of MIP; and 3) sham (control): IT at 15% of MIP. Randomized allotment will be stratified within 4 groups based on obesity status and sex. All study staff that interact with participants will be masked to group assignment. This study will enroll 76 participants. The study was reviewed and approved by the Duke University Health Systems (DUHS) Institutional Review Board (Protocol #: 00112858).

Study population

The study population will be 6-17 years of age with documented clinician-diagnosed asthma and require 2 or more controller prescriptions to treat disease symptoms (i.e., moderate to severe persistent disease). Participants can be either obese (≥95th percentile body mass index [BMI] and less than 170% of the 95th Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC] percentile) or have a normal BMI (BMI 5th-84th CDC percentile). We have chosen 170% of the 95th percentile as an upper limit for obesity because it will exclude children with an average BMI of approximately >40 kg/m2, which roughly corresponds to class III obesity in adults. Participants will be excluded if they have an asthma control test (ACT) score <17, have ever been intubated for asthma, or have a forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) <50% of predicted at enrollment. Participants will also be excluded if they are currently pregnant, have a legal guardian unable to consent in English or Spanish, or have any major chronic illness that could interfere with participation. Prior to randomization, participants are required to have an asthma provider managing their asthma; an active asthma action plan that addresses both a controller plan, a plan for increased symptoms (yellow-zone/red-zone plans), and a plan for exercise pre-treatment; no systemic steroids in the previous 6 weeks for asthma management; and no clinically significant respiratory infection in the previous 4 weeks.

Run-in period

During a run-in period (14-28 days) between visits 1 and 2, participants will complete IT training and activity tracking to assess compliance with study instructions and proper use of the PrO2™ device (respiratory training device) and physical activity monitors (Garmin Vivasmart 4 wristband and Vivalink wearable vv350 electrocardiogram [ECG] patch). If needed, participants will receive encouragement and follow-up video calls to troubleshoot and support adherence. Study staff will initiate IT training at visit 1 with the PrO2 device and accompanying smartphone application (app). The smartphone app will be downloaded to the participant's (or guardian's) smartphone. Study staff will provide login information and train the participant to open the app and breathe into the PrO2 device; data is recorded via the app. IT will be performed 3 times per week at 15% of MIP during the run-in period to demonstrate compliance and proper engagement with the study device. Participants will be instructed on the use of two physical activity trackers (wristband activity monitor [Vivasmart 4 Garmin Inc, Olathe, KS, USA] and adhesive actigraphy patch [Multi-vital ECG, vv350, Vivalink, Inc, Campbell, CA, USA]) at visit 1; wear time will be tracked to ensure adherence.

Activity monitoring

Physical activity levels will be measured daily during the run-in period and again during the final 2 weeks of the intervention period using the Garmin and Vivalink devices.

Study intervention The IT Intervention will be conducted with the PrO2 device (PrO2Health, Inc, Smithfield, RI, USA). Each participant will receive a PrO2 device (Fig. 1A) and study staff will assist in downloading the accompanying PrO2 Fit app (Fig. 1B). Study staff will train participants in the use of both the device and app and provide follow-up training sessions as needed. The PrO2 is an intuitive device that offers individualized and calibrated resistance loads, real-time biofeedback, and quantitative adherence monitoring. Device characteristics

The PrO2 is a flow-resistive IT device with a 1-cm mouthpiece that provides inspiratory resistance via a fixed 2-mm orifice. PrO2 has a pressure sensor and Bluetooth connectivity to most iOS/Android devices, which allows immediate user biofeedback (Fig. 1C). Integration of user data to a cloud-based platform allows the study team to track adherence in real time.

Participant instructions

At the start of each session, the participant's MIP will be measured using the PrO2 device. After performing the MIP, participants will be instructed to inspire forcefully until the device signals that the user has achieved the target resistance (via an audible alarm and app-voice coaching). Biofeedback signals at a specific inspiratory resistance allow for a precise and individualized training target for each participant. Successful IT repetitions will require that subjects achieve a pressure target that is 75% (higher dose), 40% (lower dose), or 15% (sham) of their MIP.

Training parameters

Training parameters were selected based on our Breathe-Fit trial in children with obesity [40]. The target IT dose will be 3 sets of 50 inspirations per session completed 3 times per week for 8 weeks. Participants will increase the dose gradually during weeks 1 and 2 to achieve and sustain the target dose during weeks 3-8. This will be done by gradually increasing the sets per session (i.e., week 1: 1 set per session; week 2: 2 sets per session; weeks 3-8: 3 sets per session). Each IT repetition will take 2-4 s with 1 complete set of 50 taking about 12 min.

Randomization and blinding

Random allocation will be 2:1, such that for every 1 participant allocated to the sham group, 2 will be allocated to low-dose and high-dose IT groups. Among active IT participants, half will be allocated to higher dose (75% MIP) and half will be allocated to lower dose (40% MIP). To provide balance among treatment groups regarding sex and obesity status, randomization will occur within 4 strata (males obese, females obese, males non-obese, females non-obese). Treatment assignment will be obtained through Duke REDCap.

Participants in the active IT and sham arms will use the same device. Although masking of allocation status may not be possible in all cases, participants will only be told that they are receiving 1 of 3 possible resistance interventions. Staff members assessing endpoints and performing statistical analyses will be blinded to treatment assignment until the study is closed and the database is locked.

Screening and baseline procedures

Research staff will collect height, weight, resting blood pressure, dyspnea and asthma-related questionnaires, and medical history. Active eczema, allergic rhino-conjunctivitis, or food allergy will be considered atopic disease.

Pulmonary function testing

Participants will complete impulse oscillometry, spirometry, and plethysmography before and after bronchodilator administration according to American Thoracic Society (ATS) standards [41,42]. Impulse oscillometry will be performed prior to spirometry.

Small airway dysfunction

Raw data and percentage of predicted values for impulse oscillometry, plethysmography, and spirometry will be recorded and based on accepted normative data. Small airway closure will be estimated as an exploratory outcome by the opening index (OI) computed as the post-bronchodilator change in FVC divided by the post-bronchodilator change in FEV1. By determining the degree of change in FEV1 attributable to changes in FVC, we can compute the degree to which airflow limitation is caused by complete small airway closure [43,44].

Inspiratory testing

Inspiratory strength will be measured as MIP using the PrO2 device by having participants inhale forcefully against a resistance from RV to TLC according to the ATS/European Respiratory Society (ERS) statement on inspiratory muscle testing [45,46]. Inspiratory endurance will be measured using sustained MIP (SMIP) [47]. SMIP is obtained from the MIP maneuver as the ‘area under the pressure-time curve’ measured in pressure time units (PTUs) (Fig. 1C). Exploratory measures include changes in MIP and SMIP following the 3-min step test.

Asthma-related questionnaires

Participants will complete the Composite Asthma Severity Index (CASI) [48], the ACT [49], and the Asthma Control Questionnaire 6 (ACQ6) [50]. The CASI is a composite numeric measure designed and validated to represent the level of asthma severity; it provides a composite clinical characterization of asthma [48]. Scoring ranges from 0 to 20 with a lower score representing better-controlled asthma. The ACT integrates frequency and type of symptoms, resulting in a score from 5 to 25 with a higher score representing fewer symptoms [5-15 = poor, 16-19 = not well controlled, 20+ = well-controlled] for children ages ≥12. The childhood ACT will be used for 6-11-year-olds. The ACQ6 results in a score from 0 to 6 with a lower score representing fewer symptoms.

Exercise capacity

Exercise capacity will be measured using a 3-min step test and a 6-min walk test (6MWT), with a minimum 15-min rest between tests. The 3-min step test begins with the participant at rest, during which baseline (time 0) measurements of heart rate and pulse oxygen saturation are recorded. The participant then performs a stepping exercise using a stable 12-inch step stool, stepping up and down at a pace of 96 beats per minute for a total of 3 min. Throughout the exercise, dyspnea (breathlessness) scores are assessed at the 1-min, 2-min, and 3-min marks to monitor perceived exertion. Immediately upon completing the 3 min of stepping, heart rate and pulse oxygen saturation are measured again (referred to as post-1). The participant then sits down to begin the recovery phase, during which heart rate and pulse oxygen saturation are recorded at 1 min (post-2) and 2 min (post-3) after exercise. A lower heart rate after exercise indicates higher exercise capacity.

The 6MWT is a validated measure of functional exercise capacity that has been shown to reliably predict absolute VO2max and overall exercise performance. In this study, the 6MWT will be used to assess participants’ perception of dyspnea during submaximal exercise. Dyspnea will be evaluated using 3 validated instruments: the Modified Borg Dyspnea Scale, the Dalhousie Dyspnea and Perceived Exertion Scales, and the Multidimensional Dyspnea Profile. Borg scores will be recorded at 2-min intervals throughout the 6MWT to monitor changes in perceived breathlessness over time. The Dalhousie scale, specifically designed for pediatric populations, uses pictorial representations to quantify breathlessness, as well as body and leg fatigue. Additionally, the Multidimensional Dyspnea Profile will be administered to capture both the sensory and affective dimensions of dyspnea, along with qualitative descriptors of the dyspnea experience following exercise.

Dyspnea scales during daily living

Participants will complete the Baseline Dyspnea Index (BDI), modified Medical Research Council (MRC) Breathlessness scale, and Nijmegen questionnaire. BDI measures the resulting functional impairment, the magnitude of task, and the magnitude of effort associated with dyspnea in the prior 2 weeks [51]. The modified MRC Breathlessness scale ranges from 0 to 5 with higher scores representing worse dyspnea. The Nijmegen questionnaire is used to screen for and quantify abnormal subjective sensations of breathing (or “functional respiratory complaints”) [52]. The score involves 16 items with the sum related to stress, respiration, and anxiety. A score >19 is considered abnormal.

Study oversight

A data and safety monitoring board (DSMB) will monitor the study. The DSMB will meet biannually with additional meetings as needed.

Statistical analysis plan

Demographic and baseline characteristics

All study participants will provide written informed consent. The numbers of participants who complete and discontinue the study early, and the reasons for discontinuation, will be summarized by treatment group. Demographic and baseline characteristics will also be summarized by treatment group. Variables include race, age, sex, and selected clinical variables recorded prior to IT.

Statistical analysis plan

We will use standard statistics to describe the distribution measurements by group and treatment. Patient disposition, demographics including sex as a biologic variable, baseline measurements, and adverse and serious adverse events (AEs) will be summarized overall and by treatment group. We will report the extent of missing data and will exclude missing data from analyses (complete case analysis) unless described otherwise below. We will examine outcome measures to assess distribution and normality assumptions, identify outliers, and check for unusual patterns of longitudinal data as appropriate. We will use transformations (including categorization) as appropriate. There will be no interim analysis. Data derivations, calculations, and analysis programs will be archived to allow future replication of our results. We will use the latest version of SAS (version 9.4 or later, SAS Institute, Inc, Cary NC) or other standard statistical software.

We will use linear regression to estimate and test the difference in mean values of baseline measures between cohorts with and without obesity. Covariates will include age, sex (male/female), race/ethnicity, and atopy status. We will report descriptive summaries and adjusted (least square) means and their standard errors for each cohort, and the estimated between-cohort difference in means with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and P-value for testing the null hypothesis of no difference between cohort means.

We will use general estimating equations (GEE) to estimate and test the difference in mean 8-week measures between treatment groups (low- and high-dose IT) versus sham. Our primary outcomes for aims 2 and 3 will be 8-week measures of MIP, SMIP, and the percent predicted values of 3 individual impulse oscillometry measures (X5, R5-20, AX) controlled for baseline values by including them as covariates in each model. Covariates will also include baseline participant characteristics: sex (male/female), obesity status (obese vs. non-obese), age (in years), and baseline controller medications (long-acting beta-agonist [LABA] vs. no LABA). We will use an exchangeable correlation structure with robust variance estimates [53]. If sample size and prevalences allow, additional covariates will include race/ethnicity and atopy status. We will report adjusted (least square) means and their standard errors for each treatment group, and the estimated between-group difference in means with a 95% CI and P-value for testing the null hypothesis of no difference between treatment group means.

Analysis of safety endpoints

All AEs recorded during the study period will be coded with Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA). All participants enrolled in the study will be evaluated for safety. Sample size calculation

Primary outcome variables were used for sample size determination with nominal power estimated separately for each aim, and no adjustment to control type 1 error (α) across aims. Enrolling 38 children with obesity and 38 without obesity will provide ≥80% power to detect a standardized difference (Cohen's d) ≥0.7 for the comparison of continuous measures of inspiratory muscle strength (i.e., MIP), continuous measures of oscillometry, and dyspnea scores in children with versus without obesity. A total of 76 children will be randomized into low-dose IT, high-dose IT, and sham groups in the trial. Assuming 10% of participants withdraw or are lost to follow-up, we anticipate this sample size will approach 82% power to detect a moderate effect size (Cohen's d) ≥0.7 for MIP, SMIP, and impulse oscillometry measures. For MIP, we estimated the standard deviation (SD) from analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) on preliminary data from the Duke Healthy Breathing Study 2 (N = 9). For R5 and X5, calculations are based on SD = 2.1 and 1.5, respectively [9]. All hypothesis tests are 2-sided with nominal α = 0.05 for determination of statistical significance.

Discussion

For many children suffering from obesity and asthma, there are few effective treatment options beyond increasing asthma medications. Often, even with step-up of treatment, children remain symptomatic, especially during exercise. SAD and IMD may be underappreciated mechanistic explanations for exertional symptoms. IT is a simple, non-pharmacologic training intervention that improves inspiratory muscle function and dyspnea with activity; this research will further our understanding of whether SAD and IMD contribute to dyspnea, activity limitation, and asthma symptoms among children with obesity and asthma. Small airways (<2 mm in diameter) make up 90% of total lung volume, but their contribution to total airway resistance, in a healthy lung, is theoretically minimal because of their large cross-sectional area [54,55]. This is why asthma was traditionally considered to be a disease of the large (or central) airways, which inhaled asthma drugs consequently targeted. Large airway obstruction is easily and consistently measured using spirometry via maximal expiratory flow volume loops. SAD, on the other hand, has been harder to measure effectively and no gold-standard measurement currently exists. Impulse oscillometry uses sound waves of different frequencies to measure airway resistance, reactance, and impedance in the large and small airways during normal breathing. The principle of the forced oscillation technique, on which impulse oscillometry is based, was first described in 1956 [56]. Commercial impulse oscillometry devices have been available for clinical and research use since the early 1990s [57]. Impulse oscillometry is increasingly being used to measure SAD. A large longitudinal observational study (ATLANTIS) in 773 adults identified resistance between 5 and 20 Hz (R5-20), reactance area (AX), and reactance at 5 Hz (X5) from impulse oscillometry as markers predictive of asthma control and exacerbations [58]. The study authors calculated percent of predicted values for R5-20, AX, and X5; classified patients into tertiles (score of 1, 2, or 3); and summed the tertile score to create a composite score of SAD ranging from 3 to 9. While no similar scoring system is published for children, this present study will select the same 3 variables and calculate the average of the percent of predicted values for R5-20, AX, and X5 as a composite score of SAD.

One of the challenges associated with the use of impulse oscillometry in pediatric populations is the limited availability of normative reference data, particularly for children with obesity. The Jaeger impulse oscillometry software includes unpublished reference equations developed by Berdel and Lechtenberger for children aged 5 to 17 years. These equations provide predicted values for most impulse oscillometry parameters based on age, height, weight, and sex. However, these equations do not include predicted values for AX. Frei et al. [59] derived equations for AX from a cohort of 222 children aged 3 to 10 years and 100-150 cm in height. This equation is based solely on height and does not differentiate by sex, leaving a gap in predicted values for AX among children aged 11 to 17 years. An additional pediatric reference set by Nowowiejska et al., based on data from 626 children aged 3.1 to 18.9 years, provides height-based prediction equations with sex-specific values for several impulse oscillometry parameters [60]. However, this dataset also lacked prediction equations for AX and excluded children with obesity. In 2015, Gochicoa-Rangel et al. published reference equations based on data from 283 healthy children ages 2.7-15.4 years based on age, height, and sex [61]. The BMI distribution in this cohort was narrow (z score interquartile range of −0.2 – 1.3), suggesting that children with obesity were underrepresented, although this was not explicitly stated [61,62]. In 2023, the authors pooled data from their studies to publish new equations from 2.7 to 90 years based on age, height, BMI, and sex [63]. Among 830 participants included in the new analysis (511 children), only 2% (n = 17) were classified as obese. Because linear growth during childhood is associated with changing absolute BMI (kg/m2) values, the use of BMI rather than weight in a pediatric prediction equations may influence the accuracy of reference estimates across developmental stages. For the present study, we have selected the Berdel and Lechtenberger equations for most impulse oscillometry parameters due to their incorporation of age, sex, height, and weight. For AX, we selected the prediction equation from Frei et al [59].

SAD may be particularly relevant for exertional symptomatology. A recent study that investigated large versus small airway dysfunction during exercise in children with asthma found that 10 of 16 children experienced small airway bronchoconstriction, while only 3 of 16 experienced both large and small airway bronchoconstriction with exercise [64]. Inhaled albuterol was effective in preventing small and large airway bronchoconstriction [64]. This study, although it had a small sample size, points to the role of small airways in provoking symptoms such as exertional dyspnea. SAD may contribute to ventilation heterogeneity, which can be assessed using techniques such as multiple breath nitrogen washout and ventilation-perfusion imaging. Future studies incorporating these methods could provide additional insight into SAD among obese asthmatic children.

IT is expected to increase the strength and endurance of inspiratory muscles, particularly the diaphragm. Children with obesity and asthma experience 2 concurrent issues – the airway narrowing from asthma and low lung volume breathing (i.e., reduced functional residual capacity and increased airway closure) from the mechanical effect of obesity. When ventilatory demand increases during exercise, the small and large airway resistance from asthma limits the maximum expiratory flow rate causing expiratory flow limitation. Premature curtailment of expiration leads to gas trapping, which is also known as lung hyperinflation. Lung hyperinflation flattens and shortens the diaphragm making it difficult to generate the force needed for inspiration, while also increasing the elastic work of breathing [65,66]. Strengthening the inspiratory muscles can help children meet ventilatory demand during exercise by reducing the amount of airway closure through reduction in inspiratory muscle fatigue. This will be particularly salient for children with asthma and obesity who have limited effective treatment options. The MICA study will be the first and most comprehensive study on the mechanistic effects of IT and SAD. While the optimal dose of IT with respect to intensity, volume, and duration of training are unclear, that there is reasonable evidence that a wide range of inspiratory intensities (i.e. 40-80% of MIP) are likely to be effective, with session frequencies of at least 2 days per week [39]. This study will provide the foundation for determining dose selection and optimal target population of a larger trial in children with asthma. IT could represent a novel therapy to help children with asthma and obesity improve quality of life through reduced exertional symptoms and enhanced activity levels.
