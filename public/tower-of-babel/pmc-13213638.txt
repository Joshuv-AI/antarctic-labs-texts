Nitrous oxide–related neurotoxicity: Clinical features, key investigations, and a proposed diagnostic approach from a single-center cohort
Authors: Si Tri Le, Khang Vinh Nguyen, Len Thi Duong, Hung Hoang Dang, Tinh Quang Dang, Thang Ba Nguyen, Umapathi Thirugnanam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecreational use of nitrous oxide (N2O) is a growing public health concern. Its neurological complications include peripheral neuropathy and posterior column myelopathy. Our study aims to characterize the clinical, laboratory, and radiological features of N2O neurotoxicity, and to explore the clinical relevance of various commonly employed investigations in real-world setting.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of 52 consecutive patients diagnosed with N2O-related neurological disorders admitted between 2019 and 2024. Clinical characteristics, serum markers, spinal MRI, and electrodiagnostic (EDX) data were collected. Analyses were primarily descriptive. Exploratory unadjusted associations between MRI findings, EDX classifications, homocysteine status, and symptom-onset variables were assessed using appropriate statistical tests.ResultsThe median age was 24.0 [21.8;28.0] years, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.7:1. Acute onset (< 4 weeks) was observed in 61.5% of cases. Sensory symptoms occurred in 94.2% and gait ataxia in 86.5%, with 88.5% exhibited a pattern of distal weakness exceeding proximal. Elevated homocysteine was present in 43 (89.6%), whereas low total B12 was found in only 10.4%. MRI abnormalities were detected in 31/48 (64.6%), with the “inverted V” sign in 17 cases (54.8%). Reduced CMAPS with intact sensory potentials was the most common EDX pattern 17 (32.7%), and needle electromyography showed ongoing denervation in 66%. Exploratory association analyses did not identify any significant relationships between EDX classifications, MRI findings, symptom onset, or homocysteine levels.ConclusionNitrous oxide neurotoxicity is characterized by predominant sensory symptoms, gait ataxia and elevated homocysteine. EDX patterns are heterogenous and non-specific. A combined approach using spinal MRI, serum homocysteine and EDX could play a key role in differentiating this pathology from other mimickers and achieving a timely and accurate diagnosis.

Introduction

Nitrous oxide has been used in anesthesia since the 19th century and its side effects have been well documented. However, increasing recreational use of nitrous oxide with reports of associated neurotoxicity, especially among young and working-age population, has become an emerging public health problem that requires early diagnosis and intervention [1], [2]. Nitrous oxide rapidly oxidizes the Co(I) form of vitamin B12 to Co(III), inactivating its coenzyme function in methylmalonic acid and homocysteine metabolism—two pathways essential for maintaining myelin integrity. The clinical manifestations of nitrous oxide neurotoxicity are heterogenous due to the involvement of both central and peripheral myelination pathways [3]. In many cases, its presentations can closely mimic other neurological conditions such as Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP), or even acute myelitis, whose management can be time-sensitive and requires resource-intensive therapies like plasma epheresis or intravenous immunoglobulines [1], [2]. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that N₂O-induced neurotoxicity may extend beyond classic demyelination on nerve conduction studies, further complicating the electrodiagnostic classification [4].

Despite increasing recognition of this condition, standardized diagnostic criteria are lacking. While elevated homocysteine or methylmalonic acid may offer early etiologic clues, they remain underutilized in diagnostic workups. Imaging characteristics such as longitudinal dorsal column lesions or inverted V sign in spinal cord MRI, and the classification of peripheral nerve injury via electrodiagnostic testing play a crucial role, yet their relative importance when used alone or in combination with other modalities remains unknown, particularly in the context of multiple differential diagnosis in the neuraxis.

In this study, our aims are to characterize the clinical, laboratory, electrodiagnostic (EDX) and spinal cord MRI features of nitrous oxide-related neurotoxicity in a tertiary neurology center, where ancillary tests are available for all admitted patients. The overlap of clinical features with other diagnoses may confound early recognition and could potentially lead to inappropriate management. We sought to highlight the contribution of homocysteine level, MRI abnormalities, and electrodiagnostic features to distinguish from mimics such as GBS or inflammatory myelopathy in a timely manner. The findings would serve as a basis to construct a structured diagnostic approach to this increasingly prevalent condition.

Method

Study design and setting

A retrospective observational study was conducted at the Department of Neurology, University Medical Center, Ho Chi Minh City, from January 2019 to March 2024. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City (Approval No. 457/2025/HĐ-ĐHYD) on September 30th, 2025. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, informed consents were waived by the ethics committee. No identifiable personal information was disclosed. Patients were included if they had: (1) documented history of nitrous oxide exposure preceding symptom onset (2) neurological deficits consistent with myelopathy, peripheral neuropathy, or both, and (3) availability of at least one of the following paraclinical investigations: serum vitamin B₁₂, serum homocysteine, spinal cord MRI, or electrodiagnostic studies. During follow-up, patients with alternative diagnoses (e.g., autoimmune myelitis, Guillain–Barré syndrome, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy), hereditary neuropathies, other metabolic myelopathies, or incomplete medical records were excluded.

Clinical, serum markers, MRI, and EDX evaluation

Age, sex, and relevant clinical history (history of N₂O exposure and symptom onset, functional status) were collected. Neurological examination included sensory assessment, motor strength, deep tendon reflexes, gait ataxia, and pyramidal signs. Functional disability at admission was scored using the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) [5]. Patients were grouped by disease phases: acute (<4 weeks), subacute (4–8 weeks), or chronic (>8 weeks) from symptom onset.

Serum vitamin B₁₂ levels were measured using chemiluminescence immunoassays (reference range 138–625 pmol/L). Homocysteine levels were determined using standard enzymatic assays (reference range 5–15 μmol/L) with levels above 15 μmol/L deemed elevated. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, aimed at excluding inflammatory neuropathies and myelopathies, were performed as clinically required. We did not record the quantity or frequency of N₂O use among these patients because it is difficult to reliably determine the dose from various N₂O-containing apparatus, which differs greatly in sizes and forms.

Spinal MRI imaging included sagittal T1- and T2-weighted sequences of the entire spinal cord and axial sequences of the abnormal levels. Lesions were characterized by its cross-sectional topography (posterior column, anterior horn, or both), longitudinal extent (vertebral segments), and specific imaging features such as the “inverted V” sign. MRI interpretation was reviewed in collaboration with neuroradiologists, with particular attention to axial T2-weighted images when assessing the topography of spinal cord signal abnormalities.

All patients underwent nerve conduction studies (NCS) and electromyography. Parameters assessed included distal latencies, conduction velocities, CMAP (compound muscle action potential), and SNAP (sensory nerve action potential). Motor and sensory response status were based on CMAP and SNAP recordings, and classified as “reduced” when amplitudes were below the lower limits of the local reference range, and “absent” when no recordable response was present. Needle electromyography evaluated spontaneous activity (e.g., fibrillations, positive sharp waves) and motor unit recruitment patterns. EDX patterns were classified according to the scheme by Van Asseldonk et al. which was originally developed for chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy. The criteria was shown to be better at distinguishing demyelinating from axonal patterns than previous classifications [6]. We included the following 5 subtypes: motor axonal neuropathy (or motor neuron injury), motor–sensory axonal neuropathy, motor-sensory mixed axonal and demyelinating neuropathy, motor–sensory demyelinating neuropathy, pure motor demyelinating neuropathy, and normal findings [6].

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Statistical analyses were descriptive. As for exploratory secondary analyses, unadjusted associations between MRI involvement patterns and electrodiagnostic classification, homocysteine category, and onset variables were assessed using chi-square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Multivariable modeling was not undertaken because the limited sample size and sparse subgroup counts would have made the estimates unstable. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio version 2025.9.1.401.

Results

Fifty-two consecutive patients were recruited. The mean age was 24.0 [21.8;28.0] years, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.7:1 (33 females and 19 males). Most patients were admitted with acute symptoms (32 cases), while 5 and 15 cases presented with subacute and chronic symptoms, respectively. During history-taking, all patients reported themselves having using nitrous oxide as a form of recreational substance.

The recorded symptoms were as follows: 94.2% of patients experienced limb paresthesia, 86.5% had sensory ataxia, 65% had absent deep tendon reflexes, 4% exhibited upper motor neuron signs (Babinski's sign), and 15,3% had dermatomal sensory disturbances. No patient presented with facial weakness or any other cranial neuropathy or respiratory distress (Fig. 1).

Muscle strength according to the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale.

A total of 46 out of 52 patients (88.5%) showed a distal more than proximal weakness, consistent with a length-dependent neuropathy. The same finding was also documented in electromyography, where motor responses were significantly more diminished in the lower limbs compared to the upper limbs. Most patients experienced significant difficulties in ambulation due to the combination of ataxia and lower limb weakness. The distribution of Modified Rankin Scale scores ranging from 1 to 5 was 20, 24, 25, 25, and 6%, respectively. No cognitive complaint was documented.

Electrodiagnosis results were available in all patients. Motor axonal loss with normal SNAP was the most common EDX subtype, (n = 17; 32.7%), with a mean sensory sural nerve response of 10.9 μV. Motor and sensory axonal loss polyneuropathy was present in 14 cases (26.9%), mixed demyelinating and axonal loss polyneuropathy in 9 cases (17.3%), sensory and motor demyelinating polyneuropathy in 2 cases (3.8%), motor demyelinating polyneuropathy in 5 cases (9.6%) and normal findings in 5 cases (9.6%). In the lower limbs, reduced CMAP amplitudes were observed in the tibial and deep peroneal nerves in 28 and 27 patients, respectively, with absent motor responses in 4 and 8 cases (Chart 1). In the upper limbs, CMAP amplitude reduction was less frequent, affecting the median and ulnar nerves in 1 and 5 patients, respectively, and no absent responses were recorded (Chart 1). Sensory studies showed reduced or absent sural SNAPs in 11 patients, while reduced SNAP amplitudes in the median and ulnar nerves were found in 7 and 8 patients, respectively, without complete sensory response loss (Chart 2). Conduction block was documented in 5 cases with the electrodiagnostic classification of sensory and motor demyelinating neuropathy. Needle electromyography showed ongoing denervation in 66% of cases.

Motor conduction studies showing a length-dependent neuropathy affecting predominantly the lower limbs.

Sensory conduction studies showing a length-dependent neuropathy with a more severe deficit in the lower limbs.

Abnormally low B12 levels were found in only 10.2% of patients. In contrast, homocysteine levels were elevated in 90% of cases. Notably, 50% of cases (26 patients) had homocysteine levels exceeding the upper quantification limit (>60 μmol/L).

Lumbar puncture was performed in all patients. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was normal in 50 out of 52 patients, with 2 cases showing very mild elevation of protein (45 mg/dL and 55 mg/dL). All patients underwent LP at least 7 days after onset.

A total of 48 out of 52 patients underwent standard spinal MRI protocol. Spinal cord lesions were present in 31 out of 48 cases (64.6%). The length of hyperintense T2 lesions in the spinal cord spanned from 2 to 12 segments (median was 6.0 [5.0; 8.75]). Cervical segments C2 to C6 were most commonly affected. Inverted V sign, a specific radiological finding of B12 deficiency, was found in 17/31 (54.8%). Whole posterior cord involvement was observed in 7 cases (22.6%), transverse myelitis-like appearance in 3 cases (9.7%), and non-specific findings in 2 cases.

Exploratory analysis of the extent of spinal lesions, involvement of posterior columns, anterior horn, and full spinal lesions, level of homocystein, EDX classifications, and onset weeks found no significant correlation. (Supplement tables 1, 2 and 3)

Discussion

This study represents the largest single-center cohort of nitrous oxide–related neurotoxicity in Vietnam to date and highlights the diagnostic value of integrating clinical evaluation with laboratory, neuroimaging, and electrodiagnostic findings. Comprehensive ancillary data were available for nearly all patients, with examinations and electrodiagnostic explorations performed by neurologists of a tertiary teaching hospital.

The patient profile in our cohort is compatible with previous reports, with young adults presenting predominantly with sensory symptoms, gait ataxia, and lower limb weakness [2], [7]. While several previous studies have reported various axonal and demyelinating patterns as the predominant electrodiagnostic phenotype [8], [9], our study identified isolated motor axonal loss with intact SNAP as the most frequent abnormality. In the presence of predominant sensory symptoms, such EDX pattern implies an underlying concomitant central origin (i.e spinal cord). Axonal neuropathy and motor axonal injury in the lower limbs are also the most common abnormalities in a large series described by Fang X et al. This result is consistent with our findings that axonal injury is the most prevalent EDX finding, although the different classification criteria makes subtype comparison difficult [8].

MRI abnormalities in N₂O-related neurotoxicity have been predominantly reported through case reports or small case series [10], [11]. T2 or FLAIR hyperintense lesions in the cervical spinal cord can be subtle and easily overlooked. Indeed, among the 17 patients in our study who presented with the most common EDX pattern of motor axonal loss with preserved SNAPs, only 9 (52.9%) had abnormal spinal MRI, whereas 8 (47.1%) had no detectable spinal cord abnormality on MRI despite the predominant sensory symptoms. The most frequently observed topography involves the posterior column. The “inverted V” sign (Fig. 2) in the posterior column appears to be a more characteristic imaging marker of vitamin B12 deficiency-related spinal cord injury [7].

“Inverted V” sign in the posterior column.

While posterior column involvement is the most common MRI finding, signal abnormalities involving the anterior horns may also be present but much less noticeable than the “inverted V” sign. In our cohort, after multidisciplinary discussion with neuroradiologists, signal abnormalities suggestive of anterior horn region involvement were noted in 24 out of 31 cases with spinal cord MRI abnormalities (Fig. 3). These findings were often subtle and should be interpreted with caution, given the limited spatial resolution of MRI in assessing the small caliber of the spinal cord. Nevertheless, these findings may account for the frequent occurrence of isolated motor abnormalities with normal SNAP found on electrodiagnostic studies in our cohort. In the absence of corroborating clinical history and complementary investigations, such electrophysiological pattern may be misleading or difficult to interpret. Indeed, the combination of length-dependent motor axonal polyneuropathy with preserved sensory conduction on EDX in the presence of predominant sensory symptoms is hardly seen in any other peripheral or medullary pathology. Given the appropriate clinical context, this pattern may be highly suggestive of nitrous oxide–related neuropathy, although future studies are required to confirm this observation in a prospective cohort.

Proposed diagnostic approach for nitrous oxide myelo-neuropathy

Core features:

- History of nitrous oxide exposure prior to symptom onset.

- Acute or subacute onset of mainly sensory disturbances affecting predominantly the lower limbs in a length-dependent manner.

- At least one of the 2 following findings:

1. MRI abnormalities: typically inverted V sign involving the posterior column or non-specific non-enhanced hyperintense T2 lesions of various lengths affecting the cervical or upper thoracic segments.

2. EDX abnormalities: symmetric but heterogeneous patterns (motor axonal polyneuropathy with normal sensory conduction, motor and sensory axonal loss polyneuropathy, mixed demyelinating and axonal loss polyneuropathy, sensory and motor demyelinating polyneuropathy, motor demyelinating polyneuropathy)

Supportive features:

- Elevated homocysteine levels or diminished B12 levels in serum.

Excluding or red flag features:

- Asymmetry in clinical presentations including onset and progression.*

- Asymmetry in EDX findings.*

- Cranial neuropathy.

- Respiratory distress.

- Abnormal protein or cell counts in CSF findings.

- Presence of a different myelopathy on MRI (tumoral, compressive…) that can better explain the clinical findings.

*Unless the aforementioned clinical or EDX asymmetry can be explained by another concomitant pathology.

In our cohort, elevated homocysteine was observed in 90% of patients, making it the most prevalent abnormality. This result closely aligns with previous report [6]. Among the five patients with normal homocysteine, two had spinal MRI abnormalities compatible with N₂O-related myelopathy, while only three patients had both normal homocysteine and normal spinal MRI, all of whom reported prior self-supplementation with vitamin B₁₂. In contrast, total serum B₁₂ were abnormal in only 10% of cases, confirming the limited diagnostic utility of B₁₂ measurement alone. Normal serum B₁₂ levels in the presence of elevated homocysteine imply that the disorder is driven by functional inactivation of vitamin B₁₂ rather than true deficiency.

Our findings strongly support a multimodal diagnostic approach in patients with a compatible history of N₂O exposure. Reliance on a single diagnostic modality risks underdiagnosis or missing important mimicks, particularly since one modality only reflect a certain aspect of this complex pathology affecting both the peripheral and central nervous system. All patients exhibited abnormalities in at least one paraclinical modality — spinal MRI or electrodiagnostic studies — regardless of disease onsets. While electrodiagnostic testing reliably reflected peripheral nervous system involvement, the patterns were heterogeneous and non-specific, underscoring the limitation of relying on EDX findings in isolation. On the other hand, prominent posterior column involvement on spinal MRI and elevated homocysteine levels also appear to be the most specific confirmatory features, particularly when associated with sensory ataxia and a length-dependent neuropathy pattern.

Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was normal in the vast majority of patients when performed after 7 days of onset. This may assist in differentiating this condition from Guillain–Barré syndrome or inflammatory myelopathy in the acute phase which typically presents with albuminocytologic dissociation or elevated CSF protein beyond the first week. When clinical features and other paraclinical investigations are concordant, lumbar punctures are non-contributory.

The proposed diagnostic framework could serve as a hypothesis-driven criteria set that warrants further validation studies. In theory, the utility of our approach can be measured against GBS and acute myelitis, which are the two most important differential diagnoses. Diagnosis should primarily be driven by clinical suspicion and supported by complementary investigations. The core features consist of typical clinical history and examination, and at least one abnormal result on either spinal MRI or EDX. Abnormal spinal lesions help excluding the possibility of a rapidly progressive GBS that requires urgent treatment and monitoring. Likewise, the presence of a length-depedent symmetric neuropathy on nerve conduction study could categorically rule out typical demyelinating myelopathy. In both cases, the presence of an elevated homocystein is a red flag. Excluding features are those completely or nearly absent in our cohort and includes the following: asymmetry in clinical or EDX findings, cranial neuropathy, respiratory distress, all of which were absent in our cohort regardless of the disease onset. The presence of one of these features makes the diagnosis of nitrous oxide neurotoxicity very unlikely.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design limited accurate quantification of N₂O exposure, as patients were often hesitant or unable to estimate dosage reliably. In real-life settings, various containers of different sizes and forms (e.g., canisters, sprays, balloons, etc.) are available. Several earlier studies indicate that sensory symptoms only appear after prolonged exposure, typically months to years [12]. Furthermore, exposure history relied primarily on patient self-report, which may introduce recall or reporting bias. Not all patients underwent methylmalonic acid testing, which may have provided complementary information. Long-term outcomes following cessation of N₂O exposure and vitamin B₁₂ supplementation were not systematically documented due to high rate of loss to follow-up. This could be explained by the rapid improvement of symptoms as well as the stigma associated with addiction, particularly in Asian societies. Lastly, our cohort consisted only of patients with significant subjective symptoms that resulted in functional impairment and compelled patients to seek medical attention. The possibility of milder forms of N2O-induced neuropathy or myelopathy that do not manifest itself on either MRI or EDX cannot be excluded. The application of our diagnostic features could be less accurate in such settings and further studies are needed to clarify this issue. A potential incorporation bias should also be acknowledged, as homocysteine levels, spinal MRI, and electrodiagnostic testing were part of the clinical diagnostic workup used to identify these patients.

Conclusions

Our study reinforces the fact that nitrous oxide neurotoxicity is a multisystem neurological disorder, from dorsal column myelopathy to peripheral neuropathy. While electrodiagnostic studies allow the identification and classification of neuropathy, the patterns vary significantly. Early recognition and diagnosis should be achieved through a combination of clinical suspicion, homocysteine testing, EDX and spinal MRI, thereby enabling proper management in the acute phase.
