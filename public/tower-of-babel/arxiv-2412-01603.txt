Abstract
Weak-identification-robust tests for instrumental variable (IV) regressions are typically developed separately depending on whether the number of IVs is treated as fixed or increasing with the sample size, forcing researchers to make a stance on the asymptotic behavior, which is often ambiguous in practice. This paper proposes a bootstrap-based, dimension-agnostic Anderson-Rubin (AR) test that achieves correct asymptotic size regardless of whether the number of IVs is fixed or diverging, and even accommodates cases where the number of IVs exceeds the sample size. By incorporating ridge regularization, our approach reduces the effective rank of the projection matrix and yields regimes where the limiting distribution of the AR statistic can be a weighted chi-squared, a normal, or a mixture of the two. Strong approximation results ensure that the bootstrap procedure remains uniformly valid across all regimes, while also delivering substantial power gains over existing methods by exploiting rank reduction.

Anderson-Rubin Test, Weak Identification, Bootstrap, High Dimension, Quadratic Form, Ridge Regularization

Motivated by this issue, we propose a bootstrap-based, dimension-agnostic AR test. First, by deriving strong approximations for the proposed test statistic and its bootstrap counterpart (under both the null and alternative hypotheses), we show that the new bootstrap test has a correct asymptotic size, regardless of whether the number of IVs
K
is fixed or diverging. Our proof, which relies on the Lindeberg swapping strategy, contributes a general result on the strong approximation for quadratic forms with independent and heteroskedastic errors. Additionally, our (conditional) strong approximation derivation for bootstrap statistics involving quadratic forms is novel and may be of independent interest. Second, by employing a ridge-regularized projection matrix, our AR test remains valid in high-dimensional cases where
K
exceeds the sample size
n
.
Third, the characterization of the errors in strong approximation offers a theoretically sound basis for selecting the ridge regularizer without taking a stance on the specific regime of
K
. Our choice of the regularizer also helps to reduce the effective rank of the projection matrix, which can potentially improve the power performance of the test. Fourth, we show that depending on the asymptotic behavior of both
K
and
R_{\lambda}
(the effective rank of the regularized projection matrix), the limit distribution of the test statistic can be (1) normal, (2) weighted chi-squared, or (3) a mixture of weighted chi-squared and normal distributions. Given the strong approximation result, our bootstrap inference remains uniformly valid regardless of the asymptotic regimes, and we further provide its power properties under each scenario. Fifth, the strong approximation and uniform inference results are all established when the number of control variables is allowed to diverge at the same rate or even faster than
\sqrt{n}
. Sixth, simulation experiments and an empirical application to the dataset of
Card (2009)
confirm the excellent size and power properties of our bootstrap test compared to alternative methods.

Furthermore, in their seminal paper,

Our test also remains valid even when the number of control variables diverges at a rate of

or faster, provided it remains of a smaller order than

is fixed or diverging. As pointed out by

Lastly,
Anatolyev and
Sølvsten (2023)
proposed an analytical dimension-agnostic
F
test for linear regressions by analyzing the asymptotic behavior of quadratic forms under two distinct regimes: (1) a fixed number of restrictions, resulting in a weighted chi-squared limiting distribution, and (2) a growing number of restrictions, yielding a normal limiting distribution. Their
F
-test is, in principle, applicable to our setting by testing zero restrictions on the IV coefficients in a linear regression under the null, and it is more general in two respects: (1) it accommodates control variables whose dimension can be of the same order as the sample size
n
, and (2) it allows for testing general linear restrictions. However, our bootstrap inference offers several key advantages. First, although we require the number of controls to be of a smaller order than
n
, we allow the number of instruments
K
to exceed
n
, a case not covered by their framework. Second, our use of ridge regularization reduces the effective rank of the projection matrix and gives rise to a third asymptotic regime, where
K
diverges but a Lindeberg-type condition for asymptotic normality fails, resulting in a limiting distribution that is a mixture of weighted chi-squared and normal variables, akin to the regime analyzed in
Kline
et al. (2020, Sections 6 and 7)
and
Yang et al. (2024)
. This regime does not arise in
Anatolyev and
Sølvsten (2023)
due to the absence of regularization. Analytical inference in this setting requires knowledge of the number of dominant eigenvalues, which can be a challenging task. In contrast, our bootstrap approach circumvents such difficulty by directly employing the strong approximation and remains uniformly valid across all three regimes. In our simulations, when the number of instruments is proportional to the sample size, the use of ridge regularization places the test statistic in the third asymptotic regime. Our bootstrap inference procedure has excellent size control even in this challenging setting, and further provides substantial power gains compared to alternative methods because of the rank reduction.

2.1
Setup

Consider the linear instrumental variable regression

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{i}=\widetilde{X}_{i}\beta+W_{i}^{\top}\Gamma+\widetilde{e}_{i}

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{i}=\widetilde{\Pi}_{i}+\widetilde{v}_{i},

(2.1)

where

2.2
Test Statistic

Given that we allow
K_{n}
to be greater than

, the matrix
Z^{\top}Z
is not necessarily invertible. Therefore, we define
P_{\lambda}=Z(Z^{\top}Z+\lambda I_{K_{n}})^{-1}Z^{\top}
as the ridge-regularized projection matrix of

\lambda
that will be chosen based on

and

only. As we treat the instruments and control variables as non-random or conditioned upon, so are the ridge-regularizer
\lambda
and matrix
P_{\lambda}
. We also allow
\lambda
to depend on the sample size
n
. For notational simplicity, we suppress this dependence throughout. The
(i,j)
element of
P_{\lambda}
is denoted as

. Further denote

. Then, our dimension-agnostic AR test statistic is written as

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}e_{i}(\beta_{0})P_{\lambda,ij}e_{j}(\beta_{0})}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}-\frac{\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2}}\kappa_{ij}e_{j}^{2}(\beta_{0})A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}},

(2.3)

where
\kappa=(M_{W}\circ M_{W})^{-1}
,
6
6

6

Here

.

A_{\lambda,ii}=2P_{\lambda,ii}P_{W,ii}-B_{\lambda,ii}
,

B_{\lambda,jk}=\sum_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ik}P_{W,ij}P_{\lambda,ii}=[P_{W}D_{\lambda}P_{W}]_{jk}
,

\displaystyle D_{\lambda}=\text{diag}(P_{\lambda,11},\cdots,P_{\lambda,nn})=\text{diag}(P_{\lambda}),

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}=\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Xi_{\lambda,ij}^{2},

(2.4)

and

\displaystyle\Xi_{\lambda,ij}=\begin{cases}P_{\lambda,ij}+(P_{\lambda,ii}+P_{\lambda,jj})P_{W,ij}-B_{\lambda,ij}&\quad i\neq j\\
0&\quad i=j\end{cases},

where we adopt the convention for the diagonal operator

is chosen as

\displaystyle\lambda=\max\left\{\theta\in[0,\overline{\theta}]:\left(\frac{\max_{i\in[n]}P_{\theta,ii}^{2}}{R_{\theta}}\right)\left(1+\sum_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}^{2}\right)\leq c_{1},\frac{\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Xi_{\theta,ij}^{2}}{R_{\theta}}\leq\frac{c_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right\},

where

,

Remark 2.1
.

Several remarks regarding the choice of the regularizer are in order.
First, we select the regularizer
\lambda
as the largest value over the interval
[0,\overline{\theta}]
that both

\displaystyle\frac{\max_{i\in[n]}P_{\lambda,ii}^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}\left(1+\sum_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}^{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Xi_{\lambda,ij}^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

remain small. These are two critical conditions for ensuring the validity of our strong approximation results for both the test statistic and the bootstrap critical value. We will discuss the theoretical properties of these two terms in detail below. Moreover, since the choice of
\lambda
depends solely on the instruments and controls, which are treated as non-random or conditioned upon, it does not introduce any model selection bias.

Second, given that the conditions for strong approximation are satisfied, we choose the regularizer
\lambda
as large as possible over
[0,\bar{\theta}]
. Such a choice is inspired by
Carrasco (2012)
,
Carrasco and
Tchuente (2015)
;
Carrasco and
Tchuente (2016a)
, and
Carrasco and
Doukali (2017)
, who showed that their proposed regularized IV estimators can be more efficient than those without regularization by employing a sufficiently large value of the regularizer relative to the overall instrument strength (concentration parameter).
8
8

8

For example, see Proposition 1 of
Carrasco (2012)
, Proposition 2 of
Carrasco and
Tchuente (2015)
, and Proposition 2 of
Carrasco and
Tchuente (2016a)
, in which regularized IV estimators are shown to achieve the semiparametric efficiency bound under homoskedastic errors, given a sufficiently large value of the regularizer relative to the concentration parameter.

Third, our choice of the upper bound for
\lambda
as
\overline{\theta}=||Z^{\top}Z||_{op}
is motivated by the fact that the ridge regularization transforms the eigenvalues of
Z^{\top}Z
. Specifically, consider the case where
K_{n}\leq n
and the singular value decomposition of
Z
as

Z=\mathcal{U}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}^{\top},
where
\mathcal{U}\in\Re^{n\times K_{n}}
with
\mathcal{U}^{\top}\mathcal{U}=I_{K_{n}}
,
S=\text{diag}(s_{1},\cdots,s_{K_{n}})
is a diagonal matrix of non-zero singular values in descending order,
\mathcal{V}\in\Re^{K_{n}\times K_{n}}
, and
\mathcal{V}^{\top}\mathcal{V}=I_{K_{n}}
. Then, the regularized projection matrix is given by

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}=\mathcal{U}\text{diag}\left(\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{s_{1}^{2}+\lambda},\cdots,\frac{s_{K_{n}}^{2}}{s_{K_{n}}^{2}+\lambda}\right)\mathcal{U}^{\top}.

If for some
k\in[K_{n}]
, the ratio
s_{k}/s_{1}
is close to zero, then choosing
\lambda
on the order of
s_{1}^{2}=||Z^{\top}Z||_{op}
will cause the
k
-th singular value of
P_{\lambda}
(i.e.,
\frac{s_{k}^{2}}{s_{k}^{2}+\lambda}
) to be close to zero. Intuitively, a large
\lambda
attenuates the contributions of directions associated with small singular values, effectively reducing the rank of
P_{\lambda}
and helping to improve the power performance of our test. We will give more details on this point in Section
5
(e.g., see Remark
5.2
).

2.3
Bootstrap Critical Value

To implement the dimension-agnostic test, we propose to use bootstrap critical values. Specifically, let
\{\eta_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
be an independent sequence of random variables with zero mean and unit variance that are generated independently from the samples.
Our bootstrap AR test statistic is denoted as
\widehat{Q}^{*}(\beta_{0})
and defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}^{*}(\beta_{0})=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\eta_{i}e_{i}(\beta_{0})\Xi_{\lambda,ij}\eta_{j}e_{j}(\beta_{0})}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}.

(2.6)

Then, the bootstrap critical value is denoted as
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\beta_{0})
and defined as the
(1-\alpha)
-th percentile of
\widehat{Q}^{*}(\beta_{0})
conditional on data, where
\alpha
is the nominal level of rejection under the null. We reject the null hypothesis of
\beta=\beta_{0}
if
\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})>\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\beta_{0}).

Remark 2.2
.

Unlike the first term of
\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})
defined in (
2.3
), we use
\Xi_{\lambda}
instead of
P_{\lambda}
to define the bootstrap AR statistic. Note that under the null,
e(\beta_{0})=M_{W}\tilde{e}
, whose elements are not independent from each other. When the dimension of controls
d_{w}
diverges at a rate
\sqrt{n}
or higher, such a cross-sectional dependence is not asymptotically negligible. However, the bootstrap multipliers
\{\eta_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
are independent and, thus, unable to mimic the dependence. Instead, we explicitly account for this difference by adjusting the middle matrix
P_{\lambda}
in the original statistic to
\Xi_{\lambda}
, so that valid bootstrap inference can be achieved under many controls. Additionally, we impose the null on the bootstrap data-generating process, following the recommendations in the literature of bootstrap for IV regressions or non-homoskedastic errors, such as
Cameron
et al. (2008)
,

Davidson and
MacKinnon (2010)
,
Roodman
et al. (2019)
, and
MacKinnon
et al. (2023)
, among others.

Remark 2.3
.

As pointed out by
Anatolyev and
Gospodinov (2011)
and
Mikusheva and
Sun (2022)
, when
K
is fixed, no regularization is used, and the errors are homoskedastic, the jackknife AR test statistic admits the usual re-centered chi-squared approximation:

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})}{c_{n}}\rightsquigarrow\frac{\chi^{2}_{K}-K}{\sqrt{2K}}

for some normalization scalar
c_{n}
computed under homoskedasticity.

Furthermore,
Mikusheva and
Sun (2022)
noted that this re-centered chi-squared distribution converges quickly to the standard normal distribution as
K
increases. This suggests that critical values based on
\frac{\chi^{2}_{K}-K}{\sqrt{2K}}
remain valid whether
K
is fixed or diverging, making it a dimension-agnostic strong approximation for (the re-scaled)
\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})
under homoskedasticity. In this paper, we extend this idea to the heteroskedastic setting by deriving a weighted re-centered chi-squared approximation for
\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})
and establishing conditions under which a bootstrap critical value yields valid inference uniformly across different asymptotic regimes. In doing so, we also accommodate a diverging number of controls and ridge regularization, which allows the number of instruments
K
to exceed the sample size and provides power improvement as well.

Remark 2.4
.

Our proposed bootstrap test is AR-based. It is possible to extend our dimension-agnostic inference procedure to score-based Lagrangian Multiplier (LM) tests provided that the first-stage residual
\tilde{v}
is consistently estimable. Given the consistency of residuals, we conjecture that our bootstrap inference remains valid for score-based statistics, including the cases where the effect of the endogenous variable
\tilde{X}
may be heterogeneous and the structural equation (
2.1
) is thus misspecified.
9
9

9

In such settings of heterogeneous treatment effects, especially when the number of instruments diverges with the sample size, researchers typically assume that the reduced-form models for both endogenous variables
\tilde{Y}
and
\tilde{X}
are linear; see, for example,
Kolesár (2018)
,
Evdokimov and
Kolesár (2018)
,
Boot and
Nibbering (2024)
, and
Yap (2025)
. In such cases, we require the consistency of reduced-form residuals for the bootstrap validity.
Specifically, this may require restricting the dimension of
(W,\tilde{Z})
to be of a smaller order of
n
, imposing some sparsity conditions, and/or assuming that the reduced form regressions for
(\tilde{Y},\tilde{X})
are approximately linear. One advantage of our AR-based inference procedure is that it imposes minimal assumptions on the first stage. For instance, we do not have any restriction on
\tilde{\Pi}
, aligned closely with the setting in
Mikusheva and
Sun (2022)
.

3
Strong Approximation of the Test Statistic

This section is concerned with the conditions under which the null distribution of the test statistic defined in (
2.3
) can be approximated by its bootstrap counterpart, no matter whether the dimension
K_{n}
of the IVs is fixed or diverging with the sample size. We make the following assumptions on the data-generating process (DGP) to establish this result.

Assumption 1
.

1.

Let (
2.1
) hold in which
W
and
Z
are treated as fixed (i.e., non-random or conditioned upon),
\{\tilde{e}_{i},\tilde{v}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
are independent, mean zero, but potentially heteroskedastic.

2.

There exist constants
C\in(0,\infty)
and
q>6
such that
\max_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{e}_{i}^{2q}+X_{i}^{2q})\leq C
.

3.

Let
\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\tilde{e}_{i}^{2}
. Then, there exist constants
\infty>\bar{c}>\underline{c}>0
such that

\bar{c}\geq\max_{i\in[n]}\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\geq\min_{i\in[n]}\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\geq\underline{c}.

4.

The matrix
W^{\top}W
is invertible and
\max_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}=o(1)
, where
P_{W,ii}
denotes the i-th diagonal element of the projection matrix
P_{W}
.

5.

Let
p_{n}=\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Xi_{\lambda,ij}^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}
and
{p_{n}^{\prime}}=\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{P_{\lambda,ii}^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}
. Then, we have
p_{n}n^{3/q}=o(1)
and
{p_{n}^{\prime}}(1+\sum_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}^{2})=o(1)
.

6.

Let
\{\eta_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
be i.i.d. and independent of data, have mean zero, unit variance, and sub-Gaussian tail in the sense that
\inf\left\{u>0:\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{|\eta|}{u}\right)^{2}\leq 2\right\}\leq C<\infty
for some fixed constant
C\in(0,\infty)
.

Assumptions
1
.1–
1
.3 are standard regularity conditions. Assumption
1
.4 allows the dimension of control variables to diverge at a rate that is slower than the sample size, i.e.,
d_{w}=o(n)
. The impact of partialling out
W
from both
Y
and
X
becomes asymptotically negligible only when
d_{w}=o(\sqrt{n})
, reflecting a broader phenomenon commonly referred to as the
quadratic barrier
. See, for example,
Cattaneo
et al. (2019)
and
Lin
et al. (2024)
for further discussions. We overcome this barrier and establish bootstrap validity by carefully debiasing the AR statistic and further adjusting the middle matrix of the bootstrap quadratic form (as noted in Remark
2.2
).
To the best of our knowledge, this is the mildest rate condition regarding the number of controls established for bootstrap inference with high-dimensional IVs (without imposing a sparsity assumption).
We note that analytical inference remains feasible even when
d_{w}
is proportional to
n
, as demonstrated in
Anatolyev and
Sølvsten (2023)
. However, in such a high-dimensional control setting, our current bootstrap inference procedure may fail to control size. At present, it is unclear whether any valid resampling-based inference method exists in the proportion regime, let alone one that remains valid uniformly over the dimensions of both
Z
and
W
. We leave this important question for future research. In the following, we provide further comparisons between analytical and bootstrap inference approaches in Remarks
3.2
and
5.3
.

Assumption
1
.5 requires that
p_{n}
and
{p_{n}^{\prime}}
vanish sufficiently fast. Consider the case without ridge regularization (i.e.,
\lambda=0
) and where the projection matrix is well-defined (i.e.,
K_{n}<n
). If the diagonal elements of
P_{\lambda}
(with
\lambda=0
) are well-balanced in the sense that
P_{\lambda,ii}=K_{n}/n
, then we have

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}\geq CK_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Xi_{\lambda,ij}^{2}\leq CK_{n}/n.

This implies
p_{n}=O(n^{-1})
and
{p_{n}^{\prime}}=O(n^{-1})
. Importantly, we note that these results hold regardless of whether
K_{n}
is fixed or increasing with
n
. If
P_{W}
is also well-balanced such that
\max_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}\leq Cd_{w}/n
, then

\displaystyle{p_{n}^{\prime}}(1+\sum_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}^{2})\leq C(1/n+d_{w}^{2}/n^{2})=o(1)

as long as
d_{w}=o(n)
. In the minimum, even we only have
{p_{n}^{\prime}}=o(1)
, if
d_{w}=O(\sqrt{n})
, then
\sum_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}^{2}=O(1)
, which still guarantees that
{p_{n}^{\prime}}(1+\sum_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}^{2})=o(1)
.

These calculations imply that our inference procedure remains valid even when the number of control variables diverges at the rate
\sqrt{n}
or faster. Moreover, in high-dimensional settings where
K_{n}>n
, our ridge-regularized approach with the choice of
\lambda
in (
2.5
) ensures that Assumption
1
.5 holds provided
q>6
.

Assumption
1
.5 is closely related to Assumptions 2 and 3 in
Dovì
et al. (2024)
, but it accommodates a broader set of scenarios, which lead to different limiting behaviors of the test statistic. In particular, it allows for cases in which
K_{n}\to\infty
while

\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],\,j\neq i}\Xi_{\lambda,ij}^{2}

remains bounded, as well as cases in which
K_{n}
is bounded. These settings give rise to the second and third asymptotic regimes analyzed in Section
5
, respectively.

Finally, Assumption
1
.6 requires the bootstrap weights
\eta_{i}
to have sub-Gaussian tails. In practice, we recommend using standard normal or Rademacher random variables, both of which satisfy this condition. We use Rademacher random variables in our simulations and the empirical application.
10
10

10

The recommendation follows those in the literature on wild/multiplier bootstrap methods. In particular,
Davidson and
Flachaire (2008)
show favorable finite-sample properties of the Rademacher weights, and
Roodman
et al. (2019, section 3.4)
use the Rademacher weights as their default choice.

To proceed, we need to introduce some more notation. Define
\Delta=\beta-\beta_{0}
,
\tilde{\tau}_{i}=\mathbb{E}(\tilde{e}_{i}\tilde{v}_{i})
,
\tilde{\varsigma}_{i}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\tilde{v}_{i}^{2}
,
\breve{e}_{i}(\beta_{0})=\tilde{e}_{i}(\beta_{0})+\Pi_{i}\Delta
, and
\tilde{e}_{i}(\beta_{0})=\tilde{e}_{i}+\tilde{v}_{i}\Delta
. Then, we denote

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}(\beta_{0})=Var(\breve{e}_{i}(\beta_{0}))=\mathbb{E}\tilde{e}_{i}^{2}(\beta_{0})=\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}+2\Delta\tilde{\tau}_{i}+\Delta^{2}\tilde{\varsigma}_{i}^{2},

\displaystyle\breve{\sigma}_{i}^{2}(\beta_{0})=\mathbb{E}\breve{e}_{i}^{2}(\beta_{0})=\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}+2\Delta\tilde{\tau}_{i}+\Delta^{2}(\tilde{\varsigma}_{i}^{2}+\Pi_{i}^{2})=\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}(\beta_{0})+\Pi_{i}^{2}\Delta^{2}.

In addition, let

\displaystyle Q(\beta_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}(g_{i}\tilde{\sigma}_{i}(\beta_{0})+\Pi_{i}\Delta)\Xi_{\lambda,ij}(g_{j}\tilde{\sigma}_{j}(\beta_{0})+\Pi_{j}\Delta)}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}

(3.1)

and

\displaystyle Q^{*}(\beta_{0})=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}g_{i}\breve{\sigma}_{i}(\beta_{0})\Xi_{\lambda,ij}g_{j}\breve{\sigma}_{j}(\beta_{0})}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}},

(3.2)

where
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
are i.i.d. standard normal random variables that are generated independent of data. The following theorem shows that our proposed AR test statistic
\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})
can be strongly approximated by
Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)
in Kolmogorov distance, where

\displaystyle C(\Delta)=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Pi_{i}\left(P_{\lambda,ij}-\Xi_{\lambda,ij}\right)\Pi_{j}\Delta^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}.

Furthermore, Theorem
4.1
in the next section shows the bootstrap statistic
\widehat{Q}^{*}(\beta_{0})
can be strongly approximated by
Q^{*}(\beta_{0})
in Kolmogorov distance conditionally on data.
Note that
Q^{*}(\beta_{0})
is equal to
Q(\beta_{0})
under the null hypothesis.
11
11

11

Under the null, we have
\Delta=0
and
\tilde{\sigma}_{i}(\beta_{0})=\breve{\sigma}_{i}(\beta_{0})
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds, and
||\Pi||_{2}^{2}\Delta^{2}/\min\left(R_{\lambda}^{1/2},R_{\lambda}^{2/3}\right)
is bounded. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)\leq y)\right|=o(1).

Remark 3.1
.

We note that
Q(\beta_{0})
is implicitly indexed by the sample size
n
, which explains why we call it a strong approximation rather than a limit of our AR statistic
\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})
.
Second, as noted in Remark
2.2
, the cross-sectional dependence between the elements of
e(\beta_{0})
is not asymptotically negligible when
d_{w}
diverges at a rate
\sqrt{n}
or higher.
On the other hand,
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
in
Q(\beta_{0})
and
Q^{*}(\beta_{0})
are i.i.d. standard normal random variables. We account for this by adjusting
P_{\lambda}
in the original statistic to
\Xi_{\lambda}
in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
). Third, we can see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Pi_{i}P_{\lambda,ij}\Pi_{j}\Delta^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}},

(3.3)

which is the non-centrality parameter for the AR statistic under the alternative.

Remark 3.2
.

The strong approximation
Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)
encompasses three asymptotic regimes in a unified framework:
(1) when both
K
and
R_{\lambda}
are bounded,

Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)
asymptotically follows a weighted non-central chi-squared distribution;
(2) when both
K
and
R_{\lambda}
diverge so that a Lindeberg-type condition holds, it converges in distribution to a normal random variable; and (3)
when
K
diverges but
R_{\lambda}
is bounded, it converges to a mixture of a weighted sum of non-central chi-squared distributions and a normal distribution. These three regimes are discussed separately by
Kline
et al. (2020, Sections 4, 5, and 6)
in the setting of estimation of variance components. For testing linear restrictions,
Anatolyev and
Sølvsten (2023)
proposed an analytical inference procedure that is valid under regimes (1) and (2). However, in their setting, where ridge regularization is not employed, the third regime does not arise.
A key advantage of our bootstrap procedure and the associated strong approximation results is that they are valid irrespective of the asymptotic regime, including the challenging case with a mixture of distributions. We will provide further details on the regimes in Section
5
.

Remark 3.3
.

Theorem
3.1
is valid under both the null and alternative hypotheses. The nature of the alternatives depends on the magnitude of
||\Pi||_{2}^{2}\Delta^{2}/\min\left(R_{\lambda}^{1/2},R_{\lambda}^{2/3}\right)
. When
R_{\lambda}
is bounded, we have weak (strong) identification when the concentration parameter
||\Pi||_{2}^{2}
is bounded (diverging). When
R_{\lambda}
is diverging, as shown by
Mikusheva and
Sun (2022)
, weak (strong) identification arises when the concentration parameter
||\Pi||_{2}^{2}/\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}
is bounded (diverging). Under either regime with regard to
R_{\lambda}
, Theorem
3.1
accommodates (i) fixed alternatives under weak identification and (ii) local alternatives scaled by the square root of the concentration parameter under strong identification.

4
Strong Approximation of the Bootstrap Statistic

This section concerns the strong approximation of the bootstrap statistic defined in Section
2.3
in Kolmogorov distance conditionally on data.
The approximation in Theorem
4.1
is the same as that for the original statistic under the null hypothesis, as established in Theorem
3.1
, which directly implies that the proposed test with bootstrap critical values achieves a correct asymptotic size. Such a result holds no matter whether the dimension of IVs
K_{n}
is fixed or diverging to infinity.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote all observations in our sample. Suppose Assumption
1
holds and
\Delta
is bounded. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\Re}|\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}^{*}(\beta_{0})\leq y|\mathcal{D})-\mathbb{P}(Q^{*}(\beta_{0})\leq y)|=o_{P}(1).

Remark 4.1
.

Theorem
4.1
remains valid under both the null and alternative hypotheses. In contrast to Theorem
3.1
, it accommodates fixed alternatives even in the presence of strong identification (without requiring
||\Pi||_{2}^{2}\Delta^{2}/\min\left(R_{\lambda}^{1/2},R_{\lambda}^{2/3}\right)
to be bounded). This distinction originates from the fact that the alternative hypothesis
\Delta
affects
Q(\beta_{0})
and
Q^{*}(\beta_{0})
differently – introducing non-centrality bias in the former and variance in the latter (e.g., see the non-centrality bias in (
3.3
) and the definition of
Q^{*}(\beta_{0})
in (
3.2
), respectively). The distinction also underpins the power of our dimension-agnostic AR test, which will be analyzed in detail in Section
5
.
12
12

12

Similar phenomenon of an inflated variance under the alternative hypothesis also occurs with the jackknife AR tests using analytical variance estimators that impose the null hypothesis (e.g.,
Crudu
et al. (2021)
,
Mikusheva and
Sun (2022)
, and
Dovì
et al. (2024)
) so that the resulting tests can be robust to weak identification.

Remark 4.2
.

Theorem
4.1
can be viewed as a general result of strong approximation for the bootstrap version of the quadratic forms. The proof extends the Lindeberg swapping strategy mentioned in the previous section. Indeed, compared with Theorem
3.1
, it is substantially more involved to establish Theorem
4.1
because the second moment of the bootstrap statistic
\widehat{Q}^{*}(\beta_{0})
conditional on data is random and does not exactly match that of its strong approximation (i.e.,
Q^{*}(\beta_{0})
). We rely on the concentration inequalities for quadratic forms (i.e., Hanson-Wright inequality) and linear forms of martingale difference sequence to bound the approximation error in Kolmogorov distance due to the mismatch of the second moments. This technique seems new to the literature and may be of independent interest.

Remark 4.3
.

In addition, we observe from (
3.1
)-(
3.2
) that
Q(\beta_{0})
and
Q^{*}(\beta_{0})
have the same marginal distribution under the null hypothesis (
\Delta=0
). This means that, under the null, the bootstrap statistic closely approximates the test statistic in Kolmogorov distance when conditioned on the data, whether
K_{n}
is fixed or diverging.
This equivalence forms the basis for our bootstrap test to achieve the correct size.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose we are under the null hypothesis
\beta=\beta_{0}
and Assumption
1
holds. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})>\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\beta_{0}))\rightarrow\alpha,

where
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{*}_{\alpha}(\beta_{0})
is the
(1-\alpha)
-th percentile of
\widehat{Q}^{*}(\beta_{0})
conditional on data.

5
Asymptotic Power

In this section, we discuss the power of the bootstrap inference by focusing on three separate cases: (I) both
K
and
R_{\lambda}
diverge, (II)
K
diverges but
R_{\lambda}
is bounded, and (III) both
K
and
R_{\lambda}
are bounded.

5.1
The Case with Diverging
K
and
R_{\lambda}

To proceed, we let

\Psi(\beta_{0})=2\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{i}(\beta_{0})\Xi_{\lambda,ij}^{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}(\beta_{0})/R_{\lambda}
, where
\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}(\beta_{0})=Var(\tilde{e}_{i}(\beta_{0}))=\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}+2\Delta\tilde{\tau}_{i}+\Delta^{2}\tilde{\varsigma}_{i}^{2}
.

Assumption 2
.

1.

K\rightarrow\infty
,
R_{\lambda}\rightarrow\infty
, and
||\Pi||_{2}^{2}\Delta^{2}/\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}
is bounded.

2.

\Delta
and
\max_{i\in[n]}|\Pi_{i}|
are bounded.

3.

\Psi^{-1/2}(\beta_{0})\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Pi_{i}P_{\lambda,ij}\Pi_{j}\Delta^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\rightarrow\mu(\beta_{0})
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})>\widehat{C}^{*}_{\alpha}(\beta_{0}))\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu(\beta_{0}),1)>z_{\alpha}\right),

where
\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)
is a normal random variable with mean
\mu
and unit variance and
z_{\alpha}
is the
(1-\alpha)
quantile of a standard normal random variable.

Remark 5.1
.

Let us denote
(\varpi_{1},\cdots,\varpi_{n})
as the eigenvalues of the matrix

\displaystyle\text{diag}(\tilde{\sigma}_{1}(\beta_{0}),\cdots,\tilde{\sigma}_{n}(\beta_{0}))\Xi_{\lambda}\text{diag}(\tilde{\sigma}_{1}(\beta_{0}),\cdots,\tilde{\sigma}_{n}(\beta_{0})),

ordered such that
|\varpi_{1}|\geq|\varpi_{2}|\geq\cdots\geq|\varpi_{n}|
. From the proof of Theorem
5.1
, we note that under the null,

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(g_{i}^{2}-1)\varpi_{i}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}+o_{P}(1),

where
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
is an i.i.d. sequence of standard normal random variables. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\varpi_{1}=O(1)\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i\in[n]}\varpi_{i}^{2}\geq\underline{c}R_{\lambda},

for some constant
\underline{c}>0
. This implies when

,

\displaystyle\frac{\varpi_{1}^{2}}{\sum_{i\in[n]}\varpi_{i}^{2}}=o(1),

which is a Lindeberg-type condition that guarantees asymptotic normality of the test statistic, as established in Theorem
5.1
.

Remark 5.2
.

When
d_{w}=o(\sqrt{n})
, Theorem
5.1
holds if we replace
\Xi_{\lambda}
by
P_{\lambda}
in the definition of
\Psi(\beta_{0})
as the effect of partialling out controls is asymptotically negligible. If
K_{n}<n
and we set
\lambda=0
so that

(i.e., without ridge regularization), the local power of our test is asymptotically equivalent to that of the jackknife AR tests proposed by
Crudu
et al. (2021)
and
Mikusheva and
Sun (2022)
.
13
13

13

Crudu
et al. (2021)
and
Mikusheva and
Sun (2022)
proposed different variance estimators for the jackknife AR statistic. Under local alternatives characterized under Assumption

, the two variance estimators are asymptotically equivalent.

In general, the regularizer
\lambda
can affect the power through
\mu(\beta_{0})
, which depends on
P_{\lambda}
and
R_{\lambda}
.
Specifically, following Remark

, we note that the alternative
\Delta

, given by

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Pi_{i}P_{\lambda,ij}\Pi_{j}\Delta^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\equiv\mathbb{C}_{\lambda}\Delta^{2},

(5.1)

where

denotes the concentration parameter under the ridge regularization, and (2) the variance of the statistic, captured by

\mu(\beta_{0})
of the limiting distribution.

Furthermore, we note that (
5.1
) also motivates our choice of the regularizer
\lambda
.
In particular, as we restrict the upper bound
\bar{\theta}
for the regularizer to be
||Z^{\top}Z||_{op}
, the ridge regularization
\lambda I_{K_{n}}
will not dominate
Z^{\top}Z
. Then it is plausible that the numerator of the concentration parameter, i.e.,
\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2},i\neq j}\Pi_{i}P_{\lambda,ij}\Pi_{j}
does not change order for the range of
\lambda
we consider. On the other hand, as
\lambda
increases, the effective rank
R_{\lambda}
decreases, which typically causes the non-centrality in (
5.1
) to increase and thus lead to power improvement. Notice that it is possible for

\mathbb{C}_{\lambda}
in (
5.1
) to achieve a higher order of magnitude than the concentration parameter without ridge regularization

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Pi_{i}P_{ij}\Pi_{j}}{\sqrt{K}},

(5.2)

as long as
R_{\lambda}=o(K)
(e.g.,
K
diverges but
R_{\lambda}
is fixed).
Such an advantage of regularization has been pointed out in previous studies, such as
Carrasco and
Tchuente (2015)
;
Carrasco and
Tchuente (2016a)
and
Carrasco and
Doukali (2017)
.
In the next section, we further study in detail the case where
R_{\lambda}
is bounded while
K
diverges.

5.2
The Case with Diverging
K
but Bounded
R_{\lambda}

Following Remark
5.1
, we now consider the case where
R_{\lambda}
remains bounded, resulting in the failure of the Lindeberg-type condition for asymptotic normality.

Assumption 3
.

1.

Suppose there exists a fixed positive integer
R
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\varpi_{i}}{(\sum_{j\in[n]}\varpi_{i}^{2})^{1/2}}\rightarrow r_{i}\neq 0,\quad\forall i=1,\cdots,R,\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\varpi_{R+1}^{2}}{\sum_{i\in[n]}\varpi_{i}^{2}}=o(1).

2.

Denote
(\varpi_{1}^{*},\cdots,\varpi_{n}^{*})
as the eigenvalues of the matrix

\displaystyle\text{diag}(\breve{\sigma}_{1}(\beta_{0}),\cdots,\breve{\sigma}_{n}(\beta_{0}))\Xi_{\lambda}\text{diag}(\breve{\sigma}_{1}(\beta_{0}),\cdots,\breve{\sigma}_{n}(\beta_{0})),

ordered such that
|\varpi_{1}^{*}|\geq|\varpi_{2}^{*}|\geq\cdots\geq|\varpi_{n}^{*}|
. Suppose there exists a fixed positive integer
R^{*}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\varpi_{i}^{*}}{\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}\varpi_{i}^{*2}\right)^{1/2}}\rightarrow r_{i}^{*}\neq 0,\quad\forall i=1,\cdots,R^{*},\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\varpi_{R^{*}+1}^{*2}}{\sum_{i\in[n]}\varpi_{i}^{*2}}=o(1).

3.

Suppose
||\Pi||_{2}^{2}\Delta^{2}/\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}
,
\Delta
, and
\max_{i\in[n]}|\Pi_{i}|
are bounded.

4.

\Psi^{-1/2}(\beta_{0})\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Pi_{i}P_{\lambda,ij}\Pi_{j}\Delta^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\rightarrow\mu(\beta_{0})
and
\frac{\breve{\Psi}(\beta_{0})}{\Psi(\beta_{0})}\rightarrow\psi(\beta_{0})>0
, where

\breve{\Psi}(\beta_{0})=2\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\breve{\sigma}^{2}_{i}(\beta_{0})\Xi_{\lambda,ij}^{2}\breve{\sigma}_{j}^{2}(\beta_{0})/R_{\lambda}.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
3
hold. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})>\widehat{C}^{*}_{\alpha}(\beta_{0}))\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\left(\chi(\{r_{i}\}_{i\in[R]})+\mu(\beta_{0})>\psi^{1/2}(\beta_{0})\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\{r_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in[R^{*}]})\right),

where the random variable
\chi(\{r_{i}\}_{i\in[R]})
has the distribution

\frac{\sum_{i\in[R]}(g_{i}^{2}-1)r_{i}}{\sqrt{2}}+\left(1-\sum_{i\in[R]}r_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}g_{R+1},

with
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in[R+1]}
being i.i.d. standard normal random variables, and
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\{r_{i}\}_{i\in[R]})
is the
(1-\alpha)
-th quantile of
\chi(\{r_{i}\}_{i\in[R]})
.

Remark 5.3
.

When the Lindeberg-type condition fails due to
R_{\lambda}
being bounded, the limiting distribution of our test statistic becomes a mixture of a weighted sum of chi-squared random variables and a standard normal random variable. This is similar to the scenario described in
Kline
et al. (2020, Sections 6 and 7)
and
Yang et al. (2024)
. Analytical inference in this regime is difficult, as it requires estimating the number of dominant eigenvalues
R
driving the asymptotic distribution or reporting (the union of) confidence intervals corresponding to consecutive values of
R
(see, e.g., Section 7.2 of
Kline
et al. (2020)
).
A key advantage of our bootstrap inference procedure is that it does not require prior knowledge of the number of dominant eigenvalues
R
or associated weights
\{r_{i}\}_{i\in[R]}
, since it is valid regardless of the asymptotic regime. In our simulations in Section
6
with
K=160
and our choice of
\lambda
, we observe one dominant eigenvalue (
R=1
) and
r_{1}=\sqrt{0.948}
.
Furthermore, in the empirical application in Section
7
, we find that
R_{\lambda}
equals 2.015 and 1.550 for the specifications with 38 and 342 IVs, respectively, suggesting that this regime also applies to our application of
Card (2009)
’s dataset.

Remark 5.4
.

As mentioned earlier, the alternative
\Delta
affects the location and scale of the test statistic and the bootstrap critical value, represented by
\mu(\beta_{0})
and
\psi(\beta_{0})
, respectively. When
R_{\lambda}
diverges, the scale effect becomes asymptotically negligible, as indicated by
\psi(\beta_{0})=1
in Theorem
5.1
. However, when
R_{\lambda}
is bounded,
\psi(\beta_{0})
may differ from one, and the scale effect remains relevant in the limiting distribution.

Remark 5.5
.

Furthermore, we note that the ridge-regularized concentration parameter
\mathbb{C}_{\lambda}
in (
5.2
) can achieve a higher divergence rate than that without regularization, given that
R_{\lambda}
is bounded while
K
diverges. In particular, as established by
Mikusheva and
Sun (2022, Theorems 1 and 4)
,
\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Pi_{i}P_{ij}\Pi_{j}/\sqrt{K}\rightarrow\infty
is required for the jackknife AR test (without regularization) to be consistent. By contrast, with the help of regularization, our test only requires

\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Pi_{i}P_{\lambda,ij}\Pi_{j}\rightarrow\infty
to be consistent if
R_{\lambda}
is bounded.

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Omega^{1/2}(\beta_{0})S_{0}(S_{0}^{2}+\lambda I_{K})^{-1}S_{0}\Omega^{1/2}(\beta_{0})}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}=\mathbb{U}\text{diag}(\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{K})\mathbb{U}^{\top}.

Assumption 4

1.

Suppose the IVs

\max_{i\in[n]}||\mathcal{U}_{1,i}||_{2}=o(1)

The following theorem establishes our AR test’s power property in the fixed

Theorem 5.3
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
4

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})>\widehat{C}^{*}_{\alpha}(\beta_{0}))\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{k}\chi_{k}^{2}(\nu_{k}^{2}(\beta_{0}))>\mathcal{C}_{\omega}(1-\alpha)\right),

where
\omega=(\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{K})

is the

is the

\{\chi^{2}_{k}\}_{k\in[K]}

Next, we demonstrate that when

is fixed, our dimension-agnostic AR test is (asymptotically) admissible within a specific class of tests, which includes the standard (heteroskedasticity-robust) AR test designed for fixed

Let

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(\beta_{0})=\mathbb{U}\hat{\Omega}^{-1/2}(\beta_{0})\mathcal{U}_{1}^{\top}e(\beta_{0}),

(5.3)

and

is fixed, the standard AR test rejects if

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{G}}^{\top}(\beta_{0})\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(\beta_{0})=\sum_{k\in[K]}\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{k}^{2}(\beta_{0})>\mathcal{C}_{\iota_{K}}(1-\alpha),

(5.4)

where

is a

is just the

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{k}\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{k}^{2}(\beta_{0})>\mathcal{C}_{\omega}(1-\alpha).

In addition, the proof of Theorem

shows

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{2}(\beta_{0}),\cdots,\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{K}^{2}(\beta_{0})\right)\rightsquigarrow\left(\chi_{1}^{2}(\nu_{1}^{2}(\beta_{0})),\cdots,\chi_{K}^{2}(\nu_{K}^{2}(\beta_{0}))\right).

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha}=\begin{Bmatrix}\phi(\cdot):\Re^{K}\mapsto[0,1],\quad\mathbb{E}\phi(\chi_{1}^{2}(\nu_{1}^{2}(\beta_{0})),\cdots,\chi_{K}^{2}(\nu_{K}^{2}(\beta_{0})))\leq\alpha,\\

\text{the set of discontinuities of $\phi(\cdot)$ has zero Lebesgue measure}\end{Bmatrix}.

Theorem 5.4
.

Suppose Assumptions

and

\hat{\Omega}(\beta_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Omega(\beta_{0})

in the sense that if there exists a test

such that for all values of

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\phi^{*}(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{2}(\beta_{0}),\cdots,\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{K}^{2}(\beta_{0}))\geq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\phi_{0},

then we must have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\phi^{*}(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{2}(\beta_{0}),\cdots,\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{K}^{2}(\beta_{0}))=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\phi_{0},

for all
\nu(\beta_{0})\in\Re^{K}
.

Remark 5.6
.

It is reasonable to assume there exists a consistent estimator
\hat{\Omega}(\beta_{0})
for
\Omega(\beta_{0})
, which is a
K\times K
matrix with
K
fixed.

Remark 5.7
.

Because the standard AR test defined in (
5.4
) belongs to
\Phi_{\alpha}
, Theorem
5.4
implies our bootstrap test
\phi_{0}
is not dominated by the standard AR test for all alternatives. In fact, the standard AR test is also admissible among the tests in
\Phi_{\alpha}
so that it is not dominated by
\phi_{0}
either. However, our bootstrap test is dimension-robust, while the standard AR test does not have the correct size under the regimes in Sections
5.1
and
5.2
.

Remark 5.8
.

Under a fixed number of IVs and strong identification against local alternatives, the K test proposed by
Kleibergen (2002)
exhibits better power properties than our test. However, similar to the standard AR test, the K test is not dimension-robust. Indeed,
Lim
et al. (2024)
propose a counterpart of the K test tailored to the setting with many weak instruments and heteroskedastic errors, although this test may be invalid when the number of IVs is fixed. Moreover, both the K test and its many-weak-IV counterpart suffer from power ditches and therefore have no power against certain fixed alternatives, even under strong identification, see, for example, Section 3.1 of
Andrews (2016)
and Lemma 2.3 of
Lim
et al. (2024)
. Additionally,
Chernozhukov et al. (2009)
establish admissibility of the weighted average power likelihood ratio tests derived by
Andrews
et al. (2006)
under a fixed
K
. In particular, they show that the class of tests covered by this admissibility result contains the standard AR test, so that there is no “waste of degrees of freedom” in the conventional statistical sense.

Remark 5.9
.

Navjeevan (2023)
proposed a dimension-robust version of the K test, which de-correlates the endogenous variable
X_{i}
and outcome error
e_{i}
conditionally on
Z_{i}
. This approach requires consistently estimating the conditional correlation
\rho(Z_{i})=\mathbb{E}(X_{i}e_{i}|Z_{i})
. However, when the dimension of
Z_{i}
is large, in general,
\rho(Z_{i})
cannot be consistently estimated. Instead,
Navjeevan (2023)
imposes a sparsity condition and estimates
\rho(Z_{i})
by an
\ell_{1}
-regularized regression. According to his simulations, the dimension-robust K test can also suffer from the power ditch issue due to the (null-imposed) decorrelation. Unlike
Navjeevan
’s (
2023
) procedure, our test achieves robustness against the dimension of IVs without imposing any additional structure. Furthermore, if one is comfortable with imposing the sparsity assumption on
\rho(Z_{i})
, then it is possible to combine our test and
Navjeevan
’s (
2023
) K test (e.g., by constructing a dimension-robust version of the conditional linear combination test in
Lim
et al. (2024)
, which is efficient under strong identification and also solves the power ditch issue).

Size Properties:
Table

\rm RJAR
,
\rm JAR_{std}
,
\rm AR_{\rm Robust}
,

,
\rm Empirical
,
\rm LM
, and
\rm JK

Second, the classical
\rm AR
test for fixed

and the
\rm AS
test control size for all values of

K
is large.
Similarly, we observe that the
\rm BCCH_{1.1}
test is relatively conservative across different numbers of IVs, while
\rm BCCH_{1.01}
also becomes conservative when
K
is large. By contrast, our proposed dimension-robust
\rm BS
test largely resolves the size-distortion issues for all values of

considered.
Overall, our
\rm BS

We observe that
{\rm AR_{Robust}}
is undefined at times due to the estimated variance
\widehat{\Phi}_{W}
being negative. As in
Dovì

Several factors contribute to the deviation of empirical sizes from the nominal level in existing procedures. When
K
is small, the limiting distributions of JAR
std
, AR
Robust
, RJAR, CT, and LM are not normal, so the use of normal critical values can lead to size distortions. When
K
is large, some observations may have very high leverage. For instance, when
K=160
and
n=200
, we observe that
\max_{i\in[n]}P_{ii}\approx 0.9595
. High leverage can lead to numerical problems with AR-type statistics, such as JAR
std
and AR
Robust
.

RJAR and our statistics instead rely on ridge regularization to mitigate the large-leverage problem. Regularization, however, may lead to a non-Gaussian limiting distribution (corresponding to the asymptotic regime studied by Section
5.2
) and invalidate analytical inference procedures. We therefore employ bootstrap critical values, which remain valid irrespective of asymptotic regimes.

Furthermore, the classical
\rm AR
test suffers from the many-IV bias. The
\rm JAR_{std}
,
\rm RJAR
,
\rm CT
,
\rm LM
, and
\rm JK
tests are valid when the number of controls is fixed but may suffer from the many-control bias. The
\rm AR_{\rm Robust}
,
\rm BCCH
, and
\rm AS
procedures are designed for data-rich environments. However, in our simulations
\rm AR_{\rm Robust}
tends to over-reject, whereas
\rm AS
tends to under-reject, due to the numerical issues discussed above. The
\rm BCCH
procedure relies on Bonferroni-adjusted critical values, and is conservative with the default tuning constant
1.1
. Using a smaller constant instead, such as
1.01
, can mitigate the conservative size, which also leads to power improvement.

Additionally, we note that the normalization by the effective rank is less crucial for our size control, since our inference is based on the bootstrap. Specifically, as can be seen from (
2.3
) and (
2.6
), any such normalization appears in both the test statistic and the bootstrap critical value, and therefore cancels out. However, this is not the case for analytical inference procedures that rely on critical values derived from the limiting distribution. This provides another advantage of our bootstrap-based inference.

The empirical bootstrap (“Empirical”) exhibits relatively large size distortions compared with our multiplier bootstrap, as it is not robust to heteroskedasticity. Let
\mathbb{E}^{*}
denote the conditional expectation under the residual bootstrap. The conditional variance of

where the equality follows from conditional independence of

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{E}(f_{n,y}(\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})))-\mathbb{E}(f_{n,y}(Q(\beta_{0})))\right|\leq\sum_{k\in[n]}\frac{\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{S}_{k}|^{3}+|\breve{\mathcal{S}}_{k}|^{3})}{C_{h}h_{n}^{3}}.

(A.13)

Recall
\tilde{e}_{i}(\beta_{0})=\tilde{e}_{i}+\tilde{v}_{i}\Delta
,
\tilde{g}_{i}(\beta_{0})=g_{i}\tilde{\sigma}_{i}(\beta_{0})
, and

\displaystyle\theta_{k,i}=\begin{cases}\Xi_{\lambda,ki}\tilde{e}_{i}(\beta_{0})\quad i<k\\
\Xi_{\lambda,ki}\tilde{g}_{i}(\beta_{0})\quad i>k.\end{cases}

Then, we have

\displaystyle\breve{e}_{i}(\beta_{0})=\tilde{e}_{i}(\beta_{0})+\Delta\Pi_{i},\quad\breve{g}_{i}(\beta_{0})=\tilde{g}_{i}(\beta_{0})+\Delta\Pi_{i},\quad\mathcal{S}_{k}=2\breve{e}_{k}(\beta_{0})\frac{\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}(\theta_{k,i}+\Xi_{\lambda,ki}\Delta\Pi_{i})}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{S}_{k}|^{3})

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{R_{\lambda}^{3/2}}\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}(\theta_{k,i}+\Xi_{\lambda,ki}\Delta\Pi_{i})\right|^{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{R_{\lambda}^{3/2}}\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\theta_{k,i}\right|^{3}+\frac{1}{R_{\lambda}^{3/2}}\left|\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\Xi_{\lambda,ki}\Delta\Pi_{i}\right|^{3}.

(A.14)

Note that
\{\theta_{k,i}\}_{i\in[n],i\neq k}
is a sequence of independent mean zero random variables. Then, by Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\theta_{k,i}\right|^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[(\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\theta_{k,i}^{2})^{3/2}\right]\leq C\left[\mathbb{E}\left((\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\theta_{k,i}^{2})^{2}\right)\right]^{3/4}

\displaystyle\leq C\left[\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq k}\Xi_{\lambda,ik}^{2}\Xi_{\lambda,jk}^{2}\right]^{3/4}

\displaystyle\leq C\left[\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\Xi_{\lambda,ik}^{2}\right]^{3/2}.

(A.15)

This implies

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[n]}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\theta_{k,i}\right|^{3}\right)}{R_{\lambda}^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in[n]}\frac{\left[\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\Xi_{\lambda,ik}^{2}\right]^{3/2}}{R_{\lambda}^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\max_{k\in[n]}\left[\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\Xi_{\lambda,ik}^{2}\right]^{1/2}}{R_{\lambda}^{1/2}}=O\left(p_{n}^{1/2}\right).

For the second term on the RHS of (
A.14
), we have

\displaystyle\max_{k\in[n]}\left|\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\Xi_{\lambda,ki}\Delta\Pi_{i}\right|/R_{\lambda}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{k\in[n]}\left[\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\Xi_{\lambda,ki}^{2}\right]^{1/2}R_{\lambda}^{-1/2}|\Delta|\left\|\Pi\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq p_{n}^{1/2}|\Delta|\left\|\Pi\right\|_{2}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[n]}\frac{\left|\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\Xi_{\lambda,ki}\Delta\Pi_{i}\right|^{3}}{R_{\lambda}^{3/2}}\leq p_{n}^{1/2}|\Delta|\left\|\Pi\right\|_{2}\sum_{k\in[n]}\frac{\left|\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}\Xi_{\lambda,ki}\Delta\Pi_{i}\right|^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=p_{n}^{1/2}|\Delta|\left\|\Pi\right\|_{2}\frac{\Pi^{\top}\Xi_{\lambda}^{2}\Pi\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}\leq p_{n}^{1/2}\left(\frac{\left\|\Pi\right\|_{2}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}^{2/3}}\right)^{3/2}=O(p_{n}^{1/2}),

which implies

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[n]}\frac{\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{S}_{k}|^{3})}{C_{h}h_{n}^{3}}=O\left(\frac{p_{n}^{1/2}}{h_{n}^{3}}\right).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[n]}\frac{\mathbb{E}(|\breve{\mathcal{S}}_{k}|^{3})}{C_{h}h_{n}^{3}}=O\left(\frac{p_{n}^{1/2}}{h_{n}^{3}}\right),

and thus,

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{E}(f_{n,y}(\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})))-\mathbb{E}(f_{n,y}(Q(\beta_{0})))\right|=O\left(\frac{p_{n}^{1/2}}{h_{n}^{3}}\right).

(A.16)

In addition, by Lemma
H.1
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\Re}\mathbb{P}(|Q(\beta_{0})-y|\leq 3\delta_{n})\leq C_{\zeta}(3C_{h})^{(1-\zeta)/2}h_{n}^{(1-\zeta)/2}

(A.17)

for any
\zeta\in(0,1)
and
C_{\zeta}\in(0,\infty)
that only depends on
\zeta

\underline{c}
in Assumption
1
.3.

Then, combining (
A.10
), (
A.16

A.17

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{P}(\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})\leq y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{E}(f_{n,y}(\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})))-\mathbb{E}(f_{n,y}(Q(\beta_{0})))\right|+\varepsilon+\sup_{y\in\Re}\mathbb{P}(|Q(\beta_{0})-y|\leq 3\delta_{n})

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{p_{n}^{1/2}}{h_{n}^{3}}\right)+\varepsilon+C_{\zeta}(3C_{h})^{(1-\zeta)/2}h_{n}^{(1-\zeta)/2}.

By letting

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{P}(\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})\leq y)\right|\leq\varepsilon.

Because

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)\right|=o(1).

(A.18)

Step 3: Concluding the Proof

For any sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)\leq y)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y,|\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})-\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})-C(\Delta)|\leq\varepsilon)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)\leq y)+\mathbb{P}(|\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})-\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})-C(\Delta)|\geq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{P}(\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})\leq y)\right|+\sup_{y\in\Re}\mathbb{P}(|Q(\beta_{0})-y|\leq\varepsilon)+\sup_{y\in\Re}\mathbb{P}(|\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})-\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})-C(\Delta)|\geq\varepsilon).

Similarly, we can show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})>y)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)>y)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})>y,|\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})-\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})-C(\Delta)|\leq\varepsilon)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)>y)+\mathbb{P}(|\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})-\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})-C(\Delta)|\geq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)>y-\varepsilon)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)>y)+\mathbb{P}(|\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})-\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})-C(\Delta)|\geq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{P}(\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})\leq y)\right|+\sup_{y\in\Re}\mathbb{P}(|Q(\beta_{0})-y|\leq\varepsilon)+\sup_{y\in\Re}\mathbb{P}(|\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})-\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})-C(\Delta)|\geq\varepsilon),

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{P}(\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})\leq y)\right|+\sup_{y\in\Re}\mathbb{P}(|Q(\beta_{0})-y|\leq\varepsilon)+\sup_{y\in\Re}\mathbb{P}(|\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})-\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})-C(\Delta)|\geq\varepsilon).

Combining the two results, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{P}(\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})\leq y)\right|+\sup_{y\in\Re}\mathbb{P}(|Q(\beta_{0})-y|\leq\varepsilon)+\sup_{y\in\Re}\mathbb{P}(|\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})-\breve{Q}(\beta_{0})-C(\Delta)|\geq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\zeta}\varepsilon^{(1-\zeta)/2}+o(1),

where the last inequality is by Lemma
H.1
and the above two steps.

As
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, by letting

shrink to zero, we obtain the desired result that,

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\Re}\left|\mathbb{P}(\widehat{Q}(\beta_{0})\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(Q(\beta_{0})+C(\Delta)\leq y)\right|=o(1).

(A.19)

Step 2: Bound

Applying

\kappa=(M_{W}\circ M_{W})^{-1}
,

\displaystyle A_{\lambda,ii}=2P_{W,ii}P_{\lambda,ii}-B_{\lambda,ii},\quad B_{\lambda,ii}=[P_{W}D_{\lambda}P_{W}]_{ii},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\Xi_{\lambda,ij}=P_{\lambda,ij}+(P_{\lambda,ii}+P_{\lambda,jj})P_{W,ij}-B_{\lambda,ij},

where
D_{\lambda}=\text{diag}(P_{\lambda,11},\cdots,P_{\lambda,nn})
. Then, we have

(1)

\max_{i\in[n]}A_{\lambda,ii}=o(1)
;

(2)

\max_{i\in[n]}A_{\lambda,ii}^{2}/\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}=o(1)
;

(3)

\sum_{i\in[n]}A_{\lambda,ii}^{2}/R_{\lambda}=o(1)
;

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2}}\kappa_{ij}e_{j}^{2}(\beta_{0})A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}(\beta_{0})A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}+o_{P}(1);

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{2\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Pi_{i}\Delta\left(P_{\lambda,ij}-\Xi_{\lambda,ij}\right)\tilde{e}_{j}(\beta_{0})}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}=o_{P}(1).

Proof.

For the first claim, we have

\displaystyle\max_{j\in[n]}A_{\lambda,jj}\lesssim\max_{j\in[n]}P_{W,jj}P_{\lambda,jj}+\max_{j\in[n]}\sum_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ij}^{2}P_{\lambda,ii}\lesssim\max_{j\in[n]}P_{W,jj}=o(1).

For the second claim, we have

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[n]}A_{\lambda,ii}^{2}/\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{P_{W,ii}^{2}P_{\lambda,ii}^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}+\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{\sum_{j\in[n]}P_{W,ij}^{2}P_{\lambda,jj}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\max_{j\in[n]}\frac{P_{\lambda,jj}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)\max_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}=o(1).

For the third claim, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}A_{\lambda,ii}^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}^{2}P_{\lambda,ii}^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}+\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}B_{\lambda,ii}^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim{p_{n}^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}^{2}\right)+\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}(\sum_{j\in[n]}P_{W,ij}^{2}P_{\lambda,jj})^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim{p_{n}^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii}^{2}\right)=o(1).

For the fourth claim, we note that

\displaystyle e_{j}(\beta_{0})=\sum_{k\in[n]}M_{W,jk}\tilde{e}_{k}(\beta_{0})+\Pi_{j}\Delta,

and thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2}}\kappa_{ij}e_{j}^{2}(\beta_{0})A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2}}\kappa_{ij}(\sum_{k\in[n]}M_{W,jk}\tilde{e}_{k}(\beta_{0}))^{2}A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2}}\kappa_{ij}\Pi_{j}^{2}\Delta^{2}A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\sum_{i,j,k\in[n]^{3}}\kappa_{ij}M_{W,jk}^{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{2}(\beta_{0})A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)+\left(\frac{\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2}}\kappa_{ij}\Pi_{j}^{2}\Delta^{2}A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}(\beta_{0})A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)+\left(\frac{\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2}}\kappa_{ij}\Pi_{j}^{2}\Delta^{2}A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right),

where last inequality is by construction that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[n]}\kappa_{ij}M_{W,jk}^{2}=1\{i=k\}.

In addition, by Theorem 1 of
Varah (1975)
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{j\in[n]}\sum_{i\in[n]}|\kappa_{ij}|\leq 1/(1/2-\max_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii})\lesssim 1,

(I.1)

which implies

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2}}\kappa_{ij}\Pi_{j}^{2}\Delta^{2}A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)\lesssim(\max_{i\in[n]}A_{\lambda,ii})\left(\max_{j\in[n]}\sum_{i\in[n]}|\kappa_{ij}|\right)||\Pi||_{2}^{2}\Delta^{2}/\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}=o(1).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2}}\kappa_{ij}e_{j}^{2}(\beta_{0})A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}(\beta_{0})A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)+o(1).

In addition, we have

\displaystyle Var\left(\frac{\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2}}\kappa_{ij}e_{j}^{2}(\beta_{0})A_{\lambda,ii}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\underbrace{Var\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}\kappa_{ij}A_{\lambda,ii}\right)(\sum_{k\in[n]}M_{W,jk}\tilde{e}_{k}(\beta_{0}))^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)}_{R_{1}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{Var\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}\kappa_{ij}A_{\lambda,ii}\right)(\sum_{k\in[n]}M_{W,jk}\tilde{e}_{k}(\beta_{0}))\Pi_{j}\Delta}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right),}_{R_{2}}

where

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sum_{k,l\in[n]^{2}}\left[\sum_{j\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}\kappa_{ij}A_{\lambda,ii}\right)(M_{W,jk}M_{W,jl})\right]^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k,l,i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}\in[n]^{6}}\left[\kappa_{ij}A_{\lambda,ii}M_{W,jk}M_{W,jl}\right]\left[\kappa_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}A_{\lambda,i^{\prime}i^{\prime}}M_{W,j^{\prime}k}M_{W,j^{\prime}l}\right]}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}\in[n]^{4}}\kappa_{ij}A_{\lambda,ii}M_{W,jj^{\prime}}^{2}\kappa_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}A_{\lambda,i^{\prime}i^{\prime}}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sum_{j\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}\kappa_{ij}A_{\lambda,ii}\right)^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i,k\in[n]^{2}}A_{\lambda,ii}\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}\kappa_{ij}\kappa_{kj}\right)A_{\lambda,kk}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}A_{\lambda,ii}^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}=o(1),

where the second inequality is by
||M_{W}\circ M_{W}||_{op}\leq 1
and the last inequality is due to the fact that by Section 3 in the Appendix of
Cattaneo
et al. (2018)
, we have

\displaystyle||\kappa^{2}||_{op}

\displaystyle=\left[\lambda_{\min}\left(M_{W}\circ M_{W}\right)\right]^{-2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{2\min_{i\in[n]}(M_{W,ii}(M_{W,ii}-1/2))}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\left[(1-\max_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii})(1/2-\max_{i\in[n]}P_{W,ii})\right]}\lesssim 1.

Next, we have

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in[n]}\frac{(\sum_{i,j\in[n]^{2}}\kappa_{ij}A_{\lambda,ii}M_{W,jk}\Pi_{j}\Delta)^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[n]^{4}}\kappa_{ij}A_{\lambda,ii}\Pi_{j}M_{W,jj^{\prime}}\kappa_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}A_{\lambda,i^{\prime}i^{\prime}}\Pi_{j^{\prime}}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sum_{j\in[n]}(\sum_{i\in[n]}\kappa_{ij}A_{\lambda,ii})^{2}\Pi_{j}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\max_{i\in[n]}A_{\lambda,ii}^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\frac{||\Pi||_{2}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}=o(1),

where we use (
I.1
). This leads to the desired result.

For the fifth claim, we have

\displaystyle Var\left(\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Pi_{i}\Delta\left(P_{\lambda,ij}-\Xi_{\lambda,ij}\right)\tilde{e}_{j}(\beta_{0})}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)

\displaystyle=Var\left(\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n],j\neq i}\Pi_{i}\Delta\left((P_{\lambda,ii}+P_{\lambda,jj})P_{W,ij}-B_{\lambda,ij}\right)\tilde{e}_{j}(\beta_{0})}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{\left[\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq j}\Pi_{i}\left((P_{\lambda,ii}+P_{\lambda,jj})P_{W,ij}-B_{\lambda,ij}\right)\right]^{2}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{\left[\sum_{i\in[n]}\Pi_{i}\left((P_{\lambda,ii}+P_{\lambda,jj})P_{W,ij}-B_{\lambda,ij}\right)\right]^{2}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{\Pi_{j}^{2}\left((P_{\lambda,jj}+P_{\lambda,jj})P_{W,jj}-B_{\lambda,jj}\right)^{2}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}\Pi_{i}P_{\lambda,ii}P_{W,ij}\right)^{2}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}+\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}\Pi_{i}P_{\lambda,jj}P_{W,ij}\right)^{2}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}\Pi_{i}B_{\lambda,ij}\right)^{2}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}+{p_{n}^{\prime}}^{1/2}\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{\Pi_{j}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i,k\in[n]^{2}}\frac{\Pi_{i}P_{\lambda,ii}P_{W,ik}\Pi_{k}P_{\lambda,kk}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}+\sum_{i,k\in[n]^{2}}\frac{\Pi_{i}\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}P_{\lambda,jj}^{2}P_{W,ij}P_{W,kj}\right)\Pi_{k}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,k\in[n]^{2}}\frac{\Pi_{i}\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}B_{\lambda,ij}B_{\lambda,kj}\right)\Pi_{k}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}+o(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,k\in[n]^{2}}\frac{\Pi_{i}[D_{\lambda}P_{W}D_{\lambda}]_{i,k}\Pi_{k}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}+\sum_{i,k\in[n]^{2}}\frac{\Pi_{i}[P_{W}D_{\lambda}^{2}P_{W}]_{i,k}\Pi_{k}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,k\in[n]^{2}}\frac{\Pi_{i}[B_{\lambda}B_{\lambda}]_{i,k}\Pi_{k}\Delta^{2}}{R_{\lambda}}+o(1)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{||D_{\lambda}P_{W}D_{\lambda}||_{op}+||P_{W}D_{\lambda}^{2}P_{W}||_{op}+||B_{\lambda}B_{\lambda}||_{op}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}\frac{||\Pi||_{2}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}+o(1)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{{p_{n}^{\prime}}^{1/2}||\Pi||_{2}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{\lambda}}}+o(1)=o(1),

where we repeated use the fact that

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[n]}B_{\lambda,ii}\leq||B_{\lambda}||_{op}\leq||D_{\lambda}||_{op}=\max_{i\in[n]}P_{\lambda,ii}.

This leads to the desired result.

∎
