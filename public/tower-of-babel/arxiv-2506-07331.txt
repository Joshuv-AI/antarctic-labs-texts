On the stationary Navier-Stokes equations in distorted pipes under energy-stable outflow boundary conditions
Alessio Falocchi – Ana Leonor Silvestre – Gianmarco Sperone

Abstract
The steady motion of a viscous incompressible fluid in distorted pipes, of finite length, is modeled through the Navier-Stokes equations with mixed boundary conditions: the inflow is given by an arbitrary member of the Lions-Magenes class with positive influx, and the fluid motion is subject to a directional do-nothing boundary condition on the outlet, together with the standard no-slip assumption on the remaining walls of the domain. Existence of a weak solution to such Navier-Stokes system is proved without any restriction on the data (that is, inlet velocity and external force) by means of the Leray-Schauder Principle, in which the required a priori estimate is obtained by a contradiction argument that employs Bernoulli’s law for solutions of the stationary Euler equations, as well as some properties of harmonic divergence-free vector fields. Under a suitable smallness assumption on the data, we also prove the unique solvability of the boundary-value problem. Mathematics Subject Classification: 35Q30, 35G60, 76D05, 35M12. Keywords: incompressible flows, mixed boundary conditions, pipes, unrestricted solvability.

Introduction and presentation of the problem

The study of the laminar motion of a viscous incompressible fluid in a junction of pipes
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n=2
or
n=3
), whose bounding walls are rigid and impermeable, represents an essential and ubiquitous subject-matter in Fluid Mechanics
[
34
]
due to its applications in a variety of fields such as aerodynamics
[
50
]
, hemodynamics
[
17
]
and petroleum engineering
[
8
]
. Mathematically
[
32
]
, this motion is traditionally analyzed through the stationary Navier-Stokes equations in
\Omega
; they consist in determining a velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a scalar pressure
p:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following system of Partial Differential Equations in
\Omega
:

-\eta\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p=\boldsymbol{f}\,,\qquad\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0\qquad\text{in}\ \ \ \Omega\,,

(1.1)

where
\eta>0
denotes the (constant) kinematic viscosity coefficient of the fluid and
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents an external force acting on the liquid. Now, whether the junction of pipes
\Omega
is assumed to be unbounded or not, depends on the particular configuration intended to be modeled. On the one hand, problems arising from real-world applications rarely allow for a characterization of the fluid motion at large distances, and their numerical implementation inherently requires a bounded computational domain. On the other hand, some theoretical approaches suggest the approximation of problems, originally formulated in unbounded regions, through the introduction of consecutive
invading domains
; we recall the celebrated method originally proposed by Leray in
[
36
]
for exterior problems in hydrodynamics, and successfully applied by Amick
[
1
]
and Ladyzhenskaya & Solonnikov
[
33
]
in pipes (see also the recent contribution
[
18
]
). In any case, a truncation of the fluid domain necessarily induces
artificial boundaries
in the system of pipes, on which appropriate boundary conditions ought to be prescribed, see the articles by Blazy, Nazarov & Specovius-Neugebauer
[
6
,
38
]
and references therein. The choice of boundary conditions to be imposed on this artificial outlet becomes a delicate question
[
23
]
(in both mathematical and physical terms), because the inflow is usually given, and the effects of viscosity dictate that the fluid velocity must equal zero on the walls of the system of pipes (no-slip assumption). Moreover, such boundary conditions should ensure the well-posedness of the mathematical model and the numerical stability of computational simulations; we refer to
[
7
,
9
,
10
,
15
,
31
,
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
48
]
for a thorough discussion on this topic.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

, be an admissible domain in the sense of Definitions

1.2
. The outward unit normal to

is denoted by

. Henceforth we will refer to
\Gamma_{I}
and

in (

)-(

) as the

of
\Omega

\Gamma_{W}
includes all the
physical walls
of
\Omega
.

Given a (constant) kinematic viscosity coefficient
\eta>0
, an inlet velocity field
\boldsymbol{g}_{*}:\Gamma_{I}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a function
\sigma_{*}:\Gamma_{O}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, by
reference flow
we mean a pair
(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*},\Pi_{*})\in W^{2,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
satisfying the following system in
\Omega
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}=0\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Omega\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}=\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{I}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{W}\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\eta\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}-\Pi_{*}\,\boldsymbol{\nu}=\sigma_{*}\,\boldsymbol{\nu}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{O}\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.4)

whose existence will be proved in Lemma
2.1
. In the present article we analyze the steady motion of a viscous incompressible fluid (having constant kinematic viscosity equal to
\eta
) along
\Omega
, which is characterized by its velocity vector field
\boldsymbol{u}:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and its scalar pressure
p:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, under the action of an external force
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Such stationary motion will be modeled through the the following boundary-value problem (with mixed boundary conditions) associated to the steady-state Navier-Stokes equations in
\Omega
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\eta\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p=\boldsymbol{f}\,,\quad\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Omega\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{I}\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{W}\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\eta\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}-p\,\boldsymbol{\nu}+\dfrac{1}{2}[\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})=\sigma_{*}\,\boldsymbol{\nu}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{O}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.5)

While (
1.5
)
2
prescribes the inlet velocity on
\Gamma_{I}
and (
1.5
)
3
describes the usual no-slip boundary condition on the physical walls
\Gamma_{W}
, identity (
1.5
)
4
dictates that the fluid motion is subject to a
directional do-nothing
(DDN) boundary condition on
\Gamma_{O}
, where

[z]^{-}\doteq\dfrac{|z|-z}{2}\qquad\forall z\in\mathbb{R}\,.

In comparison with the standard
constant traction
or
do-nothing
boundary condition on
\Gamma_{O}
, which reads

\eta\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}-p\,\boldsymbol{\nu}=\sigma_{*}\,\boldsymbol{\nu}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{O}\,,

(1.6)

the additional (non-linear) term appearing in (
1.5
)
4
accounts for the presence of the convection term in the Navier-Stokes equations (
1.5
)
1
, incorporating the reference flow as well. Moreover, as explained in
[
7
]
, condition (
1.5
)
4
has the advantage of being energy-stable and ensuring the uniqueness of the rest state, while, from a computational viewpoint, it helps prevent
backflow
at outflow boundary portions
[
5
,
14
]
. Since the formulation by Gresho
[
21
]
in 1991, the boundary condition (
1.6
) has been widely employed in Computational Fluid Dynamics
[
7
,
23
,
24
,
35
,
47
]
, even though it is well-known that (
1.6
) does not allow for a control of the kinetic energy of the fluid flow, as the possibility of a backwards flow coming into
\Omega
from
\Gamma_{O}
is not excluded, see the works by Kračmar & Neustupa
[
30
,
31
]
. Mathematically, as explained by Galdi in
[
17
, Chapter III]
, this translates into the fact that, up to nowadays, existence of solutions for the Navier-Stokes equations (
1.5
)
1
with boundary conditions (
1.5
)
2
-(
1.5
)
3
-(
1.6
) is known to hold only under a smallness assumption on the data of the problem (inlet velocity and external force). The fundamental goal of the present paper is, indeed, to show the unrestricted solvability (in a sense to be made precise later) of the system (
1.5
).

The method we employ to establish the existence of a weak solution in Theorem
3.2
, the main result of the paper, is based on a contradiction argument that traces back to the seminal work of Leray
[
36
]
. The idea, which uses the Leray-Schauder Principle, has subsequently been extended and adapted in numerous mathematical studies concerning the steady-state Navier-Stokes equations, including the long-standing
flux problem
solved in 2015 by Korobkov, Pileckas & Russo
[
27
]
(see also their recent book
[
29
]
). Here, we shall employ similar arguments to obtain uniform bounds on solutions by assuming the contrary, rescaling the equations, and then finding a solution to the steady Euler equations in
\Omega
through a limiting process. A crucial ingredient is the fact that, as a consequence of the Bernoulli law, the pressure corresponding to the weak solution of the Euler equations takes a constant value on
\Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{W}
. To proceed toward a contradiction, a novel feature of our method relies on a property of some solenoidal harmonic functions (namely, the normal component of the normal derivative is null on
\Gamma_{O}
) established in Corollary
2.1
, which is then combined with the boundary condition of type (
1.5
)
4
on
\Gamma_{O}
to conclude that the pressure of the Euler flow is non-positive on
\Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{W}
. In order to deduce such properties of harmonic velocity fields, the specific geometry of the domain is exploited in the proofs of Lemmas
2.2
-
2.3
. In the final step, the contradiction is derived by testing the Euler equations with
\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}
, the extension constructed in Lemma
2.1
, followed by an integration by parts, where the sign of the flux of
\boldsymbol{g}_{*}
across the inlet
\Gamma_{I}
, along with the specific structure of the energy-stable outflow boundary condition (
1.5
)
4
, play a key role.

2
Preliminary results

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\{2,3\}
, be an admissible domain. Since the portions
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
are given by rectangles (
n=2
) or cylinders (
n=3
), we can define a family of
fully developed flows
, categorized by the fact that the only nonzero component of the associated velocity field is directed along the axis of
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
. Given any
\Phi\geq 0
, consider the following:

(I)
For
n=2
and
j\in\{1,2\}
, introduce the horizontal rectangle
\mathcal{P}_{j}\doteq(0,\ell_{j})\times(-h_{j},h_{j})
. The steady-state
Hagen-Poiseuille flow
associated to
\mathcal{P}_{j}
, having kinematic viscosity
\eta
and flow rate
\Phi
, is characterized by the velocity field
\boldsymbol{U}^{(j)}_{\!\Phi}:\overline{\mathcal{P}_{j}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
and scalar pressure
Q^{(j)}_{\Phi}:\overline{\mathcal{P}_{j}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

\boldsymbol{U}^{(j)}_{\!\Phi}(x,y)\doteq\left(\dfrac{3\Phi}{4h_{j}^{3}}(h_{j}^{2}-y^{2})\,,\,0\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q^{(j)}_{\Phi}(x,y)\doteq-\dfrac{3\eta\Phi}{2h_{j}^{3}}\left(x-\ell_{j}\right)\qquad\forall(x,y)\in\overline{\mathcal{P}_{j}}\,.

(2.1)

(II)
For
n=3
and
j\in\{1,2\}
, introduce the horizontal cylinder
\mathcal{P}_{j}\doteq\Theta_{j}\times(0,\ell_{j})
. Denote by
u_{j}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\Theta_{j};\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Theta_{j}};\mathbb{R})
the unique classical solution to the following torsion problem in
\Theta_{j}
:

-\Delta u_{j}=1\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Theta_{j}\,,\qquad u_{j}=0\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \partial\Theta_{j}\,,

and define

\varrho_{j}\doteq\int_{\Theta_{j}}u_{j}=\int_{\Theta_{j}}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}>0\,.

By the Maximum Principle we have

u_{j}(x,y)\geq 0\qquad\forall(x,y)\in\overline{\Theta_{j}}\,.

(2.2)

The steady-state Hagen-Poiseuille flow associated to
\mathcal{P}_{j}
, having kinematic viscosity
\eta
and flow rate
\Phi
, is characterized by the velocity field
\boldsymbol{U}^{(j)}_{\!\Phi}:\overline{\mathcal{P}_{j}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
and scalar pressure
Q^{(j)}_{\Phi}:\overline{\mathcal{P}_{j}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

\boldsymbol{U}^{(j)}_{\!\Phi}(x,y,z)\doteq\dfrac{\Phi}{\varrho_{j}}(0,0,u_{j}(x,y))\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q^{(j)}_{\Phi}(x,y,z)\doteq-\dfrac{\eta\Phi}{\varrho_{j}}\left(z-\ell_{j}\right)\qquad\forall(x,y,z)\in\overline{\mathcal{P}_{j}}\,.

(2.3)

Now, given the geometry of
\Omega_{j}
, there exists a point
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a rotation matrix
\mathcal{Q}_{j}\in\text{SO}(n)
such that the set
\overline{\Omega_{j}}
can be mapped onto
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{j}}
through the following rigid transformation:

\mathbf{T}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}_{j}+\mathcal{Q}_{j}\,\boldsymbol{x}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\overline{\Omega_{j}}\,.

Then, the steady-state Hagen-Poiseuille flow associated to
\Omega_{j}
, having kinematic viscosity
\eta
and flow rate
\Phi
, can be defined as

\boldsymbol{V}^{(j)}_{\!\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\doteq\mathcal{Q}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}^{(j)}_{\!\Phi}(\mathbf{T}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}))\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\Phi}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{x})\doteq Q^{(j)}_{\!\Phi}(\mathbf{T}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}))\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\overline{\Omega_{j}}\,,

(2.4)

so that

\left\{\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} &-\eta\Delta\boldsymbol{V}^{(j)}_{\!\Phi}+\nabla P^{(j)}_{\Phi}=\boldsymbol{0}\,,\quad\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{V}^{(j)}_{\!\Phi}=0\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Omega_{j}\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\boldsymbol{V}^{(j)}_{\!\Phi}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{W}\cap\partial\Omega_{j}\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\int_{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{(j)}_{\!\Phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}=\Phi\quad\text{for any inner cross-section $\Sigma\subset\overline{\Omega_{j}}$ of $\Omega_{j}$}\,.\end{aligned}\end{aligned}\right.

(2.5)

A smooth reference flow, capturing the inlet velocity on
\Gamma_{I}
and subject to a do-nothing boundary condition on
\Gamma_{O}
, needs to be constructed. For this, recall the
Lions-Magenes class
of inflows having a non-negative influx, defined as

W^{3/2,2}_{+}(\Gamma_{I};\mathbb{R}^{n})\doteq\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\in W^{3/2,2}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \Big|\ \boldsymbol{g}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ \partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{I}\,,\quad\int_{\Gamma_{I}}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}\leq 0\,\right\}\,,

see
[
13
, Chapter VII]
for further details. In other words,
W^{3/2,2}_{+}(\Gamma_{I};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the closed convex subset of
W^{3/2,2}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
comprising restrictions, to
\Gamma_{I}
, of vector fields having non-negative flux across
\Gamma_{I}
and support contained in
\Gamma_{I}
. In fact, given
\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\in W^{3/2,2}_{+}(\Gamma_{I};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we define its
flux across
\Gamma_{I}
as

\Phi_{*}\doteq-\int_{\Gamma_{I}}\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}\geq 0\,.

(2.6)

Additionally, given a bounded Lipschitz domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, consider the space of scalar functions with zero mean value in
D
, defined as

L^{2}_{0}(D;\mathbb{R})\doteq\left\{g\in L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R})\ \Big|\ \int_{D}g=0\right\}\,.

(2.7)

It is well-known (see
[
16
, Section III.3]
and Lemma
2.4
below) that this space plays a fundamental role in the inversion of the divergence operator on
L^{2}_{0}(D;\mathbb{R})
, and therefore, also for the obtainment of pressure estimates in the equations of hydrodynamics in general.

We then prove the following:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\{2,3\}
, be an admissible domain. For any
\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\in W^{3/2,2}_{+}(\Gamma_{I};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\sigma_{*}\in W^{1/2,2}(\Gamma_{O};\mathbb{R})
, there exists a pair
(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*},\Pi_{*})\in W^{2,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}=0\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Omega\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}=\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{I}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{W}\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\eta\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}-\Pi_{*}\,\boldsymbol{\nu}=\sigma_{*}\,\boldsymbol{\nu}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{O}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.8)

Moreover,

\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}=\boldsymbol{V}^{(2)}_{\!\Phi_{*}}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{O}\,,

(2.9)

where
\boldsymbol{V}^{(2)}_{\!\Phi_{*}}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\Omega_{2};\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Omega_{2}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the Hagen-Poiseuille flow defined in (
2.4
), and the estimates

\|\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|_{W^{2,2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{*}\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\Pi_{*}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{*}\|\sigma_{*}\|_{W^{1/2,2}(\Gamma_{O})}\,,

(2.10)

hold for some constant
C_{*}>0
that depends exclusively on
\Omega
and
\eta
.

Proof.
In view of Definitions
1.1
-
1.2
, let us introduce (in local coordinates) the sets

\displaystyle\Omega_{*}\doteq\Omega_{0}\cup\Omega_{1}\cup\left\{(x_{2},y_{2})\in\Omega_{2}\ \Big|\ x_{2}<\dfrac{\ell_{2}}{3}\,\right\}\ \ \text{($n=2$)}\,;

\displaystyle\Omega_{*}\doteq\Omega_{0}\cup\Omega_{1}\cup\left\{(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2})\in\Omega_{2}\ \Big|\ z_{2}<\dfrac{\ell_{2}}{3}\,\right\}\ \ \text{($n=3$)}\,,

together with

Next, we prove two elementary, although useful, results concerning harmonic solenoidal vector fields.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an admissible domain. Let
\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{2,3/2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a harmonic vector field such that

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\ \ \mbox{in}\ \ \Omega\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ \Gamma_{W}\,.

(2.20)

Then,

\boldsymbol{v}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{in}\ \ \overline{\Omega}\,.

(2.21)

Proof.

Introduce the horizontal rectangle
\mathcal{R}_{1}\doteq(0,\ell_{1})\times(-h_{1},h_{1})
, and denote by
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
its outward unit normal. Given the geometry of
\Omega_{1}
, there exists a point
(x_{1},y_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a rotation matrix
\mathcal{Q}_{1}\in\text{SO}(2)
such that the set
\overline{\Omega_{1}}
can be mapped onto
\overline{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
through the following rigid transformation:

\mathbf{T}_{1}(x,y)=(x_{1},y_{1})+\mathcal{Q}_{1}\,(x,y)\qquad\forall(x,y)\in\overline{\Omega_{1}}\,.

Moreover,

\mathbf{T}_{1}(\Gamma_{I})=\{0\}\times(-h_{1},h_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{T}_{1}(\Gamma_{W}\cap\partial\Omega_{1})=((0,\ell_{1})\times\{-h_{1}\})\cup((0,\ell_{1})\times\{h_{1}\})\,.

(2.22)

We are in position to define the vector field
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W^{2,3/2}(\mathcal{R}_{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}(x,y)\doteq\mathcal{Q}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{v}(\mathbf{T}^{-1}_{1}(x,y))\qquad\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\,.

Recalling that the Laplace operator is invariant under rigid transformations, a simple computation and (
2.22
) imply that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}
is a harmonic vector field in
\mathcal{R}_{1}
such that

\nabla\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}=0\ \ \mbox{in}\ \ \mathcal{R}_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ (0,\ell_{1})\times\{\pm h_{1}\}\,.

(2.23)

Decompose
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}=(u_{1},u_{2})
in
\mathcal{R}_{1}
, for some harmonic scalar functions
u_{1},u_{2}\in W^{2,3/2}(\mathcal{R}_{1};\mathbb{R})
. Since
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W^{2,3/2}(\mathcal{R}_{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
(0,\ell_{1})\times\{\pm h_{1}\}
(which is the union of two horizontal segments), we certainly have

\dfrac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial x}=\dfrac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial x}=0\quad\text{on}\ \ (0,\ell_{1})\times\{\pm h_{1}\}\,.

(2.24)

Additionally, as
\nabla\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}=0
in
\mathcal{R}_{1}
, (
2.24
) implies that

\dfrac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\dfrac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial y}=0\quad\text{on}\ \ (0,\ell_{1})\times\{\pm h_{1}\}\,.

(2.25)

In conclusion, we have that
u_{2}
is a harmonic function in
\mathcal{R}_{1}
such that

u_{2}=\dfrac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}=0\quad\text{on}\ \ (0,\ell_{1})\times\{\pm h_{1}\}\,,

from where it follows that
u_{2}\equiv 0
in
\overline{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
(see, for example,
[
19
, Exercise 2.2]
). From the divergence-free condition we infer that the harmonic function
u_{1}
must be an affine linear function depending only on the variable
y\in[-h_{1},h_{1}]
. The boundary condition in (
2.23
) then guarantees that
u_{1}\equiv 0
in
\overline{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
. In conclusion, we have proved
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
in
\overline{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
, meaning that
\boldsymbol{v}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
in
\overline{\Omega_{1}}
. As
\boldsymbol{v}
is harmonic in
\Omega
, this further implies the claim (
2.21
).

\Box

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an admissible domain. Let
\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{2,3/2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
be a harmonic vector field such that

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\ \ \mbox{in}\ \ \Omega\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ \Gamma_{I}\,.

(2.29)

Then,

\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{*}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{in}\ \ \overline{\Omega}\,.

(2.30)

Proof.

Introduce the horizontal cylinder
\mathcal{P}_{1}\doteq\Theta_{1}\times(0,\ell_{1})
, and denote by
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
its outward unit normal. Given the geometry of
\Omega_{1}
, there exists a point
(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a rotation matrix
\mathcal{Q}_{1}\in\text{SO}(3)
such that the set
\overline{\Omega_{1}}
can be mapped onto
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{1}}
through the following rigid transformation:

\mathbf{T}_{1}(x,y,z)=(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})+\mathcal{Q}_{1}\,(x,y,z)\qquad\forall(x,y,z)\in\overline{\Omega_{1}}\,.

Moreover,

\mathbf{T}_{1}(\Gamma_{I})=\Theta_{1}\times\{0\}\,,\quad\mathbf{T}_{1}(\Gamma_{W}\cap\partial\Omega_{1})=\partial\Theta_{1}\times(0,\ell_{1})\,,\quad\mathcal{Q}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{*}=(0,0,1)\,.

(2.31)

We are in position to define the vector field
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W^{2,3/2}(\mathcal{P}_{1};\mathbb{R}^{3})
by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}(x,y,z)\doteq\mathcal{Q}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{v}(\mathbf{T}^{-1}_{1}(x,y,z))\qquad\forall(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{P}_{1}\,.

(2.32)

Recalling that the Laplace operator is invariant under rigid transformations, a simple computation and (
2.31
) imply that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}
is a harmonic vector field in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
such that

\nabla\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}=0\ \ \mbox{in}\ \ \mathcal{P}_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ \Theta_{1}\times\{0\}\,.

(2.33)

Decompose
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
, for some harmonic functions
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\in W^{2,3/2}(\mathcal{P}_{1};\mathbb{R})
. Given that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W^{2,3/2}(\mathcal{P}_{1};\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\Theta_{1}\times\{0\}
(which is a planar domain contained in the

\dfrac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial x}=\dfrac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial y}=0\quad\text{on}\ \ \Theta_{1}\times\{0\}\,.

(2.34)

Additionally, as
\nabla\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}=0
in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
, (
2.34
) implies that

\dfrac{\partial u_{3}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}=-\dfrac{\partial u_{3}}{\partial z}=\dfrac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial x}+\dfrac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial y}=0\quad\text{on}\ \ \Theta_{1}\times\{0\}\,.

(2.35)

In conclusion, we have that
u_{3}
is a harmonic function in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
such that

u_{3}=\dfrac{\partial u_{3}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}=0\quad\text{on}\ \ \Theta_{1}\times\{0\}\,,

from where it follows that
u_{3}\equiv 0
in
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{1}}
(see, again,
[
19

\boldsymbol{v}(x,y,z)\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{*}=\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}(\mathbf{T}_{1}(x,y,z))\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{*}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}(\mathbf{T}_{1}(x,y,z))\cdot(0,0,1)=0\qquad\forall(x,y,z)\in\overline{\Omega_{1}}\,.

As
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{*}
is also harmonic in
\Omega
, this further implies the claim (
2.30
).

By merging the results of Lemmas
2.2
-
2.3
we derive the following:

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\{2,3\}
, be an admissible domain. Let
\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{2,3/2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a harmonic vector field such that

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\ \ \mbox{in}\ \ \Omega\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ \Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{W}\,.

(2.36)

Then,

\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}=0\quad\text{on}\ \ \Gamma_{O}\,.

(2.37)

Notice that the directional do-nothing boundary condition (
1.5
)
4
forbids us from modifying the scalar pressure in (
1.5
) by an additive constant. In particular, in contrast with the standard case of Dirichlet boundary conditions, we can no longer assume that the scalar pressure is an element of
L_{0}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
, see (
2.7
). Another essential preliminary result, therefore, concerns the inversion of the divergence operator on the space
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
, which is achieved by properly adapting the classical results involving
L_{0}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
(see, for example,
[
16
, Section III.3]
) to the geometry of our setting. Inspired by
[
28
, Lemma 4.2]
, we now prove the following:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\{2,3\}
, be an admissible domain. Given
q\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
, there exists a vector field
\boldsymbol{X}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{X}=q\ \ \mbox{in}\ \ \Omega\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ \Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{W}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\nabla\boldsymbol{X}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{*}\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

(2.38)

for some constant
C_{*}>0
that depends exclusively on
\Omega
.

Proof.

In what follows,
C>0
will always denote a generic constant that depends exclusively on
\Omega
, but that may change from line to line.

For
j\in\{1,2\}
, let
\boldsymbol{V}^{(j)}_{\!1}\in W^{2,2}(\Omega_{j};\mathbb{R}^{n})
be as in (
2.4
), with
\eta=\Phi=1
. Also, consider the unique weak solution
(\boldsymbol{Z},\Pi)\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L_{0}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
to the following Stokes system in
\Omega
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta\boldsymbol{Z}+\nabla\Pi=\boldsymbol{0}\,,\quad\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{Z}=0\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Omega\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{V}^{(1)}_{\!1}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{I}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{V}^{(2)}_{\!1}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{O}\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{W}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Therefore,

\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\boldsymbol{Z}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}=1\,.

(2.39)

Now, let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open ball such that

\overline{\Omega_{2}}\subsetneq B\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{\Omega_{1}}\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{B}\,.

Accordingly, take a cutoff function
\zeta\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(B;[0,1])
such that
\zeta\equiv 1
in
\overline{\Omega_{2}}
; in particular,

\zeta=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ \Gamma_{I}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\zeta=1\ \ \text{on}\ \ \Gamma_{O}\,.

Then, introduce the vector field

\boldsymbol{Q}\doteq\left(\int_{\Omega}q\right)\zeta\,\boldsymbol{Z}\qquad\text{in}\ \ \Omega\,,

(2.40)

which is clearly an element of
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{W}
, together with the estimate

\|\nabla\boldsymbol{Q}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

(2.41)

On the other hand, from the Divergence Theorem and (
2.39
) we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}=\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\boldsymbol{Q}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}=\left(\int_{\Omega}q\right)\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\boldsymbol{Z}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}=\int_{\Omega}q\,,

so that
q-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
. Then, standard results such as
[
45
, Lemma 1]
(see also
[
29
, Lemma 1.8.13]
) ensure the existence of another vector field
\boldsymbol{Y}\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{Y}=q-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}\ \ \text{in}\ \ \Omega\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\nabla\boldsymbol{Y}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|q-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

(2.42)

We set
\boldsymbol{X}\doteq\boldsymbol{Y}+\boldsymbol{Q}
which, in view of (
2.41
)-(
2.42
), is an element of
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying (
2.38
).

3
Unrestricted solvability of the boundary-value problem

We introduce the functional spaces (of vector fields) that will be employed hereafter:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\Omega)\doteq\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ |\ \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\ \ \mbox{in}\ \ \Omega\,,\quad\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ \Gamma_{W}\right\}\,,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)\doteq\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ |\ \boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ \Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{W}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\doteq\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ |\ \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\ \ \mbox{in}\ \ \Omega\,,\quad\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ \Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{W}\right\}\,,

which are Hilbert spaces if endowed with the Dirichlet scalar product of the gradients, denoted by

[\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}]_{\Omega}\doteq\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\,.

(3.2)

We recall the definition of weak solutions for the velocity component of problem (
1.5
):

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\{2,3\}
, be an admissible domain. Let
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
be an external force,
\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\in W^{3/2,2}_{+}(\Gamma_{I};\mathbb{R}^{n})
an inflow,
\sigma_{*}\in W^{1/2,2}(\Gamma_{O};\mathbb{R})
and
(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*},\Pi_{*})\in W^{2,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
the reference flow from Lemma
2.1
. A vector field
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
is called a
weak solution
of problem (
1.5
) if
\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
and

\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})\,,

(3.3)

for every
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
.

Remark 3.1
.

We emphasize that the integrals over
\Gamma_{O}
appearing in the weak formulation (
3.3
) are bounded in the functional spaces considered, as the trace embedding
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{4}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
(which holds for
n\in\{2,3\}
) yields

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{4}(\Gamma_{O})}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|_{L^{4}(\Gamma_{O})}\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{O})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

for every
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi})\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)\times\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
, for some constant
C_{*}>0
that depends exclusively on
\Omega
.

In the next result we explain how a uniquely defined scalar pressure can be associated to the velocity component of any weak solution to problem (
1.5
). Moreover, we prove that the resulting pair is a strong solution of (
1.5
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\{2,3\}
, be an admissible domain. Given an external force
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\in W^{3/2,2}_{+}(\Gamma_{I};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\sigma_{*}\in W^{1/2,2}(\Gamma_{O};\mathbb{R})
, suppose that
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
is a weak solution of problem (
1.5
), in the sense of Definition (
3.1
). Then, there exists a unique pressure
p\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
such that

\displaystyle\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\int_{\Omega}p(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)\,.

Moreover,
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in W^{2,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
, so that the pair
(\boldsymbol{u},p)
satisfies (
1.5
)
1
in strong sense in
\Omega
. The boundary conditions for
\boldsymbol{u}
in (
1.5
)
2
-(
1.5
)
3
are verified in the sense of
W^{3/2,2}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, while the boundary condition (
1.5
)
4
is verified in the sense of
W^{1/2,2}(\Gamma_{O};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Additionally, there holds the bound

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{W^{2,3/2}(\Omega)}+\|p\|_{W^{1,3/2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\Big(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(3.5)

\displaystyle+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}+\|\sigma_{*}\|_{W^{1/2,2}(\Gamma_{O})}\Big)\,,

for some constant
C_{*}>0
that depends exclusively on
\Omega
and
\eta
.

Proof.

We follow closely the proof of
[
16
, Theorem III.5.3]
(see also
[
20
, Lemma 2.1]
). Denote by
\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
the dual space of
\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)
(see (
3.1
)
2
) and by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)}
the
duality product
between
\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)
. The adjoint of the (strong) divergence operator
\text{div}_{\Omega}:\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)\longrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
is defined by the map
\text{div}^{*}_{\Omega}:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
such that

\langle\text{div}^{*}_{\Omega}(q),\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)}\doteq\int_{\Omega}q(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})\qquad\forall q\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)\,.

Therefore, the Closed Range Theorem of Banach
[
51
, Chapter VII]
can be applied to deduce that

\text{Range}(\text{div}^{*}_{\Omega})=\text{Ker}(\text{div}_{\Omega})^{\perp}=\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)^{\perp}\doteq\{\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Omega)\ |\ \langle\mathcal{F},\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)}=0\quad\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\,\}\,.

Given a weak solution
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
of (
1.5
), we define

\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\doteq\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)\,.

Due to (
3.3
),
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\text{Range}(\text{div}^{*}_{\Omega})
. This ensures the existence of a pressure
p\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
verifying (
3.4
). Suppose there exists another function
q\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
satisfying (
3.4
), thereby implying

\int_{\Omega}(p-q)(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})=0\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)\,.

(3.6)

Lemma
2.4
provides the existence of a vector field
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{H}_{*}(\Omega)
such that
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{X}=p-q
in
\Omega
. Taking
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{X}
in (
3.6
) ensures that
p=q
almost everywhere in
\Omega
.

Define
\boldsymbol{v}\doteq\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}
and
q\doteq p-\Pi_{*}
, so that
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
satisfies the system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\eta\Delta\boldsymbol{v}+\nabla q=\boldsymbol{f}+\eta\Delta\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}-\nabla\Pi_{*}-(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}-(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}-(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}-(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Omega\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Omega\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{W}\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\eta\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}-q\,\boldsymbol{\nu}+\dfrac{1}{2}[(\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{O}\end{aligned}\right.

(3.7)

in weak form. Now, the Sobolev embedding
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{6}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
implies
(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{3/2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and as consequence, that

\boldsymbol{F}\doteq\boldsymbol{f}+\eta\Delta\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}-\nabla\Pi_{*}-(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}-(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}-(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}-(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{3/2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\,.

From the Hölder, Sobolev and Young inequalities we infer the estimate

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{F}\|_{L^{3/2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|_{W^{2,2}(\Omega)}+\|\Pi_{*}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\,.

(3.8)

Now, the restriction of
\boldsymbol{\nu}
to
\Gamma_{O}
is a constant vector, denoted by
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We certainly have

[(\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{*}]^{-}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})\,,

together with

\|\nabla[(\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{*}]^{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

(3.9)

By Sobolev embedding there also holds
[(\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{*}]^{-}\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{1,3/2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. From (
3.9
) and the inequalities of Hölder, Sobolev and Young we obtain the bound

\displaystyle\|\nabla([(\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{*}]^{-}\boldsymbol{v})\|_{L^{3/2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\,.

(3.10)

In particular, the previous observations imply that

\boldsymbol{h}\doteq-\dfrac{1}{2}[(\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{\frac{1}{3},\frac{3}{2}}(\Gamma_{O};\mathbb{R}^{n})\,,

with

\|\boldsymbol{h}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{3},\frac{3}{2}}(\Gamma_{O})}\leq C\|\nabla([(\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}\boldsymbol{v})\|_{L^{3/2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\,,

(3.11)

as a consequence of the trace inequality and (
3.10
). In conclusion, the pair
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
can be considered to be a weak solution to the following Stokes system in
\Omega
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\eta\Delta\boldsymbol{v}+\nabla q=\boldsymbol{F}\,,\quad\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Omega\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{W}\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\eta\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}-q\,\boldsymbol{\nu}=\boldsymbol{h}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{O}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.12)

Now, let
\boldsymbol{\chi}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the unique weak solution to the following Zaremba problem in
\Omega
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta\boldsymbol{\chi}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Omega\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\boldsymbol{\chi}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{W}\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{\chi}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\dfrac{1}{\eta}\boldsymbol{h}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{O}\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(3.13)

whose existence follows as a simple application of the Lax-Milgram Theorem. Since the sets
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
correspond to rectangles (
n=2
) or cylinders (
n=3
), and the boundary portions
\Gamma_{W}
and
\Gamma_{O}
meet at a right angle, regularity results such as
[
22
, Chapter 4]
(for
n=2
) and
[
37
, Corollary 8.3.2]
(for
n=3
; see also
[
44
]
for a relevant discussion in both cases

) ensure that
\boldsymbol{\chi}\in W^{2,3/2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, together with

\|\boldsymbol{\chi}\|_{W^{2,3/2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{h}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{3},\frac{3}{2}}(\Gamma_{O})}\,.

(3.14)

We then set
\boldsymbol{z}\doteq\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\chi}
in
\Omega
, so that, due to (
3.12
)-(
3.13
), the pair
(\boldsymbol{z},q)\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
constitutes a weak solution to the following Stokes system in
\Omega
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\eta\Delta\boldsymbol{z}+\nabla q=\boldsymbol{F}\,,\quad\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{z}=-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\chi}\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Omega\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{W}\,,\\[5.0pt]
&\eta\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{z}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\nu}}-q\,\boldsymbol{\nu}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \ \Gamma_{O}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.15)

We can then invoke
[
40
, Theorem 3.1]
and
[
3
, Corollary A.3]
(when

and
n=3
, respectively, see also
[
4
, Theorem A.1]
for a closely related result when

; notice that the non-zero divergence condition in (

)
1
is irrelevant from a regularity perspective, as shown by
[

, Equation (A.10)]
and
[

, Equation (A.6)]
) to deduce that
(\boldsymbol{z},q)\in W^{2,3/2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times W^{1,3/2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
, together with

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{W^{2,3/2}(\Omega)}+\|q\|_{W^{1,3/2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|\boldsymbol{F}\|_{L^{3/2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\chi}\|_{W^{1,3/2}(\Omega)}\right)

(3.16)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|_{W^{2,2}(\Omega)}+\|\Pi_{*}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\,,

where the second inequality is obtained as a consequence of (

)-(

)-(

). Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{W^{2,3/2}(\Omega)}+\|q\|_{W^{1,3/2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(3.17)

\displaystyle+\|\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\|_{W^{2,2}(\Omega)}+\|\Pi_{*}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}\Big)\,.

Recalling that
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}
and

in

, (

) then follows directly from (

)-(

). Afterwards, a standard bootstrap argument enables us to deduce that

.

The main result of this article guarantees the unrestricted solvability (concerning the size of the data) of problem (
1.5
).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\{2,3\}
, be an admissible domain. Given an external force
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\in W^{3/2,2}_{+}(\Gamma_{I};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\sigma_{*}\in W^{1/2,2}(\Gamma_{O};\mathbb{R})
, there exists at least one weak solution
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
of problem (
1.5
), in the sense of Definition (
3.1
).

Proof.
To prove the existence of a weak solution
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
of (
1.5
) amounts to show the existence of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}\left[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}+(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})

(3.18)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})-\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\,,

so that the solution will be given by
\boldsymbol{u}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}
. For a fixed
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
, the applications

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\longmapsto\int_{\Omega}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\longmapsto\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})-\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}

clearly define linear continuous functionals on
\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
, see Remark
3.1
. Then, in view of the Riesz Representation Theorem, the identity (
3.18
) may be written as

[\eta\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})-\mathcal{F},\boldsymbol{\varphi}]_{\Omega}=0\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\,,

see (
3.2
), for some (unique) elements
\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}),\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle[\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}),\boldsymbol{\varphi}]_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,,

\displaystyle[\mathcal{F},\boldsymbol{\varphi}]_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})-\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\,.

We have so defined an operator
\mathcal{P}:\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
and we are led to find a solution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
of the following nonlinear operator equation:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+\dfrac{1}{\eta}(\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})-\mathcal{F})=0\quad\text{in}\ \ \mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\,.

(3.19)

Exactly as in
[
32
, Chapter 5, Theorem 1]
one can show that the operator
\mathcal{P}
is compact. Therefore, as a consequence of the Leray-Schauder Principle
[
52
, Chapter 6]
, in order to prove that (
3.19
) possesses at least one solution, it suffices to guarantee that any
\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
such that

\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}+\dfrac{\lambda}{\eta}(\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda})-\mathcal{F})=0\quad\text{in}\ \ \mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\,,

(3.20)

is uniformly bounded with respect to
\lambda\in[0,1]
. From now on,
C>0
will denote a generic constant that depends on
\Omega
and
\eta
(independent of
\lambda\in[0,1])
, but that may change from line to line.

Given
\lambda\in[0,1]
and
\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
such that (
3.20
) holds, we clearly have

\displaystyle\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\lambda\int_{\Omega}\left[(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}+(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}+(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}

(3.21)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\lambda\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})-\lambda\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\lambda\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\,.

A standard integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}|\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}|^{2}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}|\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}|^{2}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})\,.

(3.22)

After putting
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}
in (
3.21
) and enforcing (
3.22
), the following identity is obtained:

\displaystyle\eta\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}+\lambda\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})-\lambda\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}-\lambda\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle-\lambda\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}-\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\left((\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}+[(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}\right)|\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}|^{2}\,,

so that

\displaystyle\eta\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}+\lambda\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})-\lambda\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}-\lambda\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}

(3.23)

\displaystyle-\lambda\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\lambda}\qquad\forall\lambda\in[0,1]\,.

The integral identity (
3.21
), together with Theorem
3.1
, guarantee the existence of a unique pressure
p^{\lambda}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
such that

4.7
), together with (
2.10
), we can further bound in the following way:

\displaystyle\eta\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})-\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}:\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\int_{\Omega}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\sigma_{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{O})}+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|^{2}_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}\right)\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\dfrac{M_{*}}{S_{*}}\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

that is,

\left(\eta-\dfrac{M_{*}}{S_{*}}\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}\right)\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\sigma_{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{O})}+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|^{2}_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}\right)\,,

(4.9)

from where the desired estimate (
4.2
) is readily obtained, under the assumption (
4.1
) and with the choice of
\omega_{*}>0
given in (
4.4
).

\Box

We are now in position to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\{2,3\}
, be an admissible domain. Let
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
be an external force,
\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\in W^{3/2,2}_{+}(\Gamma_{I};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\sigma_{*}\in W^{1/2,2}(\Gamma_{O};\mathbb{R})
. There exists
\varrho_{*}>0
, depending exclusively on
\Omega
and
\eta
, such that, if

\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}+\|\sigma_{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{O})}<\varrho_{*}\,,

(4.10)

then the problem (
1.5
) admits a unique weak solution in the sense of Definition (
3.1
).

Proof.
Under the assumptions of the statement, the existence of (at least) one weak solution
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
to problem (
1.5
) is ensured by Theorem
3.2
. Now, suppose there exists another weak solution
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
to problem (
1.5
). Define
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\doteq\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\doteq\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}
in
\Omega
, so that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
satisfy the identities

\displaystyle\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}\left[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}+(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})-\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)

and

\displaystyle\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}\left[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}+(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\sigma_{*}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})-\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\,.

see (
4.5
). Define now
\boldsymbol{z}\doteq\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}=\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}
in
\Omega
, so that
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)
, and by taking the difference between the integral identities (
4.11
)-(
4.12
), we observe that

\displaystyle\eta\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{z}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}\left[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}-(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{\Omega}\left[(\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}+(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{z}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}

(4.13)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}+\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{V}_{*}(\Omega)\,.

We take
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{z}
in (
4.13
), and recalling the first identity in (
4.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\eta\|\nabla\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\left[(\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{z}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{z}-\int_{\Omega}\left[(\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}+(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{z}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{z}

(4.14)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}[\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}|\boldsymbol{z}|^{2}(\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})\,.

Observe that, almost everywhere on
\Gamma_{O}
, there holds the following:

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})-[\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})-(\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})|\boldsymbol{z}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left([\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}-[\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}\right)(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})|\boldsymbol{z}|^{2}-\left([\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})\right)|\boldsymbol{z}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left([\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}-[\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}\right)(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})|\boldsymbol{z}|^{2}\,,

which, once inserted into the right-hand side of (
4.14
), entails

\displaystyle\eta\|\nabla\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\Omega}\left[(\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{z}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{z}-\int_{\Omega}\left[(\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}+(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{z}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{z}

(4.15)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\left([\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}-[\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}\right)(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{O}}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})|\boldsymbol{z}|^{2}\,.

By the Hölder, Poincaré and Sobolev inequalities, together with (
2.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\left[(\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{z}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{z}+\int_{\Omega}\left[(\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}+(\boldsymbol{W}_{\!\!*}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{z}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{z}\right|

(4.16)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}\right)\|\nabla\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

On the other hand, since

\left|[r]^{-}-[s]^{-}\right|\leq|r-s|\qquad\forall r,s\in\mathbb{R}\,,

we may employ the trace inequality (see Remark
3.1
) to estimate the boundary integrals appearing on the right-hand side of (
4.15
) in the following way:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Gamma_{O}}\left([\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}-[\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{-}\right)(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})+\int_{\Gamma_{O}}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})|\boldsymbol{z}|^{2}\right|\leq\int_{\Gamma_{O}}|\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}||\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{z}|+\int_{\Gamma_{O}}|\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}||\boldsymbol{z}|^{2}

(4.17)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{O})}+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{O})}\right)\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Gamma_{O})}\leq C\left(\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}\right)\|\nabla\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

Upon insertion of (
4.16
)-(
4.17
) into the right-hand side of (
4.15
), we deduce that

\|\nabla\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}\right)\|\nabla\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

(4.18)

Now, let
\omega_{*}>0
be as in the statement of Lemma
4.1
. For any
\varrho_{*}\in(0,\omega_{*})
, under the assumption (
4.10
) we can certainly claim that the condition (
4.1
) is fulfilled, implying that (
4.2
) holds. Inserted into the right-hand side of (
4.18
), this means that

\|\nabla\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\sigma_{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{O})}+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{*}\|^{2}_{W^{3/2,2}(\Gamma_{I})}\right)\|\nabla\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

(4.19)

Thus, denoting by
M_{*}>0
the constant entering the right-hand side of (
4.19
), and choosing
\varrho_{*}\in(0,\omega_{*})
in such a way that
M_{*}(\varrho_{*}+\varrho^{2}_{*})<1
, we reach the desired conclusion under the assumption (
4.10
).
