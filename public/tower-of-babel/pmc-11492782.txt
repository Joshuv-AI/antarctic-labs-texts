Fixed-bearing medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty restores pre-arthritic coronal alignment and achieves satisfactory functional outcomes: a retrospective study
Authors: Yuhu Zhao, Xiaolong Liang, Yijun Wang, Xiaoheng Lu, Chengyao Lu, Yaozeng Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to (1) analyze the Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification in patients undergoing unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA), (2) assess whether UKA can restore pre-arthritic coronal alignment by utilizing the arithmetic hip-knee-ankle angle (aHKA), and (3) evaluate the relationship between patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and postoperative alignment following UKA.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 152 consecutive patients who underwent fixed-bearing medial UKA. A radiological analysis was conducted using an EOS imaging system. Postoperative alignment was classified as neutral (varus ≤ 3°), mild varus (3° < varus ≤ 7°), or marked varus (varus > 7°) based on the mechanical hip-knee-ankle angle (mHKA). The aHKA was calculated and the CPAK classification was used to categorize knee phenotypes. The PROMs were obtained both before and after the surgery.ResultsA total of 152 knees from 113 patients were included with a mean two-year follow-up. The mean difference between the postoperative mHKA and the estimated aHKA was 0.80° (90% CI 0.35 to 1.24; P = 0.003). Postoperatively, 63 (41.45%) of the 152 knees were in neutral group, 54 (35.53%) mild varus, and 35 (23.03%) marked varus. The neutrally aligned cohort did not exhibit significantly higher scores in range of motion, VAS, HSS, WOMAC, or FJS-12 scores compared to the mild or marked varus cohort (P = 0.205, 0.118, 0.076, 0.140, and 0.788, respectively) during the short-term follow-up.ConclusionNon-robotically assisted, fixed-bearing medial UKA can restore pre-arthritic coronal alignment and achieve satisfactory PROMs. Fixed-bearing medial UKA aims to restore the pre-arthritic alignment rather than achieving neutral mechanical alignment.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is often associated with malalignment and deformities of the lower limb. Alignment influences load distribution at the knee and disease progression in OA [1]. Anteromedial osteoarthritis (AMOA) is the most common type of knee osteoarthritis in clinical settings, affecting over 85% of all patients [2].

Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) is an effective treatment and an alternative to total knee arthroplasty (TKA) with shorter recovery time and improved patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) [3–7]. Despite its advantages, the optimal alignment, strategies for alignment restoration, and the factors that affect postoperative alignment and clinical outcomes after medial UKA remain controversial [8–14]. Surgical errors and inaccurate placement of components are associated with over-correction or under-correction of lower limb alignment, which can ultimately lead to failure after UKA [15, 16]. Unfavorable consequences, such as increased polyethylene wear and deterioration of the non-arthroplasty side, may be associated with malalignment [9, 17, 18].

The belief that mechanically neutral limb alignment is optimal for all patients is increasingly being questioned [19–21]. Meanwhile, there has been increasing interest in restoring constitutional (or pre-arthritic) alignment in recent years. However, determining the constitutional alignment was challenging without pre-arthritic long-leg radiographs, which are inaccessible in most cases. As a possible alternative, the arithmetic hip-knee-ankle angle (aHKA) and Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification were recently introduced [22–24]. The CPAK classification is a practical and comprehensive categorization for coronal knee alignment, based on aHKA and joint line obliquity (JLO) [24]. The aHKA has been confirmed to estimate the constitutional limb alignment in arthritic patients [22, 23]. Therefore, whether a limb achieves pre-arthritic alignment postoperatively can be estimated by post-operative mHKA and pre-operative aHKA. However, the application of aHKA in UKA and the utilization of the CPAK classification in patients undergoing UKA are limited.

Therefore, the present study aimed to (1) demonstrate the CPAK classification of patients undergoing UKA, (2) analyze whether fixed-bearing medial UKA can restore pre-arthritic coronal limb alignment, and (3) estimate clinical assessment and PROMs between mechanically neutral aligned (varus ≤ 3°), mild varus (3° < varus ≤ 7°), and marked varus (varus > 7°) groups. The hypothesis of this study was that fixed-bearing medial UKA with standard surgical techniques can restore pre-arthritic coronal alignment and achieve satisfactory PROMs.

Materials and methods

Patient enrollment

For this Institutional Review Board-approved study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of consecutive patients who underwent UKA by our team between May 1, 2020, and July 31, 2023, at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. The surgical inclusion criteria comprised end-stage isolated medial compartment osteoarthritis or spontaneous osteonecrosis of the knee, with an intact medial collateral ligament and anterior cruciate ligament. The surgical exclusion criteria included Kellgren and Lawrence grade (K-L grade) III-IV osteoarthritis in the lateral compartment, active infection, and inflammatory disease. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they underwent primary fixed-bearing medial UKA and had complete radiological data. Patients were excluded if they had a history of joint arthroplasty or fracture on the operative side, lacked preoperative and postoperative radiograph records, if the records were of poor quality, or if they declined to participate. In total, 113 patients (152 UKAs) met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

Surgical technique

All enrolled patients underwent a non-robotically assisted, cemented fixed-bearing medial UKA (Smith & Nephew), performed by a team led by an experienced, high-volume senior surgeon (Y. X) at our institution. During the procedure, accessible osteophytes were removed, but ligaments were not released. A tibial resection was made first at 90° to the tibia anatomical axis using an extramedullary cutting guide. A flexion gap of 10–11 mm was preferred after tibial resection, with the tibial component accounting for 8–9 mm, resulting in a 2 mm gap. The tibial component was intended to have a posterior slope of approximately 5–7°. Subsequently, the extension gap was measured, and a distal femoral osteotomy was performed. The amount of the distal femoral osteotomy could be modified (adjusted by 1–2 mm more or less) to achieve balance in the flexion and extension gap. The femoral prosthesis was positioned 1–2 mm lateral to the center. Then, complete the posterior and chamfer resections of the femur. Before the cementation of the definitive prostheses, trial prostheses were implanted, and alignment, stability, flexion and extension gap, and range of motion (ROM) were assessed. By removing accessible osteophytes, performing osteotomy, and inserting an appropriate thickness insert to achieve a 2 mm medial gap in flexion and extension, normal tension and ligament balance can be restored. Consequently, the restoration of normal knee kinematics and pre-arthritic alignment is achieved.

Data collection and functional scores

Preoperative data collection included age, sex, BMI, history of symptoms, linear pain visual analog scale (VAS) score, knee ROM, and radiographic measurements. Perioperative data were recorded, including operative duration, the difference in hemoglobin (Hb) levels before and after the operation, and length of hospital stays post-surgery. The clinical function was assessed preoperatively and postoperatively using the PROMs, including the VAS, Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) score, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) score, and Forgotten Joint Score-12 (FJS-12). The potential complications associated with the UKA procedure, including deep venous thrombosis (DVT), fracture, dislocation, and infection, were analyzed. The overall satisfaction was rated on a scale of 1 to 5 points, ranging from “very unsatisfied” to “very satisfied”.

Radiographic evaluation

EOS® biplanar X-ray imaging was used to acquire radiographs of the lower extremities in standing full-length, anteroposterior, and lateral views both before and after surgery. For each knee included in the series, the mechanical hip-knee-ankle angle (mHKA), lateral distal femur angle (LDFA), medial proximal tibia angle (MPTA), joint line convergence angle (JLCA), and posterior tibial slope (PTS) were measured (Fig. 2). The aHKA and JLO were calculated: aHKA = MPTA - LDFA, JLO = MPTA + LDFA. The CPAK classification, which consists of nine subgroups based on a combination of aHKA and JLO, was performed [24].

Data analyses

Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess normal distribution. For variables that followed a normal distribution, the data are expressed as means with standard deviations, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to compare outcomes. In contrast, for continuous variables that exhibited a nonparametric distribution, the data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and the Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized to compare outcomes. The Bonferroni correction was used as a post hoc test for the multivariate analysis. To compare categorical variables, chi-square test was employed. The equivalence of the calculated pre-arthritic alignment parameters and the postoperative mechanical alignment of the operated knee was assessed using a two one-sided t-test (TOST) of paired data [25]. All P values were two-tailed with a significance level of 0.05. Data analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0, SPSS Inc., USA).

Results

In total, 152 fixed-bearing medial UKAs in 113 patients were included. Among them, there were 39 (34.5%) underwent bilateral UKAs, and 35 (31.0%) underwent simultaneous bilateral UKAs.

The CPAK classification

The various CPAK knee phenotypes were analyzed in this study. The most common category was Type I (53.95%). In addition, Types IV (21.05%) and II (19.74%) were observed to account for a large proportion. The traditional ideal neutral knee (namely Type V in the CPAK classification) only accounts for 0.66% in our study (Fig. 3).

According to the CPAK classification, 75.66% of patients in our study had constitutional varus knee (aHKA < -2°). While using the traditional mHKA as an evaluation index, 96.71% of the patients had varus knees (mHKA < 180°), with 85.53% exhibiting varus alignment of more than 3 degrees. In addition, 75.01% of patients had preoperative apex distal JLO (JLO < 177°).

The restoration of pre-arthritic alignment

To facilitate comparison, the estimated pre-arthritic alignment parameter—preoperative aHKA (pre-aHKA)—was calculated as 180° + MPTA - LDFA. This calculation aligns with the CPAK classification. The equivalence margins were set at 0 ± 2° [22]. According to the TOST for statistical equivalence, the mean difference between the mean postoperative mHKA (post-mHKA) of the operative knees and the mean estimated pre-aHKA was 0.80° (90% CI 0.35 to 1.24; P = 0.003) (Table 1).

According to Plancher’s definition, where pre-arthritic alignment is defined as a postoperative mHKA within 3° of the aHKA [12]. In our study, a total of 93 knees (61.68%) were found to be pre-arthritically aligned after the surgery, with 67 (44.08%) falling within 2° of pre-arthritic alignment. In the 93 cases that restored the pre-arthritic alignment, only 37 (39.78%) postoperative alignment fell within the neutrally aligned group, while the remaining 56 (60.22%) were within mild or marked varus groups.

The relationship between PROMs and postoperative alignment

Preoperative and postoperative limb alignment data were available for all 152 knees included. They were categorized as neutral (mHKA ≤ 3°), mild varus (3° < mHKA ≤ 7°), and marked varus (mHKA > 7°) according to postoperative mechanical alignment. Seventeen knees with a valgus angle of less than 3° were assigned to the neutral group, while five knees with a valgus angle greater than 3° were assigned to the marked varus group due to their significant deviation from conventional alignment. Out of the 152 knees, 63 (41.45%) were in the neutral-aligned group, 54 (35.53%) were mildly varus, and 35 (23.03%) were markedly varus. The groups showed no significant differences in age, sex, operated side, BMI, K-L grade, and history of symptoms (Table 2).

There were no postoperative revisions in the knees of any of the groups for any reason until the last follow-up. Postoperatively, there was one tibia fracture that was repaired after treatment. Furthermore, one patient in the mild varus group showed progress in the lateral compartment. The neutral-aligned cohort did not show significantly higher VAS, HSS, WOMAC, or FJS-12 scores compared to the mild varus or marked varus cohort (P = 0.118, 0.076, 0.140, and 0.788, respectively). In addition, knee range of motion and overall satisfaction were not significantly different among the groups (P = 0.205 and 0.486, respectively) (Table 4). In general, patients with neutral mechanical alignment did not have better postoperative functional scores than those with mild or marked varus alignment after cemented, fixed-bearing medial UKA.

Discussion

In our study, the most common CPAK category was Type I followed by Types IV and II, which differs from MacDessi’s study [24]. A study in asymptomatic adults showed that 32% of men and 17% of women had constitutional varus knees with at least 3° varus in their natural mechanical alignment [19]. The traditional ideal neutral knee (namely Type V in the CPAK classification), which is pursued as mechanically aligned in TKA with a horizontal joint line and vertical limb alignment, only accounts for 0.66% in our study. Constitutional varus (CPAK Types I, IV, and VII) accounted for the majority (75.66%). Individuals with constitutional varus alignment may experience higher contact stresses in the medial compartment, potentially increasing their risk of developing medial compartment arthritis [26]. The varus alignment in patients with constitutional varus knees has been present since they reached skeletal maturity. Therefore, in these situations, achieving neutral alignment would probably be abnormal and undesired, which may necessitate a certain amount of soft tissue release [19].

Our study aligns with the previous study by Kennedy et al., indicating that restoring pre-arthritic alignment through medial UKA led to successful functional outcomes and 12-year survivorship, irrespective of postoperative alignment being neutral, mildly varus, or markedly varus [10]. As a result, the authors concluded that an increased postoperative varus alignment did not affect the outcomes. However, Kennedy et al. conducted mobile-bearing UKA and restored ligament tension to restore pre-arthritic alignment without using parameters such as aHKA to assess the restoration of pre-arthritic alignment [10]. In this present study, we retrospectively analyzed the functional and clinical outcomes among different postoperative mechanically aligned groups following fixed-bearing medial UKA. We used aHKA to evaluate whether pre-arthritic alignment is restored.

Although the absolute constitutional alignment could not be determined, the aHKA algorithm was proven to estimate it [22, 23]. The study found equivalence between postoperative mHKA and preoperative aHKA, suggesting equivalence between postoperative alignment and estimated constitutional alignment following non-robotically assisted, cemented fixed-bearing medial UKA. Hence, the restoration of pre-arthritic alignment following medial UKA is achieved. The results are consistent with previous studies on robotic-assisted medial UKA, which also restored the estimated pre-arthritic coronal alignment [27]. The preservation of pre-arthritic coronal alignment resulted in improved Kujala scores following robotic-assisted, medial UKA [28].

In a survey of 138 members of the European Society of Sports Traumatology, Knee Surgery and Arthroscopy (ESSKA) from 34 different countries, 51% expressed a preference for coronal alignment that was the same as the pre-arthritic alignment [29]. To maximize postoperative clinical results, some studies have recommended postoperative varus alignment [9, 30, 31]. Vasso et al. reported that minor varus (< 7°) alignment yields superior outcomes compared to neutral alignment [31]. Zuiderbaan et al. preferred a postoperative lower limb alignment of 1–4° varus after medial UKA [9]. Our study revealed that, in comparison to the marked varus group, the neutral and mild varus groups exhibited higher HSS scores and better WOMAC scores, however, the statistical significance was not achieved (P = 0.076 and 0.140, respectively). In this study, 17 knees with valgus less than 3° were assigned to the neutral group, while five knees with valgus greater than 3° were assigned to the marked varus group because of their significant deviation from the conventional alignment. Among them, 12 patients had valgus alignment preoperatively as classified using the aHKA. However, valgus alignment is not recommended for medial UKA as increasing valgus may be associated with the need for revision due to the progression of lateral compartment osteoarthritis [30]. After synthesizing various studies, it seems that mild varus alignment postoperatively was recommended.

Our study shows that functional outcomes are independent of postoperative mechanical alignment following fixed-bearing medial UKA during short-term follow-up. However, the current investigation does not support the notion that alignment following UKA is unimportant. Instead, the ideal and key point of UKA is “restoring the old as the old”. The “old” here refers to restoring the alignment before arthritis, not the alignment after arthritis, which can be estimated and measured by aHKA and mHKA, respectively. Therefore, it can be explained why the functional outcomes are independent of postoperative alignment following medial UKA, similar to the results of previous studies [8, 10, 32]. Given the enhanced designs and materials of modern UKA systems and the biomechanical benefits linked to reproducing pre-arthritic alignment [33], it is highly probable that achieving pre-arthritic alignment restoration could lead to similar outstanding postoperative clinical results in the short term, regardless of the varus angle of the postoperative limb.

The study’s strengths included abundant perioperative data, comprehensive radiographic data, and a combination of subjective and objective outcome measures for the included patients. However, this study has several limitations. The design of the retrospective study could have introduced bias in the selection process. Another limitation of the study was the lack of a longer follow-up period, resulting in a lack of data on the long-term clinical outcomes and implant survivorship in this cohort. Due to the limited number of failures in this study, we were unable to draw any conclusions about postoperative alignment and specific failure modes. Therefore, studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods may provide more robust support. In addition, this study was limited to assessing only the postoperative alignment of different groups. There were only a few types of research studies on the outcomes of pre-arthritic and non-pre-arthritic aligned groups after UKA [12, 14]. Plancher et al. found that after fixed-bearing medial UKA, knees restored to their pre-arthritic alignment (within ± 3.0°) had longer implant survival and better PROMs at the 10-year follow-up than knees restored to > 3.0° outside of their pre-arthritic alignment [12]. While Bayoumi et al. suggested that restoring (within ± 2.0°) or relatively overcorrecting (mHKA > 2.0° overcorrected relative to the aHKA) pre-arthritic alignment optimizes outcomes after medial UKA [14]. However, as it is still unclear to what degree variation from these margins is acceptable when using a pre-arthritic alignment technique, further study is necessary to assess the impact of restoring pre-arthritic alignment on postoperative outcomes after medial UKA.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the study demonstrates the application of the CPAK classification and aHKA in analyzing knees undergoing UKA. Non-robotically assisted, fixed-bearing medial UKA following the standard operative technique can restore constitutional alignment and achieve satisfactory patient-reported outcomes, with no significant differences between alignment groups during short-term follow-up. We promote that fixed-bearing medial UKA should aim to restore the pre-arthritic alignment rather than achieving neutral mechanical alignment.
