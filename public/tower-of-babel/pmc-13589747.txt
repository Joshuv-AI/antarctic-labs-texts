Beyond ER-Golgi trafficking: unconventional protein secretion as a new design frontier for synthetic secretion switches in mammalian cells
Authors: Bingshun Shi, Xinyuan Qiu, Yongheng Jiang, Jiabo Cai, Lingyun Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mammalian gene switches enable programmable cell behavior. However, current switches on transcriptional and translational layers require de novo RNA and protein synthesis, and secreted outputs must additionally undergo folding, post-translational processing, and intracellular trafficking, imposing delays that limit rapid extracellular responses. Rapid secretion-control switches instead act on pre-synthesized proteins by controlling retention, trafficking, storage, or release. Most current platforms exploit the classical endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-Golgi pathway, including engineered stimulus-secretion coupling in specialized secretory cells and ER retention, retrieval-signal cleavage, or synchronized trafficking in general mammalian hosts. Although these strategies improve response kinetics, they remain limited by ER dependence, host-cell specificity, cargo compatibility, basal leakage, and post-release transport delays. Here, we review current secretion-control architectures and highlight unconventional protein secretion as an underexplored source of design principles for positioning regulatory control closer to terminal protein export and expanding the architectures available for mammalian secretion control.

Introduction

Engineering mammalian cells with the capacity to sense defined input signals and execute programmable functional outputs is a central objective in synthetic biology and cell engineering1,2. Gene switches constitute the basic regulatory units of such systems, allowing user-defined signals to control the timing, amplitude, and context of gene expression3,4. Over the past decade, mammalian gene switches and synthetic circuits have been designed to respond to a broad range of inputs, including clinically approved drugs5, dietary molecules6,7, odorants8, and light9,10. These systems have substantially expanded the application scope of engineered mammalian cells in disease treatment, metabolic regulation, and intelligent biomanufacturing. However, for many therapeutic or physiological applications, the output of interest is not merely intracellular gene expression but the timely extracellular delivery of functional proteins. This requirement makes response speed a critical design parameter. Most established mammalian gene switches operate at the transcriptional or translational level. Although these strategies provide versatile and scalable access points for programming cell behavior, they place the regulatory node upstream of protein production. As a result, input stimulation must be converted into a functional output through a series of sequential biological processes, including transcriptional activation, mRNA processing and export, protein translation, folding, post-translational modification, and intracellular trafficking. For secreted protein outputs, these steps are followed by transport through the secretory pathway before extracellular activity can be detected. Consequently, the delay between input stimulation and measurable extracellular protein output commonly ranges from several hours to days, depending on promoter architecture, mRNA and protein stability, cargo maturation, cell type, and secretion efficiency4,11,12. For applications that require responses to rapidly fluctuating physiological signals or near-real-time intervention, such as metabolic homeostasis control, acute inflammatory modulation, neuroendocrine mimicry, or closed-loop cell-based therapy, transcription- and translation-based switches may therefore fail to provide the required temporal dynamics13–16. One way to overcome this intrinsic latency is to relocate the regulatory node from gene expression to the protein level. Recent advances in chemically induced dimerization17,18, photosensitive protein domains19, engineered allosteric proteins20, and conditional protease-mediated cleavage systems21–23 have enabled direct control over the activity, localization, stability, and interaction state of pre-synthesized proteins. Because these systems act on molecules that have already been synthesized, they can substantially shorten the delay between input sensing and functional execution when compared with conventional de novo expression circuits21,23. Protein-level regulation can also provide advantages beyond response kinetics by separating protein production from the timing of functional deployment. Synthetic transcriptional regulators act directly at genomic loci, where their activity can depend on chromatin and regulatory context and, in some settings, extend beyond the intended target to influence neighboring or more distant genes24,25. By contrast, post-translational control acts on pre-existing proteins without requiring stimulus-dependent transcriptional activation, thereby reducing the dependence of acute output regulation on nuclear gene control and limiting this class of genomic regulatory perturbation. Within this broader class of protein-level regulation, secretion control represents a particularly attractive strategy because it directly governs the transition from intracellular protein storage or trafficking to extracellular biological function.

Here, we refer to such systems as rapid protein secretion switches: engineered regulatory devices that control the retention, trafficking, storage, or release of pre-synthesized cargo proteins to accelerate extracellular output. In principle, secretion control is well suited for mammalian cell engineering because protein secretion is already a central route through which mammalian cells communicate with their environment, regulate tissue physiology, and deliver bioactive molecules. From an engineering perspective, the secretory pathway can therefore be viewed not only as a passive output route but also as a regulatory layer in which cargo localization, vesicular transport, and membrane fusion may be repurposed as controllable steps. Importantly, secretion-level control does not necessarily eliminate all kinetic delays: systems based on regulated exocytosis can release pre-stored cargo within minutes, whereas endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-retention or synchronized trafficking systems bypass de novo synthesis but still require post-release transport through the classical secretory pathway. Thus, the kinetic advantage of a given secretion switch depends on where the engineered control node is positioned relative to cargo storage, ER-Golgi transport, and plasma-membrane fusion. Notably, current rapid protein secretion switches are still built largely on the classical secretory pathway, either by rewiring stimulus-secretion coupling in specialized secretory cells like pancreatic β-cells or by imposing artificial control over cargo retention and trafficking along the ER-Golgi axis26,27. These strategies have established secretion as a useful regulatory layer for accelerating extracellular protein output, but they also reveal a central design trade-off: regulated secretion provides rapid and pulsatile release but depends on specialized cellular types, whereas the designs based on constitutive secretion are more portable but remain kinetically constrained by post-release ER-to-Golgi trafficking. Recent advances in cell biology further suggest that mammalian protein export is not limited to this canonical route. Unconventional secretion mechanisms, including terminal membrane translocation, pore-mediated release, extracellular vesicle secretion, and bypass trafficking routes, indicate that cells possess alternative protein-output pathways that remain largely unexplored by synthetic biologists28,29. Although these pathways have not yet yielded mature, modular secretion-control platforms, they reveal alternative regulatory nodes and release architectures that may inspire secretion-switch designs beyond some of the architectural constraints of classical ER-Golgi trafficking. In this review, we first summarize current rapid protein secretion switches based on the classical secretory machinery, with emphasis on regulated exocytosis in specialized secretory cells and controllable trafficking designs in general mammalian hosts. We then discuss unconventional protein secretion (UcPS) as an underexplored design frontier for synthetic biology, focusing on mechanisms that may provide new control nodes for faster, more terminally regulated, or more diverse protein-output systems. Finally, we examine the major bottlenecks that currently limit secretion-switch engineering, including ER dependence, basal leakage, cargo compatibility, host-cell specificity, and the lack of modular interfaces for unconventional secretion. By organizing rapid secretion control around these design principles, we aim to provide a conceptual framework for developing next-generation mammalian protein switches beyond classical ER-Golgi trafficking.

Classical secretion as the current foundation for rapid secretion switches

The ER-Golgi axis: shared architecture and divergent secretion modes

Classical secretion is the principal route by which mammalian cells export most soluble secretory proteins and membrane proteins. Centered on the ER-Golgi axis, this pathway integrates multiple sequential processes, including cotranslational entry into the ER, protein folding and quality control, vesicular transport, compartment-specific processing, cargo sorting, and terminal membrane fusion30. From the viewpoint of secretion-switch engineering, the classical pathway is important not only because it provides the dominant route for protein export, but also because several of its trafficking and retention steps can be repurposed as regulatory nodes for controlling extracellular protein output.

The intrinsic timescale of classical secretory transport provides an important benchmark for evaluating rapid secretion-control systems. Quantitative live-cell imaging of vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein fused to green fluorescent protein (VSVG-GFP) showed mean residence times of approximately 39 min in the ER and 42 min in the Golgi, although these values are strongly dependent on cargo and experimental context31–34. For example, kinetic analysis of secretory protein traffic and Golgi-to-plasma-membrane transport intermediates showed that Golgi residence and post-Golgi carrier transport are not instantaneous but occur on a timescale of minutes to tens of minutes34. Analysis of the Wnt-family signaling protein WNT3A with retention using selective hooks (RUSH) similarly resolved sequential trafficking after synchronized ER release: WNT3A began accumulating in the Golgi after approximately 10 min, post-Golgi carriers became apparent at approximately 28-35 min, and the Golgi cargo pool began to decline after approximately 45 min35,36. Together, these studies illustrate that removing the requirement for de novo protein synthesis does not eliminate trafficking-associated latency; secretion-control systems acting upstream of ER-Golgi transport remain constrained by the time required for cargo to complete the remaining secretory itinerary.

Constitutive secretion and regulated secretion are the two major modes of classical secretion. These routes share early steps of ER entry, folding, and Golgi trafficking, but diverge at the trans-Golgi network (TGN) and during subsequent vesicle maturation and release37,38. In the constitutive secretory pathway, cargo proteins are continuously transported from the ER through the Golgi apparatus, packaged into vesicles, and delivered to the plasma membrane through the coordinated action of coat proteins, Rab GTPases, tethering complexes, and soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE)-mediated membrane fusion39,40. Because this pathway operates broadly across mammalian cell types, it provides a generalizable substrate for engineering secretion control. However, it is not naturally designed for rapid burst-like release, and cargo output remains coupled to the speed and capacity of forward ER-Golgi trafficking.

By contrast, regulated secretion is specialized for rapid and stimulus-dependent protein release. In secretory cells such as pancreatic β-cells, endocrine cells, and neurons, selected cargoes are sorted at the TGN into immature secretory granules, concentrated through aggregation and condensation, and further processed during granule maturation41–43. Mature secretory granules are transported to the cell periphery, where they undergo docking and priming to enter a release-competent state. Upon stimulation, a local increase in Ca²⁺ triggers membrane fusion through Ca²⁺-dependent secretory regulators such as synaptotagmins and Ca²⁺-dependent activator protein for secretion (CAPS), enabling cargo release on extremely short timescales44,45. This mechanism supports rapid exocytosis in neurons and endocrine cells over timescales ranging from milliseconds to minutes. Recent studies on ultrafast synaptic vesicle exocytosis and endocytic retrieval have further refined our understanding of the kinetic organization of this process46.

This difference between constitutive and regulated secretion defines a central engineering trade-off. Regulated secretion provides the closest natural template for rapid, high-amplitude, and pulsatile protein output, but it depends on specialized secretory cell types and cargo-sorting programs. Constitutive secretion, in contrast, is more broadly available across mammalian cells and more compatible with modular engineering, but its response speed is constrained by the need for cargo proteins to traverse the classical ER-Golgi pathway after release from an engineered retention state. Current rapid protein secretion switches have therefore developed mainly along two complementary directions: rewiring stimulus-secretion coupling in specialized secretory cells and engineering controllable retention or trafficking steps within the constitutive secretory pathway.

Rewiring stimulus-secretion coupling in specialized secretory cells

Specialized secretory cells provide the most direct biological template for rapid secretion switches because they naturally separate cargo biosynthesis from stimulus-triggered release. In these cells, secretory proteins are synthesized, processed, concentrated, and stored before stimulation, so that the post-stimulation response is dominated by vesicle fusion and cargo discharge rather than by de novo gene expression. Typical examples include pancreatic β-cells, pituitary endocrine cells, neurons, and other neuroendocrine cell types38,47. For synthetic biology, the key advantage of these cells is that the most difficult components of a rapid secretion device, such as cargo storage, vesicle maturation, docking, priming, and Ca²⁺-triggered exocytosis, are already encoded by endogenous cellular programs.

Accordingly, engineering strategies based on specialized secretory cells usually do not attempt to rebuild the secretory machinery from scratch. Instead, they rewire user-defined inputs to native stimulus-secretion coupling. Pancreatic β cells and β-cell-derived models provide representative examples of this design principle because insulin secretion is itself a rapid and tightly regulated physiological process. Under native conditions, glucose metabolism alters membrane excitability and intracellular Ca²⁺ dynamics, which in turn triggers exocytosis of insulin-containing granules to maintain blood glucose homeostasis43,48. Optical control provides one example of this strategy. Light is an attractive input for cellular regulation because it offers high spatiotemporal precision, reversibility, and precise controllability9,49. By ectopically expressing melanopsin, a blue-light-responsive G protein-coupled receptor, in the engineered pancreatic β-cell line INSvesc, light stimulation could be converted into intracellular signaling events that promote insulin secretion48,50. In this system, insulin activity peaks within approximately 15 min after stimulation, illustrating how native storage in secretory granules can compress the post-stimulation response into a minute-scale release phase rather than an hour-scale de novo expression process.

A related strategy uses ion-channel engineering to connect physical inputs to Ca²⁺ influx and vesicle fusion. In temperature-responsive engineered β-cells, ectopic expression of the TRPV1 ion channel allowed transient heat stimulation, capsaicin, or sunlight pulses to trigger Ca²⁺ entry and insulin release51. Electrical control has also been achieved by engineering mammalian cells to express the L-type voltage-gated calcium channel CaV1.2 together with the inward-rectifying potassium channel Kir2.1, thereby enabling electronic regulation of vesicular insulin secretion in real time52. Similarly, by expressing mechanosensitive ion channels, such as Piezo1, MscS, or MscL, engineered β-cells can couple music-driven or vibration-associated mechanical inputs to Ca²⁺ influx and rapid insulin secretion53. Although these systems use different input modalities, their underlying logic is similar: synthetic sensors are installed upstream of an endogenous Ca²⁺-dependent exocytotic apparatus.

Beyond these signal-responsive calcium channels, recent work has begun to modularize stimulus-secretion coupling itself. The synthetic drug-inducible exocytosis system StimExo provides an example of this more engineering-oriented direction54. This design was inspired by the STIM-Orai calcium release-activated calcium channel system, in which STIM proteins sense ER Ca²⁺ depletion and activate Orai channels at ER-plasma membrane contact sites to induce Ca²⁺ influx55 (Fig. 1b). By redesigning STIM1-derived sensing and effector modules and coupling their assembly to conditional protein-protein interactions, StimExo converted a naturally Ca²⁺-dependent secretion process into a user-defined, drug-inducible exocytosis module54. These works indicated that endogenous exocytotic regulation can be abstracted into modular parts that may be reconfigured for synthetic secretion control.

Overall, specialized secretory cells offer intrinsic advantages in speed, output amplitude, and pulsatile release. They are particularly suitable for applications in which rapid endocrine-like output is required, such as metabolic control, neuroendocrine mimicry, or on-demand hormone delivery. From a kinetic perspective, the principal advantage of specialized secretory-cell types is that the upstream processes such as cargo biosynthesis, processing, granule maturation, docking, and priming are completed prior to induction. Consequently, the post-stimulation interval is dominated by stimulus-secretion coupling, membrane fusion, and cargo discharge rather than by cargo biosynthesis and maturation. However, these advantages are accompanied by important constraints. First, specialized secretory cells are not universal engineering hosts, and their sourcing, expansion, long-term stability, and transplantation compatibility are more complex than those of conventional mammalian cell platforms48,56. Second, these cells possess strong endogenous physiological programs, thereby limiting the engineering choices since disrupting cellular programs such as vesicle biogenesis and secretory homeostasis would compromise stimulus-responsive protein secretion in these cells. Third, cargo compatibility remains a major limitation: many heterologous proteins may not be efficiently sorted into dense-core granules, processed correctly, or released with the same efficiency as native peptide hormones. Thus, regulated secretion provides the fastest natural template for rapid secretion switches, but its broader use is constrained by host-cell specificity and cargo dependence.

Engineering controllable trafficking along the constitutive secretory pathway

Unlike specialized secretory cells, most mammalian cells do not possess dense-core secretory granules for stimulus-coupled burst release. Instead, they export proteins primarily through the constitutive secretory pathway. Although this route lacks the native storage-and-release architecture of regulated secretion, it is widely conserved across mammalian cell types and therefore offers clear advantages in host-cell generality, modular implementation, and compatibility with synthetic circuit design. For this reason, many rapid protein secretion switches in general mammalian hosts have been built by engineering controllable steps within constitutive secretion, particularly cargo retention, retrieval, trafficking, and release.

One of the earliest strategies was to control secretion through reversible protein aggregation in the ER (Fig. 2a). Rivera et al. constructed a chemically regulated secretion system by fusing target secretory proteins to multiple copies of a modified FK506-binding protein (FKBP) domain. In the absence of ligand, these fusion proteins formed higher-order aggregates that were retained in the ER and prevented from entering the forward trafficking pathway. Upon addition of synthetic FKBP ligand AP21998, the aggregates dissociated, allowing the cargo to resume trafficking through the classical secretory pathway and be released extracellularly57. Similar logic was later adapted to optical control using the plant ultraviolet-B photoreceptor 8 (UVR8), enabling light-triggered release of retained secretory cargo58. These systems established an important principle: pre-synthesized cargo can be stored intracellularly and released on demand by regulating its physical state. A more modular strategy is to exploit endogenous ER retention and retrieval signals. Protein localization within the early secretory pathway is governed by short sequence motifs that regulate compartmental residence and retrograde transport. For example, soluble ER resident proteins often contain a C-terminal Lys-Asp-Glu-Leu (KDEL) motif, whereas the ER resident membrane proteins can carry a cytosolic Lys-Lys-X-X (KKXX) motif or Arg-X-Arg-like retention signals that promote retrieval from downstream compartments through coat protein complex I (COPI)-dependent transport59,60. These motifs provide compact and portable regulatory handles for secretion-switch engineering. By fusing cargo proteins to removable retention or retrieval signals, and/or placing protease cleavage sites between the cargo and the retention motif, secretion can be controlled post-translationally. In the OFF state, the cargo protein is retained within the early secretory pathway. Upon activation of an orthogonal protease, the retention signal is removed, allowing the cargo to re-enter the forward secretory flux and be released extracellularly within a relatively short timescale (Fig. 2b).

This principle has been implemented in systems such as lumER and membER, two engineered secretion-control platforms, in which chemically controlled protease activity removes ER retrieval signals to initiate protein secretion on a timescale of approximately 45 min61. Compared with aggregation-based designs, retrieval-signal cleavage is more closely aligned with native trafficking logic and is therefore more amenable to modular construction. It also benefits from the growing toolkit of orthogonal proteases and conditional protease-regulation strategies, which can be coupled to small molecules, light, protein-protein interactions, or multi-input logic designs21,23. In this sense, the ER retrieval system is not merely a retention mechanism but an engineerable control node that connects protein-level regulation to secretory flux.

Subsequent platforms have expanded this logic from simple inducible secretion to programmable secretion control. The programmable protease-mediated post-translational switch (POSH) platform incorporated plant-derived small molecule inputs as well as optical and electrical control modalities into a protease-regulated framework, thereby broadening the range of inputs that can be linked to rapid extracellular output62. Protease-controlled secretion systems have also been used to implement multi-input logic operations such as OR, AND, and NOT gates through dual-protease cascades and conditional protein-protein interactions63,64. These examples indicate that the constitutive secretory pathway can be repurposed not only as a protein export route, but also as a programmable post-translational information-processing layer that can be integrated into multi-layered biocomputation frameworks like the tristate-logic-based TriLoS system65,66. The recently developed protease-based rapid protein secretion system (PASS) further extended this strategy toward therapeutic applications, demonstrating that controlled storage, trafficking, and release of pre-synthesized proteins can generate functional outputs in contexts such as glucose regulation, tumor recognition, and cell fate control27.

A related but mechanistically distinct strategy controls secretion at the level of ER-associated degradation (ERAD) rather than through the release of a pre-synthesized intracellular cargo reservoir67. Gao et al. fused conditional degron modules to therapeutic secretory proteins, enabling small-molecule-dependent, tunable and reversible control over whether the cargo is targeted by ER quality-control machinery or proceeds toward secretion. This ERAD-gated architecture is comparatively compact and compatible with adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene delivery, and has been demonstrated for remote control of therapeutic protein output in vivo. Importantly, however, its kinetic logic differs from ER-retention-based switches. Activation does not primarily discharge a pre-accumulated cargo pool; extracellular output remains dependent on the availability of secretion-competent cargo and its subsequent passage through the conventional ER-Golgi pathway. ERAD-level control should therefore be regarded as a related protein-level secretion-control strategy rather than as a direct equivalent of rapid release from pre-synthesized intracellular storage.

In addition to ER retrieval motifs, reversible anchoring systems provide another design principle for synchronizing and controlling secretory trafficking. The RUSH system uses a compartment-localized “hook” protein to retain a cargo fused to a streptavidin-binding peptide through high-affinity interaction with streptavidin. Addition of biotin competitively releases the cargo, allowing synchronized trafficking through the secretory pathway36 (Fig. 2c). Although RUSH was originally developed as a cell-biological tool for studying secretory transport rather than as a therapeutic secretion switch, its architecture illustrates an important modular principle: cargo can be retained at a defined intracellular location and released synchronously by disrupting a reversible interaction. Such temporal control also makes RUSH-based systems powerful tools for mechanistic discovery. For example, the recently developed cytoRUSH assay adapted this principle to probe the timing of ER translocation and revealed that the native signal peptide of PD-L1 is suboptimal, resulting in delayed ER entry and transient cytosolic exposure of the nascent protein68. This temporal window permits cytosolic post-translational modification before ER translocation and thereby contributes to the regulation of PD-L1 maturation and expression. More broadly, controllable trafficking systems can experimentally separate normally coupled steps in protein biogenesis, providing a means to identify regulatory checkpoints in translocation, processing, and intracellular trafficking.

Together, constitutive secretion-based systems provide a more generalizable engineering route than specialized secretory cells. They can be implemented in common mammalian hosts, coupled to diverse input modalities, and integrated with protein-level logic circuits. Nevertheless, their kinetic performance remains bounded by the architecture of the classical secretory pathway. Once released from the engineered retention state, cargo proteins must still undergo ER exit, Golgi transit, sorting, and plasma-membrane delivery. For retention- and anchoring-based architectures, extracellular output is accelerated primarily by pre-synthesizing and storing cargo before stimulation. ERAD-based switches represent a related but distinct strategy in which the regulated checkpoint is protein quality control rather than the release of a pre-existing intracellular reservoir. Despite this mechanistic difference, both classes remain dependent on downstream classical secretory trafficking. In addition, prolonged intracellular retention can increase folding burden, basal leakage can reduce the dynamic range of induction, and cargo-specific differences in folding, processing, and trafficking may strongly influence output performance.

Beyond the ER-Golgi axis: unconventional secretion as a future design space

Recent advances in cell biology have increasingly challenged the classical view that extracellular proteins must proceed through the signal peptide-dependent entry into the ER, anterograde ER-Golgi trafficking, and vesicle fusion at the plasma membrane. A growing number of leaderless or alternatively routed proteins have been shown to reach the extracellular space through unconventional secretion pathways69–71. Consistent with this broader view of extracellular protein transport, recent analyses of the human plasma proteome indicate that a substantial fraction of plasma-detected proteins lack conventional signal peptides72, highlighting the broader contribution of unconventional secretion and other non-canonical export or release routes to mammalian protein output. These mechanisms are highly diverse and differ substantially in cargo selectivity, membrane topology, trafficking intermediates, and cellular context71. For synthetic biology, however, their significance does not lie simply in providing additional biological routes for protein export. More importantly, unconventional secretion reveals that mammalian cells possess a broader repertoire of protein-output mechanisms than those currently exploited in engineered secretion switches. From an engineering perspective, some of these routes are conceptually attractive because they may place the regulatory step closer to terminal release at the plasma membrane or expand the range of intracellular reservoirs available for cargo storage. However, these potential advantages remain largely inferential, and their kinetic benefit has not yet been established in engineered mammalian secretion systems. Although these pathways are not yet sufficiently modular, predictable, or orthogonal for immediate use as standardized secretion switches, they represent an underexplored source of design principles for next-generation rapid protein-output systems. It is important to emphasize that the unconventional secretion mechanisms discussed below are primarily native physiological pathways rather than established synthetic secretion switches. To our knowledge, unconventional secretion has not yet yielded well-established, modular secretion-control platforms comparable to current ER-Golgi-based systems. Accordingly, these mechanisms are discussed here mainly as sources of potential engineering principles, including alternative cargo reservoirs, trafficking routes, and membrane-proximal control points that might be adapted for future synthetic secretion systems.

Terminal release at the plasma membrane: translocation and pore-mediated export

Among unconventional secretion mechanisms, direct plasma-membrane translocation is conceptually attractive because it shifts the control node to the final step of protein export. Fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) provides one of the best-characterized examples of direct plasma-membrane translocation. Lacking an N-terminal signal peptide, FGF2 does not enter the classical ER-Golgi pathway. Instead, FGF2 is recruited to the cytosolic leaflet of the plasma membrane, where interaction with phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate [PI(4,5)P2] promotes cargo enrichment, oligomerization, and formation of a membrane-spanning translocation intermediate73,74. This process is facilitated by upstream factors including the α1 subunit of the Na,K-ATPase, which contributes to FGF2 recruitment at the inner plasma-membrane leaflet75. On the extracellular side, membrane-proximal heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) are essential for efficient net export: they capture translocated FGF2 and thereby promote directional movement across the membrane. Glypican-1 has recently been identified as a major HSPG involved in this extracellular capture step76,77. Thus, FGF2 secretion is driven by a cargo-intrinsic and membrane-proximal mechanism that couples membrane recruitment, oligomerization, translocation, and extracellular trapping, rather than by vesicular transport through the ER-Golgi system78 (Fig. 3a). In principle, if membrane recruitment, oligomerization, or translocation could be coupled to user-defined inputs, cargo release might be triggered without post-induction ER-to-Golgi transit. Here, it should be noted that FGF2 export depends on cargo-specific molecular interactions that mediate plasma-membrane recruitment, oligomerization, translocation, and extracellular capture by heparan sulfate proteoglycans. Whether these requirements can be transferred efficiently to unrelated protein cargos remains unclear, and modular targeting elements comparable to those available for classical secretion have not yet been established for this route. Pore-mediated release provides another terminal unconventional secretion route. In inflammasome-associated secretion, inflammatory caspases cleave gasdermin D (GSDMD), releasing its N-terminal fragment, which oligomerizes in the plasma membrane to form pores79,80(Fig. 3b). These pores enable the release of leaderless inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18)81,82. From an engineering perspective, this mechanism is conceptually interesting because it illustrates a route that directly connects the cytosol with the extracellular space. However, its liabilities are substantial. GSDMD is a central executor of pyroptosis, and extensive pore formation can cause ionic imbalance, membrane permeabilization, inflammatory signaling, and ultimately lytic cell death. In its native form, this pathway therefore appears poorly suited to repeated secretion from long-lived therapeutic cells. However, it should be noted that GSDMD-dependent IL-1β release can also occur from viable hyperactivated macrophages in a non-pyroptotic manner81 and endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT)-mediated membrane repair can restrain the progression from GSDMD activation toward terminal pyroptosis83. These observations indicate that pore formation and immediate cell lysis are not obligatorily coupled. Thus, the relevant engineering challenge would be to achieve tightly controlled, sublytic cargo release while minimizing inflammatory activation and irreversible membrane damage.

Alternative cargo reservoirs: secretory autophagy, lysosome-related exocytosis, and extracellular vesicles

A second major category of unconventional secretion is built around membrane-bound compartments that differ from the classical ER-Golgi route. These pathways may provide alternative intracellular reservoirs for controlled cargo loading, storage, and release. Instead of retaining cargo in the ER and waiting for anterograde trafficking after induction, future secretion switches might be designed to preload cargo into autophagy-related compartments, lysosome-related organelles, multivesicular bodies, or extracellular vesicles, and then trigger their extracellular release (Fig. 3c).

Secretory autophagy provides one example of such an alternative reservoir. In canonical degradative autophagy, cytosolic cargo is enclosed within autophagosomes and delivered to lysosomes for degradation. In secretory autophagy, however, selected cargoes associated with autophagy-related structures are redirected toward extracellular release rather than degradation84,85. Importantly, autophagosome biogenesis is closely connected to the broader endomembrane system. The ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) provides membranes that support lipidation of the autophagy protein LC3 and early autophagosome formation86, whereas mammalian autophagy-related (ATG) protein ATG9 cycles between the trans-Golgi network and endosomal compartments and redistributes toward peripheral endosomal membranes during starvation87. ATG9- and ATG16L1-containing membrane sources also converge at recycling endosomes during autophagosome biogenesis88. These findings indicate that both pre- and post-Golgi compartments contribute membrane and trafficking components to autophagosome formation. Secretory autophagy can subsequently redirect selected cargo-bearing autophagy-related compartments toward extracellular release rather than lysosomal degradation84,85,89,90. Thus, autophagy-associated unconventional secretion is not completely isolated from the classical endomembrane system, but repurposes selected compartments and trafficking machinery toward a secretory destination. This process involves distinct cargo-loading mechanisms, trafficking decisions, and membrane-fusion events, suggesting that the autophagy machinery can be repurposed from an intracellular degradation pathway into a noncanonical secretion route. Factors such as ATG proteins, Golgi reassembly stacking protein (GRASP) family members, tripartite motif (TRIM)-family cargo receptors, Rab GTPases, and SNAREs have been implicated in selected secretory autophagy settings, but their requirements vary with cargo, cell type, and stimulus89–91. For secretion-switch engineering, this raises an attractive possibility: autophagy-related compartments might, in principle, provide alternative routes for loading and releasing cytosolic proteins that do not naturally enter the ER. However, secretory autophagy is strongly dependent on cargo, cellular context, and stimulus, and its cargo-selection and sorting mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In addition, secretory trafficking must compete with the canonical degradative fate of autophagic compartments, creating an important challenge for predictable control of cargo release.

Lysosome-related exocytosis and multivesicular body-associated release further expand this design concept. These routes can intersect with secretory autophagy but should not be treated as a single pathway. Lysosomal exocytosis involves fusion of lysosome- or endolysosome-related compartments with the plasma membrane and release of their luminal contents. By contrast, multivesicular body-associated release involves fusion of multivesicular bodies with the plasma membrane and extracellular release of intraluminal vesicles as exosomes92,93. Herein, multivesicular bodies can either fuse with lysosomes for degradation or fuse with the plasma membrane to release intraluminal vesicles as exosomes94. Recent work has shown that the biogenesis of these exosomes and the cargo selection are regulated by multiple molecular systems, including ESCRT complexes, lipid-dependent sorting mechanisms, Rab GTPases, and cell-state-dependent trafficking programs95–97. Extracellular vesicle (EV) release provides a distinct output mode in which proteins are delivered in a membrane-encapsulated form. EV-associated cargos may remain vesicle-bound after release and often require uptake or membrane interaction by recipient cells, whereas soluble secreted proteins enter the extracellular milieu directly97,98. Vesicular delivery may therefore offer advantages for cargo stabilization, intercellular transfer, localized activity, or delivery of protein complexes. However, it also introduces important engineering challenges, including heterogeneous vesicle populations, variable cargo-loading efficiency, and difficulty in quantitatively relating vesicle release to a defined extracellular cargo dose.

Reshaping secretory trafficking: ERGIC entry and Golgi-independent export

Some unconventional secretion pathways do not completely avoid the endomembrane system, but they deviate from the canonical ER-Golgi route in ways that are highly relevant to secretion-switch design. These pathways suggest that protein export does not always follow a rigid linear pipeline from ER entry to Golgi processing and plasma-membrane fusion. Instead, cargoes may enter intermediate compartments, bypass selected processing steps, or use alternative trafficking routes depending on cargo identity and cellular state. The ER-Golgi intermediate compartment is particularly relevant in this context because it is positioned between classical ER export, Golgi entry, recycling endosomes, and Golgi-bypass trafficking routes. Recent studies further suggest that the ERGIC may function not only as a transit station of the conventional secretory pathway, but also as a sorting hub where classical and unconventional cargoes diverge72.

The recently described unconventional protein secretion pathway mediated by transmembrane emp24 domain-containing protein 10 (TMED10) provides a representative example of how leaderless cargos can access the endomembrane system without signal peptide-dependent ER import. In this pathway, TMED10 mediates translocation of cytosolic cargos into an ERGIC-associated compartment, providing an alternative entry point into the secretory system99,100 (Fig. 3d). Mechanistically, TMED10 has been proposed to form oligomeric assemblies at ERGIC-associated membranes that facilitate the entry of selected cytosolic cargos into a membrane-bound compartment72,100. More recent work further indicates that ERGIC-localized Rab proteins, including Rab1-family proteins and Rab2A, as well as the motor protein KIF5B, can regulate TMED10 oligomerization, ERGIC spatial organization, and cargo routing72. Importantly, this model is distinct from conventional ER import and does not imply the entry of leaderless cargos, such as mature IL-1β, into the conventional Golgi lumen. Rather, TMED10-dependent ERGIC translocation represents a distinct unconventional cargo-entry step, while the downstream routes leading from these compartments to extracellular release remain less completely defined.

Golgi-bypass trafficking provides another example of secretory plasticity, but its occurrence appears to be highly cargo-, organism-, and condition-dependent. GRASP family proteins have been implicated in several mechanistically distinct forms of unconventional secretion, including GRASP-dependent export of acyl-CoA-binding proteins such as Acb1/AcbA101,102 and noncanonical trafficking of selected membrane proteins such as integrins or CFTR in specific experimental contexts103–105(Fig. 3d). In yeast, starvation-induced Acb1 secretion involves specialized Golgi-bypass compartments such as such as compartments for unconventional protein secretion (CUPS), which are associated with membranes positive for the yeast GRASP homolog Grh1, ESCRT-related components, and modified trans-Golgi/TGN organization102,106. In mammalian cells, additional stress-adaptive routes such as misfolding-associated protein secretion have been described, in which misfolded cytosolic proteins are routed through late endosome-related intermediates with the involvement of factors such as the deubiquitinase USP19 and the chaperones HSC70 and DNAJC5107,108. These studies indicate that GRASP proteins participate in several forms of unconventional trafficking involving cargo sorting, membrane organization, or routes that bypass parts of the canonical ER-Golgi-TGN pathway. However, the requirement for GRASP proteins varies substantially with cargo, organism, and physiological context, and several GRASP-associated pathways have been characterized under stress or developmental conditions. Thus, these mechanisms provide useful insights into alternative trafficking architectures, but do not yet constitute a generalizable secretion-control module105–107.

Beyond secretion: directed intercellular transfer

Beyond extracellular release into the surrounding medium, unconventional output mechanisms also include direct cell-to-cell transfer. Tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) are thin membranous structures connecting adjacent or distant cells and can mediate direct intercellular transfer of proteins, nucleic acids, organelles, and even pathogens109,110. Unlike classical secretion, in which cargoes are first released into the extracellular space and then diffuse or are captured by recipient cells, TNTs provide a direct intercellular route to mediate cell-cell communications (Fig. 3e).

The TNT mechanism has attracted particular attention in the context of pathological protein propagation. For example, α-synuclein fibrils can spread between cells through TNTs in association with lysosome-related trafficking processes111. Other neurodegeneration-associated proteins, such as tau, may use similar routes110. Tunneling nanotubes also contribute to the intercellular spread of viral components, including HIV-1 Nef112. These examples highlight the ability of cells to transfer complex biological cargoes in a directional manner without relying on classical secretion or extracellular diffusion.

Taken together, unconventional secretion pathways substantially expand the conceptual landscape of rapid secretion-switch engineering. Direct plasma-membrane translocation and pore-mediated release suggest ways to position control nodes close to terminal export. Secretory autophagy, lysosome-related exocytosis, and extracellular vesicle secretion suggest alternative cargo reservoirs and vesicle-based output modes. ERGIC-mediated export and Golgi-bypass trafficking indicate that secretory routing may be more flexible than the classical ER-Golgi model implies. Directed intercellular transfer further broadens the range of protein-output mechanisms to include targeted cell-to-cell delivery.

Despite their conceptual appeal, most unconventional secretion routes currently face limitations that are more severe than those of established ER-Golgi-based secretion switches. These include poor modularity, unclear cargo-selection rules, strong dependence on cell state or stress context, limited quantitative predictability, and in some cases, liabilities that may be fundamentally incompatible with reusable living-cell devices. These pathway-specific constraints indicate that unconventional secretion currently provides a diverse set of mechanistic design principles, but substantial barriers remain before these mechanisms can be converted into robust and modular synthetic secretion-control systems. Conclusion

Rapid protein secretion switches mark a broader transition in mammalian synthetic biology: from controlling when genes are expressed to controlling when and how pre-existing biological effectors are deployed. By relocating the regulatory node from transcription or translation to protein retention27,57,58,61–64, trafficking36, and release50–54, these systems offer a practical route to reduce the latency between signal sensing and extracellular functional output. This acceleration arises because cargo synthesis, folding, and accumulation can occur before stimulation, so that induction acts mainly on storage, trafficking, or terminal release. However, different secretion-control architectures overcome kinetic barriers to different extents. Regulated exocytosis in specialized secretory cells can generate minute-scale output by releasing mature secretory granules, whereas ER-retention and synchronized-trafficking systems bypass de novo protein synthesis but still require residual ER-Golgi and post-Golgi transport. Beyond kinetics, secretion-level control also separates protein production from functional deployment, reducing the dependence of acute extracellular output on transcriptional regulation and its associated genomic context24,25. Existing ER-Golgi-based systems have already demonstrated the feasibility of this strategy, either by exploiting regulated exocytosis in specialized secretory cells or by engineering controllable retention and release within the constitutive secretory pathway. Beyond serving as output devices, controllable trafficking systems can also provide perturbation tools for identifying regulatory checkpoints in protein biogenesis and secretion. Nevertheless, they also expose the current limits of secretion-switch design, including dependence on specialized cellular types26,54, cargo compatibility51–53, basal leakage48,57,62, ER retention burden57,58, and the kinetic ceiling imposed by post-release ER-Golgi trafficking113.

In a sense, unconventional secretion provides a conceptually important but still underexplored direction for synthetic biology. Although these pathways are not yet mature plug-and-play platforms, they reveal alternative principles for extracellular protein output, including membrane-proximal release, non-ER cargo reservoirs, vesicle-based delivery, bypass trafficking, and directed intercellular transfer. Some of these mechanisms, such as direct plasma-membrane translocation or pore-mediated release, are particularly interesting from a kinetic perspective because, if placed under orthogonal synthetic control, they could position the regulated checkpoint downstream of classical ER-Golgi trafficking and thereby remove ER exit, Golgi transit, Golgi-dependent sorting, and post-Golgi delivery from the post-stimulation interval. However, it should be noted that whether this architectural opportunity can actually be translated into faster extracellular output remains largely unresolved because direct comparative kinetic measurements are currently lacking.

In addition, whether unconventional secretion can be transformed from mechanistic inspiration into robust modular technology remains an open question. Its pathways are heterogeneous, context-dependent, and often coupled to stress-associated cellular states, while their cargo-selection rules and regulatory checkpoints remain incompletely understood. However, precisely because they challenge the classical ER-Golgi-centered view of protein export, unconventional secretion mechanisms may define the next conceptual frontier for rapid mammalian secretion control. Future progress will likely depend on the ability to abstract useful design principles from these native mechanisms, interface them with established protein-level switches, and develop secretion systems whose response latency, programmability, repeatability, cargo compatibility, and physiological safety can be quantitatively matched to the intended application.
