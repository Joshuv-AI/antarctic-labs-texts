Osteoporosis in children and adolescents with transfusion-dependent thalassemia and the diagnostic value of different indicators
Authors: Xinxia Li, Liuhua Liao, Li Wang, Yang Zhang, Baifeng Huang, Rufen Chen, Chushan Wu, Xiuling Huang, Qianwen Guan, Guohua Chen, Zhe Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe study aimed to investigate the prevalence of osteoporosis in children and adolescents with transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT) and evaluate the diagnostic value of different osteoporosis indicators.MethodsClinical data were collected from children and adolescents with TDT treated with blood transfusion between March 2022 and January 2024 at Huizhou Central People’s Hospital and Huizhou First Hospital. The patients were grouped according to the presence of osteoporosis (International Society for Clinical Densitometry [ISCD] criteria).ResultsOf 138 patients included in the study, 48 (34.8%) had osteoporosis. The patients with osteoporosis mainly had asymptomatic grade I fractures of the spine. Using the dual-X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) standards from the World Health Organization, height-corrected TBLH Z-score ≤-2 was associated with osteoporosis (30.4% vs. 14.9%, P = 0.035), displaying 30.4% sensitivity and 85.1% specificity. According to the Chinese DXA standards, height-corrected and age-specific Z-scores ≤-2 were not associated with osteoporosis.ConclusionThe prevalence of osteoporosis among children and adolescents with TDT was 34.8%, indicating the need for screening for osteoporosis in that population. In TDT, the diagnosis of osteoporosis requires early detection of spinal fractures because bone density assessed by DXA is of limited value.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06955-8.

Introduction

Thalassemia is a group of hereditary blood disorders caused by mutations in the globin genes, resulting in impaired hemoglobin production [1–5]. About 5% of the world population carries a thalassemia mutation [3, 4, 6]. In China, over 200 million individuals carry at least one mutated allele for a globin gene, with two provinces (Guangdong and Guangxi) accounting for 40% of the cases [7]. Patients with transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT) require lifelong regular red blood cell transfusions [1, 3, 8]. One of the most common complications of thalassemia is osteoporosis, observed in 22.9% of the patients in the OPTIMAL CARE study (291 male and 293 female patients of 25.4 ± 13.9 years old from Lebanon, Italy, Iran, Egypt, United Arab Emirates, and Oman) [9] and 22.3% in the study by Karimi et al. [10] (726 patients aged 2.5–80 years from Iran, Italy, Turkey, Greece, Oman, Qatar, Jordan, Cyprus, and the United Kingdom). Patients with thalassemia major and intermedia may present skeletal complications such as reduced bone mineral density (BMD), spontaneous fractures, and spinal deformities. Patients with β-thalassemia often have lower BMD compared with individuals without thalassemia and have a higher fracture prevalence that increases with age [3, 6]. In recent years, expert groups have emphasized that osteoporosis in TDT constitutes a major cause of morbidity and requires structured, disease-specific guidance for screening, diagnosis, and management across the lifespan [11, 12].

Osteoporosis in children and adolescents with TDT will inevitably lead to an escalating fracture risk in adulthood. The development of osteoporosis and osteopenia in patients with TDT is primarily attributed to ineffective erythropoiesis and subsequent bone marrow expansion. In addition, severe iron overload, often found in patients with TDT due to repeated blood transfusions, may directly interfere with osteoid maturation and mineralization [3, 6]. Iron chelation therapy with deferoxamine or deferasirox also contributes to the risk of osteoporosis [3, 6, 8].

Few studies have comprehensively investigated osteoporosis in children and adolescents with TDT. Gaudio et al. [13] suggested that the diagnosis of osteoporosis in children with TDT should not rely solely on BMD measurements because of the interference from the growing skeleton. In addition, BMD deficit may be exaggerated in patients with iron overload [14]. Several knowledge gaps persist regarding the diagnosis and risk factors for osteoporosis in children and adolescents with TDT. Furthermore, there is a lack of comparisons of different criteria of BMD for the diagnosis of osteoporosis in children and adolescents with TDT.

Current osteoporosis guidelines increasingly recognize the role of vertebral fracture assessment (VFA) as an integral component of fracture risk evaluation, because even asymptomatic vertebral fractures substantially increase the risk of subsequent fractures and may be missed when relying solely on areal BMD [11, 12, 15]. In this context, integrating VFA with densitometric measurements is particularly relevant in TDT, where spinal deformities, growth impairment, and altered bone geometry can complicate the interpretation of BMD and lead to underdiagnosis of clinically important vertebral fractures [11, 12, 15]. Available data regarding TDT patients suggest early and periodic assessment of bone health, including lumbar spine and hip BMD, evaluation for vertebral fractures, and timely initiation of pharmacological and supportive interventions to prevent fractures and preserve quality of life [16]. However, these data are largely derived from adults, and robust evidence on optimal diagnostic thresholds and imaging strategies in pediatric and adolescent TDT populations remains limited.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the prevalence of osteoporosis in children and adolescents with TDT and evaluate the diagnostic value of different BMD criteria for osteoporosis.

Methods

Study design and patients

The present study included children and adolescents with TDT treated with blood transfusion between March 2022 and January 2024 at Huizhou Central People’s Hospital and Huizhou First People’s Hospital. The study was approved by the ethics committees of Huizhou Central People’s Hospital and Huizhou First Hospital (approvals #ky112022097 and #HZFH2023002). The patients or their legal guardians signed the informed consent forms.

The inclusion criteria were (1) met the diagnostic criteria for TDT [3, 11, 12, 17], including β thalassemia major, transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia intermedia, and transfusion-dependent α-thalassemia intermedia, (2) 5–18 years old, (3) without bone marrow transplantation or gene therapy, (4) without anti-osteoporosis treatment (e.g., bisphosphonates), and (5) agreed to participate in the study.

The exclusion criteria were (1) suffering from other chronic diseases (e.g., severe liver or renal function impairment, mental retardation, chromosomal abnormality, long-term use of glucocorticoids, hereditary osteopathy, etc.), (2) non-removable objects in the body that can affect BMD assessments (e.g., prosthesis, steel plates, etc.), (3) height or weight exceeding the instrument’s detection range, i.e., height > 197.5 cm or weight > 204 kg, (4) pregnancy, (5) participated in research involving ionizing radiation in the past year, or (6) incomplete data.

All participants in this study were transfusion-dependent. The initiation of transfusion was based on hemoglobin levels persistently being < 70 g/L or hemoglobin being > 70 g/L but accompanied by severe anemia symptoms, growth and developmental delays, facial deformities, pathological fractures, or significant extramedullary hematopoiesis. The participants were receiving transfusions of allogenic red blood cells every 2–5 weeks [17, 18]. At the study centers, patients on regular transfusion were managed according to disease‑specific thalassemia guidelines, under which pre‑transfusion hemoglobin levels are intentionally maintained at 90–105 g/L as part of a hypertransfusion regimen, rather than at the lower thresholds commonly used in the general population [3, 17, 19].

Data collection

Age, sex, diagnosis, time of starting blood transfusion, average hemoglobin before blood transfusion, and average ferritin in the last year were extracted from the patient’s medical records data. Questionnaires (presented in the Supplementary Materials) were administered to the patient’s family members to collect information on the use of iron chelation drugs, whether splenectomy was performed, and whether there were previous fractures.

Trained pediatric endocrinologists performed standard height and weight measurements of the patients to calculate body mass index (BMI). They also determined Tanner’s staging for each patient.

Asymptomatic osteoporosis was diagnosed based on the presence of painless spine fractures [20]. The fractures were graded as grades I (height of the vertebrae decreased by 20% to 25%), II (height of the vertebrae decreased by 25%-40%), and III (height of the vertebrae decreased by > 40%) (Fig. 1). The 2019 International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD) criteria for osteoporosis diagnosis in children and adolescents are (1) spine fracture resulting from low trauma without localized disorders, or (2) if no spinal fracture is present, a BMD Z-score ≤-2.0 accompanied by a significant history of fracture, i.e., ≥ 2 long bone fractures before the age of 10 years or ≥ 3 long bone fractures occurring before the age of 19 years [21]. The diagnostic criteria for spinal fractures were based on the Genant semiquantitative determination method [22].

The imaging examinations included lateral spine film to assess spinal fractures and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) to measure BMD. The DXA assessments for children and adolescents were based on the lumbar spine (L1-L4) and total body less head (TBLH) [23, 24]. Age-specific and height-corrected BMD Z-scores were obtained using the World Health Organization (WHO) [25, 26] and Chinese [27] reference values.

Fasting blood samples were taken before blood transfusion for routine blood counts, electrolytes, liver and kidney function, blood lipids, serum ferritin, endocrine function, and bone turnover markers.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Continuous data that conformed to the normal distribution were described as means ± standard deviations and analyzed using Student’s t-test. Continuous data that did not conform to the normal distribution were described as medians (interquartile range [IQR]) and analyzed using the rank sum test. Categorical data were described as n (%) or [n/n total (%)] and analyzed using the chi-squared test. Considering the characteristics that change with increasing age, height [28], BMI [29], IGF-1 [30], AMH [31, 32], PINP, and β-CTX [33] were analyzed using standard deviation scores (SDS). The different parameters for the diagnosis of osteoporosis were calculated using contingency tables. Sensitivity (true positives divided by true positives plus false negatives), specificity (true negatives divided by true negatives plus false positives), positive predictive value (true positives divided by true positives plus false positives), and negative predictive value (true negatives divided by true negatives plus false negatives) were calculated. The Youden index was calculated as sensitivity + specificity − 1. Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the patients

During the study period, 184 patients with TDT (5–18 years old) received blood transfusions at Huizhou Central People’s Hospital or Huizhou First People’s Hospital. Among them, 139 underwent a DXA examination and lateral spine examination, but one patient with comorbid Turner syndrome (TS) was excluded. Therefore, 138 patients were included in the present study.

Based on the presence of spine fractures, the prevalence of osteoporosis was 34.8% (48/138), without significant differences between β-thalassemia (34.6%, 37/108) and α-thalassemia (36.7%, 11/30). When stratified into 2-year age intervals, the prevalence of osteoporosis was 33.3% in those aged 5–6 years, 35.3% in 7–8 years, 39.1% in 9–10 years, 37.5% in 11–12 years, 33.3% in 13–14, 33.3% in 15–16 years, and 26.7% in 17–18 years.

Among the 48 patients with osteoporosis, 45 had asymptomatic grade I mild thoracic or lumbar fractures (45/48, 93.8%), presenting as compression deformities, wedge deformities, or biconcave deformities. Two patients (4.1%) had moderate grade II fractures characterized by compression and deformation of T12 or L5. One patient (2.1%) had grade III severe fractures characterized by compression and deformation of T5, T7, T8, and T9. Among the 48 patients with vertebral fractures, 83 fractured vertebral levels were identified. Fifteen patients had multiple vertebral fractures, ranging from two to nine affected levels. The most frequently involved vertebra was L5 (31/48, 64.6%), followed by T12 (9/48, 18.8%), L1 (7/48, 14.5%), T11 (6/48, 12.5%), and L4 (5/48, 10.4%). The types of deformation included grade I compressive deformations (n = 38 fractures), grade I wedge-shaped deformations (n = 31 fractures), grade I double-convex deformations (n = 8 fractures), grade II compressive deformations (n = 2 fractures), and grade III compressive deformations (n = 4 fractures). Among the patients who could not be diagnosed with osteoporosis according to the ISCD standard, seven patients (7/90, 7.8%) had a history of limb fractures.

There were no significant differences between the osteoporosis and non-osteoporosis groups regarding demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics (all P > 0.05) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1).

Association of BMD parameters with osteoporosis according to the WHO and Chinese reference values

According to the WHO reference values, using TBLH-BMD, height-corrected Z-score ≤-2 was associated with osteoporosis (30.4% vs. 14.9%, P = 0.035), while age-specific Z-score ≤-2 was not (62.5% vs. 66.7%, P = 0.625). Using TBLH-BMD, height-corrected Z-score ≤-2 or age-specific Z-score ≤-2 was not associated with osteoporosis when determined using the Chinese reference values. Using L1-L4 BMD, height-corrected Z-score ≤-2 and age-specific Z-score ≤-2 were not associated with osteoporosis when determined using the WHO reference values (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In the present study, the prevalence of osteoporosis among children and adolescents with TDT was 34.8%, indicating the need for screening for osteoporosis in that population. The results suggested that in patients with TDT, the diagnosis of osteoporosis requires the early detection of spinal fractures because BMD assessed by DXA is of limited value. These findings can potentially enhance the management of children and adolescents with TDT and improve their long-term outcomes.

Recent recommendations on vertebral fracture assessment emphasize that identifying morphometric vertebral deformities is a critical component of osteoporosis evaluation, because many vertebral fractures are clinically silent yet strongly predict future fracture risk and adverse outcomes. These data support integrating systematic spine imaging (e.g., VFA or lateral spine radiographs) into routine bone health assessment rather than relying on DXA-derived BMD alone [15, 34], which is consistent with the present findings in pediatric TDT.

In the present study, the prevalence of osteoporosis among patients with TDT (34.8%) was higher than that of 23% of patients with β-thalassemia aged 2.5–80 years and from several countries [9, 10]. On the other hand, the prevalence of osteoporosis in TDT has been reported to vary widely from 18% to 75% [35–38], depending upon the diagnostic criteria, transfusion status, iron chelation therapy status, and patient age. Of note, those previous studies [35–38] were performed in adults or the general population of patients regardless of age. Studies revealed a higher prevalence of fractures in patients with TDT than in non-transfusion-dependent thalassemia [39, 40]. Previous studies in children are limited by small numbers of patients but suggest a prevalence of osteoporosis of 10.5%-10.8% [41, 42].

A recently developed guideline dedicated to osteoporosis in transfusion-dependent thalassemia underscores that skeletal complications, particularly vertebral and long-bone fractures, remain a major cause of morbidity despite advances in transfusion and chelation therapy and recommends proactive case-finding using both BMD and vertebral imaging in this high-risk group. These recommendations align with our observation that a substantial proportion of pediatric patients with TDT already fulfill diagnostic criteria for osteoporosis, highlighting the need to implement structured, guideline-based screening pathways from childhood to mitigate cumulative fracture burden over the life course [11, 12].

The early screening and diagnosis of osteoporosis in patients with TDT is important to reduce the fracture risk and related complications [43]. It is particularly important in children and adolescents since such patients are at a very high risk of fracture in adulthood [44]. Unfortunately, the present study did not identify any demographic, clinical, or biochemical factors that could help identify patients needing more aggressive screening and prevention strategies. The discrepancies among studies can be due to several factors, including the sample size, the variables included in the study, the characteristics of the study population, and the diagnostic criteria and reference values. Patients with TDT are more at risk of having traditional factors associated with low BMD and fractures, such as physical inactivity, small body weight, and nutritional deficiencies [3, 45, 46]. Other non-traditional factors have been reported to be associated with low BMD in patients with TDT, including genetic factors [47], bone marrow expansion [48], iron toxicity [49], and hormonal imbalances [50]. Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) levels are associated with low BMD in TDT [43], but it was not observed here. Therefore, those results emphasize the importance of monitoring for spine fractures.

In the present study, osteoporosis was diagnosed using the ISCD criteria [21]. Among the patients who could not be diagnosed, seven patients (7/90, 7.8%) had a history of limb fractures, and their height-corrected TBLH-BMD (WHO criteria) Z-score showed no statistically significant difference compared with those with osteoporosis, highlighting the complexity of the diagnosis. It also emphasizes the importance of preventing fractures among patients without a formal diagnosis of osteoporosis. No osteoporosis diagnosis does not imply a low fracture risk, and these patients need to be closely monitored. Indeed, fracture risk remains high in all patients with TDT [13, 51, 52]. TBLH-BMD height-corrected Z-score ≤-2 based on the WHO reference values was associated with osteoporosis, while BMD height-corrected Z-score ≤-2 based on the Chinese reference values was not associated with osteoporosis. Indeed, the WHO and the Chinese reference values were derived from different populations. Surprisingly, the present study included Chinese patients who might be expected to fit the Chinese reference values more than international values, mainly derived from Caucasian populations. Since TDT significantly affects somatic development [9, 10], it could explain the discrepancy of Chinese patients with TDT not matching the Chinese reference values. Indeed, all patients with TDT suffer from some growth retardation [53], and about 33% of patients with TDT have a short stature [54]. Iron overload, a consequence of repeated blood transfusions [1], is also an issue since it may cause growth retardation and organ damage in children with TDT [55]. The differences between the two reference values can explain the discrepancy observed here in the diagnosis of osteoporosis. TBLH-BMD height-corrected Z-score ≤-2 (WHO reference values) was reported to be associated with osteoporosis in patients with types 2 and 3 spinal muscular atrophy [56]. In the present study, TBLH-BMD height-corrected Z-score ≤-2 had a low sensitivity for osteoporosis, indicating that it cannot be used for detecting osteoporosis, but it had a high specificity, suggesting that it could be used to rule out other diagnoses. It has been suggested that all patients with TDT should start to undergo BMD screening at age 8 to detect osteoporosis as soon as possible [57]. A study from Hong Kong indicated that DXA was insufficient to predict the fracture risk [52], supporting the present study. Additional studies are necessary to determine the optimal screening strategy and its impact on patient prognosis. Nevertheless, the present study suggests that because the diagnostic value of BMD changes with different criteria, screening for spinal fractures is more important than screening for BMD for the diagnosis of osteoporosis in TDT. Nevertheless, the present study was cross-sectional, and not all patients had reached the 19-year threshold for determining the history of pediatric TDT-related osteoporosis. In addition, the fracture risk can evolve over time. Therefore, follow-up studies are important to determine the actual risk of pediatric TDT-related osteoporosis.

The present study had several limitations. First, the patients were recruited from only two centers in Huizhou, and all patients were from a single geographical area, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Secondly, children < 5 years old were excluded because there are no WHO reference standards for children < 5 years old. Third, the biochemical analyses were limited to routine tests conducted at the authors’ hospital for patients with TDT. Fourth, complaints like pain, limitation of movement, or neurological symptoms or signs were not collected. Finally, only patients without osteoporosis treatment (e.g., bisphosphonates) were included, and no data were available about those on treatment; such patients should be the focus of future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the prevalence of osteoporosis among children and adolescents with TDT was 34.8%, indicating the need for attention and screening. In TDT, the diagnosis of osteoporosis requires the early detection of spinal fractures because NMD assessed using DXA is of limited value.
