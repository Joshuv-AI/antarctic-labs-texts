Copy number variation analysis of cerebrospinal fluid metagenomic next‐generation sequencing data in assisting the diagnosis of pediatric brain tumors
Authors: Changhong Ren, Xiuwei Zhuo, Xinying Yang, Xingfeng Yao, Shuai Gong, Hui Xiong, Fang Fang, Weihua Zhang
Published: 2026

Pediatric central nervous system (CNS) malignancies are rare but represent the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality in children. 1 Early diagnosis is crucial for improving prognosis, yet it remains challenging because CNS malignancies, infections, and inflammatory syndromes often present with overlapping clinical and radiologic features. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology has low sensitivity due to the paucicellular nature of CSF, and brain biopsy in children is difficult due to surgical risks, deep anatomical locations, and parental concerns. 2 Therefore, a minimally invasive test that can simultaneously screen for infections and tumors is highly desirable.

Metagenomic next‐generation sequencing (mNGS) of CSF is increasingly used for pathogen detection. 3 , 4 Beyond pathogen identification, the same sequencing data can be analyzed for copy number variations (CNVs), including aneuploidy and large chromosomal gains or losses, which are hallmarks of many human cancers. 5 , 6 While mNGS uses shallow whole‐genome sequencing primarily for pathogen detection, the depth of coverage across the human genome, although non‐uniform, is sufficient to detect large‐scale copy number gains or losses. Previous studies have shown that CNV analysis of CSF mNGS data can detect CNS tumors in adults, 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 but its utility in pediatric populations remains underexplored. This single‐center study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of CSF mNGS‐derived CNV analysis in children with clinically ambiguous presentations suspected of CNS malignancy.

We enrolled consecutive patients aged <18 years hospitalized at Beijing Children's Hospital (a national referral center in China) between January 2022 and January 2024 whose conditions were difficult to differentiate clinically as infectious, inflammatory, or neoplastic. CSF samples were obtained as part of routine diagnostic workup. The reference standard for a final diagnosis of CNS malignancy was histopathological examination of tumor tissue or definitive CSF cytology/flow cytometry. For non‐malignant conditions, the final diagnosis was based on a composite reference standard including clinical follow‐up (≥6 months), radiographic stability or improvement, and identification of a specific pathogen or autoimmune antibody when applicable. CNV analysis was performed by personnel blinded to all clinical, radiological, and pathological data. Final clinical diagnoses were established by clinicians who were unaware of the CNV results.

CSF DNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were performed using commercial kits on the Illumina Nextseq platform (Illumina Nextseq CN550; Illumina Inc., USA) at V‐Medical laboratory (China). Raw sequencing reads were processed through a strict quality control pipeline. Human reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using BWA software (version 0.7.17‐r1188). For CNV analysis, the human‐aligned reads were analyzed using CNVkit (version 0.9.9) with default parameters (a window size of 1000 bp and a minimum bin count of 10). A baseline dataset was constructed from CSF samples of 27 patients with confirmed autoimmune encephalitis and no evidence of neoplasia on 6‐month follow‐up (median sequencing depth 65 million reads). In these inflammatory controls, no false‐positive CNVs >10 Mb were detected, suggesting that inflammation does not generate large‐scale CNV calls. A CNV segment greater than 10 Mb in length was considered positive, a threshold derived from previous literature 7 and validated against our baseline dataset, where no such large CNVs were detected. For pathogen detection, non‐human sequences were analyzed using an in‐house metagenomic analysis pipeline (MiTopia‐PAI) to identify clinically relevant pathogens. Median sequencing depth per sample was 65 million raw reads (range 52–75 million), providing sufficient coverage for CNV detection at >10 Mb resolution. Among the four CNV‐positive cases, raw sequencing reads ranged from 53 to 73 million (DNA library), with Q30 scores >91%, ensuring robust CNV detection.

A total of 75 CSF samples from 75 patients were initially examined. After excluding 12 patients without a definitive final diagnosis during the study period, 63 cases were included in the final analysis. The median age was 9.0 years (range 1.0–16.0 years), and 26 (41%) were male. Final diagnoses revealed CNS malignant neoplasms in nine patients: six cases of glioma, one case of ganglioglioma, one case of primary CNS erythroid sarcoma, and one case of primary meningeal melanocytoma. The remaining 54 patients had non‐malignant conditions: autoimmune encephalitis (n = 18), viral meningitis/encephalitis (n = 15), bacterial meningitis (n = 8), tuberculous meningitis (n = 5), fungal meningitis (n = 2), and other non‐inflammatory neurological disorders (n = 6).

CNVs >10 Mb were detected in four of the nine malignant neoplasm cases, yielding a sensitivity of 44% (95% confidence interval [CI], 15%–77%). Among the four positive cases, one exhibited 10 large CNVs (>10 Mb), while the other three showed three, four, and six large CNVs, respectively. The specificity was 100% (95% CI, 91%–100%) in the 54 clinically non‐malignant specimens, indicating no false positives were identified. mNGS for pathogen detection identified no significant pathogenic microorganisms in any of the four CNV‐positive cases. Among the five CNV‐negative malignancy cases, most were low‐grade or certain high‐grade gliomas (including H3K27M‐mutant cases). This suggests that lower chromosomal instability or limited shedding of tumor DNA into CSF may account for the negative findings, regardless of histological grade.

Case 1 was a 1‐year‐old girl with recurrent seizures and limb weakness. Brain MRI showed diffuse nodular leptomeningeal enhancement. CSF revealed white blood cell (WBC) 24/µL, elevated protein (1824 mg/L), and atypical erythroblasts confirmed by flow cytometry. CSF mNGS detected three large CNVs, and the final diagnosis was primary CNS erythroid sarcoma. Case 2 was a 12‐year‐old boy with eye pain, vomiting, decreased visual acuity, and facial nerve palsy. CSF opening pressure was 300 mm H2O, flow cytometry showed 45% malignant non‐hematopoietic cells, and MRI demonstrated diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement. CSF mNGS detected six large CNVs, and biopsy confirmed primary meningeal melanocytoma. Case 3 was an 8‐year‐old girl with headache, vomiting, and seizures. CSF showed elevated opening pressure (400 mmH2O), WBC 21/µL (95% monocytes), and elevated protein (1029 mg/L). CSF cytology was benign, but CSF mNGS revealed 4 large CNVs. Brain biopsy ultimately diagnosed high‐grade glioma. Case 4 was a 6‐year‐old boy with headache, diplopia, vomiting, and seizures. CSF opening pressure was >330 mmH2O, WBC 42/µL, and flow cytometry showed 39% malignant non‐hematopoietic cells despite negative cytology. CSF mNGS detected 10 large CNVs, and biopsy confirmed diffuse midline glioma with H3K27M mutation (World Health Organization 2021 grade IV).

Our mNGS‐CNV approach offers several practical advantages. First, compared to invasive brain biopsy, CSF CNV analysis is minimally invasive and has high acceptability in pediatric populations. Second, it requires only 0.6 mL of CSF, sufficient for simultaneous pathogen and tumor analysis. Third, results are available within 24–48 h, significantly reducing diagnostic delay. Fourth, it demonstrates high diagnostic specificity (100%), meaning a positive CNV result strongly supports a neoplastic process and can prompt early confirmatory biopsy. In our cohort, a positive CNV result led to earlier brain biopsy in three of four cases (Cases 2–4), facilitating timely access to targeted therapies.

The use of CNV analysis in CSF to aid diagnosis of CNS tumors is predicated on the chromosomal instability characteristic of human tumors, which can manifest as CNVs, including chromosomal duplications, deletions, or aneuploidy. 5 , 6 A comprehensive pan‐cancer study examining CNVs across 33 tumor types revealed significant variability in CNV detection rates among different tumor categories. 9 In particular, melanoma (including primary meningeal melanocytoma) showed a high susceptibility to commonly detected CNVs; 11 similarly, gliomas and lymphomas also exhibited an increased frequency of CNVs, whereas certain other malignancies lacked detectable CNVs altogether, suggesting that CNV detection rates are associated with neoplasm type. Previous studies have also suggested that imaging characteristics such as leptomeningeal involvement or tumor adjacency to CSF spaces may increase the sensitivity of CSF mNGS assays, although this did not reach statistical significance. 4 We did not observe this association in our cohort, but further large‐sample studies are warranted.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged: the small cohort of confirmed malignancies (n = 9) limits statistical power to assess sensitivity across tumor subtypes; the observed sensitivity of 44% indicates that nearly half of pediatric CNS tumors may not shed sufficient aberrant DNA into CSF or may lack large CNVs detectable by this method; CNV analysis cannot replace histologic or molecular diagnosis and should be viewed as a complementary triage tool to prioritize patients for biopsy rather than a definitive diagnostic test; finally, the single‐center design and use of a specific mNGS platform may limit generalizability, and validation in larger multicenter cohorts is needed.

In conclusion, CSF CNV detection via mNGS demonstrates high specificity (100%) and moderate sensitivity (44%) for diagnosing pediatric CNS malignancies. Its ability to concurrently screen for infections and tumors from a single minimally invasive test offers significant clinical utility for children with diagnostically challenging presentations. A positive CNV result provides strong impetus for confirmatory brain biopsy, while a negative result does not exclude malignancy. Further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to refine this approach and explore its utility across different tumor types.
