Abstract
. A general construction of Laakso-type fractal spaces was recently introduced by the first two authors. In this paper, we establish a simple condition characterizing when the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension of a symmetric Laakso-type fractal space is attained. The attaining metrics are constructed explicitly. This gives new examples of attainment and clarifies the possible obstructions.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

Conformal dimension
is a quasisymmetric invariant introduced by Pansu
[
36
]
. Given a metric space, its
(Ahlfors regular) conformal dimension
is the infimum of the Hausdorff dimensions of all Ahlfors regular metric spaces quasisymmetrically equivalent to it; see Subsection
2.1
for detailed definition. While the value of this infimum can often be estimated numerically
[
11
]
, in most cases it remains unknown whether the infimum is realized — that is, whether the conformal dimension of the metric space is
attained
. This question is known as the
attainment problem
.

A better understanding of attainment could provide answers to several open problems. For example, Bonk and Kleiner
[
4
]
showed that Cannon’s conjecture, a long-standing conjecture in geometric group theory, hinges on addressing the attainment problem for the boundaries of certain hyperbolic groups. A version of the attainment problem is also equivalent to the so-called Kleiner’s conjecture
[
26
, Conjecture 7.5]
on metric spaces satisfying the
combinatorial Loewner property
. While counterexamples to this conjecture were recently found by the first two named authors
[
2
]
, the problem of characterizing attainment was left open.

In this paper, we fully resolve the attainment problem for a class of metric spaces called
symmetric Laakso-type fractal spaces
. These include the counterexamples considered in
[
2
]
and the well-known fractals introduced by Laakso
[
30
,
31
,
29
]
. These fractals also give simpler models for boundaries of hyperbolic buildings and Fuchsian buildings considered in
[
8
]
. This significantly enlarges the class for which the attainment problem is understood. While interesting examples, such as the Sierpński carpet, remain open, the class of symmetric Laakso-type fractal spaces is a natural first step towards understanding the attainment problem in a more general context.

1.2.
Main Theorem

Each Laakso-type fractal space arises as a certain
limit space
of a sequence of graph approximations generated by an
iterated graph system (IGS)
. A detailed description of this construction is provided in Section
3
; here we provide a brief outline of the procedure.

The construction of a Laakso-type fractal begins with a
generator
graph
G_{1}
. One then produces a sequence of graphs
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
inductively as follows. Given
G_{n}
, each edge of
G_{n}
is first replaced with a copy of
G_{1}
. The copies meeting at junctions are then glued together by identifying vertices according to a fixed gluing rule, thereby producing
G_{n+1}
. After equipping each graph
G_{n}
with a suitable semi-metric
d_{n}
, one obtains the Laakso-type fractal space
(X,d_{L_{*}})
as the Gromov–Hausdorff limit of the sequence
(G_{n},d_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. The limit metric
d_{L_{*}}
is called the intrinsic metric; see Definition
4.11
. While the construction of the metric is slightly technical, in many cases, this metric is comparable to a re-scaled limit of weighted path metrics on
G_{n}
; see Remark
4.20
. We call a Laakso-type fractal space
symmetric
, if
G_{1}
has a certain mirror symmetry; see the next subsection for more discussion.

Figure 1.2.
The Laakso diamond.

Our main results demonstrate that the quasiconformal geometry of a symmetric Laakso-type space can be understood by solving a discrete modulus problem on the generator. In particular, the challenging attainment problem can be resolved in this setting by determining whether the generator contains a so-called
removable edge
.

Theorem 1.3
.

A symmetric Laakso-type fractal space
(X,d_{L_{*}})
attains its Ahlfors regular conformal dimension if and only if it has no removable edges.

We now proceed to describe the terminology appearing in this theorem and to sketch the techniques used in the proof.

\eta(I_{-})=I_{+}

I_{-}

As our main tool, we construct a family of quasisymmetrically equivalent metrics on the limit space, including the intrinsic metric

. Inspired by

, each metric in the family is given by an

on the generator. Similarly as in these references, we denote the family of edge paths from

to

by
\Theta^{(1)}
, and we say that a function
\rho\colon E_{1}\to[0,1]
is a
\Theta^{(1)}

\sum_{e\in\theta}\rho(e)\geq 1
for every
\theta\in\Theta^{(1)}
. A density
\rho

if
\rho(\eta(e))=\rho(e)
. The following theorem describes the family of metrics associated to such densities.

Theorem 1.5
.

For every symmetric
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density
\rho\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
, there exists a metric
d_{\rho}
on
X
so that
(X,d_{\rho})
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular, where
Q\geq 1
is the unique value that satisfies the equation:

(1.6)

\sum_{e\in E_{1}}\rho(e)^{Q}=1.

Moreover, for any two such densities
\rho
and
\nu
, the spaces
(X,d_{\rho})
and
(X,d_{\nu})
are quasisymmetrically equivalent.

As a result of Theorem
1.5
, we obtain upper bounds for the conformal dimension. Obtaining lower bounds is generally much harder. Using an approach motivated by a series of works
[
36
,
40
,
41
,
22
,
11
]
, we obtain the conformal dimension as a critical exponent of a discrete modulus problem. For any
p\geq 1
, the
p
-edge modulus of
\Theta^{(1)}
is the value

{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1}):=\inf\bigg\{\sum_{e\in E_{1}}\rho(e)^{p}\ \bigg|\ \rho\colon E_{1}\to[0,1]\text{ is }\Theta^{(1)}\text{-admissible}\bigg\}.

Theorem 1.7
.

The Ahlfors regular conformal dimension of
(X,d_{L_{*}})
is the unique value
Q_{*}\geq 1
satisfying
{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1
.

In contrast to a similar characterization of conformal dimension in
[
11
]
, our special case offers some key simplifications. While one generally needs to consider the asymptotics of certain modulus problems on a sequence graphs with exponentially growing complexity, in our setting we only need to solve one modulus problem for a single graph. Compare for example to the involved numerical estimation of the conformal dimension of the Sierpiński carpet in
[
28
]
.

It follows from convex optimization theory that whenever
Q_{*}>1
, there exists a unique admissible density
\rho^{*}\colon E_{1}\to[0,1)
that is
Q_{*}
-optimal, in the sense that

\sum_{e\in E_{1}}\rho^{*}(e)^{Q_{*}}={\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1.

Due to the symmetry of the generator, the optimal density
\rho^{*}
is also symmetric. It then follows from Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.7
that the conformal dimension is attained if
\rho^{*}(e)>0
for all
e\in E_{1}
. Our main theorem, Theorem
1.3
, asserts that this condition is also necessary.

Any edge
e^{*}\in E_{1}
for which
\rho^{*}(e^{*})=0
is called a
removable edge
. Intuitively, the removal of such an edge leaves the value of the discrete modulus problem, and thus also the conformal dimension, unchanged. Using this observation, we prove Theorem
1.3
by showing that the presence of a removable edge implies the existence of a porous subset
\widehat{X}\subset X
with the same conformal dimension, which is a known obstruction to attainment.

1.4.
Kleiner’s conjecture and Sobolev spaces

One of the original motivations for the study of Laakso-type spaces was Kleiner’s conjecture on the attainment of conformal dimension for a certain family of self-similar spaces.
See Section
6
for a precise definition and detailed discussion. In
[
2
]
, the first two authors constructed counter-examples to this conjecture by exhibiting Laakso-type spaces having removable edges. Our results here show that there is also large family of Laakso type spaces that yield non-trivial positive examples for this conjecture; see Figure
1.8
, Corollary
6.7
and Theorem
6.6
. In these cases, the attaining metrics have an explicit description, but they lack a simple formula in terms of e.g. snowflaking. The attaining metrics also seem interesting in their own right, since they satisfy the Loewner property.

Another motivation for this work comes from the study of Sobolev spaces on fractals defined by re-scaled energies, an approach spearheaded by
[
27
]
and further developed for all
p>1
in
[
25
,
39
,
34
]
. One can see hints of such constructions also in
[
36
,
6
]
. For Laakso type spaces these spaces and their properties were studied in
[
1
]
; see therein also for an introduction on Sobolev spaces on fractals. Since
[
34
]
, it has been known that Sobolev spaces and their energy constructions are closely related to attainment problems; see
[
1
, Remark 8.7]
for further discussion. The functions
f
in the Sobolev space are associated with a natural energy measure
\Gamma_{p}\langle f\rangle
, and a measure class of minimal energy dominating measures
\Lambda
that are minimal measures s.t. absolute continuity of energy measures,
\Gamma_{p}\langle f\rangle\ll\Lambda
, holds for all Sobolev functions
f
. In
[
34
,
21
]
, it was suggested that the attainment problem is closely related to the existence of a doubling energy dominating measure. For Laakso type spaces, our work shows that the relationship between these problems becomes an equivalence. Indeed, for Laakso type spaces, the existence of a doubling minimal energy dominant measure is characterized in terms of removable edges
[
1
, Theorem 8.4]
. This is the same condition as we obtained for attainment. Thus, for these spaces, attainment is equivalent to the existence of a doubling minimal energy dominant measure. This closely ties together the energy based approach to attainment in
[
34
,
1
]
and the explicit metric constructions used in this paper. These remarks prove the following alternate characterization for attainment.

Theorem 1.9
.

A symmetric Laakso-type fractal space attains its Ahlfors regular conformal dimension if and only if there is a doubling minimal energy dominant measure for the Sobolev space constructed in
[
1
]
.

For general spaces such a simple characterization is not expected to hold. See for example
[
21
]
, where a related notion of conformal walk dimension is discussed. There, the Sierpiński tetrahedron is shown to be a space with a doubling energy dominant measure, but not attaining its conformal walk dimension. The case of conformal dimension (greater than one) is analogous, but different, and seems to require the study of more complicated examples. For general spaces and for conformal dimension instead of conformal walk dimension, the attainment problem seems to depend on a much more delicate study of the associated energy measures. To indicate how difficult these problems are, we note that in many cases, such as the Sierpiński carpet, it is not presently even known if a doubling minimal energy dominant measure exists. Much more work is needed to understand these points fully.

2.1.
Metric geometry

Given a metric space
(X,d)
, we define for every
x\in X
and
r>0
the open ball of center
x
and radius
r
as the set

B_{d}(x,r):=\{y\in X\mid d(x,y)<r\}.

Given two nonempty sets
A,B\subseteq X
, we define the diameter of
A
and the distance between
A
and
B
as

{\rm diam}(A,d):=\sup_{x,y\in A}d(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm dist}(A,B,d):=\inf_{a\in A,b\in B}d(a,b),

respectively. When the metric is clear from the context, we omit it from the notation and simply write
B(x,r)
,
{\rm diam}(A)
and
{\rm dist}(A,B)
. For
Q>0
, the
Q
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
of a subset
A\subset X
is

\mathcal{H}^{Q}(A):=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{Q}(A),

where for all
\delta>0
the
Q
-dimensional Hausdorff
\delta
-content
of
A
is defined as

\mathcal{H}^{Q}_{\delta}(A):=\inf\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm diam}(A_{i})^{Q}\;\bigg|\;A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i},\;{\rm diam}(A_{i})\leq\delta\bigg\}.

The
Hausdorff dimension
of
(X,d)
is the value

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(X,d):=\inf\{Q>0\mid\mathcal{H}^{Q}(X)=0\}.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a metric space
(X,d)
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular
for
Q>0
if there is a Radon measure
\mu
on
(X,d)
and a constant
C\geq 1
such that

(2.2)

C^{-1}r^{Q}\leq\mu(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{Q}\text{ for all }x\in X\text{ and }r\in(0,2{\rm diam}(X)].

If
(X,d)
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular for some
Q>0
, we say that
(X,d)
is Ahlfors regular.

We now review two standard properties of Ahlfors regular metric spaces. First, it follows from a volume counting argument that Ahlfors regular metric spaces satisfy the
metric doubling property
. This means that there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
x\in X
and
r>0
, there are
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in X
satisfying

B(x,2r)\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}B(x_{i},r).

Second, if a metric space is
Q
-Ahlfors regular for
Q>0
, then its Hausdorff dimension is equal to
Q

[
20
, Pages 61-62]
. Moreover,
\mu=\mathcal{H}^{Q}
satisfies (
2.2
).

2.2.
Conformal dimension

We now present the terminology and some results related to conformal dimension; see the monograph
[
32
]
by Mackay and Tyson for a comprehensive introduction.
Given a homeomorphism
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
, we say that a homeomorphism between metric spaces
f\colon(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
is an
\eta
-quasisymmetry
if for all triples
x,y,z\in X
with
x\neq z
,

\frac{d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))}{d_{Y}(f(x),f(z))}\leq\eta\bigg(\frac{d_{X}(x,y)}{d_{X}(x,z)}\bigg).

If
f:(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
is an
\eta
-quasisymmetry for some
\eta
, we say that
f
is a
quasisymmetry
. We say that two metrics
d
and
\delta
on a set
X
are
quasisymmetrically equivalent
and denote
(X,d)\sim_{\text{qs}}(X,\delta)
, if the identity map
{\rm id}\colon(X,d)\to(X,\delta)
is a quasisymmetry. The Ahlfors regular conformal gauge of
(X,d)
is the collection

\mathcal{G}_{{\rm AR}}(X,d):=\left\{\delta\ \middle|\begin{array}[]{l}\ \delta\text{ is a metric on }X,\ (X,d)\sim_{\text{qs}}(X,\delta)\text{ and}\\
(X,\delta)\text{ is }Q\text{-Ahlfors regular for some }Q\geq 1\end{array}\right\},

and the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension of
(X,d)
is the value

\dim_{\text{AR}}(X,d)=\inf\{\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(X,\delta)\mid\delta\in\mathcal{G}_{\text{AR}}(X,d)\}.

We note that the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension is not monotone with respect to inclusion. For example, the unit square
X=([0,1]^{2},\lvert\cdot\rvert)
has conformal dimension
\dim_{{\rm AR}}(X)=2
, but there are no Ahlfors regular metrics on the subset
Y=\{1/n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{0\}\subset X
, and hence
\dim_{{\rm AR}}(Y)=\infty
. Thus, without some additional assumptions on the subset, it is not automatic that
\dim_{{\rm AR}}(Y)\leq\dim_{{\rm AR}}(X)
whenever
Y\subset X
. For this reason, we consider the Assouad dimension.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. For all
A\subset X
, denote

N_{r}(A):=\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ \middle|\ \exists(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\subset X\text{ such that }A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B(x_{i},r)\right\}.

The
Assouad dimension
of a metric space
X
is defined as the value

\dim_{\rm A}(X):=\inf\left\{\alpha>0\ \middle|\ \begin{array}[]{l}\exists C\geq 1\text{ such that for all }0<r<R\\
\text{and }x\in X,\ N_{r}(B(x,R))\leq C(R/r)^{\alpha}\end{array}\right\}.

The Assouad dimension is monotone; if
Y\subset X
, then
\dim_{\rm A}(Y)\leq\dim_{\rm A}(X)
. This follows quite directly from the definition. Moreover, if a metric space
(X,d)
is Ahlfors regular, then it follows from a standard volume counting argument that
\dim_{\rm A}(X,d)=\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(X,d)
. We make use of these facts by considering subsets that are complete and
uniformly perfect
.

Definition 2.4
.

A metric space
(X,d)
is uniformly perfect if there exists a constant
C\geq 1
so that

B(x,r)\setminus B(x,r/C)\neq\varnothing

for every
x\in X
and
r\in(0,{\rm diam}(X,d))
.

We remark that a uniformly perfect metric space lying inside an Ahlfors regular metric space supports an Ahlfors regular metric. That is,
\mathcal{G}_{{\rm AR}}(X,d)\neq\emptyset
, and thus
\dim_{{\rm AR}}(X)<\infty
. In fact, this is an equivalence because every Ahlfors regular space is uniformly perfect.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
20
, Proposition 14.14]
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be an Ahlfors regular metric space. If
Y\subset X
is complete and uniformly perfect, then
\dim_{\rm AR}(Y,d|_{Y})\leq\dim_{\mathrm{A}}(X,d)
.

It follows quite directly that monotonicity of conformal dimension, with respect to inclusion, holds for subsets that are complete and uniformly perfect.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space with
\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d)<\infty
. If a subset
Y\subset(X,d)
is complete and uniformly perfect, then
\dim_{\rm AR}(Y,d|_{Y})\leq\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d)
.

Proof.

Let
\delta\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X,d)
. As uniform perfectness is preserved by quasisymmetric maps (
[
20
, Exercise 11.2]
), the space
(Y,\delta|_{Y})
is complete and uniformly perfect. Consequently, we have by Lemma
2.5
that

\dim_{\rm AR}(Y,d|_{Y})=\dim_{\rm AR}(Y,\delta|_{Y})\leq\dim_{\rm A}(Y,\delta|_{Y})\leq\dim_{\rm A}(X,\delta)=\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(X,\delta).

Since
\delta\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X,d)
was arbitrary, it follows that

\dim_{\rm AR}(Y,d|_{Y})\leq\inf_{\delta\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X,d)}\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(X,d)=\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d).

∎

A known obstruction to attainment, is the existence of suitable
porous
subsets. We say that a subset
Y
of a metric space
X
is porous, if there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that for all
y\in Y
and
r\in(0,{\rm diam}(X))
there exists a point
x\in X
so that
B(x,\delta r)\subset B(y,r)\setminus Y
.

Proposition 2.7

(
[
2
, Proposition 2.9]
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a compact Ahlfors regular metric space and let
Y\subset X
be a porous subset. If
\dim_{\rm AR}(Y,d|_{Y})=\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d)
, then the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension of
(X,d)
is not attained.

We remark, that the proof for
[
2
, Proposition 2.9]
contains a small gap, since the uniform perfectness of the subset
Y
is not discussed. This is implied by the assumption of
\dim_{\rm AR}(Y,d|_{Y})=\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d)
, together with the implied assumption that
\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d)<\infty
; see the paragraph before Lemma
2.5
. In fact, the same proof and statement appears in
[
10
, Proposition 8.3]
, where the same omission occurs.

2.3.
Graphs and Modulus

A
graph
is an ordered triple
G:=(V,E,\xi)
where
V
is a finite non-empty set of
vertices
,
E
is a finite set of
edges
and

\xi\colon E\to\{\{v,w\}\mid v,w\in V,\;v\neq w\}

is an
endpoint map
, which assigns each edge to an unordered pair of distinct vertices called the
endpoints
of the edge. We often write
v\in e
as a shorthand for
v\in\xi(e)
to indicate that a vertex
v\in V
is an endpoint of an edge
e\in E
. Likewise, we write
e\cap f
to denote
\xi(e)\cap\xi(f)
, the set of endpoints shared by some edges
e,f\in E
. The
degree
of a vertex
v\in V
is the number of edges that have it as an endpoint:

\deg(v):=\lvert\{e\in E\mid v\in e\}\rvert.

Convention 2.8
.

Whenever it is necessary to distinguish between the endpoints of an edge
e\in E
, we may denote them by
e^{-}
and
e^{+}
, so that

\xi(e)=\{e^{-},e^{+}\}.

The assignment of the labels
e^{-}
and
e^{+}
is arbitrary, except for graphs arising from simple iterated graph systems, for which we will always use a canonical assignment specified in Definition
3.2
.

Let
G=(V,E,\xi)
be a graph. If
V^{\prime}\subset V
and
E^{\prime}\subset E
so that
\xi(E^{\prime})\subseteq E
, then the graph
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},\xi|_{E^{\prime}})
is a
subgraph
of of
G
. Given a collection
\{G_{i}=(V_{i},E_{i},\xi_{i})\}_{i\in I}
of subgraphs of
G
, we define their union and intersection, respectively, as the subgraphs

\bigcup_{i\in I}G_{i}:=\big(\bigcup_{i\in I}V_{i},\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i},\xi|_{\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i}}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\bigcap_{i\in I}G_{i}:=\big(\bigcap_{i\in I}V_{i},\bigcap_{i\in I}E_{i},\xi|_{\bigcap_{i\in I}E_{i}}\big).

An alternating sequence
\theta=[v_{0},e_{1},v_{1},\ldots,e_{k},v_{k}]
, where
\{v_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\subset V
,
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}\subset E
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, is a path from
v_{0}
to
v_{k}
if
\xi(e_{i})=\{v_{i-1},v_{i}\}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. We say that an edge
e\in E
belongs to
\theta
and denote
e\in\theta
if
e=e_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq k
. Given any two non-empty sets of vertices
A,B\subset V
, we denote the collection of paths joining
A
to
B
by

\Theta(A,B):=\{\theta\mid\theta\text{ is a path from }v\in A\text{ to }w\in B\}.

The length of a path is the number of edges it contains, and given a path
\theta
, we denote its length by

{\rm len}(\theta):=\lvert\{e\in E\mid e\in\theta\}\rvert.

If there exists a path from
v
to
w
for every pair
v,w\in V
, then we say that
G
is
connected
and we define the
path metric

d_{G}
on
V
by

d_{G}(v,w):=\min_{\theta\in\Theta(\{v\},\{w\})}{\rm len}(\theta).

We will often omit writing more than the first vertex of a path, simply denoting
\theta=[v_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k}]
, as the rest of the vertices are unambiguously determined by the equalities
\xi(e_{i})=\{v_{i-1},v_{i}\}
.

Given two paths
\theta_{1}=[v_{0},e_{1},\ldots e_{k},v_{k}]
and
\theta_{2}=[u_{0},f_{1},\ldots u_{l},f_{l}]
for which
v_{k}=u_{0}
, we define the concatenation

\theta_{1}*\theta_{2}:=[v_{0},e_{1},\ldots e_{k},v_{k},f_{1},\ldots,f_{l},u_{l}].

A key tool in our arguments, is the concept of edge modulus of a family of paths.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
G=(V,E,\xi)
be a graph. A function
\rho\colon E\to[0,\infty)
is called a density. The support of a density
\rho
is the set
{\rm supp}(\rho):=\{e\in E\mid\rho(e)\neq 0\}
and the
\rho
-length of any non-constant path
\theta=[v_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k}]
in
G
is

L_{\rho}(\theta):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\rho(e_{i}).

Given a family of paths
\Theta
in
G
, we say that a density
\rho
is
\Theta
-admissible
if
L_{\rho}(\theta)\geq 1
for every path
\theta\in\Theta
. We denote the set of
\Theta
-admissible densities is by
\operatorname{Adm}(\Theta)
.

For every
p\in[1,\infty)
, we define the
p
-mass of a density
\rho
as the value

\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho):=\sum_{e\in E}\rho(e)^{p},

and the
p
-edge modulus of a family of paths
\Theta
as the value

{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G):=\inf_{\rho\in\operatorname{Adm}(\Theta)}\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho).

A density
\rho^{*}\in\operatorname{Adm}(\Theta)
satisfying
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho^{*})={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G)
is called
p
-optimal
for
\Theta
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\Theta
be a non-empty family of paths on a graph
G=(V,E,\xi)
. Then, there exists a
p
-optimal
\rho^{*}\in\operatorname{Adm}(\Theta)
. If
p>1
, then the
p
-optimal
\rho^{*}
is unique. Further, if
{\rm len}(\theta)\geq 2
for all
\theta\in\Theta
, then
\rho(e)<1
for all
e\in E
.

Proof.

The existence follows from the optimization problem being convex, and since the optimum must be attained for
\rho
that satisfy
\rho(e)\in[0,1]
. The uniqueness for
p>1
follows from strict convexity.

Finally, assume that
{\rm len}(\theta)\geq 2
for all edges
e
and assume for the sake of contradiction that
\rho
is optimal and admissible with
\rho(e)=1
for some
e\in E
. Let
L(e)=\min_{e\in\theta\in\Theta}L_{\rho}(\theta)\geq 1
, where the minimum is attained since
\{L_{\rho}(\theta):\theta\in\Theta\}
is a discrete set.

If
L(e)>1
, then we can decrease
\rho(e)
slightly without altering admissibility. This contradicts minimality. If
L_{\rho}(e)=1
, then there is some edge
f\in E
with
\rho(f)=0
. In this case define a new admissible weight function for
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)

\rho_{\epsilon}(f)=\begin{cases}1-\epsilon&\text{ if }f=e\\
\epsilon&\text{ if }\rho(f)=0\\
\rho(f)&\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}

It is direct to verify that
\rho_{\epsilon}
is admissible. Further
\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{\epsilon})|_{\epsilon=0}=-p
.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\Theta
be a non-empty family of paths on a graph
G=(V,E,\xi)
so that
{\rm len}(\theta)\geq 2
for all
\theta\in\Theta
. Then, the mapping
p\mapsto{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G)
is continuous and strictly decreasing for
p\in[1,\infty)
.

Proof.

Let
\rho
be the
p
-minimal density for
\Theta
, for any
p\in(1,\infty)
. By Lemma
2.10
,
\rho
is sub-unitary, and therefore the mapping
t\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{t}(\rho)
is strictly decreasing for all
t\in[1,\infty)
. Let
1<q<p
, and let
\nu
and
\rho
be the
q
- and
p
-minimal densities for
\Theta
, respectively. We then have that

{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G)=\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho)<\mathcal{M}_{q}(\rho)\leq\mathcal{M}_{q}(\nu)={\rm Mod}_{q}(\Theta,G),

and therefore the mapping
t\mapsto{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta,G)
is strictly decreasing for all
t\in[1,\infty)
. Consequently, to verify continuity, it is sufficient to show that

\lim_{t\to p^{-}}{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta,G)={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G)=\lim_{t\to p^{+}}{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta,G)

for all
p\in[1,\infty)
. For the first limit, let
\rho
be the
p
-optimal density for
\Theta
. We then have that
\mathcal{M}_{t}(\rho)\geq{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta,G)\geq{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G)
for all
t\leq p
. Since the mapping
t\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{t}(\rho)
is continuous, we have that
\lim_{t\to p}\mathcal{M}_{t}(\rho)=\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho)={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G)
, and so it follows that

\lim_{t\to p^{-}}{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta,G)={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G).

For the second limit let
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be the sequence of
(p+1/i)
-minimal densities for
\Theta
. Since
E
is finite and
\rho_{i}(e)\in[0,1)
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
e\in E
, we may use a standard diagonal argument to pick a sub-sequence
\{\rho_{i_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
for which
\{\rho_{i_{j}}(e)\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
converges for every
e\in E
. Define the density
\nu\colon E\to[0,1]
by
\nu(e):=\lim_{j\to\infty}\rho_{i_{j}}(e)
for all
e\in E
. We then have by construction that

\lim_{t\to p^{+}}{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta,G)=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathcal{M}_{p+\frac{1}{i}}(\rho_{i})=\mathcal{M}_{p}(\nu).

Since
{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta,G)\leq{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G)\leq\mathcal{M}_{p}(\nu)
for all
t\geq p
, it then follows that

\lim_{t\to p^{+}}{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta,G)={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G).

∎

For some of our arguments, we need to show that certain admissible densities are minimal. To this end, we use the dual relationship between moduli of path families and
resistances
of flows. Let
G=(V,E,\xi)
be a graph and let
A,B\subset V
be non-empty and disjoint. The divergence of a function
\mathcal{J}\colon V\times E\to\mathbb{R}
at a vertex
v\in V
is defined as

{\rm div}(\mathcal{J})(v):=\sum_{e\in E:v\in e}\mathcal{J}(v,e).

A function
\mathcal{J}\colon V\times E\to\mathbb{R}
is called a
flow
from
A
to
B
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\mathcal{J}(e^{-},e)=-\mathcal{J}(e^{+},e)
for all
e\in E
,

(2)

{\rm div}(\mathcal{J})(v)=0
for all
v\not\in A\cup B
,

(3)

\mathcal{J}(v,e)=0
whenever
v\not\in e
.

For a flow
\mathcal{J}
and
e\in E
we write
|\mathcal{J}(e)|:=|\mathcal{J}(e^{\pm},e)|
, where the choice of sign is irrelevant by (1). A flow
\mathcal{J}
from
A
to
B
is called a
unit flow
if the net flux leaving
A
equals one, that is

\sum_{v\in A}{\rm div}(\mathcal{J})(v)=1,\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\sum_{v\in B}{\rm div}(\mathcal{J})(v)=-1.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and denote it’s dual exponent by
q:=p/(p-1)
. The
p
-energy
of a unit flow
\mathcal{J}
is

\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{J}):=\sum_{e\in E}\lvert\mathcal{J}(e)\rvert^{q},\quad\text{where }\quad\lvert\mathcal{J}(e)\rvert:=\lvert\mathcal{J}(e^{-},e)\rvert=\lvert\mathcal{J}(e^{+},e)\rvert.

The
p
-resistance between
A
and
B
is defined as the value

{\rm Res}_{p}(A,B,G):=\inf\{\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{J})\mid\mathcal{J}\text{ is a unit flow from }A\text{ to }B\}

The following proposition presents the dual relationship between modulus and resistance, see e.g.
[
35
, Theorem 5.1]
.
In the present work, however, we need a slightly more general version that also works for some disconnected graphs.
It follows fairly directly from the standard duality result by a slight modification of the graph involved.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
q=p/(p-1)
. Let
G=(V,E,\xi)
be a graph and let
A,B\subset V
be a pair of disjoint non-empty subsets such that
\Theta(A,B)\neq\varnothing
.
Then

{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta(A,B),G)^{\frac{1}{p}}\cdot{\rm Res}_{p}(A,B,G)^{\frac{1}{q}}=1.

Further, if
\mathcal{J}
is a unit flow from
A
to
B
and
\rho\in\operatorname{Adm}(\Theta(A,B))
, such that

\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho)^{\frac{1}{p}}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{\mathcal{J}})^{\frac{1}{q}}=1,

then both
\mathcal{J}
and
\rho
are optimal. For optimal
\mathcal{J}
and
\rho
we have

(2.13)

\lvert\mathcal{F}(e)\rvert={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta(A,B),G)^{-1}\rho(e)^{p-1}

Proof.

As stated, the connected case follows directly from
[
35
, Theorem 5.1]
and
[
2
, Lemma 4.13]
. So, we are left to consider the case of a disconnected
G
. Let
\tilde{G}=(\tilde{V},\tilde{E})
be the graph obtained in these four steps: first identify all vertices in
A
with a given vertex
a\in A
, then also identify all vertices in
B
with a given vertex
b\in B
, remove possible loops and finally take the connected component containing
a
and
b
in the resulting graph. It is not too hard to see that
{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta(A,B),G)={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta(\{a\},\{b\},\tilde{G})
and
{\rm Res}_{p}(\Theta(\{a\},\{b\},\tilde{G}))={\rm Res}_{p}(A,B,G)
.
∎

3.
Construction of Laakso-type fractal spaces

In this section, we present the construction of Laakso-type fractal spaces as limit spaces of iterated graph systems. Further discussion and detailed examples may be found in
[
2
,
1
]
. In contrast to previous work, here we have adopted a notation that is explicitly suited to working with non-simple graphs.

3.1.
Iterated graph systems

Definition 3.1
.

An iterated graph system (IGS) consists of the following data:

(1)

A finite connected graph
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1},\xi_{1})
called the
generator
.

(2)

A finite non-empty set
I
called the
gluing set
.

(3)

A collection of injective functions
\{\phi_{v,e}\colon I\to V_{1}\}_{v\in e\in E_{1}}
called
gluing maps
, whose images,
I_{v,e}:=\phi_{v,e}(I)
, satisfy the following whenever
v\in e\in E_{1}
:

(a)

I_{e^{-},e}\cap I_{e^{+},e}=\varnothing
.

(b)

There are no edges
f\in E_{1}
with
\xi(f)\subset I_{v,e}
.

We refer to the pair
(I,\{\phi_{v,e}\colon I\to V_{1}\}_{v\in e\in E_{1}})
as the
gluing rule
.

An IGS determines an infinite sequence of
replacement graphs

(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and for each
G_{n}
, a corresponding collection of gluing maps
\{\phi_{v,e}\colon I\to V_{1}\}_{v\in e\in E_{n}}
, recursively, as follows: Consider an IGS with a generator
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1},\xi_{1})
and gluing rule
(I,\{\phi_{v,e}\}_{v\in e\in E_{1}})
. The first graph in the sequence is the generator
G_{1}
itself. Suppose that
G_{n}=(V_{n},E_{n},\xi_{n})
and
\{\phi_{v,e}\}_{v\in e\in E_{n}}
have been constructed for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, we define the graph
G_{n+1}=(V_{n+1},E_{n+1},\xi_{n+1})
and the collection of gluing maps
\{\phi_{v,e}\}_{v\in e\in E_{n+1}}
as follows:

(1)

Let
V_{n+1}:=(V_{1}\times E_{n})/\sim
with the identification
(\phi_{v,e}(i),e)\sim(\phi_{v,f}(i),f)
for each
i\in I
, whenever
e
and
f
share an endpoint
v
.

(2)

Let
E_{n+1}:=E_{1}\times E_{n}
, and define
\xi_{n+1}(e,f):=\{[(e^{-},f)],[(e^{+},f)]\}
for every
(e,f)\in E_{n+1}
.

(3)

Define the collection of gluing maps
\{\phi_{v,e}\colon I\to V_{1}\}_{v\in e\in E_{n+1}}
, by setting
\phi_{[(v,f)],(e,f)}:=\phi_{v,e}
for all
(e,f)\in E_{n+1}
and
v\in e
.

To clean up the notation, we drop the parentheses and simply write
[v,e]
to denote any vertex
[(v,e)]\in V_{n}
. For any edge
e\in E_{n}
, we denote it’s level by
\lvert e\rvert:=n
.

The replacement graphs of an IGS admit a natural projective structure defined as follows: For every
n\geq 1
, we define the projection
\pi_{n+1}\colon V_{n+1}\cup E_{n+1}\to V_{n}\cup E_{n}
by setting

\pi_{n+1}([v,e]):=\begin{cases}e&\text{if}\;v\not\in I_{e^{-},e}\cup I_{e^{+},e}\\
e^{-}&\text{if}\;v\in I_{e^{-},e}\\
e^{+}&\text{if}\;v\in I_{e^{+},e}\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{n+1}(e,f):=f

for any vertex
[v,e]\in V_{n+1}
and any edge
(e,f)\in E_{n+1}
, respectively. Furthermore, we define
\pi_{1}:={\rm id}|_{V_{1}\cup E_{1}}
, and for all
n\geq m\geq 1
, we define

\pi_{n,m}:=\begin{cases}{\rm id}|_{V_{n}\cup E_{n}}&\text{if }n=m,\\
\pi_{m+1}\circ\pi_{m+2}\circ\ldots\circ\pi_{n}&\text{if }n>m.\end{cases}

Definition 3.2
.

An IGS is called
simple
if there are exactly two gluing maps,
\phi_{-},\phi_{+}\colon I\to V_{1}
, so that
\{\phi_{e^{-},e},\phi_{e^{+},e}\}=\{\phi_{-},\phi_{+}\}
for every edge
e\in E_{1}
. For a simple IGS, we fix the endpoint labels
e^{-}
and
e^{+}
for each edge
e\in E_{n}
so that

\phi_{e^{-},e}=\phi_{-}\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{e^{+},e}=\phi_{+}.

We denote the gluing sets, higher-order gluing sets and the families of paths joining the
n
-th order gluing sets of a simple IGS by

I_{\pm}:=\phi_{\pm}(I),\quad I_{\pm}^{\smash{(n)}}:=\pi_{n,1}^{-1}(I_{\pm})\quad\text{and}\quad\Theta^{\smash{(n)}}:=\Theta(I_{-}^{\smash{(n)}},I_{+}^{\smash{(n)}}),

respectively, and for each pair
(v,e)
where
e\in E_{n}
and
v\in\{e^{-},e^{+}\}
, we define

I_{v,e}^{(n)}:=\begin{cases}I_{+}^{(n)}&\text{if }v=e^{+}\\
I_{-}^{(n)}&\text{if }v=e^{-}\end{cases}

For convenience, we also introduce the constant

L_{*}:={\rm dist}(I_{-},I_{+},d_{G_{1}}).

Usually, we will need to impose some minimal further assumptions on an IGS. We collect these in the following list. We say that a simple IGS is:

(1)

doubling
, if for every vertex
v\in I_{-}\cup I_{+}
we have
\deg(v)=1
;

(2)

non-degenerate
, if there are no edges
e\in E_{1}
for which
e^{-}\in I_{\pm}
and
e^{+}\in I_{\mp}
;

(3)

symmetric
, if there exists a graph isomorphism
\eta:G_{1}\to G_{1}
for which
\eta\circ\phi_{\pm}=\phi_{\mp}
;

We review some properties of our construction.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
2
, Lemma 3.21]
)
.

If a simple IGS is doubling then

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\max_{v\in V_{n}}\deg(v)=\max_{v\in V_{1}}\deg(v).

The graphs
G_{n}
are in a sense self-similar. This can be explicated using the following mappings. For every
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
e\in E_{n}
, we define the similarity map
\sigma_{e,m}\colon V_{m}\cup E_{m}\to V_{n+m}\cup E_{n+m}
inductively, by first setting

\sigma_{e,1}(v):=[v,e]\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{e,1}(f):=(f,e)

for all
v\in V_{1}
and
f\in E_{1}
, respectively, and then setting

\sigma_{e,{m+1}}([v,f]):=[v,\sigma_{e,m}(f)]\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{e,m+1}((g,f)):=(g,\sigma_{e,m}(f))

for all
[v,f]\in V_{m+1}
and
(g,f)\in E_{m+1}
, respectively. Furthermore, we denote

e\cdot V_{m}:=\begin{cases}\{e^{-},e^{+}\}&\text{if }m=0\\
\sigma_{e,m}(V_{m})&\text{if }m\geq 1\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad e\cdot E_{m}:=\begin{cases}e&\text{if }m=0\\
\sigma_{e,m}(E_{m})&\text{if }m\geq 1\end{cases}

and define
e\cdot G_{m}
as the subgraph of
G_{n+m}
with vertices
e\cdot V_{m}
and edges
e\cdot E_{m}
.

The following Proposition is equivalent to
[
2

(

SM2

)

Proposition 3.4
.

The similarity mapping
\sigma_{e,m}
has the following properties for every

and each
e\in E_{n}
:

(
SM1
)

The mapping is injective and the collections

V_{n+m}
and

\phi_{v_{\pm},f}=\phi_{\sigma_{e,m}(v_{\pm}),\sigma_{e,m}(f)}

(

For distinct edges

For any two edges

The operation is associative, and gives us a convenient way of representing any edge

e_{i}\in E_{1}

e\cdot\theta:=[\sigma_{e,1}(v_{0}),\sigma_{e,1}(f_{1}),\ldots,\sigma_{e,1}(f_{k}),\sigma_{e,1}(v_{k})].

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\theta=[v_{0},e_{1},v_{1},\dots,e_{k},v_{k}]

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\theta=[v_{0},e_{1},\ldots,e_{k},v_{k}]\in\Theta^{(n)}

If a simple IGS is non-degenerate and doubling, then for every

3.2.
Limit space

The
symbol space
of an IGS is the collection of projective sequences

\Sigma:=\big\{(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\mid e_{i}\in E_{i}\text{ and }\pi_{i+1}(e_{i+1})=e_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{N}\big\}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
e\in E_{n}
, we denote
\Sigma_{e}:=\{(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Sigma\mid e_{n}=e\}
. The symbol space admits a natural
word metric
, given as

\delta_{\Sigma}((e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty},(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}):=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }e_{i}=f_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{N},\\
2^{-\max\left\{0,\,k\,\big|\,(e_{i})_{i=1}^{k}=(f_{i})_{i=1}^{k}\right\}}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

By construction,
(\Sigma,\delta_{\Sigma})
is a compact metric space. The
limit space
of an IGS is defined as the quotient space
X:=\Sigma/\sim
, where we identify

(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\sim(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\iff e_{i}\cap f_{i}\neq\varnothing\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{N}.

The canonical projection
\chi\colon\Sigma\to X
is given by
\chi((e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}):=[(e)_{i=1}^{\infty}]
, and we denote
X_{e}:=\chi(\Sigma_{e})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
e\in E_{n}
. For a simple IGS, we also define the sets
X_{-},X_{+}\subset X
by setting

X_{\pm}=\{[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\mid e_{n}\cap I_{\pm}^{(n)}\neq\varnothing\;\;\text{for all}\;\;n\in\mathbb{N}\}

Remark 3.8
.

By construction, every edge
e\in E_{n}
may be uniquely represented as a product
e_{1}\cdot e_{2}\cdot\ldots\cdot e_{n}=e
, where
e_{i}\in E_{1}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Likewise, every such product corresponds to a unique edge
e_{1}\cdot e_{2}\cdot\ldots\cdot e_{n}\in E_{n}
. Consequently, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we may identify the edge set
E_{n}
with the set of sequences

E_{1}^{n}:=\{(e_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\mid e_{i}\in E_{1}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n\}

via the mapping
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\mapsto e_{1}\cdot e_{2}\cdot\ldots\cdot e_{n}
. Similarly, we may identify the symbol space
\Sigma
with the set of sequences

E_{1}^{\mathbb{N}}=\{(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\mid e_{i}\in E_{1}\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{N}\}

via the mapping
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\mapsto(e_{1}\cdot e_{2}\cdot\ldots\cdot e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
. It is often very convenient to think of the edges in terms of the corresponding product decompositions, which motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.9
.

We define mappings
T:E_{1}^{n}\to E_{n}
and
T:E_{1}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\Sigma
by

T((e_{i})_{i=1}^{n}):=\begin{cases}\;e_{1}\cdot e_{2}\cdot\ldots\cdot e_{n}&\text{if }n\in\mathbb{N},\\
(e_{1}\cdot e_{2}\cdot\ldots\cdot e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}&\text{if }n=\infty.\end{cases}

Furthermore, if
x=T((e_{i})_{i=1}^{n})
, we denote
T^{-1}(x)_{i}=e_{i}\in E_{1}
.

As is made clear by the discussion in Remark
3.8
, the inverse map
T^{-1}
is well defined. Moreover, we may conveniently refer to a specific term
T^{-1}(x)_{i}
in the product decomposition of an element
x\in\Sigma\cup(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n})
and we have

x=\begin{cases}\,\ T^{-1}(x)_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot T^{-1}(x)_{n}&\text{if }x\in E_{n}\text{ for some }n\in\mathbb{N},\\
\big(T^{-1}(x)_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot T^{-1}(x)_{i}\big)_{i=1}^{\infty}&\text{if }x\in\Sigma.\end{cases}

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\Sigma
be the symbol space of a doubling and symmetric IGS. Suppose that
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\sim(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Sigma
are such that
e_{n}\cap f_{n}\neq\varnothing
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there exists
m\leq n
, such that

T^{-1}((e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i}=\begin{cases}T^{-1}((f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i}&\text{for }1\leq i<m\\
T^{-1}((f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i}\;\text{ or }\;\eta\circ T^{-1}((f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i}&\text{for }m<i\leq n\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
m=\min\{n,k\mid e_{k}\neq f_{k}\}
and assume that
n>1
, as otherwise there is nothing to prove. As
e_{i}=f_{i}
for all
1\leq i<m
, we must have that

T^{-1}((e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i}=T^{-1}(e_{i})_{i}=T^{-1}(f_{i})_{i}=T^{-1}((f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i}

for all
1\leq i<m
. Since
e_{n}\cap f_{n}\neq\varnothing
, we have by

(

SM2

)

that for all
m\leq i<n
there exists a vertex
v_{i}\in e_{i}\cap f_{i}
and an element
a_{i}\in I
, so that

e_{i+1}\cap f_{i+1}=[\phi_{v_{i},e_{i}}(a_{i}),e_{i}]=[\phi_{v_{i},f_{i}}(a_{i}),f_{i}].

Since the IGS is symmetric, we have that
\phi_{v_{i},e_{i}}(a_{i})\in\{\phi_{v_{i},f_{i}}(a_{i}),\>\eta\circ\phi_{v_{i},f_{i}}(a_{i})\}
. By definition, the vertices
\phi_{v_{i},e_{i}}(a_{i})
,
\phi_{v_{i},f_{i}}(a_{i})
and
\eta\circ\phi_{v_{i},f_{i}}(a_{i})
are endpoints of the edges
\sigma_{e_{i},1}^{-1}(e_{i+1})
,
\sigma_{f_{i},1}^{-1}(f_{i+1})
and
\eta\circ\sigma_{f_{i},1}^{-1}(f_{i+1})
, respectively. Moreover, we have that
\sigma_{e_{i},1}^{-1}(e_{i+1})=T^{-1}((e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i+1}
and
\sigma_{f_{i},1}^{-1}(f_{i+1})=T^{-1}((f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i+1}
. Therefore, the edge
T^{-1}((e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i+1}
has a common endpoint
\phi_{v_{i},e_{i}}(a_{i})
with either
T^{-1}((f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i+1}
or
\eta\circ T^{-1}((f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i+1}
. However, since the IGS is doubling, we have that
\deg(\phi_{v_{i},e_{i}}(a_{i}))=1
, and so there exists a unique edge
\mathfrak{e}(\phi_{v_{i},e_{i}}(a_{i}))\in E_{1}
with
\phi_{v_{i},e_{i}}(a_{i})
as one of it’s endpoints. Therefore, for all
m<i\leq n
, we have that

T^{-1}((e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i}=\mathfrak{e}(\phi_{v_{i-1},e_{i-1}}(a_{i-1}))\in\{T^{-1}((f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i},\;\eta\circ T^{-1}((f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty})_{i}\}

∎

Remark 3.11
.

Let
X_{e,\pm}=\{[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\in X:e_{n}=e,e_{k}\in e\cdot I^{k-n}_{\pm},k>n\}
. The proof of Lemma
3.10
shows that for two adjacent edges
e,f\in E_{n}
we have
X_{e}\cap X_{f}\subset X_{f,\pm}
.

Definition 3.12
.

A symmetric Laakso-type fractal space is the limit space
X
of a simple IGS that is doubling, non-degenerate and symmetric.

4.
Construction of metrics

In this section, we construct a family of metrics on the limit space. Most importantly, this construction yields a subfamily of quasisymmetrically equivalent metrics with visual-metric-like properties, and as we will show in Section
5
, this subfamily constitutes a dimensionally exhaustive subset of the related conformal gauge.

In contrast to an earlier construction
[
2
]
, the present one allows us to remove the uniform scaling assumption, and to handle metrics that are not necessarily quasiconvex. Similar constructions that involve the chaining of balls, sets, or pairs of points have appeared earlier in many places, such as
[
5
,
3
,
11
,
37
]
.

For the rest of this section, we fix a doubling, non-degenerate IGS with generator
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1},\xi_{1})
. Later on, we also require the IGS to be symmetric, in which case we denote the associated symmetry map by
\eta
.

4.1.
Density cascade

A density
\rho\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
induces a sequence
(\rho_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of densities as a
multiplicative cascade
, where
\rho_{n}\colon E_{n}\to(0,1)
is defined recursively for each
e\in E_{n}
as

(4.1)

\rho_{n}(e):=\begin{cases}\rho(e)&\text{if }\;n=1,\\
\rho_{n-1}(\pi_{n}(e))\cdot\rho_{1}(\sigma_{\pi_{n}(e),1}^{-1}(e))&\text{if }\;n>1.\end{cases}

Equivalently, each density in the sequence can be given as a product

(4.2)

\rho_{n}(e)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho(T^{-1}(e)_{i}).

From now on, given any density
\rho\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
, we refer by
\rho_{n}
to the density
\rho_{n}\colon E_{n}\to(0,1)
given equivalently by equations (
4.1
) and (
4.2
). For convenience, we also introduce the constants:

M_{\rho}:=\max_{e\in E_{1}}\rho_{1}(e)\;\;\text{and}\;\;m_{\rho}:=\min_{e\in E_{1}}\rho_{1}(e).

Note, that whenever
e\in E_{n}
and
f\in e\cdot E_{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows directly from the construction that

(4.3)

m_{\rho}^{k}\rho_{n}(e)\leq\rho_{n+k}(f)\leq M_{\rho}^{k}\rho_{n}(e).

Recall that for
e\in E_{n}
, we write
\lvert e\rvert:=n
. Whenever
n>m
and
\Omega\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}E_{i}
, we define the sub-graph covered by
\Omega
in
G_{m}
as

p_{n}(\Omega):=\bigcup_{e\in\Omega}e\cdot G_{n-\lvert e\rvert}.

For every pair of vertices
v,w\in V_{n}
we define the collection

\Lambda_{v,w}^{n}:=\left\{\Omega\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}\ \middle|\ v\text{ and }w\text{ are connected by a path in $p_{n}(\Omega)$ }\right\}.

We then define the metric
d_{\rho,n}\colon V_{n}\times V_{n}\to[0,\infty)
as

d_{\rho,n}(v,w):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\;v=w,\\
\displaystyle\inf_{\Omega\in\Lambda_{v,w}^{n}}\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\Omega}\rho_{\lvert e\rvert}(e)&\text{if }\;v\neq w.\end{cases}

For any pair
e,f\in E_{n}
, we denote
d_{\rho,n}(e,f):=\min\{d_{\rho,n}(v,w)\mid v\in e,\>w\in f\}
.

Remark 4.4
.

To see that
d_{\rho,n}
satisfies the triangle inequality, observe for any three vertices
v,w,u\in V_{n}
, if
\Omega_{1}\in\Lambda_{v,w}^{n}
and
\Omega_{2}\in\Lambda_{w,u}^{n}
, then
\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}\in\Lambda_{v,u}^{n}
. Since

\sum_{e\in\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}}\rho_{\lvert e\rvert}(e)\leq\sum_{e\in\Omega_{1}}\rho_{\lvert e\rvert}(e)+\sum_{e\in\Omega_{2}}\rho_{\lvert e\rvert}(e),

it follows by passing to the infimum, that that
d_{\rho,n}(v,u)\leq d_{\rho_{n}}(v,w)+d_{\rho,n}(w,u).

If
m>n
, we say that
x_{m}\in V_{m}
is an ancestor of
x_{n}\in V_{n}
if
\pi_{m,n}(x_{m})=x_{n}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\rho\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
be
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible and
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
so that
n<m
.

(
DL1
)

If
x_{m},y_{m}\in V_{m}
are ancestors of
x_{n},y_{n}\in V_{n}
, and
x_{n}\neq y_{n}
, then

d_{\rho,m}(x_{m},y_{m})=d_{\rho,n}(x_{n},y_{n}).

(
DL2
)

If
e\in E_{n}
and
x_{m},y_{m}\in e\cdot V_{m-n}
, then

d_{\rho,m}(x_{m},y_{m})\leq\rho_{n}(e).

(
DL3
)

If
e,f\in E_{n}
,
v\in e
and
w\in f
, then

d_{\rho,n}(v,w)-\rho_{n}(e)-\rho_{n}(f)\leq d_{\rho,n}(e,f)\leq d_{\rho,n}(v,w).

(
DL4
)

If
e,f\in E_{m}
, then

\begin{split}&d_{\rho,n}(\pi_{m,n}(e),\pi_{m,n}(f))\leq d_{\rho,m}(e,f)\\
&\leq d_{\rho,n}(\pi_{m,n}(e),\pi_{m,n}(f))+\rho_{n}(\pi_{m,n}(e))+\rho_{n}(\pi_{m,n}(f)).\end{split}

Proof.

We begin with

(

DL1

)

. It is enough to only consider the case
m=n+1
since the general case then follows by induction. By construction, we have that
\Lambda^{n}_{x_{n},y_{n}}\subset\Lambda^{n+1}_{x_{n+1},y_{n+1}}
, and therefore it follows directly that

d_{\rho,n+1}(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})\leq d_{\rho,n}(x_{n},y_{n}).

For the reversed inequality, let
\Omega\in\Lambda^{n+1}_{x_{n+1},y_{n+1}}
. By definition, there is a simple path
\theta
in
p_{n+1}(\Omega)
joining
x_{n+1}
to
y_{n+1}
. By Proposition
3.5
, since
x_{n+1}\neq y_{n+1}
, the path
\theta
admits a lift to
G_{n}
; that is, there exists a simple path
\vartheta=[x_{n},e_{1},\ldots,e_{k},y_{n}]
in
G_{n}
and, for each
1\leq i\leq k
, a simple path
\theta_{i}\in\Theta^{(1)}
such that
\{e_{i}\cdot\theta_{i}\}_{i+1}^{k}
is a collection of edge-disjoint sub-paths of
\theta
. As
\rho
is
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible, we have for every
1\leq i\leq k
that

(4.6)

\rho_{n}(e_{i})\leq\rho_{n}(e_{i})\cdot L_{\rho_{1}}(\theta_{i})=L_{\rho_{n+1}}(e_{i}\cdot\theta_{i})=\sum_{e\in e_{i}\cdot\theta_{i}}\rho_{n+1}(e).

Define

\Omega_{u}:=\{e_{i}\in\vartheta\mid e_{i}\not\in p_{n}(\Omega\setminus E_{n+1})\},

so that
\vartheta\subset p_{n}((\Omega\setminus E_{n+1})\cup\Omega_{u})
and hence
(\Omega\setminus E_{n+1})\cup\Omega_{u}\in\Lambda^{n}_{x_{n},y_{n}}
. For each
e_{i}\in\Omega_{u}
, the corresponding sub-path
e_{i}\cdot\theta_{i}
is contained in
p_{n+1}(\Omega)
but cannot meet
p_{n+1}(\Omega\setminus E_{n+1})
as otherwise there would be some
f\in\Omega\setminus E_{n+1}
with an edge of
e_{i}\cdot\theta_{i}
in
f\cdot E_{n+1-\lvert f\rvert}
which would force
e_{i}\in f\cdot E_{n-\lvert f\rvert}\subset p_{n}(\Omega\setminus E_{n+1})
, contradicting
e_{i}\in\Omega_{u}
. Consequently, if
e_{i}\in\Omega_{u}
, then every edge of
e_{i}\cdot\theta_{i}
must belong to
p_{n+1}(\Omega\cap E_{n+1})
and therefore to
\Omega\cap E_{n+1}
.

By equation (
4.6
), we have that

\sum_{e\in\Omega_{u}}\rho_{n}(e)\leq\sum_{e\in\Omega\cap E_{n+1}}\rho_{n+1}(e),

and therefore

\sum_{e\in(\Omega\setminus E_{n+1})\cup\Omega_{u}}\rho_{\lvert e\rvert}(e)\leq\sum_{e\in\Omega\setminus E_{n+1}}\rho_{\lvert e\rvert}+\sum_{e\in\Omega\cap E_{n+1}}\rho_{\lvert e\rvert}=\sum_{e\in\Omega}\rho_{\lvert e\rvert},

and finally, since
(\Omega\setminus E_{n+1})\cup\Omega_{u}\in\Lambda^{n}_{x_{n},y_{n}}
, we obtain

d_{\rho,n}(x,y)\leq\sum_{e\in\Omega\setminus E_{n+1}\cup\Omega_{u}}\rho_{\lvert e\rvert}(e)\leq\sum_{e\in\Omega}\rho_{\lvert e\rvert}.

Since
\Omega\in\Lambda^{n+1}_{x_{n+1},y_{n+1}}
was arbitrary, it follows that

d_{\rho,n}(x_{n},y_{n})\leq d_{\rho,n+1}(x_{n+1},y_{n+1}).

We move on to

(

DL2

)

. For any
e\in E_{n}
, the sub-graph
p_{m}(\{e\})=e\cdot G_{m-n}
is connected and contains
e\cdot V_{m-n}
. Thus, if
x_{m},y_{m}\in V_{m-n}
, then
\{e\}\in\Lambda^{m}_{x_{m},y_{m}}
and consequently

d_{\rho,m}(x_{m},y_{m})\leq\rho_{n}(e).

Next, we prove

(

DL3

)

. Directly from the definition of
d_{\rho,n}
for edges, we obtain

d_{\rho,n}(e,f)=\min_{x\in e,y\in w}d_{\rho,n}(x,y)\leq d_{\rho,n}(v,w).

On the other hand, since
\{e\}\in\Lambda^{n}_{e^{-},e^{+}}
and
\{f\}\in\Lambda^{n}_{f^{-},f^{+}}
it follows from the triangle inequality that

\begin{split}d_{\rho,n}(v,w)&\leq d_{\rho,n}(e,f)+d_{\rho,n}(e^{-},e^{+})+d_{\rho,n}(f^{-},f^{+})\\
&\leq d_{\rho,n}(e,f)+\rho_{n}(e)+\rho_{n}(f).\end{split}

Thus, we obtain the remaining inequality

d_{\rho,n}(v,w)-\rho_{n}(e)-\rho_{n}(f)\leq d_{\rho,n}(e,f).

Finally, we prove

(

DL4

)

. We begin with the first inequality. First, note that if
\pi_{m,n}(e)\cap\pi_{m,n}(f)\neq\varnothing
, then it follows immediately that

d_{\rho,n}(\pi_{m,n}(e),\pi_{m,n}(f))=0\leq d_{\rho,m}(e,f).

Therefore, suppose that
\pi_{m,n}(e)\cap\pi_{m,n}(f)=\varnothing
and let
x,y\in V_{m}
be such that
d_{\rho,m}(e,f)=d_{\rho,m}(x,y)
. Let
\Omega\in\Lambda_{x,y}^{m}
. By definition, there exists a path
\theta
joining
x
and
y
in
p_{m}(\Omega)
, and by Proposition
3.5
there exists ancestors
x_{m},y_{m}
of some vertices
x_{n}\in\pi_{m,n}(e)
and
y_{n}\in\pi_{m,n}(f)
, respectively, so that
\theta
has a sub-path from
x
to
x_{m}
and another sub-path from
y
to
y_{m}
. Therefore we have that
\Omega\in\Lambda_{x_{m},y_{m}}^{m}
, and since

(

DL1

)

gives us that
d_{\rho,m}(x_{m},y_{m})=d_{\rho,n}(x_{n},y_{n})
, it follows that

(4.7)

d_{\rho,n}(\pi_{m,n}(e),\pi_{m,n}(f))\leq d_{\rho,n}(x_{n},y_{n})=d_{\rho,m}(x_{m},y_{m})\leq\sum_{g\in\Omega}\rho_{\lvert g\rvert}(g).

Since the collection
\Omega\in\Lambda_{x,y}^{m}
was arbitrary, we obtain the first inequality

d_{\rho,n}(\pi_{m,n}(e),\pi_{m,n}(f))\leq d_{\rho,m}(e,f).

For the second inequality, let
x,y\in V_{m}
be such that
d_{\rho,m}(e,f)=d_{\rho,m}(x,y)
and let
v,w\in V_{n}
be such that
d_{\rho,n}(v,w)=d_{\rho,n}(\pi_{m,n}(e),\pi_{m,n}(f))
. Let
\Omega\in\Lambda_{v,w}^{n}
and define

\Omega_{0}:=\Omega\cup\{\pi_{m,n}(e),\pi_{m,n}(f)\}.

Since
e\in\pi_{m,n}(e)\cdot E_{m-n}
and
f\in\pi_{m,n}(f)\cdot E_{m-n}
, we have that
\Omega_{0}\in\Lambda_{x,y}^{m}
and therefore

(4.8)

d_{\rho,m}(e,f)\leq d_{\rho,m}(x,y)\leq\sum_{g\in\Omega_{0}}\rho_{\lvert g\rvert}(g)=\rho_{n}(\pi_{m,n}(e))+\rho_{n}(\pi_{m,n}(f))+\sum_{g\in\Omega}\rho_{\lvert g\rvert}(g).

Since
\Omega\in\Lambda_{v,w}^{n}
was arbitrary, we obtain the second inequality

d_{\rho,m}(e,f)\leq d_{\rho,n}(\pi_{m,n}(e),\pi_{m,n}(f))+\rho_{n}(\pi_{m,n}(e))+\rho_{n}(\pi_{m,n}(f)).

∎

4.2.
Limit metrics

Let
\rho\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
be a
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density. We define the metric
d_{\rho}
on the limit space
X
of the IGS by

d_{\rho}([(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]):=\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},f_{i}).

The metric is well defined since the limit does not depend on the chosen representatives for
[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]
and
[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]
, as will be shown in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

The function
d_{\rho}
is a well-defined metric on
X
.

Proof.

Given
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty},(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty},(h_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Sigma
so that
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\sim(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
, we have by definition that
e_{i}\cap f_{i}\neq\varnothing
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\{e_{i}\}\in\Lambda_{e_{i}^{-},e_{i}^{+}}^{i}
and
\{f_{i}\}\in\Lambda_{f_{i}^{-},f_{i}^{+}}^{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the triangle inequality of the metrics
d_{\rho,i}
gives us that

\lvert d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},h_{i})-d_{\rho,i}(f_{i},h_{i})\rvert\leq d_{\rho,i}(e_{i}^{-},e_{i}^{+})+d_{\rho,i}(f_{i}^{-},f_{i}^{+})\leq\rho_{i}(e_{i})+\rho_{i}(f_{i}),

and since
\rho_{i}(e_{i}),\rho_{i}(f_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
, we have that

\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},h_{i})=\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(f_{i},h_{i}).

Therefore, if
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty},(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty},(g_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty},(h_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Sigma
are such that
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\sim(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
(h_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\sim(g_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
, then

\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},h_{i})=\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(f_{i},h_{i})=\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(f_{i},g_{i})=\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(f_{i},g_{i}).

Consequently,
d_{\rho}
is well defined.

Next, we will show that
d_{\rho}
is indeed a metric. Let
[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\in X
. If
[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]=[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]
, then by definition
e_{i}\cap f_{i}\neq\varnothing
and hence
d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},f_{i})=0
, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, from which it follows that

d_{\rho}([(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}])=\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},f_{i})=0.

If
[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\neq[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]
, then there exists some
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
e_{i}\cap f_{i}=\varnothing
, and hence
d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},f_{i})>0
, for all
i\geq j
. Particularly, since the sequence
d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},f_{i})
is increasing by

(

DL4

)

, we have that
d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},f_{i})>d_{\rho,i}(e_{j},f_{j})>0
for all
i\geq j
, and so it follows that

d_{\rho}([(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}])=\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},f_{i})\geq d_{\rho,i}(e_{j},f_{j})>0.

Consequently, we have
d_{\rho}([(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}])=0
if and only if
[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]=[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]
. Symmetry of
d_{\rho}
follows directly from the symmetry of the metrics
d_{\rho,i}
. For the triangle inequality,

(

DL3

)

gives us that

\lvert d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},f_{i})-d_{\rho,i}(e_{i}^{-},f_{i}^{-})\rvert\leq d_{\rho,i}(e_{i}^{-},e_{i}^{+})+d_{\rho,i}(f_{i}^{-},f_{i}^{+})\leq\rho_{i}(e_{i})+\rho_{i}(f_{i}),

and since
\rho_{i}(e_{i}),\rho_{i}(f_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
, it follows that

(4.10)

d_{\rho}([(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}])=\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(e_{i},f_{i})=\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(e_{i}^{-},f_{i}^{-}).

Given a third point
[(h_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\in X
we have that
d_{\rho,i}(e_{i}^{-},f_{i}^{-})\leq d_{\rho,i}(e_{i}^{-},h_{i}^{-})+d_{\rho,i}(h_{i}^{-},f_{i}^{-})
for all

, from which it follows that

\begin{split}d_{\rho}([(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}])&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Equation: Edge to Vertex}}}{{=}}\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(e_{i}^{-},f_{i}^{-})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\eqref{Equation: Edge to Vertex}}}}{{\leq}}\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(e_{i}^{-},h_{i}^{-})+\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{\rho,i}(h_{i}^{-},f_{i}^{-})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Equation: Edge to Vertex}}}{{=}}d_{\rho}([(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],[(h_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}])+d_{\rho}([(h_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]),\end{split}

and so
d_{\rho}
satisfies the triangle inequality.
∎

Definition 4.11

For every
\Theta^{(1)}

Remark 4.20
.

While the space
(X,d_{\rho})
need not be quasiconvex in general, we can recover quasiconvexity with relatively mild additional assumptions. One can show that if the IGS is symmetric and
\rho\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
is a symmetric
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible function, then the space
(X,d_{\rho})
is quasiconvex if and only if there exists a path
\theta\in\Theta^{(1)}
with
L_{\rho}(\theta)=1
. For example, the intrinsic metric
d_{L_{*}}
is quasiconvex whenever the IGS is symmetric.

In the quasiconvex case, the metric
d_{\rho}
has also another simpler construction: up to a bi-Lipschitz deformation it can be realized as the Gromov-Hausdorff limit of re-scaled path metrics on
G_{n}
, where edges are weighted by
\rho_{n}
. This gives in particular a slightly simpler description of the intrinsic metric
d_{L_{*}}
in the symmetric case.

We now briefly sketch the proof of quasiconvexity. This is not needed later in the paper, and is only included for the sake of completeness.

Since
L_{\rho}(\theta)=1
, we have for any
e\in E_{n}
that
L_{\rho_{n+1}}(e\cdot\theta)=\rho_{n}(e)\cdot L_{\rho}(\theta)=\rho_{n}(e)
. Thus, given any path
\theta_{n}
from
v_{a}
to
v_{b}
in
G_{n}
, we may construct a path
\theta_{n+1}
in
G_{n+1}
from
w_{a}\in\pi^{-1}_{n+1}(v_{a})
to
w_{b}\in\pi^{-1}_{n+1}(v_{b})
as a concatenation, using symmetry in the same way as in the proof of Lemma
5.12
, so that
L_{\rho_{n+1}}(\theta_{n+1})=L_{\rho_{n}}(\theta_{n})
. Moreover, one can show that the path
\theta_{n}
can be promoted to a
1
-Lipschitz path
\gamma
on
X
with endpoints in
X_{v_{a}}
and
X_{v_{b}}
, so that
\ell(\gamma)\leq L_{\rho_{n}}(\theta_{n})
.

Now, for any
x=[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]
,
y=[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]
, one first fixes a path
\theta_{n}
joining an endpoint of
e_{n}
to an endpoint of
f_{n}
, where
n=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid e_{n}\cap f_{n}=\varnothing\}
. One then promotes it to an equal-length path in
G_{n+1}
and adds tail sections to obtain a path
\theta_{n+1}
joining
e_{n+1}
to
f_{n+1}
. Continuing this process, at stage
i>n
, the contribution of the tail sections to
L_{\rho_{i}}(\theta_{i})
is bounded above by
K\cdot M_{\rho}^{i-n}
for some
K\geq 1
, and hence one obtains a sequence of
1
-lipschitz paths
(\gamma_{i})_{i=n}^{\infty}
from
X_{e_{i}}
to
X_{f_{i}}
, so that
\ell(\gamma_{i})\leq L_{\rho_{n}}(\theta_{n})+K\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M_{\rho}^{j-n}\leq Cd(x,y)
for all
n\leq i<\infty
, for some constant
C\geq 1
. As
x=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}X_{e_{i}}
and
y=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}X_{f_{i}}
, the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem then quarantees the existance of a path
\gamma
from
x
to
y
on
X
with
\ell(\gamma)\leq Cd(x,y)
.

4.3.
Gromov-Hausdorff convergence

So far, the space
X
was called a limit space for mostly intuitive reasons. In this subsection, which is not used in other parts of the paper, we prove that the limit space can also be obtained as a Gromov–Hausorff limit of replacement graphs; see e.g.
[
9
]
for background on this notion.

A sequence
(X_{n},d_{X_{n}})

Gromov-Hausdorff converges
to
(X,d)
if for some sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0

•

there exist mappings
\iota_{n}:X_{n}\to X
s.t.

d_{X_{n}}(x,y)-\varepsilon_{n}\leq d(\iota_{n}(x),\iota_{n}(y))\leq d_{X_{n}}(x,y)+\varepsilon_{n};

for all
x,y\in X_{n}
; and

•

for every
x\in X
there exists an
x_{n}\in X_{n}
s.t.
d(\iota_{n}(x_{n}),x)\leq\varepsilon_{n}
.

.

For any
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density

For all

Then, for all

DL3

)

and

)

for all

4.4.
Quasi-visual approximation

To verify the Ahlfors-regularity, we establish a good quasi-visual approximation for the limit space.

Definition 4.22

(
[
5

)

be a bounded metric space. A sequence

{\rm dist}(X,Y)\geq C^{-1}{\rm diam}(X)
for all
X,Y\in\mathbf{X}^{n}
with
X\cap Y=\varnothing
.

C^{-1}{\rm diam}(Y)\leq{\rm diam}(X)\leq C{\rm diam}(Y)
for all

and

.

For limit spaces of IGS:s we use the coverings

\mathbf{X}^{0}:=\{X\}

\mathbf{X}^{n}:=\{X_{e}\mid e\in E_{n}\}

)

(

\frac{m_{\rho}^{k+4}}{M_{\rho}^{2}}{\rm diam}(X_{e},d_{\rho})\leq{\rm diam}(X_{f},d_{\rho})\leq\frac{M_{\rho}^{k+2}}{m_{\rho}^{4}}{\rm diam}(X_{e},d_{\rho}).

Since
M_{\rho}<1
, there exists a value
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
M_{\rho}^{k+2}<m_{\rho}^{4}
. Therefore, properties (iii) and (iv) follow from equation (
4.25
) with choices
k=1
and
k=k_{0}
, respectively.
∎

4.5.
Ahlfors regularity and quasisymmetry

We now have all the tools to prove Theorem
1.5
. This proof is given at the end of the section after some preparatory work.

Let
\rho:E_{1}\to(0,1)
be a symmetric
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density. By the admissibility of
\rho
, we have

\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho)=\sum_{e\in E_{1}}\rho(e)\geq 1.

Since
M_{\rho}=\max_{e\in E_{1}}\rho(e)<1
, there is a unique
Q\geq 1
such that

\mathcal{M}_{Q}(\rho)=\sum_{e\in E_{1}}\rho(e)^{Q}=1.

Indeed, this follows from the fact that the map
Q\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{Q}(\rho)
, that is continuous and strictly decreasing, vanishes at infinity and
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho)\geq 1
.

Define a probability measure
\tau_{1}(\{e\})=\rho(e)^{Q}
on
E_{1}
and let
\tau
be the product measure on
E_{1}^{\mathbb{N}}
by the pushforward of
T
as
\mathfrak{m}_{\rho}=T_{*}(\tau_{\infty})
on
\Sigma
, where
T
was as defined in
3.9
. Note, that the pushforward of a measure
\nu
on
X
under a measurable map
f:X\to Y
is given by
f_{*}(\nu)(A)=\nu(f^{-1}(A))
.

This measure satisfies
\mathfrak{m}_{\rho}(\Sigma_{e})=\rho_{\lvert e\rvert}(e)^{Q}
for
e\in E_{n}
. Next, let
\mu_{\rho}=\chi_{*}(\mathfrak{m}_{\rho})
. Observe that
\chi^{-1}(X_{e})=\Sigma_{e}\bigcup_{f\cap e\neq\emptyset}\chi^{-1}(X_{f}\cap X_{e})\cap\Sigma_{f}
. A simple calculation using Remark
3.11
and
\mathfrak{m}_{\rho}(\chi^{-1}(X_{e,\pm}))=0
shows then that
\mu_{\rho}(X_{e})=\rho_{n}(e)^{Q}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
e\in E_{n}
; see
[
1
, Proposition 3.42]
for more details.

Lemma 4.26
.

Let
\rho\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
be a symmetric
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density. Then, the metric measure space
(X,d_{\rho},\mu_{\rho})
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular, where
Q\geq 1
is the unique exponent satisfying

\sum_{e\in E_{1}}\rho(e)^{Q}=1.

Proof.

Fix
x=[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\in X
and let
0<r\leq 2{\rm diam}(X,d_{\rho})
. We will start by showing that
\mu_{\rho}(B(x,r))\gtrsim r^{Q}
. To that end, let
n=\min\{i\in\mathbb{N}\mid r\geq\rho_{i}(e_{i})\}
. We then have that
X_{e_{n}}\subseteq B(x,r)
, and

(4.27)

\begin{cases}\rho_{n}(e_{n})\leq r\leq 2{\rm diam}(X,d_{\rho})\ &\text{if }n=1,\\
\rho_{n}(e_{n})\leq r<\rho_{n-1}(e_{n-1})&\text{if }n>1.\end{cases}

Since
\rho_{i}(e_{i})\geq m_{\rho}\rho_{i-1}(e_{i-1})
for all
i>1
, and since
\rho_{1}(e_{1})\geq m_{\rho}
, it follows from equation (
4.27
) that
\rho_{n}(e_{n})\geq r\cdot m_{\rho}/(2{\rm diam}(X,d_{\rho}))
, and so we approximate:

\mu_{\rho}(B(x,r))\geq\mu_{\rho}(X_{e_{n}})=\rho_{n}(e_{n})^{Q}\geq\frac{m_{\rho}^{Q}r^{Q}}{2^{Q}{\rm diam}(X,d_{\rho})^{Q}}.

Next, we show that
\mu_{\rho}(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{Q}
. Let
k=\min\{i\in\mathbb{N}\mid r\geq\rho_{i}(e_{i})\cdot m_{\rho}^{2}/M_{\rho}\}
. If
k=1
, then we have that
r\geq\rho_{1}(e_{1})\cdot m_{\rho}^{2}/M_{\rho}\geq m_{\rho}^{3}/M_{\rho}
, and hence
r\cdot M_{\rho}/m_{\rho}^{3}\geq 1
, from which it follows that

\mu_{\rho}(B(x,r))\leq\mu_{\rho}(X)=1\leq\bigg(\frac{M_{\rho}}{m_{\rho}^{3}}\bigg)^{Q}r^{Q}.

On the other hand, if
k>1
, then we have that

\frac{m_{\rho}^{2}}{M_{\rho}}\rho_{k}(e_{k})\leq r<\frac{m_{\rho}^{2}}{M_{\rho}}\rho_{k-1}(e_{k-1})\leq\frac{m_{\rho}}{M_{\rho}}\rho_{k}(e_{k}),

and so it follows from

(

DL7

)

and

(

DL8

)

that
X_{f}\cap B(x,r)\neq\varnothing
for some
f\in E_{k}
if and only if
f\cap e_{k}\neq\varnothing
. Therefore,
B(x,r)\subseteq\bigcup_{f\in E_{k}:f\cap e_{k}\neq\varnothing}X_{f}
, where we have by

(

DL7

)

that

\max_{f\in E_{k}:f\cap e_{k}\neq\varnothing}\rho_{k}(f)\leq\frac{M_{\rho}}{m_{\rho}}\rho_{k}(e_{k})\leq\frac{M_{\rho}^{2}r}{m_{\rho}^{3}}\leq\frac{M_{\rho}r}{m_{\rho}^{3}}.

Now, since the IGS is doubling, there exists due to Lemma
3.3
a constant
C_{\deg}\in\mathbb{N}
, so that

C_{\deg}=\max_{v\in V_{1}}\deg(v)=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\max_{v\in V_{n}}\deg(v).

Therefore, we have that
\lvert\{f\in E_{k}\mid f\cap e_{k}\neq\varnothing\}\rvert\leq 2C_{\deg}+1
, and thus may approximate:

\begin{split}\mu_{\rho}(B(x,r))&\leq\sum_{f\in\Omega}\mu_{\rho}(X_{f})\leq(2C_{\rm deg}+1)\bigg(\frac{M_{\rho}}{m_{\rho}}\rho_{k}(e_{k})\bigg)^{Q}\\
&\leq(2C_{\rm deg}+1)\bigg(\frac{M_{\rho}^{2}r}{m_{\rho}^{3}}\bigg)^{Q}=(2C_{\rm deg}+1)\bigg(\frac{M_{\rho}^{2}}{m_{\rho}^{3}}\bigg)^{Q}r^{Q}.\end{split}

∎

Corollary 4.28
.

The metric space
(X,d_{L_{*}})
is
\log_{L_{*}}(\lvert E_{1}\rvert)
-Ahlfors regular.

The motivation to quasi-visual approximations in the present work is the following lemma that gives us a short proof of the quasisymmetry.

Lemma 4.29

(
[
5
, Proposition 2.7]
)
.

Let
(S,d)
and
(T,\delta)
be bounded metric spaces, the map

be a bijection, and
\{\textbf{X}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a quasi-visual approximation of

. Then
F\colon S\to T
is a quasisymmetry if and only if
\{F(\textbf{X}^{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a quasi-visual approximation of
(T,\delta)
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
\rho,\nu\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
be symmetric
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible densities. By Lemma
4.26
, the spaces
(X,d_{\rho},\mu_{\rho})
and
(X,d_{\nu},\mu_{\nu})
are
Q_{\rho}
- and

-Ahlfors regular, respectively, where

and

are the unique exponents for which

By Lemma

both spaces are compact and thus bounded, and by Lemma

the collection

∎

5.
Moduli and Conformal Dimension

In this section, we characterize the value and attainment of the conformal dimension of a symmetric Laakso-type fractal space. For the rest of the section, we fix a simple IGS that is doubling, non-degenerate and symmetric, with a generator
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1},\xi_{1})
and symmetry map by
\eta
. We denote the limit space by
X
.

5.1.
Critical exponent

Conformal dimension for general metric spaces can be studied via discrete moduli of graph approximations at different levels; see e.g.
[
11
,
22
,
38
,
32
]
. In our case, this involves solving a moduli problem for the sequence of graphs
G_{n}
approximating the limit space
X
. Individually solving these modulus problems is computationally hopeless because the sizes of these graphs grow exponentially with the level of approximation. The following lemma, however, shows that it actually suffices to solve the modulus problem for
G_{1}
, and the solutions for
G_{n}
follow from a multiplicative construction. This is one of the most important aspects of our approach, and expresses the idea that
G_{1}
contains all of the information needed to study the conformal geometry of
X
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in(1,\infty)
the unique
p
-minimal
\Theta^{(n)}
-admissible density
\rho_{n}\colon E_{n}\to[0,1]
is symmetric, satisfies
\rho_{n}(e)<1
for all
e\in E_{n}
, and admits a multiplicative cascade representation

(5.2)

\rho_{n}(e)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{1}(T^{-1}(e)_{i})\quad\text{for all }e\in E_{n},

where
\rho_{1}\colon E_{1}\to[0,1)
is the
p
-minimal
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density. Furthermore, the mapping
t\mapsto{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta^{(n)},G_{n})
is continuous and strictly decreasing for all
t\in[1,\infty)
.

Proof.

Fix
p\in(1,\infty)
. Since the IGS is non-degenerate, we have that
{\rm len}(\theta)\geq 2
for all
\theta\in\Theta^{(n)}
. It then follows directly from Lemma
2.10
that the unique
p
-minimal
\Theta^{(n)}
-admissible density
\rho_{n}\colon E_{n}\to[0,1]
satisfies
\rho_{n}(e)<1
for all
e\in E_{n}
, and that the mapping
t\mapsto{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta^{(n)},G_{n})
is continuous and strictly decreasing for all
t\in[1,\infty)
. Since the IGS is symmetric, we have

\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{n}\circ\eta)=\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{n})={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta^{(n)},G_{n}).

Since the minimizer
\rho_{n}
is unique, we necessarily have
\rho_{n}\circ\eta=\rho_{n}
.

We now verify (
5.2
) for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. First, by applying Proposition
3.5
inductively, we see that

\min_{\theta\in\Theta^{(n)}}L_{\rho_{n}}(\theta)\geq\big(\min_{\theta\in\Theta^{(1)}}L_{\rho}(\theta)\big)^{n}\geq 1.

Therefore,
\rho_{n}
is
\Theta^{(n)}
-admissible. Due to Proposition
2.12
and the uniqueness of
\rho_{n}
, it is enough to show that there exists a unit flow
\mathcal{J}_{n}
from
I^{(n)}_{-}
to
I^{(n)}_{+}
such that, if
\rho_{n}
satisfies equation (
5.2
), then

\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{n})^{\frac{1}{p}}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{n})^{\frac{1}{q}}=1.

Since
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{1})={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})
, there exists by Proposition
2.12
a unit flow
\mathcal{J}_{1}
from
I_{-}
to
I_{+}
so that
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{1})^{\frac{1}{p}}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{1})^{\frac{1}{q}}=1
. Note, that since
\rho_{1}\circ\eta=\rho_{1}
, it follows that
\mathcal{J}_{1}(\eta(z),\eta(e))=-\mathcal{J}_{1}(z,e)
for every
z\in e\in E_{1}
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\mathcal{J}_{n+1}
as follows: For every
z\in e\in E_{1}
and
f\in E_{n}
we set

\mathcal{J}_{n+1}([z,f],(e,f)):=\mathcal{J}_{n}(f^{-},f)\mathcal{J}_{1}(z,e).

We will show by induction, that
\mathcal{J}_{n+1}
is a unit flow for from
I_{-}^{(n+1)}
to
I_{+}^{(n+1)}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. To that end, assume that
\mathcal{J}_{n}
is a unit flow. Since
\mathcal{J}_{1}
is unit flow, we have for all
e\in E_{1}
and
f\in E_{n}
that

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{n+1}([e^{+},f],(e,f))=\mathcal{J}_{n}(f^{-},f)\mathcal{J}_{1}(e^{+},e)&=-\mathcal{J}_{n}(f^{-},f)\mathcal{J}_{1}(e^{-},e)\\
&=-\mathcal{J}_{n+1}([e^{+},f],(e,f)).\end{split}

Now, let
v=[z,\hat{e}]\in e\in E_{n+1}
for some
z\in V_{1}
and
\hat{e}\in E_{n}
, and suppose that
z\not\in I_{-}\cup I_{+}
. The edges with
v
as an endpoint are all contained in
\hat{e}\cdot G_{1}
, and since
\mathcal{J}_{1}
is a flow, we have

\begin{split}{\rm div}(\mathcal{J}_{n+1})(v)=\sum_{\{f\mid v\in f\}}\mathcal{J}_{n+1}(v,f)&=\sum_{\{f\mid z\in f\}}\mathcal{J}_{n}(\hat{e}^{-},\hat{e})\mathcal{J}_{1}(z,f)\\
&=\mathcal{J}_{n}(\hat{e}^{-},\hat{e})\cdot{\rm div}(\mathcal{J}_{1})(z)=0.\end{split}

Suppose next that
z\in I_{-}\cup I_{+}
. Then,
v\in\pi_{n+1}^{-1}(\hat{v})
for some
\hat{v}=\pi_{n+1}(v)\in V_{n}
. Since the IGS is doubling, for every edge
\hat{f}\in E_{n}
with
\hat{v}
as an endpoint the set
\hat{f}\cdot E_{1}\subset E_{n+1}
contains exactly one edge
f
with
v
as an endpoint, and by construction no other edges with
v
as an endpoint can exist. Since the IGS is symmetric, there exists a vertex
x\in I_{-}
so that
z\in\{x,\eta(x)\}
and hence, for each of the edges
\hat{f}
with
\hat{v}
as an endpoint, the vertex
v
satisfies:

v=[z,\hat{e}]=\begin{cases}[x,\hat{f}]&\text{if }\hat{v}=\hat{f}^{-},\\
[\eta(x),\hat{f}]&\text{if }\hat{v}=\hat{f}^{+}.\end{cases}

Therefore, we have that

\mathcal{J}_{n+1}(v,f):=\begin{cases}\mathcal{J}_{n}(\hat{v},\hat{f})\mathcal{J}_{1}(x,\mathfrak{e}(x))&\text{if }\hat{v}=\hat{f}^{-},\\
-\mathcal{J}_{n}(\hat{v},\hat{f})\mathcal{J}_{1}(\eta(x),\mathfrak{e}(\eta(x)))&\text{if }\hat{v}=\hat{f}^{+},\end{cases}

and since
\mathfrak{e}(\eta(x))=\eta(\mathfrak{e}(x))
and
\mathcal{J}_{1}(x,\mathfrak{e}(x))=-\mathcal{J}_{1}(\eta(x),\eta(\mathfrak{e}(x)))
, it then follows that

\mathcal{J}_{n+1}(v,f)=\mathcal{J}_{n}(\hat{v},\hat{f})\mathcal{J}_{1}(x,\mathfrak{e}(x)),

from which we may compute

(5.3)

\begin{split}{\rm div}(\mathcal{J}_{n+1})(v)=\sum_{\{f\mid v\in f\}}\mathcal{J}_{n+1}(v,f)&=\sum_{\{\hat{f}\mid\hat{v}\in\hat{f}\}}\mathcal{J}_{n}(\hat{v},\hat{f})\mathcal{J}_{1}(x,\mathfrak{e}(x))\\
&=\mathcal{J}_{1}(x,\mathfrak{e}(x))\cdot{\rm div}(\mathcal{J}_{n})(\hat{v}).\end{split}

If
v\not\in I_{-}^{(n+1)}\cup I_{+}^{(n+1)}
, then
\hat{v}\not\in I_{-}^{(n)}\cup I_{+}^{(n)}
, and consequently
{\rm div}(\mathcal{J}_{n})(\hat{v})=0
, and so we have by (
5.3
) that
{\rm div}(\mathcal{J}_{n+1})(v)=0
. Hence,
\mathcal{J}_{n+1}
is a flow. To see that it is a unit flow, we compute inductively the total flow:

\begin{split}\sum_{v\in I_{-}^{(n+1)}}{\rm div}(\mathcal{J}_{n+1})(v)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Equation: Divergence Lift}}}{{=}}\sum_{\hat{v}\in I_{-}^{(n)}}\sum_{x\in I_{-}^{(1)}}\mathcal{J}_{1}(x,\mathfrak{e}(x))\cdot{\rm div}(\mathcal{J}_{n})(\hat{v})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\eqref{Equation: Divergence Lift}}}}{{=}}\sum_{\hat{v}\in I_{-}^{(n)}}{\rm div}(\mathcal{J}_{n})(\hat{v})\sum_{x\in I_{-}^{(1)}}\mathcal{J}_{1}(x,\mathfrak{e}(x))\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\eqref{Equation: Divergence Lift}}}}{{=}}\sum_{\hat{v}\in I_{-}^{(n)}}{\rm div}(\mathcal{J}_{n})(\hat{v})=1\end{split}

Having constructed the unit flow
\mathcal{J}_{n}
from
I^{(n)}_{-}
to
I^{(n)}_{+}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we only need show that if
\rho_{n}
satisfies equation (
5.2
) then
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{n})^{1/p}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{n})^{1/q}=1
. To that end, first note that for every
e\in E_{n}
we have by construction that

(5.4)

\lvert\mathcal{J}_{n}(e)\rvert=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\mathcal{J}_{1}(T^{-1}(e)_{i})\rvert.

Then, if
\rho_{n}
satisfies equation (
5.2
), we may compute:

(5.5)

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{n})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Equation: Optimal flow is a cascade}}}{{=}}\sum_{e\in E_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\mathcal{J}_{1}(T^{-1}(e)_{i})\rvert^{q}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{e\in E_{1}}\lvert\mathcal{J}_{1}(e)\rvert^{q}=\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{1})^{n},\\
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{n})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Equation: Optimal density is a cascade}}}{{=}}\sum_{e\in E_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\rho_{1}(T^{-1}(e)_{i})\rvert^{p}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{e\in E_{1}}\lvert\rho_{n}(e)\rvert^{p}=\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{1})^{n}.\end{split}

Since
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{1})^{1/p}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{1})^{1/q}=1
, it then follows that

\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{n})^{\frac{1}{p}}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{n})^{\frac{1}{q}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Equation: Energy and mass cascade}}}{{=}}\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{1})^{\frac{n}{p}}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{1})^{\frac{n}{q}}=1.

∎

As a direct corollary of equations (
5.2
) and (
5.5
), we obtain the following explicit formula for the moduli at different levels.

Corollary 5.6
.

For any
p\in(1,\infty)
, we have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta^{(n)},G_{n})={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})^{n}.

The critical exponent
Q_{*}
is obtained by solving the equation
{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1
. Solutions to this equation are found by the intermediate value theorem, since its fairly easy to see that
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})\geq 1
, since there is at least one path in
\Theta^{(1)}
. In the next lemma we characterize the case
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1
.

Lemma 5.7
.

The following four conditions are equivalent:

(A)

{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1
.

(B)

There exists an edge
e^{*}\in E_{1}
that is contained in every path
\theta\in\Theta^{(1)}
.

(C)

There are no edge disjoint paths in
\Theta^{(1)}
.

Proof.

First, we prove that
(A)\Longrightarrow(C)
.
Suppose
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1
.
Then, there exists a
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density
\rho\colon E_{1}\to[0,1]
, for which
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho)=1
. If there is a simple path
\theta\in\Theta^{(1)}
which does not contain every edge
e\in{\rm supp}(\rho)
, then

L_{\rho}(\theta)=\sum_{e\in\theta}\rho(e)<\sum_{e\in E_{1}}\rho(e)=\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho)=1,

which contradicts the admissibility of
\rho
. Therefore, every path in
\Theta^{(1)}
contains all of the edges in
{\rm supp}(\rho)
. In particular now two edge disjoint paths exist in
\Theta^{(1)}
.

The implication
(C)\Longrightarrow(B)
is a direct consequence of Menger’s Theorem
[
18
]
.

Finally
(B)\Longrightarrow(A)
follows by setting
\rho(e^{*})=1
and
\rho(e)=0
for all
e\neq e^{*}
and observing that
\rho
is
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

If
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1
, it is not difficult to conclude from the previous lemma that there exist a cut-point
p\in X
, i.e. points
p
s.t.
X\setminus\{p\}
is disconnected. Moreover, this point can be chosen such that
p
disconnects
X_{-}
from
X_{+}
. Also the converse of this is true, but a bit involved to show. In Theorem
1.5
, we will also show that all these conditions are equivalent with the limit space having conformal dimension one. In short: the presence of cut points is equivalent to conformal dimension being
1
, and the lack thereof with conformal dimension being greater than
1
. These should be compared with
[
12
, Theorem 1.2]
and
[
33
, Corollary 1.2]
, which are general statements on the connection between conformal dimension and the presence of cut points.

Lemma 5.9
.

There exists a unique value
Q_{*}\geq 1
for which
{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1
. Furthermore, the value
Q_{*}
satisfies

\begin{split}Q_{*}&=\inf\left\{Q\geq 1\ \middle|\begin{array}[]{l}\exists\rho\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)\text{ s.t. }\mathcal{M}_{Q}(\rho)=1,\\
\rho\text{ is symmetric and }\Theta^{(1)}\text{-admissible}\end{array}\right\}\\
&=\inf\left\{Q\geq 1\ \middle|\lim_{n\to\infty}{\rm Mod}_{Q}(\Theta^{(n)},G_{n})=0\right\}.\end{split}

Proof.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and let
\rho\colon E_{1}\to[0,1)
be the
p
-minimal
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density for some
p\in(1,\infty)
. We then have by Lemma
5.1
that
\rho(e)<1
for all
e\in E_{1}
, and therefore

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})\leq\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{M}_{t}(\rho)=0.

Since we have that
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})\geq 1
due to the admissibility condition, and since the mapping
t\to{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})
is continuous and strictly decreasing for all
t\in[1,\infty)
by Lemma
5.1
, it follows that there exists a unique value
Q_{*}\geq 1
, such that
{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1
.

Next, we will show that
Q_{*}=Q_{\inf}
, where

Q_{\inf}:=\inf\left\{Q\geq 1\ \middle|\begin{array}[]{l}\exists\rho\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)\text{ s.t. }\mathcal{M}_{Q}(\rho)=1,\\
\rho\text{ is symmetric and }\Theta^{(1)}\text{-admissible}\end{array}\right\}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. By Lemma
5.1
, there exists a symmetric
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density
\rho_{\varepsilon}\colon E_{1}\to[0,1)
, so that
\mathcal{M}_{Q_{*}+\varepsilon}(\rho_{\varepsilon})<1
. Thus, for some value
\delta\in(0,1-\mathcal{M}_{\rho_{\varepsilon}})
, the symmetric
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density
\nu_{\varepsilon}\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
given by

\nu_{\varepsilon}(e):=\rho_{\varepsilon}(e)+\delta\quad\text{for all }e\in E_{1}

satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{Q_{*}+\varepsilon}(\nu_{\varepsilon})=1
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, it follows that
Q_{\inf}\leq Q_{*}
.

On the other hand, since we have by Lemma
5.1
that the map
t\mapsto{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})
is strictly decreasing, it follows that

\mathcal{M}_{Q}(\rho)\geq{\rm Mod}_{Q}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})\geq{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1

whenever
1\leq Q\leq Q_{*}
and
\rho\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
is a symmetric
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density, and therefore we have that
Q_{*}\leq Q_{\inf}
.

Finally, we show that
Q_{*}=\inf\{Q\geq 1\mid\lim_{n\to\infty}{\rm Mod}_{Q}(\Theta^{(n)},G_{n})=0\}
. Using again the fact that
t\to{\rm Mod}_{t}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})
is strictly decreasing, it follows from Corollary
5.6
that for all
Q\in[1,\infty)
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\rm Mod}_{Q}(\Theta^{(n)},G_{n})=\begin{cases}\infty&\text{if }Q<Q_{*},\\
1&\text{if }Q=Q_{*},\\
0&\text{if }Q>Q_{*}.\end{cases}

The claim now follows directly.
∎

We now obtain the critical exponent
Q_{*}
as an upper bound for the conformal dimension.

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
Q_{*}\geq 1
be the unique value for which
{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1
. Then
\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d_{L_{*}})\leq Q_{*}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.9
, there exists a sequence of symmetric
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible densities
(\rho_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
and a sequence
(Q_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in(Q_{*},\infty)
with
\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}Q_{n}=Q_{*}
, so that
\mathcal{M}_{Q_{n}}(\rho_{n})=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, we have by Theorem
1.5
that
d_{\rho_{n}}\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X,d_{L_{*}})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and thus

(5.11)

\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d_{L_{*}})\leq\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(X,d_{\rho_{n}})=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=Q_{*}.

∎

Next, we prove the corresponding lower bound for conformal dimension. This will rely on finding appropriate porous subsets.

5.2.
Porous subsets

Let
F\subset E_{1}
. We define the subsets
E_{n}(F)\subset E_{n}
and
V_{n}(F)\subset V_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
by

\begin{split}E_{n}(F)&:=\{e_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot e_{n}\in E_{n}\mid e_{i}\in F\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n\},\\
V_{n}(F)&:=\{v\in\{e^{-},e^{+}\}\mid e\in E_{n}(F)\},\end{split}

and denote by
G_{n}(F)
the sub-graph of
G_{n}
with edges
E_{n}(F)
and vertices
V_{n}(F)
. We define the subset
X(F)\subset X
as

X(F):=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{e\in E_{n}(F)}X_{e}=\{[(e_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}]\in X\mid e_{n}\in E_{n}(F)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\},

and for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
e\in E_{n}(F)
the subset
X_{e}(F):=X_{e}\cap X(F)
.

The following lemma is needed to study the geometry of the subset
X(F)
.

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
F\subseteq E_{1}
so that
G_{1}(F)
contains a path
\theta=[v_{0},e_{1},\ldots,e_{k},v_{k}]
for which
v_{k}=\eta(v_{0})
. Then

(1)

G_{n}(F)
contains a path in
\Theta^{(n)}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(2)

For every
e\in E_{n}(F)
, there exists a point
x\in\mathrm{int}(X_{e}(F))
such that

B_{d_{L_{*}}|_{{X(F)}}}(x,L_{*}^{-(n+2)})\subset X_{e}(F).

(3)

X(F)
is complete and uniformly perfect.

(4)

For every
\delta\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X(F),d_{L_{*}})
there exists a constant
K>1
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
e\in E_{n}(F)
there exists
x\in X_{e}(F)
and
r>0
so that

B_{\delta}(x,r)\subset X_{e}(F)\subset B_{\delta}(x,Kr).

Proof.

First, we will prove claim (
1
) by induction. Suppose
G_{n}(F)
contains a path
[u_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{m},u_{m}]\in\Theta^{(n)}
. Since
v_{0}\in I_{-}
and
v_{k}=\eta(v_{0})\in I_{+}
, it follows for all
1\leq i\leq m-1
that

(5.13)

\begin{cases}(v_{k},f_{i})\sim(v_{0},f_{i+1})&\text{if and only if}\quad u_{i}=f_{i}^{+}=f_{i+1}^{-},\\
(v_{k},f_{i})\sim(v_{k},f_{i+1})&\text{if and only if}\quad u_{i}=f_{i}^{+}=f_{i+1}^{+},\\
(v_{0},f_{i})\sim(v_{0},f_{i+1})&\text{if and only if}\quad u_{i}=f_{i}^{-}=f_{i+1}^{-},\\
(v_{0},f_{i})\sim(v_{k},f_{i+1})&\text{if and only if}\quad u_{i}=f_{i}^{-}=f_{i+1}^{+}.\end{cases}

Define for all
1\leq i\leq m
the path

\theta_{i}:=[v^{i}_{0},e^{i}_{1},\ldots,e^{i}_{k},v^{i}_{k}]=\begin{cases}\theta&\text{if}\quad u_{i}=f_{i}^{+},\\
\theta^{-1}&\text{if}\quad u_{i}=f_{i}^{-}.\end{cases}

Then, by (
5.13
), we have for all
1\leq i\leq m-1
that
[v^{i}_{k},f_{i}]=[v^{i+1}_{0},f_{i}]
, and hence the concatenation
\vartheta:=(f_{1}\cdot\theta_{i})*(f_{2}\cdot\theta_{2})*\ldots*(f_{m}\cdot\theta_{m})
is well defined. Moreover, we have that
[v^{1}_{0},f_{1}]\in I^{(n+1)}_{-}
and
[v^{m}_{k},f_{m}]\in I^{(n+1)}_{+}
, and therefore
\vartheta\in\Theta^{(n+1)}
. Since
f_{i}\in E_{n}(F)
and
e_{j}\in E_{1}(F)
, we have by definition that
f_{i}\cdot e_{j}\in E_{n+1}(F)
for all
1\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq j\leq k
, and so the path
\hat{\theta}
is contained in
G_{n+1}(F)
. This concludes the induction.

We will now prove claim (
2
). By claim (
1
), there exists a path
\theta\in\Theta^{(2)}
with every edge contained in
E_{2}(F)
. Since the IGS is non-degenerate, we have that
{\rm len}(\theta)\geq 4
, and may thus choose an edge
h\in E_{2}(F)
so that
h\cap I_{\pm}^{\smash{(2)}}=\varnothing
. Consequently, we may choose an edge
f=(e\cdot h)\in e\cdot E_{2}(F)
so that
f\cap g=\varnothing
for all
g\in E_{n+2}(F)\setminus(e\cdot E_{2}(F))
. By definition, for any
x\in X_{f}(F)
and
y\in X(F)\setminus X_{e}(F)
, there exists representatives
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
(g_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
, respectively, so that
e_{n+2}=f
and
g_{n+2}\not\in e\cdot E_{2}(F)
and therefore
e_{n+2}\cap f_{n+2}=\varnothing
. It then follows from

(

DL8

)

that
d_{L_{*}}(x,y)\geq L_{*}^{-n-2}
whenever
x\in X_{f}(F)
and
y\in X(F)\setminus X_{e}(F)
, and therefore

x\in B_{d_{L_{*}}|_{X(F)}}(x,L_{*}^{-n-2})\subset\mathrm{int}(X_{e}(F)).

Next, we prove claim (
3
), beginning with completeness. Since the set
X_{e}
is closed for any
e\in E_{n}
, the finite union
\bigcup_{e\in E_{n}(F)}X_{e}
is closed for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed,
X(F)
is a closed subset of a compact metric space. Thus
X(F)
is complete. For uniform perfectness, let
C=2L_{*}^{3}{\rm diam}(X(F),d_{L_{*}})
and
x=[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\in X(F)
. By claim (
1
), there exists a path
\theta\in\Theta^{(2)}
with every edge contained in
E_{2}(F)
. Since the IGS is non-degenerate, we have that
{\rm len}(\theta)\geq 4
, and may thus choose for any edge
e\in E_{2}(F)
an edge
f\in E_{2}(F)
so that
e\cap f=\varnothing
. Consequently, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we may choose an edge
f_{n+2}\in e_{n}\cdot E_{2}(F)
so that
e_{n+2}\cap f_{n+2}=\varnothing
, and a point
y_{n}\in X_{f_{n+2}}(F)
. It then follows that

L_{*}^{-n-2}\leq d_{L_{*}}(x,y_{n})\leq L_{*}^{n}

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
r\in(0,{\rm diam}(X(F),d_{L_{*}})
and

k:=\max\{k\in\mathbb{N}\mid 2L_{*}<r\leq 2L_{*}^{-k+1}{\rm diam}(X(F),d_{L_{*}})\}.

We then have that
y_{k}\in B(x,2L_{*}^{-k})\setminus B(x,L_{*}^{-k-2})\subset B(x,r)\setminus B(x,r/C)
and there
B(x,r)\setminus B(x,r/C)\neq\varnothing
. Since
x\in X(F)
and
r\in(0,{\rm diam}(X(F),d_{L_{*}}))
were arbitrary, it follows that
(X(F),d_{L_{*}})
is
C
-uniformly perfect.

Finally, we prove claim (
4
). By claim (
3
), the metric space
(X(F),d_{L_{*}})
is complete and uniformly perfect, and hence a metric
\delta\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X(F),d_{L_{*}})
exists. By claim (
2
), there exists a point
x\in X_{e}(F)
so that

B_{d_{L_{*}}|_{X(F)}}(x,L_{*}^{-n-2})\subset X_{e}(F).

Let
\eta
be the distortion function of the quasisymmetry
{\rm id}\colon(X(F),d_{L_{*}})\to(X(F),\delta)
and let
K=4\eta(2L_{*}^{2})
. We may assume that
{\rm diam}(X_{e}(F),\delta)>0
, since otherwise
{\rm diam}(X(F),\delta)=0
and the claim becomes trivial. Let
r=2K^{-1}{\rm diam}(X_{e}(F),\delta)
. Then, since
{\rm diam}(X_{e}(F),d_{L_{*}})\leq L_{*}^{-n}
, it follows that
X_{e}(F)\subset B_{\delta}(x,Kr)
, and so it suffices to show that
B_{\delta}(x,r)\subset X_{e}(F)
. To that end, let
y\in B_{\delta}(x,r)
and pick
z\in X_{e}(F)
with
\delta(x,z)\geq{\rm diam}(X_{e}(F),\delta)/2=Kr/4
. By the quasisymmetry condition

\frac{K}{4}\leq\frac{\delta(x,z)}{\delta(x,y)}\leq\eta\bigg(\frac{d_{L_{*}}(x,z)}{d_{L_{*}}(x,y)}\bigg).

Thus,

\frac{d_{L_{*}}(x,y)}{d_{L_{*}}(x,z)}\leq\frac{1}{\eta^{-1}(K/4)},

and

d_{L_{*}}(x,y)\leq\frac{d_{L_{*}}(x,z)}{\eta^{-1}(K/4)}\leq\frac{L_{*}^{-n}}{\eta^{-1}(K/4)}<L_{*}^{-n-2}.

Hence,
y\in X_{e}(F)
. As
y\in B_{\delta}(x,r)
was arbitrary, it follows that
B_{\delta}(x,r)\subset X_{e}(F)
as claimed.
∎

With the aim of using porosity to obstruct attainment of conformal dimension, we establish the following.

Lemma 5.14
.

If
F\subsetneq E_{1}
is non-empty, then
X(F)
is a closed porous subset of
(X,d_{L_{*}})
.

Proof.

Fix a constant
c=L_{*}^{-5}\cdot{\rm diam}(X,d_{L_{*}})^{-1}
. Let
y=[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\in X(F)
,
0<r\leq{\rm diam}(X,d_{L_{*}})
and choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
so that

L_{*}^{-n}\leq r\leq L_{*}^{-(n-1)}\cdot{\rm diam}(X,d_{L_{*}}).

Since
{\rm diam}(X_{e_{n+1}},d_{L_{*}})\leq L_{*}^{-(n+1)}
, it follows that
X_{e_{n+1}}\subset B(y,L_{*}^{-n})\subset B(y,r)
. Now, choose any
g\in E_{1}\setminus F
and let
f=e_{n+1}\cdot g\in E_{n+2}
. Then, by definition,
f\not\in E_{n+2}(F)
and therefore
\mathrm{int}(X_{f})\subset X\setminus X(F)
, from which it follows that

\mathrm{int}(X_{f})\subset X_{e_{n+1}}\setminus X(F)\subset B(y,r)\setminus X(F).

Note that
X=X(E_{1})
. Thus, by Lemma
5.12
, there exists
x\in\mathrm{int}(X_{f})
, so that
B(x,L_{*}^{-(\lvert f\rvert+2)})=B(x,L_{*}^{-(n+4)})\subset\mathrm{int}(X_{f})
. Since
r\leq L_{*}^{-(n-1)}\cdot{\rm diam}(X,d_{L_{*}})
, the constant
c
then satisfies

B(x,cr)\subset B(x,L^{-(n+4)})\subset\mathrm{int}(X_{f})\subset B(y,r)\setminus X(F).

Since the point
y\in X(F)
and the radius
0<r\leq{\rm diam}(X,d_{L_{*}})
were arbitrary, we conclude that
X(F)
is a porous subset of
(X,d_{L_{*}})
.
∎

5.3.
Characterization of attainment

For this subsection, we fix a simple path

\theta^{*}:=[v^{*}_{0},e^{*}_{1},\ldots,e^{*}_{L_{*}},v^{*}_{L_{*}}]\in\Theta^{(1)}

for which
\eta(v^{*}_{0})=v^{*}_{k}
and
{\rm len}(\theta^{*})=L_{*}
. If
Q_{*}>1
, we also fix
\rho^{*}
, which is the minimizer for
{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1}).

Definition 5.15
.

We define the subset
\widehat{E}\subseteq E_{1}
by

\widehat{E}:=\begin{cases}\{e:e\in\theta^{*}\}&\text{if}\quad Q_{*}=1,\\
{\rm supp}(\rho^{*})&\text{if}\quad Q_{*}>1,\end{cases}

and define for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
the family of paths

\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}:=\{[v_{0},e_{1},\ldots,e_{k},v_{k}]\in\Theta^{(n)}\mid e_{i}\in E_{n}(\widehat{E})\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq k\}.

Furthermore, we denote
\widehat{X}:=X(\widehat{E})
,
\widehat{X}_{\pm}:=X_{\pm}\cap\widehat{X}
, and
\widehat{X}_{e}:=X_{e}(\widehat{E})
. We say that the IGS has a removable edges if
\widehat{E}\neq E_{1}
.

The edges in
\widehat{E}\neq E_{1}
are called removable, since removing them from
E_{1}
does not alter the value of modulus, as the following lemma shows. Recall that
{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(n)},G_{n})=1
.

Lemma 5.16
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the family of paths
\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}
is non-empty and satisfies
{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)},G_{n}(\widehat{E}))=1
.

Proof.

Let us first consider the case
Q_{*}=1
. It follows directly from Lemma
5.12
that
\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}
is non-empty. Since every edge
e\in E_{n}(\widehat{E})
is of the form
e_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot e_{n}
, where
e_{i}\in\theta^{*}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, and since
\theta^{*}
is a simple path with
{\rm len}(\theta^{*})=L_{*}
, it follows that
\lvert E_{n}(\widehat{E})\rvert={\rm len}(\theta^{*})^{n}=L_{*}^{n}
. We also have that
\min\{{\rm len}(\theta)\mid\theta\in\Theta^{(n)}\}=L_{*}^{n}
, and so every path in
\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}
must contain every edge in
E_{n}(\widehat{E})
. Consequently, the density
\rho_{n}\equiv L_{*}^{-n}
is
\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}
-admissible with
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho_{n})=1
, and therefore

1={\rm Mod}_{1}(\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)},G_{n}(\widehat{E}))={\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)},G_{n}(\widehat{E})).

Next, we consider the case
Q_{*}>1
. Let
\rho^{*}_{n}
be the optimal density function for
{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(n)},G_{n})
and
\mathcal{J}^{*}_{n}
the optimal flow for
{\rm Res}_{p}(X_{-},X_{+},G_{n})
. By Lemma
5.1
we have that
\rho^{*}_{n}(e_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot e_{n})>0
if and only if
e_{i}\in{\rm supp}(\rho^{*}_{1}(e))=\widehat{E}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Moreover, by Proposition
2.12
, we have that
\lvert\mathcal{J}^{*}_{n}(e)\rvert>0
if and only if
\rho^{*}_{n}(e)>0
. Therefore,

E_{n}(\widehat{E})=\{e\in E_{n}\mid\rho^{*}_{n}(e)>0\}=\{e\in E_{n}\mid\lvert\mathcal{J}^{*}_{n}(e)\rvert>0\}.

The support of a non-trivial flow must contain a path and thus by Lemma
5.12
it follows that
\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}
is non-empty. It is clear that
\mathcal{J}^{*}_{n}|_{V_{n}(\widehat{E})\times E_{n}(\widehat{E})}
is a unit flow and that
\rho^{*}_{n}|_{E_{n}(\widehat{E})}
is a
\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}
-admissible density. Since

\mathcal{M}_{Q}(\rho^{*}_{n}|_{E_{n}(\widehat{E})})=\sum_{e:\rho^{*}_{n}(e)\neq 0}\rho^{*}_{n}(e)^{Q}=\mathcal{M}_{Q}(\rho^{*}_{n}),

and

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\mathcal{J}^{*}_{n}|_{\mathcal{I}(G_{n}(\widehat{E}))})=\sum_{e:\lvert\mathcal{J}^{*}_{n}(e)\rvert\neq 0}\lvert\mathcal{J}^{*}_{n}(e)\rvert=\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\mathcal{J}^{*}_{n}),

for all
1\leq Q<\infty
, it follows that

\mathcal{M}_{Q_{*}}(\rho^{*}_{n}|_{E_{n}(\widehat{E})})^{\frac{1}{Q_{*}}}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{\frac{Q_{*}}{Q_{*}-1}}(\mathcal{J}^{*}_{n}|_{\mathcal{I}(G_{n}(\widehat{E}))})^{\frac{Q_{*}-1}{Q_{*}}}=1,

and therefore, since
\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}
is non-empty, it follows from Proposition
2.12
, that

{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)},G_{n}(\widehat{E}))=\mathcal{M}_{Q_{*}}(\rho^{*}_{n}|_{E_{n}(\widehat{E})})=\mathcal{M}_{Q_{*}}(\rho^{*}_{n})={\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(n)},G_{n})=1.

∎

Corollary 5.17
.

Let
q\geq 1
. If for every
p>q
there exists a
\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}
-admissible density
\rho:E_{n}\to(0,1)
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho)<1
, then
q\geq Q_{*}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.16
, we have that
{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)},G_{n}(\widehat{E}))=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, by Lemma
2.11
, we have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

{\rm Mod}_{q}(\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)},G_{n}(\widehat{E}))>{\rm Mod}_{p}(\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)},G_{n}(\widehat{E}))>1

whenever
1\leq q<p<Q_{*}
. Therefore, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq q<p<Q_{*}
, there cannot exists a
\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}
-admissible density
\rho
for which
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho)<1
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the lower bound for conformal dimension. The following proposition could be proved also with the techniques from
[
11
]
. This would be quite indirect, and we instead prefer to give a short and self-contained proof.

Proposition 5.18
.

Let
Q_{*}\geq 1
be the unique value for which
{\rm Mod}_{Q_{*}}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1
. Then,
\dim_{\rm AR}(\widehat{X},d_{L_{*}})\geq Q_{*}

Proof.

Let
p,q\in(1,\infty)
so that
p>q>\dim_{\rm AR}(\widehat{X},d_{L_{*}}|_{\widehat{X}})
, and choose a
q
-Ahlfors regular metric
\delta\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(\widehat{X},d_{L_{*}})
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the density
\rho_{n}\colon E_{n}(\widehat{E})\to(0,1)
by

\rho_{n}(e):=\frac{{\rm diam}(\widehat{X}_{e},\delta)}{{\rm dist}(X_{-},X_{+},\delta)}.

Since
X_{-}\cap X_{+}=\emptyset
we have
{\rm dist}(X_{-},X_{+},\delta)>0
. Given
\theta=[v_{0},e_{1},\ldots,e_{k},v_{k}]\in\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}
, we have that
\widehat{X}_{e_{i}}\cap\widehat{X}_{e_{i+1}}\neq\varnothing
for all
1\leq i\leq k-1
, and that
\widehat{X}_{e_{1}}\cap X_{-}\neq\varnothing
and
\widehat{X}_{e_{k}}\cap X_{+}\neq\varnothing
. It therefore follows from the triangle inequality that
{\rm dist}(\widehat{X}_{-},\widehat{X}_{+},\delta)\leq\sum_{e\in\theta}{\rm diam}(\widehat{X}_{e},\delta)
, and hence

L_{\rho_{n}}(\theta)=\sum_{e\in\theta}\rho_{n}(e)=\sum_{e\in\theta}\frac{{\rm diam}(\widehat{X}_{e},\delta)}{{\rm dist}(X_{-},X_{+},\delta)}\geq\frac{{\rm dist}(X_{-},X_{+},\delta)}{{\rm dist}(X_{-},X_{+},\delta)}=1.

Thus, the density
\rho_{n}
is
\widehat{\Theta}^{(n)}
-admissible for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, we wish to estimate
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{n})
. By Lemma
5.12
there exists a constant
K>1
, such that we may choose for every edge
e\in\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E_{n}(\widehat{E})
a point
x(e)\in\widehat{X}_{e}
and a radius
r(e)>0
so that

(5.19)

B_{\delta}(x(e),r(e))\subset\widehat{X}_{e}\subset B_{\delta}(x(e),Kr(e)).

Denote
B_{e}:=B_{\delta}(x(e),r(e))
. Recall that the measure
\mu=\mathcal{H}^{q}
(with respect to the metric
\delta
) satisfies the condition in (
2.2
). By equation (
5.19
) and the
q-
Ahlfors regularity of
\delta
, there exists some constant
C>1
such that

(5.20)

C^{-1}\mathcal{H}^{q}(B_{e},\delta)\leq{\rm diam}(\widehat{X}_{e},\delta)\leq CK^{q}\mathcal{H}^{q}(B_{e},\delta)

for every edge
e\in\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E_{n}(\widehat{E})
. Since
\mathrm{int}\widehat{X}_{e}\cap\mathrm{int}\widehat{X}_{f}=\varnothing
whenever
e,f\in E_{n}(\widehat{E})
and
e\neq f
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the collection of balls
\{B_{e}\}_{e\in E_{n}(\widehat{E})}
is disjoint and

(5.21)

\sum_{e\in E_{n}(\widehat{E})}\mathcal{H}^{q}(B_{e},\delta)\leq\mathcal{H}^{q}(X,\delta)<\infty.

Let
M_{n}:=\max\{\rho_{n}(e)\mid e\in E_{n}(\widehat{E})\}
. From the quasisymmetry condition, it follows easily that
M_{n}\leq{\rm diam}(\widehat{X},\delta)\eta(L_{*}^{1-n})
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{n}=0
. Since
\rho_{n}(e)^{p}\leq\rho_{n}(e)^{p-q}\rho_{n}(e)^{q}\leq M_{n}^{p-q}\rho_{n}(e)^{q}
for all
e\in E_{n}
, we may estimate:

(5.22)

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{n})&\leq M_{n}^{p-q}\sum_{e\in E_{n}(\widehat{E})}\rho_{n}(e)^{q}=M_{n}^{p-q}\sum_{e\in E_{n}(\widehat{E})}\frac{{\rm diam}(\widehat{X}_{e},\delta)^{q}}{{\rm dist}(X_{-},X_{+},\delta)^{q}}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Equation: Diameter Estimate}}}{{\leq}}M_{n}^{p-q}\sum_{e\in E_{n}(\widehat{E})}\frac{CK^{q}\mathcal{H}^{q}(B_{e},\delta)}{{\rm dist}(X_{-},X_{+},\delta)^{q}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Equation: Measure Estimate}}}{{\leq}}M_{n}^{p-q}\frac{CK^{q}\mathcal{H}^{q}(X,\delta)}{{\rm dist}(X_{-},X_{+},\delta)^{q}}.\end{split}

Since
p-q>0
and
M_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\to}}0
, it follows that
M_{n}^{p-q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\to}}0
, and so there exists some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{n})<1
. Since,
p>q
was arbitrary, it follows from Corollary
5.17
that
q\geq Q_{*}
. Since
q>\dim_{\rm AR}(\widehat{X},d_{L_{*}}|_{\widehat{X}})
was arbitrary, it follows that
\dim_{\rm AR}(\widehat{X},d_{L_{*}})\geq Q_{*}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main Theorems.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

It follows from Lemmas
5.16
and
5.12
, that the subset
\widehat{X}
is complete and uniformly perfect. Thus, as
\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d_{L_{*}})\leq\log_{L_{*}}(\lvert E_{1}\rvert)<\infty
, it follows Corollary
2.6
that

(5.23)

\dim_{\rm AR}(\widehat{X},d_{L_{*}})\leq\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d_{L_{*}}).

By Corollary
5.10
we have that
\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d_{L_{*}})\leq Q_{*}
and by Proposition
5.18
that
\dim_{\rm AR}(\widehat{X},d_{L_{*}})\geq Q_{*}
. Therefore it follows that

Q_{*}=\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d_{L_{*}})=\dim_{\rm AR}(\widehat{X},d_{L_{*}}).

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

If the IGS has removable edges, meaning that
E_{1}\neq\widehat{E}_{1}
, then Lemma
5.14
implies that the subset
\widehat{X}
is porous. As shown in the proof of Theorem
1.7
,
\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d_{L_{*}})=\dim_{\rm AR}(\widehat{X},d_{L_{*}})
and hence, by Proposition
2.7
, the conformal dimension of
(X,d_{L_{*}})
is not attained. On the other hand, if the IGS does not have removable edges, then, by definition, there exists a symmetric
\Theta^{(1)}
-admissible density
\rho^{*}\colon E_{1}\to(0,1)
so that
\mathcal{M}_{Q_{*}}(\rho^{*})=1
. It then follows from Theorem
1.5
that

d_{\rho^{*}}\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X,d_{L_{*}})
and

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(X,d_{\rho^{*}})=Q_{*}=\dim_{\rm AR}(X,d_{L_{*}}),

where the last equality is given by Theorem
1.7
. Thus, the conformal dimension of
(X,d_{L_{*}})
is attained.
∎

6.
On the Combinatorial Loewner Property

One of the motivations for this work comes from the combinatorial Loewner property (CLP) discussed in
[
7
]
, and the related
Kleiner’s conjecture

[
26
, Conjecture 7.5]
. The conjecture states that the combination of CLP and approximate self-similarity implies that a space is quasisymmetric to a Loewner space. By
[
14
, §1.6]
(or
[
34
, Proposition 11.14]
), this problem is equivalent to the attainment of conformal dimension for such spaces. In
[
2
]
, the first two authors found counterexamples to this conjecture. All of these examples came from IGSs with removable edges, and the reason for non-attainment was the same as in Theorem
1.3
. While these counterexamples resolved the conjecture for specific spaces, it remains a difficult open problem to understand the general case; see for example the discussion in
[
16
]
. In particular, it would be important to understand if the conjecture has an affirmative answer for some classes of spaces. Which additional assumptions are needed to guarantee that a space is quasisymmetric to a Loewner space?

In this section, we find a large class of non-trivial cases where Kleiner’s conjecture is true. This is done by verifying the approximate self-similarity and the combinatorial Loewner properties for the limit spaces
X
that we have constructed. Thus, in the absence of removable edges, Theorem
1.3
implies attainment of the conformal dimension and hence verifies Kleiner’s conjecture for many symmetric Laakso-type spaces. As seen in earlier sections, the metrics attaining the conformal dimension are explicit, but do not arise from a snowflake deformation, or some other trivial construction.

An additional motivation for this section is to observe that symmetric Laakso-type spaces of minimal Hausdorff dimension are
Loewner spaces
. This follows immediately from
[
13
, §1.6]
(or
[
34
, Proposition 11.14]
), and is stated at the end of the section. For the definition of a Loewner space, see
[
13
, Definition 1.11]
or
[
19
]
.

In order discuss Kleiner’s conjecture, we will need to establish approximate self-similarity and CLP for the spaces of interest. Most of the work on verifying CLP was already done in
[
2
]
, and the only difference here is that we used a slightly different metric. For expository purposes, we define approximate self-similarity and CLP, and outline the general approach to prove these for our metrics. For most of the technical steps we refer to
[
2
]
, and we simply highlight any changes.

Definition 6.1
.

A metric space
(X,d)
is approximately self-similar if there exists a constant
L>1
s.t. for every
x\in X,r\in(0,{\rm diam}(X)]
there exists an open set
U_{x,r}\subset X
and a
L
-bi-Lipschitz map
f:(B(x,r),d/r)\to U_{x,r}
.

We recall the definition of the intrinsic metric
d_{L_{*}}
from Definition
4.11
.

Proposition 6.2
.

A symmetric Laakso-type fractal space
(X,d_{L_{*}})
is approximately self-similar.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to
[
2
, Section 3.8]
and we simply sketch the details. For every
e\in G_{n}
, let
{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e)
be the subgraph of size
1
about
e
and
\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(e)
a subgraph of size two. We say that two edges
e\in G_{n},f\in G_{m}
are combinatorially equivalent, if
\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(e),\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(f)
are isomorphic as graphs via an isomorphism
\psi
, which satisfies
\phi_{\psi(u),\psi(a)}=\phi_{u,a}
for each edge
u
in
{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e)
and
a\in u
.

Further, any edge path in the definition of the metric connecting
y,z\in{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e)
which is shortest must be contained in
\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e)}
. Using these facts, and the proof of
[
2
, Corollary 3.34]
one sees that there is a
L_{*}^{n-m}
-homothety
{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e)\cdot X\to{\rm\mathcal{N}}(f)\cdot X
, where
A\cdot X=\bigcup_{e\in A}e\cdot A
for any
A\subset\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}E_{m}
.

Finally, approximate self-similarity follows from finiteness of isomorphism classes and these homotheties.
It is direct to see that there are finitely many isomorphism classes of edges, and there is some
N\in\mathbb{N}
so that each isomorphism class already contains a representative for some
e\in G_{n}
with
n\leq N
. Let
x\in e\cdot X
and
r<L_{*}^{-N}
. By Property

(

DL4

)

, we have
B(x,r)\subset{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e)\cdot X
for some edge
e\in G_{m}
with
L_{*}^{-m-1}\leq r\leq L_{*}^{-m}
. Thus, the previous paragraph constructs the desired homothety. This suffices to show approximate self-similarity; see
[
2
, Proposition 3.35.]
for further details.
∎

The Combinatorial Loewner Property (CLP) is more technical to state, and we refer to
[
2
]
for the classical definition from
[
7
]
and
[
15
]
. Here, we will present a simpler definition better suited for our setting.

For
v\in V_{n}
, let
X_{v}=\bigcup_{e\in E_{n},v\in e}e\cdot X
. This set resembles a star around
v
, and
X_{v}\cap X_{w}
if and only if
v,w
are connected by an edge. If
A,B\subset X
are two subsets, let
\Gamma(A,B)
be the collection of continuous paths connecting
A
to
B
, and
\Gamma_{L}(A,B)
the collection of continuous curves with diameter at most
L
. A function
\rho:V_{n}\to[0,1]
is called
admissible
for a collection of curves
\Gamma
if

\sum_{v\in V_{n},X_{v}\cap\gamma\neq\varnothing}\rho(v)\geq 1\text{ for all }\gamma\in\Gamma.

Let

{\rm Mod}_{p}^{D}(\Gamma;G_{n})=\inf\sum_{v\in V_{n}}\rho(v)^{p},

where the infimum is taken over all admissible functions
\rho
for
\Gamma(E,F)
.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
Q>1
. We say that
(X,d_{L_{*}})
satisfies the
Q
-Combinatorial Loewner property
if the following hold.

(
CLP1
)

For every
A\geq 4
there exists a
\delta>0
and
L\geq 1
s.t. for all
x,y\in X,r>0
with
d_{L_{*}}(x,y)\leq Ar
, and all
n>0
s.t.
L_{*}^{-n}<r/2
,

{\rm Mod}_{Q}^{D}(\Gamma_{Lr}(B(x,r),B(y,r);G_{n}))\geq\delta.

(
CLP2
)

For every
A\geq 2
there exists a
\delta_{A}
with
\lim_{A\to\infty}\delta_{A}=0
st. the following holds. For every
x\in X
,
r<{\rm diam}(X)=1/2
and
n>0
s.t.
L_{*}^{-n}<r/2
, we have

{\rm Mod}_{Q}^{D}(\Gamma(B(x,r),X\setminus B(x,Ar));G_{n})\leq\delta_{A}.

This definition is different from the classical one, but can easily be seen to be equivalent. Unlike the classical definition, here we do not use the notion of an
\alpha
-approximation and additionally

(

CLP1

)

only gives a lower bound for moduli of curves connecting balls instead of all pairs of sets; see
[
2
]
for the classical definition. The equivalence is seen by combining two observations. First, one can reduce from all sets to balls by
[
7
, Proposition 2.9]
. Second, our graphs
G_{n}
correspond isomorphically with an
\alpha
-approximation. This follows by an argument in
[
2
, Proposition 3.37]
combined with Lemma
5.12
and Lemma
4.5
– which account for the fact that we are using a slightly different metric. This argument shows that
G_{n}
is the incidence graph of the covering
\{X_{v}:v\in V_{n}\}
, and that this covering forms an
\alpha
-approximation.

So far, in this paper we have studied the edge modulus, and fortunately this is comparable to the corresponding vertex modulus. If
A\subset X
is a set, let
G_{n}(A)=\{v\in V_{n}:X_{v}\cap A\neq\varnothing\}
.

Lemma 6.4

(
[
2
, Proposition 5.4.9]
)
.

There exists a constant
C\geq 1
s.t. the following holds. If
A,B\subset X
s.t.
G_{n}(A),G_{n}(B)
are disjoint, then

C^{-1}{\rm Mod}_{p}^{D}(A,B;G_{n})\leq{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta(G_{n}(A),G_{n}(B));G_{n})\leq C{\rm Mod}_{p}^{D}(A,B;G_{n}),

where the comparability constant is independent of
n
.

Further, a simple argument allows us to compute moduli at different levels of the graph. Let
M_{Q}={\rm Mod}_{Q}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})
.

Lemma 6.5

(
[
2
, Theorem 4.38]
)
.

Let
n>m.
For every
A,B\subset V_{m}
we have

{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta(\pi_{n,m}^{-1}(A),\pi_{n,m}^{-1}(B)),G_{n})={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta(A,B),G_{m})M_{Q}^{m-n}.

We can now prove CLP for the limits space
X
by adapting
[
2
, Theorem 5.2]
. Notice that the value
Q>1
s.t. a metric space is
Q
-CLP is unique. Indeed, if there is any such
Q
, then
Q
is equal to the conformal dimension; see e.g.
[
17
, Lemma 4.2]
.

Theorem 6.6
.

A symmetric Laakso-type fractal space
(X,d_{L_{*}})
is
Q
-CLP for some
Q>1
if and only if the associated family of paths
\Theta^{(1)}
contains two edge-disjoint paths.

Proof.

The necessity of the condition is shown by contrapositive.
If
G_{1}
does not have two edge disjoint paths, then by Lemma
5.7
, we have that
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Theta^{(1)},G_{1})=1
and by Theorem
1.7
, we have that the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension is
1
. On the other hand a
Q
-CLP space with
Q>1
has
\dim_{AR}(X)=Q>1
by
[
17
, Lemma 4.2]
. Thus, in this case the limit space can not be
Q
-CLP.

Next, we show sufficiency. Let
Q=\dim_{{\rm AR}}(X)
, which by Lemma
5.7
, Proposition
5.18
and Corollary
5.10
satisfies
Q>1
. The bound

(

CLP2

)

follows from the argument in
[
2
, Proposition 5.2]
, where the relevant bound
{\rm diam}(X_{v})\leq 2L_{*}^{-n}
follows from

(

DL6

)

.

Next, the bound

(

CLP1

)

follows from
[
2
, Proposition 5.7]
. Here, one needs to use Lemma
5.12
to replace the use of
[
2
, Proposition 3.37]
.
∎

Finally, from the CLP we also obtain the classical Loewner property for minimizers. See
[
19
]
for the classical definition of a Loewner space, and
[
14
]
for more discussion on this notion.

Corollary 6.7
.

A symmetric Laakso-type fractal
(X,d_{L_{*}})
is quasisymmetric to a
Q
-Ahlfors regular
Q
-Loewner space for
Q>1
if and only if the associated IGS doesn’t have a removable edge and
Q=\dim_{{\rm AR}}(X)>1
. Moreover, if
(X,d)
is any minimizer for the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension and
Q=\dim_{{\rm AR}}(X)>1
, then
(X,d)
is
Q
-Loewner.

Proof.

If
(X,d_{L_{*}})
is quasisymmetric to a
Q
-Ahlfors regular
Q
-Loewner space for some
Q>1
, then by
[
40
, 1.7. Corollary]
,
Q
must be equal to the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension of
(X,d_{L_{*}})
. Thus,
X
attains its Ahlfors regular conformal dimension
Q
and by Theorem
1.3
there cannot be any removable edge.

On the other hand, if
(X,d_{L_{*}})
does not have a removable edge and
\dim_{{\rm AR}}(X)>1
, then by Theorem
1.3
it attains its Ahlfors regular conformal dimension and
Q=\dim_{{\rm AR}}(X)>1
. By Theorem
6.6

(X,d_{L_{*}})
is also
Q
-CLP, and by
[
14
, §1.6]
any minimizer of Ahlfors regular conformal dimension is
Q
-Loewner. This completes the proof of the converse statement and also the moreover-clause.

∎
