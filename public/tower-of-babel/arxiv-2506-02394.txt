Joint Modeling for Learning Decision-Making Dynamics in Behavioral Experiments
Yuan Bian, Xingche Guo, Yuanjia Wang

Abstract
Major depressive disorder (MDD), a leading cause of disability and mortality, is associated with reward-processing abnormalities and concentration issues. Motivated by the probabilistic reward task from the Establishing Moderators and Biosignatures of Antidepressant Response in Clinical Care (EMBARC) study, we propose a novel framework that integrates the reinforcement learning (RL) model and drift-diffusion model (DDM) to jointly analyze reward-based decision-making with response times. To account for emerging evidence suggesting that decision-making may alternate between multiple interleaved strategies, we model latent state switching using a hidden Markov model (HMM). In the “engaged” state, decisions follow an RL-DDM, simultaneously capturing reward processing, decision dynamics, and temporal structure. In contrast, in the “lapsed” state, decision-making is modeled using a simplified DDM, where specific parameters are fixed to approximate random guessing with equal probability. The proposed method is implemented using a computationally efficient generalized expectation-maximization algorithm with forward-backward procedures. Through extensive numerical studies, we demonstrate that our proposed method outperforms competing approaches under various reward-generating distributions, both with and without strategy switching. When applied to the EMBARC study, our framework reveals that MDD patients exhibit lower overall engagement than healthy controls and experience longer decision times when they do engage. Additionally, we show that neuroimaging measures of brain activities are associated with decision-making characteristics in the “engaged” state but not in the “lapsed” state, providing evidence of brain-behavioral association specific to the “engaged” state.



Keywords:

Brain-behavior association, Cognitive modeling, Drift-diffusion models, Mental health, Reinforcement learning, State switching

1
Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a mood disorder characterized by negative emotions, anhedonia (i.e., a diminished ability to feel pleasure), and psychomotor symptoms, significantly contributing to disability and mortality. Currently, MDD affects approximately 280 million people worldwide
(
World Health Organization, 2023
)
and is recognized as the leading global cause of disability
(
Friedrich, 2017
)
. Depressed individuals exhibit cognitive deficits
(
Rock et al., 2014
)
and concentration problems
(
Rice et al., 2019
)
, along with abnormalities in reward processing and altered learning abilities
(
Pizzagalli et al., 2005
)
. Recent studies have demonstrated that individuals with MDD take longer to make decisions compared to healthy controls.

Reinforcement learning (RL)
(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
)
is widely used to model cognitive processes and characterize decision-making behaviors, assuming that individuals learn through trial and error to optimize long-term rewards by developing an optimal policy. While specific RL methodologies may vary, their core principle remains consistent: individuals form and maintain internal representations of expected value, make decisions based on these expectations, and update these representations using the discrepancy between the expected and actual outcomes, known as the reward prediction error.
Huys et al., 2013
introduced a prediction-error RL approach to characterize decision-making through key behavioral phenotypes, while
Guo et al., 2024
extended this to a more general semiparametric RL framework.

These error-driven RL models primarily focus on decision patterns and assume that decision-making model follows a generalized linear model with a softmax link function. Although these models effectively capture trial-by-trial decision behavior, they fail to account for the complex dynamics underlying decision-making processes. In particular, they cannot distinguish between fast, accurate decisions and slow, conservative ones. Additionally, decision-making behaviors are known to involve a trade-off with response times, which can be under voluntary control. Ignoring concurrent changes in response times when studying decision behaviors may therefore lead to biased or incomplete conclusions.

In contrast, drift-diffusion models
(
Ratcliff, 1978
, DDMs;)
provide an alternative to softmax-based RL models by positing that decisions arise from the continuous accumulation of noisy evidence until a decision boundary is reached. A key advantage of DDM is its ability to simultaneously model decision outcomes and their temporal dynamics, revealing underlying cognitive processes such as information processing speed. For instance,
White et al., 2010
demonstrated that DDM could reveal deficits in decision-making that are undetectable through traditional response time analyses. Under the two-arm action setup, the softmax model can be understood as a special case of the decision-making submodel of DDM. However, DDMs are limited in that they cannot account for how decision-making processes (e.g., processing speed or response caution) evolve with learning over time
(
Miletić et al., 2020
)
.

To summarize, RL methods effectively model learning across trials but lack a mechanistic account of decision-making processes and fail to consider response time distributions; conversely, DDMs describe how decisions emerge from evidence accumulation and model response time distributions but often overlook the aspect of learning over time
(
Miletić et al., 2020
)
. To address these limitations, recent studies
(
Pedersen et al., 2017
;
Fontanesi et al., 2019a
;
Fontanesi et al., 2019b
)
have sought to integrate RL and DDM into a unified framework, known as the reinforcement learning diffusion decision model (RL-DDM). This integrated model improves parameter recovery and enhances the stability of model estimates
(
Shahar et al., 2019
)
. It also outperforms standard RL or DDM methods by jointly model learning and response time of decision-making processes and thus reveal comprehensive characteristics of their underlying mechanisms
(
Miletić et al., 2020
)
.

Furthermore, classical RL, DDM, and RL-DDM methods typically assume that individuals employ a consistent decision-making strategy. Recent research, however, indicates that decision-making often switches between multiple strategies. Specifically,
Ashwood et al., 2022
and
Guo et al., 2025
demonstrated that decision-making can alternate between “engaged” and “lapsed” strategies, which conceptually align with the notions of “exploitation” and “exploration” in RL. These strategy switches can be effectively captured using a hidden Markov model (HMM). Under the “engaged” strategy, individuals make decisions following a generalized linear model, while under the “lapsed” strategy, decisions are made based on a fixed probability, largely ignoring external stimuli.
However, the information contained in observed action or decision data alone is often insufficient to reliably estimate the parameters of such mixture models
(
Shahar et al., 2019
)
.
Moreover,
Iigaya et al., 2018
,
Dillon et al., 2024
, and
Guo et al., 2025
showed that distinct decision-making strategies were associated with different response times, emphasizing the importance of accounting for strategy-switching and response times in decision-making models.

In this work, we propose an RL-HMM-DDM framework to enhance the modeling of reward-based decision-making dynamics by incorporating multiple strategies and response times. In the “engaged” state, subjects make decisions based on an RL-DDM method, while in the “lapsed” state, decisions are modeled using a DDM method with certain parameters fixed to approximate random guessing with equal probabilities. Our framework improves RL-DDM models by allowing strategy switching, making it more reflective of real-world decision-making. The proposed method is applied to the Probabilistic Reward Task
(
Pizzagalli et al., 2005
, PRT;)
within the Establishing Moderators and Biosignatures of Antidepressant Response for Clinical Care (EMBARC) study
(
Trivedi et al., 2016
)
, a large-scale randomized clinical trial recruiting participants diagnosed with MDD and healthy controls. Our goal is to analyze decision-making behavior, identify differences between MDD patients and healthy individuals, and examine associations with brain measures and clinical outcomes. Key variables derived from our framework, such as the group engagement rates, individual engagement scores, individual response time during engagement, and individual response time during lapses (to be defined in Section
2.4
), provide crucial behavioral insights. Group engagement rates highlight distinct decision-making patterns between MDD patients and healthy controls, while individual engagement scores and response time measures serve as potential behavioral markers that are associated with brain function and clinical outcomes. Understanding engagement and response time patterns can offer information into the cognitive processes underlying MDD, assisting researchers and clinicians refine treatment strategies to enhance patient engagement and improve outcomes. Note, our approach aims to infer and replicate observed behavior, aligning with inverse reinforcement learning methods
(
Ng and Russell, 2000
;
Abbeel and Ng, 2004
)
, behavioral cloning
(
Torabi et al., 2018
)
, and imitation learning
(
Ross and Bagnell, 2010
)
, but does not presume behavior is driven by an optimal reward-maximizing policy.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces general RL and DDM methods, outlines our proposed RL-HMM-DDM framework, and details the parameter estimation process. Section
3
presents simulation studies to evaluate the performance of the proposed method. In Section
4
, we demonstrate the application of the proposed methods to the EMBARC study. We conclude with a discussion in Section
5
and defer technical details to the Supplementary Material.

Methods

2.1
Objective and Data

Consider a sample of
n
subjects drawn from a population, each observed over
J
trials. For
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,J
, the dataset consists of
\{S_{i,j},A_{i,j},T_{i,j},R_{i,j}\}
, where
S_{i,j}
,
A_{i,j}
,
T_{i,j}
, and
R_{i,j}
represent the random variables corresponding to the state, action, response time, and reward, respectively. Throughout the paper, we use lowercase letters
s
,
a
,
t
, and
r
to denote the realizations of
S_{i,j}
,
A_{i,j}
,
T_{i,j}
, and
R_{i,j}
, respectively.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{i,j}=\{S_{i,k},A_{i,k},T_{i,k},R_{i,k}\}_{k=1}^{j-1}
represent the observed history for subject
i
up to trial
j
. We assume that the state
S_{i,j}
is confined to a bounded state space, denoted as
\mathcal{S}
, and is generated from an arbitrary non-trivial distribution of transitions
\Pr(S_{i,j}|\mathcal{H}_{i,j})
. In the special case of the contextual bandit, the states
S_{i,j}
are assumed to be generated independently, i.e.,
\Pr(S_{i,j}|\mathcal{H}_{i,j})=\Pr(S_{i,j})
. We consider that the action
A_{i,j}
is selected from a binary action space
\mathcal{A}
with
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
. The reward
R_{i,j}
follows a reward-generating distribution
\Pr_{j}(R_{i,j}|A_{i,j},S_{i,j},\mathcal{H}_{i,j})
, with the bounded reward space
\mathcal{R}\subset[0,R_{\max}]
. The reward-generating mechanisms may be either stochastic or deterministic. Our objective is to model human decision-making behavior driven by immediate rewards in behavioral experiments, where rewards are provided at the end of each trial. Accordingly, we do not consider any downstream effects of these rewards.

2.2
Reward Processing with Reinforcement Learning

In general, reward processing refers to how individuals perceive, evaluate, and seek rewards, such as money or social approval. Biological evidence supports the use of simple reward prediction error, comparing actual and expected reward, to update value functions associated with different decisions
(
Schultz et al., 1997
;
Waelti et al., 2001
;
O’Doherty et al., 2003
)
, laying the foundation for modeling reward processing utilizing reinforcement learning (RL). Let
Q_{i,j}(a,s)=E(R_{i,j}|A_{i,j}=a,S_{i,j}=s)
represent the expected reward or value of taking action
a
in state
s
for subject
i
at trial
j
. The reward prediction error for this trial is then given by
R_{i,j}-Q_{i,j}(a,s)
. Following the Rescorla-Wagner equation
(
Rescorla and Wagner, 1972
)
, each subject’s value evolves across trials and is updated based on a weighted reward prediction error as:

Q_{i,j+1}(a,s)=Q_{i,j}(a,s)+\beta\{R_{i,j}-Q_{i,j}(a,s)\},

which can also be expressed as a weighted combination of the expected reward and the actual reward from the previous trial:

Q_{i,j+1}(a,s)=(1-\beta)Q_{i,j}(a,s)+\beta R_{i,j},

where
\beta\in(0,1)
is the learning rate, quantifying the speed at which the value of a state-action pair is updated. A larger
\beta
indicates faster learning, where decisions are heavily influenced by rewards from recent trials. Conversely, a smaller
\beta
results in slower updates, leading to decisions that integrate information from a longer history of past trials.

2.3
Decision Making with Reinforcement Learning-Drift-Diffusion Model

In a standard RL, the binary action
A_{i,j}
is modeled as:

\Pr(A_{i,j}=1|S_{i,j},\mathcal{H}_{i,j})=\frac{1}{1+\exp{\{-\rho Z_{i,j}(S_{i,j})\}}},

(1)

where
\rho
represents reward sensitivity and
Z_{i,j}(S_{i,j})\triangleq Q_{i,j}(1,S_{i,j})-Q_{i,j}(0,S_{i,j})
is the contrast between the expected rewards for the two actions in state
S_{i,j}
. This formulation emphasizes that the decision-making probability depends on the reward contrast
Z_{i,j}
, but it does not fully capture the dynamic complexities of decision-making processes.

In contrast, the drift-diffusion model (DDM)
(
Ratcliff, 1978
)
conceptualizes decision-making as a continuous process of evidence accumulation. In this framework, the decision process begins at an initial state and proceeds until the accumulated evidence reaches one of two absorbing boundaries corresponding to the available choices
(
Ratcliff and McKoon, 2008
)
(see Figure
1(a)
).
Let
W_{i,j}(t)
denote the evidence state at time
t
for subject
i
during trial
j
. The process is bounded by two absorbing boundaries: a lower boundary at
0
(yielding
A_{i,j}=0
) and an upper boundary at
\alpha
(yielding
A_{i,j}=1
), where
\alpha>0
governs the speed-accuracy trade-off.
A larger value of
\alpha
indicates higher accuracy, as the decision-making process takes longer to reach a decision. The initial evidence state
z\in(0,\alpha)
reflects the initial bias, with the relative bias
b\triangleq z/\alpha
. A value of
b>0.5
indicates a bias toward the decision associated with
A_{i,j}=1
. The drift rate
v\in\mathbb{R}
characterizes the speed of evidence accumulation and is influenced by stimulus quality:
v\approx 0
suggests an ambiguous stimulus, while
v>0
or
v<0
implies evidence favoring
A_{i,j}=1
or
A_{i,j}=0
, respectively.

The DDM describes
W_{i,j}(t)
as a Wiener process with drift:

\frac{dW_{i,j}(t)}{dt}\sim\text{Normal}(v,\sigma^{2}),\text{ with }W_{i,j}(0)=z=b\alpha,

where the marginal distribution of
W_{i,j}(t)
is
\text{Normal}(vt+b\alpha,\sigma^{2}t)
. For simplicity,
\sigma
is typically set to one
(
Smith, 2000
;
Navarro and Fuss, 2009
;
Blurton et al., 2012
, e.g.,)
; to convert to a scale where the variance parameter is fixed at any value
\sigma^{2}
,
\alpha
and
v
are rescaled by multiplying them by
\sigma
. Decisions occur when
W_{i,j}(t)
reaches either absorbing boundary. The response time
T_{i,j}
, defined as the interval between state presentation and decision-making, comprises the decision time
T^{\text{D}}_{i,j}\triangleq\inf_{t}\{W_{i,j}(t)\leq 0
or
W_{i,j}(t)\geq\alpha\}
and a non-decision time
\tau
, which accounts for perceptual encoding and motor execution. The sub-densities
(
Horrocks and Thompson, 2004
)
, also known as the defective density
(
Ratcliff and Tuerlinckx, 2002
)
, of
T^{\text{D}}_{i,j}
for
A_{i,j}=0
and
A_{i,j}=1
are denoted by
f_{0}(t;\alpha,b,v)\triangleq f(T=t,A=0;\alpha,b,v)
and
f_{1}(t;\alpha,b,v)\triangleq f(T=t,A=1;\alpha,b,v)
, respectively (see the two densities visualized in Figure
1(a)
).
The two sub-densities are related as:

f_{1}(t;\alpha,b,v)=f_{0}(t;\alpha,1-b,-v).

The three-parameter sub-density
f_{0}(t;\alpha,b,v)
can be factorized into an one-parameter sub-density
f_{0}(t;1,b,0)
as:

f_{0}(t;\alpha,b,v)=\alpha^{-2}\exp(-\alpha bv-0.5v^{2}t)f_{0}(t;1,b,0),

where
f_{0}(t;1,b,0)
is often specified using either the large-time representation
(
Feller, 1968
, p.359)
or the small-time representation
(
Feller, 1968
, p.370)
. The large-time representation converges more rapidly for larger response times, while the small-time representation offers higher efficiency for shorter response times. The choice of representation and its truncation parameters depends on the desired degree of approximation accuracy. Decision rules provided by
Navarro and Fuss, 2009
,
Blurton et al., 2012
, and
Gondan et al., 2014
can be applied to select the appropriate representation. It is important to note that
f_{0}(t;\alpha,b,v)
and
f_{1}(t;\alpha,b,v)
are not probability density functions, as indicated in the name. However, the joint distribution function

f(t,a;\alpha,b,v)\triangleq\{f_{0}(t;\alpha,b,v)\}^{1-a}\{f_{1}(t;\alpha,b,v)\}^{a}

forms a valid probability density function.
Note that the previous discussion is a special case with a non-decision time of
\tau=0

(i.e., the joint density is
f(t,a;\alpha,b,v,\tau=0)
). To incorporate a non-decision time
\tau>0
,
we substitute
t-\tau
for
t
in the function
f(t,a;\alpha,b,v)
; that is,

f(t,a;\alpha,b,v,\tau)=f(t-\tau,a;\alpha,b,v,0)
, which yields the Wiener first-passage time (WFPT) distribution:

(T_{i,j},A_{i,j})\sim\text{WFPT}(\alpha,b,v,\tau).

(2)

The marginal density of
T_{i,j}
is obtained by summing over the possible values of
A_{i,j}
:

f(t;\alpha,b,v,\tau)=f(t,a=0;\alpha,b,v,\tau)+f(t,a=1;\alpha,b,v,\tau).

The probability of
A_{i,j}=1
under (
2
) is given by

\displaystyle\Pr(A_{i,j}=1;\alpha,b,v,\tau)=\begin{cases}\frac{\exp(-2vb\alpha)-1}{\exp(-2v\alpha)-1}&\text{if }v\neq 0,\\
b&\text{if }v=0.\end{cases}

(3)

To account for the negative association between
T_{i,j}
and
Z_{i,j}
observed in experiments
(
Frank et al., 2009
;
Krajbich et al., 2015
)
, an integrated framework, known as the reinforcement learning drift-diffusion model
(
Pedersen et al., 2017
, RL-DDM;)
, has been proposed, where the drift rate varies between subjects and trials as
v_{i,j}=cZ_{i,j}
, with
c
being a scaling parameter. This approach ensures that when the choice options are similar (and
Z_{i,j}
is small), the drift rate
v_{i,j}
is also small, thereby increasing the average time required to reach an absorbing boundary. Furthermore, when
b=0.5
(indicating that the RL-DDM is initially unbiased), (
3
) simplifies to:

\displaystyle\Pr(A_{i,j}=1;\alpha,0.5,cZ_{i,j},\tau)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\alpha cZ_{i,j})},

which is equivalent to (
1
) by setting
\rho=\alpha c

(
Miletić et al., 2020
)
.This equivalence reinterprets reward sensitivity
\rho
as being proportional to decision caution through
\alpha
, demonstrating that RL-DDM generalizes standard RL with softmax model by integrating response times into modeling. However, when
b\neq 0.5
, the equivalence between (
1
) and (
3
) no longer holds, but (
3
) still approximates (
1
).

2.4
Strategies Switching with Hidden Markov Model

The preceding development hinges on the assumption that individuals employ only one decision-making strategy, which is often violated in practice. In this paper, we consider a framework where individuals alternate between two decision-making strategies, as illustrated in Figure
1(b)
. Let
X_{i}
be a
p\times 1
vector of covariates for subject
i
, and let
U_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
denote a latent variable indicating the strategy used by subject
i
in trial
j
. When
U_{i,j}=1
, individuals employ an “engaged” strategy, making decisions based on an RL-DDM model. Conversely, when
U_{i,j}=0
, they adopt a “lapsed” strategy, where decisions are modeled using a DDM model with
v
and
b
fixed at 0 and 0.5, respectively. When
v=0
and
b=0.5
, (
3
) reduces to 0.5, reflecting the probability of random guessing for each action.

Within this behavioral decision-making framework, we assume that individuals’ decision-making strategies are independent of how they learn from the actions they take and the rewards they receive. Even during the “lapsed” phase, individuals internally update their expectations of rewards, suggesting the presence of a computational mechanism for reward processing, known as implicit learning
(
Frensch and Rünger, 2003
)
. Engagement, however, depends on various external and internal factors such as mood, emotions, and potentially depression status, which may vary significantly between trials.

By integrating the HMM and RL-DDM, we propose a novel RL-HMM-DDM framework to characterize perceptual decision-making behavior:

\displaystyle f(T_{i,j},A_{i,j}|U_{i,j},S_{i,j},\mathcal{H}_{i,j})=

\displaystyle\ I(U_{i,j}=0)f(T_{i,j},A_{i,j};\alpha_{0},0.5,0,\tau)

\displaystyle+I(U_{i,j}=1)f\{T_{i,j},A_{i,j};\alpha_{1},b,c\cdot Z_{i,j}(S_{i,j}),\tau\},

(4)

\displaystyle\Pr(U_{i,1}=k)=

\displaystyle\ \pi_{k},

(5)

\displaystyle\Pr(U_{i,j+1}=1|U_{i,j}=k)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{\exp{(\zeta_{k,0}+\zeta_{k,1}^{\top}X_{i})}}{1+\exp{(\zeta_{k,0}+\zeta_{k,1}^{\top}X_{i})}},\quad k=0,1,

(6)

where
f(T_{i,j},A_{i,j};\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the joint density of the WFPT distribution;
0<\pi_{0},\pi_{1}<1
with
\pi_{0}+\pi_{1}=1
; and
\zeta_{k,0}
and
\zeta_{k,0}
represent the intercepts and slopes for (
6
), respectively.

For individual
i
, let
\mathbf{A}_{i}=A_{i,[1:J]}=(A_{i,1},\ldots,A_{i,J})^{\top}
and
\mathbf{T}_{i}=T_{i,[1:J]}=(T_{i,1},\ldots,T_{i,J})^{\top}
denote the sequences of actions and response times, respectively.

We define the individual engagement probability for individual
i
at trial
j
(denoted by
\gamma_{i,j,1}
) and the group engagement rate at trial
j
(denoted by
\bar{\gamma}_{j,1}
) as

\displaystyle\gamma_{i,j,1}=\Pr(U_{i,j}=1|\mathbf{T}_{i},\mathbf{A}_{i})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\bar{\gamma}_{j,1}=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\gamma_{i,j,1}.

Here,
\gamma_{i,j,1}
can be interpreted as the posterior probability that
U_{i,j}=1
given the complete sequence of actions and response times for individual
i
, serving as a predictive measure of that individual’s engagement at trial
j
; while
\bar{\gamma}_{j,1}
quantifies the average engagement probability across all individuals at trial
j
.
We define the individual engagement score as

\frac{1}{J}\sum^{J}_{j=1}\log\left(\frac{\gamma_{i,j,1}}{1-\gamma_{i,j,1}}\right),

(7)

and the individual response time during engagement (
U_{i,j}=1
) and lapses (
U_{i,j}=0
) as

\frac{1}{\left\{\sum^{J}_{j=1}\mathbbm{1}(U_{i,j}=k)\right\}}\sum^{J}_{j=1}\mathbbm{1}(U_{i,j}=k)T_{i,j},\ k=0,1,

(8)

where
\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function. (
7
) represents the average logit-transformed individual engagement probability over
J
trials, while (
8
) quantifies the average response time during engagement and lapses.

Plug-in estimates for (
7
) and (
8
) are obtained by substituting the parameters with their estimates and replacing
U_{i,j}
with its predicted value.

2.5
Parameter Estimation via EM Algorithm

Let
\bm{\theta}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},b,c,\tau,\beta,\pi_{1},\zeta_{0,0},\zeta_{0,1}^{\top},\zeta_{1,0},

\zeta_{1,1}^{\top})^{\top}
denote the parameters of interest. The likelihood function for
\bm{\theta}
derived from the observed data can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\prod^{n}_{i=1}\left\{\prod^{J}_{j=1}f(T_{i,j},A_{i,j}|U_{i,j},S_{i,j},\mathcal{H}_{i,j})\Pr(S_{i,j}|\mathcal{H}_{i,j})\Pr(R_{i,j}|S_{i,j},A_{i,j},\mathcal{H}_{i,j})\right\}\times\left\{\Pr(U_{i,1})\prod^{J-1}_{j=1}\Pr(U_{i,j+1}|U_{i,j})\right\}.

Rather than focusing on the process by which rewards and states are generated, representing participants’ goals, we are interested in jointly modeling behaviors and decision times during human reward-based decision-making in behavioral tasks. Consequently, the parameters in the state-generating model
\Pr(S_{i,j}|\mathcal{H}_{i,j})
and the reward-generating model
\Pr(R_{i,j}|S_{i,j},A_{i,j},\mathcal{H}_{i,j})
are distinct from the parameter
\bm{\theta}
, allowing the log-likelihood function to be factorized into:

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left\{\log\Pr(U_{i,1})+\sum^{J-1}_{j=1}\log\Pr(U_{i,j+1}|U_{i,j})+\sum^{J}_{j=1}\log f(T_{i,j},A_{i,j}|U_{i,j},S_{i,j},\mathcal{H}_{i,j})\right\}

plus a term independent of
\bm{\theta}
.

We estimate
\bm{\theta}
using a computationally efficient generalized expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm
(
Dempster et al., 1977
)
. In the E-step, the forward-backward procedure
(
Baum et al., 1970
)
is employed to efficiently handle the challenge through recursion and memorization. This ensures that both the forward and backward passes require only a single traversal through all trials in a session. The main algorithm is outlined below, with derivation details deferred to the Supplementary Material.

E-step with the Forward-Backward Procedure

Define
\gamma_{i,j,k}=\gamma_{i,j,k}(\bm{\theta})=\Pr(U_{i,j}=k|\mathbf{T}_{i},\mathbf{A}_{i};\bm{\theta})
,
\xi_{i,j,k,l}=\xi_{i,j,k,l}(\bm{\theta})=\Pr(U_{i,j+1}=l,U_{i,j}=k|\mathbf{T}_{i},\mathbf{A}_{i};\bm{\theta})
,
\eta_{i,j,k}=\eta_{i,j,k}(\bm{\theta})=f(T_{i,j},A_{i,j}|U_{i,j}=k,S_{i,j},\mathcal{H}_{i,j};\bm{\theta})
, and
\varphi_{i,j,k,l}=\varphi_{i,j,k,l}(\bm{\theta})=\Pr(U_{i,j+1}=l|U_{i,j}=k;\bm{\theta})
. The expectation of
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\bm{\theta})
in terms of
P(U_{1:n}|T_{1:n},\mathcal{H}_{1:n};\bm{\theta})
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\left(\mathcal{L}_{n}(\bm{\theta})|T_{1:n},A_{1:n};\bm{\theta}^{\text{old}}\right)=\sum^{n}_{i=1}

\displaystyle\left[\sum^{1}_{k=0}\gamma_{i,1,k}\left(\bm{\theta}^{\text{old}}\right)\cdot\log\pi_{k}+\sum^{J-1}_{j=1}\sum^{1}_{k=0}\sum^{1}_{l=0}\xi_{i,j,k,l}\left(\bm{\theta}^{\text{old}}\right)\cdot\log\left\{\varphi_{i,j,k,l}\left(\bm{\theta}^{\text{old}}\right)\right\}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\sum^{J}_{j=1}\sum^{1}_{k=0}\gamma_{i,j,k}(\bm{\theta}^{\text{old}})\cdot\log\left\{\eta_{i,j,k}\left(\bm{\theta}^{\text{old}}\right)\right\}\right].

The forward and backward variables are computed using the Markov property. Let

\rho_{i,j,k}\triangleq\Pr\left(U_{i,j}=k,T_{i,[1:j]},A_{i,[1:j]}\right)
be the forward variable, initialized as
\rho_{i,1,k}=\pi_{k}\eta_{i,1,k}
. For
j=2,\ldots,J
,
\rho_{i,j,k}
is updated as:

\rho_{i,j,k}=\eta_{i,j,k}\sum^{1}_{l=0}\rho_{i,j-1,l}\varphi_{i,j-1,l,k}.

Similarly, let
\varpi_{i,j,k}\triangleq f\left(T_{i,[j+1:J]},A_{i,[j+1:J]}|\right.\\
\left.U_{i,j}=k,T_{i,[1:j]},A_{i,[1:j]}\right)
be the backward variable, initialized as
\varpi_{i,J,k}=1
. For
j=T-1,\ldots,1
,
\varpi_{i,j,k}
is updated as:
\varpi_{i,j,k}=\sum^{1}_{l=0}\varpi_{i,j+1,l}\eta_{i,j+1,l}\varphi_{i,j,k,l}.

Using these forward and backward variables, the posterior distributions for
U_{i,j}
and
U_{i,j+1}
are computed as

\gamma_{i,j,k}=\frac{\rho_{i,j,k}\varpi_{i,j,k}}{\sum^{1}_{l=0}\rho_{i,J,l}}\quad\text{and}\quad\xi_{i,j,k,l}=\frac{\rho_{i,j,k}\varpi_{i,j+1,l}\eta_{i,j+1,l}\varphi_{i,j,k,l}}{\sum^{1}_{l=0}\rho_{i,J,l}},

respectively. Let
\tilde{\eta}_{i,j,k}=f(T_{i,j},1-A_{i,j}|U_{i,j}=k,S_{i,j},\mathcal{H}_{i,j};\bm{\theta})
, the posterior distribution for
A_{i,j}
, given the response time
T_{i,j}
, decision-making strategy
U_{i,j}=k
, current state
S_{i,j}
, and observed history
\mathcal{H}_{i,j}
is calculated as

\Pr(A_{i,j}|T_{i,j},U_{i,j}=k,S_{i,j},\mathcal{H}_{i,j};\bm{\theta})=\frac{\eta_{i,j,k}}{\eta_{i,j,k}+\tilde{\eta}_{i,j,k}}.

(9)

This quantity can be used to evaluate how incorporating response time into the model improves the identification of subjects’ decisions.

M-step

The objective function is given by:

\mathcal{J}(\bm{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{U}\left(\mathcal{L}_{n}(\bm{\theta})|T_{1:n},A_{1:n};\bm{\theta}^{\text{old}}\right),

(10)

and it can be partitioned into components involving
\pi_{1}
,
\zeta_{k}\triangleq\left(\zeta_{k,0},\zeta_{k,1}\right)^{\top}
for
k=0,1
, and
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}\triangleq(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},b,c,\tau,\beta)^{\top}
. Each component is minimized separately. In the
(m+1)
-th iteration, the updates are as follows:

For
\pi_{1}
, the update is
\pi_{1}^{(m+1)}=n^{-1}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\gamma_{i,1,1}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
. For
\zeta_{k,0}
,
\zeta_{k,1}
with
k=0,1
, we define
\xi_{i,j,k,l}^{(m)}=\xi_{i,j,k,l}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
and
\varphi_{i,j,k,1}^{(m)}=\varphi_{i,j,k,1}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
and the objective (
10
) involving
\zeta_{k,0}
and
\zeta_{k,1}
becomes:

\mathcal{J}(\zeta_{k})=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{J-1}_{j=1}\left\{-\xi_{i,j,k,1}^{(m)}(\zeta_{k,0}+\zeta_{k,1}^{\top}X_{i})+\left(\xi_{i,j,k,0}^{(m)}+\xi_{i,j,k,1}^{(m)}\right)\log\{1+\exp(\zeta_{k,0}+\zeta_{k,1}^{\top}X_{i})\}\right\}.

which is optimized using the BFGS algorithm.

For
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
, where
\gamma_{i,j,k}^{(m)}\triangleq\gamma_{i,j,k}(\bm{\theta}^{(m)})
, the objective (
10
) involving
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}\right)=-\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{J}_{j=1}

\displaystyle\left(\gamma_{i,j,0}^{(m)}\log\{f(T_{i,j},A_{i,j};\alpha_{0},1/2,0,\tau)\}+\gamma_{i,j,1}^{(m)}\log[f\{T_{i,j},A_{i,j};\alpha_{1},b,c\cdot Z_{i,j}(S_{i,j}),\tau\}]\right).

which is also optimized using the BFGS algorithm. To satisfy the boundary constraints on
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
, the parameters
\alpha_{0}
,
\alpha_{1}
,
b
,
c
,
\tau
, and
\beta
are transformed to allow for optimization over the entire real line, and then transformed back after optimization.

To illustrate the proposed method, we analyzed the Establishing Moderators and Biosignatures of Antidepressant Response for Clinical Care

4.1
Probabilistic Reward Task in the EMBARC Study

The EMBARC study is a randomized clinical trial of major depressive disorder (MDD). As part of the study, participants completed the probabilistic reward task
(
Pizzagalli et al., 2005
, PRT;)
, a computer-based task designed to assess differences in reward learning abilities between patients with MDD and healthy control (CTL) participants, as well as their ability to adjust behavior in response to rewards. Each participant performed a baseline PRT session consisting of two blocks of
100
trials, separated by a
30
-second break. In each trial, participants were shown a cartoon face displaying one of two subtly different stimuli: a face with either a short or long mouth. Their task was to correctly identify the presented stimulus by pressing one of two buttons. The PRT is structured to reinforce reward-based learning by disproportionately rewarding correct responses for one stimulus over the other. Specifically, correct identifications of the short-mouth face (rich stimulus) were rewarded more frequently than correct identifications of the long-mouth face (lean stimulus). Participants were instructed that their goal was to maximize their total rewards and that not all correct responses would receive rewards. Critically, the difference in mouth length between the two stimuli was deliberately subtle, making accurate discrimination challenging. This perceptual difficulty often biases participants toward prioritizing the more frequently rewarded stimulus (rich stimulus), reflecting the natural tendency to adjust behavior based on reward contingencies rather than perceptual accuracy.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{0,1\}
, where
0
and
1
represent lean and rich stimulus, respectively; and let
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
, where

5
Discussions

In this paper, we propose a framework that integrates RL, HMM, and DDM to jointly model reward-based decision-making with response times. Our approach accounts for the switching of decision-making strategies between two states: an “engaged” state, where decisions follow an RL-DDM, and a “lapsed” state, where decisions are based on a simplified DDM, approximating random guessing. Extensive simulation studies demonstrate the robustness of our method across various reward-generating distributions, both with and without strategy switching. Applying our method to the EMBARC study, we find that MDD patients show lower overall engagement than healthy controls and take longer to make decisions when they do engage. The engagement score and response time during engagement are associated with several brain measures and clinical outcomes, but response time during lapses is not.

Our method extends the DDM by incorporating an RL component into the drift rate
v
of DDM in a linear fashion. However, it could be further extended in a nonlinear manner, and alternative RL-DDM formulations could be explored. For instance, integrating the RL component into the absorbing boundary. Another potential extension involves incorporating group-specific or subject-specific variations in RL-DDM parameters, such as the learning rate, absorbing boundary, initial bias, and non-decision time. Our methods are broadly applicable to analyzing behavioral tasks that include response time data. Furthermore, jointly modeling BP with other modalities, such as fMRI, presents a future direction for research.

While this paper focuses on binary actions, the framework can be extended to accommodate multiple actions using race models
(
Marley and Colonius, 1992
)
. These models conceptualize decision-making as a competition among parallel evidence-accumulation processes, with each option represented by a separate accumulator. The decision outcome is determined by the first accumulator to reach its threshold. Lastly, jointly model multiple behavioral tasks to distinguish between-subject associations and within-subject associations would be interesting.
