Retrospective study on the magnetic resonance imaging integration rate of acellular dermal matrix and complications after breast-conserving surgery plus radiotherapy
Authors: Eunju Shin, Soo Jung Hong, Hye Joung Eom, Tae-Kyung Yoo, Jisun Kim, Il Yong Chung, Beom Seok Ko, Hee Jeong Kim, Jong Won Lee, Byung Ho Son, Sae Byul Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast-conserving surgery (BCS) is increasingly preferred for early-stage breast cancer; however, conventional oncoplastic approaches may not adequately address large or irregular defects. Acellular dermal matrix (ADM), particularly paste-type formulations, offers a potential solution for volume replacement, though evidence regarding safety, integration, and imaging outcomes remains limited.MethodsThis retrospective review included 74 patients who underwent BCS with intraoperative paste-type ADM between June 2022 and August 2023. MRI and ultrasonography evaluated ADM integration and postoperative morphology, and complications were recorded. Stratified statistical analyses examined associations among imaging results, clinicopathologic characteristics, ADM volume, and outcomes.ResultsPaste-type ADM demonstrated safe integration with a low complication rate (4.1%) and no severe events such as infection or extrusion. MRI showed favorable integration grades (G0–G1) in over half of the patients. Ultrasonography identified three post-ADM morphologies: mass-like, cystic, and gap-type, which were associated with initial ADM volume and patient characteristics. No clinical or imaging variables predicted adverse outcomes, and minor complications were managed conservatively.ConclusionPaste-type ADM is a reliable, safe, and robust option for volume replacement in breast-conserving surgery, with favorable integration confirmed by MRI and ultrasonography. These findings support its broader use in oncoplastic breast surgery, improving reconstructive strategies and facilitating enhanced oncologic and aesthetic outcomes through multidisciplinary imaging surveillance.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women and has been extensively studied, resulting in continuous advancement in therapeutic strategies [1]. With improved neoadjuvant therapy, the extent of resection has decreased, and studies have shown no significant difference in oncologic outcomes between total mastectomy (TM) and breast-conserving surgery (BCS). Consequently, both patients and physicians have increasingly favored BCS [2–4]. Psychological considerations, including the desire to preserve the breast, and medical concerns related to challenges and complications of reconstruction have further supported the growing use of BCS [5–7].

Oncoplastic breast surgery (OBS) seeks to minimize contour deformities following BCS while ensuring oncologic safety and aesthetic outcomes [8–10]. However, conventional OBS techniques remain limited in managing substantial defects in some cases. To improve volume replacement, acellular dermal matrix (ADM) has been developed as a biomaterial, and its clinical use continues to expand. The combination of advanced surgical techniques, ADM, and imaging technology has transformed breast cancer surgery by reducing compromise between oncologic control and cosmetic satisfaction. This strategy enhances immediate surgical results and long-term quality of life for patients with breast cancer, representing major progress in patient-centered care [11–13].

ADM has traditionally been derived from bovine or human sources and is available in sheet, diced, and paste formulations. Sheet-type ADM is widely used for reconstruction after nipple-sparing mastectomy, supported by long-term safety and clinical recommendations [14–16]. However, it is less suitable for BCS due to anatomical and technical constraints, encouraging exploration of alternative types. Paste-type ADM has recently gained attention for BCS procedures [17, 18], yet studies specifically evaluating its efficacy, safety, and integration remain limited.

This study aims to assess the integration rate and complications associated with paste-type ADM in BCS, analyze correlations using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and describe ultrasonographic (US) findings after ADM application.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study included patients who underwent BCS with ADM at Asan Medical Center from June 2022 to August 2023. A total of 74 consecutive patients who underwent BCS with ADM application were identified from the institutional database. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) patients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent BCS for breast cancer; (2) intraoperative use of paste-type ADM (Reallo Putty®); and (3) availability of complete medical records and imaging. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) incomplete follow-up data; (2) immediate or delayed breast reconstruction; and (3) bilateral breast cancer requiring simultaneous surgery.

Surgical methods

BCS was performed in all patients. The incision location and length were determined by the operator based on tumor size and position. After confirming negative surgical margins and performing en-bloc resection, tissue rearrangement was conducted according to excised volume, remaining breast shape, and expected contour depression. ADM was applied to correct the defect prior to closure.

MRI technique

Patients underwent dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI using 3.0-T scanners (MAGNETOM Vida or Skyra, Siemens Medical Solutions; Ingenia, Philips) with a dedicated 16-channel breast coil. Imaging included a T2-weighted sequence (repetition time/echo time, 1,000/102 ms; field of view, 341 × 210 mm; matrix, 512 × 832; slice thickness, 1.5 mm) and a dynamic fat-suppressed axial three-dimensional T1-weighted sequence (TR/TE, 5.5/2.5; field of view, 360 × 360 mm; matrix, 384 × 384; slice thickness, 0.9 mm). One pre-contrast and five post-contrast acquisitions were obtained with 59-s temporal resolution. Gadolinium-based contrast material (UNIRAY, Dongkook Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) was intravenously administered at 0.2 mL/kg and a 2 mL/s flow rate (Spectris; Medrad, Pittsburgh, Penn).

MRI evaluation

A breast radiologist (H.J.E., 10 years of experience in breast imaging) retrospectively reviewed postoperative MRI. The radiologist was aware of ADM location. Fluid collection surrounding ADM was graded on a six-level scale using T2-weighted images. Relative excised volume and ADM amount were not evaluated. Fluid collection was graded as follows: Grade 1, no fluid collection; Grade 2, minimal fluid collection around or within ADM; Grade 3, fluid occupying < 25% of the parenchymal defect; Grade 4, fluid occupying 25–50% of the parenchymal defect; Grade 5, fluid occupying 50–70% of the parenchymal defect; and Grade 6, fluid occupying > 70% of the parenchymal defect.

Ultrasound assessment of ADM

All participants underwent standardized breast US examinations during postoperative follow-up. High-frequency linear transducers (10–15 MHz) were utilized to maximize spatial resolution and accurately characterize ADM within the breast parenchyma. Ultrasound imaging was performed at intervals—usually at 6 months, 12 months, or both thereafter—by experienced ultrasound sonographers.

Following BCS and ADM placement, the ultrasound assessment focused on ADM morphology and integration with breast tissue. Findings were classified into three types: mass-like (M), ADM appears as a well-defined, hypoechoic or mixed-echogenicity lesion resembling a solid mass; cystic (C), ADM displays fluid-filled or complex cystic features, indicating possible seroma or localized tissue response; and gap (G), ADM is observed as a hypoechoic linear defect or separation, corresponding to incomplete integration or postoperative remodeling. An experienced breast US specialist interpreted imaging while blinded to clinical outcomes and other patient characteristic.

Statistical analysis

Associations among MRI grades, US findings, and clinicopathologic variables were analyzed. Variables included age, neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy, hormone receptor status, HER2 expression, ADM volume (3 ml, 5 ml, or 8 ml), excised specimen weight, pathologic T and N staging, and postoperative complications. Categorical data were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Continuous variables were assessed using Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY).

Patients’ characteristics

Regarding hormone receptor status, 81.8% (n = 54) of tumors were estrogen receptor (ER) positive, and 72.7% (n = 48) were progesterone receptor (PR) positive. HER2 overexpression was found in 11.7% (n = 7). Pathologic T stages included pT0 in 29.6% (n = 21), pT1 in 38.0% (n = 27), pT2 in 29.6% (n = 21), and pT3 in 2.8% (n = 2). Node-negative disease (pN0) was present in 81.7% (n = 58).

ADM volume varied with defect size: 60.8% (n = 45) received 8 mL, 36.5% (n = 27) received 5 mL, and 2.7% (n = 2) received 3 mL. Most specimens (73.0%, n = 54) weighed 10–50 g, while 14.9% (n = 11) weighed < 10 g, 12.2% (n = 9) weighed > 50 g, and the average weight was 36.9 g.

Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered to 25.7% (n = 19), radiotherapy to 97.3% (n = 72), and hormonal therapy to 74.3% (n = 55), reflecting the high prevalence of hormone receptor positivity.

MRI findings and grades

MRI evaluation was performed after 1 year of surgery in 40 patients and demonstrated variable ADM integration and tissue response. MRI grading showed G0 in 20.0% (n = 8), G1 in 37.5% (n = 15), G2 in 22.5% (n = 9), G3 in 7.5% (n = 3), G4 in 5.0% (n = 2), and G5 in 7.5% (n = 3). Overall, 57.5% of patients had favorable findings (G0–G1), while 42.5% showed more prominent postoperative changes (G2–G5).

Patients were categorized into G0–G1 (favorable, n = 23) and G2–G5 (less favorable, n = 17) for comparison (Table 2). Among 64 patients evaluated with ultrasound, 29.7% (n = 19) demonstrated mass-like findings, 32.8% (n = 21) demonstrated cystic changes, and 37.5% (n = 24) exhibited gap-like patterns.

Complications

The overall complication rate was low at 4.1% (n = 3), and all events were minor and managed conservatively. These were limited to mild red breast syndrome as a skin reaction and resolved spontaneously without additional medical or surgical intervention. No major complications, including infection, significant seroma, or ADM extrusion, occurred.

When stratified by MRI grade, complications were reported in 4.3% (n = 1) of G0–G1 patients and none in the G2–G5 group (p = 1.000). Discussion

This retrospective study provides updated real-world evidence on the use of paste-type ADM as a volume complement strategy in BCS, emphasizing its safety, integration efficacy, and characteristic imaging findings. The overall complication rate was low, and no major adverse events occurred. More than half of the patients showed favorable ADM integration on MRI. These results support paste-type ADM as a reliable and safe approach for volume replacement in BCS and contribute to the increasing recognition of ADM as an important adjunct in oncoplastic breast surgery.

ADM has become a key biomaterial in reconstructive breast surgery due to its biological compatibility and versatility. Our findings demonstrate a low complication rate (4.1%), consistent with recent large cohort and multi-center studies, indicating that ADM is safe in BCS settings [19]. The absence of major complications such as infection, even with a high proportion of younger patients and varied tumor characteristics, underscores its broad applicability without significantly increasing postoperative risk.​

A recent population-based study confirmed similar outcomes, reporting that paste-type and other ADM forms are well tolerated, with most complications being minor and managed conservatively [20]. In a 3-year follow-up study using multilayer sheet-type ADM, breast deformity was observed in 11.9% of patients and seroma in approximately 4.8%; only one patient required ADM removal due to skin irregularity rather than infection or graft failure [12]. Systematic reviews show that ADM does not increase long-term oncologic risk or adverse events when compared to standard oncoplastic procedures, reinforcing its role as a safe adjunct to breast conservation [21].​ Practical considerations include selecting candidates with low comorbidity and avoiding skin incisions directly over the tumor site, instead placing them at a distance for improved outcomes. Ensuring adequate subcutaneous fat coverage has also produced favorable results. When postoperative complications such as red breast syndrome or seroma are suspected, early use of steroids or antihistamines has been beneficial [22].

Stable ADM integration and volume retention were demonstrated by MRI and ultrasonography, with favorable integration (G0–G1) in more than half of assessed cases. This aligns with MRI-based studies and volumetric analyses confirming that ADM maintains structural integrity and tissue support for at least 1–2 years postoperatively [23]. The different US morphologies (mass-like, cystic, and gap-like) correlate with initial ADM volume and may represent phases of tissue remodeling and scaffold resorption. Comparative studies describe similar morphologic evolution and emphasize recognizing these sonographic patterns to differentiate normal integration from pathology [24]. Regarding integration, a study analyzing histologic samples obtained during reoperation for expander or implant exchange demonstrated fibrotic ingrowth within the grafted region, representing fibroblast and cellular infiltration. These findings provide definitive histologic evidence of successful ADM integration [25, 26].​

MRI and high-resolution ultrasound remain essential tools for postoperative surveillance, allowing differentiation between normal ADM remodeling and recurrent disease or complications. Recognizing benign post-ADM findings, such as fluid collections or mass-like appearances, is critical to prevent unnecessary intervention. Recent 2025 studies describe an MRI grading schema for ADM and support tailored radiologic follow-up, particularly in younger or higher-risk populations, consistent with our approach [11]. Updated systematic reviews continue to demonstrate the reliability of both modalities for early detection of complications [12, 14].​ Recent studies describing various imaging characteristics of sheet-type and pellet-type ADM using mammography (MMG), US, and MRI report that ADM typically appears as a mass-like structure on MMG, shows posterior shadowing on US, and lacks enhancement on MRI [24]. Additionally, sheet-type ADM is more likely to develop calcifications, whereas pellet-type ADM tends to show irregular margins. A false-positive rate for cancer of 8.4% was observed, primarily in the sheet-type group, while specificity was higher for pellet-type ADM. These findings suggest that selecting an ADM formulation suited to the BCS environment may lead to better outcomes, and knowledge of ADM history is essential for accurate imaging interpretation.

Although this study supports the expanded use of ADM in BCS, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design, variability in follow-up imaging intervals, and single-institution scope may restrict generalizability. Therefore, multicenter trials and prospective studies with standardized imaging protocols are necessary to further define ADM safety and surveillance strategies. As the field advances, comparative effectiveness data and patient-reported outcomes, supported by long-term follow-up, will be increasingly important.​

A principal strength of this study is its focused evaluation of paste-type ADM, a relatively recent reconstructive option. Prior studies and reviews, including large-scale cohort analyses, have predominantly addressed sheet- or pellet-type ADM, leaving paste-type formulations insufficiently characterized. To our knowledge, this study is the first to prospectively and systematically assess both safety profiles and stepwise imaging integration of paste-type ADM in a consecutive BCS cohort, providing real-world evidence and a reproducible evaluation protocol for future research. Additionally, the combined use of MRI and ultrasound for assessing ADM integration and complications ensures comprehensive surveillance and broadens the clinical understanding of ADM application.

Conclusion

Paste-type ADM demonstrated favorable integration, consistent imaging findings, and minimal complications in this cohort of patients with BCS. Its safety profile and versatility support continued adoption as a reconstructive complement in BCS, enhancing achievement of oncologic and aesthetic goals.
