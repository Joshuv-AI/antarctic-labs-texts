Rigorously Characterizing Dynamics with Machine Learning
Marcio Gameiro 1 , Brittany Gelb 1 , and Konstantin Mischaikow 1

Abstract
. The identification of dynamics from time series data is a problem of general interest. It is well established that dynamics on the level of invariant sets, the primary objects of interest in the classical theory of dynamical systems, is not computable. We recall a coarser characterization of dynamics based on order theory and algebraic topology and prove that this characterization can be identified using approximations that can be realized by feedforward neural networks.



1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to present a framework that using machine learning allows for the rigorous characterization of the nonlinear dynamics generated by iterates of a continuous function
f
defined on a compact subset
X
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
To focus the discussion we present three theorems and discuss their relevance.

The first theorem is a classical result from machine learning, the universal approximation theorem
[
16
,
22
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider a compact set
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and assume that
f\colon X\to X
.
Given
\rho>0
there exists a fully-connected feedforward neural network
G\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\sup_{x\in X}\|f(x)-G(x)\|<\rho
.

The premise of this paper relies on the existence of the function
G
from Theorem
1.1
.
To simplify the analysis we add the restriction that the activation functions are non-constant and piecewise linear (the results of
[
22
]
guarantee that Theorem
1.1
still apply), which allows us to work with approximating functions of the following form.

Theorem 1.2

(Grigsby and Lindsey
[
12
]
)
.

Let
G\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a fully-connected feedforward neural network with piecewise linear activation.
Then
G
is affine-linear on the cells of a realization of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as a polyhedral complex
{\mathcal{P}}
.

Our strategy is to use
G
to characterize the dynamics of
f
.
The classical theory of dynamical systems focuses on understanding the existence and structure of
invariant sets
, i.e., sets of the form
S\subset X
such that
f(S)=S
.
This leads to a rich mathematical theory but, as we argue below, a theory that is too rich from the perspective of deducing dynamics from data.

With regard to the goals of this paper, the fundamental question is: is there a
\rho
such that
G
(satisfying the conditions of Theorem
1.1
) generates the same dynamics as
f
?
In the classical setting this is equivalent to asking: can we guarantee that
f
and
G
are
conjugate
, i.e., does there exist a homeomorphism
h\colon X\to X
such that

f\circ h=h\circ G?

The work of
[
9
]
proves that in general this is not possible via computations.
More precisely, regarding their work the authors state

“This can be interpreted as saying that there is no method or protocol that involves a countable amount of information and countable number of steps that reliably distinguishes between nonisomorphic ergodic measure preserving transformations. We view this as a rigorous way of saying that the classification problem for ergodic measure preserving transformations is intractable.”

An alternative approach is to focus on individual invariant sets.
However, as shown by the following example, even this is challenging.

Returning to the goal of this paper, we are allowed to assume that the map we want to learn the dynamics of is
f_{1}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, but we are not allowed to assume that we know the functional form of
f_{1}
.
We are also allowed to assume that we have a finite (but arbitrarily large) set of data

\left\{{(x_{k},f_{1}(x_{k}))\mid x_{k}\in[-2,2]}\right\}

that can be used to train
G\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and that as a result of this training,
\sup_{x\in[-2,2]}|f_{1}(x)-G(x)|<\rho
.
Observe that even for arbitrarily small
\rho>0
, we cannot guarantee that
G
has exactly two fixed points, and therefore we cannot guarantee that we can learn the number of fixed points of
f_{1}
from

.

While Example
1.3
may appear to be too trivial to be of interest, it is indicative of a more serious challenge.
Consider a continuous parameterized family of maps
f\colon X\times[0,1]^{n}\to X
, where for each
\lambda\in[0,1]^{n}
,
f_{\lambda}:=f(\cdot,\lambda)
.
A parameter value
\lambda_{0}
is a
bifurcation point
if for any neighborhood
U
of
\lambda_{0}
there exists
\lambda\in U
such that
f_{\lambda}
is not conjugate to
f_{\lambda_{0}}
.
In particular,
\theta=1
is a bifurcation point in Example
1.3
.
As is shown in
[
33
,
34
]
if
d\geq 2
(recall that
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
), then there are parameterized families of maps for which the bifurcation points contain a Cantor set of positive measure.
As a consequence, in general it is not possible to identify the dynamics of
f
via an approximation provided by Theorem
1.1
.

We avoid the above mentioned challenges by employing a coarser framework for dynamics that we believe to be sufficiently refined for many applications.
Since we do not expect that the typical reader is familiar with this framework and its associated machinery, a significant portion of this paper is spent introducing the necessary definitions and concepts.
In particular, in Section
2
we provide precise definitions of the terms used in the following theorem, which is our main result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a
finite union of closed, bounded, convex
d
-dimensional polytopes.
Let
f
be a continuous function such that
f(X)\subset\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(X)
where
\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits
denotes interior.
Let
({\mathsf{MR}},\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}})
be a Morse representation of
f
.
For each Morse set
M\in{\mathsf{MR}}
, let
{\mathsf{Con}}_{*}(M)
denote the homology Conley index of
M
under the dynamics induced by
f
.

Let
G\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a fully-connected feedforward neural network with piecewise linear activation such that
\sup_{x\in X}\|f(x)-G(x)\|<\rho
.
If
\rho>0
is sufficiently small, then using
G
we can

(i)

Compute a Morse graph
({\mathsf{MG}},\leq_{\mathsf{MG}})
for which there exists a poset epimorphism,

\nu\colon{\mathsf{MG}}\to{\mathsf{MR}},

and

(ii)

Use
\nu
to determine
\mathop{\mathrm{Con}_{*}}\nolimits(M)
for each
M\in{\mathsf{MR}}
.

An imprecise statement of Theorem
1.4
is as follows.
Choose a Morse representation
{\mathsf{MR}}
(see Definition
2.4
) for the function
f
.
In general
f
has many (possibly countably infinitely many) Morse representations, thus the choice of
{\mathsf{MR}}
can be interpreted as a choice of resolution of the dynamics of
f
that is of interest.
The theorem implies that we can employ a neural network
G
to (i) Organize the global dynamics of
f
in terms of its gradient-like behavior, and (ii) Compute homological invariants (Conley indices) from which we can obtain rigorous statements concerning the dynamics of
f
.

To provide slightly more detail, a Morse graph (see Definition
3.2
) is a partially ordered set (poset) whose elements identify potential recurrent dynamics and whose order structure indicates the asymptotic direction of nonrecurrent dynamics.
The Conley index
{\mathsf{Con}}_{*}
(see Definition
2.9
) is a computable
[
31
]
algebraic topological extension of the Morse index that we employ to identify nonlinear dynamics.
Most fundamentally, a non-trivial Conley index implies the existence of a non-empty invariant set, however the converse is
not
true.
The Conley index can also be used to prove the existence of equilibria
[
39
,
24
]
, periodic orbits
[
27
]
, heteroclinic orbits
[
4
]
, chaotic dynamics
[
29
,
42
,
8
,
7
]
, and semi-conjugacies onto nontrivial dynamics
[
28
,
26
,
25
]
.
Thus, the framework being considered in this paper is capable of identifying many of the building blocks of traditional nonlinear dynamics.

Remark 1.5
.

Theorem
1.4
guarantees that given the constraint that
\rho
is sufficiently small there is a constructive method for identifying the desired dynamics.
This may appear to be a weak result since no quantitative bounds are provided for
\rho
.
However, the complexity of the geometry of attractors and the boundaries of their domains of attraction can be extreme
[
21
]
, and this geometry plays an intrinsic role in the necessary value of
\rho
.
Given the level of generality at which we are working (the only essential assumption is that
f
is continuous) we see no path forward, based on finite data, to provide a sharp quantification of potential values of
\rho
.

Example 1.6
.

We use the family of maps introduced in Example
1.3
to provide intuition about the content of Theorem
1.4
.
We leave it to the reader to check that
f_{1}([-2,2])=[0,1]\subset\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits([-2,2])
.
Consider (using the language of Theorem
1.4
)
a Morse representation
({\mathsf{MR}},\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f_{1}}})
for
f_{1}
whose elements, called Morse sets, are the fixed points (invariant sets)
x=0
and
x=1
, and for which the partial order is
0\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f_{1}}}1
.
The partial order captures the fact that if
x\in(0,1)
then
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{1}^{n}(x)=0
and
\lim_{n\to-\infty}f_{1}^{n}(x)=1
.
The Morse sets identify the recurrent dynamics of
f_{1}
and the partial order characterizes the non-recurrent (gradient-like) dynamics of
f_{1}
.
The Conley index of
0
is that of an attracting fixed point, while the Conley index of
1
is trivial.

Theorem
1.4
states that if
\rho>0
is sufficiently small and
\sup_{x\in[-2,2]}\|f(x)-G(x)\|<\rho
, then we can use
G
to recover essential information about the dynamics of
f_{1}
.

As discussed above, in general, invariant sets (Morse sets) are not computable, and therefore, Morse representations are not computable.
What are computable are Morse graphs (see Definition
3.2
).
Theorem
1.4
(i) provides the link between the computable Morse graph and the desired Morse representation.
The computation of the Morse graph
{\mathsf{MG}}
is based on Theorem
1.2
that provides a finite polyhedral decomposition of phase space and the ability to easily bound the images of these polytopes under
G
.
For this example the polytopes are intervals.
This allows us to produce a combinatorial representation of the dynamics in the form of a directed graph
{\mathcal{G}}
(see Sections
3
and
4
) where the vertices correspond to the above mentioned intervals and the directed edges indicate how intervals are mapped amongst each other.
The nodes of the associated Morse graph capture recurrence within the directed graph and the order relation provides information about the existence of nonrecurrent paths.

To gain intuition as to why a combinatorial representation is sufficient, assume (this is not true in general) that the approximation
G
equals
f_{\theta}
for some
\theta\approx 1
.
If
G=f_{1}
, then there is no surprise that we can capture the dynamics of
f_{1}
.

So, assume that
G=f_{\theta}
for
\theta<1
.
By assumption
\sup_{x\in[-2,2]}\|f(x)-G(x)\|<\rho
.
Let
I_{0}
denote the interval that contains
0
.
Then
G(I_{0})\cap I_{0}\neq\emptyset
.
Thus, under
G
the interval
I_{0}
that contains
0
clearly maps to itself, and hence the directed graph
{\mathcal{G}}
contains the self edge
I_{0}\to I_{0}
.
Let
I_{1}
denote the interval that contains
1
.
Similarly, (since
\rho
is assumed small or equivalently
\theta\approx 1
) the above mentioned bounds implies that
G(I_{1})\cap I_{1}\neq\emptyset
and hence
{\mathcal{G}}
contains the self edge
I_{1}\to I_{1}
.
Ideally, intervals between
I_{0}
and
I_{1}
are mapped strictly to the left, in which case the Morse graph
{\mathsf{MG}}
has the form
I_{1}\rightarrow I_{0}
.
The poset morphism
\nu\colon{\mathsf{MG}}\to{\mathsf{MR}}
has the form
\nu(I_{0})=0
and
\nu(I_{1})=1
, and in this idealized case
\nu
is an isomorphism.

As described in Section
4.2
,
{\mathcal{G}}
can be used to compute Conley indices and furthermore these Conley indices are valid for the continuous dynamics generated by
G
.
The Conley index associated with
I_{0}
is that of an attracting fixed point.
The Conley index associated with
I_{1}
is trivial; it must be since under
G
the invariant set associated with
I_{1}
is the empty set.
The fact that
\nu
is a poset morphism allows us to conclude that the Conley indices for the fixed points
0
and
1
of
f_{1}
are that of an attracting fixed point and trivial, respectively.
Referring again to
[
39
,
24
]
, knowledge that the Conley index of
\nu(I_{0})
is that of a fixed point allows us to conclude that the invariant set associated with
\nu(I_{0})
contains a fixed point.
As emphasized above the fact that the Conley index of
\nu(I_{1})
is trivial does not imply that the invariant set associated with
\nu(I_{1})
is empty.
The Morse graph
{\mathsf{MG}}
identified
I_{1}
and the claim that
\nu
is an epimorphism implies that
\nu(I_{1})
is a non-empty invariant set, but suggests that it may not be robust with respect to perturbations.

For a variety of reasons
\nu
need not be an isomorphism (these reasons are what make the proof of Theorem
1.4
challenging).
Again, with the goal of gaining intuition, assume that
G=f_{\theta}
for
\theta>1
.
The explicit form of
G
(see Figure

(c)) and our assumption that
G
satisfies Theorem
1.2
allows us to deduce that there are distinct intervals
I_{0}
, containing
0
,
I_{1}

1
, and
I_{\theta}

\theta
, on which
G
is affine-linear.
The combinatorial representation of the dynamics of
G
must contain the self edges
I_{0}\to I_{0}
,
I_{1}\to I_{1}
, and
I_{\theta}\to I_{\theta}
.

With regard to additional edges one possibility is that the above mentioned bounds force the existence of
I_{1}\to I_{\theta}
and
I_{\theta}\to I_{1}
.
This implies that there is recurrence between
I_{1}
and
I_{\theta}
, and thus they are identified as a single node in the Morse graph

.
Again, assuming an ideal setting this leads to

being an isomorphism as above.

However, an alternative option is that

, but there is no edge from

to

.
In this case the simplest Morse graph consists of nodes

In this case

, and

Observe that in this case we have learned dynamics that is more complicated than that expressed by

, however the existence of

guarantees that we can recover

, the chosen Morse representation of

.

As indicated above,
{\mathcal{G}}
can be used to compute Conley indices.
For this case the Conley indices of

and

are that of a stable fixed point, while the Conley index of

is that of a fixed point with an unstable manifold of dimension one.
However,

also allows us to determine that under

and

Remark 1.7
.

We remind the reader that Theorem
1.4
demonstrates that using the coarser framework of dynamics of this paper, i.e. a Morse representation, dynamics generated by a continuous function can be recovered by a fully-connected feedforward neural network
G
.
In the context of applications, we do not know
f
and hence cannot know a Morse representation
({\mathsf{MR}},\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}})
.
In practice,
G
is learned from data,
{\mathcal{G}}
is constructed, and then conclusions are drawn about the dynamics of
f
.
We return to this point in Section
6
.

An outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce Conley theory in the context of continuous maps and describe how dynamics can be characterized in this framework. To demonstrate the generalizability of this framework, we prove Theorem
2.25
, which loosely states that
for any continuous function
g
sufficiently close to a continuous function
f
, the characterization of dynamics of
f
also characterizes the dynamics of
g
. Section
3
contains an introduction to combinatorial dynamics and culminates by describing how a Morse graph is computed. In Section
4
, we connect this perspective back to continuous dynamics and introduce the piecewise linear approximations that are the focus of this work. In Section
5
, we prove the main result of the paper, Theorem
1.4
. Section
6
contains concluding remarks about future work and how the ideas of this paper may be used in practice.

2.
Nonlinear Dynamics

This section provides an introduction to Conley theory in the context of dynamics generated by continuous functions.

2.1.
Continuous Dynamics

We consider continuous functions
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and restrict our attention to the dynamics on a
finite union of closed, bounded, convex
d
-dimensional polytopes
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for which
f(X)\subset\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(X)
.
However, the discussion of this section applies to continuous maps on compact metric spaces
[
4
,
30
]
.

We do not assume that
f
is invertible, and therefore, some care must be taken to discuss trajectories of
f
.
In particular, given
x\in X
,
\gamma_{x}\colon{\mathbb{Z}}\to X
is a
full trajectory through

x
if
\gamma_{x}(0)=x
and
\gamma_{x}(n+1)=f(\gamma_{x}(n))
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
As indicated in the introduction,
S\subset X
is an invariant set of
f
if
f(S)=S
. This is equivalent to requiring that for every
x\in S
, there exists a full trajectory
\gamma_{x}\colon{\mathbb{Z}}\to S
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
N\subset X
.
The
maximal invariant set in
N
under
f
is

\mathop{\mathrm{Inv}}\nolimits(N,f):=\left\{{x\in N\mid\exists\text{ a full trajectory }\gamma_{x}:\mathbb{Z}\to N}\right\}.

Definition 2.2
.

An invariant set
A\subset X
is an
attractor
if there exists a compact set
N\subset X
such that

(1)

A=\omega(N,f):=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}\mathop{\mathrm{cl}}\nolimits\left(\bigcup_{k=n}^{\infty}f^{k}(N)\right)\subset\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(N)

where
\mathop{\mathrm{cl}}\nolimits
and
\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits
respectively denote closure and interior.

The set of all attractors of
f
is denoted by
{\mathsf{Att}}(f)
and forms a bounded distributive lattice under the operations
\vee=\cup
and
A_{1}\wedge A_{2}=\omega(A_{1}\cap A_{2})

[
18
]
(for basic definitions associated with lattices see Section
7
).
The minimal element is
{\mathbf{0}}=\emptyset
and the maximal element is
{\mathbf{1}}=\omega(X,f)
.
Given
A\in{\mathsf{Att}}(f)
, the
dual repeller
to
A
is defined to be
A^{*}:=\{x\in{\mathbf{1}}\mid\omega(x,f)\cap A=\emptyset\}
.

Example 2.3
.

Recall
f_{\theta}
defined in Example
1.3
.
We leave it to the reader to check that

{\mathbf{1}}_{\theta}:=\omega([-2,2],f_{\theta})=\begin{cases}\left\{{0}\right\}&\text{if $\theta<1$}\\
[0,\theta]&\text{if $1\leq\theta$.}\end{cases}

The dynamics of
f_{\theta}
is simple, so
{\mathsf{Att}}(f_{\theta})
is finite.
In particular,

{\mathsf{Att}}(f_{\theta})=\begin{cases}\left\{{\emptyset,{\mathbf{1}}_{\theta}}\right\}&\text{if $\theta<1$,}\\
\left\{{\emptyset,\left\{{0}\right\},{\mathbf{1}}_{1}}\right\}&\text{if $\theta=1$,}\\
\left\{{\emptyset,\left\{{0}\right\},\left\{{\theta}\right\},\left\{{0,\theta}\right\},{\mathbf{1}}_{\theta}}\right\}&\text{if $1<\theta$.}\end{cases}

Attractors are invariant sets, and therefore, it is not surprising that
{\mathsf{Att}}(f_{\theta})
changes abruptly as a function of
\theta
.

Conley’s decomposition theorem
[
4
,
37
]
states that

(2)

CR(f)=\bigcap_{A\in{\mathsf{Att}}(f)}A\cup A^{*}

is an invariant set, called the
chain recurrent set
.
Furthermore, all recurrent dynamics of
f
occurs in
CR(f)
and
f
exhibits gradient-like dynamics in the complement of
CR(f)
.

In general
{\mathsf{Att}}(f)
may be countably infinite
[
4
]
.
With this in mind, we restrict our attention to finite sublattices of
{\mathsf{Att}}(f)
containing both
{\mathbf{0}}
and
{\mathbf{1}}
.
We leave it to the reader to check that if
{\mathsf{A}}
is such a finite sublattice, then
CR(f)\subset\bigcap_{A\in{\mathsf{A}}}A\cup A^{*}
.

Definition 2.4
.

A finite sublattice
{\mathsf{A}}
gives rise to a
Morse representation

(3)

({\mathsf{MR}}({\mathsf{A}}),\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}}):=\{A\cap(A^{<})^{*}\mid A\in{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{A}})\}

where
{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{A}})
denotes the join-irreducible elements of
{\mathsf{A}}
and
A^{<}
denotes the immediate predecessor of
A\in{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{A}})

[
20
]
.
The elements of
{\mathsf{MR}}({\mathsf{A}})
, called
Morse sets
, are nonempty, pairwise disjoint invariant sets.

Observe that
({\mathsf{MR}}({\mathsf{A}}),\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}})
is finite since
{\mathsf{A}}
is finite.
The partial order
\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}}
is defined via the minimal transitive extension of the following relation.
Given distinct Morse sets
M,M^{\prime}\in{\mathsf{MR}}({\mathsf{A}})
, set
M<_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}}M^{\prime}
if there exists
x\in X
such that
\omega(x,f)\subset M
and there exists a full trajectory
\gamma_{x}\colon{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbf{1}}
such that
\bigcap_{n>0}\mathop{\mathrm{cl}}\nolimits(\gamma_{x}(-\infty,-n])\subset M^{\prime}
.
Observe that the partial order
\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}}
provides explicit information about the gradient-like structure of the dynamics; the dynamics moves from higher-ordered Morse sets to lower-ordered Morse sets.

Remark 2.5
.

As presented above, a finite sublattice of
{\mathsf{Att}}(f)
gives rise to a well defined Morse representation.
The converse is also true, i.e., by
[
20
, Theorem 6]
given a Morse representation
{\mathsf{MR}}
of
f
there is a unique finite lattice of attractors
{\mathsf{A}}
such that (
3
) holds.
In particular, (
3
) gives rise to a poset isomorphism defined by

\displaystyle\psi\colon{\mathsf{MR}}

\displaystyle\to{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{A}})

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\mapsto A

for
M=A\cap(A^{<})^{*}
.

Example 2.6
.

A given dynamical system can have multiple Morse representations.
The lattice of attractors
{\mathsf{Att}}(f_{\theta})
gives rise to the following Morse representation:

{\mathsf{MR}}({\mathsf{Att}}(f_{\theta}),\leq_{f_{\theta}})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\theta<1$}\\
(\left\{{0,1}\right\},0<_{f_{\theta}}1)&\text{if $\theta=1$}\\
(\left\{{0,1,\theta}\right\},0<_{f_{\theta}}1>_{\theta}\theta)&\text{if $\theta>1$.}\end{cases}

An alternative lattice of attractors for
\theta\geq 1
is

{\mathsf{A}}_{\theta}:=\left\{{0,[0,\theta]}\right\}

in which case

{\mathsf{MR}}({\mathsf{A}}_{\theta},\leq_{f_{\theta}})=(\left\{{0,[1,\theta]}\right\},0<_{f_{\theta}}[1,\theta])
.
Both Morse representations provide a decomposition of the dynamics of
f_{\theta}
.
The existence of the order preserving epimorphism
\mu\colon{\mathsf{MR}}({\mathsf{Att}}(f_{\theta}))\to{\mathsf{MR}}({\mathsf{A}}_{\theta})
defined by
\mu(\left\{{0}\right\})=\left\{{0}\right\}
and
\mu(\left\{{1}\right\})=\mu(\left\{{\theta}\right\})=[1,\theta]
indicates that the decomposition provided by
{\mathsf{MR}}({\mathsf{Att}}(f_{\theta}))
is finer than that of
{\mathsf{MR}}({\mathsf{A}}_{\theta})
.
In this sense the choice of Morse representation can be viewed as a choice of the scale at which one wants to understand the dynamics.

As Examples

demonstrates, Morse representations need not be stable with respect to perturbations, and thus, cannot be expected to be computable in general.

The partial order associated with a Morse representation provides a characterization of the structure of the non-recurrent dynamics.
To identify finer structures we make use of the Conley index that we introduce via an extremely terse series of definitions.
For more detailed discussion we refer the reader to
[

,

,

Definition 2.7
.

A subset
N\subset X
is an
isolating neighborhood
if
\mathop{\mathrm{Inv}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathrm{cl}}\nolimits(N),f)\subset\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(N)
.
An invariant set
S
is
isolated
if there exists an isolating neighborhood
N
such that
S=\mathop{\mathrm{Inv}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathrm{cl}}\nolimits(N),f)
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
S
be an isolated invariant set of
f
.
Consider a pair
P=(P_{1},P_{0})
of compact sets satisfying
P_{0}\subset P_{1}\subset X
with the property that
P_{1}\setminus P_{0}
is an isolating neighborhood for
S
.
Let
(P_{1}/P_{0},[P_{0}])
denoted the pointed quotient space in which
P_{0}
is collapsed to a point.
Define
f_{P}\colon(P_{1}/P_{0},[P_{0}])\to(P_{1}/P_{0},[P_{0}])
by

f_{P}(x):=\begin{cases}f(x)&\text{if $x,f(x)\in P_{1}\setminus P_{0}$}\\
[P_{0}]&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

If
f_{P}
is continuous, then
P
is called an
index pair
and
f_{P}
is called an
index map
for
S
.

By definition if
P=(P_{1},P_{0})
is an index pair, then
f_{P}
is continuous, and thus,
f_{P}
induces a map on homology denoted by
f_{P*}
.

Definition 2.9
.

The
(homology) Conley index

{\mathsf{Con}}_{*}(S)
of an isolated invariant set
S
is the shift equivalence class of

f_{P*}\colon H_{*}(P_{1}/P_{0},[P_{0}])\to H_{*}(P_{1}/P_{0},[P_{0}])

where
P=(P_{1},P_{0})
is an index pair for
S
.

For the definition of shift equivalence, the reader is referred to
[
23
,
2
,
10
,
31
]
.
Over fields, the homology Conley index can be characterized in a more simple way: it is determined by the rational canonical form of
f_{P*}
, excluding nilpotent blocks
[
31
]
. See
[
23
, 7.3–7.5]
for an explanation of this characterization.
Examples of computing the homology Conley index using rational canonical form are contained in
[
3
]
and use an efficient algorithm due to Storjohann
[
40
]
.
See also
[
31
]
for a discussion of the limitations of using field coefficients.
For the purposes of this paper, it is sufficient to know that in the context of the homology Conley index, shift equivalence is computable
[
31
]
.

The Conley index of
S
is
trivial
, denoted by
{\mathsf{Con}}_{*}(S)=0
, if
f_{P*}
is nilpotent, in which case it is shift equivalent to the zero map.
Rephrasing what is stated in the introduction, if
{\mathsf{Con}}_{*}(S)\neq 0
, then
S\neq\emptyset
.

Definition 2.10
.

A compact set
N\subset X
is an
attracting block
for

Let

denote the set of all attracting blocks for

The following proposition follows from the work of

We provide an elementary proof to make this paper more self-contained.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
X
be a regular compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Let
f
be a continuous function such that
f(X)\subset\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(X)
.
Then, the set
{\mathsf{ABlock}}(X,f)
is a bounded, distributive lattice with respect to
\vee=\cup
and
\wedge=\cap
.
The neutral elements of
{\mathsf{ABlock}}(X,f)
are
{\mathbf{1}}_{b}=X
and
{\mathbf{0}}_{b}=\varnothing
.

Proof.

Let
U_{1},U_{2}\in{\mathsf{ABlock}}(X,f)
. By definition,
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are compact. Since
X
is a metric space,
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
and
U_{2}\cup U_{2}
are compact. Furthermore,

\displaystyle f(U_{1}\cup U_{2})

\displaystyle=f(U_{1})\cup f(U_{2})

\displaystyle\subset\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(U_{1})\cup\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(U_{2})

\displaystyle\subset\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(U_{1}\cup U_{2}),

which proves that
U_{1}\cup U_{2}\in{\mathsf{ABlock}}(X,f)
.

Also,

\displaystyle f(U_{1}\cap U_{2})

\displaystyle\subset f(U_{1})\cap f(U_{2})

\displaystyle\subset\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(U_{1})\cap\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(U_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(U_{1}\cap U_{2}),

which proves that
U_{1}\cap U_{2}\in{\mathsf{ABlock}}(X,f)
.

The elements
X
and
\varnothing
are attracting blocks that satisfy the definitions of the maximal and minimal elements, respectively. Distributivity follows from the fact that
{\mathsf{ABlock}}(X,f)
is a sublattice of the powerset lattice for
X
, which is distributive.
∎

2.3.
Summary of Theory

The goal of this subsection is to indicate how concepts from Sections
2.1
and
2.2
suggest the possibility of Theorem
1.4
.
We begin with a theorem, an imprecise statement of which is as follows.
Assume that we have obtained a characterization of the dynamics of
f
using the framework of Conley theory.
Then, for any continuous function
g
sufficiently close to
f
, the characterization of dynamics of
f
characterizes the dynamics of
g
.

Theorem 2.25
.

Let
f\colon X\to X
be a continuous function on a compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and assume that
f(X)\subset\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(X)
.
Let
({\mathsf{MR}}_{f},\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}})
be a Morse representation of
f
.
Furthermore, for each Morse set
M_{f}\in{\mathsf{MR}}
, let
{\mathsf{Con}}_{*}(M_{f},f)
denote the homology Conley index of
M_{f}
under the dynamics induced by
f
.

Then, there exists
\epsilon>0
for which the following result holds.
If
g\colon X\to X
is a continuous function such that
\sup_{x\in X}\|f(x)-g(x)\|<\epsilon
, then there is a Morse decomposition for
g
, i.e., a monomorphism of partially ordered sets

\mu\colon({\mathsf{MR}}_{g},\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{g}})\to({\mathsf{MR}}_{f},\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}})

where
{\mathsf{MR}}_{g}
is a Morse representation of
g
, such that if
M_{g}\in{\mathsf{MR}}_{g}
, then
{\mathsf{Con}}_{*}(M_{g},g)
is equivalent to
{\mathsf{Con}}_{*}(\mu(M_{g}),f)
.

For the sake of completeness we provide a formal proof of Theorem
2.25
, even though it is essentially a reformulation of classical results.

Proof.

Let
({\mathsf{MR}}_{f},\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}})
be a Morse representation of
f
with minimal partial order
\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}}
. By
[
20
, Theorem 6]
there exists a unique finite sublattice of
{\mathsf{Att}}(f)
and a poset isomorphism
\psi\colon({\mathsf{MR}}_{f},\leq_{{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}})\to({\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{A}}),\subset)
.
By
[
19
, Theorem 1.2]
there exists a sublattice
{\mathsf{N}}
of
{\mathsf{ABlock}}(f)
and a lattice isomorphism
k\colon{\mathsf{A}}\to{\mathsf{N}}
such that
\omega(k(A),f)=A
.
This implies there is a poset isomorphism
\kappa:{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{A}})\to{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{N}})
.

Recall that by definition there exists a poset isomorphism
\iota\colon{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{N}})\to{\mathsf{MTile}}({\mathsf{N}})
.
Observe that by construction if
M\in{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}
, then
M=\mathop{\mathrm{Inv}}\nolimits(\iota(\kappa(\psi(M)),f)
.

As indicated by Remark
2.16
, for each
N\in{\mathsf{N}}
there exists
\epsilon_{N}>0
such that if
\sup_{x\in X}\|f(x)-g(x)\|<\epsilon_{N}
, then
N
is an attracting block for
g
.
Set
\epsilon=\min\left\{{\epsilon_{N}}\,\left|\,{N\in{\mathsf{N}}}\right.\right\}
.
Then
{\mathsf{N}}
is a finite sublattice of
{\mathsf{ABlock}}(g)
.
By Proposition
2.22
we have the existence of a Morse decomposition
\mu\colon{\mathsf{MR}}_{g}\to{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{N}})
where

{\mathsf{MR}}_{g}=\left\{{M(N):=\mathop{\mathrm{Inv}}\nolimits(N\setminus N^{<},g)\neq\emptyset}\,\left|\,{N\in{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{N}})}\right.\right\}.

Since
{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{N}})\cong{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{A}})\cong{\mathsf{MR}}_{f}
, we have the desired Morse decomposition.

Let
M_{g}\in{\mathsf{MR}}_{g}
. Then by definition,
M_{g}=\mathop{\mathrm{Inv}}\nolimits(N\setminus N^{<},g)
for some
N\in{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{N}})
.
Since
\mathop{\mathrm{cl}}\nolimits(N\setminus N^{<})
is an isolating neighborhood for all functions
g\colon X\to X
such that
\sup_{x\in X}\|f(x)-g(x)\|<\epsilon
, by
[
10
]

\mathop{\mathrm{Con}_{*}}\nolimits(M_{g},g)\cong\mathop{\mathrm{Con}_{*}}\nolimits(\mu(M_{g}),f).

∎

Theorem
2.25
implies that we have a robust framework for characterizing dynamics.
Given knowledge of a Morse representation of
f
, we can deduce information about the Morse representation of
g
for
g
sufficiently close to
f
.
However, it does not state that we can learn the dynamics of
f
.
Thus, we turn to the main result of this paper.

3.
Combinatorial Dynamics

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a finite set.
A
combinatorial multivalued map

{\mathcal{F}}\colon{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}
satisfies two conditions for every
\xi\in{\mathcal{X}}
: (i)
{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)\subset{\mathcal{X}}
, and (ii)
{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)\neq\emptyset
.

Observe that a combinatorial map
{\mathcal{F}}
is equivalent to a directed graph;
{\mathcal{X}}
is the set of vertices and there exists a directed edge
\xi\to\xi^{\prime}
if and only if
\xi^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)
, with the additional constraint that there is a directed edge from
\xi
for every
\xi\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
We use both perspectives in this section.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}\colon{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}
be a combinatorial multivalued map.
A
forward invariant set
of
{\mathcal{F}}
is a set
{\mathcal{N}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}
such that
{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{N}})\subset{\mathcal{N}}
.
The set of forward invariant sets of
{\mathcal{F}}
is denoted by
{\mathsf{Invset}}^{+}({\mathcal{F}})
.

As shown in
[
19
]
,
{\mathsf{Invset}}^{+}({\mathcal{F}})
is a distributive lattice under the operations
\vee=\cup
and
\wedge=\cap
, with minimal element
{\mathbf{0}}=\emptyset
and maximal element
{\mathbf{1}}={\mathcal{X}}
.

We now describe how the Morse graph that appears in Theorem
1.4
is computed.
A
walk
in
{\mathcal{F}}
of length
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
from
\xi_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}
to
\xi_{K}\in{\mathcal{X}}
is a sequence of vertices

\{\xi_{k}\mid 0\leq k\leq K\text{ and }\xi_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}(\xi_{k-1})\text{ for all }1\leq k\leq K\}.

We denote a walk from
\xi_{0}
to
\xi_{K}
by
\xi_{0}{\rightsquigarrow}\xi_{K}
. Under this definition, a loop is a walk of length one. For all vertices
\xi
, there exists a walk of length zero
\xi{\rightsquigarrow}\xi
.

Define the relation
\sim
on
{\mathcal{X}}
by
\xi\sim\xi^{\prime}
if there exists walks
\xi{\rightsquigarrow}\xi^{\prime}
and
\xi^{\prime}{\rightsquigarrow}\xi
. We note that
\sim
is an equivalence relation. A directed graph
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}
is
strongly connected
if the quotient set
{\mathcal{X}}/\sim
consists of one equivalence class, meaning there exists a walk between every pair of vertices.

Given an arbitrary directed graph
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}
, the
strongly connected components
are the induced subgraphs with vertex sets equal to equivalence classes of
{\mathcal{X}}/\sim
. Equivalently, the strongly connected components are the maximal strongly connected subgraphs of
{\mathcal{F}}
.
Various linear time algorithms for computing strongly connected components have been developed: for example, Tarjan’s algorithm
[
43
]
and Kosaraju-Sharir’s algorithm
[
1
,
38
]
.

Let
\pi\colon{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathsf{SCC}}({\mathcal{F}}):={\mathcal{X}}/\sim
denote the quotient map from
{\mathcal{X}}
to the equivalence classes of strongly connected components.
The graph obtained by contracting edges that have incident vertices in the same strongly connected component is the
condensation graph
of
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}
, denoted by
\bar{{\mathcal{F}}}:{\mathsf{SCC}}({\mathcal{F}})\rightrightarrows{\mathsf{SCC}}({\mathcal{F}})
.
The condensation graph is an acyclic directed graph
[
5
]
, and it may be viewed as a compression of
{\mathcal{F}}
that retains reachability information.
Due to acyclicity,
{\mathsf{SCC}}({\mathcal{F}})
is a poset under the relation
\zeta^{\prime}\leq_{{\mathsf{SCC}}({\mathcal{F}})}\zeta
if
\zeta{\rightsquigarrow}\zeta^{\prime}
in
\bar{{\mathcal{F}}}
.
A
recurrent component
of
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}
is a strongly connected component with at least one edge in
{\mathcal{F}}

4.2.
Outer Approximations

We use a combinatorial multivalued map
{\mathcal{F}}\colon{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}
defined on a geometric simplicial complex
{\mathcal{X}}
to encode dynamics and set

|{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)|:=\bigcup_{\xi^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)}|\xi^{\prime}|\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

To pass from combinatorial dynamics of multivalued maps to continuous dynamics we make use of the following construct.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
f\colon X\to X
be a continuous function.
A combinatorial multivalued map
{\mathcal{F}}\colon{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}
is an
outer approximation
of
f
if for every
\xi\in{\mathcal{X}}

f(|\xi|)\subset\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits(|{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)|).

If
{\mathcal{F}}
is an outer approximation of
f
, then
f
is called a
selector
of
{\mathcal{F}}
.

To compare combinatorial multivalued maps, we use the notion of enclosure.

Definition 4.3
.

A combinatorial multivalued map
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}

encloses

{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}
if for all
\xi\in{\mathcal{X}}
,
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}(\xi)\subset{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)
.

A natural class of outer approximations arises as follows.

Definition 4.4
.

Given
\rho\geq 0

\rho
-minimal combinatorial outer approximation

{\mathcal{F}}_{\rho}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{X}}
of
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}
is defined inductively as follows.
If
\xi\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\text{top}}
, then

{\mathcal{F}}_{\rho}(\xi):=\left\{{\eta\in{\mathcal{X}}}\,\left|\,{B_{\rho}(f(|\xi|))\cap|\eta|\neq\emptyset}\right.\right\},

where
B_{\rho}
denotes the closed ball of radius
\rho

For all other
\xi\in{\mathcal{X}}
, set

{\mathcal{F}}_{\rho}(\xi):=\bigcup_{\text{$\xi$ a face of $\xi^{\prime}$}}{\mathcal{F}}_{\rho}(\xi^{\prime}).

Proposition 4.5
.

[
17
, Corollary 2.6]

A combinatorial multivalued map

is an outer approximation of
f
if and only if
{\mathcal{F}}
encloses the minimal combinatorial outer approximation

of
f
.

In practice, to incorporate errors we assume that

, in which case the
\rho
-minimal combinatorial outer approximation encloses the minimal combinatorial outer approximation of
f
.
As a consequence any
\rho
-minimal combinatorial outer approximation of

is also a combinatorial outer approximation of

The following result is a special case of
[

Proposition 4.6

Corollary 4.7

6.
Concluding Remarks

The focus of this paper is on demonstrating via Theorem
1.4
that, at least conceptually, it is possible to characterize global nonlinear dynamics from time series via machine learning.
The fundamental idea of Theorem
1.4
is as follows: given any Morse representation
{\mathsf{MR}}
of the dynamics generated by a continuous map
f
and a sufficiently good approximation
G
of
f
, one can construct a combinatorial representation
{\mathcal{G}}
of the dynamics from which it is possible to reconstruct
{\mathsf{MR}}
.

Of course, in applications
f
is unknown and hence
{\mathsf{MR}}
is unknown.
Furthermore, the (nontrivial) development of strategies for the machine learning of
G
and efficient computation of
\mathcal{G}
are left to future work (a preliminary experimental implementation of a related machine-learning framework is carried out in the context of differential equations in
[
11
]
).

Nevertheless, it is possible to discuss how the results of this paper could be used in practice.
For the remainder of these concluding remarks we assume that

is a finite union of closed, bounded, convex
d
-dimensional polytopes; that

is a continuous function such that

; that

is a fully-connected feedforward neural network with piecewise linear activation; that

is the finite simplicial complex arising from

We have taken

As detailed in Section
5
and stated in Theorem

, for each

we construct a Morse graph

Of course, since

is not known,

is not a priori determined.
However, if we choose

We recall elementary definitions associated with lattices and refer readers to

Definition 7.1
.

A
lattice
is a set
{\mathsf{L}}
together with binary operations
\wedge
and
\vee
that are commutative and associative and furthermore satisfy the following properties.

(i)

(\forall a\in{\mathsf{L}})

a\vee a=a=a\wedge a
.

(ii)

(\forall a,b\in{\mathsf{L}})

a\wedge(a\vee b)=a\vee(a\wedge b)=a
.

A lattice is
distributive
if it satisfies the distributive law,

(\forall a,b,c\in{\mathsf{L}})\hskip 5.69046pta\wedge(b\vee c)=(a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge c).

The meet operation induces a partial order defined by
a\leq_{\mathsf{L}}b
if and only if
a\wedge b=a
.

A lattice is
bounded
if there exist minimal and maximal elements
{\mathbf{0}}
and
{\mathbf{1}}
with respect to the partial order
\leq_{\mathsf{L}}
.
Throughout this paper we always work with bounded lattices.

A lattice morphism
f\colon{\mathsf{L}}\to{\mathsf{L}}^{\prime}
between two bounded lattices
{\mathsf{L}}
and
{\mathsf{L}}^{\prime}
preserves the lattice operations, i.e.,
f(a\vee b)=f(a)\vee f(b)
and
f(a\wedge b)=f(a)\wedge f(b)
for all
a,b\in{\mathsf{L}}
, and
f({\mathbf{0}})
and
f({\mathbf{1}})
are minimal and maximal elements in
{\mathsf{L}}^{\prime}
.

An element
c\in{\mathsf{L}}
is
join-irreducible
if there exists a unique element
c^{<}\neq c\in L
such that for all
b<c
,
b\leq c^{<}
.
In this case,
c^{<}
is called the
immediate predecessor
of
c
.
The set of join-irreducible elements of a lattice
L
is denoted by
{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{L}})
.
The predecessor map
{}^{<}:{\mathsf{J}}^{\vee}({\mathsf{L}})\to L
sends a join-irreducible element

to its unique predecessor,

.
