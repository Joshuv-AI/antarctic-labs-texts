Acute Kidney Injury Following Oral Urografin Exposure: A Case Report
Authors: Ching-Chieh Lin, Chih-Yang Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContrast-induced acute kidney injury (CI-AKI) is a well-recognized cause of hospital-acquired AKI, typically associated with intravascular contrast exposure. Oral contrast-induced nephrotoxicity, however, is exceedingly rare. We report a patient who developed AKI after oral administration of Urografin for postoperative bowel evaluation. The diagnosis of CI-AKI was established based on a characteristic temporal pattern of serum creatinine elevation within 48 hours, peaking at day 3-5 and returning to baseline by day 7-14, after exclusion of other causes of AKI. The patient’s risk factors included type 2 diabetes mellitus, recent intra-abdominal surgery with ileal neobladder reconstruction disrupting intestinal integrity, and small bowel obstruction, which prolonged intraluminal contrast retention and increased systemic absorption. The patient’s kidney function recovered fully with hydration alone, confirming a transient and self-limited course typical of CI-AKI. This case highlights that even oral administration of high-osmolar ionic contrast can induce nephrotoxicity under conditions of impaired intestinal barrier and delayed bowel transit. Clinicians should remain vigilant and monitor kidney function closely when prescribing oral contrast in postoperative or high-risk patients.Index Words: Acute kidney injury, bowel obstruction, delayed gastrointestinal transit, oral, radiocontrast

Introduction

Contrast-induced acute kidney injury (CI-AKI) is the third most common cause of AKI in hospitalized patients and is typically associated with intravenous or intra-arterial administration of iodinated contrast media.1 High-osmolality (>1,500 mOsm/kg) and ionic contrast agents are particularly nephrotoxic, especially in patients with comorbid conditions such as chronic kidney disease, chronic heart failure, or diabetes mellitus. In CI-AKI, serum creatinine levels usually begin to increase within 48 hours after exposure, peak at 3-5 days, and return to baseline within 1-2 weeks.1,2

In contrast to the well-recognized risk associated with intravascular contrast exposure, nephrotoxicity following oral administration of iodinated contrast agents has rarely been reported and remains poorly characterized. We report a rare case of CI-AKI following repeated oral administration of an ionic high-osmolar contrast agent (Urografin) in a patient who had recently undergone major intra-abdominal surgery.

Case Report

A 71-year-old man was admitted to our hospital for a robotic cystoprostatectomy with orthotopic neobladder reconstruction because of muscle-invasive urothelial carcinoma of the bladder. He underwent transurethral resection of the bladder tumor followed by neoadjuvant chemotherapy with gemcitabine and cisplatin 2 months before this admission. His medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension, for which he was receiving oral antihypertensive therapy. He had no known family history of kidney or genetic disease and was not taking any nephrotoxic medications before surgery.

The postoperative course was initially stable, with no evidence of bleeding or infection and adequate urine output through the single-J ureteral catheter and Foley catheter. On postoperative day (POD) 6 (with POD 0 defined as the day of surgery), the patient complained of abdominal distension and decreased passage of flatus. An abdominal radiograph obtained after oral administration of Urografin (70%, 50 mL) showed small bowel obstruction without evidence of urethroenteric anastomotic leakage (Fig 1). To facilitate both diagnostic evaluation and conservative treatment, 70% oral Urografin at a dose of 50 mL every day was prescribed for 5 consecutive days.

Serial abdominal radiographs demonstrating small bowel obstruction and progression of oral Urografin transit. (A) Baseline radiograph (kidney, ureter, and bladder) obtained on postoperative day (POD) 6 when the patient presented with abdominal distension, demonstrating dilated small bowel loops with a stack-of-coins sign, consistent with small bowel obstruction. (B) Follow-up radiograph obtained 12 hours after oral Urografin administration on POD 7, demonstrating slow progression of contrast through the small bowel with persistent obstruction pattern.

Two days after the final administration, the patient developed decreased urine output and signs of fluid retention, presenting with pitting edema of the lower extremities. The patient reported no new symptoms, and his abdominal distension gradually improved. On examination, the patient was alert and hemodynamically stable. There was no pallor, rash, or peripheral cyanosis. Follow-up laboratory testing demonstrated an increase in serum creatinine level from 0.85 mg/dL to 1.41 mg/dL and serum urea nitrogen level from 11 mg/dL to 25 mg/dL compared with values obtained 3 days earlier, consistent with AKI (Fig 2); the corresponding institutional reference range are provided in the Table S1.

Temporal changes in Scr and UOP after repeated oral Urografin administration. The patient received oral Urografin 76% at a dose of 50 mL once daily from POD 6 to POD 10. POD 10 corresponded to the fifth (final) oral dose, with a Scr level of 0.85 mg/dL. Over the subsequent days, the patient developed decreased urine output after Urografin exposure, followed by an elevation in Scr that peaked on POD 14 and gradually returned to baseline by approximately POD 20. Abbreviations: Scr, serum creatinine; POD, postoperative day; UOP, urine output.

Urinalysis showed a specific gravity of 1.011, mild proteinuria (1+), no hematuria or pyuria, and no casts or granular debris. During this period, the patient received piperacillin-tazobactam for suspected intra-abdominal infection; however, there was no fever, no leukocytosis on laboratory testing, and noncontrast abdominal computed tomography on POD 7 demonstrated ileus without evidence of infection. Renal ultrasonography showed normal kidney size and echogenicity without hydronephrosis.

Postrenal obstruction was excluded based on the absence of hydronephrosis, and prerenal azotemia was unlikely given stable hemodynamics, adequate urine output, and a serum urea nitrogen-creatinine ratio <20. The patient also received intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam during hospitalization; however, β-lactam-associated acute interstitial nephritis was considered less likely given the absence of systemic features such as fever, rash, or eosinophilia, as well as the lack of urinary findings suggestive of interstitial nephritis, including pyuria or white blood cell casts. The urinalysis demonstrated bland sediment, and the temporal pattern of renal dysfunction and creatinine elevation within 72 hours after oral contrast exposure, peaking at 5 days and returning to baseline within 14 days, was highly compatible with CI-AKI after exclusion of other potential causes of AKI (Fig 2).

The patient was treated with isotonic saline hydration (approximately 1,500 mL/d), which was gradually tapered after relief of the small bowel obstruction and improvement in oral intake. No diuretics or renal replacement therapy were required. After hydration, kidney function improved gradually, with serum creatinine returning to baseline (0.84 mg/dL) within 10 days after the last dose of Urografin. Mild residual lower-extremity edema persisted briefly but resolved during convalescence (Fig 2).

Discussion

Most CI-AKI cases occur after intravenous or intra-arterial administration, particularly during cardiovascular procedures such as percutaneous cardiac intervention, in which preprocedural hydration and withdrawal of nephrotoxic agents are routinely practiced, especially in high-risk patients with systemic underlying disease such as heart failure or type 2 diabetes mellitus. In contrast, oral CI-AKI is relatively rare and often overlooked.2,3 Urografin (diatrizoate meglumine and sodium) is an ionic, high-osmolar contrast medium (∼2,100 mOsm/L) that exerts stronger tubular toxicity and renal vasoconstrictive effects compared with nonionic or iso-osmolar agents. Although primarily administered intravenously or trans-urethrally, it is also used orally for gastrointestinal imaging or as therapy for small bowel obstruction.4

In the present case, CI-AKI was diagnosed after excluding alternative etiologies of AKI. The patient showed stable hemodynamics, no anemia, no urinary obstruction, and a bland urinalysis without active sediment, indicating intrinsic tubular injury (Fig S1). The patient had received cisplatin approximately 2 months before the current hospitalization; however, this remote exposure was considered unlikely to account for the acute increase in serum creatinine level. In addition, intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam was administered for intra-abdominal infection from POD 2 to POD 12. β-lactam-associated acute interstitial nephritis is a type IV hypersensitivity reaction that typically develops after 1-2 weeks of drug exposure.5 Although the timing of AKI overlapped with approximately 1 week of antibiotic therapy, the patient did not exhibit typical systemic features such as fever, rash, or eosinophilia. Furthermore, urinalysis did not demonstrate findings suggestive of acute interstitial nephritis, such as pyuria or white blood cell casts.

The patient developed decreased urine output and fluid retention 2 days after contrast exposure, followed by an increase in serum creatinine level that peaked on day 4 and returned to the normal range by day 10, which is consistent with CI-AKI.2 Although serum creatinine is an imperfect surrogate of glomerular filtration rate and may lag behind the actual decline in kidney function, the temporal relationship between contrast exposure and kidney injury supported this diagnosis. Several factors likely contributed to his susceptibility, including advanced age (71 years), type 2 diabetes mellitus, and repeated high-dose exposure to oral Urografin (approximately 50 mL daily for 5 consecutive days). In addition, his recent intra-abdominal surgery with ileal neobladder reconstruction may have disrupted intestinal integrity, while his small bowel obstruction likely prolonged intraluminal retention of the contrast agent, increasing mucosal contact time and systemic absorption. Together, these risk factors predisposed him to nephrotoxicity despite normally minimal gastrointestinal absorption.

A prospective study of 49 participants, including 30 with previous gastrointestinal surgery but without anastomotic leakage, showed significantly higher renal excretion of Gastrografin, a contrast with identical composition to Urografin, in the surgical group than in controls, suggesting enhanced systemic uptake.6 Another cohort of patients with adhesive small bowel obstruction demonstrated that AKI was the most frequent in-hospital complication following oral Gastrografin challenge, although the contrast in that study was administered preoperatively rather than postoperatively.7 These findings collectively suggested that impaired intestinal barrier or prolonged mucosal contact might permit systemic absorption of ionic contrast, resulting in renal vasoconstriction, oxidative stress, and tubular hypoxia leading to transient AKI.

This case emphasizes that even oral administration of high-osmolar ionic contrast can cause nephrotoxicity under conditions of facilitated systemic absorption. In particular, delayed gastrointestinal transit because of bowel obstruction, prolonged mucosal contact with contrast media, and disruption of intestinal barrier integrity following recent intra-abdominal surgery may collectively increase systemic reabsorption of iodinated contrast agents. Clinicians should be aware of this potential risk in postoperative patients or those with bowel obstruction and ensure adequate hydration and renal monitoring to prevent this rare but clinically significant complication.
