Transformers Provably Solve Parity Efficiently with Chain of Thought
Juno Kim *, Taiji Suzuki

Abstract
This work provides the first theoretical analysis of training transformers to solve complex problems by recursively generating intermediate states, analogous to fine-tuning for chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning. We consider training a one-layer transformer to solve the fundamental k k -parity problem, extending the work on RNNs by Wies et al. (2023) . We establish three key results: (1) any finite-precision gradient-based algorithm, without intermediate supervision, requires substantial iterations to solve parity with finite samples. (2) In contrast, when intermediate parities are incorporated into the loss function, our model can learn parity in one gradient update when aided by teacher forcing , where ground-truth labels of the reasoning chain are provided at each generation step. (3) Even without teacher forcing, where the model must generate CoT chains end-to-end, parity can be learned efficiently if augmented data is employed to internally verify the soundness of intermediate steps. Our findings, supported by numerical experiments, show that task decomposition and stepwise reasoning naturally arise from optimizing transformers with CoT; moreover, self-consistency checking can improve multi-step reasoning ability, aligning with empirical studies of CoT.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) based on the transformer architecture
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
)
have achieved astounding success across a variety of natural language processing and machine learning tasks
(
Wan et al., 2024
;
Minaee et al., 2024
;

;
Zhao et al., 2024

(
Geva et al., 2021
;
Rae et al., 2022
;
Arkoudas, 2023
;
Wang et al., 2024

. These failures are particularly evident in tasks requiring multi-hop reasoning or compounded logical steps

A promising approach to overcome these limitations is chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, where the model is prompted or fine-tuned to solve complex tasks step-by-step by explicitly making intermediate reasoning steps to arrive at the desired answers
(
Wei et al., 2022
;
Kojima et al., 2022
)
. Since its discovery, CoT reasoning has been shown to significantly enhance the problem-solving capabilities of LLMs while also increasing the interpretability and trustworthiness of the reasoning process, and has spawned numerous prompting techniques
(
Liu et al., 2023
;
Qiao et al., 2023
)
and applications for a variety of downstream tasks including common-sense reasoning, mathematical problem-solving, and symbolic or multi-modal reasoning; see e.g.
Zhang et al. (2023b)
;
Yu et al. (2023)
;
Chu et al. (2024)
for surveys on CoT. In particular, besides being used as a prompting method, directly training or fine-tuning models to generate CoT has also been shown to significantly improve multi-step reasoning performance
(
Nye et al., 2021
;
Wei et al., 2022
;
Zelikman et al., 2022
;
Lightman et al., 2024
)
.

Despite these empirical successes, however, the theoretical understanding of the CoT mechanism and task decomposition in transformers is still limited. Existing works focus on characterizing the expressivity of transformers equipped with CoT, providing constructions which can solve certain complexity classes
(
Feng et al., 2023
;
Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023
;
Merrill & Sabharwal, 2024
;
Li et al., 2024b
)
, studying the class of functions that can be learned in-context with CoT
(
Li et al., 2023
;
Bhattamishra et al., 2024
)
, or analyzing the estimation error of multi-step models
(
Hu et al., 2024
)
. Nevertheless, such approaches do not indicate how such capabilities might emerge when training transformers to generate reasoning chains.
Li et al. (2024a)
analyze the training dynamics of a one-layer transformer in an in-context learning setting and show that CoT ability may be acquired; however, they do not consider explicitly training with CoT chains, which is a more difficult problem since the objective depends on the recursive application of the transformer to itself.

In this paper, we seek to formalize the mechanism through which stepwise reasoning emerges in transformers optimized to generate CoT chains. We focus on the specific problem of
bit subset parity
(learning the parity of an unknown subset of
k
bits from a
d

Shalev-Shwartz et al., 2017
;
Shamir, 2018
)
. In contrast,
Wies et al. (2023)
have demonstrated that recurrent neural networks (RNNs) can solve parity efficiently when provided with intermediate supervision. We build on this direction to establish positive optimization guarantees for the transformer architecture. Our object of study is a one-layer transformer incorporating a softmax attention layer, feedforward layer and positional encoding, that is recursively applied to its own output to generate a sequence of intermediate parity computations to arrive at the desired output, analogous to CoT generation. 1.1
Related Works

Complexity of transformers.

A line of work aims to understand the effectiveness of CoT from the perspective of complexity theory.
Feng et al. (2023)
show that autoregressive transformers of constant size can solve basic arithmetic tasks by recursively generating CoT reasoning steps, which is not possible when directly generating the solution; this separation arises because looping the generated outputs back to its inputs increases the ‘effective depth’ of the model. Works such as
Chiang et al. (2023)
;
Merrill & Sabharwal (2023)
study the expressivity of fixed-precision transformer architectures in terms of classes of formal languages.
Merrill & Sabharwal (2024)
;
Li et al. (2024b)
show that CoT reasoning enables recognizing wider language classes, and characterizes the increased expressivity depending on the length of the reasoning chain.
Sanford et al. (2024)
studies the relation between transformers and massively parallel computation protocols, showing that logarithmic depth suffices to solve multi-hop induction tasks that cannot be efficiently solved by other sequence models. Additionally,
Li et al. (2023)
;
Bhattamishra et al. (2024)
study the class of functions that can be learned in context by transformers with CoT from the point of view of in-context learning.

Optimization and generalization of CoT.

Zhu et al. (2024)
study the ‘reversal curse’ via the training dynamics of a one-layer transformer and shows that the model fails to generalize from
A\to B
,
B\to C
to
A\to C
as an argument for the necessity of explicit step-by-step reasoning.
Hu et al. (2024)
study CoT prompting from a statistical estimation perspective by introducing a multi-step latent variable model for CoT and analyzing its approximation, generalization and prompting-based errors. Notably,
Li et al. (2024a)
study the training dynamics of a one-layer attention-only transformer model in an in-context learning setting and show that CoT generalization capability can be obtained. However, this does not address the possibility or benefits of training with CoT chains.
Lightman et al. (2024)
empirically study training LLMs with either process or outcome supervision, showing that the former significantly outperforms the latter when training to solve challenging reasoning tasks.

Parity and task decomposition.

The difficulty of learning parity without task decomposition is established in
Shalev-Shwartz et al. (2017)
;
Shamir (2018)
. The work most relevant to our paper is
Wies et al. (2023)
, which study task decomposition for parity with classical Elman RNNs. They show that by incorporating intermediate states into the loss function and utilizing teacher forcing, parity can be solved with polynomial iterations and embedding size. Our Theorem
5
extends this positive result to autoregressive transformers, rigorously establishing the benefits of CoT-based training.

Chain of thought.

Consider repeatedly applying
\TF(\cdot)
to its own output to generate a ‘reasoning chain.’ Since the input tokens are fixed, the token
\hat{\bm{x}}_{d+1}
will be updated once and then always yield the same value afterwards. Next, since
\hat{\bm{x}}_{d+2}
depends on the input tokens and
\hat{\bm{x}}_{d+1}
, it will be updated twice before becoming fixed. Repeating this, the entire chain stops updating after at most
k-1
steps, yielding the output

\displaystyle\TF^{(k-1)}(\bm{x}_{1},\cdots,\bm{x}_{d},\bm{0}_{n},\cdots,\bm{0}_{n};\mathbf{W})=(\hat{\bm{x}}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\bm{x}}_{d+k-1})

where the intermediate predictions are recursively computed as
\hat{\bm{x}}_{m}=\phi(\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(\bm{w}_{m})\hat{\bm{x}}_{j})
. Finally, the top node is returned as the model prediction
\hat{\bm{y}}=\hat{\bm{x}}_{d+k-1}
.

This process can be seen as a simplified version of CoT reasoning, albeit not in an in-context learning setting: instead of one-shot predicting
y^{i}
from
\bm{x}^{i}
, the model starts by solving simpler subtasks and uses the information to attack compound problems, learning to generate intermediate reasoning steps
\bm{x}_{d+1}\to\cdots\to\bm{x}_{d+k-1}
to finally arrive at the desired solution. Importantly, this process is not possible if the model is only trained on the one-shot data
(\bm{x}^{i},y^{i})_{i\in[n]}
as we show in Section
3.1
. Instead, we incorporate the prediction error for all intermediate states directly into our loss function
(
Lightman et al., 2024
)
. We also consider shortening the reasoning chain by using a different causal mask in Section
3.3
, which will result in improved control of error and faster convergence.

3
Main Results

3.1
Hardness of Parity without CoT

Before analyzing our transformer model, we first prove a negative learning result in the absence of intermediate supervision that extends Theorem
1
, which was stated with respect to the population objective
\bar{L}
and zero-one test loss
\ell_{0-1}
, to finite samples and mean squared loss.

Let
f_{\theta}:\{\pm 1\}^{d}\to\RR
be any differentiable parametrized model and suppose we select the target parity
p
uniformly at random from
P
. In the finite-sample setting,
n
i.i.d. samples
(\bm{x}^{i},y^{i})_{i\in[n]}
are generated as
\bm{x}^{i}\sim\Unif(\{\pm 1\}^{d})
,
y^{i}=p(\bm{x}^{i})
and we are given access to (approximate) gradients from the empirical loss

\displaystyle L_{n}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y^{i}-f_{\theta}(\bm{x}^{i}))^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\lVert p-f_{\theta}\rVert_{n}^{2},

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{n}
is the empirical norm. It is important that the model
f_{\theta}
is applied to each
\bm{x}^{i}
separately and does not cross-reference between different samples, as there exist more efficient parity-learning algorithms if the data is allowed to be manipulated freely. For example, Gaussian elimination can solve parity with
O(d)
samples and
O(d^{3})
iterations
(
Raz, 2018
)
. Moreover, this implies that neural networks trained with stochastic gradient descent can also solve parity in polynomial time
(
Abbe & Sandon, 2020
)
. Instead, in our setting the model is forced to learn from the averaged gradient signal and can only implicitly utilize the correlation between samples.

We show the following result for learning parities with finite-samples in Appendix
A
:

Theorem 2

(hardness of finite-sample parity)
.

Suppose
k=\Theta(d)
.

(1)

If
n=e^{\Omega(d)}
and
f_{\theta}
has polynomially bounded gradients, there exists an
e^{-\Omega(d)}
-approximate gradient oracle
\widetilde{\nabla}
such that with probability
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
over random sampling, the output
\theta(\mathcal{A})
of any iterative (possibly randomized) algorithm which makes at most
O(\poly(d))
queries to
\widetilde{\nabla}L_{n}
has
L_{2}
-loss lower bounded as

\mathbb{E}_{p\in P,\bm{x}}\left[(p(\bm{x})-f_{\theta(\mathcal{A})}(\bm{x}))^{2}\right]\geq 1-e^{-\Omega(d)}.

(2)

If
n=\Omega(d^{\nu})
and
\lVert\nabla f_{\theta}\rVert=O(d^{\nu_{1}})
, there exists an
O(d^{-\nu_{2}})
-approximate gradient oracle
\widetilde{\nabla}
such that with probability
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
over random sampling, the output
\theta(\mathcal{A})
of any iterative (possibly randomized) algorithm which makes at most
O(d^{\nu_{3}})
queries to
\widetilde{\nabla}L_{n}
has
L_{2}
-loss lower bounded, where
\nu=4\nu_{1}+4\nu_{2}+2\nu_{3}+2\nu_{4}+1
, as

\mathbb{E}_{p\in P,\bm{x}}\left[(p(\bm{x})-f_{\theta(\mathcal{A})}(\bm{x}))^{2}\right]\geq 1-O(d^{-\nu_{4}}).

We remark that the bounds are asymptotically optimal since
f_{\theta}\equiv 0
is a valid estimator. Moreover, the expectation over
p\in P
can be replaced by the corresponding ‘with high probability’ statement.

A counter-intuitive aspect of the above result is that parity becomes potentially more difficult when the number of samples increases. Indeed, with exponential samples

We first note that the direct model (red) completely fails to learn the target, plateauing almost immediately. We observed that the weights become nearly uniform so that

and the prediction error is stuck at 0.5. This was not improved by using a multilayer transformer instead of repeated composition. The basic CoT model (yellow) is able to significantly decrease CoT loss but fails to fully solve the problem and eventually becomes unstable. Moreover, the prediction loss never improves beyond 0.5. Indeed, due to the hierarchical structure of parity, the model has no chance of making an informative prediction at the last level
\bm{x}_{d+k-1}
unless all preceding levels have been fully solved. In contrast, we verify that CoT with teacher forcing (blue) solves parity efficiently as predicted in Section

CoT with self-consistency (green) is also able to solve parity efficiently as predicted. Furthermore, the corresponding CoT loss curve clearly exhibits multiple learning stages. In the beginning, the model is essentially optimizing only the first level as subsequent outputs are zeroed out. After a short burn-in phase, the weights are optimized so that the softmax scores concentrate on the relevant nodes, at which point the CoT loss sharply decreases and the filters for the next level are deactivated, unlocking the next learning stage. This phased optimization repeats until all levels are fully solved and is crucial to arriving at the correct answer (in essence, teacher forcing is doing this at all levels simultaneously). Notably, a similar behavior seems to arise in the basic CoT model as well but fails due to accumulating error, further justifying the use of the filtering mechanism.

These results confirm that training explicitly for CoT generation can improve performance on multi-step tasks, and that controlling error accumulation via teacher forcing or self-consistency is key to ensuring proper step-by-step learning.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, by focusing on the
k
-parity problem, we provide an initial theoretical foundation for training transformers with CoT to perform stepwise reasoning. Our results show that gradient-based learning of parity requires significant iterations without intermediate supervision, but task decomposition using teacher forcing enables efficient learning in a single gradient update. Furthermore, when transformers are trained to generate reasoning chains end-to-end, data augmentation and self-consistency checks can enhance their ability to solve complex tasks. Our work takes the first steps towards understanding how CoT can be leveraged to improve multi-step reasoning capability of foundation models.

Denote the empirical inner product on

\lVert f\rVert_{n}^{2}=\langle f,f\rangle_{n}

(d/k)^{k}\leq\binom{d}{k}\leq(ed/k)^{k}

Bounding gradient variance.

Consider the variance of the empirical gradient
\nabla L_{n,p}

p

\Var_{n}(\theta;P):=\mathbb{E}_{p\in P}\left[\lVert\nabla L_{n,p}(\theta)-\mathbb{E}_{p^{\prime}\in P}[\nabla L_{n,p^{\prime}}(\theta)]\rVert^{2}\right].

We proceed to evaluate the magnitude of
\Var_{n}(\theta;P)
. For
p,p^{\prime}\in P
with
p\neq p^{\prime}
it holds that

\displaystyle\langle p,p^{\prime}\rangle_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\prod_{j\in p}x_{j}^{i}\prod_{j^{\prime}\in p^{\prime}}x_{j^{\prime}}^{i}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\prod_{j\in p\Delta p^{\prime}}x_{j}^{i}\right).

Since
\prod_{j\in p\Delta p^{\prime}}x_{j}^{i}
is i.i.d.
\Unif(\{\pm 1\})
for fixed
p,p^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\sup_{p\neq p^{\prime}}|\langle p,p^{\prime}\rangle_{n}|\geq\delta\right)\leq|P|(|P|-1)\exp\left(-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\binom{d}{k}^{2}e^{-2d}\leq\left(\frac{2}{e}\right)^{2d}.

Then with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
over random sampling, every off-diagonal component of the Gram matrix
G_{P}:=(\langle p,p^{\prime}\rangle_{n})_{p,p^{\prime}\in P}
has magnitude at most
\delta
, while the diagonal entries are equal to
1
. By the Gershgorin circle theorem, the maximum eigenvalue of
G_{P}
satisfies

|\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(G_{P})-1|\leq(|P|-1)\delta,

\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(G_{P})\leq 2(1\vee|P|\delta)

P

f=\sum_{p\in P}c_{p}\cdot p+f_{0},\quad f_{0}\in(\spn P)^{\perp},

for some coefficient sequence

\lVert f\rVert_{n}^{2}\geq\lVert f-f_{0}\rVert_{n}^{2}=\sum_{p,p^{\prime}\in P}c_{p}c_{p^{\prime}}\langle p,p^{\prime}\rangle_{n}=\lVert G_{P}^{1/2}c\rVert^{2}

\sum_{p\in P}\langle f,p\rangle_{n}^{2}=\sum_{p\in P}\Bigg(\sum_{p^{\prime}\in P}c_{p^{\prime}}\langle p,p^{\prime}\rangle\Bigg)^{2}=\lVert G_{P}c\rVert^{2}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(G_{P})\lVert f\rVert_{n}^{2}.

\displaystyle\Var_{n}(\theta;P)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mu\in\RR^{D}}\mathbb{E}_{p\in P}\left[\lVert\nabla L_{n,p}(\theta)-\mu\rVert^{2}\right]

By union bounding, we see that during
T
steps the oracle always defaults to the mean gradient and does not reveal any information on the true parity
p
, with probability at least

\Pr(Q)\geq 1-\frac{2T}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{|P|}\vee\sqrt{\frac{4d}{n}}\right)\sup_{\theta,\bm{x}}\lVert\nabla f_{\theta}(\bm{x})\rVert^{2},

where
Q\subseteq P
denotes the corresponding subset of the hypothesis space. Note that the argument can be extended to any randomized algorithm and random initialization in a straightforward manner by lifting to the product probability space, and so we consider
Q
to be fixed. Then for any target parity
p\in Q
, the output
\theta(\mathcal{A})
of the algorithm after
T
steps does not depend on
p
, so the predictor
f=f_{\theta(\mathcal{A})}
is also fixed.

Lower bounding the loss.

We first remark that a simpler proof can be given for the sup norm error, which is enough to establish a separation. Consider arbitrary
p,p^{\prime}\in P
with
p\neq p^{\prime}
and let
\bm{x}\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
be such that
p(\bm{x})\neq p^{\prime}(\bm{x})
, then

\left\lvert p(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x})\right\rvert+\left\lvert p^{\prime}(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x})\right\rvert\geq\left\lvert 1-f(\bm{x})\right\rvert+\left\lvert-1-f(\bm{x})\right\rvert\geq 2.

Now let
\sigma:Q\to Q
be any automorphism of
Q
with no fixed points. The
L_{\infty}
error can be bounded below by restricting to the noninformative set
Q
as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p\in P}\left[\sup_{\bm{x}}\left\lvert p(\bm{x})-f_{\theta(\mathcal{A})}(\bm{x})\right\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{p\in P}\left[1_{\{p\in Q\}}\sup_{\bm{x}}\left\lvert p(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x})\right\rvert\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2|P|}\sum_{p\in Q}\left({\sup_{\bm{x}}}\left\lvert p(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x})\right\rvert+{\sup_{\bm{x}}}\left\lvert\sigma\circ p(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x})\right\rvert\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2|P|}\cdot 2|Q|=\Pr(Q).

For mean squared error, we similarly restrict to
Q
so that

\mathbb{E}_{p\in P,\bm{x}}\left[(p(\bm{x})-f_{\theta(\mathcal{A})}(\bm{x}))^{2}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{p\in P,\bm{x}}\left[1_{\{p\in Q\}}(p(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x}))^{2}\right].

Since the range of
p
is contained in
[-1,1]
, the above loss will not increase when
f
is replaced by its clipped version
\bar{f}(\bm{x})=(f(\bm{x})\wedge 1)\vee(-1)
. Moreover, in Lemma
8
(proved at the end of the section) we show that
\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{p\in P}[p(\bm{x})]\right\rvert\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}
holds with probability
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
over the sample space of
\bm{x}
, so that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{p\in P,\bm{x}}\left[p(\bm{x})\bar{f}(\bm{x})\right]\right\rvert\leq(1-e^{-\Omega(d)})\mathbb{E}_{p\in P}\left[p(\bm{x})\right]+e^{-\Omega(d)}\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}

and also

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p\in P,\bm{x}}\left[1_{\{p\in Q\}}p(\bm{x})\bar{f}(\bm{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p\in P,\bm{x}}\left[p(\bm{x})\bar{f}(\bm{x})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p\in P,\bm{x}}\left[1_{\{p\notin Q\}}p(\bm{x})\bar{f}(\bm{x})\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}+(1-\Pr(Q))\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\left[|\bar{f}(\bm{x})|\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}+\frac{(1-\Pr(Q))^{2}}{2\Pr(Q)}+\frac{\Pr(Q)}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\left[\bar{f}(\bm{x})^{2}\right].

Therefore we may bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p\in P,\bm{x}}\left[(p(\bm{x})-f_{\theta(\mathcal{A})}(\bm{x}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{p\in P,\bm{x}}\left[1_{\{p\in Q\}}(p(\bm{x})-\bar{f}(\bm{x}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr(Q)-2\mathbb{E}_{p\in P,\bm{x}}\left[1_{\{p\in Q\}}p(\bm{x})\bar{f}(\bm{x})\right]+\Pr(Q)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\left[\bar{f}(\bm{x})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr(Q)-\frac{(1-\Pr(Q))^{2}}{\Pr(Q)}-2e^{-\Omega(d)}

\displaystyle\geq 2-\frac{1}{\Pr(Q)}-2e^{-\Omega(d)}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{4T}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{|P|}\vee\sqrt{\frac{4d}{n}}\right)\sup_{\theta,\bm{x}}\lVert\nabla f_{\theta}(\bm{x})\rVert^{2}-2e^{-\Omega(d)},

(5)

where we have used the inequality
2-(1-t)^{-1}\geq 1-2t
, valid for
t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
.

The proof is completed by evaluating the following cases.

(1)

If
n=e^{\Omega(d)}
and
T,\lVert\nabla f_{\theta}\rVert=O(\poly(d))
, the gradient variance is bounded as
\Var_{n}(\theta;P)\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}
. By taking
\varepsilon=\Var_{n}(\theta;P)^{1/3}
, it follows that
\Pr(Q)=1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
and (
5
) yields the lower bound
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
.

(2)

If

,
\lVert\nabla f_{\theta}\rVert=O(d^{\nu_{1}})
,
\varepsilon=\Theta(d^{-\nu_{2}})
and

\Var_{n}(\theta;P)\leq O(d^{2\nu_{1}+\nu_{3}+1/2-\nu/2})=O(d^{-2\nu_{2}-\nu_{4}})
and (

) yields the lower bound
1-O(d^{-\nu_{4}})
.

∎

Lemma 8
.

If
k=\Theta(d)
, it holds with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
over random sampling that

\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{p\in P}[p(\bm{x})]\right\rvert\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}.

Proof.

Let
m
denote the number of
-1
s in
\bm{x}
. By the Chernoff bound for the binomial distribution,

\Pr\left(\left\lvert m-\frac{d}{2}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\delta d}{2}\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}d}{6}\right)

for a constant
\delta\in(0,1)
to be determined, so we assume the above event throughout the proof. Moreover denoting the complement parity
p^{c}=[d]\setminus p
, it holds that
p(\bm{x})=x_{1}\cdots x_{d}\cdot p^{c}(\bm{x})
and
\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{p\in P}[p(\bm{x})]\right\rvert=\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{p\in P}[p^{c}(\bm{x})]\right\rvert
, so it suffices to consider the case where
2k\leq d
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\bm{x}=(-1,\cdots,-1,1,\cdots,1)
so that
p(\bm{x})
is decided as
(-1)^{\left\lvert p\cap[m]\right\rvert}
. We bound the cardinality of the set
P_{+}:=\{p\in P\mid p(\bm{x})=1\}
. Each parity in
P_{+}
can be determined by choosing
2j
elements from
[m]
and
k-2j
elements from
[d]\setminus[m]
. Denoting by
[t]_{j}
the
coefficient
of operator of order
j
, we can evaluate

\displaystyle|P_{+}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\binom{m}{2j}\binom{d-m}{k-2j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\binom{m}{2j}[t]_{k-2j}(1+t)^{d-m}=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\binom{m}{2j}[t]_{k}(1+t)^{d-m}t^{2j}

\displaystyle=[t]_{k}(1+t)^{d-m}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\binom{m}{2j}t^{2j}=\frac{1}{2}[t]_{k}(1+t)^{d-m}((1+t)^{m}+(1-t)^{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\binom{d}{k}+\frac{1}{2}[t]_{k}(1-t^{2})^{m^{\prime}}(1+st)^{d-2m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\binom{d}{k}+\frac{s^{k}}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}(-1)^{j}\binom{m^{\prime}}{j}\binom{d-2m^{\prime}}{k-2j},

where
m^{\prime}=m\wedge(d-m)
and
s=\pm 1
. It further follows that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{|P_{+}|}{|P|}-\frac{1}{2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2|P|}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\binom{m^{\prime}}{j}\binom{d-2m^{\prime}}{k-2j}\leq\frac{1}{2|P|}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\binom{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}{j}\binom{\lfloor\delta d\rfloor}{k-2j}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}{2}\binom{d}{k}^{-1}\binom{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\binom{\lfloor\delta d\rfloor}{\lfloor\delta d/2\rfloor}\leq\frac{d}{4}\binom{d-\lfloor d/2\rfloor-\lfloor\delta d\rfloor}{k-\lfloor k/2\rfloor-\lfloor\delta d/2\rfloor}^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d}{4}\binom{\lfloor d/4\rfloor}{\lfloor k/4\rfloor}^{-1}\leq\frac{d}{4}\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)^{-k/4}=e^{-\Theta(d)}.

Here, we have chosen
\delta=\frac{1}{4}\wedge\frac{k}{2d}=\Theta(1)
and used the inequality
\binom{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}}{b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}}\geq\binom{a_{1}}{b_{1}}\binom{a_{2}}{b_{2}}\binom{a_{3}}{b_{3}}
. From this, we conclude that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{p\in P}[p(\bm{x})]\right\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{|P\setminus P_{+}|-|P_{+}|}{|P|}\right\rvert\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}

with probability
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
.
∎

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\langle\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}^{3},\bm{x}_{j}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{n(m-1)^{3}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\left\langle\bm{x}_{\alpha},\bm{x}_{\beta},\bm{x}_{\gamma},\bm{x}_{j}\right\rangle=\frac{O(d)}{(m-1)^{3}}+O(\kappa)=O(d^{-2}+\kappa).

Combining the above, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n(m-1)}\left\langle-\bm{1}_{n}+c\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}^{2},2c\hat{\bm{z}}_{m},\bm{x}_{j}-\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}\right\rangle=-\frac{2c}{(m-1)^{2}}+\frac{2c}{(m-1)^{2}}+\frac{O(\kappa)}{m-1}=O(d^{-2-\epsilon/4}).

For term (
9
), we note that
\left\langle|\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}|^{4}\right\rangle=\left\langle\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}^{4}\right\rangle=O(nd^{-2}+n\kappa)
as derived above. Then since each component of
\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}
,
\bm{x}_{j}-\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}
are contained in
[-1,1],[-2,2]
, respectively, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n(m-1)}\left\langle O(|\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}|^{4}),2c\hat{\bm{z}}_{m},\bm{x}_{j}-\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}\right\rangle=\frac{4c}{n(m-1)}O(\left\langle|\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}|^{4}\right\rangle)=O(d^{-2-\epsilon/4}).

Finally for term (
10
), by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\langle|\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}|^{3}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\hat{z}_{m,i}|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{z}_{m,i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{z}_{m,i}^{4}\right)^{1/2}=\frac{1}{n}\left\langle\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}^{2}\right\rangle^{1/2}\left\langle\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}^{4}\right\rangle^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}O(nd^{-1})^{1/2}\cdot O(nd^{-2}+n\kappa)^{1/2}=O(d^{-1-\epsilon/8}),

and so we may bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n(m-1)}\left\langle\phi(\hat{\bm{z}}_{m})-\bm{x}_{m},O(|\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}|^{3}),\bm{x}_{j}-\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}\right\rangle=\frac{4}{n(m-1)}O(\left\langle|\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}|^{3}\right\rangle)=O(d^{-2-\epsilon/8}).

From (
7
)-(
10
) we conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{j,m}}(\mathbf{W})=-\frac{2c}{(m-1)^{2}}1_{\{\mathsf{p}[j]=m\}}+O(d^{-2-\epsilon/8}),

and the same result applies to the approximate gradient
\widetilde{\nabla}_{w_{j,m}}L
at initialization since the cutoff does not apply and each component of the noise is bounded by
O(d^{-2-\epsilon/8})
.

Concentration of softmax scores.

Taking
\eta=d^{2+\epsilon/16}
, the updated weights
\mathbf{W}^{(1)}=-\eta\widetilde{\nabla}L(\mathbf{W})
become

\displaystyle w_{j,m}^{(1)}=\frac{2cd^{2+\epsilon/16}}{(m-1)^{2}}1_{\{\mathsf{p}[j]=m\}}+O(d^{-\epsilon/16}).

In particular, for each
j\neq\mathsf{c}_{1}[m],\mathsf{c}_{2}[m]
the softmax scores satisfy

\sigma_{j}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})=e^{w_{j,m}^{(1)}}/\textstyle\sum_{\alpha}e^{w_{\alpha,m}^{(1)}}\leq e^{w_{j,m}^{(1)}-w_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m],m}^{(1)}}\leq\exp(-\Omega(d^{\epsilon/16})).

As softmax scores must sum to
1
, it holds that
\sigma_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})+\sigma_{\mathsf{c}_{2}[m]}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(d^{\epsilon/16}))
and moreover

\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})}{\sigma_{\mathsf{c}_{2}[m]}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})}=e^{w_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m],m}^{(1)}-w_{\mathsf{c}_{2}[m],m}^{(1)}}\leq\exp(O(d^{-\epsilon/16}))\leq 1+O(d^{-\epsilon/16})

from the inequality
e^{t}\leq 1+O(t)
for small
t>0
. By symmetry,
\sigma_{\mathsf{c}_{2}[m]}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})/\sigma_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})\leq 1+O(d^{-\epsilon/16})
. By simple algebraic manipulation, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-O(d^{-\epsilon/16})\leq\sigma_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)}),\sigma_{\mathsf{c}_{2}[m]}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})\leq\frac{1}{2}+O(d^{-\epsilon/16}).

That is, the updated attention layer
\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}^{(1)}=\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})\bm{x}_{j}
has learned to take the average of the two child nodes and ignore the remaining input tokens at each step.

Evaluating the forward pass.

Now to bound the updated prediction loss, we evaluate the error
\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}_{m}^{(1)}-\bm{x}_{m}\rVert_{\infty}
of each step of the forward pass for
d+1\leq m\leq d+k-1
. More precisely, define the increasing sequence

\epsilon_{m}=\max_{d<j\leq m}\left\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}_{j}^{(1)}-\bm{x}_{j}\right\rVert_{\infty},\quad\epsilon_{d}=0.

Then

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]}^{(1)}-\bm{x}_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]}\right\rVert_{\infty},\left\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{c}_{2}[m]}^{(1)}-\bm{x}_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]},\epsilon_{\mathsf{c}_{2}[m]}\leq\epsilon_{m-1},

and for the intermediate values
\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}^{(1)}
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}^{(1)}-\frac{\bm{x}_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]}+\bm{x}_{\mathsf{c}_{2}[m]}}{2}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left\lVert\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}^{(1)}-\frac{\hat{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]}^{(1)}+\hat{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{c}_{2}[m]}^{(1)}}{2}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\epsilon_{m-1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathsf{p}[j]\neq m}\sigma_{j}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})+\left\lvert\sigma_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})-\frac{1}{2}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\sigma_{\mathsf{c}_{2}[m]}(\bm{w}_{m}^{(1)})-\frac{1}{2}\right\rvert+\epsilon_{m-1}

\displaystyle\leq 2d\exp(-\Omega(d^{\epsilon/16}))+O(d^{-\epsilon/16})+\epsilon_{m-1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}d^{-\epsilon/16}+\epsilon_{m-1},

for some constant
C_{1}>0
. Since
\phi
behaves like a quadratic near
0,\pm 1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\epsilon_{m}=\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}_{m}^{(1)}-\bm{x}_{m}\rVert_{\infty}=\left\lVert\phi(\hat{\bm{z}}_{m}^{(1)})-\phi\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{\mathsf{c}_{1}[m]}+\bm{x}_{\mathsf{c}_{2}[m]}}{2}\right)\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{2}(C_{1}d^{-\epsilon/16}+\epsilon_{m-1})^{2}

for some constant
C_{2}>0
depending only on
\phi
. Then for sufficiently large
d

C_{3}
such that
C_{2}(C_{1}+C_{3}d^{-\epsilon/16})^{2}\leq C_{3}
, for
\epsilon_{m-1}\leq C_{3}d^{-\epsilon/8}
it follows that

\displaystyle\epsilon_{m}\leq C_{2}(C_{1}d^{-\epsilon/16}+C_{3}d^{-\epsilon/8})^{2}\leq C_{3}d^{-\epsilon/8},

thus
\epsilon_{m}=O(d^{-\epsilon/8})
inductively for all
m
. We conclude that
\lVert\hat{\bm{y}}-\bm{y}\rVert_{\infty}=\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}_{d+k-1}^{(1)}-\bm{x}_{d+k-1}^{(1)}\rVert_{\infty}
is bounded for all inputs as
O(d^{-\epsilon/8})
. ∎

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=d+1}^{d+k-1}\lVert\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}f_{\alpha}^{\times}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{v}\sum_{\alpha=d_{\ell-1}+1}^{d_{\ell}}\lVert\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}f_{\alpha}^{\times}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{v}\sum_{\alpha=d_{\ell-1}+1}^{d_{\ell}}\sum_{m=d+1}^{d+k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\left\lvert\frac{\partial\hat{x}_{\alpha}}{\partial w_{j,m}}\right\rvert^{2}

We first provide a concentration bound for the augmented data, which we take to hold throughout the proof by conditioning on the high probability event.

Lemma 10

(concentration of augmented data)
.

For
n^{\prime}=\poly(d)
, with probability
1-e^{-\Omega(\sqrt{d})}
over random sampling of the augmented data
\bm{u}_{1},\cdots,\bm{u}_{d}
, it holds that
\lVert\bm{u}_{j}+\bm{1}_{n^{\prime}}\rVert_{\infty}=2
for all

and

\max_{0\leq\ell\leq v}\left\lVert\frac{1}{d_{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\bm{x}_{j}^{+}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq O(d^{-1/4}).

Proof.

The nodes
x_{d_{\ell-1}+1},\cdots,x_{d_{\ell}}
at each level
\ell
compute independent parities, even though parities at different levels can be correlated. By Hoeffding’s inequality and union bounding over coordinates, it follows that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{j=d_{\ell-1}+1}^{d_{\ell}}\bm{x}_{j}^{+}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2(d_{\ell}-d_{\ell-1})\log\frac{2n^{\prime}}{p}}=2^{\frac{v-\ell+1}{2}}\sqrt{\log\frac{2n^{\prime}}{p}}

with probability at least
1-p
. Again union bounding, the above holds for all levels
0\leq\ell\leq v
simultaneously with probability at least
1-vp
, so that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{1}{d_{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\bm{x}_{j}^{+}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=0}^{\ell}\frac{2^{\frac{v-\ell^{\prime}+1}{2}}}{d}\sqrt{\log\frac{2n^{\prime}}{p}}\leq 2(\sqrt{2}+1)\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\log\frac{2n^{\prime}}{p}}=O(d^{-1/4})

for all
\ell
if
p=e^{-\sqrt{d}}
. In addition, the probability that
\bm{u}_{j}=-\bm{1}_{n^{\prime}}
for some

is bounded by
2d\cdot 2^{-n^{\prime}}=e^{-\Omega(d)}
; otherwise, at least one entry is equal to
2
.

it holds that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}^{(t)}}{\partial w_{j,m}}\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\phi^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\sum_{\beta=1}^{d_{t}}\sigma_{\beta}(\bm{w}_{\alpha}^{(t)})\left\lVert\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{x}}_{\beta}^{(t)}}{\partial w_{j,m}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert\phi^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\xi_{j,m,t}\leq O(\exp(-Cd^{\epsilon/16})).

Thus we have for all
\alpha
with
\mathsf{h}[\alpha]\leq t+1
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{j,m}}\left(\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}^{(t)}-\bm{x}_{\alpha}\rVert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left\langle\hat{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}^{(t)}-\bm{x}_{\alpha},\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}^{(t)}}{\partial w_{j,m}}\right\rangle\leq 2\left\lVert\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}^{(t)}}{\partial w_{j,m}}\right\rVert_{\infty}=O(\exp(-Cd^{\epsilon/16})),

and the nodes on level
t+2
or above are zeroed out due to the filter. Hence the gradient signal is exponentially small and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{w_{j,m}}L(\mathbf{W}^{(t)},\mathbf{U})=O(d\exp(-Cd^{\epsilon/16}))+O(d^{-2-\epsilon/8}),

so that
w_{j,m}^{(t+1)}=\mathsf{r}[w_{j,m}^{(t)}+O(d^{-\epsilon/16})]=w_{j,m}^{(t)}
. This concludes the proof of (
11
).

Finally, after time
t=v
the weights at all levels have been updated, so that repeating the analysis of the forward pass yields that

\lVert\hat{\bm{y}}_{\test}-\bm{y}_{\test}\rVert_{\infty}=\left\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}_{d+k-1}^{(t)}-\bm{x}_{d+k-1}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{v}\leq\exp(-Cd^{\epsilon/16}),

as was to be shown. ∎

Appendix D
Experimental Details

For the transformer architecture, the feedforward layer was fixed to the following piecewise quadratic link function:

\displaystyle\phi(t)=\begin{cases}-4t^{2}-8t-3&t\in[-1,-0.5)\\
4t^{2}-1&t\in[-0.5,0.5)\\
-4t^{2}+8t-3&t\in[0.5,1].\end{cases}

For all CoT models, learning rates were fixed to
\eta=15,50,100
for
k=8,16,32
. For the direct model, the learning rate was scaled to
0.01\eta
to ensure stability of training. For the self-consistency model, filtering was done through an equivalent weight-based filter, which checks if any softmax score exceeds a threshold value, here set to 0.4. Moreover, we found that adding a 10% fraction of the gradient from the prediction loss to that of the CoT loss resulted in more stable training.
