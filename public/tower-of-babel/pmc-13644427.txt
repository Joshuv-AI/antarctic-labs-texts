Oral delivery of recombinant Lactococcus lactis expressing an IL-23 inhibitor as a potential therapeutic strategy for experimental colitis
Authors: Eliska Kopecna, Leona Raskova Kafkova, Petr Kosztyu, Kristyna Sloupenska, Katerina Zachova, Alica Cutkova, Jozef Skarda, Jana Vaculova, Milan Kuchar, Natalya Panova, Stephen Walimbwa, Tina Vida Plavec, Ales Berlec, Petr Maly, Milan Raska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterleukin 23 (IL-23) is a key mediator of immune-mediated intestinal inflammation and plays an important role in the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease. Inhibition of IL-23 signaling has therefore been validated as a therapeutic target in IBD. Here, we tested small binding proteins of non-immunoglobulin origin targeting either the IL-23 cytokine p19 subunit or the IL-23 receptor subunit IL-23R, designated ILP and REX binders, respectively. ILP317 and REX115 variants of formerly identified protein collections were tested in murine experimental colitis. Lactococcus lactis bacteria engineered for ILP317 or REX115 expression were administered to experimental mice via yogurt-coated food pellets before and during dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) and trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS)-induced colitis. To compare both protein blockers, ILP317 and REX115 binders were administered intraperitoneally as soluble recombinant proteins. General health status, colon macroscopic and histological changes, mucosal tissue cell infiltration, local and systemic inflammatory cytokines were analyzed. In DSS- and TNBS-induced colitis, intraperitoneal administration of soluble ILP317 or REX115 reduced disease severity, including disease activity index, colon shortening, and mucosal inflammatory cell infiltration. Oral administration of recombinant L. lactis expressing ILP317 also showed consistent protective effects, whereas oral L. lactis expressing REX115 produced less pronounced effects in several experimental settings. Overall, ILP317 appeared more effective than REX115 when delivered orally by recombinant L. lactis. Oral administration of non-colonizing L. lactis expressing an IL-23p19 inhibitor may represent a promising preclinical strategy for the development of novel, non-invasive approaches to modulate intestinal inflammation.Key words: IL-23, small binding protein, experimental colitis, recombinant Lactococcus lactis , oral therapy

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract, characterised by persistent intestinal inflammation and tissue injury. Its pathogenesis involves the coordinated activity of innate immune cells, as well as T and B lymphocytes (Xavier and Podolsky 2007). IBD includes Crohn’s disease (CD), ulcerative colitis (UC), and indeterminate colitis (Geremia and Jewell 2012). Elevated levels of IL-23 are considered a key driver in IBD development (Friedrich et al. 2019). Increased IL-23 level has been associated with enhanced inflammatory responses in several murine colitis models, including dextran sulphate sodium (DSS)- and trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS)-induced colitis (Cox et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2016). Under non-diseased conditions, IL-23 contributes to gut epithelial barrier integrity, limits epithelial permeability, and supports mucosal healing, partly by stimulating Th17 and innate lymphoid cells 3 (ILC3) to release cytokines such as IL-17 and IL-22 (Verstockt et al. 2023).

In murine models of colitis, IL-23 is produced by mucosal resident and inflammatory monocyte/macrophages, as well as by intestinal epithelial cells, in response to diverse triggers, including signals from commensal and pathogenic microbiota, activated dendritic cells, and damaged intestinal epithelial cells (Uhlig et al. 2006; Ivanov et al. 2009; Cox et al. 2012; Macho-Fernandez 2015; Fung et al. 2016). IL-23 is essential for the induction of murine intestinal inflammation, which is mediated by both innate and adaptive immune mechanisms. Global deletion of IL-23 or its receptor resulted in resistance to autoimmune and inflammatory disorders in many murine models (Cox et al. 2012). High susceptibility to murine TNBS-induced colitis was also associated with IL-23p19 deficiency. Thus, IL-23 exhibits not only pro-inflammatory, but also anti-inflammatory receptor-mediated effects in a context dependent manner (Oppmann et al. 2000; Becker et al. 2006). The IL-23 receptor is a complex of IL-12Rβ1 and IL-23R (IL-23Rα) subunits. The IL-23R subunit has been identified as an important susceptibility gene in Crohn’s disease by genome-wide association studies (Duerr et al. 2006; Van Limbergen et al. 2009; Sewell and Kaser 2022). IL-23R is expressed and signals in many cells including Th17 cells, CD8+ T cells, γδ T cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, intestinal epithelial cells, and ILC3.

In our study, we used two colitis models - DSS- and TNBS- induced colitis. DSS is a water-soluble, negatively charged sulfated polysaccharide that directly disrupts the epithelial integrity of the colon, leading to the development of severe colitis that most closely resembles human UC (Okayasu et al. 1990). TNBS is administered in an ethanol solution, where ethanol disrupts the intestinal barrier integrity and facilitates the interaction of haptenating TNBS with autologous colon tissue proteins or microbiota-derived proteins, resulting in significant activation of innate and T cells-mediated immune responses (Morris et al. 1989). In both DSS- and TNBS-induced colitis, disruption of epithelial barrier integrity promotes the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and increases exposure of mucosal immune cells to microbial signals. DAMPs generated during epithelial injury activate intestinal epithelial cells, resident phagocytes, dendritic cells, and infiltrating myeloid cells, leading to enhanced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-18, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-12, and IL-23 (Kontoyiannis et al. 1999; Siegmund et al. 2001; Sedhom et al. 2013; Hunter and Jones 2015). If this initial inflammatory response is not resolved, sustained production of these mediators supports recruitment and activation of innate immune cells and promotes pathogenic Th17-associated responses. In particular, prolonged IL-23 signalling contributes to the maintenance of pathogenic Th17 cells, epithelial damage, altered barrier integrity, and suppression of regulatory mechanisms, thereby amplifying intestinal inflammation. Thus, epithelial injury-driven cytokine induction in DSS- and TNBS-induced colitis provides a relevant experimental setting to evaluate therapeutic strategies targeting the IL-23/IL-23R axis. The importance of this pathway has been confirmed not only in these models, but also in other murine models of intestinal inflammation, where pharmacological inhibition of IL-23 or IL-23R reduced colitis activity (Kullberg et al. 2006; Elson et al. 2007; Cox et al. 2012; Guan et al. 2018).

Based on experimental colitis data, antibodies targeting IL-12p40 were tested and finally clinically approved for CD therapy (Feagan et al. 2017). In addition, monoclonal antibodies brazikumab, guselkumab, mirikizumab, and risankizumab targeting IL-23p19 were also approved for CD therapy (Feagan et al. 2017; Sands et al. 2017; McDonald et al. 2022).

Here, we used small binding proteins (binders) of non-immunoglobulin origin, targeting either the IL-23p19 subunit (binder ILP317) or IL-23R subunit (binder REX115) previously demonstrated as inhibitors of IL-23-mediated signalling using in vitro and ex vivo experiments (Kuchar et al. 2014; Krizova et al. 2017). Unlike conventional monoclonal antibodies, these small non-immunoglobulin binders represent a distinct class of targeting molecules with potential advantages, including reduced molecular size, engineering flexibility, and suitability for recombinant expression in microbial delivery systems. Their use therefore provides a novel strategy for local modulation of the IL-23 pathway directly within the gastrointestinal tract. To enable oral delivery, we generated recombinant L. lactis bacteria expressing either REX115 or ILP317 on their surface (Skrlec et al. 2018; Plavec et al. 2019) as a non-colonising, non-invasive system for delivery of active pharmaceutical ingredient to the gastrointestinal tract (Medici et al. 2025). Compared with systemic administration of purified proteins or monoclonal antibodies, this approach may allow transient local production and presentation of therapeutic binders in the intestinal lumen, thereby increasing mucosal exposure while potentially limiting systemic side effects. Moreover, recombinant L. lactis may help overcome major limitations associated with oral administration of protein-based therapeutics, particularly their reduced stability in the stomach and duodenum. Since the L. lactis prepared by our team can produce binders in vivo and the binders are stable, our blockers have a great potential for per os administration, as they can overcome the obstacles associated with the limited stability of proteins in the stomach and duodenum after oral administration. This strategy is particularly relevant in the context of the dual role of IL-23 in the intestine. Under homoeostatic conditions, IL-23 can contribute to epithelial barrier integrity, mucosal repair, and host defence. However, excessive or dysregulated IL-23 signalling promotes inflammatory responses and has been implicated in the pathogenesis of IBD and experimental colitis. Therefore, local and transient inhibition of the IL-23 pathway may provide a more targeted approach to reduce pathogenic inflammation while minimising broad systemic interference with IL-23-associated protective functions.

We compared the effectiveness of per os administration of a yogurt containing L. lactis bacteria expressing ILP317 or REX115 binders with intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration of respective ILP317 or REX115 proteins. We conclude that i.p. administered ILP317 or REX115 proteins are able to mitigate DSS- or TNBS-induced colitis by reducing colon tissue inflammation. Furthermore, oral administration of L. lactis expressing ILP317 demonstrated comparable efficacy, supporting recombinant L. lactis-mediated delivery of small IL-23-targeting binders as a promising preclinical strategy for the development of non-invasive approaches to modulate intestinal inflammation.

Methods

Bacterial cultivation and protein expression

L. lactis strains containing pSD-REX115-FLAG and pSD-ILP317-Myc (Zadravec et al. 2015; Skrlec et al. 2018; Plavec et al. 2019; Plavec et al. 2023) were grown in M17 medium (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) supplemented with 0.5% glucose and 10 µg/ml chloramphenicol (GM17) without agitation at 30 °C. Protein expression was induced with 25 ng/ml of nisin (Merck) (Berlec and Strukelj 2009; Berlec et al., 2012Berlec et al. 2017) for 3 hours at an optical density A600 = 0.50. Bacterial cultures, both nisin-induced and non-induced, were stored at 4 °C for up to 5 days. Protein expression was monitored by western blot using a mouse monoclonal anti-FLAG tag antibody (1:1,000) (GenScript Biotech, NJ, U.S.A.) for REX115-FLAG and an anti-Myc tag mouse monoclonal antibody, clone 4A6 (1:1,000) (Merck), for ILP317-Myc. Proteins were visualised using a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse Ig antibody (1:1,000) (BD Pharmingen, Franklin Lakes, NJ, U.S.A.). Images were captured using a SynGene G:BOX Chemi XX9 device (Syngene, Cambridge, UK).

Flow cytometry analysis of L. lactis expressing ILP317 or REX115 isolated from pellets

One stool pellet was collected from each mouse directly into a tube, soaked in PBS for 20 min, resuspended, and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min. Subsequently, 100 μl of the supernatant was transferred to a new tube and centrifuged at 6,000 × g for 10 min. The resulting pellets were washed with PBS and stained with anti-Myc tag FITC antibody, clone 9E10 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, U.S.A.), for detection of L. lactis expressing ILP317 (L. lactis ILP317), or with anti-FLAG tag FITC antibody, clone OctA-Probe H-5 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), for detection of L. lactis expressing REX115 (L. lactis REX115). Samples were analysed using a SONY SP6800 Spectral Analyser (Sony Biotechnology Inc., CA, U.S.A.).

Ethical approval statement and license numbers, experimental design

Animals were housed in the animal facility of the Faculty of Medicine and Dentistry, Palacký University Olomouc, which is approved by the Ministry of Agriculture of the Czech Republic for the housing and handling of laboratory animals (approval No. MZE-45559/2022-13143, valid until October 11, 2027). Animals were maintained in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and institutional animal care standards. All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine and Dentistry, Palacký University Olomouc, and by the Ministry of Education, Youth and Sports of the Czech Republic under animal license number MSMT-10947/2021-3. All experiments were conducted in accordance with EU Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes.

Eight- to nine-week-old female C57BL/6 or BALB/c mice (AnLab, Prague, Czech Republic), weighing 18–22 g before treatment, were maintained under standardised conditions at 21–22 °C, with a 12/12-h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were allowed to acclimatise for 1 week before the start of the experiments (Table S2 and S3). Sample size justification and power analysis are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups at the beginning of the study. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were established a priori. Animals were excluded from the colon analysis if they died during the study or required premature humane euthanasia due to welfare considerations or clinical necessity. No individual data points were excluded from the analysis unless valid measurement of the primary endpoint was not possible. To minimise bias during data acquisition and analysis, each mouse was identified only by a unique numerical code throughout the experiment. All downstream analyses, including clinical evaluation, histological assessment, cytokine measurements, and flow cytometry analyses, were performed using these coded identifiers without knowledge of the corresponding treatment groups. Group allocation was revealed only after completion of data analysis.

Induction, termination, and sample processing in mouse models of acute colitis

DSS-induced colitis. Acute DSS-induced colitis was induced in C57BL/6 mice by oral administration of 2.5% (w/v) DSS (MW approximately 40 kDa; TdB Labs, Uppsala, Sweden) in drinking water for 4 days.

TNBS-induced colitis. BALB/c mice were sensitised 7 days before TNBS colitis induction by epicutaneous application of 100 µl of 2.5% TNBS (Merck) in 50% EtOH onto shaved abdominal skin. Colitis was induced under anaesthesia with ketamine/xylazine (100 mg/kg ketamine and 5 mg/kg xylazine, i.p.; Bioveta, Ivanovice na Hané, Czech Republic) by rectal administration of 100 µl of 2.5% TNBS in 50% EtOH. Two days later, the experiment was terminated.

Sample collection. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under ketamine/xylazine anaesthesia. Blood was collected, and the colon was removed, photographed, and its length from the caecum to the proximal rectum was measured. The terminal colon was dissected for histochemistry, quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR), and immune cell quantification by flow cytometry (Figure S1).

Oral administration of L. lactis

Preparation of food for one mouse: 2 × 109 CFU of recombinant or wild type L. lactis were mixed with 3 g of white yogurt (2.7% fat; OLMA, Olomouc, Czech Republic) supplemented with 100 ng of nisin. 5 g of regular chow were coated with prepared yogurt mixture. Mice were housed in groups of three or four animals per cage (for a total of 10 mice per experimental group). In combination with ad libitum access to a sufficient amount of food supplemented with yogurt, this housing arrangement ensured comparable access to diet intake among animals and minimised variability in oral intake between mice. A freshly prepared mixture was provided to the mice every morning.

In two experimental groups, L. lactis expressing ILP317 or REX115 was administered by gavage at 2 × 109 cells per mouse as a single daily dose.

Administration of ILP317, REX115 binders

Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 25 μg of binder (REX115 or ILP317) diluted in 100 μl of sterile PBS daily. The selected doses of ILP317 and REX115 were based on our previous studies describing the inhibitory effects of binders targeting IL-22 (Kuchař et al. 2024) and IL-6 (Groza et al. 2024). In these studies and the applied binder concentration (25 µg/mouse/dose) consistently demonstrated effective pathway inhibition and biological activity; therefore, the same concentration range was used in the present experiment.

Disease activity index calculation

Disease Activity Index calculation (DAI) describing body weight decrease, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding was calculated as reported by Morris et al. (1989) Table 1. DAI scores were used to evaluate the severity of DSS- and TNBS-induced colitis. The DAI index was calculated as a sum of scores for body weight decrease, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding.

Measurement of serum cytokine concentration

The concentration of cytokines IL-2, IL-10, IL-12p40, IL-13, IL-17A, IL-18, IL-22, IL-23 and IFN-α in serum was measured using fluorescent microbeads (LEGENDplex™ Custom Mouse Assay, Biolegend, CA, U.S.A.) conjugated with the particular antibody according to the manufacturer ´ s instructions. The beads were analysed using a spectral cell analyser SONY SP6800 (Sony Biotechnology Inc, CA, U.S.A.).

RNA isolation and reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Colon tissues were stored in RNAlater (Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, U.S.A.) until RNA extraction. Pre-weighed samples were homogenised using QIAshredder (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and total RNA was extracted using RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). Column DNase treatment (gDNA Eliminator spin column, Qiagen) was used to eliminate potential DNA contamination. RNA purification was performed using a lithium chloride method (Juritsch and Moreau 2019) to avoid inhibitory activity of traces of DSS. qRT-PCR (Gb SG PCR Master Mix, Generi Biotech) was performed in triplicates according to the manufacturer's protocol on LightCycler® 480 System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). All primers used in this study are listed in Table S1. Ct values were normalised to the reference gene, GAPDH, and the relative RNA transcription was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

Histology

Colon tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (Merck), stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Merck), and evaluated by a pathologist blinded to the clinical parameters. Tissue histology was examined using a BX43 microscope equipped with a CCD camera (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Tissue samples were scored for inflammatory cell infiltration, mucosal architecture, and epithelial changes according to Erben et al. (2014), as summarised in Table 2 and 3. Necrosis was classified as focal when involving ≤15% of the affected tissue (score 10) and diffuse when involving >15% of the affected tissue (score 20).

Flow cytometry analysis of intestinal cells

Intestinal tissues were pre-incubated in a pre-digestion solution consisting of Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution supplemented with 4% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 5 mM EDTA, followed by incubation in a digestion solution consisting of D-PBS with Ca2+ and Mg2+, 0.1% collagenase D, 50 U/ml DNase, and 4% FBS. Surface Fc receptors were blocked with TruStain FcX antibody (BioLegend) for 10 min, followed by surface staining with antibodies against CD3-BV785, CD4-BV650, CD90.2-Pacific Blue, CD19-BV570, CD11b-BV750, CD206-PerCP-Cy5.5, CD163-BV421, Ly-6C-BV711, Ly-6G-PE-Cy5, and CD170-PE-Dazzle594 (BioLegend), together with FVD-eFluor506 viability dye (eBioscience, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After permeabilization, intracellular staining was performed using GATA-3-PE-Cy7 and RORγT-PE antibodies (BD Biosciences). Samples were analysed using a SONY SP6800 Spectral Analyser (Sony Biotechnology), and the resulting FCS files were processed using FlowJo 10.10 software (BD Biosciences).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 Software (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, U.S.A.). Statistical differences among multiple independent groups were analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. The analyses were based on ranks, and adjusted P values were taken directly from the GraphPad Prism output. Prism reported approximate rather than exact P values for the Kruskal–Wallis analyses. Tied values and small group sizes were handled according to the standard rank-based procedures implemented in GraphPad Prism. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Assumptions for the Kruskal–Wallis test were considered met, as observations were independent, animals were randomly assigned to groups, and the distributions showed comparable spread across groups.

Results

Binding affinity of ILP317 and REX115 to murine IL-23 and IL-23R

Albumin-binding domain (ABD)-derived small binding proteins were originally selected to target either the human p19 subunit of IL-23 or the human IL-23 receptor as part of the IL-23 receptor signalling complex. The cDNA sequences encoding ILP317 and REX115 were subsequently used to generate genetically modified L. lactis strains secreting or surface-displaying the binders individually (Skrlec et al. 2018; Plavec et al. 2019) or concomitantly (Plavec et al. 2023). Since the present study evaluates these binders in murine models of experimentally induced colitis, we first assessed whether ILP317 and REX115 also interact with the corresponding murine targets.

The binding of ILP317 to murine IL-23 was evaluated using an ELISA-based binding assay. As shown in Figure S2A, ILP317 bound to immobilised murine recombinant IL-23-Fc-IgG in a concentration-dependent manner, whereas only minimal signal was detected in BSA-blocked control wells. The apparent Kd was estimated to be in the range of 30–40 nM, supporting specific binding of ILP317 to murine IL-23 and confirming its suitability for further evaluation in murine colitis models.

To validate REX115 binding to murine IL-23R, binding affinity and kinetics were measured using NIH-3T3 murine fibroblast cells and a LigandTracer Green Line instrument, which enables real-time monitoring of interactions between fluorescently labelled ligands and cell-surface targets. Based on kinetic analysis, the Kd of REX115 binding to murine IL-23R-expressing NIH-3T3 cells was calculated to be approximately 0.1 nM. For comparison, we also analysed binding to COS-7 cells, an African green monkey kidney fibroblast-like cell line, since monkey IL-23R shares high sequence identity with human IL-23R. REX115 binding to COS-7 cells was also detected, although the binding signal was lower than that observed with NIH-3T3 cells, with an estimated Kd of approximately 1 nM. Together, these results demonstrate high-affinity interaction of REX115 with murine IL-23R and support its use in murine in vivo models of colitis (Figure S2B).

Stability of ILP-, REX- expressing recombinant L. lactis bacteria in vitro and in vivo

IL-23 signalling inhibition, production of ILP and REX binders, and their modes of administration are schematically presented in Figure 1. Protein binders ILP317 and REX115 used for intraperitoneal administration were produced in Escherichia coli BL21 as 38 kDa fusion proteins and purified as previously reported (Kuchar et al. 2014; Krizova et al. 2017). L. lactis ILP317 and L. lactis REX115 used for oral administration were cultivated and prepared as described in the Methods section (Skrlec et al. 2018; Plavec et al. 2019). Yogurt served as a vehicle for oral administration of the recombinant L. lactis bacteria. The potential contribution of intrinsic yogurt bacteria on colitis course was considered by using an appropriate control (L. lactis producing no binders in yogurt designated as L. lactis ‘Wild’). During the experiment, the viability of the binder-producing bacteria L. lactis was not affected after 24 hours of storage at 4 °C or 22 °C (Figure S3). To confirm the intra-intestinal viability of L. lactis and the delivery of binders, we isolated recombinant L. lactis from faecal pellets and verified its viability by in vitro cultivation for at least 11 h after chow administration containing L. lactis (Figure S4). We further assessed the presence of recombinant ILP317 and REX115 proteins on the surface of L. lactis isolated from faecal samples by flow cytometry (Figure 2). These analyses confirmed both the viability of L. lactis and the stability of ILP317 and REX115 surface expression within the intestinal tract under both DSS- and TNBS-treatment conditions.

Before termination of the murine experiment, the DAI score was assessed (Figure 3b). Following euthanasia, the colon was dissected, photographed (Figure S5), and its length measured (Figure 3c).

C57BL/6 mice exposed to DSS (No therapy group) or treated with yogurt containing L. lactis wild (L. lactis Wild group) exhibited significantly increased DAI scores compared to Naïve group of animals (from 0 ± 0 in Naïve to 6.5 ± 0.5 in DSS No therapy or 6.4 ± 0.5 in DSS L. lactis Wild group) (Figure 3b). Intraperitoneal treatment with either ILP317 or REX115, as well as oral administration of L. lactis ILP317 or L. lactis REX115, reduced DAI scores from 2.3 ± 1.5 to 3.0 ± 1.3, depending on treatment, corresponding to an approximately 54–65% reduction compared with the DSS No therapy group. This reduction reached statistical significance in the intraperitoneally treated groups. Rectal bleeding, a typical feature of DSS-induced colitis observed in the No therapy and L. lactis Wild groups, was absent in all ILP317- and REX115-treated mice.

DSS-induced intestinal alterations were initially characterised by a reduction in colon length, from 9.1 ± 0.4 cm in Naïve mice to 6.5 ± 0.5 cm in both DSS No therapy and DSS L. lactis Wild groups. Consistent with the DAI scores, colon shortening was markedly attenuated in mice treated i.p. with REX115 or i.p. with ILP317, or orally with L. lactis ILP317. These differences in colon length were statistically significant compared to the DSS No therapy group, except for mice treated with L. lactis REX115 (Figure 3c).

For TNBS-induced colitis, binders were administered to BALB/c mice for 7 consecutive days, with the first dose given five days prior to colitis induction (Figure 3d). Binders were delivered using the same methods as in DSS-induced colitis—i.p. or orally as L. lactis recombinant ILP317 or REX115 cells in yogurt. As a control for the oral route in yogurt, L. lactis expressing ILP317 or REX115 were administered by gavage (2 × 109 cells in a single daily dose).

The No therapy group and the L. lactis Wild group exhibited significantly increased DAI scores compared with Naïve mice, increasing from 0.0 ± 0 in the Naïve group to 7.6 ± 1.6 in the No therapy group and 7.7 ± 1.5 in the L. lactis Wild group. After intraperitoneal delivery, DAI scores decreased to 48% and 52% of the No therapy group for ILP317 and REX115, respectively, whereas oral delivery via L. lactis in yogurt reduced DAI scores to 32% and 42% of the No therapy group for L. lactis ILP317 and L. lactis REX115, respectively (Figure 3e). Administration of L. lactis ILP317 or L. lactis REX115 by gavage resulted in a more moderate decrease in DAI scores, reaching 75% and 69% of the No therapy group, respectively. Anal bleeding, observed in the No therapy and L. lactis Wild groups, was absent in the i.p. ILP317 group and in the groups receiving oral L. lactis expressing ILP317 or REX115.

BALB/c mice exposed to TNBS exhibited significant colon shortening (10.0 ± 1.5 cm in the Naïve group vs. 8.3 ± 0.6 cm in the No therapy group and 7.9 ± 1.0 cm in the L. lactis Wild group). However, colon length did not differ significantly between any of the treatment groups and the No therapy group (Figure 3f).

Discussion

IBD animal models provide a powerful platform to evaluate causal cytokine-targeted therapies. In clinical practice, parenteral monoclonal antibodies against TNF-α and IL-12/IL-23 have proven effective in moderate to severe IBD, improving clinical outcomes, supporting mucosal healing, and prolonging relapse-free periods (Hanauer et al. 2002). In line with these established therapeutic principles, our study demonstrates that selective inhibition of the IL-23/IL-23R axis with recombinant binders (ILP317 and REX115) confers significant protection in experimental colitis. Importantly, oral administration of L. lactis engineered to express these binders represents a convenient and non-invasive alternative to systemic therapies, with expected minimal unwanted systemic reactions and evidence of local therapeutic efficacy.

The safety of L. lactis as a delivery vehicle has been previously demonstrated in murine IBD models and in a phase I clinical trial (Braat et al. 2006). In particular, genetic replacement of the thymidylate synthase gene with an IL-10 construct generated a transgenic L. lactis strain that circumvents safety concerns associated with plasmid-based genetic modification, including the potential dissemination of antibiotic resistance genes, since this engineered L. lactis strain cannot survive in the environment in the absence of thymidine (Braat et al. 2006). Thus, the development of strategies for IBD treatment using genetically modified L. lactis is promising. Furthermore, systemic side effects associated with conventional administration are avoided (Zadravec et al. 2015; Berlec et al. 2017; Plavec et al. 2019, 2023).

Mechanistically, the protective effect of this delivery strategy is supported by the cytokine and immune-cell changes observed in our experimental models. In the context of DSS- and TNBS-induced epithelial injury, the observed reduction in IL-6, IL-10, and IL-22 expression after ILP317 and REX115 treatment may reflect attenuation of the inflammatory cascade initiated by barrier disruption and DAMP-driven mucosal immune activation. The decrease in IL-6 suggests reduced inflammatory signalling and weaker support for Th17-associated responses (Stockinger and Omenetti 2017), whereas lower IL-22 and IL-10 levels may indicate reduced inflammatory burden and a diminished requirement for compensatory tissue-protective and regulatory responses. Together with reduced neutrophil and eosinophil infiltration, these findings support the interpretation that inhibition of the IL-23/IL-23R axis suppresses both cytokine-mediated and cellular components of intestinal inflammation in these acute colitis models (Siddiqui et al. 2010; Arnold et al. 2016). This interpretation is consistent with the known role of sustained IL-23 signalling in promoting pathogenic Th17 responses, epithelial barrier disruption, and recruitment of inflammatory myeloid cells during intestinal inflammation (Powrie et al. 1993; Siegmund et al. 2001; Uhlig et al. 2006; Yen et al. 2006; Schiering et al. 2014; Kvedaraite et al. 2016).

In this study, using DSS and TNBS colitis models, we compared the effects of IL-23- (ILP317) and IL-23R- (REX115) binding inhibitory proteins administered either via i.p. injection of soluble proteins or by oral delivery using yogurt containing recombinant L. lactis engineered to express the individual binders (Skrlec et al. 2018; Plavec et al. 2019). This strategy may circumvent degradation of orally administered naked molecules within the gastrointestinal tract (Wang et al. 2016) and could represent a potentially less expensive alternative to currently investigated oral IL-23-targeting therapies, such as the peptide IL-23R blocker Icotrokinra (Mullard 2025). To validate the feasibility of oral administration, we first confirmed that recombinant L. lactis remained viable for at least 24 hours after preparation of the yogurt-bacteria mixture. Stool analysis further confirmed the presence of L. lactis carrying surface-bound binders in treated animals several hours after administration, consistent with previous reports describing recombinant protein expression by engineered L. lactis (Medici et al. 2025). Functionally, our results demonstrate that both ILP317 and REX115 exert significant anti-inflammatory effects; however, the magnitude and range of these effects differed depending on both the colitis model and the route of administration. Interestingly, several differences between intraperitoneal and oral delivery were observed. Intraperitoneal administration ensures systemic bioavailability of soluble binders and likely enables rapid access to mucosal immune compartments. In contrast, oral administration using recombinant L. lactis may preferentially provide localised delivery at the intestinal mucosal surface, from where the binders must reach the subepithelial compartment, where IL-23-driven inflammatory responses are initiated and maintained. Local production of inhibitory binders directly within the gastrointestinal tract may therefore represent an important advantage by reducing systemic exposure; however, its efficacy may differ between binders targeting IL-23p19 and those targeting IL-23R.

Although this was not addressed experimentally, one possible explanation is that IL-23R-targeting binders may be partially retained at the epithelial surface through interaction with epithelial IL-23R. Although epithelial IL-23R expression is lower or more context-dependent than that observed in immune cells, such interaction could reduce the availability of REX115 for deeper mucosal immune cell populations, including Th17 cells, ILC3s, γδ T cells, MAIT cells, and colonic Tregs (Arnold et al. 2016).

In DSS-induced colitis, treatment with ILP317 or REX115 significantly ameliorated clinical symptoms, particularly after i.p. injection of ILP317 or REX115 or after oral delivery with L. lactis ILP317, including reduced DAI scores, prevention of rectal bleeding, and attenuation of colon shortening. Histologically, these treatments significantly decreased inflammatory cell infiltration, preserved mucosal architecture, and prevented epithelial damage. Consistently, ILP317 and REX115 reduced colonic transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-22, IL-10, and IL-23, with ILP317 demonstrating a more robust effect when delivered orally via L. lactis. Notably, L. lactis ILP317 also decreased neutrophil and eosinophil infiltration in the colon, highlighting its ability to modulate innate immune cell populations locally. Together, these cytokine and immune cell changes support functional modulation of IL-23-associated inflammatory pathways, as IL-23 signalling is closely linked to pathogenic Th17-associated responses, cytokine-mediated amplification of mucosal inflammation, and recruitment of innate immune cells. These findings indicate that both systemic and local IL-23 pathway blockade can suppress DSS-induced epithelial injury and associated inflammation, in line with previous studies showing that IL-23 is essential for neutrophil- and eosinophil-mediated tissue damage in acute colitis (Griseri et al. 2015).

In TNBS-induced colitis with frequent colon tissue necrosis and severe ulceration, all tested treatment methods produced a highly significant protective effect at the histological level. Both ILP317 and REX115, administered either intraperitoneally or orally via L. lactis in yogurt or, as a control of oral administration, by gavage, markedly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, preserved mucosal architecture, and prevented epithelial damage, ulceration and necrosis. The protective effects on tissue structure highlight the efficacy of tested topical therapy.

A less pronounced therapeutic effect of the IL-23R-binding protein REX115 delivered orally via recombinant L. lactis was observed in both DSS- and TNBS-induced colitis models. We hypothesise that this may result from steric limitations associated with the presentation of REX115 on the bacterial surface, potentially restricting efficient interaction with IL-23R expressed on target cells within the intestinal mucosa. In contrast to intraperitoneal administration of soluble REX115 protein, orally delivered REX115 remains anchored to the bacterial surface, which may reduce accessibility to receptor-binding domains in the complex intestinal microenvironment. Although REX115 is attached to peptidoglycan through a C-terminal AcmA anchoring motif and predicted structural models suggest that this configuration does not directly prevent IL-23R interaction (Plavec and Berlec 2020), the length and flexibility of the peptidoglycan-associated linker may nevertheless limit efficient access to the cytokine homology region of IL-23R. Supporting this interpretation, our previous biosensor studies demonstrated that spacer length within the REX115 fusion construct critically influences IL-23R engagement efficiency (Smejkal et al. 2023). These observations suggest that optimisation of linker architecture may further improve the therapeutic efficacy of orally delivered REX115.

Although the present findings are promising, several translational aspects must be considered before clinical application. The use of genetically modified bacteria in humans requires careful evaluation of biosafety, environmental containment, host immune responses, and long-term safety. Previous studies using engineered L. lactis strains demonstrated favourable safety profiles and proposed biological containment strategies limiting environmental persistence. However, while L. lactis itself has already been evaluated as a delivery platform in preclinical and early clinical studies, recombinant strains expressing IL-23- or IL-23R-targeting binders have not yet been clinically tested and therefore require further pharmacological and safety validation.

The route of administration is another important translational consideration. Systemic delivery of soluble cytokine-targeting binders may increase the risk of off-target immune modulation and unwanted interference with physiological IL-23 signalling outside the intestinal compartment. In contrast, orally administered recombinant L. lactis may provide localised production of inhibitory binders directly at the intestinal mucosal surface, thereby reducing systemic exposure while maintaining therapeutic activity at the site of inflammation.

Dose extrapolation also supports the potential feasibility of this approach, although further optimisation will be required. In the present study, mice received approximately 25 μg of inhibitory binder per 20 g of body weight per day, corresponding to approximately 1.25 mg/kg/day. Using standard body surface area normalisation with the FDA-recommended conversion factor for mice (Km = 3) and humans (Km = 37), this corresponds to a human equivalent dose of approximately 0.1 mg/kg/day. For a 75 kg adult, the estimated dose would therefore be approximately 7.5 mg/day. Although this estimated dose appears pharmacologically feasible, further studies will be required to determine the stability, bioavailability, optimal dosing regimen, and persistence of recombinant L. lactis-derived binders within the human gastrointestinal tract under clinical conditions.

Finally, regulatory approval of live biotherapeutic products requires extensive characterisation of genetic stability, manufacturing reproducibility, biological containment, and long-term safety. Future studies should therefore focus on long-term efficacy, detailed mechanistic characterisation, and translational applicability in human IBD, including identification of patient populations that may preferentially benefit from IL-23 versus IL-23R targeting approaches.

Together, these findings support a mechanistic model in which DSS- and TNBS-induced epithelial injury promotes IL-23-associated mucosal inflammation, whereas ILP317- and REX115-mediated interference with the IL-23/IL-23R axis attenuates cytokine-mediated and cellular inflammatory responses, resulting in preserved intestinal architecture and reduced clinical severity of experimental colitis (Figure 8).

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that inhibition of the IL-23/IL-23R axis using ILP317 and REX115 attenuates experimental colitis in murine DSS- and TNBS-induced models. Both soluble binders administered intraperitoneally and recombinant L. lactis-based oral delivery showed anti-inflammatory effects, including improvement of clinical and histological parameters and modulation of inflammatory cytokine expression. Among the orally delivered approaches, L. lactis expressing ILP317 showed more consistent protective effects, likely reflecting efficient neutralisation of soluble IL-23 within the inflamed intestinal environment.

These findings support recombinant L. lactis as a promising preclinical platform for localised mucosal delivery of cytokine-targeting binders. However, the results should be interpreted within the limitations of acute murine colitis models, and further studies are required to assess long-term efficacy, safety, dosing, regulatory feasibility, and translational relevance in human IBD.
