Comparison of nice knot internal fixation with Kirschner wire external fixation and tension band wiring for Lawrence zone I fractures of the fifth metatarsal base: a retrospective case-control study
Authors: Fan Xu, Maoqiang Fan, Chen Fei, Wei Fan, Zhe Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare the efficacy of Nice Knot Internal Fixation with Kirschner Wire External Fixation versus conventional Tension Band Wiring for the treatment of Lawrence zone 1 fractures at the base of the fifth metatarsal bone.MethodsThis study employed a retrospective case-control design, which included 37 patients with Lawrence zone I fractures of the fifth metatarsal base, treated surgically at our department from October 2023 to December 2024. Patients were divided into Group TB, receiving tension band wiring with K-wire internal fixation, and Group NK, undergoing Nice knot internal fixation combined with K-wire external fixation. Foot function was assessed using the Manchester–Oxford Foot Questionnaire (MOXFQ-ch) and the American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) midfoot score at 1, 2, and 3 months post-surgery.ResultsGroup NK included 22 patients (11 males, 11 females; mean age: 39.50 years (range: 18–64)). Group TB comprised 15 patients (6 males, 9 females) with a mean age of 42.33 years (range: 20–62). Both groups exhibited a decline in MOXFQ-ch scores and an improvement in AOFAS midfoot scores. the 2-month postoperative follow-up, Group NK exhibited significantly superior outcomes in the pain domain of the AOFAS score compared to Group TB (p < 0.05). However, at 2 months post-surgery, the mean MOXFQ-ch score of Group NK was lower than that of Group TB (16.27 ± 6.95 < 22.19 ± 5.19, P < 0.05).ConclusionThe method combining Nice knot internal fixation with K-wire external fixation is a straightforward and effective approach for treating Lawrence zone I fractures of the fifth metatarsal base, yielding satisfactory clinical results.

Introduction

Fractures of the base of the fifth metatarsal bone are common injuries and account for approximately 70% of all metatarsal fractures [1]. They often occur during plantar flexion and inversion of the forefoot, such as in sprains, crush injuries, or motor vehicle accidents. When the foot is plantar-flexed and the forefoot is inverted, the peroneus brevis muscle contracts violently, potentially causing a fracture of the base of the fifth metatarsal bone [2, 3]. Previously, these fractures were commonly treated with external fixation using plaster casts [4]. However, after reduction, due to the pull of the peroneus brevis muscle, it is easy for the bone fragment to displace, leading to difficult healing. Improper treatment can alter foot weight-bearing, causing delayed union, nonunion, and long-term complications such as joint pain and functional impairment. Currently, most researchers agree that surgical treatment should be performed when the fracture displacement exceeds 2.0 mm, involves over 30% of the cuboid joint surface of the distal fifth metatarsal bone, or when conservative treatment fails. For Lawrence zone I fractures requiring surgery, previous surgical options have included Kirschner wire (K-wire) tension bands, cannulated screws, mini-plates, and lag screws [5, 6]. However, these methods may cause local skin irritation and require a second surgery to remove the internal fixation, potentially increasing the patient’s physical, emotional, and financial burden.

The Nice knot, a high-tension knot invented by Dr. Pascal-Boileau from Nice, France, is a high-strength, self-sliding, and user-friendly bidirectional sliding knot that has been progressively applied in various orthopedic repair surgeries in recent years (Fig. 1). Its advantages include simplicity, versatility, and reliable fixation, with the key benefit being its ability to maintain constant compression between bone fragments, thereby reducing the risk of separation and improving healing quality [7–10].

This study compared the efficacy of Nice Knot Internal Fixation with Kirschner Wire External Fixation versus conventional Tension Band Wiring for the treatment of Lawrence zone 1 fractures at the base of the fifth metatarsal bone through a retrospective case-control analysis, thereby validating the advantages and generalizability of the former in managing such fractures.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Xi’an Honghui Hospital (Approval Number: 2025-KY-147-01). Informed consent was obtained from all patients and their families.

This study was a single-center, retrospective cohort study. By reviewing our hospital’s patient database, we screened all patients who underwent surgery for fractures of the fifth metatarsal base (Lawrence zone 1) between October 2023 and December 2024. The patients were divided into Group TB and Group NK based on the actual surgical procedure they received for comparison. The choice of surgical technique was based solely on standard clinical protocols and shared decision-making with the patient, rather than randomized allocation. The Group TB received treatment with tension band wiring and internal K-wire fixation, whereas the Group NKunderwent fixation with the Nice knot technique combined with external K-wire fixation. In total, 37 patients (92.5%) were finally included in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: meeting the diagnostic criteria for fractures of the base of the fifth metatarsal bone; having surgical indications with fracture displacement of more than 2 mm or involvement of over 30% of the cuboid joint surface of the fifth metatarsal as shown on radiography; having fresh fractures; and providing informed consent and the consent from the family members. The exclusion criteria were as follows: other metatarsal fractures besides the fifth metatarsal fracture (zone 1); pathological fractures; open fractures; osteoporotic fractures; and lack of medical records or follow-up data. Preoperatively, fracture assessment was performed using anteroposterior and oblique radiography, computed tomography (CT) scans, and three-dimensional reconstruction of the injured foot.

Surgical procedure

All patients were administered general anesthesia or nerve blocking anesthesia. They were placed in the supine position with the affected hip elevated. Standard preoperative procedures, including iodine disinfection, draping, exsanguination, and inflation of a pneumatic tourniquet at the upper one-third of the thigh to 50 kPa or application of an exsanguination band to the calf, were performed.

In Group NK, a longitudinal incision was made over the fifth metatarsal tuberosity. After incising the skin and subcutaneous tissue while sparing the surrounding sural nerve branches, the bone membrane along the fifth metatarsal tuberosity was incised and subperiosteal dissection was carried out. The exposed fracture revealed the fracture ends of the fifth metatarsal tuberosity, with the proximal avulsed bone fragment displaced by contraction of the peroneus brevis tendon. Blood clots and fibrous tissue between the fracture fragments were removed. Using a towel clamp, the avulsed fragment at the base of the fifth metatarsal bone was directly reduced under visualization. Two to three 1.5-mm titanium K-wires were percutaneously inserted from the proximal to the distal end of the tuberosity to fixate the fracture. An additional 1.5-mm K-wire was used to create a bony tunnel by drilling vertically from the dorsal to the plantar side of the fifth metatarsal bone distal to the fracture line. A medium round-bodied needle was then used to pass a double-stranded No. 1 absorbable suture (Ethicon, China) through the bone tunnel. The proximal limbs of the suture were passed around the K-wire and beneath the insertion of the peroneus brevis tendon to construct a figure-of-eight tension band, which was finally secured with a Nice knot (Figs. 2 and 3). Fluoroscopy confirmed fracture reduction. After rinsing with saline, the wound was checked for hemostasis and iatrogenic injuries. The incision was closed in layers with 2/0 absorbable sutures, and a splint was applied to maintain the foot in a functional position.

In Group TB, a similar longitudinal incision was made over the fifth metatarsal tuberosity. After incising the skin and subcutaneous tissue and avoiding the sural nerve branches, the bone membrane was incised and subperiosteal dissection was performed. The exposed fracture showed the fracture ends of the fifth metatarsal tuberosity, with the proximal fragment displaced by the peroneus brevis tendon. Blood clots and fibrous tissue were removed, and the avulsed fragment was reduced under direct vision using a towel clamp. Two to three 1.5-mm titanium K-wires were inserted from the proximal to the distal end of the tuberosity to fixate the fracture. An additional 1.5-mm K-wire was used to create a bony tunnel by drilling vertically from the dorsal to the plantar side of the fifth metatarsal bone distal to the fracture line. A 0.8-mm wire was threaded through the tunnel, passed around the K-wire proximally and through the peroneus brevis tendon insertion, forming a figure-of-eight tension band, which was then tightened (Fig. 4). Fluoroscopy confirmed reduction. The wound was irrigated with saline, checked for hemostasis and injuries, and closed with 2/0 absorbable sutures, and a splint was applied for functional immobilization.

Postoperative management and data collection

Postoperative radiographs were used to assess reduction, fixation, and fracture healing. Both groups followed the same rehabilitation plan. From postoperative day 1, the patients were encouraged to perform active and passive movements of the toes and ankle, including plantar flexion and dorsiflexion, as pain allowed. The sutures were removed at around 2 weeks. In the observation group, the K-wires were removed at 6 to 8 weeks based on radiographic review. Weight-bearing was permitted as follows: partial weight-bearing at 4 weeks, full weight-bearing at 6 to 8 weeks, and full activity upon radiographic confirmation of bony union. All of the patients underwent radiographic follow-up at 1, 2, 3, 6, and 12 months post surgery and at the final follow-up.

Data were collected for both groups, namely: preoperative time (from injury to surgery), hospital stay (from surgery to discharge), surgical duration, incision length, time to full activity, and patient satisfaction. The Manchester–Oxford Foot Questionnaire (MOXFQ-ch) and the American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) midfoot score were used to assess the foot function at 1, 2, and 3 months post surgery. Postoperative complications were also monitored.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or the median (interquartile range), as appropriate. For variables that were approximately normally distributed, comparisons between the groups were made using the independent-samples t test. For non-normally distributed variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Categorical data were presented as percentages (%) and were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (when n < 40 or expected frequency < 1). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Group characteristics

The average follow-up duration was 9 months (range, 4–18 months). A total of 37 patients were included in the study, with three patients excluded. The Group TB consisted of 15 patients, namely six males and nine females, with an age range of 20–62 years and a mean age of 42.33 ± 13.18 years. The duration from injury to surgery ranged from 3 to 15 days, with a mean of 6.27 ± 3.24 days. There were 10 left-sided and five right-sided fractures, and all of the patients in this group underwent fixation with tension band wiring and K-wires. The Group NK included 22 patients, namely 11 males and 11 females, with an age range of 18–64 years and a mean age of 39.50 ± 14.32 years. The duration from injury to surgery ranged from 1 to 15 days, with a mean of 6.18 ± 3.90 days. There were eight left-sided and 14 right-sided fractures, and all of the patients in the group underwent fixation with the Nice knot and K-wires (Fig. 2). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, gender, side of surgery, preoperative time, hospital stay, surgical duration, time to full activity, or patient satisfaction (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Complications

In the Group TB, three patients (20%) reported a sensation of dysesthesia and discomfort in the foot. One patient (6.7%) underwent a second surgery to remove the implant 10 months after the initial surgery. Eight patients (53.3%) indicated that they planned to have a second surgery to remove the implant at the final follow-up, while six patients (40%) stated that they would not undergo further surgery due to intolerance. In the Group NK, all of the patients had the K-wires removed at an average of 7.3 ± 2.08 weeks post surgery. One patient (4.5%) experienced wound exudate at 1 month post surgery, which healed after treatment with medicated soaking. The incidence of implant-related and surgery-related complications differed significantly between the two groups (P < 0.05). No infections or sural nerve injuries were observed.

Outcome scores

The MOXFQ-ch scores decreased over time, while the AOFAS midfoot scores increased in both groups. There were no significant differences in the mean AOFAS midfoot scores between the two groups at 1and 3 months post surgery (P > 0.05). Likewise, there were no significant differences in the mean MOXFQ-ch scores at 1 and 3 months post surgery (P > 0.05). However, at 2 months post surgery, Group NK exhibited significantly superior outcomes in the pain domain of the AOFAS score compared to Group TB (p < 0.05), the mean MOXFQ-ch score was lower in Group NK than Group TB. (16.27 ± 6.95 < 22.19 ± 5.19, P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Discussion

The Nice knot technique combined with K-wire external fixation provides reliable fixation strength compared with the K-wire tension band. It does not require a second surgery for removal, can fixate the fracture as well as the peroneus brevis tendon, involves minimal soft tissue dissection, and avoids further damage to the bone fragments and blood supply. Statistical analysis demonstrated that at the 2-month postoperative follow-up, the Group NK exhibited significantly superior outcomes in the pain domain of the AOFAS score and the MOxFQ-Ch questionnaire compared to the Group TB (p < 0.05). This clinical advantage may be primarily attributable to the earlier removal of Kirschner wires (typically at 6–8 weeks postoperatively) in the Group NK, which eliminates prolonged external fixation irritation, thereby contributing to improved patient comfort and reduced pain perception.

For the most common clinical type, Lawrence zone 1 fractures, treatment remains controversial. It is generally accepted that non-displaced zone I fractures can be managed conservatively with methods such as elastic bandages, casts, or splints. However, a drawback is the extended immobilization period, which carries a risk of nonunion, making this approach less suitable for younger, active populations with high demands on foot function [5, 11]. Consequently, with advancements in sports medicine, some researchers advocate for more proactive surgical management of Zone I fractures [12]. Attia AK et al. [13], in a systematic review and meta-analysis of 718 fractures, demonstrated the role of biologically augmented fixation in promoting union for fifth metatarsal fractures, particularly Jones fractures in athletes. Some scholars, through randomized controlled trials comparing surgical and conservative treatment, have observed that surgically treated patients can return to work earlier [14], with a significantly shorter time to bony union [15]. Various surgical techniques exist [16], such as Kirschner wire (K-wire) tension band, cannulated screw, double-headed compression screw, and locking plate fixation. These often require a second surgery for hardware removal, increasing the risk of infection and patient costs. The K-wire tension band technique is applicable to all types of fifth metatarsal base fractures and is relatively straightforward to perform. However, it has inherent disadvantages: prominent wire ends can cause skin irritation, pain, and discomfort, along with complications such as soft tissue cut-out and wire migration [17]. The postoperative discomfort observed in the tension band group in this study is consistent with literature reports. Lutsky et al. [17], in a retrospective study, found a complication rate of 7–18% associated with K-wire use, necessitating secondary hardware removal and increasing costs. Given that the fracture fragment in Lawrence zone 1 is often thin and small, cannulated screws, double-headed compression screws, or locking plates may lose purchase and compression, leading to fixation failure. Furthermore, the skin and soft tissue overlying the fifth metatarsal are thin; bulky internal implants like plates and screws occupying subcutaneous space can cause a palpable foreign body sensation, potentially leading to localized stiffness and pain [18]. In this study, Group NK could have the percutaneous K-wires removed at 6–8 weeks postoperatively, significantly reducing patient discomfort. Consequently, at 2 months post-surgery, Group NK exhibited significantly superior outcomes in the pain domain of the AOFAS score compared to Group TB (p < 0.05), and the mean MOXFQ-ch score was lower in Group NK than in Group TB. The Nice knot is a unidirectional sliding knot with dual sutures, known for its excellent strength, adjustability, simplicity of application, and versatility [10, 19]. By pulling the free ends forcefully, the knot slides and sequentially tightens, achieving high tension and maintaining stable fixation under load over time. The sliding and compression of the Nice knot during tightening facilitates compression at the fracture site, reducing the probability of fragment separation and thereby potentially improving the union rate. Studies by Boileau et al. [7] and Collin et al. [8] have confirmed its reliable biomechanical performance in tendon/ligament repair and bone fragment fixation, particularly its effectiveness in reducing knot elongation risk under dynamic strain. We applied this knot to fifth metatarsal fractures. Its sliding compression helps minimize fracture gap formation, potentially enhancing healing rates. Simultaneously, when combined with K-wire external fixation, it avoids the subcutaneous placement of plates or large screws, thereby circumventing the foreign body sensation and pain associated with thin soft tissue coverage. It also addresses the problem of traditional internal fixation devices losing hold in small fracture fragments.

Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study, despite its preliminary demonstration of the advantages of The Nice knot internal fixation combined with K-wire external fixation. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, the follow-up period was relatively short, and thus the study lacks an assessment of long-term fracture healing, sustained functional recovery, and late-stage complications such as arthritis. Future prospective, multi-center studies with large sample sizes and extended follow-up periods are warranted to further validate and optimize the clinical applicability of this technique.

Conclusions

The Nice knot internal fixation combined with K-wire external fixation method is a simple, minimally invasive, and effective treatment for fifth metatarsal base fractures. It eliminates the need for secondary removal of internal fixation, reducing patient discomfort and economic burden, and is suitable for zone I fractures, yielding satisfactory clinical outcomes.
