Generalized rational Prony and Bernoulli methods Publication type: Research Paper
Tamás Dózsa 1,2 \orcid 0000-0003-0919-4385, Matthias Voigt 1 \orcid 0000-0001-8491-1861, Zoltán Szabó 2 \orcid 0000-0001-6183-7603, József Bokor 2 \orcid 0000-0002-3461-4807 and Péter Kovács 3 \orcid 0000-0002-0772-9721

Abstract
The generalized operator-based Prony method is an important tool for describing signals which can be written as finite linear combinations of eigenfunctions of certain linear operators. On the other hand, Bernoulli’s algorithm and its generalizations can be used to recover the parameters of rational functions belonging to finite-dimensional subspaces of H 2 H_{2} Hardy-Hilbert spaces. In this work, we discuss several results related to these methods. We discuss a rational variant of the generalized operator-based Prony method and show that in fact, any Prony problem can be treated this way. This realization establishes the connection between Prony and Bernoulli methods and allows us to address some well-known numerical pitfalls. Several numerical experiments are provided to showcase the usefulness of the introduced methods. These include problems related to the identification of time-delayed linear systems and parameter recovery problems in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

The generalized operator-based Prony method

We shall begin with a short review of the generalized operator-based Prony (GOP) method as introduced in
stampfer2020generalized
. Whenever possible we will follow the notations introduced in
peter2013generalized
and
stampfer2020generalized
. For an excellent and deeper discussion of the GOP method, we recommend
stampfer2019generalized
.

2.1
Preliminaries

First, we recite some important definitions and theorems. Consider the Prony problem defined in Eq. (
1
) with all of the assumptions mentioned there. Without loss of generality, one may assume that there exists a linear operator
A:V\to V
, such that the nonlinear parameters
\lambda_{k}
are included among the eigenvalues of
A
. Denote the
active
part of the (point) spectrum of
A
by
\Lambda:=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M}\}
with
\lambda_{k}\neq\lambda_{j}
for all
j\neq k
and consider the set

\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}(A,\Lambda):=\operatorname{span}\{v_{\lambda_{k}}:\ k=1,\ldots,M\}=\left\{f=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}v_{\lambda_{k}}:\ c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

(6)

This set is called
signal space
which is indeed a vector space for any fixed
\Lambda
. However, we will typically assume that
c_{k}\neq 0

(k=1,\ldots,M)
, because then the signal complexity coincides the number of Prony atoms.
In practical cases, not only is
A
assumed to have a point spectrum, but we also assume that
f
is sparse in the sense that at most
M
pairwise distinct eigenvalues belonging to
\mathbb{C}
will be considered. For simplicity, we shall denote both the full and this restricted point spectrum of
A
with
\Lambda(A)
or simply
\Lambda
henceforth and specify the difference whenever necessary. In fact, we shall consider

\Lambda=\Lambda(A):=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{M}\}\subset\mathbb{C}\quad(M\in\mathbb{N})

(7)

and use the notation
\lambda_{k}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
to refer to the nonlinear parameters of Prony signals. Following
stampfer2019generalized
, we shall say that the operator
A

generates
the signal space
\mathcal{M}
, or
A
is a
generator
of
\mathcal{M}
. First we define of iteration operators as introduced in
stampfer2019generalized
;
stampfer2020generalized
:

Definition 2.1

(Iteration operator)
.

Let
V
be a vector space and
A:V\to V
be a generator of the signal space
\mathcal{M}
. Furthermore, let
\varphi:\Lambda(A)\to\mathbb{C}
be injective. When a bounded linear operator

\Psi_{\varphi}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}

satisfies

\Psi_{\varphi}v_{\lambda_{k}}=\varphi(\lambda_{k})v_{\lambda_{k}}\quad(k=1,\ldots,M),

it shall be referred to as an
iteration operator
.

We note again, that in this paper,
\Lambda(A)
denotes the restricted (sparse) spectrum of
A
, for a more general definition, we refer to
(
stampfer2019generalized
, Def. 2.2.4)
. In order to recover the parameters of Prony signals using the GOP method, so-called evaluation schemes need to be defined
stampfer2019generalized
;
stampfer2020generalized
.

Definition 2.2

((Admissible) evaluation scheme)
.

Let
V
be a vector space and
A:V\to V
be a generator of the signal space
\mathcal{M}
.
A sequence
\mathcal{F}:={(\mathcal{F}_{m})}_{m\geq 0}
of linear functionals

\mathcal{F}_{m}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{C}\quad(m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\})

is called an
evaluation scheme
. Furthermore, define
E_{\mathcal{F}}:=\left[\mathcal{F}_{m}(v_{\lambda_{k}})\right]_{m=0,k=1}^{N,M}\in\mathbb{C}^{(N+1)\times M}
. The evaluation scheme is called
admissible
, if for any
N\geq M-1
we have
\rank(E_{\mathcal{F}})=M
.

An important special case is given by
\mathcal{F}_{m}:=\mathfrak{F}\circ\Psi_{m}
, where
\Psi_{m}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
are appropriate linear operators and
\mathfrak{F}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{C}
is a linear functional which satisfies
\mathfrak{F}(v_{\lambda_{k}})\neq 0\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
. Often, such an evaluation scheme is generated by powers of a single iteration operator
\Psi_{\varphi}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{m}:=\mathfrak{F}\circ\Psi_{\varphi}^{m}
. In this case, the evaluation scheme is referred to as a
canonical evaluation scheme
. In
stampfer2019generalized
, Stampfer shows that a canonical evaluation scheme is always admissible.
Using evaluation schemes, one can define so-called sampling schemes
stampfer2019generalized
;
stampfer2020generalized
.

Definition 2.3

((Realizable/admissible) sampling scheme)
.

Consider an evaluation scheme
{(\mathcal{F}_{m})}_{m\geq 0}
and an iteration operator
\Psi_{\varphi}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
acting on the signal space
\mathcal{M}
. The family of maps
{(S_{m,j})}_{m,j\geq 0}
defined by

S_{m,j}:=\mathcal{F}_{m}\circ\Psi_{\varphi}^{j}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{C}\quad(m,j\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\})

is called a
sampling scheme
for
\mathcal{M}
. A sampling scheme is called
admissible
, if
{(\mathcal{F}_{m})}_{m\geq 0}
is admissible. A sampling scheme is called
realizable
, if it can be written as a family of linear functionals that, when applied to
f
(the Prony signal from Eq. (
1
)), can be evaluated without any derivatives of
f
.

Fixing upper indices
N,M
in the sampling scheme allows us to acquire the so-called sampling matrix
\mathcal{X}_{N,M}:=\left[S_{m,j}(f)\right]_{m,j=0}^{N,M}\in\mathbb{C}^{(N+1)\times(M+1)}
.

The key idea behind the GOP method is the following theorem proven by Stampfer and Plonka in
stampfer2020generalized
.

Theorem 2.1

(Generalized operator-based Prony (GOP) method)
.

Let
{(S_{m,j})}_{m,j\geq 0}:={(\mathcal{F}_{m}\circ\Psi_{\varphi}^{j})}_{m,j\geq 0}
be an admissible sampling scheme over the
M
-dimensional signal space
\mathcal{M}
as in (
6
). Then every
f\in\mathcal{M}
is uniquely determined by the sampling matrix

\mathcal{X}_{N,M-1}:=\left[\mathcal{F}_{m}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{j}f)\right]_{m,j=0}^{N,M-1},

(8)

where
N\geq M-1
.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
can be found in
stampfer2019generalized
and
stampfer2020generalized
. We now proceed to demonstrate the GOP algorithm. Consider the so-called Prony polynomial

P_{\Lambda(A)}^{\varphi}(z):=\prod_{k=1}^{M}(z-\varphi(\lambda_{k}))=\sum_{k=0}^{M}p_{k}z^{k}\quad(z\in\mathbb{C}).

(9)

With the conditions of Theorem
2.1
, the so-called annihilation equation holds, i.e.,

\sum_{k=0}^{M}p_{k}\Psi_{\varphi}^{k}f=0.

(10)

Exploiting this, the monic nature of the polynomial defined in Eq. (
9
) (i.e.,
p_{M}=1
), and the admissibility of the evaluation scheme
{(\mathcal{F}_{m})}_{m\geq 0}
, we obtain the Hankel-like system (see
(
stampfer2019generalized
, Lem. 2.3.8)
)

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{0}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{0}f)&\mathcal{F}_{0}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{1}f)&\ldots&\mathcal{F}_{0}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{M-1}f)\\
\mathcal{F}_{1}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{0}f)&\mathcal{F}_{1}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{1}f)&\ldots&\mathcal{F}_{1}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{M-1}f)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathcal{F}_{N}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{0}f)&\mathcal{F}_{N}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{1}f)&\ldots&\mathcal{F}_{N}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{M-1}f)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}\\
p_{1}\\
\vdots\\
p_{M-1}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{0}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{M}f)\\
\mathcal{F}_{1}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{M}f)\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{F}_{N}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{M}f)\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

According to Theorem
2.1
, this system has a unique solution and the zeros
\varphi(\lambda_{k})
for
k=1,\ldots,M
of the polynomial
P_{\Lambda(A)}^{\varphi}
can be recovered using a preferred numerical scheme. Since
\varphi
is injective, the nonlinear parameters
\lambda_{k}
can also be identified. The linear parameters can be recovered using
(
stampfer2019generalized
, Lem. 2.2.14)
. This lemma states that if the conditions of Theorem
2.1
are satisfied and the nonlinear parameters in
\Lambda(A)
have been successfully recovered, then for any choice of
\omega:=(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{N})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N+1}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\Gamma:=\operatorname{diag}\left(\left[\sum_{m=0}^{N}\omega_{m}\mathcal{F}_{m}(v_{\lambda_{k}})\right]_{k=1}^{M}\right)
is invertible, the equation

\mathcal{X}_{N,M-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\omega=\left[\varphi^{j}(\lambda_{k})\right]_{j=0,k=1}^{M-1,M}{[\widetilde{c}_{\lambda_{k}}]}_{k=1}^{M},

(12)

holds, where
{[\widetilde{c}_{\lambda_{k}}]}_{k=1}^{M}:=\Gamma{[c_{\lambda_{k}}]}_{k=1}^{M}
. This is a Vandermonde-like equation for the variable
{[\widetilde{c}_{\lambda_{k}}]}_{k=1}^{M}
which can be poorly conditioned, however methods exist to mitigate this problem (see, e.g.,
higham1988fast
). In this paper, we show how a rational take on the GOP method can be leveraged to introduce a triangular system to recover the linear parameters. Furthermore, we shall empirically verify that this triangular system is often better conditioned than the matrix appearing in Eq. (
12
).

2.2
The GOP method for rational problems

In this section we shortly summarize the main results in
dozsa2025system
which will be important for the results presented in this paper. The motivation behind
dozsa2025system
is to develop a realization of the GOP method, which can be used to identify so-called discrete-time single-input single-output linear time-invariant (SISO LTI) dynamical systems.
These can be characterized by the discrete convolution equation

y=h\ast u,

(13)

where
u,y,h\in\ell
are called the input, the output, and the impulse response of the system, respectively. It is usually assumed, that for a bounded input sequence
u
, the output
y
is also bounded. This is known as bounded-input bounded-output (BIBO) stability
szidar
;
vandenhof
and it coincides with the condition
h\in\ell_{1}
, where
\ell_{1}
denotes the set of absolutely summable complex sequences. Because we deal with unilateral sequences
h
, i.e.,
h_{n}=0
for all
n<0
, we also implicitly assume that the system is causal which means that future input values cannot influence the current output of the system.

Under these assumptions, it can be shown (see, e.g.,
vandenhof
;
dozsa2025system
) that for all
n\geq 0
, the impulse response defining the system behavior can be written as

h_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\overline{\lambda_{k}}^{n},

(14)

where
M\in\mathbb{N}
is known as the minimal system order or McMillan degree.
BIBO stability clearly implies that
|\lambda_{k}|<1\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
. For simplicity, we henceforth assume that
\lambda_{k}\neq\lambda_{j}
if
k\neq j
.

Since according to Eq. (
14
), the impulse response can be written as a weighted sum of complex powers, it might be tempting to apply the classical Prony method to recover the parameters
c_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
. This has indeed been done, for example in
foyen2018prony
. However, the application of the classical Prony scheme comes at the cost of numerical problems and noise intolerance. In order to remedy this, in
dozsa2025system
the authors propose using the GOP method with the following sampling scheme.

First, define the
\mathcal{Z}
-transform of a unilateral complex sequence as

\mathcal{Z}[x](z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x_{n}z^{n}\quad(z\in\mathbb{C}),

(15)

whenever the sum exists. In
dozsa2025system
, the authors propose to consider the system in the frequency domain by applying the
\mathcal{Z}
-transform to both sides of Eq. (
13
), i.e.,

\mathcal{Z}[y](z)=\mathcal{Z}[h\ast u](z)\iff Y(z)=H(z)U(z),

(16)

where
H(z)
is referred to as the transfer function of the system. If the system is causal and BIBO stable with an impulse response satisfying Eq. (
14
), then the transfer function
H(z)=\mathcal{Z}[h](z)
is a rational function that belongs to the Hardy space

H_{2}(\mathbb{D}):=\left\{f\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D}):\ \sup_{r<1}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left|f(r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/2}<\infty\right\},

where
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
denotes the space of complex analytic functions defined on the open unit disk. Note that
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
is a Hilbert space with the inner product

{\langle f,g\rangle}_{H_{2}(\mathbb{D})}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t})\overline{g(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t})}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(17)

Indeed, since
h\in\ell_{1}
, the transfer function
H=\mathcal{Z}[h]
belongs to
H_{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, the set of bounded functions in
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
. The statement thus follows from
H_{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\subset H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
. In fact, with the above assumptions the transfer function
H
can be written as

\mathcal{Z}[h](z)=H(z):=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}r_{\lambda_{k}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\frac{1}{1-\overline{\lambda_{k}}z}.

(18)

Remark 2.1
.

We note that the definition of the transfer function
H
is different from the definition usually found in the systems theory literature (see, e.g.,
vandenhof
). By Eq. (
18
), the poles of
H
are
\frac{1}{\overline{\lambda_{k}}}
,
k=1,\ldots,M
. They are the mirror image reflections of the nonlinear parameters
\lambda_{k}
across
\mathbb{T}
. If the transfer function
H
is real-rational, then these are the poles of the function
\widehat{H}(z):=H(\frac{1}{z})
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
which is the transfer function typically found in the literature. However, this is different here, because we use a non-standard definition of the
\mathcal{Z}
-transform where the exponents of

|\gamma(\lambda_{1})|>|\gamma(\lambda_{k})|\quad(k=2,\ldots,M).

If a dominant nonlinear parameter exists, then Bernoulli’s classical method (see Eq. (
5

;
malmquistorig

\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(z):=\frac{\sqrt{1-|a_{n}|^{2}}}{1-\overline{a}_{n}z}\cdot\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}B_{a_{j}}(z)\quad(\boldsymbol{a}\in\ell_{\mathbb{D}},\ z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}),

(28)

where

denotes the set of complex sequences in

and

B_{a}(z):=\frac{z-a}{1-\overline{a}z}\quad(a\in\mathbb{D},\ z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}})

(29)

is a so-called Blaschke factor. Both Blaschke factors and TM functions have interesting and important properties with far reaching consequences for Hardy spaces. For example, Blaschke factors are bijective self maps on
\mathbb{T}
and
\mathbb{D}
and can be used to describe congruence transformations in the Poincaré disc model of the Bolyai-Lobachevskiy hyperbolic geometry. In addition, they form a group with respect to function composition. Products of Blaschke factors are called Blaschke products and are also self maps on
\mathbb{T}
and
\mathbb{D}
. Blaschke products are inner functions and play an important role in the factorization of Hardy spaces. They can also be interpreted as transfer functions of all pass filters
vandenhof
;
AllPassFilters

TM functions (
28

. They are also complete in

provided that the generating sequence

satisfies the so-called Szász condition

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-|a_{n}|)=\infty.

By

{\langle r_{\lambda},f\rangle}_{H_{2}(\mathbb{D})}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\overline{f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t})}}{1-\overline{\lambda}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t}}\,\mathrm{d}t=\overline{f(\lambda)}.

(31)

Thus, if

, its Fourier coefficients with respect to a TM function can be written as

c^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{n}:=\langle H,\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle_{H_{2}(\mathbb{D})}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\langle r_{\lambda_{k}},\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle_{H_{2}(\mathbb{D})}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\overline{\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(\lambda_{k})}.

We call a TM system
p

\Phi_{n+kp}^{\boldsymbol{a}}=\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}B_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{k}\quad(k\in\mathbb{N}),

For ease of notation, for

\nu_{k}^{n,p}:=n+pk\quad(k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},\ 0<n<p,\ p\geq 1).

Theorem 3.1

(Generalized Bernoulli (GB) schemes)

Let

-periodic TM function system (see Eq. (

4
Rational Prony and Bernoulli methods

In this section we state our main results related to generalized operator-based Prony and Bernoulli schemes. We begin by observing that any general Prony problem as introduced in sections

\mathcal{Z}

\mathcal{Z}_{w}[x](z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x_{n}\left(\frac{z}{w}\right)^{n}\quad(z\in\mathbb{C},\ w\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\},\ x\in\ell),

(35)

where we assume that the series on the right-hand side is convergent.

Theorem 4.1

(Generalized operator-based Prony methods and pole finding problems)
.

Let the signal space
\mathcal{M}
be defined as in (
6
) and let
f\in\mathcal{M}
, i.e.,

f=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}v_{\lambda_{k}}.

Then the following statements are satisfied:

a)

There exists a linear operator
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{M}\to H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
such that

\mathcal{G}f:=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mu(c_{k})r_{\varrho(\lambda_{k})},

(36)

where
\mu:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
and
\varrho:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{D}
are injective functions and
r_{\varrho(\lambda_{k})}\in H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
with
r_{\varrho(\lambda_{k})}(z):=\frac{1}{1-\overline{\varrho(\lambda_{k})}z}
for all
k=1,\ldots,M
(cf. Eq. (
20

b)

Assume that we have given a canonical evaluation scheme
\mathcal{F}={(\mathcal{F}_{m})}_{m\geq 0}={(\mathfrak{F}\circ\Psi_{\varphi}^{m})}_{m\geq 0}
with an iteration operator
\Psi_{\varphi}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
. Then,
\mathcal{G}
can be expressed explicitly as

\mathcal{G}f:=\mathcal{Z}_{w}[\mathcal{F}f],

for a sufficiently large
w>0
and where
\mathcal{F}f:=(\mathcal{F}_{0}f,\mathcal{F}_{1}f,\ldots)
.

Proof.

First we show the existence of such an operator, then we define it in a constructive manner without assuming a priori knowledge of the parameters
\lambda_{k}
and
c_{k}
.

a)

A linear mapping
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{M}\to H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
which satisfies Eq. (
36
) exists. Indeed, since
\Lambda
is bounded, there exists a
C_{\Lambda}>0
such that

\max_{k=1,\ldots,M}|\lambda_{k}|<C_{\Lambda}.

(37)

Then, we can define
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{M}\to H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
as

(\mathcal{G}f)(z):=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}r_{\lambda_{k}/C_{\Lambda}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{c_{k}}{1-\overline{\lambda_{k}/C_{\Lambda}}z}=:\widehat{G}(z).

Then,
\widehat{G}
clearly belongs to an
M
-dimensional subspace of
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
and the linearity of
\mathcal{G}
is obvious.

b)

Next, we define
\mathcal{G}
in a constructive manner. Consider the sequence
g^{f}:=\big(g_{m}^{f}\big)_{m\geq 0}
with

g^{f}_{m}:=\mathcal{F}_{m}(f)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\mathfrak{F}(\Psi_{\varphi}^{m}v_{\lambda_{k}})=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\varphi(\lambda_{k})^{m}\mathfrak{F}(v_{\lambda_{k}})=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mu(c_{k})\varphi(\lambda_{k})^{m}\quad(m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}),

where
\mu(c_{k}):=c_{k}\mathfrak{F}(v_{\lambda_{k}})

Choosing

we get

\displaystyle G(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}_{w}\big[g^{f}\big](z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}g^{f}_{n}\left(\frac{z}{w}\right)^{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mu(c_{k})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\varphi(\lambda_{k})}{w}\right)^{n}z^{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{\mu(c_{k})}{1-{\frac{\varphi(\lambda_{k})}{w}}z},

where in the notation of the theorem, we have
\varrho(\lambda)=\overline{\frac{\varphi(\lambda)}{w}}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.
The function
G
clearly belongs to
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Using the above, the explicit form of the transformation

can be written as

\mathcal{G}f=G=\mathcal{Z}_{w}[\mathcal{F}f].

∎

Remark 4.1
.

Given a Prony problem as defined in Theorem

Lemma 4.2

(Exponential dual evaluation schemes)
.

Let
V
be a vector space and
A:V\to V
be a generator with active spectrum
\Lambda:=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M}\}\subset\mathbb{C}
of the signal space
\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}(A,\Lambda)

Let
\ell_{\mathcal{M}^{\ast}}

\mathcal{M}^{\ast}

\mathcal{F}:=(\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2},\ldots)\in\ell_{\mathcal{M}^{\ast}}
which satisfies

\mathcal{F}_{m}f:=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\varphi(\lambda_{k})^{m}\quad(c_{k}\in\mathbb{C},\ m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}),

(38)

where
\varphi:\Lambda\to\mathbb{C}
is injective, is an admissible evaluation scheme.

Proof.

It is clear that any sampling scheme based on
\mathcal{F}
is realizable, because it does not depend on any information other than

E_{\mathcal{F}}:=[\mathcal{F}_{m}(v_{\lambda_{k}})]_{m=0,k=1}^{N,M}

has rank
M

N\geq M-1
. Writing

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&c_{2}&\ldots&c_{M}\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{1}\varphi(\lambda_{1})^{M-1}&c_{2}\varphi(\lambda_{2})^{M-1}&\ldots&c_{M}\varphi(\lambda_{M})^{M-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\ldots&1\\
\varphi(\lambda_{1})&\varphi(\lambda_{2})&\ldots&\varphi(\lambda_{M})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\varphi(\lambda_{1})^{M-1}&\varphi(\lambda_{2})^{M-1}&\ldots&\varphi(\lambda_{M})^{M-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&c_{2}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&c_{M}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

We observe that
E_{\mathcal{F}}
can be written as the product of a transposed Vandermonde matrix generated by a vector with pairwise different components and a non-singular diagonal matrix. Thus,
E_{\mathcal{F}}
is regular with rank
M
. Increasing
N
does not change this property.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In
stampfer2019generalized
, Stampfer already proposes a so-called dual sampling scheme which is based on a similar evaluation scheme as the one proposed in Lemma
4.2
(see, e.g.,
(
stampfer2019generalized
, Sect. 4.1)
). It should be emphasized that this sampling scheme assumes knowledge of an iteration operator
\Psi_{\varphi}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
. Lemma
4.2
makes no such assumptions which can be helpful in some applications (since sometimes obtaining iteration operators for a given Prony problem is difficult).

Using Theorem
4.1
and Lemma
4.2
it is (in theory) possible to obtain an equivalent SISO LTI identification task for any general Prony problem. One might ask why treating Prony problems this way is beneficial. One benefit of treating general Prony problems as pole finding tasks is that in this setting, the recovery of the linear parameters no longer requires a Vandermonde system (see Eq. (
12
) and (
26
)) which is usually poorly conditioned. Indeed, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 4.3

(Triangular system for linear parameter recovery)
.

Let
\Lambda:=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M}\}\subset\mathbb{D}
be known for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider the function

H:=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}r_{\lambda_{k}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}.

Then,
[c_{1},\ldots,c_{M}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
can be obtained by solving the upper triangular linear system of equations

\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\Phi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}}(\lambda_{1})&\overline{\Phi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}}(\lambda_{2})&\ldots&\overline{\Phi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}}(\lambda_{M})\\
0&\overline{\Phi_{1}^{\boldsymbol{a}}}(\lambda_{2})&\ldots&\overline{\Phi_{1}^{\boldsymbol{a}}}(\lambda_{M})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&\overline{\Phi_{M-1}^{\boldsymbol{a}}}(\lambda_{M})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\\
\vdots\\
c_{M}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\langle H,\Phi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle_{H_{2}(\mathbb{D})}\\
\langle H,\Phi_{1}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle_{H_{2}(\mathbb{D})}\\
\vdots\\
\langle H,\Phi_{M-1}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle_{H_{2}(\mathbb{D})}\end{bmatrix},

(39)

where
\boldsymbol{a}:=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M})\in\mathbb{D}^{M}
and
\{\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\}_{n=0}^{M-1}
are the corresponding first
M
Takenaka-Malmquist functions as in Eq. (
28
).

Proof.

It is well known, that
{\{\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}_{n=0}^{M-1}
spans the model space
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
. Exploiting this and the orthogonality of TM functions, we have

H:=\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}\langle H,\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle_{H_{2}(\mathbb{D})}\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}.

Examining the TM-Fourier coefficients as in Eq. (
32
), for
n=0,\ldots,M-1
we find

\begin{split}&\langle H,\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle_{H_{2}(\mathbb{D})}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\overline{\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}}(\lambda_{k}).\end{split}

By the definition of the TM functions (see Eq. (
28
)) and the fact that
\boldsymbol{a}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M})\in\mathbb{D}^{M}
, we have

\overline{\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}}(\lambda_{k})=0

whenever
n>k-1
. Thus, we obtain Eq. (
39
).

∎

Remark 4.3
.

In practical applications, the
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
inner product cannot be evaluated. We note that in this case, discrete TM-Fourier coefficients (see, e.g.,
(
siamdozsa
, Sect. 4)
) can also be used in Eq. (
39
).

Another important benefit of transforming general Prony problems to an
H_{2}
setting is that GB algorithms can now be used to recover the nonlinear parameters of Prony signals. This is a direct consequence of Theorem
4.1
. As mentioned earlier, the use of GB schemes to recover nonlinear parameters
\lambda_{k}
is most beneficial, when
M
is unknown, since Bernoulli methods are capable of iteratively finding new dominant nonlinear parameters without a priori information about the number
M
of Prony atoms. We note that for the Bernoulli method, the Prony atoms coincide with the rational functions given in Eq. (
20
)). Another scenario, when Bernoulli methods might be beneficial is when
M
is large and we are only interested in recovering the first
M^{\prime}\ll M
dominant nonlinear parameters of the function. Similar ideas, such as the dominant pole algorithm
martins2002dominant
;
rommes2008convergence
have long been used in model order reduction, however these assume an a priori known system model. In contrast, the Bernoulli scheme only assumes that it has access to (a method for sampling) the rational function to be identified on the unit circle. In this way, Bernoulli schemes can be viewed as data-driven dominant pole algorithms.

The next theorem summarizes how the GB algorithm can be used to identify the parameters of general Prony signals.

Theorem 4.4

(Prony signal identification with Bernoulli schemes)
.

Let
V
be a vector space and
A:V\to V
be a generator with active spectrum
\Lambda:=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M}\}\subset\mathbb{C}
of the signal space
\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}(A,\Lambda)
as defined in (
6
). Let
f\in\mathcal{M}
, i.e.,

f=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}v_{\lambda_{k}}.

Let furthermore
{(\mathcal{F}_{m})}_{m\geq 0}\in\ell_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
be an evaluation scheme satisfying the conditions of Lemma
4.2
. Then a GB scheme as described in Theorem
3.1
can be used to recover the nonlinear parameters in
\Lambda
.

Proof.

The proof follows immediately from Theorem
4.1
.
Consider the sequence of functionals
\mathcal{F}:=(\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2},\ldots)
and the rational function

G:=\mathcal{Z}_{w}[\mathcal{F}f],

where the components of
\mathcal{F}
satisfy the conditions of Lemma
4.2
and
\mathcal{Z}_{w}
is defined according to Eq. (
35
) with
w>\max_{k=1,\ldots,M}|\varphi(\lambda_{k})|
. Clearly,
G\in\mathcal{H}_{\varphi(\Lambda)/w}\subset H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Therefore, for any choice of
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{D}^{p}\ (p\in\mathbb{N})
, the TM-Fourier coefficients
\langle G,\Phi_{n}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle_{H_{2}(\mathbb{D})}
exist and we may compute the limit in Eq. (
34
).
∎

Remark 4.4
.

a)

We note that using the TM-Fourier coefficients, the limit in Eq. (
34
) always exists, except if we choose the elements of the generating vector
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{D}^{p}
from a certain zero measure set in
\mathbb{D}

SS1

b)

SBS

SSSAB

\Lambda
.

5
Examples

In this section we present some numerical experiments and examples related to the application of the proposed rational Prony and Bernoulli frameworks for various parameter recovery problems.

5.1
Linear parameter recovery with Vandermonde and Takenaka-Malmquist matrices

We empirically verify, that the Takenaka-Malmquist expansion based upper triangular problem to recover the linear parameters of a Prony signal (Theorem
4.3
) is indeed better conditioned than the usual Vandermonde-based formulations (see Eqs. (
12
) and (
26
)). Since here we are only interested in comparing the condition numbers of the corresponding matrices, for each experiment we assume the following:

a)

The considered Prony signal is the transfer function of a discrete-time BIBO stable and causal SISO LTI system and can be written as

H:=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}r_{\lambda_{k}}\quad(\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{D},\ c_{k}\in\mathbb{C},\ k=1,\ldots,M),

where
M\in\mathbb{N}
is given and
r_{\lambda}
are defined according to Eq. (
20
).

b)

The nonlinear parameters
\lambda_{k}

39
) and the Vandermonde formulation in (
26
).

In our first experiment, we consider the (discrete-time) SISO LTI system proposed in
SSSAB
, which describes the dynamics of a flexible wing aircraft. This type of aircraft is characterized by the so-called Body Freedom Flutter (BFF) phenomenon, which causes elastic wing deformations resulting in fluttering. In
SSSAB
, a Laguerre expansion-based variant of the GB method is used to identify dominant nonlinear parameters of a transfer function obtained from real measurements describing such a system.
The transfer function is characterized by
M=15
nonlinear parameters in
\mathbb{D}
.

In the second experiment we consider the building benchmark model from the SLICOT Benchmark Examples for Model Reduction
slicot
. This system describes the displacement of a multi-story building during seismic activity. It is frequently used as a benchmark model to verify LTI model order reduction methods
morAntSG01
. The continuous-time system’s transfer function is described by
M=48
nonlinear parameters. In order to make use of Theorem
4.3
, we discretize the system. Discretization is carried out using MATLAB’s
c2d
function with the zero-order hold option, which assumes that the inputs are piecewise constant functions over the sampling time. In the experiment presented, this sampling time is chosen as
T=0.01
which yields a stable discretized system.

Finally, we consider a benchmark all pass system introduced in
morwiki_allpass
whose transfer function satisfies
\left|H(\mathrm{i}\omega)\right|=1\ (\omega\in\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, the zeros and poles of the transfer function are mirror images of each other across the imaginary axis. The system is discretized as the building movement example but with the sampling time
T=0.1
. The corresponding transfer function coincides with an
M
-term Blaschke product defined by multiplying exactly
M
Blaschke factors defined in Eq. (
29
). For this experiment, we use
M=200
and follow the zero-pole arrangement proposed in the benchmark
morwiki_allpass
.

The nonlinear parameters of the considered (discrete-time) systems are illustrated in Fig.
1
. The condition numbers of the matrices corresponding to the Vandermonde and the proposed triangular matrices are recorded in Table

.

Based on the above experiments, we conclude that the triangular formulation is very beneficial when there is a large number of nonlinear parameters close to the unit circle. In fact, the benefit of the proposed formulation seems to become more pronounced as the complexity of the Prony signal (expressed by the number
M
) increases. These problems cover a large class of real-world applications. Finally, we note that Takenaka-Malmquist systems can also be defined on the open right complex half-plane (see, e.g.,
eisner2014discrete
). Because of this, recovering the linear parameters of continuous-time LTI systems can also be done with triangular matrices of the form shown in Eq. (
39
). Indeed, one can replace the inner products Eq. (
39
) with inner products involving TM functions defined on the open right complex half-plane. Thus, for this particular class of problems, the discretization step used in the above examples can be omitted. Nevertheless, the proposed methodology can be applied to
any
Prony signal, not just rational transfer functions of continuous-time stable LTI systems. That is, transforming the Prony signal into a finite dimensional subspace of
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
, then using our GROP or GB schemes to recover the nonlinear parameters followed by an application of Theorem
4.3
to find the linear parameters can be carried out for any general Prony problem. Hence, the above example regarding the building model includes the additional discretization step.

5.2
Pole identification of time-delayed continuous-time LTI systems

In this section we consider an example application. Namely, we attempt to solve an identification problem of a continuous-time SISO LTI system with an unknown time delay
\tau
in the input, i.e., in state-space form we have

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=Ax(t)+Bu(t-\tau),

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=Cx(t)+Du(t-\tau),

where the matrix
A
, the column vector
B
, the row vector
C
, and the scalar
D
are of compatible dimensions.
It is well known, that the transfer function
H
of a BIBO stable continuous-time system belongs to
H_{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
, where
\mathbb{C}_{+}
denotes the open right complex half-plane. If no time delay is present, then the transfer function is rational and can be written as

H(s):=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{c_{k}}{s-\lambda_{k}},

(40)

where
c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
and with the nonlinear parameters
\Lambda:=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{M}\}\subset\mathbb{C}_{-}
, where
\mathbb{C}_{-}
denotes the open left complex half-plane. If the system contains a time delay
\tau>0
, then the transfer function
H
ceases to be rational and attains the form

H_{\tau}(s):=\mathrm{e}^{-\tau s}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{c_{k}}{s-\lambda_{k}}.

(41)

Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the space of all signals adhering to (
41
). In our example we shall assume knowledge of
H_{\tau}(\mathrm{i}\omega)
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
(or at least a sufficiently fine discrete sampling of it). Our primary objective is to use the proposed GROP and GB methods to recover the nonlinear parameters in
\Lambda
. To this end, we shall first construct an intuitive evaluation scheme adhering to the form presented in Lemma
4.2
. Once the evaluation scheme
{(\mathcal{F}_{m})}_{m\geq 0}
has been generated, we shall use it to construct a rational function in
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
whose mirror image poles can be used to recover the nonlinear parameters in
\lambda_{k}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
. This is in accordance with the findings presented in Theorem
4.1
. We emphasize that the purpose of this example is to demonstrate the usefulness of GROP and GB schemes even when the Prony signal (in Eq. (
41
)) is not a rational function. The development of identification schemes based on the proposed methodology would require a much more careful analysis which falls outside of the scope of the current study.

We consider now the following evaluation scheme
{(\mathcal{F}_{m})}_{m\geq 0}
with

\mathcal{F}_{m}H:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}H(\mathrm{i}\omega)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega m}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\quad(m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}).

(42)

For any
H_{\tau}\in\mathcal{M}
, the evaluation functionals from Eq. (
42
) are well-defined. Furthermore, we have

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}H_{\tau}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\,\mathrm{d}\omega=h_{\tau}(t),

(43)

where the delayed impulse response
h_{\tau}
is defined according to

h_{\tau}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{k}(t-\tau)}u(t-\tau),

where
u
denotes the Heaviside function. This is a consequence of the time shift property associated with the Laplace transform (see, e.g.,
(
sigsys
, Eq. (9.87))
). Using Eq. (
43
) we find that for each
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{m}H_{\tau}&=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{k}(m-\tau)}u(m-\tau)\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{k}\tau}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{k}m}u(m-\tau)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\widetilde{c}_{k}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{k}m}u(m-\tau),\end{split}

(44)

where
\widetilde{c}_{k}:=c_{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{k}\tau}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
. In other words, the evaluation scheme
{(\mathcal{F}_{m})}_{m\geq 0}
samples the delayed impulse response
h_{\tau}
. Since we assume
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists an
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
for which
u(m-\tau)=1\ (m\geq m_{0})
. Notice also that since
\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{k})<0\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
, we have
\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{k}}\in\mathbb{D}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
. Thus, we can consider the sequence

g:=(0,\mathcal{F}_{m_{0}}H_{\tau},\mathcal{F}_{2m_{0}}H_{\tau},\ldots)\in\ell_{2}

(45)

and define the notation

\alpha_{k}:=\mathrm{e}^{m_{0}\lambda_{k}}\quad(k=1,\ldots,M).

(46)

We shall henceforth assume that
-\pi<\operatorname{Im}(m_{0}\lambda_{k})\leq\pi\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
.
The
\mathcal{Z}
-transform of
g
yields

G(z):=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\widetilde{c}_{k}\alpha_{k}^{m}z^{m}-\sum_{k=1}^{M}\widetilde{c}_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{\widetilde{c}_{k}}{1-{\alpha}_{k}z}-\sum_{k=1}^{M}\widetilde{c}_{k}\quad(z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}).

(47)

Note that the transfer function
G
does not only have the mirror image poles
\alpha_{k}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
, but due to the constant term, there is an additional mirror image pole at
0
. Since
0
cannot be written as
\mathrm{e}^{m_{0}\lambda}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{-}
as in Eq. (
46
), this mirror image pole can be safely isolated from the
\alpha_{k}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
.
Due to the injectivity of the map
\lambda\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{m_{0}\lambda}
on the stripe
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\ -\pi<\operatorname{Im}(m_{0}\lambda)\leq\pi\}
, the values
\alpha_{k}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
can be used to recover the nonlinear parameters
\lambda_{k}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
.

Given
g
from Eq. (
45
), we can apply the GOP method (see subsection
2.1
) to recover the nonlinear parameters
\lambda_{k}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
. Indeed, considering Eq. (
44
), we obtain

\left[\mathfrak{E}\big((S^{\#})^{m+j}g\big)\right]_{m,j=0}^{M-1}\left[p_{k}\right]_{k=0}^{M-1}=-\left[\mathfrak{E}\big((S^{\#})^{M+k}g\big)\right]_{k=0}^{M-1},

where
\mathfrak{E}:\ell\to\mathbb{C}
,
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)\mapsto x_{0}
is the point evaluation functional,
S^{\#}
denotes the backward shift operator for sequences, and
p_{k}\ (k=0,\ldots,M-1)
denote the algebraic coefficients of the Prony polynomial
P_{\Lambda(S^{\#})}
(see Eq. (
9
)). The zeros of this polynomial coincide with the nonlinear parameters to be recovered.

According to Theorem
4.1
, we can also recover the nonlinear parameters using the GROP method reviewed in subsection
2.2
. In this case, the result of the transformation proposed in Theorem
4.1
, when applied to the sequence
g
coincides with
G
from Eq. (
47
). Then, choosing the evaluation functional
\mathfrak{F}
according to Eq. (
23
) and considering the definition of the
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
shift operator
S^{\ast}
from Eq. (
21
), leads to the linear equation system

\left[\mathfrak{F}\big((S^{\ast})^{m+j}G\big)\right]_{m,j=0}^{M-1}\left[p_{k}\right]_{k=0}^{M-1}=-\left[\mathfrak{F}\big((S^{\ast})^{M+k}G\big)\right]_{k=0}^{M-1}.

Its solution matches the coefficients of the Prony polynomial
P_{\Lambda(S^{\ast})}
from Eq. (
25
). The zeros of this polynomial are the parameters
\alpha_{k}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
defined in Eq. (
46
), from which the nonlinear parameters
\lambda_{k}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)
can be recovered.

Finally, we note that, as a consequence of Theorem
4.4
, the GB scheme can also be applied to recover the nonlinear parameters of the delayed transfer function
H_{\tau}
. In this case, since
G
from Eq. (
47
) can be written as a sum of elementary rationals, we can apply Theorem
3.1
. This method of recovering the nonlinear parameters is especially useful when
M
is not given a priori. Indeed, the GB method recovers
\gamma
-dominant (see Eq. (

)) nonlinear parameters one-by-one and does not rely on any information about
M
. Note that
G=\mathcal{Z}_{w}[g]
, where the transformation is defined according to Eq. (
35
) with
w=1
. In practice, we can only approximate the
\mathcal{Z}_{w}
-transformation by truncating the infinite sum, however as our experimental results show, the nonlinear parameters can still be recovered with a high precision.

In order to demonstrate the usefulness of the proposed scheme, we simulate a small delay system, where

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}):=(-0.157+0.359\mathrm{i},-0.157-0.359\mathrm{i},-2.3)

The time delay for this example is chosen as

5.3
Recovering the parameters of reproducing kernels

In our final example, we consider the following application of the rational methods presented here. Suppose
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{C}
is compact and let
\mathfrak{H}
be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) of functions
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}
. Since
\mathfrak{H}
is an RKHS, by Aronszajn’s theorem
aronszajn1950
, there exists a unique mapping

K:\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}\to\mathbb{C}

such that for any
f\in\mathfrak{H}
and
K_{y}:=K(\cdot,y)\in\mathfrak{H}
,

{\langle K_{y},f\rangle}_{\mathfrak{H}}={\langle K(\cdot,y),f\rangle}_{\mathfrak{H}}=f(y)\quad(y\in\mathcal{X}).

Furthermore,
K(x,y)={\langle K_{x},K_{y}\rangle}_{\mathfrak{H}}
for any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
. The RKHS property is equivalent to all point evaluation functionals on
\mathfrak{H}
being bounded. Suppose that
\mathfrak{H}
contains all polynomials
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}
. Examples of such
\mathfrak{H}
include RKHS given by RBF kernels
jayasumana2015kernel
and those defined by Sobolev-type kernels on compact sets
narcowich2002approximation
. We are interested in problems, where the Prony signal attains the form

\begin{split}&f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}K_{\lambda_{k}}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}K(x,\lambda_{k})\quad(x\in\mathcal{X},\ \Lambda:=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{M}\}\subset\mathcal{X},\ c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}).\end{split}

(48)

Exploiting the fact that
\mathcal{X}
is compact (and thus bounded), there exists a
C\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
|x|<C
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. If we assume that
\mathfrak{H}
has a polynomial basis, we can consider evaluation schemes
{(\mathcal{F}_{m})}_{m\geq 0}
of the form

\mathcal{F}_{m}f:={\langle f,p_{m}\rangle}_{\mathfrak{H}}\quad(f,p_{m}\in\mathfrak{H},\ p_{m}(\cdot)=(\cdot/C)^{m},\ m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}).

(49)

Using Theorems
4.1
and
3.1
, it is possible to apply the GB scheme to recover the linear and nonlinear parameters of
f
in Eq. (
48
). A good example of a concrete RKHS where this scheme might be applicable is the following. Consider
\mathcal{X}:=[-1,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
and the RKHS
\mathfrak{H}
defined by the kernel

K(x,y):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{2k+1}{1+k^{2}}P_{k}(x)P_{k}(y),

(50)

where
P_{k}
denotes the
k
-th Legendre polynomial
szego

m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
C>1
.

In the following example, instead of the infinite sum given in Eq. (
50
), we consider the
N
-dimensional RKHS defined by

K(x,y):=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\pi_{k}(x)\pi_{k}(y)\quad(x,y\in[-1,1]).

(51)

Here
\pi_{k}:=\sqrt{\frac{2k+1}{2}}P_{k}
. Then,
\{\pi_{k}:\ k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\}
forms an orthonormal system in
L_{2}([-1,1])
. We note that for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
and any
N
-element system of orthogonal polynomials (not necessarily Legendre), the Christoffel-Darboux formula (see, e.g.,
szego
;
gautschi
;
vandenhof
) provides a closed form for
K
. The RKHS defined by the kernel in Eq. (
51
) is the space of all polynomials defined over
[-1,1]
of degree at most
N
.

In our experiment, we choose
N=512
. In effect this means that we can only apply the evaluation scheme from Eq. (
49
) up to
m=512
. In this way, we can only approximate the SISO LTI impulse responses which are required to transform the problem to
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
(see Theorem
4.1
). Nevertheless, due to the geometric decay of
\mathcal{F}_{m}f
for
m\to\infty
, we can still achieve meaningful results with the proposed rational methods. In particular, we consider a Prony signal
f
defined according to Eq. (
48
), where the kernel is given by Eq. (
51
). In this experiment, we want to showcase how the GB algorithm can be used to recover only a few, dominant parameters from
\Lambda

M=30
and
\Lambda
from Eq. (
48

[-0.9,-0.7]

\lambda_{k}:=-0.9+0.2\cdot\frac{k-1}{M-1}\ (k=1,\ldots,M)

49
) is applied to
f
to produce the sequence
g={(g_{m})}_{m\geq 0}

g_{m}:=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}(\lambda_{k}/C)^{m}\quad(m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}).

Indeed,

\begin{split}&\mathcal{F}_{m}f=\mathcal{F}_{m}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}K(\cdot,\lambda_{k})\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\left\langle K(\cdot,\lambda_{k}),\left((\cdot)/C\right)^{m}\right\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}(\lambda_{k}/C)^{m},\end{split}

where in the last equality we exploited the reproducing property of the kernel
K
. Similarly to the previous experiment, the GOP method is applied to

to recover the parameters
\lambda_{k}
and

6
Conclusions

As a main contribution of this study we showed that parameter recovery problems which can be solved using the generalized operator-based Prony method always corresponds to solving SISO LTI identification tasks. Indeed, given access to a Prony signal we can always find a transformation, which maps the Prony signal to an appropriate finite-dimensional subspace of
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
. This subsequently allowed rational identification algorithms to be applied to general Prony problems. In particular, we considered the application of generalized Bernoulli schemes. We gave examples where the parameters of irrational Prony signals could be successfully recovered using rational Prony and Bernoulli schemes. In addition we showed that treating such problems in a rational setting has practical benefits as certain numerical problems (e.g., solving large Vandermonde systems) can be overcome.

In our future work, we would like to demonstrate the benefit of using the considered approach (especially the generalized Bernoulli scheme) on real-world identification problems. To this end, we shall consider the problem of more efficiently optimizing the parameters (i.e., the TM system parameters) of the Bernoulli algorithm. Furthermore, we shall investigate the effect of measurement noise and propose theoretically sound ways to mitigate it.
