Sporadic sarcoma arising from a previously resected benign vestibular schwannoma without neurofibromatosis or radiosurgery: illustrative case
Authors: Simon A. Menaker, Michelot Michel, Catherine M. Garcia, Edward Robinson, Xuemo Fan, Gregory P. Lekovic, John S. Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDVestibular schwannomas (VSs) are common benign tumors of the cerebellopontine angle, often treated with surgery or stereotactic radiosurgery to preserve nerve function. Malignant transformation of VS (MTVS) is exceedingly rare and has been primarily associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), neurofibromatosis type 2–related schwannomatosis (NF2-SWN), or prior radiation exposure. However, cases of MTVS occurring without these risk factors are rare and remain poorly understood.OBSERVATIONSA 58-year-old woman without neurofibromatosis underwent subtotal VS resection in 2019, with stable residual tumor and no adjuvant treatment. In 2023, she developed acute neurological decline, and imaging suggested malignant transformation or a new tumor. Surgery confirmed a high-grade neoplasm, likely leiomyosarcoma or malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, with genetic analysis revealing no NF1 or NF2-SWN mutations. She received adjuvant stereotactic radiation therapy, and at the 3-month follow-up, imaging showed stable residual disease. However, she continued to experience persistent neurological deficits, including facial weakness, ataxia, and intention tremor, with a Karnofsky Performance Scale score of 60%.LESSONSMTVS is extremely rare without neurofibromatosis or prior radiation therapy, potentially driven by surgical inflammation or unidentified genetic alterations. While increased surveillance after subtotal resection may be considered, more research is needed to identify predictive markers and guide clinical management. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE251003

Vestibular schwannomas (VSs) are the third most common benign intracranial tumor overall and the most common tumor found at the cerebellopontine angle (CPA).1–3 They have a predilection for adult patients and their incidence increases with age.4 Microsurgical resection with simultaneous intraoperative neuromonitoring generally results in good outcomes, with the primary goal of achieving a safe, maximal resection and preservation of facial nerve function, as well as hearing if not absent or significantly impaired preoperatively.2,3 In certain instances, stereotactic radiosurgery may be used in lieu of microsurgical resection or as an adjuvant therapy for residual or recurrent disease following surgery.

Malignant transformation of a benign VS (MTVS) is extremely rare.2,5–9 While neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) and neurofibromatosis type 2–related schwannomatosis (NF2-SWN) mutations have been identified in malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs), the rarity of these cases prevents definitive conclusions about their role in MTVS.5,7 Radiosurgery has also been suggested as a potential factor in MTVS development, even in patients without neurofibromatosis. However, a recent multicenter case series found no association between NF2-SWN–positive VSs and malignant transformation after radiosurgery.10 Even rarer are cases of MTVS occurring after surgery without NF2-SWN or prior radiation exposure.

We herein present the case of sporadic malignant transformation of an initially benign WHO grade 1 VS to a high-grade spindle cell sarcoma without any history of neurofibromatosis or radiotherapy (RT).

Illustrative Case

A 58-year-old female with no history of neurofibromatosis presented with left-sided hearing loss in 2019 and was found to have a 2.7 × 2.2–cm left CPA tumor concerning for VS. She underwent subtotal resection (STR) via left retrosigmoid craniotomy at an outside institution with a small residual left in the internal auditory canal (IAC) (Fig. 1). Pathology was consistent with WHO grade 1 VS. She had left facial weakness, House-Brackmann grade V, and hearing loss postoperatively. By 2021, she gradually improved to House-Brackmann grade III. Per report, serial interval imaging demonstrated a small stable area of enhancement along the petrous bone and on the cerebellar cortex, as well as known stable residual tumor within the IAC. The patient did not receive any adjuvant treatment such as radiation therapy after her index surgery. Several years later, in 2023, 48 hours after undergoing vaccination against COVID, she acutely developed worsening facial weakness, ataxia, dysarthria, and excessive drooling. At this time, the patient was awake, alert, and oriented, with cranial nerve deficits including left VI nerve palsy, House-Brackmann grade V facial weakness, and complete left-sided hearing loss. Additional findings included left-sided sensory deficits and dysmetria, a positive right Hoffmann sign, unsteady ataxic gait, and dysarthric but fluent speech.

She underwent follow-up MRI of the brain with and without gadolinium showing a heterogeneously enhancing expansile infiltrative tumor within the left pons with extension into the left middle cerebellar peduncle and the left cerebellum, as well as superior extension into the left midbrain and effacement of the IAC (Fig. 2). This raised concern for malignant transformation of her previously resected VS versus a new de novo lesion. A redo left retrosigmoid craniotomy was performed for partial debulking of the tumor. The tumor was noted to be mostly firm, tannish, and hemorrhagic. Frozen analysis demonstrated normal cerebellum with focal atypical spindle cells. Permanent histopathological examination revealed high-grade epithelioid/spindle cell neoplasm exhibiting myoid differentiation with diffuse positivity for smooth muscle actin, focal positivity for calponin, and negativity for desmin and H-caldesmon per immunohistochemical analysis, compatible with MPNST given the patient’s clinical history. The tumor appeared to have a noninfiltrative growth pattern with probable involvement of the subarachnoid space and Virchow-Robin spaces; tumor cells formed tight clusters and exhibited epithelioid and focally spindled morphology with brisk mitosis, although no necrosis was identified (Fig. 3). The tumor was also noted to have retained INI-1/SMARCB1 and H3K27me3 expression. Genomic profiling via next-generation sequencing (NGS) did not detect any clinically significant variants, and a specimen was also sent to the Laboratory of Pathology at the National Institutes of Health/National Cancer Institute in Bethesda, Maryland, for ­methylation-based tumor profiling; however, neoplastic content in the submitted tissue was not sufficient for analysis. The patient subsequently underwent adjuvant hypofractionated stereotactic radiation therapy to a total dose of 30 Gy over five fractions. At her 3-month postoperative follow-up, imaging showed a stable residual lesion, and her Karnofsky Performance Scale score remained at 60%. Neurologically, she exhibited persistent left facial weakness and sensory loss on the left side of her body, which we presume resulted from inadvertent disruption of shared blood supply between the brainstem and adjacent highly infiltrative tumor at the time of surgery. While saccadic eye movements were present, diplopia was no longer reported. Motor strength remained 5/5 bilaterally without drift, but a new left-sided intention tremor was noted. Gait assessment was limited due to constant shaking, and speech remained fluent with intact comprehension.

Discussion

Literature Review

We conducted a comprehensive literature search of an electronic online database (PubMed/MEDLINE) to identify relevant case reports or studies published up to March 2025. A combination of keywords and MeSH terms related to the transformation of benign VS or acoustic neuroma to sarcoma or MPNST were used to identify relevant articles. All articles published in the English language were included. Inclusion criteria required the initial lesion to be a benign VS and the secondary lesion to be a malignant entity originating from a similar or adjacent anatomical location as the primary lesion. Articles were excluded if the patients had a history of radiation therapy or neurofibromatosis prior to diagnosis and treatment of the malignant tumor.

Five cases reporting malignant transformation of an initially benign VS (MTVS) without a prior history of radiation therapy or a diagnosis of either neurofibromatosis type 1 or 2 were identified in the literature.6–8,11 Age at the time of the initial tumor diagnosis ranged from 33 to 75 years. Four patients were male, and 1 was female. Latency period between diagnosis of benign and transformed malignant tumors ranged from 2 to 11 months. Four patients underwent initial resection of their VS, and in 2 of those cases gross-total resection (GTR) was achieved. The malignant lesion was primarily treated by surgery in all 5 patients, and in 2 patients fractionated RT was used for recurrence. Three patients died of tumor recurrence within 2 months. The 2 patients who received adjuvant radiation therapy demonstrated either no tumor recurrence or clinical stability at 12 months following diagnosis of their malignant tumors. Observations

VSs are tumors that arise from Schwann cells that myelinate the vestibulocochlear nerve, more specifically either the superior or inferior vestibular nerves. They account for a vast majority of CPA tumors and can present with various symptoms depending on specific size, location, and mass effect on the brainstem or other vital neurovascular structures.1 While a neurosurgeon’s encounter with a benign VS is common, MTVS and primary intracranial MPNST, subtypes of sarcomas, are exceedingly rare. MPNSTs carry a dismal prognosis with a median overall survival of 9 months and a 5-year survival rate of approximately 39%.11,12 In a recent single-center study, Li et al. reported that only 0.3% of benign VSs transformed to MPNST.13 In the present report, we describe a case of likely malignant transformation of an initially benign VS to a high-grade spindle cell sarcoma without a history of radiation therapy or diagnosis of neurofibromatosis. To our knowledge, this represents just the sixth such occurrence reported in the literature to date.

While most MPNSTs arise in the setting of the disrupted tumor suppressor genes (predominantly in NF1, although also seen in NF2-SWN) or are radiation induced, there is a paucity of studies to confidently classify these clinical variables as risk factors for malignant transformation.8 Patients with stigmata of NF1 or NF2-SWN are followed more closely as clinicians maintain a higher degree of suspicion for MPNSTs and other tumors. In a retrospective study of 1000 surgically treated VSs, MPNSTs were observed in a significantly higher number of patients with neurofibromatosis disorders compared to their benign VS counterpart.14 More recent studies have also confirmed this finding, with up to 50% of MPNSTs being associated with NF1.12,15 It is therefore very unusual that our case did not present with associated disruptions in either of the tumor suppressor genes implicated in NF1 and NF2-SWN. As such, other plausible mechanisms of transformation warrant discussion. It is possible that a disruption in neurofibromatosis was missed with NGS, particularly if the variant lies deep within an intron, although this is exceedingly unlikely given the quality of current NGS assays. Malignant potentiation may also be influenced by disruption of other pathogenic gene alterations that have yet to be discovered. In fact, only approximately 10% of all NF1 patients will develop MPNST during their lifetime, further supporting the notion that NF1 mutations alone are not sufficient to induce malignancy.16 NF1 mutations lead to increased activity of Ras, which influences the MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase), mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin), and PI3K/Akt pathways.17,18 Microsurgical resection of a benign VS represents a significant local insult to both normal tissue and tumor cells, and the resulting inflammation may lead to changes in the tumor microenvironment that predispose a lesion to malignant transformation over time.19 Of note, our patient exhibited the longest latency period between initial resection of benign VS and MTVS at 48 months, compared to the next longest latency period of 11 months reported in a similar sporadic case of MTVS.7 This extended period of time may have allowed for other somatic gene mutations or genetic alterations to occur in residual tumor cells, leading to the development of a malignant sarcoma.

Given the rarity of these tumors, it is difficult to pool enough histopathological data to garner insight into predictive prognostic features or future molecular targets. In the present case, histopathological analysis revealed a sarcoma with myoid differentiation. Although our understanding of this histological feature is not well understood in intracranial MPNSTs, Deyrup and colleagues evaluated 42 cases of patients with MPNSTs in the upper extremity and found that myoid differentiation was an independent predictor of worse outcomes.20 In their study, patients with myoid differentiation had 5-year survival rates of 35.3% compared to 64.9% in patients without myoid differentiation (p = 0.0054). Further analysis via immunohistochemistry revealed retention of trimethylated histone 3 at the 27th lysine residue (H3K27me3). Interestingly, Cleven et al. reported that loss of this epigenetic mark was diagnostic of MPNSTs and conferred worse overall survival21—a conclusion that was also supported by the work of Prieto-Granada et al.22 However, in our present case this sarcoma retained expression, further signaling the possibility of other genetic or epigenetic alterations that induce the malignant potential of Schwann cells.

Regardless of the specific etiology or oncogenic driver, the mainstay of treatment for MPNSTs and sporadic MTVSs is resection with clear margins. Achieving a GTR of intracranial lesions, particularly in our case given the tumor’s location within the posterior fossa, can be difficult due to adherence to cranial nerves, the brainstem, and critical blood vessels. Li et al. conducted a retrospective review and pooled analysis of 71 intracranial MTVS cases, combining their own institutional experience with cases previously reported in the literature.13 Their analysis of these treated tumors demonstrated a decreased likelihood of achieving GTR in patients with MTVS who presented with diffuse tumor infiltration, prominent bone invasion, and a multilobulated appearance on imaging.13 In such cases, RT can be used but with varied efficacy. Li et al. further concluded that postoperative RT improved overall survival in MTVS patients, but this benefit did not extend to patients with radiation-induced MTVS. Interestingly, of the 5 reported cases of sporadic MTVS, the 2 patients with stable disease at the 12-month follow-up received adjuvant fractionated RT, whereas the remaining 3 did not. In our case, the patient received adjuvant fractionated RT at a total dose of 30 Gy following resection of the MTVS with clinically stable disease at the 3-month follow-up.

Lessons

This case highlights the exceedingly rare occurrence of MTVS in the absence of neurofibromatosis or prior radiation therapy for benign VS. Given the scarcity of reported cases, the exact mechanisms driving malignant transformation remain unclear, although inflammation from prior surgical insult and undetected genetic alterations may contribute. The extended time between the initial benign diagnosis and malignant transformation suggests that gradual changes in the tumor microenvironment or molecular pathways may contribute to malignancy. While resection remains the primary treatment for MTVS, achieving GTR is often challenging due to brainstem and cranial nerve involvement. Emerging evidence suggests that adjuvant fractionated RT may improve disease stability, although its role requires further validation. Given the potential for delayed malignant transformation, it is not far-fetched to consider more frequent surveillance, particularly in cases of STR of VS. However, because of the rarity of MTVS and the limited available data, there is insufficient evidence to make concrete clinical recommendations at this time. Ultimately, this case underscores the need for ongoing molecular and genetic research to identify predictive markers and optimize treatment strategies for these rare but aggressive tumors.
