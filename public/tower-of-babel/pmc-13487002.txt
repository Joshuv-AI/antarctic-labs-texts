wbp-encoded LPS O-antigen architecture as a prognostic and therapeutic target in Pseudomonas aeruginosa keratitis
Authors: Mingda Wei, Zijun Zhang, Xudong Zhao, Boling Xu, Qingquan Shi, Zhen Cheng, Xizhan Xu, Zhenyu Wei, Xinxin Lu, Li Li, Qingfeng Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) keratitis can progress rapidly to vision-threatening disease, even with intensive therapy. Virulence-associated genes are key determinants of ocular-surface pathogenesis. We therefore sought to develop a composite wbp-exo genotyping framework for risk stratification and to guide wbp-dependent, LPS-directed, levofloxacin-polymyxin B (LVX-POL) combination therapy for high-risk corneal infections.MethodsA well-characterised clinical P. aeruginosa keratitis cohort was integrated with whole-genome sequencing. Based on comprehensive virulence-gene identification and annotation, the relationship between strain-level genetic features and clinical prognosis was analysed. The differences between WBP1 strains and WBP2 strains in adhesion, invasion, and biofilm formation in corneal epithelial cells were further evaluated. To establish biological plausibility, wbp genotypes were correlated with LPS O-antigen electrophoretic profiles and in vivo corneal inflammatory phenotypes in murine infection, including the observation of leucocyte recruitment and cytokine responses. To further confirm the key role of wbp gene status and LPS O-antigen in pathogenicity, wbpL knockout and reconstitution strains were constructed. Their appearances in vitro and in vivo were evaluated. Finally, a mechanistic rationale for an LPS-directed LVX-POL regimen was tested in a high-risk WBP1 P. aeruginosa murine keratitis.FindingsWhole-genome sequencing was performed on 46 clinical P. aeruginosa isolates and identified an average of 332 virulence- and fitness-associated genes per strain. The exo and wbp gene families were significantly associated with patient prognosis. A fusion model (AUC = 0.86) outperformed single-gene-family models (EXO: 0.66; WBP: 0.72) for predicting clinical outcomes. Intact wbp cassettes were enriched in poor-outcome isolates, and electrophoretic LPS profiles indicated that WBP1 strains produce highly polymerised O-antigen associated with sustained neutrophil recruitment and cytokine production. Murine experiments further implicated wbp genes in clinical pathogenesis, showing stronger immune responses and higher expression of TLR4, MyD88, TRAF6, p65, p-p65, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β throughout the inflammatory course. After knocking out wbpL gene, the WBP1 strain got stronger in biofilm formation and adhesion but weaker in inflammation and ocular surface survival. In the high-risk WBP1 P. aeruginosa keratitis model, LVX-POL combinations achieved complete ulcer resolution and markedly improved stromal infiltration and hypopyon, outperforming LVX monotherapy.InterpretationThe wbp gene family was identified as a key genetic factor that contributes to the LPS O-antigen structure, inflammatory intensity, bacterial ocular surface survival and poor prognosis in P. aeruginosa keratitis. A WBP1-targeted, LPS-directed LVX-POL regimen was proposed as a mechanistically informed option for high-risk strains.FundingThis research was supported by Beijing Public Health High-level Talent Training Program (Phase III-03-14), Prevention and Control of Emerging and Major Infectious Diseases-National Science and Technology Major Project (2026ZD01909300) and Beijing Natural Science Foundation “QiYan” Undergraduate Research Fund (QY26496).Keywords: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Whole-genome sequencing, wbp gene, Lipopolysaccharide, Bacterial keratitis, Polymyxin B

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Web of Science up to [date] using the terms “Pseudomonas aeruginosa”, “keratitis”, “corneal infection”, “whole-genome sequencing”, “virulence”, “O-antigen”, “lipopolysaccharide”, “wbp”, and “antimicrobial resistance”. P. aeruginosa keratitis is known to cause rapidly progressive corneal destruction and poor visual outcomes. Several studies have highlighted the importance of type III secretion system (T3SS) and have consistently associated exoU-positive “cytotoxic” strains with more severe corneal damage and worse visual outcomes. However, exoU is detected in only a minority of ocular isolates (approximately 14–29%). Its presence alone cannot fully explain the broad spectrum of clinical severity in practice, where poor prognosis or visual outcomes still occur in up to half of culture-proven P. aeruginosa ulcers despite appropriate therapy.

Added value of this study

This study combined clinical outcome analysis, comparative genomics, antimicrobial resistance gene annotation, and experimental validation in 46 corneal P. aeruginosa isolates, together with 215 publicly available genomes. Whole-genome sequencing was performed on 46 clinical P. aeruginosa isolates and identified an average of 332 virulence- and fitness-associated genes per strain. The exo and wbp gene families were significantly associated with patient prognosis. A fusion model (AUC = 0.86) outperformed single-gene-family models (EXO: 0.66; WBP: 0.72) for predicting clinical outcomes. Intact wbp cassettes were enriched in poor-outcome isolates, and electrophoretic LPS profiles indicated that WBP1 strains produce highly polymerised O-antigen associated with sustained neutrophil recruitment and cytokine production. Murine experiments further implicated wbp genes in clinical pathogenesis, showing stronger immune responses and higher expression of TLR4, MyD88, TRAF6, p65, p-p65, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β throughout the inflammatory course. After knocking out wbpL gene, the WBP1 strain got stronger in biofilm formation and adhesion but weaker in inflammation and ocular surface survival. In the high-risk WBP1 P. aeruginosa keratitis model, LVX-POL combinations achieved complete ulcer resolution and markedly improved stromal infiltration and hypopyon, outperforming LVX monotherapy.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings support a broader view of P. aeruginosa keratitis pathogenesis in which bacterial virulence, host inflammatory responses, and antimicrobial susceptibility jointly shape clinical outcomes. The wbp gene family was identified as a key genetic factor that contributes to the LPS O-antigen structure, inflammatory intensity, bacterial ocular surface survival and poor prognosis in P. aeruginosa keratitis. A WBP1-targeted, LPS-directed LVX-POL regimen was proposed as a mechanistically informed option for high-risk strains.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) is a common opportunistic Gram-negative pathogen that causes pneumonia, urinary tract infections, skin and soft-tissue infections, especially for patients with metabolic, haematologic, or pulmonary disease.1, 2, 3 It is also a leading cause of rapidly progressive microbial keratitis, particularly in contact lens wearers, patients with prior trauma, and those with immune-mediated ocular disease. The clinical characteristics of P. aeruginosa keratitis can progress within hours from a small ulcer to deep stromal necrosis, perforation, and permanent vision loss. Soleimani et al. found that up to one-third of affected eyes with P. aeruginosa ultimately require emergency penetrating keratoplasty despite intensive, guideline-instruction antimicrobial therapy.4 Thus, timely and accurate diagnosis is crucial for a favourable prognosis.

Although routine microbiologic tests can identify P. aeruginosa at the species level with high accuracy,5,6 clinicians need more information to distinguish infections likely to remain localised and treatment-responsive from those at risk of progressing to vision-threatening disease. Therefore, strain-level determinants of virulence will become a critical, yet incompletely defined, component of precision management in bacterial keratitis.7 Several studies have highlighted the importance of type III secretion system (T3SS) and have consistently associated exoU-positive “cytotoxic” strains with more severe corneal damage and worse visual outcomes.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 However, exoU is detected in only a minority of ocular isolates (approximately 14–29%).14,15 Its presence alone cannot fully explain the broad spectrum of clinical severity in practice, where poor prognosis or visual outcomes still occur in up to half of culture-proven P. aeruginosa ulcers despite appropriate therapy.16

Meanwhile, several functional and genetic studies17,18 have shown that the wbp gene cluster regulates the biosynthesis and polymerisation of the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) O-antigen by encoding key components for O-antigen assembly, including glycosyltransferases, flippase, polymerase, and the chain-length regulator. This O-antigen forms the most variable region of the P. aeruginosa outer membrane and contributes to complement resistance, epithelial adhesion, biofilm formation and Toll-like receptor signalling.19,20 Evans et al. also revealed that LPS-deficient mutants decreased the intracellular survival of P. aeruginosa strains in the cornea, suggesting that LPS components, including O-antigen, core polysaccharide and lipid A, are important for the establishment and progression of ocular surface infection.19 Notably, these key clinical questions remain unanswered: Is the capacity to establish ocular surface infection maintained by complete outer membrane (whole LPS structure) or just by polysaccharide barrier (O-antigen)? How do wbp-defined O-antigen structures interact with T3SS profiles to shape corneal inflammation and clinical prognosis?

Recent advances in whole-genome sequencing (WGS) allow high-resolution profiling of virulence and resistance determinants across diverse P. aeruginosa lineages, including ocular isolates. Hilliam et al.21 summarised that P. aeruginosa keratitis isolates had a broad phylogenetic range with highly variable accessory genomes containing secretion-, quorum-sensing-, and biofilm-associated loci. However, the link between specific virulence gene combinations and prospectively graded corneal outcomes remains unclear. Foundational biochemical and genetic work by Huszczynski and colleagues,22 and later by King et al.,23 defined the genetic composition and organisation of the wbp locus, the pathways for A- and B-band O-antigen assembly, and the impact of O-antigen chain length and chemotype on serum resistance and endotoxicity. But these studies were largely derived from non-ocular settings or chronic infection paradigms, with limited direct relevance to acute keratitis. Based on the biochemical function of LPS O-antigen and the high-affinity of polymyxin B binding to LPS, its potential use as an LPS-directed adjunct in predominantly fluoroquinolone-susceptible ocular P. aeruginosa infections remains unexplored.

Accordingly, we hypothesise that wbp-defined O-antigen architecture and T3SS genotype form a core virulence axis shaping inflammatory intensity and visual prognosis. We aim to establish wbp–exo composite genotyping as a mechanistically grounded framework for risk stratification and to generate preclinical evidence supporting WBP1-targeted, LPS-directed combination therapy as a plausible, testable strategy to improve outcomes in high-risk P. aeruginosa keratitis.

Methods

Establishment of P. aeruginosa keratitis cohort

Forty-six patients (46 eyes) with P. aeruginosa keratitis were included in the study from Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, between 2004 and 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients exhibited typical characteristics of bacterial keratitis, with P. aeruginosa successfully isolated and cultured from corneal samples. All these participants were informed of the aims of the study and provided written informed consents in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University (Ethics approval number: TRECKY2021-024.F1).

Demographic characteristics (first visit date, age and sex; self-reported by study participants), clinical history (risk factors, onset time, and duration), and imaging records (external eye photographs) were collected. Based on the external eye photographs taken at the first visit, the severity of keratitis was graded as mild, moderate, or severe using a standardised infectious keratitis severity grading scheme.24 The prognosis was classified as follows: a good prognosis (G) was defined as recovery with conservative treatment and no dense central corneal scar, while a bad prognosis (B) was defined as corneal perforation, need for penetrating keratoplasty, enucleation, or development of a dense central corneal scar.4 Prognosis was assessed only when enough clinical information was available. Patients whose prognosis could not be reliably assessed were excluded.

Identification and susceptibility of corneal P. aeruginosa strains

A total of 46 strains of P. aeruginosa were isolated from corneal scrapings between 2004 and 2024 and stored at −80 °C in the Ocular Microbial Resource Bank (OMRB) at the Beijing Institute of Ophthalmology, China. To determine the antimicrobial resistance profiles of P. aeruginosa isolates, minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined using broth microdilution method in accordance with EUCAST guidelines. All 46 P. aeruginosa strains were revived from −80 °C storage on Luria–Bertani (LB) agar plates (Servicebio, Cat# G3101). Following confirmation as P. aeruginosa by MALDI-TOF MS (Smart MS, DL Biotech), a single colony from each strain was amplified and purified in liquid LB medium. For antimicrobial susceptibility testing, bacterial suspensions were standardised to 1 × 106 CFU/mL using Mueller-Hinton broth. A 100 μL aliquot of the bacterial mixture was added to each well of a 96-well plate, which contained lyophilised antibiotics (Levofloxacin, Polymyxin B, Meropenem, Cefepime, Ceftazidime, Aztreonam, Amikacin, Piperacillin, and Piperacillin-Tazobactam) in each well (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# CMV3AGNF). Plates were incubated at 35 °C for 24 h. Antimicrobial resistance breakpoints were determined according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines (v15.0).

DNA extraction, sequencing, annotation, and pan-genomics of P. aeruginosa

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from all 46 strains using the FastPure Bacteria DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, Cat# DC10301). Purity and concentration were evaluated using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). gDNA samples were then submitted for WGS on the Illumina Novaseq platform in PE150 mode (Novogene, Tianjin, China), ensuring that the sequencing depth exceeded 100-fold, with a read length of 150 bp. Raw sequencing data were filtered to remove low-quality reads (e.g., >40% low-quality bases, >10% N, or adaptor overlap >15 bp) using FastQC and Trimmomatic. Clean data were assembled with Bactopia (v2.2.0).

To compare the phylogenetic lineage and genomic structure of ocular P. aeruginosa with strains from other clinical sources (lung, blood, wound), genome sequences of 215 isolates were retrieved from the NCBI database (accessed in March 2024). A total of 261 genomes were included in the analysis. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using GTDB-Tk (v2.3.2), and the results were visualised using iqtree (v2.4.0) and iTOL (v7.2.2). The core genome, accessory genome, and unique genome were identified and analysed with Roary (v3.11.2), followed by functional annotation based on clusters of orthologous groups (COGs) using eggNOG-mapper (v2.1.12).

Analysis of virulence genes and antibiotic resistance genes

For all genomes, protein-coding sequences were annotated against the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) using BLASTp to identify virulence-associated genes. Antimicrobial-resistance genes (ARGs) were identified using the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) against the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD). For each VFDB- or CARD-annotated entry, gene presence or absence was summarised according to isolation source, including cornea, blood, lung, and wound isolates.

For the 46 corneal P. aeruginosa isolates, virulence genes and ARGs were further analysed to investigate their association with clinical prognosis. The data were imported into R for clustering of differentially present virulence gene families and ARGs using the ggtree package (v3.16.3). Cluster analysis revealed the associations between specific gene families and clinical prognosis, and their predictive power was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (caret package, v7.0-1, R).

Development and validation of P. aeruginosa keratitis murine model

Ethical approval for all animal experimental procedures was provided by the Animal Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital (Ethics approval number: TRLAWEC05-1.0). P. aeruginosa strains BIO4965 and BIO3001, which are phylogenetically related and share an identical exo genotype but differ in wbp genotypes, were retrieved from −80 °C storage, resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Servicebio, Cat# G4207), and adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Four-week-old female C57BL/6N mice (Charles River, China) were purchased and housed under specific-pathogen-free conditions for 2 weeks of acclimatization before the experiments. The six-week-old mice were anaesthetised with 20% urethane (5 × 10−3 mL/g; MCE, Cat# HY-B1207). Corneal epithelial disruption was induced in a crisscross pattern using an insulin needle. Mice were divided into three groups (9 mice/group, 1 cornea/mouse) and received 2 μL of either BIO4965, BIO3001, or PBS. Anterior segment photographs under diffuse illumination and cobalt blue light with sodium fluorescein staining were acquired on days 1, 3, and 5 post-modelling.

Bacterial load of P. aeruginosa keratitis murine model

3 corneas per group were harvested, cut into pieces, soaked in 200 μL PBS, and then mechanically ground into a homogeneous paste. It was serially diluted 10-fold in sterile PBS to obtain concentrations of 1 × 10−1 to 1 × 10−5 CFU/mL. These suspensions were spread on LB plates and incubated overnight. Plates with 30–300 colonies were picked for colony counting and total bacterial load calculation.

Haematoxylin and eosin staining

Eyeballs were harvested, fixed in 10% formalin for 24 h, and embedded in paraffin. Corneal tissues were cut into 5-μm sections, deparaffinized, and rehydrated using a graded alcohol series. The sections were stained with haematoxylin for 20 min, treated with differentiation solution for 60 s and then stained with eosin for 2 min. The above reagents were from kit G1120 (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The result was observed under a microscope (Olympus BX51).

Immunohistochemical staining

The processing of the tissue sections was the same as the haematoxylin-eosin staining procedure. Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer in a microwave oven. After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity and non-specific binding with goat serum, sections were incubated with primary antibodies (IL-17A: 1:200, Proteintech, Cat# 26163-1-AP; IL-1β: 1:500, Abcam, Cat# ab315084; TNF-α: 1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab215188; MPO: 1:500, Proteintech, Cat# 22225-1-AP) overnight at 4 °C. Sections were then incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000; Abcam, Cat# 205718) at room temperature for 1 h. Immunostaining was visualised with a DAB kit (Beyotime Biotech, Cat# P0201M), observed at 400× magnification (Olympus BX51), and analysed using ImageJ software (v2.16.0).

Western blotting

Corneas were collected from mice on days 1, 3, and 5 post-modelling. Tissue was homogenised and lysed (3 corneas in 1 sample, 1 sample per group). Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer (Affinibody, Cat# AIWB-002), boiled, and subjected to sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE; 12%, GeneScript, Cat# M00668) at 120 V, then transferred to PVDF membranes at 400 mA. Membranes were blocked with NcmBlot blocking buffer (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China) and incubated with primary antibodies (TLR-4: 1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 19811-1-AP; MyD88: 1:1000, ThermoFisher, Cat# PA5-19918; TRAF6: 1:500, Abcam, Cat# ab137452; P-p65: 1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab76302; p65: 1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab32536; IL-6: 1:500, Proteintech, Cat# 26404-1-AP; TNF-α: 1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab215188; IL-1β: 1:500, Abcam, Cat# ab315084; β-actin: 1:1000, Abcam Cat# ab213262) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000; Abcam, Cat# 205718) for 1 h. Protein bands were visualised with NcmECL Ultra (NCM Biotech, Cat# P10100) using the Tanon 5200 chemiluminescent imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Assessment of P. aeruginosa adhesion, invasion, and biofilm formation

Besides BIO4965 and BIO3001, five more pairs of strains (BIO5997 & BIO4653; BIO2293 & BIO299; BIO5604 & BIO4741; BIO4673 & BIO6031; BIO2279 & BIO2272), which were also phylogenetically related, had the same exo genotype but different wbp genotype, were selected to perform the following experiments. Human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs), which was derived from HCE-T cell line (Procell, RRID# CVCL_1272, mycoplasma free) and amplified in HCE-T Cell Complete Medium (Procell, Cat# CM-0743), were co-cultured with P. aeruginosa at an MOI of 100 in DMEM. Adhesion and invasion were assessed at 1, 2, and 3 h. After washing with PBS, cells were lysed with Triton X-100 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 85111), and bacterial counts were determined via colony counting after plating on LB agar plates. For invasion quantification, cells were treated with 300 μg/mL gentamicin (MCE, Cat# HY-A0276A) for 1 h to eliminate extracellular bacteria. Invasive bacteria were quantified by colony counting, and the difference from total counts indicated adherent bacteria. Threefold repetition was performed for each strain and the average value was used for statistical analysis.

P. aeruginosa strains were grown to the logarithmic phase, labelled with 50 μM CFSE-DMSO solution (MCE, Cat# HY-D0938), and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. Bacteria were washed, resuspended, and co-cultured with HCECs for 1, 2, and 3 h. After co-culture, cells were washed, nuclei stained with DAPI (Beyotime Biotech, Cat# C1005), and observed using fluorescence microscopy. P. aeruginosa was grown to the logarithmic phase and seeded into a 96-well plate at 1 × 106 CFU/mL in LB medium (100 μL/well). Biofilms were fixed, stained with 0.5% crystal violet (MCE, Cat# HY-B0324A), and quantified by OD600 measurement. Six-times repetition was performed for each strain and the average value was used for statistical analysis. Biofilm morphology was examined under a microscope after incubation on glass slides for 6, 12, and 24 h and stained with crystal violet.

Extraction and analysis of P. aeruginosa lipopolysaccharide by SDS-PAGE

P. aeruginosa strains were cultured to logarithmic phase, harvested, and LPS were extracted using the phenol-water method. Electrophoresis was performed using a 4% stacking gel at a constant current of 9 mA and a 12% separating gel at 12 mA. Notably, SDS was present only in the running buffer but not incorporated within the gels. The resulting gel was stained using the Skygen LPS PAGE Silver Staining Kit (Skygen, Cat# 220509).

Assessment of lipopolysaccharide toxicity

LPS from P. aeruginosa strains BIO4965 and BIO3001 were extracted and applied to C57BL/6N mice (6 mice/group, 1 cornea/mouse). After corneal epithelial disruption, 2 μL of LPS solution or PBS was applied to each group. Inflammation severity was evaluated based on corneal epithelial defects documented by anterior segment photographs under natural light and after sodium fluorescein staining on days 1, 3, and 5 post-modelling.

Establishment of wbpL knockout and complementation strains

wbpL was determined as the bottleneck gene of wbp status. The upstream and downstream homologous recombination arms of wbpL gene in strain BIO4965 were amplified using a high-fidelity DNA polymerase. These fragments were then linked using overlap PCR to form targeting fragment: ΔwbpL. It was cloned into the suicide plasmid pCVD442Gm to form the target-shooting plasmid pCVD442Gm-ΔwbpL. pCVD442Gm-ΔwbpL was then introduced into E. coli and conjugation was performed between E. coli/pCVD442Gm-ΔwbpL and BIO4965. Gentamicin selective plate, PCR and sequencing were used to select BIO4965/ΔwbpL strain.

These fragments were subsequently fused by overlap PCR to generate the wbpL deletion construct (ΔwbpL), which was then cloned into the suicide plasmid pCVD442Gm to yield the recombinant plasmid pCVD442Gm-ΔwbpL. The recombinant plasmid was introduced into E. coli, followed by conjugation with BIO4965. Candidate mutants were selected on gentamicin-containing plates and confirmed by PCR and sequencing, yielding the BIO4965/ΔwbpL strain.

To establish wbpL complementation strain, wbpL gene was amplified from strain BIO4965 and cloned into the BamHI site of the pRK415 plasmid. The complementation plasmid pRK415-wbpL was introduced into E. coli by electroporation and then conjugated with strain BIO4965/ΔwbpL. Tetracycline selective plate, PCR and sequencing were used to select wbpL complemented strain.

Efficacy of levofloxacin-polymyxin B combination therapy in WBP1-infected mice model

P. aeruginosa keratitis mouse models were established with BIO4965, and the mice were randomly divided into four groups: PBS (6 times a day), levofloxacin (5 g/L, 6 times/day; MCE, Cat# HY-B0330), polymyxin B (30 mg/L, 6 times/day; MCE, Cat# HY-A0248), and levofloxacin-polymyxin B (5 g/L–30 mg/L, 6 times/day). Symptom severity was monitored by anterior segment photographs under natural light and after sodium fluorescein staining on day 1, 3, and 5. Corneas were harvested at each time point to perform WB to evaluate the inflammatory level (3 corneas in 1 sample, 3 samples per group) and to analyse bacterial load (3 corneas per group). The anti-p-STAT1 primary antibody (Proteintech, Cat# 28977-1-AP) was diluted 1:1000, whereas all other antibodies consistent with those detailed in the prior section. Disease progression was evaluated using Fantes grading scale.25

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed and visualised using R software 4.2.2 (The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Gene-sources, genotype-prognosis and genotype-O-antigen correlation analysis were performed using chi-square test or Fisher's exact test when appropriate. exo-wbp predictive model was constructed through multivariate binary Logistic regression analysis. For all in vitro experiments (biofilm formation, adhesion, invasion) and immumohistochemical staining, statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent Student's t-test. Comparisons involving three groups were analysed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Data for corneal bacterial load and clinical scores were analysed using Kruskal–Wallis H followed by Dunn post hoc test. The result of WB was analysed using mixed-effects model. All P values were 2-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

P. aeruginosa keratitis cohort

This study included 46 patients (46 eyes) diagnosed with P. aeruginosa keratitis, with a mean age of 50.0 ± 25.1 years (range 10–88 years) and a male predominance (65.2%). The main predisposing factors included ocular trauma (15.2%), previous surgeries (10.9%), and systemic comorbidities (8.7%). The median interval between symptom onset and hospital admission was 5 days (IQR, 3–14 days). Based on clinical presentation, disease severity was classified as mild (13.0%), moderate (8.7%), and severe (78.3%). A favourable outcome was achieved with medical therapy in 52.2%, while 13.0% required surgical intervention (4 PKP, 1 evisceration), all within the severe disease group (Table 1).

The antimicrobial resistance profiles of P. aeruginosa isolates are summarised in Table 2. Nearly all 46 isolates were highly susceptible to meropenem (95.6%) and amikacin (93.5%). Levofloxacin also showed strong activity, with 80.4% of isolates remaining susceptible (MIC50 = 0.5 mg/L, MIC90 = 16 mg/L), though 15.3% of isolates were resistant. The β-lactam antibiotics exhibited divergent efficacy: ceftazidime and cefepime both showed 60.9% susceptibility, whereas aztreonam resistance reached 47.8% (MIC50/MIC90 = 16/64 mg/L). For polymyxin B (no EUCAST susceptibility breakpoint), 69.6% (32/46) of isolates exhibited intermediate MICs (2 mg/L), while 30.4% were resistant (MIC50/MIC90 = 2/4 mg/L).

Whole-genome and pan-genome analysis of P. aeruginosa

In this study, whole-genome sequencing was successfully performed on 46 clinical P. aeruginosa isolates. The assembled genomes showed average nucleotide identity (ANI) values above 95%, confirming accurate species identification relative to reference P. aeruginosa genomes. To investigate the evolutionary relationships between our collected strains and P. aeruginosa isolates from other sources (pulmonary, blood, and wound isolates), a circular whole-genome phylogenetic tree was constructed (Fig. 1). In addition to the 46 clinical isolates from this study, 215 genomes were obtained from the NCBI database, including 92 from lung (42.8%), 63 from blood (29.3%), and 60 from wound infections (27.9%). The mean genome sizes of cornea, lung, blood, and wound isolates were 6.73 Mb, 6.57 Mb, 6.61 Mb, and 6.59 Mb, respectively, with corresponding mean GC contents of 66.4%, 66.3%, 66.7%, and 65.5%. The resulting phylogenetic tree, comprising 261 P. aeruginosa genomes labelled by species identification and strain name, revealed that 197 isolates (75.5%) were collected in or after 2010, predominantly from the United States (60, 23.0%) and China (59, 22.6%). Phylogenetic analysis showed that strains from various clinical sources and geographic regions were dispersed throughout the tree without distinct clustering, indicating extensive genetic diversity and evolutionary intermixing within P. aeruginosa populations.

To explore the functional diversity among P. aeruginosa isolates from different clinical sources, pan-genome analysis was performed on 261 genomes using Roary with a 50% sequence identity cutoff. The total number of genes identified in cornea, lung, blood, and wound isolates was 14,333, 21,267, 21,642, and 19,104, respectively (Fig. 2A–D). Each group contained a conserved core genome and a variable accessory genome. Core genes (present in all isolates) numbered 5219 for cornea, 5224 for lungs, 5012 for blood, and 5100 for wound isolates. Corresponding accessory gene counts were 9114, 16,043, 16,630, and 14,004, respectively. All four groups exhibited open pan-genomes (γ > 0.1), with γ values of 0.32, 0.35, 0.41, and 0.38, indicating ongoing gene acquisition and genomic plasticity.

Functional annotation using eggNOG-mapper identified 26 COG functional categories shared across all isolates (Fig. 2E). Core genes were predominantly enriched in functions related to essential cellular processes such as transcription, translation, and energy production, whereas accessory and unique genes were mainly associated with environmental adaptation, signal transduction, and membrane transport. Despite minor variations in the relative abundance of specific categories, the overall functional composition was highly conserved across cornea, lung, blood, and wound isolates, indicating that P. aeruginosa maintains a stable functional repertoire regardless of its infection niche.

Analysis of antibiotic resistance genes among P. aeruginosa strains

CARD annotation was further performed to compare ARGs. Across the available genomes, 218 ARGs were identified, of which 23 showed significant source-associated differences. Corneal isolates showed a high prevalence of intrinsic resistance or efflux-associated determinants, including nalD and mexY, and relatively higher frequencies of β-lactamase-related genes such as PDC-3 and OXA-486. In contrast, several acquired resistance-associated genes, including aadA, tet(A), OXA-2, OXA-10, VIM-2, GES-19, GES-20, VEB-9, and QnrVC1, were absent or uncommon in corneal isolates but more frequently detected in blood or wound isolates. Detailed source-wise distributions of CARD-annotated ARGs are provided in Supplementary File 1.

We then focused on the 46 corneal P. aeruginosa isolates to determine whether ARG distribution within ocular strains was associated with clinical prognosis. Six genes: mexA, mexE, mexX, fosA, catB7, aph(3’)-Ⅱb were distributed across all 46 strains. These genes were mainly responsible for aminoglycoside, fosfomycin or multidrug resistance. Besides, beta-lactam resistance genes (blaOXA, blaPDC, oprD) were the second most widely distributed, 34 strains (73.9%) were covered. No single gene or gene family exhibited a correlation with prognosis.

Virulence genes analysis and the role of wbp genes in P. aeruginosa pathogenesis

VFDB-based annotation was next performed across the whole 261 genomes, which identified a total of 537 virulence genes, of which 29 showed significant source-associated differences. Corneal isolates showed a high prevalence of several T3SS- or secretion-associated genes, including exoS (87.0%), exoY (93.5%), and vgrG2b (89.1%). In contrast, exoU was less frequent in corneal isolates than in blood and wound isolates, being detected in 13.04% of corneal isolates compared with 46.0% of blood isolates and 75.0% of wound isolates. Some immune-modulation or O-antigen-associated genes, such as wbpM, wbpA, wbpB, wbpE, and wzz, also showed source-associated variation. Detailed source-wise distributions of VFDB-annotated virulence genes are provided in Supplementary File 2.

In corneal isolates, a total of 347 virulence-associated genes across 46 strains, with each genome containing an average of 332 genes involved in effector delivery (32%), metabolism (15%), adherence (14%), biofilm formation (9%), and immune regulation (6%). Variation was primarily observed in effector delivery systems: for T3SS, six strains (13.0%) carried exoU while 40 (87.0%) harboured exoS, with exoY and exoT conserved; for T6SS, tse1 and tse4 were present in 76.1% and 95.7% of strains, respectively. The pch gene family, related to phenazine precursor synthesis, was highly conserved, though variability occurred within the phz operon. Among adherence and biofilm-associated genes, algP (73.9%), pilA (43.5%), and pilY2 (78.3%) showed the greatest diversity, whereas others were conserved. Immune regulation genes exhibited distinct clustering patterns: PLES_RS, PSPA7_RS, and PA14_RS were mutually exclusive, while wbp genes displayed a tiered distribution, with only ten strains containing all 13 wbp genes (Fig. 3). These results reveal a predominantly conserved virulent repertoire with selective gene variability that may underline strain-specific adaptation and pathogenic potential.

Cluster analysis was performed on the gene families containing differential virulence genes, and associations with clinical prognosis were evaluated. Among them, the wbp, exo, pil gene families had the smallest P-values (0.001, 0.004, and 0.150, respectively). Based on the exo gene family, the strains were classified into Group EXO1 (containing exoU gene, 6 strains, 13.0%), Group EXO2 (containing exoS gene, 40 strains, 87.0%) (Fig. 4A). Similarly, based on the pil gene family, cluster analysis stratified the P. aeruginosa strains into three groups: Group PIL1 (containing all key pil genes, 20 strains, 43.5%), PIL2 (missing one pil gene, 14 strains, 30.4%), and PIL3 (missing at least two pil genes, 12 strains, 26.1%), while the wbp gene family classified them into two groups: Group WBP1 (containing all 13 wbp genes, 10 strains, 21.7%) and WBP2 (lacking at least one wbp gene, 36 strains, 78.3%) (Fig. 4B and C).

Genotypic classifications of the exo, pil, and wbp gene families were independently matched to clinical prognosis, and the exo and wbp families showed a significant association with patient outcome (Fig. 4D). Under the single-gene-family model, P. aeruginosa strains in the Group WBP1 and Group EXO1 had poorer prognoses. However, a significant proportion of strains in the Group WBP2 and Group EXO2 also showed poor outcomes, indicating limited predictive accuracy of this classification. To improve prognostic stratification, a fusion model combining wbp and exo classifications was developed, categorising P. aeruginosa into four groups: Group A (WBP1/EXO1, 1 strain, 2.2%), Group B (WBP2/EXO1, 5 strains, 10.9%), Group C (WBP1/EXO2, 9 strains, 19.6%), and Group D (WBP2/EXO2, 31 strains, 67.4%). Consequently, Groups A, B, and C were associated with worse outcomes than Group D. ROC curve analysis showed that the fusion model (AUC = 0.86) outperformed the single-gene-family models (Group EXO: AUC = 0.66; Group WBP: AUC = 0.72) in predicting clinical outcomes in P. aeruginosa keratitis (Fig. 4E).

Under the current classification model, P. aeruginosa strains in Groups A and B, which carry the exoU gene, consistently result in poor keratitis prognosis, aligning with previous research. Interestingly, Group C strains, which carry exoS rather than exoU, also showed unfavourable outcomes, implicating the wbp gene family in pathogenicity. To explore this further, isolates from patients in the WBP1 and WBP2 groups were selected for in-depth analysis. Despite identical treatment, these patients had markedly different prognoses (Fig. 4F).

Role of wbp genes in the clinical pathogenesis of mouse P. aeruginosa keratitis

To evaluate the pathogenic differences between P. aeruginosa strains classified by wbp gene profiles, a mouse keratitis model was established. Clinical scoring, photographic documentation of corneal ulcers (with and without 1% sodium fluorescein staining), and HE staining of corneal tissue were performed at days 1, 3, and 5 (Fig. 5A). In the control group, corneas remained intact with minor procedural changes. In contrast, all intervention groups exhibited corneal infiltrates, purulent discharge, and peripheral corneal oedema. On day 1, Group WBP1 exhibited more severe inflammation, with prominent cell aggregation in the subepithelial region and a significant central ulcer (2.25 ± 0.48 mm2). Group WBP2 showed milder symptoms, including central corneal oedema and a few punctate epithelial defects (0.22 ± 0.08 mm2). By day 3, Group WBP1 displayed worsening pathology, with a full corneal abscess, thinning, and a large ulcer (2.47 ± 0.40 mm2), indicating a high risk of perforation, confirmed by extensive inflammatory cell infiltration and focal necrosis. Group WBP2 showed only mild progression, with slight central oedema, limited inflammatory infiltration, and a small epithelial defect (0.62 ± 0.27 mm2). On day 5, Group WBP1 showed marked improvement with residual central ulcers (1.53 ± 0.33 mm2), while Group WBP2 was nearly recovered, showing only a faint corneal nebula and subtle epithelial defects (0.45 ± 0.14 mm2) (Fig. 5A).

Discussion

P. aeruginosa keratitis is an important cause of infectious corneal blindness worldwide, yet the strain-level virulence factors that govern its striking heterogeneity in clinical outcome remain unresolved.15,21,26 In this study, we integrated a well-characterised clinical cohort with whole-genome sequencing, cellular assays, and murine infection models to identify the wbp gene family as a prognostic-associated factor in P. aeruginosa keratitis. We further defined the impact of wbp gene status on adhesion, invasion, and biofilm formation in corneal epithelial and abiotic models. By linking strain genotypes to LPS O-antigen profiles and downstream inflammatory responses, we provide a mechanistic rationale for an LPS-directed polymyxin B-levofloxacin regimen in high-risk WBP1 P. aeruginosa keratitis.

In this cohort, 78.3% of P. aeruginosa keratitis cases were severe and 13.0% required surgical intervention, consistent with prior evidence that P. aeruginosa keratitis remains a sight-threatening entity and that surgical requirements were approximately 30–40% in infectious keratitis overall.27 Virulence factors are central to P. aeruginosa pathogenesis. We identified a mean of 347 virulence factors/genes per isolate, including key determinants of corneal pathogenicity. Consistent with prior work, the exo gene family encoding type III secretion system showed a strong association with the severity of disease, and exoU-positive strains were enriched among eyes with poor outcomes.16,28 However, exoU-positive isolates accounted for only 13% of strains in this study, comparable to previous reports (∼20%),29,30 whereas poor prognosis occurred in approximately 50% of cases, indicating that exoU alone cannot explain the full burden of severe keratitis.28 In contrast, we identified the wbp gene family, which governs LPS O-antigen biosynthesis, as an important factor associated with clinical prognosis in P. aeruginosa keratitis. Deletion of wbp resulted in O-antigen–deficient, hypo-inflammatory LPS, attenuated corneal virulence, and more favourable clinical outcomes, whereas strains carrying an intact wbp cassette (WBP1 strains) produced long-chain, highly repetitive O-antigen structures associated with exaggerated inflammation and poor prognosis. These findings extend the current paradigm in which exoU largely defines “hyper-virulent” ocular strains by establishing surface glycan architecture as an equally critical axis for risk stratification.

Despite marked heterogeneous clinical outcomes among patients, antibiotic testing of P. aeruginosa showed broadly preserved in vitro susceptibility to levofloxacin and other first-line ophthalmic agents. With multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa remaining relatively uncommon in community-acquired keratitis,31,32 antibiotic resistance is unlikely to be the primary driver of vision loss. Instead, our data highlight strain-specific virulence profiles and host–pathogen interactions as the principal factors associated with clinical outcomes, consistent with comparative genomics studies linking accessory genome content to keratitis severity.15,33

Phylogenetic analysis of 215 publicly available P. aeruginosa genomes showed that ocular isolates did not form a distinct cluster and displayed no clear geographical or temporal structure. This pattern is consistent with previous genomic studies reporting a broadly mixed population structure with limited association between genotype and anatomical niche or ecological setting.34, 35, 36 Pan-genome analysis revealed an open genome with a highly diverse accessory repertoire and a broadly conserved core, reinforcing the view that corneal infection is an opportunistic event arising from a broad environmental and clinical reservoir rather than a specialised ocular lineage.

In this study, ocular isolates revealed substantial diversity in classical virulence gene families (phz, pil, fli, alg and rhl), previously linked to keratitis severity in experimental models,37, 38, 39 but these loci did not retain a stable association with prognosis after accounting for exo- and wbp-defined status, suggesting that many classical pathways function as permissive contributors rather than major contributors to outcome variability. Instead, wbp emerged as a central prognostic locus: although essential for O-antigen assembly and known to produce “rough” LPS when disrupted,23,40,41 it has not previously been linked to acute ocular outcomes. Here, we found that intact wbp cassettes are enriched in poor-outcome keratitis isolates and that composite genotyping integrating wbp and exo provides superior prognostic discrimination to either marker alone, supporting a model in which wbp-defined O-antigen architecture, together with exoU-mediated cytotoxicity, constitutes the core virulence axis driving visual prognosis. Our animal experiments support this model. Two phylogenetically related exoU-negative clinical isolates differing only in wbp genotype produced markedly divergent disease courses in mice: WBP1 strains induced larger ulcers, denser inflammatory infiltrates, and greater tissue destruction than wbp-deficient strains, despite similar bacterial burdens.

The knockout of wbpL gene in WBP1 strain further confirmed this theory. wbpL encodes an initiating glycosyltransferase, which generates the first lipid-linked O-antigen precursor and initiating O-antigen biosynthesis.42,43 Loss of wbpL destroyed the function of wbp status and transferred the pathogenicity of the strain on ocular surface to an extremely weak level. This result strongly proved that wbp status and O-antigen are crucial for pathogenicity of P. aeruginosa on ocular surface. Interestingly, contrary to the weaker pathogenicity, ΔwbpL strain showed significantly increased biofilm formation, adhesion and unchanging invasiveness. This suggests that these virulence phenotypes may play a less important role compared to highly polymerised O-antigen in ocular surface infections. However, the bacterial viability and LPS inflammatory ability was observed significantly decreased in ΔwbpL strain. This indicates that the high virulence mediated by wbp is more likely to be achieved through high inflammation and better adaptation of the ocular surface environment, rather than through the traditional direct toxic damage. The most notable point is that with the same initial bacterial load and no drug intervention, original WBP1 strain showed 105 times higher bacterial load than ΔwbpL strain. This strongly suggests that complete polysaccharide barrier (O-antigen) is the necessary condition for the survival of bacteria on the eye surface, and wbpL can serve as an important target for infection intervention.

Besides the significant decline in the survival ability, disruption of the wbpL-mediated O-antigen biosynthesis attenuated LPS-mediated inflammation, suggesting that the O-antigen/LPS axis represents dual-functional vulnerability. Therefore, rather than solely targeting bacterial viability, therapeutic strategies that neutralise its inflammatory activity may provide an alternative and potentially more effective approach for controlling infection and limiting tissue damage. This concept is particularly relevant in ocular surface infections, where excessive inflammation—consistent with TLR-based models in which early protective innate signalling becomes sight-threatening when excessive or prolonged—is a major contributor to pathology.44, 45, 46 Aligned with this mechanism, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of polymyxin B, an LPS-targeting cationic peptide, in combination with levofloxacin. Although polymyxin B showed high phenotypic non-susceptibility in our cohort, the combination improved outcomes in murine WBP1 keratitis compared with either agent alone. We believed that this effect was caused by neutralising LPS, as the concentration of polymyxin B was much lower than bactericidal concentration and the bacterial load showed no significant difference between levofloxacin and levofloxacin-polymyxin B groups.

Polymyxins are increasingly used as partner drugs against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa,47,48 and topical polymyxin B is already established in ophthalmic practice. However, polymyxin B-fluoroquinolone combinations, particularly with high-concentration levofloxacin, have been little studied. Our data provide preliminary in vivo support for this regimen against virulent but largely fluoroquinolone-susceptible ocular isolates, suggesting that polymyxin B may contribute clinically through adjunctive LPS neutralisation, even when classic susceptibility-based bactericidal expectations are not met.47 With both agents available as ophthalmic formulations, the approach is readily translatable, although dedicated clinical trials are needed to define efficacy, epithelial safety and the risk of selecting polymyxin-tolerant strains.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small, single-centre cohort limits statistical power and geographical generalisability, and the prognostic value of wbp-exo composite genotyping requires confirmation in larger, multi-centre cohorts with standardised outcome definitions. Second, our genomic analyses were restricted to gene presence/absence and did not assess SNP-level or regulatory variation, potentially underestimating contributions from other loci, including quorum-sensing and efflux systems. Third, although we link WBP1 status to O-antigen architecture and inflammatory phenotypes, the specific structural features and host receptors involved remain unresolved and will require high-resolution glycomics and receptor-targeted perturbation studies. Fourth, the mice bred with a single female, which may lead to potential bias. Finally, evaluation of polymyxin B-levofloxacin was confined to preclinical models; its pharmacokinetics, epithelial safety and clinical efficacy should be established in the future.

In summary, integrating clinical phenotyping, comparative genomics, and mechanistic experiments revealed the wbp gene family as a key factor associated with LPS O-antigen structure, inflammatory intensity, and prognosis in P. aeruginosa keratitis. In combination with established exoU-based classifications, wbp-mediated high virulence may further refine strain-level risk stratification and highlight host inflammatory amplification as a central driver of vision loss. We further propose WBP1-targeted polymyxin B-levofloxacin therapy as a mechanistically informed, readily implementable option for high-risk strains.
