Elderly abuse and depressive symptoms among older adults: loneliness and social participation as mediators and moderators
Authors: Yisong Yao, Yuemei Xiao, Yumei Li, Wen Hou, Ruying Peng, Limin Li, Qiang Zhou, Yiting Wang, Kunfeng Liao, Na Zhang, Xicheng Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have identified an association between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms among older adults. However, the associations and influence mechanism between specific kinds of elderly abuse and depressive symptoms remained largely unknown.MethodsCross-sectional data were derived from the 2018 wave of China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey. We employed multiple linear regression analyses and propensity score matching method to explore the association between elderly abuse, its three dimensions (psychological abuse, physical abuse, and economic exploitation) and depressive symptoms. Mediation and moderation analyses were conducted to confirm.the mediating role of loneliness and moderating role of social participation.ResultsA total of 6,153 participants were included in this study. Our findings indicated that there was a significant relationship between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms, with loneliness serving as a critical mediator across abuse subtypes. Social participation emerged as a moderator, specifically mitigating the association between psychological abuse and depressive symptoms. Residence may also shape the association between psychological abuse and depressive symptoms among older adults.ConclusionThis study provides insight into the systematic relationship between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms among older adults in China based on a cross-sectional study. This is conducive to promoting the in-depth and comprehensive implementation of China’s active ageing policy. Future policies should prioritize abuse prevention, foster community support networks, and integrate psychosocial strategies to improve mental health in this vulnerable population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25661-1.Keywords: Elderly abuse, Depressive symptoms, Older adults, Social participation, Loneliness

Introduction

According to data from the Seventh National Population Census, as of 2024, China’s population aged 60 and above has reached 310 million, accounting for 22.0% of the total population [1]. It is projected that over the next decade, the elderly population will expand at a rate exceeding ten million per year [2]. China is thus rapidly transitioning into a moderately aged society. With the continuous intensification of population aging, the associated challenges have become increasingly prominent. Among the many factors influencing active aging, mental health has emerged as a critical and widely discussed issue [3].

Depression is one of the most common mental disorders among older adults. Relevant studies have indicated that approximately 19.2% of the global elderly population is at risk of depression, whereas in China, the prevalence exceeds 17.5%−22.8% [4, 5]. Depression not only contributes to the onset and worsening of various physical illnesses but also severely affects the physical health, psychological well-being, and overall quality of life of older adults [6]. In severe cases, it may even lead to suicide, resulting in significant losses to both individuals and society [4–6]. Therefore, under the national strategy of promoting active aging, identifying risk factors for depression in older adults is of vital importance. Such efforts are essential to improving both physical and mental health outcomes and enhancing the overall quality of life among the aging population.

Elderly abuse refers to single or repeated acts, or the lack of appropriate action, occurring within any relationship where there is an expectation of trust, which causes harm or distress to an older person [7]. It is generally categorized into three main types: psychological abuse, physical abuse, and economic exploitation [8]. Research has shown that experiences of abuse are significantly associated with a wide range of health problems, including chronic conditions such as heart disease, hypertension, and chronic pulmonary diseases, as well as adverse social outcomes such as economic hardship and social isolation [9]. In particular, it has been noted that the experience of abuse is strongly associated with the emergence of depressive symptoms in older adults. Abusive behavior from family members is a trigger for depression in older adults, while depressive symptoms in older adults also increase the risk of facing abuse later in life [10]. However, the complicated mechanisms between different types of elderly abuse and depressive symptoms among older adults seem to differ. Therefore, it is important to explore in depth the relationship between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms in older adults.

Psychological abuse damages an individual’s self-esteem and well-being primarily through behaviors such as verbal insults, put-downs, and neglect, leading to long-term emotional distress [11], while physical abuse triggers psychological trauma through physical injuries, causing fear and anxiety [12]. There has been clear evidence of an association between psychological abuse, physical abuse and depressive symptoms, but relatively few studies have addressed the relationship between economic exploitation and depressive symptoms. Notably, the negative impact of economic exploitation on an individual’s health tends to be more insidious and long-lasting than direct forms of abuse [12, 13]. Although elderly abuse is very common in both developed and developing countries, research on elderly abuse started late in China, and few studies have focused on the variability of the relationship between different dimensions of elderly abuse and depressive symptoms among older adults. We propose Hypothesis 1: Elderly abuse is significantly associated with depressive symptoms among older adults.

Previous studies have revealed that loneliness may serve as a mediator in the relationship between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms among older adults [14]. Loneliness has been defined as an adverse subjective experience due to unmet intimate and social needs [15]. Research has demonstrated a significant correlation between loneliness and elderly abuse. Older adults who have been subjected to abuse exhibit elevated levels of loneliness in comparison to those who have not experienced abuse [16]. Additionally, a persistent positive correlation between loneliness and depressive symptoms has been well-documented [17]. However, to date, no study has examined the mediating role of loneliness in this relationship. In light of these findings, we propose Hypothesis 2: Loneliness serves as a mediator in the relationship between the elderly abuse and depressive symptoms among older adults.

While elderly abuse has been shown to increase the risk of depressive symptoms in older adults, social participation may play a protective role in this process, thereby mitigating the negative impact of elderly abuse on older adults’ mental health. According to social activity theory, older adults can rebuild new roles through social engagement to adapt to society and gain psychological satisfaction. This can effectively alleviate the negative emotions triggered by role loss and help reduce their risk of depression [18]. Furthermore, emotion regulation theory posits that individuals employ a variety of strategies to manage their emotional responses. Social participation offers older adults significant opportunities to regulate their emotions, facilitating more effective expression in interpersonal interactions and fostering emotional resonance and support. This, in turn, effectively alleviates depressive symptoms [19]. In light of these findings, we propose Hypothesis 3: Social participation plays a moderating role between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms in older adults.

The present study aims to delve into the relationship between elderly abuse, its three dimensions and depressive symptoms in older adults, and further explore the mediating mechanism of loneliness as well as the moderating role of social participation. This approach has the dual benefit of elucidating the psychosocial factors that contribute to the development of depressive symptoms in older adults and providing a novel theoretical framework for the study of older adults’ mental health. The findings of the study can provide a robust theoretical foundation for the development of social policies and intervention programs for older adults, thereby promoting the comprehensive development of mental health protection for this demographic.

Method

Data and sample

This study used secondary data from the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS) database, which is a nationally representative, ongoing large-scale social survey project targeting the Chinese elderly population aged 60 and above [16]. The CLASS employs a multi-stage stratified probability sampling design, selecting one eligible respondent from each sampled family. The CLASS mainly focuses on investigating the current situation of Chinese elderly people in terms of sociology, psychology, and behavior. For this study, we utilized the CLASS-2018 data, comprising 11,417 respondents aged 60 and above. In this study, the analysis sample excluded cases with missing key variables and those with invalid responses, such as “unable to answer” or “don’t know” (N = 5,264). After the exclusion of cases with missing values, the final analysis sample consisted of 6,153 respondents for analysis in this study.

Measures

Elderly abuse

Elderly abuse was assessed using seven items adapted from a previously validated measure in China [16]. Respondents were asked whether they had experienced any abusive behaviors from family members over the past year, encompassing three dimensions: psychological abuse (any behavior that causes significant psychological trauma to older adults), physical abuse (assault or injury to the body of older adults), and economic exploitation (unfairly disposing of older adults’ property, or otherwise unfairly benefiting from that property).

Specifically, psychological abuse included: (1) being ignored, not greeted, or not spoken to for a long period; (2) being neglected when in need of care; and (3) being insulted, verbally abused, or intimidated. Economic exploitation was measured by the item: being denied basic living expenses or having one’s money misappropriated without consent. Physical abuse was assessed through: (1) being denied a fixed residence; (2) not being provided with adequate food; and (3) being restricted from leaving the house.

Each item was coded dichotomously (0 = No, 1 = Yes), and scores were summed to yield a total abuse score ranging from 0 to 7, with higher scores indicating greater levels of abuse. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.839.

Depressive symptoms

The study employed nine items from the Center for Epidemiological Studies—Depression scale [20] to measure potential depressive symptoms, covering three aspects: feelings of marginalization, somatic symptoms, and positive affect. Respondents were asked to report the frequency of the following feelings over the past week: happy, enjoyed life, felt pleasure, felt lonely, felt upset, had sleep disturbances, felt useless, had nothing to do, felt lethargic, and had a poor appetite. The scale used a Likert-3 rating method (0 = little or no time, 1 = some time, 2 = most of the time). The three questions regarding happiness, enjoyment of life, and pleasure were reverse-coded, and all items were summed to represent the respondent’s depressive symptom score, with a total scale score of 18, where higher scores indicate a higher degree of depressive symptoms. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.839.

Loneliness

Loneliness was assessed using the Three-Item Loneliness Scale which was previously used in the Health and Retirements Studies in the United States [13]. Responses were scored as “never” = 1 point, “rarely” = 2 points, and “often” = 3 points. The total score range was from 3 to 9, with higher scores indicating greater loneliness. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.766.

Social participation

The social participation questionnaire from the CLASS was used to measure the status of social activities participation in this study [21, 22]. This questionnaire includes volunteer-type social participation: “community security patrols”, “caring for other elderly people and children”, “environmental protection”, “mediating neighborhood disputes”, “accompanying for chats”, “volunteer services for professional skills”, “caring about the education of the next generation”; and personal-type social participation: “attending elderly university or course training”, “playing mahjong/chess/cards”, “square dancing”, “religious activities”, “watching TV/listening to the radio/reading newspapers/reading books/listening to dramas”, “singing/playing musical instruments”. Participation in these activities scored 1 point, non-participation scored 0 points. There were a total of 13 items, with a score range of 0–13. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.716.

Covariates

We included sex, age, nationality, and residence, number of chronic diseases, self-rated health status, cognitive function, basic activities of daily living (BADL), education level, and annual income as covariates. Self-rated health status was assessed by the following question: “Currently, how would you evaluate your current health condition?“. Respondents can choose from the options “Excellent”, “Very good”, “Good”, “Fair” or “Poor”, which are assigned scores ranging from 1 to 5, respectively. A higher score indicates a better self-rated health status. Cognitive function was measured using the Mini-Mental State Examination from the CLASS database, which includes 16 items in five aspects: orientation in time, immediate recall, calculation ability, delayed recall, and orientation in place. A higher total score indicates stronger cognitive function (range: 0–16). BADL was measured by the Basic Activities of Daily Living Assessment Scale, which included 11 items: bathing, phone calls, eating, taking medicine, dressing up, mobility, dressing, toileting, and controlling bowel movements. A higher total score indicates a lower ability (range: 0–11).

Statistical analysis

In this study, we employed t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) for descriptive statistical analysis. Subsequently, we utilized Pearson correlation analysis to assess the potential correlations between depressive symptoms and other variables. We then explored the impact of elderly abuse and its three dimensions on depressive symptoms using multiple linear regression analyses. Propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to control for confounding biases and to perform robustness checks. We modeled the propensity score using logistic regression with elderly abuse (yes or no) as the dependent variable and sex, age, ethnicity, residence, education level, self-rated health status, chronic diseases, BADL, and cognitive function as independent variables. The study employed five methods for PSM: 1:1 nearest neighbor matching, 1:4 nearest neighbor matching, radius matching (with a caliper of 0.01), kernel density matching (with default bandwidth), and local linear matching. We also tested the balance of covariates between the abused and non-abused groups after matching. Then, we constructed four structural equation models to analyze the mediating effect of loneliness on the relationship between elderly abuse, its three dimensions and depressive symptoms, employing the Bootstrap method with a 95% confidence interval and a sample size of 5,000 for verification to validate the mediation effect. We also explored whether social participation has a moderating effect on the relationship between elderly abuse, its three dimensions and depressive symptoms. Additionally, we examined whether sex, age, and residence influence the relationship between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms. Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and Stata 15.1, with a P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Examination of mediating effect of loneliness between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms

After controlling for sex, age, nationality, residence, education level, smoking, self-rated health status, chronic diseases, BADL and cognitive function, we examined the mediating effect of loneliness on the relationship between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms. The results proved the significant association between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms (Coef = 1.257, P < 0.001), loneliness (Coef = 0.453, P < 0.001). There was also significant association between loneliness and depressive symptoms (Coef = 0.946, P < 0.001). After the addition of loneliness, the association between abuse and depressive symptoms was still significant (Coef = 0.829, P < 0.001), indicating that loneliness played a partial mediating role between abuse and depressive symptoms. See Table 4 for details. We used Bootstrap method to test the accuracy of the results and showed that the direct effect of elderly abuse on depressive symptoms was 0.829 (95% CI = [0.525, 1.133]) and the indirect effect of loneliness on elderly abuse and depressive symptoms was 0.429 (95% CI = [0.245, 0.602]). The total effect of elderly abuse on depressive symptoms was 1.257 (95% CI = [0.908, 1.607]), with 65.9% direct and 34.1% indirect. The confidence intervals for the total, indirect, and direct effects did not include 0, which further suggests that elderly abuse was associated with higher depressive symptoms, partly explained by loneliness. Discussion

This study revealed a significant association between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms among older adults. Loneliness mediated the relationships between all forms of abuse and depressive symptoms, with complete mediation for physical abuse and partial mediation for overall elderly abuse, psychological abuse, and economic exploitation. Furthermore, social participation had significant moderating effects on the association between psychological abuse and depressive symptoms. These insights support the development of multi-level interventions that simultaneously address abuse prevention, social connection enhancement, and community participation promotion.

This study found that elderly abuse was correlated with depressive symptoms among older adults, which was similar to the previous research [23]. In addition, psychological abuse and economic exploitation exhibited stronger associations with depressive symptoms compared to physical abuse. According to a study in US psychological abuse was more significantly associated with depressive symptoms than physical abuse [24]. These differential effects may stem from distinct psychosocial mechanisms. Psychological abuse may lead to sustained emotional distress and low self-worth, while economic exploitation can trigger long-term financial stress and feelings of dependency [8, 25]. Although physical abuse remains harmful, its influence on emotional and social functioning may be less direct [8]. These findings underscore the importance of distinguishing abuse subtypes when assessing their psychological consequences. Healthcare providers and social workers need training to recognize the subtle but pervasive signs of psychological abuse and economic exploitation, which may present without obvious physical evidence yet carry greater risk for depressive outcomes.

More importantly, we cannot overlook the possibility that depressive symptoms may also influence the perception of abuse, as this relationship is likely bidirectional [26]. Individuals experiencing depression are more prone to interpreting neutral or mildly negative behaviors as abusive, given that depressive symptoms encompass negative cognitive biases, diminished self-worth, and pessimistic interpretations of others’ intentions [27]. Such cognitive distortions may be associated with heighten sensitivity to potential threats among depressed individuals, making them more likely to perceive ordinary criticism or interpersonal conflicts as malicious or abusive behaviors. This relationship suggests a potentially self-reinforcing pattern wherein depressive symptoms are associated with an increased likelihood of perceiving abuse, while perceptions of abuse correlate with more severe depressive symptoms, which may further relate to distorted environmental cognition and a progressively deteriorating psychological state. Consequently, implementing comprehensive screening protocols for elder maltreatment among aging populations represents a critical priority for future healthcare initiatives. Concurrently, developing structured frameworks to address elder mistreatment cases is essential. Effective preventive strategies and intervention protocols targeting vulnerable elderly populations susceptible to both maltreatment and depressive symptoms.

Loneliness is a important psychological risk factor for depressive symptoms [28]. A meta-analysis and systematic review revealed that nearly one-quarter of older adults globally experience loneliness [29]. Theoretical models propose that loneliness operates through interconnected cognitive and biological pathways that collectively elevate depression risk [30]. Cognitively, loneliness fosters negative social expectations, diminished self-belief, and heightened vigilance for social threats, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of anticipated rejection and social withdrawal [31]. Biologically, chronic loneliness triggers stress responses manifested as inflammation and dysregulated stress hormones, while simultaneously reducing neural reward responsiveness to positive stimuli [30]. Therefore, reducing the sense of loneliness is an effective way to solve depressive symptoms.

This study provided evidence that psychological abuse, economic exploitation, and physical abuse are all associated with depressive symptoms through loneliness. In terms of subtype differences, psychological abuse and economic exploitation exhibited partial mediation effects on depressive symptoms, accounting for 34.1% and 35.9% of the total effect, respectively. For older adults, life transitions such as retirement, bereavement or functional limitations may restrict social participation, which is associated with more severe depressive symptom. Our findings suggest that in cases of elderly abuse whether psychological, or economic exploitation by caregivers or family members there appears to be a relationship with deeper perceived isolation, creating a toxic cycle where abuse is linked to loneliness, which in turn correlates with amplified depressive symptoms [8, 12]. Notably, the effect of physical abuse on depression was fully mediated by loneliness. This observed association could potentially be explained by post-traumatic stress responses triggered by physical violence. One hypothesis is that victims may engage in voluntary social isolation due to a perceived threat to their safety, leading to the collapse of social support systems, which then becomes a key mechanism in the development of depressive symptoms [32]. However, further research is needed to confirm these mechanisms. Given loneliness’s mediating role, clinical interventions for elder abuse survivors should prioritize rebuilding social connections alongside elderly abuse-focused treatments to more effectively prevent and manage depressive symptoms.

This study found that social participation significantly moderated the relationship between psychological abuse and depressive symptoms among older adults, which may stem from the emotional nature of the abuse. Specifically, higher levels of social engagement were associated with a buffering effect, reducing psychological trauma. Psychological abuse, such as verbal hostility, emotional neglect, and social rejection, can severely undermine one’s self-worth and interpersonal trust [23]. In such situations, social engagement can help older adults feel supported and connected. This sense of belonging may reduce the risk of depression [33, 34].

However, no significant moderating effects were observed for economic exploitation or physical abuse. This may be closely related to the resource-deprivation mechanism of harm. When older adults lose economic autonomy due to financial exploitation, their fundamental capacity to engage in social activities, such as affording transportation costs or offering gifts to maintain reciprocal relationships, is substantially undermined, leading to voluntary withdrawal from social participation [35]. According to the revised model of Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, when an individual’s survival and safety are directly threatened through physical abuse or economic exploitation, psychological resources tend to be prioritized for environmental risk assessment and activation of defense mechanisms, rather than for the establishment of social participation [36].

The present study further examined the moderating effects of gender, age, and place of residence. The findings revealed that neither gender nor age significantly moderated the associations between overall elderly abuse or its specific dimensions and depressive symptoms. These results suggest that the impact of elder abuse on depression may be broadly consistent across gender and age groups. The findings align with prior research, revealing that while women demonstrate higher rates of abuse exposure compared to men, this gender difference does not reach statistical significance [37]. Rather than tailoring assessment protocols based on demographic characteristics, healthcare providers should maintain heightened vigilance for abuse-related depression across all older adult populations.

The association between psychological abuse and depressive symptoms was significantly moderated by residence, with rural older adults demonstrating greater vulnerability than their urban counterparts. This difference is attributed to disparities in psychosocial resources. In rural areas, older adults often face limited access to formal emotional support systems and community-based mental health infrastructure due to persistent disparities in healthcare access and resource availability [38]. Compared to rural areas, urban environments generally offer broader access to mental health professionals and specialist services, which help buffer the emotional consequences of abuse through improved psychological support, thereby may be associated with reduced depressive symptoms [39]. One interpretation is that psychological abuse occurs, such as verbal aggression or emotional neglect, these individuals is more likely to experience intensified depression due to the absence of alternative social resources [5]. In contrast, the interaction effects of residence with physical abuse, financial exploitation, and overall elder abuse on depressive symptoms were not statistically significant. This suggests that the moderating role of residence may be specific to certain forms of abuse, particularly those involving emotional harm rather than physical or material dimensions. Additionally, healthcare policy should prioritize equitable distribution of mental health resources, with targeted funding for rural mental health infrastructure to ensure that geographic location does not determine treatment outcomes for elder abuse survivors.

However, this study had some limitations. Firstly, this cross-sectional study found a link between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms, but it cannot determine causation and directionality. Longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to clarify causal directions in the future. Secondly, elderly abuse and depressive symptoms based on self-assessment data may be prone to self-report bias. Thirdly, there were still some unmeasured confounders that may influence both elderly abuse and depressive symptoms. Finally, while this study’s preliminary findings confirmed that social participation moderates the link between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms, research on specific social activities and their mechanisms remains limited. More social participation activities, such as volunteering, community services, should be included. These clarifications are essential to align the conclusions with the strength of the evidence.

Conclusion

These findings underscore the associations between elderly abuse and depressive symptoms among older adults in China, with loneliness serving as a critical mediator across abuse subtypes. Social participation emerged as a correlate that buffer the psychological abuse-depressive symptoms link. Based on these cross-sectional findings, interventions targeting loneliness reduction and enhancing social engagement represent promising directions that warrant investigation for alleviating depressive symptoms in abused older adults. Residence was associated with the psychological consequences of specific abuse subtypes. Future policies should prioritize abuse prevention, foster community support networks, and integrate psychosocial strategies to improve mental health in this vulnerable population.
