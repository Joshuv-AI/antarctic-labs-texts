A critical case of anaphylaxis triggered by exercise in a pediatric asthma patient
Authors: Fereshteh Moshfegh, Zahra Hosseinzade, Mohammad Javanbakht, Maryam Sakhaei, Ali Manafi Anari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA 13-year-old boy with well-controlled asthma presented with acute respiratory distress, generalized urticaria, and cyanosis after playing soccer, raising concerns for food-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis (FDEIA) or exercise-induced anaphylaxis (EIA).The patient initially underwent evaluation at a nearby clinic and was later admitted to the hospital where the initial management involved intubation and intensive resuscitation after a preliminary diagnosis of anaphylaxis.Case presentationAt the presentation in the hospital, the patient had severe neurological impairment with a Glasgow Coma Scale of 3/15. On physical examination, abnormalities included hypotension, tachycardia, fever, and severe hypoxemia, with a SpO2 of 99%. The neurological examination was positive for dilated pupils and absent deep tendon reflexes. Laboratory results showed significant metabolic derangements, elevated liver enzymes, and markedly elevated lactate levels. The patient received fluid resuscitation, neuroprotection, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and antiepileptic treatment. CT scan was positive for cerebral edema and diffuse hypoxia. Despite maximum medical therapy, including sedation and inotropic support, the patient eventually progressed to evidence of brain death three days after admission.ConclusionThe case illustrates the difficulty of managing severe anaphylactic reactions in an asthmatic patient after playing soccer, resulting in neurological sequelae. Background

Anaphylaxis is a rapid, life-threatening allergic reaction requiring immediate treatment, accounting for up to 0.26% of hospital admissions [1, 2]. In recent years, the global prevalence of anaphylaxis and related hospitalizations has increased, driven largely by rising food allergy rates, especially in children, as well as greater awareness and improved diagnosis of the condition. Children and the young are more vulnerable to emergency department visitation and hospitalization [3, 4].

Anaphylaxis is also a significant risk in pediatric populations due to foods (i.e., peanuts and tree nuts), drugs, and insect venoms[5]. Common triggers in children include foods (peanuts, tree nuts), drugs, and insect venom. Fatal presentations are often linked to drug and iatrogenic triggers, followed by food, with teenagers and young adults at higher risk of food-induced anaphylaxis [1, 6]. Awareness of age-related predispositions and common allergens like cow’s milk, peanuts, and tree nuts is crucial for prevention and management [6]. While some cases are idiopathic, anaphylaxis’s multifactorial etiology includes allergens, infection, exercise, and emotional distress, complicating management [7].

Most pediatric anaphylaxis cases present with cutaneous symptoms like urticaria (hives) and/or angioedema (swelling) [7]. Cardiovascular symptoms are less frequent in children than adults, possibly due to age, comorbidities, and trigger variations (food allergies in children, drug/venom reactions in adults), [8–10]. Lethal food-induced anaphylaxis is commonly is often associated with a history of severe or uncontrolled asthma and this may be the reason behind such clinical differences[6]. Further, conditions such vigorous physical exertion, acute infection, emotional stress, routine disruptions, and premenstrual states tend to enhance the risk and severity of anaphylactic reactions. In addition, concurrent conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, systemic mastocytosis, and uncontrolled asthma further increase risk [1].

Exercise-induced anaphylaxis (EIA) is a rare but a heterogeneous form of anaphylaxis that can occur independently or in combination with food ingestion as Food-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis (FDEIA). Approximately 0.05% of people experience EIA during their lifetime. Among these cases, 30–50% are food-dependent, where reactions occur exclusively when exercise follows ingestion of a specific trigger food [11–13].

EIA and FDEIA cause rapid-onset allergic symptoms (diffuse erythema, urticarial, itching, hives, wheezing, etc.,) that typically resolve after stopping exercise but may require prompt intervention. Although EIA without food triggers is rare, confirmed cases demonstrating elevated biomarkers during provocation tests emphasize its clinical relevance [14]. EIA involves mast cell degranulation, evidenced by elevated histamine, tryptase, and vascular leakage, though triggering mechanisms remain unclear. While IgE is often normal in non-allergic EIA patients, factors like pH changes (supported by sodium bicarbonate’s protective effect) and increased intestinal permeability (enhanced by aspirin or exercise) may contribute [12].

Diagnosis and treatment of anaphylaxis are based on the history of allergen exposure and the current clinical presentation of the patient, [1, 15].

FDEIA presents a diagnostic challenge since the triggering food and exercise are only problematic when combined within a narrow timeframe (> 4 h apart, both are tolerated). This complicates identification of causative foods and highlights the need for careful history-taking and patient education on avoidance strategies [16].

Early detection and management of anaphylaxis are important in preventing severe and fatal outcomes. The unpredictability of reactions in addition to nonspecific symptoms leaves families with tremendous amounts of emotional weight [17]. Parents are key to early identification, but inadequate health literacy and a lack of standard guidelines can hinder prevention and care, negatively impacting the child and family’s quality of life [18]. Prevention of EIA and FDEIA relies on avoiding exercise after eating trigger foods, and prompt treatment mirrors standard anaphylaxis care. Increased awareness is key to improving outcomes.

This case presentation presented a 13-year-old patient who had previously well-controlled asthma and developed acute respiratory distress, urticaria, and cyanosis after exercise that precipitated a clinical emergency anaphylaxis requiring extensive medical treatment.

Case presentation

A 13-year-old male with a 2-year history of asthma, managed with salbutamol, as-needed montelukast, and inhaled corticosteroids (fluticasone spray bid), presented with sudden, severe dyspnea, generalized urticaria, and cyanosis of the lips and extremities. These symptoms developed after playing soccer the previous evening. He was immediately brought to a local clinic by his parents and then referred to a local hospital, where a provisional diagnosis of FDEIA or EIA was made.

Upon local hospital arrival after 20 min of the attack, he was intubated, received two 15-minute cycles of CPR with serum therapy, and epinephrine 1/10,000 (0.01 mg/kg as IV therapy), and was then transferred to Hazrat Ali Asghar Hospital with a Glasgow Coma Scale of 3/15. It is worth noting that there was an estimated delay of approximately 20 min in administering epinephrine, both as the initial treatment for anaphylaxis and during CPR, until the patient arrived at a facility equipped for advanced resuscitation. The child experienced urticarial lesions on two occasions—initially at home after dinner and exercise, and subsequently approximately 10 h after hospital admission at Hazrat Ali Asghar Hospital. Notably, there was no prior history of severe asthma attacks or urticaria, making this presentation unusual.

On admission to Ali Asghar Center, the patient’s vital signs were: SpO2 99%, BP 96/42, HR 140, and T 39. Pupils were initially dilated but reactive to light. Auscultation of the heart and lungs was normal. The abdomen was soft, without organomegaly or tenderness. Peripheral pulses were symmetrical and full, with good capillary refill. Plantar and deep tendon reflexes were absent. The patient had no skin lesions. Other tests were normal. His asthma was well-controlled, with no history of anaphylaxis, new food consumption, surgery, or hospitalization.

Following admission and considering the CPR performed, a 3% saline drip was initiated for neuroprotection. The head of the bed was elevated, the patient was placed in a midline position, and norepinephrine was started at 0.1 µg/kg/min due to low SVRI as indicated by USCOM, despite good peripheral pulses. Subsequently, fluid resuscitation was performed, involving the infusion of approximately 20 mL/kg of normal saline, following initial supportive therapy. This measure was undertaken in light of the markedly reduced fractional shortening (FS), with the aim of improving blood volume and supporting cardiac function.

Broad-spectrum antibiotics meropenem (40 mg/kg/tds) and vancomycin (15 mg/kg/qid) were initiated at meningeal doses due to loss of consciousness and other symptoms suggestive of meningitis. The patient was scheduled to undergo after a normal brain CT scan. The patient was also started on an anticonvulsant medication, levetiracetam.

The CT scan revealed obvious cerebral edema and loss of white-grey matter distinction. Cerebellar tonsil ectopia, diffuse hypoxia of the brain, and bilateral caudate and putamen hypodensities were present. The chest X-ray revealed findings consistent with aspiration pneumonia. Around 10 h after admission, the patient developed elevated blood pressure, tachycardia, and urticarial rash over the whole body, along with dilated and fixed pupils. Fixed pupils and hypertension in an intubated patient can be signs of cerebral edema and possible herniation, as indicated by clinical references. Therefore, in such cases, in addition to continuing hypertonic saline infusion, the administration of mannitol should be considered, and an urgent CT scan should be performed to evaluate for increased intracranial pressure and potential herniation. Mannitol, as an osmotic agent, was administered every 6 h to promote diuresis. Based on the neurology consult, and with a follow-up CT scan showing no evidence of herniation, the treatment was continued accordingly. Phenobarbital, as an anticonvulsant, was loaded, and maintenance was initiated at 5 mg/kilogram/day. Inotropic support was also continued. He received a single dose of intravenous hydrocortisone for his urticarial lesions.

Recurrent desaturation and bradycardia episodes prompted a cardiology consult. Echocardiogram showed an ejection fraction (EF) of 50% with a “smoky pattern” and inferior vena cava (IVC) distension. Aspirin and milrinone drip were recommended to improve systemic perfusion as his SVRI was also found to be elevated on USCOM.

Since the patient’s level of consciousness was critical, phenobarbital was stopped, and levetiracetam was continued for the patient. Two days later, based on the patient’s status, gradual correction of sodium levels, and ongoing unresponsiveness, a neurology consult was ordered. It was recommended to stop the midazolam drip, which was used for sedating the patient, and treat with mannitol and hypertonic saline for another two days. An MRI of the brain and an electroencephalogram (EEG) were also ordered, along with supportive care for neuroprotection after the patient’s stabilization.

Sadly, even after discontinuation of the sedative medications and correction of sodium levels to normal, the patient showed very deep unresponsiveness and diffuse brain injury, raising the suspicion of brain death. Following coordination with the transplant team, their arrival at the center, and the performance of the atropine test and other applicable tests, brain death was confirmed three days following admission. Following extensive discussion with the family and coordination with them for organ donation, the patient was shifted to another center for cornea, kidney, heart, and liver transplantation. The laboratory profile, marked by extreme lactic acidosis (25 mmol/L), rhabdomyolysis (CK-MB 132 ng/mL), and hypoxic hepatitis (AST/ALT 142/120 U/L), irrefutably demonstrates the catastrophic systemic effects of delayed anaphylaxis management or maybe prolonged resuscitation. CPK can be markedly elevated in EIA, mainly due to muscle damage from exercise, possibly worsened by shock and LFTs rise only with severe shock or multiorgan dysfunction maybe due to prolonged resuscitation. A mild elevation in liver enzymes, D-dimer, decreased calcium levels, and increased ammonia can all be explained in the context of prolonged resuscitation in a child and are consistent with this clinical scenario. Discussion

This case report highlights key considerations for pediatric emergency medicine, specifically concerning asthmatic patients presenting with acute respiratory episodes. The sudden onset of dyspnea, generalized urticaria, and cyanosis following exertion should raise suspicion for EIA and/or FDEIA.

Anaphylaxis is a life-threatening and serious hypersensitivity reaction affecting multiple organ systems, commonly manifests with cutaneous signs, respiratory distress, and circulatory compromise, often triggered by food, medications, or insect venom. While rare, EIA is another potential cause [19].

FDEIA is a condition where anaphylaxis occurs only when exercise is performed within hours of eating certain foods. Although FDEIA and EIA can affect any age group, they are most prevalent in adolescents and young adults [20, 21]. Comorbidities, particularly poorly controlled asthma exacerbate anaphylaxis severity and increase the risk of adverse outcomes [22]. Anaphylaxis in asthmatic patients may be misdiagnosed as an asthma attack, leading to inadequate treatment with inhalers alone [23]. A history of asthma may make the patient more prone to hyperreactivity of the bronchi, which may complicate the presentation of anaphylaxis. Asthma, food allergies to some foods (like peanuts and tree nuts), and adolescence are significant risk factors for anaphylaxis and other severe allergic reactions. In children with food-induced anaphylaxis, a history of asthma is common (over two-thirds), which may account for the fact that fatal reactions frequently result from bronchospasm and laryngeal angioedema, with subsequent cardiovascular complications due to hypoxia and respiratory arrest [24].

Exercise triggers allergic reactions ranging from erythematous, irritating cutaneous eruptions to life-threatening anaphylaxis [25]. Admission vital signs showed severe hypotension (BP 96/42 mmHg), tachycardia (HR 140), and fever (T 39 °C). The persistent hypoxemia and impaired neurological status (GCS 3/15) highlighted the necessity for immediate life-saving interventions and vigilant monitoring. The development of respiratory distress, urticaria, and cyanosis following exercise is suggestive of anaphylaxis. Urticaria is a heterogeneous skin disorder caused by acute or chronic, intermittent or persistent symptoms. It may also occur alone or in combination with others such as angioedema and be severe enough to be included in anaphylaxis. However, urticaria must be distinguished from anaphylaxis, as anaphylaxis may require immediate treatment, such as epinephrine injection [26] The differential diagnosis between them is emergent because, for the administration of anaphylaxis to be proper, epinephrine injection must be started immediately. Early assessment and early epinephrine administration are imperative in the treatment of anaphylaxis, particularly in asthmatic individuals who continue to experience symptoms, as this may signal a severe allergic reaction rather than just an asthma exacerbation [23]. Our point is that clinical presentations in teenagers, such as our patient, are consistent with trends in the literature for anaphylaxis symptomatology by age differences like dyspnea, respiratory distress, dizziness, hypotension and neurological symptomatology in teenagers. Our case also identified cyanosis as a potential early sign of severe anaphylaxis, which presents with symptoms such as hypotension and neurological features, in addition to oxygen saturation below 92% or systolic blood pressure below 90 mmHg [26, 27]. Intubation and two 15-minute CPR cycles were required due to severe respiratory failure and risk of hypoxic injury. The urgency of airway management in acute respiratory distress is emphasized, whereby airway compromise can lead to progressive worsening of the neurological condition. Rapid symptom progression in anaphylaxis necessitates prompt diagnosis and treatment to prevent neurological injury and other life-threatening complication [28]. A 3% saline drip was initiated for neuroprotection due to the risk of potential brain injury following CPR. Positioning—head elevation and midline position can assist with breathing in anaphylaxis. USCOM confirmed SVRI, and norepinephrine at 0.1 µg/kg/min was initiated for cardiovascular support in our patient. Inotropic support and fluid resuscitation are important in managing shock in critically ill anaphylaxis patients [29]. Following the atropine test and other assessments, brain death was confirmed three days after admission. Unfortunately, even after discontinuing sedative drugs and normalization of sodium levels, the patient remained severely unresponsive and suffered diffuse brain injury. Approximately 1% of anaphylaxis-related hospitalizations and 0.1% of emergency department cases result in fatal outcomes [30]. Overall, available evidence confirms that fatal anaphylaxis remains exceedingly rare [31, 32]. EIA fatalities are particularly uncommon but have been documented in case reports, primarily among adults [33, 34]. Noma et al. reported an 8-year-old girl who developed FDEIA after consuming buckwheat noodles and swimming, progressing to cardiorespiratory arrest within 40 min and death 13 days later [33]. Flannagan and Wolf reported a fatal FDEIA in an individual who collapsed during vigorous dancing after consuming nuts, presenting with sudden cardiovascular collapse without cutaneous or respiratory symptoms [34]. Postmortem testing revealed elevated tryptase and allergen-specific IgE levels.

Conclusion

This tragic case highlights three key lessons: (1) Delayed epinephrine in EIA can prove fatal, especially in asthmatics; (2) Post-exercise cyanosis, fever, and hypotension signal severe systemic involvement requiring immediate intervention; and (3) Education gaps persist—families, coaches, and clinicians must recognize EIA’s rapid progression in adolescents.

While rare, EIA fatalities are preventable. This case underscores the need for accessible epinephrine, emergency action plans for youth sports, and heightened suspicion of anaphylaxis in asthmatic patients with exertional symptoms. The patient’s outcome compels us to transform awareness into action.
