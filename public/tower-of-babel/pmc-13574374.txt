Fluctuation of intraocular pressure in Chinese patients with pigment dispersion syndrome after drug-induced mydriasis
Authors: Liping Pu, Yiyan Wang, Yanmin You, Yue Liu, Guoping Qing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the characteristics of intraocular pressure (IOP) fluctuations in Chinese patients with pigment dispersion syndrome (PDS) after drug-induced mydriasis. In this prospective cohort study, 26 PDS patients were identified and instilled with 0.5% tropicamide and phenylephrine twice randomly in one of the 2 eyes, after which IOP and pupil diameter (PD) were measured hourly for 8 hours. A slit-lamp examination was performed after each measurement. Post-mydriatic IOPs and PDs were compared with baseline. The average IOP increased to a maximum of 18.3 ± 4.9 mm Hg (vs 16.7 ± 4.5 mm Hg at baseline; P = .002) 1 hour after dilation. Accordingly, the PD reached its peak (8.3 ± 0.6 mm vs 5.9 ± 1.0 mm; P < .001). By the end of the 8-hour observation period, mean IOP had decreased to 17.4 mm Hg and mean PD to 7.1 mm. The majority of these eyes (88.5%, 23/26) exhibited mild IOP elevation of 2.5 ± 1.4 mm Hg (range: 0–4.9 mm Hg). In the non-laser peripheral iridotomy group, 2 eyes (13.3%, 2/15) developing moderate anterior chamber pigment shedding showed an increase in IOP of ≥ 5 mm Hg after dilation (21.1 and 12.6 mm Hg); 1 eye (9.1%, 1/11) receiving laser peripheral iridotomy showed a similar IOP increase (5.1 mm Hg). All clinically significant IOP elevations were resolved without treatment. No post-mydriatic IOP ≥ 40 mm Hg was detected during the study. Drug-induced mydriasis appears relatively safe in the majority of Chinese PDS patients, with generally mild effect in most. However, caution remains warranted in eyes with prominent anterior chamber pigment shedding.

1. Introduction

The clinical features of pigment dispersion syndrome (PDS) and pigmentary glaucoma (PG) may not be identical among different racial groups.[1–5] The most classic and typical features of PDS in White patients include a Krukenberg spindle (KS), midperipheral iris transillumination defects (ITDs), and homogeneous trabecular meshwork (TM) pigmentation,[6–9] among which the ITDs are of the most important value for the diagnosis of PDS in that typical spoke-like ITDs are extremely rare in any other ocular disorders or diseases. Tough iris transillumination may be found near the margin of the pupil in patients with exfoliation syndrome.[10] With typical ITDs, it is not difficult to detect and diagnose PDS. While in pigmented races, such findings are usually absent because of the thick and heavily pigmented iris stroma. Without typical ITDs, diagnosing PDS becomes difficult. The dark iris in pigmented racial groups makes it hard to detect KS under a slit-lamp, which is usually brown due to pigment granule deposition on the endothelial corneal surface.[11]

Under such circumstances, the first diagnostic clue for PDS in pigmented racial groups is homogeneous TM pigmentation, which can only be detected with gonioscopy. As a matter of fact, PDS is frequently misdiagnosed with primary open-angle glaucoma until posterior iris bowing, heavy TM pigmentation, or circular pigmentation of zonular attachment known as the Zentmayer ring or Scheie line under a large pupil is detected.[11–14] In pigmented patients such as Asian or Black populations, in whom ITDs are usually absent and KS is rare and indistinct, zonular pigmentation is of great diagnostic value for PDS as well as ITDs in White patients, which, however, can only be discerned after dramatic pupil dilation. To confirm the diagnosis of PDS, it is not uncommon to dilate pupils in PDS suspects of Asian or Black people. Nevertheless, pupil dilation is not as commonly observed in glaucoma patients or suspects, because it may lead to intraocular pressure (IOP) fluctuation or even an IOP spike. Literature indicates that pupil dilation in patients with PDS may cause IOP elevation and dramatic fluctuations.[15–19] Such findings have discouraged medical practitioners from dilating pupils in glaucoma patients or suspects.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the characteristics of IOP fluctuations in Chinese patients with PDS after drug-induced mydriasis.

2. Methods

This prospective cohort study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Beijing Tongren Hospital, China (TRECKY2021-039). The protocol was registered on the China Clinical Trial Registry website (ChiCTR2100046005). After providing informed consent, all participants were enrolled and continued their preexisting IOP-lowering medications without washout before dilation.

2.1. Patient selection

From October 2022 to March 2024, study subjects were consecutively recruited from patients referred to the glaucoma clinic at our hospital with a diagnosis of PDS/PG in both eyes. The diagnostic criteria for PDS/PG in the Chinese population were identified based on the results of our previous study.[2] Patients exhibiting any signs of exfoliation material in the anterior segment, previous intraocular surgery or laser therapy (except for laser peripheral iridotomy [LPI]), and those using medicines that affect pupil size were excluded.

2.2. Examination procedure

Prior to mydriasis, all subjects underwent ophthalmic examinations, including visual acuity, IOP, pupil diameter (PD), slit-lamp examination, gonioscopy, ultrasonic biomicroscopy, and stereoscopic fundus photography. The gonioscopy was performed by the same observer (Q.G.). The TM pigmentation score for each eye was calculated as the cumulative sum of all 4 quadrants.[20] The ultrasonic biomicroscopy (MD-300L; Maid) was conducted in a relaxed state following the methodology outlined by Mora et al.[21] The iris configuration was classified into concave, convex, and plateau forms.

Each patient was instilled with 1 drop (approximately 30–40 µL) of 0.5% tropicamide and phenylephrine (Mydrin-P; Santen) twice in one of the 2 eyes at 15-minute intervals (8:00 am and 8:15 am) to induce pupil dilation. Measurements in both eyes were conducted at least 5 minutes after adjusting to the room light in the following order: IOP, PD, and anterior chamber (AC) pigment shedding. These were monitored hourly for 8 hours after dilation (9:00 am to 4:00 pm). If post-mydriatic IOP ≥ 40 mm Hg occurred at any time, the affected eye received medical intervention.

One investigator (L.Y.) measured the PD using a pupillometer incorporated in the NIDEK ARK-1 (NIDEK). The IOP was measured automatically by another investigator (W.Y.) using a TX-F noncontact tonometer (Canon). If the coefficient of variation for the 3 results was below 10% (or ≤ 2 mm Hg), the mean value was recorded; otherwise, the procedure was repeated.

The AC pigment shedding was labeled qualitatively with a slit-lamp by a single masked examiner (Y.Y.) according to the standards modified by Epstein et al[19] (none = 0, trace = 1+, mild = 2+, moderate = 3+, or severe = 4+). To ensure a standardized observation field, the assessment of pigment particle density was confined to the area illuminated within the range of a single beam. The beam width was standardized by setting the slit-lamp to a width of “1” under 16 × magnification.

2.3. Statistical analysis

PASS software version 11 (NCSS, Kaysville) was used to calculate the minimum required sample size. This study required 11 patients to achieve a power of 0.9 and an alpha value of 0.05. Data analysis was performed using the SPSS software version 25.0 for Windows (IBM, Armonk).

Between groups, the mean value differences in continuous variables were analyzed using Student t test. Discrete variables were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test. Fisher exact test was used to compare proportions, as needed. Measurement consistency was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient for repeated measures and the weighted Kappa coefficient for inter-observer agreement between the masked examiner and the reference standard. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

Twenty-six eyes from 26 patients (20 males and 6 females), with an average age of 39.0 ± 8.4 years, were recruited. No participants were excluded after enrollment, withdrew, or had missing data for any of the repeated measurements. Eight had PDS and 18 had PG. None of the eyes had typical ITDs. Three eyes (11.5%, 3/26) had KS. Twenty eyes (76.9%, 20/26) showed ring-like pigmentation at the equator of the zonules and lens capsule after pupil dilation to more than 8 mm. All eyes were phakic with full-quadrant wide AC angles, as observed under gonioscopy.

Eleven eyes (42.3%, 11/26) underwent LPI. A plateau iris was observed in these eyes after LPI, whereas the remaining eyes without LPI had a concave iris. Nineteen eyes (69.2%, 18/26) were administered topical antiglaucoma medications. The average number of medications used was 1.1 ± 0.9, with prostaglandin analogues being the most prescribed (50%), followed by β-blockers (42.3%), carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (30.8%), and α-agonists (15.4%).

Repeated measurements of IOP and PD yielded intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.972 and 0.755, respectively (both P < .001). Inter-observer agreement for the pigment shedding grade showed a weighted Kappa of 0.972 (95% CI: 0.938–0.988; P < .001).

3.2. Main outcome measurements

One hour after dilation, the average IOP increased to a maximum of 18.3 ± 4.9 mm Hg (vs 16.7 ± 4.5 mm Hg at baseline; P = .002). Accordingly, the PD reached its peak at 8.3 ± 0.6 mm (vs 5.9 ± 1.0 mm at baseline; P < .001). Both curves showed a relatively flat trajectory with a gentle decline after the peak (Fig. 1). By the end of the 8-hour observation period, mean IOP had decreased to 17.4 mm Hg and mean PD to 7.1 mm.

The median IOP change (control △IOP = maximal IOP–baseline IOP) in the contralateral non-dilated eyes was 1.8 mm Hg (interquartile range, 0.6–2.9 mm Hg) during the study period. Three eyes (11.5%, 3/26) showed a post-dilation IOP elevation of ≥ 5 mm Hg. One eye receiving LPI showed a similar increase (dilated △IOP = 5.1 mm Hg). This case involved a 34-year-old male patient with PDS, who had been receiving dual IOP-lowering medications (Brinzolamide and Timolol/Tafluprost) for a long time. His baseline IOP before dilation was 17.3 mm Hg, which increased to a peak of 22.4 mm Hg 1 hour after dilation. No AC pigment shedding was detected during this period.

In the non-LPI group, 2 PG eyes met this criterion. Their baseline IOPs were 18.2 mm Hg and 18.9 mm Hg, respectively. In both cases, the IOP increase initiated early, becoming evident (≥ 5 mm Hg rise) by the 2nd hour after dilation. The elevation was sustained for approximately 4 hours. One eye not on any IOP-lowering therapy reached a peak of 39.3 mm Hg (dilated △IOP = 21.1 mm Hg) at 4 hours, whereas the other eye on long-term dual IOP-lowering medication (Timolol/Brimonidine Tartrate) peaked at 31.5 mm Hg (dilated △IOP = 12.6 mm Hg) at 5 hours. Both eyes exhibited moderate (3+) AC pigment shedding during the IOP elevation, but no visible pigment granules remained in the AC by the time they left the clinic.

No significant differences in baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were observed between the LPI and non-LPI groups (Table 1). In both groups, the most common time window for IOP peak occurrence was within the first hour after dilation, accounting for 27.3% (3/11) of eyes in the LPI group and 33.3% (5/15) in the non-LPI group. Within 4 hours post-dilation, 63.6% (7/11) of eyes in the LPI group reached their peak IOP, compared to 73.3% (11/15) in the non-LPI group. Notably, among eyes that exhibited an IOP spike in the non-LPI group, the elevation occurred earlier, with 90% (9/10) peaking within the first 2 hours.

4. Discussion

The present study investigated IOP fluctuations following pharmacologic mydriasis in Chinese patients with PDS/PG. Our cohort did not show a general increase in IOP after dilation with 0.5% tropicamide and phenylephrine, a combination widely used in clinical practice. In some cases, clinically significant IOP increases (≥ 5 mm Hg) occurred but naturally decreased without medical interventions. No emergency event with IOP ≥ 40 mm Hg occurred over the study period.

Three eyes (11.5%) exhibited an IOP increase of ≥ 5 mm Hg, 2 of which developed moderate AC pigment shedding. This incidence of clinically significant pigment release is notably lower than the rates reported in predominantly Caucasian cohorts, where “active” pigment reactions are considered a major risk factor for post-dilation IOP spikes.[15,16,18,22] These may be attributed to ethnic differences in iris anatomy. Our previous histologic study demonstrated that Chinese irides possess dense stromal pigmentation and a continuous multilayer of anterior surface melanocytes, whereas White irides show minimal pigmentation with a thinner, discontinuous melanocyte layer.[11]

This structural difference has clear clinical implications. In our previous cohort[2] and the current study, ITDs were either absent or appeared only as isolated slits, in stark contrast to the confluent, spoke-like defects usually reported in Caucasians. Chinese irides, which are typically thicker and more heavily pigmented than those of Caucasians,[11] are plausibly less prone to deformation and frictional contact with the lens zonules during pupillary movement. Consequently, even under identical pharmacological stimulation, pigment liberation is minimized in pigmented races. This concept is supported by previous clinical observations: transient pigment showers visible under slit-lamp examination were observed following pupil dilation in Caucasian patients with PDS.[15] This phenomenon appears to be less evident among Chinese patients.

Nevertheless, a small subset of eyes still exhibited notable pigment release and IOP elevation. These IOP spikes, however, resolved spontaneously without intervention. The overall limited IOP response suggests that in most Chinese PDS/PG patients, the balance between pigment liberation and its clearance remains compensatory following pharmacologic mydriasis. Even in the few cases where pigment release surpasses the baseline threshold (the level at which pigment granules become visible on slit-lamp examination), the disturbance appears to be transient.

The effect of LPI on PDS/PG is still a matter of debate.[23–25] In our cohort, clinically significant IOP elevations after dilation were observed in the eyes both on and off-laser treatment. The non-LPI group tended to have a higher amplitude of post-mydriatic IOP elevation than the LPI group; however, this difference was not statistically significant. In addition, the non-LPI group showed more severe mydriatic-related AC pigment shedding than did the LPI group. This finding is consistent with that of an earlier report in the literature. Küchle et al[16,22] observed that LPI could significantly reduce the number of aqueous pigments in dilated eyes with PDS. Based on this, we speculated that the long-term effects of LPI may play a role, at least in part, in stabilizing IOP fluctuations after dilation. However, the small number of patients involved in this study made the results insufficient for any possible further inferences. Future investigations should incorporate subgroup analyses based on risk factors (e.g., disease course stages: PDS vs PG) and diurnal IOP monitoring to identify which patient populations are most likely to benefit from prophylactic LPI.

No washout phase for antiglaucoma medications was conducted. This means that the absolute IOP values observed may underestimate true physiological levels, and medications may have influenced both the timing and magnitude of IOP responses. The fluctuations reported herein should be interpreted as conservative estimates of the true physiological response in the unmedicated or undiagnosed PDS population. We attempted to replicate a realistic clinical scenario, offering pupil dilation to PDS patients with newly diagnosed, medically controlled, or those with poor intolerance to IOP-lowering interventions. From this perspective, our findings offer practical insights for routine clinical care regarding the safety profile in a real-world, heterogeneous treatment setting.

This study had some limitations. First, the small sample size and homogeneous racial groups limited the generalizability of our results. This is partly attributable to the low incidence and high misdiagnosis rate of PDS in Asian populations. Our subgroup analyses lacked sufficient statistical power to detect small but potentially clinically relevant differences. Future studies would benefit from a prospective, multicenter cohort drawn from broader Asian populations. Second, although Goldmann applanation tonometry is the gold standard for IOP measurement, multiple topical anesthetics and repeated applanation over a short period can potentially cause corneal abrasion. We highlight within-individual magnitudes of pressure change and comparisons of response trends across subgroups, rather than focusing on cross-group differences in absolute measurements. A noncontact tonometer with proven high repeatability was considered sufficient and safer for this purpose.[26] Third, we did not measure corneal biomechanical properties such as central corneal thickness. As the primary endpoint was the intraindividual fluctuation pattern of IOP over time rather than absolute values at single time points, each participant served as their own control. Since corneal properties are stable structural characteristics over a short period, any potential bias they introduced would be systematic and constant for each subject.

The observation period in this study was limited to 8 hours post-dilation. While this duration was specifically chosen to capture the acute hemodynamic response to pupil dilation and pigment dispersion, the period during which the most dramatic IOP fluctuations are expected, it does not allow for the assessment of delayed or cumulative IOP elevations. It is plausible that pigment liberated during dilation may gradually accumulate in the TM over 24 to 48 hours, leading to a secondary, subacute IOP rise that would escape detection within our 8-hour window. Furthermore, the long-term consequences of repeated pupil dilation in PDS patients, including the risk of glaucomatous progression or the development of synechiae, remain entirely unaddressed by the current data. Therefore, the findings of this study should be strictly interpreted within the context of short-term IOP safety and should not be extrapolated to inform the safety profile of chronic or repeated dilation protocols. Longitudinal studies with extended follow-up are warranted to validate long-term safety.

In summary, this study indicates that drug-induced mydriasis appears to be a relatively safe procedure to perform in the majority of Chinese patients with PDS. This may partially alleviate physicians’ concerns about dilating the pupils of the eyes in this patient population.
