A numerical and experimental study of light obscuration in laparoscopy
Authors: Hossein Hadi Najafabadi, Meng Wai Woo, Vinod Suresh, Callum James Thomas Spence, Eu-Lee Teh, John Edward Cater
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeAerosol particles produced during laparoscopic electrosurgery (ES) obscure light and reduce visibility. This work investigates the effect of the flow rate of insufflating carbon dioxide on light obscuration.MethodsMonopolar ES was performed on an excised pig liver, and the light obscuration between a viewing laparoscope and surgical site was measured in an idealised computational domain and a corresponding rigid experimental model. To replicate typical surgical use, this study assumed a 60 s period of intermittent ES with no inlet flow, followed by a 60 s period of inlet flow with no ES. Particle size distributions in the aerosol were measured, and a specific light extinction coefficient was calculated for use as an input to simulations to predict light obscuration.ResultsExperimental results showed that obscuration increased exponentially, reaching a value of 47±2.3% after 60 s of ES. After ceasing ES, obscuration did not change significantly in the following 60 s. Introduction of carbon dioxide at flow rates of 5 l/min, 10 l/min, and 15 l/min reduced obscuration to 13±1%, 5±0.5%, and 2±0.2%, respectively, 60 s after stopping ES. Decay curves were well described by an exponential fit. Simulated decay rates agreed with experiments when inlet flow was applied.ConclusionIt is concluded that insufflation with carbon dioxide can reduce light obscuration, and computational models are capable of capturing the dynamics of aerosol transport in laparoscopic ES. The numerical model developed in this study can be used as a predictive tool to determine how light obscuration during laparoscopy depends on factors such as insufflation flow rate and to improve surgeon visibility.

Introduction

Laparoscopy is a minimally invasive surgical and diagnostic technique that uses a camera introduced through a rigid tube (laparoscope) for visualisation. Surgical and other instruments are introduced through other small incisions [15]. Laparoscopy is commonly carried out in the abdomen. Gas is pumped (insufflated) into the abdominal cavity to create sufficient space during laparoscopic surgery so that the surgeon can manipulate the instruments and visualise the organs [6]. Carbon dioxide is the most commonly used gas for insufflation because it is inexpensive, colourless, does not support combustion, and is highly soluble, which reduces the risk of gas embolism.

Electrosurgery is the process of using a high-frequency electrical current to carry out surgical procedures, such as cutting tissue [4]. It is commonly used in laparoscopic surgery. Pyrolysis of tissue during electrosurgery produces aerosols referred to as ‘surgical smoke’ containing water vapour and suspended particles. The precision of the surgical act can be decreased due to surgical smoke, which can remain as a mist or deposit on the lens of a laparoscope [14]. The optical clarity of the laparoscopic surgical field is, therefore, essential to maintain operative awareness, performance, and safety.

Lawrentschuk et al. (2010) [14] reviewed the literature on the causes of poor vision during laparoscopic surgery and the approaches for minimising such events. It was found that laparoscopic lens fogging caused by condensation as well as smoke, particulate debris, and blood accumulation on the scope lens are the major impediments to a clear visual field during laparoscopy. The deposition of smoke on the lens may be lessened if the assistant briefly moves the lens away from the tissue at the time of electrosurgery [13]. Another method for reducing poor vision is regular ventilation.

Different parameters can influence the amount and composition of surgical smoke, including the type and setting of an electrosurgical device (i.e., electrosurgery versus ultrasonic scissors [9], monopolar compared to bipolar devices [28], and the mode of cutting or coagulation [17].

A more recent literature review on the quantification of surgical smoke also concluded that surgical smoke impairs the surgeon’s vision during laparoscopic and robotic-assisted procedures [7]. It recommended the selection of electrosurgical instruments with the least surgical smoke generation to minimise the impact of surgical smoke. The need for further research was identified to determine other characteristics of smoke, such as smoke particle size and the distribution of smoke during laparoscopic surgery.

One of the first studies to quantify the effect of surgical smoke on visibility compared optical obscuration for different types of instruments, including bipolar macroforceps, harmonic scalpel, floating ball, and monopolar shears [28]. It was found that obstruction of the surgical field is mainly caused by high concentrations of particles smaller than 500 nm in diameter. In addition, a bipolar instrument degraded visibility the least, whereas monopolar shears generated the most surgical smoke, which caused the most deterioration of visibility compared to other instruments.

An Australian-German minimally invasive surgery group developed a list of principles to assist clear vision during laparoscopy [1]. The following principles were suggested: a constant flow of insufflation gas; smoke evacuation during electrosurgery; insufflation directly over the front lens; maintaining the temperature of the laparoscopic front optical lens at 37 °C; warming the insufflation gas to 37–45°C; and humidification of insufflation gas to 50–80% relative humidity.

Another study compared the surgical smoke emitted from lamb bone and liver tissue using monopolar electrosurgery, ultrasonic cutting, and a sagittal saw [3]. An impingement filtration system was used to collect and suspend the particulate matter in water. Particle size distribution was obtained using a laser diffraction system (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Panalytical, UK). A wide range of particle sizes was found for bone (0.93–806.93 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m) and liver (0.05–1040.05 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m), with 50% of the particles smaller than 2.7 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m, and 90% smaller than 10 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m.

Surgical smoke obscuration depends on the light wavelength and illuminance, and interactions with smoke particles such as scattering, absorption, diffraction, and other less significant effects [29]. The scattering of light is the dominant contributor to light extinction for smoke plumes with small particle sizes (less than 3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m), such as pyrolysis-generated smoke. For smoke plumes with larger particle sizes, such as flame-generated smoke, light absorption is the major contributor [20].

Numerical simulations have been used to investigate the dynamics of smoke particles and predict light obscuration in fires. Cohan (2010) [5] analysed the implementation of particle deposition mechanisms in Fire Dynamic Simulator (FDS) version 5.5.1 and compared the results with experimental measurements for a large range of compartment geometries and configurations. FDS is a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) program for fire-induced fluid flow developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology [19]. It was found that the inclusion of the soot deposition model improved the results for optical density. However, the model over-predicted the optical density and under-predicted the mass deposition on surfaces, possibly due to the assumption of invariant soot yield with fire size and an inadequate mesh resolution for small fires.

We have previously developed a numerical model of surgical smoke behaviour for a rectangular chamber geometry using FDS [22]. An experimental study was carried out to determine the smoke composition, smoke generation rate, water evaporation rate, and heat flux at the electrosurgery site. These parameters were provided as inputs to the numerical simulations. The computational model predicted that 31 - 35 % of the generated smoke was evacuated from an outlet port, and the remainder was accumulated close to the upper surface of the chamber due to buoyancy. In this work, we use that modelling and experimental framework to study light obscuration.

The particle size distribution of surgical smoke was measured, and a specific light extinction coefficient was calculated using Mie theory. The specific light extinction coefficient was applied in the numerical model to compute light obscuration. Electrosurgery was modelled using a heat-flux boundary condition with a specified particulate matter (PM) generation rate. Experiments were conducted to measure the light obscuration at different inlet flow rates of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {CO_2}$$\end{document}, and the results are compared to the numerical results. To the knowledge of the authors, this is the first time that the effect of inlet flow rate on visibility in electrosurgery has been studied.

Materials and methods

Experimental methodology: particle size distribution

Fresh liver samples originally intended for human consumption were obtained from a local abattoir and used in experiments to determine the particle size distribution in electrosurgery. A commercially available electrosurgery unit, the Megadyne Mega Power electrosurgical generator (Ethicon, Inc., Cincinnati, OH, USA), was utilised in cut mode at the 60 W power setting in the experiments. A monopolar hook (Erbe Elektromedizin GmbH, 20191-331) was applied to pig liver samples located on the top of a return electrode plate. The samples were placed between the laser source and lens of a laser diffraction instrument (Spraytec, Malvern, UK) which was used to measure the particle size distribution of smoke generated in tissue cutting. A lens with 300 mm focal length was used in the receiver module, which is capable of measuring the particle size within the range of 0.1 - 900 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu m$$\end{document}. Each measurement was repeated 3 times to obtain mean values. The instrument records particle size distribution and volume fraction data to a computer which were then used to calculate the specific light extinction coefficient.

Light extinction coefficient

The light sources used extensively for laparoscopic surgery are light-emitting diodes (LED) and xenon lamps with an intensity peak in the wavelength range of 450 nm to 475 nm [26]. The refractive index of water vapour in the surgical smoke at 475 nm was set to be 1.34 [25]. The density of particulate matter and water vapour particles were taken to be 1900 kg/m3 and 997 kg/m3, respectively [20]. Nonetheless, the actual density of particulate matter in surgical smoke may deviate from this approximation.

Experimental methodology: light obscuration

Boundary conditions

The initial temperature within the computational domain was set to 21°C. The surface of the cannulas, monopolar hook, laparoscope, chamber, and the sample were set to a fixed temperature of 21°C. The lateral non-solid exterior boundaries of the numerical domain were assumed to be ‘open’ boundaries. Gases are permitted to flow out, and the species mass fractions and temperature adopt their corresponding values in the grid cell adjacent to the boundary, enforcing a zero gradient condition.

The inlet flow temperature was set to be 21 °C to match the experimental conditions. The pyrolysis of the tissue was modelled as a source of surgical smoke with a constant generation rate and constant heat flux as specified according to Najafabadi et al. (2021) [22]. A constant mass flux is specified for the particulate matter (1.9 mg/s) and water vapour (11.7 mg/s) at the electrosurgery site [22]. The mass flux is applied periodically during the electrosurgery cycles. The deposition mechanisms considered in computing the deposition velocity are thermophoretic deposition, gravitational settling, and turbulent deposition. The calculation of deposition velocity for each mechanism is described in the FDS technical guide [19].

The initial time step size was set automatically by dividing the nominal size of the mesh cell by the characteristic velocity of the flow. The time step is adjusted during the computation to satisfy the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition. The lower and upper limits for the CFL were set to be 0.8 and 1.0. A second-order explicit time advancement scheme was used. A conservative second-order total variation diminishing (TVD) scheme for scalar transport was used with a Superbee flux limiter to prevent over-shooting and under-shooting in species concentrations and temperature. The ‘Van Driest’ damping function was employed as the near-wall turbulence model. The damping function causes the eddy viscosity to approach zero at the correct rate near the wall where the no-slip condition applies.

Domain discretisation

The required resolution for mesh-independent results was determined by computing solutions over a range of different mesh resolutions. The obscuration between the tip of the scope and the centre of the light sensor was used as the metric for convergence. The obscuration was calculated at 5 mesh resolutions with cell numbers (and mean cell sizes) of 1,516,200 (1.6 mm), 3,228,120 (1.2 mm), 4,433,600 (1.1 mm), 5,940,000 (1.0 mm), and 7,667,520 (0.9 mm) after 10 s with an inlet flow rate of 15 l/min and an initial PM density of 0.002 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {kg/m^3}$$\end{document}. In the mesh sensitivity study, the CFL number was bounded in the range of 0.8 and 1.0, so the time step was adjusted based on the cell size. According to the results, the estimated error associated with a cell number of 3,228,120 was −0.9 % using Richardson extrapolation.

One source of difference between the numerical and experimental results is the under-estimation of deposited PM computed in the numerical study. In higher concentrations of smoke, the collision rate of particles increases, and particles tend to agglomerate and form larger particles [10]. Consequently, the rate of deposition increases for larger particles. Since the agglomeration of particles is not modelled in the numerical study, the numerical model predicts higher values for obscuration, particularly for higher smoke concentrations.

Discussion

The specific light extinction coefficient of particulate matter in the surgical smoke calculated in the present study (8,962 m2/kg) is in the range of specific light extinction coefficients for flaming smoke suggested by Mulholland et al. (2000) [20] (7,600-9,800 m2/kg). The value is smaller for pyrolysis or smoulder-generated smoke with a mean value of about 3,000 m2/kg to 5,000 m2/kg as light absorption is lower for these types of smoke. The smaller value for pyrolysis/smouldering smoke compared to flaming is a result of the low light absorption of this smoke [20].

The numerical results for obscuration over-predicted the experimental data by 14% at the end of the electrosurgery period. This over-prediction of numerical results was more evident for higher concentrations of smoke. At higher smoke concentrations, smoke particles undergoing Brownian motion agglomerate to form larger particles with a higher settling velocity under gravitation [27]. Since FDS does not include an agglomeration mechanism, it is expected that the computations will under-predict deposition and over-predict optical density, as observed here and previously in Cohan (2010) [5].

Conclusions

This study presents new experimental and numerical results for smoke distribution in a model surgical geometry. The results show that obscuration inside the chamber decays exponentially with carbon dioxide insufflation. Higher flow rates led to a more rapid smoke evacuation and less obscuration. The agreement between experimental and computational results demonstrates that the numerical model is able to capture the behaviour of surgical smoke inside the chamber as a function of time.

This work showed that CFD can be used as a predictive tool for pre-operation planning of laparoscopic surgeries and visual impairment during electrosurgery. The numerical method can estimate the surgical smoke obscuration in surgical scenarios which are not easily achievable using other methods. The numerical method does not require images of the surgical field and can provide detailed information on different variables of interest inside the abdomen.
