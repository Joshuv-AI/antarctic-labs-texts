Comparative diagnostic accuracy of a You Only Look Once-11s deep learning model and human observers for detection of periapical lesions on intraoral periapical radiographs
Authors: Rajinder Kumar Bansal, Ashtha Arya, Birmohan Singh, Mamta Singla, Jatinder Pal Singh, Marut Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Context:Interpretation of intraoral periapical radiographs (IOPAs) is influenced by image quality and observer-related factors. This study evaluated a lightweight You Only Look Once (YOLO)-11s model for automated detection of periapical lesions and compared its output with that of a human observer.Aim:The study aimed to assess and compare the accuracy and diagnostic performance of YOLO-11s and human observers for detecting periapical lesions on IOPAsMaterials and Methods:Radiographic records from 500 patients were reviewed retrospectively, yielding 1330 annotated tooth-level images. The dataset was divided into training (80%), validation (10%), and test (10%) subsets. Images were normalized, contrast-enhanced, and resized to 640 × 640 pixels. YOLO-11s was trained for up to 200 epochs. Performance was assessed using precision, sensitivity (recall), F1-score, mean average precision (mAP), confusion matrix analysis, precision-recall curves, and inference time. Cohen’s kappa was used to assess interobserver agreement.Results:The YOLO-11s model demonstrated high diagnostic performance for periapical lesion detection, with a sensitivity of 89.4%, precision of 88.0%, and an F1-score of 88.4%. Out of 133 true tooth-level targets, 121 were detected, and 106 were correctly predicted as the periapical lesion (PLA) or healthy (PRA), giving an overall accuracy of 79.7%. The mAP50 and mAP50-95 values were 0.85 and 0.48, respectively, indicating good detection ability but moderate localization precision. However, no background region was correctly identified.Conclusion:The YOLO-11s model demonstrated high sensitivity for automated detection of periapical lesions on IOPAs and may serve as a valuable adjunctive diagnostic tool in endodontic practice.

INTRODUCTION

Accurate identification of periapical lesions is fundamental to endodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. Radiographic interpretation, however, is not entirely objective. It may be affected by the clinician’s experience, fatigue, image quality, and the subtle appearance of early disease. Intraoral periapical radiographs (IOPAs) also provide a two-dimensional representation of three-dimensional anatomy, so geometric distortion and superimposition can obscure changes in the periapical region. These factors contribute to both interobserver and intraobserver variation.[12]

Furthermore, machine learning and artificial intelligence (AI) progress in the last few years have completely changed medical imaging by facilitating automated, more reproducible, and standardized interpretations.[3] Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have shown excellent results in visually detecting the tiniest changes on dental radiographs, for example, in caries, identification of anatomical structures, and apical periodontitis.[4] There are several publications regarding AI-based methods on panoramic radiographs, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), and IOPAs with accuracies regularly reported to be over 90%.[567] Several challenges continue to impede progress in this research area, including limited dataset availability, lack of multicenter validation, overfitting issues, and insufficient real-world testing.

The You Only Look Once (YOLO) series of models has recently become the preferred choice for dental radiographic applications due to fast object detection in real-time and high sensitivity to small radiolucent features. Newer YOLO versions, like YOLO-v8 to YOLO-v11, include transformer-based layers, multiscale feature fusion, and improved bounding-box regression. YOLO-11s, the smaller and thus faster model, can be used in clinical settings with limited resources where a fast response is needed and, at the same time, high accuracy is required.[8]

Considering the growing interest in AI-assisted diagnosis in dentistry, the present study evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of the YOLO-11s deep learning model for detecting periapical lesions on IOPAs. The model’s performance was assessed using standard diagnostic metrics and compared with expert clinical interpretation used during dataset annotation and validation. The study aimed to explore the potential role of AI-assisted image analysis as a supportive tool for improving diagnostic consistency and efficiency in endodontic practice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and ethical considerations

This diagnostic accuracy study was conducted on the retrospective dataset of IOPAs of patients who have undergone routine dental care. The Institutional Ethical Committee approved the study (Protocol number IEC-SGTDCHRI/FDS/CONS and ENDO/PhD/2024/03), and radiographs were anonymized before the analysis to ensure confidentiality. The study complied with the Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies guidelines, in which the design mainly emphasized the performance evaluation of an AI model,[9] YOLO-11s, that can detect periapical radiolucencies and also differentiate them from healthy periapical bones. A total of 500 patient radiographs were examined, from which 1330 tooth-level images representing both periapical lesions and healthy periapical regions were extracted for model development and testing.

Sample size estimation

The sample size estimation was undertaken to ascertain sufficient statistical power for the assessment of diagnostic accuracy. Based on a sensitivity of 81% estimated by Ba-Hattab et al.[10] Using a 5% margin of error and 95% confidence level, the minimum number of periapical lesions required was calculated using the formula n = Z2 P (1 − P)/d2, yielding a sample size of 237 lesion images. To keep the number of diseased and nondiseased cases equal, at least the same number of healthy periapical images was added, making the target sample approximately 500 radiographs. The final dataset of 1330 tooth-level images surpassed this requirement and thus provided a solid base for the training, validation, and testing of the model.

Data acquisition, preprocessing, and augmentation

A standardized imaging protocol was followed for the acquisition of all radiographs. Intraoral radiographs were obtained using the Optima Wall Mount X-Ray Unit operating at 65 kV and 7 mA, along with the Carestream RVG 5200 sensor, which provided a resolution of 20-line pairs/mm and 16-bit grayscale depth. Radiographs exhibiting motion artifacts, improper angulation, excessive noise, or overlapping anatomical structures were excluded to maintain diagnostic quality.

The selected images underwent preprocessing to reduce variability and standardize model training. Grayscale intensities were normalized to a 0–1 range, followed by contrast enhancement using Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization with a clip limit of 2.0 and an 8 × 8 tile grid to improve visualization of subtle apical radiolucencies. Subsequently, all images were resized to 640 × 640 pixels using bicubic interpolation with zero padding while preserving the original aspect ratio. This preprocessing workflow minimized interimage variability while preserving diagnostically relevant features essential for AI-based lesion detection.

To enhance model robustness and generalization, data augmentation techniques were applied. Images were flipped horizontally and vertically to enable the model to learn orientation-invariant features. Random rotations ranging from −20° to +20° were introduced to simulate variations in X-ray positioning. In addition, shear transformations of up to ± 10° in both horizontal and vertical directions were performed to mimic real-world distortions commonly observed in dental radiographs. Low-level Gaussian noise was also added to simulate minor imaging variations and improve the model’s resistance to noise-related artifacts.

Dataset annotation and ground-truth establishment

Ground-truth labels were established through expert evaluation of all radiographs. A certified endodontist with more than 20 years of clinical experience performed the initial annotation using LabelImg (Tzutalin, GitHub, Inc., San Francisco, California, USA). Each radiograph was carefully assessed for the presence or absence of periapical radiolucencies. The lesions measuring more than 1 mm in diameter were annotated using bounding boxes and classified as periapical lesion area (PLA). Images showing an intact periodontal ligament space and normal periapical appearance were classified as healthy periapical root area (PRA) [Figure 1a and b].

To improve reliability and reduce subjective bias, a second experienced endodontist independently reviewed a randomly selected 10% of the annotated images. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus. Interobserver agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient and demonstrated excellent agreement (κ = 0.85), indicating high consistency in lesion identification and annotation.

Dataset split

The dataset was divided into training, validation, and test subsets to ensure reliable model development and evaluation. The training set comprised 80% of the total dataset and included 1064 images, which were used for model learning and feature extraction for accurate dental anomaly detection. The validation set accounted for 10% of the dataset, containing 133 images, and was utilized during training to monitor model performance, optimize hyperparameters, and minimize overfitting. The remaining 10% of the dataset, consisting of 133 images, was allocated to the test set for final performance evaluation and assessment of the model’s ability to generalize to previously unseen data.

Artificial intelligence model development (You Only Look Once-11s Architecture)

The AI model was built on YOLO-11s, which is a lightweight object detection architecture intended for efficient real-time performance. YOLO-11s uses a modified CSPDarknet backbone for feature extraction and a Feature Pyramid Network neck to improve multiscale detection of small- and medium-sized objects. Three separate detection heads allow the simultaneous prediction of bounding boxes, objectness scores, and classification probabilities for the lesion and healthy categories. The model has 9.4 million parameters and runs at 21.5 billion floating–point operations (FLOPs), which is a good mix of computational efficiency and detection accuracy. Nonmaximum suppression was applied to remove overlapping bounding boxes with an intersection over union (IoU) threshold of 0.5. The architecture was fine-tuned to work on both central processing unit (CPU) and graphics processing unit (GPU) hardware, thus enabling subsequent integration into clinical workflows where real-time inference is a requirement.

Training and validation protocol

The model was trained on the 1064-image dataset using the Adam optimizer, with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and weight decay of 0.0005 to enhance generalization. A batch size of 16 was used due to the limitations of the graphics card, and the model was trained for 200 epochs with early stopping if the validation loss did not get better for 10 successive epochs.

The loss functions included Complete Intersection over Union for bounding-box regression, classification loss for categorical predictions, and distribution focal loss to better control boundary localization. The validation set was used during the entire training period to fine-tune the hyperparameters and check the model’s performance at each epoch [Figure 1b].

Performance evaluation metrics

The model was tested on a completely independent 133-image test set. The primary performance metrics used were sensitivity (recall), specificity, precision, F1-score, and mean average precision at IoU thresholds of 0.5 (mAP50) and 0.5–0.95 (mAP50-95). The sensitivity was calculated as true positive (TP)/(TP + false negative [FN]), specificity as true negative (TN)/(TN + false positive [FP]), precision as TP/(TP + FP), accuracy as (TP + TN)/(TP + TN + FP + FN), and F1-score as the harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity. These metrics measure the accuracy of classification as well as the capacity for spatial localization. The secondary metrics consisted of inference time and precision-recall curves for each class. The evaluation was also based on confusion matrices of periapical lesions (PLA), healthy periapical region (PRA), and background categories to perform a detailed analysis of misclassification types. The overall sensitivity was calculated using all 133 true tooth-level test targets, including those missed as background.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25, IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Diagnostic performance was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, precision, sensitivity (recall), F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to assess interobserver agreement during annotation. The Chi-square analysis was performed to examine the association between model predictions and ground-truth labels. Fisher’s exact test was used for the teeth-versus-background table because of the zero cell and small expected frequencies. The areas under the class-specific area under the precision-recall curves (AUC-PR) were interpreted descriptively. Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05. These analyses provided a comprehensive assessment of the classification performance and reliability of the YOLO-11s model.

RESULTS

Patients’ demographic and clinical data

The study included 500 individuals, with males outnumbering females (55.6%, n = 278 vs. 44.4%, n = 222). Regarding age distribution, the largest proportion of participants belonged to the 31–40 years of age group (35.6%, n = 178). They were followed by the 20–30 years of age group (25.6%, n = 128), whereas almost 19% each was made up by the 41–50 and 51–60 years of age groups. In terms of the diagnosis of the specific periapical diseases, the chronic apical abscess was the most common diagnosis (35.52%, n = 378), followed by symptomatic apical periodontitis (24.06%, n = 256), acute apical abscess (17.29%, n = 184), and finally, asymptomatic apical periodontitis (15.78%, n = 168). The results suggest that it is mostly male and middle-aged persons who suffer from a higher prevalence of periapical diseases and that the cases of chronic disease outnumber the cases of acute disease in such circumstances.

Performance of You Only Look Once-11 model

The YOLO-11s model went through the learning process using 1064 images (80% out of 1330 images in total) and was then tested on 133 (10%) images, which were healthy teeth (PRA), periapical lesions (PLA), and background. During the training, the performance of metrics improved primarily over 200 epochs when the box loss was going down from 2.25 to 0.75, and the classification loss was reduced from 3.0 to 0.5. Precision and recall both achieved values higher than 0.85. The validation results confirmed the good generalization of the model as mAP50 came close to 0.9; however, the more difficult mAP50-95 metric only reached the value of 0.5, thereby indicating that the model had difficulties with accurate localization [Figure 2a]. The model reached the testing phase with a precision of 0.84 and a sensitivity (recall) of 0.90 while maintaining an average inference time of 25.4 ms [Table 1]. The three performance curves depict that the trained model is very effective in detecting PLA and PRA. The precision-recall curve demonstrates a high area under the curve (exceeding 0.90) for both classes, suggesting robust performance across a wide range of operating points. The decrease in confidence threshold from 0.8 to near 0.3 demonstrated that the recall rises significantly, which means that almost all TPs are detected with moderate-to-high confidence. The F1-confidence curve indicates that the highest performances are achieved at confidence thresholds of about 0.35 for the orange class (F1 ≈ 0.84) and about 0.55 for the blue class (F1 ≈ 0.87), which are significantly better than the default 0.5 threshold [Figure 2b].

Comparison with clinician performance

The YOLO-11s model achieved an overall accuracy of 79.7% for differentiating periapical lesions from healthy periapical areas. In the test set, 121 of 133 true tooth-level targets were detected as either PLA or PRA; 12 targets were not detected and were assigned to background. Based on the confusion matrix analysis, the model correctly identified 59 true-positive and 47 true-negative cases, with 8 false-positive, and 7 false-negative classifications. For lesion detection, the model demonstrated a sensitivity of 89.4%, precision of 88.0%, and an F1-score of 88.4% [Figure 3], indicating strong performance in identifying diseased cases. The area under the precision-recall curve was 0.75, reflecting good discriminatory ability [Figure 2b].

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of a custom-developed YOLO-11s deep learning model for the automated detection of periapical lesions on intraoral periapical (IOPA) radiographs. The model was able to achieve an overall accuracy of 79.7% along with a very high sensitivity of 89.4% when identifying periapical lesions, together with a strong precision rating of 88.0%. This study’s results are consistent with the numerous reports that have been published on the application of AI in the interpretation of radiographs for endodontic diagnostic purposes, demonstrating the capability of the model to recognize minute changes in radiolucency that can be easily missed even by experienced clinicians during a routine oral examination.

Comparison with existing literature

A number of studies published recently have illustrated how effectively AI models can detect periapical pathosis. Pauwels et al. reported that CNNs achieved a sensitivity and specificity of 79% and 88%, respectively, thus performing better than human observers (58% sensitivity and 83% specificity) in the same diagnostic tasks.[11]

According to Endres et al., the deep learning algorithm outperforms half of the Oral and maxillofacial surgeons within the group, achieving an average precision of 0.60 in detecting periapical disease.[12] Other authors have also documented similar results.[410]

Although the diagnostic performance of YOLO-11s in the present study was slightly lower than that reported for models trained on larger or multimodal datasets (e.g. CBCT), it was comparable to that of CNN-based models developed using two-dimensional periapical radiographs. Furthermore, the high sensitivity (89.4%) for periapical lesion detection indicates that the YOLO-11s model effectively identified the majority of true lesion cases.

This high recall rate is important from a clinically important as missing a diagnosis can cause a disease to deteriorate and delay treatment. The precision score (88.0%) indicates a fairly low number of FPs, thus confirming the findings of Ba-Hattab et al.[10] who found that AI models tend to overdiagnose lesions on panoramic radiographs due to the presence of overlapping structures and image noise.

Detection of healthy periapical regions resulted in relatively fewer classification errors, with a precision of 87.0% and a sensitivity (recall) of 85.4% [Figure 3]. Similar findings have been reported by Estrela et al., who noted that healthy periapical bone often lacks well-defined radiographic borders and may resemble early-stage periapical pathology on radiographs.[13] This may be because deep learning models trained predominantly on lesion-rich datasets are more likely to interpret subtle variations in normal bone density as radiolucent lesions, leading to false-positive predictions.

Interpretation of model performance

The YOLO-11s model demonstrated encouraging performance for the automated detection of periapical lesions. High sensitivity and precision values suggest that the model was capable of identifying most lesions while maintaining a relatively low false-positive rate. Clinically, this finding is important because missed lesions may delay diagnosis and treatment planning.

The model also showed satisfactory object-detection performance, with an mAP50 value of 0.85. However, the lower mAP50-95 value of 0.48 indicates that accurate localization of lesion boundaries remains challenging, particularly on two-dimensional radiographs where anatomical overlap is common.[14]

One of the principal limitations observed in this study was the poor performance of the background class. Background regions were frequently classified as tooth-related structures, likely due to class imbalance and the limited representation of background images during training. Similar observations have been reported in previous AI-based radiographic studies, where underrepresented classes contributed to biased model predictions.[1516]

Although the lesion-detection results are promising, the present findings suggest that additional optimization of dataset composition, annotation strategies, and class-balancing techniques is necessary before widespread clinical implementation can be recommended.

Clinical‍‌ implications

The findings of this study suggest that YOLO-11s may serve as a useful adjunctive tool for radiographic interpretation in endodontics. The high sensitivity of the YOLO-11s model suggests that it could serve as a valuable adjunct to clinicians by improving the detection of subtle periapical radiographic changes that might otherwise be missed during routine assessment. AI-assisted analysis may also contribute to improved diagnostic consistency by reducing interobserver variability. Such tools may be particularly valuable in busy clinical environments and in settings where specialist consultation is not readily available.

Nevertheless, AI-generated findings should always be interpreted alongside clinical examination, patient history, and conventional radiographic assessment. At present, AI should be regarded as a supportive aid rather than a replacement for clinician judgment.

Future recommendations

Future studies should focus on the development of larger multicenter datasets incorporating greater diversity in patient demographics, anatomical variations, and imaging conditions. Such datasets would improve model generalizability and reduce the risk of overfitting.

Addressing class imbalance, particularly the underrepresentation of background images, should be considered a priority. Advanced sampling strategies, targeted augmentation methods, and balanced training datasets may improve performance across all classes.

Further research should also investigate the integration of CBCT data, multimodal learning approaches, and explainable AI frameworks to enhance diagnostic accuracy and clinician acceptance. Prospective clinical studies evaluating real-time chairside performance will be necessary before routine implementation can be recommended.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations. First, the study relied exclusively on two-dimensional IOPAs, which are inherently affected by anatomical superimposition and image distortion. These factors may influence lesion localization and diagnostic accuracy.

Second, although the dataset was adequate for model development, it was derived from a single institution and may not fully represent the variability encountered in broader clinical populations.

Third, a substantial class imbalance was present, particularly with respect to the background category, which adversely affected background classification performance. Finally, the model was designed to detect the presence or absence of periapical lesions and was not trained to distinguish among specific pathological entities such as granulomas, cysts, or abscesses. External validation and prospective clinical evaluation are required before conclusions can be drawn about performance in routine practice.

CONCLUSION

The YOLO-11s deep learning model demonstrated promising performance for the detection of periapical lesions on IOPAs, achieving high sensitivity and good precision. These findings support the potential role of AI as an adjunctive tool for radiographic interpretation in endodontic practice.

However, limitations related to background classification, dataset imbalance, and the use of a single-center dataset should be carefully considered. Further validation using larger and more diverse datasets, together with prospective clinical studies, is required before routine clinical implementation can be recommended.
