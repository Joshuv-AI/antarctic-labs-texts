Impact of Cosmic Filaments on Galaxy Morphological Evolution and Predictions of Early Cosmic Web Structure for Roman
Farhanul Hasan, Haowen Zhang (张昊文), Viraj Pandya, Marc Rafelski, Joseph N. Burchett, Douglas Hellinger, Kalina V. Nedkova, Ilias Goovaerts, Nir Mandelker, Daisuke Nagai, Grecco A. Oyarzún, Joel R. Primack, Joanna Woo

Abstract
We leverage the IllustrisTNG cosmological simulations to test how the large-scale cosmic web shapes galaxy morphology and to forecast the early cosmic web structure that the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will reveal. In the hydrodynamic TNG50 and N N -body TNG50-Dark runs, we reconstruct the cosmic web at redshifts z = z= 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 4 with the Monte Carlo Physarum Machine density estimator and the DisPerSE structure identification framework. We confirm that dark matter halos start out predominantly prolate (elongated) and their shapes are aligned with their nearest filaments; prolate galaxies retain strong shape-alignment with their outer halos to later times. The fraction of prolate galaxies and halos increases toward lower stellar mass, higher redshift, and lower-density filaments. Oblate and spheroidal galaxies show weaker trends with filament density, but spheroidal halos preferentially reside in higher-density filaments. We also find that higher-density filaments favor extended rotationally-supported disks, whereas lower-density filaments more often host smaller dispersion-supported systems. Then, generating mock galaxy samples from TNG100 and TNG50, we predict the early cosmic web accessible to Roman. We find that the spectroscopic emission-line depth planned for the High-Latitude Wide-Area Survey (HLWAS) yields a highly incomplete galaxy sample that does not accurately trace the z = 1 z=1 cosmic web. A survey ≥ \geq 2.5 × \times deeper over a few square degrees would enable a proper reconstruction and reveal qualitatively correct filament-galaxy morphology relationships. Nevertheless, the planned HLWAS Deep field should still identify most galaxy overdensities; targeted deeper spectroscopy of these regions would efficiently and adequately map the early filamentary structure.



I
Introduction

The anisotropic collapse of matter under the force of gravity results in the formation of the web-like patterns of the large-scale structure known as the cosmic web, consisting of filaments, sheets, nodes, and voids. During this process, tidal torques of the cosmic web impart angular momentum, or spin, to galaxies as they form
(
Peebles 1969
;
Zel’dovich 1970
;
White 1984
;
Codis et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
. This has major implications for the development of galaxy structure, including the formation of disks
(
Pichon et al. 2011
;
Stewart et al. 2013
;
Danovich et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
and elongated or prolate galaxies
(
Ceverino et al. 2015
;
Tomassetti et al. 2016
;
Pandya et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
.
Crucially, this effect of the
anisotropic
configuration of the cosmic web depends on geometry and is distinct from the long-established morphology-density relation, where the morphology of a galaxy depends on some
isotropic
measure of environment such as local density
(
Dressler 1980
;
Postman & Geller 1984
;
Kauffmann et al. 2004
, e.g.,)
.

A growing body of evidence suggests that galaxies and/or their parent dark matter (DM) halos retain some of the signatures of angular momentum acquisition from the cosmic web out to later times.
Most cosmological simulations predict that the spin axes or the major axes of galaxies/halos are aligned in some way to the direction of their nearest filaments
and these alignments often vary with factors such as redshift, mass, morphology, and even density of the filaments themselves

(
Laigle et al. 2015
;
Codis et al. 2018
;
Ganeshaiah Veena et al. 2021
;
Wang & Kang 2018
;
Kraljic et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. Broadly, the simulations predict a tendency for the major axes of halos to be preferentially aligned with the direction of the nearest filament (so-called “shape-filament alignment”), more so for higher halo masses and higher density filaments, while the alignment between filaments and galaxy/halo spin axes (“spin-filament alignment”) transition from aligned (parallel) to misaligned (perpendicular) for lower-redshift (
z\lesssim 1
) and massive (
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\gtrsim 10-10.5
) galaxies
(
Hahn et al. 2007
;
Codis et al. 2012
;
Dubois et al. 2014
;
Welker et al. 2014
;
Wang et al. 2018
;
Ganeshaiah Veena et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. While observational studies have lent support for these phenomena
(
Tempel et al. 2013
;
Blue Bird et al. 2020
;
Welker et al. 2020
;
Kraljic et al. 2021
;
Barsanti et al. 2022
;
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2025a
, e.g.,)
, several others have either found no statistically significant alignment signals or
contradicted some of the expected trends above
(
Pahwa et al. 2016
;
Hirv et al. 2017
;
Krolewski et al. 2019
;
Tudorache et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
.
We may expect stronger alignments with filaments in the outer regions of DM halos which are sensitive to more recent mass accretion
(
Wang et al. 2011
, e.g.,)
, while the inner regions bear complexities beyond the linear transfer of tidal forces from the large-scale structure
(
Codis et al. 2012
;
Codis et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
.

The transfer of angular momentum from the halo scale to the galaxy scale involves non-linear and baryonic processes acting on the inflowing gas. This gas will be subject to torques and other forces from the circumgalactic medium (CGM) and interstellar medium (ISM) of galaxies, leading to a loss of angular momentum of accreting gas
(
Danovich et al. 2015
;
Cadiou et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
.
Once infalling gas cools and forms stars, angular momentum is imparted to the stars, and the salient morphological characteristics of galaxies, such as stellar structure, including spheroidal (bulge-dominated), oblate (disk-dominated), or prolate (elongated) shapes, and spin, which determines the degree of rotational vs. dispersion support, are established. Thus, despite the complexities of baryonic physics, we expect the cosmic web to be causally linked to the emergence of galaxy morphology.

In the classical
\Lambda
CDM picture of galaxy formation, disk galaxies emerge naturally from gravitational collapse of baryons in the early Universe
(
Fall & Efstathiou 1980
;
Blumenthal et al. 1984
;
Mo et al. 1998
, see, e.g., )
, but observations of high-redshift galaxy morphologies have revealed a richer and more complicated story.
Many lines of (decades-long) evidence from the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) and other observatories hinted towards a large population of galaxies with elongated and/or irregular morphologies
(
Cowie et al. 1995
;
Elmegreen et al. 2005
;
Ravindranath et al. 2006
;
Law et al. 2012
;
Yuma et al. 2012
, e.g.,)
.
By comparing the projected axis ratio distributions of local (
z<0.1
) galaxies in the Sloan Digital Sky Survey
(
York et al. 2000
, SDSS;)
and
z\sim 1.5-2
galaxies observed by HST,
van der Wel et al. 2014
concluded that a significant fraction of high-redshift galaxies must have preferentially elongated stellar structures, in contrast to the oblate structures that dominate at low-redshift.
Zhang et al. 2019
jointly modeled galaxy axis ratios and sizes (semi-major axes) and extended the sample to redshifts
z=0.5-2.5
to robustly demonstrate that the
majority
of
z\geq 2
galaxies with stellar masses
9.5<{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}<10
and
z\geq 1
galaxies with
9<{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}<9.5
are elongated.

Recently, using higher resolution and deeper James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) imaging,
Pandya et al. 2024
confirmed this phenomenon of low-mass high-redshift galaxies being more likely prolate than disky. They showed that the fraction of prolate galaxies with
9<{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}<9.5
drops from
\sim
80% to
\sim
25% from
z=8
to
z=1
, while the fraction of oblate galaxies of this mass rises from
\sim
20% to
\sim
60% between
z=8
and 1. High-mass (
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}>10
) galaxies tend to be a mix of oblates and spheroids at
z<3
and mostly oblate at
z\geq 3
. However, establishing the link between the emergence of these structures and their location within the cosmic web remains elusive in observational data.

Some zoom-in hydrodynamical simulations predict a crucial role of the cosmic web in the formation of high-redshift prolate dwarf galaxies and their subsequent evolution to oblate and spheroidal shapes.

Ceverino et al. 2015
and
Tomassetti et al. 2016
found that the
VELA
simulations
(
Ceverino et al. 2014
)
produce
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\lesssim 9.5
prolate galaxies inside preferentially prolate halos at
z\approx 2-4
, which they argue is a consequence of asymmetric accretion of DM and gas from narrow filaments and mergers. Feedback from early star formation prevents the overabundance of baryons at the cores of these halos, resulting in DM-dominated cores, where DM exerts torques on the stars and causes strong alignment between the major axes of the halo and stars.

Pandya et al. 2019
proposed that intrinsic alignments of these early elongated galaxies could be used as a novel cosmological probe of high-redshift cosmic web filaments, and
Pandya et al. 2025
recently found preliminary evidence for such alignments in sub-regions of one JWST “blank” deep field.
At later times, the buildup of stellar mass causes baryons to dominate the cores and lose angular momentum, causing a so-called “compaction” event wherein gas falls to the center of the galaxy and forms compact stellar disks
(
Dekel & Burkert 2014
;
Zolotov et al. 2015
;
Tacchella et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. Combining these phenomena,
Lapiner et al. 2023
outlined a morphological evolutionary sequence of galaxies from prolate to oblate/spheroidal, and dispersion-dominated to rotation-dominated kinematics.

spite the compelling picture painted by these results, the
VELA
simulations evolve individual halos and as of such cannot explicitly characterize the cosmic web filaments and therefore directly constrain their effect on galactic morphological evolution.
To date, no hydrodynamical simulations with a fully cosmological volume has probed the effect of the cosmic web on the angular momentum acquisition and morphological evolution of a statistically large sample of galaxies, and evolution therein
(see
Woo et al. 2025, submitted
, for the relationship between accreting gas and galactic gas at
z=0
)
.

Accordingly, the first goal of this paper is to better understand how the accretion of matter from the cosmic web affects the angular momentum acquisition and morphological evolution of galaxies using state-of-the-art hydrodynamical cosmological simulations. To this end, we investigate galaxies in the context of their cosmic web environment in the high-resolution TNG50 simulation
(
Nelson et al. 2019a
;
Pillepich et al. 2019
)
of the IllustrisTNG
(
Nelson et al. 2018
)
simulation suite.
We first characterize the cosmic web filaments in TNG50 using a novel filament identification framework first presented in
Hasan et al. 2024
.
Their approach enables a highly complete filament identification, including both high- and low-density filaments, as well as a simple characterization of a filament’s 1D line density.
At redshifts,
z=4
, 3, 2, 1, 0.5, and 0, we measure the 3D shapes of both the stellar and DM components of galaxies down to stellar mass
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}=8
and determine their alignment with respect to the nearest filament. We further study the connection between the density of the nearest filament and kinematic morphological properties of galaxies, such as stellar spin and fraction of stars in bulge- and disk-like orbits.

The second goal of this paper is to predict the structure of the early cosmic web, as we enter the golden age of wide-field galaxy surveys with groundbreaking facilities such as the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument
(
DESI Collaboration et al. 2024
;
DESI Collaboration et al. 2025
, DESI;)
, Euclid
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2025b
;
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2025a
)
, Subaru Prime Focus Spectrograph
(
Takada et al. 2014
;
Greene et al. 2022
, PFS;)
, SPHEREx
(
Doré et al. 2016
;
Feder et al. 2024
)
, and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope
(
Wang et al. 2022
, Roman;)
.
These observatories will provide the wide-area coverage and sensitivity required to map the cosmic web – and for the first time, at higher redshifts (
z\!\geq\!1
).
This is a major step towards establishing how well we predict the influence of the cosmic web on the formation of galaxy morphology.
Our analyses will uniquely set the scene for us to understand the role of large-scale environment in assembling galactic structure at this peak epoch of galaxy formation in the real Universe
(
Madau & Dickinson 2014
;
Förster Schreiber & Wuyts 2020
, e.g.,)
.

)
, with

,

, and

II
Simulation Data and Analysis

II.1
TNG Simulation Data

We analyze the outputs from the IllustrisTNG magneto-hydrodynamical cosmological simulations
(
Marinacci et al. 2018
;
Nelson et al. 2018
;
Nelson et al. 2019b
;
Pillepich et al. 2018
;
Springel et al. 2018
)
, which use the
AREPO
moving-mesh hydrodynamic code
(
Springel 2010
)
to simulate the evolution of gas, stars, dark matter, and black holes from redshifts
z\!=\!127
to
z\!=\!0
assuming a
Planck 2016
cosmology
(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2016
)
.
Since we investigate the morphologies of galaxies, we primarily use the highest resolution run of the TNG50 simulation
(
Nelson et al. 2019a
;
Pillepich et al. 2019
)
, TNG50-1, which has a box size of approximately 51.7 comoving Mpc per side, a minimum baryonic particle mass of
\sim\!8.4\!\times\!10^{4}~{\mathrm{M}_{\odot}}
, DM particle mass of
\sim\!4.6\!\times\!10^{5}~{\mathrm{M}_{\odot}}
, a
z=0
gravitational force softening length in DM and stars of 288 pc, and a minimum gas softening length of 72 physical pc.

We obtain galaxy, halo, and particle data for 6 snapshots – at
z=4
, 3, 2, 1, 0.5, 0 – of the TNG50-1 simulation (hereafter TNG50) from the online data repository
, as presented first in
Nelson et al. 2019b
. In each snapshot, the “Group” catalogs were constructed using the friends-of-friends (FoF) substructure identification algorithm, and the “subhalo” catalogs were constructed using the
Subfind
algorithm
(
Springel et al. 2001
;
Dolag et al. 2009
)
. The “subhalo” catalogs contain gravitationally bound objects within a FoF group, while the “Group” catalogs identify halos, i.e., FoF groups.
We extract data from both of these types of catalogs. Hereafter, following the convention of TNG, we refer to groups as halos and subhalos as galaxies.
We also make use of gas, DM, and star particle data in all snapshots.
Finally, to constrain the effects of baryonic feedback physics on the morphological properties of galaxies, we also analyze the
N
-body version of TNG50, i.e., TNG50-Dark, for all 6 snapshots, making use of the subhalo, halo, and DM particles in them.

We limit our analyses to galaxies with stellar mass
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\geq 8
at each redshift, which ensured that all galaxies contain over 1000 star particles each. This allows us to confidently resolve the morphologies of galaxies with
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\geq 8
, at spatial scales
\lesssim
100 pc. We recognize that TNG100, which has
\sim
8 times the volume of TNG50, would yield a larger statistical sample of galaxies and cosmic web structures. However, its particle mass resolution is
\sim 16
times lower, and gravitational softening length, hence spatial resolution,
\approx
2-3 times lower than that of TNG50. Thus, we could only resolve the morphologies of much more massive galaxies (
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\gtrsim 9.5
) in TNG100 at the same confidence that we resolve
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\geq 8
galaxies in TNG50.

II.2
Measurements of galaxy morphological properties

We first measure the intrinsic shapes of stars and DM in TNG50 galaxies using particle data. For these measurements, we closely followed the methods of
Pillepich et al. 2019
.
First, we obtain the comoving center of mass of each galaxy from the TNG data access site. We then measure the 3D radius containing half of the stellar mass of each galaxy, hereafter the stellar half-mass radius,
r_{\star}
. Our measurements do not take into account observational considerations, such as 2D projection effects, surface brightness dimming, and instrumental sensitivity, and therefore these 3D radii are not meant to be “mock” measurements that replicate observational choices for a truly apples-to-apples comparison with real data.
We discuss some observational comparisons later in the paper.
We similarly measure the DM half-mass radius,
r_{\mathrm{DM}}
, containing half of the DM particles of a galaxy (or subhalo). This choice is made such that we compare DM properties at the same relative scale as the stellar component – 2
r_{\mathrm{DM}}
vs. 2
r_{\star}
. While 2
r_{\mathrm{DM}}
is smaller than the halo virial radius
R_{\mathrm{vir}}
, it is an appreciable fraction of
R_{\mathrm{vir}}
, given typical halo concentrations
(
Navarro et al. 1996
;
Klypin et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
, and therefore a large enough distance to consider as the outer part of a halo where baryonic effects are minimal.

Next, we calculate the 3D shapes of galaxies by determining the mass tensor of the stars and DM and diagonalizing them. Below, we describe the shape measurements for stars, but the same method is followed for DM as well.
We define
A
,
B
, and
C
, as the length of the semi-major, -middle, and -minor axes, respectively.
We initialize 3D elliptical shells to have axis ratios
B/A=C/A=1
, at a distance of 2
r_{\star}
away from the galaxy center and with thickness 0.4
r_{\star}

(
Pillepich et al. 2019
, same as that used for shape measurements in)
. If the initial shell contains less than 3 particles, we simply do not calculate the shape of this galaxy and label it with a flag.
We then iteratively diagonalize the mass tensor and constantly update the elliptical shells until the direction and magnitude of the eigenvectors of the mass tensor converge. The length of the major axis is fixed to 2
r_{\star}
, but the direction is allowed to vary. We measure the 3D stellar and DM shapes at 2
r_{\star}
, and the DM shape at 2
r_{\mathrm{DM}}
.
Lastly, we also measure the shapes of DM subhalos in TNG50-Dark at 2
r_{\mathrm{DM}}
and 0.1
r_{\mathrm{DM}}
. The latter is chosen to enable comparison against 2
r_{\star}
; as there are no stars in TNG50-Dark, 0.1
r_{\mathrm{DM}}
is approximately equivalent to the galaxy center scale that is defined by 2
r_{\star}
in TNG50.

We characterize the shapes of stellar and DM components of galaxies using several different metrics commonly used in the literature.
We follow the classification method originally devised in
van der Wel et al. 2014
, where the intrinsic middle-to-major axis ratio
B/A
and minor-to-major axis ratio
C/A
are used to categorize the shape into spheroidal, oblate (disky), and prolate (elongated). Spheroidal shapes have similar lengths
A
,
B
, and
C
, whereas oblates have much larger
B/A
than
C/A
(i.e., strongly flattened along one dimension), Prolates have a much larger
A
than
B
or
C
, with the smaller axes being closer to each other than oblates (i.e., flattened along two dimensions).
We refer the reader to details of the classifications and visual depictions presented in
Zhang et al. 2019
and
Pandya et al. 2024
.
We also verify that our estimates of shapes and sizes are virtually identical to those obtained by
Pillepich et al. 2019
.

We further leverage publicly available catalogs for additional characterizations of TNG50 galaxy morphological properties, based on measurements of
Genel et al. 2015
and
Genel et al. 2018
.
We obtain kinematics-based morphological quantities, including the specific angular momentum (sAM) of stars in galaxies, which is a measure of stellar spin. We also make use of the mass fraction of stars in disk-like and bulge-like orbits.
By aligning a galaxy such that its spin vector points upward along the
z
-axis, the circularity parameter
\epsilon
is defined for each particle as the ratio of the sAM along the
z
-axis and maximum sAM possible at the specific binding energy of the particle
(
Vogelsberger et al. 2014
, see)
.
Stars in disk-like orbits, i.e., with a high degree of rotation about the center, have
\epsilon
close to 1, while those in more random orbits comprising of the bulge have very small positive to highly negative
\epsilon
.
Following common definitions in in the literature
(
Abadi et al. 2003
, e.g.,)
, we define the mass fraction of stars (1) in disk-like orbits as the fraction with
\epsilon>0.7
minus those with
\epsilon<-0.7
, and (2) in bulge-like orbits as the fraction with
\epsilon<0
.
For (1), we remove the
\epsilon<-0.7
fraction to remove any contribution of bulge stars to the disk – assuming the bulge is symmetric around
\epsilon=0
.
Not doing so does not change any of our results appreciably. We use all the measurements above for all stars, but limiting them to a 10
r_{\star}
radius
(
Genel et al. 2015
, as also available from the catalog curated by)
yields identical results.

II.3
Cosmic Web Reconstruction

Our methods to reconstruct the cosmic web and identify filaments from the locations of galaxies are based on the method outlined in
69
. Here, we briefly describe the method and outline the specific choices made for this work.

II.3.1
DisPerSE
and MCPM

To identify filamentary structure, we leverage the widely-used topologically-motivated Discrete Persistent Structure Extraction (
DisPerSE
) algorithm
(
Sousbie 2011
;
Sousbie et al. 2011
)
.
DisPerSE
takes point sets, typically galaxies or halos, as input tracers. In the standard workflow, it then estimates a cosmic matter density field by applying the Delaunay Tessellation Field Estimator
(
Schaap & van de Weygaert 2000
;
van de Weygaert & Schaap 2009
, DTFE;)
technique and segmenting space into tetrahedrons, whose vertices are the locations of the tracers. Following this, the gradients of the density field are computed, and critical points are identified where the gradient vanishes. Filaments are composed of individual line segments (filament segments) that connect maxima (nodes) to saddle points, while each filament starts and ends at a maximum. Structures are characterized by their “persistence,” which quantifies their robustness with respect to shot noise in the input data. In practice, a persistence threshold is defined, which is akin to a signal-to-noise threshold of identified structures.

Using the TNG100 simulation,
69
showed that the use of DTFE in the density estimation step of
DisPerSE
leads to an incomplete characterization of the cosmic web. They found, in agreement with past studies
(
Busch & White 2020
, e.g.,)
, that DTFE, by only estimating density at the locations of tracers, does not accurately estimate the “true” matter density, as traced by DM, especially in low-density regions. Instead, they substituted the DTFE density field with the MCPM density field, based on the algorithm first introduced in
Burchett et al. 2020
. MCPM mimics the growth patterns of the unicellular
Physarum polycephalum
or
slime mold
organism, by employing virtual “agents” that navigate through a domain to connect “food sources,” which in this context are galaxies/halos weighted by mass
(
Elek et al. 2021
;
Elek et al. 2022
)
. It generates an equilibrium density field as a probabilistic solution of the agents’ trajectories across many different realizations of this process, which serves as a robust proxy of the cosmic matter density field.
The MCPM model was thoroughly calibrated by
69
such that its outputs most closely map to cosmic matter density
(see also
Elek et al. 2022
, for more details)
. Furthermore, MCPM density in real data qualitatively agrees with several other large-scale environmental metrics such as galaxy overdensity
(
Wilde et al. 2023
;
Oyarzún et al. 2024
)
.

MCPM accurately traces the true cosmic matter density across a large range of densities
(
Burchett et al. 2020
;
Elek et al. 2022
)
, and its use in the
DisPerSE
pipeline, as opposed to DTFE, results in a more complete characterization of cosmic web structure
(
69
)
. From the same inputs (galaxies with
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\geq 8
), MCPM drastically outperforms DTFE in tracing DM density, and is able to identify filaments of a wide range of persistence values. This results in the MCPM-based reconstruction identifying
low-density
filamentary structures that the DTFE-based method misses, similar to “thin” filaments or “tendrils,” which were also reported in previous studies
(
Alpaslan et al. 2014
;
Crone Odekon et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
. These structures have dramatically different effects than conventional high-density filaments on galactic phenomena such as star formation activity and gas supply
(
69
, see)
.

We characterize the 1D filament line density,
\Sigma_{\mathrm{fil}}
, as the sum of the MCPM overdensity per unit length along a filament segment, as defined in
69
.
This quantity is directly measurable in observational data, as it uses the MCPM density field. Recently,
\Sigma_{\mathrm{fil}}
was also found to tightly correlate with gas density in TNG100 filaments
(
Woo et al. 2025, submitted
)
.
In the following sections, we often quote the filament line density of the nearest filament segment to a galaxy, but note that galaxies are often not at the center of a filament.

II.3.2
The Cosmic Web in TNG50

We apply the techniques described above to reconstruct and characterize the cosmic web in each of our six chosen snapshots in TNG50. The inputs to our pipeline are the locations and masses of all galaxies with stellar mass
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\geq 8
. For a given redshift, we keep most of the MCPM density field estimation parameters the same as those used by
69
(see their Table 1). We make a small change in the
sense distance
parameter, which is related to the volume of the domain and the data resolution, as these change between TNG100 and TNG50. We also smoothed the density field on
256^{3}
grids, which correspond to
\approx
0.2 Mpc in length for the TNG50 box (for TNG100, the same grid resolution is about
\approx
0.4 Mpc)
We verify that all of these choices maximize the correlation of the MCPM density field with the DM density field.
69
provides further detail on how the quality of the MCPM reconstruction only varies mildly with different input parameters.

We verify that the persistence cut of 25 that we use in
DisPerSE

(
69
, see)
maximizes the match between locations of the identified cosmic web nodes and peaks of the DM density field (centers of the most massive halos), as we expect a strong correspondence between the two
(see, e.g.,
Galárraga-Espinosa et al. 2024
, for more discussion regarding matching the cosmic web skeleton to the underlying density field)
.
However, the results of this paper are not sensitive to small changes in our cosmic web extraction parameter. Finally, we apply a single degree of smoothing to the final filamentary skeleton in order to smooth out any sharp or unphysical edges of the filament spine.

Figure
1

III.2.2
Residual dependence of shapes on filament density

It is clear that the stellar mass plays the dominant role in the prolate fractions, but a subtle dependence of filament density at fixed mass is also noticed.
Here, we aim to remove the mass dependence and measure any remaining residual dependence of the density of the nearest filament.
Therefore, we extract the external effect of cosmic web filaments on these morphological properties
beyond
the internal effects parameterized by mass. While there are many possible approaches to this, we consider the residuals of a given parameter with respect to the stellar mass
(
Crone Odekon et al. 2018
, somewhat similar to the approach of)
.

Briefly, our method is as follows, where we use stellar prolate fraction as an example.
First, for a given redshift, we estimate the median stellar prolate fraction as a function of stellar mass, which we denote the expected stellar prolate fraction. Then, for the population at large, we compute the deviation from this expected stellar prolate fraction in a few bins of nearest filament line density
1
1

1

Our filament density bins are chosen such that they have roughly equal numbers of galaxies.
, which we denote the “residual” stellar prolate fraction at fixed mass.
This allows us to take into account and separate the effect arising from the mass alone and determine the residual effect of the filament line density on the prolate fraction.
Finally, we apply the Savitzky-Golay filter
(
Savitzky & Golay 1964
)
to smooth the binned residual prolate fraction-filament density relation and its
\pm 1\sigma
bootstrapped uncertainties (with 1000 resamples). The smoothing helps us remove random stochastic variations in the data and obtain a simple qualitative description of the residual dependence of the filament density on the prolate fraction.

We follow this procedure for the prolate, spheroidal, and oblate fraction of stars at 2
r_{\star}
, and the prolate and spheroidal fraction of DM at 2
r_{\star}
and 2
r_{\mathrm{DM}}
, for all six redshifts. Once again, we note that DM halos rarely form oblate shapes, for which only the stellar oblate fraction is considered.
Figure
7
shows the residual prolate (top row) and spheroidal and oblate (bottom row) fractions as functions of the nearest filament density at different redshifts. The solid lines and shaded regions represent the smoothed medians and
\pm 1\sigma
bootstrapped uncertainties therein.

As we qualitatively inferred from Figure
6
, there is a residual dependence of filament density on the prolate fraction for stars and DM halos.
Generally, the residual prolate fraction decreases with increasing density of the nearest filament. The strongest residual dependence is seen at
z=4
and the weakest dependence is seen at
z\leq 0.5
, For both halos and stars, the relationship is almost flat at
z\leq 1
.
Quantitatively, the residual stellar prolate fraction decreases by up to
\sim 10\%
, while the residual halo prolate fraction decreases by up to
\sim 20\%
, from the lowest to the highest density filaments.

For both the residual spheroidal and oblate stellar fractions, the residual dependence of filament density is unclear, if it exists at all. However, there is a small rise in the residual spheroidal fraction with rising filament density at
z\geq 1
(of order
\sim
few percent), while the relationship somewhat reverses at later times.
The residual oblate fraction does not show significant variation with filament density above stochastic variations.
For halos, the residual spheroidal fraction, as opposed to the residual prolate fraction, increases with filament density at
z\leq 1
. But this dependence also effectively disappears at lower redshifts. The total change in the residual fraction from low to high-density filaments is also up to
\sim 20\%
.

We can conclude that the residual filament density-dependence of prolate stellar shapes is much stronger than that of spheroidal or oblate structures. The mass-dependence mostly overshadows any secondary filament dependence of disk and even spheroid shapes. Low-density filaments are more likely to host prolate galaxies than high-density filaments, especially at higher redshifts, while spheroidal galaxies are slightly more likely to live in high-density filaments at these early times.

The picture for halos is clearer; low-density (high-density) filaments are more likely to host prolate (spheroidal) halos, but only down to
z\sim 1
. At later times, no statistically significant correlation is seen for either prolate or spheroidal fractions, suggesting that internal mechanisms represented by stellar mass almost entirely governs the shapes of galaxies and their halos.

Finally, we briefly comment on the total morphological fractions in TNG50 at a given redshift.
Across all redshifts, fewer spheroidal and more oblate stellar structures are formed with increasing redshift. The majority of low-mass (
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}8-9
) galaxies at almost all redshifts are spheroidal, but spheroidal shapes are increasingly rare in
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\gtrsim 10
galaxies (
\lesssim
40%) at
z\leq 2
. The majority of
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\gtrsim 10
galaxies at
z\leq 2
are instead oblate. Conversely, hardly any
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}=8-9
oblate galaxies exist at
z\geq 2
, and only a handful such galaxies exist at lower redshifts. Prolate shapes are always subdominant at
z\leq 4
at any masses above
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}=8
. These results, identical to those first presented by
Pillepich et al. 2019
, confirm the predominant morphological transformation of galaxies in TNG50 from mostly spheroids to increasing disk-dominance with increasing time and mass assembly,

We note that while qualitatively reproducing many observed morphological properties of galaxies, TNG50 still falls short in many aspects.
The oblate fraction approximately matches the most recent observational constraints with JWST
(
Kartaltepe et al. 2023
;
Pandya et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, but too few prolates and somewhat too many spheroids with
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\sim 9-10
are formed in the simulation. The tension in the former is very strong, with almost 2 times lower-than-observed prolate fractions at
z=3-4
and up to
\sim 4
times lower fractions at
z=1-2
. The simulated intermediate-mass spheroidal fraction is
\gtrsim
twice as large as observed at
z>1

These tensions may be due to insufficient numerical resolution, treatment of baryonic physics (including stellar/AGN feedback), or other reasons
(
Pillepich et al. 2019
;
Tacchella et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
.
While it is not our intent to strictly compare simulated morphologies to observations, we can glean important insights into fundamental galaxy formation mechanisms by understanding the source of any disagreements.

Beyond the intrinsic shape, galaxy morphologies can also be characterized by various kinematic properties of stellar populations. Here, we investigate the specific angular momentum (sAM) of stars, and the fraction of stars in disk-like and bulge-like orbits in TNG50, as defined by
Genel et al. 2015

Similar as the previous section, we quantify the residual dependence of these kinematic morphological parameters on the nearest filament density, after removing the stellar mass dependence.
Here, we compute, for each galaxy, the deviation of a given parameter (e.g., stellar sAM) from the median value given its stellar mass.
Afterwards, we compute the median of this deviation as a function of the nearest filament density as before.

In Figure

The residual effect of filament density is far stronger on the stellar sAM than the other quantities at most redshifts, for which the vertical scale on the left panel is
\sim
3 times larger in dynamic range than the other panels.

The density of nearby filaments has a significant effect on the sAM of stars, but this effect decreases progressively with time. The residual stellar sAM clearly increases with higher filament densities, such that galaxies with higher stellar sAM tend to live in high-density filaments, down to
z=0.5
. At the present day, this relationship almost plateaus, but a small correlation still exists.
Similarly,
Woo et al. 2025, submitted
recently found that higher-density filaments host relatively higher-sAM galaxies in TNG100 at
z=0
.
Between
z=3
and
z=0.5
, the residual stellar sAM is almost redshift-invariant at intermediate filament densities.
The large-scale tidal field generates high-vorticity flows in higher density filaments
(
Codis et al. 2012
;
Laigle et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
, which likely results in the supply of high-specific angular momentum gas into galaxies lying along these filaments. These vorticities are also larger at higher redshifts, likely contributing to the stronger filamentary-dependence at these times. At later times, the increased baryonic dominance of galaxies, as well as mergers, modifies the acquired angular momentum from vorticity-rich filaments, thus reducing the filamentary effect.

The neighboring filament density has smaller effects on the residual fraction of bulge and disk stars. At
z\geq 1
, the residual disk fraction increases with filament density by up to
\sim

\lesssim

z
.
In contrast, the residual bulge fraction drops with increasing filament density by
\lesssim
7% at
z\geq 0.5
, and the trend stays roughly flat at later times. For both, there is a strong, almost redshift-invariant overlap in the residual at intermediate filament densities, much like we find for the residual stellar sAM.
At first glance, the decrease in residual bulge fraction and slight increase in residual spheroidal fractions may seem to be contradictory results. But this is not the case, as the definition of “bulge-like” orbits only excludes stars in the disk, but not stars in more irregular or boxy orbits typical of prolate structures. Rather, it is more appropriate to think of the bulge fraction as the fraction of
velocity dispersion-dominated
stars.
Thus, at fixed mass, more dispersion-dominated stellar systems reside in low-density filaments and more rotation-dominated systems reside in high-density filaments, until about
z\sim 1
.
The lack of a filament density-dependence at later times once again likely indicates the prevalence of internal mechanisms over cosmic web accretion signatures.

A strong dependence of the stellar size on the stellar sAM is found, as is a noticeable dependence on the density of the nearest filament, particularly at high redshifts (
z\gtrsim 1
).
The stellar spin is on average much higher for galaxies larger than the median size for a given mass (i.e., extended galaxies) than for galaxies below the median relation (i.e., compact galaxies). These galaxies retain the original angular momentum acquired from their parent halos
(
Genel et al. 2015

, which helps them form extended stellar disks with ordered rotation. Galaxies that lose most of their halo angular momentum instead form more compact spheroidal structures. This is true at all times. We also find a
z\geq 1
trend of more extended galaxies that reside in higher density filaments. By similar reasoning as above, we can attribute this to high-vorticity gas accretion from high-density filaments at early times, producing more extended oblate galaxies.
Woo et al. 2025, submitted

z=0
in TNG100.
We further discuss our results and their physical implications in §
V

IV
Predictions of the Cosmic Web for Large Galaxy Surveys

The predictions presented in this paper on the fundamental influences of the cosmic web on galaxy formation can be tested with new and upcoming observatories.
Roman, NASA’s next great observatory scheduled for launch in a couple of years, promises to be a revolutionary effort in uncovering the galaxy formation-cosmic web connection in the Universe.
Its combination of high-resolution imaging and spectroscopy will allow us to both trace the early cosmic web with large galaxy samples and constrain how the morphological properties of galaxies are shaped by the cosmic web.
The Wide-field instrument (WFI), with its
\sim
0.28
\mathrm{deg^{2}}
field-of-view,
\sim
0.11″ pixel
-1
resolution, and high spectroscopic sensitivity
(
Schlieder et al. 2024
)
, is poised to transform our understanding of large-scale structure itself and its role in setting morphologies and other observable properties of galaxies.

This is especially true at
z>0.5
, where current constraints are very limited due to several reasons. Most existing wide-field galaxy surveys are conducted from the ground, for which there are high UV and IR backgrounds. This makes it very difficult to probe very faint magnitudes that are necessary to sample higher
z
galaxies to reasonable completeness levels. The ubiquitous and well-known optical emission lines used to identify and accurately characterize the redshifts of galaxies such as
H
\alpha
,
H
\beta
, and
[O
iii
]
, are shifted to NIR bands at redshifts significantly greater than 0.5. For example, due to its r-band magnitude limit
m_{r}<17.77
mag, SDSS rapidly loses completeness at
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\gtrsim 10
at
z\gtrsim 0.1
. Thus, only the rarer massive galaxies are accessible for cosmic web reconstruction at even intermediate redshifts.
Deeper surveys over smaller areas have shown some potential for
z>0.5
reconstruction, for instance the VIPERS survey
(
Guzzo et al. 2014
)
at
z\sim 0.7
by
Malavasi et al. 2017
. But even deep ground-based surveys have often been limited to fairly bright galaxies (in addition to smaller volumes).

With the launch and early data release of Euclid
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2025b
)
, the landscape for high-
z
cosmic web reconstructions has changed dramatically. Roman represents the next leap in this regard, with its slightly higher spatial and spectral resolution, high-sensitivity, and the accessibility of data from its core community surveys. The time is ripe to predict how well Roman can reconstruct the high-
z
cosmic web from its galaxy surveys. Below, we undertake such a task, by generating “mock” galaxy samples from the deeper observations planned with the High-Latitude Wide-Area Survey
(
Wang et al. 2022
, HLWAS;)
. The goal is to understand both what some of the planned observational depths will allow us to learn, and the effect of depth in accurately reconstructing the high-
z
cosmic web (focusing on
z=1
). These will lead us to predict how accurately we can constrain the galaxy morphology-cosmic web connection from real data.

We find that the deeper mock HLWAS samples are able to qualitatively capture much of the true filamentary dependence on galactic morphological properties, but they cannot quite unveil these trends quantitatively. Both the HLWAS 2.5
\times
Spec-Deep and 5
\times

At higher masses, there is somewhat greater between the full and mock samples at higher masses. Neither of the mock samples exhibit a rise in the high-mass spheroidal fraction in high-density filaments or the modest drop in the high-mass prolate fraction at these densities. The 5
\times

\times

The experiment above implies that
\geq 2.5
times deeper spectroscopic flux limits on HLWAS can, in principle, allow us to qualitatively extract the true cosmic web-dependence of morphological fraction. Therefore, deeper grism spectroscopy than is currently planned for HLWAS is essential to reliable test the predictions herein. Deep grism spectroscopy is a requirement for a fairly complete sample of tracer galaxies, which helps reconstruct the cosmic web structure to high accuracy and completeness, and this in turn helps paint a (more) complete picture of the impact of cosmic filaments on galaxy morphologies. High-quality photometry, combined with advanced techniques that exploit projected shapes and sizes
(
Pandya et al. 2024

z\geq 1

However, we stress that even a 5
\times

IV.3
Possible strategies for real observations

Figure 14:

Similar to Fig.
11
; the cosmic web from HLWAS Spec. Deep in TNG100 (top) and from HLWAS 2.5

Spec-Deep in TNG50 (bottom) are shown, with the Roman WFI FOV overlaid in lime-green.
Targeted deep spectroscopy over selected overdense regions can allow better cosmic web reconstruction.

In light of our results, we assert that a 2.5
\times
lower line flux limit than that planned for the HLWAS Ultra-deep field is sufficient to trace the cosmic web with satisfactory accuracy and reveal its true effect on galactic morphological evolution. In practice, this would require a highly expensive observational effort on Roman. We estimate that it would require an appreciable fraction of the total general observing time of Roman’s nominal 5-year mission (separate from the core community surveys) to achieve a 5
\times
or even 2.5
\times
flux limit over the entire HLWAS Ultra-deep field.
Due to the practical limitations of undertaking this major task,
we consider alternative strategies to attain our goals.

From the top row of Figure
11
, we note that while HLWAS Spec-Deep will not yield an accurate characterization of the cosmic web filaments, it will still identify most of the dominant galaxy overdensities (small gray circles embedded in the dark-blue regions). Accordingly, a viable strategy to optimize the identification of filamentary structure is to target the galaxy overdensities identified by spectroscopic redshifts from HLWAS Spec-Deep. These regions can then be targeted with much deeper spectroscopic observations to help reconstruct filaments in those regions with much higher fidelity. This would produce a much more more efficient (and thus less expensive) mapping of cosmic filaments than performing very deep spectroscopy over
entire
volume of the HLWAS Deep or Ultra-deep field. We visually illustrate the size of the Roman WFI field-of-view (FOV) within the HLWAS Deep (top) and Ultra-deep (bottom) fields at
{z=1}
in Figure
14
. The Roman FOV, which consists of 18 detectors covering a sky area of 0.281
\mathrm{deg^{2}}
(excluding gaps), is shown in green, following specifications of the detector array arrangement outlined in
Wang et al. 2022
and
Schlieder et al. 2024
. The same 2D slices are shown as Figure
11
.

Each individual pointing of Roman will easily cover virtually any identified galaxy overdensity. Several such pointings can be used to map filaments with much greater detail in most of the area covered by the HLWAS Deep field. For the much smaller Ultra-deep field, we would require
\sim
4 times fewer pointings to attain the same goals (note that the FOV covers a much smaller area in the bottom panel than the top panel). This targeted strategy would ignore the voids, which contain very few galaxies but much of the volume of the cosmic web. But on the flip side, one could tremendously reduce the observing time required to map cosmic web filaments accurately to just a fraction of the time it would require to observe an entire field. It is possible to optimize this observing strategy further by combining high-quality photometric redshifts, if available
(
Yang et al. 2021
;
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2025c
, e.g.,)
. We reserve further discussion of photometric redshifts in §
V.2
.

V
Discussion

In this paper, we present one of the first statistical studies of the physical effect of the cosmic web on the morphological evolution of galaxies in a fully cosmological hydrodynamic simulation. We also forecast the detectability of the
z=1
cosmic web from the observational limits of the planned HLWAS on Roman.
Below, we discuss (1) our predictions of the TNG simulations in a physical context and (2) the prospects of observing the high-redshift cosmic web and testing our predictions.

V.1
Physical Interpretations

V.1.1
Alignments between Filaments and Galaxies/Halos

In TNG50, the major axes of galaxies become less aligned with the direction of the nearest cosmic web filaments from redshifts
z=4
to 0, but the results are very different between the inner DM halo/galactic scales and the outer halo scales. Stars lose their preferential shape alignment with the nearest filament by
z=3
, inner DM halos lose this alignment later, around
z=2
, and outer halo shapes retain a slight preferential alignment down to
z=0.5
.
This indicates that anisotropic matter accretion from the cosmic web determines halo shapes the most at large halo-centric scales and at early times.
At later times and with further build-up of stellar mass, various internal mechanisms such as star formation-driven feedback (including SNe and stellar winds) dominate
(
Madau & Dickinson 2014
, e.g.,)
, which likely helps disrupt this alignment.

Indeed, in the
N
-body counterpart of TNG50, we see a smaller drop in the DM-filament alignment at the inner and outer halo scales across all redshifts. However, even in this simulation, there is a significant decline in this alignment at low redshifts, implying that halo mergers from gravitational forces alone inevitably disrupt the early signature of shape alignment with filaments. Increase in mergers at late times is known to increase misalignment with filaments
(
Welker et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
.

Prolate halos shapes are more aligned with filaments than spheroidal halos at almost all redshifts. This implies that collimated mass accretion from filaments forms elongated halos along the direction of the filaments.
Furthermore, we find in prolate galaxies, the stars are strongly aligned with the outer DM halos down to
z=0
, indicating that torques from the halos align the stars in the same direction and could support the elongation of the stellar structure.
This picture, in line with previous predictions from both
N
-body
(
Bailin & Steinmetz 2005
, e.g.,)
and hydrodynamical simulations
(
Ceverino et al. 2015
;
Tomassetti et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
, hints at how the formation of prolate structures is linked to filamentary accretion even in a fully cosmological context.
Finally, our results also support the idea proposed by
Pandya et al. 2019
that intrinsic alignments of early low-mass prolate galaxies can be used to map the higher-redshift cosmic web
(
Pandya et al. 2025
, see also)
.

While there is not much dependence of the shape alignment of inner halos/galaxies on filament density or mass, these play a strong role on the alignment at outer halo scales. At almost all times, outer DM halos are most strongly aligned with the highest density filaments, and the alignment is strongest in the most massive halos.
Our physical explanation for this is that halos that reside in thin or low-density filaments grow by isotropic accretion of matter and do not grow aligned with the filament, whereas halos residing in thick or high-density filaments form earlier and only allow mass accretion along the axis of the filament
(
Borzyszkowski et al. 2017
;
Wang & Kang 2018
;
López et al. 2019
)
. In this picture, massive halos, which typically form early at a given redshift
(
Tojeiro et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
, are “stalled” and aligned with respect to high-density filaments.
Another factor contributing to a lack of shape alignment of spheroidal halos is that such halos have less well-defined major axes unlike prolate halos.

V.1.2
Galaxy Morphologies and Filaments

As expected, the morphology of galaxies depend primarily on their stellar mass. But we do also recover an important secondary role played by their cosmic web environments. Due to the action of gravity alone (i.e., in TNG50-Dark), at any given time, more massive DM halos are more elongated. The effect is particularly prominent in inner halos, which retain their prolate structures at later times despite outer halos becoming mostly spherical. This long-established result can be attributed to dissipationless DM falling anisotropically to the centers of halos and making them more triaxial or elongated in shape, while outer halos accrete more isotropically and are much more spherical
(
Bailin & Steinmetz 2005
;
Allgood et al. 2006
)
.

In stark contrast to the DM-only case, the inclusion of baryons results in both stars and halos becoming less prolate with increasing mass, and many fewer prolate structures forming at any given redshift.
Many low-mass halos (and their central galaxies) at
z>1
are prolate, which have DM-dominated cores due to early star formation episodes expelling most of the baryons from their cores
(
Ceverino et al. 2015
;
Tomassetti et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. With increasing mass accretion from the cosmic web, the baryonic component starts to dominate and accreting gas loses angular momentum and causes centrally concentrated star formation – a process dubbed “wet compaction”
(
Dekel & Burkert 2014
;
Zolotov et al. 2015
;
Tacchella et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. Thus, higher mass (
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\gtrsim 9.5
) galaxies lose their prolate shapes and instead form rotationally-supported disks or more spheroidal shapes. This is the general evolutionary sequence of galactic morphologies from low- to high-masses and from high- to low-redshifts that is seen in the zoom-in
VELA
simulations as well
(
Lapiner et al. 2023
)
.

It is not clear if the TNG simulations generate the same wet compaction events that produce star-forming disks at higher redshift and then quenched spheroids at later times.

De Almeida et al. 2024
tracked the formation of compact dwarf galaxies (
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}=8.4-9.2
at
z=0
) in TNG50, finding that they are not formed by gas-rich (wet) mergers, but rather by inflows of lower-angular momentum gas.
Tacchella et al. 2019
found that the link between star formation and morphology in
{{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}=9-11.5}
galaxies in TNG100 does not necessarily require wet compaction events as in
VELA
, and that gas-poor (dry) mergers can also play a significant role in the morphological evolution.
We propose that detailed follow-up studies tracing the evolution of individual galaxies of masses
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\gtrsim 9.5-10
are required to explicitly test the wet compaction picture (gas-rich inflows causing central pile-up of gas, leading to nuclear starbursts and eventual contraction and quenching) in TNG and other large-volume cosmological simulations.
In addition, specifically tracing the star formation and black hole accretion histories of galaxies (which we do not do here) will be essential to differentiate the role of internal mechanisms from external ones in the morphological evolution in such simulations.

Unlike
VELA
, we do not find clear evidence for a transition stellar mass in TNG50 above which galaxies are no longer prolate
(
Ceverino et al. 2015
;
Tomassetti et al. 2016
, a characteristic value of
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\gtrsim 9-9.5
was reported by)
. Instead, there is a small, but non-negligible population of high-mass (
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\gtrsim 10
) galaxies at
z<2
with prolate stellar shapes that do not fit in the evolutionary picture outlined above.
These massive prolates are plausibly the result of major dry mergers, which has shown to form
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\gtrsim 11
prolates in Illustris at
z=0

(
Li et al. 2018
)
. The discrepancy in the masses between Illustris and TNG50 could very well be an effect of the updated physical model in TNG
(
Weinberger et al. 2017
)
or the change in numerical resolution.
A simpler alternative explanation is that the massive prolates in TNG50 have irregular shapes that do not fit our ellipsoid-based classification of shapes.
In any case, this population deserves further scrutiny to help us better understand their characteristics and formation mechanisms.

Notably, TNG50 predicts a small but significant dependence of prolate shapes on the nearest filament density, after removing the stellar mass dependence. At fixed mass, galaxies and their DM halos in lower density filaments are more prolate than those in higher density filaments. This effect is stronger for halo shapes than for stellar shapes, but it persists in both until
z\sim 0.5
. The effect is also dominated by low-mass (
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}=8-9
) galaxies.
This suggests that low-density filaments can be conducive to forming lower mass prolate galaxies.
Low-density filaments are thin, thus comparable in width to halos (especially at early times),
so gas and DM can accrete smoothly along these structures in a dipole fashion.

High-density filaments, on the other hand, are much wider than halos and form complex quadrupolar vortices
(
Laigle et al. 2015
)
, which can disrupt smooth, coherent accretion on to halos and galaxies and help form fewer prolate structures. Instead, they can impart additional angular momentum to galaxies and help form disky or spheroidal structures.
Indeed, we find that spheroidal halos are more likely to reside in higher-density filaments, opposite to prolate halos.
Inner DM halo shapes are more strongly correlated with filament density at fixed mass than stellar shapes, which is likely due to the complex gas physics associated with stars.

After removing the mass-dependence, the sAM of stars in galaxies strongly increases with increasing filament density at all redshifts. This suggests that stars retain the high vorticity of gas accreted from dense filaments, which bestows them with higher spin.
Thus, the filament density plays a role in the build-up of stellar spin, which helps stars develop rotational support and form disks.
We also uncover a (smaller) residual effect of the filament density on the mass fraction of bulge and disk stars.
At higher redshifts, accretion from low-density filaments is associated with relatively more stars in bulge-like orbits and fewer in the disk-like orbits. Consistent with the filamentary dependence on stellar spin, this can be interpreted as high-density (low-density) filaments enabling more rotationally-supported (dispersion-dominated) stellar structures.
All of these correlations decrease or disappear with time, which again may be due to torques from internal mechanisms such as disk instability and stellar feedback redistributing the acquired spin.

We also showed that higher redshift (
z\geq 1
) galaxies that are more extended relative to the average mass-size relation tend to live in higher density filaments than their more compact counterparts. These galaxies also have higher stellar sAM, consistent with the picture of high-density filaments transferring higher angular momentum gas to galaxies. Gas accretion from high-density filaments can therefore aid in the formation of more extended galaxies with significant rotational support.
Our results imply a connection in TNG50 between the sizes and spins of galaxies and the spin of matter accreting on to them from the cosmic web, which is consistent with the expectation of abundance matching models that propose that galaxy sizes are determined by the angular momentum of their parent halos, with very little dependence on stellar mass, morphology, or even redshift
(
Kravtsov 2013
;
Somerville et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
.

Our results are similar to those of the recent study of
Woo et al. 2025, submitted
in TNG100, finding higher residual stellar spin and size in high-density filaments using the same filament identification method as us, albeit at
z=0
. They also showed how the relative alignment of galactic disk gas with respect to accreting gas from filaments plays a crucial role in setting various internal properties such as star formation/color gradient, but we neglect this aspect of galaxy-filament alignments throughout the current paper.

V.1.3
A combined narrative and further considerations

, is shown by the sketch in the middle.

Synthesizing our results together, we present a simple cartoon schematic in Figure
15
to describe the effect of the cosmic web on the early (
z\gtrsim 1
) morphological evolution of galaxies in TNG50. We sketch out the rough qualitative dependence of various morphological properties on the density of the nearest filament in the middle, and illustrate the the types of morphological characteristics associated with lower- and higher-density filaments on the left and right, respectively. The arrows inside the low- and high-density filaments indicate the relative dynamics of accretion from different densities as alluded to in the previous section.
We highlight a differential residual effect of filaments; low-density filaments are associated with the formation of prolate galaxies and halos, low-spin galaxies, and stars in velocity dispersion-dominated orbits, while high-density filaments are associated with the formation of spheroidal halos, oblate/high-spin galaxies, and stars in highly rotation-supported orbits.
Many of these trends disappear or reverse slightly in the last
\sim
8 Gyr, but some remain at lower redshifts, notably the rise in stellar spin in high-density filaments.

In terms of temporal evolution, at early times, coherent anisotropic mass accretion along predominantly thin/low-density filaments forms low-mass prolate structures and low-spin galaxies with dispersion-dominated stellar orbits.
We infer that similar to the
VELA
simulations, many early low-mass prolates in TNG50 transform into oblates by baryonic dominance in the core causing the accreting gas from filaments to lose angular momentum and form stable disks. But this describes a relatively small fraction of galaxies in TNG50. The majority of early low-mass galaxies are low-spin spheroids that likely transform into disks by external mechanisms such as gas-rich mergers. Wet mergers can exert powerful mechanical forces on the stars in spherical orbits and transport them into rotational orbits, as well as produce gas disks as post-merger remnants within which further star formation can occur.
High-density filaments can help retain the oblate structure by channeling high-angular momentum gas on to these galaxies.
While wet mergers can help form highly star-forming disks, dry mergers can form massive spheroids or even massive prolates, as we deduce here.

It is possible that low-mass spheroids are preceded by very short-lived prolates at even higher redshifts than those studied here (
z>4
).
First, highly prolate and radially anisotropic configurations are dynamically unstable, which can result in elongated systems buckling into rounder shapes in short timescales
(
Merritt & Hernquist 1991
;
Merritt & Sellwood 1994
, e.g.,)
.
Second, if stellar feedback in early prolate galaxies is not strong enough, then their halos could not maintain a DM-dominated core for long. In the absence of DM-induced torques, accreting gas would lose its alignment with the inner halo and the halo core would experience an earlier baryon-dominated phase. The stars would then move in more random orbits, producing a more spheroidal system.

While we do not study the shape of gas explicitly, this plays an important role in the overall narrative.

Pillepich et al. 2019
found that the majority of
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}=8-9.5
galaxies in TNG50 form prolate star-forming gaseous structures at
z<5
. Yet, the fraction of prolate stellar structures is always subdominant.
In higher mass galaxies, the star-forming gas is dominated by oblate shapes at most times. Star-forming gas therefore typically forms disky or elongated shapes. This reveals an intrinsic difference in how stars and gas evolve. Namely, unlike gas, stars cannot dissipate energy and can therefore stay in high-velocity dispersion orbits for a long time, while gas can quickly settle into disky or boxy orbits with higher rotational support.
However, we note that the gas in a prolate galaxy, while being disky, can accrete at different angles and precess, affecting the orbits of the stars
(
Meng & Gnedin 2021
, see)
.

It is vital to understand whether the picture suggested by our results is applicable to the cosmic evolution of real galaxies, or simply a consequence of the physical model and/or numerical effects of the TNG simulations.
Virtually all cosmological hydrodynamical simulations predict that feedback is essential in forming a diversity of galactic morphologies
(see reviews by
Primack 2024
;
Feldmann & Bieri 2025
, and references therein)
.
Genel et al. 2015
showed that the feedback plays a key role in establishing distinctive galactic structures in the Illustris simulation, and the same is true for its successor, TNG
(
Pillepich et al. 2019
;
Tacchella et al. 2019
)
, and other simulations such as EAGLE
(
Lagos et al. 2017
)
.
Unfortunately, the highly uncertain physics of feedback mechanisms such as SN/stellar winds and radiation pressure limits such simulations to only qualitatively reproducing broadly observed characteristics of galaxies, such as their morphological variety, but often falling short of quantitatively reproducing many observations. As in
Pillepich et al. 2019
, we found that TNG50 produces too few low-mass prolate stellar systems at early times – by factors of two or more relative to HST/JWST observations, depending on the redshift and mass
(
van der Wel et al. 2014
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Pandya et al. 2024
)
.

One possibility to explain why TNG50 produces so few prolates and so many spheroids at the high-redshift low-mass regime is that the fiducial model requires some modification(s).

Tomassetti et al. 2016
showed that in
VELA
, turning off stellar feedback produces much rounder galaxies compared to the full feedback run, and likewise increasing feedback results in more early prolates and late oblates, and fewer spheroids. It stands to reason, therefore, that the stellar feedback in the TNG model produces more spheroids and less prolates than expected due to stellar feedback not being strong enough.
The TNG model was modified from its precursor, Illustris, in order to better match fundamental observations such as the galaxy-color bimodality
(
Nelson et al. 2018
)
.
Even if increasing or otherwise modifying the stellar feedback helps alleviate some of the aforementioned tensions with observations of galactic morphologies, this may very well cause tensions with other observed galaxy properties.

The delicate balance between various uncertain parameters of the feedback models and subgrid physics of hydrodynamical simulations is analogous to tuning different knobs by the “right amount”. Simplified star formation prescriptions in the subgrid model, calibration to lower-resolution simulations, and even the specific numerical scheme employed have sizable effects on the physical mechanisms that shape the baryonic structure of galaxies
(see, e.g., the discussion in
Luber et al. 2025
, and references therein)
.

Regardless of the physical model, the numerical resolution of TNG50 itself may be insufficient for accurately modeling stellar shapes.
In this simulation, the gravitational force softening length of stars and DM is of order
\sim
300 pc and of gas is of order
\sim
70 pc, resulting in typical Voronoi sizes of
\approx
100 pc in the star-forming regions of galaxies
(
Pillepich et al. 2019
, see)
. While this is much higher resolution than TNG100 and most other cosmological simulation, it still results in uncertain star particle orbits in most higher-redshift lower-mass galaxies with effective sizes less than 1 kpc. As a result, the 3D shapes of many galaxies can be fairly uncertain.

It would therefore be useful to perform higher-resolution zoom-in re-simulations of such galaxies (and their halos) to understand how sensitive their morphologies are to numerical resolution alone, keeping the physical model fixed
(see, e.g.,
Lu et al. 2024
, for an example of such zoom-ins)
. This would enable us to assess how the statistics we find in this paper, such as overall morphological fractions and their variation with filamentary environment, change with resolution parameters such as gravitational force softening.
Additionally, repeating our analyses in other hydrodynamical models, particularly in high-resolution cosmological boxes such as FIREbox
(
Feldmann et al. 2023
)
and NewHorizon
(
Dubois et al. 2021
)
, would help gain further insight into the physical mechanisms that govern morphological evolution.

V.2
Testing predictions: caveats and observational limitations

In §
IV
, we presented the prospects for accurately mapping the early cosmic web with galaxy surveys with deep photometry and spectroscopy as would be enabled by Roman. The deepest emission line flux limits currently planned on the HLWAS would only be able to detect a very biased sample of massive and highly star-forming galaxies at
z=1
, which cannot accurately trace the filamentary structure. By boosting the spectroscopic survey sensitivity, more representative tracer galaxy samples can be obtained.
However, even the planned line flux limit of HLWAS-Deep is sufficient to identify most of the galaxy overdensities in the field. These specific regions can be spectroscopically followed up to reach
\sim
2.5 lower emission line flux limits, which represents a far more efficient and practically viable approach to map cosmic filaments than performing such observations over the entire HLWAS Deep or Ultra-deep fields.
Generally, 2.5
\times
deeper spectroscopy will enable a highly accurate and complete reconstruction of the
z=1
cosmic web, but increasing this depth two-folds does not improve the reconstruction very much..
Assuming that galaxy shapes and spins can be accurately measured, such deep surveys would be able to qualitatively recover the dependence of galaxy morphological properties on filament densities.

We present a fairly idealized analysis of the ability of high-redshift galaxy surveys to reconstruct the cosmic web. We made several assumptions in this process (see Appendix
B
) and these will have different degrees of impact on the final reconstructed structure and its correlation with galaxy morphologies. Moreover, observational data is much more complex and uncertain to interpret than simulation snapshots. These factors all but ensure that our predictions should be interpreted as the most optimistic scenarios for high-
z
cosmic web reconstruction from wide-field surveys on Roman and other state-of-the-art observatories. We discuss some the uncertainties in our results stemming from the assumptions we made (see Appendix
B
).

One of the largest sources of uncertainty is the conversion from observational emission line flux limits to simulated SFRs. It is very challenging to generate detailed mock photometry of galaxies which self-consistently models the dust attenuation within each galaxy
(
Nelson et al. 2018
;
Donnari et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. This being beyond the scope of our paper, we simply assume the dust attenuation in our simulated galaxies follows the population-averaged properties of observed
z\!\sim\!1
galaxies
(
Nedkova et al. 2024
)
according to their stellar mass. If we instead allow the individual values to vary within the
\pm 1\sigma
confidence interval of the observations, the number of tracers in our mock samples do change. This change is within
\approx
10% for the HLWAS 2.5
\times
Spec-Deep and 5
\times
Spec-Deep samples, but the mass and SFR completeness limits change by less than 10%. Consequently, cosmic web reconstructions from these samples would not change significantly from our fiducial results. In contrast, the number and completeness of tracers in the HLWAS Spec-Deep sample is much more sensitive to the assumed dust attenuation. Despite this, our conclusion that the HLWAS Spec-Deep sample cannot accurately reconstruct the cosmic web remains unchanged.

Nevertheless, observations suggest that there is significant galaxy-to-galaxy variation in dust attenuation with not only stellar mass
(
Battisti et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
, but also SFR
(
Kriek & Conroy 2013
, e.g.,)
, metallicity
(
Shivaei et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
, and other properties.
By simply considering the mass-dependence, we sidestep the well-established complexities and uncertainties in dust extinction and scattering in galaxies
(
Salim & Narayanan 2020
, and references)
in favor of a statistically averaged description of dust at
z\sim 1
. Accurate modeling of the dust attenuation on an individual galaxy basis is well beyond the scope of this paper, and thus we reserve such an exercise for future work.

There are also some uncertainties in our assumed mock Roman photometric magnitudes. We assume a single value for the average
J\!-\!H
color observed at
z\!\sim\!1

(
Skelton et al. 2014
)
, instead of accounting for variation with mass or other properties. This carries a small uncertainty on the mock
H
-band magnitudes, and would not significantly change the mock samples. There is an even smaller uncertainty in considering the Roman and HST
H
-bands equivalent. For TNG50, we also performed a more uncertain conversion from mock SDSS
z
-band magnitudes and
i\!-\!z
colors to Roman
H
-band. But this does not significantly alter the tracer samples we get either. A complete treatment of mock photometric magnitudes requires careful transformations between different filters
(
Durbin et al. 2023
;
Durbin et al. 2025
, see, e.g.,)
, which is beyond the scope of this paper.

Thus far, we have only considered spec-
z
for our cosmic web reconstruction, but not photometric redshifts (photo-
z
).
Imaging is less expensive than spectroscopy, thus significant effort has been expended in the last decade or so to explore photo-
z
as a viable alternative to photo-
z
for mapping the distribution of galaxies from large surveys
(
Laigle et al. 2016
;
Mendes de Oliveira et al. 2019
;
Yang et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
.
The key underlying issue is that photo-
z
typically have much lower precision, thus making 3D localization of galaxies far more uncertain, relative to spec-
z
.

Laigle et al. 2016
showed that a large number of photometric bands increases photo-
z
precision to a large degree, by combining photometry from 30 bands from the NUV to the MIR in the well-studied COSMOS
(
Scoville et al. 2007
)
field. Another promising avenue to improve photo-
z
estimates is the use of medium-band photometry, instead of the wide filters on Roman/WFI and most other space-based instruments
(
Suess et al. 2024
, see, e.g.,)
.

Yang et al. 2021
developed a technique which simultaneously combines spec-
z
and photo-
z
to map the distribution of halos from large galaxy surveys. Applying their method to the DESI legacy imaging surveys, they found that they typically correctly identify the redshifts of more massive halos (
\gtrsim 10^{12.7}~{\mathrm{M}_{\odot}}
) and that a very high redshift accuracy can be achieved when a halo has
\geq
10 galaxies. Thus, in the absence of many spec-
z
, a sufficient number of photo-
z
measurements can potentially yield sufficiently accurate redshifts, at least in overdense regions. We posit that photo-
z
measurements can supplement the spec-
z
identified in the HLWAS-Deep field, helping better identify the galaxy overdensities which can be followed up with deeper spectroscopy. For instance, such photo-
z
measurements could be obtained by the DESI Bright Galaxy Survey, which targets massive galaxies
(
Hahn et al. 2023
)
, plausibly anchoring the nodes of the cosmic web
(
Cohn 2022
)
.
Recently,
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2025c
demonstrated that the persistence (robustness) of filaments identified by
DisPerSE
using photo-
z
from Euclid is well-correlated with persistence of filaments from spec-
z
.

A large number of photo-
z
may be sufficient to identify the highest-density filaments in proximity to high galaxy overdensities, but not the orders of magnitude lower-density filamentary structures that our simulation analyses reveal. The large uncertainty from photo-
z
– typically 100-1000 times larger than spec-
z

(
Salvato et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
– would result in uncertainties of 100s of Mpc in physical location.
This would make it impossible to detect statistically significant filamentary structure on all but the largest scales and as a result, the true diversity of filamentary structures would be missed.
Consequently, the dependence of galaxy morphologies on filaments would also be poorly constrained. Therefore, the higher redshift accuracy afforded by spec-
z
is needed to reconstruct more detailed filamentary structure over much smaller areas such as the HLWAS Deep and Ultra-deep fields.
However, even grism-based redshift errors can sometimes be fairly large, on the error of
\sim 10
Mpc
(
Momcheva et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. This is enough to degrade the quality of the cosmic web reconstruction in 3D space, as shown by
Pandya et al. 2019
.

We have performed the reconstructions on 3D cartesian space and assumed that all galaxies above observational limits will be observed with spec-
z
with very low uncertainty. In practice, this is very difficult to do for various reasons. To get secure spec-
z
for a galaxy, we require at least two emission lines with high-significance. While
H
\alpha
would be observable at
z=1
, other common lines such as
H
\beta
or
[O
iii
]
would not be in the WFI G150 grism pass-band (
\sim
1–1.9 µm) and we would have to rely on less common lines such as
[S
ii
]
. Furthermore, the resolution of G150 grism may not be able to resolve some of the narrower features. which may be a concern, especially for lower-mass galaxies.

Redshift space distortions affect the distribution of observed structures in the Universe
(
Kaiser 1984
, e.g.,)
, but the analyses in this paper have been limited to real space. While we expect redshift space distortions to be less of an issue at higher redshifts, this needs to be taken into account and corrected for when applying our reconstruction methods on observed data. A simple correction was applied to MCPM tracers on low-
z
SDSS data by
Wilde et al. 2023
, but in future work we plan to quantify the effect of these distortions in much more detail in our MCPM+
DisPerSE
methodology.

We assume negligible errors on the stellar masses, which are used as weights of the tracers in our MCPM algorithm and are integral to the structure identification. Precise estimates of
M_{\ast}
require SED-fitting on many bands of photometry, and the fewer the bands, the larger the errors on average. The HLWAS-Deep survey is slated to observe seven photometric bands of observations from the optical to the NIR
(
Zasowski et al. 2025
)
, but further ancillary data may be needed to obtain more confident stellar masses. Some of this may be obtained from existing deep surveys with areal overlap, such as COSMOS
(
Scoville et al. 2007
)
.
In follow-up work, we wish to explore and quantify the impact of both redshift and stellar mass uncertainties on the structure identified from our MCPM+
DisPerSE
framework.

A major assumption in our statistical analyses of galaxy morphologies and the cosmic web using mock samples is that intrinsic shapes will be perfectly recovered in real data. In practice, we can only observe galaxies in 2D projection on the sky. The best available tools for inferring the intrinsic 3D shapes from these measurements provide a
likelihood
that a galaxy has a given shape
(
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Pandya et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, and therefore only allow statistical estimates of the fractions of 3D shapes. Thus, we cannot estimate the true 3D shape of any galaxy with 100% confidence. The degree of uncertainty increases for fainter, higher-redshift, and more edge-on galaxies.

VI
Conclusion

We have analyzed the IllustrisTNG cosmological simulations to achieve two primary goals. (1) Understand the role played by the cosmic web in the morphological evolution of galaxies; and (2) predict the early cosmic web structure that can be identified from upcoming galaxy surveys with the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope.
For (1), we reconstructed the cosmic web structure from
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\geq 8
galaxies at redshifts
z=4
, 3, 2, 1, 0.5, and 0, in the TNG50 simulation. We applied a well-calibrated method that combines density field estimation from the MCPM model
(
Burchett et al. 2020
;
Elek et al. 2022
)
with the
DisPerSE
structure identification algorithm to generate highly complete and accurate cosmic web filament catalogs. We first studied the alignment between stars and DM in galaxies and their nearest cosmic filaments across different redshifts. We then investigated the dependence of intrinsic 3D shapes and kinematic morphological properties of galaxies on their nearest filaments.
For (2), we reconstructed the cosmic web at
z=1
in both the TNG50 and TNG100 simulations, using both mass-complete and mock samples based on observational limits. We quantified how well different mock samples can uncover the true dependence of galactic morphological properties on the cosmic web.
Our main findings are as follows.

•

Galaxies generally lose preferential major axis alignment with their nearest filaments with decreasing redshift, with stars losing this shape-alignment earlier (
z\leq 3
) than inner DM halos (
z\leq 2
) and outer DM halos (
z\leq 0.5
).
The strongest shape alignment is seen for halos that are prolate, high-mass (
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\geq 10
), and near high-density filaments. Prolate galaxies retain strong shape alignment with their outer halos down to
z=0
, indicating the potential use of prolate galaxy alignments to map the early cosmic web (§
III.1
).

•

For
z\gtrsim 1
, at fixed stellar mass, there is an increase in the fraction of prolate galaxies and halos (especially at
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\sim 8-9
) near low-density filaments, while the fraction of spheroidal halos increases in high-density filaments (§
III.2
). More These trends weaken substantially or mildly reverse at
z\lesssim 1
.

•

At fixed stellar mass, increasing filament density is strongly associated with increasing stellar sAM of galaxies, and to a lesser extent, associated with a larger (smaller) fraction of stars in rotation-supported/disk-like (velocity dispersion-supported/bulge-like) orbits (§
III.3
). At early times, galaxies that are more extended relative to the average mass-size relation at that redshift have higher stellar sAM and reside in high-density filaments than those that are more compact (§
III.4
). All of these trends weaken at later times.

•

Baryonic physics, including stellar feedback, plays a sizable role in the evolution of galaxy morphologies, as (1) the DM-only TNG50-Dark simulation generates more prolate halos with increasing mass at any redshift, in direct contrast to the hydrodynamic TNG50 simulation, and (2) TNG50-Dark shows a smaller drop in shape alignment from outer to inner halos than TNG50.

•

The deepest grism spectroscopic survey planned on the HLWAS on Roman will
not
enable an accurate and complete reconstruction of the cosmic web at
z=1
(§
IV.2
). This is because the proposed spectroscopic line flux limit of HLWAS-Deep leads to highly-incomplete galaxy samples consisting of the rarest galaxies with high mass and SFR (§
IV.1
), thus making them poor tracers of the large-scale matter distribution. Increasing the spectroscopic depth by a factor of
\geq
2.5 times HLWAS-Deep (
\geq
2.5 times fainter line flux limit) yields much more complete galaxy samples that enable much more improved cosmic web reconstruction at
z=1
. Such mock galaxy samples will be able to qualitatively uncover the true cosmic web-dependence of galaxy morphological fractions, but fall short of quantitatively.

•

HLWAS-Deep will still be able to identify most of the galaxy overdensities, which can be followed up with deeper spectroscopy in those specific regions (§
IV.3
). Thus, it is possible to efficiently map the early cosmic web with Roman.

We have presented a picture wherein the morphological evolution of galaxies, including their 3D shapes, kinematics, sizes, and alignment with nearby filaments are determined by a complex interplay between accretion of matter from the large-scale cosmic web and secular processes occurring near galactic centers.
The formation of prolate and low-spin galaxies is linked to mass accretion from predominantly low-density filaments, whereas the emergence of disks and highly-ordered systems is strongly associated with internal galactic processes such as star formation-driven feedback and external mechanisms such as wet mergers and accretion from high-density filaments.
The relative effect of the cosmic web over internal mechanisms declines with time, but even at later times, the effect of the cosmic web on galaxy morphology does not disappear completely.

Many of our predictions are likely dependent on technical details of the TNG model, including implementation of baryonic physics and numerical resolution of TNG50.
Similar analyses in separate high-resolution cosmological simulations or zoom-in re-simulations of halos in TNG50 can help constrain the effect of such uncertainties on the evolution of galactic structure.
Understanding the source of tensions with observations (including, notably, the high-redshift prolate fraction) is also crucial for understanding the causal effect of phenomena such as outflows and stellar winds.

We also determined that properly mapping the early cosmic web and testing our predictions in the real Universe will be challenging, but possible. With its combination of high-sensitivity grism spectroscopy and HST-like imaging resolution over a
\sim
100 times wider field of view, Roman can help reconstruct the
z\geq 1
cosmic web in unprecedented detail and unveil its role in the emergence of galactic morphologies. While pushing to lower line flux limits results in much higher exposure times, we showed that a practically effective strategy is to focus on patches of overdensities that can be followed up to identify most of the filamentary structures. Such targeted deep spectroscopy may be achieved not only with Roman, but also much more sensitive spectrographs on JWST.
In addition, supplementing spectroscopic redshifts with high-quality photometric redshifts – especially over well-studied patches of the sky – will go a long way in mapping the 3D positions of distant galaxies (and therefore the large-scale structure) accurately.

This work is our first attempt at predicting the early cosmic web with large surveys, and we defer detailed investigations to follow-up studies. We deliberately limit our predictions to the deep but small fields of the HLWAS, as it is infeasible to survey low-mass galaxies over larger areas. In the future, we will apply our reconstruction method to much larger volumes comparable to the medium and wide tiers of the HLWAS, which will provide spectroscopic redshifts of millions of galaxies
(
Zhai et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Madar et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. We aim to employ larger volume simulation boxes such as the
\sim
Gpc-sized MilleniumTNG
(
Pakmor et al. 2023
)
and mock lightcones based on semi-analytic models
(
Yung et al. 2023
)
. This will help us develop a more complete understanding of the large-scale structure of the Universe and its role in regulating galaxy evolution.

Appendix A
Satellite prolate galaxies

Here we briefly examine the fraction of satellite galaxies that are prolate, as a function of mass, redshift, and environment. The top row of Figure
16
illustrates the satellite fraction as a function of
M_{\ast}
and local overdensity at different redshifts. In the bottom row, the
y
-axis shows the distance to the nearest massive halo scaled by the virial radius of the halo. “Massive halos” are defined in this context as the 1% most massive halos at that redshift, ranging from
\sim\!3\!\times\!10^{12}~{\mathrm{M}_{\odot}}
at
z=4
to
\sim\!2\!\times\!10^{14}~{\mathrm{M}_{\odot}}
at
z\!=\!0
.
These environmental quantities are different from the cosmic web-based quantities in the rest of the paper, as we expect a stronger effect of local environment on satellites.

Figure 16:

Prolate fraction for only satellite galaxies, as a function of mass and environment, at six different redshifts. The
x
-axis is for stellar mass; the
y
-axis in the top row shows the local (within
\sim
0.2 Mpc) MCPM overdensity and in the bottom row shows the distance to the most massive halos at that redshift scaled by halo virial radius (see text).

The results show that prolate satellites form via two different types of local environmental effects, depending on mass and redshift. In lower mass (
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\leq 9
) satellites at a fixed mass, the prolate fraction is higher at lower matter overdensity and similarly at higher distances from the most massive halos – at least for
z\geq 1
. This suggests that early low-mass prolate satellites prefer low-density environments. In contrast, high-mass (
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\gtrsim 9.5
) satellites at
z\leq 1
are more prolate in high-density environments at fixed mass. The highest satellite prolate fractions at later times are found within the virial radius of the most massive halos. This could possibly be an effect of tidal forces from massive halos distorting and elongating the shapes of satellites
(
Martin et al. 2008
, e.g.,)
. However, such galaxies could also be misclassified by our shape classification scheme as prolates and instead simply be irregular-shaped.

Appendix B
Mock galaxy sample selection based on photometry and spectroscopy

We first estimate mock magnitudes of TNG galaxies in the Roman
H
-band, i.e., F158W filter.
We utilize the dust-corrected mock magnitudes calculated according to the procedure presented in
Nelson et al. 2018
.
Stellar magnitudes are modeled by taking into account dust attenuation from both (1) extinction due to dense star-forming clouds surrounding young stars
(
Charlot & Fall 2000
)
, and (2) extinction and scattering from the spatial distribution of neutral gas and metals in the galaxy
(
Calzetti et al. 1994
)
. Each stellar population spectrum is convolved with this dust attenuation and by an observed passband, and the magnitudes are computed within 30 pkpc radius from the galaxy center.
For TNG100, we use mock 2MASS
J
-band
(
Skrutskie et al. 2006
)
magnitudes as also utilized by
Donnari et al. 2019
. This passband is almost equivalent to the F125W filter of HST/WFC3 as well as the F129W filter of Roman/WFI, albeit somewhat narrower than these space-based filters, and prone to atmospheric telluric absorption features which cause some divergence
(
Durbin et al. 2023
, see)
. Recently, detailed filter transformations between a variety of NIR filters, including those on HST and Roman, have also been released
(
Durbin et al. 2025
)
, which we do not adopt. However, for our purpose of obtaining approximate magnitude values, these small uncertainties are acceptable, and are unlikely to change our results on a population basis.

We then convert the
J
-band magnitudes to
H
-band using observed
J\!-\!H
colors from HST. We use observed F125W-F160W colors of galaxies in the five HST frontier fields observed by the CANDELS
(
Grogin et al. 2011
;
Koekemoer et al. 2011
)
and 3D-HST
(
Brammer et al. 2012
)
surveys, as presented in
Skelton et al. 2014
. Based on their reported (roughly field-invariant) median F125W-F160W colors for extended sources, we adopt an average
J\!-\!H
color of 0.3 for each galaxy at
z\!=\!1
. While this is not as precise as implementing a magnitude-dependent
J\!-\!H
color, the variations are small enough that this would not strongly change the mock magnitudes and therefore the selection of our galaxy samples.

For TNG50,
J
-band magnitudes are not publicly available, so instead we use mock SDSS
(
York et al. 2000
)

z
-band magnitudes and
i\!-\!z
colors. We derive an empirical transformation from SDSS
z
-magnitudes and
i\!-\!z
colors to Roman F158W magnitudes. We do this using synthetic photometry based on Flexible Stellar Population Synthesis
(
Conroy et al. 2009
, FSPS;)
models. In these models, the stellar populations span a range of ages (0.1–5 Gyr) and redshifts (
0.5\!\leq\!z\!\leq\!3
), assuming a
Chabrier 2003
initial mass function (IMF), solar metallicity, and the
Reddy et al. 2015
dust attenuation curve (see more below). Each model spectrum was redshifted and convolved with the SDSS
i
,
z
and Roman F158W filter transmission curves using the
speclite
package
(
Kirkby et al. 2024
)
. A linear relation was then fit between the mock SDSS
i\!-\!z
color and the offset between mock Roman F158W and SDSS
z
-band magnitudes as a function of redshift, enabling us to estimate F158W magnitudes for TNG50 galaxies.

We also convert spectroscopic line flux limits to SFR limits, making a few assumptions, and adopt these limits to make additional sample selections for our galaxies. Note that below we adopt SFRs averaged over 10 Myr timescales, using measurements presented in
Donnari et al. 2019
, as this is appropriate for the short timescales of star formation traced by nebular emission lines such as
H
\alpha
. Regardless, these values are not severely different from the SFR averaged over shorter/longer timescales or even the instantaneous SFRs across most of the sample.
In order to convert observed emission line flux,
F_{\mathrm{obs}}
, to SFR, we adopt the following common scaling relation

SFR=C~4\pi D_{L}^{2}~F_{\mathrm{obs}}~10^{0.4A_{\lambda}},

(B1)

where
D_{L}
is the luminosity distance,

A_{\lambda}
is the wavelength-dependent dust attenuation, and
C
is a constant factor to scale the observed luminosity to SFR.
According to
Kennicutt & Evans 2012
, the constant value for the
H
\alpha
emission line luminosity in the equation above is
C\!=\!1.86\!\times\!10^{41}
. Following
Madau & Dickinson 2014
, we also divide this constant by 2.369, to convert from the
Kroupa 2001
to the
Chabrier 2003
IMF, calculated using FSPS models.

A_{\lambda}
, in magnitudes, is defined as

A_{\lambda}=\frac{k_{\lambda}A_{V}}{R_{V}},

(B2)

where
A_{V}
is the V-band dust attenuation in magnitudes and the factor
k_{\lambda}/R_{V}
is derived from the specific dust attenuation law assumed.
We assume the
Reddy et al. 2015
dust law, which is based on measurements of
z\!\sim\!1.4\!-\!2.6
galaxies from the Keck/MOSDEF survey
(
Kriek et al. 2015
)
and is more representative of higher-redshift galaxy SEDs than more common local dust models by
Calzetti et al. 2000
,
Gordon et al. 2003
, etc. The
Reddy et al. 2015
dust law, while close in shape and normalization to these more widely-used dust laws, yields
\approx
20% lower SFRs.

Once the dust model is chosen,
A_{V}
is the only variable in Eq.
B2
.
We adopt a stellar mass-dependent
A_{V}
from the UVCANDELS survey
(
Nedkova et al. 2024
)
. We calculate the median
A_{V}
for a given
M_{\ast}
at the redshift bin
0.5\!\leq\!z\!\leq\!1.5
, which is appropriate for our
z\!=\!1
analysis. We use a smoothed median function to assign
A_{V}
to each galaxy in TNG, given its
M_{\ast}
. The median
A_{V}
is less than 0.5 at
{\log(M_{\ast}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})}\!<\!10
, and rises sharply at higher masses, in agreement with previous observations at
z\!\sim\!1

(
Reddy et al. 2015
;
Battisti et al. 2020
;
Salim & Narayanan 2020
, e.g.,)
.
