Numerical approximation of effective diffusivities in homogenization of nondivergence-form equations with large drift by a Lagrangian method
Timo Sprekeler, Han Wu, Zhiwen Zhang

Abstract
. In this paper, we study numerical methods for the homogenization of linear second-order elliptic equations in nondivergence-form with periodic diffusion coefficients and large drift terms. Upon noting that the effective diffusion matrix can be characterized through the long-time variance of an associated diffusion process, we construct a Lagrangian numerical scheme based on a direct simulation of the underlying stochastic differential equation and utilizing the framework of modified equations, thereby avoiding the need to solve the Fokker–Planck–Kolmogorov equation. Through modified equation analysis, we derive higher-order weak convergence rates for our method. Finally, we conduct numerical experiments to demonstrate the accuracy of the proposed method. The results show that the method efficiently computes effective diffusivities, even in high dimensions.

1.
Introduction

We study the numerical approximation of the effective diffusion matrix arising in the periodic homogenization of linear nondivergence-form second-order elliptic equations with large drift terms. Specifically, we consider the problem

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}:=-A\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right):\nabla^{2}u^{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,b\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a sufficiently regular bounded domain,
\varepsilon>0
is a small scaling parameter,
A\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d})
is a uniformly elliptic diffusion matrix satisfying
\lambda I_{d}\leq A\leq\Lambda I_{d}
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
for some
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
,
b\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a drift coefficient, and
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
g:\partial\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are sufficiently regular. Throughout this work,
\mathbb{T}^{d}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
denotes the
d
-dimensional flat torus, and
I_{d}
the
d\times d
identity matrix.

The differential operator
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}
in (
1.1
) is the infinitesimal generator for the stochastic differential equation

(1.2)

\mathrm{d}X^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,b\left(\frac{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}}{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\left(\frac{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}}{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}^{\varepsilon}=x,

where
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}
is a
d
-dimensional stochastic processes,
W_{t}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Wiener process (Brownian motion),
\sigma:=\sqrt{2}A^{\frac{1}{2}}
is the diffusion coefficient matrix, and the initial condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is deterministic.

By a scaling argument, we consider the rescaled process

(1.3)

X_{t}:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}X^{\varepsilon}_{\varepsilon^{2}t},

which preserves the probability law of the original diffusion. The rescaled stochastic process
X_{t}
is the solution to the problem

(1.4)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}=\frac{x}{\varepsilon}.

The infinitesimal generator of this process is given by

(1.5)

\mathcal{L}v:=-A:\nabla^{2}v-b\cdot\nabla v.

For the rescaled SDE (
1.4
), there exists a unique invariant probability measure
\pi
on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure with a density
r(y)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}y}
. This density satisfies the following properties. For every
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, there exist constants
K,\rho>0
such that

(1.6)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|\,\mathbb{E}[\phi(X_{t})\mid X_{0}=x]-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\phi(y)r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\right|\leq Ke^{-\rho t}.

Moreover, the density
r
is the unique solution to the stationary Fokker–Planck–Kolmogorov equation

\mathcal{L}^{*}r:=-\nabla^{2}:(Ar)+\nabla\cdot(br)=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},

subject to the normalization condition
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=1
.

The existence, uniqueness, and regularity of
r
follow from classical results in ergodic theory and PDEs (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
35
]
). In particular, the connection between homogenization theory for (
1.1
) and SDEs has been rigorously studied in
[
6
, Chapter 3]
using probabilistic methods.

We introduce the corrector
\chi=(\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{d}):\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the unique solution to the cell problem

(1.7)

\mathcal{L}\chi=b\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\chi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0,

where
\mathcal{L}
is the infinitesimal generator of the rescaled SDE defined in (
1.5
). The existence and uniqueness of
\chi
are guaranteed by the Fredholm alternative, provided the following centering condition holds:

(1.8)

\displaystyle\bar{b}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}b(y)r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0,

where
r
is the invariant density from the stationary Fokker–Planck–Kolmogorov equation. Assuming (
1.8
), it is a classical result in homogenization theory that the solution
u^{\varepsilon}
of (
1.1
) converges weakly in
H^{1}(\Omega)
to the solution
u
of the homogenized problem

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{L}}\overline{u}:=-\overline{A}:\nabla^{2}\overline{u}

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\overline{u}

\displaystyle=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where the effective diffusion matrix
\overline{A}
is a constant, symmetric and positive definite matrix given by

(1.9)

\displaystyle\overline{A}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi(y)\right)A(y)\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi(y)\right)^{\mathrm{T}}r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y;

see, e.g.,
[
6
,
13
,
25
,
26
]
. This representation of
\overline{A}
in terms of the solution to the cell problem (
1.7
) is called the Eulerian framework for the effective diffusivity.

We refer to
[
11
,
18
,
21
,
28
,
40
,
42
]
for optimal rates in periodic homogenization, and to
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
19
,
20
]
for stochastic homogenization of nondivergence-form equations. Regarding the numerical homogenization of nondivergence-form equations, recent work has led to significant developments with
[
11
,
15
,
40
]
addressing linear problems and
[
16
,
27
,
38
]
focusing on Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations. For divergence-form equations with large drift, we refer to
[
7
,
9
,
23
,
31
,
32
,
45
]
.

In this paper, our objective is to develop and rigorously analyze a probabilistic numerical method to approximate the density of the invariant measure
r
and the effective diffusion matrix
\overline{A}
. While finite element methods for approximating the effective diffusion matrix
\overline{A}
have been rigorously analyzed in
[
40
,
41
]
, their computational cost becomes
expensive in high-dimensional settings.

The homogenization theory developed in
[
6
,
35
]
establishes that the stochastic process
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}
defined in (
1.2
)
converges in distribution to a Brownian motion with covariance matrix
\sqrt{2}\,\overline{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, that is,
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}\overset{d}{\to}\sqrt{2}\,\overline{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}W_{t}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
W_{t}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Wiener process. See also
[
14
,
17
,
22
,
30
,
35
]
for homogenization of convection-diffusion equations via probabilistic methods. This probabilistic perspective motivates our particle-based approach, which exploits the Lagrangian representation of the effective diffusivity, i.e.,

\overline{A}^{L}:=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X_{T})}{2T},

where the covariance tensor is defined as
\mathrm{Var}(X_{T}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X_{T}-\mathbb{E}[X_{T}]\right)\otimes\left(X_{T}-\mathbb{E}[X_{T}]\right)\right]
and
X_{t}
is the
diffusion process from (
1.3
). This formulation naturally circumvents the curse of dimensionality through Monte Carlo sampling.

The Monte Carlo approach for computing the effective diffusivity has been successfully employed in various settings. In particular,
[
36
]
developed a Monte Carlo method to approximate the effective diffusivity of inertial particles. For divergence-free velocity fields,
[
37
]
implemented a stochastic splitting method that demonstrated superior performance compared to standard Euler–Maruyama discretizations. This advantage was further corroborated by
[
43
]
, in which a structure-preserving symplectic scheme was introduced. Both studies found that the Euler–Maruyama scheme fails to accurately capture effective diffusivities, especially in the vanishing molecular diffusivity regime, i.e., as the diffusion coefficient tends to zero.

We will establish a rigorous connection between the Lagrangian and Eulerian formulations for computing the effective diffusivity. By the Itô formula, it is proved that a universal diffusion process with sufficiently regular drift and diffusion terms still preserves the cell problem

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\chi^{\prime}=b-\bar{b}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\chi^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0,

and the Eulerian form limit

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X_{T})}{2T}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(y)\right)A(y)\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(y)\right)^{\mathrm{T}}r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

where centering
\bar{b}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}b(y)r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0
may not hold so that the standard homogenization theorem does not apply. Our analysis also clarifies the limitations of the Euler–Maruyama method observed in
[
37
,
43
,
44
]
.

To overcome these limitations, we use a Milstein scheme with a modified drift
b_{h}
and a modified diffusion
\sigma_{h}
, i.e.,

\tilde{X}_{t_{n+1}}^{h}=\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}+b_{h}\left(\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}\right)h+\sigma_{h}\left(\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}\right)\sqrt{h}\,\xi_{n}+M_{n}\left(\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}\right),\quad\tilde{X}_{0}^{h}=0,\quad n=1,2,\dotsc,N.

The effective diffusivity is approximated via the empirical variance

\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bm{x}_{t_{N}})}{2T}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{(\bm{x}^{i}_{t_{N}}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{t_{N}})\otimes(\bm{x}^{i}_{t_{N}}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{t_{N}})}{2MT},

where
\bm{x}_{t_{N}}=\left(\bm{x}_{t_{N},[1]},\bm{x}_{t_{N},[2]},\dotsc,\bm{x}_{t_{N},[d]}\right)^{T}
represents
M
independent realizations of the numerical solution at time
T=Nh
and
\bar{\bm{x}}_{t_{N}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{x}^{i}_{t_{N}}
is the sample mean.

Using backward error analysis, we obtain the error estimate of the invariant measure and the solution of the cell problem from the modified flow. Then, an approximation error for the effective diffusivity of order
\mathcal{O}\left(h^{2}+\frac{1}{T}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\right)
can be deduced from these estimates. Finally, we present numerical experiments to demonstrate the accuracy of the proposed method. The results show that our method efficiently computes effective diffusivities, even in three-dimensional settings.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the theoretical foundations of the Lagrangian framework for homogenization, proving the equivalence between pathwise and PDE-based formulations of effective diffusivity. Building on this framework, Sections 3 and 4 develop our probabilistic numerical method featuring a modified high-order discretization scheme. Further, Section 4 provides a complete convergence analysis, deriving rigorous error estimates for both the approximation of the invariant measure and the computation of the effective diffusivity in high dimensions. Numerical validation in Section 5 demonstrates the accuracy of the method through benchmark tests and applications to convection-enhanced diffusion in chaotic flows.

2.
Theoretical Foundations of the Lagrangian Method

We begin by analyzing the relationship between the Lagrangian and Eulerian frameworks for computing the effective diffusivity. The homogenization theory developed in
[
6
,
26
,
35
]
provides a rigorous analysis of diffusion processes with large drift, while
[
6
]
establishes the probabilistic connection between the elliptic problem (
1.1
) and stochastic differential equations.

The long-time behavior of diffusion processes in periodic potentials has been studied in
[
10
,
17
]
, though these results require the centering condition (
1.8
) to hold. When implementing numerical integrators, the modified SDE obtained through backward error analysis introduces both a perturbed invariant measure and modified drift terms. This modification typically violates the centering condition, thereby preventing the standard homogenization result from being achieved. This phenomenon provides a theoretical explanation for the failure of the Euler–Maruyama scheme observed in
[
37
,
43
]
. A detailed analysis of these effects will be presented in Section 4.

When the centering condition fails to hold, our analysis nevertheless establishes that
X_{t}
converges in distribution to a Brownian motion with covariance matrix
\overline{A}^{L}
.
This convergence result reveals the fundamental connection between the pathwise diffusion coefficient
\overline{A}^{L}
and its Eulerian counterpart, even in non-centered systems. These theoretical findings directly motivate our computational framework, where we approximate
\overline{A}^{L}
through long-time statistics of large particle ensembles, as developed in Section 3.

The following theorem establishes the rigorous connection between the Lagrangian and Eulerian formulations, thereby providing the theoretical foundation for our proposed Lagrangian method. For
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we write
\lvert M\rvert:=\sqrt{M:M}
to denote the Frobenius norm of
M
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider the diffusion process
X_{t}
governed by the SDE

(2.1)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x_{0},

where the drift
b:\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and diffusion coefficient
\sigma:\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are assumed to be smooth and such that the diffusion matrix
A:=\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}
is uniformly elliptic. Let
r:\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
denote the density function of the invariant measure satisfying (
1.6
). Let
\chi^{\prime}:\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the unique solution to the cell problem

(2.2)

\mathcal{L}\chi^{\prime}=b-\bar{b}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\chi^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0,

where
\bar{b}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}b(y)r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
(which may be nonzero). Then, there holds

\displaystyle\bar{b}^{L}

\displaystyle:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{t}|X_{0}=x_{0}]-x_{0}}{t}=\bar{b},

\displaystyle\overline{A}^{L}

\displaystyle:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X_{t})}{2t}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(y)\right)A(y)\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(y)\right)^{\mathrm{T}}r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Proof.

Applying the Itô formula to
\chi^{\prime}
and using (
2.2
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\chi^{\prime}(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\chi^{\prime}(x_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\chi^{\prime}(X_{s})\,\sigma(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}\chi^{\prime}(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\chi^{\prime}(x_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\chi^{\prime}(X_{s})\,\sigma(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}\left(b(X_{s})-\bar{b}\right)\mathrm{d}s.

In view of the SDE (
2.1
), we deduce that

(2.3)

X_{t}=x_{0}-\left(\chi^{\prime}(X_{t})-\chi^{\prime}(x_{0})\right)+t\,\bar{b}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(X_{s})\right)\sigma(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

Taking expectations on both sides, noting that
\int_{0}^{t}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(X_{s})\right)\sigma(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}
is a martingale whose expectation vanishes, and noting that
\chi^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
by elliptic regularity theory and Sobolev embeddings, we obtain that

(2.4)

\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}[X_{t}]-x_{0}}{t}-\bar{b}\right|\leq\frac{\left|\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}[\chi^{\prime}(X_{t})]-\chi^{\prime}(x_{0})\right|}{t}\leq\frac{2\|\chi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}{t},

where
\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}[X_{t}]:=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}|X_{0}=x_{0}]
. Hence, we find that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}[X_{t}]-x_{0}}{t}-\bar{b}\right|=0.

Meanwhile, writing

\displaystyle\overline{A}^{L}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(y)\right)A(y)\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(y)\right)^{\mathrm{T}}r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

taking the variance of the term
\int_{0}^{t}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(X_{s})\right)\sigma(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}
from (
2.3
), and using Fubini’s theorem yields that

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2t}\,\mathrm{Var}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(X_{s})\right)\sigma(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\right]-\overline{A}^{L}

\displaystyle\;=\frac{1}{t}\,\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(X_{s})\right)A(X_{s})\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(X_{s})\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right]-\overline{A}^{L}

\displaystyle\;=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(y)\right)A(y)\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(y)\right)^{\mathrm{T}}r_{s}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s-\overline{A}^{L}

\displaystyle\;=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(y)\right)A(y)\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(y)\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(r_{s}(y)-r(y)\right)\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
A:=\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}
and
r_{s}
denotes the probability density of
X_{s}
. In view of (
1.6
), we deduce that there exist constants
K,\rho>0
such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lvert\alpha_{t}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{t}\left|\int_{0}^{t}K\mathrm{e}^{-\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\frac{K}{\rho t}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\rho t})\leq\frac{K}{\rho t}.

Then, we use (
2.3
) and (
2.5
) to find that

(2.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X_{t})}{2t}-\overline{A}^{L}\right\rvert\\
&\leq\frac{\left\lvert\mathrm{Var}\left(\chi^{\prime}(X_{t})\right)\right\rvert}{2t}+\frac{1}{t}\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\hskip-4.26773pt\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(X_{s})\right)\sigma(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\hskip-2.84544pt\right)\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\chi^{\prime}(X_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}[\chi^{\prime}(X_{t})]\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]\right\rvert+\lvert\alpha_{t}\rvert\\
&\leq\frac{2\|\chi^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\infty}}{t}+\frac{K}{\rho t},\end{split}

where we used in the final step that
\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}(X_{s})\right)\sigma(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\right)\left(\chi^{\prime}(X_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}[\chi^{\prime}(X_{t})]\right)^{\mathrm{T}}
is a martingale whose expectation vanishes.

Taking the limit as
t\rightarrow\infty
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X_{t})}{2t}-\overline{A}^{L}\right\rvert=0,

which completes the proof.
∎

.

3.
Development of Numerical Schemes

In this section, we present a numerical framework for computing the effective diffusivity through stochastic simulation of the rescaled SDE (
1.4
). Our approach integrates high-order discretization schemes with Monte Carlo estimation, utilizing enhanced versions of the Euler–Maruyama and Milstein methods
[
33
]
improved by the modified equation approach
[
1
]
to achieve high-order accuracy in solving the underlying SDEs.

3.1.
One-step numerical integrators

For the numerical approximation of the diffusion process
X_{t}
in (
2.1
), we implement a one-step numerical integrator defined recursively by

(3.1)

X^{h}_{t_{n}}=\Phi^{b,\sigma}(X^{h}_{t_{n-1}},h,\zeta_{n-1}),\quad t_{n}=nh\in(0,T],\quad n=1,\dots,N,

where
h>0
denotes the fixed time-step size,
\{\zeta_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
are i.i.d.
d
-dimensional random vectors, and
\Phi^{b,\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the flow map that incorporates both drift
b
and diffusion
\sigma
terms. The numerical approximations
X^{h}_{t_{n}}
correspond to the exact solution at times
t_{n}=nh\in(0,T]
,
n=1,2,...,N
. The accuracy of the approximation can be measured in terms of the weak order of the numerical integrator defined as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

[
33
]

A numerical integrator (
3.1
) starting from the exact initial condition
X_{0}^{h}=X_{0}
is said to have weak order
p
for the approximation of
X_{t}
if for all test functions
f\in C_{P}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
there holds

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(X_{t_{n}}^{h}\right)-\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t_{n}})]|X_{0}=x\right]\right|=\mathcal{O}(h^{p}),\qquad 0\leq t_{n}=nh\leq T.

Here, we used the notation
C_{P}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to denote the class of smooth functions that together with their partial derivatives are of polynomial growth.

Two of the most widely used approaches to approximate the stochastic process are the Euler–Maruyama scheme

X_{t_{n+1}}^{h}=X_{t_{n}}^{h}+b\left(X_{t_{n}}^{h}\right)h+\sigma\left(X_{t_{n}}^{h}\right)\sqrt{h}\,\xi_{n},\quad n=1,2,\dotsc,N,

and the Milstein scheme

(3.2)

X_{t_{n+1}}^{h}=X_{t_{n}}^{h}+b\left(X_{t_{n}}^{h}\right)h+\sigma\left(X_{t_{n}}^{h}\right)\sqrt{h}\,\xi_{n}+M_{n}\left(X_{t_{n}}^{h}\right),\quad n=1,2,\dotsc,N,

where
\xi_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is a standard
d
-dimensional normal random vector, and
M_{n}(X_{t_{n}}^{h})
denotes the Milstein correction term. It is known that both the Euler–Maruyama and Milstein method have first-order convergence in the weak sense
[
33
]
.

In the works
[
1
,
2
,
46
]
, the idea behind the modified equation is that for a numerical integrator (
3.1
) with order
p
for the invariant measure of the SDE (
1.3
), we can construct a modified SDE

(3.3)

\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{t}=b_{h}(\tilde{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{h}(\tilde{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad\tilde{X}_{0}=x,

where
b_{h}
and
\sigma_{h}
admit asymptotic expansions of the form

(3.4)

b_{h}=b+h^{p}b_{p}+\dotsc+h^{p+m-1}b_{p+m-1},\qquad\sigma_{h}=\sigma+h^{p}\sigma_{p}+\dotsc+h^{p+m-1}\sigma_{p+m-1}

with
m\in\mathbb{N}
. When the numerical integrator (
3.1
) is applied to this modified SDE (
3.3
), i.e.,

\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{n}}=\Phi^{b_{h},\sigma_{h}}\left(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{n-1}},h,\zeta_{n-1}\right),\quad n=1,2,\dotsc,N,

the resulting approximation achieves weak order
p+m
. For a rigorous analysis and construction of modified equations, we refer to
[
1
,
2
,
46
]
. By employing the methodology of modified equations, we can approximate
X_{t}
with a higher convergence rate.

3.2.
Improved Milstein scheme based on the modified equation approach

We propose an improved Milstein scheme constructed via the modified equation approach, which achieves second-order weak convergence while preserving the computational efficiency of the classical method. Specifically, the second-order numerical integrator based on the Milstein scheme takes the form

(3.5)

\tilde{X}_{t_{n+1}}^{h}=\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}+b_{h}\left(\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}\right)h+\sigma_{h}\left(\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}\right)\sqrt{h}\,\xi_{n}+M_{n}\left(\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}\right),\quad\tilde{X}_{0}^{h}=0,\quad n=1,2,\dotsc,N,

where

\xi_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is a
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian random vector, and the Milstein correction term
M_{n}\left(\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}\right)=\left(M_{n,[i]}\left(\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}\right)\right)_{i=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined component-wise by

M_{n,[i]}(\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h})=\Xi_{i}(\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}):J_{n}=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{d}\Xi_{i,[j_{1},j_{2}]}J_{n}^{[j_{1},j_{2}]},\quad i=1,2,\dotsc,d.

Here, the coefficient matrix
\Xi_{i}=(\Xi_{i,[j_{1},j_{2}]})_{1\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq d}
is the
d\times d
matrix given by

\Xi_{i,[j_{1},j_{2}]}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\partial_{k}\sigma_{[i,j_{2}]}\sigma^{[k,j_{1}]},\quad i,j_{1},j_{2}=1,2,\dotsc,d,

and the double Itô integral
J_{n}=(J_{n}^{[j_{1},j_{2}]})_{1\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq d}
is defined as the
d\times d
matrix with entries

J_{n}^{[j_{1},j_{2}]}=\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\left(\int_{t_{n}}^{s}\mathrm{d}W^{j_{1}}_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}W^{j_{2}}_{s},\quad j_{1},j_{2}=1,2,\dotsc,d.

The integral matrix
J_{n}
is difficult to evaluate in general. However, under the commutativity condition

\Xi_{i,[j_{1},j_{2}]}=\Xi_{i,[j_{2},j_{1}]}\quad\forall i,j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,\dots,d\},

the matrix
J_{n}
can be approximated by

\frac{h}{2}\left(\xi_{n}^{j_{1}}\xi_{n}^{j_{2}}-\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}}\right),\quad j_{1},j_{2}=1,\dots,d,

where
\xi_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is a
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian random vector and
\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}}
is the Kronecker delta.

For the non-commutative case, we approximate
J_{n}
using a truncated multiple Fourier–Legendre series of order
q
, i.e.,

J_{n}^{q,[j_{1},j_{2}]}=\frac{h}{2}\left(\xi^{j_{1}}_{n}\xi_{n}^{j_{2}}+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4k^{2}-1}}(\xi^{(k-1),j_{1}}_{n}\xi_{n}^{(k),j_{2}}-\xi^{(k),j_{1}}_{n}\xi_{n}^{(k-1),j_{2}})-\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}}\right),

where
\xi_{n}^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
are i.i.d. standard normal random vectors with
\xi_{n}^{(0)}=\xi_{n}
. Without altering the weak convergence rate, we can simply take
q=2
. See Theorem 2 in
[
29
]
for more details.

The modified equation coefficients
b_{h}
and
\sigma_{h}
are expanded as

b_{h}=b+h\,b_{1},\quad\sigma_{h}=\sigma+h\,\sigma_{1},

where the first-order corrections
b_{1}=(b_{1,[i]})_{1\leq i\leq d}
and
\sigma_{1}=(\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]})_{1\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq d}
are given by

(3.6)

\displaystyle b_{1,[i]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}b\cdot\nabla b_{[i]}+\frac{1}{4}(\sigma\sigma^{T}):\nabla^{2}b_{[i]},\qquad i=1,\dots,d,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((\nabla b)\sigma\right)_{[j_{1},j_{2}]}+\frac{1}{2}b\cdot\nabla\sigma_{[j_{1},j_{2}]}+\frac{1}{4}(\sigma\sigma^{T}):\nabla^{2}\sigma_{[j_{1},j_{2}]},\qquad j_{1},j_{2}=1,\dots,d,

This is a specific case of the
\theta
-Milstein method provided in
[
1
]
.
More precisely, the first-order correction terms
b_{1}
and
\sigma_{1}
can be expressed using Einstein summation convention as follows:

\displaystyle b_{1,[i]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}b_{[i]}b^{[k]}+\frac{1}{4}(\sigma\sigma^{T})_{[k_{1},k_{2}]}\partial^{k_{1}}\partial^{k_{2}}b_{[i]},

\displaystyle\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{[k,j_{2}]}\partial^{k}b_{[j_{1}]}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}\sigma_{[j_{1},j_{2}]}b^{[k]}+\frac{1}{4}(\sigma\sigma^{T})_{[k_{1},k_{2}]}\partial^{k_{1}}\partial^{k_{2}}\sigma_{[j_{1},j_{2}]}.

In Section 5, we will employ the improved Milstein scheme, constructed using the modified equation approach, to numerically approximate invariant measures and effective diffusivities for SDEs associated with nondivergence-form elliptic equations involving large drift terms.

4.
Convergence analysis

In this section, we establish error estimates for the proposed numerical scheme in computing both the invariant measure and solutions to the cell problem through backward error analysis. Furthermore, we derive approximation error bounds for the effective diffusivity in the associated SDEs.

4.1.
Error analysis for the invariant measure

We analyze the weak convergence properties of the first modified equation, following
[
1
]
. Consider the backward Kolmogorov equation associated with (
1.3
), i.e.,

(4.1)

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}u,\qquad u(x,0)=\phi(x),

where
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is an arbitrary test function. With
X_{t}
denoting the solution to (
1.4
), we have that the solution to (
4.1
) is given by
u(x,t)=u^{b,\sigma}(\phi,x,t)
, where

u^{b,\sigma}(\phi,x,t)=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(X_{t})\,|\,X_{0}=x\right].

Since
b,\sigma
, and
\phi
are smooth functions,
u^{b,\sigma}
admits the formal Taylor expansion

u^{b,\sigma}(\phi,x,h)=\phi(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{h^{j}}{j!}\mathcal{L}^{j}\phi(x).

For the numerical integrator (
3.1
), we define the numerical solution to (
4.1
) as

(4.2)

U^{b,\sigma}(\phi,x,h)=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(X_{t_{1}}^{h})\,|\,X_{0}=x\right].

We make the following assumption about the numerical solution.

Assumption 1
.

The numerical solution (
4.2
)
admits a weak Taylor expansion of the form

U^{b,\sigma}(\phi,x,h)=\phi(x)+h\,\mathcal{L}\phi(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}h^{j+1}\mathcal{A}_{j}^{b,\sigma}\phi(x),

where
\mathcal{A}_{j}^{b,\sigma}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, are numerical operators depending on the coefficients of the SDE (
1.4
).

The following result is a special case of
[
1
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Consider a first weak-order numerical integrator (
3.1
) satisfying Assumption
1
. If there exist modified coefficients
b_{h}=b+h\,b_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\sigma_{h}=\sigma+h\,\sigma_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
as in (
3.4
) such that the differential operator

\mathcal{L}_{1}:=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{u^{b,\sigma}(\cdot,x,h)-U^{b_{h},\sigma_{h}}(\cdot,x,h)}{h^{2}}

can be expressed in the form

(4.3)

\mathcal{L}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma_{1}\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}):\nabla^{2}+b_{1}\cdot\nabla,

then the numerical integrator with the modified equation

(4.4)

\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{n}+1}=\Phi_{b_{h},\sigma_{h}}(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{n}},h,\xi_{n})

achieves second-order weak convergence for the original SDE. Specifically, for any test function
f\in C_{P}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, sufficiently small time-step
h>0
, and any fixed time
t_{n}=nh
, we have that

\left|\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{n}})]-\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t_{n}})]\right|\leq Ch^{2}.

For the Milstein scheme (
3.2
), one can verify that the coefficients
b_{h}
and
\sigma_{h}
defined in (
3.6
) satisfy the operator form (
4.3
). Consequently, the modified scheme (
3.5
) achieves second-order convergence in the weak sense.

Remark 4.1
.

In the case of non-constant
\sigma
, the operator
\mathcal{L}_{1}
associated with the Euler–Maruyama scheme contains third-order derivative terms. This prevents its expression in the form (
4.3
). Therefore, a modified integrator cannot be constructed for the Euler–Maruyama method. If we use Milstein’s method instead of the Euler method, such a derivation
of a modified Itô SDE is possible under appropriate smoothness assumptions for the drift
b
and the diffusion coefficient
\sigma
.

Corollary 4.1

(Convergence of invariant measure)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
, for any test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, there exist constants
C,K,\rho>0
, such that for
t_{n}=nh
, the numerical solution
\tilde{X}_{t_{n}}^{h}
with smooth modified coefficients
b_{h},\sigma_{h}
satisfies

\left|\mathbb{E}[\phi(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{n}})]-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\phi(y)r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq Ke^{-\rho t}+Ch^{2}.

4.2.
Error analysis for effective diffusion matrix

The backward error analysis approach
[
39
]
provides an effective framework for analyzing numerical approximations. This technique modifies the original SDE (
2.1
) to construct a continuous-time process
\hat{X}^{h}_{t}
whose SDE takes the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\hat{X}^{h}_{t}=\hat{b}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}t+\hat{\sigma}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

that better approximates the discrete numerical solution
X^{h}_{t_{n}}
from (
3.1
). Specifically, for any smooth test function
f\in C_{P}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, sufficiently small time step
h>0
, and discrete times
t_{n}=nh
, we obtain the error estimate

(4.5)

\left|\mathbb{E}[f(X^{h}_{t_{n}})-\mathbb{E}f(\hat{X}^{h}_{t_{n}})]\right|\leq Ch^{p+q},

where
p
is the base order of the numerical method,
q
represents the order improvement, and
C>0
is a constant independent of
h
.

For the Milstein scheme (
3.2
), the first-order modified SDE in backward error analysis
[
12
]
takes the form

\mathrm{d}\hat{X}^{h}_{t}=(b-h\,b_{1})\,\mathrm{d}t+(\sigma-h\,\sigma_{1})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

where the correction terms
b_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
\sigma_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
are identical to those defined in (
3.6
). This means that the modified coefficients
b_{h},\sigma_{h}
of modified equations for backward error analysis and for modified integrators of second order are identical up to the multiplicative factor
-1
; see
[
1
]
.

Regarding
b+h\,b_{1}
and
\sigma+h\,\sigma_{1}
as the new
b
and
\sigma
, we take backward error analysis for the numerical integrator with modified equation
\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{n}}
(
4.4
) of the given SDE (
1.3
) which yields

\mathrm{d}X^{\prime}_{t}=(b+h^{2}b^{\prime}_{1})\,\mathrm{d}t+(\sigma+h^{2}\sigma_{1}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

where the second-order correction terms
b_{1}^{\prime}=(b_{1,[i]}^{\prime})_{1\leq i\leq d}
and
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}=(\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}^{\prime})_{1\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq d}
are given by

\displaystyle b_{1,[i]}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(b_{1}\cdot\nabla b_{[i]}+b\cdot\nabla b_{1,[i]}\Big)+\frac{1}{4}\Big[(\sigma_{1}\sigma^{T}+\sigma\sigma_{1}^{T}):\nabla^{2}b_{[i]}+(\sigma\sigma^{T}):\nabla^{2}b_{1,[i]}\Big]

\displaystyle+\frac{h}{2}\,b_{1}\cdot\nabla b_{1,[i]}+\frac{h}{4}\Big[(\sigma_{1}\sigma^{T}+\sigma\sigma_{1}^{T}):\nabla^{2}b_{1,[i]}+(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{T}):\nabla^{2}b_{[i]}\Big],

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\nabla b_{[j_{1}]}\cdot\sigma_{1,[\cdot,j_{2}]}+\nabla b_{1,[j_{1}]}\cdot\sigma_{[\cdot,j_{2}]}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\nabla\sigma_{[j_{1},j_{2}]}\cdot b_{1}+\nabla\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}\cdot b\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\Big((\sigma\sigma^{T}):\nabla^{2}\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}+(\sigma_{1}\sigma^{T}+\sigma\sigma_{1}^{T}):\nabla^{2}\sigma_{[j_{1}.j_{2}]}\Big)+\frac{h}{2}\Big(\nabla b_{1,[j_{1}]}\cdot\sigma_{1,[\cdot,j_{2}]}+\nabla\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}\cdot b_{1}\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{h}{4}\Big((\sigma_{1}\sigma^{T}+\sigma\sigma_{1}^{T}):\nabla^{2}\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{T}:\nabla^{2}\sigma_{[j_{1},j_{2}]}\Big)

for
i,j_{1},j_{2}=1,2,\dotsc,d
. Equivalently, using Einstein’s summation convention,

\displaystyle b_{1,[i]}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(b_{1,[k]}\partial^{k}b_{[i]}+\partial_{k}b_{1,[i]}\,b^{[k]}\Big)+\frac{1}{4}\Big[(\sigma_{1}\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{T}})_{[k,l]}\partial^{k}\partial^{l}b_{[i]}+(\sigma\sigma^{T})_{[k,l]}\partial^{k}\partial^{l}b_{1,[i]}\Big]

\displaystyle+\frac{h}{2}\,b_{1,[k]}\partial^{k}b_{1,[i]}+\frac{h}{4}\Big[(\sigma_{1}\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{T}})_{[k,l]}\partial^{k}\partial^{l}b_{1,[i]}+(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{T}})_{[k,l]}\partial^{k}\partial^{l}b_{[i]}\Big],

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\partial_{k}b_{[j_{1}]}\sigma_{1}^{[k,j_{2}]}+\partial_{k}b_{1,[j_{1}]}\sigma^{[k,j_{2}]}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\partial_{k}\sigma_{[j_{1},j_{2}]}b_{1}^{[k]}+\partial_{k}\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}b^{[k]}\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\Big[(\sigma\sigma^{\mathrm{T}})_{[k_{1},k_{2}]}\partial^{k_{1}}\partial^{k_{2}}\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}+(\sigma_{1}\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{T}})_{[k_{1},k_{2}]}\partial^{k_{1}}\partial^{k_{2}}\sigma_{[j_{1},j_{2}]}\Big]+\frac{h}{2}\Big[\partial_{k}b_{1,[j_{1}]}\sigma_{1}^{[k,j_{2}]}+\partial_{k}\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}b_{1}^{[k]}\Big]

\displaystyle+\frac{h}{4}\Big((\sigma_{1}\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{T}})_{[k_{1},k_{2}]}\partial^{k_{1}}\partial^{k_{2}}\sigma_{1,[j_{1},j_{2}]}+(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{T}})_{[k_{1},k_{2}]}\partial^{k_{1}}\partial^{k_{2}}\sigma_{[j_{1},j_{2}]}\Big).

By combining Proposition
4.1
with the backward error estimate (
4.5
), we obtain the following key estimate that for any test function
f\in C_{P}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, sufficiently small time step
h>0
, and discrete times
t_{h}=nh
, we have

(4.6)

\left|\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{n}})-\mathbb{E}f(X^{\prime}_{t_{n}})]\right|\leq Ch^{2}.

This result justifies using the modified SDE (
3.3
) to approximate the effective diffusion matrix. Defining the modified coefficients

(4.7)

A_{h}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma+h^{2}\sigma_{1}^{\prime})(\sigma+h^{2}\sigma_{1}^{\prime})^{\mathrm{T}},\qquad b_{h}^{\prime}=b+h^{2}b_{1}^{\prime},

we obtain the associated generator and its adjoint operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{h}v:=-A_{h}^{\prime}:\nabla^{2}v-b_{h}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla v,\qquad\left(\mathcal{L}^{h}\right)^{*}v:=-\nabla^{2}:\left(A_{h}^{\prime}v\right)+\nabla\cdot\left(b_{h}^{\prime}v\right).

When
h>0
is small enough, we can preserve that
A_{h}^{\prime}
is still uniformly elliptic. By the Fredholm alternative, there exists a unique solution
r_{h}^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
to

(\mathcal{L}^{h})^{*}r_{h}^{\prime}=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r_{h}^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=1,

and there exists a unique solution
\chi_{h}^{\prime}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
to

\mathcal{L}^{h}\chi_{h}^{\prime}=b_{h}^{\prime}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}b_{h}^{\prime}(y)r_{h}^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\chi_{h}^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0.

The function
r_{h}^{\prime}
is an approximation of the invariant measure
r
. The corresponding Lagrangian effective diffusivity
\overline{A}^{L}_{h}
is given by

(4.8)

\overline{A}^{L}_{h}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}[I_{d}+\nabla\chi_{h}^{\prime}(y)]A_{h}^{\prime}(y)[I_{d}+\nabla\chi_{h}^{\prime}(y)]^{\mathrm{T}}r_{h}^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

A quick argument reveals an explicit stability bound for nondivergence-form problems with drift:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
be a uniformly elliptic diffusion matrix with ellipticity constant
\lambda>0
, let
b\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a drift vector field, and let
r\in C(\mathbb{T}^{d};(0,\infty))
denote the corresponding invariant measure satisfying
\mathcal{L}^{*}r=0
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=1
. For a source term
z\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}z(y)r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0
, the weak solution
w\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
to

(4.9)

\mathcal{L}w:=-A:\nabla^{2}w-b\cdot\nabla w=z\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}w(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0

satisfies the energy estimate

\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\lambda}\frac{\sup_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r}{\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r}\|z\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},\qquad\|\nabla^{2}w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\|z\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Proof.

By elliptic regularity theory, we have
w\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Furthermore, the invariant measure
r
satisfies
r\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for all
p<\infty
and
r\geq\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r=:r_{0}>0
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
; see e.g.,
[
8
,
26
]
. Rewriting equation (
4.9
) in divergence form after multiplication by
r
, we obtain

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(rA\nabla w)+\beta\cdot\nabla w=rz\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},

where
\beta=\mathrm{div}(rA)-rb
. In particular, we find that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r(y)A(y)\nabla w(y)\cdot\nabla w(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\beta(y)\cdot\nabla w(y))w(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r(y)z(y)w(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Note that
A\geq\lambda I_{n}
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
for some
\lambda>0
, that
\nabla\cdot\beta=0
weakly in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
by definition of
r
, and that
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r(y)z(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0
. Let
\hat{w}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}w(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
be the mean value of
w
. Using Poincaré and Hölder inequalities and the uniform lower bound on
r
, we obtain that

\displaystyle r_{0}\lambda\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}rA\nabla w\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}rA\nabla w\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\beta\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{w^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}rzw\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}rz(w-\hat{w})\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\|r\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|z\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|w-\hat{w}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\|r\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|z\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

When
A
is a constant symmetric positive definite matrix and
\nabla\cdot b=0
(weakly), then the invariant measure is
r\equiv 1
.

Let
\chi^{\prime}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
\chi^{\prime}_{h}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
be the solutions of following cell problems corresponding to the original SDE and the modified SDE with modified equation, respectively, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\chi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=b-\langle b\rangle_{r}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\chi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{h}\chi_{h}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=b_{h}^{\prime}-\langle b_{h}^{\prime}\rangle_{r^{\prime}_{h}}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\chi_{h}^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0,

where
\langle b\rangle_{r}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}b(y)r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
and
\langle b_{h}^{\prime}\rangle_{r^{\prime}_{h}}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}b_{h}^{\prime}(y)r_{h}^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
denote the averaged drift terms with respect to the original and modified invariant measure. These solutions satisfy the relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\chi^{\prime}-\chi_{h}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\chi^{\prime}-\mathcal{L}^{h}\chi_{h}^{\prime}+(\mathcal{L}^{h}-\mathcal{L})\chi_{h}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(b-b_{h}^{\prime})+(\langle b_{h}^{\prime}\rangle_{r_{h}^{\prime}}-\langle b\rangle_{r})+(A-A_{h}^{\prime}):\nabla^{2}\chi_{h}^{\prime}+(b-b_{h}^{\prime})\cdot\nabla\chi_{h}^{\prime}=:S_{h}.

Introducing the discrepancy term

(4.10)

\displaystyle\alpha_{h}:=\|A-A_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|b-b_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},

and noting that, for
h>0
sufficiently small,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\beta_{h}:=\left\lvert\langle b\rangle_{r}-\langle b_{h}^{\prime}\rangle_{r_{h}^{\prime}}\right\rvert&\leq\left\lvert\langle b-b_{h}^{\prime}\rangle_{r}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\langle b_{h}^{\prime}-b\rangle_{r}-\langle b_{h}^{\prime}-b\rangle_{r_{h}^{\prime}}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\langle b\rangle_{r}-\langle b\rangle_{r_{h}^{\prime}}\right\rvert\\
&\leq\|b-b_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\left(\|b\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+1\right)\|r-r_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},\end{split}

we apply Lemma
4.1
to obtain that

\displaystyle\|\chi^{\prime}-\chi_{h}^{\prime}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\|S_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\left(\|b-b_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\beta_{h}+\alpha_{h}\|\chi^{\prime}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\alpha_{h}\|\chi^{\prime}-\chi_{h}^{\prime}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right).

Since
\alpha_{h}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
, for
h>0
sufficiently small we can absorb the last term into the left-hand side to deduce the error bound

(4.12)

\displaystyle\|\chi^{\prime}-\chi_{h}^{\prime}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\left(\|b-b_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\beta_{h}+\alpha_{h}\|\chi^{\prime}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)=\mathcal{O}(h^{2}),

where we used
\beta_{h}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
which follows from (
4.11
) and the following lemma (with
\alpha_{h}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
A,A_{h}^{\prime}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d})
be uniformly elliptic diffusion matrices and
b,b_{h}^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
be drift vector fields.
Consider the corresponding invariant measures
r,r_{h}^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
that solve (weakly) the following problems:

(4.13)

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}:(Ar)+\nabla\cdot(br)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=1,

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}:(A_{h}^{\prime}r_{h}^{\prime})+\nabla\cdot(b_{h}^{\prime}r_{h}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r_{h}^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=1.

Then, if the discrepency term
\alpha_{h}
defined as in (
4.10
) satisfies
\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\alpha_{h}=0
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(4.14)

\|r-r_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\|r\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\left(1+\|r\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)\alpha_{h}

for
h>0
sufficiently small.

Proof.

Let
w\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
be the unique solution to the elliptic problem

\displaystyle-A:\nabla^{2}w-b\cdot\nabla w=(r-r_{h}^{\prime})-\langle r-r_{h}^{\prime}\rangle_{r}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}w(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0.

Multiplying the above equation by
r-r_{h}^{\prime}
, integrating over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, noting
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(r(y)-r_{h}^{\prime}(y))\,\mathrm{d}y=0
, and using the (weak) solution properties of
r
and
r_{h}^{\prime}
for (
4.13
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\|r-r_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(r-r_{h}^{\prime})(-A:\nabla^{2}w-b\cdot\nabla w)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}r_{h}^{\prime}\left((A-A_{h}^{\prime}):\nabla^{2}w+(b-b_{h}^{\prime})\cdot\nabla w\right)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\|r_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|w\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\alpha_{h}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|r-r_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|r\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)\|r-r_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\left(1+\|r\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)\alpha_{h}

\displaystyle\leq C\|r-r_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\left(1+\|r\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)\alpha_{h}+C\|r\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\left(1+\|r\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)\|r-r_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\alpha_{h},

where we used Lemma
4.1
in the penultimate step. Since
\alpha_{h}\rightarrow 0
as
h\rightarrow 0
, we can absorb the first term on the right-hand side into the left-hand side and obtain the estimate (
4.14
) for
h>0
sufficiently small.
∎

Building upon the preceding estimates, we establish the following error bound for the numerical approximation of the effective diffusivity.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let the situation be as in Theorem
2.1
, and let
A_{h}^{\prime},b_{h}^{\prime}
be given by (
4.7
). Then, for the effective diffusivities
\overline{A}^{L}
and
\overline{A}^{L}_{h}
defined in (
1.9
) and (
4.8
) respectively, there holds

\left\lvert\overline{A}_{h}^{L}-\overline{A}^{L}\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(h^{2}).

Proof.

Using triangle and Hölder inequalities, we find that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\overline{A}_{h}^{L}-\overline{A}^{L}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}_{h}\right)A^{\prime}_{h}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}_{h}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}r^{\prime}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}\right)A\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}r\,\mathrm{d}y\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}\right)A\left(\nabla(\chi^{\prime}-\chi_{h}^{\prime})\right)^{\mathrm{T}}r\,\mathrm{d}y\right\rvert+\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(\nabla(\chi^{\prime}-\chi_{h}^{\prime})\right)A\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi_{h}^{\prime}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}r\,\mathrm{d}y\right\rvert

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}_{h}\right)\left(A-A^{\prime}_{h}\right)\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}_{h}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}r^{\prime}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}y\right\rvert+\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}_{h}\right)A\left(I_{d}+\nabla\chi^{\prime}_{h}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(r-r^{\prime}_{h}\right)\mathrm{d}y\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\nabla e_{\chi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\left(1+\|\nabla\chi_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)+\left(\|e_{A}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|r_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|e_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)\left(1+\|\nabla\chi_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)^{2}\right)

for some constant
C>0
, where we have written
e_{\chi}:=\chi^{\prime}-\chi_{h}^{\prime}
,
e_{A}:=A-A_{h}^{\prime}
, and
e_{r}:=r-r_{h}^{\prime}
. From (
4.12
) and Lemma
4.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\|\nabla e_{\chi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2}),\qquad\|e_{A}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2}),\qquad\|e_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2})

for some constant
C>0
. Further, similarly to the derivation of (
4.12
), but using
W^{2,p}
estimates for solutions to (
4.9
) instead of Lemma
4.1
, we can find that
\|\nabla\chi_{h}^{\prime}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}
remains uniformly bounded as
h\rightarrow 0
. We conclude that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\overline{A}_{h}^{L}-\overline{A}^{L}\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(h^{2}),

as required.
∎

The following corollary reveals the connection between the effective diffusivity and the long-time covariance of the process.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let the situation be as in Theorem
4.1
. Let
\{\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{N}
be numerical solutions obtained from the modified Milstein scheme (
3.5
) with time step
h
, and let
\bm{x}_{t_{N}}=(\bm{x}_{t_{N},[1]},\bm{x}_{t_{N},[2]},\dotsc,\bm{x}_{t_{N},[d]})^{T}
be

M
independent Monte Carlo realizations, where each
\bm{x}_{t_{N},[i]}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
X_{t_{N}}=X_{T}
. Then, for
h>0
sufficiently small, the empirical variance estimator satisfies

(4.15)

\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bm{x}_{t_{N}})}{2T}-\overline{A}^{L}\right\rvert\leq C\left(h^{2}+\frac{1}{T}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\right)

for some constant
C>0
independent of
h
,
T
, and
M
.

Proof.

Using the triangle inequality, we split the error into four parts:

(4.16)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bm{x}_{t_{N}})}{2T}-\overline{A}^{L}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bm{x}_{t_{N}})}{2T}-\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{N}})}{2T}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{N}})}{2T}-\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X^{\prime}_{t_{N}})}{2T}\right\rvert

\displaystyle+\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X^{\prime}_{t_{N}})}{2T}-\overline{A}_{h}^{L}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\overline{A}_{h}^{L}-\overline{A}^{L}\right\rvert.

For the first component in (
4.16
), by the Central Limit Theorem, there exists
L_{1}>0
such that

\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bm{x}_{t_{N}})}{2T}-\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{N}})}{2T}\right\rvert\leq\frac{L_{1}}{\sqrt{M}}.

Applying (
4.6
) to the moment functions
f_{1}(x)=x
and
f_{2}(x)=xx^{\mathrm{T}}
, we obtain that there exist constants
K_{1},K_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{N}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X^{\prime}_{t_{N}}\right]\right\rvert\leq K_{1}h^{2},\qquad\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{N}}\right)\left(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{N}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X^{\prime}_{t_{N}}\right)\left(X^{\prime}_{t_{N}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]\right\rvert\leq K_{2}h^{2}

for
h>0
sufficiently small. From the covariance structure, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2T}\left\lvert\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{X}_{t_{N}}^{h})-\mathrm{Var}(X^{\prime}_{t_{N}})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2T}\Big\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{N}}\right)\left(\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{N}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X^{\prime}_{t_{N}}\right)\left(X^{\prime}_{t_{N}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]\Big\rvert

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2T}\Big\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{N}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X^{\prime}_{t_{N}}\right]\Big\rvert\left(\Big\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{N}}\right]\Big\rvert+\Big\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[X^{\prime}_{t_{N}}\right]\Big\rvert\right).

Next, note that from (
2.4
) in Theorem
2.1
that there exist
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
L_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}^{h}_{t_{N}}\right]}{2T}-M_{1}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[X^{\prime}_{t_{N}}\right]}{2T}-M_{2}\right\rvert\leq\frac{L_{2}}{T}.

Hence, using the triangle inequality, we deduce that for
h>0
sufficiently small there holds

\frac{1}{2T}\left\lvert\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{X}_{t_{N}}^{h})-\mathrm{Var}(X^{\prime}_{t_{N}})\right\rvert\leq L_{3}\left(h^{2}+\frac{1}{T}\right)

for some constant
L_{3}>0
. Finally, (
2.6
) in Theorem
2.1
establishes that there exists a constant
L_{4}>0
such that

\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X^{\prime}_{t_{N}})}{2T}-\overline{A}_{h}^{L}\right\rvert\leq\frac{L_{4}}{T}.

Combining these estimates with (
4.16
) and Theorem
4.1
, which bounds
\lvert\overline{A}_{h}^{L}-\overline{A}^{L}\rvert
, yields the final estimate (
4.15
).
∎

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed a probabilistic numerical framework for accurately computing the effective diffusion matrix in periodic homogenization of nondivergence-form elliptic equations with large drift. Our rigorous analysis bridges the Lagrangian and Eulerian perspectives for effective diffusivity computation, extending previous results to scenarios where the centering condition fails.
This helps explain previous numerical difficulties observed with certain schemes. We introduced a modified equation approach that achieves second-order weak convergence, improving upon standard first-order methods. We derived complete error estimates for the approximation of the effective diffusivity.

Numerical experiments in 2D and 3D demonstrated the superior convergence properties of our modified scheme compared to standard methods, achieving second-order accuracy even for non-constant diffusion coefficients. The Lagrangian particle method proves particularly effective for studying convection-enhanced diffusion in time-dependent chaotic flows. Our framework offers an efficient, accurate approach for homogenization problems with large drift, with potential applications to turbulent transport in fluid dynamics and anomalous diffusion in biological systems.
