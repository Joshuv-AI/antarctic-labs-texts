Reprogramming the oxidative microenvironment: Antioxidant nanoplatforms for central nervous system repair
Authors: Jae Min Lee, Hye Ok Kim, Seong Wook Byun, Youn-Jung Kim, Manish Kumar Singh, Sung Soo Kim, Seung Geun Yeo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute central nervous system (CNS) injuries—such as ischemic stroke, traumatic brain injury (TBI), and spinal cord injury (SCI)—trigger a severe, self-reinforcing oxidative microenvironment. This reactive oxygen species (ROS)-driven cascade accelerates secondary injury mechanisms, including sustained neuroinflammation, blood-brain and blood-spinal cord barrier (BBB/BSCB) disruption, and irreversible mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately forming a hostile niche that impedes spontaneous neural repair. While current clinical modalities address the primary physical or ischemic insults, they largely fail to mitigate this persistent oxidative stress. Furthermore, conventional small-molecule antioxidants are severely limited by poor pharmacokinetic profiles, inefficient barrier penetration, and an inability to provide spatiotemporally controlled ROS scavenging. To overcome these therapeutic bottlenecks, advanced antioxidant nanoplatforms have emerged as active regulators of the injury milieu. This review comprehensively examines the design principles of next-generation nanotherapeutics, highlighting strategies such as receptor-mediated transcytosis for active barrier traversal, stimuli-responsive mechanisms for on-site activation, and the integration of multi-catalytic inorganic nanozymes for subcellular precision. Beyond stoichiometric ROS scavenging, these platforms facilitate precise redox modulation that reprograms cellular fate. We detail how restoring redox homeostasis promotes the crucial M1-to-M2 polarization of microglia/macrophages, preserves mitochondrial integrity against apoptotic cascades, and creates a permissive microenvironment conducive to axonal regeneration and remyelination across stroke, TBI, and SCI models. Finally, we outline the critical translational challenges that must be addressed to transition these nanoplatforms into clinical practice, emphasizing the need for long-term biodistribution and immunotoxicity profiling, refined pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) modeling, and the establishment of scalable, Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compatible protocols.

CNS injury–induced oxidative microenvironment

Acute central nervous system (CNS) injuries, including ischemic stroke, traumatic brain injury (TBI) and spinal cord injury (SCI), remain major causes of long-term neurological disability [1,2]. Despite distinct initiating insults and anatomical locations, these conditions converge on a common secondary pathological hub: a rapidly established oxidative microenvironment driven by mitochondrial dysfunction, NADPH oxidase (NOX) activation, and, in hemorrhagic settings, iron-catalyzed Fenton chemistry and lipid peroxidation [3,4]. In ischemic stroke, reperfusion further accelerates reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and cell death signaling, extending damage beyond the initial ischemic core [[5], [6], [7]]. Concurrently, M1-polarized microglia and reactive astrocytes reinforce this niche through nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB)/NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent cytokine release and sustained NOX-derived ROS production, establishing a feed-forward cycle of oxidative stress and inflammation [8]. This hostile milieu not only promotes ferroptosis and apoptosis, but also impairs endogenous repair by disrupting blood–brain barrier (BBB)/blood–spinal cord barrier (BSCB) integrity, enhancing astrogliosis and biasing neural stem/progenitor cells (NSCs) away from regenerative programs [9,10].

Although conventional small-molecule antioxidants showed early preclinical promise, clinical translation has been disappointing [11]. The major barriers are unfavorable pharmacokinetics, chemical instability, poor BBB/BSCB penetration and insufficient lesion-site accumulation, which together limit therapeutic exposure within the injured CNS [5]. Dose escalation can partly compensate for poor delivery, but at the cost of increased systemic toxicity and a narrowed therapeutic window [3]. Nanoplatforms have therefore emerged as attractive alternatives because they offer tunable pharmacokinetics, barrier-targeting capability, and spatiotemporally controlled release [12]. However, platforms designed only as passive carriers or stoichiometric ROS scavengers remain inadequate, because they do not sufficiently modulate the dynamic injury niche that governs degeneration versus repair, including mitochondrial ROS generation, NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, microglial polarization, and NSC fate [13].

Recent reviews have summarized CNS-directed nanocarrier strategies, particularly receptor-mediated BBB delivery, exosome/lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-based formulations, and the catalytic mechanisms of inorganic nanozymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD)- and catalase-mimetic systems [14,15]. However, most remain organized by platform type or single-disease context, with limited integration of the shared oxidative-inflammatory cascades across ischemic stroke, TBI, and SCI [7,16,17]. As a result, a mechanistically unified framework linking nanoplatform design principles to microenvironmental reprogramming in acute CNS injury is still lacking.

Here, we frame acute CNS injury as a shared ROS-driven, regeneration-inhibitory niche and position antioxidant nanoplatforms as active modulators of this microenvironment rather than simple ROS scavengers. Within this framework, nanoplatforms can be evaluated based on their ability to traverse biological barriers, respond to local stimuli, target subcellular ROS sources, and redirect redox-sensitive cell-fate programs. Through these functions, they can suppress mitochondrial- and NOX-derived ROS, limit iron-driven lipid peroxidation, dampen NF-κB/NLRP3-mediated neuroinflammation, preserve BBB/BSCB integrity, promote M1-to-M2 microglial polarization, restore pro-neurogenic signaling, and support axonal regeneration and remyelination. This perspective shifts the focus of strategies for neural repair across ischemic stroke, TBI, and SCI from antioxidant delivery alone to rational microenvironment reprogramming.

ROS-driven pathological cascades and barriers to regeneration

Cellular and subcellular sources of the oxidative burst

Following acute CNS injury, excessive ROS generation at the lesion site is sustained by three convergent sources: mitochondrial dysfunction, NOX overactivation, and dysregulated iron handling [18,19]. Ischemic or mechanical insults trigger calcium overload and glutamate excitotoxicity, leading to rapid mitochondrial membrane depolarization and impaired electron transport at complexes I and III, which increases electron leak and mitochondrial superoxide production [20,21]. In parallel, activated microglia and infiltrating leukocytes upregulate NOX complexes, particularly NOX2 in myeloid-lineage cells and NOX4 in vascular and astroglial compartments, thereby imposing strong oxidative pressure on the extracellular and pericellular environment [22,23]. In hemorrhagic settings, especially TBI and SCI, erythrocyte lysis releases hemoglobin and heme, which are further catabolized to liberate ferrous iron; this iron accelerates Fenton chemistry, converts hydrogen peroxide into hydroxyl radicals, and amplifies lipid peroxidation [24,25]. The resulting ROS surge intensifies secondary injury by coupling oxidative stress to neuroinflammation and maladaptive stress responses, including ferroptosis and autophagy disruption [4]. Importantly, these sources are not independent: mitochondrial ROS can potentiate NOX activation, NOX-derived hydrogen peroxide further damages mitochondria, and iron-driven Fenton reactions amplify both [26]. Together, these interactions establish a self-reinforcing oxidative niche that rapidly stabilizes the lesion microenvironment and resists conventional pharmacological control.

ROS-driven pathological cascades: ferroptosis, apoptosis, and neuroinflammation

Pathological ROS accumulation acts not only as a source of indiscriminate macromolecular damage, but also as a signaling hub that drives neuroinflammation and multiple programmed cell death pathways [24,27]. The CNS is particularly vulnerable because of its high oxygen demand and polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)-rich membranes, which are highly susceptible to peroxidation under conditions of oxidative stress and catalytic iron overload [28]. When glutathione peroxidase 4 is depleted or inactivated, the consequent unchecked lipid peroxidation triggers ferroptosis, an iron-dependent death program that contributes substantially to secondary lesion expansion in ischemic stroke and SCI models [5,7]. At the same time, excessive mitochondrial ROS promotes sustained opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, resulting in cytochrome c release and activation of caspase-dependent apoptosis [7,29]. ROS also sustain NF-κ B signaling and prime the NLRP3 inflammasome in microglia and astrocytes, leading to increased release of interleukin (IL)-1β, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and related cytokines, which disrupt BBB/BSCB integrity and reinforce the secondary injury milieu [8,30]. Taken together, ferroptosis, apoptosis, and neuroinflammation are not parallel by-products of injury, but mutually reinforcing outputs of dysregulated redox signaling. Effective neuroprotection therefore requires intervention at these upstream redox control nodes rather than isolated suppression of downstream damage.

ROS-mediated inhibition of neural regeneration

Beyond promoting cell death and inflammation, the persistent oxidative microenvironment also suppresses endogenous repair [31]. Efficient tissue resolution requires microglia to shift from an early pro-inflammatory M1 state toward a reparative M2 phenotype, but sustained ROS maintains NF-κ B activation and NLRP3 priming, thereby locking microglia in a dysfunctional pro-inflammatory program and preventing timely resolution [27,32,33]. In parallel, oxidative stress exacerbates reactive astrogliosis, often toward an M1-analagous, neurotoxic A1-like phenotype, and promotes overproduction of inhibitory extracellular matrix components, such as chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs), which consolidate a dense glial scar and obstruct axonal regrowth [[34], [35], [36]]. NSCs are similarly redox-sensitive. Although physiological ROS levels support self-renewal and neurogenesis through PI3K/AKT signaling [37], pathological ROS disrupt PI3K/AKT and Wnt/beta-catenin pathways, biasing NSCs toward reactive astrogliogenesis rather than functional neuronal differentiation [5,33]. Elevated ROS also destabilize growth-cone cytoskeletal organization, impairing axonal elongation competence. Collectively, these changes establish a regeneration-inhibitory niche that cannot be adequately reversed by conventional antioxidant approaches alone. Nanoplatform design principles for microenvironment reprogramming

Acute CNS injury creates an oxidative, regeneration-inhibitory niche that conventional antioxidants cannot meaningfully modulate owing to poor BBB/BSCB penetration, short half-lives, and lack of cellular or subcellular specificity [38]. To convert this hostile milieu into a pro-regenerative state, next-generation antioxidant nanoplatforms must satisfy three design principles: active barrier traversal with lesion-selective accumulation, spatiotemporally controlled and stimuli-responsive activation, and redox-mediated control of cell fate that goes beyond stoichiometric ROS scavenging. Polymeric and subcellular-targeted platforms: eradicating ROS at the source

Encapsulation of hydrophobic antioxidants within clinically tractable polymers—most commonly PLGA [poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid)], an FDA-approved compound with poly (ethylene glycol) (PEG) surface modification as an excipient—is a foundational nanoengineering approach to improve stability, bioavailability and dosing flexibility for translation [39]. To breach the BBB/BSCB, polymeric carriers are frequently equipped with receptor-mediated transcytosis ligands, for example, lactoferrin or transferrin, which engage endothelial receptors and clathrin-mediated endocytosis, increasing brain or spinal cord delivery and lesion-site accumulation [40]. Because endosomal sequestration can limit cytosolic bioavailability, many designs also incorporate pH-responsive or membrane-active elements that promote endosomal escape, preserving payload activity within target cells. Building further, subcellular targeting preemptively blocks mitochondrial dysfunction—the dominant engine of the ROS burst. Conjugation with lipophilic cations such as triphenylphosphonium (TPP) exploits the large, negative inner mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) to enrich carriers within mitochondria, thereby concentrating payloads at the primary site of ROS generation [41,42]. Mitochondria-directed release attenuates mitochondrial ROS (mtROS), preserves ΔΨm and electron transport, and limits cytochrome c efflux with downstream suppression of caspase-dependent apoptosis—an essential first step toward comprehensive microenvironmental reprogramming. In practice, effective constructs balance BBB/BSCB traversal (typically 50–150 nm, near-neutral surface charge) with ΔΨm-dependent uptake, and include chemistry that supports endosomal escape to ensure that antioxidant payloads reach the mitochondrial matrix at therapeutic concentrations.

Multi-catalytic inorganic nanozymes: dismantling macroscopic oxidative stress

A powerful, emerging alternative to small-molecule antioxidants, which are consumed upon reaction with ROS and thus impose stoichiometric limits, are inorganic nanozymes with self-renewing catalytic activity [43]. Cerium oxide (CeO2) and Prussian blue nanozymes (PBzymes) exhibit multi-enzyme-mimetic behavior, including SOD- and catalase-like activities. In CeO2, redox cycling between Ce3+/Ce4+ and oxygen vacancies underpins catalytic turnover; facet and defect engineering can bias activity profiles (e.g., enhancing SOD-like dismutation vs. H2O2 disproportionation), enabling rational tuning of neuroprotective function [44]. Notably, peroxidase/catalase mimicry is context-dependent—ionic strength and pH modulate reaction pathways—so architectures are increasingly optimized to favor catalase-like detoxification in physiological milieus while limiting peroxidase-like reactivity in acidic compartments. These design levers support continuous scavenging of extracellular and intracellular ROS pools without depleting the platform. Beyond chemical detoxification, multi-catalytic nanozymes can de-escalate injury biology. In ischemic models, PBzymes reduce lesion-site ROS burden, secondarily suppressing NF-κ B signaling and apoptotic execution, and alleviating the redox pressure that sustains microglial M1 polarization; the resulting shift toward an M2-skewed, reparative phenotype coincides with improved neurological outcomes in preclinical studies [45]. Conceptually, this indirect, redox-first mechanism—lowering O2•-, H2O2, and related species to blunt inflammatory amplification—anchors nanozymes as actuators of microenvironmental reprogramming rather than mere “ROS sponges”.

Biomimetic and smart MOF hybrids: intelligent bypass and precision control

Hybrid designs that couple biomimetic cloaking with metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have gained traction as an approach for reducing reticuloendothelial/mononuclear phagocyte system (RES/MPS) clearance while navigating stringent biological barriers [46]. Nanoparticles camouflaged with cell membranes or functional vesicles, such as macrophage membranes or mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived exosomes, inherit a complex repertoire of native surface proteins and glycans, improving immune evasion and circulation time while leveraging inflammation-homing interactions within injured brain and spinal cord tissue [47]. Because membrane cloaking alone does not guarantee productive intracellular delivery, many constructs also incorporate pH-responsive or membrane-active features to promote endosomal escape after receptor-mediated uptake, thereby preserving payload and catalytic activity at the intended intracellular site. Smart MOF hybrids—exemplified by zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8)—provide stimuli-responsive control, which serves to mitigate interparticle aggregation and off-target catalysis typical of exposed inorganic cores. In ischemic models, a ZIF-8 shell can sequester the active catalytic core during circulation and then undergo pH-responsive disassembly within acidic endosomes/lysosomes, unmasking multi-catalytic activity preferentially in target cells [48]. This on-site activation reduces nonspecific reactivity and focuses redox modulation within the lesion niche, dampening pro-inflammatory cytokine cascades and supporting NSC survival and differentiation. In practice, effective hybrids balance three levers: biomimetic interfaces for RES/MPS evasion and lesion tropism, MOF gating for spatiotemporal activation, and endosomal-escape chemistry for intracellular bioavailability. Safety-by-design remains essential—shell composition and degradation kinetics (e.g., Zn2+ release from ZIF-8) should be tuned to minimize ion-related cytotoxicity while maintaining on-target efficacy. Orchestrating microenvironmental reprogramming: biological mechanisms of the regenerative shift

Antioxidant nanoplatforms are not mere ROS scavengers; they function as active actuators that reprogram the hostile niche established after acute CNS injury. Through precise spatiotemporal control of oxidative stress and redox signaling, they modulate defined biological nodes—attenuating NF-κB/NLRP3-driven immune activation and enabling microglial M1-to-M2 polarization, preserving mitochondrial membrane potential and electron transport to prevent apoptotic execution, stabilizing NSC fate toward neurogenesis, and maintaining cytoskeletal dynamics to sustain axonal elongation. These coordinated shifts dismantle biochemical and structural barriers, including cytokine amplification, BBB/BSCB instability and CSPG-rich glial scarring, thereby promoting a microenvironmental transition that supports anatomical repair and measurable functional recovery.

M1-to-M2 microglial polarization and resolution of neuroinflammation

Microglia are rapid first responders to CNS injury and major sources of inflammatory mediators. In the acutely oxidized niche, sustained redox stress establishes a feed-forward loop that locks microglia in a neurotoxic, pro-inflammatory M1 state characterized by persistent TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 release. Antioxidant nanoplatforms disrupt this loop by lowering intracellular ROS, thereby suppressing upstream triggers of inflammation, reducing NF-κB nuclear translocation and limiting NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and caspase-1 activation [55]. By attenuating inflammasome-dependent cytokine maturation, these interventions relieve M1 pressure and permit a shift toward a phagocytic, tissue-repairing M2 phenotype that facilitates resolution of neuroinflammation [45]. Cell-free, exosome-based strategies, which avoid the tumorigenic and immunogenic liabilities associated with direct stem-cell transplantation, have gained prominence as modulators of the injury microenvironment. MSC-derived exosomes (MSC exosomes) and nanoplatforms functionalized with MSC membranes or exosomal motifs demonstrate preferential uptake by microglia/macrophages within injured brain and spinal cord regions, where they blunt NF-κB and p38 MAPK signaling and bias microglial polarization toward M2 in TBI models [34]. Consistent effects in SCI include reduced NLRP3 activation and lower pro-inflammatory cytokine output; in addition, exosomal cargo (select miRNAs/proteins) can mitigate A1-skewed reactive astrogliosis and help preserve BSCB integrity [51]. When integrated with precisely engineered antioxidant nanocarriers, this division of labor becomes synergistic: the nanoarchitecture neutralizes acute oxidative stress and concentrates therapy at the lesion, while the exosomal payload delivers sustained biological cues that consolidate M1-to-M2 reprogramming and stabilize the neurovascular interface.

Mitochondrial protection and anti-apoptotic signaling

Mitochondria are highly vulnerable to the oxidative storm that follows acute CNS injury, serving as key mediators of the secondary injury cascade. Excessive ROS accumulation directly disrupts the mitochondrial electron transport chain, leading to a precipitous drop in mitochondrial membrane potential and the irreversible opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). This structural collapse permits the leakage of cytochrome c into the cytosol, which subsequently triggers apoptosome assembly and initiates the execution of the caspase-dependent apoptotic cascade—predominantly via caspase-9 and caspase-3 [56].

Antioxidant nanoplatforms directly intervene in this destructive process by serving as highly efficient, sustained ROS scavengers within the injured neural niche. By internalizing into endangered neurons and restoring redox homeostasis, these advanced nanomaterials actively preserve mitochondrial structural integrity and sustain oxidative phosphorylation. Mechanistically, they shift the balance of Bcl-2 family proteins by upregulating the anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 while downregulating the pro-apoptotic Bax, thereby effectively sealing the outer mitochondrial membrane. Consequently, the downstream activation of effector caspases is robustly inhibited, effectively halting the apoptotic execution of endangered neurons and glial cells in the ischemic or traumatized lesion microenvironment [57].

NSC differentiation and neurogenesis

Endogenous NSCs retain the latent capacity to rebuild damaged circuits, but lineage decisions are exquisitely redox-sensitive. Physiological ROS levels tonically support self-renewal and neurogenesis via PI3K/AKT-dependent signaling, a delicate balance that is disrupted by the pathological ROS surge in injured niches [37]. Excess oxidative pressure engages aberrant redox-sensing mechanisms—including reversible cysteine oxidation/S-glutathionylation of signaling effectors and redox-linked epigenetic shifts—that bias NSCs toward reactive astrogliogenesis at the expense of neuronal differentiation, thereby contributing to scar consolidation [58]. Antioxidant nanoplatforms can function as a redox rheostat, restoring physiological redox tone to re-enable productive developmental signaling. By lowering pathological ROS, these systems stabilize Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT pathways, normalize neuronal differentiation programs, and help prevent the premature depletion of the NSC pool. Beyond redox normalization, advanced designs deliver instructive cues—such as neurogenic small molecules or controlled nitric oxide (NO)—spatiotemporally, creating a synergistic milieu that biases NSCs toward neuronal lineage commitment [59]. This combined strategy—redox modulation plus targeted morphogenic cues—provides a mechanistic route to reconstruction of functional neural circuits and replenishment of lost neuronal populations.

Promotion of axonal regeneration and remyelination

Substantial functional recovery after CNS injury requires not only neuronal survival but also regeneration of severed axons and restoration of myelin. The hostile oxidative microenvironment imposes both physical and chemical barriers to neurite extension: pathological ROS destabilize growth-cone cytoskeletal dynamics, increasing microtubule catastrophe and disrupting bundled tracks needed for fast axonal transport [60]. Targeted redox modulation via advanced nanoplatforms counteracts this degradation, stabilizing actin–microtubule architectures at the growth cone and facilitating extension across CSPG-rich fibrotic glial scars. In parallel, excessive lipid peroxidation jeopardizes myelin-forming oligodendrocytes and oligodendrocyte precursor cells, impairing survival and differentiation. Antioxidant nanoplatforms can intercept this peroxidation cascade and preserve oligodendroglial lineages, supporting remyelination and maturation of myelin architecture. The resulting neurotoxicity causes mitochondrial damage with cytochrome c leakage, culminating in neuronal apoptosis. Furthermore, the hostile niche forces NSCs to aberrantly differentiate into reactive A1 astrocytes, contributing to glial scar formation and severe demyelination. Targeted intervention utilizing antioxidant nanoplatforms effectively scavenges ROS and restores redox homeostasis. This pivotal shift successfully reprograms the niche into a regenerative microenvironment. The restored redox balance promotes M1-to-M2 microglial polarization, characterized by the secretion of anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair factors (IL-10, TGF-β, Arg-1). Concurrently, nanoplatform-mediated ROS depletion ensures mitochondrial protection that supports cell survival, directs NSCs toward functional neurogenesis rather than astrogliosis, and actively facilitates axonal regeneration and remyelination, ultimately driving comprehensive neural repair.

Disease-specific applications of the reprogramming framework

The cellular and molecular reprogramming mechanisms outlined above provide a common blueprint for neural repair, but therapeutic deployment must be tailored to the distinct anatomy and pathophysiology of each acute CNS injury. Here, we map the microenvironment-reprogramming framework onto in vivo models of ischemic stroke, TBI and SCI, aligning design levers—barrier traversal and delivery window, stimuli-responsive activation, organelle targeting and multi-catalytic redox control—with disease-specific constraints such as reperfusion-driven oxidative surges, hemorrhage-associated iron chemistry, BBB/BSCB dynamics, and glial scarring. This structure highlights where platform principles converge across diseases and where they must diverge to achieve meaningful functional benefit.

Ischemic stroke: taming reperfusion injury and BBB disruption

In ischemic stroke, abrupt reperfusion precipitates a surge of ROS that disrupts BBB integrity and accelerates infarct expansion; ferroptotic and apoptotic programs further compound tissue loss [7]. Accordingly, stroke-oriented nanomedicine prioritizes rapid intravascular delivery within the early reperfusion window and targeted suppression of oxidative stress at the lesion. Implementing organelle targeting with vascular guidance, shell-sheddable polymeric micelles co-loaded with resveratrol and dual-functionalized with cyclic RGD peptide (cRGD) and TPP were shown to increase lesion-site accumulation in transient middle cerebral artery occlusion mice. cRGD facilitated transit across the compromised BBB and engagement of αvβ3-positive endothelium in the peri-infarct vasculature, whereas TPP concentrated payloads in microglial mitochondria within the penumbra. This spatiotemporal design reduced mtROS, biased microglia toward M2 polarization, supported BBB repair, and lowered infarct volume in vivo [49].

Addressing both delivery and on-site activation, CeO2@ZIF-8 architectures combine prolonged circulation and enhanced BBB access with pH-responsive shell disassembly in acidic endo/lysosomes. Local unmasking of the multi-catalytic CeO2 core within ischemic tissue was found to constrain lipid peroxidation and apoptotic signaling while dampening astroglial activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine output [48]. In parallel, systemically administered multi-enzyme-mimetic PBzymes curtailed the early reperfusion ROS burst, reducing acute neuronal apoptosis and improving neurological deficit scores in rodent models, consistent with a redox-first intervention strategy in the hyperacute phase [45].

TBI: counteracting hemorrhagic oxidative stress

TBI features microvascular rupture and parenchymal hemorrhage, yielding substantial hemoglobin/heme release and ferrous iron accumulation, which catalyzes Fenton chemistry. The resulting iron-amplified oxidative stress accelerates secondary injury by coupling ROS surges to inflammation and cell-death pathways [4]. Accordingly, TBI-oriented nanoplatforms are designed to neutralize hemorrhagic, iron-driven oxidative pressure and to retain activity within high-ROS foci. Exploiting the localized ROS burst for site-specific retention, ultrasmall (∼3 nm) organic nanozymes have been engineered to undergo ROS-triggered aggregation upon exposure to Fenton-derived •OH and H2O2. Initial sub-5-nm dimensions promote deep parenchymal penetration; subsequent 75–100-fold expansion increases intralesional residence. In rodent TBI models, this stimulus-responsive morphological switch enhanced mitochondrial targeting and produced ∼9-fold higher in situ brain accumulation versus non-aggregating controls, sustaining redox modulation at the lesion site [52]. In a complementary approach leveraging multi-catalytic redox control, chromium-doped ceria nanozyme patches—engineered to elevate the Ce3+ fraction and enhance catalytic turnover—reduced ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), attenuated neuroinflammation and limited histopathology, with associated improvements in spatial memory in Morris water maze tests [50]. Together, these strategies operationalize two design levers for hemorrhagic TBI: ROS-responsive retention to localize therapy within iron-rich niches, and catalytically self-renewing nanozymes to continuously de-escalate oxidative injury biology.

SCI: bridging the cavity and resolving the glial scar

SCI—and the ischemia/reperfusion that often follows severe contusion or compression—presents intertwined anatomical and biochemical barriers. Early oxidative surges disrupt the BSCB, amplify apoptosis and couple with robust neuroinflammation, while later phases feature CSPG-rich fibrotic scarring and fluid-filled cystic cavities that interrupt axonal continuity [6]. Accordingly, SCI-oriented nanoplatforms must pair acute redox control with strategies that stabilize the neurovascular interface and, in more severe lesions, physically bridge tissue gaps and modulate scar architecture.

Leveraging lesion-associated permeability and inflammatory receptor expression, hyaluronic acid-encapsulated curcumin nanoparticles exploit HA–CD44 interactions to achieve enrichment within inflamed spinal tissue. Combined ROS scavenging and suppression of the CD74 signaling hub was shown to bias microglia/macrophages toward M2 polarization, limiting glial scarring and improving motor outcomes in preclinical models [61]. Injectable, self-healing curcumin-loaded hydrogels, designed to address macroscopic cavities and extracellular-matrix disruption, provide a dual lever: a conformal scaffold that maintains tract geometry, and a sustained-release depot that extends local redox modulation, preserving axons and restraining scar consolidation in transection models [53].

Applying intelligent bypass with on-site activation, activatable cell-penetrating peptide (ACPP)-functionalized mesoporous PBzymes deliver rapamycin selectively to matrix metalloproteinase-rich lesions. This dual-action construct couples multi-catalytic ROS scavenging with inhibition of MAPK/AKT/mTOR signaling, reducing glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-positive reactive astrogliosis, constraining physical scar formation, and improving locomotor recovery [62]. Finally, an integrated nanofiber scaffold embedded with CeO2 nanozymes (NS@COP) synchronizes structural guidance with immune–metabolic reprogramming: up-regulation of RAMP1 (receptor activity modifying protein 1) restores CGRP-receptor responsiveness in pro-inflammatory macrophages to favor resolution (M1→M2), while mitochondrial support preserves neuronal energy metabolism. This combined control of immune tone and axonal guidance promoted axon extension across severe lesions and reduced maladaptive scarring [54]. Translational challenges and future perspectives

Despite growing preclinical evidence that antioxidant nanoplatforms can reprogram the oxidative niche and confer neuroprotection across acute CNS injury models, a substantial translational gap persists between rodent studies and human pathology. Closing this gap will require a pivot from efficacy-centric experimentation to rigorously addressing three agendas central to clinical deployment: decoding the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of the BBB/BSCB, defining the long-term biodistribution and immunotoxicity of inorganic and hybrid systems, and establishing Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP)-compatible manufacturing with clinically relevant dosing and quality controls.

Decoding the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of the BBB/BSCB

Most nanoplatforms have been engineered against simplified, quasi-static formulations of blood–brain and blood–spinal cord barriers. In practice, barrier integrity, transporter expression, and paracellular permeability are highly dynamic and vary with injury type, severity, and disease stage. In the hyperacute phase of ischemic stroke or TBI, tight-junction disruption transiently increases passive access; by subacute and chronic stages, inflammatory remodeling, perivascular scarring, and efflux upregulation re-establish a formidable, albeit pathological, barrier. Failure to account for this stage-dependent heterogeneity limits efficacy and reproducibility [63].

Bridging this gap requires a dual strategy. First, embed explicit stage-specific delivery logic: early intravascular dosing synchronized to reperfusion or hemorrhagic windows; later-stage routes that bypass systemic barriers (intranasal, intrathecal) combined with receptor-mediated transcytosis to overcome restored tight junctions [64]. Second, validate transport and uptake in advanced human microphysiological systems that capture astrocyte–endothelial–pericyte crosstalk and polarized aquaporin-4 distribution; 3D BBB-on-a-chip platforms can resolve transcytosis versus paracellular flux and prescreen candidates prior to clinical translation [65]. Incorporating human microphysiological systems (MPS)-derived parameters (permeability, efflux ratios, receptor density) into pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) modeling will help align dosing windows and exposure targets across species.

Long-term fate and immunotoxicity profiling

Multi-catalytic inorganic nanozymes and stimuli-responsive MOFs exhibit strong redox-modulating capacity, but their long-term biosafety remains incompletely defined. For example, advanced oligomeric nanozymes were shown to improve 1-month survival in brain trauma models (50%→90%) [66], but like most reports this study prioritized short-term efficacy over systematic analyses of chronic biodistribution and clearance. Prolonged intracerebral retention of inorganic scaffolds or slow metal-ion release can perturb metal homeostasis, dysregulate microglial signaling, and engage cell-death pathways implicated in delayed neurodegeneration [67]. Moreover, multiscale profiling has flagged certain MOFs (e.g., ZIF-8) for context-dependent pro-inflammatory activity, including IL-6 induction [68].

To mitigate these risks, future strategies should adopt a hierarchical safety-by-design paradigm, prioritizing biodegradable or organo-inorganic hybrids with controllable disassembly, inert surface chemistries that minimize inflammatory protein coronas, and dopant/shell architectures that limit ion leaching while preserving on-target catalysis. In parallel, evaluation should shift from acute tolerability to long-horizon fate and immune profiling—resolving brain-regional persistence, glymphatic/meningeal-lymphatic clearance, microglial/astroglial activation states and systemic RES/MPS sequestration—under clinically relevant dosing and repeat-exposure regimens. Where suitable, biocompatible organic platforms (e.g., hydroxyl-terminated dendrimers co-loaded with anti-inflammatory agents) can reduce inorganic-specific liabilities while enabling precise immunomodulation and support of pro-regenerative niches [69].

Scale-up manufacturing and clinical dosing (GMP standardization)

The same design sophistication that enables precise microenvironmental reprogramming often impedes clinical translation by complicating batch-to-batch reproducibility at scale. Progressing to trials requires a nano-CMC (Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls) framework grounded in quality-by-design, systematically mapping critical quality attributes to critical process parameters. Continuous manufacturing platforms, such as microfluidics or Flash Nano Precipitation, stabilize assembly kinetics, enabling a seamless transition from milligram-to kilogram-scale production without compromising CQAs [70]. GMP execution demands rigorous orthogonal analytics for sizing, surface chemistry, and potency. Furthermore, sterilization remains a critical hurdle; aseptic processing is typically required since standard 0.22-μm filtration can shear nanoparticles, while terminal sterilization (gamma radiation or ethylene oxide) may alter delicate surface chemistries. Determining clinically relevant dosing likewise requires moving beyond empirical milligram-per-kilogram extrapolations. While body-surface-area normalization provides an initial human-equivalent dose, species-specific differences in clearance and protein-corona composition demand exposure-matched scaling. This necessitates rigorous analytical separation of nanocarrier-bound versus free active pharmaceutical ingredients in biological matrices [71]. First-in-human doses should be justified by PK/PD modeling and the minimal anticipated biological effect level, with the maximum recommended starting dose bounded by rigorous immunotoxicology evaluations [72]. Ultimately, aligning robust continuous manufacturability with exposure-based dose translation is essential to convert laboratory-grade nanoplatforms into clinically reliable therapeutics.

Current clinical management and the rationale for nanoplatform intervention

Currently, clinical management of acute CNS injuries relies primarily on surgical intervention and single-target pharmacotherapy [73]. For ischemic stroke, intravenous tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) remains the gold standard; however, its application is restricted by a narrow therapeutic window and the risk of hemorrhagic transformation [74], while fundamentally failing to mitigate the profound ischemia-reperfusion oxidative burst. In spinal cord injury, standard clinical management—including surgical decompression, high-dose methylprednisolone, and subsequent physical rehabilitation—aims to relieve mechanical pressure, mitigate acute inflammation, and restore motor function [75]. Yet, these interventions are insufficient to resolve the self-reinforcing oxidative microenvironment and subsequent glial scar formation [76]. Furthermore, while conventional small-molecule antioxidants have been explored, their clinical translation has been severely hindered by unfavorable pharmacokinetics, including poor BBB and blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB) penetration, short half-lives, and a lack of cellular or subcellular specificity.

These persistent clinical limitations underscore the urgent necessity for advanced nanoplatforms capable of active microenvironmental reprogramming. Unlike conventional modalities that offer passive or stoichiometric ROS scavenging, nanoplatforms serve as active regulators of the injury milieu. They are engineered to actively traverse biological barriers via receptor-mediated transcytosis and accumulate selectively at the lesion site, thereby overcoming traditional pharmacokinetic bottlenecks. Moreover, the integration of multi-catalytic inorganic nanozymes and stimuli-responsive mechanisms allows for precise, spatiotemporally controlled redox modulation, which is crucial for neutralizing the multifactorial oxidative niche at the subcellular level.

Beyond acting as standalone therapeutics, antioxidant nanoplatforms exhibit significant synergistic potential when combined with existing modalities. For example, the co-administration of nanoplatforms with thrombolytics could neutralize the reperfusion-induced ROS burst, potentially preserving BBB integrity and safely extending the tPA therapeutic window [77]. Similarly, by restoring redox homeostasis and alleviating oxidative stress, nanoplatforms can create a permissive, redox-balanced microenvironment that dramatically enhances the survival, engraftment, and directed neurogenic differentiation of transplanted stem cells [78]. Therefore, the transition from conventional single-target therapies to nanoplatform-mediated microenvironment reprogramming represents a critical step forward in achieving meaningful neural repair.

Conclusion

Acute CNS injuries—ischemic stroke, TBI and SCI—converge on a rapidly emerging, highly oxidative microenvironment. This persistent ROS-driven state amplifies secondary damage via mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation, while imposing biochemical and structural barriers that suppress endogenous repair. Accordingly, precise modulation of oxidative stress and its downstream signaling networks is a prerequisite for halting secondary injury and restoring a pro-regenerative niche. A conceptual shift is underway. Beyond the pharmacokinetic and targeting limitations of conventional small-molecule antioxidants, next-generation nanoplatforms function as active regulators of the injury milieu. Through engineered barrier traversal, stimuli-responsive activation, organelle-level precision and multi-catalytic redox control, these systems move past stoichiometric scavenging to enable spatiotemporal reprogramming of cell fate, promoting microglial M1-to-M2 polarization, preserving mitochondrial integrity, preventing apoptotic execution, and biasing NSCs toward neurogenesis while supporting axonal regeneration and remyelination. Across stroke, TBI and SCI models, such microenvironmental control translates to neuroprotection and measurable functional improvement. Translational success will depend on closing the gap between rodent studies and human pathology. Three imperatives stand out: (1) decoding the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of the BBB/BSCB and aligning delivery routes and timing accordingly; (2) defining long-term biodistribution, clearance and immunotoxicity for inorganic and hybrid systems; and (3) implementing GMP-compatible, scalable manufacturing with exposure-based dose translation anchored by body surface area, refined by PK/PD modeling, and bounded by minimal anticipated biological effect level and maximum recommended starting dose. If these bottlenecks are addressed through coordinated materials, neurobiology, and regulatory and clinical efforts, nanoplatform-mediated microenvironment reprogramming can progress from a promising framework to a clinically relevant, disease-modifying strategy for CNS injury.
