Relationship between alexithymia and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in patients with a history of early pregnancy loss (EPL) after frozen-thawed embryo transfer (FET): mediating effects of intolerance of uncertainty (IU)
Authors: Lidan Xu, Ya Ma, Yuying Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe risk factors for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and its relationship with alexithymia and intolerance of uncertainty (IU) remain unclear in patients undergoing frozen-thawed embryo transfer (FET) with a history of early pregnancy loss (EPL). This study aims to investigate the prevalence and contributing factors of PTSD symptoms in FET patients with prior EPL and to examine the associations among IU, alexithymia, and PTSD in this population.MethodsThis study employed a cross-sectional design. A total of 270 participants were recruited through convenience sampling from a gynecology and obstetrics hospital in Zhejiang Province, Mainland China, between June 2024 and January 2025. They were asked to complete a set of questionnaires, comprising the General Information Questionnaire, the Toronto Alexithymia Scale, the Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Checklist-Civilian Version, and the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale. Multiple linear regression was used to identify factors associated with PTSD symptoms, and a mediation analysis was conducted to test the mediating role of IU.ResultsThe mean PTSD score among patients with a history of EPL after FET was (36.89 ± 13.89), with a positive screening rate of 39.02%. PTSD symptoms were positively correlated with both alexithymia (r = 0.469, P < 0.01) and IU (r = 0.662, P < 0.01). Multiple linear regression analysis identified alexithymia and IU as significant factors influencing PTSD symptoms. Furthermore, mediation analysis showed that IU partially mediated the relationship between alexithymia and PTSD, accounting for 61.0% of the total effect.ConclusionThis study identified relatively high alexithymia and PTSD scores among patients with a history of EPL after FET, accompanied by elevated levels of IU. The findings revealed a significant positive correlation between alexithymia and PTSD symptoms, and also confirmed that IU was significantly associated with both. Clinically, these observed correlations suggest that integrating routine screening for PTSD, alexithymia, and IU into clinical practice could provide an evidence-based strategy for improving the management of this population.

Introduction

Early pregnancy loss (EPL), defined as biochemical pregnancy, embryonic arrest, or spontaneous abortion within 12 weeks of gestation, represents the most common adverse pregnancy outcome in in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET) [1, 2]. Although the clinical pregnancy rate of frozen-thawed embryo transfer (FET) has exceeded 50%, [3] the EPL rate remains as high as 70%-80% [4]. This traumatic event not only affects the physical health of infertile women but also leaves them vulnerable to persistent psychological trauma, which exerts a profound impact on subsequent assisted reproductive decisions and pregnancy outcomes [5].

As a key component of assisted reproductive technology (ART), FET offers renewed hope for pregnancy to women with a history of EPL. However, uncertainties in the FET process and fear of recurrent pregnancy failure may further exacerbate the psychological burden of infertile patients. Cheung et al. suggested that EPL can be considered a traumatic event, with some individuals developing post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) following EPL [5]. PTSD is defined as a delayed and long-lasting mental disorder that occurs after exposure to an extreme traumatic or catastrophic event [6]. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5), PTSD comprises four core symptom clusters: intrusion symptoms (re-experiencing the event through flashbacks or nightmares), avoidance (actively avoiding thoughts, people, or places related to the trauma), negative alterations in cognition and mood (persistent negative beliefs or emotional states), and alterations in arousal and reactivity (heightened startle response, irritability, or hypervigilance) [7]. It is one of the most common forms of psychological distress experienced by women with a history of EPL [8].

There is substantial evidence supporting the association between alexithymia and PTSD [9]. As a personality trait, alexithymia refers to difficulties in expressing emotions, feelings, and needs, primarily manifested as difficulty in identifying feelings (DIF), difficulty in describing feelings (DDF), and externally oriented thinking (EOT)—factors that can hinder emotional communication between individuals [10]. Multiple studies have indicated that women with a history of EPL exhibit higher levels of anxiety and depression, and are prone to negative emotions such as fear and self-blame. Their tendency to suppress their emotions and reluctance to actively disclose inner experiences make them susceptible to alexithymia [11–14]. Patients with persistent avoidance of PTSD-related events and unwillingness to elaborate on feelings often develop emotional numbness, leading to the misattribution of symptoms to the traumatic event itself rather than recognizing their post-PTSD emotional expression deficits [15]. Bagby et al. found that alexithymia causes individuals to excessively focus on, amplify, and misinterpret somatic sensations associated with emotional arousal, leading to heightened alertness and thus facilitating PTSD development [16]. However, the psychological mechanisms underlying the relationship between alexithymia and PTSD specifically in women with a history of EPL remain unclear.

Ann et al. indicated that alexithymia predicts anxiety and dysphoria primarily through intolerance of uncertainty (IU), thereby suggesting that IU mediates the relationship between alexithymia and negative affect [17]. Previous studies have found that PTSD is a common psychological problem among women with a history of EPL [18]. The psychodynamics of IU describe it as a cognitive bias in how individuals perceive, interpret, and respond to uncertain situations or events, which in turn influences their cognitive, emotional, and behavioral reactions [19]. Schuette et al. reported that IU is considered a key factor affecting post-traumatic emotional regulation and symptom maintenance. The multiple uncertainties in assisted reproductive treatment (such as embryo implantation outcomes and pregnancy results) may lead to increased IU levels in infertile patients, and high IU levels are closely associated with PTSD symptoms [20]. Furthermore, a study of autistic and non-autistic adults showed a significant positive correlation between alexithymia and IU [21]. Therefore, we hypothesize that IU may mediate the relationship between alexithymia and PTSD in women with a history of EPL after FET.

Multiple studies have found a significant positive correlation between IU and PTSD, indicating that high levels of IU pose specific risks for the development and maintenance of PTSD [22, 23]. From the psychodynamic perspective of IU, individuals are prone to experiencing frustration and depression when facing uncertain situations or events. If they fail to cope proactively, this can lead to excessive worry and anxiety, and even the emergence of adverse post-traumatic psychological symptoms [24]. Currently, there is a paucity of research on the psychological mechanisms underlying the relationship between IU and PTSD in women with a history of EPL after FET. Therefore, this study aims to explore the associations among alexithymia, IU, and PTSD, as well as their potential psychological pathways, in such patients. Specifically, we hypothesize that alexithymia is associated with PTSD in women with a history of EPL after FET, and that IU may act as a mediating factor in the association between alexithymia and PTSD. That is, alexithymia is associated with higher IU, which in turn is associated with the increased severity of PTSD symptoms.

Methods

Design

This study adopted a cross - sectional research design and adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist [25].

Study population

This study was conducted in Zhejiang Province, China, from June 2024 to January 2025. All subjects were recruited from a Class A tertiary obstetrics and gynecology hospital. Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients who underwent FET; (2) Patients with a previous history of EPL;[26, 27] (3) All patients provided informed consent and were willing to cooperate with the survey. Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients with a history of mental illness, intellectual or language impairment, and severe physical diseases; (2) Patients who had experienced other major life traumatic events recently; (3) Patients who were participating in other studies simultaneously. The structural equation model requires that the sample size should be 10–15 times the number of observed variables, and the sample size should be ≥ 200 [28]. This study included 8 observed variables. Considering about 20% invalid questionnaires, the minimum sample size should be 200 cases. Ultimately, 246 cases were included in this study.

Measures

The general information questionnaire

The General Information Questionnaire was designed by the research team of this study. This questionnaire consists of two parts: sociodemographic data and fertility-related information. The sociodemographic data include age, place of residence, educational level, occupation, average monthly per capita family income, marital status, etc. The fertility-related information includes the cause of infertility, type of infertility, and duration of infertility.

The 20-item toronto alexithymia scale

The 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20) was revised by Bagby et al. [29] in 1994 based on the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-26) [30]. In 2003, Yuan Yonggui et al. [31] adapted and translated the scale into Chinese, which was localized in line with the Chinese cultural background. The scale includes three dimensions: DIF, DDF, and EOT, with a total of 20 items. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”, with 1 to 5 points per item. Items 4, 5, 10, 18, and 19 are reverse-scored. The total score ranges from 20 to 100 points. The higher the score, the more severe the alexithymia. Specifically, 20–50 points indicate non-alexithymia, 51–60 points indicate borderline alexithymia, and ≥ 61 points indicate alexithymia. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.858.

PTSD checklist-civilian version

The PTSD Checklist-Civilian Version (PCL-C) was developed by the National Center for PTSD in the United States in November 1996 according to the fourth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) [32]. Yang Xiaoyun translated and adapted the PCL-C into Chinese in 2007 [33]. This checklist is an internationally recognized general-purpose PTSD screening scale with good reliability and validity. The scale is mainly used to report the PTSD symptoms of the research subjects in the past month. It includes three dimensions: re-experience of symptoms from severe traumatic incidents, emotional numbness symptoms, and intense arousal symptoms, with a total of 17 items. Based on the symptom severity, each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, from “not at all” to “extremely”, with 1 to 5 points per item. The total score ranges from 17 to 85 points. A higher score indicates a greater risk of PTSD, and a total score of ≥ 38 points indicates a positive PTSD result. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.965.

Intolerance of uncertainty scale

The Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS-12) was developed by Carleton et al.[34] in 2007 based on the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS-27). It includes two dimensions: IUS-Prospective (7 items) and IUS-Inhibitory (5 items), with a total of 12 items. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, from “not at all” to “completely”, with 1 to 5 points per item. The total score ranges from 12 to 60 points. A higher score is associated with a higher level of intolerance of uncertainty. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.929.

Data collection

The data were collected through an anonymous online survey platform (Wjx.cn; https://www.wjx.cn/) from June 2024 to January 2025. The purpose and significance of the study were explained to the patients, and they were sent a link to the questionnaire. Before the start of the study, the researcher provided each participant with an easy-to-understand electronic informed consent form. The informed consent form was written in plain language, elaborating on the research content in detail, and was explained face-to-face by the researcher before signing to ensure that the participants signed it voluntarily after fully understanding it. In this study, only questionnaires that can truthfully, accurately, and completely reflect the actual situations and thoughts of the participants are regarded as valid questionnaires. We determined its validity by setting some specific rules. In addition, to avoid duplicate data collection, we set up the condition that each internet protocol address could only access the questionnaire once. To improve the number of valid responses, we established the rule that the questionnaire could only be submitted successfully after all questions were answered and that unanswered questions were not allowed. Questionnaires with answering times less than 180 s or more than 25 min were excluded after double-checking. During the recruitment phase, we informed patients that if they participated in the survey, they could receive additional free online consultations and guidance throughout their treatment, which significantly increased patient participation in the study. Ultimately, out of 270 infertile patients, 246 patients submitted valid questionnaires, and the effective response rate was 91.11%. This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Women’s Hospital (No. IRB-20240060-R).

Statistical analysis

In this study, SPSS 29.0 was used for statistical analysis of the data. Descriptive statistical calculations were performed on all variables. All measurement data in this study conformed to a normal distribution. Frequencies, percentages, and mean ± standard deviation were used to describe the general information of EPL patients after FET, as well as the scores of alexithymia, PTSD, and IU. The independent- samples t-test and analysis of variance were employed to statistically analyze the differences in alexithymia among different groups of EPL patients’ general information after FET. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to explore the relationships among alexithymia, PTSD, and IU in EPL patients after FET. We used AMOS 25.0 software to construct a tructural equation model (SEM) to analyze the pathways of interaction among alexithymia, PTSD, and IU in patients with EPL following FET. The maximum likelihood method was employed to modify and fit the model. The significance level of this study was α = 0.05, and a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The mediating effect of IU on the relationship between alexithymia and PTSD in patients with a history of EPL after FET

Based on the results of the correlation analysis, the following hypotheses are proposed: Alexithymia is positively correlated with PTSD; alexithymia is positively correlated with IU; IU is positively correlated with PTSD; and IU may act as a mediating factor influencing the impact of alexithymia on PTSD. A structural equation model was constructed with alexithymia (in patients with a history of EPL after FET) as the independent variable, IU as the mediating variable, and PTSD as the dependent variable, as shown in Fig. 1. All fit indices are within the acceptable range, indicating a good fitting effect and a relatively reasonable model, as shown in Table 5.

Model fit assessment

The results of this study indicated that there was a significant correlation between PTSD and alexithymia (P < 0.05). Following the introduction of IU into the model, the significant correlation between PTSD and alexithymia remained (P < 0.05). As shown in Tables 6 and 7, neither the upper nor the lower limits of the confidence intervals for the direct correlation between PTSD and alexithymia nor the mediating effect of IU included zero. These results suggest that there is a direct correlation between PTSD and alexithymia, and IU may play a partial mediating role in the association between PTSD and alexithymia in patients with a history of EPL after FET. The proportion of the mediating effect relative to the total association accounted for 61.0%.

Discussion

This is the first study to systematically explore the association between alexithymia, IU, and PTSD among Chinese patients with a history of EPL after FET, and to conduct an in-depth analysis of the potential mediating role of IU in this population. Our findings clearly reveal the association pattern between alexithymia and PTSD, as well as the possible mediation pathway of IU in their association. This means that there is an association between alexithymia and PTSD, and this association may form a chain of connections through IU. Specifically, when patients with a history of EPL after FET have a higher degree of alexithymia, their IU levels tend to increase accordingly; patients with higher IU levels may have more prominent PTSD-related symptoms. This population is more sensitive to perceptions of uncertain situations such as treatment outcomes and physical status, and such overreaction to uncertainty may be associated with the severity of PTSD symptoms.

Notably, the mediating effect of IU in the association between alexithymia and PTSD accounts for 61.0% in this study. This significant mediating effect provides solid empirical support for the proposed association model of these three variables. The findings not only enrich theoretical research at the intersection of trauma psychology and reproductive medicine but also offer targeted insights for clinical intervention.

First, our findings revealed a significant positive correlation between alexithymia and PTSD in patients with a history of EPL after FET. This result is consistent with previous research conclusions: the incidence of alexithymia in infertile patients is significantly higher than that in women with normal fertility, and the prevalence of PTSD in these two populations is comparable [35]. Infertile patients undergoing assisted reproductive technologies (e.g., IVF-ET) with higher levels of alexithymia are more likely to adopt avoidant coping strategies (such as emotional suppression), which in turn exacerbates psychological distress [36]. Compared with general infertile patients, patients with a history of EPL after FET—who have experienced physical and psychological trauma from pregnancy loss and accumulated stress from multiple previous invasive procedures—struggle to distinguish between physical changes and emotional experiences triggered by the EPL event. This deficit in emotional recognition and expression (a core feature of alexithymia) leads them to suppress inner feelings, avoid emotional disclosure, remain stuck in superficial cognition of the traumatic event, and lack the ability to perceive and manage their own emotions—ultimately increasing the risk of PTSD. This process conforms to the potential pathway of “emotional suppression-cognitive bias-physiological stress”. Specifically, alexithymia exacerbates PTSD symptoms by inhibiting emotional expression, solidifying traumatic cognition, and activating persistent physiological stress, a pattern that is particularly prominent in infertility-related traumatic scenarios such as EPL.

Second, the psychodynamic theory of IU suggests that IU can act as a strong stressor when individuals face situations involving unknown potential threats [37]. High levels of IU may increase the sense of difficulty and anxiety in expressing emotions and needs, leading individuals to be more inclined to adopt negative coping styles (e.g., concealing negative emotions), thereby exacerbating psychological distress [38, 39]. Our findings demonstrated a significant correlation between IU and PTSD in patients with a history of EPL after FET. On one hand, due to their previous experience of EPL, these patients tend to have a stronger perception of uncertainty regarding situations such as treatment outcomes and physical recovery after FET—a factor that is likely to induce high levels of IU. On the other hand, if patients lack a scientific understanding of EPL, they are prone to interpreting the traumatic event from a negative perspective (e.g., attributing pregnancy loss to their own defects). This will further strengthen their intolerance of uncertainty and contribute to the occurrence and development of PTSD through the accumulation of negative emotions and psychological stress.

Furthermore, this study confirmed that the mediating role of IU between alexithymia and PTSD is statistically significant, suggesting that reducing IU levels may serve as an effective entry point for alleviating PTSD symptoms. This finding is consistent with the psychodynamic theory of IU, which proposes that when individuals face ambiguous and unpredictable situations, the “uncertainty threat” mental schema is activated, thereby triggering defensive strategies such as hypervigilance, repeated confirmation, and avoidance of decision-making [19]. The entire process of assisted reproductive treatment (e.g., embryo implantation success rate, possibility of pregnancy maintenance) is filled with uncertain situations. Such defensive strategies are prone to being overactivated in patients with a history of EPL after FET, ultimately exacerbating PTSD symptoms. Therefore, clinicians can focus on IU as a key mediating target and design intervention programs based on the psychodynamic theory of IU. For instance, cognitive restructuring can help patients adjust their negative perceptions of “uncertainty in treatment efficacy” and “uncertainty in future pregnancy outcomes”; mindfulness-based training can enhance their ability to accept uncertain situations. These approaches aim to reduce patients’ sensitivity to uncertainty, thus providing practical evidence for improving PTSD-related manifestations.

Clinical implications

Several clinical implications should be emphasized in practice. First, during FET treatment and follow-up for prognosis, it is necessary to focus on the psychological state of patients with a history of EPL, especially PTSD-related symptoms. PTSD may affect patients’ quality of life and subsequent pregnancy outcomes through persistent psychological stress. Second, IU is a key mediator in the association between alexithymia and PTSD, and its psychodynamic mechanism (perception of threat from uncertainty) can be improved through cognitive adjustment. Based on the psychodynamic theory of IU, a “cognitive restructuring for uncertainty” module can be designed. For example, case illustrations (such as “clinical data on FET success rates after EPL”) can be used to correct patients’ catastrophic cognition of “unknown outcomes”; “small goal setting” (such as “focusing first on blood test results 14 days after embryo transfer rather than directly worrying about pregnancy outcomes”) can reduce excessive attention to long-term uncertainty. Finally, clinicians can design “simplified trauma narrative” exercises. For instance, replacing catastrophic memories with statements like “This was a pregnancy loss, not a denial of my ‘fertility’”, and strengthening the cognition that “the current FET treatment is a new opportunity”, can reduce the interference of traumatic memories on the current treatment, which is expected to have positive significance for alleviating PTSD manifestations.

Limitation of the study

Nevertheless, the limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, participants were recruited from a provincial-level obstetrics and gynecology hospital using convenience sampling, which may have introduced selection bias. In the future, random sampling can be adopted, and multi-center, large-sample surveys could be conducted across different geographical regions to enhance generalizability. Second, the cross-sectional study design limits the ability to infer causal relationships. Additionally, PTSD symptoms in patients with a history of EPL after FET may change over time. Therefore, future cohort studies could track patients starting from the day of EPL diagnosis, through various follow-up time spoints, and even during subsequent FET cycles to monitor changes in PTSD levels and their association with pregnancy outcomes. Furthermore, future research could include experimental studies—for example, exploring active and effective intervention strategies to reduce PTSD severity in this population. It is worth mentioning that we explicitly acknowledge the potential gap between the PCL-C (DSM-IV-aligned) and DSM-5, and we propose that future studies in this population adopt the PCL-5, once more robust validation data for the PCL-5 in Chinese reproductive trauma patients become available, to fully align with the latest diagnostic criteria. Finally, the data in this study were collected via patient self-reports, which are inherently subjective. Therefore, a more objective outcome measurement method still needs to be explored. While interventions targeting these factors could be explored, it is essential to emphasize that our retrospective data do not support causal inferences. Thus, such approaches should therefore be interpreted as preliminary strategies pending prospective validation to clarify their potential for alleviating PTSD and improving quality of life.

Conclusions

This study found that both alexithymia and IU are significantly positively correlated with PTSD. Meanwhile, IU may play a mediating role between alexithymia and PTSD in Patients with a history of EPL after FET. Therefore, both alexithymia and IU play a crucial role in influencing PTSD in these patients. Hence, clinical medical staff, spouses, and the entire society should attach importance to the assessment of alexithymia, IU, and PTSD in these patients. Through multiple channels and resources, scientific and effective interventions should be provided to these patients in a timely and appropriate manner to reduce the level of PTSD.
