Prevalence and comorbidity of mental health concerns, alcohol and drug use among young women in the urban slums of Kampala, Uganda: findings from the TOPOWA study
Authors: Monica H Swahn, Jane Palmier, Charles Natuhamya, Kate Mobley, Kevin B Gittner, Matthew Lyons, Godfrey S Bbosa, Gideon Matovu, Frank Mubiru, Anna Kavuma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYoung women in Kampala’s urban slums face a growing public health crisis as mental health issues and substance use escalate, fueled by poverty, violence, and limited access to care. These overlapping challenges significantly heighten the risks of suicide, premature mortality, and diminished quality of life. This study investigates the prevalence of mental health concerns, substance use, and their co-occurrence among this vulnerable population in Kampala, Uganda.MethodsData were drawn from the ongoing TOPOWA study, involving 300 women recruited via convenience sampling in 2023 from three study sites (i.e., Banda, Bwaise, and Makindye). Baseline assessments examined the prevalence and associations between mental health outcomes (i.e., anxiety, depression, suicidality), substance use (i.e., alcohol, drugs), and self-rated mental health (SRMH).ResultsDepression (57.7%) was the most prevalent mental health condition, followed by suicidality (46.0%) and anxiety (34.7%). The prevalence of alcohol and drug use were 27.7% and 10.7%, respectively. More than two-fifths (45.7%) reported multiple mental health conditions, which increased the likelihood of substance use. Low SRMH was significantly higher among women with anxiety, depression, suicidality, and drug use, although alcohol use alone was not significantly associated with low SRMH.ConclusionMental health concerns and comorbidity were commonly noted among the young women and were strongly associated with substance use. These findings highlight the need for integrated mental health interventions to address the complex challenges faced by young women in Kampala’s slums, and especially the impact of comorbidity on mental health satisfaction.Keywords: Mental health, Anxiety, Suicidality, Substance use, Depression, Comorbidity

Introduction

The prevalence and comorbidity of mental health issues, alcohol, and drug use among young women in Kampala’s urban slums is a critical public health concern due to their unique challenges and unmet needs [1, 2]. Socioeconomic factors such as poverty, limited education, food scarcity, community violence, and restricted healthcare access contribute to comorbid mental health issues and substance use [3–6]. Comorbidity, defined as two or more co-occurring conditions [7–9], is often overlooked despite being crucial in predicting adverse outcomes [10].

Mental health comorbidity significantly increases the risk of suicidality [11] and surpasses the risk posed by distinct depressive disorders [12]. Comorbidity between mental health conditions and substance use reflects a bidirectional relationship: individuals with mental illness are more likely to use substances, and vice versa [13–16]. Adolescents with substance use disorders often have co-occurring psychiatric disorders, but psychiatric conditions can also precede substance use [17]. However, research on adolescent mental health comorbidity in low-resource settings remains limited and inconclusive, highlighting a need for further study [18].

Living in slum areas exacerbates women’s mental health issues due to conditions such as overcrowding and food insecurity [19]. Household food insecurity is particularly linked to mental health problems among teenage girls in urban slums, emphasizing the interplay between socio-economic factors and well-being [20]. Systematic reviews show that household income and socio-economic status strongly influence mental health in slum communities [19]. Additionally, economic pressures and material deprivation drive transactional sex among young people, further compounded by challenges such as unwanted pregnancies [21–23].

This study also addressed participants’ self-rated mental health (SRMH), and the association between mental health conditions and satisfaction with one’s mental health. SRMH correlates strongly with multi-item mental health scales and predicts outcomes such as service utilization, depressive symptoms, and suicidality [24, 25]. SRMH is a validated single-item measure to explore its association with other mental health concerns among young women in Kampala’s urban slums, offering a rapid and contextually relevant indicator of psychological distress in low-resource settings. In this study, we use SRMH to explore associations with other mental health concerns and comorbidities in order to evaluate its utility as an indicator in this context. This approach enables a better understanding of mental health disparities and supports the development of contextually relevant, gender-sensitive public health interventions. We have previously assessed self-rated physical health and related disparities among youth in Uganda [26].

The importance of psychiatric epidemiology in understanding mental illness and scalable treatments for young people in Kampala has grown, as reflected in Uganda’s 2017 Child and Adolescent Mental Health Policy Guidelines. However, there is still a need for studies on co-occurring mental health issues with alcohol and drug use in this population [27, 28]. Addressing mental health and substance use among young women in Kampala’s urban slums requires understanding the social, economic, and environmental factors driving these issues. The TOPOWA cohort study aims to generate critical insights into the mental health challenges of young women living in poverty. TOPOWA, meaning “to not give up” in Luganda, examines the social drivers and pathways of mental illness in this population. By addressing these intersecting challenges, tailored interventions can better support the mental health and well-being of young women in urban slums.

This study examines the baseline prevalence and comorbidity of mental health concerns, alcohol, and drug use among young women enrolled in the TOPOWA study, alongside their association with SRMH. The analysis addresses three key questions: (1) What is the prevalence and severity of self-reported mental health concerns, alcohol, and drug use? (2) What is the prevalence of comorbidity among these conditions? (3) How are mental health concerns, alcohol, and drug use associated with SRMH? Findings will address critical gaps, guiding future research and informing mental health outreach, substance use recovery, and dual diagnosis treatment in low-resource settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

Between July and November 2023, 300 young women aged 18–24 were enrolled in a prospective observational cohort study across three Kampala neighborhoods (Banda, Bwaise, and Makindye) to examine how a Socio-economic Strengthening Targeted Training (SeSTT) program moderates the impact of poverty on mental health outcomes. Details of the study have been reported previously [29, 30]. Participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling approach, facilitated by Uganda Youth Development Link (UYDEL) staff and community leaders who identified eligible young women residing within 2 km of the study sites and had at least a primary five education level. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, severe mental illness or substance use requiring hospitalization, and significant intellectual disability. Women who knew they were pregnant at baseline data collection were excluded because one of the components of the TOPOWA study is an analysis of salivary biomarkers, which are impacted by pregnancy. The target was 300 women: 150 in the SeSTT cohort and 150 in a community comparison cohort, each with 50 participants from the three neighborhoods. Cohorts were matched by education, income, and number of children. Of 495 women screened, 137 were ineligible, 58 declined, and 300 were enrolled. The SeSTT cohort was recruited first, and recruitment continued until 150 women were enrolled in the cohort. The community comparison cohort was recruited second and recruitment continued until 150 women were enrolled. Through the community leaders, potential participants (young women) were approached by UYDEL site staff, briefed about the study, and then asked if they are willing to take part in the study. Willing participants were then screened for eligibility, provided detailed information about the study, and enrolled in the study if they consented to participant. The SeSTT cohort received vocational training and psychosocial support over three months, while the comparison cohort did not. The psychosocial support provided to the SeSTT cohort included programs aimed at equipping the young women with skills in decision making, problem solving, coping with stress, emotional regulation, self-awareness, and crisis response and coping. One-on-one counseling sessions with social workers were also provided to the SeSTT cohort. Both cohorts received remuneration for their time and received meals and snacks throughout their time at the research hub. Additionally, because women were asked sensitive questions about their mental health including questions about suicidal ideation, all participants were provided with a list of community resources for no-cost mental health services. If a participant exhibited imminent risk of harming themselves, then a mental health professional was contacted and the participant kept in a safe location until the mental health professional arrived. For these baseline analyses, the groups were combined as the intervention took place post baseline assessment.

Data collection procedure

As part of the TOPOWA cohort study baseline assessment, participants completed a research assistant-administered survey covering demographics, psychosocial characteristics, and life experiences. To minimize contamination, the baseline data was collected from the community participants first, followed by the intervention participants before the SeSTT program. The survey was comprised of multiple previously published questionnaires. The previously published questionnaires used in this study included the Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7), a 7 item tool for measuring generalized anxiety, the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) for measuring depression, the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) for measuring suicide risk, the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) for assessing alcohol use, and the Drug Abuse Screening Test (DAST-10) for assessing drug use [31–37]. The study followed the Ethical Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by Kennesaw State University, Makerere University School of Health Sciences (MAKSHSREC-2023-532), and the Uganda National Council of Science and Technology (HS2959ES). Participants provided written informed consent and received remuneration for their participation.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

The survey collected information that included age, education level, the living status of participants’ parents, and whether the participant was a parent, among others.

Anxiety

The GAD-7 [31], a 7-item tool for assessing generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), was used to measure anxiety severity. Items included symptoms like “feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge” and “feeling afraid something awful might happen.” Participants reported symptom frequency over the past two weeks with responses scored as 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). The sum score (0–21) indicated anxiety severity: none/minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and severe (15+) [32]. The sum score was calculated according to the recommendations of the GAD-7 authors and previous studies that have used the questionnaire. Anxiety was categorized as no significant anxiety (0–9) or significant anxiety (10–21) [31]. The tool showed moderate internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.77). This measure for assessing generalized anxiety has been validated in a similar population in Uganda to measure anxiety among people with HIV/AIDS, and >10 was also used as the threshold for significant anxiety [38].

Depression

The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), an instrument that is broadly used to assess depression and its severity, was used to measure depressive symptoms [33]. The participants were asked how often in the past 2 weeks they had been bothered by nine problems, including: “feeling down, depressed, or hopeless” and “poor appetite or overeating.” Responses were coded 0 (not at all), 1 (several days), 2 (more than half the days), and 3 (nearly every day). The PHQ-9 sum score ranges from 0 to 27, and a higher score indicates more severe depressive symptoms. In this study, a sum score of 8 or greater suggested significant depression. To measure its severity, depression was categorized as minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27) [33, 34]. The scale’s internal consistency was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.79). The PHQ-9 has previously been validated in Uganda for detecting major depressive disorder [39].

Suicidal ideation

The Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) [35] was used to assess suicidality over the past year. Four questions from the suicide ideation section were analyzed, which asked about wishing to be dead, thoughts of self-harm, planning methods, and intent to act on a suicide plan. Respondents were categorized as having no suicide ideation (no agreement), minimal/moderate ideation (agreement with any of the first three items), or severe ideation (agreement with the fourth item) [35]. The tool showed high internal consistency for these items (Kuder-Richardson coefficient = 0.88). The C-SSRS has been validated in the neighboring country of Tanzania [40] and has been previously used among adolescents in Uganda to measure suicide risk [41].

Mental health comorbidity

A binary outcome variable “mental health comorbidity” was generated with two levels, 0 (less than two mental health conditions) and 1 (two or more co-occurring mental health conditions) to measure mental health comorbidity. A binary variable was used because it increases the stability of the model coefficient during statistical analysis. The thresholds to meet for each condition were a GAD-7 sum score ≥ 10 for anxiety, a PHQ-7 sum score ≥ 8 for depression, and a suicidal ideation classification of minimal/moderate or severe ideation.

Self-rated mental health

Self-Rated Mental Health was measured by a single question: “Overall, how satisfied are you with your mental health?” Responses to the question ranged from “very satisfied” to “very dissatisfied” on a 5-point Likert scale [24].

Alcohol use

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) [36], a 10-item screening tool developed by the World Health Organization (WHO), was used to assess alcohol use and alcohol-related problems. The questions assessed alcohol consumption in the past year, e.g. “How often do you have a drink containing alcohol?” The total score ranged from 0 to 40 with higher scores indicating higher severity of alcohol use. Alcohol use was categorized as no risk (0), low risk (1–7), hazardous (8–15), harmful (16–19), and dependence (20–40) [42]. The scale’s internal consistency was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.90). The AUDIT tool has been used to study alcohol use among adolescents in Uganda in a previous study, and the same cut-off values were used [43].

Drug use

The Drug Abuse Screening Test (DAST-10), a 10-item brief screening tool for drug use, excluding alcohol or tobacco use, originally a 28-item drug abuse screening test [37] was used in this study. The questions required information during the past 3 months, including “Have you used drugs other than those required for medical reasons?” and “Have you neglected your family because of your use of drugs?” Responses to these questions were scored as either 0 (no) or 1 (yes). The third item that asked, “Are you always able to stop using drugs when you want to?” was reverse coded to 1 (no) and 0 (yes). Endorsement of any of the ten items signified some level of drug use. Further, drug use severity was categorized as no problem (0), low (1–2), moderate (3–5), substantial (6–8), and severe (9–10) [44]. The internal consistency was acceptable (Kuder-Richardson coefficient = 0.74). The DAST-10 has been used in a recent study to measure substance use among adolescents in Uganda [45].

Co-occurrence of mental health conditions and alcohol/drug use

Mental health condition and any alcohol or drug use co-occurrence was defined as having any mental health condition and any use of alcohol or drugs simultaneously. The thresholds to meet for each mental health condition were a GAD-7 sum score ≥ 10 for anxiety, a PHQ-9 sum score ≥ 8 for depression, and a minimal/moderate or severe ideation classification for suicide. The threshold for alcohol use was AUDIT total score > 0 and the threshold for drug use was DAST-10 sum score > 0. Cut-off values of 0 were chosen because we were concerned with the co-occurrence of any alcohol and/or drug use and mental health concerns. Co-occurrence of one or more mental health conditions and alcohol use only, drug use only, and both alcohol and drug use together were examined.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and analyses were performed using Stata 15 (StataCorp, TX, USA). Ordinal logistic regression models, suitable for the ordinal outcome variable [46] were used instead of multinomial logistic models [47] to assess associations between mental health conditions, alcohol and drug use, and SRMH. The dependent variable was SRMH. Independent variables include anxiety (M1), depression (M2), suicidality (M3), alcohol use (M4), drug use (M5), comorbid mental health conditions (M6), and combinations of mental health conditions with alcohol and/or drug use (M7-M10). Anxiety (GAD-7 score ≥ 10), depression (PHQ-9 score ≥ 8), suicidality (any ideation), any alcohol use (AUDIT score >0), and any drug use (DAST-10 score >0) were defined for models. The proportional odds assumption was met in all models (p >0.05).

Participant characteristics

Of the 300 young women enrolled in the study, more than half of the women (66.0%) had attained at least some secondary education and 62.0% had at least one child. Most women had both of their parents alive (65.0%) and lived in households of two generations (51.3%) (Table 1) The details of these results were previously presented [30]. There were no statistically significant differences in demographic characteristics between the intervention and control cohorts at the baseline assessment.

Prevalence and severity of mental health outcomes

The most prevalent mental health condition was depression (57.7%) followed by suicidality (46.0%), which included any indication of suicidal ideation. Suicidality was followed by anxiety (34.7%) while the prevalence of alcohol use and drug use was 27.7% and 10.7% respectively.

Comorbidity of mental health conditions and co-occurrence with alcohol or drug use

The prevalence of any mental health condition was 74.0% and mental health comorbidity was 45.7% (Fig. 1). More than 2 in every 5 women had 2 or more of the studied mental health outcomes. The proportion of women with any alcohol use, any drug use, or both alcohol and drug use was higher among women with more mental health conditions (Fig. 1).

The most common co-occurring mental health and substance use outcome was depression and alcohol use, reported by 51 women (17.0%). Suicidality and alcohol use co-occurred among 46 women (15.3%). Overall, the prevalence of alcohol or drug use increased with the number of mental health conditions reported. Association of mental health conditions, alcohol and drug use with SRMH

The Brant test for the parallel regression assumption was not significant in all models, confirming the proportional odds assumption. The ordinal Hosmer–Lemeshow and Lipsitz goodness-of-fit tests showed all ordered logistic regression models fit the data well (p > 0.05). Among demographic variables, only the number of generations in the household was significantly associated with SRMH.

Findings in Table 4 indicate that the likelihood of reporting low SRMH was higher among women with anxiety (Odds ratio, OR = 1.73; 95% CI, 1.08–2.77), depression (OR = 2.53; 95% CI, 1.58–4.05), suicidality (OR = 2.88; 95% CI, 1.80–4.60) and among women who use drugs (OR = 2.69; 95% CI, 1.32–5.50) compared to those without the respective condition. Alcohol use was not significantly associated with SRMH.

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence and comorbidity of mental health concerns during the TOPOWA cohort study baseline assessment of young women in Kampala’s urban slums and their association with SRMH. Results reveal significant unmet mental health needs, with 74.0% reporting any anxiety, depression, or suicidality. This prevalence is notably higher than the Ugandan Ministry of Health’s estimate that 32% of the population experiences mental illness [48]. However, the Ministry’s figure may underestimate mental illness due to stigma [48], while our findings highlight the unique challenges faced by impoverished young women. People living in urban slums are also at higher risk for both physical and mental health concerns [49], so it is possible that the population in this study has a much higher prevalence of mental health concerns than the general Ugandan population.

In this study, depression was the most prevalent mental health condition, affecting 57.7% of participants, followed by suicidality (46.0%) and anxiety (34.7%). This prevalence of suicidality is higher than previous reports. Research in Kampala found that 23.5% of participants experienced suicidal ideation in the past year, and another study reported just under 25% [50, 51]. Among Kenyan high school students, suicidal ideation ranged from 25.6% to 26.8%, while 32.5% of Ugandan schoolgirls reported suicidal ideation [52–54]. In Lesotho, self-harm and suicidality among girls and young women was lower at 9% [55]. A Kenyan study found higher prevalence ofsuicidality among slum residents than non-slum residents, highlighting the heightened risk for slum communities, and potentially explaining the higher rate of suicidality found among women enrolled in the TOPOWA study [56].

The levels of depression (57.7%) and anxiety (34.7%) in our study align with previous findings. For instance, Abbo and colleagues [57] reported a 29.7% prevalence of anxiety among female adolescents in North-Eastern Uganda. Past-year alcohol use in our study (27.7%) mirrors prior research indicating 30% among girls aged 12–18 in Kampala [58]. However, drug use in the past three months (10.7%) was lower than the 17.5% reported by Kalungi and colleagues [59] and the 35.1% lifetime use reported by Mayanja and colleagues [59], likely because these studies focused on high-risk adolescents.

Mental health comorbidity, present in 45.7% of participants, was higher than the 4.1% reported by Abbo and colleagues [57] in rural Uganda and the 29.7% found in a Kenyan primary care study [56]. These findings highlight severe unmet mental health needs and significant comorbidity among the women in our study, signaling the need for comprehensive, multifaceted interventions. The intersection of mental health concerns, alcohol, and drug use underscores the importance of integrated care approaches and enhancing mental health training within the healthcare system.

The findings also reveal a strong link between the number of mental health concerns, including alcohol and drug use, and lower SRMH. Young women with two mental health conditions were twice as likely to report low satisfaction compared to those with fewer issues. This issue is also intertwined with life satisfaction as examined in a study in Germany which used the Satisfaction with Life Scale and found that psychiatric comorbidity was linked to lower life satisfaction than having one mental illness [60]. A systematic review by Sadowska and colleagues [61] also found mental health comorbidity among individuals with serious illnesses associated with poorer quality of life. Given the high comorbidity in this population, researchers should assess the utility of SRMH as a potential screening tool and consider assessing multiple mental health outcomes to better understand their combined impact on SRMH.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the TOPOWA baseline assessment is cross-sectional, preventing causal inferences or time-sequencing of mental health concerns. Second, the data are self-reported via an interviewer-administered survey, which may lead to underreporting due to social desirability bias or discomfort disclosing sensitive information. Third, the classification of mental health concerns relied on specific cut points, with no universally established standards for comorbidity in the literature. Fourth, the sample was limited to women from three areas of Kampala, and while findings may be generalizable to similar urban slum settings, this was not tested. Moreover, because the study relied on a convenience sample, the findings may not be generalizable to all young women living in Kampala’s urban slums. Selection bias is possible if women who chose to participate differed systematically from those who declined. Fifth multiple testing adjustments were not applied across the 10 multivariable models. Finally, inclusion and exclusion criteria may have omitted more disadvantaged women, potentially underestimating the true prevalence of mental health concerns.

Addressing mental health interventions in Uganda requires understanding the mechanisms contributing to mental health, alcohol, and drug use. Evidence highlights the vulnerability of youth in urban slums to health issues, including sexual and reproductive health risks [6, 62] and HIV [63], which elevate mental health risks [64]. Poor neighborhood conditions and violence further exacerbate mental health problems and substance use [64, 65] and speaks to the importance of factoring in the local realities and context [66]. Intimate partner violence, poverty, and overcrowding amplify mental health concerns for young women [4, 67]– [68], while social support and violence shape outcomes for displaced youth [69]. Improved access to mental health care must address these intersecting social drivers [69] and will be a key priority for the TOPOWA cohort study.

Uganda has made strides in mental health policy, including decentralization and integrating mental health services into Primary Health Care (PHC) [70]. However, significant treatment gaps remain for young people and also for age-appropriate services [71]. The World Health Organization’s Assessment Instrument for Mental Health Systems (WHO-AIMS) has been used to evaluate Uganda’s mental health system [70]. Despite capacity challenges, interventions like economic empowerment for AIDS-orphaned adolescents [72] and group interpersonal psychotherapy for depression in war-affected populations [73] show promise. Scalable, community-based programs are urgently needed to address widespread mental health issues. Research by our team highlights the potential of mHealth and technological innovations to improve health care and reach [74]. Programs like Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) have also shown success in connecting youth to care [75]. Future efforts should prioritize low-cost, scalable programs focusing on resilience, access to care, and community support for Ugandan youth, particularly young women in Kampala.

Conclusion

The next steps for the TOPOWA cohort study will assess the impact of a community-based vocational and women’s empowerment intervention on mental health trajectories. While vocational training is commonly provided in Uganda, its effects on mental health remain underexplored [76, 77]. Since such programs address key social drivers of mental illness, combining them with life skills and entrepreneurship training may improve wellbeing [78–81]. Prior TOPOWA findings highlight resilience as protective against depression and suicidal ideation [82], and qualitative work underscores how social and environmental inequities shape women’s health risks and opportunities for intervention [83]. Integrating social determinants of mental health into future analyses will advance understanding of contextual drivers of wellbeing and support the design of effective, sustainable interventions [84]. Continued analyses and data collection can further guide prevention strategies and address unmet mental health needs among Ugandan women, emphasizing the high prevalence of comorbidities often overlooked in prior research.
