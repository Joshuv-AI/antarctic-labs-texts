Multimorbidity in Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Sigamani Alben, Calambur Narasimhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

For decades, the care of atrial fibrillation (AF) turned on a single question at first contact: Is the patient anticoagulated? The priority was justified, as AF carries an excess risk of ischaemic stroke and systemic embolism, and oral anticoagulation remains the one intervention with an unambiguous benefit. But oral anticoagulants (OACs) carry the risk of bleeding, which is higher among Asian patients. The typical AF patient today rarely presents with an isolated arrhythmia. As populations age, AF arrives bundled with multimorbidity, frailty, and polypharmacy. Guidelines have responded by recasting AF as a systemic condition: frameworks such as the AF Better Care (ABC) pathway now place comorbidity and cardiovascular risk-factor management alongside stroke prevention and symptom control.

What these frameworks, the 2023 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/American College of Clinical Pharmacy/Heart Rhythm Society guideline, and the 2024 European Society of Cardiology AF-CARE pathway share is an untested assumption worth challenging: that a single baseline clinic visit predicts the patient's long-term risk. Risk in AF is not static; it accrues with age and incident disease. In this issue of JACC: Asia, Pumprueg et al1 quantify this using the prospective, nationwide COOL-AF (Cohort of Antithrombotic Use and Optimal INR Level in Patients With Nonvalvular Atrial Fibrillation in Thailand) registry, reframing comorbidity from a fixed label into a moving trajectory.

Among 3,096 Thai patients with nonvalvular AF followed for 3 years, 1 in 5 (20.9%) crossed into a higher multimorbidity stratum within a single year, and 37.3% worsened over 3 years.1 This progression was not benign. After adjustment, the rapid accrual of new conditions carried a 39% higher risk of the composite of death, heart failure, major bleeding, and stroke or systemic embolism (HR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.13-1.71)—driven almost entirely by death (HR: 1.42) and heart failure (HR: 1.85), with stroke and bleeding essentially unchanged. Chronic kidney disease, anemia, and heart failure were the dominant drivers—the downstream effect of poorly controlled hypertension and metabolic disease, while incident peripheral arterial disease carried the highest single hazard (HR: 2.02, 95% CI: 1.20-3.41).

Two features deserve a harder look. First, the ABC pathway roughly halved the risk in the worsening group (HR: 0.49) despite a strikingly thin definition of risk-factor control: Adherence required only that a guideline-concordant drug class was prescribed, not that blood pressure, glucose, or lipids were actually controlled. If prescription-deep management helps this much, achieving true physiological targets should plausibly help more—a hypothesis that dedicated, target-based trials must now confirm. The future of risk-factor optimization lies in measuring control, not cataloguing prescriptions.

Second, even within a monitored registry, adherence to the holistic pathway sat at only 43% to 45% across the 3 years. This is the central paradox of contemporary AF management: Robust evidence for an integrated pathway exists but is seldom followed. A registry can show that comprehensive comorbidity care works; it cannot, by itself, make it happen.

The implications for practice follow directly. Clinicians should restratify risk at every encounter rather than anchoring to the day of diagnosis, treating renal function, hemoglobin, and blood pressure as dynamic trends and managing them to verified targets rather than assumed control. Because more than half of deaths in Asian AF patients are noncardiovascular, the cardiologist cannot remain the sole manager: Integrated care needs a designated coordinator linking cardiology with primary care, nephrology, endocrinology, and pharmacy, and, in resource-limited settings, task-sharing with nurses and pharmacists supported by digital tools. The region also needs its own evidence to define what “good control” means for its populations.

The message from COOL-AF is both sobering and actionable. Effective anticoagulation is the entry fee, not the total bill. A patient's long-term prognosis is written by the slow, unyielding accrual of systemic comorbidity, and an active, integrated, multidisciplinary care pathway remains our best antidote, offering its greatest protection to precisely those patients whose burden is actively worsening.
