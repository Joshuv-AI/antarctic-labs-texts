Characteristics of extracellular vesicle-derived lncRNAs during the progression of HBV-related hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yanan Ma, Cheng Lou, Jing liang, Chunyue Guo, Jinjuan Zhang, Chengjun Lu, Jiandong Zhang, Yingtang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Liver diseases, including hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), represent significant global health challenges with limited non-invasive biomarkers for early detection. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), enriched with disease-specific RNAs, offer a promising avenue for biomarker discovery. This study characterized EV-derived long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) across various stages of liver disease and explored their mechanistic roles. Serum EVs were isolated from 24 participants (5 healthy controls, 5 chronic hepatitis B patients, 5 liver cirrhosis patients, 4 hepatocellular adenoma patients, and 5 HCC patients) using ultracentrifugation and validated by transmission electron microscopy, nanoparticle tracking analysis, and Western blot. High-throughput transcriptome sequencing systematically analyzed RNA expression profiles in EVs across clinical stages, identifying 133 significantly differentially expressed lncRNAs in the HCC group. Multi-step screening and time-series analysis revealed 10 core lncRNAs associated with HCC progression, and a lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory network (62 nodes, 68 edges) was constructed. Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated involvement in cell proliferation regulation, transmembrane ion transport, cytosol/plasma membrane localization, protein binding, and autophagy/MAPK pathways, while PPI network analysis identified 10 hub genes (e.g., NTRK2, KCNJ10). Validation in an independent plasma cohort confirmed consistent expression patterns of core lncRNAs and downstream genes. The identified HCC-specific lncRNA biomarkers and regulatory network provide theoretical support for developing novel non-invasive liquid biopsy approaches, holding significant promise for early diagnosis and clinical management of HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15237-y.

Introduction

Liver cancer poses a significant threat to global public health security, presenting substantial challenges in clinical prevention and management. According to the latest World Health Organization statistics, approximately 760,000 deaths annually are attributed to liver cancer worldwide [1]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), accounting for 75–85% of primary liver cancer cases, represents the predominant pathological type [2]. Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection serves as the primary etiological factor for HCC development [3, 4]. Studies have demonstrated that chronic HBV carriers face a 15–20 times higher risk of developing HCC compared to non-infected populations [5]. Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) serves as the leading cause of HCC. It can progress to HCC via liver cirrhosis (LC), or may directly lead to HCC through malignant transformation of infected hepatocytes even in the absence of significant fibrosis or cirrhosis [6, 7]. Hepatic adenoma (HA) is a benign tumor but possesses malignant potential; The radiological similarity between small HA lesions and HCC leads to diagnostic challenges [8]. Although surgical resection remains the preferred curative approach to achieve long-term survival in HCC patients, approximately 80% of cases are already diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages, missing the optimal window for definitive treatment [9]. Therefore, early identification of high-risk populations and timely detection of HCC in CHB patients constitute critical priorities for improving clinical outcomes and long-term survival.

While histopathological examination remains the gold standard for HCC diagnosis, its invasive procedures pose inherent risks of hemorrhage and tissue damage [10]. Conventional imaging modalities are constrained by insufficient diagnostic accuracy due to suboptimal sensitivity and specificity. Although alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) is the most widely adopted serological biomarker in clinical practice, its sensitivity is significantly reduced in early-stage HCC detection, limiting diagnostic utility [11]. These critical limitations underscore the urgent demand for developing innovative non-invasive biomarkers to enhance diagnostic precision and improve clinical outcomes in early HCC management. Over the past decade, extensive research on liquid biopsy technology has provided a minimally invasive means for precision personalized medicine [12]. Recently, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as a “rising star” in the field of liquid biopsy, with EV-based liquid biopsies better reflecting the dynamic changes of diseases and becoming a developmental direction for future molecular diagnostics [13]. Molecular evaluation of these circulating biomarkers can complement or even replace tissue biopsies. Due to this research, liquid biopsy has been introduced into clinical practice, particularly in the fields of oncology, prenatal screening, and transplantation. Here, we review the biological characteristics, methodological approaches, and clinical applications of the main biomarkers involved in liquid biopsy.

Transcriptome sequencing and corresponding big data mining methods, which can detect the expression status of genes in batches, have rapidly developed and become widely popular research strategies for studying cell phenotype and function [14]. Studies have shown that the differential expression levels of genes carried by EVs play significant roles in various cancers and inflammatory diseases, including HCC and CHB [15, 16]. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), and messenger RNAs (mRNAs) play critical roles as RNA molecules within EVs, contributing to their essential biological functions. Among these, lncRNAs are defined as RNA molecules exceeding 200 nucleotides in length that lack protein-coding capacity. In recent years, lncRNAs have emerged as a focus of intensive investigation due to their critical regulatory functions in pivotal pathophysiological mechanisms, including cellular migration, angiogenesis, and tumorigenesis [17, 18]. A growing number of studies have confirmed the role of lncRNA or miRNA carried by EVs in HCC. For instance, the high level of lncRNA HDAC2-AS2 in EVs secreted by liver cancer cells promotes the progression of HCC by inhibiting the cytotoxicity of CD8 + T cells [19]. Hsa-miR-3129 is significantly up-regulated in the plasma EVs of HCC patients and promotes the growth and metastasis of HCC by targeting Thioredoxin Interacting Protein (TXNIP) [20]. In addition, hsa-let-7a, hsa-miR-21, hsa-miR-125a, hsa-miR-200a, and hsa-miR-150 in serum EVs have also been identified as non-invasive diagnostic biomarkers for HCC [21]. These studies mainly focus on the role of RNA carried by EVs in HCC. The dynamic changes of characteristic markers in EVs during the malignant progression from hepatitis to HCC remain unclear, and the specific mechanisms have not been systematically elucidated. In this study, we isolated EVs from serum samples of liver disease patients and systematically analyzed the characteristic RNA expression profiles carried by EVs across different clinical stages (CHB, LC, HA, and HCC) using high-throughput transcriptome sequencing technology. The research aims to screen potential biomarkers with diagnostic value for early-stage HCC, providing a theoretical foundation for developing novel non-invasive liquid biopsy approaches.

Materials and methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Nankai University Affiliated Third Center Hospital from January to March 2024. A total of 34 patients were enrolled in this study, including 24 patients (5 CHB, 5 LC, 4 HA, 10 HCC) and 10 healthy controls (CON group). Serum samples were randomly selected from a subset of the test cohort (n = 24: 5 CON, 5 CHB, 5 LC, 4 HA, and 5 HCC) for EVs RNA sequencing and biomarker screening. The remaining participants (5 CON and 5 HCC) formed the validation cohort, and their plasma samples underwent RNA sequencing for independent verification and stability assessment. The grouping information and clinical baseline data of all included patients were presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Inclusion Criteria

(1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Newly diagnosed patients; (3) CHB patients were diagnosed according to the guidelines for the prevention and treatment of CHB, version 2022 [22]; (4) LC patients were diagnosed according to the guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of LC [23]; 5)HA patients were diagnosed according to the histological examination; 6) HCC patients were diagnosed according to the guidelines for Primary Liver Cancer (2022) criteria [24], all patients in the disease cohorts were newly diagnosed and had not yet received any drug treatment, surgical procedures, or minimally invasive therapies; 7) CON group: No history of liver diseases, negative hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), and absence of high-risk factors for liver cancer.

Exclusion Criteria

(1) Coexisting alcohol-related/fatty liver disease/autoimmune hepatitis or non-hepatocellular liver tumors; (2) History of extrahepatic malignancies or prior liver-related treatments; (3) Severe systemic comorbidities including cardiovascular/renal/metabolic disorders, Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection, active tuberculosis, or pregnancy/lactation; (4) Use of hormonal agents, antiviral drugs, or immunosuppressants within the past year.

Blood sample collection

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from all enrolled patients before treatment initiation. Blood for serum preparation was drawn into vacuum tubes containing inert separation gel and a procoagulant; blood for plasma preparation was drawn into anticoagulant tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid​ (EDTA) (SEKISUI MEDICAL TECHNOLOGY LTD, Beijing, China). The samples were centrifuged, and the separated serum and plasma were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses. The entire procedure was completed within 2 h of sample collection.

Isolation and characterization of EVs

EVs were isolated from serum and plasma using a size-exclusion chromatography and ultrafiltration method. After thawing, samples were pretreated with a 0.8 μm filter, then separated via a gel-permeation column (ES911, Echo Biotech, China), with PBS eluent from tubes 7–9 collected and concentrated using a 100kD ultrafiltration tube.

The particle size distribution of EVs was detected by nano-flow cytometry (Flow NanoAnalyzer, NanoFCM Inc., Xiamen, China). The morphology of EVs was observed by transmission electron microscopy (Transmission Electron Microscope H-7650, Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan​) with uranyl acetate staining, and the marker proteins tumor Susceptibility 101(TSG101, ab125011, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), ALG-2-interacting protein 1(Alix, ab186429, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), CD9 (ab263019, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and negative control Calnexin (10427-2-AP, Proteintech Group, Inc., Rosemont, IL, USA) were detected by Western Blot [25].

RNA extraction

Total RNA was isolated from EVs using the RNA Purification Kit (Simgen, cat. 5202050). Briefly, 700 µL Buffer TL and 100 µL Buffer EX were sequentially added to 100 µL extracellular vesicle suspension, followed by vortexing and centrifugation (12,000 × g, 4 °C, 15 min). The supernatant was combined with ethanol, loaded onto a purification column, and centrifuged (12,000 × g, 30 s). After discarding flow-through, the column was washed with Buffer WA and Buffer WBR (12,000 × g, 30 s each), air-dried (14,000 × g, 1 min), and RNA eluted with 35 µL RNase-free Water (room temperature, 1 min; 12,000 × g, 30 s). RNA was stored at − 80 °C.

Library preparation and sequencing

Stranded long RNA libraries were constructed from 250 pg to 10 ng total RNA using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit (Takara Bio USA, Inc.), while small RNA libraries were generated from 1 ng to 500 ng input RNA with the UMI-incorporated QIAseq miRNA Library Kit (Qiagen, Frederick, MD), enabling unique molecular identifier (UMI) tagging for individual small RNAs. Both library types were indexed for sample multiplexing. Library quality was assessed via Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 and quantitative PCR. Indexed libraries were pooled, clustered on the cBot system using the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq platform.

Raw data processing and transcriptome analysis

Raw sequencing data underwent stringent quality control, including the removal of adapter-containing reads and low-quality sequences (reads with >10% N bases or ≥ 50% bases with Q ≤ 10). Clean reads were validated using Q30 scores (≥ 89.08%) to ensure data reliability. Transcriptome analysis was performed by aligning reads to the GRCH38 reference genome using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1, Daehwan Kim et al., Johns Hopkins University, United States), followed by transcript assembly and expression quantification (FPKM normalization) via StringTie (version 2.2.1, Mihaela Pertea et al., Johns Hopkins University, United States). Novel transcripts were identified by comparison with existing annotations, and lncRNAs were predicted through coding potential assessment using Coding Potential Calculator (CPC, version 0.9-r1, Li A. et al., University of Delaware, United States) [26], Coding-Non-Coding Index (CNCI, version 2.0, Lu Sun et al., University of California, United States) [27], Coding Potential Assessment Tool (CPAT, version 3.0.4, Li J. et al., University of Texas, United States) [28], and PFAM tools (version 35.0, The Pfam Consortium et al., European Molecular Biology Laboratory, United Kingdom) [29].

The miRNA transcription start sites are mostly located in the spacer region, intron, and reverse complementary sequence of the coding sequence. The precursor of miRNA has the signature hairpin structure, and the formation of mature miRNA is realized by the cleavage of Dicer/DCL enzyme. For the biological characteristics of miRNAs, the miRDeep2 software (version 2.0.1.2, Sebastian Mackowiak et al., Max Delbrück Center for Molecular Medicine, Germany) was used to identify known and novel miRNAs in sequences aligned to the reference genome [30].

Global expression profiling and data normalization validation

To evaluate transcriptomic data quality and normalization efficacy, FPKM-normalized expression values were calculated for all samples. Global expression patterns were visualized using boxplots generated with the ggplot2 package (version 3.5.1, Hadley Wickham et al., ​​Posit Software, United States) of R Software (version 4.3.2, R Core Team et al., R Foundation, Austria/Global) to assess intra-sample heterogeneity and cross-sample comparability. This step aimed to validate the robustness of the normalization pipeline and establish a foundation for downstream analyses.

Differential expression analysis of lncRNAs

Differential expression analysis of serum EVs-derived LncRNAs was performed using the edger package (version 3.42.4, Gordon K. Smyth et al., Walter and Eliza hall Institute of medical Research, Australia) of R Software, comparing CON group with four liver disease groups: CHB, LC, HA, and HCC. Differentially expressed LncRNAs (DE-lncRNAs) were identified with thresholds of |log2FC| >1.0 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction). Disease-specific DE-lncRNA groups (CHB: DE-lncRNAs1; LC: DE-lncRNAs2; HA: DE-lncRNAs3; HCC: DE-lncRNAs4) were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps (R package pheatmap: version 1.0.12, Raivo Kolde et al., university of Tartu, Estonia)

Screening strategy for HCC-specific lncRNAs

To identify HCC-specific lncRNAs, a multi-step intersection analysis was designed. The Venn diagram tool (Venny, version 2.1.0, Juan Carlos Oliveros et al., Spanish National Centre for Biotechnology, Spain) was employed to intersect DE-lncRNAs from non-HCC disease groups (DE-lncRNAs1-3) with those from the HCC group (DE-lncRNAs4), thereby excluding transcripts commonly shared across all disease states.

Time series analysis

Mfuzz (version 2.58.0, Matthias E. Futschik et al., University of Salzburg, Austria) is an R package used for clustering and analyzing the expression patterns of transcriptome data at different time points. It can identify the time series expression patterns of genes, helping researchers understand the time changes and functional connections of genes. To further determine the key lncRNAs involved in the progression of HCC, we conducted a time series analysis of lncRNA expression data at five stages: CON, CHB, LC, HA, and HCC. The analysis parameters were the minimum standard deviation = 0.3; membership value = 0, c Optimal = 9. The key lncRNAs were identified by intersecting the lncRNAs that were gradually upregulated during the progression of HCC with the characteristic lncRNAs of HCC.

Construction of lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory network

Differential analysis of miRNA and mRNA sequencing data from serum EVs samples of HCC patients was performed using the edgeR package of R software. Taking |log2FC| >1.0 and p-value < 0.05 as the threshold, differentially expressed miRNAs and mRNAs were identified. To further explore the molecular mechanism of the identified lncRNAs, DINATools-LncBase Predicted v.2 (http://diana.imis.athena-innovation.gr/DianaTools, version 2.0, Theocharis G. Theocharidis et al., Foundation for Research and Technology Hellas, Greece) predicted the downstream miRNAs of lncRNAs; The miRDB (https://mirdb.org/) (version 6.0, Yufei Huang et al., University of Texas at San Antonio, United States) and miRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/) (version 3.0, Hilmar Stutz et al., University of Tübingen, Germany) databases predicted the target genes of miRNAs. miRNAs predicted by two databases simultaneously were used for the next step of analysis. The prediction results of the database were intersected with differentially expressed miRNAs and mRNAs to narrow the screening range. Based on the predicted molecular regulatory relationships, the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory network diagram was constructed by Cytoscape (version 3.10.1, Institute for Systems Biology et al., Cytoscape Consortium, United States) software.

Functional enrichment analysis

To further explore the molecular mechanism of mRNAs in the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory network, enrichment analysis was conducted using the KOBAS (http://bioinfo.org/kobas/genelist/) (version 3.0, Chengjie Mao et al., Peking University, China) database. Enrichment analysis includes two parts: Gene Ontology (GO) annotation (Biological Process, Cellular Component, Molecular Function) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis.

Protein-protein interaction analysis and core gene screening

To further explore the association between the proteins expressed by the genes in the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network, protein-protein interaction analysis (PPI) was carried out by the String (https://cn.string-db.org, version 12.0, Damian Szklarczyk et al., Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, Switzerland) database. The hub genes in this PPI network were screened using the Cytohubba plug-in (version 0.1, Chin-Chun Hung et al., National Taiwan University, Taiwan, China) of Cytoscape software.

External cohort validation of core lncRNAs and downstream genes

To further explore whether the identified lncRNAs and downstream hub genes were stably and significantly differentially expressed in both plasma and serum EVs samples, we included 5 HCC patients and 5 healthy individuals for sequencing. Gene expression trends were verified based on relative expression data.

Statistical analysis

This study used R (edgeR package) for data standardization and differential expression analysis. The MFuzz package and Venny Tools were used for further data processing. Molecular interactions were predicted using multiple databases, and networks were constructed with the Cytoscape software. KOBAS and STRING were employed for functional enrichment and PPI analyses, and CytoHubba identified hub genes. In the validation cohort, the Independent Samples t-test was used to compare the differences in continuous variables between the two groups. All measurement data were verified for normality by the Shapiro-Wilk test and evaluated for homogeneity of variance by the Levene test. If the data conform to the normal distribution and have uniform variances, the independent sample t-test is adopted. If the variances were not uniform, the corrected Welch’s t-test was used. The significance threshold was set at α = 0.05 (two-sided test). Non-normal distribution data were tested using the Mann-Whitney U non-parametric test.In external validation, after ComBat correction, Mann-Whitney U tests and Spearman correlations assessed expression stability, with ICC evaluating technical reproducibility. A stepwise multiple testing control ensured analytical robustness.

Discussion

In recent years, EVs and extracellular vesicles have received increasing attention as promising cancer biomarkers in the liquid biopsy environment. EVs stably encapsulate molecular information from the tumor microenvironment, and their early non-invasive diagnostic value in various tumors, such as pancreatic cancer, lung cancer, and cancer, has been reported [31, 32]. In the field of liver cancer, some studies have also reported the diagnostic value of molecules such as hsa_circ_0004001, miR-638, and CCT3 contained in EVs for HCC [33–35]. Technically, the large-scale production of EVs extraction and identification kits has further promoted their clinical application. As current research often focuses on the diagnosis of HCC stages, it neglects the dynamic process of HCC progression. Therefore, this study aims to identify the EVs characteristic molecules in the dynamic process of hepatitis progressing to liver cancer, providing a basis for the early warning and non-invasive diagnosis of HCC.

This study is based on sequencing and successfully identified a group of HCC-specific lncRNA biomarkers from EVs. The physical characteristics (via Nanoscale Flow Cytometric Analysis) and surface markers (e.g., Tsg101, Alix) of EVs were validated using Western blot, confirming their reliability as a robust source of biomarkers. High-throughput transcriptome profiling further revealed the lncRNAs that were characteristically expressed in the serum EVs of HCC patients. We employed time series analysis to explore the dynamic changes of lncRNAs. As an effective temporal sequence data analysis method, it identifies dynamic molecular expression patterns across multiple groups and has been widely applied in biomarker screening and functional module mining research. For instance, Peng et al. utilized Mfuzz to reveal the dynamic upregulation trends of HMGB1 and other proteins during the progression of diabetic nephropathy [36], while the Li team applied this method to dissect the temporal characteristics of lysine crotonylation modifications in thyroid cancer [37], highlighting its versatility and reliability in multi-omics analyses. The time series analysis revealed that 10 core lncRNAs exhibited stage-specific upregulation patterns during liver disease progression, with some molecules (e.g., DIP2C-AS1, ETS2-AS1, and LINC01013) being significantly activated as early as the LC or HA stages and remaining persistently highly expressed in HCC.

In this study, plasma was chosen for the validation cohort to emulate the variability in sample types. Although the training cohort (serum) and validation cohort (plasma) had matrix differences, the same sample processing and consistent library construction were carried out to minimize technical variation. This approach not only strengthened the generalizability of the conclusions but also highlighted the clinical applicability of the validation results, proving that the identified lncRNA biomarkers were stable and reliable across different sample matrices, which was crucial for the future clinical translation and application of these biomarkers. External cohort validation further confirmed the stable upregulation trends of these core lncRNAs. As antisense lncRNAs, DIP2C-AS1 and ETS2-AS1 target and regulate the proto-oncogenes DIP2C and ETS2, respectively, and may contribute to cancer pathogenesis and progression through multifaceted molecular mechanisms [38]. Among these, ETS2-AS1 may accelerate disease progression by amplifying the oncogenic functions of ETS2. Literature reports that ETS2 drives cell death via the TUG1/miR-129-5p/ATG7 axis and serves as a core regulator in inflammatory macrophages [39, 40]. DIP2C-AS1 showed enrichment in the circulatory system, suggesting its potential as a non-invasive diagnostic biomarker. Notably, this molecule exhibited sustained downregulation during the transformation from ulcerative colitis to colorectal cancer [41]. Furthermore, deletion of its host gene DIP2C has been demonstrated to disrupt the expression of key neuroactive ligand-receptor pathway genes such as Grin2a and Gabrq [42], implying that the DIP2C-AS1/DIP2C axis may exert cross-tissue regulatory functions in inflammation-associated carcinogenesis. Additionally, LINC01013 has been reported as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in HCC [43]. These findings highlight promising molecular targets for non-invasive HCC detection and stratification.

The lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory network constructed in this study reveals potential molecular mechanisms underlying HCC progression. For instance, ETS2-AS1 may regulate the expression of the downstream target gene NTRK2 by competitively binding miR-106a, thereby activating the MAPK signaling pathway and promoting tumor cell proliferation. NTRK2 gene fusion has been identified as an oncogenic driver event in specific tumor types, leading to constitutive activation of downstream signaling pathways and subsequent tumorigenesis [44]. The core gene KCNJ10 in the regulatory network has been confirmed by Liu et al. as a critical hub gene in HCC, showing significant upregulation in tumor tissues and potentially playing a role in molecular pathogenesis, highlighting its potential as a biomarker for early detection and therapeutic targeting [45]. This study further discovered its abnormal expression pattern in circulating EVs, providing direct evidence for non-invasive monitoring.

Systematic analysis of an external validation cohort confirmed that the core lncRNAs and their downstream regulatory genes exhibited stable yet significantly variable expression patterns in plasma/serum-derived EVs. Notably, three key lncRNAs and seven hub genes exhibited consistently upregulated expression profiles in both plasma and serum EVs of HCC patients, with this expression trend maintaining high reproducibility across different sample processing protocols, highlighting their translational clinical value as liquid biopsy biomarkers.

However, this study presents several limitations: The small sample size (n = 5 per group) constrains the generalizability of the conclusions, and further diagnostic experiments with large samples are needed to verify the diagnostic value of the identified molecules; potential technical interference in EV isolation and sequencing requires optimization through standardized protocols. Developing diagnostic kits based on high-sensitivity and high-specificity molecules to simplify the detection process and shorten the detection time has great clinical significance; additionally, predictive findings related to lncRNA regulatory networks necessitate experimental validation (e.g., gene interactions and functional mechanisms). Notably, the clinical translation of EV-lncRNA biomarkers must address challenges such as standardization of detection thresholds and optimization of biomarker combinations. Despite these limitations, this study identified dynamic EV-lncRNAs during the progression from CHB to HCC through temporal sequence analysis, providing novel research directions for elucidating early molecular mechanisms in hepatocarcinogenesis. This work contributes to refining analytical strategies for liquid biopsy, advancing the field from merely descriptive discoveries toward an in-depth exploration of molecular mechanisms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study determined the dynamic expression profiles of EV-derived lncRNAs during HBV-related HCC progression and identified 10 core lncRNAs, including DIP2C-AS1, ETS2-AS1, and LINC01013, which are progressively upregulated from CHB to HCC. Besides, our results showed that these lncRNAs form a regulatory network of 62 nodes, implicating autophagy/MAPK pathways and hub genes such as NTRK2 and KCNJ10, validated in independent plasma cohorts. Furthermore, these findings provide novel non-invasive biomarkers for early HCC detection and theoretical support for liquid biopsy applications, and require validation in prospective studies.
