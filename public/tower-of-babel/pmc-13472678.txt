Algorithmic fairness in machine-learning models for determining the progression or recurrence of cardiovascular disease in racialized populations: a scoping review
Authors: Imeth Illamperuma, Bhavya Gandhi, Ronin Offman, Morgan Rosenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine how algorithmic fairness is measured, operationalized, and reported in machine learning (ML) models designed to predict or support secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease (CVD) outcomes including progression, recurrence, readmission, and post-index mortality in racialized populations.Materials and MethodsThis scoping review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines and registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF registration: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/9W67V). Systematic searches of OVID MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, and CENTRAL were performed to identify studies evaluating fairness in ML models applied to secondary cardiovascular outcomes.ResultsOf 2669 records screened, three retrospective cohort studies met inclusion criteria. All studies used large-scale electronic health record data from the United States and evaluated model performance across racial subgroups. Only one study implemented fairness-aware model development, reporting improvements of approximately 5%-12% in equity-related metrics, accompanied by modest trade-offs in calibration and sensitivity. The remaining studies assessed fairness post hoc and demonstrated limited ability to mitigate subgroup performance differences.DiscussionMost full-text studies excluded during screening addressed fairness in predicting primary CVD incidence rather than secondary outcomes, highlighting a substantial gap in the literature. Across included studies, observed fairness limitations appeared to be driven largely by upstream structural and data-generating factors such as representation, care patterns, and documentation rather than algorithmic design alone.ConclusionEvidence on algorithmic fairness in ML models for secondary cardiovascular outcomes remains sparse. Improved reporting of subgroup performance, missingness, and calibration, alongside integration of fairness throughout model development, is necessary before equitable clinical deployment.Study RegistrationOpen Science Framework: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/9W67V

Background

Evolution of machine learning in cardiovascular medicine

Advancements in machine learning (ML) computation and artificial intelligence (AI) continue to transform cardiovascular medicine, from helping healthcare practitioners diagnose to stratifying longitudinal risk of disease. The Global Burden of Disease study estimates that in 2021, cardiovascular disease (CVD) caused 19 million deaths globally and accounted for 438 million years of adjusted life in disability that was lost as a result of CVD.1 AI-powered tools offer precision in detecting and predicting a range of CVDs based on patient presentations and risk factors, including myocardial infarction, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and structural heart disease.2 The high incidence and prevalence of these diseases highlight the immense value of effective AI tools for CVD patients. ML models utilizing specific algorithms and large-scale health data have demonstrated notable incremental performance; common models used in this context include Support Vector Machine (SVM), XGBoost, Random Forest (RF), Adaboost, and K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN).3 The KNN and XGBoost models also demonstrate impressive performances in CVD detection, 96.55% and 99.14%, respectively.4

Emerging concerns of algorithmic bias in CVD prediction

Despite promising results from many ML models in CVD assessment, there remain underlying biases in training datasets, underrepresentation of certain demographic groups, and consequent inequities in algorithmic fairness.5 Current literature defines that ML models are predominantly trained on Caucasian populations, leading to less equitable and predictable performance outcomes for racialized patient populations.6 The Canadian Consensus identifies racialized populations to include visible minorities, including South Asian, Chinese, Black, Filipino, Arab, Latin American, Southeast Asian, West Asian, Korean, Japanese, Indigenous, and Multiple Racialized Groups—an individual whose family heritage includes members from two or more racial groups.7 Addressing the inequity present in applications of clinical ML models to diverse patient populations requires intentional and rigorous validation and application of fairness metrics to the model design.

Study aim and rationale

This scoping review aims to explore the current landscape of algorithmic fairness in ML-based predictive CVD models by identifying the current fairness metrics, assessing the effectiveness of bias mitigation strategies, and outlining the challenges of applying these methods within racialized patient populations. By synthesizing the available evidence, this review will highlight any gaps in the current body of literature and inform the equitable integration of ML within cardiovascular care.

Methods

This scoping review was structured in adherence to the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines (See Supplementary S1 for checklist). This study was registered within the Open Science Framework (OSF) database (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/9W67V). A scoping review methodology was chosen, given that initial literature searches demonstrated a lack of recent reviews addressing our research question.

The scoping review approach was selected as it allowed for a comprehensive mapping of the available literature evaluating algorithmic fairness within ML models applied to CVD progression and recurrence in racialized populations. This design was appropriate to synthesize studies with diverse methodologies, fairness definitions, and bias mitigation strategies, thereby clarifying the breadth and depth of current evidence while identifying persisting knowledge gaps.

Literature search

The search strategy that was implemented primarily identified published peer-reviewed studies rather than unpublished or gray literature sources. Using OVID MEDLINE, keywords, and specific terminology, our fully developed search strategy was designed to optimally identify articles relating to our research topic. The search strategy was peer-reviewed by a McMaster University research librarian. The search strategy focused on four major compulsory concepts: ML, racialized populations, CVD, and algorithmic fairness. Our search strategy included synonymous wording/phrasing for these concepts as well as Boolean operators. No search filters were used to limit the search results.

The databases searched included OVID MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) databases. Sources of unpublished studies and gray literature were not included in the search.

Eligibility criteria

This scoping review considered peer-reviewed empirical studies with both experimental and observational designs that evaluated bias and fairness within ML models for CVD progression or recurrence across racialized subpopulations. In addition, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies and cross-sectional studies were considered for inclusion.

Inclusion criteria

This scoping review included studies focusing on the application of ML models in CVD to address fairness and equity for racialized populations. Included studies involved adult patients, aged 18 years and older, who had an existing diagnosis with a CVD-related condition. The studies had to utilize ML models for tasks such as detection, prediction, and recurrence of CVD (secondary prevention). Studies were required to incorporate or report on algorithmic fairness metrics such as demographic parity, equalized odds, or to employ bias mitigation strategies (pre-processing or post-processing models). Studies had to report model performance across racialized and non-racialized groups, measure the effectiveness of fairness and bias mitigation interventions, or identify challenges in implementing the proper methods within a clinical context. Included study settings consisted of clinical care, healthcare systems, and analyses of large datasets (electronic health records/biobanks).

Exclusion criteria

Studies that did not align with the core objectives of the review were excluded. Studies focusing exclusively on non-racialized populations or general cohorts without specific subgroup analysis were excluded. Research involving individuals under the age of 18 or those without a specific CVD diagnosis was excluded. Studies involving primary detection methods with no secondary prediction of progression/recurrence methods were excluded. As the focus remained on ML, studies that employed only traditional statistical methods [such as logistic regression (LR)] were excluded. A key exclusion criterion was the absence of metrics evaluating algorithmic fairness, bias, or equity. Research conducted in non-healthcare settings, such as finance, education, animal, or pre-clinical studies, was not included in our analysis.

Study selection process

Citations yielded by the literature search were uploaded into the application Covidence, and duplicates were automatically removed. Titles and abstracts were screened by independent reviewers based on the inclusion criteria outlined. Full text review was subsequently conducted, acting as a more detailed inclusion/exclusion based on the criteria. Conflicts in the inclusion or exclusion of papers by independent reviewers were verbally discussed prior to resolution. Data extraction and synthesis

Data was extracted from the selected citations by independent reviewers using the application Covidence. The data extracted included specific details regarding the study, participant population, concept, context, and key findings that were relevant and applicable to the review question. This process involved the detailed extraction of article bibliographic information, authors, year of publication, study characteristics, objectives, design, and data collection periods. Key details on the study population were extracted with a focus on the racial and ethnic groups included and their respective sample sizes. Extraction also documented technical aspects of the ML models used, including specific methods and the function of the models. Crucially, the extraction focused on identifying outcomes of ML prediction, bias mitigation strategies, algorithmic fairness metrics, and differences in model performance across racial subgroups. Key findings, reported limitations, and the authors’ future directions/remarks were presented in the discussion and interpretation of extracted data from each study. Results

Study selection and screening outcomes

The search strategy yielded 4705 records across MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, and CENTRAL. After the removal of duplicates the literature search identified 2669 unique records, of which 45 articles were retrieved for the full-text review. 42 studies were excluded due to reasons including wrong outcomes (primary incidence rather than secondary cardiovascular progression), absence of fairness metrics, or exclusive focus on non-racialized populations. Three studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis.

Characteristics of included studies

All included studies were retrospective cohort analyses conducted in the United States, each utilizing EHR data to evaluate ML models applied to post-diagnosis cardiovascular outcomes, including heart failure readmission, stroke risk in atrial fibrillation, and mortality prediction in a chronic disease cohort that included congestive heart failure. Weissman et al examined the prediction of 30-day heart failure readmissions with and without the inclusion of neighborhood-level socioeconomic data (Area Deprivation Index [ADI]).8 Gao et al developed a 2-year stroke risk model for atrial fibrillation patients using LightGBM, incorporating explicit fairness-focused optimization strategies within the All of Us Research Program dataset.9 Wang et al assessed racial fairness in 1-year mortality prediction for chronic disease cohorts, including CHF, using matched Black-White patient counterparts and multiple ML models.10

Across studies, sample sizes ranged from 1316 patients in the Weissman cohort8 to 195 627 patients in Wang et al’s overall cohort, including 59 806 patients in the CHF subgroup,10 while Gao et al included 7379 patients with atrial fibrillation.9 Only Gao et al employed fairness-aware model development; the other two studies assessed fairness post hoc.8,10

Predictive performance across racial subgroups

Weissman et al: 30-heart failure readmission

Weissman et al developed elastic net (EN) and gradient boosting machine (GBM) models to predict 30-day hospital readmission of patients with congestive heart failure at a single urban academic medical center, using clinical, demographic, and limited socioeconomic predictors (including, in a second iteration model, the ADI).8 In the held out test set, the baseline model that excluded ADI demonstrated moderate overall performance, with a Brier Score (BS) of 0.13 (95% CI 0.13-0.14), and nearly identical performance across racial subgroups: BS 0.12 (95% CI 0.12-0.13) in White patients, and BS 0.13 (95% CI 0.13-0.14) in non-White patients.8

AUROC values were not the central performance metric but fell within a moderate range; classification performance did not differ meaningfully by race, and there were no statistically significant subgroup gaps in prediction error or discrimination.8 When ADI was added to the predictor set, neither overall nor subgroup BS changed meaningfully: overall BS remained 0.13, while subgroup BS remained approximately 0.12 in White patients and 0.13 in non-White patients, with overlapping confidence intervals across groups.8

Gao et al: 2-year stroke prediction in atrial fibrillation

Gao et al elevated ML-based extension of CHADS2 and CHA2DS2-VASc scores for predicting 2-year stroke risk among atrial fibrillation patients using de-identified EHR data from the All of Us Research Program.9 Baseline ML models (LightGBM) improved discrimination relative to traditional clinical scores, raising AUROC from approximately 0.70 to >0.80 for the overall population.9 Prior to fairness optimization, racial subgroup analyses showed measurable gaps in AUROC and true positive rates (TPR), particularly between Black and White patients.

After implementing the tailored “disparity aware” modeling framework, including fairness-weighted tuning and race-specific thresholds, subgroup AUROCs converged.9 Tailored CHA2DS2-VASc + LightGBM models achieved AUROCs of 0.823-0.838 across race groups, with minimal AUROC differences (≤0.01-0.02) and substantially narrowed TPR gaps compared with conventional tuning. Sensitivity analyses that removed temporal and geographic shifts confirmed that performance and fairness improvements were preserved across US regions and time periods.9

Wang et al: 1-year mortality in chronic disease cohorts

Wang et al studied mortality prediction across five chronic disease cohorts (CHF, CKD, COPD, CLD, dementia) using four models (LR, RF, AdaBoost, multilayer perceptron [MLP]) and assessed performance for Black patients, White counterparts through propensity scores, and the total White population.10 The CHF cohort, MLP achieved the highest sensitivity at 0.513 (SD 0.027) and F1 score 0.55; AdaBoost yielded a similar F1 value (∼0.55) but slightly lower sensitivity (∼0.47).10 There was a fluctuation between disease cohorts, generally between 0.31 and 0.51, and F1 scores between 0.45 and 0.55, with AUROC values between 0.70 and 0.78, indicating that all models missed a substantial proportion of deaths.10

When stratified by race, significant subgroup performance differences emerged only in the CHF cohort, and only when comparing Black patients to matched White counterparts.10 The CHF cohort using AdaBoost, Black vs White-matched vs White-total F1 scores were 0.494 vs 0.510 vs 0.562, and sensitivities were 0.411 vs 0.418 vs 0.477, respectively.10 There was a statistically significant difference (ΔF1, ΔSe) between fairness metrics between counterpart-based and group-level evaluations (P = .021 and P = .001, respectively).10 The MLP model demonstrated F1 scores of 0.488 vs 0.491 vs 0.555, and sensitivities of 0.479 vs 0.461 vs 0.516, again with significant discrepancy between fairness evaluation approaches (P = .014 for ΔF1; P = .003 for ΔSe).10 Overall predictive performance was uniformly modest, and racial performance gaps were limited in scope.

Fairness metrics and bias mitigation approach

Weissman et al assessed “algorithmic equity” using several perspectives, including: Error-based fairness (BS stratified by race), Classification parity (threshold ranges), and Model reweighting and race removal sensitivity analyses.8 The study, however, did not implement any explicit bias mitigation technique. Factors of Race and ADI, instead of targets of fairness constraint, were treated as predictors.8 Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that reweighting the EN model to reflect US Census race distributions did not change model performance, and removing race as a feature also did not materially alter either performance or equity metrics.8

Gao et al introduced a multi-component fairness optimization framework that integrated fairness into model development instead of restricting it to post hoc evaluation.9 Some key elements included: Disparity penalized tuning objectives, tuning set enrichment for underrepresented groups, race-specific classification thresholds, and model agnostic fairness evaluation.9 In terms of disparity penalized tuning objectives, the validation loss in combination with overall performance with a penalty term for subgroup disparities (TPR gaps) effectively traded a small amount of global performance to reduce intergroup differences.9 In terms of tuning set enrichment, the tuning subset was deliberately enriched with encounters from states and settings with higher proportions of Black patients, increasing the sensitivity of the tuning process to minority performance.9 Model-agnostic fairness evaluation frameworks tested with LightGBM, as well as LR and RF, demonstrated similar improvements in fairness across model classes.9 This study is the only one that included work to treat fairness as a primary optimization target rather than solely a descriptive outcome.

Wang et al used a counterpart-based fairness evaluation without modifying the model training process.10 The fairness framework relied on Equal performance metrics quantifying ΔF1, ΔSe, and equal F1 between race groups, Propensity score matching between black and white counterparts on sociodemographic and clinical variables, as well as paired t-tests comparing fairness metric magnitudes between group-level and counterpart-based evaluations.10

Quantitative impact of fairness adjustments

Across the three studies, only Gao et al reported explicit and quantitatively meaningful fairness gains attributable to algorithmic interventions.9 Their disparity-aware framework decreased disparities across specific fairness metrics, including equal opportunity difference and disparate impact, while preserving AUROC above 0.80 across racial groups.9 Sensitivity and calibration were modestly affected by did not collapse to clinically unacceptable levels. In contrast with this, Weissman et al’s inclusion of ADI had no measurable fairness impact; BS remained 0.13 overall and by race, with fully overlapping CI and no evidence of improved quality of prediction error.8 Wang et al counterpart-based evaluation uncovered stronger apparent bias in the CHF cohort; however, these were not connected to any mitigation strategies, and there were no improvements in fairness improvement over baseline; the fairness metrics were purely diagnostic and not interventionally based.10

Methodological limitations identified across studies

Across the included studies, several methodological limitations constrained the strength and interpretability of fairness findings. Social determinants of health were captured coarsely, as exemplified by Weissman et al’s reliance on neighborhood-level ADI rather than patient-level socioeconomic measures, limiting the ability to interrogate structural contributors to inequity.8 Subgroup underrepresentation persisted even in diverse datasets such as All of Us, reducing precision in AUROC and TPR estimates for racialized groups and weakening the robustness of fairness assessments.9 In Wang et al, modest baseline model performance, particularly persistently low sensitivity, restricted clinical utility and rendered fairness evaluations secondary to broader performance inadequacies.10 Heterogeneous fairness definitions and inconsistent reporting of subgroup sizes, confidence intervals, missingness, and calibration further impeded comparability across studies. Moreover, none of the models underwent external validation on demographically distinct cohorts, raising concerns about generalizability. No study incorporated intersection analyses across race, sex, socioeconomic position, or multimorbidity.

Discussion

Gaps in the current evidence base: primary vs secondary CVD outcomes

A central finding is the scarcity of fairness-focused research on secondary cardiovascular outcomes. The majority of full-text articles screened were excluded because they focused on Primary CVD incidence or risk prediction in the general population without subgroup fairness analysis. As a result, little is known about fairness in progression, recurrence, readmission, and post-index mortality. These outcomes directly govern long-term prognosis, resource allocation, and chronic disease management. The lack of investigation means the potential for algorithmic bias in these high-stakes, post-diagnosis predictions, and their subsequent impact on patient trajectories remains largely unexamined.

Structural and sociotechnical drivers of algorithmic bias

The findings from the included studies suggest that observed disparities in model performance are closely linked to, and may be driven by, preexisting structural embedded in the data, rather than being solely a product of model architecture or the explicit inclusion of race as a variable.

For instance, in Weissman et al: the ADI neighborhood level composite was strongly correlated with race (r = 0.49), reflecting the geographical segregation and uneven distribution of socioeconomic resources.8 White patients were associated with better ADI scores, but did not improve fairness when introduced into the model.8 This suggests that coarse ecological proxies may track structural inequity but fail to translate into equitable prediction, particularly when not accompanied by individual-level SDoH or equity-oriented model design. Wang et al: Large systematic differences in comorbidity, socioeconomic status, and care patterns were observed between Black and White patients after propensity score matching; these differences narrowed substantially.10 The fact that fairness signals were stronger in counterpart-based evaluations than in overall group comparisons underscores how unadjusted fairness metrics can conflate underlying structural differences with algorithmic discrimination. Gao et al: Underrepresentation of racialized patients constrained precision in subgroup estimates, and fairness improvements required deliberate reweighting of underrepresented populations in the tuning process.9 This again ties fairness feasibility to data generation practices and representation. These observations support a sociotechnical view in which EHR-derived ML models inherit and potentially amplify historically produced inequalities in access, diagnosis, documentation, and follow-up, even when race is not explicitly targeted during modeling.

Tradeoffs between fairness optimization and predictive performance

The only study to implement fairness-aware tuning (Gao et al) showed that fairness improvements are achievable but not cost-free.9 Incorporating disparity penalties and subgroup-specific thresholds reduced performance gaps while introducing modest tradeoffs, with overall discrimination preserved.9 This reflects redistribution of error across racial groups rather than elimination of error. Clinically, such tradeoffs must be weighed against harms of false negatives, including missed strokes, recurrent events, and deaths, and false positives, including unnecessary treatment and resource use across racial groups. Fairness adjustments that slightly reduce performance for well-served majority groups while improving performance for historically marginalized groups may be ethically justified, but they require transparent reporting and careful threshold selection. By contrast, Wang et al evaluated fairness in models with limited absolute performance, making it difficult to separate algorithmic inequity from overall inadequacy.10 When baseline predictive performance is poor, fairness optimization becomes secondary: redistributing errors may improve relative equity, but the model may remain clinically unusable for any group.

Operationalizing fairness within model development

The collective findings from the three reviewed studies suggest that post hoc fairness evaluation alone is likely insufficient to ensure equitable model performance. The evidence indicates that effective operationalization of fairness may require integrating it into the model development lifecycle. Specifically, this could involve: (1) Fairness-aware objectives during model training, (2) Representative and balanced training data, especially for racialized subgroups, (3) Threshold optimization explicitly constrained by fairness criteria, (4) Prospective monitoring of subgroup performance following deployment. The studies illustrate that simply reporting subgroup metrics or adding coarse SDoH proxies does not inherently produce equitable models. Therefore, based on this limited but consistent evidence, treating fairness as a core design requirement from the outset, rather than an add-on evaluation, should be considered a critical component of model validity for cardiovascular outcome predictions.

Cultural and institutional determinants shaping data generation

These studies illustrate that institutional and cultural factors, including care access patterns, referral norms, and historical segregation, shape which patients are captured in EHR datasets, how often they are seen, and the extent of clinical documentation. In Weissman et al, White race was strongly correlated with more favorable ADI scores, reflecting uneven socioeconomic advantage across neighborhoods.8 In Wang et al, Black and White patients differed substantially in sociodemographic and clinical profiles before matching, suggesting persistent differences in disease burden and healthcare utilization rather than model-induced bias alone.10 Together, these findings suggest that fairness limitations are influenced by upstream structural and data-generating factors. Structural inequities may also appear as delays in presentation, referral, and access to treatment, shaping both secondary cardiovascular outcomes and the data recorded in EHR systems.11

Implications for clinical translation and patient safety

Given the limited number and scope of available studies (n = 3), the current fairness literature on secondary CVD outcomes is not sufficient to support the widespread deployment of the evaluated models in real-world care. Key concerns include: Suboptimal sensitivity and F1 scores in mortality prediction even before considerations,10 lack of performance or fairness gain from adding neighborhood-level SDoH proxies alone,8 and dependence on complex unstandardized fairness frameworks that have not yet been prospectively evaluated in clinical settings.9 At present, ML models for secondary cardiovascular outcomes remain experimental, particularly for racialized populations at risk of misclassification. Given the modest predictive performance reported by Wang et al, they are not yet suitable for routine clinical use.10

Limitations of the included evidence and of this review

The conclusions of this review are limited by the limited number of included studies (n = 3). Another limitation was the heterogeneity in outcomes, including readmission, stroke, mortality, algorithms, and fairness definitions. None of the included studies performed external validation in an independent healthcare system, and intersectional analyses were also lacking. In addition, the included studies relied on EHR-derived datasets that may underrepresent or misclassify racialized groups.

Future directions for equity-driven cardiovascular AI

To advance equity-driven cardiovascular AI, fairness research must expand beyond readmission, stroke, and mortality to evaluate the full spectrum of secondary CVD outcomes, including disease progression, heart failure exacerbation, and treatment adherence. Data fidelity and representation must improve by moving beyond coarse neighborhood-level proxies and integrating patient-level social determinants of health into EHR-derived models. Methodological frameworks, including fairness-aware algorithms and evaluation metrics tailored to clinical contexts, should be developed, validated, and standardized, with best practices for external and prospective validation across diverse healthcare settings, as seen in Figure 2. Future models must also embrace intersectionality and multimorbidity. Development should remain grounded in a sociotechnical, stakeholder-engaged framework that recognizes how historical and structural inequities shape clinical data generation and model deployment.

Limitations

The limitations of this review mirror those within the primary literature. The small number of eligible studies, the narrow racial scope of analyses, and the absence of standardized fairness definitions constrain the generalizability of findings. Documentation opacity regarding preprocessing and missing data impedes study reproducibility. Most critically, no included study evaluated the downstream clinical impact of fairness interventions, leaving open the question of whether improved algorithmic equity translates into improved health equity. However, by identifying and quantifying the absence of fairness evaluations in secondary cardiovascular outcomes, this scoping review provides an evidence-based direction for where future equity-driven ML research must focus.

Conclusion

This scoping review demonstrates that while ML models have substantially advanced cardiovascular prediction and risk stratification, algorithmic fairness remains incomplete. Across existing studies, fairness metrics and mitigation strategies have yielded measurable but partial improvements, with persistent disparities across racialized groups. These inequities likely reflect both technical and systemic influences, including imbalanced data, inconsistent fairness definitions, and broader social determinants of health that shape data generation and interpretation.

Moving forward, fairness must be recognized as a clinical performance standard rather than an optional quality measure. Integrating fairness constraints during model design, transparently reporting subgroup performance, and embedding equity audits throughout the model lifecycle will be crucial. This requires collaboration among clinicians, data scientists, and policy leaders to define regulatory frameworks that treat fairness violations as patient safety risks.

Ultimately, algorithmic fairness in cardiovascular ML is not simply a matter of computational optimization but of ethical obligation. The promise of AI in cardiovascular care can only be fulfilled when all populations, particularly those historically marginalized, benefit equally from its advances.
