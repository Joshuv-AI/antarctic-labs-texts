Sexual dimorphism of cranial fossae in forensic anthropology: A systematic review
Authors: Haszianaliza Haslan, Nur Damia Iwani Zulkiflee, Aspalilah Alias, Helmi Hadi, Choy Ker Woon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSex estimation is an essential step in forensic anthropology because it significantly narrows the identification pool. Traditional approaches emphasize extracranial traits but cranial fossa structures (CFS) have gained attention due to their potential roles in sex determination. This review summarizes the current methods and classification accuracies associated with CFS.MethodsSystematic search was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, adhering to PRISMA guidelines. Studies published from 2000 to 2024 on sexual dimorphism in CFS were included, excluding those utilizing plain radiographs. The risk of bias was evaluated using a modified methodology quality score. Data extraction followed the PICO framework: population (origin of skulls), intervention (various materials and methods), comparison (male versus female differences), and outcome (accuracy of classification, size, and shape).ResultsSex classification using CFS yielded accuracy rates ranging from 77.9% to 92%. Crista galli length was most sexually dimorphic in the anterior cranial fossa, with 83.7% accuracy (male: 14.50–21.26 mm; female: 11.06–21.24 mm). In the middle cranial fossa, the distance between the foramina ovale had a sex classification accuracy of 63.9%, with mean measurements of 57.3–62.5 mm in males and 55.6–60.1 mm in females. The clivus length was the most sexually dimorphic structure within the posterior cranial fossa, where the sex classification accuracy was 67%, with mean values of 43.99–44.79 mm in males and 41.39–42.53 mm in females.ConclusionMeasurable sexual dimorphism traits are found in the CFS, particularly the crista galli, and can be used as indicators in forensic sex estimation.Keywords: Cranial fossa, Forensic anthropology, Sex classification accuracy, Sexual dimorphism, Sex estimation, Skull

Introduction

The main aim of forensic anthropology is the accurate identification of skeletal remains. Biological profiling of the skeleton involves four main parameters: sex, age, population affinity, and stature. Identifying the sex of a skeleton can provide crucial insights in forensic anthropology investigations because it narrows down the identification process by up to 50%, either as male or female.1 This is particularly relevant in cases involving decomposed, skeletonized, or burned remains, where skeletal parts are mainly used for identification.2,3 The cranial fossae, with their protected intracranial location and relatively thick structure, are preferentially preserved compared with other skeletal parts. Fragmented remains are commonly encountered in forensic contexts and the resilience of cranial fossae allows them to provide crucial information even in thermally altered or commingled specimens.4

The skull is one of the most sexually dimorphic regions in the human skeleton after the pelvis.5 In forensic anthropology, the morphological traits of the skull can be studied intracranially and extracranially. The cranial fossa is located in the intracranial part of the skull and forms the floor of the cranial cavity.6 Cranial fossae can be divided into anterior, middle, and posterior fossae. The anterior cranial fossa is the shallowest fossa and characterized by the crista galli, ethmoid plate, and optic canals. The middle cranial fossa has a butterfly shape and its central depression is known as the sella turcica. It contains several foramina, including the foramen rotundum, ovale, and spinosum. The foramen magnum is the largest foramen of the skull and it is located in the posterior cranial fossa, anterior to the clivus.6

Previous forensic anthropological studies mainly focused on evaluating sexual dimorphism in extracranial structures, particularly those of the cranial vault and facial skeleton, due to their accessibility and established diagnostic value in sex estimation.7 By contrast, the cranial fossae have not been systematically assessed as integrated anatomical regions for sex estimation, despite their distinct developmental features. The cranial fossae constitute complex endocranial surfaces formed through coordinated cranial base development and neurocranial accommodation, and thus they reflect morphological processes that fundamentally differ from those governing external cranial morphology.8

Various methods have been applied in forensic anthropology assessments for biological profiling. Visual inspection enables rapid analysis but this method is highly dependent on the level of expertise, and prone to error in less experienced investigators.7 Metric analysis, including linear measurements, provides more reliable estimates and can reduce inter-investigator discrepancy.7 Geometric morphometrics is an advanced and increasingly widely adopted technique for quantifying morphological variation through coordinate-based analysis. The routine use of computed tomography (CT) imaging in disaster victim identification (DVI) protocols has allowed geometric morphometrics to gain prominence in forensic anthropology.2 This approach allows high-resolution analysis of intricate regions such as the cranial fossae, which cannot be examined using morphoscopic techniques focused on external cranial traits, and facilitates the identification of sexually dimorphic features.5

Despite measurable dimorphism, cranial fossae remain underrepresented in forensic literature, and a comprehensive synthesis has not been published. Thus, there is a lack of systematic reviews regarding the forensic potential of cranial fossae as diagnostic regions in biological profiling. The cranial fossae are resilient to taphonomic changes and characterized by unique internal morphology and development, and can now be examined via routine CT imaging in DVI contexts to provide forensic insights that complement analyses of external cranial traits. Fragmented skull segments are commonly used in biological profiling based on data from a specific area that has been preserved. Therefore, a systematic review is required in order to consolidate existing knowledge about sexual dimorphism in distinct cranial fossae structures across different populations and summarize the methods employed in forensic anthropology applications. By synthesizing data from diverse sources, this review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the sexual dimorphic variations within different cranial fossae structures.

Material and Methods

Protocol and registration

This review was conducted based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) protocol using the PRISMA 2020 flow chart for new systematic reviews (https://www.prisma-statement.org/). The review has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under reference number CRD42025639597 (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/home).

Search strategy, inclusion criteria, and exclusion criteria

A literature search was conducted using three electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Other methods including citation searching via Google Scholar were used to ensure the inclusion of all relevant literature. The search was completed on December 31, 2024. Studies were included in this review that analyzed human intracranial anatomical structures and foramina within the cranial fossa, focusing on sexual dimorphism. Studies were excluded that used plain radiographs and without full text. Only studies published in English between 2000 and 2024 were included in this review. Screening process, data synthesis, and extraction

The retrieved studies were exported and organized using EndNote reference management software. Duplicates were identified and removed. Screening of the abstracts and assessments of eligibility for retrieved papers were performed independently by HH and NDIZ. Any conflicts were discussed with CKW (Figure 1).

Flow chart illustrating the systematic review process based on PRISMA 2020. ∗Reason 1: studies involving foramina of the skull investigated extracranially. Reason 2: studies using plain radiographs. Reason 3: studies without a known sex identity. Reason 4: studies involving irrelevant structures.

Data synthesis and extraction were conducted based on the PICO framework during full paper screening, according to the geographical origins of adult skull samples (Population), anthropological approaches (Intervention), and comparisons between males and females (Comparison) in terms of the size, shape, and classification accuracy (Outcome). Outcomes were tabulated based on anthropology approaches, i.e., morphometry, morphoscopic and geometric morphometry; and the means and p-values for the size, percentage of shape, and classification accuracy were organized according to regions of the cranial fossae. Data were extracted independently by HH and NDIZ. Discrepancies were addressed and resolved with CKW.

Quality assessment

Quality assessment involved investigating the methods used in the selected studies to ensure reliable and valid findings. The quality of the selected studies was assessed using the methodology quality score with modification9 based on four domains, i.e., study design, data collection process, area/region measurement, and statistical analysis. The 16 evaluation criteria employed are labeled from A to P in Table 2. For each criterion, 0 was assigned if the study was free from bias, 0.5 if partially free from bias, and 1 if bias was present. For each study, the score was calculated by dividing the total score by the total number of criteria. Scores that were the same or less than 0.4 were considered to indicate a low risk of bias, and scores greater than 0.4 were considered high risk. Quality assessments were conducted by HH and NDIZ independently (Table 2). Any conflicts were resolved by discussion with CKW.

Results

Literature research

The database search identified 679 studies comprising 57 studies from PubMed, 148 studies from Scopus, and 474 studies from Web of Science (Figure 1). In total, 90 duplicate studies were removed, leaving 589 studies for title and abstract screening. Following screening, 536 studies were excluded and 53 studies were selected for retrieval. Six studies could not be retrieved and 47 studies were successfully obtained for eligibility assessment. Following eligibility assessment, 34 studies were excluded because 20 involved investigating the foramina of the skull extracranially (Reason 1), two studies used plain radiographs (Reason 2), one study lacked a known sex identity (Reason 3), and 11 studies involved irrelevant structures (Reason 4). Finally, 15 studies were included in this review, among which two were identified by citation searching (Figure 1).

Descriptions of studies

This systematic review covered studies from various populations, including KSA, Bosnia and Herzegovina, China, Colombia, Denmark, Greece, India, Iran, Nepal, Taiwan, Türkiye, and the United Arab Emirates (UAE). The sample size of the studies ranged from 102 to 533, and all studies had an approximately balanced male-to-female ratio. All studies included in this review utilized CT scans, except for one study by Natsis et al.18 who analyzed dry skulls (Table 3). All studies relied on retrospective CT scan data from hospitals, except for a study by Ajanovic et al.23 who used CT scans of dry skulls from an osteological collection. Three studies by Isaza et al.,10 Zhan et al.,11 and Chou et al.12 analyzed parameters from all three cranial fossae, whereas the remaining studies focused on parameters for specific cranial fossae structures (Table 3). In general, all studies used linear measurements except for a study by Ajanovic et al.23 who used geometric morphometrics, and a study by Mana et al.15 who used both geometric morphometrics and angular measurements.

Sex classification accuracy of cranial fossa

The sex classification accuracy of the cranial fossa was determined in four studies (Table 4). Using landmark-based measurements, the cranial fossa in the Colombian population could determine sex with an accuracy of up to 92%.10 The classification accuracy was also high in the Chinese and UAE populations using cranial fossa at up to 89.3% and 77.9%, respectively (Table 4). In the Colombian population, comparisons of the three parts of the cranial fossae showed that the posterior region (79.9%) had the highest accuracy, followed by the middle (77.1%) and anterior fossa regions (67.5%).10 Using the geometric morphometric method, the classification accuracy of the posterior cranial fossa appeared to be slightly lower in the Danish population (76.6%).15

Comparative morphometry of anterior cranial fossa structures

The length, height, and width of crista galli were measured in five studies. There were no significant differences in the crista galli between males and females in 4/5 studies (Table 5). However, a study by Komut and Golpinar16 found that crista galli was sexually dimorphic, where the accuracy rates for the three linear measurements ranged from 81.2% to 83.7%. Two length ranges were found for both males and females, where the length in males was between 20.9 and 21.26 mm in the Colombian and Chinese populations. The range was between 14.50 and 14.59 mm in the Taiwanese and Turkish populations. In females, the lengths ranged between 21.10 and 21.24 mm in the Colombian and Chinese populations, and between 11.06 and 14.57 mm in the Taiwanese and Turkish populations. In the UAE population, the heights were greater in females than males, but significantly shorter in females in the Turkish population. The width of crista galli was significantly greater in females compared with males in the Turkish population (Table 5).

The mean width of the ethmoid plate was 9.88 mm for males in the Colombian population, and 13.74 mm in the Taiwanese population (Table 5). The mean was 9.61 mm for females in the Colombian population, and 13.47 mm in the Taiwanese population. There were no significant differences between males and females in both populations.

The distances between the right and left optic canals differed significantly between males and females in the Colombian, Chinese, Taiwanese, and Iranian populations. The distance was longer in males at between 24.10 and 30.0 mm compared with females at between 22.45 and 28.7 mm. The accuracy of sex classification based on this parameter was 67% in the Chinese population. The width of the cranial opening was longer in males in the Indian population and significantly different from that in females, with a mean of 4.34 mm in males compared with 3.99 mm in females (Table 5).

The maximum width of the anterior clinoid process (ACP) differed significantly between males and females in three studies conducted in the Colombian, Chinese, and Taiwanese populations, with 33.27–39.04 mm in males and 31.14–36.21 mm in females. Similarly, the minimum width of the ACP in these three populations differed significantly between sexes, with a range of 22.2–22.94 mm in males but 20.70–21.31 mm in females. The accuracies of sex classification in the Chinese population based on the maximum and minimum widths of ACP were 56.3% and 65.4%, respectively. However, a study by Salmanipour et al.13 found no significant difference in the minimum width of the ACP in the Iranian population. Another study by Natsis et al.18 also found no significant differences in the distance, length, and width of the ACP. Chou et al.12 found a significant difference in the distance of the ACP at p < 0.001 (Table 5).

Comparative morphometry of middle cranial fossa structures

The maximum distance between the right and left of the foramen ovale in the Chinese, Taiwanese, and Iranian populations differed significantly between males and females, with a longer range between 57.3 and 62.5 mm in males and 55.6–60.1 mm in females (Table 6). Another study in the Colombian population also found a significant difference between males and females, where the mean in females (8.64 mm) was longer than that in males (8.13 mm), and these values were relatively shorter than those in other populations. In the Indian population, Sthapak et al.19 found significant differences between males and females in terms of the length, breadth, and size of the foramen ovale on both sides (Table 6).

The distances between the right and left foramen rotundum were sexually dimorphic in the Colombian, Chinese, Taiwanese, and Iranian populations. In the Greek population, the width of the middle clinoid process did not differ significantly between males and females, where the mean width was 11.18 mm in males and 10.92 mm in females (Table 6).

The values were higher in the Taiwanese population, with 21.48 mm in males and 19.56 mm in females. The difference between males and females was significant in both populations. However, the minimum width of the posterior clinoid process was sexually dimorphic only in the Taiwanese and Colombian populations, and no significant differences were found in the Greek populations (Table 6).

Comparative morphometry of posterior cranial fossa structures

The width of the foramen magnum was the most widely documented parameter, with reports in seven studies, followed by its length. The width of the foramen magnum in seven populations ranged between 30.1 and 32.66 mm in males and 27.75–31.0 mm in females (Table 7). In Chinese, Taiwanese, Saudi Arabian, Nepalese, and Indian populations, the length of the foramen magnum ranged from 34.5 to 37.6 mm in males and 32.2–35.6 mm in females, but the measurements were shorter in the Colombian population, with a mean of 22.66 mm in males and 20.97 mm in females (Table 7). The accuracy of the foramen magnum width for sex classification was up to 67.3%, and up to 63.2% for its length (Table 7). Aljarrah et al.20 compared different parameters for the foramen magnum and showed that the accuracy for sex estimation was 62.5% for the length and width. The accuracy for the foramen magnum index calculated as the product of its length and width was lower at 52.3%, and the accuracy was highest for its area at 66.1%.

Morphoscopic and geometric morphometric analysis of cranial fossa structures

In the Danish population, geometric morphometric analysis of the posterior cranial fossa found significant differences in the Procrustes and Mahalanobis distances between males and females. The accuracies of sex estimation based on the shape of the posterior cranial fossa were 73.5% in males and 79.6% in females (Table 9).

Discussion

The accurate determination of biological sex from skeletal remains is a fundamental part of forensic anthropology, where morphoscopic, linear measurements, and geometric morphometrics play roles in sex estimation. This review examined the sexual dimorphism of cranial fossa structures by focusing on the anterior, middle, and posterior fossae, highlighting the significant differences in size and shape between males and females, as well as the variations in different cranial fossa structures and their accuracy for use in sex estimation. Morphoscopic analysis is a method focused on examining observable morphological traits, and it is essential in forensic anthropology for identifying and differentiating human skeletal remains. Morphoscopic analyses of the crista galli and foramen magnum conducted by Komut and Golpinar16 and Gargi et al.,22 respectively, identified and categorized the various morphological shapes of these structures and quantified their prevalence in percentage terms, thereby facilitating objective comparisons between males and females. The use of percentage-based analyses enhances precision, but morphoscopic analysis is still affected by interobserver variability because it relies greatly on visual assessment of skeletal traits, which can be influenced by an analyst's experience and interpretation.7 The various morphological shapes of cranial fossa structures reflect neurodevelopmental processes and region-specific, nonlinear growth during postnatal ontogeny, which may underlie the observed sex-related differences.8

The analysis of size based on mean differences in linear measurements is a key statistical analysis step in sex estimation. This approach provides insights into the average value of the variable considered and it is especially useful for comparing different groups or conditions.5 Overall, the dimensions were greater in males than females, as shown by the higher maximum width of ACP, distance of foramen ovale, and length of the foramen magnum (Table 4, Table 5, Table 6). These greater dimensions can be partly attributed to the differential effects of sex hormones: androgens promote radial bone expansion, whereas estrogens tend to restrict periosteal expansion.25 Furthermore, skeletal plasticity associated with the sexual division of labor and gender-based social differentiation in the past, where activities of males involved higher mechanical loadings with greater mobility, also contributed to the observed size differences between males and females.5,26

Linear measurements allow objective comparisons of the size of cranial structures, whereas geometric morphometrics facilitate detailed statistical analysis of the morphology of skeletal structures.5 Linear measurements of the posterior cranial fossa allow comparison of the size of its structures and the accuracy of their use in estimations for sex classification.10 Geometric morphometric analysis of the posterior cranial fossa provides additional value by statistically analyzing the shape of structures and the effects of size on shapes.15 Hence, combining both approaches provides more insights and increases the accuracy of sex estimation for skeletal remnants.

The accuracy of sex determination in forensic anthropology is greatly enhanced using population-specific data. Reference data derived from the same ancestral or geographic group improve the accuracy classification by considering intra-population morphological variation.27 For example, the maximum width of the clivus is significantly greater in males than females within both Chinese and Colombian populations. There are also notable differences in the mean clivus length between these populations, measuring 63.7 mm in Chinese males and longer compared with 48.11 mm in Colombian males, and 61.7 mm in Chinese females versus 43.48 mm in Colombian females, and these variations highlight the critical roles of population-specific data in the biological profiling of skeletal remains.10,11 Comparable trends are found in measurements of the crista galli length and distance of the foramen ovale (Tables 5 and 6). Skeletal morphological variation is often attributed to adaptive responses to environmental pressures. Geographic factors, including altitude and climate, influence patterns of skeletal growth and development, thereby contributing to population-specific anatomical differences that are relevant to forensic analysis.28 The robustness of male craniofacial features is associated with high latitude, which may explain the variations in clivus length.29

Cranial traits that exhibit pronounced sexual dimorphism contribute to increasing the reliability and accuracy of biological sex estimation, which is especially important for incomplete skeletal remains.30 The most sexually dimorphic structure of the anterior cranial fossa is the length of crista galli, with an accuracy of up to 83.7% in sex estimation. The accuracy of sex estimation using the middle cranial fossa represented by the distance of the foramen ovale is up to 63.9%, and the accuracy of the length of the clivus representing the posterior cranial fossa is up to 69% (Table 4). Accuracy of sex classification based on morphological traits exceeding 80% is regarded as high quality, and traits with lower accuracy should not be used as a single indicator in sex estimation.30 The accuracies of cranial fossa structures in sex estimation are comparable to those of extracranial structures, i.e., the accuracies of the zygomatic process, mastoid process, glabella, and superciliary arch for sex estimation range between 60.5% and 80.9%.31 Similarly, among the regions of the skull, the accuracy of the cranial fossa for sex classification ranges from 82% to 77.9%, which is comparable to other regions of the skull, i.e., the accuracies for the face, cranial vault, and cranial base range between 76% and 87%.5

Conclusion

According to this systematic review, sexual dimorphism exists in the cranial fossae, where males generally have larger and more robust cranial features than females. However, the variability of the findings across studies highlights the need for standardized methodologies and a broader range of population-based research. Cranial fossa analysis remains a useful tool in forensic anthropology but future studies should address the methodological limitations identified in this review to ensure that more reliable and consistent sex determination results are obtained for skeletal remains.
