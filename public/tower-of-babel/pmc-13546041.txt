Kidney Parenchymal Volume Loss and Functional Loss After Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy
Authors: Warit Charukumchon, Worapat Attawettayanon, Sarayuth Boonchai, Teeravut Tubtawee, Kamonwon Cattapan, Choosak Pripatnanont, Chanatipat Boonyok, Virote Chalieopanyarwong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeRenal stone is a common global disease. Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is the standard treatment for large stones but may cause renal parenchymal injury. Most studies have focused on global renal function rather than localized renal changes. This study evaluated renal parenchymal volume and renal function after PCNL, hypothesizing modest ipsilateral volume loss with preserved global glomerular filtration rate (GFR).Materials and MethodsThis study included 80 patients who underwent PCNL. All patients had preoperative and postoperative computed tomography (CT), with follow-up CT performed 3–6 months after surgery. Patients with postoperative complications related to stone burden were excluded. Renal parenchymal volume was measured using 3D volumetric software, and both volumetric and functional outcomes were analyzed.ResultsThe median age was 59 years, and 49% were male. Most patients had mild-to-moderate hydronephrosis with a median stone burden of 7.55mL. Single-tract PCNL was performed in 96% of cases, with a median estimated blood loss of 135mL. Amplatz sheath sizes of 28, 26, and 24Fr were used in 69%, 20%, and 7%, respectively. Median preoperative and new-baseline GFR values were 83.5 and 84.0mL/min/1.73m2, indicating preserved global renal function. Acute kidney injury occurred in 10%. Ipsilateral GFR decreased slightly (39.7 to 36.6mL/min/1.73m2), while ipsilateral renal volume declined from 123.7 to 115.6cm3 (median loss 5.6cm3, 4.3%). Contralateral kidney volume remained stable Larger Amplatz sheath size and female were independently associated with ipsilateral renal volume loss >15%.ConclusionPCNL caused modest ipsilateral renal volume loss while preserving global renal function. Larger Amplatz sheath size and female were associated with significant volume loss.

Introduction

Urolithiasis represents a pervasive and escalating global public health challenge. Calculi may manifest at various anatomical sites within the urinary tract, including the kidneys, ureters, bladder, or urethra. Clinical management strategies are dictated by stone localization; if left unaddressed, these stones may precipitate urinary tract obstruction, subsequent infection, or life-threatening urosepsis. Such complications not only impair renal function but also significantly compromise patient survival.

Renal stones are the most prevalent form of urolithiasis, affecting approximately 5–10% of the general population, with a high recurrence rate of up to 50%.1,2 Therapeutic interventions are tailored to stone size, composition, and patient physiology, ranging from conservative management and Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy (ESWL) to more invasive modalities such as Retrograde Intrarenal Surgery (RIRS), Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy (PCNL), and open surgery.3

PCNL remains the gold standard for treating large renal stones ≥ 2 cm and high stone burdens.2,4 Per the American Urological Association (AUA) guidelines, it is the primary recommendation for staghorn calculi.5 Despite achieving superior stone-free rates, PCNL is inherently invasive, with a complication profile ranging from 10% to 30%.2,5,6 While most adverse events are minor, the creation of the percutaneous access tract constitutes a Grade IV renal injury. This procedural trauma can lead to a quantifiable loss of renal parenchymal volume, potentially resulting in diminished postoperative function, particularly in patients requiring multiple access tracts or those with pre-existing renal insufficiency.7,8 Traditionally, postoperative renal outcomes have been assessed using global functional parameters such as serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). However, these measurements reflect the combined function of both kidneys and are influenced by compensatory hyperfiltration of the contralateral kidney, making them relatively insensitive to localized unilateral renal injury. In contrast, renal parenchymal volume provides a direct structural assessment of nephron mass and may detect irreversible tissue loss that is not apparent from global renal function alone Recent evidence indicates that PCNL may be associated with up to a 20% reduction in ipsilateral renal parenchymal volume, suggesting that substantial structural injury may occur despite preserved overall renal function.9

The primary objective of this study is to quantify the gross morphological alterations in both ipsilateral and contralateral renal volumes following PCNL. Additionally, we aim to evaluate longitudinal changes in split renal function. We hypothesize that despite a projected 5% reduction in ipsilateral renal volume due to procedural trauma, global GFR will remain stable This functional maintenance is likely attributable to either compensatory hypertrophy in the contralateral kidney or the functional restoration of the treated renal unit following the resolution of the obstruction.

Patients and Methods

Patient Population

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, Prince of Songkla University (REC. 68–107-10-4). Our study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki. Subsequently, a comprehensive retrospective review was performed of the Songklanagarind database (2019–2023) which identified 80 patients who perform PCNL. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants prior to study commencement. Study inclusion also required data regarding Computed tomography (CT) preoperative and post-operative PCNL (3 months after PCNL). The patients with postoperative complications related to stone condition were excluded from the study. Overall, patients qualified for parenchymal volume analysis.

Data Collection

Baseline characteristics including demographics, comorbidities, stone characteristics, functional outcomes, and parenchyma volume were collected. The CKD-EPI 2021 (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) equation formula was used to estimate glomerular filtration rate (GFR).10 Preoperative and postoperative parenchymal volumes measurements were obtained by three-dimensional volume-calculating software (Fujifilm Medical Systems Inc., Tokyo, Japan).11 Renal parenchymal volume analysis was independently performed by two urologists (WA and WC) and subsequently re-evaluated by two radiologist (TT and KC). The GFR of each kidney was estimated by multiplying the global GFR by the parenchymal volume analysis (PVA)-based split renal function. The PVA-based split renal function was calculated by dividing the parenchymal volume of each kidney (ipsilateral or contralateral) by the total renal parenchymal volume (ipsilateral + contralateral).

The new baseline GFR (NBGFR) was defined as the most recent GFR measured between 1 and 3 months after percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL). Acute kidney injury (AKI) was classified according to the RIFLE (Risk, Injury, Failure, Loss, End-stage kidney disease) criteria. Grade 1 (risk), grade 2 (injury), and grade 3 (failure) corresponded to 1.5–2.0-fold, 2.0–3.0-fold, and ≥3.0-fold increases in peak postoperative serum creatinine levels within the first 2 weeks after PCNL, respectively.12 Based on prior research, the cutoff value for a significant decrease in parenchymal volume was designated as greater than 15%.13

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Univariate and multivariable regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between clinical variables, planimetric parameters, and ipsilateral renal parenchymal volume loss. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.2). A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Renal stones represent the most prevalent form of urolithiasis, affecting approximately 5–10% of the population and exhibiting recurrence rates of up to 50%.1 Large or untreated stones may lead to pain, hematuria, urinary obstruction, and progressive renal deterioration. Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) remains the reference standard for the management of large renal calculi, providing superior stone-free rates with an acceptable safety profile.2,3 Nevertheless, PCNL is inherently invasive, and the creation of the percutaneous access tract constitutes a direct parenchymal injury that may result in postoperative scarring and loss of functional renal tissue.8,14 Although global renal function is often preserved,7,15 recent evidence indicates that PCNL may be associated with up to a 20% reduction in ipsilateral renal parenchymal volume—changes that may not be captured by conventional global functional metrics alone.9 To address this limitation, the present study employs semi-automated three-dimensional volumetric analysis to objectively quantify parenchymal volume changes in both the treated and contralateral kidneys before and after PCNL, thereby providing a more precise structural correlate of functional renal outcomes.16

Our main findings were that single-tract PCNL was performed in 96% of cases, with a median estimated blood loss of 135 mL. Amplatz sheath sizes of 28 Fr, 26 Fr, and 24 Fr were used in 69%, 20%, and 7% of patients, respectively. Global renal function was preserved and slightly improved after PCNL, with the median GFR increasing from 83.5 to 84.0 mL/min/1.73 m2. Postoperative acute kidney injury occurred in 10% of patients, with grade 2–3 AKI observed in 2%. In contrast, localized analyses demonstrated measurable ipsilateral renal impairment, with a median reduction in renal parenchymal volume of 5.6 cm3 (4.3%) and a corresponding median ipsilateral GFR loss of 0.83 mL/min/1.73 m2 (2%). The contralateral kidney volume remained stable, indicating preservation of compensatory renal reserve. Larger Amplatz sheath size and female were associated with ipsilateral kidney volume loss greater than 15%.

During percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), a percutaneous tract is created from the skin to the renal collecting system to facilitate insertion of an Amplatz sheath. Renal parenchymal injury occurs sequentially during initial needle access, tract dilation, and subsequent sheath compression. Most clinical evidence suggests that PCNL does not result in significant long-term deterioration of global renal function or substantial loss of healthy renal parenchyma in the majority of patients.13,17 However, advanced imaging modalities—particularly three-dimensional (3D) volumetric planimetry and dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA) renal scintigraphy—demonstrate a measurable but localized structural impact on the treated kidney. Wang et al9 reported an average renal parenchymal volume decline of 21% after PCNL while overall kidney function was preserved. In contrast, our study demonstrated that contemporary volumetric analysis revealed a more modest ipsilateral renal parenchymal volume reduction of approximately 3–6% (3–5 mL). This structural change corresponded to a localized decline in ipsilateral glomerular filtration rate (GFR) of approximately 0.5–3 mL/min/1.73 m2, whereas global GFR remained preserved. (Figure 2A) Our findings therefore confirm that PCNL is associated with measurable ipsilateral parenchymal volume loss, but without clinically significant impairment of overall renal function. The discrepancy between our results and those reported by Wang et al may be explained by differences in surgical technique. In our cohort, the majority of patients underwent single-tract PCNL, whereas the median number of tracts in Wang et al’s study was two. Multiple access tracts may result in greater cumulative parenchymal injury and consequently larger volume reduction.18

After PCNL, several factors may influence tract-related renal parenchymal loss and subsequent renal functional decline. These include large stone burden, prior PCNL, use of larger Amplatz sheaths, multiple access tracts, and intraoperative bleeding. In our study, larger Amplatz sheath size, and female sex were identified as independent factors associated with ipsilateral renal parenchymal volume loss >15%. Previous studies have reported that multiple access tracts are associated with greater parenchymal volume reduction.13–19 However, in our cohort, the majority of patients underwent single-tract PCNL. Therefore, multiple access tracts were not included in the univariate or multivariable analyses due to the limited number of cases.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) commonly occurs after renal surgery, particularly in patients with a solitary kidney.16,20,21 In our cohort, AKI occurred in 10% of patients following PCNL, with most cases classified as RIFLE grade 1. Importantly, all affected patients returned to their baseline GFR within three months. The impact of AKI on functional recovery in our study appeared to be minimal.22 Although some studies have suggested that kidneys exposed to AKI may be more vulnerable to progressive long-term functional decline,23 our data did not demonstrate a significant adverse effect on longitudinal renal function. However, the number of patients with AKI in our cohort was relatively small, and the follow-up duration was limited. Therefore, further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are warranted to better clarify the long-term impact of postoperative AKI on renal functional outcomes.

Our study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center with a retrospective design, which may introduce selection and attrition biases. Second, the follow-up duration was limited to an intermediate timeframe, and longer-term outcomes could not be fully assessed. Missing data were also a concern, and we were unable to obtain complete and reliable information regarding subsequent treatments. In addition, as with any retrospective imaging-based study, CT protocols were not standardized. The majority of scans were non-contrast studies, which may have affected the accuracy of renal parenchymal volume measurements. In our cohort, 96% of patients underwent single-tract PCNL, whereas only a small proportion required multi-tract access. Consequently, the limited number of multi-tract procedures resulted in insufficient statistical power to evaluate the independent effect of tract multiplicity on renal parenchymal volume loss. Finally, we were unable to account for potential intermittent stone-related obstruction, which may have influenced renal parenchymal volume and functional outcomes.

Conclusions

PCNL is associated with a measurable but modest reduction in ipsilateral renal parenchymal volume. Importantly, overall renal function remained preserved, and contralateral kidney volume remained stable, suggesting adequate compensatory reserve. Larger Amplatz sheath size and female GFR were independently associated with significant ipsilateral parenchymal volume loss (>15%). Contemporary volumetric analysis provides valuable insight into subtle unilateral parenchymal injury that may not be detected by conventional global renal function parameters alone.
