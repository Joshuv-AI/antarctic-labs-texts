Student perceptions of a three-minute thesis presentation activity in undergraduate pharmacology education: a post-intervention evaluation
Authors: Eman O. Alsheikh, Saad M. Wali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommunication skills are a core competency in pharmacy education; however, traditional presentation approaches often place limited emphasis on clarity, engagement, and meaningful integration of knowledge. This study evaluated student perceptions and immediate post-activity knowledge following implementation of a Three-Minute Thesis (3MT) group presentation task among third-year Doctor of Pharmacy (Pharm.D) undergraduate students in a pharmacology course at Umm Al-Qura University, Makkah, Saudi Arabia. A single-group, post-intervention, cross-sectional evaluation design was used. Learning outcomes were assessed using a structured 20-item evaluation questionnaire measuring five domains (engagement, perceived learning, communication confidence, satisfaction, and peer perception) and a five-item knowledge quiz administered immediately after the activity. A total of 59 students participated (79.7% response rate). Perceptions were highly positive across all domains, with the highest mean scores observed for satisfaction ($M = 4.37$, $SD = 0.80$) and peer perception ($M = 4.32$, $SD = 0.79$), followed by engagement ($M = 4.21$, $SD = 0.74$), perceived learning ($M = 4.16$, $SD = 0.81$), and communication confidence ($M = 4.11$, $SD = 0.77$). Subscale reliability was strong (Cronbach’s $\alpha = 0.79–0.92$). Quiz performance reflected high immediate post-activity knowledge, with a mean score of 3.86 out of 5 ($SD = 1.18$), and 72.9% of participants achieving scores of 4 or 5. The 3MT-style format is a feasible and highly engaging active-learning strategy in undergraduate pharmacology. While the descriptive single-group design limits direct causal inferences, the strong student reception and solid immediate knowledge profiles demonstrate its pedagogical viability and educational value. Future controlled, multi-institutional studies are warranted to evaluate long-term impacts.

Background

In global healthcare systems, in which pharmacists are progressively taking on more patient-facing and interdisciplinary roles, effective communication has become a key competency. Organisations such as the World Health Organisation and the International Pharmaceutical Federation emphasise that medication safety depends not only on scientific knowledge but also on the ability to communicate complex information to diverse audiences. As pharmacy education increasingly adopts competency-based models, universities are expected to equip students with robust communication, critical-thinking, and knowledge-synthesis skills that align with real-world professional demands [1,2].

In learning environments, conventional instructional tools may be insufficient for developing these competencies. Traditional text-based reading tasks and slide-heavy presentations often emphasise content coverage over critical engagement, information extraction, and synthesis. They are frequently lengthy, contain excessive detail, and may overwhelm learners, encourage passive learning and dilute key concepts. According to Cognitive Load Theory, presenting large volumes of information without effective organisation can impair comprehension and long-term retention [3]. Furthermore, reliance on passive reading and extended presentations provides limited opportunities for students to practice concise scientific communication, critical appraisal of sources, and the integration of knowledge into clear, audience-focused messages [4,5]. These limitations underscore the need for more interactive, structured, and skill-oriented communication approaches within undergraduate health-sciences education.

To address this challenge, educational institutions have implemented short, structured presentation formats such as the 3-minute thesis (3MT). The 3MT format was originally developed at the University of Queensland, requiring participants to present complex concepts using a single slide within three minutes. This framework promotes disciplined organisation, clarity of message, and audience-centred delivery [6–8]. Research in health sciences education suggests that brief, structured presentations can be effective in engaging students and developing their summarisation and presentation confidence [9]. The format also has the potential to reduce cognitive load and support retention through the principles of microlearning, which emphasise short, task-based activities. [9,10].

Although there has been growing interest in using the 3MT model, most applications have been within postgraduate and research-intensive environments. Comparatively little attention has been given to undergraduate applications, especially in content-dense subjects like pharmacology. This gap is significant given the need to help students communicate mechanisms of action, therapeutic uses, and adverse effects both accurately and concisely. Moreover, most existing evaluations rely primarily on subjective assessments of student perceptions, despite evidence that perceived learning does not necessarily correspond to actual learning outcomes. A more rigorous evaluation approach therefore requires combining self-reported perceptions with objective measures of knowledge [11–13].

Given these gaps, it is important to investigate the potential of the 3MT format to support communication and learning in undergraduate pharmacy education. Such a structured communication intervention is particularly well suited to pharmacology education, a discipline characterised by dense scientific content where clinical clarity and precise knowledge translation are essential [4,5]. Understanding how students engage with the activity and its relationship with their confidence and knowledge may inform curriculum design. Therefore, this study aims to describe student perceptions following the implementation of a 3MT-style presentation activity within a third-year pharmacology course. Specifically, the study examines whether students reported positive engagement, perceived learning, communication confidence, and satisfaction. In addition, it reports immediate post-activity knowledge as measured by performance on a short pharmacology quiz.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a quantitative, cross-sectional, single-group, post-intervention educational evaluation design. Data were collected at a single time point immediately following the implementation of a structured 3MT-style presentation activity. The study was conducted among third-year Doctor of Pharmacy (Pharm.D) undergraduate students enroled in a pharmacology course at Umm Al-Qura University, Makkah, Saudi Arabia. The course covers multiple therapeutic areas, including cardiovascular pharmacotherapy (hypertension, angina, congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, arrhythmia control, and stroke) and haematological pharmacology (anaemia disorders). The 3MT-style activity was integrated into the existing course schedule as a planned active-learning session, delivered after completion of the relevant pharmacology teaching topics. The activity was formative and ungraded.

Participants

A total of 74 third-year Pharm.D students were enroled in the pharmacology course during the study semester. Of these, 59 were present on the day of the 3MT activity and all agreed to participate (response rate: 79.7%; 15 students were absent). No students declined participation. The sample was gender-homogeneous due to the single-gender admission policy of the campus where the study was conducted. Students in this cohort are typically aged 20–22 years, based on the standard progression of the Pharm.D programme; however, age was not formally collected. Inclusion criteria comprised enrolment in the pharmacology course during the study semester, attendance on the day of the 3MT-style activity, and provision of digital written informed consent. Students who were absent during the session or declined participation were excluded. A convenience sampling approach was employed, which is commonly used in educational research when intact classes are studied due to practical and ethical considerations. Although this sampling method limits generalisability to other institutional contexts, it enhances ecological validity by evaluating the intervention within an authentic course setting.

3MT activity

Students were divided into 10 small groups, each consisting of 5–6 students, and each group was assigned a specific pharmacology topic (congestive heart failure, hypertension, post-myocardial infarction management, arrhythmia control, and anaemia disorders). Groups were given four weeks to prepare for the activity. All group members collaborated on content development; however, only one representative from each group delivered the presentation. The remaining students participated through group preparation, peer observation, and in-class discussion. This adaptation differs from the original individual 3MT format, and the term “3MT-style” is used throughout to reflect this modification. Clear guidelines were provided for the activity. Each group was permitted to use only one static slide, with no animations or embedded videos allowed. The content was required to focus on mechanisms of action, key adverse effects, and clinically relevant considerations. Presentations had to be delivered using language that was clear and appropriate for peers at the same academic level. Although rehearsal was encouraged informally, no structured rehearsal sessions were mandated due to course time constraints. On the presentation day, the instructor strictly enforced the three-minute time limit using a visible timer. No formal rubric or structured feedback was provided to students after their presentations.

Data collection instruments

3MT evaluation questionnaire

A 20-item self-administered questionnaire was developed to assess student perceptions of the 3MT-style activity across five domains: engagement (4 items), perceived learning (4 items), communication confidence (4 items), satisfaction with the 3MT format (5 items), and peer perception and learning environment (3 items). Items were generated based on a review of existing literature on student engagement, communication skills, and active learning in health professions education [1,2,4,5,14,15], and adapted to the 3MT context and pharmacology content. Face and content validity were established through independent review by three pharmacology faculty members, who each rated the relevance of every item to its intended domain and assessed item clarity and domain alignment. Reviewers provided their ratings independently, and items flagged by more than one reviewer for ambiguous wording or weak domain fit were revised by the study team before pilot testing; a formal content validity index was not calculated, which is acknowledged as a limitation of the instrument development process. The instrument was pilot-tested with a separate group of students (n = 7) who were excluded from the final study sample. Following the pilot, minor refinements were made, ambiguous wording in two items was clarified, and items were reorganised to improve alignment with their respective domains. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.70 was considered the threshold for acceptable internal consistency. Corrected item-total correlations were computed to assess item discrimination. The full questionnaire is provided as supplementary material (Appendix A).

Immediate post-activity knowledge quiz

Immediate post-activity knowledge was assessed using a five-item multiple-choice quiz administered directly after the 3MT presentations during the same class session. The quiz was developed by the course instructor and focused on pharmacology topics covered during the 3MT presentations, including drug indications, mechanisms of action, adverse effects, and clinical management of anaemia disorders. Each question consisted of one correct answer and three plausible distractors. Content validity was supported by alignment with the course learning outcomes and the specific topics presented during the 3MT activity, and by expert review by the course instructor. Although brief, the five-item assessment was considered appropriate for measuring immediate post-activity knowledge in a classroom-based educational intervention, while minimising testing fatigue and disruption to instructional time. No baseline or pre-test assessment was administered; consequently, quiz scores reflect post-activity knowledge status rather than measured improvement attributable to the intervention.

Data analysis

The data were analysed using SPSS. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations (SD), frequencies, and percentages, were calculated for all questionnaire items, domain scores, and quiz scores. Subscale scores were computed by averaging the items assigned to each domain. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α for each subscale and for the overall instrument, with α ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable and α ≥ 0.80 considered good. Corrected item-total correlations were computed for each subscale to evaluate item discrimination. As the questionnaire and quiz were administered anonymously, individual-level linkage between perception scores and quiz performance was not possible; therefore, correlation analyses were not performed. There were no missing data (59 complete responses across all 20 items and all 5 quiz questions).

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 74 third-year Pharm.D students enroled in the course, 59 were present on the day of the 3MT-style activity and all consented to participate, yielding a response rate of 79.7%. No students declined participation. The sample consisted entirely of male students, consistent with the single-gender campus policy. Students in this cohort are typically aged 20–22 years based on standard programme progression, though individual age data were not formally collected. All participants shared a homogeneous academic background, as they were enroled in the same pharmacology module and had been exposed to identical course content.

Overall, students reported positive perceptions of the 3MT-style activity across all evaluated domains. Satisfaction achieved the highest mean score (M = 4.37, SD = 0.80), indicating that students found the 3MT-style format enjoyable, motivating, and more engaging than conventional presentations. Peer perception also scored highly (M = 4.32, SD = 0.79), suggesting that students perceived their classmates’ presentations as clear, effective, and delivered within a supportive learning environment. Communication confidence yielded the lowest mean score (M = 4.11, SD = 0.77); although still positive, this finding suggests that while students perceived benefits in clarity and pacing, some may have felt less confident about delivering scientific content under strict time constraints. It should be noted that communication confidence items captured perceptions of the overall 3MT experience, including preparation and peer observation; however, only 10 of the 59 students delivered the oral presentation, which may have influenced responses in this domain. Engagement (M = 4.21, SD = 0.74) and perceived learning (M = 4.16, SD = 0.81) were likewise high, reflecting strong motivation, active involvement, and cognitive stimulation.

Immediate post-activity knowledge quiz performance

Quiz performance was strong, with 72.9% of students achieving scores of 4 or 5 out of 5 (Table 2). Only one student scored 0, and few scored 1 or 2, suggesting that difficulties with the assessed concepts were limited to a minority of participants. At the item level, the highest correct response rate was observed for the question on preferred iron preparations for iron-deficiency anaemia (93.2%), followed by adverse effects of oral iron supplements (84.7%) and factors enhancing iron absorption (81.4%). Lower correct response rates were noted for the mechanism of action of hydroxyurea in sickle cell disease (76.3%) and adverse effects of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (50.8%), suggesting that these topics may require additional reinforcement.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study described student perceptions and immediate post-activity knowledge following the implementation of a structured 3MT-style presentation activity within a third-year undergraduate pharmacology course. Overall, the findings indicate that the 3MT-style format was well received and associated with high levels of student satisfaction, engagement, perceived learning, and post-activity knowledge. The strongest ratings were observed for satisfaction and peer perception, suggesting that students valued both the concise structure of the activity and the quality of learning derived from peer-delivered explanations. Engagement and perceived learning were also rated highly, indicating that the activity promoted active cognitive involvement rather than passive content consumption. Although communication confidence yielded comparatively lower scores, it remained clearly positive, reflecting meaningful, though incremental, perceived gains in students’ self-efficacy when communicating complex pharmacological content under strict time constraints. Notably, the lower confidence scores may partly reflect the fact that only 10 of the 59 participants delivered the oral presentation, while the remaining students contributed through preparation and observation. Beyond this presenter-to-non-presenter imbalance, several additional and non-mutually exclusive explanations merit consideration. The strict three-minute time limit, while pedagogically central to the format, may have heightened performance anxiety for students with little prior experience condensing scientific content into such a constrained format. Most participants are also unlikely to have had substantial prior practice with concise, time-limited scientific communication before this activity, which could independently temper confidence regardless of presenter status. Finally, the absence of structured rubric-based feedback after the presentations means that students received no formal external validation of their performance, which may have limited the consolidation of confidence that such feedback typically supports. These explanations are speculative given the present design and warrant direct investigation in future studies.

Alignment with active learning and learning theories

These findings align closely with the broader literature on active learning in health sciences education. Constructivist learning theories posit that knowledge is developed through active engagement, reflection, and social interaction rather than passive reception [16,17]. The 3MT-style activity required students to synthesise pharmacological mechanisms, prioritise core concepts, and translate them into audience-appropriate explanations, thereby fostering deeper cognitive processing. Consistent with prior evidence, such engagement-driven learning approaches are associated with improved motivation, conceptual understanding, and academic performance compared with traditional lecture-based instruction [15,18].

It is also useful to situate the 3MT-style activity alongside other active-learning approaches more commonly described in pharmacy education, such as peer teaching, team-based learning, flipped-classroom designs, and case-based discussions. Peer teaching, in particular, has been reported to improve accessibility of complex pharmacology content and to foster reciprocal learning among students, broadly consistent with the peer-perception findings of the present study. Unlike team-based learning or flipped-classroom models, which typically distribute structured pre-class preparation and in-class problem-solving across an entire term, the 3MT-style activity offered a single, time-bounded, high-constraint exercise focused specifically on disciplined synthesis and audience-oriented communication rather than on iterative content mastery. This positions the format as a complementary, low-resource addition to the active-learning repertoire in pharmacology curricula rather than a replacement for more sustained approaches, and future work could usefully compare its relative contribution to communication skill development against these established methods.

Cognitive load reduction and knowledge retention

The strong engagement and perceived learning outcomes observed in this study can also be interpreted through the lens of Cognitive Load Theory. Pharmacology is inherently content-dense, and traditional slide-heavy presentations may overwhelm learners by imposing excessive extraneous cognitive load. The strict time and slide limitations of the 3MT format likely reduced this burden, compelling students to focus on essential mechanisms, clinical relevance, and therapeutic implications. This aligns with Sweller’s assertion that well-structured, concise learning tasks facilitate schema construction and knowledge consolidation [3]. The relatively high quiz performance, with the majority of students achieving near-maximum scores, is consistent with the view that the activity supported immediate post-activity knowledge, although the absence of a pre-test or control group precludes causal attribution [18].

Short, structured communication and microlearning

The findings also reinforce evidence from microlearning and micro-teaching literature, which highlights the educational benefits of short, focused learning activities. Time-limited presentations encourage learners to distinguish essential content from peripheral details, enhancing conceptual clarity, metacognitive awareness, and self-regulation [9,10,19]. In pharmacology education, where students must routinely condense complex mechanisms into clinically actionable explanations, such structured brevity is particularly relevant. The present results suggest that the 3MT format supports not only comprehension but also disciplined organisation and prioritisation of scientific knowledge.

Peer learning and communication skill development

Peer learning emerged as a key mechanism underpinning the observed outcomes. High peer-perception scores indicate that students benefited from both delivering and listening to peer presentations. According to social learning theory, observing peers model communication strategies and conceptual explanations enhances understanding and confidence through vicarious learning and social comparison [20,21]. Previous studies have shown that peer-delivered explanations may improve accessibility of complex concepts, as students often use language, analogies, and examples that resonate more strongly with their cohort [22–24]. This effect is particularly valuable in pharmacology, where abstract mechanisms can present substantial cognitive barriers.

The group-based adaptation of the 3MT format also warrants specific consideration, as it substantially changes the educational experience being evaluated relative to the original individual model. Because only one representative per group delivered the oral presentation while the remaining members contributed through content preparation and peer observation, the group format likely distributed engagement and accountability unevenly across participants: presenters experienced the time pressure and public-speaking demands characteristic of the traditional 3MT exercise directly, whereas non-presenting students engaged primarily through collaborative preparation and vicarious learning. This distinction may explain why communication confidence scores, though positive, were comparatively lower than other domains, since most respondents reported on a largely observational experience rather than on their own delivery. At the same time, the group format reflects the collaborative, team-based nature of pharmacy practice and lowered the logistical burden of delivering an oral exercise to a full class of 59 students within a single session, which would not have been feasible under the original individual format given course time constraints. Future iterations could rotate presenter responsibility across sessions or topics to allow more students to experience direct delivery while preserving the practical advantages of group preparation.

Relevance to pharmacy practice and professional competencies

This study also contributes to the growing literature on the 3MT model in higher education. While most existing research has focused on postgraduate populations, reporting improvements in confidence, audience engagement, and scientific storytelling [8,11–13,25], the present findings extend these observations to undergraduate pharmacy education. They suggest that the pedagogical potential of the 3MT-style format may be translated to content-intensive courses such as pharmacology, where clarity, precision, and integration of theory with clinical relevance are essential.

From a professional perspective, the development of concise communication skills is directly relevant to pharmacy practice. Pharmacists frequently engage in brief yet high-stakes communication during patient counselling, interprofessional collaboration, and medication safety interventions [26–28]. The 3MT-style activity simulates these real-world demands by requiring students to communicate accurate, evidence-based information clearly and efficiently under time constraints. As such, it aligns well with international competency frameworks, including those of the International Pharmaceutical Federation (FIP) and accreditation standards that emphasise communication as a core professional competency [29].

Beyond discrete competencies, the activity may also contribute to broader professional identity formation. The core demands placed on students—condensing complex pharmacological information, prioritising the most clinically essential points, and adapting language to a specific audience—mirror tasks that pharmacists perform routinely in practice, including medication counselling delivered within limited consultation time, concise medication reconciliation summaries communicated to other clinicians, brief interdisciplinary handoffs, and structured clinical case presentations during ward rounds or case conferences. Repeated practice with these constraints during undergraduate training may help students begin to internalise the communicative habits expected of practicing pharmacists, even though the present single-session design cannot establish whether such gains are durable or transfer to authentic clinical encounters. Framing the 3MT-style activity in these terms may strengthen its relevance not only as a communication exercise but as an early, low-stakes opportunity for professional socialisation within the pharmacy curriculum.

Curricular and pedagogical implications

Beyond its educational value, the 3MT model offers practical advantages for curriculum design. The format is time-efficient, allowing multiple groups to present within a single session without compromising instructional time—an important consideration in pharmacology curricula characterised by heavy content loads. Additionally, the format lends itself to formative assessment, enabling instructors to rapidly gauge students’ conceptual understanding and communication skills. It may also be adapted for use in drug-class summaries, case-based discussions, capstone projects, and research dissemination activities across pharmacy programmes. From an implementation standpoint, the activity as delivered required instructor time for assigning topics, setting the four-week preparation window, and supervising 10 group presentations within a single class period, but did not require specialised equipment beyond a single static slide per group and a visible timer, keeping logistical demands modest relative to more elaborate active-learning formats. Scalability to larger cohorts would likely require either additional class sessions to accommodate more presenting groups or, alternatively, rotation schemes in which only a subset of groups present in any given session while others are evaluated through written summaries or peer assessment, an approach that future implementations and studies should formally evaluate. Instructors considering adoption should also anticipate the need to coordinate group formation and topic allocation in advance, since these logistical steps determine much of the preparation burden ahead of the presentation day itself.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The integration of subjective perceptions with an objective knowledge measure provides a more comprehensive evaluation than reliance on self-report alone. The evaluation questionnaire demonstrated strong internal consistency and adequate item discrimination across domains. The intervention was implemented in a structured, reproducible manner within an authentic course setting, enhancing ecological validity.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted at a single institution with a gender-homogeneous sample (all male), which limits generalisability to other settings. Second, knowledge was assessed only immediately after the activity, and longer-term learning outcomes remain unknown. Third, the absence of a control group or pre-test precludes causal conclusions about the effectiveness of the 3MT-style format; the findings reflect post-activity perceptions and knowledge status rather than measured improvement. Fourth, the questionnaire and quiz were administered anonymously, preventing individual-level linkage between perception and knowledge scores. Fifth, the group presentation format means that only 10 students delivered the oral presentation, while 49 participated through preparation and observation; presenter identity was not coded in the dataset, precluding subgroup analysis. Sixth, self-reported perceptions may be subject to social desirability bias. Seventh, all domain means exceeded 4.0 on a 5-point scale, suggesting potential ceiling effects; while this pattern may reflect a genuinely positive reception of the activity, it could also be partly attributable to social desirability bias, acquiescence bias, a novelty effect associated with an unfamiliar activity format, or limited discriminatory power of the evaluation instrument, and the present data cannot distinguish among these possibilities. Eighth, the five-item quiz, while appropriate for minimising testing fatigue and instructional disruption in a classroom setting, has limited measurement precision: with only five items, a single incorrect response changes the overall score by 20%, which constrains the ability to draw fine-grained conclusions about knowledge acquisition; item difficulty and discrimination indices were not calculated for the quiz, and future studies should report these metrics or otherwise ground brief assessments in the educational measurement literature to better justify their precision. Finally, formal construct validity assessment (e.g. exploratory factor analysis) was not feasible given the sample size (n = 59 for 20 items) and is recommended for future studies with larger samples.

Future research directions

Future research should address these limitations by incorporating multi-institutional, gender-diverse samples and longitudinal designs to assess sustained knowledge outcomes and skill development. Comparative studies including traditional presentation formats or control groups would strengthen causal inference. Studies should record presenter status to enable subgroup analysis comparing presenters and non-presenters. Rubric-based or qualitative assessments of presentation quality could offer deeper insights into communication skill acquisition; rather than relying predominantly on student self-report, such assessments should draw on standardised rubrics completed by faculty or trained peer assessors, or on objective structured educational exercises, to evaluate actual communication competence more directly. Longitudinal follow-up several weeks or months after the intervention would similarly allow knowledge retention, as distinct from immediate post-activity knowledge, to be assessed directly. Extending the intervention across multiple pharmacology topics and therapeutic systems would further clarify its applicability within complex curricula. Larger sample sizes would also permit confirmatory psychometric analyses, including exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses of the evaluation questionnaire.

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that the 3MT-style presentation format is a feasible and favourably received active-learning approach in undergraduate pharmacology education. Students reported high levels of engagement, satisfaction, and perceived learning, while post-activity quiz performance was consistent with satisfactory immediate knowledge. By requiring students to synthesise complex pharmacological concepts and communicate them clearly within strict constraints, the 3MT-style model engaged students in conceptual integration, peer learning, and concise scientific communication—skills relevant to contemporary pharmacy practice. Although communication confidence was comparatively lower than other domains, it remained clearly positive, suggesting that students perceived some development in their self-efficacy for structured oral communication. Taken together, these findings provide preliminary evidence that the 3MT-style format is a promising, low-cost instructional approach well suited to content-intensive courses such as pharmacology. While the lack of a baseline pre-test prevents the formal verification of direct causal improvements, the strong student reception and successful post-activity knowledge profiles demonstrate the immediate pedagogical viability and educational value of this format in undergraduate pharmacy curricula.

Limitations and future recommendations

As detailed in the Strengths and Limitations and Future Research Directions sections above, the principal constraints of this study are the single-institution, gender-homogeneous sample, the absence of a control group and pre-test, the anonymous data collection precluding individual-level linkage between perception and knowledge scores, the unequal presenter-to-non-presenter ratio inherent to the group format, and the limited measurement precision of a five-item knowledge quiz. Consequently, these findings serve as an important baseline description of student perceptions and immediate knowledge performance, establishing a foundation for future research to test formal causal educational effectiveness. Future research employing multi-institutional, gender-diverse, and longitudinal designs, incorporating control or comparison groups, recording presenter status, and using rubric-based or objective structured assessments of communication competence is recommended to evaluate the sustained effects of the 3MT-style format on learning outcomes, communication competence, and professional preparedness.
