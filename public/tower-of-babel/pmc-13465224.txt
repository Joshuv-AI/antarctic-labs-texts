Personalized orbital decompression surgery for thyroid-associated orbitopathy: a comprehensive retrospective analysis of efficacy, safety, and surgical strategy
Authors: Pei Wang, Zijian Han, Xiuhong Li, Ru Lin, Yan Xun, Zexi Sang, Hongfeng Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of personalized multimodal orbital decompression for thyroid-associated orbitopathy (TAO) via large-scale retrospective analysis, and to optimize surgical strategies accordingly.MethodsA retrospective cohort study included 199 TAO patients (263 eyes) who underwent orbital decompression between September 2021 and June 2025. Patients were stratified into four groups by surgical modality: transconjunctival fat decompression, endoscopic transnasal medial wall decompression, combined transconjunctival medial-lateral wall decompression, and combined endoscopic transnasal medial-lateral wall decompression. Primary outcomes were changes in LogMAR best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), proptosis, and intraocular pressure (IOP); secondary outcomes included ocular motility, diplopia, and postoperative complication rates.ResultsAll surgical strategies resulted in significant clinical improvements. Overall mean proptosis was reduced from 21.16 ± 2.75 mm to 15.01 ± 1.86 mm (p < 0.0001). Mean BCVA improved from 0.67 ± 0.68 LogMAR to 0.34 ± 0.42 LogMAR (p < 0.0001), with the most significant improvement seen in patients with preoperative Dysthyroid Optic Neuropathy (DON). DON was present in 46.4% of eyes, with the highest prevalence in Groups 2 (76.6%) and 4 (62.2%). Combined bony wall decompression (Groups 3 & 4) yielded the greatest proptosis reduction (7.67 mm and 7.29 mm, respectively), significantly more than single-modality decompression (p < 0.0001). The overall complication rate was 9.1%, with new-onset or worsening diplopia being the most common, but often transient or successfully managed with subsequent strabismus surgery. Endoscopic medial wall decompression (Group 2) was associated with the lowest complication rate (4.3%).ConclusionIn this large single-centre retrospective cohort, individualized orbital decompression was associated with significant improvements in proptosis, visual function, IOP, ocular motility, and diplopia-related outcomes in patients with TAO. Combined medial–lateral wall decompression achieved greater proptosis reduction, whereas procedures involving medial wall decompression were associated with visual improvement in eyes with DON. These findings support an indication-driven approach to surgical selection, while the retrospective design, baseline differences among groups, and eye-level analysis should be considered when interpreting comparative results.

Introduction

Thyroid-associated orbitopathy (TAO), also known as Graves’ orbitopathy (GO), is the most common extrathyroidal manifestation of Graves’ disease and a significant cause of functional impairment and psychosocial distress worldwide [1]. This complex autoimmune disorder primarily targets orbital and periorbital tissues, leading to a cascade of inflammation and fibrosis. Epidemiological studies show a marked gender predilection, with an incidence of approximately 16 cases per 100,000 women and 2.9 cases per 100,000 men, making it the leading cause of both unilateral and bilateral proptosis in adults [2]. The clinical burden of TAO is substantial, with manifestations ranging from disfiguring cosmetic changes to sight-threatening complications, profoundly impacting patients’ quality of life.

The pathophysiology of TAO is still under intense investigation but is understood to be a multifactorial process involving genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers (such as smoking), and a dysregulated autoimmune response [3]. The core pathogenic event is the aberrant activation of orbital fibroblasts by autoantibodies, primarily those targeting the thyrotropin receptor (TSHR). Mediated by a complex network of T-cell subsets (including Th1, Th2, and Th17) and various pro-inflammatory cytokines, this activation triggers two main pathological processes: adipogenesis (proliferation and differentiation of orbital fat cells) and the synthesis of hydrophilic glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) like hyaluronic acid [4, 5]. The accumulation of newly formed adipose tissue and the osmotic edema induced by GAGs, coupled with the inflammatory swelling of extraocular muscles, collectively lead to a dramatic increase in the volume of orbital contents. Within the rigid confines of the bony orbit, this volume expansion results in a corresponding rise in intraorbital pressure, precipitating a cascade of characteristic clinical sequelae: periorbital edema, eyelid retraction, disabling proptosis, restrictive strabismus leading to diplopia, and, in its most severe form, dysthyroid optic neuropathy (DON) [6–11].

The management of TAO follows a biphasic principle, tailored to the inflammatory activity of the disease. The active inflammatory phase is typically managed with anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive therapies, including high-dose systemic glucocorticoids, orbital radiotherapy, and, more recently, targeted biologics such as the insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor (IGF-1R) inhibitor teprotumumab, which has shown remarkable efficacy in reducing proptosis and inflammation [12–15]. However, once the disease enters the inactive, fibrotic phase, residual deformities such as strabismus and significant proptosis often persist, necessitating surgical rehabilitation. Orbital decompression surgery is the definitive intervention for addressing severe, disfiguring proptosis and for urgently relieving optic nerve compression in patients with DON [16]. The fundamental goal of this surgery is to expand the effective volume of the bony orbit, allowing the hypertrophied orbital contents to decompress, thereby reducing proptosis and alleviating pressure on the optic nerve.

The techniques for orbital decompression have evolved significantly. While early procedures focused on single-wall removal, modern techniques, since the work of Ogura and Walsh, have generally adopted multi-wall decompressions to achieve more significant and predictable results [17]. Surgeons today can choose from a variety of approaches, including removal of the orbital floor (via the maxillary sinus), the medial wall (via the ethmoid sinus), the lateral wall, or a combination thereof in a so-called “balanced decompression.” Additionally, surgeons may opt to remove excess orbital fat as an alternative or adjunct to bony decompression [18]. Surgical access has also diversified, ranging from traditional external incisions (e.g., Lynch, lateral canthotomy, transconjunctival) to minimally invasive transnasal endoscopic techniques, which have gained popularity for their lack of external scarring and excellent exposure of the medial orbit and apex [19, 20].

This wealth of surgical options, however, presents a clinical challenge: how to select the optimal procedure for a given patient. The ideal surgical plan must be highly personalized, balancing the desired degree of proptosis reduction against the risk of iatrogenic complications, especially new-onset or worsening diplopia. The decision-making process must integrate multiple factors, including the severity of proptosis, the type of tissue involved (fat-predominant vs. muscle-predominant, as determined by imaging), the presence and urgency of DON, and the patient’s unique anatomy and aesthetic goals [21]. Although numerous studies have reported the outcomes of specific techniques, large-scale comparative analyses that can guide a personalized, indication-driven surgical strategy remain scarce [17]. This study aims to fill this gap through a comprehensive retrospective analysis of a large, single-center cohort of 263 eyes. By stratifying patients according to the specific surgical technique employed and conducting detailed subgroup analyses, we aim to quantify the differential efficacy and safety of various surgical protocols, thereby providing a robust, evidence-based framework for decision-making in the personalized management of TAO by the modern surgeon.

Patients and methods

Study design and patient cohort

This study conducted a comprehensive retrospective analysis of the medical records of TAO patients who underwent orbital decompression surgery performed by a single senior surgeon at our hospital between September 2021 and June 2025. This was a retrospective, single-centre, single-surgeon study, and treatment allocation was not randomized.The study strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Chongqing Aier Eye Hospital. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for individual patient informed consent was waived.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of TAO based on clinical signs and necessary orbital imaging; (2) clear surgical indications, including disfiguring proptosis (with the patient’s desire for cosmetic improvement) or sight-threatening complications such as DON or severe exposure keratitis unresponsive to conservative treatment; and (3) complete preoperative and postoperative data with a minimum follow-up of at least three months.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) a history of previous orbital fractures, or other significant orbital trauma; (2) the presence of other orbital diseases that could interfere with surgical outcomes, such as tumors or vascular malformations; and (3) incomplete or missing clinical records that would prevent a comprehensive analysis of key outcome variables.

Based on these criteria, a total of 199 patients (263 eyes) were ultimately included in the final analysis of this study. Among them, 64 patients underwent bilateral surgery, and 135 patients underwent unilateral surgery.The primary unit of analysis was the operated eye. Demographic variables were summarized at the patient level when appropriate, whereas surgical outcomes were analyzed at the eye level because each operated orbit had its own preoperative and postoperative measurements.

Clinical assessment and surgical strategy

All patients included in the study underwent a standardized, comprehensive ophthalmological evaluation both preoperatively and at regular postoperative follow-ups. The preoperative assessment included: best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA); slit-lamp biomicroscopy of the anterior segment; Goldmann applanation tonometry to measure intraocular pressure (IOP); detailed assessment of ocular motility and ductions; evaluation of diplopia in primary and various gaze positions; and Hertel exophthalmometry to quantify the degree of proptosis. All patients were required to undergo high-resolution orbital computed tomography (CT) to precisely delineate the anatomy, assess the extent of extraocular muscle belly enlargement and orbital fat compartment expansion, and plan the surgical approach accordingly.

Surgical selection followed an indication-driven, individualized decision process rather than random allocation. Fat decompression was preferentially selected for patients with mild-to-moderate proptosis and fat-predominant orbital expansion on CT. Endoscopic transnasal medial wall decompression was favored when DON or apical crowding was the primary concern and maximal cosmetic reduction was not the main goal. Combined medial and lateral wall decompression was selected for patients with more severe proptosis requiring greater orbital volume expansion, with the endoscopic medial wall approach preferentially used when apical decompression was also required. Final decisions incorporated the patient’s dominant complaint, baseline proptosis, DON status, CT-based fat or muscle predominance, orbital anatomy, and the surgeon’s assessment of the risk–benefit balance.

Our center employs a personalized, indication-driven algorithm for selecting the surgical protocol. This strategy is based on a comprehensive consideration of the patient’s chief complaint, the severity of proptosis, the presence of DON, and specific pathological findings on CT imaging. For the purpose of analysis, the surgical procedures were categorized into the following four well-defined groups:

• Group 1 (Transconjunctival Orbital Fat Decompression): This procedure involves the removal of intraconal and extraconal fat via a transconjunctival incision without any bony wall removal. It is primarily indicated for patients with mild proptosis where fat hypertrophy is the main pathological feature(Fig. 1).

Postoperative assessments were conducted at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, and annually thereafter.Patients were included only if postoperative follow-up data of at least 3 months were available. The primary outcome measures for this study included the quantitative change in proptosis (mm), change in BCVA, and change in IOP (mmHg). Secondary outcome measures included the incidence of postoperative complications, with a particular focus on new-onset or worsening diplopia, and the improvement in extraocular motility restriction.TAO severity in this study was not stratified by Clinical Activity Score(CAS), because CAS was not consistently documented as a structured variable in the retrospective surgical database. Instead, surgical severity and indication were characterized using clinically relevant surgical parameters, including baseline proptosis, DON status, CT evidence of fat-predominant or muscle-predominant orbital expansion, apical crowding, ocular motility restriction, diplopia, and exposure-related symptoms.

Statistical analysis

All collected data were entered into an electronic database and subsequently analyzed using Python (version 3.11) with its pandas and numpy libraries. Statistical analysis analyses were primarily performed at the eye level because the surgical intervention and the main outcomes, including BCVA, proptosis, IOP, ocular motility, diplopia, and complications, were eye-specific. For patients who contributed both eyes, sensitivity analyses using patient-level clustered robust standard errors were performed to account for within-patient correlation. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Within-group preoperative and postoperative comparisons were assessed using paired tests. Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test according to distributional assumptions, with post hoc pairwise comparisons adjusted for multiple testing when applicable. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariable linear or logistic regression models were additionally used as exploratory sensitivity analyses to adjust for clinically relevant baseline differences, including age, sex, baseline proptosis, baseline IOP, and DON status. All tests were two-sided, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Proptosis and intraocular pressure reduction

Statistical analysis of proptosis reduction

Overall, the mean proptosis decreased from 21.16 ± 2.75 mm preoperatively to 15.01 ± 1.86 mm postoperatively, with an average reduction of 6.15 ± 2.85 mm. To evaluate the impact of different surgical methods on proptosis reduction, we performed a one-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA). The results showed a highly significant statistical difference in the amount of proptosis reduction among the four groups (F = 74.32, p < 0.0001).

Specifically, the combined bony wall decompression in Groups 3 and 4 yielded the most significant effects, with mean reductions of 7.67 ± 2.42 mm and 7.29 ± 2.21 mm, respectively, significantly outperforming the fat-only decompression in Group 1 (3.57 ± 1.20 mm) and the medial-wall-only decompression in Group 2 (3.55 ± 1.73 mm). The improvement effects in Groups 3 and 4 were comparable (p > 0.05) and were approximately double that of Groups 1 and 2.

Statistical Analysis of IOP Reduction.

The mean IOP of all patient groups decreased to varying degrees postoperatively. The overall mean IOP decreased from 18.28 ± 6.22 mmHg to 14.23 ± 2.62 mmHg. A one-way ANOVA showed a statistically significant difference in IOP reduction among the four groups (F = 2.67, p = 0.0478). The IOP reduction was most pronounced in Groups 2 and 3, with mean decreases of 4.40 ± 6.12 mmHg and 5.07 ± 5.89 mmHg, respectively, significantly greater than the 1.86 ± 3.05 mmHg in Group 1.

Visual acuity improvement

Visual acuity was a key functional outcome. The overall mean BCVA improved significantly from 0.67 ± 0.68 LogMAR preoperatively to 0.34 ± 0.42 LogMAR postoperatively (p < 0.0001). The improvement was most pronounced in groups with a high prevalence of preoperative DON. As shown in Table 3, Group 4 demonstrated the largest mean improvement in visual acuity (0.44 ± 0.58 LogMAR), followed by Group 2 (0.31 ± 0.36 LogMAR). A one-way ANOVA confirmed a statistically significant difference in visual acuity improvement among the four groups (F = 4.55, p = 0.0039), underscoring the differential impact of surgical techniques on visual function.

Ocular motility and diplopia

Statistical analysis of ocular motility improvement

This study conducted a detailed analysis of the functional improvements in ocular motility restriction and diplopia. As shown in Table 2, ocular motility improved in all groups postoperatively, with the highest improvement rates in Groups 3 and 4, at 33.3% and 32.6%, respectively. To compare the impact of different surgical methods on ocular motility improvement, we performed a Chi-squared test. The results showed a highly significant statistical difference in the improvement rate of ocular motility among the four groups (χ² = 22.63, p < 0.0001), indicating that different surgical methods have significantly different effects on improving ocular motility restriction.

Further analysis revealed that the ocular motility improvement rates in Group 3 (transconjunctival medial wall decompression + lateral orbitotomy) and Group 4 (endoscopic transnasal medial wall decompression + lateral orbitotomy) were 33.3% and 32.6%, respectively, significantly higher than the 25.5% in Group 1 and 19.1% in Group 2.

Detailed statistical analysis of diplopia

A detailed analysis of diplopia is presented in Table 4. Preoperatively, a total of 164 patients (62.4%) reported diplopia. After orbital decompression, 33.5% of patients experienced resolution or improvement of diplopia. Notably, among patients with persistent postoperative diplopia, a significant portion (48/109, 44.0%) successfully eliminated their diplopia through subsequent strabismus surgery.

Among patients with preoperative diplopia, there were marked differences in improvement among the groups. Group 1 (transconjunctival orbital fat decompression) had the highest rate of postoperative diplopia resolution or improvement, at 54.2% (12/24), followed by Group 4 at 37.8% (31/82). In contrast, the improvement rates in Groups 2 and 3 were relatively low, at 21.9% (9/41) and 11.8% (2/17), respectively.

The overall incidence of postoperative complications was 9.1% (24 of 263 eyes). Discussion

This study represents a comprehensive, large-scale retrospective analysis of 199 patients (263 eyes) with TAO who underwent personalized orbital decompression surgery using four distinct surgical strategies. Our primary objective was to quantify the differential efficacy and safety profiles of these tailored approaches, thereby providing an evidence-based framework for optimizing surgical selection in this complex patient population. The findings robustly support the core hypothesis that a personalized, indication-driven surgical strategy is highly effective and safe for managing TAO, with the choice of technique significantly impacting both functional and aesthetic outcomes.

Efficacy in proptosis reduction and comparison with literature

Orbital decompression surgery is the definitive intervention for reducing proptosis and restoring orbital volume. Our overall mean proptosis reduction of 6.15 ± 2.85 mm (from 21.16 ± 2.75 mm to 15.01 ± 1.86 mm) is substantial and demonstrates the high efficacy of the personalized approach employed at our center.

A key finding of our study is the significant difference in proptosis reduction across the four surgical groups (p < 0.0001). The combined bony wall decompression techniques, Group 3 (Transconjunctival Medial + Lateral) and Group 4 (Endoscopic Medial + Lateral), yielded the most pronounced reductions, with mean values of 7.67 ± 2.42 mm and 7.29 ± 2.21 mm, respectively. These results are significantly superior to the single-modality approaches of Group 1 (Fat Decompression) and Group 2 (Endoscopic Medial Wall), which achieved comparable reductions of 3.57 ± 1.20 mm and 3.55 ± 1.73 mm.

These quantitative results align with established principles in orbital surgery and are consistent with recent meta-analyses [1, 17]. Our combined decompression results (Groups 3 and 4) surpass the mean reduction reported for two-wall balanced decompression in the systematic review by Guo et al. (5.23 mm) [17]. This suggests that the deep, personalized nature of the combined decompression performed in our cohort, particularly the extensive removal of the medial and lateral walls, provides a greater expansion of orbital volume than the average multi-wall procedure reported in the literature. Conversely, the reduction achieved by our fat-only decompression (Group 1, 3.57 mm) is highly consistent with the reported mean of 3.46 mm for fat removal in the same meta-analysis [17], confirming its predictable efficacy for patients with fat-predominant TAO and mild-to-moderate proptosis.

Functional outcomes: visual acuity and IOP

The most critical clinical concern in TAO is the preservation and restoration of vision, particularly in the presence of DON. Our study demonstrates that procedures involving medial wall decompression are superior for visual function improvement. The overall mean BCVA improved significantly by 0.33 ± 0.51 LogMAR (p < 0.0001).

Crucially, the greatest visual acuity gains were observed in Group 4 (Endoscopic Medial + Lateral) and Group 2 (Endoscopic Medial Wall), with mean improvements of 0.44 LogMAR and 0.31 LogMAR, respectively. Both groups had a high prevalence of DON (62.2% and 76.6%). This finding strongly validates the clinical strategy of prioritizing medial wall removal, especially via the endoscopic transnasal approach, for DON patients. Medial wall decompression allows for the removal of bone near the orbital apex and the medial aspect of the optic nerve, directly relieving the compression caused by the enlarged medial rectus muscle and surrounding tissues [6]. Our quantitative LogMAR data provides robust evidence, supporting the consensus that apical decompression is paramount for visual recovery in DON [22].

Furthermore, the significant reduction in Intraocular Pressure (IOP) observed in Groups 2 and 4 (4.40 mmHg and 4.30 mmHg, respectively) further supports the effectiveness of these techniques in relieving posterior orbital pressure, a key mechanism in DON pathogenesis [7].

Management of ocular motility and diplopia

Postoperative diplopia remains the most challenging complication of orbital decompression. Our study provides a nuanced perspective on the role of decompression in the multi-stage management of TAO-related diplopia, a significant clinical concern.

While the overall incidence of new-onset or worsening diplopia was low (9.1%), our detailed analysis of patients with preoperative diplopia (62.4% of the cohort) revealed complex outcomes. Only a small percentage (6.7%) achieved complete resolution immediately post-decompression, but a substantial portion (26.8%) experienced improvement. This suggests that decompression primarily functions by reducing intraorbital pressure and providing space for the extraocular muscles, which can partially restore function but often cannot fully resolve the motility issues caused by long-term muscle fibrosis and restrictive changes.

Importantly, our data highlights the necessity of a two-stage approach. Among patients with persistent postoperative diplopia, 44.0% successfully eliminated their diplopia through subsequent strabismus surgery. This finding is critical for clinical counseling, emphasizing that orbital decompression serves as a foundational step in the functional rehabilitation sequence. By stabilizing the globe position and maximizing orbital volume, it creates the optimal anatomical prerequisite for a successful second-stage strabismus correction [4].

Interestingly, Group 1 (Fat Decompression) showed the highest rate of diplopia improvement/resolution (54.2%), which is consistent with the understanding that fat decompression, being less disruptive to the extraocular muscle cone, is associated with a lower risk of iatrogenic diplopia and is often preferred when proptosis reduction is the main goal and muscle enlargement is minimal [17].

An additional clinically relevant observation was the relatively favorable diplopia outcome in the fat-only decompression group. Among eyes with preoperative diplopia, Group 1 showed the highest rate of postoperative resolution or improvement. Although this finding should be interpreted cautiously because treatment allocation was not randomized and the baseline disease phenotype differed among groups, it supports the concept that fat-predominant TAO may benefit from less disruptive decompression strategies when the primary goal is moderate proptosis reduction rather than apical decompression. This information may be useful during preoperative counselling, particularly when discussing the trade-off between the desired amount of proptosis reduction and the risk of ocular motility disturbance.

Safety profile and surgical strategy optimization

The overall complication rate of 9.1% in our cohort is favorable compared to the 10%–20% range reported in some literature [5]. The statistically significant difference in complication rates among groups (p = 0.0278) underscores the importance of surgical selection.

Group 2 (Endoscopic Medial Wall) had the lowest complication rate (4.3%), demonstrating the safety advantage of the minimally invasive, bone-only approach when the primary goal is apical decompression for DON. This technique avoids external scarring and minimizes manipulation of the lateral orbital structures.

Conversely, Group 1 (Fat Decompression) had the highest complication rate (19.6%), primarily consisting of transient issues like eyelid edema and new/worsening diplopia. While the diplopia risk is lower than bony decompression, the transconjunctival approach itself can lead to temporary soft tissue swelling and bruising, which accounts for the higher overall transient complication rate.

The data from our study, particularly the comparative efficacy and safety profiles, strongly support the personalized, indication-driven algorithm used at our center:

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective, single-centre, single-surgeon study, and treatment allocation was not randomized. Surgical technique was selected according to clinical indication and imaging findings; therefore, baseline differences among groups and confounding by indication are unavoidable. Second, the primary analyses were performed at the eye level because the surgical procedures and outcomes were eye-specific. Although sensitivity analyses using patient-level clustered robust standard errors were added to account for bilateral cases, residual within-patient correlation cannot be fully excluded. Third, CAS was not consistently available as a structured variable in the retrospective database, and disease activity could not be analyzed in a standardized manner. Fourth, patient-reported outcomes, such as GO-QoL, were not routinely collected, limiting assessment of patient-perceived functional and cosmetic benefit. Finally, follow-up duration varied across patients, and longer prospective follow-up is needed to evaluate the durability of proptosis reduction, visual recovery, and final diplopia outcomes after staged strabismus surgery.

Despite these limitations, the results of this study still provide an important reference for clinical practice. It confirms the necessity of a personalized surgical plan and provides strong evidence-based support for how to select the most appropriate surgical method (such as fat-only decompression, single-wall decompression, or combined multi-wall decompression) based on the patient’s specific condition (such as the degree of proptosis and pathological type).

Future research should focus on: (1) Prospective, Comparative Studies to eliminate selection bias and provide Level I evidence; (2) Integration of Advanced Imaging to develop more precise predictive models, as suggested by the literature on 3D CT morphometrics and biomechanical modeling [21, 23]; and (3) Long-term Follow-up to fully evaluate the stability of proptosis reduction and the final success rate of the two-stage approach (decompression + strabismus surgery) for diplopia.

Conclusion

This single-centre retrospective study supports the clinical utility of an individualized, indication-driven approach to orbital decompression in TAO. Surgical selection should be guided by the patient’s dominant indication, including cosmetic or exposure-related proptosis, DON, CT-based fat or muscle predominance, and the expected trade-off between decompressive effect and diplopia risk. In this cohort, combined medial–lateral wall decompression was associated with the greatest proptosis reduction, whereas medial wall decompression, particularly when apical decompression was required, was associated with visual improvement in DON. These results may help inform surgical planning and patient counselling, but they should be interpreted in light of the retrospective design, non-randomized treatment allocation, baseline imbalance, and potential within-patient correlation in bilateral cases.
