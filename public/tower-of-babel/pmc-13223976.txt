ESM1 drives cisplatin resistance and ferroptosis resistance via ERBB2/FAK/SRC/HSPB1/NF-κB signaling in ovarian cancer
Authors: Juan Zhang, Yingzheng Tan, Haibing Yang, Xing Tang, Dan Liu, Wenchao Zhou, Tian Zeng, Xueru Liu, Miao Li, Xun Chen, Juan Zou, Yukun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCisplatin stands as a highly effective chemotherapeutic agent for ovarian cancer (OC); yet, the development of resistance to it poses a significant clinical challenge. In this study, we utilized prior RNA-sequencing and immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) data to identify downstream genes and interacting proteins altered following ESM1 knockdown, and further confirmed the impact of these changes on ferroptosis and cisplatin resistance in OC cells through electron microscopy and various molecular biology experiments. Additionally, public databases, tissue microarrays, and multiplex immunofluorescence staining were employed to assess the prognostic value of genes like ESM1 for OC patients. The results demonstrate that HSPB1 acts as a pivotal gene in ESM1-mediated cisplatin and ferroptosis resistance. Mechanistically, ESM1 binds to ERBB2 to promote HSPB1 transcription, thereby activating the FAK/SRC and NF-κB pathways, which ultimately inhibits ferroptosis and enhances cisplatin resistance in OC. Collectively, these findings elucidate a regulatory mechanism by which ESM1 drives cisplatin and ferroptosis resistance via the ERBB2/FAK/SRC/HSPB1/NF-κB axis in ovarian cancer.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) ranks among the malignant diseases with the highest mortality rate in gynecological oncology.1 This is attributed to the extremely low rate of early diagnosis in OC patients, who usually miss the opportunity for surgical treatment by the time they seek medical help.2 The combination of cisplatin and targeted drugs represents one of the most prevalently adopted treatment regimens for OC patients.3 Nevertheless, the emergence of drug resistance over time constitutes one of the crucial factors contributing to the decreased five-year survival rate of OC patients.4 Consequently, how to suppress the emergence of cellular drug resistance is one of the principal research directions in current OC treatment.

Ferroptosis represents a form of cell death, which is associated with iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and is distinct from conventional cell death modalities such as apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy.5,6 In the realm of tumor treatment, the activation of ferroptosis is considered a potential strategy for surmounting the drug resistance of chemotherapy drugs.7 Recent research has demonstrated that ferroptosis plays a crucial role in cisplatin resistance. For instance, a study revealed that combined treatment with shikonin and cisplatin can overcome the resistance of OC to cisplatin via ferroptosis.8 Inhibiting SCD1/FADS2 can impede tumor growth, restrain the formation of OC stem cells, and diminish platinum resistance.9 Furthermore, another study indicated that in two cisplatin-resistant OC cell lines (A2780CIS and PEO4), suppressing HSP27 or FAO can render cisplatin-resistant OC cell lines susceptible and result in greater generation of ROS following cisplatin treatment, along with a decline in the level of reduced glutathione and an elevation in the level of ferroptosis.10

Endothelial cell-specific molecule 1 (ESM1), a secreted protein, plays a crucial role in the genesis and progression of numerous cancers, such as OC, lung cancer, cervical cancer and colorectal cancer.11 ESM1 is highly expressed in various cell types, including tip endothelial cells, fibroblast-like synoviocytes, and cancer cells.12,13,14 These cells can regulate the tumor microenvironment by secreting ESM1, thereby inducing immune evasion, and angiogenesis.11 In our prior research, we discovered that ESM1 is capable of facilitating the proliferation, migration, invasion, evasion of apoptosis, and angiogenesis of OCs by activating the Akt signaling pathway.15 Additionally, ESM1 and ANGPTL4 form a positive feedback loop to influence lipid metabolism and angiogenesis in OC.16 In a recent study, we found that shikonin can diminish the glycolytic reprogramming and vasculogenic mimicry in OC cells through the inhibition of ESM1-mediated PKM2 SUMOylation.17 Meanwhile, lactic acid can upregulate ESM1 expression and augment the SCD1-mediated Wnt pathway, thus reducing the cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells and the cisplatin sensitivity within the OC tumor microenvironment.18 However, the mechanism by which ESM1 affects OC cisplatin resistance remains unclear.

In this study, we discovered that, within OC cells, cisplatin is capable of suppressing the expression of ESM1 and enhancing the level of ferroptosis. HSPB1, a crucial gene downstream of ESM1, restrains OC ferroptosis through the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. ESM1, by binding to ERBB2 and facilitating its phosphorylation, activates the FAK/SRC pathway, thereby promoting HSPB1 transcription. Moreover, we observed that ESM1, ERBB2, HSPB1, Iκβα (inhibitor of NF-κB alpha), and GPX4 are significantly associated with the prognosis of OC patients. Our study reveals that the ESM1/ERBB2/FAK/SRC/HSPB1/NF-κB signaling axis drives chemotherapy resistance in OC by suppressing ferroptosis. Our study underlines that this pathway is a vital regulatory pathway for OC chemotherapy resistance, indicating that targeting ESM1 might be a strategy for preventing OC progression and surmounting treatment resistance.

Results

ESM1 upregulates HSPB1 to promote OC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, but inhibits ferroptosis

In our previous study, we found that ESM1 promotes lipid metabolic reprogramming in OC cells through multiple molecular mechanisms.16,17,19 Given that the regulation of ferroptosis is closely associated with lipid metabolic reprogramming,20 to preliminarily investigate whether ESM1 is related to ferroptosis, we reanalyzed the RNA-sequencing data from our previous study.19 The results revealed that among the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) downstream of ESM1, HSPB1 is an important ferroptosis-related gene (SRA database, PRJNA1082038 dataset)21 (Figure 1A). Meanwhile, as a secreted protein, ESM1 can bind to multiple growth factor receptors, and HSPB1 promotes trastuzumab resistance by reducing ferroptosis in ERBB2-targeted therapy.21 Activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway is a critical downstream signaling pathway for HSPB1-mediated inhibition of ferroptosis.22 Subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that ESM1 might be involved in a variety of molecular biological processes, including growth factor binding, iron-ion binding, and the NF-κB signaling pathway (Figure 1B). This further supports our hypothesis that ESM1 may inhibit ferroptosis by activating the ERBB2/NF-κB pathway via HSPB1. Consequently, we further constructed SKOV3 and A2780 cell lines with ESM1 knockdown and HSPB1 over-expression (Figure 1C). 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) and 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays demonstrated that HSPB1 over-expression could significantly reverse the decrease in proliferation mediated by ESM1 knockdown (Figures 1D and 1E). Transwell migration and invasion assays showed that HSPB1 over-expression could markedly reverse the reduction in migration and invasion abilities mediated by ESM1 knockdown (Figure 1F). Moreover, we found that ESM1 could regulate the expression of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related markers in OC cells through HSPB1, including fibronectin, vimentin, N-cadherin, and E-cadherin (Figure 1G). According to the results of gene ontology (GO) enrichment (Figure 1B), we studied the effect of the ESM1-HSPB1 axis on ferroptosis in OC cells using electron microscopy. The results demonstrated that overexpression of HSPB1 significantly reversed the mitochondrial damage caused by ESM1 gene knockout in OC cells. This suggests that ESM1 protects mitochondria and inhibits ferroptosis via the HSPB1 pathway (Figure 1H). Further flow cytometry experiments suggested that ESM1 knockdown could significantly increase the reactive oxygen species (ROS) level in OC cells, and HSPB1 over-expression could reverse this molecular biological effect (Figure 1I). Western blot (WB) analysis further revealed that ESM1 knockdown increased the expression of LPCAT3 and ACSL4 while decreasing GPX4 expression levels. Subsequent overexpression of HSPB1 antagonized this molecular effect (Figure 1J). ESM1-HSPB1 axis attenuates ferroptosis to induce cisplatin resistance of OC cells in vitro

A large body of literature indicates that ferroptosis is a critical molecular mechanism by which cisplatin kills OC cells.8,9,23 In our previous study, we found that ESM1 promotes cisplatin resistance in OC cells.18 In this study, we further overexpressed HSPB1 on the basis of ESM1 knockdown to investigate the effect of the ESM1-HSPB1 signaling axis on cisplatin resistance in OC cells, and found that HSPB1 overexpression significantly antagonized the cisplatin-sensitizing effect mediated by ESM1 knockdown (Figure S1). Therefore, we speculate that ESM1 is likely to promote cisplatin resistance by regulating ferroptosis. Subsequently, we discovered that cisplatin was capable of diminishing the expression of ESM1 and HSPB1 in a concentration-gradient-dependent fashion, while facilitating the expression of the ferroptosis-related proteins LPCAT3 and ACSL4 (Figure 2A). Further immunofluorescence staining also corroborated these findings (Figure 2B). Additionally, we observed that cisplatin could elevate the levels of ROS in a concentration-gradient-dependent manner (Figure 2C), suggesting that cisplatin could induce ferroptosis in OC cells. In our prior research, it was identified that ESM1 could augment the tolerance of OC cells to cisplatin. In comparison with the cells transfected with vector, the cisplatin resistance of OC cells transfected with HSPB1 was enhanced (Figure 2D). Moreover, HSPB1 could counteract the elevation of ROS and MDA levels and the decline of cell viability induced by cisplatin (Figures 2E–2G). WB analysis revealed that HSPB1 over-expression could oppose the reduction of NF-κB pathway activity mediated by ESM1 knockdown or cisplatin treatment, as well as the expressions of downstream proteins Twist1, IL6, and survivin and the phosphorylation of Akt (Figures 2H and 2I). Via the NF-κB reporter gene assay, we observed that the knockdown of ESM1 and treatment with cisplatin resulted in the attenuation of NF-κB transactivation activity. Moreover, the over-expression of HSPB1 counteracted the effects induced by ESM1 knockdown and cisplatin treatment in OC cells (Figure 2J). HSPB1 activates the NF-κB pathway by promoting the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of Iκβα in OC

Iκβα (NFKBIA), an intracellular protein, modulates a variety of biological processes, including inflammation and immune responses, by binding to and suppressing the activity of the transcription factor NF-κB.24 NF-κB is typically located in the cytoplasm and forms an inactive complex in conjunction with Iκβα. When the cell is subjected to diverse signals, Iκβα will be phosphorylated by specific kinase complexes, subsequently ubiquitinated and degraded via the proteasome pathway.25 The inactivation of Iκβα liberates NF-κB, permitting it to translocate into the nucleus to activate the transcription of target genes.26

In this research, we discovered that cisplatin treatment can markedly inhibit the nuclear translocation of NF-κB, and HSPB1 knockdown can also impede the nuclear translocation of NF-κB (Figures 3A and 3B). Additionally, co-IP experiments demonstrated that HSPB1 knockdown can attenuate the binding between Iκβα and NF-κB, and cisplatin treatment can further augment the mutual dissociation between Iκβα and NF-κB mediated by HSPB1 knockdown (Figure 3C). Moreover, we found that cisplatin can induce the formation of a complex between HSPB1 and Iκβα (Figure 3D). OC cells were treated with 15 μM MG132 (a proteasome inhibitor) or 25 μM chloroquine (CQ, a lysosome inhibitor) to investigate the probability of proteasome or lysosome degradation. The results revealed that HSPB1 over-expression led to a reduction in Iκβα expression in the control group and the CQ group (Figure 3E). In comparison with the other two groups, MG132 treatment did not significantly alter the expression of Iκβα, and in the MG132 group, the expression of Iκβα was less affected by HSPB1 over-expression (Figure 3E). Subsequently, OC cells transfected with an empty vector or an HSPB1 over-expression vector were treated with 100 μg/mL cycloheximide (CHX, a protein synthesis inhibitor). The results showed that over-expression of HSPB1 significantly decreased the half-life of Iκβα in SKOV3 cells and A2780 cells, indicating that HSPB1 can diminish the protein stability of Iκβα (Figure 3F). According to previous studies, HSPB1 inhibits the expression of IκBα via ubiquitination in cancer cells.22 Therefore, these results indicate that HSPB1 is capable of modulating the stability of Iκβα via ubiquitination modification, thus facilitating the activity of the NF-κB pathway.

Iκβα is a significant factor in OC ferroptosis, and cisplatin sensitivity is regulated by the HSPB1-NF-κB axis

To further verify whether HSPB1 mediates cisplatin resistance and ferroptosis resistance in OC cells via the NF-κB pathway mediated by Iκβα, we knocked down HSPB1 and Iκβα in OC cells (Figure 4A). Our MTT and EdU assays revealed that the reduction in the proliferative capacity of OC cells caused by HSPB1 knockdown could be recovered by Iκβα knockdown (Figures 4B and 4C). Transwell assays demonstrated that the decline in the migratory and invasive abilities of OC cells due to HSPB1 knockdown could be reversed by Iκβα knockdown (Figure 4D). Compared to the cells transfected with HSPB1 shRNA, the cisplatin resistance of OC cells transfected with Iκβα shRNA was augmented (Figure 4E). Electron microscopy observations revealed that knockdown of HSPB1 increased mitochondrial damage in OC cells, whereas further knockdown of IκBα restored this mitochondrial damage. This finding suggests that HSPB1 inhibits ferroptosis by regulating IκBα (Figure 4F). Through NF-κB reporter gene assays, we found that HSPB1 knockdown led to a down-regulation of NF-κB trans-activation activity, and Iκβα knockdown restored the effect caused by HSPB1 knockdown (Figure 4G). In HSPB1-knockout cells, the inhibition of cell viability and the enhanced production of ROS triggered by cisplatin or erastin could be recovered by simultaneous knockout of Ikβ-α. Moreover, Fer-1, a highly efficient and selective synthetic antioxidant inhibitor of ferroptosis,27 further demonstrated that inhibiting ferroptosis with Fer-1 attenuated the proliferation inhibition and ROS overproduction in OC cells induced by cisplatin and HSPB1 knockdown (Figures 4H and 4I). Further WB experiments suggested that, with or without cisplatin treatment, knocking down Iκβα could restore the NF-κB activity and the expression of downstream proteins (including Twist1, IL6, and survivin) in OC cells mediated by HSPB1 knockdown (Figure 4J). ESM1-ERBB2 system promotes HSPB1 transcription to inhibit ferroptosis by activating the FAK/SRC pathway in OC

ESM1 is a crucial exocrine protein. Recent studies have shown that it can bind to EGFR, thereby activating the downstream Akt signaling pathway and promoting the EMT process in tumors.28 In the RNA-sequencing work carried out by us, it was found that knocking down ESM1 can reduce the mRNA level of HSPB1 in OC cells (Figure 1A). In order to clarify the molecular mechanism by which ESM1 regulates HSPB1 mRNA, with the aid of the previous IP-MS data of ESM1 in OC cells,16 ERBB2 was found to be a potential binding receptor of ESM1. Molecular docking technology was used to confirm that ESM1 and ERBB2 can bind (Figure 5A). Through the Co-IP technology, it was further verified that there is a binding relationship between ESM1 and ERBB2 in OC cells (Figure 5B). Subsequently, we used the STRING database to construct the protein-protein interaction network of ERBB2 (Figure 5C), and extracted the upstream transcription factor data regulating HSPB1 from the hTF target database for intersection screening. The Venn diagram showed that SRC is a key transcription factor for ERBB2 to regulate HSPB1 transcription (Figure 5D; Table S1). The Chip-PCR experiment indicated that SRC is significantly enriched in the DNA promoter region of HSPB1 (Figure 5E). Further qPCR experiments also showed that over-expression of SRC can significantly enhance the transcriptional level of HSPB1 (Figure 5F). WB experiments found that cisplatin can inhibit the expression or phosphorylation modification of key proteins (including ERBB2, p-ERBB2, p-SRC, p-FAK, IL-6, GPX4, survivin, vimentin and HSPB1) in the ERBB2/FAK/SRC pathway, and additional knocking down of ESM1 can additively enhance the pharmacological effects of cisplatin on OC cells (Figures 5G and S2A). Flow cytometry analysis also indicates that ESM1 knockdown significantly enhances cisplatin-induced ROS production (Figure S3A). After treating OC cells with the recombinant human endothelial cell-specific molecule 1 (rhESM1), we found that the activity of the ERBB2/FAK/SRC pathway was significantly up-regulated, and this molecular biological change could be reversed by additionally knocking down ERBB2 (Figures 5H and S2B). Flow cytometry analysis also revealed that rhESM1 significantly reduced ROS production in OC cells, whereas ERBB2 knockdown markedly inhibited the pharmacological effect of ESM1 recombinant protein (Figure S3B). In addition, we also found that the ERBB2-targeted drug trastuzumab can significantly inhibit the effect of rhESM1 on the activation of the ERBB2 pathway (Figures 5I and S2C). Further flow cytometry analysis also revealed that the reduction in ROS production induced by rhESM1 was antagonized by trastuzumab (Figure S3C). ESM1 enhances the malignant behaviors of OC cells by driving IL6 expression

IL-6, a multifunctional cytokine, plays a crucial role in the occurrence and development of OC.29 Researches have shown that abnormally high expression of IL-6 can be detected in OC patients and that this elevation is closely associated with poor clinical prognosis.30 IL-6 activates relevant signal transduction pathways, such as the JAK/STAT, Ras/ERK/MAPK, and P13K/AKT pathways, thereby promoting the proliferation of OC cells, anti-apoptosis, and inducing angiogenesis, macrophage polarization, immune cell infiltration, and drug resistance.31,32,33

In view of the significant impact of ESM1 knockdown on IL-6 expression in OC cells detected in our previous results, we further explored whether ESM1 would affect the secretion of IL-6 (Figure 2H). The results of ELISA indicated that ESM1 knockdown could inhibit the secretion of IL-6 (Figure 6A). Moreover, rhESM1 could also significantly enhance the secretion of IL-6 in OC cells (Figure 6B). Subsequently, we exposed macrophages/endothelial cells to the conditioned medium of OC cells and found that in the conditioned medium of OC cells with ESM1 overexpression, macrophage migration and endothelial cell angiogenesis were significantly enhanced. However, IL-6 neutralizing antibody significantly inhibited the effects of ESM1 overexpression on microenvironmental macrophages and endothelial cells (Figure 6C). IL-6 neutralizing antibody can significantly inhibit ESM1-mediated cisplatin resistance in OC cells (Figure S4A). We further analyzed the differences in the landscape of immune cell infiltration abundance between high and low ESM1 expression groups in OC patients using the TCGA database, and found significant differences in immune infiltration between the two groups. Additionally, there were significant differences in macrophage infiltration. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test showed that the infiltration level of M0 macrophages was significantly higher, but M1 macrophages was significantly lower in high-ESM1-level OC patients compared to low ESM1 (Figure S5). These results may be attributed to the fact that ESM1 can enhance the motility of macrophages and recruit them to gather in tumors. Additionally, it inhibits the polarization of M0 macrophages toward the M1 phenotype while having little effect on M2 macrophages. Therefore, when the number of M1 macrophages decreases, and the number of M2 macrophages remains unchanged, a large number of M0 macrophages remain in the tumor tissue. Upon exposure to tumor cell-secreted transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) and interleukin-10 (IL-10), these M0 macrophages can secrete matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2), and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) to degrade the extracellular matrix. Moreover, they can activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to induce EMT in tumor cells, enhancing their invasive and metastatic capabilities. Furthermore, M0 macrophages can suppress T cell activation by secreting IL-10 and TGF-β while promoting the differentiation of regulatory T cells. Thus, excessive ESM1 can also promote tumor development through macrophages.34

Previous studies have reported that tumor cells can produce a large number of chemotactic factors to attract macrophages, which further determines the fate of tumor development through the process of secreting various cytokines. It has been confirmed that IL-6 is an important chemotactic factor for macrophage recruitment in cancers such as breast cancer and pancreatic cancer.35,36 Therefore, we further evaluated whether ESM1 could regulate macrophage infiltration in OC. It was found that the conditioned medium from ESM1-low-expression cells led to a decrease in the migratory ability of macrophages and a weakened chemotactic property for macrophages. To confirm that the enhancement of macrophage behavior induced by ESM1 was mediated by IL-6, we used IL-6 recombinant protein. The results showed that the addition of IL-6 recombinant protein could eliminate the inhibitory effect of the supernatant of ESM1-knockdown OC cells on THP-1 cell migration (Figure 6C).

ESM1-HSPB1 axis inhibits OC ferroptosis and cisplatin sensitivity in vivo

Subsequently, we constructed a xenograft model using nude mice to further evaluate the function of HSPB1 in OC in vivo. SKOV3 cells stably overexpressing HSPB1 or vector plasmids were injected into the flanks of BALB/c nude mice, and phosphate buffer saline (PBS) or cisplatin was further intraperitoneally injected. As shown in Figures 7A–7C, HSPB1 overexpression led to an enhancement in tumor volume and weight. Moreover, the tumors in nude mice injected with cisplatin were smaller and lighter than those in mice treated with DMSO. In our previous study,18 we also found that ESM1 knockdown resulted in a decrease in tumor volume and weight, and the tumors in the mice injected with cisplatin were smaller and lighter than those in the mice treated with PBS. IHC analysis showed that the expression changes of Iκβα, NF-κB, and their target genes in tumor tissues were consistent with the in vitro experimental results. IHC staining of GPX4 confirmed that cisplatin enhanced ferroptosis in tumor tissues, while HSPB1 overexpression could significantly inhibit ferroptosis in tumor tissues (Figure 7D). In addition, cisplatin decreased the protein expression of PCNA and IL-6, and HSPB1 overexpression rescued the protein expression of PCNA and IL-6. Furthermore, we examined the expression levels of HSPB1, PCNA, Iκβα, NF-κB, IL-6, and GPX4 by using the previous xenograft tumor samples.18 The results showed that the knockdown of ESM1 could markedly suppress the expression of HSPB1, PCNA, NF-κB, IL-6, and GPX4 and also reduce the expression level of Iκβα. Cisplatin could further have additive effects with ESM1 knockdown to influence the expression of these proteins (Figure 7E).

Subsequently, to further consolidate the effect of the ESM1-HSPB1 axis on the malignant progression of OC, we performed ESM1 overexpression and/or HSPB1 knockdown in CAOV3 cells (Figure S6A). IC50 assays revealed that ESM1 overexpression enhanced the resistance of CAOV3 cells to cisplatin, whereas HSPB1 knockdown counteracted this molecular biological effect (Figure S6B). Flow cytometry showed that ESM1 overexpression significantly increased ROS production in CAOV3 cells, while HSPB1 knockdown reduced ROS production (Figure S6C). Using Transwell assays to further clarify the effect of the ESM1-HSPB1 axis on the migration and invasion abilities of CAOV3 cells, we found that HSPB1 knockdown significantly inhibited the enhanced migration and invasion abilities of CAOV3 cells induced by ESM1 overexpression (Figure S6D). Additionally, EdU assays further demonstrated that HSPB1 knockdown counteracted the pro-proliferative effect induced by ESM1 overexpression (Figure S6E). These results indicate that HSPB1 overexpression enhances the resistance of OC to cisplatin in vivo through the NF-κB signaling pathway. These results suggest that ESM1-HSPB1 plays an important role in OC ferroptosis and cisplatin sensitivity.

Subsequently, we performed multi-index immunofluorescence staining using OC pathological tissue microarrays. Discussion

Cisplatin, as a classic chemotherapeutic drug for OC, exhibits a significantly superior efficacy compared to its use in other cancers.37 Nevertheless, the emergence of drug resistance inevitably leads to distant metastasis and cachexia in OC patients at later stages.38 Consequently, the search for new targets, the elucidation of underlying mechanisms, and overcoming cisplatin resistance are crucial for improving the prognosis of OC patients. In this study, we verified that the ESM1-ERBB2-FAK-SRC-HSPB1 signaling axis can antagonize cisplatin-induced ferroptosis in OC cells and plays an important role in the progression of OC and cisplatin resistance.

ESM1 plays a crucial role in the occurrence and development of various tumors.39,40 In our previous research, we found that ESM1 was significantly enhanced in OC and promoted OC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, apoptosis evasion, vasculogenic mimicry, and angiogenesis.15,16,17 Moreover, glucose and lipid metabolism reprogramming play important parts in this series of malignant phenotypes mediated by ESM1.16,17 Ferroptosis, a special form of cell death, is characterized by iron dependence and lipid peroxidation, which is different from traditional programmed cell death, such as apoptosis.41 The lipid peroxidation of the cytoplasmic membrane is the core event in ferroptosis, and this process is decisive for the integrity of the plasma membrane and the life or death of cells.42 In ferroptosis, lipid metabolism plays an important role.20 Unsaturated fatty acids are substrates for the synthesis of lipid signal-transduction mediators, but they must be esterified into membrane phospholipids and oxidized to transmit ferroptosis signals.43 Lipoxygenases and cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase initiate lipid peroxidation through lipid dioxygenation.44 Studies have shown that phosphatidylethanolamine is a key phospholipid for inducing ferroptosis, and enzymes involved in its biosynthesis and remodeling, such as acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3), are crucial for the occurrence of ferroptosis.45 In this study, we found that ESM1 knockdown can significantly affect iron ion binding and fatty acid metabolism by RNA sequencing. HSPB1, an important ferroptosis suppressor, was significantly reduced in RNA-sequencing results. Subsequent molecular biology experiments also confirmed that ESM1 could inhibit ferroptosis in OC cells through HSPB1 (Figure 1). These results suggest that the ESM1-HSPB1 signaling axis is one of the important mechanisms regulating ferroptosis in OC cells.

As a key protein in the regulation of ferroptosis, HSPB1 plays a significant role in promoting the proliferation, migration, invasion, and doxorubicin resistance of breast cancer.22 Kong et al. found that paclitaxel can enhance the sensitivity of SKOV3 cells to hyperthermia by inhibiting HSPB1.46 However, in the studies related to OC, the relationship between HSPB1 and cisplatin resistance in ferroptosis, as well as the underlying molecular mechanisms have not been elucidated yet. In this research, we discovered that ESM1 can activate the NF-κB pathway through HSPB1 to inhibit ferroptosis, thereby inducing cisplatin resistance.22 The NF-κB pathway is an important signaling pathway leading to ferroptosis.42 Ni et al. found that shikonin combined with cisplatin can promote ferroptosis in OC cells, thus inhibiting cisplatin resistance.8 In our previous study, we found that lactic acid can promote the expression of ESM1 in OC cells and activate the Wnt signaling pathway through SCD1 to promote cisplatin resistance,18 and shikonin is also a crucial herbal monomer for inhibiting the function of ESM1 in OC cells.17 These results further corroborate our view that ESM1 activates the NF-κB pathway through HSPB1 to inhibit ferroptosis and cisplatin sensitivity.

IL-6, a pleiotropic cytokine, plays a crucial role in the development and progression of tumors.47 IL-6 can promote tumor growth, metabolism, invasion, metastasis, and chemoresistance through multiple mechanisms.48 In this study, we discovered that ESM1 activates the NF-κB pathway via HSPB1 to stimulate IL-6 secretion in OC cells. This results in enhanced migratory ability of macrophages, improved tube-forming capacity of endothelial cells, and increased proliferative ability of OC cells in the OC tumor microenvironment. Wang et al. found that platinum induces cancer-associated fibroblasts in the tumor microenvironment to secrete IL-6, which promotes the enrichment of OC stem cells (OCSCs) in the residual tumors after chemotherapy. Moreover, IL-6 can transform non-OCSCs into OCSCs by activating STAT3 and up-regulating the expression of ALDH1A1.29 Xu et al. demonstrated that IL-6 up-regulates the expression and nuclear translocation of HIF-1α under hypoxic conditions through the STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby enhancing the chemoresistance of OC cells to cisplatin.49 Lo et al. found that the transduction signal of IL-6 on endothelial cells can prevent chemotherapy-induced apoptosis, chemotherapy-induced high endothelial permeability, and increase the trans-endothelial migration of OC cells. By activating SRC kinase and phosphorylating VE-cadherin at Y685, the IL-6/soluble IL-6 receptor α (sIL-6Rα) complex activates ERK, which can increase the integrity of endothelial cells, accelerate ascites formation, and inhibit taxane sensitivity.50 Pascale Jeannin et al. found that IL-6 exists at high concentration in OC ascites and promotes the immunological escape of OC by increasing the consumption of macrophage colony stimulating factor and differentiating monocytes into tumor-associated macrophage-like cells.51 Wu et al. also pointed out that the macrophage phenotype is a prognostic indicator in patients with platinum-resistant recurrent OC and is closely related to the over-activation of IL-6-related pathways.52 These results suggest that IL-6 is a key factor for ESM1 to mediate the malignant phenotype of OC cells, macrophage reprogramming in the microenvironment, and angiogenesis through the activation of the NF-κB pathway by HSPB1.

ERBB2 (also known as HER2) is a receptor tyrosine kinase, which plays a crucial role in the development of various cancers, including OC.53 In OC, the increased expression of ERBB2 is associated with tumor invasiveness and poor prognosis.54 Wang et al. discovered that Cdc25A augments the ERBB2 level through the PKM2-pH3T11-H3K9Ac pathway and inhibits autophagy-induced ferroptosis.55 Moreover, Cdc25A enhances the resistance of cervical cancer to sorafenib, while the down-regulation of ErbB2 blocks these effects. Marth et al. found that the occurrence of cisplatin resistance in OC is related to increased expression of ERBB2.56 FAK and SRC, as key kinases downstream of ERBB2, play significant roles in tumorigenesis and development.57 In this study, we found that ESM1 can bind to ERBB2 and further activate the downstream FAK/SRC pathway to promote HSPB1 transcription and inhibit ferroptosis and cisplatin sensitivity. Previous studies have pointed out by some researchers that ESM1, as an exocrine protein, can bind to EGFR/ERBB3 to activate the expression of the downstream Akt signaling pathway, thereby promoting the EMT cascade in gastric cancer cells.28 These results further reveal the potential molecular mechanism by which ESM1, as a secreted protein, regulates ferroptosis and cisplatin resistance in OC cells through the receptor-binding pathway.

Taken together, our research has found that ESM1 can promote HSPB1 transcription through the ERBB2/FAK/SRC pathway and accelerate the NF-κB pathway, thus accelerating cisplatin resistance in OC, inhibiting ferroptosis and promoting the malignant progression. Meanwhile, we have also demonstrated that ESM1 plays a crucial role in promoting OC progression through the secretion of IL-6 for angiogenesis and macrophage reprogramming in the microenvironment. The mechanism is shown in Figure 8G. Our study has further clarified the molecular mechanism by which ESM1 promotes the progression of OC.

Limitations of the study

This study also has some limitations. Although we found that ESM1 inhibits ferroptosis and induces cisplatin resistance through the ERBB2/FAK/SRC/HSPB1/NF-κB signaling axis, this is still largely confined to OC cells themselves. Whether ESM1 can affect ferroptosis in endothelial cells and macrophages within the OC tumor microenvironment via this molecular mechanism remains unknown. We also hope to validate and explore this direction one by one in future studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture and treatment

A2780, SKOV3 cells and HUVECs (AW-CCH152, AW-CCH110, and AW-CNH115, Abiowell) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Sigma) or McCoy's 5A medium (iCell), respectively. All media contained 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Beyotime). All cell lines undergo rigorous validation via Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) spectroscopy and are subjected to quarterly mycoplasma contamination screening using validated PCR-based detection kits. To detect the effect of secreted ESM1 on ferroptosis, OC cells were treated with rhESM1 (2 μg/ml) for 24 hours. To further verify the effects of cisplatin on ferroptosis and ROS formation, OC cells were treated with 0.5 μM cisplatin for 24 hours. The stability of HSPB1 and Iκβα proteins in OC cells after the abovementioned treatments was analyzed by cycloheximide treatment. To investigate whether HSPB1 regulates the ubiquitination of Iκβα, OC cells were treated with 5 μM proteasome inhibitor MG132 for 2 hours. To study whether autophagy regulates the expression of HSPB1 and Iκβα proteins, OC cells were treated with 25 μM chloroquine (CQ) for 48 hours.

According to the manufacturer's instruction, sh-ESM1, oe-ESM1, sh-HSPB1, oe-HSPB1, sh-Iκβα, sh-ERBB2, and their negative controls (HonorGene) were transfected into OC cells with Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Invitrogen). After 48 hours of transfection, the OC cells were collected for further analysis.

Construction of a xenograft tumor model

Female BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks) were purchased from SJA Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Hunan, China), and acclimatized for one week. Each group was subcutaneously injected with 5 × 106 SKOV3 cells into mice. The size of the subcutaneous tumors was examined with calipers every 12 days, and the tumor volume was calculated. On the 48th day, the mice were euthanized, and the transplanted tumors were collected and weighed. For the female nude mice in the cisplatin group, cisplatin (4 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally injected once every 3 days; the control group was intraperitoneally injected with normal saline. All of the abovementioned experiments were carried out in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) animal laboratory under specific procedures. In addition, the animal experiments in this study were carried out in accordance with the approved IRB protocol (#ZZCHEC2024091-02) and approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhuzhou Hospital, Central South University. All animal experiments were reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

OC tissue microarray

The OC tissue chip (AF-OvaSur2201) was purchased from AiFang Biology and included 94 cancer tissues. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures were approved by the research ethics committee of Zhuzhou Hospital Affiliated to Xiangya School of Medicine with Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval (#ZZCHEC2024091-02).

Method details

Bioinformatic analysis

We extracted the RNA-seq data of ESM1 knockdown in OC cells from the PRJNA1082038 dataset19 in the SRA database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1082038). Key downstream genes after ESM1 knockdown were screened by differential expression analysis. GO enrichment analysis and KEGG enrichment analysis were subsequently carried out for these downstream genes via the DAVID and KEGG databases. The RNA-seq data of OC patients and the corresponding clinical information were obtained from the OC dataset of the TCGA database. Subsequently, the data in TPM format are extracted and normalized with log2(TPM+1). Finally, after retaining the samples with both RNA-seq data and clinical information, a total of 376 samples were used for subsequent analysis. For the construction of the prognostic model, we constructed a prognostic model via multivariate Cox regression analysis. All of the above analyses were performed via the survival package in R software. For the data of ESM1, ERBB2, HSPB1, NFKBIA and GPX4 expressions, the ggplot2 package was used to visualize the data.

To analyze differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between control and ESM1 knockdown OC cells, the following bioinformatics methods were employed, integrating multi-omics data and functional validation: limma Package (R/Bioconductor): Applied to identify DEGs between ESM1 knockdown and control groups, using thresholds of adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1.

Western blot

Take an appropriate amount of the mixture of RIPA (P0013B, Beyotime) and PMSF (ST505, Beyotime) and add it to the tissue or cell samples required in the experiment for lysis. Subsequently, utilize the BCA kit (E112-01, Vazyme) to determine the protein concentration of the obtained protein supernatant. Next, the protein mixture was mixed with 5× protein loading buffer (SL1170-1ML, Solarbio) in a specific proportion, and the mixture was incubated at 100°C for 5 minutes. Then, successively added the corresponding protein samples to the 10% SDS-PAGE gel (SK6010-250T, Coolaber), and complete the electrophoresis and membrane transfer. After that, gently picked up the PVDF membrane obtained after membrane transfer with tweezers. After the samples were washed with TBST, they were placed in 5% skim milk and incubated at room temperature for 2 hours. Immediately afterwards, the PVDF membrane was washed thoroughly with TBST, the corresponding primary antibody working solutions were added, and the membrane was incubated on a shaker in a 4°C refrigerator for 12 hours. Upon completion of the primary antibody incubation, the PVDF membrane was washed with TBST, the corresponding HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (L3012, Solarbio) or HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody working solution (L3032, Solarbio) was added, and the membrane was incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. Finally, the PVDF membrane was washed with TBST, a general ECL luminescent solution (K-12045-D10, Advansta) was added, and the membrane was developed and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging analysis system (MiniChemi610, Sagecreation). The primary antibodies were listed in key resources table.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

The cells were washed twice with precooled PBS, and the PBS was aspirated completely after the last wash. Next, 0.5 mL of precooled RIPA buffer was added to every 5 × 106 cells. The cells were scraped from the culture dish or flask via a precooled cell scraper, and then the suspension was transferred into a 1.5-mL EP tube. The tube was incubated with gentle shaking at 4°C for 15 minutes. The mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C, and the supernatant was immediately transferred to a new EP tube. The protein A-agarose mixture was washed twice with PBS and then prepared at a 50% concentration with PBS. One hundred microlitres of protein A-agarose beads (50% for every 1 mL of protein lysate) were added to the supernatant, and the mixture was shaken gently on a horizontal shaker at 4°C for 10 minutes to eliminate nonspecific impurities and reduce the background. The sample was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C, the supernatant was transferred to a new EP tube, and the protein A beads were removed. A protein standard curve was generated via the Bradford method to measure the protein concentration. The total protein was diluted to approximately 1 μg/with PBS to decrease the concentration of detergent in the lysate. Two microlitres of antibody was added to 100 μL of the total lysate, and the antigen‒antibody mixture was gently agitated overnight at 4°C. One hundred microlitres of protein A-agarose beads were added to capture the antigen‒antibody complex, and the mixture was gently agitated again overnight at 4°C. The mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 seconds, the agarose-bead-antigen-antibody complex was collected, the supernatant was removed, and the complex was washed three times with precooled RIPA buffer and then three times with PBS. The agarose-bead-antigen-antibody complex was resuspended in 60 μL of 2X loading buffer and mixed gently. The sample was incubated in a metal bath for 5 minutes to dissociate the antigen, antibody, and beads, after which it was centrifuged, and the supernatant was stored at -20°C for further electrophoresis.

qRT-PCR

The method was described in our previously published papers.58 These RNAs were extracted by TRIzol® (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and reverse transcribed by HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme Biotech). Sequences of the primers: HSPB1-F: TGGCTACATCTCCCGGTGCTT; HSPB1-R: TCCACGGTCAGTGTGCCCTC. β-actin-F: CCTGGCACCCAGCACAAT; β-actin-R:GGGCCGGACTCGTCATAC.

ChIP-PCR

Digest cells with trypsin (at 37°C for 5 minutes), and complete medium was added to stop the digestion. The mixture was centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 minutes at room temperature, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were washed with 1 ml of PBS and centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 minutes at room temperature. The cells were resuspended in a mixture of buffer A, PBS and formaldehyde, with the final concentration of formaldehyde being 1.1%. The mixture was mixed well and incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes. Subsequently, glycine was added, and the mixture was mixed well to quench the formaldehyde. The mixture was centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C to pellet the cells. The cells were washed with 1 ml of precooled PBS and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C to pellet the cells. The cells were resuspended in Buffer B (ChIP kit, ab500, Abcam). The mixture was mixed well and incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C, after which the supernatant was removed. The cells were resuspended in Buffer C, mixed well and incubated at 4°C for 10 minutes. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C, after which the supernatant was removed. Next, 100 μl of Buffer D and protease inhibitor mixture were added. Sonicate (4 seconds of sonication, 6-second intervals, total time of 60 seconds, power of 20%). The mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected. A total of 20 μl of the sonicated supernatant was mixed with 100 μl of ddH2O. Then, 100 μl of the purified DNA mixture was added, and the mixture was mixed well. The mixture was incubated at 98°C for 10 minutes and then incubated at room temperature for 20 minutes. Centrifuge at 10,000 × g for 10 seconds. One microliter of protease K was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 5 seconds. The mixture was incubated at 55°C for 30 minutes and then at 98°C for 10 minutes. Centrifuge at 14,000 × g for 1 min at room temperature. The supernatant was removed and transferred to a new 1.5-ml centrifuge tube. A total of 10 μl was loaded onto a 1.5% agarose gel to analyze the size of the DNA fragments.

The 5x ChIP buffer was diluted to 1x with ddH2O, and protease inhibitors were added. Then, 1x ChIP buffer was added to the sonicated supernatant, and the mixture was vortexed for 5 seconds. Each CHIP sample was divided into 4 groups, and 100 μl was used as the input group. An appropriate amount of antibody was added to the sample, which was rotated at 4°C overnight. The agarose beads were washed and resuspended in 1x ChIP buffer. A total of 120 μl of the agarose bead suspension was mixed with 1 ml of 1x ChIP buffer and centrifuged at 500 × g for 3 minutes at 4°C, after which the supernatant was removed. One milliliter of 1x ChIP buffer was added, and the mixture was mixed well. Three hundred microliters of the bead/ChIP buffer mixture was aliquoted into three new 1.5-ml centrifuge tubes. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 500 × g for 2 minutes at 4°C, after which the supernatant was removed. The sample was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected and transferred to a centrifuge tube containing agarose beads. The mixture was rotated and mixed at 4°C for 40 minutes. The mixture was centrifuged at 500 × g for 3 minutes at 4°C, and the supernatant was removed. The samples were washed three times with 1x ChIP buffer.

The agarose beads were resuspended in 100 μl of purified DNA slurry, 50 μl of chromatin from the input group was removed, and 100 μl of purified DNA slurry was added. The mixture was mixed well, incubated at 98°C for 10 minutes and then incubated at room temperature for 20 minutes. Centrifuge at 10,000 × g for 10 seconds. One microliter of protease K was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 5 seconds. The mixture was incubated at 55°C for 30 minutes and then at 98°C for 10 minutes. Centrifuge at 14,000 × g for 1 min at room temperature. Seventy microliters of the supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5-ml centrifuge tube. The target genes of the samples were subsequently detected via qPCR. Sequences of the primers: HSPB1-F: TCAGGAGTTCAAGACCAGC; HSPB1-chip-R: CGCATGACAGAACAAGACCC.

Proliferation analysis

For MTT analysis, the cells in the logarithmic growth phase were counted, the concentration of the cell suspension was adjusted to 5000 cells per well, and then the mixture was added to a 96-well plate. The 96-well plate was incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 hours. The MTT solution was subsequently added to each well, and the mixture was incubated for another 4 hours to complete the formation of formazan. After the incubation was stopped, the culture medium in the wells was removed, and the mixture was gently shaken with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to dissolve the formazan crystals. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader was used to measure the absorbance of each well at 570 nm by a multifunctional microplate reader (MB-530,HEALES). The absorbance is directly proportional to the cell viability. The relative survival rate or inhibition rate of the cells was calculated according to the absorbance values. For EdU staining, operations were carried out according to the instructions of the EdU kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China).

Transwell assay

Serum-free culture medium was added to the upper chamber of the Transwell chamber for basilar membrane hydration treatment. For the Transwell invasion assay, prechilled Matrigel should be added in advance, but this step can be omitted in the Transwell migration assay. After digestion, centrifugation and washing, the cells were resuspended in serum-free medium, and the cell density was adjusted to 2.5 × 105. Serum-containing medium was added to the lower chamber, and then the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber of the Transwell system. The Transwell chamber was placed in the incubator for 6 hours of cultivation. After cultivation was complete, the culture medium was removed, the cells were fixed, and then, crystal violet staining was performed. The number of cells that migrated through the membrane to the lower chamber was counted under a microscope to evaluate the migratory and invasive abilities of the cells.

Tube formation assay

An appropriate amount of Matrigel precooled at 4°C was added to the cell culture plate, and the plate was then incubated in an incubator for 20 minutes until the Matrigel had completely solidified into a gel-like state. The endothelial cell suspension was inoculated onto a Matrigel-coated cell culture plate. The cell culture plate was placed in the cell incubator, allowing the endothelial cells to migrate on the surface of the Matrigel and form tubular structures. The incubation time was set as 5 hours. A microscope was used to take pictures of the tubular structures formed by endothelial cells, and then, quantitative analysis was performed with the help of imaging software.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) analysis

The cells that had undergone different treatments were digested and then washed twice with PBS (phosphate-buffered saline). The cells were subsequently placed in DMEM without serum or antibiotics, 10 μM 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein (H2DCFDA, ID3130, Solarbio, Beijing, China) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37°C in the dark for 30 minutes. After the cells were washed with PBS, a flow cytometer was used to analyze the level of ROS in the cells.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

We detected lipid peroxidation levels via a malondialdehyde (MDA) detection kit (Beyotime, S0131S, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, we collected 2 × 106-treated or transfected cells and mixed them with 150 μl of lysis buffer on ice for 30 minutes. Then, we mixed 100 μl of the sample supernatant with 200 μl of Thiobarbituric Acid (TBA) working solution at 100°C for 15 minutes. Moreover, we used the remaining supernatant of each sample for protein concentration detection. After centrifugation, we collected the supernatant, and the absorbance value measured at 532 nm indicated the MDA content. We calculated the MDA concentration to obtain the relative content of MDA, which is the ratio of the MDA concentration to the protein concentration.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

The cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde. Triton X-100 (a permeabilizing agent) was used to permeate the cell membranes, enabling antibodies to enter the interior of the cells. Goat serum blocking buffer was used to prevent nonspecific binding between the samples and the antibodies, and the samples were incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. After the sections were washed with PBST (phosphate-buffered saline with Tween-20), the primary antibody was added and incubated at 37°C for 2 hours. After the sections were washed with PBST, the secondary antibody was added, and the samples were incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. After the sections were washed with PBST again, DAPI dye was added, and the samples were incubated at 37°C for 15 minutes. After the sections were washed with PBST, they were mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium to prevent fluorescence quenching. Finally, the samples were observed and recorded under a fluorescence microscope (BA410E, Motic), and images were acquired. The primary antibodies were listed in key resources table.

Transmission electron microscope detection

The glutaraldehyde samples were prefixed for 6 hours and then refixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 hours. Acetone was used for gradient dehydration successively, with the concentration gradient of the dehydrating agent being 30%→50%→70%→80%→90%→95%→100% (the 100% concentration was changed three times), and the dehydration time was 10 min each time. The dehydrating agent and Epon-812 embedding agent were subsequently added at ratios of 3:1, 1:1, and 1:3, and infiltration was carried out in turn, with each infiltration lasting for 1.5 hours. Embedding was carried out with pure Epon-812 embedding agent. An ultramicrotome was used to make 80-nm ultrathin sections, which were then scooped onto copper grids. First, the samples were stained with uranyl acetate for 12 minutes and then with lead citrate for 2 minutes at room temperature. Finally, copper grids were used for image acquisition via a JEM-1400FLASH transmission electron microscope.

Multiplex immunofluorescence analysis of the OC tissue microarray

The OC tissue sections were carefully removed from the section box and placed face-up in a slide-baking machine at 65°C for 2 hours. After they were removed from the slide-baking machine, they were placed into a xylene staining tank for dewaxing for 40 minutes and then dewaxed in 100% ethanol for 15 minutes. Subsequently, gradient ethanol hydration (95%, 85%, 75%) was performed, with each group lasting for 10 minutes. The samples were then washed with PBS three times (5 minutes each time) and finally soaked in PBST for 30 seconds.

The tissue sections were placed in a repair box filled with pH 6.0 citric acid repair buffer, and antigen retrieval was performed in a microwave oven (medium heat for 8 minutes, heating for 8 minutes, and then turning to medium-low heat for 7 minutes to prevent excessive evaporation of the buffer and drying of the sections). After natural cooling, the slides were placed in PBS with a pH of 7.4 and shaken and washed three times (5 minutes each time) on a decolorization shaker. The samples were blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide solution at room temperature for 15 minutes and then soaked in PBST three times (5 minutes each). One hundred microliters of nonimmune normal goat serum was added, and the samples were incubated in a 37°C incubator for 30 minutes. The blocking solution was removed, all primary antibodies were added, and the samples were incubated overnight at 4°C.

The next day, the samples were soaked in PBST three times (5 minutes each time), HRP-polymer anti-rabbit immunohistochemical secondary antibody was added, and the samples were incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes. The samples were soaked in PBST five times (5 minutes each). TYR-570, TYR-520, TYR-690, TYR-780, and TYR-480 fluorescent dyes were allowed to react for 5 minutes each. After each reaction, the samples were soaked in PBST three times (5 min each).

The slides were placed in PBST with a pH of 7.4 and shaken and washed three times (5 minutes each time) on a decolorization shaker. After the sections were slightly dried by shaking, DAPI staining solution was added, and the samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 minutes. The slides were placed in PBS at a pH of 7.4 again and shaken and washed three times (5 min each time) on a decolorization shaker. After the sections were slightly dried, they were mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching mounting solution.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis

The OC cell culture medium was collected after treatment when it had been cultured in serum-free medium for 24 hours. The medium was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C to obtain the supernatant. The supernatant was subsequently added to the ELISA plate, and then biotin-labeled IL-6 antibody working solution and hrp-avidin working solution were added successively for interaction. Next, 90 μl of transmembrane domain substrate was added to the ELISA plate, which was incubated for 30 minutes in the dark, after which 50 μl of stop solution was added. Finally, the absorbance value at 450 nm was read via an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader, and the concentration of IL-6 was calculated. The CUSABIO® Human IL-6 ELISA kit (CUSABIO Biotech Co. Ltd., CSB-E04638h, Wuhan, China) was used.

Immunohistochemistry staining

The tissue was immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours to preserve the morphological structure of the tissue cells. The fixed tissue was then dehydrated with a series of ethanol gradients (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 100% ethanol). The sample was subsequently made transparent with xylene, and the tissue was subsequently embedded in paraffin to form a paraffin block for easy sectioning. A microtome was used to cut the paraffin-embedded tissue into 4-μm-thick slices, which were attached to glass slides. The slices were first dewaxed in xylene for 40 minutes and then rehydrated in distilled water through a series of ethanol gradients (100%, 95%, 90%, 80%, 70% ethanol). The slices were placed in a citric acid buffer solution with a pH of 6.0 and heat-treated in a pressure cooker for 15 minutes after the cooker started to steam. The slices were treated with a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution for 20 minutes to block the activity of endogenous peroxidase and prevent it from interfering with subsequent immune reactions. The slices were washed with PBST three times for five minutes each. The diluted primary antibody was added to the slices, which were placed in a humid box and incubated overnight at 4°C. The slices were washed with PBST three times for five minutes each. The secondary antibody was added to the slices, which were placed in a humid box and incubated for 2 hours in a 37°C incubator. The slices were washed with PBST three times for five minutes each. The DAB solution was dropped onto the slices, the degree of staining was observed under a microscope, and the reaction was stopped when brown‒yellow or brown‒brown coloration clearly appeared. The slices were thoroughly washed with the DAB staining solution with clean water. The slices were counterstained with hematoxylin to color the cell nuclei, facilitating the observation of the overall cell structure. The hematoxylin on the slices was thoroughly washed with clean water. Finally, the slices were mounted with mounting medium such as neutral balsam to complete the preparation of the immunohistochemical slices, and the results were observed under a microscope. The immunohistochemical kit used was Zhongshan Jinqiao PV-9001. The primary antibodies were listed in key resources table.

Molecular docking

The specific experimental methods for this section can be referenced from our previous studies.17 The predicted 3D conformation of the underlying protein ESM1 was obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (AlphaFold DB), while the protein structure of ERBB2 was acquired from the Protein Data Bank (PDB).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R software (version 4.3.0) and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Detailed information, including the exact value of n, definition of center, and measures of dispersion and precision, are all presented in the figure legends section. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed unpaired Student's t-tests were performed. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test was applied for multiple group comparisons. Two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was used for time-course analyses across groups. Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) tests evaluated survival curve differences. All experiments were randomized and blinded to minimize experimental bias. Statistical significance was defined as p< 0.05.
