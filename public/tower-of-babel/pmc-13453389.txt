Occupational hazards in hip and knee adult reconstruction surgery
Authors: Matteo Innocenti, Roberto Civinini, Filippo Leggieri, Sébastien Lustig, Cécile Batailler, Simon N. van Laarhoven, Gijs van Hellemondt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsAdult reconstruction surgeons face unique occupational challenges during joint arthroplasty procedures, including sustained physical demands, ergonomic strain, noise exposure, and psychological pressure from high patient expectations. This systematic review aimed to synthesize existing evidence regarding physical and psychological stressors encountered by surgeons performing hip and knee arthroplasty.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, we searched PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library through to December 2024. Studies evaluating occupational hazards during adult hip and knee arthroplasty were included, focusing on noise exposure, ergonomic challenges, musculoskeletal strain, and robotic-assisted platforms’ impact on surgeon performance. Risk of bias was assessed using Appraisal tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS) and Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) tools.ResultsIn total, 38 studies were included, revealing five key hazard categories. Work-related injury prevalence ranged from 66% to 96.5%, predominantly affecting spine (28% to 34.2%), shoulders, and upper limbs. Risk factors included age > 55 years, high surgical volume (> 100 procedures annually), and > 20 years’ practice. CO2 accumulation in helmet systems exceeded safety limits in 10/11 configurations tested. Joint arthroplasties generated the highest noise levels (77 dBA to 88.2 dBA) among orthopaedic procedures, with arthroplasty surgeons showing nearly triple the hearing loss rate compared with non-surgical clinicians (31% vs 11%, odds ratio 3.63). Total hip arthroplasty demonstrated higher physiological demands than total knee arthroplasty (heart rate: 95.7 vs 90.2 bpm; energy: 290.2 vs 189.4 kcal). Revision surgeries imposed doubled mental demand scores compared with primary procedures. Robotic assistance improved ergonomics through reduced neck and lumbar flexion, but increased operating times and generated higher noise levels.ConclusionArthroplasty surgeons face substantial occupational hazards with high musculoskeletal injury rates, significant noise-induced hearing loss, and considerable physiological demands. While emerging technologies show promise in reducing some physical demands, they introduce new ergonomic considerations, highlighting the need for comprehensive occupational health strategies to ensure sustainable surgical careers.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(8):1037–1045.

Introduction

Adult reconstruction surgeons routinely perform complex joint arthroplasty procedures that require exceptional physical endurance, technical precision, and psychological resilience.1

These surgeries place significant physical demands on the surgeon, including sustained application of force during critical operative steps such as bone preparation, broaching, reaming, and implant impaction.2-5 Additionally, these procedures often require surgeons to maintain ergonomically challenging positions for an extended time while applying considerable force during component reduction or placement.2,6,7 Surgeons must also manage extensive instrument sets and implant inventories while adhering to strict sterile techniques, frequently working in constraining personal protective equipment such as surgical space suits and double gloves. The prolonged use of power tools and impaction instruments further exposes surgeons to excessive vibration and elevated noise levels throughout the operations.8-12 Modern technological advances such as robotic-assisted surgical systems have introduced additional complexities, requiring surgeons to integrate and manage these technologies within the already confined and demanding operative environment.13,14

While previous expert reviews have identified specific occupational hazards in hip and knee adult reconstruction surgery,15-17 and systematic reviews have documented work-related musculoskeletal disorders broadly across orthopaedic surgery,2 no systematic review has comprehensively synthesized evidence across all categories of occupational hazards specifically affecting adult reconstruction surgeons.

To address this gap, the present systematic review aims to synthesize the existing evidence regarding the physical and psychological stressors encountered by adult hip and knee reconstruction surgeons.

Understanding these occupational hazards is crucial for developing evidence-based interventions to preserve surgeon health, ensure sustainable surgical careers, and ultimately maintain the quality and availability of arthroplasty care for an ageing population with increasing demand for joint arthroplasty procedures.

Methods

Design

This systematic review adheres to the PRISMA guidelines.18 This review was not listed in PROSPERO. Informed consent and Ethical Committee approval were not required. A comprehensive search of electronic databases was conducted to identify the relevant studies published from inception to 31 December 2024. The reference lists from the articles were evaluated to uncover any additional articles not identified in the initial research. The combination of search terms and the research question employed to systematically retrieve pertinent studies can be found in the Supplementary Material. The initial search across the databases generated 4,108 studies. The PRISMA 2020 flow diagram used to summarize the research strategy is shown in Figure 1.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for systematic reviews.

Eligibility

The articles were selected based on the eligibility criteria outlined in the PICOS question (Table I). Studies were included if they were experimental or observational analyses evaluating occupational hazards during adult hip and knee arthroplasty procedures, focusing specifically on noise exposure, surgical helmet systems and space suits, ergonomics, musculoskeletal strain, and the impact of robotic-assisted platforms on surgeon physical and cognitive performance. Exclusion criteria comprised in vitro studies, letters to the editor, previous systematic reviews, book chapters, conference papers, and studies for which the full text was not accessible.

Data collection

Zotero (Roy Rosenzweig Center for History and New Media, 2016; USA) was used to remove duplicates. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts (MI and FL), with senior reviewer arbitration for disagreements (GvH). Data extraction included study design, measured parameters, sample size, population characteristics, and outcomes. Studies were categorized into five domains: CO2 accumulation in surgical helmet systems, ergonomics and musculoskeletal strain, noise exposure, robotic compared with conventional arthroplasty impact, and physical/mental demands.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment was conducted using the Appraisal tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS)19 and the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) for the other study designs.

Statistical analysis

Due to the heterogeneity in study designs, measurement techniques, and outcome definitions across the included studies, formal meta-analyses were not feasible. Data synthesis was performed through narrative review and descriptive analysis. Prevalence rates, means, and 95% CIs were extracted and presented as reported in the original studies.

Results

After full-text assessment, 38 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis.3,5,6,12-15,20-49 A total of 43 articles were excluded for failing to meet the inclusion criteria for the following reasons: four were Letter to Editor,50-53 full texts of seven articles were not available,54-61 seven were not related to orthopaedic surgery,62-68 one was not related to orthopaedic surgeons,69 and 24 were related to hazards for orthopaedic surgeons but did not address arthroplasty surgeons specifically.70-94

CO2 accumulation in surgical helmet systems

Studies which measured CO2 levels in surgical helmet systems revealed concerning safety threshold exceedances. Current standards had set occupational limits at 5,000 ppm (ACGIH eight-hour threshold) and 1,000 ppm for indoor spaces (World Health Organization).95,96

Laboratory simulations demonstrated significant CO2 accumulation. Rubman et al20 found that ten of 11 helmet configurations exceeded 5,000 ppm during 20 W exercise simulation, with bidirectional air flow systems showing higher levels (mean 10,324 ppm) compared with unidirectional systems (5,744 ppm). Patel et al21 documented progressive CO2 increases in Stryker helmets from baseline (1,400 ppm (SD 200)) to light exercise (2,600 ppm (SD 800), p = 0.0109) and moderate exercise (3,100 ppm (SD 500), p = 0.0002). Fan failure caused rapid accumulation to 5,000 ppm within 12 seconds (SD 9), peaking at 18,100 ppm during moderate exercise (p < 0.001), with incomplete recovery after reactivation.21

Real-world validation by Board et al22 in 30 arthroplasty procedures confirmed laboratory findings, showing mean CO2 levels of 3,007 ppm (SD 1,214) with 72.6% of operating time above 2,500 ppm and 5.4% ≥ 5,000 ppm. Surgeons experienced higher exposure (3,001 ppm) than assistants (2,448 ppm, p < 0.05), while fan speed variations had minimal impact.

Ergonomics and musculoskeletal strain

Eight studies investigated work-related injuries and ergonomic challenges among arthroplasty surgeons.3,5,6,15,23-26 Work-related injuries were highly prevalent among arthroplasty surgeons, with rates ranging from 66%3 to 96.5%.15 Most studies reported rates between 68% and 83%, indicating this is a widespread occupational health concern.5,6,15,24-26 Lower back pain affected 28% to 34% of surgeons,3,15 with both lumbar and cervical regions frequently involved.15 Upper limb involvement varied significantly, from 14% for lateral epicondylitis and shoulder tendinitis,3 to 79% for forearm, wrist, and/or hand injuries and 49% for shoulder injuries.6 Shoulder overuse disorders were particularly common.25

Injury consequences included time off work (10% to 27%),3,6 decreased work effectiveness (56%),23 and concerns about future surgical performance (59%).15 Despite these challenges, most surgeons (88%) believed they could meet job demands until retirement.24

Poor surgical posture was prevalent, with 61% reporting excessive head tilting (> 15°) and 51% experiencing back flexion (> 10°).23 Surgical positioning was found to significantly impact performance: surgeons standing on the operative side compared with the hand-dominant side showed shorter operative times (32.3 vs 60.4 mins) and lower caloric expenditure (114 vs 243 calories).26 Biomechanical analysis during acetabular reaming demonstrated peak torques of 1.54 to 1.76 Nm, with higher feed rates increasing physical strain.5

Key risk factors included age > 55 years,3 high surgical volume (> 100 procedures annually), > 20 years’ practice, higher BMI, and increased age correlating with higher pain scores.15 Technology impact remained mixed: robotic impaction systems reduced fatigue,25 while navigation/robotics showed no correlation with injury prevalence.26

Noise exposure

A total of 12 studies evaluated noise exposure in arthroplasty procedures,12,27-38 measuring equivalent continuous sound levels (LAeq), maximum levels (MDL), peak sound pressure levels (LCpeak), and time-weighted averages (TWA). Joint arthroplasties consistently generated the highest noise levels across surgical specialties, with arthroplasty procedures reaching 77 to 88.2 dBA compared with other surgeries (65 to 76 dBA).29,34

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) showed similar average exposures (65 to 80 dBA) but differed in peak characteristics. THA generated higher peak levels (up to 140.6 dB vs 134.4 dB for TKA)37 and more frequent excessive exposures,34 while showing lower average levels (78.67 vs 80.9 dBA).27 Time-weighted exposures remained consistent (THA: 64.7 dBA, TKA: 64.5 dBA),32 while robotic TKA produced even higher levels (LAeq 88.2 dBA).34

Peak noise events occur during specific surgical steps. In THA, femoral canal rasping reached 135 dB(C) peak/99 dB(A) LAeq, followed by cup impaction (131 dB(C)/97 dB(A)).27 TKA hammering produces maximum levels of 105.6 dB(A).38 Additionally, specific instruments showed distinct noise patterns: hammers (90.2 dBA), saws (80.6 to 88.0 dBA), chisels (84.1 dBA), and drills (74.6 to 79.1 dBA).12,31 These high-noise instruments are used for relatively brief periods, with saws having the longest duration (200 seconds) followed by hammers (82 seconds).30 Peters et al28 documented metal-on-metal hammering peaks ranged from 121.0 to 128.1 dB(C) across different steps.

Clear exposure gradients exist by operating theatre position. Surgeons experienced the highest exposure (135.3 to 140.6 dB LCPeak), decreasing for assistants (132 to 134 dB), scrub nurses (125.8 to 126.5 dB), and anaesthesiologists (121 to 125.8 dB).84 Surgeons showed asymmetrical exposure with higher levels at the right shoulder (81.4 to 86.8 dBA) compared with the left (77.9 to 83.2 dBA, p = 0.04).37

Palmer et al36 documented significant occupational hearing risk, with arthroplasty surgeons showing nearly triple the rate of hearing loss compared with non-surgical clinicians (31% vs 11%, odds ratio (OR) 3.63, 95% CI 1.52 to 8.67, p = 0.004). Self-reported hearing loss was similarly elevated (61% vs 40%, OR 2.79, p = 0.003).

Safety standard compliance varies: 28.6% exceeded National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) limits while none exceeded Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) standards.35 Surgeons spend 54% to 72% of shifts in surgery, with 10% to 18% of surgical time exceeding 85 dBA.33

Ergonomic, physical, and environmental impact of conventional versus robotic-assisted joint arthroplasty

Five studies evaluated robotic compared with conventional arthroplasty across ergonomics, efficiency, and environmental parameters.13,14,39-41 Robotic assistance demonstrated significant ergonomic improvements with procedure-specific variations in efficiency and physiological demands. Robotic-assisted TKA demonstrated superior ergonomic outcomes compared with manual techniques, with surgeons experiencing reduced neck movement at T3 (14° (SD 8°) vs 19° (SD 8°)) and occiput (36° (SD 14°) vs 51° (SD 10°)), and spending significantly less time in non-neutral positions at both T3 (63% (SD 36%) vs 78% (SD 25%), p = 0.02) and occiput levels (94% (SD 7%) vs 100% (SD 2%), p = 0.00).13

Lumbar ergonomics improved with less flexion (-13.1° (SD 6°) vs -22.3° (SD 5°), p < 0.001) and reduced time in demanding positions (23% vs 55%, p < 0.001).41 For unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA), robotic assistance showed even greater neck benefits with reduced flexion (-23.4° vs -49.1°, p < 0.001) and less time in demanding positions (60% vs 94%, p = 0.001).14

Energy expenditure patterns differed by procedure. Robotic TKA showed similar total expenditure (122.4 (SD 29) vs 112.3 (SD 37) calories, p = 0.119) but lower per-minute rates (2.53 (SD 0.4) vs 3.50 (SD 0.7) cal/min, p < 0.001).41 Conversely, robotic UKA increases both total (154.0 vs 89.1 calories, p < 0.001) and per-minute energy demands (3.3 vs 2.8 cal/min, p = 0.015).14

Cardiovascular effects varied by procedure. Robotic TKA reduced mean (SD) heart rate (81.5 (4) vs 90.1 (5) bpm, p < 0.001) with higher variability, suggesting reduced stress.41 However, robotic UKA increased heart rate (88.7 vs 84.7 bpm, p = 0.027).14

Noise levels differed between robotic systems, with MAKO producing higher levels (81.7 dB(A)) than NAVIO (77.4 dB(A)) and CORI (77.9 dB(A)).39 Surgeons experienced highest exposure during TKA (83.7 dB(A) (SD 3.4)).40 Daily exposure from four TKAs exceeded safety thresholds (82 dB vs 78 dB for THAs).

Significant improvements occurred within 20 cases, with surgical times decreasing (53.2 mins (SD 10) vs 43.2 mins (SD 5), p = 0.016) and energy expenditure reducing (137.6 calories (SD 27) vs 107.1 calories (SD 24), p = 0.015).41

Physical and mental demands

Nine studies analyzed physical and mental demands during hip and knee arthroplasty.42-49,97

Overall, THA consistently demonstrated higher physiological demands compared with TKA. Sharkey et al48 and Khan et al49 both reported significantly higher energy expenditure and cardiovascular strain during THA procedures.

Direct anterior approach increased physiological stress compared with posterior approach, with higher stress index (17.4 vs 12.4, p < 0.001), elevated heart rate (101 vs 98.3 bpm, p = 0.007), and increased energy expenditure (349 vs 295 cal/hr, p < 0.001), despite shorter operating times (71.4 vs 82.1 minutes, p = 0.001).44 Among these, higher patient BMI correlated with increased surgeon energy expenditure (slope: 4.71, p = 0.027) and elevated heart rate (slope: 0.24, p = 0.037).45 Later case timing (fourth or later) increases respiratory rate (p = 0.001) and minute ventilation (p = 0.006) without affecting energy expenditure or heart rate.45

Mental stress patterns also varied by training configuration: surgeries performed by solo trainees produced the highest mean arterial pressure values for both trainers and trainees, while trainer-led cases showed lower pressure increases for both participants.43 However, surgical experience did not significantly affect cardiovascular responses.46 The introduction of surgical technology demonstrated potential benefits for reducing mental stress and improving efficiency in arthroplasty procedures. Sappey-Marinier et al42 found that individualized TKA systems resulted in lower NASA TLX scores across all dimensions, including a 5.6-point improvement in mental demand compared with traditional systems, while also achieving shorter operating theatre times (10 minutes less, 95% CI 2 to 17, p = 0.018) and requiring fewer instrument trays (five vs eight trays). Similarly, Caba et al97 reported significantly lower mental demand scores with robotic-assisted THA compared with manual THA, particularly during acetabular reaming (3.2 (SD 1.5) vs 5.7 (SD 1.0), p < 0.01), along with modest reductions in task duration (11 vs 12 minutes) and caloric expenditure (83.5 kcal (SD 0.34) vs 100 kcal (SD 28)), though the latter differences were not statistically significant.

Revision surgeries imposed substantially higher mental demands across both procedures. For THA, revision cases showed 106% increased NASA TLX workload (10.85 vs 5.26, p < 0.001) with elevated physical demands (11.59 vs 6.08, p < 0.01).47 TKA revisions demonstrate even greater increases with 176% higher workload (8.67 vs 3.13, p < 0.001) and significantly elevated mental demand scores (p < 0.001).48

Risk of bias assessment

Tabulated risk of bias assessment results are provided in the Supplementary Material. The risk of bias assessment using the AXIS tool was performed for all cross-sectional studies,3,6,15,22-24,36 with total scores indicating relatively consistent methodological quality across studies with no substantial variations in overall scoring.

The MINORS assessment for non-cross-sectional studies demonstrated scores out of maximum of 16 or 24 for non-comparative and comparative studies, respectively. All the studies showed a risk of bias from low to moderate risk.5,12,13,20,21,25-35,37-49,97 No studies were classified as having high risk of bias.

Discussion

This systematic review assessed occupational hazards facing arthroplasty surgeons, including musculoskeletal injuries, noise exposure, and mental demands. The findings reveal multifaceted risks that threaten both wellbeing and career longevity.

Injuries predominantly affect the spine, shoulders, and upper limbs, correlating with biomechanical stresses inherent in arthroplasty procedures. Surgeon-related risk factors included age > 55 years, high surgical volume (> 100 procedures annually), and > 20 years practice. Procedure-related factors such as higher patient BMI, THA surgery, direct anterior approach, and revision procedures significantly increased surgeon workload and stress. A previous review found musculoskeletal disorders are the second most common occupational disease among operating room personnel, with lumbar injuries most prevalent.98 The physical demands mirror those of competitive athletes, involving sustained, repetitive movements in challenging positions.99-102 The greater physiological demands observed for THA compared with TKA could be explained by anatomical constraints requiring work through deep muscle layers and sustained awkward postures. Acetabular reaming demands continuous rotational force against dense bone in confined spaces, while femoral preparation involves repetitive mallet strikes creating cumulative musculoskeletal stress. The predominantly manual nature of hip instruments compared with TKA’s battery-powered tools may require greater direct force generation, potentially accounting for the observed differences in heart rates and energy expenditure.

Current noise levels in operating theatres frequently exceed those of a busy highway, reaching peaks of up to 120 dBA during surgical procedures and far surpassing federal limits for occupational noise exposure.9 Moreover, arthroplasty procedures generate the highest noise levels among orthopaedic procedures, with specific steps like femoral canal broaching reaching harmful peaks.17 High-powered surgical tools in orthopaedics theatres can generate noise as loud as 145 dB, placing all operating theatre personnel at risk of varying grades of noise-induced hearing loss.103 A recent systematic review demonstrated that listening to music during surgery shows a net positive effect on surgical performance despite contributing to overall noise levels.104

Current interventions follow NIOSH hierarchy: elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and personal protective equipment.105 While elimination is impractical in orthopaedics, newer generation surgical tools such as saws and drills, hammers, and suction devices may reduce the hazard. Engineering controls may include regular equipment maintenance, while administrative controls may target high-noise procedure exposure. Personal protective equipment remains problematic; surgical hoods provided no benefit, and hearing protection impairs communication which is essential for surgical flow and psychological stress prevention.106

Robotic assistance presents mixed occupational impacts. While reducing musculoskeletal strain through improved ergonomics, robotics introduces longer operating times, increased static positioning, and higher noise levels. However, automated systems significantly reduce repetitive stress injuries affecting 96% of surgeons, and decrease mental workload through preoperative templating. This is particularly important given that revision procedures show doubled cognitive demands and 40% of healthcare professionals experience high emotional exhaustion.47,48

Robotic-assisted surgery may reduce physical demands through automated phases and elimination of repetitive gestures.16 Mental workload may also decrease via preoperative templating that shifts decisions from intraoperative to planning phases and guided implant positioning.107,108 Moreover, the potential impact of robotics could be meaningful in revision surgery, particularly in alleviating physical and mental stress by mitigating the cognitive burden during complex cases.

These interconnected hazards require comprehensive occupational health strategies addressing ergonomics, noise mitigation, and stress management to ensure sustainable surgical careers.

The findings indicate three critical implementation needs. Comprehensive ergonomic interventions should promote neutral postures, minimize excessive force and motion, maintain proper height and clearance, reduce fatigue and pressure points, and encourage movement within a comfortable environment.109 Emerging technologies such as wearable exoskeletons and structured microbreak protocols show promise for injury prevention.110 The high injury prevalence necessitates robust occupational health surveillance programmes with standardized screening and early intervention frameworks.111 The substantial noise exposure suggested the need for substituting quieter equipment, implementing regular audiometric screening programmes, and developing custom hearing protection which is specifically designed for surgical environments, balancing noise reduction with essential communication needs.11 Identification of high-risk activities and positions should inform ergonomic training programmes for residents. The cumulative nature of occupational risks suggests career planning must include workload management and technique modification strategies as surgeons age.

Critical research gaps include biomechanical analysis of surgical movement patterns to identify inefficient gestures that increase energy expenditure and injury risk, motion analysis studies to optimize surgical technique training, and comprehensive assessment of how emerging technologies alter fundamental surgical ergonomics and workflow efficiency over extended practice periods.

This systematic review has several important limitations affecting the interpretation of these findings. Most studies relied on self-reported injury and symptom data, introducing potential recall bias and under-reporting, particularly for less severe injuries. The predominantly cross-sectional study design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between specific surgical practices and occupational injuries. Included studies showed considerable variability in methodological approaches, measurement techniques, and outcome definitions, making direct comparisons challenging and preventing formal meta-analyses. Sample sizes varied considerably, with several small cohorts potentially unrepresentative of the broader arthroplasty surgeon population. Geographical distribution was limited, with most studies from developed healthcare systems, potentially affecting generalizability to different practice settings and resource environments. Measurement of physiological demands and ergonomic parameters often occurred in controlled settings that may not fully reflect real-world surgical practice complexities and variability. The impact of emergency cases, complex revisions, and varying team dynamics was not thoroughly captured in available literature, potentially underestimating the true scope of occupational hazards faced by arthroplasty surgeons in clinical practice.

In conclusion, arthroplasty surgeons face substantial occupational hazards, with an alarmingly high prevalence of musculoskeletal injuries, significant noise exposure, and considerable physiological demands that vary by procedure type and surgical approach. While emerging technologies like robotic assistance show promise in reducing some physical demands, they introduce new ergonomic considerations and may increase other occupational stresses, highlighting the need for comprehensive ergonomic assessment in surgical innovation.

Take home message

- This systematic review provides the first comprehensive assessment of occupational hazards facing arthroplasty surgeons, revealing a pattern of work-related health risks that threaten both individual surgeon wellbeing and healthcare system sustainability.

- The findings demonstrate that these hazards are multifaceted, interconnected, and may compromise long-term career viability for arthroplasty surgeons.
