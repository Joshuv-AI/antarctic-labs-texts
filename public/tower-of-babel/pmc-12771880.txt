A multiple criteria framework for value-based assessment of health care services applied to a radiology case
Authors: Ana Moreira, Nazaré Rego, José Crispim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditionally, value-based health care assessment initiatives have: (1) not explicitly evaluated value; (2) been complex (which limits reproducibility) and not admitted customization; and 4) not operationalized value in a way that addresses the concerns of the involved stakeholders. This research developed a four-step framework for value-based assessment of health care services that aims to overcome these limitations. The development of the framework was supported by a case study conducted in the radiology department of the second largest general hospital in Portugal and involved the participation of administrators, physicians, radiographers, and patients, and incorporates several Multiple Criteria Decision Analysis methods. A set of 160 indicators to assess the value of radiology departments was identified in the literature and synthesized into 7 value-assessment dimensions. The framework considers specific indicators for each stakeholder type and suggests customizable ways to determine the benefit of the service. Additionally, we determined the value of the radiology department (considering benefits perceived by stakeholders and direct clinical costs) and propose a dashboard to monitor its evolution. The developed management tool enables an explicit valuation of the department and uncovers service features that need to be improved. If its use is continued, it will report the evolution of the department. The framework can easily be adapted to other hospitals or departments or be used by researchers as a basis for the development of other models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13288-2.Keywords: Performance indicators in radiology, Value assessment framework, Value-based healthcare, Value-based radiology

Introduction

A radiology service (either a department of a larger medical facility or an independent organization) aggregates a set of health care resources shared across all levels of health care delivery and medical specialties. Radiology has been viewed as an adjunct to quality care, but not a primary contributor to value. Much of the literature in the area describes image acquisition and diagnostic accuracy rather than the contributions to the health outcomes of patients [1]. In a hospital, radiology is typically seen as a center of costs and volumes and its budget is more dependent on the decisions taken in previous years than on the value the department creates. Although several studies have documented the value added by radiology [e.g., 2], its precise contribution to the health care value chain remains uncertain [3].

Studies on the implementation of value-based health care (VBHC) models in radiology departments are scarce and those available have been conducted in the United States. The existing studies have highlighted that the significant lack of specific quality indicators and the disconnection between the available indicators and costs (which are fundamental for payment or reimbursement) have limited the availability of information thus affecting the implementation of more developed VBHC models. This gap has been referred to independently of the model implemented (for example, in radiology, it was mentioned when a merit-based incentive payment [4] or a bundled payment [5] system was implemented), the medical specialty (it was, for example, noted in cardiology [6]), or the department/hospital at stake (for example [7, 8]),. The development, testing, and validation of meaningful improved quality indicators require significant effort and resources. In health care services that are already burdened and have limited resources, this can perpetuate the reporting of irrelevant quality indicators [9]. These gaps emphasize the need for specific and relevant VBHC indicators that improve the quality of care and reliably identify practice variation.

VBHC seeks to place patients at the center of the care model [3], aiming at encouraging the improvement of results for patients, therefore centered on quality instead of quantity. Within VBHC, value should always be defined around the customer (patient), not the supplier (health care or technology provider), as the latter tends to follow a strictly economic perspective [10]. This expanded concept of value desirably encompasses efficiency, safety, patient compliance, and satisfaction [10], and is crucial to achieving goals such as access and equity [11]. As such, VBHC is inherently multidimensional and can vary from patient to patient (or other stakeholders) and in time. Patients may assign different weights to outcomes in the hierarchy [10], i.e., valuation requires comprehensive frameworks that allow for customization based on preferences [12]. The inputs for value definition (costs and benefits) vary widely across the relevant stakeholders (health professionals, patients, payers, caregivers, hospital administrators, etc.) [13]. “Active and continual multi-stakeholder engagement […] is needed to sustain positive feedback loops allowing VBHC to adapt, scale-up and spread in a new context, preventing it from fading […]” [14]. The customization of value-based models is indispensable because “no single framework with a predefined set of weighted variables can reflect the needs of disparate stakeholder groups or even the inevitable heterogeneity within a particular group” [15]. Usually, when the hospital board of directors uses the profit obtained at the department to assess contribution to the hospital, only the perspective of some stakeholders is considered. This traditional business model has a limited ability to improve value (i.e., patient health outcomes) [16]. A recent literature review on VBHC implementation initiative concluded that a major gap related to the generalized inability to measure outcomes that matter to patients remains [17]. This means that ambiguity and complexity inherent to VBHC have not yet been tackled [18]. Multiple criteria decision analysis (MCDA) methods can be used to handle ambiguity and to combine value attributes into a single measure that facilitates value interpretation [19]. Tools that address the multidimensionality of the VBHC from a multi-stakeholder systems-based perspective (i.e., that account for the impacts of actions in the various components of the system) and are easiest to understand and implement are needed.

Health care value assessment frameworks (VAF) (i.e., tools that combine methods and indicators to determine heath care value) have emerged as tools to assist decision-makers in assessing the relative value of services and treatments [15]. Typically, these frameworks [20] have identified indicators, but their full implementation (i.e., collecting data, calculating the indicators, and determining and interpreting value) has not been described, probably because their implementation needs a built-in capability to collect and analyze outcome and costing data [21].

Most VBHC frameworks (e.g., IVI-RA, ICER, ACC-AHA, ESMO, etc.) have been created to support economic evaluations among decision-makers concerning drugs or technology [22, 23]. Recent reviews concluded that VBHC frameworks or VAF have:

In summary, three main gaps have been identified in the literature related to value-based initiatives: (1) they do not explicitly evaluate value [25]; (2) they are complex (which limits their reproducibility) and do not admit customization [26]; and (3) at operationalizing value, they do not address the concerns of the various, sometimes conflicting, involved perspectives [25]. This study aimed to develop a complete, comprehensive, and customizable VAF that considers the different perspectives of several different stakeholders and measures the effects of an action in the value from those perspectives.

Methods

Research design

This study adopted a Multiple Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) bottom-up, 3-step methodological process (Fig. 1) for building a value-based assessment framework. The study applied qualitative and quantitative methods under the scope of a single case study: the radiology department of the second biggest hospital in Portugal (that provides direct assistance to a population of around 300,000 people), and operates 24/7 for emergencies and operates, and 11 h/day, 6 days a week for scheduled exams. Approximately 115 professionals (i.e., radiographers, radiologists, administrators, and receptionists) work in the department; in 2019, the department performed more than 484,000 exams (on average, 1327 exams per day) of different types (conventional radiology, ultrasounds, tomographs and biopsies, magnetic resonances, interventional radiology, etc.), using 33 pieces of equipment.

Multiple criteria decision analysis framework-building methodological process

Data were collected through questionnaires, and quantitative information was collected from the hospital databases. Costs in Portuguese NHS hospitals follow a top-down approach. Therefore, to assess the costs of a given department, we have to rely on the institution’s central accounting with the limitation that only average costs per patient are known. This approach limits the internal management of the organization since production centers and cost centers are not uniformly defined. For example, the radiology department and the administrative department contribute to the production of other departments (e.g., cardiology) but are seen only as cost sources. The department’s costs include direct costs (i.e., consumables, personnel, subcontracting, depreciation of assets, etc.), and indirect costs that arise from shared services (e.g., administrative). In this study, we considered the total annual cost value attributed to the service as recorded in the hospital’s central accounting. The framework proposed incorporates several statistical tests, and multicriteria decision-making methods, as described in the sections below.

Literature review

The study started with a scoping review of the literature published from 2014 to 2021 following JBI’s Population (or participants), Concept, Context (PCC) [27] and PRISMA-ScR [28] guidelines (Supplement 1) aimed at answering the question What benefit indicators are available for radiology services? - The concept of interest was benefit indicators, and the context was radiology services [27]. Thus, the inclusion criteria were: focusing on value; in radiology; and identifying benefit indicators. We considered literature from 2014 onwards because this was the year when published research started synthesizing metrics related to radiology (e.g., in terms of quality [29], or performance-based reimbursement [30]). Only original research published in peer-reviewed journals was considered. Medline (through PubMed), Scopus, and Web of Science were searched for (“value” OR “value-based” OR “value-added”) AND (“measurement” OR “measurements” OR “metric” OR “metrics” OR “measure” OR “measures”) AND (“radiology department” OR “imaging department” OR “radiology departments” OR “imaging departments”) in titles, abstracts, or keywords. Only documents written in English were considered. After duplicates were removed, the abstracts of the remaining studies were read and their full texts were skimmed in order to select a final set of studies that were fully and carefully read. The purpose of the scoping review was descriptive and the analysis registered, for each article in the dataset, the benefit dimensions or indicators identified in a data extraction table prepared for that purpose.

The indicators and categories identified in the literature (Supplement 2) were then synthesized and adapted to the radiology department, which involved various opinion exchanges among the researchers, a senior expert, and the professionals of the department.

Delphi method

Delphi [31] is a method to organize and structure an expert discussion aiming to generate insights on controversial topics with limited information [32]. It assures the anonymity of participating experts who are invited to assess and comment on questions related to a specific research topic across multiple discussion rounds [33]. After each round, experts can review the aggregated results and reconsider their assessment based on the added information. The Delphi method was used to develop and validate the framework. The collection and analysis of the expert opinions were used to (1) select the final set of indicators and their weights [34] and (2) to assess the framework’s ability to consistently guide the process for which it was designed and meet the operational needs of users [35]. The questionnaire (that was developed for this study to include a synthesis of the indicators identified in the literature review and adapted to the radiology department) is composed of two parts (Supplement 3): (1) benefit indicators organized per categories, and (2) categories of benefit indicators. In the first round, we used rating scales, and, in the second, we turned to rank-order questions. In this study, it was decided that the experts would be members of two groups of stakeholders: internal (radiographers and the administrators of the department) and external (patients or their caregivers and prescribing physicians). For internal stakeholders, the criteria considered were: belonging to a professional group of the radiology service and having at least 2 years of working experience in radiology. External participants were required to know the department well (i.e., persons with sporadic contacts were excluded - invitations were sent to persons with higher interactions with the department). The internal participants were: (1) 6 radiographers out of 62, 73% female, 12 years of experience on average (range: 2 to 26 years); and (2) 2 qualified administrators out of 2 (including a radiologist; other biographic details about this group cannot be provided due to confidentially issues). The external participants were: 1) 20 patients aged between 20 and 73 years old (average 42 years), 45% undergraduate; 55% female; and 3) 4 prescribing physicians (2 orthopedists, 1 internal medicine doctor, and 1 general practitioner; any hospital specialist doctor can prescribe so the total number of prescribing physicians is difficult to estimate), 3 of which female, all with 3 years of experience. The sample can be considered representative of the department. In the analyzing phase, there was a quantitative analysis of the answers. The consensus among participants was assessed using Kendall W [36]. Face validity (i.e., the ability to be understandable and relevant for the targeted population [37]) and content validity (i.e., ensuring that the indicators measure what they are intended to measure [38]) of the indicators were assured by incorporating experts’ feedback obtained through the Delphi method: (1) in the first round, for each indicator, the questionnaire had a field where respondents could signal they could not understand it (Supplement 3); in case the field was selected (none did), the respondent would be contacted by email to give further details on the issue; (2) the rounds were used select the most (exclude the least) appropriate indicators to measure value.

MCDA methods

In a MCDA-based methodological process, the choices with the highest impact on the overall decision outcomes relate to model building (including methods selection) and selection of the assessment criteria [39]. The selection of the benefit indicators (criteria) was decided using Delphi. MCDA methods were used to convert the participants’ subjective assessments of the relative importance of the benefit indicators (within each category), and of the different benefit categories to weights, to aggregate the individual indicators, and ultimately to determine the value of the department. Several possible MCDA methods could have been used; the specific ones to be used were chosen considering the specificities of each stakeholder type in terms of availability, knowledge about the department, and ability to understand the method.

To calculate the benefit weights two different approaches were followed: (1) Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) [40], for internal participants; and (2) Rank-Order Centroid (ROC) [41], for external participants. AHP is more demanding given its complexity, and time needed to fulfil information. AHP has been widely applied (and validated) in many research fields because its underlying tree structure eases the understanding of the decision-making problem [42].

The internal participants performed pairwise comparisons across criteria using Saaty’s 9-point scale [40], which can be quite time-consuming (for n criteria, n(n-1)/2 comparisons are required). Given the multiple comparisons, inconsistencies were checked. ROC is an approximate weighting method that, from a simple rank of criteria (obtained through Delphi), uses the weights table of [41] to determine weights.

To aggregate the individual weights, again, two approaches were used: (1) a weighted arithmetic average, assuming different levels of expertise, for internal participants; and (2) a simple arithmetic average, for external participants, assuming that all participants had the same level of expertise. For internal stakeholders, the Delphi results showed a non-negotiable group decision-making situation [43] (i.e., there was no consensus, and participants were unwilling or unable to revise their judgments), forcing the use of an additional technique: the majority rule of Scala [44] based on Principal Component Analysis (PCA) [45]. This statistical procedure divides the group of experts into groups and determines weights for each group. Such weights, assumed as different levels of expertise, were used to determine the final weight vector. Alternative methods can be found in [46]. Tucker’s congruence coefficient is used as a measure of compatibility between two Decision Makers (DMs), and it is directly based on their judgments rather than their priority vectors, unlike previous compatibility indices such as Garuti or Saaty coefficients [47]. Its calculation is important for determining the level of compatibility between decision-makers and identifying the DMs with greater influence.

The Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) [48] was used to determine the value of the radiology department. Using this method, the indicators were ranked relative to an ideal value (the shortest distance to the ideal solution and the farthest to the negative ideal solution) [49].

For each indicator, the radiology department defined the ideal (and anti-ideal) benchmarks that it aimed at reaching (e.g., for the indicator F8: Number of hours of training about radiation protection, an ideal of 165 h/year and an anti-ideal of 0 h/year were defined). Taking these benchmarks into account, the observed value of the indicator (e.g., for F8, 45 h, in 2019) was standardized to a 0 (worst value possible) – 1 (best value possible) scale.

A final overall benefit value for each stakeholder type was determined by aggregating the weighted standardized values of the indicators into the benefit value of the corresponding categories (in the 0–1 scale) and then aggregating the weighted categories.

Categories and indicators to be selected by the stakeholders

Stage 1 – value (benefit) indicators identification

The VAF starts with the identification of benefit indicators from the literature, followed by their synthetization, and adaptation to the service at stake (in the studied case, the radiology department).

A detailed analysis of the literature showed similar categories and indicators recommended by different authors - e.g., the same category in different studies [3, 50 ], the same indicator in different studies but in different categories [2, 30], or the same indicator and identical categories in different works [30, 51]. Most studies namely [30, 2, 50–53] were conducted by researchers affiliated in the USA, where the topic has been more developed. Only the study conducted by the ESR originated in Europe.

The literature review resulted in the identification of 160 indicators (Supplement 2). After synthetization, 56 were maintained for internal stakeholders and 46 for external stakeholders (Table 2). (The number of indicators to be presented to the external stakeholders was lower because those that were so specific that the external stakeholders would not fully understand them were excluded.)

Stage 2 – value (benefit) indicators selection

The benefit indicators to assess the service were selected through Delphi. In the case study, the following undesired situations occurred in the first round: (1) lack of consensus in all four groups of participants (Supplement 4); (2) with the exception of the 2 administrators, all participants considered all indicators as ‘important’ or ‘very important’; and (3) the lists of indicators was considered too large by the participants. As a consequence, the following adaptations were made: 1) round 2 was started, and, instead of asking the participants to state the importance of the indicators, a forced ranking scale was used for prioritizing them: we asked the participants to rank the indicators and the categories from the most to the least important; and 3) considering the participants’ comments, indicator inclusion/exclusion rules were introduced: radiographers suggested that only the four best-scored indicators in each category were selected (a total of 28 indicators); administrators suggested that only the indicators with an average score of ‘important’ or ‘very important’ were selected (21 indicators); and patients and physicians suggested to rewrite and regroup some indicators (43 indicators were selected) (only the selected benefit indicators were considered for indicator weight calculation in Step 1 of Supplement 5). From the first to the second round, 6 out of the initial 20 participant patients abandoned the study. A third round was needed due to a lack of consensus; nevertheless, almost no changes occurred. Because of that, it was decided to stop the Delphi. Three patients abandoned the study from the second to the third round, having 11 patients completed all the rounds. At this stage, it was considered that each stakeholder type’s indicators and preference hierarchies were determined based on the rankings provided by the participants.

When the Delphi results showed a non-negotiable group decision-making situation (i.e., consensus could not be obtained) as was the case with internal stakeholders in this study, it is possible to conduct complementary analyses to understand which of the “decision-makers” assume a more predominant role, for example, using Tucker’s congruence coefficient (Table 3). Based on the preference responses given by each participant (for 28 indicators, in the case of radiographers, and 21 indicators, in the case of administrators), the congruence coefficient indicates which participants have an opinion about the indicators more similar to that of other participants (coefficient close to 1) or completely divergent (coefficient close to −1). It is possible to observe the dispersion among participants, and it seems that majorities can be formed among 3 groups (PR2, PR3, and PR4); (PR5, PR6); and PR1. After this validation of the non-negotiable situation, the internal participant weights were determined using Scala’s approach [44] (details available in Step 2, Supplement 5). For radiographers, the weights were 0.116 for PR1, 0.245 for PR2, 0.143 for PR3, 0.191 for PR4, 0.101 for PR5 and 0.204 for PR6.

Value assessment dashboard

Stage 4 – value assessment

The overall benefit of the department was determined using the general TOPSIS formulae. Figure 2 shows part of a proposed value assessment dashboard (at the category level) that includes: at the top, a chart that combines category importance with the benefit value per stakeholder type, determined using the indicators and weights decided by participants, (‘department’ bars with all indicators or categories having the same (equal) weight were included for comparison purposes); below, in the right, a chart that shows the overall value (benefit) vs. observed clinical cost, €9,819,284 in 2019) of the radiology department per stakeholder type; and, in the left, the contribution of each category for the value per stakeholder type (determined by multiplying the weights by the benefit value per category for each stakeholder type); the aggregation of the value of all categories per stakeholder type is represented in the chart in the right. Supplement 5 (step 4) presents the calculations of the value assessment per indicator and category (represented by the right-hand side bars in the chart at the top of Fig. 2).

In order to prioritize resource allocation towards improving categories, both the current value and the importance assigned to them should be observed. For example, Contribution to the institution could be improved, but it was considered the less important category for all stakeholders; thus, its improvement would only contribute a little to value.

Looking at the overall value of the department, the valuation considering the combination of indicators suitable for the administrators resulted in the higher value (0.710), which may come as no surprise as they may steer the department to the goals they consider important. Accessibility of the service, a category important for external stakeholders, had, if their point of view was considered, a margin to be improved (to know where the resources should be directed at, a similar analysis at the indicator level should be made – see Supplement 5). By looking at the dashboard (Fig. 2), it can be seen that the department could significantly improve its value (either by increasing benefit or reducing cost).

The main obstacles to the adoption of the framework expressed by the participants were the high number of indicators, time unavailability, and some inability to distinguish between some benefit indicators. The lack of historical data to calculate the indicators was the most important obstacle to framework implementation. On the positive side, the administrators considered that the interpretation of the value as displayed in the proposed dashboard (Fig. 2) was easy, and the (lack of) understandability of multicriteria methods was not observed as an obstacle to framework implementation.

Discussion

This study developed a VAF in a radiology department taking a multi-stakeholder perspective, thus addressing the European Society of Radiology (ESR) [53] request that meaningful key performance indicators that demonstrate the breadth of radiology contributions to health care more accurately are defined, thus contributing to shorten the gap that existing VBHC models do not acknowledge the central role of radiology to health care [1].

The number of indicators (and, in the case of administrators, categories) that internal participants considered relevant for value assessment was much lower than that of external participants. Probably, they anticipated that, if the framework is fully implemented in the service, their collaboration will be needed to collect and analyze information and want to be sure that the value assessment process will be simple.

Contradictions in the weights assigned to categories and benefit indicators by different participant groups clearly showed that different stakeholders assign different importance to different value components. Value was higher when an indicator combination oriented towards the goals of internal stakeholders was used; on the other hand, it was lower when the calculations were made from the perspective of external stakeholders. It must be noted that, currently, the decisions and actions of the internal stakeholders have a higher influence on how the service is shaped than those of the external stakeholders. If, from now on, information about the indicators is gathered and computed, it will be possible to see the evolution of value from the perspective of the various stakeholders and there will be more visibility on what external stakeholders value.

The lack of data about the services provided by the department was a major limitation. The data needed for the indicators were not always available or, when available, were aggregated. Consequently, some indicators had to be estimated qualitatively. Thus, the benefit values obtained must be interpreted with caution and primarily serve illustrative purposes. In the future, the department needs to establish systematic processes of data collection supported by information and communication technology to feed the framework. Despite this limitation, obtaining an explicit evaluation of value and addressing the first gap identified in the introduction section was possible.

We did not observe (lack of) understandability of MCDA methods as an obstacle to framework implementation, which somehow gives confidence for future applications. The framework can easily be adapted to other radiology services as the screening of the literature may be used only to update the review and the methods to be used may change in accordance with the circumstances (e.g., stakeholders involved, size of the sample, ease of use or understandability) or to other health care services. The ability to use different indicators and methods in the aggregation and ranking of indicators broadens the framework’s potential use in different contexts and addresses the second gap highlighted in the introduction section.

As far as we are aware, this was the first study to determine the value of a hospital department. Nevertheless, it is possible to compare the features of the proposed VAF with those of other frameworks. The literature about frameworks to determine the value of drugs has discussed topics like usability, different stakeholder perceptions about definitions of value, validity, patient-centered care, impact on stakeholders’ actions, and uncertainty and gaps in data. In terms of usability, there have been hesitations about applying value frameworks in practice because stakeholders do not understand how to apply them and how to extract value from the frameworks’ inputs [23]. The framework proposed in this study has the advantage of being flexible and adapting to the characteristics of involved stakeholders because assessment methods are selected taking into account their ability to understand and use them.

Stakeholders’ different definitions of value have limited the application of VAF when in practice due to silo-specific approaches [22]. The proposed framework selects the indicators to be used from a large portfolio of indicators (from the literature), common to all stakeholders, in order to obtain a unifying notion of value that eases communication among stakeholders.

Concerning validity issues, value frameworks should: (1) contain relevant and reasonable criteria for prioritizing the different types of benefit; (2) be transparent about the evidence/benefits to be (or not) used and how they will be analyzed and evaluated, and about issues related with conflicts of interest, and (3) have wide applicability, and facilitate reproducibility [54]. The proposed framework complies with all these requirements.

In what concerns patient-centered care, none of the frameworks analyzed in the review by Slomiany, Madhavan [23] considered the perspective of individual patients; and, in their review, Zhang, Bao [24] concluded that patients or the public were rarely or only superficially engaged in the development process of VAF. This study involved patients in the selection of the benefit indicators to be considered to assess the value of the radiology department and their weight. By engaging different perspectives (i.e., those of patients, physicians, radiographers, and administrators) in operationalizing value, we contribute to narrowing the third gap noted in the introduction section.

Interpretation of value was one of the biggest concerns in this study and the reason why we expressed the results in a scale that is easy to understand and eases comparisons. As long as the meaning of the output(s) of the framework is understood by the various stakeholders, decision-makers have an opportunity to shape their communication or clinical strategies [23]. Nevertheless, the use of data on a single year may limit the interpretability of the matrix that compares benefit to cost in the dashboard. With the addition of more data, it will be possible to analyze the evolution of both the benefit and cost.

Uncertainty and gaps in data have frequently been referred to as major limitations in the literature about value frameworks [e.g., 55]. This study faced similar difficulties and the implementation that was attempted highlighted the need to plan information flows and stocks to feed the indicators.

Conclusions

The main objective of this study was to develop a framework to determine and interpret the value of a hospital department (the radiology service) considering the perspectives of multiple stakeholders. Three major research gaps related to value-based initiatives have been identified: (1) not explicitly determining value, (2) being complex (which limits reproducibility) and not admitting customization; and (3) not addressing the various and sometimes conflicting concerns of involved stakeholders. The proposed customized framework contributes to tightening these gaps.

A set of alternative methods (selected to fit the knowledge of the participants, data availability, and the level of accuracy needed) were used to estimate indicators, aggregate individual perspectives, and determine the overall value. Using the concept of distance to determine the gap between ideal and current benefit helps prioritize alternative management improvement initiatives.

The application of the framework to a real case uncovered the lack of quality of the available data. The development of methods to measure value is also a motivation to enhance meaningful data collection.

Future research developments of this study should follow the following two directions: (1) reinforcing the empirical validation of the indicators to be incorporated in the framework (enhancing the reproducibility of the framework); and (2) empirically studying the incorporation of the framework in the information system of institutions (e.g., addressing usability issues).
