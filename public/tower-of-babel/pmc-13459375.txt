Lower respiratory microbiota as modulators of tumor immunity in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yingchun Chen, Minliang Kuang, Qingqi Li, Zhongan Chen, Hongyan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung adenocarcinoma is common type of cancer with high morbidity and mortality. Emerging evidence suggests that microbial colonization in the lower respiratory airways may modulate immune activation and response to immunotherapy. However, the immunologic relevance of microbiome diversity in lung cancer remains unclear.MethodsWe analyzed bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), serum cytokines, immune cell subsets, and checkpoint molecule expression from 350 patients with lung adenocarcinoma. Microbiome diversity was quantified by phylum- and genus-level richness derived from binary detection of specific bacterial groups. Associations between microbial diversity, immune parameters, and clinical prognosis were evaluated using correlation and comparative statistical analyses.ResultsAmong 350 samples, there was no major compositional difference between patients who died and those who recovered. Microbial diversity showed no significant associations with systemic cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, and IFN-γ. Genus richness showed positive correlation with PD-1 expression, while phylum richness correlated inversely with PD-L1. Specific taxa demonstrated distinct immune associations: TM7 correlated with increased regulatory T cells, Actinobacteria and Veillonella correlated with lower IL-1β, while Neisseria correlated with CTLA-4 expression.ConclusionLower airway microbiota in lung adenocarcinoma display moderate diversity and meaningful immunologic associations, particularly with PD-1/PD-L1 pathways, Tregs, and IL-1β regulation. Background

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with consideration of lung adenocarcinoma as the most common histologic subtype [1, 2]. Lung cancer is one of top causes of cancer-related mortality in China with an estimated 478,000 deaths according to statistics in 2024 [3]. Lung microbiome has emerged as potential marker for prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma. It has been believed that lower respiratory tract is sterile, but it is now known to harbor diverse microbial communities even in healthy individuals [4]. Recently, there is growing evidence indicating that patients exhibit an altered airway microbiota compared to healthy controls [5]. Some commensal bacteria such as Streptococcus and Staphylococcus are often found in higher abundance in the lungs of in patients with lung cancer compared to healthy population. Moreover, the overall microbial diversity in tumor-afflicted lungs tends to be decreased [6–8]. Although the clinical implications of these shifts are still being unraveled, lower diversity and enrichment of specific taxa in lung cancer patients suggest a possible link between dysbiotic microbial communities and carcinogenesis or tumor progression [8, 9].

The tumor immune microenvironment plays an important role in adenocarcinoma progression since the balance and regulation of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, the presence of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs), and levels of cytokines shape the anti-tumor immune response in the host [10]. An effective anti-tumor immunity generally requires active cytotoxic T cells and pro-inflammatory signals, whereas an immunosuppressive milieu characterized by abundant Tregs and inhibitory cytokines can facilitate tumor immune evasion. High infiltration of FoxP3 + Tregs are associated with worse patient outcomes, and Tregs are known to suppress anti-tumor immunity and even reduce the efficacy of immunotherapies in lung adenocarcinoma [11, 12]. Similarly, chronic inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17) in the tumor microenvironment can paradoxically promote tumor growth and survival by creating protumorigenic inflammation or exhaustion of effector T cells. The factors that drive this immune dysregulation are multifactorial, and emerging data suggest the resident microbiota could be one such factor [13]. Recent studies investigated how the lung microbiome might modulate local immune function in lung cancer, which demonstrated that commensal bacteria in the lung can accelerate growth of adenocarcinoma [6, 14].

Despite these insights, there is remaining gap of knowledge regarding the association between the lung microbiome and immune landscape in lung adenocarcinoma. Most studies have been limited in scope, often focusing on either microbiota profiling or immune landscape characterization alone [15]. Therefore, this study aims to characterize the lower respiratory tract-specific microbiota in lung adenocarcinoma patients and to evaluate its association with key immune function markers and disease progression.

Methods

Study design and participants

We obtained the data of microbiome, biochemical and clinical data of adenocarcinoma patients. The study included a total of 350 patients with lung adenocarcinoma who received immunotherapy and presented to The Second Affiliated Hospital of Yichun University. The eligible patients had pathological confirmation through bronchoscopy-mediated needle biopsy tissue samples. These patients were then divided into two groups according to clinical prognosis either to death group or survival or improvement group. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and complies with data protection regulations. This was approved by ethical committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Yichun University (Approval no. 2025.019), which waived patient consent due to the retrospective nature of the study. Patient data and confidentiality were maintained.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria:

histologically confirmed lung adenocarcinoma;

availability of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid samples obtained during diagnostic bronchoscopy;

availability of baseline immune profiling data including cytokine levels and T-cell subsets; and.

availability of follow-up clinical outcome data.

availability of follow-up clinical outcome data.

Exclusion criteria included:

evidence of active pulmonary infection at the time of sampling;

incomplete clinical or microbiome data.

Data collection

Patient data including age, sex, height, body mass index (BMI), smoking status and type of immunotherapy in addition to lung function measured with forced vital capacity (FVC) at enrollment were recorded. Recorded comorbidities included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and cardiovascular disease. Patients had regular follow-up visits with recording of clinical prognosis of patients and patients lost to follow-up were excluded from survival outcome analysis to avoid misclassification bias. Peripheral blood samples were collected at the time of diagnostic bronchoscopy prior to initiation of immunotherapy, ensuring that immune measurements reflected baseline immune status. Clinical treatment data including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy exposure were extracted from medical records. Patients were followed for a median duration of 24 months (interquartile range 18–30 months) to determine clinical outcomes.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and biochemical analysis

Cytokine levels measured in BALF and serum included interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ). These cytokines were quantified using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was obtained using electronic bronchoscopy, with preheated sterile saline. BALF was collected in sterile containers, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 40 min at 4 °C, and the separated fractions were stored at − 80 °C. Regarding biochemical analysis, peripheral blood samples were drawn from each patient on the morning, then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Approximately 200 µL of serum was aliquoted into labeled tubes and stored at − 80 °C for systemic inflammatory markers analysis. Cytokine levels in BALF and serum were m easured using immunoassays with identification of various inflammatory cytokines.

Diversity of microbiota quantification

The richness and diversity scores used to characterize the patient microbiota and computed based on the binary detection (Yes = 1, No = 0) of specific microbial groups. Phylum Richness was calculated by summing the binary presence (1) or absence (0) of following eight microbial phyla (Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria, Actinobacteria, Tenericutes, phylum TM7, and other Phylum).

Genus Richness was derived as a numerical score by summing the binary presence (1) or absence (0) of following nine genera (Neisseria, Veillonella, Streptococcus, Haemophilus, Fusobacterium, Prevotella, Alloprevotella, Megasphaera, Terrabacter and other Genus). Total Diversity calculated by the sum of Phylum Richness and Genus Richness. This simplified richness-based scoring system was adapted from commonly used microbiome diversity metrics that quantify microbial richness based on the presence of detected taxa. Similar approaches have been widely applied in respiratory microbiome studies to estimate microbial diversity and community complexity [16] T-cell subsets were identified using flow cytometry. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies targeting CD3, CD4, CD8, and regulatory T-cell markers (CD4⁺CD25⁺FoxP3⁺). Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a BD FACSCanto II cytometer, and results were expressed as percentages of total CD3⁺ T lymphocytes.

Microbiome sequencing and analysis

Microbial DNA was extracted from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples using a standardized microbial DNA isolation kit. The bacterial community was characterized using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing targeting the V3–V4 hypervariable regions. Sequencing libraries were prepared according to Illumina protocols and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform. Raw reads were processed using the QIIME2 bioinformatics pipeline, including quality filtering, sequence denoising, operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering at 97% similarity, and taxonomic assignment against the SILVA reference database. Microbial presence was defined based on detection thresholds and used to calculate richness indices. Cytokine concentrations (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10 and IFN-γ) were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) according to manufacturer protocols.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges according to their distribution, while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Associations between microbiota diversity indices, specific bacterial taxa, and immune parameters were evaluated using Spearman or Pearson correlation analyses depending on variable distribution. To account for potential confounding factors, multivariable linear regression analyses were performed adjusting for age, sex, smoking status, treatment modality, and disease stage. These variables were selected based on their known clinical relevance in lung adenocarcinoma outcomes and immune regulation. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Variables with normal distribution were summarized using mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Results

Baseline data and clinical characteristics

A total of 350 patients with lung adenocarcinoma were included in the analysis, of whom 253 (72.3%) died during follow-up and 97 (27.7%) achieved survival group The median age of the cohort was 60.0 years (IQR 51.0–69.0) and was identical across prognosis groups (p = 0.466). Men comprised 35.1% of the sample, with no significant difference in sex distribution between patients who died and those who recovered (34.0% vs. 38.1%, p = 0.466). Smoking history was common (62.6% overall) and similarly distributed between the death and survival groups (63.6% vs. 59.8%, p = 0.506). Median BMI was 24.1 kg/m² (IQR 21.5–26.5) in the whole cohort, with no difference by prognosis (p = 0.921). Comorbidities were present in 43.1% of patients, again without significant variation between groups (43.1% vs. 43.3%, p = 0.971) (Table 1). Physiological measures, including FVC% (mean 74.60 ± 9.30) and anthropometric parameters (weight and height), did not differ significantly between the death and survival groups (all p > 0.3).

Tumor stage was determined according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system (8th edition). For analysis and presentation purposes, TNM stages were grouped into overall disease stages (Stage I–IV). Among recovered patients, early stages predominated (Stage I: 45.4%; Stage II: 53.6%), whereas advanced stages were almost exclusively observed in the death group (Stage III: 50.6% vs. 1.0%; Stage IV: 30.8% vs. 0.0%; p < 0.001). Radiation therapy was also more frequent among patients who died (74.3%) than among those who recovered (12.4%; p < 0.001), likely reflecting underlying disease severity and stage (Table 1).

Immune function by disease prognosis

Systemic cytokine profiles were broadly similar between prognosis groups. Median IL-6, IL-1β, IL-10 and IFN-γ levels did not differ significantly between patients who died and those who recovered (all p > 0.05). In contrast, TNF-α was modestly but significantly higher in the death group compared with the survival group (p = 0.024), suggesting a potential link between heightened pro-inflammatory signaling and poorer outcome (Table 2).

Associations between microbiota and baseline clinical characteristics

Microbial diversity showed limited association with demographic and physiological characteristics (Table 6). Neither age nor sex correlated significantly with phylum richness, genus richness, or total diversity (all p > 0.2). Measures of pulmonary function (FEV₁% and FVC%) also did not demonstrate significant correlations with any of the diversity indices, although FVC% showed a non-significant trend toward positive correlation with phylum richness and total diversity (both p = 0.077). Disease stage was the only baseline characteristic that correlated significantly with microbiome metrics: genus richness increased with more advanced stage of adenocarcinoma (Spearman r = 0.119, p = 0.026), whereas phylum richness and total diversity were not significantly associated with stage (Table 5).

Associations between global microbiome diversity and immune function

Global diversity indices were not strongly linked to systemic immune markers (Table 6). None of the measured cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, IFN-γ) showed statistically significant correlations with phylum richness, genus richness, or total diversity (all p > 0.1). Likewise, CD4 + and CD8 + T-cell percentages, Treg proportion, and the CD4+/CD8 + ratio were not significantly associated with any diversity index (all p > 0.5). Figure 3.

Cytokine boxplots (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) for survival vs. death

Among immune checkpoint molecules, genus richness demonstrated a modest positive correlation with PD-1 expression (r = 0.142, p = 0.008). Phylum richness was inversely correlated with PD-L1 expression (r = − 0.132, p = 0.013), whereas total diversity was not significantly associated with any checkpoint marker (all p > 0.18). CTLA-4 expression did not correlate significantly with phylum or genus richness or total diversity (Table 6).

Associations between specific taxa and immune parameters

Exploratory analyses of specific phyla and genera highlighted several significant microbiome–immune relationships. IL-1β levels were inversely correlated with Actinobacteria (r = − 0.138, p = 0.010), Veillonella (r = − 0.147, p = 0.006), and Terrabacter (r = − 0.138, p = 0.010), suggesting that higher relative prevalence of these taxa may be associated with reduced IL-1β–mediated inflammatory signaling.

Treg cells percentages showed a positive correlation with TM7 (r = 0.120, p = 0.024), indicating that this phylum may be linked to a more regulatory or immunosuppressive T-cell profile in the lower respiratory tract. Figure 4.

Correlation scatter plots (PD-1 vs. Genus richness, PD-L1 vs. Phylum richness)

Several specific taxa were associated with immune checkpoint expression. PD-1 expression correlated positively with Firmicutes (r = 0.108, p = 0.043) and Terrabacter (r = 0.134, p = 0.012), and inversely with TM7 (r = − 0.112, p = 0.036). PD-L1 expression showed significant negative correlations with Tenericutes (r = − 0.162, p = 0.002) and TM7 (r = − 0.162, p = 0.002). Additionally, CTLA-4 expression correlated positively with Neisseria (r = 0.134, p = 0.012). Other tested associations between specific taxa and immune parameters did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

This study investigated the burden of lower respiratory tract microbiota in patients with lung adenocarcinoma, which revealed moderate microbial diversity in study cohorts. Notably, broad microbiome measures did not differ markedly by clinical outcome since adenocarcinoma patients who eventually died showed no significant differences in the prevalence of baseline dominant phyla or genera compared to survival groups. Firmicutes was the most common phylum across patients with estimated prevalence of 90%, followed by Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria, Actinobacteria, Tenericutes, and TM7, with no significant richness of phylum in either prognosis group. Similarly, frequent genera such as, Streptococcus, Prevotella, Veillonella, Haemophilus, Neisseria were detected at comparable rates in both groups. Consequently, that baseline lung microbiota composition alone was not a decisive factor for short-term prognosis in this cohort.

While demographic data of patients such as, age and sex, in addition to lung function status did not show significant correlations with microbial diversity indices, more advanced cancer stages were associated with a slight increase in genus-level richness. Phylum-level richness and overall diversity index did not significantly vary by clinical stage. These insights indicated that advanced cancer might harbor a broader spectrum of bacteria in the lower airways. However, advanced stages (Stage III–IV) were strongly correlated with worse prognosis, yet the microbiome differences by stage did not translate into significant differences by outcome. Although statistically significant correlations were identified, the correlation coefficients were relatively small (r < 0.2), suggesting weak associations that should be interpreted cautiously.

Our findings are consistent with emerging literature suggesting that the respiratory microbiome can influence immune signaling pathways relevant to tumor biology. Previous studies have demonstrated that specific bacterial taxa in the lung microenvironment may modulate immune responses through cytokine regulation and immune checkpoint pathways. For example, Jin et al. reported that commensal lung microbiota can promote lung tumor progression by activating γδ T cells and stimulating inflammatory signaling within the tumor microenvironment [6]. Similarly, Liang et al. demonstrated that microbial communities in lung adenocarcinoma are associated with immune cell infiltration and immune regulatory networks [14]. In addition, Jang et al. reported associations between airway microbiome composition and PD-L1 expression in lung cancer patients receiving immunotherapy [17]. Our findings extend these observations by showing that microbial richness and specific bacterial taxa correlate with immune checkpoint expression and regulatory T-cell proportions, suggesting that airway microbiota may contribute to shaping systemic immune responses in lung adenocarcinoma.

Regarding the correlation between lung microbiome and systemic immune response, global diversity indices showed limited associations with immune parameters. There were no significant correlations between microbiome diversity and circulating cytokine levels including, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, IFN-γ. For instance, patients with either high or low bacterial diversity showed no consistent differences in pro-inflammatory cytokines (all p > 0.1). Similarly, CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, Tregs, and the CD4/CD8 ratio were not significantly related to any diversity metric. These findings suggest that the overall richness or evenness of the lung microbiome may not strongly reflect systemic immune status.

Interestingly, Genus-level richness showed a positive correlation with PD-1 expression, indicating that patients with greater bacterial genera tended to have higher PD-1 levels. Conversely, phylum-level richness was inversely correlated with PD-L1 expression (r = − 0.132, p = 0.013). Total diversity was not significantly associated with checkpoint molecules, and CTLA-4 expression showed no correlation with phylum or genus richness which aligns with Jang et al. [17]. The PD-1 and PD-L1 correlations indicate that greater diversity of airway microbiome might be associated with an immune landscape in which T cells are more activated with subsequent higher PD-1 and attenuated expression of PD-L1. Meanwhile, the inverse link between diversity and PD-L1 might indicate that with balanced microbiome, there is less inflammatory signaling (IFN-γ) to induce PD-L1, or that tumors in those hosts are less compelled to express PD-L1. These insights contrast with Hilmi et al., who reported significant association between intra-tumoral bacteria and tumor immunity in patients with breast, lung, and colorectal cancers [18]. They reported correlation between microbiome richness with infiltrating lymphocytes and elevated PD-L1 expression, in addition to worse prognosis and progression-free in patients with low microbial richness. This inconsistency may be attributed to different samples since our study focused on respiratory airway samples in specific lung adenocarcinoma.

Notably, we observed inverse relationship between certain bacteria and IL-1β as relative abundance of Actinobacteria (phylum), Veillonella, and Terrabacter (genus) correlated with lower systemic IL-1β levels, which suggests that these microbes exert anti-inflammatory effects with lower IL-1β-mediated inflammation. For instance, Actinobacteria in the lung might activate immune pathways that counter-regulate IL-1 production. Veillonella is an anaerobic genus often found in oral and airway microbiota; its higher presence being linked to reduced IL-1β is notable, given that IL-1β can promote tumor-associated inflammation. One possibility is that Veillonella and similar bacteria engage pattern-recognition receptors that skew cytokine responses away from IL-1β. Clinically, lower IL-1β might indicate less chronic inflammation but also might indicate a less activated innate immune surveillance. However, this association highlights a potential microbial influence on inflammatory tone in lung cancer patients, which fill gap of knowledge in this area, since Bailey et al., and Bou Zerdan et al., who reported inconclusive correlations between specific taxa and cytokine levels in lung cancer patients [19, 20].

Obviously, greater TM7 abundance, group of ultrasmall bacteria often detected in oral and respiratory samples, was positively correlated with a higher proportion of Foxp3⁺ regulatory T cells. These findings suggest that certain commensal bacteria may drive an immunosuppressive phenotype. Tregs which are key inhibitors of anti-tumor immunity, an increased Treg fraction associated with TM7 could imply that this phylum’s presence helps foster tolerance. This finding aligns conceptually with prior observations in mouse models where commensal lung microbiota can induce Tregs that promote metastatic tumor growth through immune suppression [21]. In addition to cytokines and Tregs, several taxa were linked to immune checkpoint molecule expression on lymphocytes. PD-1 expression was positively correlated with the abundance of Firmicutes (a dominant bacterial phylum) and with the genus Terrabacter, but inversely correlated with TM7. Meanwhile, PD-L1 expression had significant negative correlations with Tenericutes (a phylum that includes Mycoplasma species) and again TM7. Neisseria, which represents oropharyngeal flora showed a positive correlation with CTLA-4 expression. These finding is consistent with a recent study by Papavassiliou et al., who reported that patients with lung cancer with good response to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors tend to have specific bacterial colonization, supporting the potential role of microbiome in improve immunotherapy response [22].

Clinical and biological implications

This study highlights the potential implications of airway microbiota in context of lung adenocarcinoma and association with immunotherapy. The association between microbial richness and PD-1 expression suggests that greater diversity of airway microbiota may drive chronic immune stimulation, increasing the role of tumor immunity like PD-1 with subsequent better selection of immunotherapy. Although such cells are often exhausted, they may also be more responsive to PD-1 blockade, offering a possible microbiome-related explanation for variations in prognosis for patients receiving immunotherapy. In contrast, the inverse relationship between microbial phylum richness and PD-L1 expression raises the possibility that patients with broader microbial representation with lower PD-L1 levels, reflecting a less inflamed tumor microenvironment but also reduced tumor-mediated adaptive immune resistance. These patterns hint that distinct microbial communities may shape different axes of immune checkpoint biology.

Specific taxa also appear to influence immune cell responses. The correlation of TM7 abundance with regulatory Tregs points to a potential microbial driver of immunosuppression with subsequent resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. Additionally, the association between Neisseria and CTLA-4 expression suggests that certain microbes may influence CTLA-4 dependent immune regulation and help to identify certain populations who can benefit from CTLA-4 inhibition. Further clinical studies are required to confirm these associations and enhance immunotherapy role in lung cancer patients.

Strengths and limitations

Despite multiple microbiota–immune correlations, the study did not find strong evidence regarding the association between microbiome diversity and clinical outcomes in adenocarcinoma patient. Moreover, initial immune status in peripheral blood and lower airway microbiota do not obviously capture the critical differences that determine clinical prognosis for those patients. However, airway microbiota showed potential clinical role when leveraging immunotherapy or other immune-modulating treatments, highlighting potential role for further clinical and biological implications.

Although this study has relatively large sample size investigating the association between airway microbiota, tumor immunity and clinical prognosis, this study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. A limitation of this study is that immune parameters were measured in peripheral blood rather than within the tumor microenvironment or BALF immune cell populations. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and local cytokine profiles may provide more accurate insights into tumor-specific immune responses. Future studies integrating tumor tissue immune profiling or BAL immune cell characterization may better elucidate the interaction between lung microbiota and tumor immunity. Potential confounding by unmeasured factors such as prior or concomitant antibiotic use, detailed dietary patterns, environmental exposures, or specific oncologic treatments may also have influenced both microbial profiles and immune status. Bronchoscopic sampling of the lower respiratory tract carries inherent risks of oropharyngeal contamination. A healthy control group was not included in the present study. Therefore, comparisons between lung adenocarcinoma patients and healthy individuals could not be performed. Future studies including matched healthy controls will be necessary to determine disease-specific microbial alterations. Finally, an external validation in independent multicenter cohorts is warranted to confirm the generalizability of these results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, airway microbiome diversity did not obviously predict outcomes in patients with lung adenocarcinoma. However, specific microbial taxa are closely associated with immune pathways, including PD-1/PD-L1 signaling, Treg expansion, and IL-1β regulation. These findings highlight the modulatory role of microbiome in lung adenocarcinoma and outline the potential role of targeted microbial modulation in enhancing future immunotherapy strategies.
