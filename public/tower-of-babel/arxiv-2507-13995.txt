On the non-uniqueness of locally minimizing clusters via singular cones
Lia Bronsard, Robin Neumayer, Michael Novack, Anna Skorobogatova

Abstract
. We construct partitions of ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} into three sets { 𝒳 ⁡ ( 1 ) , 𝒳 ⁡ ( 2 ) , 𝒳 ⁡ ( 3 ) } \{\mathcal{X}(1),\mathcal{X}(2),\mathcal{X}(3)\} that locally minimize interfacial area among compactly supported volume preserving variations and that blow down at infinity to singular area-minimizing cones. As a consequence, we prove the non-uniqueness of the standard lens cluster in a large number of dimensions starting from 8 8 .

1.2.
Main Results

The basic scheme to construct the local minimizing
(1,2)
-clusters of Theorem
1.1
goes as follows. Fix
n\geq 8
and let
K
be a perimeter minimizing cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose boundary is not a plane, e.g., the region
K=\{x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{4}^{2}<x_{5}^{2}+\cdots+x_{8}^{2}\}
bounded by the Simons cone in
\mathbb{R}^{8}.

For
R>0
large, we set up an energy minimization problem in the class of
(1,2)
-clusters
\mathcal{X}
with
\mathcal{X}(1)\subset B_{3R}
of volume
1
for which, outside of
B_{3R}
, the chambers
\mathcal{X}(2)
and
\mathcal{X}(3)
coincide with
K
and
K^{c}
respectively. Morally one wants to minimize the cluster perimeter
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X};B_{4R})
, though by instead minimizing the energy
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X};B_{4R})+\mathcal{G}_{R}(\mathcal{X}(1))
for a carefully constructed confinement potential
\mathcal{G}_{R}
, we circumvent technical challenges that would arise from the possibility of
\mathcal{X}(1)
saturating the constraint
\mathcal{X}(1)\subset B_{3R}
.

For a sequence
R_{k}\to\infty
, take a sequence of minimizers
\mathcal{X}_{k}
to this minimization problem with
R=R_{k}
. We wish to obtain a local minimizer of
\mathcal{P}
as a limit of the
\mathcal{X}_{k}
, and to this end we use a “partial concentration compactness approach,” where the volume of
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
is the quantity we wish to preserve in the limit. Recall how classical concentration compactness methods are often used to prove existence in variational problems with critical scaling or on non-compact domains:

Step 1:
Characterize precisely how a sequence can lose compactness and the cost in energy to exhibit this behavior.

Step 2:
Establish compactness for a (minimizing) sequence by showing its energy lies below this loss-of-compactness threshold.

For instance, to produce constant scalar curvature metrics in a given conformal class in the Yamabe problem, one shows that a noncompact minimizing sequence for the Yamabe functional must concentrate at a point and pay precisely the Yamabe constant of the sphere in energy
[
24
]
. Similarly, in the theory of stationary harmonic maps, loss of strong
H^{1}
-compactness for equibounded sequences is characterized by the formation of “bubbles”, and the energy loss is realized exactly by the sum of energies of such bubbles; see
[
27
,
33
]
.

In the present setting, Step 1 as stated seems out of reach, as there are in principle myriad possible asymptotic behaviors, and importantly, possible energy costs, of
\{\mathcal{X}_{k}\}
if the sequence loses compactness due to volume loss at infinity of
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
. The main reason for this is that in contrast to, e.g., the classical cluster problem, there is an asymptotically infinite contribution to the energy coming from the minimal surface
\partial\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)\cap\partial\mathcal{X}_{k}(3)
, which may twist around slowly as it transitions from the boundary data
\partial K
at scale
R_{k}
and connects to the bounded components of
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
. In doing so, it may transition to resembling some other singular minimal cone with lower area density than
K
at scales
\ll R_{k}
, and so there is no clear way to propagate information about this minimal surface down to the scale of
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
; see below Theorem
1.2
for further discussion of this point. The possibility of this type of behavior is the main difficulty in the problem, and would need to be ruled out in order to characterize the minimizers obtained in Theorem
1.1
.

One possible way for a piece of
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
to escape to infinity is along approximately planar portions of the minimal surface
\partial\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)\cap\partial\mathcal{X}_{k}(3)
. Using the rigidity result in
[
10
]
, we deduce that in this case,
\mathcal{X}_{k}
locally looks like a rescaling of the standard lens cluster. In particular, its local energy contribution in a large ball, after rescaling by the escaping volume of
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
, is approximately equal to sum of the area of an equatorial disk in this ball and the
renormalized lens energy

(1.3)

\Lambda_{\plane}(n):=P(\mathcal{X}_{\lens}(1))-\omega_{n-1}\rho_{n}^{n-1}\,.

Here
\rho_{n}
denotes the radius of the disc
\mathcal{X}_{\lens}(1)\cap\{x_{n}=0\}
.

As described above, this is not the only way that
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
can lose mass at infinity. It may be the case that a piece of
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
drifts off to infinity along a non-planar portion of the minimal surface
\partial\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)\cap\partial\mathcal{X}_{k}(3)
. The key observation is that the latter loss of compactness, after a translation, will
still produce a local minimizer as in Theorem
1.1

with a singular blowdown cone
. Thus we do not need to show that the sequence
\{\mathcal{X}_{k}({1})\}
is compact. We only need to rule out the possibility that all of the mass of
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
is lost via asymptotic lens behavior—the one asymptotic behavior whose limiting energy we can characterize.

A basic competitor argument shows that
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{R})
is bounded above by the sum of
P(K;B_{R})
and the cone constant

(1.4)

\begin{split}\Lambda(\partial K):=\inf\{P(\mathcal{X}(1))-\mathcal{H}^{n-1}({\partial K}\cap\mathcal{X}(1)^{(1)}):&\text{ clusters $\mathcal{X}$ with }|\mathcal{X}(1)|=1,\\
&\mathcal{X}(2)=K\setminus\mathcal{X}(1),\mathcal{X}(3)=K^{c}\setminus\mathcal{X}(1)\}\,.\end{split}

In this notation,
\Lambda_{\rm plane}(n)=\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\{0\})
and we use the notation
\Lambda_{\rm plane}(n)
because it is more concise. Note that
\Lambda(\partial K)\leq\Lambda_{\rm plane}(n)
by testing (
1.4
) with a sequence of competitors exhibiting asymptotic lens behavior. When this inequality is strict, we rule out the possibility of all mass escaping in the form of a lens and thus prove that a piece of the limit of the
\mathcal{X}_{k}
yields a locally minimizing
(1,2)
-cluster that is not a standard lens cluster:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n\geq 8
and
K
be a singular perimeter minimizing cone. If

(1.5)

\displaystyle\Lambda({\partial K})<\Lambda_{\plane}(n)

then there is a locally minimizing
(1,2)
-cluster
\mathcal{X}
of
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for every blowdown
K_{\infty}
of
\mathcal{X}(2)
,
\partial K_{\infty}
is a singular area-minimizing cone.

Moreover, for any such blowdown
K_{\infty}
, we have the density comparison

(1.6)

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial K_{\infty}\cap B_{1}(0))\leq\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial K\cap B_{1}(0))\,.

Since
\mathcal{X}(2)
locally minimizes perimeter outside some compact set, all possible blowdowns have boundaries that are area-minimizing cones; we refer the reader to Section
2
for details. However, Theorem
1.2
does not guarantee that this cone only has an isolated singularity or other properties (e.g. as in
[
43
]
) that would guarantee the uniqueness of blowdowns. Thus, blowdowns are implicitly taken along a subsequence of scales
\rho_{j}\uparrow+\infty
.

The statement of Theorem
1.2
leaves open a natural question: does the blowdown
K_{\infty}
of the limiting minimizing cluster coincide with the cone
K
that was used in the setup of the minimization problems on large balls
B_{R_{k}}
? Absent some symmetrization technique which allows one to characterize directly the minimizers for the problem on
B_{R_{k}}
(which does not seem immediate; cf. Remark
1.6
), the only way to conclude
K=K_{\infty}
would be to “propagate” information about
\partial\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)\cap\partial\mathcal{X}_{k}(3)
from scale
R_{k}
around the origin to an order
1
scale around a portion of
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
, which may not be remaining close to the origin. Such an argument would require two non-trivial pieces of information in addition to (
1.5
): first, that the area ratios of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
at scale
4R_{k}
and at an order one scale comparable to
\diam\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
essentially coincide, and second, that proximity to
\partial K
at scale
4R_{k}
combined with near constancy of area ratios allows for “propagation” of proximity to
\partial K
down to an order one scale. The former piece of information only seems clear if one knew that
\partial K
was the least density singular area minimizing hypercone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(which is open however for all
n\geq 8
), and the latter is equivalent to establishing the uniqueness of tangent cones to minimal surfaces, which is also open outside of specific cases. Characterizing the blowdown, even in specific cases where
K
is, e.g., a Lawson cone, as will be the case in Theorem
1.3
below, seems to be the first step in understanding qualitative properties of the minimizers we construct in this paper. If this can be done, then natural questions arise regarding symmetries and other characteristics of minimizers; see Remark
1.6
below for further elaboration on this point.

To make use of the conditional existence result of Theorem
1.2
, we must next verify that the strict inequality (
1.5
) holds for certain choices of perimeter minimizing cones
K
.
We recall the family of
Lawson cones
4
4

4

Such cones are sometimes also referred to as
quadratic cones
in the literature, since they are quadratic varieties.
for
k,l\in\mathbb{N}

C_{k,l}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{l+1}:|x|^{2}=\frac{k}{l}|y|^{2}\right\}\,.

The cones
C_{k,l}
are area-minimizing cones with an isolated singularity in
\mathbb{R}^{k+l+2}
when
k+l>6
and when
k+l=6
, the cones
C_{3,3}
(Simons’ cone) and
C_{2,4}
are the only two area-minimizing Lawson cones in
\mathbb{R}^{8}
(see
[
21
]
*Section 5.1, Theorem 5.5.2). We further define the quantity

(1.7)

\Lambda_{\Lawson}(n):=\inf\{\Lambda(C_{k,l}):k,l\in\mathbb{N},\ k+l+2=n\}\,.

Our second main result is the validity of the hypothesis
\Lambda_{\Lawson}(n)<\Lambda_{\plane}(n)
in all sufficiently low dimensions
n\geq 8
, thus allowing us to conclude that there exist locally minimizing
(1,2)
-clusters with singular blowdowns in those dimensions.

Theorem 1.3
.

For
n\in\{8,\dots,2700\}
, we have
\Lambda_{\Lawson}(n)<\Lambda_{\plane}(n)
.
As a consequence, in these dimensions, there exist locally minimizing
(1,2)
-clusters that blow down to singular cones.

The first part of Theorem
1.3
is established through an explicit construction of
(1,2)
-clusters to be used as competitors in the variational problems (
1.7
) and (
1.4
)
to bound
\Lambda_{\Lawson}(n)
from above. Then, the second part follows by an application of Theorem
1.2
.
The competitors are modeled on
C=C_{n/2-1,n/2-1}
if
n
is even, and modeled on
C=C_{(n-1)/2-1,(n-1)/2}
if
n
is odd.
We then must compute
\Lambda_{\plane}(n)
and
P(\mathcal{X}(1))-\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(C\cap\mathcal{X}(1))
for these competitors
\mathcal{X}
, and compare the two quantities.

In light of various symmetries, these computations may be reduced to one-dimensional integrals which, although can be expressed in terms of certain special functions, are not easy to estimate by hand to a desirable level of precision in all dimensions. Thus, part of the proof of Theorem
1.3
is computer-assisted: we use the FLINT C library to provide us with a suitably precise calculation of these values in general. FLINT uses interval arithmetic and comes with rigorous error estimates; see
[
17
]
for a survey on interval arithmetic and its role in computer-assisted proofs and numerical approximations with rigorous estimates.

In even dimensions
n\in 2\mathbb{N}
, and in particular for
n=8
, we are able to obtain a reasonably clean closed formula for these integrals, thus allowing one in principle to compute both
P(\mathcal{X}(1))-\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(C\cap\mathcal{X}(1))
for our choice of competitor and
\Lambda_{\plane}(n)
by hand and avoid the need for computer assistance. We present these by-hand computations in detail for
n=8
and obtain precise values for
\Lambda_{\rm plane}(8)
and the estimate for
\Lambda_{\rm Lawson}(8)
, thereby providing a complete by-hand proof of Theorem
1.3
for
n=8
; see Section
7.4
.

In recent years, there have been a number of instances of computer-assisted proofs for variational problems, such as
[
15
,
32
,
13
]
. The computer-assisted aspect of the work
[
15
]
bears particular resemblance to ours. Therein, the authors verify the existence of a singular minimizing conical solution to the one-phase Bernoulli free boundary problem on
\mathbb{R}^{7}
. This is established via a comparison to a suitable family of sub- and super-solutions to the one-phase Bernoulli problem. The construction of such families requires
calculation of certain quantities which, as in the present setting, are difficult to perform by hand due to the presence of various special functions, and are therefore carried out in mathemtical computing software (in their case, Mathematica).

1.3.
Further Results and Discussion

We now discuss some further results shown in the paper and possible future directions.

Remark 1.5

(A capillarity problem)
.

Using arguments similar to those of Theorem
1.2
, we obtain minimizers for a capillarity problem on one side of singular minimizing cones with isolated singularities. This situation is simpler than Theorem
1.2
because the cone is “fixed”, and so we defer precise statements and a description of the proof to Section
8
.

Remark 1.6

(On possible symmetry properties)
.

One might expect that if a blowdown of a given locally minimizing
(1,2)
-cluster is a cone with certain symmetries, for instance
SO(k+1)\times SO(l+1)
invariance in the case of
C_{k,l}
, then the cluster inherits the same symmetries, but this is a challenging and delicate problem. Aside from the obvious issue of a priori lack of uniqueness of blowdowns in general, note that even in the case where such uniqueness is known, such as when a blowdown has an isolated singularity, we generally expect much less symmetry than in the classical double bubble problem (and its weighted analogue). Indeed, in the latter case, one can obtain cylindrical symmetry due to the lack of boundary conditions (see
[
19
]
), whereas here our boundary data has merely
SO(k+1)\times SO(l+1)
invariance. Similarly, in the work
[
30
]
, the authors get spherical symmetry for the interfaces by exploiting convexity together with connectivity properties of neighboring components in the cluster (see Theorem 1.9 and Remark 1.11 therein). Moreover, in the work
[
10
]
of the first and third authors, the symmetry properties of the minimizing cluster are obtained by symmetrizing with respect to a hyperplane and gluing this symmetrization into the cluster, from which the rigidity follows crucially from the precise expansion of area-minimizing hypersurfaces asymptotic to planes. The analogous expansion of area-minimizing surfaces asymptotic to singular cones do not appear to be sufficient to replicate such an argument, and symmetrization relative to Lawson cones is more complicated, in light of the presence of the chamber
\mathcal{X}(1)
, without which one could work with surfaces invariant under the symmetry group
SO(k+1)\times SO(l+1)
as in
[
23
]
.

Remark 1.7

(Weighted local
(1,2)
-minimizers)
.

Weighted analogues of multiple bubble problems have also been studied, where the perimeter of the interface is replaced by a suitably weighted perimeter, see
[
22
]
. In
[
10
]
, the first and third authors showed that the “weighted lens cluster”
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
(with interfaces given by two spherical caps and a portion of the plane
\{x_{n}=0\}
up to rotation) locally minimizes the weighted energy

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}c_{ij}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}(i)\cap\mathcal{X}(j))\qquad\qquad c_{ij}>0,\,\,c_{ij}<c_{ik}+c_{kj}\,,

and that if additionally
c_{12}=c_{13}
, then uniqueness holds under a planar growth assumption. Therefore, the existence of local minimizers which blowdown to singular cones is natural in this setting also. The proof of Theorem
1.2
should generalize to the case of local minimizers for the weighted energy as long as one replaces
\Lambda_{\rm plane}(n)
with the suitable weighted quantity

\displaystyle c_{12}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}(1)\cap\partial^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}(2))+c_{13}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}(1)\cap\partial^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}(3))-c_{23}\tilde{\rho}_{n}^{n-1}\,,

where
\tilde{\rho}_{n}
is the radius of the disc
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}(1)\cap\{x_{0}=0\}
and similarly for
\Lambda_{\rm Lawson}(n)
. In addition, in the perturbative regime around the equal weights case, the weighted analogue of Theorem
1.3
will clearly hold also since the relevant quantities are continuous in the weights.
For the sake of concreteness, we do not address these questions here.

Notation and preliminaries

For a set of finite perimeter
E
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let
P(E)
denote its perimeter,
P(E;A)
its relative perimeter in an open set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\partial^{*}E
its reduced boundary. We refer the reader to
[
28
, Chaper 12]
for the basics of sets of finite perimeter. In particular, in the definition of cluster perimeter (
1.1
), in the summation we have

P(\mathcal{X}(i);B_{\rho}(x))=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}(i)\cap B_{\rho}(x))

We will often refer to the boundaries
\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}(i)\cap\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}(j)
between pairs of chambers as
interfaces
.

For any set
E
of locally finite perimeter in the paper, we will always assume that we are working with a Lebesgue representative that satisfies

(2.1)

\displaystyle\overline{\partial^{*}E}=\partial E\,,

see
[
28
, Proposition 12.19]
. This will ensure that density properties hold for the chambers of minimizing partitions.
We let
A\Delta B
denote the symmetric difference
(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
of two Borel sets
A
and
B
.

We will often be exploiting compactness of sequences of sets of finite perimeter with uniform perimeter bounds inside a compact set. We recall that this compactness is with respect to the metric
d(A,B)=\|\mathbf{1}_{A}-\mathbf{1}_{B}\|_{L^{1}}
, where
\mathbf{1}_{A}
denotes the indicator on
A
(see e.g.
[
28
]
*Theorem 12.26), and we will simply write
A_{k}\overset{L^{1}}{\to}A
to denote the convergence of a sequence of finite perimeter sets
A_{k}
with respect to this metric. If one merely has local uniform perimeter bounds, we instead obtain
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
compactness, and thus we write
A_{k}\overset{L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\to}A
. We analogously define the notion of
L^{1}
and
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
convergence for (1,2)-clusters, which we get under the assumption of a uniform or locally uniform
\mathcal{P}
upper bound respectively. We use the notation
\mathrm{o}_{k}(1)
for terms that are converging to zero as
k\to+\infty
. We will write
B
for the ball of volume
1
centered at
0
and
B_{R}
for the ball of radius
R
centered at
0
.

Given a local minimizer
\mathcal{X}
of
\mathcal{P}
with
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X};B_{\rho})\leq C\rho^{n-1}
, we recall that an
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
limit along a subsequence of scales
\rho_{j}\uparrow+\infty
for the rescaled sets

\frac{\mathcal{X}(2)}{\rho_{j}}

is referred to as a blowdown of the set of locally finite perimeter
\mathcal{X}(2)
. Note that blowdowns not depend on the center, but may depend on the sequence
\{\rho_{j}\}
. If
\mathcal{X}
is a locally minimizing
(1,2)
-cluster, then subsequential blowdowns always exist, and are locally minimizing
(0,2)
-clusters with conical interface. This follows from standard arguments; see e.g.
[
10
, Lemma 4.1, Corollary 4.6]
.

3.
Setup of minimization problem

As described in the introduction, we will prove our main existence result Theorem
1.2
via a compactness procedure for a sequence of minimizing
(1,2)
-clusters in balls of radii increasing to infinity. However, we want to avoid the challenges that would arise if the bounded chamber
\mathcal{X}(1)
for this sequence of minimizers attached to the boundary of the ball in which we are solving the minimization problem. In order to circumvent this, we introduce a suitable penalization term to our energy along the sequence. We wish for the penalization functional to be chosen in such a way that it only penalizes
\mathcal{X}(1)
, and when comparing the energy of our sequence of minimizers to a sequence of competitors, the difference in the penalization terms decays to zero. This is needed to ensure that the limiting cluster is a local minimizer of
\mathcal{P}
without any penalization.

With this in mind, we consider the functional

\mathcal{G}_{R}(E;B_{\rho}(x)):=\int_{E\cap B_{\rho}(x)}g_{R}(|y|)\,dy\,,

where

(3.1)

g_{R}(t):=\begin{cases}\frac{t-{\sqrt{R}}}{\sqrt{R}}&t\geq{\sqrt{R}}\\
0&t\in[0,R)\,.\end{cases}

We simply write
\mathcal{G}_{R}(\mathcal{X}(1))
to mean
\mathcal{G}_{R}(\mathcal{X}(1);B_{4R}).

The function
g_{R}
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz seminorm
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{R}}
. As a consequence, for any fixed
\rho>0
and sequence of points
x_{k}
with
|x_{k}|\leq 4R_{k}\uparrow+\infty
, the sequence
\{g_{R{k}}\}

satisfies

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{y,z\in B_{4R_{k}}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{k})}|g_{R_{k}}(y)-g_{R_{k}}(z)|\to 0\,.

This will be important for extracting a limiting local minimizer of
\mathcal{P}
, as explained above.
In turn let

\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{X};B_{4R}):=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X};B_{4R})+{\mathcal{G}_{R}(\mathcal{X}(1))}\,.

We then consider the localized and penalized minimization problem

(3.3)

\mu_{R}:=\inf\left\{\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{X};B_{4R}):|\mathcal{X}(1)|=1,\ \mathcal{X}(2)\setminus B_{3R}=K\setminus B_{3R},\ \mathcal{X}(3)\setminus B_{3R}=K^{c}\setminus B_{3R}\right\},

for perimeter minimizing cone
K
satisfying
\Lambda(\partial K)<\Lambda_{\plane}(n)
.

Note that the constraints in (
3.3
) in particular ensure that
\mathcal{X}(1)\subset B_{3R}
. Furthermore, observe that the existence of a minimizer for the problem (
3.3
) for each fixed
R
follows by the the lower-semicontinuity of perimeter and the continuity of
\mathcal{G}_{R}
under
L^{1}
convergence of clusters.

Let us collect some initial properties satisfied by minimizers
\mathcal{X}_{R}
of
\mu_{R}
, beginning with various useful energy bounds. Taking as a competitor the
(1,2)
-cluster
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
with
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(1)
the volume-
1
ball
B
centered at the origin,
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(2)=K\setminus B
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(3)=K^{c}\setminus B,
we see that

(3.4)

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{R};B_{4R})\leq\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{X}_{R};B_{4R})\leq\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime};B_{4R})\leq P(B)+{P\big(K;B_{4R}\big)}.

Together with the area minimizing property of the cone
\partial K
, this yields the (asymptotically sharp) bound

(3.5)

P(K;B_{4R})\leq P(\mathcal{X}_{R}(h);B_{4R})\leq P(B)+{P\big(K;B_{4R}\big)}\quad\text{ for }h=2,3\,.

Noting that
\mathcal{X}_{R}(2)\setminus B_{3R}=K\setminus B_{3R}
and thus
P(\mathcal{X}_{R}(2);B_{4R}\setminus B_{3R})\geq P(K;B_{4R}\setminus B_{3R})
, this lower bound and the upper bound in (
3.5
) yield

(3.6)

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{X}_{R}(2);B_{3R})\leq P(B)+P(K;B_{3R})\,.

Keeping in mind that
\mathcal{X}_{R}(1)\subset B_{3R}
, together with (
3.4
) and the lower bound in (
3.5
) yield

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{X}_{R}(1))+{2}\mathcal{G}_{R}(\mathcal{X}_{R}(1))

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{X}_{R};B_{4R})-P(\mathcal{X}_{R}(2);B_{4R})-P(\mathcal{X}_{R}(3);B_{4R})

\displaystyle\leq 2P(B)+2P(K;B_{4R})-P(\mathcal{X}_{R}(2);B_{4R})-P(\mathcal{X}_{R}(3);B_{4R})

(3.7)

\displaystyle\leq 2P(B)\,.

Using (
3.7
), we see how the penalization term
\mathcal{G}_{R}
forces confinement of
\mathcal{X}_{R}(1)
to balls
B_{{\sqrt{R}}+s}
in a measure sense:
for any
s>0
, since
g(|y|)\geq s/\sqrt{R}
for
y\in\mathcal{X}_{R}(1)\setminus B_{{\sqrt{R}}+s}
, from (
3.7
) we have

(3.8)

|\mathcal{X}_{R}(1)\setminus B_{{\sqrt{R}}+s}|\leq\frac{\sqrt{R}\,P(B)}{s}\,.

Beyond (
3.4
), a different competitor gives us another energy upper bound, which will come in useful later:

(3.9)

\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{X}_{R};B_{4R})\leq\Lambda_{\rm plane}(n)+\mathcal{P}(K,B_{4R})+o_{R}(1)\,,

where
o_{R}(1)
converges to zero subsequentially as
R\to+\infty
. To see this, fix
z\in\partial^{*}K\cap B_{1/2}
,
and let
z_{R}={\sqrt{R}}\,z\in\partial^{*}K\cap B_{{\sqrt{R}}/2}
. Note that
(\partial K-z_{R})\cap B_{20\rho_{n}}
converges smoothly to a hyperplane, subsequentially as
R\to+\infty
, which up to a rotation we may assume is
\{x_{n}=0\}
. We may therefore take a competitor that agrees with
K
outside of the cylinder of radius
10\rho_{n}
centered at
z_{R}
and oriented by the plane
\{x_{n}=0\}
, with
z_{R}+\mathcal{X}_{\lens}(1)
“glued in” inside this cylinder.
Noting that
\mathcal{G}_{R}=0
for this competitor by construction, the bound (
3.9
) follows immediately.

Lastly, for any
B_{\rho}(x)\subset B_{4R}
, since
{\Lip(g_{R})}\leq 1/\sqrt{R}
,
another comparison argument but with a ball-shaped replacement inside
B_{\rho}(x)
yields the local perimeter bound

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{R};B_{\rho}(x))\leq C(n)\rho^{n-1}+\frac{2\rho\,\max\{1,\omega_{n}\rho^{n}\}}{\sqrt{R}}\,.

4.
Compactness procedure

We will prove our main theorems via a compactness procedure for a sequence of minimizers
\mathcal{X}_{k}
for the minimization problems (
3.3
) in
B_{4R_{k}}
for scales
R_{k}\uparrow\infty
. Thus, throughout the remainder of this article, we will be considering a sequence
R_{k}\uparrow+\infty
, with associated minimization problems
\mu_{R_{k}}
defined in (
3.3
), and corresponding energies
\mathcal{E}_{R_{k}}=\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{G}_{R_{k}}
. To simplify notation, we will henceforth let
\mu_{k}:=\mu_{R_{k}}
,
\mathcal{E}_{k}:=\mathcal{E}_{R_{k}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{k}:=\mathcal{G}_{R_{k}}
. The latter will have associated integrand
g_{k}:=g_{R_{k}}
.

4.1.
Lower Density Estimates for
\mathcal{X}(1)
and Consequences

We prove lower volume density estimates for
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
that are uniform in
k
.
This lemma has various important consequences, most importantly that the hard constraint
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)\subset B_{3R_{k}}
is not saturated.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{k}
be a minimizer of the variational problem
\mu_{k}
. There are constants
c_{0}>0
,
\rho_{0}\in(0,1]
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, independent of
k
, such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)\cap B_{\rho}(y)|

\displaystyle\geq c_{0}\rho^{n}\qquad\mbox{for any }y\in\partial\mathcal{X}_{k}(1),\,\,\rho<\rho_{0}\,.

Moreover, there are points

\{x_{1,k},\dots,x_{N_{0},k}\}\subset B_{{\mathrm{O}(\sqrt{R}_{k})}}

such that
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{0}}{B_{4}}(x_{k,i})
. In particular,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)\subset B_{{{\rm O}(\sqrt{R_{k}})}}\,.

Generally speaking, lower density estimates for minimizers of perimeter-driven functionals (c.f.
[
28
, Theorem 16.14]
or
[
10
]
*Lemma 4.1 in the setting of clusters) are proven using competitors
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
with
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(1)=\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)\setminus B_{r}(y)
for a point
y\in\partial\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
and scale
r<r_{0}
. For volume-constrained problems such as (
3.3
), to make
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
admissible, the removed mass
\sigma:=|\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)\cap B_{r}(y)|
must be added back to
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(1)
, and to achieve estimates with constants
C_{0}
and
\rho_{0}
independent of
k
, the added energy must be controlled
uniformly in
k
.

In order to obtain such a uniform volume fixing variation despite the fact that we have
\sup_{k}\|g_{k}\|_{C^{0}(B_{4R_{k}})}=+\infty
, the first step of the proof of Lemma
4.1
is to show that a definite amount of mass of each
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
concentrates in a region where
g_{k}
is bounded uniformly in
k
, thereby allowing us to reintroduce the mass with a uniform error.

Proof of Lemmma
4.1
.

The proof is divided into steps. In the first three steps we prove (
4.1
).
Then in the fourth step we prove the ball containment and (
4.2
).

Step 1: Nucleation.

Let
c(n)>0
be the dimensional constant from the nucleation lemma
[
28
, Lemma 29.10]
. Fix
0<\varepsilon<\min\{1,\tfrac{P(\mathcal{X}_{k}(1))}{2nc(n)}\}
. Note that
\varepsilon
can indeed be chosen depending only on
n
, due to the isoperimetric inequality and the fact that
|\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)|=1
.
Applying the nucleation lemma to
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
, and recalling (
3.7
) and the fact that
|\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)|=1
,
we may find points

\{x_{1,k},\dots,x_{N_{k},k}\}
, with
N_{k}\leq\big(\tfrac{P(\mathcal{X}_{k}(1))}{c(n)\varepsilon}\big)^{n}\leq\big(\tfrac{2P(B)}{c(n)\varepsilon}\big)^{n}
, such that

(4.3)

\Big|\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{k}}B_{2}(x_{k,i})\Big|<{\varepsilon}.

and for each
x_{k,i}
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle|B_{1}(x_{k,i})\cap\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)|\geq\bigg(c(n)\frac{\varepsilon}{P(\mathcal{X}_{k}(1))}\bigg)^{n}\geq\bigg(c(n)\frac{\varepsilon}{2P(B)}\bigg)^{n}=:\alpha{(\varepsilon)}\,,

where
B
is the ball of volume
1
. On the other hand, letting
s=s_{k}=2\sqrt{R_{k}}P(B)/\alpha{(\varepsilon)}
in (
3.8
), we have
|\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)\setminus B_{{\sqrt{R_{k}}}+s_{k}}|\leq\alpha{(\varepsilon)}/2
. As a consequence of this and (
4.4

B_{1}(x_{k,i})\cap B_{{\sqrt{R_{k}}}+s_{k}}\neq\varnothing\,,

and thus we conclude that

B_{1}(x_{k,i})\subset B_{{\sqrt{R_{k}}}+s_{k}+2}\qquad\text{ for each }\,i=1,\dots,N_{k}\,.

Importantly, this guarantees that

(4.5)

g_{k}(|x_{k,i}|)\leq g_{k}({\sqrt{R_{k}}}+s_{k}+1)=\frac{2P(B)}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{R_{k}}}\leq C

for a constant independent of
k
.

In light of the uniform bound on
N_{k}
, up to passing to an unrelabeled subsequence, we may assume that each
N_{k}
is equal to the same number
N_{0}
. Thanks to the uniform perimeter bound (
3.10
), for each
i=1,\dots,N_{0}
, the sequence
\mathcal{X}_{k}-x_{k,i}
converges locally in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

to a cluster
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
(which may have an empty second or third chamber)
with locally finite perimeter. Furthermore, we have
|\mathcal{Y}_{i}(1)|>0
by (
4.4
).

Step 2: Volume fixing variations.

We will perform volume fixing variations in
\mathcal{Y}_{1}(1)
using (
4.5
).
By the relative isoperimetric inequality and
|\mathcal{Y}_{1}(1)|\leq 1
,
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\mathcal{Y}_{1}(1)\cap\tilde{B})>0
where
\tilde{B}
denotes the ball of volume
2
centered at the origin.
Thus, since we also have the uniform perimeter bound (
3.10
), arguing as in
[
28
, Lemma 17.21]
(see also
[
35
]
*Lemma 2.3), we get the following volume-fixing variation lemma: there exist
\sigma_{0},r_{0}\in(0,1]
and
z_{1},z_{2}\in B_{2}
with
B_{2r_{0}}(z_{1})\cap B_{2r_{0}}(z_{2})=\varnothing
, all depending on
\mathcal{Y}_{1}(1)
and
n
, such that for any
|\sigma|<\sigma_{0}

k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
m\in\{1,2\}
, we can find a
(1,2)
-cluster
\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\sigma,m}
with
\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\sigma,m}(j)\Delta\mathcal{X}_{k}(j)\Subset B_{r_{0}}(x_{1,k}+z_{m})
for

and

(i)

|\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\sigma,m}(1)|=|\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)|+\sigma,

(ii)

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\sigma,m};B_{r_{0}}(x_{1,k}+z_{m}))\leq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{r_{0}}(x_{1,k}+z_{m}))+C_{0}(\mathcal{Y}_{1},n)\sigma,

where

for the constant

\mathcal{G}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\sigma,m}(1);B_{r_{0}}(x_{1,k}+z_{m}))\leq\mathcal{G}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{k}(1);B_{r_{0}}(x_{1,k}+z_{m}))+\int_{(\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\sigma,m}(1)\Delta\mathcal{X}_{k}(1))\cap B_{2}(x_{1,k})}g_{k}(|y|)\,dy\,,

together with the fact that
g_{k}(|y|)\leq g_{k}(|x_{1,k}|)+\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{R_{k}}}\leq C_{1}

We are now in a position to proceed as in
[
10
]
*Proof of Lemma 4.1. We include the details here for the purpose of clarity, due to the presence of the unbounded penalization term

[
10
]

\rho_{0}\leq\omega_{n}^{-1}\sigma_{0}^{1/n}

Without loss of generality assume that this holds for

(4.6)

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)\cap\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)\cap B_{\rho}(y))\leq\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(3)\cap\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)\cap B_{\rho}(y))\,.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\prime}(1))\leq C_{1}m_{k}(\rho)+\mathcal{G}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{k}(1))\,.

.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{k}
be a minimizer of the variational problem
\mu_{k}
.
There are constants
c_{1}\in(0,1)
and
\rho_{1}\in(0,1]
, independent of

, such that for any
h\in\{1,2,3\},

, and
\rho\in(0,\rho_{1}],

(4.9)

\displaystyle c_{1}\omega_{n}\rho^{n}\leq|\mathcal{X}_{k}(h)\cap B_{\rho}(y)|\leq(1-c_{1})\omega_{n}\rho^{n}

Proof.

Thanks to conclusion (
4.2
) of Lemma
4.1
(which in particular implies that
g_{k}
is bounded uniformly in
k
in a neighborhood of
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
), the argument of Steps 1-3 of Lemma

can be repeated with
\mathcal{X}(h)
,

, in place of
\mathcal{X}(1)
to derive the lower bound of (

,
h\in\{2,3\}
, as opposed to (
4.1
), which is for any

, is that for
y

\partial B_{3R_{k}}
, the boundary conditions only allow removal of
\mathcal{X}_{k}(h)\cap B_{\rho}(y)
from
\mathcal{X}_{k}(h)
when
h=1
. Next, notice that lower density estimates for all three chambers directly imply the corresponding upper density estimates: fix

Then

for either
h=2
or
h=3
; without loss of generality suppose it is

Then by the lower density estimate for
\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)
and the fact that the chambers are disjoint, we have

|\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)\cap B_{\rho}(x)|\leq|B_{\rho}(x)\setminus\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)|=\omega_{n}\rho^{n}-|B_{\rho}(x)\cap\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)|\leq(1-c_{1})\omega_{n}\rho^{n}.

The analogous argument yields the upper density estimates for
h=2,3.

∎

4.2.
Concentrations of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
and their limit minimization problems

Let
\mathcal{X}_{k}
be minimizers for the problem

as described in the preceding section. In order to complete our compactness procedure as

, we first identify a uniform (in

) number of disjoint balls whose union contains
\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

There exist
N\in\mathbb{N}
and

Proof.

Let
N_{0}
and

.
For

if
\limsup_{k}|x_{k,i}-x_{k,j}|<+\infty.

Observe that

defines an equivalence relation on the indices

many equivalence classes partitioning

. Furthermore, up to relabeling of the points

such that for large enough

for each

∎

For each

and

define the translated cluster

We will refer to each cluster

Lemma 4.5
.

Let

The proof is divided into steps.

Step 1

For

5.
Blowdowns of limit concentrations

Thanks to Lemma
4.5
(iii), for any sequence of scales
\rho_{j}\uparrow+\infty
, we may consider a subsequential limit (again locally in Hausdorff distance), not relabeled,

(5.1)

K_{i}:=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}(2)\cap B_{\rho_{j}}}{\rho_{j}}\,.

Note that
K_{i}
implicitly depends on the sequence
\{\rho_{j}\}
.
Observe that if
|\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}(2)|
or
|\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}(3)|
is finite, then
K_{i}
is either the empty set or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\partial K_{i}
is empty.

Let us first demonstrate the validity of the density bound (
1.6
). For this, we require the following almost-monotonicity for area ratios of the interfaces of our concentration regions.

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
k
and
i
, there are
\rho_{k,i}\in(R_{0},2R_{0})
, Radon measures
\sigma_{k}
with
\sup_{k}\sigma_{k}(B_{3R_{k}})<\infty
and
\mathrm{spt}\,\sigma_{k}\subset\cup_{j=1}^{N}\partial B_{\rho_{k,j}}(x_{k,j})
, and unit vector fields
\nu_{M_{k}}^{\rm co}
such that the surfaces

M_{k}:=\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)\cap\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(3)\setminus\cup_{j=1}^{N}B_{\rho_{k,j}}(x_{k,j})

satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{M_{k}\cap B_{3R_{k}}}\Div_{M_{k}}X\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{B_{3R_{k}}}X\cdot\nu_{M_{k}}^{\rm co}\,d\sigma_{k}\qquad\forall X\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{3R_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})

and the following monotonicity-type property for each
i
:

if
\rho_{k,i}<r_{k}<s_{k}<\dist(x_{k,i},\partial B_{3R_{k}})
, then

(5.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(M_{k}\cap B_{r_{k}}(x_{k,i}))}{r_{k}^{n-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(M_{k}\cap B_{s_{k}}(x_{k,i}))}{s_{k}^{n-1}}

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{4}(n,N)\sigma_{k}(B_{3R_{k}})\bigg[\frac{1}{r_{k}^{n-2}}+\frac{1}{\min_{j\neq i}|x_{k,i}-x_{k,j}|^{n-2}}\bigg]\,.

Proof.

To identify
\rho_{k,i}
, we first use the fact that
\rho\mapsto\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(M_{k}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{k,i}))=:g_{i}(\rho)
is increasing (and thus differentiable for a.e.
\rho
) and bounded from above by
C_{1}(n)R_{0}^{n-1}
for
\rho\in(R_{0},2R_{0})
to identify radii
\rho_{k,i}\in(R_{0},2R_{0})
such that
g_{i}^{\prime}(\rho_{k,i})
exists and
g_{i}^{\prime}(\rho_{k,i})\leq C_{2}(n,R_{0})
. Then by a cut-off argument (see e.g.
[
10
, Eq. 4.17]
) based on the existence of

and the fact that
M_{k}
has vanishing (distributional) mean curvature on
B_{3R_{k}}\setminus\cup_{i}\partial B_{\rho_{k,i}}(x_{k,i})
, it follows that the rectifiable
(n-1)
-varifolds
V_{k}:=\underline{v}(M_{k},1)
satisfy

(5.3)

\displaystyle\delta V_{k}(X)=\int_{M_{k}}\Div_{M_{k}}X\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C_{3}(n,N,R_{0})\|X\|_{L^{\infty}}\qquad\forall X\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{3R_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})\,.

Thus by Riesz’s theorem,
\delta V_{k}
is a vector-valued Radon measure which has polar decomposition
\nu_{V_{k}}^{\rm co}(\cdot)\sigma_{k}
, where
\sigma_{k}=|\delta V_{k}|
and
\nu_{V_{k}}^{\rm co}
is
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
-valued. Furthermore, by (
5.3
) and the fact that
M_{k}
has vanishing mean curvature on
B_{3R_{k}}\setminus\cup_{i}\partial B_{\rho_{k,i}}(x_{k,i})
, we have

(5.4)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(B_{3R_{k}})\leq C_{3}(n,N,R_{0})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathrm{spt}\,\sigma_{k}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\partial B_{\rho_{k,i}}(x_{k,i})\,.

Then, fixing
i
, by applying Lemma
A.1
at radii
\rho_{k,i}<r_{k}<s_{k}
and using (
5.4
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{V_{k}}(B_{r_{k}}(x_{k,i}))}{r_{k}^{n-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu_{V_{k}}(B_{s_{k}}(x_{k,i}))}{s_{k}^{n-1}}+\int_{B_{s_{k}}(x_{k,i})}\bigg|\frac{1}{\max\{r_{k},|y-x_{k,i}|\}^{n-1}}-\frac{1}{s_{k}^{n-1}}\bigg|\frac{|y-x_{k,i}|}{n-1}\,d\sigma_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{V_{k}}(B_{s_{k}}(x_{k,i}))}{s_{k}^{n-1}}+\int_{\partial B_{\rho_{k,i}}(x_{k,i})}\bigg|\frac{1}{r_{k}^{n-1}}-\frac{1}{s_{k}^{n-1}}\bigg|\frac{|y-x_{k,i}|}{n-1}\,d\sigma_{k}

\displaystyle\,+\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{B_{s_{k}}(x_{k,j})\cap\partial B_{\rho_{k,j}}(x_{k,j})}\bigg|\frac{1}{\max\{r_{k},|y-x_{k,i}|\}^{n-1}}-\frac{1}{s_{k}^{n-1}}\bigg|\frac{|y-x_{k,i}|}{n-1}\,d\sigma_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu_{V_{k}}(B_{s_{k}}(x_{k,i}))}{s_{k}^{n-1}}+C_{4}(n,N)\sigma_{k}(B_{3R_{k}})\bigg[\frac{1}{r_{k}^{n-2}}+\frac{1}{\min_{j\neq i}|x_{k,i}-x_{k,j}|^{n-2}}\bigg].

Since
V_{k}
is multiplicity one,
\mu_{V_{k}}=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}M_{k}
, and thus we conclude.
∎

, we obtain the following.

.

For any

, the cone

from (

In particular, the conclusion (

holds for

with any choice of

Proof.

By Lemma

and by construction of the points

, for any pair of scales

, the concentration
\mathcal{X}_{k,i}
with associated

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}({M_{k,i}}\cap B_{r_{k}})}{r_{k}^{n-1}}\leq\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}({M_{k,i}}\cap B_{s_{k}})}{s_{k}^{n-1}}+C_{5}\bigg[\frac{1}{r_{k}^{n-2}}+\frac{1}{\min_{j\neq i}|x_{k,i}-x_{k,j}|^{n-2}}\bigg]\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(M_{k,i}\cap B_{r_{k}})}{r_{k}^{n-1}}\leq\frac{P(K;B_{3R_{k}})+P(B)}{(3R_{k}-{\rm O}(\sqrt{R_{k}}))^{n-1}}+C_{5}\bigg[\frac{1}{r_{k}^{n-2}}+\frac{1}{\min_{j\neq i}|x_{k,i}-x_{k,j}|^{n-2}}\bigg]\,,

∎

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We are now in a position to complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
. In light of Corollary
5.2
and Lemma
4.5
, it remains to verify that for at least one of the limiting concentrations
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
, every blowdown
K_{i}
has an interface that is a singular cone.
In this proof, it will be useful to introduce terminology for the case when either
|\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}(2)|
or
|\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}(3)|
is finite; in this case we call
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
a
floater
.

Case 1:
There exists
i
and a sequence of scales
\rho_{j}\uparrow+\infty
such that
\partial K_{i}
is singular, namely it is nonempty and not equal to an
(n-1)
-dimensional plane.

In this case,
as observed at the beginning of Section
5
, we must have
|\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}(2)|=|\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}(3)|=\infty
. So, Lemma
4.5
(iii) guarantees that the corresponding limiting concentration
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
is a locally
\mathcal{P}
-minimizing
(1,2)
-cluster subject to the volume constraint
|\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}(1)|=v_{i}\in(0,1]
. By the monotonicity formula, the density of
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
at infinity, given by

\Theta_{\infty}(\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i})=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{H}(\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}(2)\cap B_{r})}{r^{n-1}}\,,

exists, and since
\partial K_{i}
is singular,
\Theta_{\infty}(\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i})>(1+\eta)\omega_{n-1}
, where
\eta
is the threshold determined by Allard’s Regularity Theorem
[
4
]
*Section 8. In particular, since
\Theta_{\infty}(\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i})
is independent of the sequence of scales going to infinity, it is not possible for
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
to have a blowdown along some sequence
r_{j}\uparrow+\infty
whose interface is contained in a plane, otherwise we would have

\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{H}(\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}(2)\cap B_{r_{j}})}{r_{j}^{n-1}}=\omega_{n-1}

along such a sequence. We therefore conclude that in this case,
any
blowdown of
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
has an interface that is a singular cone. In this case the proof of Theorem
1.2
is complete by letting
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
.

Case 2:
For each
i=1,\dots,N
, either
\mathcal{X}_{i,\infty}
is a floater, or for every sequence of scales
\rho_{j}\uparrow+\infty
, the interface
\partial K_{i}
of the blowdown of
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
along
\{\rho_{j}\}
is an
(n-1)
-dimensional plane. Note that the latter case means that
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
has planar growth at infinity and thus

[
10
]
*Theorem 2.9 implies that
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
is a (rescaled) standard lens cluster
\mathcal{X}_{\lens}^{v_{i}}
centered at a point
y_{i}
with
|\mathcal{X}_{\lens}^{v_{i}}(1)|=v_{i}
and
|y_{i}|\leq R_{0}.
Up to re-indexing, we may assume that for some
M\in\{0,\dots,N\}
,
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,1}\,\dots\mathcal{X}_{\infty,M}
are standard lens clusters and
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,M+1},\dots,\mathcal{X}_{\infty,N}
are floaters, with the convention that
M=0
means there are no standard lens clusters.

We will rule out Case 2 in two steps.

Step 1:
We claim that
M=N=1
, namely, there is just one concentration, and that it is a standard lens cluster.
We will prove this using a concavity argument together with the minimality of
\partial K
to show that if not, the energy
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{R_{k}})
must be too large, violating (
3.9
).
First, observe that

(6.1)

\Lambda_{\rm plane}(n)<n\omega_{n}^{1/n}.

The inequality follows from the fact that the standard lens cluster is
the only local minimizer with planar growth by
[
10
, Theorem 2.9]
.

Next, observe that by scaling we have that for a lens
\mathcal{X}_{\lens}^{v}
centered at a point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(6.2)

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{\lens}^{v};B_{3R_{0}}(y))-\omega_{n-1}(3R_{0})^{n-1}=\Lambda_{\plane}(n)v^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\,,

for any
v>0
small enough such that
\mathcal{X}_{\lens}^{v}(1)\subset B_{R_{0}}.

Now, if
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
is a floater, set
y_{i}=0
, while if
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
is a volume-
v_{i}
standard lens cluster, let
y_{i}
be its center as above. Let

A_{k}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}B_{3R_{0}}(x_{k,i}+y_{i})\,

and observe that
B_{3R_{0}}(x_{k,i}+y_{i})\supset B_{R_{0}}(x_{k,i}).

In light of the
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
convergence of the
\mathcal{X}_{k,i}
to
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
, as well Lemma
4.5
(iii), (
6.1
), (
6.2
), and the strict concavity of
t\mapsto t^{\frac{n-1}{n}}
, we have

\begin{split}\lim_{k\to\infty}&\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};A_{k})-M\omega_{n-1}(3R_{0})^{n-1}\\
&\geq\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{\lens}^{v_{i}};B_{3R_{0}}(y_{i}))-\omega_{n-1}(3R_{0})^{n-1}\right)+\sum_{i=M+1}^{N}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{3}P(\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}(j);B_{3R_{0}})\bigg)\\
&\geq\Lambda_{\plane}(n)\sum_{i=1}^{M}v_{i}^{\frac{n-1}{n}}+n\omega_{n}^{1/n}\sum_{i=M+1}^{N}v_{i}^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\\
&\geq\Lambda_{\plane}(n)\sum_{i}v_{i}^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\geq\Lambda_{\plane}(n)\Big(\sum_{i}v_{i}\Big)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}=\Lambda_{\plane}(n)\,,\end{split}

On the right-hand side of the first line,
\mathcal{X}_{\lens}^{v_{i}}
is the lens centered at
y_{i}.
If
M<N
, i.e. if there are floaters, the first inequality in the final line is strict in light of (
6.1
), while if
N>1,
the second inequality in the final line is strict.
Thus, for the full energy of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
, we have

(6.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{4R_{k}})&\geq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{4R_{k}})=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};A_{k})+\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{4R_{k}}\setminus A_{k})\\
&\geq\Lambda_{\plane}(n)+\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{4R_{k}}\setminus A_{k})+M\omega_{n-1}(3R_{0})^{n-1}+\mathrm{o}_{k}(1).\end{split}

Notice that

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{4R_{k}}\setminus A_{k})=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)\cap\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(3)\cap(B_{4R_{k}}\setminus A_{k})).

Next, for each
i=1,\dots,M
we know that
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,i}
is a standard lens cluster of volume
v_{i}
centered at
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|y_{i}|\leq R_{0}
.
So, for each such
i
,
\mathcal{X}_{k,i}
converges locally in the Hausdorff distance to its corresponding lens, and in particular, in the annulus
B_{4R_{0}}(x_{k,i}+y_{i})\setminus B_{3R_{0}}(x_{k,i}+y_{i})
, the interface
\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)\cap\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(3)
is arbitrarily close to a plane in the Hausdorff distance.
By extending
\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)
and
\mathcal{X}_{k}(3)
inside each
B_{3R_{0}}(x_{k,i}+y_{i})
appropriately, for instance by gluing in the relevant plane in
B_{cR_{0}}(x_{k,i}+y_{i})
and paying along the boundary of a cylinder of radius
cR_{0}
centered at
x_{k,i}+y_{i}
for a suitable choice of
c\in[1,3/2]
, we see that there are complementary sets
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{k}(2)
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{k}(3)
that agree with
\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)
and
\mathcal{X}_{k}(3)
respectively on
B_{4R_{k}}\setminus A_{k}

and which have

(6.4)

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\prime}(2)\cap\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\prime}(3)\cap B_{4R_{k}})=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{4R_{k}}\setminus A_{k})+M\omega_{n-1}(3R_{0})^{n-1}+\mathrm{o}_{k}(1).

Here we are also using the fact that for
k
sufficiently large the
\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(2)\cap\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}(3)
interface does not intersect
B_{3R_{0}}(x_{k,i})
for
i=M+1,\dots,N
, using the Hausdorff convergence of Lemma
4.5
(ii) and the definition of floater.
On the other hand, using the area-minimizing property of the cone
\partial K
, we have

(6.5)

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\prime}(2)\cap\partial^{*}\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\prime}(3)\cap B_{4R_{k}})\geq P(K;B_{4R_{k}}).

Combining (
6.4
) and (
6.5
) with (
6.3
), we find

\mathcal{E}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{4R_{k}})\geq\Lambda_{\plane}(n)+P(K;B_{4R_{k}})+\mathrm{o}_{k}(1),

with strict inequality unless
N=M=1
. Combining this with the energy upper bound (
3.9
) for
k
sufficiently large, we conclude by contradiction that
M=N=1.

Step 2:

Now that we have a single concentration
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,1}
, we are in a position to exploit the fact that
\Lambda(\partial K)<\Lambda_{\plane}(n)
to use the fact that
\mathcal{X}_{\infty,1}
is a standard lens cluster to contradict the minimality of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
with an argument similar to the one in Step 1 above. Recall that we write
y_{1}
to denote the center of this standard lens cluster and that
B_{3R_{0}}(y_{1})\supset B_{R_{0}}.

Since
\Lambda(\partial K)<\Lambda_{\plane}(n)
, there exists a
(1,2)
-cluster
\bar{\mathcal{X}}
with
|\bar{\mathcal{X}}(1)|=1
,
\bar{\mathcal{X}}(1)
bounded, and
\bar{\mathcal{X}}(2)=K\setminus\bar{\mathcal{X}}(1)
, and
\bar{\mathcal{X}}(3)=K^{c}\setminus\bar{\mathcal{X}}(1)
such that

(6.6)

P(\bar{\mathcal{X}}(1))-\mathcal{H}^{n-1}({\partial K}\cap\bar{\mathcal{X}}(1)^{(1)}){+\alpha}\leq\Lambda_{\plane}(n)

for some
\alpha>0
. We therefore have for large
k

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{4R_{k}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{3R_{0}}(x_{k,1}+y_{1}))-\omega_{n-1}(3R_{0})^{n-1}+\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{4R_{k}}\setminus B_{3R_{0}}(x_{k,1}+y_{1}))+\omega_{n-1}(3R_{0})^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\plane}(n)+\mathrm{o}_{k}(1)+\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{k};B_{4R_{k}}\setminus B_{3R_{0}}(x_{k,1}+y_{1}))+\omega_{n-1}(3R_{0})^{n-1}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha+\big(P(\bar{\mathcal{X}}(1))-\mathcal{H}^{n-1}({\partial K}\cap\bar{\mathcal{X}}(1)^{(1)})\big)+\mathcal{P}(K;B_{4R_{k}})+\mathrm{o}_{k}(1)

\displaystyle=\alpha+\mathcal{P}(\bar{\mathcal{X}};B_{4R_{k}})+\mathrm{o}_{k}(1)\,.

In the final inequality we have used (
6.6
) as well as a repetition of the same gluing argument giving us (
6.4
) above and the perimeter minimality of the cone
K
as in (
6.5
).
However, since
\bar{\mathcal{X}}
is an admissible competitor for
\mu_{k}
and
\mathcal{P}(\bar{\mathcal{X}};B_{4R_{k}})=\mathcal{E}_{k}(\bar{\mathcal{X}}_{k};B_{4R_{k}})
for large
k
, for
k
sufficiently large we reach a contradiction to the minimality of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
as desired.

7.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
. This relies on an explicit computation of the values of
\Lambda_{\plane}(n)
, together with an upper bound on
\Lambda_{\Lawson}(n)
by
\Lambda(C_{n/2-1,n/2-1})
when
n
is even, and by
\Lambda(C_{(n-1)/2-1,(n-1)/2})
when
n
is odd. The computation requires one to evaluate integral expressions, which may simplified using special functions, but are still difficult to evaluate explicitly by hand in general. We therefore use the FLINT library to do our interval arithmetic. The reader who does not have this installed can use Mathematica to check the computations for small
n
. For the reader who wishes to try pen and paper calculations, we also describe how to do the computations entirely by hand in all even dimensions, and carry them out explicitly for
n=8
.

7.1.
Computing
\Lambda_{\plane}(n)

Recall the quantity
\Lambda_{\plane}(n)

defined in (
1.3
). Our first step is to derive the following exact expression for
\Lambda_{\rm plane}(n)
in terms of special functions:

(7.1)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\plane}(n)

\displaystyle=\omega_{n-1}^{\frac{1}{n}}\,\frac{(n-1)\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}-{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{3-{n}}{2};\,\frac{3}{2};\,\frac{1}{4}\right)\Big)-\Big(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\Big)^{n-1}}{\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{{n}}{2}\right)}-{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{1-{n}}{2};\,\frac{3}{2};\,\frac{1}{4}\right)\Big)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}}\,.

Here
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
is the Gauss hypergeometric function.

Let
A_{1}
be a spherical cap of unit radius in the upper halfspace
\{x_{n}\geq 0\}
meeting the hyperplane
\{x_{n}=0\}
with interior contact angle
\pi/3
and let
A_{2}
be its reflection across
\{x_{n}=0\}
. Let
V_{\lens}(n)
denote the volume of the region enclosed by
A_{1}\cup A_{2}
.
By the coarea formula,

(7.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A_{1})

\displaystyle=(n-1)\omega_{n-1}\int_{1/2}^{1}\big(1-t^{2}\big)^{\frac{n-3}{2}}\,dt

(7.3)

\displaystyle V_{\lens}(n)

\displaystyle=2\omega_{n-1}\int_{1/2}^{1}\big(1-t^{2}\big)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,dt

Note that when
n\geq 3
is odd, the integrands in both expressions above are polynomials and thus can easily be integrated by hand. However, since we will also consider even dimensions, we proceed in generality.
Since the intersection of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
is an
(n-2)
-dimensional sphere in
\{x_{n}=0\}
of radius
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
,

(7.4)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\plane}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A_{1})-\omega_{n-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^{n-1}}{V_{\lens}(n)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}}

To write this expression more explicitly, we first compute the antiderivative of the integrand in the integral expression (
7.3
) for
V_{\lens}(n).

The change of variables
s=t^{2}
gives us

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x^{2}}\left(1-s\right)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}s^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,ds=\frac{1}{2}\,{\rm B}\left(x^{2};\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}},\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{n+1}{2}$}}\right)

where
{\rm B}(z;a,b)=\int_{0}^{z}s^{a-1}(1-s)^{b-1}\,ds
is the incomplete beta function; see e.g.
[
34
, §8.17(i)]
. Therefore, keeping in mind that
{\rm B}(1;a,b)
is the (complete) beta function satisfying the identify
{\rm B}(1;a,b)=\frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}
and that
\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}
, we evaluate the integral in the expression (
7.3
) for
V_{\lens}(n)
as

\int_{1/2}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{(n-1)/2}\,dt=\frac{1}{2}{\rm B}\left(x^{2};\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}},\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{n+1}{2}$}}\right)\Big|_{1/2}^{1}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n+2}{2})}-\frac{1}{2}{\rm B}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{4}$}};\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}},\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{n+1}{2}$}}\right)\,.

To further simplify the final term, we apply the identity

{\rm B}(z;a,b)=\frac{z^{a}}{a}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(a,1-b;a+1;z\right);

see
[
34
, §8.17(ii)]
. This leads us to the final expression

V_{\lens}(n)=\omega_{n-1}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{{n}}{2}\right)}-{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}},\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1-n}{2}$}};\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3}{2}$}};\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{4}$}}\right)\bigg)

Analogously evaluating the integral in the expression (
7.2
) for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A_{1})
shows

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A_{1})=\frac{(n-1)\omega_{n-1}}{2}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}-{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}},\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3-{n}}{2}$}};\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3}{2}$}};\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{4}$}}\right)\bigg)

where again
{}_{2}F_{1}
is the Gauss hypergeometric function. Substituting these expressions for
V_{\lens}(n)
and
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A_{1})
into (
7.4
) yields (
7.1
).

7.2.
Estimating
\Lambda_{\Lawson}(n)

Next, we estimate
\Lambda_{\Lawson}(n)
from above via an explicit competitor construction.
Choose two positive integers
k,l
such that
n=k+l+2.

Below we construct a set of finite perimeter
E
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with finite volume depending on
k
and
l
.
Letting

K=\{x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{k+1}^{2}<\tfrac{k}{l}(x_{k+2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2})\}

denote the region bounded by the Lawson cone
C_{k,l}
, we then let
\mathcal{X}
be the
(1,2)
-cluster with
\mathcal{X}(1)=E/|E|^{1/n}
(so that it has the same volume as
\mathcal{X}_{\rm lens}(1)
),
\mathcal{X}(2)=K\setminus\mathcal{X}(1)
and
\mathcal{X}(3)=(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus K)\setminus\mathcal{X}(1)
.
For this cluster, we set

(7.5)

M(k,l)=P(\mathcal{X}(1))-\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(C\cap\mathcal{X}(1))

Then, keeping in mind the definition of
\Lambda_{\Lawson}(n)
in (
1.7
) and by taking
\mathcal{X}
as a competitor in the variational problem (
1.4
) for
C=C_{k,l}
, we have

(7.6)

\Lambda_{\Lawson}(n)\leq M(k,l)\,.

We define the set
E
be invariant under the action of
SO(k+1)\times SO(l+1)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, it suffices to define its “slice”
\hat{E}
in a two-dimensional quadrant
Q=\{(u,v):u>0,v>0\}
, where
u=|(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})|
and
v=|(x_{k+2},\dots,x_{n})|
. Letting
\lambda:=\sqrt{{k}/{l}}
, notice that

\partial K\cap Q=\{(u,v):u>0\,,\ v>0\,,\ u=\lambda v\}\,.

In the quadrant, define
\hat{E}\cap\{u<\lambda v\}

to be the circular arc of radius

r>0
and center
(0,-h)
that forms angle
\pi/3
with the line
\{u=\lambda v\}
and angle
\pi/2
with the
v
-axis
\{u=0\}
.
We may parameterize this arc as the graph over the
v
-axis of the function
f:[1,-h+r]\to[0,\lambda]
given by
f(v)=\sqrt{r^{2}-(v+h)^{2}}
. Let
\theta=\arctan(\lambda)
denote the angle that the line
\{u=\lambda v\}
makes with the
v
-axis
\{u=0\}
.
Since
f(1)=\lambda=\sqrt{r^{2}-(1+h)^{2}}
and
f^{\prime}(1)=-\tan(2\pi/3-\theta)=-\frac{1+h}{\lambda}
, this yields

(7.7)

h=\lambda\tan(2\pi/3-\theta)-1,\qquad r=\lambda\sec(2\pi/3-\theta)\,.

We define
\hat{E}\cap\{u>\lambda v\}
analogously. Namely, we parameterize the arc of a circle of radius
\rho>0
and center
(-d,0)
that forms angle
\pi/3
with the line
\{u=\lambda v\}
and angle
\pi/2
with the
u
-axis
\{v=0\}
, via a function
g:[\lambda,\rho-d]\to[0,1]
defined by
g(u)=\sqrt{\rho^{2}-(u+d)^{2}}
. This time, we have
g(\lambda)=1=\sqrt{\rho^{2}-(\lambda+d)^{2}}
and
g^{\prime}(\lambda)=-\tan(\pi/6+\theta)=-(\lambda+d)
, which in turn yields

(7.8)

d=\tan(\pi/6+\theta)-\lambda\,,\qquad\rho=\sec(\pi/6+\theta)\,.

See Figure
1
below for a diagram of the part of
\hat{E}
, together with the cone
C_{k,l}
, lying in the upper-right quadrant of the
(u,v)
-plane.

Figure 1.
A depiction of the competitor
\hat{E}
in the upper-right quadrant of the
(u,v)
-plane.

The coarea formula (see e.g.
[
12
]
) yields

(7.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial E)

\displaystyle=(k+1)(l+1)\omega_{k+1}\omega_{l+1}\int_{\partial\hat{E}}u^{k}v^{l}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

(7.10)

\displaystyle|E|

\displaystyle=(k+1)(l+1)\omega_{k+1}\omega_{l+1}\int_{\hat{E}}u^{k}v^{l}\,du\,dv\,.

Since, by the same formula,

P(K;E)=\lambda^{k}\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}(k+1)(l+1)\omega_{k+1}\omega_{l+1}\int_{0}^{1}v^{k+l}\,dv=\frac{(k+1)(l+1)\omega_{k+1}\omega_{l+1}\lambda^{k}\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}{k+l+1}\,,

the quantity
M(k,l)
defined in (
7.5
) is given by

(7.11)

M(k,l)=\frac{1}{|E|^{\frac{n-1}{n}}}\bigg(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial E)-\frac{(k+1)(l+1)\omega_{k+1}\omega_{l+1}\lambda^{k}\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}{k+l+1}\bigg)\,.

The right-hand side of (
7.11
) is made more explicit by substituting
|E|
and
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial E)
for the following expressions, which follow from plugging definition of
\hat{E}
into (
7.9
) and (
7.10
):

\displaystyle|E|=\omega_{k+1}\omega_{l+1}\Big(\lambda^{k+1}+(k+1)

\displaystyle\int_{\lambda}^{\rho-d}u^{k}\big(\rho^{2}-(u+d)^{2}\big)^{\frac{l+1}{2}}\,du

(7.12)

\displaystyle+(l+1)\int_{1}^{r-h}v^{l}\big(r^{2}-(v+h)^{2}\big)^{\frac{k+1}{2}}\,dv\Big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial E)=\omega_{k+1}\omega_{l+1}(k+1)(l+1)\Big(\rho

\displaystyle\int_{\lambda}^{\rho-d}u^{k}\big(\rho^{2}-(u+d)^{2}\big)^{\frac{l-1}{2}}\,du

(7.13)

\displaystyle+r\int_{1}^{r-h}v^{l}\big(r^{2}-(v+h)^{2}\big)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\,dv\Big)\,.

In fact, by analogous reasoning to that for
\Lambda_{\plane}
in the preceding section, recalling in addition the recursion formula
(k+1)\Gamma(k+1)=\Gamma(k+2)
both of these expressions (and thus
M(k,l)
) can be expressed explicitly in terms special functions as follows:

(7.14)

\displaystyle|E|

\displaystyle=\omega_{k+1}\omega_{l+1}\left({\lambda^{k+1}}+\big(\rho^{2}-d^{2}\big)^{\frac{l+1}{2}}\,A+{(r^{2}-h^{2})}^{\frac{k+1}{2}}B\right),

(7.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial E)

\displaystyle=\omega_{k+1}\omega_{l+1}\left((l+1)\rho\big(\rho^{2}-d^{2}\big)^{\frac{l-1}{2}}C+(k+1)r\big(r^{2}-h^{2}\big)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}D\right)

where

(7.16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}A&=\frac{(\rho-d)^{k+1}\,\Gamma(k+2)\,\Gamma(\frac{l+3}{2})}{\Gamma(k+1+\frac{l+3}{2})}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{l+1}{2}$}},\,k+1,\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{l+1}{2}$}}+k+2;\,-\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\rho- d}{\rho+d}$}}\right)\Big)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-{\lambda^{k+1}}\,F_{1}\Big(k+1,\,-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{l+1}{2}$}},\,-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{l+1}{2}$}},\,k+2;\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\lambda}{\rho- d}$}},\,-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\lambda}{\rho+d}$}}\Big),\end{split}

(7.17)

\displaystyle\begin{split}B&=\frac{(r-h)^{l+1}\,\Gamma(l+2)\,\Gamma(\frac{k+3}{2})}{\Gamma(l+1+\frac{k+3}{2})}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{k+1}{2}$}},\,l+1,\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{k+1}{2}$}}+l+2;\,-\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{r-h}{r+h}$}}\right)\Big)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-F_{1}\Big(l+1,\,-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{k+1}{2}$}},\,-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{k+1}{2}$}},\,l+2;\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r-h}$}},\,-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r+h}$}}\Big)\,,\end{split}

(7.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}C&=\frac{(\rho-d)^{k+1}\Gamma(k+2)\Gamma(\frac{l+1}{2})}{\Gamma(k+1+\frac{l+1}{2})}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1-l}{2}$}},k+1;\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{l-1}{2}$}}+k+2;\,-\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\rho-d}{\rho+ d}$}}\right)\Big)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\lambda^{k+1}F_{1}\Big(k+1,\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1-l}{2}$}},\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1-l}{2}$}},\,k+2;\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\lambda}{\rho- d}$}},\,-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\lambda}{\rho+ d}$}}\Big)\,,\end{split}

(7.19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}D&=\frac{(r-h)^{l+1}\Gamma(l+2)\,\Gamma(\frac{k+1}{2})}{\Gamma(l+1+\frac{k+1}{2})}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1-k}{2}$}},l+1;\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{k-1}{2}$}}+l+2;-\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{r-h}{r+h}$}}\right)\Big)\\

7.4.
By-hand computations in even dimensions and proof of Theorem

for

While the computer-assisted proof of Proposition
7.1
includes rigorous error estimates, it is still interesting to understand the extent to which the proof of Proposition
7.1
and the associated computations can be carried out by hand. In this section, we demonstrate how this can be done for any even dimension

. More specifically, we show that in this case it is possible to express
M(k,k)
(if
k
is odd) or
M(k-1,k+1)
(if
k
is even) entirely in terms of rational powers of integers and
\pi
. Moreover, in any dimension, it is possible to express
\Lambda_{\rm plane}(n)
as a finite sum of products and quotients of polynomials, square roots, and inverse trigonometric functions evaluated at
z=1/4
. We carry out these details explicitly in
n=8
. From here, verifying Proposition
7.1
is a matter of estimating these numbers, which again we carry out in
n=8
.

7.4.1.
Further simplifying
\Lambda_{\rm plane}(n)

To accompany our numerical approximation of
\Lambda_{\rm plane}(n)
, we obtain here a closed form expression for
\Lambda_{\rm plane}(n)
. In this section,
n
can be even or odd.

We first explain how the functions

(7.20)

\displaystyle z\mapsto{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}},\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3-{n}}{2}$}};\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3}{2}$}};\,z\right)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad z\mapsto{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}},\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1-{n}}{2}$}};\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3}{2}$}};\,z\right)\qquad n\geq 8

can be expressed as a finite sum of products and quotients of polynomials, square roots, and inverse trigonometric functions (and as observed above, when

is odd, it is a polynomial).
First, one may check by term by term series comparison that

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}},\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}};\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3}{2}$}};\,z\right)=\frac{\arcsin(\sqrt{z})}{\sqrt{z}}\qquad 0<z<1\,;

this formula is known in the literature, e.g.
[
6
, Equation 2.1.5]
.

Next, we recall the formula

(7.22)

\displaystyle(c-b)\,{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b-1;c;z)=z(1-z)\frac{d[{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)]}{dz}-(b-c+az)\,{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)\,,

which is one of Gauss’s “contiguous relations”
[
6
, Equation 2.5.9]
. Equation (

) implies that

(7.23)

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}},\,b-1;\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3}{2}$}};\,z\right)=\frac{1}{\frac{3}{2}-b}\left[z(1-z)\frac{d[{}_{2}F_{1}(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}},\,b;\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3}{2}$}};\,z)]}{dz}-\big(b-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3}{2}$}}+\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{z}{2}$}}\big)\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}},\,b;\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3}{2}$}};\,z\right)\right],

allowing us to start with (
7.21
) and iteratively compute
{}_{2}F_{1}(1/2,m/2;3/2;z)
for any
1\geq m\in\mathbb{Z}
. We thus obtain closed form expressions for each of the functions in (
7.20
). We then evaluate these functions at

as they appear in (
7.1
).

Additionally,

can be computed by hand for positive integers

by using the property

together with the facts that

and

. Therefore, a closed form expression for

for these values of

can be obtained.

For an open

Remark 8.1

(On
\Lambda(K,\beta)
)
.

We have chosen this notation because when

and

. This follows from the characterization of the upper half of the first chamber of the lens cluster as the unique minimizer in a classical capillarity problem
[

and the local minimality of the lens. While it is plausible to expect the connection between locally minimizing
(1,2)
-clusters and minimizers for capillarity problems to extend to cones other than planes which possess some symmetry analogous to reflection over a hyperplane, such as invariance under the map
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1},y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1})\mapsto(y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1},x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})
for the cones
C_{k,k}
, this is still open. Note that in fact it is not known whether the singular minimizer constructed in Theorem
1.2
blows down
C_{k,k}
even if starting with
C_{k,k}
in the penalized problem (
3.3
). The only relationship follows readily from the definitions is for example

\displaystyle 2\Lambda(\{x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{k+1}^{2}<y_{1}^{2}+\dots+y_{k+1}^{2}\},1/2)\geq\Lambda(C_{k,k})\,,

which follows from the fact that the admissible class for (
8.1
) is contained in that of
\Lambda(C_{k,k})
via reflection over
C_{k,k}
.

Theorem 8.2
.

Suppose that
K
is an open cone,
\partial K
has an isolated singularity and either
\beta\in(-1,0)
, or
\beta\in[0,1)
and
K
is a perimeter minimizer. If in addition

(8.2)

\displaystyle\Lambda(K,\beta)<\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(0,\infty),\beta)\,,

then there exists a minimizer to the problem (
8.1
).

Remark 8.3

(On the assumption that
\partial K
is area-minimizing if
\beta\in[0,1)
)
.

We only use this assumption in one place, namely to obtain a short proof of the bound

\displaystyle P(E)\lesssim P(E;K)-\beta P(E;\partial K)\,.

If this bound can be generalized to other open cones
K
with isolated singularity, then the existence result in these cases follow as well. We have not attempted to optimize these assumptions here, partially because the cases in which we are interested are coming from locally minimizing
(1,2)
clusters involve area-minimizing cones.

Proof of Theorem
8.2
.

The proof involves a concentration compactness argument which is similar to but simpler than Theorem
1.2
, due to the fact that the reference cone is fixed in the capillarity problem. Therefore, we provide summaries in lieu of complete details.

Penalized problem and energy bounds
: Fix a sequence of radii
R_{k}\uparrow\infty
. With
g_{k}
and
\mathcal{G}_{k}
as in Section
4
we consider the energy

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{k}(E;B_{4R_{k}}):=P(E;B_{4R_{k}}\cap K)-\beta P(E;B_{4R_{k}}\cap\partial K)+\mathcal{G}_{k}(E)\,.

and the corresponding variational problem

(8.3)

\displaystyle\nu_{k}=\inf\{\mathcal{F}_{k}(E;B_{4R_{k}}):|E|=1\,,E\subset K\cap B_{3R_{k}}\}\,.

When
\beta\leq 0
, existence follows by quoting the lower-semicontinuity result
[
28
, Proposition 19.1]
. When
\beta>0
, since
\partial K
only has an isolated singularity and is smooth elsewhere, straightforward modifications to the classical lower-semicontinuity for capillarity energies
[
28
, Proposition 19.3]
yield the existence of a minimizer
E_{k}
for
\nu_{k}
. Note that since the infimum
\Lambda(K,\beta)
can be achieved by bounded sets (via a truncation argument) and
\mathcal{F}_{k}(E;B_{4R_{k}})=P(E;K)-\beta P(E;\partial K)
if
E\subset B_{\sqrt{R_{k}}}
, via an energy comparison for
k
sufficiently large we have the upper bound

(8.4)

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{k}(E_{k};B_{4R_{k}})\leq\Lambda(K,\beta)\,,

which is the capillarity analogue of the admissibility of
\bar{\mathcal{X}}
for
\mu_{k}
in Section
6
.

Towards obtaining uniform perimeter bounds for
E_{k}
, we first note that if
\beta<0
, then due to the fact that
E_{k}\subset B_{3R_{k}}
we have the inequality

(8.5)

\displaystyle|\beta|P(E_{k})<P(E_{k};K)-\beta P(E_{k};\partial K)\leq\mathcal{F}_{k}(E;B_{4R_{k}})

On the other hand, if
\beta\geq 0
and we are assuming perimeter minimality of
K
, then for any
E\subset K\cap B_{3R_{k}}
,

\displaystyle P(K;B_{4R_{k}})\leq P(K\setminus E;B_{4R_{k}})=P(E;K)+P(K;B_{4R_{k}})-P(E;\partial K)\,.

Upon subtracting
P(K;B_{4R_{k}})-P(E;\partial K\cap B_{4R_{k}})
from both sides, we obtain

\displaystyle P(E;\partial K\cap B_{4R_{k}})\leq P(E;K\cap B_{4R_{k}})\,,

which implies that for any
E\subset K\cap B_{3R_{k}}
,

\displaystyle(1-\beta)P(E)/2

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)P(E;K)/2+(1-\beta)P(E;\partial K\cap B_{4R_{k}})/2

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{F}_{k}(E;B_{4R_{k}})\,.

Thus by comparison arguments, replacing
E_{k}
and
E_{k}\cap B_{\rho}(y)
respectively with balls (cf. Section
3
), we obtain the respective
k
-independent energy bounds

(8.6)

\displaystyle P(E_{k})/C_{1}\leq\mathcal{F}_{k}(E_{k};B_{4R_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}

(8.7)

\displaystyle P(E_{k};B_{\rho}(y))

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\rho^{n-1}+\frac{2\rho\max\{1,\omega_{n}\rho^{n}\}}{\sqrt{R}}\qquad\forall y\in\overline{K}

for some
C_{1}>1
and
C_{2}>0
. As a consequence of (
8.6
), the penalization term again forces confinement of
E
to balls
B_{\sqrt{R_{k}}+s}
in a measure sense (cf. (
3.8
)): there is a constant
C_{3}>0
(independent of
k
) such that for any
s>0
,

(8.8)

|E\setminus B_{\sqrt{R_{k}}+s}|\leq\frac{C_{3}\sqrt{R_{k}}}{s}\,.

Nucleation, volume fixing variations, and almost-minimality
: As in Theorem
1.2
, the global perimeter bound (
8.6
) allows for the application of the nucleation lemma
[
28
, Lemma 29.10]
to identify a finite number, say
N_{k}
, of balls
B_{1}(x_{k,i})
(with
N_{k}\leq N_{0}
), each containing at least a fixed amount of volume of
E_{k}
and whose union contains most of the volume of
E_{k}
(again independently of
k
). By choosing the parameters in the same fashion as in Step 1 of Lemma
4.1
, we obtain the analogue of (
4.5
) for the capillarity problem, which guarantees that

(8.9)

\displaystyle|x_{k,i}|\leq\mathrm{O}(\sqrt{R_{k}})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad g_{k}(|x_{k,i}|)\leq C_{4}\qquad\forall k,\,i

for some
C_{4}
independent of
k
.

Now we are going to construct uniform volume fixing variations and use it to derive a “one-sided” minimality inequality that will yield lower density estimates (such a property was used in (
4.7
) in Lemma
4.1
). First, note that since
\partial K
is a regular cone, then for any
x_{k,i}
escaping to infinity as
k\to\infty
,
K\cap B_{1}(x_{k,i})
is approximately the intersection
B_{1}(x_{k,i})
with the halfspace determined by the nearby tangent hyperplanes of
K
. By the uniform lower bound on
|B_{1}(x_{k,i})\cap E_{k}|
from the nucleation lemma and the fact that
E_{k}\subset K
, the measures of
|K\cap B_{1}(x_{k,i})|
are uniformly bounded from below in
i
and
k
. Combining all these facts, we see that for any
i
, up to subsequence, not relabelled, there is an open set
A_{i}
such that
K\cap B_{1}(x_{k,i})
converges to some set
A_{i}\subset\bar{K}
as
k\to+\infty
in the Hausdorff sense. By the uniform perimeter bound (
8.6
) and the lower bounds on
|B_{1}(x_{k,i})\cap E_{k}|
, for each
i
there is a limiting set of finite perimeter
E_{\infty,i}\subset A_{i}
for
B_{1}(x_{k,i})\cap E_{k}
. Thus by combining the usual volume fixing variations along a subsequence with the uniform bound (
8.9
) for
g_{k}
as in Step 2 of the proof of Lemma
4.1
, we may obtain volume fixing variations with two important properties: first, the variations are compactly supported away from
\partial K
(so they do not influence the energy along
\partial K
) and second, for each volume increment
\sigma
, the variation in the energy
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is at most
C_{5}|\sigma|
for some
C_{5}>0
independent of
k
.

Next, by combining the minimality of
E_{k}
for
\nu_{k}
with the volume fixing variations, we claim there are
r_{0}>0
and
\Lambda>0
, both independent of
k
, such that
E_{k}
satisfies the “one-sided” almost-minimizing condition

\displaystyle P(E_{k};K\cap B_{4R_{k}})-\beta P(E_{k};\partial K\cap B_{4R_{k}})

(8.10)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq P(E;K\cap B_{4R_{k}})-\beta P(E;\partial K\cap B_{4R_{k}})+\Lambda\big||E_{k}|-|E|\big|

whenever
E\subset E_{k}
and
E_{k}\setminus E\subset B_{r_{0}}(y)\subset B_{3R_{k}}
. Indeed, choosing
r_{0}
small enough so that
\omega_{n}r_{0}^{n}
of volume can be added using the volume fixing variations, this follows by a direct comparison argument using the minimality of
E_{k}
for
\nu_{k}
.

Lower density estimates
: We claim now that there exists
c_{0}
and
\rho_{0}
, both independent of
k
, such that if
y\in B_{1}^{c}\cap\mathrm{spt}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}(\partial^{*}E_{k}\cap K)
and
0<\rho<\rho_{0}
, then

(8.11)

\displaystyle|E_{k}\cap B_{\rho}(y)|\geq c_{0}\rho^{n}\,.

If
y
is at a fixed distance
d
from
\partial K
, then such an estimate for radii
\rho<\min\{d,\rho_{1}\}
holds by the usual comparison argument with
E_{k}\setminus B_{\rho}(y)

[
28
, Chapter 21]
. On the other hand, for
y
nearby
\partial K
(and away from the singularity by assumption), we instead transform the isotropic capillarity problem in
K\cap B_{\rho}(y)
to an anisotropic one in
H\cap A
, where, for some boundary flattening diffeomorphism
\Phi
and halfspace
H
, we have
\Phi(K\cap B_{\rho}(y))=H\cap A
. We will appeal to lower density results for anisotropic capillarity functionals in halfspaces from
[
14
]
, as we now describe. After this boundary flattening, the one-sided minimality condition (
8.10
) holds for
\Phi(E_{k})
in
A\cap H
with respect to an anisotropic energy

\displaystyle\int_{H\cap\partial^{*}\Phi(E_{k})}\Psi(\nu_{\Phi(E_{k})})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\beta\int_{\partial H\cap\partial^{*}\Phi(E_{k})}\Psi(\nu_{\Phi(E_{k})})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

and new constants
\Lambda^{\prime}
,
r_{0}^{\prime}
that depend on the diffeomorphism
\Phi
, in place of
\Lambda
,
r_{0}
respectively; see e.g.
[
14
, Lemma 2.17, Proof of Theorem 1.2]
for details on how elliptic anisotropies and almost minimizers behave under such transformations. Furthermore, we may further transform this energy by eliminating the adhesion term along
\partial H
and changing the anisotropy
[
14
, Lemma 6.1]
, so that the one-sided minimality condition holds with respect to an energy of the form

\displaystyle\int_{H\cap\partial^{*}\Phi(E_{k})}\tilde{\Psi}(\nu_{\Phi(E_{k})})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\,.

This is done so that we can repeat the argument in
[
14
, Proof of (2.48) in Lemma 2.8]
, which, similar to the interior case, is based on comparison with
\Phi(E_{k})\setminus B_{\rho^{\prime}}(\Phi(y))
and isoperimetry, and requires an anisotropic energy with no adhesion term. We thus obtain local lower density bounds for
\Phi(E_{k})
at
\Phi(y)
. These can be translated to
k
-independent lower density bounds for
E_{k}
at
y
through the diffeomorphism
\Phi^{-1}
. It remains to show that such estimates can be made independently of
y\in B_{1}^{c}\cap\mathrm{spt}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}(\partial^{*}E_{k}\cap K)
and
0<\rho<\rho_{0}
. This is where we use the fact that
\partial K
is a smooth cone away from
0
, since it implies that the diffeomorphisms used to straighten
\partial K
at a fixed order
1
scale converge to the identity as
|y|\to\infty
. The estimates in
[
14
, Lemma 2.8, Lemma 6.1]
depend only on suitable ellipticity/smoothness type properties of
\Psi
,
\tilde{\Psi}
and
\big|1-|\beta|\big|
(see the paragraph below
[
14
, Equation (6.7)]
). Since
\Phi
approximates the identity map in every
C^{k}
if
|y|
is large and
|\beta|<1
, the desired
y
-independence follows.

Conclusion
: The conclusion of the proof is a simplified version of the arguments at the end of the proof of Theorem
1.2
, since we do not need to deal with blowdown of an exterior interface. We first apply the nucleation lemma, with parameter
\varepsilon
(bounding the volume of
E_{k}
appearing outside the balls) chosen small enough so that
\omega_{n}\rho_{0}^{n}>\varepsilon
. Combining this with the density estimate (
8.11
), for each
k
we then deduce that each
E_{k}
is contained in the union of finitely many balls
B_{R_{0}}(x_{k,i})
,
1\leq i\leq I
. Let us call
v_{k,i}=|E_{k}\cap B_{R_{0}}(x_{k,i})|
, with

(8.12)

\displaystyle\dist(x_{k,i},x_{k,j})\to\infty\qquad\mbox{if $i\neq j$.}

Up to a subsequence we may assume by the lower density bound that
v_{k,i}\to v_{i}
for some
v_{i}>0
for each
i
. We claim that if
|x_{k,i}|\to\infty
for any
i
, then

(8.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{k}(E_{k};B_{R_{0}}(x_{k,i}))=v_{i}^{(n-1)/n}\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(0,\infty),\beta)+v_{i}g_{k}(|x_{k,i}|)+{\rm o}_{k}(1)\,.

Indeed, the upper bound for the energy by the right hand side follows by a comparison argument and the fact that
{\rm Lip}(g_{k})\to 0
, and the lower bound follows by straightening the boundary and using the fact that the resulting anisotropies are arbitrarily close to the identity together with the minimality of the lens. On the other hand, by (
8.12
), there is at most one
i
for which
|x_{k,i}|
remains bounded, which without loss of generality we may assume is
i=1
if it exists (otherwise we simply start counting from
i=2
and call
v_{1}=0
), for which we have

(8.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{k}(E_{k};B_{R_{0}}(x_{1,k}))=v_{1}^{(n-1)/n}\Lambda(K,\beta)+{\rm o}_{k}(1)\,.

The lower bound of the left hand side by the right hand side is a consequence of the definition (
8.1
), and the upper bound follows by energy comparison, analogously to (
8.4
). By using in order (
8.4
), (
8.13
)-(
8.14
), (
8.2
), and the concavity of
v\mapsto v^{(n-1)/n}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\Lambda(K,\beta)

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{k}(E_{k};B_{4R_{k}})

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\sum_{i\geq 2}v_{i}^{(n-1)/n}\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(0,\infty),\beta)+v_{1}^{(n-1)/n}\Lambda(K,\beta)

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\sum_{i}v_{i}^{(n-1)/n}\Lambda(K,\beta)\geq\left(\sum_{i}v_{i}\right)^{(n-1)/n}\Lambda(K,\beta)=\Lambda(K,\beta)\,.

But since each
v_{i}
is positive for
i\geq 2
and (
8.2
) holds, equality can only hold in the first inequality in the final line above if no
x_{k,i}
can diverge to infinity, which means there is one large ball
B_{R_{1}}
containing every
E_{k}
. By compactness, minimality of
E_{k}
for
\nu_{k}
, and lower-semicontinuity, we obtain limiting
E
minimizing (
8.1
).
∎

Remark 8.4
.

Note that in the setting of Theorem
1.2
, despite the validity of the strict inequality
\Lambda(\partial K)<\Lambda_{\plane}(n)
, we cannot conclude that none of the

can diverge to infinity, since we are missing both the analogue of (

) for both the concentration that might remain bounded, and for any concentration that might diverge to infinity in a singular-shaped form (i.e. not lens-shaped). On the other hand, if we knew

Appendix A
Monotonicity identity

The next lemma is the usual monotonicity identity but without the assumption that the distributional mean curvature is absolutely continuous with respect to the weight measure. This entails essentially no change in the proof, which we include for convenience.

Lemma A.1

(Monotonicity)
.

Let
V=\underline{v}(M,\theta)
be a rectifiable
m
-varifold in an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+\ell}
with locally bounded first variation
\delta V
, so that
\delta V
is a Radon measure and, setting
\nu=D_{|\delta V|}\delta V
,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\int_{U}\Div_{M}X\,d\mu_{V}=\int_{U}X\cdot\nu\,d|\delta V|\qquad\forall X\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U;\mathbb{R}^{m+\ell}),

where
\mu_{V}=\theta\mathcal{H}^{m}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}M
; cf.
[
42
, Chapter 8]
. Then, for any
x\in U
and
0<r<s<\dist(x,\partial U)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{V}(B_{s}(x))}{s^{m}}-\frac{\mu_{V}(B_{r}(x))}{r^{m}}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{s}(x)\setminus B_{r}(x)}\frac{|(y-x)^{\perp}|^{2}}{|y-x|^{m+2}}\,d\mu_{V}

(A.2)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{B_{s}(x)}\bigg[\frac{1}{s^{m}}-\frac{1}{\max\{r,|y-x|\}^{m}}\bigg]\frac{(y-x)\cdot\nu}{m}\,d|\delta V|\,.

Proof.

For ease of notation, let us take
x=0
. For
\rho\in(r,s)
, let
X(y)=\varphi(|y|/\rho)y
, where
\varphi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,1);[0,1])
. Denoting
\hat{y}^{\perp}=\mathbf{proj}_{T_{y}M^{\perp}}(y/|y|)
, direct computation yields

\displaystyle\Div_{M}X=\varphi^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{|y|}{\rho}\bigg)\frac{|y|}{\rho}\big(1-|\hat{y}^{\perp}|^{2}\big)+m\varphi\bigg(\frac{|y|}{\rho}\bigg)\,.

Testing (
A.1
) with this
X
and isolating the term with
-|y|\varphi^{\prime}|\hat{y}^{\perp}|^{2}/\rho
on one side, we obtain

\displaystyle-\int_{U}\varphi^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{|y|}{\rho}\bigg)\frac{|y|}{\rho}|\hat{y}^{\perp}|^{2}\,d\mu_{V}

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\varphi\bigg(\frac{|y|}{\rho}\bigg)y\cdot\nu\,d|\delta V|-\int_{U}\bigg[\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(|y|/\rho)|y|}{\rho}+m\varphi(|y|/\rho)\bigg]\,d\mu_{V}

(A.3)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\varphi\bigg(\frac{|y|}{\rho}\bigg)y\cdot\nu\,d|\delta V|+\int_{U}\rho^{m+1}\frac{d}{d\rho}\bigg[\frac{\varphi(|y|/\rho)}{\rho^{m}}\bigg]\,d\mu_{V}\,.

We multiply both sides of (
A.3
) by
\rho^{-m-1}
and integrate in
\rho
from
r
to
s
, which gives

\displaystyle-\int_{r}^{s}\int_{U}

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{|y|}{\rho}\bigg)\frac{|y|}{\rho^{m+2}}|\hat{y}^{\perp}|^{2}\,d\mu_{V}\,d\rho

\displaystyle=\int_{r}^{s}\int_{U}\varphi\bigg(\frac{|y|}{\rho}\bigg)\frac{y\cdot\nu}{\rho^{m+1}}\,d|\delta V|\,d\rho+\int_{r}^{s}\int_{U}\frac{d}{d\rho}\bigg[\frac{\varphi(|y|/\rho)}{\rho^{m}}\bigg]\,d\mu_{V}\,d\rho

(A.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}y\cdot\nu\int_{r}^{s}\varphi\bigg(\frac{|y|}{\rho}\bigg)\frac{1}{\rho^{m+1}}\,d\rho\,d|\delta V|+\int_{U}\bigg[\frac{\varphi(|y|/s)}{s^{m}}-\frac{\varphi(|y|/r)}{r^{m}}\bigg]\,d\mu_{V}\,,

where in the last line we have used Fubini’s theorem and the fundamental theorem of calculus. The left hand side of (
A.4
) we now rewrite using Fubini and then integration by parts in
\rho
:

\displaystyle-\int_{r}^{s}\int_{U}\varphi^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{|y|}{\rho}\bigg)\frac{|y|}{\rho^{m+2}}|\hat{y}^{\perp}|^{2}\,d\mu_{V}\,d\rho

\displaystyle=\int_{U}|\hat{y}^{\perp}|^{2}\int_{r}^{s}\bigg[-\varphi^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{|y|}{\rho}\bigg)\frac{|y|}{\rho^{2}}\bigg]\frac{1}{\rho^{m}}\,d\rho\,d\mu_{V}

(A.5)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}|\hat{y}^{\perp}|^{2}\bigg[\frac{\varphi(|y|/s)}{s^{m}}-\frac{\varphi(|y|/r)}{r^{m}}+\int_{r}^{s}\frac{m\varphi(|y|/\rho)}{\rho^{m+1}}\,d\rho\bigg]d\mu_{V}.

Inserting (
A.5
) back into the left hand side of (
A.4
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\int_{U}|\hat{y}^{\perp}|^{2}

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{\varphi(|y|/s)}{s^{m}}-\frac{\varphi(|y|/r)}{r^{m}}+\int_{r}^{s}\frac{m\varphi(|y|/\rho)}{\rho^{m+1}}\,d\rho\bigg]d\mu_{V}

(A.6)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}y\cdot\nu\int_{r}^{s}\varphi\bigg(\frac{|y|}{\rho}\bigg)\frac{1}{\rho^{m+1}}\,d\rho\,d|\delta V|+\int_{U}\bigg[\frac{\varphi(|y|/s)}{s^{m}}-\frac{\varphi(|y|/r)}{r^{m}}\bigg]\,d\mu_{V}\,.

We now let
\varphi\to\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}
and apply the dominated convergence theorem to (
A.6
) to conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{U}|\hat{y}^{\perp}|^{2}

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{s}}(y)}{s^{m}}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{r}}(y)}{r^{m}}+\int_{r}^{s}\frac{m\mathbf{1}_{B_{\rho}}(y)}{\rho^{m+1}}\,d\rho\bigg]d\mu_{V}

(A.7)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}y\cdot\nu\int_{r}^{s}\mathbf{1}_{B_{\rho}}(y)\frac{1}{\rho^{m+1}}\,d\rho\,d|\delta V|+\int_{U}\bigg[\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{s}}(y)}{s^{m}}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{r}}(y)}{r^{m}}\bigg]\,d\mu_{V}\,.

To simplify both sides of (
A.7
), we use the equivalence

\mathbf{1}_{(r,s)}(\rho)\mathbf{1}_{(|y|,\infty)}(\rho)=\mathbf{1}_{(r,s)\cap(|y|,\infty)}(\rho)=\mathbf{1}_{B_{s}}(y)\mathbf{1}_{(\max\{r,|y|\},s)}(\rho)

to compute

\displaystyle\int_{r}^{s}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{\rho}}(y)}{\rho^{m+1}}\,d\rho=\int\frac{\mathbf{1}_{(r,s)}(\rho)\mathbf{1}_{(|y|,\infty)}(\rho)}{\rho^{m+1}}\,d\rho

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{s}}(y)\mathbf{1}_{(\max\{r,|y|\},s)}(\rho)}{\rho^{m+1}}\,d\rho

(A.8)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{s}}(y)}{ms^{m}}+\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{s}}(y)}{m\max\{r,|y|\}^{m}}\,.

If we substitute (
A.8
) into (
A.7
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{U}

\displaystyle|\hat{y}^{\perp}|^{2}\bigg[\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{s}}(y)}{\max\{r,|y|\}^{m}}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{r}}(y)}{r^{m}}\bigg]d\mu_{V}

\displaystyle=\int_{U}y\cdot\nu\bigg[-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{s}}(y)}{ms^{m}}+\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{s}}(y)}{m\max\{r,|y|\}^{m}}\bigg]d|\delta V|+\int_{U}\bigg[\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{s}}(y)}{s^{m}}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{B_{r}}(y)}{r^{m}}\bigg]\,d\mu_{V}

(A.9)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{s}(0)}\frac{y\cdot\nu}{m}\bigg[-\frac{1}{s^{m}}+\frac{1}{\max\{r,|y|\}^{m}}\bigg]d|\delta V|+\frac{\mu_{V}(B_{s})}{s^{m}}-\frac{\mu_{V}(B_{r})}{r^{m}}\,.

Since the integrand on the left hand side simplifies to
\mathbf{1}_{B_{s}\setminus B_{r}}(y)|\hat{y}^{\perp}|^{2}/|y|^{m}
, we have proved (
A.2
).
∎
