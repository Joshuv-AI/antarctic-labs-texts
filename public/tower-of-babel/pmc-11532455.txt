Watchful waiting in laryngo‐tracheobronchial amyloid: A case report
Authors: Dylan Beinart, Siaw Ming Chai, Olivia Walsh, Sanjay Ramakrishnan, John Blakey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the long term follow up of a now 66‐year‐old male with laryngo‐tracheobronchial amyloidosis. The patient initially presented with dysphonia and dyspnoea. He was diagnosed with laryngeal reflux and asthma. Seven years later, the patient underwent cross sectional thoracic imaging showing diffuse thickening of the distal trachea and proximal main bronchi. The patient's FEV1 and FVC remained stable over 13 years and no intervention was required.Keywords: amyloid, tracheobronchial amyloid

INTRODUCTION

Amyloidosis is a heterogenous condition defined by extracellular deposition of insoluble, organized fibrils made up of low molecular weight subunits of various proteins. 1 Systemic amyloidosis with multiorgan involvement has a poor prognosis however cases of localized amyloidosis limited to single anatomical locations or organ systems have a variable natural history. Respiratory amyloidosis can be challenging to diagnose as it is a rare entity with non‐specific signs and symptoms that mimic other common respiratory conditions. 2 Tracheobronchial amyloid typically presents with cough, dyspnoea, dysphonia and wheeze and is often misdiagnosed as asthma or COPD.

Diagnosis of tracheobronchial amyloid is made by histopathological examination of endo‐tracheal and endobronchial biopsies. 3 Patients require ongoing monitoring with CT imaging or spirometry as tracheobronchial amyloid can cause progressive airflow obstruction and respiratory failure over years to decades. 1 Whilst there is no cure, bronchoscopic intervention remains the primary management strategy and patients with proximal tracheal involvement often require multiple interventions. 1

CASE REPORT

We report the long term follow up of a now 66‐year‐old male with laryngo‐tracheobronchial amyloid. The patient was initially referred to the respiratory clinic at age 53 with dysphonia and cough and was labelled with exercise and cold induced asthma. He was treated for gastro‐oesophageal reflux and obstructive sleep apnoea. One year prior, the patient had an episode of mild haemoptysis whilst living in Malaysia. He underwent CT imaging and bronchoscopy with findings of reflux laryngitis and lower tracheal thickening. We were unable to obtain the endotracheal biopsy reports. Written information from treating physicians reported benign inflammation and mild fibrosis.

The patient was referred for assessment of dysphonia. Fibreoptic laryngoscopy identified a polyp in the left mid vocal cord and he was again diagnosed with reflux laryngitis. After a period of therapy with proton pump inhibitors, a gastroscopy was performed and did not show any evidence of reflux.

After 2 years of persistent symptoms, the patient underwent microlaryngoscopy and excision of vocal cord polyps. Histopathology of operative specimens showed widespread deposition of waxy eosinophilic material in the subepithelial stroma with a few intervening ecstatic vessels and chronic inflammatory cells. The material stained ‘salmon pink’ on Congo red and showed patchy “apple green birefringence” on polarized light microscopy characteristic of amyloid (see Figure 1). Serum electrophoresis, urine electrophoresis, fat pad biopsy and bone marrow biopsy excluded systemic amyloidosis.

Histopathological specimens from a resected laryngeal polyp. (A) shows widespread deposition of waxy eosinophilic material in the subepithelial stroma. (B) patchy apple green birefringence at lower power. (C) apple green birefringence at high power.

The patient was then followed up for his asthma for a further 5 years in the respiratory clinic with serial spirometry. He underwent CT imaging which showed diffuse thickening of the distal trachea and proximal main bronchi consistent with tracheobronchial amyloidosis (see Figure 2). His dysphonia, respiratory symptoms and exercise tolerance have remained stable. Serial axial CT images show stable thickening of the main bronchi over 3 years (see Figure 2). Spirometry was also stable with FEV1 and FVC measuring 2.51 L and 3.58 L, respectively, at presentation and then 2.35 L and 3.33 L 10 years later. The patient also had normal resistance and reactance on forced oscillation technique.

(A) Coronal CT scan view with arrows showing the extent of airway thickening along the left main bronchus and bronchus intermedius. Focused axial view from initial CT scan (B) showing circumferential, irregular thickening of the main bronchi that was stable at 2 year (C) and 3 year (D) intervals.

DISCUSSION

This case highlights several useful points in the management of this rare and heterogenous condition. Patients with tracheobronchial amyloidosis often have substantial delays in diagnosis with years of unnecessary and ineffective treatment for airways disease. This patient who initially presented with dysphonia and cough was diagnosed with gastro‐oesophageal reflux and asthma by specialist clinicians. There was a two‐year delay to micro‐laryngoscopy and even with the diagnosis of laryngeal amyloidosis, a further five‐year delay to cross sectional thoracic imaging. This highlights the importance of considering rarer causes of wheeze and challenging historical clinical diagnoses when patient's do not behave or respond to treatment as expected. This case also demonstrates that clinically stable tracheobronchial amyloid can be monitored with serial spirometry. A published series of 15 patients with proximal or mid‐bronchial disease reported progression to respiratory failure in seven patients, all at approximately 9 years of follow up. 4 With monitoring, intervention can be delayed or in this case, completely avoided.
