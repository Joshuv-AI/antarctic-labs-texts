Electroencephalography Changes in Emotional Recognition: A Signal Processing Approach Using Wavelet Features
Authors: Beulah Snowin, Philo Hazeena, Lakshmi Narasimhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Backgroundmotion recognition using electroencephalography (EEG) has gained significant attention in the fields of affective computing and human–computer interaction due to its ability to capture real-time neural responses. EEG signal variations associated with emotional states can be analysed using wavelet-based signal processing within the arousal–valence framework.PurposeThis study aims to analyse EEG signal variations associated with emotional states using a wavelet-based signal processing approach within the arousal–valence framework.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted on 48 healthy participants. Emotional states were induced using image, video and music stimuli categorised under the arousal–valence model. EEG signals were recorded using the standard 10–20 electrode placement system. The acquired signals were pre-processed and analysed using discrete wavelet transform (DWT) to extract time–frequency features, including energy and entropy. Statistical analysis was performed to evaluate hemispheric differences and gender-based variations.ResultsDifferences in EEG activity were observed between the left and right hemispheres across multiple emotional states (p < .05). Video and music stimuli produced stronger and more distinguishable EEG responses compared to image-based stimuli. Negative emotions demonstrated higher discriminative patterns than positive emotions. Gender-based variations were also observed in EEG responses.ConclusionWavelet-based EEG feature analysis provides meaningful and discriminative information for emotion recognition and may provide a robust feature set for future machine learning-based emotion recognition systems. Further studies incorporating classification algorithms and larger data sets are required to validate their predictive performance.

Introduction

Background

While all animals exhibit certain behaviours, only a subset experiences what we define as emotions. Emotions are instinctive states arising from complex cognitive functions. Despite their importance in daily life, our scientific understanding of human emotions is still limited. 1 Emotions are complex states involving physiological, psychological and behavioural responses to external stimuli. The basic emotions—happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise and disgust—play a crucial role in daily life, making accurate recognition essential in various fields.

Emotions are regulated by the limbic system, which includes the amygdala, hippocampus, thalamus and hypothalamus. When triggered, these brain structures activate and initiate an emotional response. Electroencephalography (EEG)-based emotion recognition has potential applications in human–computer interaction (HCI), affective computing and intelligent systems.1, 2

Electroencephalography Signal

With emotions crucial in daily life, the demand for automatic emotion recognition has increased alongside human–computer interface growth. Recent research has increasingly focused on using EEG for detecting emotions.2, 3 We aim to enhance EEG-based emotion recognition by pinpointing key brain regions and frequency bands associated with emotions. This research promises to reflect societal benefits and practical applications.

Originally used in medicine, EEG is now being explored for emotion recognition due to advances in digital media. 2 This article explores recognising ‘inner’ emotions from EEG signals, which are spontaneous and less camouflaged, as emotional changes produce detectable variations despite controlled expressions. Incorporating EEG-based emotion recognition into intelligent systems can support real-time assessment of emotional states, enabling advancements in HCI and affective computing applications.

We use the arousal–valence model 4 (Figure 1) to map emotions onto a coordinate system, detecting brain activity patterns through EEG. This provides valuable insights into the link between brain activity and emotions.

Methods

Study Design and Data Acquisition

This study was conducted as a prospective observational study at the EEG laboratory, Department of Neurology, SRIHER, Chennai, between April 2024 and July 2024. A total of 48 healthy participants (29 males and 19 females), aged 18 years or older, were included.

EEG signals were recorded using the standard 10–20 international electrode placement system and a 24-channel digital EEG acquisition system (Nihon Kohden, India), ensuring uniform spatial coverage of cortical activity. 5 All participants were screened to exclude neurological, psychiatric or systemic conditions that could influence EEG recordings.

Statistical and Comparative Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Paired-sample t-tests were used to compare EEG feature values between the left and right hemispheres, while independent-sample t-tests were performed to evaluate gender-based differences in EEG features. All statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Statistical significance was determined using two-tailed tests with a significance level of p < .05 (Tables 1–4).

Analytical Framework for Emotion Recognition

The overall analytical workflow of the study can be summarised as:

EEG signal acquisition ↓ pre-processing ↓ wavelet feature extraction ↓ statistical analysis ↓ emotion feature analysis (arousal–valence model)

This framework highlights the potential of EEG signals as a feature-driven input for automated emotion recognition systems.

Results

The descriptive statistical analysis demonstrated notable gender-based differences in EEG activity across emotional states. Female participants exhibited comparatively higher mean EEG values across both hemispheres for emotions such as delight, anger and sadness, indicating enhanced emotional responsiveness. Male participants showed relatively lower variability, suggesting more stable neural responses. Statistically significant differences (p < .05) were observed for most emotional categories, except for contentment, where no significant variation was noted. These findings indicate that emotional processing may differ between genders, with female participants exhibiting higher mean EEG feature values in this sample.

The observed findings suggest possible gender-related differences that require confirmation in larger balanced cohorts.

Summary of Key Findings

The analysis of EEG signals demonstrated consistently higher mean values in female participants across all emotional stimuli, including video, image and music. The differences were particularly pronounced in high-arousal emotions such as anger and delight. Male participants exhibited comparatively lower and more stable EEG responses. These findings suggest enhanced emotional reactivity and neural activation in females, especially in frontal and temporal regions.

Discussion

In the present study, EEG signal variations were analysed to understand emotional recognition across different stimuli. EEG-based emotion recognition has been widely explored as a reliable method for assessing neural responses associated with emotional states.1, 9 The findings of this study demonstrate that video and music stimuli produced stronger EEG responses compared with image-based stimuli. This aligns with previous research in affective computing and HCI, where dynamic stimuli have been shown to elicit higher emotional engagement.2, 10

Additionally, significant gender differences were observed, with female participants showing higher EEG activity across multiple emotional states. This may be attributed to differences in neural processing and emotional sensitivity between genders.

From a neurophysiological perspective, emotional processing involves complex interactions between cortical and subcortical brain regions, including mechanisms related to emotional appraisal and response generation. 11 The proposed modified arousal–valence model further enhances the understanding of emotional mapping by incorporating stimulus-specific and gender-based EEG variations. This provides a more comprehensive framework for emotion recognition using EEG signals. The findings of this study have potential applications in brain–computer interfaces (BCI), affective computing and intelligent systems, where real-time emotion recognition can improve human–machine interaction.

Electroencephalography as a Reliable Signal for Emotion Recognition

The findings indicate that variations in EEG activity across hemispheres are strongly associated with emotional states. Consistent differences observed between the left and right hemispheres confirm that EEG signals can effectively capture neural correlates of emotional processing.

From a signal processing perspective, the extracted wavelet features successfully represented the time–frequency characteristics of EEG signals, which are essential for analysing non-stationary brain activity. This highlights the suitability of wavelet transform-based methods for EEG-driven affective computing applications.

Hemispheric Asymmetry and Feature Discrimination

The observed dominance of the left hemisphere for positive emotions and the right hemisphere for negative emotions aligns with established neurophysiological theories of emotional lateralisation. More importantly, from an engineering standpoint, this asymmetry provides a useful feature space for classification, where lateralised EEG activity can serve as a key input variable for emotion detection algorithms.

The strong statistical significance across multiple emotional states suggests that these features have high discriminative potential, which is critical for designing robust classification models.

Effectiveness of Stimulus Modalities

Among the different stimulus types, video- and music-based stimuli elicited stronger and more consistent EEG responses compared to image-based stimuli. This indicates that dynamic and multimodal stimuli enhance emotional activation, resulting in more distinguishable EEG patterns.

This observation has direct implications for system design, suggesting that emotion recognition systems incorporating multimodal inputs may achieve higher accuracy compared with those relying solely on static visual stimuli.

Gender-based Variations in Electroencephalography Signals

The study identified statistically significant differences in EEG responses between male and female participants for most emotional states. These findings suggest that individual variability, including gender differences, plays a role in emotional processing.

From a computational perspective, this highlights the importance of incorporating personalised or adaptive models in EEG-based emotion recognition systems to improve discriminative feature patterns and generalisability.

Implications for Affective Computing and Brain–Computer Interface Systems

The results of this study contribute to the growing field of affective computing, where physiological signals are used to detect and interpret human emotions. Comparison with Existing Studies

The findings of this study are consistent with previous research demonstrating the effectiveness of EEG signals in emotion recognition and the presence of hemispheric asymmetry. Additionally, the use of wavelet-based feature extraction aligns with current trends in EEG signal processing, where time–frequency analysis plays a crucial role in improving feature representation.

Unlike studies that rely heavily on complex machine learning models, this study emphasises the importance of feature-level analysis, demonstrating that even statistical approaches can reveal meaningful patterns in EEG data. This provides a computationally efficient foundation for future model development.

The present study focused on identifying and statistically evaluating wavelet-derived EEG features associated with emotion-evoked neural activity rather than developing or validating a machine learning classifier. Nevertheless, the observed feature patterns demonstrate their potential utility as informative inputs for future automated emotion recognition systems. Future studies should investigate the performance of these features using supervised classification algorithms and appropriate validation strategies.

Limitations and Future Scope

A limitation of the present study is that it was restricted to feature extraction and statistical analysis without implementing or validating a machine learning classification model. Consequently, the discriminative capability of the extracted EEG features was inferred from statistical comparisons rather than discriminative feature patterns. The present study employed basic statistical methods to explore differences in EEG features across emotional conditions. Effect size measures and corrections for multiple comparisons were not calculated; therefore, the statistical findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require confirmation in larger studies using more comprehensive statistical analyses.

Conclusion

The preliminary evidence suggests gender differences in EEG responses to emotional stimuli, with statistically significant differences observed between groups; however, the magnitude of these differences was not quantified using effect size measures and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. These findings contribute to the understanding of emotional processing and may have implications in neuroscience research, mental health assessment and HCI systems.
