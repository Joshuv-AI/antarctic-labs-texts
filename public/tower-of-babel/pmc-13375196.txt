A Systematic Review of Nurse–Patient Communication Status in Palliative Care and its Affecting Factors
Authors: Naiire Salmani, Imane Bagheri, Zahra Rezaei, Mohammad Hasanzadeh Naeini, Atena Dadgari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Investigating the status of nurse–patient communication in Palliative Care (PC) and understanding the factors affecting it helps to provide effective patient-centered care and optimize care strategies in PC settings. Therefore, this review was conducted with the aim of determining the status of nurse–patient communication in PC and factors affecting it.Materials and Methods:This systematic search was conducted in both Iranian and international databases, including Scientific Information Database (SID), Magiran, Scopus, Web of Science (WOS), Public Medical Database (PubMed), and ProQuest, using keywords: “Palliative Care” AND “Communication” AND “Nurses” to find related articles published until 2024. Inclusion criteria for the articles included original studies whose results focus on nurse–patient communication in PC.Results:Nurses do not communicate comfortably and effectively with patients in the PC setting, especially in conversations related to advanced care plans, end-of-life care, and prognosis. This communication is influenced by factors such as trust issue between patient and nurse, individual preparation of nurse and patient, effective collaboration between nurses and physicians, the nurse’s skill in being a good listener and establishing empathetic interaction with patient, and allocation of sufficient time for nurse–patient communication. The use of structured communication models, such as Sage and Thyme, Comfort, can help nurses to be a more effective communicator.Conclusions:The results of present study showed that nurses do not have effective conversations with patients in the context of PC. Therefore, in order to improve nurses’ communication skills, training programs are suggested to be designed and implemented in accordance with the identified needs of nurses in relation to difficult conversations with patients.

Introduction

Palliative care (PC), as one of the important components of patient-centered care, has become an effective intervention in the treatment of incurable diseases, with the aim of reducing suffering caused by the disease and improving the quality of life.[1] The World Health Organization (WHO) defines PC as “an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families who face problems associated with life-threatening illness, while preventing and relieving suffering through early detection, appropriate assessment, and treatment of pain and other problems, whether physical, psychosocial or spiritual.”[2] Patients suffering from a variety of conditions, such as cancer, cardiovascular disease, acute organ failure, severe burns, end-stage chronic illness, severe trauma, and many other acute or chronic illnesses, may all benefit from PC to manage symptoms, live comfortably, and a have higher quality of life.[34] According to the WHO, 40 million people worldwide need PC as part of their treatment plan, of whom about 34% are cancer patients, while the majority of them live in low- and middle-income countries.[5]

Nowadays, the need for PC has increased globally due to various factors, including the rising incidence of multiple diseases, the growing number of patients living independently, and the widespread acceptance of early referral to PC.[6] In the meantime, nurses play a fundamental role in providing PC aimed at enhancing the quality of life of patients with advanced and terminal diseases.[7] In fact, nurses play an active role in providing clinical PC and are often responsible for managing the entire process of diagnosis, treatment, and addressing the physical, psychological, spiritual, cultural, and religious needs of the end-of-life patients.[6] Furthermore, the communication skills of nurses can help these patients in resolving their issues and integrating their disease into daily life by expressing more information that addresses their concerns.[8] Effective communication in PC is a dialogue process involving patients, their relatives, and healthcare professionals where both informative and empathetic aspects are exchanged. The informational exchange includes sharing details about prognosis, treatment, and care by the healthcare professional and the sharing of knowledge and experiences by the patient or relatives. The exchange of empathetic aspects is related to the maintenance or enhancement of healthcare professional–patient relationship and the addressing of emotions by the healthcare professional and the patient or relative.[9] A study by Brito et al. conducted on the nurses’ perceptions of communication and communication strategies with terminally ill patients at the end of life confirmed that verbal communication is used and valued by nurses as a tool to promote understanding of care. It is also used as a method of providing support, security, trust, strength, and hope. Nonverbal communication is also recognized by professionals through emotional touch in order to improve the quality of life of patients.[10]

Engel et al. in a systematic review stated that providing open and honest information, having empathy, using clear and understandable language, creating an environment for positive coping strategies, having commitment and responsibility, and recognizing relatives as caregivers contribute to effective communication from the perspective of patients and relatives.[9] In contrast, ineffective communication between nurses and patients and lack of psychosocial support may lead to increased patient anxiety, decreased trust in nurses, decreased patient sense of control, and nonadherence to treatment.[11] In the meantime, identifying factors affecting the nurse–patient communication in the PC setting can help to improve patient’s understanding, maintain respect, promoting adherence to care plan, ensure the quality of care at the end of life, reduce anxiety, and ultimately improve the quality of life of patients and their families.[1213] Therefore, future studies are needed to identify effective factors that can positively influence nurse–patient communication.[14] It is evident that we must recognize and further investigate the significance of nurse–patient communication in PC setting, particularly given the existing knowledge gap in the literature. Therefore, this review was conducted with the aim of determining the status of nurse–patient communication in PC and factors affecting it.

Materials and Methods

This study is a systematic review that was conducted after receiving approval from the ethics committee at Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences, Iran, in 2024. Two authors (IB, NS) searched Public Medical Database (PubMed), Scopous, Institute for Scientific Information (ISI), Proquest, Scientific Information Database (SID), and Magiran to find related articles published until September 2024. We set the terms (“Palliative Care”, Communication, and Nurses) for the MeSH terms and free text search with English and Persian language restrictions. The spatial limitations were not taken into account in the review of the studies. The search strategy is described in Table 1. References to relevant studies were also checked to identify potential literature to include in the review. If there was a conflict between search results, senior authors were consulted to make the final decision.

The search strategy

We included studies that focused on nurse–patient communication in the PC setting and were published as original articles in English or Persian journals. Only full-text articles were selected. Also, studies that addressed the concept of interprofessional communication or communication in other settings, including emergency medicine, critical care, neonatal care, pediatric care, obstetrics, and gynecology, were excluded from the review. Additionally, articles that addressed the relationship between a nursing student and a patient or the relationship between a nurse and patient’s family or addressed interprofessional communication in PC setting, review articles, letters to the editor, case report articles, instrument design and psychometrics, and initial report articles were excluded from the review. It should be noted that after the initial search, due to the large number of articles, articles that had been published in 2014 and earlier were excluded, so the review was carried out on articles that had been published between 2015 and 2024.

Two authors, IM and NS, independently extracted data from the included literature. Additional information was also extracted, including the name of first author, the country where the study was conducted, samples, inclusion/exclusion criteria, major finding 1, and other characteristics of the study. To assess the quality of the included studies [Tables 2-5], various tools were employed based on study design: the CASP tool (10 items) for qualitative studies, the MIXED METHODS APPRAISAL TOOL (MMAT) VERSION 2018 (5 items) for mixed-methods articles, the JBI tool (9 items) for descriptive articles, and the Minor tool (12 items) for nonrandomized clinical trials. The quality evaluations were conducted independently by two authors, ZR and MH, and in the event of any disagreement, a third author, AD, made the final decision. All 12 articles scored more than 50% on the quality assessment checklist in the quality review stage, and based on the agreement of research team, their results were included in the final review.

Status of nurse–patient communication in the palliative care setting

The results indicated that two studies examined and reported on nurses’ communication skills. In a study by Burgunder-Zdravkovski et al., the focus was on nurses’ ability and comfort in discussing advanced care plans and reporting codes. The findings revealed that between 55.6% and 66.7% of nurses expressed that they felt comfortable in having these types of conversations with patients.[22] In another study by Hjelmfors et al., it was found that 62% of nurses did not discuss prognosis with patients and 90% did not discuss end-of-life care. In this study, nurses from Netherlands and Sweden were examined, and in both countries, the issues least often discussed were end-of-life care, sexual activity, and prognosis. Specifically, discussions about prognosis were reported in 41% of cases involving Dutch patients and 34% involving Swedish patients.[16]

Factors affecting nurse–patient communication in the palliative care setting

Building, strengthening, and maintaining trust

In communication, the primary prerequisite for achieving the intended goals is to establish trust because a communication based on trust creates a sense of security and comfort in patients. Therefore, it is essential for nurses to foster trust at the very beginning and talk briefly about the necessity of their presence and communication. Of course, the cultural and linguistic background of the nurse and the patient can also play a role in this regard. When the nurse and the patient have a common cultural and linguistic background, building trust is facilitated. Since communication and trust are symbiotically related and one cannot exist without the other, it is necessary for nurses to learn about patients, their characteristics, and everything that is important to them and prioritizes them. This builds and strengthens the trust. Of course, in order to better strengthen the trust, nurses can provide some information to patients and in fact engage in a kind of information exchange. Finally, to maintain the trust that has been established, nurses must remember that trust is fragile. Even seemingly minor issues—such as failing to answer a patient’s call—can potentially damage it. In such a situation, the nurse must try to improve the damaged trust by explaining the reasons and apologizing for it.[15]

Individual factors related to the nurse

The findings indicated that a nurse’s place of work, age, and work experience can influence nurse–patient communication. For instance, rural nurses tend to feel more comfortable than their urban counterparts when communicating with patients and discussing end-of-life and PC issues. Additionally, in terms of age and work experience, older and more experienced nurses report greater confidence than younger, less experienced nurses in engaging in conversations about end-of-life care. On the other hand, urban nurses’ access to PC teams may lead them to rely more on specialized support during interactions with patients related to end-of-life and PC.[21]

Assessing patient’s readiness

Being ready to communicate is a crucial factor, and nurses should first assess the patient’s preparedness for such conversations. Uncertainty regarding the nurses’ lack of knowledge about their patient’s prognosis can be an anxiety-provoking problem for them. Therefore, the nurse should gather information about the patient and evaluate how much information the patient already knows about their disease and its progression.[17] When patients ask about this issue, their questions can indicate their readiness to initiate communication.[16] Importantly, the patient should be calm and not anxious because in this situation, the nurse cannot easily discuss topics related to PC.[16]

Collaboration of nurses with physicians

In the PC setting, effective nurse–patient communication requires collaboration and interaction between nurses and physicians. Nurses should not be passive listeners or observers; instead, they must actively participate in conversations with the patient. By obtaining comprehensive explanations about the treatment process and progress from the physicians, nurses can acquire essential information; relying solely on patients’ medical records is insufficient for understanding complex medical details; therefore, it is crucial for nurses to receive direct explanations from physicians regarding the disease and its treatment.[17]

Intimate listener and empathetic interaction

Being an active listener and engaging in empathetic interaction are essential features of effective nurse–patient communication. In the PC settings, patients need to feel a sense of security and the nurse should consider the patient as a unique individual with specific needs, interests, and experiences. Therefore, maintaining empathy and genuine interest during interactions is crucial. Nurses should also strive to validate the patient’s experiences so that their communication is compassionate and attentive. In fact, the nurse should listen honestly to the patient’s words in order to be able to understand what the patient wants, establish a meaningful connection with him/her, and understand him/her deeply. In fact, the most important issue that should not be forgotten in communication is that the patient is a human being with dignity because if the patient is seen as an object or a number, the patient’s concerns, difficulties, and condition are ignored.[18]

Readiness for difficult conversations

Another factor related to nurses is the courage to maintain and continue the communication because in PC, nurses are often forced to ask uncomfortable questions from patients. In these situations, the intuitive knowledge of nurses can help them to balance courage and caution during communication, the knowledge which is acquired over time through experiences. It is important to note that sometimes patients talk about issues during communication that cannot be resolved by nurses. In these cases, nurses must remain silent and do not respond as having clinical experiences and a sense of confidence can make this part of the conversation easier for them.[19] Nurses should be aware that in the context of PC, their communication with patients may sometimes create a sense of uncertainty because sometimes, in the intimate and confidential space that they have with patients, nurses listen to conversations about various issues, including spiritual and existential, death and life, faith and doubt, deep personal thoughts, thoughts about the future, dreams and hopes, and imminent death, that they are not ready to hear and the patient has not discussed these issues with anyone until then. When patients discuss such issues with nurses, it is as if they have brought a trust or gift to the nurses, opening a door to them to have an intimate conversation with patients.[19]

Allocation of sufficient and appropriate time

Nurses need to allocate sufficient time for communicating with patients to effectively understand their psychological, emotional, social, and physical needs. Providing adequate time allows nurses to gain a deeper understanding of patients, enabling them to deliver personalized care.[18]

Use of structured models and programs

The findings showed that in some studies, structured models and programs had been used to improve the nurses’ communication with patients, including:

Sage and Thyme modelThis model is a strong, structured, and accessible approach to communication, designed to facilitate effective interactions with patients receiving PC. It enables nurses to identify and follow up on patients’ primary concerns through active, empathetic listening.[20] In this model, the “Sage” section emphasizes the importance of respecting patient privacy, asking about their concerns, listing all their issues, and demonstrating empathy by being sensitive to their feelings. The “Thyme” section encourages nurses to ask patients questions such as: “Is there anyone you can talk to about your concerns? Does this conversation help? Is there anything you believe could assist you? Are there any issues you would like the nurse to help with in this regard?” At the end of the discussion, nurses should summarize the patient’s concerns to ensure understanding and clarity.[20] In the study of Griffiths et al., this model was introduced to nurses during a 3-h workshop, where they received the necessary training through role-playing and group activities. Afterward, nurses were encouraged to implement the model in real clinical settings. To reinforce their understanding, reminder cards were provided for nurses to carry during patient visits, allowing them to refer to the components of each stage of the model during interactions. Ultimately, the use of this model fostered a sense of security among nurses and boosted their self-confidence. It also helped prevent premature consultations, empowered patients to find their own solutions, and saved time by offering a clear, structured approach.[20] Nurses noted that the Sage and Thyme model is a flexible model for communicating with patients in different languages, and nurses can adapt it according to their preferred language. Nurses also introduced this model as a useful tool for interacting with dying patients as it can identify aspects of dying process that are worrisome to patients and suggest actions that help to reduce patient’s worry. Ultimately, using this model helps nurses to have a more comfortable ending to their conversations with patients. However, there were also challenges associated with the use of this model. These included environmental factors, such as uncontrollable noise, that could interfere with the process. Discussion

This study reviewed the current state of nurses’ communication with patients in the PC setting. The findings indicated that the existing literature on this topic can be classified into two categories: the status of nurse–patient communication and the factors influencing this communication in a PC context. The discussion is organized based on this classification.

The results revealed that nurses often feel uncomfortable communicating with patients in the PC setting, particularly when discussing advanced care plans, end-of-life issues, and prognosis. In accordance with this finding, Belinda et al. (2023) emphasized the importance of strengthening nurse–patient relationships through patient-centered communication techniques, especially during the final stages of life.[12] Similarly, Momen et al. observed that under acute conditions, conversations about death and prognosis are often uncomfortable and are sometimes referred to as “the elephant on the table”. The phrase “elephant on the table” refers to an issue that is obvious and significant but is often ignored or avoided by those involved in the conversation. In the context of End-Of-life Care, this metaphor highlights the reluctance of patients and healthcare professionals to confront the reality of patient’s condition and its implications for future care. This issue can arise for a number of reasons, including fear of patients not wanting to participate in such discussions, and the possibility of causing anxiety for the patient. The focus of healthcare team may also be on the therapeutic management of symptoms and dismiss psychological and end-of-life aspects. Other contributing factors include a lack of resources and support for open discussions, communication challenges, and insufficient training in end-of-life care communication.[27]

Factors affecting nurse–patient communication in the context of palliative care

Building, strengthening, and maintaining trust

The results indicated that trust is a crucial factor influencing the development of nurse–patient communication. Building, strengthening, and maintaining trust by nurses are essential elements in fostering effective communication between patients and healthcare providers. In this regard, a study that examined the experiences of patients with PC needs by Stenman et al. showed that trust is considered a key element in communication between patients and nurses, and patients need trust and a sense of security to express their deep thoughts and feelings.[28] This study also showed that formation of trust leads to the creation of a safe space for patients to express their true selves without fear of judgment,[28] patients’ sense of comfort in expressing emotions and concerns,[8] a dignified and pain-free death,[29] and a more effective response of nurses to the patients’ needs.[30] Another study on the importance of this issue has pointed out that trust in nurses and the PC team allows patients to feel more secure and comfortable in expressing their feelings and concerns. This trust also helps nurses to respond more effectively to patients’ needs and provide the necessary care.[30] Murahashi et al. examined factors affecting the cancer patients’ trust in PC physicians and found that trust is a key factor in achieving a dignified death. They also stated that seven factors can facilitate trust building, which include a caring attitude, symptom management, providing polite and specific explanations, long-term participation in care, facing the certainty of death, a positive image of medical institution, and referral by a trusted physician.[29]

The results indicated that individual factors related to nurses—such as their place of work, age, and work experience—can affect the nurse–patient communication. A review of literature regarding the effect of age on the nurse–patient communication showed different results as Bishaw et al. stated that increasing age can lead to experience and clinical experience improves the nurse–patient communication.[31] Similarly, Pérez-Rugosa et al. reported that higher age can be associated with increased empathy levels.[13] However, it is worth noting that in some cases, age can affect communication preferences as elderly patients may respond better to elderly nurses due to shared perceptions and experiences.[32] Conversely, younger nurses might employ more modern communication techniques, which could impact their interactions with older patients.[33] However, sometimes focusing on age may hinder effective communication.[32] Regarding the nurses’ place of work, the findings indicated that rural nurses feel more comfortable than urban nurses in establishing conversations related to end-of-life and PC. In fact, the performance of nurses can be influenced by their cultural context, especially when nurses want to discuss sensitive topics.[34]

The results highlighted that assessing the patient’s readiness to communicate is a critical factor and the nurses should prioritize evaluating this aspect before engaging in meaningful conversation. Consistent with this finding, studies have emphasized that the patient’s readiness to initiate communication with nurses and the professional PC team is very important because it strengthens relationships and ensures patient-centered care. This readiness enables patients to express their needs and preferences and improves their overall care experience.[313536] Of course, the patient’s readiness to communicate with the nurse in PC is influenced by a number of key factors, including a sense of trust and security to freely express their thoughts and feelings, provision of an appropriate environment for open communication,[28] and the nurse’s active listening skills that create conditions for spontaneous and confidential conversations.[19]

Based on the findings of the present study, nurses need to cooperate and interact with the physicians in order to communicate effectively with patients in the PC setting. In line with this finding, Schulman-Green et al. introduced physician–nurse communication as one of the barriers to effective communication in the PC setting.[37] Similarly, Nilsson et al., in a qualitative study exploring nurses’ experiences of end-of-life communication, noted that physicians tend to communicate directly with patients alone. They emphasized that increased cooperation between physicians and nurses to facilitate information flow could significantly improve communication with patients. Since nurses and physicians are integral components of the patient care process, a lack of effective collaboration can hinder nurses from engaging in deeper, more meaningful communication with patients, sometimes leading to misunderstandings. Therefore, it is essential for nurses to consult with physicians, respond to patients’ questions appropriately, and clarify any uncertainties patients may have.[38]

This study also demonstrated that a key characteristic of effective nurse–patient communication is the ability of nurses to be active listeners and to engage in empathetic interactions with patients. When such effective and empathetic communication is established, it can significantly facilitate decision-making and planning for end-of-life care.[39] Supporting this, Vitale et al. in their study that examined the communication characteristics of oncology nurses concluded that nurses should actively attend to patients’ concerns, and to foster meaningful nurse–patient communication, they need empathy, creativity, and artistry.[40] The results of the present study confirm the findings of a systematic review by Engel et al. that examined the perspectives of patients and their relatives regarding effective communication in the context of PC. They showed that most patients and relatives need empathetic communication, and effective communication requires healthcare professionals to have appropriate communication and empathy skills, while empathy is reflected in the behavior and attitudes of healthcare professionals.[9] Additionally, Regina Allande-Cussó et al. analyzed the concept of patient–nurse communication and identified the key communication characteristics as active listening, empathy, and respect.[41]

The results indicated that nurses’ preparedness for difficult conversations is another important and influential factor in communicating with patients in the context of PC. This preparation can include the presence of courage, intuitive knowledge, timely silence, and acceptance of patients’ confidential conversations. In line with this finding, Gillman et al. also argued that nurses’ exposure to situations and events that evoke strong emotions is inevitable and one’s ability to manage strong emotions in difficult situations is influenced by personal, environmental, and contextual factors.[42] According to Keall et al., experience and knowledge affect the nurses’ courage to ask questions related to ontology.[43] Additionally, Joulaei et al. emphasized the importance of nurses being well prepared and skilled in delivering difficult news.[44]

Another factor influencing nurse–patient communication in the PC setting is the allocation of sufficient and appropriate time for patient interactions. In this regard, a qualitative study by Walker et al., which explored how nurses communicate with cancer patients at the end of life, demonstrated that dedicating adequate time allows nurses to conduct comprehensive assessments through active listening and gathering vital information.[12] Similarly, English et al. examined the perceptions of patients receiving PC and their family’s regarding “communication” and stated that allocating sufficient time and comprehensive assessments in end-of-life care is an important factor that should be considered.[45] The study by Bramley and Matiti further highlighted that taking time to listen fosters a sense of compassion. Patients expect nurses to allocate time for them, and in busy clinical environments, nurses who do so are perceived as more compassionate, despite workload pressures.[46] Importantly, in order to talk to patients, it is necessary to maintain the patients’ sense of autonomy, so nurses should choose an appropriate time to communicate to patients and make them feel comfortable at that time; that is why most nurses avoid planning evening and night conversations with patients.[31]

The findings of the present study indicated that using structured models and programs is a factor in improving nurses’ communication with patients in the PC setting. One of the models used in the reviewed studies is the SAGE and THYME model. This model is designed to provide patient-centered assessment and simple problem-solving strategy for Asian nurses. Through this model, nurses can examine patients’ information needs and empower them to resolve their concerns with the help of adaptive methods.[47] In fact, using this model allows nurses to create meaningful and sustainable relationships with patients and in fact provides a healing environment that optimizes health outcomes.[48] Ang et al. in their study argued the SAGE and THYME model is very important for improving nurse–patient communication in PC, especially among nurses who may lack sufficient skills to respond to patients’ information needs. They also stated that the implementation of this model strengthens therapeutic relationships and facilitates a more personalized care and better coordination with patients’ preferences.[49] Supporting this, Connolly et al. demonstrated that conducting a SAGE and THYME workshop significantly increased healthcare workers’ knowledge, communication skills, and self-efficacy in hospital settings.[50] The SAGE and THYME models have been adopted by various groups of healthcare professionals. In a survey of 412 workshop participants, Connolly et al. found that self-assessment of competence, self-confidence, and willingness to discuss emotional concerns increased significantly after the workshop.[51] Similarly, another survey of 161 workshop participants by Connolly et al. revealed that postworkshop ratings of the model’s usefulness and motivation to use it were high, self-efficacy improved significantly, and participants were significantly more positive about the benefits of appropriate communication behavior, which increased significantly after the workshop.[50] This systematic framework has several strengths, which include guiding nurses through an active cycle of empathetic listening to identify and address key concerns of patients with advanced cancer, and reducing the use of blocking behaviors that prevent patients from disclosing further concerns.[50] Such approaches allow nurses to provide PC recipients with appropriate information and focus on their real concerns. This in turn helps patients to maximize their psychological adjustment and improve their quality of life.[52] These advantages create better opportunities for nurses to facilitate therapeutic relationships with patients who require end-of-life care.[49] In this way, patients are more likely to disclose their concerns and seek informed support from nurses, and participate in decision-making about their care and treatment.[50] As a result, this allows patients to have more control over their lives and take more responsibility for their condition.[49] Another widely used model in this field is the COMFORT model, which significantly improves nurse–patient communication skills, especially in PC settings. This model offers a structured approach to communication that enhances understanding and empathy. In this regard, a study by Mahdiabadi et al. showed that teaching the COMFORT model to nursing students increased their knowledge, attitudes, and communication skills.[53] A study by Fuoto and Turner also showed that teaching the COMFORT model increases nurses’ self-confidence and satisfaction with end-of-life care.[54] It should be noted that, despite many advantages that these structured models provide in improving nurse–patient communication, some argue that traditional methods still have value, especially in settings where time constraints limit the ability to effectively implement structured models.[49] Our study had no specific limitations; however, some articles were inaccessible due to the lack of full-text availability for us.

Conclusion

The results of the present study indicated that nurses are not comfortable with having sensitive conversations with patients about advanced care planning, prognosis, and end-of-life care, and sometimes avoid having these conversations. This issue can be influenced by the state of trust between the patient and the nurse, the level of individual preparation of the nurse and the patient, the level of collaboration between nurses and physicians, the nurses’ ability to be a good listener and establish empathetic interaction with patients, and nurses’ willingness to allocate sufficient time to patient’s interaction. Therefore, it is suggested that in order to improve communication in the PC setting, the nurses’ communication skills should be improved by training and they must be prepared for difficult conversations with patients. In this regard, using structured communication models in PC settings can help to increase the nurses’ comfort in having necessary discussions with patients.
