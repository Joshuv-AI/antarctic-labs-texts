Epidemiology of bloodstream infections and infective endocarditis: new insights on typical species and echocardiography screening
Authors: Elayne Kelen De Oliveira, Kristien Van Vaerenbergh, Monika Shumkova, Monika Beles, Arthur Iturriagagoitia, Ana Moya, Raffaella Mistrulli, Sara Gharehdaghi, An Boel, Lien Cattoir, Dan Schelfaut, Thomas Castelein, Stijn Wouters, Frank Van Praet, Filip Casselman, Ivan Degrieck, Bernard Stockman, Karel Van Praet, Jerrold Spapen, Sofie Brouwers, Martin Penicka, Guy Van Camp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReal-world data on bloodstream infections (BSI) and infective endocarditis (IE) epidemiology, their implications for screening and the applicability of typical species concepts by guidelines are scarce.ObjectivesThis study aims to elucidate the relationship between BSI epidemiology and the predictors of IE, as well as the use of echocardiographic screening for IE in BSI.MethodsSingle-centre registry included all BSI of consecutive hospitalised patients from January 2015 to December 2021. IE prevalence and echocardiographic screening were assessed, also aggregating according to typical species by the European Society of Cardiology and by 2023 Duke-International Society for Cardiovascular Infectious Diseases.ResultsFrom 4939 BSIs related to 4242 admissions in 3708 patients, 2.7% had a diagnosis of definite IE. The most prevalent BSI pathogens were Enterobacterales (48.3%) and Staphylococcus aureus (11.5%). Some less frequent BSI presented the highest IE proportions: Streptococcus bovis complex (19.2%) and Listeria monocytogenes (16.7%). BSI in patients with intracardiac prosthetic material showed an IE prevalence almost five times higher than overall BSI. The IE risk was underestimated when micro-organisms were grouped as suggested by the main guidelines. Echocardiography screening was highest for S. aureus (75%) and Staphylococcus lugdunensis (72.2%). Among BSI without IE cases, the echocardiography range was between 27.3% and 59.4%.ConclusionsThe study illustrates a discrepancy between BSI pathogen prevalence and their IE risk, emphasising the need for sufficient granularity in defining species typically linked to IE. Typical microorganism concepts may underestimate high risk if species are grouped or if IE risk factors are ignored. There is a mismatch between efforts to diagnose definite IE via echocardiography for each microorganism and the IE risk associated with BSI.Keywords: Echocardiography, Diagnostic Imaging, Heart Valve Diseases, Endocarditis, Epidemiology

Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) remains a disease with high mortality and morbidity.1 In addition, the incidence increases parallel to the number of prosthetic valve and cardiac device implantations and the use of intravascular catheters.2 3 The diagnosis of IE is still mainly based on the proof of bloodstream infection (BSI) by positive hemoculture and on imaging of typical morphological features in the course of IE.4

Comparable to imaging, where some images are more or less diagnostic for IE, not every microorganism encountered in a positive BSI raises the same level of suspicion for IE. The clinician within the endocarditis team must be aware of the degree of IE suspicion by integrating imaging and microbiological data to guide diagnosis and therapeutic management. However, the current guidelines mainly emphasise the typical species within groups, which can underestimate the occurrence of IE for specific species and may lead clinicians to overlook an early IE diagnosis in a BSI. Data on this topic are scarce. Therefore, using our registry on BSI and definite IE, we aimed to answer the following questions: (I) What is the relation between the epidemiology of BSI and definite IE? (II) What is the impact of using different classifications of typical IE microorganisms on the risk estimation of the relationship between BSI and definite IE? (III) What are the risk factors for definite IE in our registries of BSI? (IV) Are there specific microorganisms associated with BSI that rarely or never lead to IE, allowing for more effective utilisation of imaging modalities, particularly echocardiography? (V) When analysing the subset of BSI with IE risk factor(s) present, are there changes in the relationship between the BSI and definite IE epidemiologies?

Methods

Study design and population

From January 2015 to December 2021, all consecutive hospitalised patients at AZORG Hospital, one of the tertiary non-university academic centres in cardiology in Belgium, older than 18 years, with blood cultures collected during their hospitalisation, were analysed for the study. The included patients with definite IE diagnosis based on the 2015 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) IE criteria had detailed data from a prospective registry from the Cardiovascular Centre Aalst.5 6

The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee of AZORG Hospital under the reference number 2022/072. Due to its retrospective and non-interventional nature, written informed consent was waived.

Definitions

A BSI was defined as one or more positive blood cultures with a significant pathogen or at least two positive blood cultures drawn at different times with the same microorganism considered a contaminant. All positive blood cultures from the same patient with the same microorganism during one hospitalisation were considered linked to the same disease case. When multiple microorganisms are detected in a positive blood culture, the microbiologist identifies which ones are clinically responsible for infection. BSIs were discussed with the clinician to correlate microbiological findings with clinical observations. The definitions of species typically associated with IE were based on the 2023 ESC IE Guidelines and the 2023 Duke-International Society for Cardiovascular Infectious Diseases (ISCVID) Criteria for IE.4 7

More detailed definitions are in online supplemental file (Extended Definitions) and online supplemental table S1.

Echocardiography screening

The echocardiography screening for IE in patients with BSIs was performed at the physician's discretion within a multidisciplinary team at the AZORG Hospital and based on transthoracic and transoesophageal indications of the 2015 ESC IE criteria and also described in our previous study.5 6 Once BSIs were detected and not considered contamination, and the physician indicated the IE screening, TTE was ordered the same day as a first step and preferably performed within 24 hours. TEE was ordered if judged necessary the day TTE was performed and preferably performed within 24 hours. All echocardiograms were performed by experienced operators at Cardiovascular Center AZORG Aalst at AZORG Hospital, which holds a European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging laboratory advanced accreditation for TTE and TEE in the Echocardiography programme. The term echocardiography screening is used to refer to transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography, except when a technique is specified.

Patient and public involvement

As part of the Endocarditis Heart Team task in a tertiary hospital with a long history of research in cardiology, all patients with suspicion of endocarditis are informed about the still extremely high mortality of IE once hospitalised and the opportunity of improving outcome by increasing knowledge with an extensive database with all possible interesting clinical data to optimise diagnosis and treatment of IE. During this study, all patients included agreed to contribute their data to an anonymised database, enabling linkage of their haemocultures to the diagnosis of IE. We would like to thank all participants for these observations, which will make future diagnoses of IE more accurate.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented in numbers and percentages and compared using the χ2 test with continuity correction. Continuous variables were presented as medians and IQRs and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. The IE prevalence was calculated by the number of BSIs associated with IE divided by the total number of BSIs and in which subgroup species. For predicting microorganisms in BSI (Staphylococcus aureus, HACEK, Streptococcus bovis complex, Enterococcus faecalis, oral streptococci, Listeria monocytogenes, Staphylococcus lugdunensis, coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, beta-haemolytic Streptococcus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, other Streptococcus-like bacteria, other Enterococci) and baseline factors (female gender, age more than 75 years, presence of prosthetic valve, presence of intracardiac device) for IE, we fitted a bias-reduced logistic regression (Firth/Jeffreys-penalised likelihood) and reported 95% cluster-robust (HC1) CIs for ORs, clustering on patient. As a sensitivity analysis, the same model was repeated in the subset of patients who underwent echocardiographic screening. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software V.29 and R Statistical Software (version 4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The Sankey charts were created using SankeyMATIC.

IE prevalence in patients with BSI and comparison between different classifications for species typically related to IE

The 2023 Duke-ISCVID IE Criteria identified high-risk microorganisms in our IE patients, including E. faecalis, HACEK, S. lugdunensis and S. aureus (table 1, figure 2, online supplemental table S4). However, Streptococcus bovis complex, other Streptococcus-like bacteria, oral streptococci and beta-hemolytic streptococci are grouped as ‘streptococci,’ reducing S. bovis prevalence from 19.2% to 8.5%. Listeria monocytogenes, with the second-highest IE prevalence among BSI, is not categorised as a separate microorganism typically linked to IE. Contrariwise, the 2023 Duke-ISCVID IE Criteria included Granulicatella adjacens, which has a 10% IE prevalence. Additionally, coagulase-negative staphylococci is considered typical when intracardiac prosthetic material is present, increasing from 5.2% overall to 18.4% in BSI with intracardiac prosthetic material. Applying the 2023 ESC IE classification for typical microorganisms, the IE high-risk E. faecalis, HACEK group, oral streptococci and S. aureus are recognised. Among streptococci, Streptococcus gallolyticus is also classified as typical by ESC, highlighting its IE prevalence of 14.33%.

Risk factors for IE

BSI caused by HACEK, other Streptococcus-like bacteria, Streptococcus bovis complex, E. faecalis, oral streptococci and S. aureus were independent predictors of IE (figure 3). Among the baseline characteristics, prosthetic valve, intracardiac device, female gender and age older than 75 years were included to adjust this model. The presence of a prosthetic valve (OR 6.74 (95% CI 4.31 to 10.52)) and an intracardiac device (2.03 (1.14 to 3.61)) remained significant predictors for IE in the adjusted model. In a sensitivity analysis restricted to the echo-screened population, the main findings remained unchanged, with broadly comparable effect estimates to those observed in the overall study population (online supplemental figure S4).

The ORs of infective endocarditis per microorganism were calculated using a multivariable regression analysis adjusted for baseline factors (prosthetic valve, intracardiac device, age more than 75 years and gender) with other Enterococci as a reference.

Screening with echocardiography for detecting IE in patients with BSIs

The highest rate of echocardiography screening occurred in the BSIs caused by S. aureus (75%), followed by the other Streptococcus-like bacteria (72.7%), S. lugdunensis (72.2%), Streptococcus bovis complex (69.9%) and E. faecalis (66.3%), respectively (figure 4). Although there was a high IE risk in the HACEK BSIs, echocardiography screening was performed in only 55% of patients; in the oral streptococci BSIs, it was only conducted in 46.3%. Among BSIs without IE cases, echocardiography screening was performed in 27.3% to 59.3%. BSI epidemiology, IE risk and echocardiography screening in the subset of patients with the IE high-risk factor presence of prosthetic valve and/or intracardiac device

Considering the high risk of prosthetic valve and/or intracardiac device presence for IE, we analysed a subset of BSIs in patients with these factors (online supplemental tables S5 and S6; figure 4 and online supplemental figure S3). The IE prevalence in this subset was almost five times higher than overall BSI, increasing from 2.7% to 12.9% (table 1 and online supplemental table S6). HACEK group and Listeria monocytogenes showed a proportion of one case of IE in two BSI. The Streptococcus bovis complex showed two cases of IE to five BSI, almost the same proportion for oral streptococci (online supplemental table S6). Discussion

This study investigated the epidemiology, echocardiography screening findings and risk factors for IE in patients with BSI during hospitalisation and examined the relationship between BSI and the diagnosis of definite IE. The main findings were: (I) some microorganisms showed a high risk of definite IE, although they represented a small portion of the total BSI cases; (II) classifying microorganisms generally associated with IE can overlook those with a high prevalence of IE if microorganisms are grouped broadly and the context of IE risk factors is not considered; (III) in the presence of intracardiac prosthetic material, BSI caused by the HACEK group, Streptococcus bovis complex, E. faecalis, oral streptococci and S. aureus were risk factors for IE; (IV) we observed an imbalance between efforts to diagnose definite IE using echocardiography for each microorganism and their associated IE risk in BSI; and (V) in the subset of BSI with intracardiac prosthetic material, the prevalence was nearly five times higher than in all BSI cases, and 2023 Duke-ISCVID IE Criteria more accurately identified high-risk microorganisms associated with IE than the 2023 ESC IE classification.

Epidemiology of BSI and IE

Enterobacterales were the most common microorganisms in BSIs among hospitalised patients, about 50%, followed by S. aureus at 12%, consistent with Gubbels et al (53.9% and 17.3%, respectively).8 Others similarly identified Escherichia coli, a member of the Enterobacterales, as the main pathogen, with S. aureus second.9 10 Reported prevalence varies with methods, populations and testing, especially when organisms are compared at genus rather than species level, as for streptococci and enterococci.9 11 12 Despite grouping them, species-specific prevalence varies, as our study and others have shown.13

Focusing on the IE prevalence, some of the least common BSIs, such as Listeria monocytogenes, HACEK group, S. lugdunensis, Streptococcus bovis complex and E. faecalis presented the highest IE risks.

Listeria monocytogenes IE cases in our population occurred in intracardiac prosthetic material patients, a pattern also found in other studies.14 15

Our 2.4% of IE linked to HACEK was close to the 1.8% in the Swedish Registry but higher than 0.9% in Østergaard et al.12 16 They grouped HACEK with other uncommon microorganisms, reporting an IE prevalence of 16.7%, similar to our 15%.12 In contrast, a population-based cohort reported 22.9% among BSIs HACEK.17

The Streptococcus bovis complex had a low prevalence of 1.5% of all BSI but with a critical pathogenic impact, representing 10.5% of all IE and a 19.4% IE prevalence. This differs from other studies reporting 3.2% of BSI and 6.9% of IE.13 18 The IE risk varies within the complex, as clearly shown in our study, emphasising the need for precise species identification. S. gallolyticus had the highest IE risk at 5.3%, close to the EURO-ENDO registry 6.6%.1 Our IE prevalence for S. gallolyticus BSI was 14.3%, similar to Seo et al (15.6%), and almost half the value observed by Scheggi et al, despite high valve disease and intracardiac prosthetic material rates in both populations.18 19

Another streptococcal group and its corresponding species that gained attention are the oral streptococci. In our study, they caused 3.8% of BSI and 11.3% of all IE cases, similar to the EURO-ENDO registry (12.4%) and lower than Scheggi et al (17%).1 19 When we examined the overall IE risk among oral streptococci, it was 8%, but species-specific risk ranged from 4.5% to 42.9%.

Beta-haemolytic streptococci also showed higher IE prevalence and were associated with intracardiac prosthetic material as a risk factor. Among 3.7% of all BSI, they caused 4.5% of all IE cases, with an IE prevalence of 3.3%. The international cohort observed 3.1% of IE cases were due to beta-haemolytic streptococcal IE, with intracardiac prosthetic material more common in these cases.20 Within the analysis, Streptococcus agalactiae was the most common and had the highest IE prevalence (5.5%), which contrasts with 9.1% in a previous study.13

IE prevalence in E. faecalis BSIs varies in literature. Østergaard et al found Enterococci species caused 15.5% of all IE, while our study found that only E. faecalis was responsible for 21.8% of all IE.12 Our 15.5% IE prevalence is similar to the Danish study’s 16.7%.11 Dahl et al reported a higher IE rate of 26.1% with systematic echocardiography screening in E. faecalis BSI patients.21 These findings enhance the importance of recognising risk factors, such as specific species, and the need to increase awareness of echocardiography screening in these patients.

In our study, S. aureus was the most frequent cause of IE (33.8%) and the fifth-highest in BSIs (7.9%). Østergaard et al showed S. aureus as the leading cause (28.1%) of IE and in the EURO-ENDO registry (31.4%).1 12 In the Spanish registry, S. aureus caused 17.1% of IE, and Scheggi et al reported 18%.3 19 The IE prevalence in BSI with S. aureus was similar to the Danish study (10.1%) and VIRSTA (11%).11 22

Classification with groups typically related to IE overlooks species with high IE prevalence

Previously, IE prevalence in BSIs mainly focused on S. aureus. The Danish study highlighted IE risk of other species but focused on few (S. aureus, E. faecalis) and grouped some as a genus (streptococci, coagulase-negative staphylococci), potentially underestimating IE prevalence for certain species.11 Our study, examining specific species like the Streptococcus bovis complex, found IE prevalence about twice as high as in the broader streptococci group by the Danish study.11 The findings suggest that a simple genus-level classification of streptococcal BSI might be inadequate, as the 2023 Duke-ISCVID criteria tend to classify nearly all streptococci as potential IE pathogens, collapsing widely differing risks into a single binary category. The HANDOC score showed the value of weighing clinical and microbiological factors, such as high-risk species like S. mutans, S. bovis, S. sanguinis or S. anginosus, in deciding the need for IE echocardiography screening in non-β-haemolytic streptococcal BSI.23 Furthermore, Chamat-Hedemand et al, in a large Danish registry study, found that the likelihood of IE varies widely across a broader range of streptococcal species in BSI.13

Under the 2023 ESC Guidelines, HACEK, Streptococcus gallolyticus, oral streptococci and S. aureus are considered typical pathogens, highlighting the associated IE risk. Nonetheless, over 25% of IE cases in our population involved non-typical microorganisms by the ESC, such as Listeria monocytogenes and coagulase-negative staphylococci.4 This could underestimate the IE risk from these species. It highlights the need for more detailed investigation into BSI species and broadening the Guidelines’ definition of typical species.

The 2023 update of the Modified Duke criteria took a more granular approach, adding E. faecalis, S. lugdunensis, Granulicatella spp and all streptococci, except S. pneumoniae and S. pyogenes, as typical pathogens, and treating coagulase-negative staphylococci, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Candida spp. as typical when intracardiac prosthetic material was present.7 Applying these definitions reduced our non-typical IE cases from 25.6% (ESC classification) to 14.33%.4 7 The recognition of specific typical species in the presence of intracardiac prosthetic material reduced misconceptions about non-typical cases. Nonetheless, by grouping streptococci, the 2023 Duke-ISCVID IE criteria still risk diverting attention from very high-risk IE species, such as the Streptococcus bovis complex and oral streptococci.7

Role of BSI as risk factors for IE

The BSI caused by Streptococcus bovis complex, Listeria monocytogenes, S. lugdunensis and E. faecalis showed intracardiac prosthetic material in 31.0%–41.1%. Where intracardiac prosthetic material was present, IE prevalence was almost five times higher than overall BSI, as well as within the HACEK group, oral streptococci and S. aureus. The intracardiac prosthetic material is an established IE risk factor in streptococcal BSIs and S. aureus BSI and confers high risk in coagulase-negative staphylococcal BSI.13 24 25

Echocardiography screening

In our study, BSIs due to oral streptococci had an echocardiography screening under 50%, and HACEK only 55%, although the IE prevalence was almost twice that of S. aureus. The second-highest IE proportion in positive BSI, Listeria monocytogenes, had echocardiography screening in approximately 42% of cases, despite higher intracardiac prosthetic material prevalence than other groups. These comparisons emphasise the potential for underdiagnosis of IE and highlight the necessity for further research to assess the impact of systematic echocardiography on the early detection.

In contrast, BSI without IE showed a significant echocardiography screening rate. Enterobacterales BSI, the most common BSI without IE, had echocardiography screening in 35,3%. Among 3910 patients with definite IE, Calderón et al observed 1.6% of all IE caused by Enterobacterales, mainly E. coli.26 In our 96 Candida spp. BSI group, no IE was observed, but echocardiography screening was done in 66.7%. A 13-year cohort showed Candida spp. IE as responsible for 2.5% of all IE, with nearly 60% caused by Candida albicans.27

Taken together, a more systematic echocardiographic screening in BSI should be triggered when BSI involves typical species, but this alone seems insufficient. The potential of the high-risk IE species observed in this study, including the Streptococcus bovis complex, Listeria monocytogenes and especially intracardiac prosthetic material, as IE screening triggers should be investigated through systematic echocardiographic IE screening studies in BSI. This study also suggests potential strategies for further investigation to optimise cardiac imaging in BSI screening. These involve conducting studies not only on systematic echocardiographic screening but also on assessing the cost-effectiveness of reducing echocardiographic screening in groups with very low IE risk. The goal is to avoid unnecessary procedures and allocate resources more efficiently by focusing on high-risk cases.28 29

Limitations

This single-centre registry offers detailed microbiological and patient data but has limitations typical of such studies. Our population may differ from others in demographics, cardiac imaging, laboratory resource utilisation and complexity arising from a tertiary, non-university academic cardiology centre. The smaller sample size might not reflect regional variation and limits generalisability compared with multicentre and national registries. Nonetheless, our data align with national epidemiological data. A limitation is microbiologists’ judgement on responsible microorganisms when multiple are present in positive blood cultures. Decisions relied on clinical correlations from the Endocarditis team, reducing subjectivity. The presence of microbiologists and the team may lead to higher IE diagnosis rates than national registries that use coded data, as they better differentiate contamination from true infections. Another limitation was that the Bruker MALDI-TOF MS method allowed identification within the Streptococcus bovis complex only to the species level. Additionally, the absence of systematic echocardiography screening in our population is a limitation of our study, potentially introducing detection bias and should be considered when interpreting our results. At the same time, the low screening rate raises concerns for clinical practice; it may also lead to biased epidemiological data due to possible lower diagnostic rates. Another limitation to consider when interpreting our results is that a comprehensive clinical data assessment for all patients was not feasible, which limited the analysis of risk factors and the ability to adjust for potential confounders. Despite these limitations, the study provides significant real-world insights into a disease with high mortality and limited data on BSI and IE links.

Conclusions

In this monocentric registry, prospectively enrolled patients with BSI were evaluated regarding definite IE. The study showed that certain species, while being found in small numbers of definite IE cases, can represent a high risk for IE once a BSI is diagnosed. The current guidelines classification for microorganisms typically related to IE overlooks those with high IE prevalence when grouping microorganisms or without considering the IE risk factor context, such as the presence of intracardiac prosthetic material. These findings suggest that further studies analysing a more precise insight into the risk of IE at the species level, once BSIs are diagnosed, have the potential to enable more accurate risk stratification and more appropriate use of cardiac imaging tools.
