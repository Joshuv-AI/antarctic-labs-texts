Optical genome mapping identifies novel ENOX2::RUNX1 t(X;21)(q26.1;q22.12) rearrangement in acute myeloid leukemia-myelodysplasia related: first case report
Authors: Giby V George, Sarmad Ali, Chauncey R Syposs, M Anwar Iqbal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute myeloid leukemia-myelodysplasia related (AML-MR) is a biologically and clinically distinct subtype of AML that arises in the context of prior dysplasia. It is characterized by adverse cytogenetics and poor prognosis compared to other AML subtypes. Several genetic mechanisms underpin the pathogenesis of AML-MR; however, additional findings are likely to come to light over time with advanced genomic technologies, enhancing our understanding of their evolution. Background

AML is a genetically heterogeneous malignancy characterized by the clonal expansion of myeloblasts in the bone marrow or peripheral blood [1, 2]. Presently, the World Health Organization (WHO) and International Consensus Classification (ICC) require that the diagnosis of AML-myelodysplasia related (MR) fulfill the following criteria: ≥20% blasts in the blood/bone marrow along with at least one of the following criteria (1) a history of a myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS) or myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm (MDS/MPN) or (2) one or more defining cytogenetic or molecular aberrations. To render a diagnosis of AML-MR, the patient must not have had a history of exposure to cytotoxic therapy, a preceding MPN, fulfill criteria for AML with defining genetic abnormalities, or possess a predisposing germline alternation [1, 2]. The AML-MR category now represents the largest subgroup of AML in the updated 2022 classification systems, comprising approximately 28–35% of all AML cases according to WHO-HAEM5 and about 26–31% based on the ICC classification [3, 4].

The identification of recurrent genetic alterations is fundamental to the diagnosis, risk stratification, and management of AML [5]. Although several cytogenetic aberrations remain yet to be discovered in AML [6, 7], the use of advanced cytogenomic technologies such as OGM is rapidly enhancing the detection of novel, cryptic, and complex structural variants [8–11]. As the resolution of genomic profiling continues to improve, the discovery rate of clinically significant aberrations is expected to increase. In clinical practice, particularly in cases exhibiting myelodysplasia-related features by morphology and next-generation sequencing (NGS), high-resolution platforms such as OGM play a critical role in uncovering defining genetic alterations [10–13].

Rearrangements involving the runt-related transcription factor 1 (RUNX1) gene, located on 21q22.12, are recurrent in several hematologic malignancies, including AML, myelodysplastic neoplasms (MDS), and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) [14, 15]. While RUNX1::RUNX1T1 is the most well-characterized fusion and its presence is AML-defining, numerous other RUNX1 rearrangements remain underexplored due to their cryptic nature and the diversity of partner genes. Advances in genomic technologies, such as OGM, RNA sequencing, and FISH have facilitated the identification of novel RUNX1 fusion partners, expanding our understanding of their clinical and biological relevance [16, 17]. Among the underexplored RUNX1 fusion partners is Ecto-NOX Disulfide-Thiol Exchanger 2 (ENOX2), a gene encoding a tumor-associated NADH oxidase involved in cell growth regulation and cancer cell surface redox activity [18]. Although ENOX2 has been implicated in several solid tumors, its role in hematologic malignancies has not been well defined [19].

Here, we report a previously uncharacterized RUNX1 fusion involving ENOX2, identified in a case of AML-MR. The identification of a ENOX2::RUNX1 fusion in AML-MR may provide new insights into the leukemogenic mechanisms driven by RUNX1 rearrangements and may suggest a potential functional role for ENOX2 in myeloid transformation.

Clinical presentation and methods

Clinical presentation

A 59-year-old female with no prior history of leukemia or cytotoxic therapy exposure, presented with multiple comorbidities, including coronary artery disease (complicated by cardiac arrest and hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy), diabetes mellitus type II, and Lance-Adams syndrome, presented with slurred speech and anemia. The patient’s hemoglobin was 7.8 g/dL and there was leukocytosis (13.3 × 10³/µL), peripheral smear review showed scattered circulating blasts (9%) (Fig. 1a). Microscopic evaluation of a bone marrow specimen revealed 31% blasts with myelomonocytic morphology and granulocytic dysplasia (Fig. 1b and c) with clusters of immature mononuclear cells observed on the core biopsy (Fig. 1d). Flow cytometry analysis showed the abnormal blast population to co-express CD45 (dim), CD34 (subset), CD117, HLA-DR, CD123, CD13, CD33, CD64 (subset), CD14 (subset), CD11b (subset), CD4 (subset), CD2 (subset), CD200 (subset), and CD38 (Fig. 1e).

Methods

Standard Wright Giemsa peripheral blood and bone marrow smears were prepared. Expression of cell surface markers was measured by flow cytometry (Navios, Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA) using the ClearLLab 10 C 10-color lymphoid and myeloid panels and analyzed using the Kaluza C analysis software version 1.2.

Chromosome analysis was conducted on short-term bone marrow cultures following standard laboratory protocols. Metaphase chromosomes were stained using the trypsin-Giemsa (GTG) banding technique for microscopic evaluation. A total of 20 cells were examined, with karyotyping performed on two to five representative metaphases. Chromosomal abnormalities were interpreted in accordance with the International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN 2024) [20].

FISH was performed on 200 interphase nuclei using the AML panel probes (Abbott Molecular/Vysis, Inc., Des Plaines, IL, USA): LSI EGR1 (5q31) SO/D5S23, D5S721 SG; LSI D7S486 (7q31) SO/CEP 7 (D7Z1) SG; LSI RUNX1T1::RUNX1 dual color, dual fusion translocation; LSI KMT2A (MLL) dual color, break apart rearrangement; LSI PML::RARA dual color, dual fusion translocation; LSI CBFB dual color, break apart rearrangement; LSI 13 (13q14) SG/LSI TP53 (17p13.1) SO deletion; and LSI RARA dual color, break apart rearrangement probes. Metaphase FISH was performed using LSI ETV6 (TEL) SG/LSI RUNX1(AML1) SO/CEP X SG probes on 10 metaphases for confirmation of RUNX1 rearrangement with chromosome X.

Targeted DNA-based next generation sequencing (NGS) was performed on bone marrow using the 34-gene Illumina TruSight Myeloid Panel (Illumina, Inc. San Diego, CA, USA). FLT3 mutational analysis was performed using rapid multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and electrophoresis as described by Blake et al. [21].

OGM was conducted as previously described [22] to locate and map the breakpoints and identify the RUNX1 fusion partner gene. Briefly, ultra-high molecular weight DNA was extracted from the patient’s bone marrow aspirate using the SP-G2 BMA DNA Isolation Kit. Fluorescent labeling of long DNA molecules was performed using the DLS DNA Labeling Kit, which targets CTTAAG sequence motifs across the genome with the DLE-1 enzyme (Bionano Genomics, San Diego, CA, USA). Labeled DNA was loaded onto a chip and imaged on the Saphyr instrument for the collection of 1500 Gb data with a molecule size of >150 kb. Rare Variant Pipeline (RVP) analyses were performed using the manufacturer’s software, the Bionano Access Software (BAS) Version 1.8.1 and Variant Intelligence Analysis (VIA) version 7.1. Bionano recommended confidence thresholds were applied as follows: insertions/deletions: 0; inversions: 0.02; duplications: 0; intrachromosomal fusions: −1 and 0.02; interchromosomal fusions: −1 and 0.02; aneuploidy: 0.95; and CNVs: 0.99. A masking filter was applied to remove repetitive and poorly mappable regions. CNV calls were limited to segments larger than 250 kilobases. SV calls were further filtered using Bionano’s human control sample SV database, which includes variants from over 300 genomes of individuals with no reported disease phenotypes. Only SVs with a population frequency below 1% were considered for analysis.

Targeted DNA-based NGS revealed ASXL1, SRSF2, and TET2 mutations (variant allele frequencies: 42–45%). In light of these defining molecular findings, a diagnosis of AML-MR was rendered based on the current WHO and ICC classifications [1, 2]. However, given the patient’s comorbidities and the risks of AML-directed therapy, the decision was made to forgo treatment, and she succumbed to disease within a couple months.

Discussion and conclusions

AML is a biologically diverse hematologic malignancy in which recurrent chromosomal rearrangements and gene fusions define distinct molecular subgroups with prognostic and therapeutic relevance [5]. Well-characterized fusions in AML such as RUNX1::RUNX1T1 are associated with favorable outcomes under current treatment regimens [23, 24], whereas fusions involving MECOM, CBFA2T3, and other partners portend a dismal prognosis, often leading to high relapse rates, poor overall survival, and intrinsic or acquired resistance to standard induction chemotherapy. RUNX1::MECOM fusions drive aggressive leukemogenesis by disrupting transcriptional regulation of hematopoietic differentiation, while RUNX1::CBFA2T3 fusions aberrantly repress transcriptional programs essential for normal myeloid differentiation, promote proliferation of immature blasts, and confer chemoresistance, explaining their poor prognosis in AML [25, 26]. De Braekeleer et al. (2011) reported 55 distinct partner chromosome bands involved in RUNX1 translocations, of which 21 fusion partners were molecularly characterized. Most of these fusions retain the Runt homology domain but lack the transcriptional activation domain, acting as dominant-negative inhibitors of wild-type RUNX1 and driving leukemic transformation [27]. Aypar et al. (2021) identified ten novel and rare RUNX1 fusion partners in myeloid neoplasms, showing frequent association with high-grade MDS/AML and poor survival [16]. Fusion-positive cases often carried additional myeloid driver mutations, supporting a complex genomic context as seen in our case. These findings underscore the necessity of precise genomic characterization to inform prognosis and therapeutic strategies, as reliance on morphology or limited cytogenetic resolution alone may obscure clinically significant alterations.

In our case, a diagnosis of AML-MR was rendered based on the presence of defining somatic mutations (i.e. ASXL1 and SRSF2), as defined by the WHO and ICC [1, 2]. Although RUNX1 mutations are recognized by the ICC as a myelodysplasia-related somatic mutation, the WHO does not make this distinction [1, 2]. Moreover, solely the RUNX1::RUNX1T1 rearrangement is presently considered AML-defining irrespective of bone marrow blast percentage [1, 2]. The identification of a novel ENOX2::RUNX1 fusion in our case of AML-MR highlights both the diagnostic power of OGM and the pathogenic versatility of RUNX1 fusions. ENOX2, an ecto-NADH oxidase of the tumor-associated ENOX family, is aberrantly re-expressed in malignancies and leads to altered redox balance, cellular proliferation, and oncogenic signaling [28]. Notably, a separate study reported a similar t(X;21)(q26;q22) translocation resulting in a different fusion RUNX1::G6PD [29], underscoring the recurrent involvement of RUNX1 in diverse rearrangements with different oncogenic collaborators. RNA sequencing or RNA fusion panel testing could not be performed in our case due to unavailability of specimen. Morré & Morré (2012) proposed ENOX2 as a potential druggable target, as its inhibition by agents such as ME-143, phenoxodiol, epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), capsaicin, doxorubicin, oxaliplatin, and idronoxil has been shown to induce apoptosis in various cancer models [30]. If future studies confirm the expression of the ENOX2::RUNX1 fusion in leukemic blasts, these inhibitors may warrant further exploration. Additionally, RUNX1 fusions are also being explored with menin, Bromodomain and Extra-Terminal (BET), and cyclin dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitors, suggesting that the dual targeting of fusion partners could offer synergistic strategies, especially in therapy-resistant AML [31, 32]. Beyond rare and novel fusions, recurrent fusions in AML reveal biologic and clinical patterns across ages. In children, KMT2A rearrangements are frequent and carry a poor prognosis [33], while in adults, core-binding factor fusions (RUNX1::RUNX1T1, CBFB::MYH11) are more common and predict good response to chemotherapy [22]. Adults also show fusions such as RUNX1::MECOM and NUP98, often linked to chemoresistance and the need for stem cell transplantation [34, 35]. These genomic events highlight the heterogeneity of leukemogenesis and the importance of precise molecular characterization for risk assessment and treatment planning. As OGM becomes more widely integrated into diagnostic workflows, its application has the potential to enhance genotype–phenotype correlations and guide the development of more individualized therapeutic strategies for patients with high-risk or atypical genomic profiles.

In conclusion, AML harboring the rare t(X;21)(q26.1;q22.12) ENOX2::RUNX1 fusion adds to the expanding landscape of RUNX1 rearrangements and illustrates the growing utility of advanced genomic technologies in hematologic diagnostics. OGM allow comprehensive detection and precise delineation of cryptic or complex structural variants that often escape conventional cytogenetic approaches. Although the functional and clinical relevance of this fusion remains to be established, its identification underscores the importance of integrating OGM into diagnostic workflows to enhance the recognition of novel and rare genomic alterations in leukemia and other genetic diseases.
