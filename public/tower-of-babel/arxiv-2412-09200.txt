Accuracy Improvements for Convolutional and Differential Distance Function Approximations
Alexander Belyaev, Pierre-Alain Fayolle

Abstract
Given a bounded domain, we deal with the problem of estimating the distance function from the internal points of the domain to the boundary of the domain. Convolutional and differential distance estimation schemes are considered and, for both the schemes, accuracy improvements are proposed and evaluated. Asymptotics of Laplace integrals and Taylor series extrapolations are used to achieve the improvements.



I
Introduction and contribution

Distance functions are widely used in analysis of signals defined on surfaces and graphs and in image analysis.
Distance function estimation and approximation remains an active research area due to applications in computational physics
[
1
]
, numerical methods for partial differential equations
[
2
,
3
]
, big data processing
[
4
]
, geometric modeling
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, graph theory
[
8
]
, and several other fields.

In this paper, we deal with the following basic distance approximation problem. Given a bounded domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
, our goal is to get an accurate estimation of
\dist(\textit{{x}},\partial\Omega)
, the distance to
\partial\Omega
for all points
\textit{{x}}\in\Omega
. Some of the distance function estimation improvements that we introduce can also be extended to more general problems including estimating geodesic distances on a surface.

Our contributions are as follows. We establish a link between convolution-based distance transforms
[
9
]
and Laplace approximations considered in
[
10
]
in the context of non-convex variational problems. We use this connection to improve the distance function approximation accuracy by combining convolutional distance approximations. Next, we demonstrate how a convolutional distance transform scheme can be combined with the popular heat method
[
11
,
12
]
for distance function approximation. Finally, and this is probably the main practical contribution of the paper, we propose two Taylor-based extrapolation schemes for enhancing and extending the heat distance method for flat domains.

II
Laplace’s method

Let
\varphi(\textit{{p}})
,
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, be smooth and
h(\textit{{p}})
,
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, be continuous. Let
\textit{{p}}^{*}
be the global minimizer of
\varphi(\textit{{x}})
,
\varphi^{*}=\varphi(\textit{{p}}^{*})
. Laplace’s method implies that

\lambda^{d}\!\int h(\textit{{p}})e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}d\textit{{x}}\sim C\,\lambda^{d/2}e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}}^{*})}h(\textit{{p}}^{*}),

(1)

where
C
is a positive constant and
\sim
means that the ratio of two sides tends to
1
, as
\lambda\to\infty
. See
[
10
]
for details.

Applying
-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log(\cdot)
to both parts of (
1
) yields

-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\!\left[\lambda^{d}\!\!\int\!\!h(\textit{{p}})e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}d\textit{{p}}\!\right]\!=\varphi^{*}-\frac{d}{2}\frac{\log\lambda}{\lambda}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\!,

(2)

as
\lambda\to\infty
. Thus, for a sufficiently large
\lambda
, the right-hand side of (
2
) underestimates
\varphi(\textit{{p}}^{*})
.

Following
[
10
]
let us consider the following self-normalized version of (
1
)

\min h(\textit{{p}})<\frac{\int h(\textit{{p}})e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}d\textit{{p}}}{\int e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}d\textit{{p}}}\sim h(\textit{{p}}^{*}).

(3)

If we set
h(\textit{{p}})=\varphi(\textit{{p}})
, then (
3
) overestimates
\varphi^{*}=\varphi(\textit{{p}}^{*})
. Thus

\frac{\int\varphi(\textit{{p}})e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}d\textit{{p}}}{\int e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}d\textit{{p}}}=\varphi^{*}+\frac{K}{\lambda}+O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)

(4)

for
\lambda\to\infty
with constant
K>0
. We can combine estimates (
2
) with
h(\textit{{p}})\equiv 1
and (
4
) to improve the approximation accuracy

\alpha\left\{\frac{\int\varphi(\textit{{p}})e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}\,d\textit{{p}}}{\int e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}d\textit{{x}}}\right\}+\beta\left\{-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\left[\lambda^{d}\!\int e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}d\textit{{p}}\right]\right\}

with weights
\alpha
and
\beta
given by

\alpha=\frac{d\log\lambda}{2K+d\log\lambda}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\beta=\frac{2K}{2K+d\log\lambda}.

(5)

The idea to combine (
2
) and (
4
) seems new.

While constant
K
in (
4
) can be determined analytically,
it depends on the values of
\varphi(\textit{{p}})
and its derivatives at
\textit{{p}}^{*}
which typically are not known to us. So, in practice we have to choose
K
heuristically.

Note that the results remain true if we consider functions
\varphi(\textit{{p}})
and
h(\textit{{p}})
defined on a
d
-dimensional closed surface (manifold). In particular, as shown in the next section, we can consider a point
x
inside domain
\Omega
bounded by
\partial\Omega
and define
\varphi(\textit{{p}})
by
\varphi(\textit{{p}})=\|\textit{{x}}-\textit{{p}}\|
, where
p
lies on
\partial\Omega
. Then (
2
) with
h(\textit{{p}})\equiv\varphi(\textit{{p}})
and (
4
) deliver estimates of
\dist(\textit{{x}},\partial\Omega)
, the distance function to the boundary of the domain.

III
Convolutional distance function estimation

The following two formulas are the main ingredients of fast convolutional distance transforms proposed in
[
9
]
.

\varphi^{*}=\min\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\}\approx-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}e^{-\lambda\varphi_{k}}\right],

(6)

\varphi^{*}=\min\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\}\approx\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varphi_{k}e^{-\lambda\varphi_{k}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{-\lambda\varphi_{k}}},

(7)

as
\lambda\to\infty
.
One can observe that (
2
) with
h(p)\equiv 1
and (
4
) are continuous counterparts of (
6
) and (
7
), respectively. In particular, it is easy to see that the right-hand side of (
6
) underestimates
\varphi^{*}
, while the right-hand side of (
7
) overestimates it.

Given a domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
bounded by
\partial\Omega
, let us set
\varphi(\textit{{p}})=\|\textit{{x}}-\textit{{p}}\|
. Then
the continuous versions of the discrete convolutions (
6
) and (
7
) used in
[
9
]
are given by the left-hand and right-hand sides of

-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\left[\lambda^{d}\!\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}d\textit{{p}}\right]\approx\varphi^{*}\approx\frac{\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(\textit{{p}})e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}d\textit{{p}}}{\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{-\lambda\varphi(\textit{{p}})}d\textit{{p}}},

respectively. Thus, in view of (
2
) and (
4
), we can improve the approximation accuracy of (
6
) and (
7
) by blending their right-hand sides with the weights defined by (
5
).

As pointed out in
[
9
]
, an equivalent way to represent the right-hand side of (
7
) consists of differentiating the logarithm in the right-hand side of (
6
):

-\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\log\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}e^{-\lambda\varphi_{k}}\right]=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varphi_{k}e^{-\lambda\varphi_{k}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{-\lambda\varphi_{k}}}.

(8)

This identity relating (
6
) and (
7
) looks trivial, but in the next section we will use it to get new formulas to estimate the distance function.

IV
Differential distance function estimation

A significant progress in distance function estimation was achieved in
[
12
]
(see also
[
13
]
) where the so-called heat method was introduced. The heat method is typically used for geodesic distance estimation and based on S. R. S. Varadhan’s classic result in differential geometry
[
11
]
. Below we consider a simplified version of the heat method adapted for planar distance function estimation.

Consider the following screened Poisson equation in a bounded domain
\Omega

-\Delta v+\lambda^{2}v=0\mbox{ in }\Omega,\qquad v=1\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

(9)

Using the substitution

v(\textit{{x}})=e^{-\lambda u(\textit{{x}})}

(10)

in (
9
) yields

\left(1-|\nabla u|^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\Delta u=0\mbox{ in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega

(11)

and, therefore, since the distance function satisfies the eikonal equation
|\nabla\!\dist(\textit{{x}},\partial\Omega)|=1
in
\Omega
, we have

\dist(\textit{{x}},\partial\Omega)\approx u(\textit{{x}})=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\left[v(\textit{{x}})\right],\quad\mbox{as }\lambda\to\infty,

(12)

where
u(\textit{{x}})
is the solution to (
11
).

We can represent
v(\textit{{x}})
using the Green’s function
G_{\lambda}(\textit{{x}})
for (
9
):

v(\textit{{x}})=\int_{\partial\Omega}\!\frac{\partial G_{\lambda}(\textit{{x}}-\textit{{p}})}{\partial\textit{{n}}}\,ds,\quad\textit{{x}}\in\Omega,\quad\textit{{p}}\in\partial\Omega,

where
n
is the outer unit normal of
\partial\Omega
. Thus
u(\textit{{x}})
, the solution to (
11
), is given by

u(\textit{{x}})\!=\!-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\!\left[\int_{\partial\Omega}\!\!\!\frac{\partial G_{\lambda}(\textit{{x}}-\textit{{p}})}{\partial\textit{{n}}}\,ds\right]\!,\mbox{ }\textit{{p}}\in\partial\Omega,\mbox{ }\textit{{x}}\in\Omega,

(13)

and one can observe a similarity between (
13
) and (
6
), and also (
2
).

In view of (
8
), an analogue of (
7
) is obtained by differentiating (
13
) w.r.t.
\lambda

-\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\log\left[\int_{\partial\Omega}\!\frac{\partial G_{\lambda}(\textit{{x}}-\textit{{p}})}{\partial\textit{{n}}}\,ds\right]=-\frac{v^{\prime}_{{}_{\lambda}}(\textit{{x}})}{v(\textit{{x}})},

(14)

where
v^{\prime}_{{}_{\lambda}}
denotes the derivative of the solution to (
9
) w.r.t.
\lambda
.
In the next section, we will see that, in practice, the right-hand side of (
14
) delivers a reasonably good approximation of the distance function:

\boxed{\dist(\textit{{x}},\partial\Omega)\approx-\left.v^{\prime}_{{}_{\lambda}}(\textit{{x}})\right/v(\textit{{x}})}

(15)

Differentiating (
9
) by
\lambda
yields

-\Delta v^{\prime}_{{}_{\lambda}}+\lambda^{2}v^{\prime}_{{}_{\lambda}}+2\lambda v=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,\quad v^{\prime}_{{}_{\lambda}}=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega

(16)

and
v^{\prime}_{{}_{\lambda}}
can be found by solving (
16
).

Now we present a more rigorous way to obtain (
15
). Namely let us consider (
11
) where
s=1/\lambda
is considered as a small parameter and
u(\textit{{x}})
, the solution to (
11
), depends on
s
. One can expect that the first-order Taylor series based estimation of
\left.u(\textit{{x}})\right|_{s=0}
delivers a good approximation of the distance

\dist(\textit{{x}},\partial\Omega)=\left.u(\textit{{x}})\right|_{s=0}\approx u(\textit{{x}})-su^{\prime}_{s}(\textit{{x}}),

(17)

where
u(\textit{{x}})
and
u^{\prime}_{s}(\textit{{x}})
are evaluated at some
s
close to zero. Simple calculations show that the right-hand sides of (
15
) and (
17
) coincide.

Nothing prevents us from using the second-order Taylor series approximation of
\left.u(\textit{{x}})\right|_{s=0}

\dist(\textit{{x}},\partial\Omega)=\left.u(\textit{{x}})\right|_{s=0}\approx u(\textit{{x}})-su^{\prime}_{s}(\textit{{x}})+\frac{s^{2}}{2}u^{\prime\prime}_{ss}(\textit{{x}})

(18)

which yields a possible improvement of (
15
)

\boxed{\dist(\textit{{x}},\partial\Omega)\approx-\frac{v^{\prime}_{{}_{\lambda}}}{v}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left[\frac{v^{\prime\prime}_{{}_{\lambda\lambda}}}{v}-\left(\frac{v^{\prime}_{{}_{\lambda}}}{v}\right)^{2}\right]}

(19)

where
v^{\prime\prime}_{{}_{\lambda\lambda}}(\textit{{x}})
is the solution to the equation obtained from (
9
) by differentiating by
\lambda
twice

-\Delta v^{\prime\prime}_{{}_{\lambda\lambda}}+\lambda^{2}v^{\prime\prime}_{{}_{\lambda\lambda}}+2v+4\lambda v^{\prime}_{{}_{\lambda}}=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,\quad v^{\prime\prime}_{{}_{\lambda\lambda}}=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

If we assume that
u(\textit{{x}})
in (
11
) does not depend on
t
(this means that
u(\textit{{x}})
is the exact distance function), then, in view of (
10
), formulas (
12
), (
15
), and (
19
) are equivalent. In other words, the closer
u(\textit{{x}})
is to the exact distance function, the smaller the differences between approximations (
12
), (
17
), and (
18
) are.

The original heat method
[
12
]
includes a gradient normalization step which, in our case of estimating
\dist(\textit{{x}}\partial\Omega
, can be described as follows. Given an approximation of the distance function
w(\textit{{x}})
, we normalize its gradient
g(\textit{{x}})=\nabla w/|\nabla w|
and then use the normalized gradient to construct an improved distance function approximation
w_{n}(\textit{{x}})
by solving a simple variational problem

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{n}-g|^{2}d\textit{{x}}\rightarrow\min,\quad w_{n}(\textit{{x}})=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

This least square gradient fitting problem is equivalent to the Poisson problem

\Delta w_{n}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(g)\mbox{ in }\Omega,\quad w_{n}=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,

which can be efficiently solved numerically. In the next section, we demonstrate that the gradient normalization step improves the approximation accuracy of the right-hand sides of (
15
) and (
19
).

Numerical experiments

Convolutional approximations.

VI
Discussion, conclusion, and future work

Our Matlab implementations of (
15
) and (
19
) and corresponding distance function approximations will be made available upon acceptance of this letter. The implementations are simple and can be easily obtained by introducing proper modifications into the Matlab scripts presented in
[
14
]
.

The main limitation of this paper is that we deal with the planar distance estimation problem. It has certain practical applications including its use within the so-called characteristic function method
[
15
]
. However the more challenging curvilinear and multidimensional distance function estimation problems are not considered in the paper. Another limitation is a lack of mathematical justification of the proposed Taylor-based extrapolation schemes (
15
) and (
19
). They look plausible but in contrast to the heat method
[
12
]
which is based on mathematical results of S. R. S. Varadhan
[
11
]
are not supported by truly rigorous mathematical results.

It is interesting to observe a certain similarity between the convolutional and differential distance function estimation schemes considered in this paper and a PDE-based surface reconstruction approach developed in
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
, where the evolution from convolutional to PDE-based methods is, in some sense, similar to how we move from the convolutional schemes to the differential ones,

It would be interesting to check if the convolution-based distance function approximation schemes we introduced in this paper can be extended to graphs by using graph Laplacian convolutions. We hope that, similar to the heat method
[
12
]
, PDE-based distance function approximations (
17
) and (
18
) can be used for computing geodesic distances on surfaces and, in particular, we would like to check if our approximations can compete with
[
6
]
in terms of robustness. We are also interested in extending (
17
) and (
18
) to Finsler metrics
[
19
,
20
]
.
Adapting our distance function approximations for path planning applications
[
21
]
constitute another direction for future research.
Finally, it would be interesting to see if Padé approximations are capable of outperforming our Taylor-based extrapolation approach.
