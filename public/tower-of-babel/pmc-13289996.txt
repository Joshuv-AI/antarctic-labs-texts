Urinary Incontinence: Epidemiology and Physiotherapeutic Approaches- A Systematic Review
Authors: Retasha Soni, Supreet Bindra, Daman Preet Singh, Pankajpreet Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urinary incontinence (UI) is a significant health concern affecting both men and women, leading to physical discomfort and psychological distress that can negatively impact quality of life. This condition affects individuals across all age groups and populations worldwide, with various risk factors contributing to its onset. A range of treatment approaches is available for managing UI. Conservative management, particularly physiotherapy, is typically the first line of treatment. This review aims to assess the current prevalence of UI in both men and women and to examine recent evidence-based literature on the effectiveness of physiotherapy interventions in managing UI. A systematic search was conducted using electronic databases such as Google Scholar and PubMed, focusing on observational studies and randomized controlled trials published in English between January 2015 and January 2024. A total of 38 studies were included in this review. To assess the quality of prevalence studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used, and the methodological quality of the randomized controlled trials was assessed by the Jadad scale. Findings indicate that the prevalence of UI among females ranges from 12% to 100%, while in males, it varies between 14.2% and 75.5%. Pelvic floor muscle training was found to be an effective and commonly used physiotherapy intervention for treating UI in this review. Reported prevalence rates differ widely across studies due to variations in UI definitions and study designs. Pelvic floor muscle training and other physiotherapy techniques play a crucial role in reducing UI symptoms. Various risk factors for both genders were identified. However, further population-based studies and high-quality randomized controlled trials—particularly in male populations—are needed to establish the long-term benefits of physiotherapy interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Urinary incontinence (UI), defined as the involuntary loss of urine,[1] is a widespread public health concern affecting over 200 million adults globally. While UI is more common in women, it also impacts men.[23] The prevalence of UI rises with age in both sexes.[4567] UI can significantly interfere with daily life, thus reducing the overall quality of life (QoL).[8] Many individuals feel embarrassed to discuss urinary symptoms; therefore, the condition is frequently underreported and undertreated, leading some researchers to describe it as a “silent” health problem.[9] This condition is frequently overlooked, with many individuals either dismissing it as a normal part of aging or hesitating to seek help.[10] In addition to physical discomfort, UI can negatively influence psychological well-being and has been linked to anxiety, depressive symptoms, and disturbances in sleep. In severe cases, it may even increase the risk of falls and fractures, further affecting overall well-being.[1112]

The causes of UI differ between men and women. In women, UI often results from damage to the pelvic floor muscles (PFMs) and endopelvic fascia, along with age-related declines in detrusor muscle function. Contributing factors include childbirth, obesity, pelvic surgeries, and chronic conditions.[3] In men, UI is frequently associated with diabetes, prostate disorders, radical prostatectomy, and neurological conditions, with post-prostatectomy incontinence rates ranging from 2% to 57%.[13] These diverse causes highlight the importance of individualized treatment approaches. Management of UI varies based on severity and underlying factors, with treatment options ranging from conservative strategies to surgical interventions. Non-invasive management strategies, particularly physiotherapy-based interventions, are generally recommended as the initial treatment approach. These include pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), electrical stimulation, interferential therapy, biofeedback, and, in women, the use of vaginal cones.[1415] Among these, PFMT has been particularly successful in managing incontinence in both men and women,[16] making it the preferred initial treatment choice.[17]

Several reviews have explored the prevalence of UI and the role of physiotherapy in its management, with a primary focus on women.[181920212223] Most of these studies emphasize the effectiveness of PFMT and biofeedback techniques. Additionally, some research has examined a wider range of physiotherapy interventions, including manual therapy, lifestyle modifications, and complementary modalities. However, these approaches are often assessed in conjunction with PFMT and biofeedback rather than as standalone physiotherapy treatments.[810] Despite growing research on UI, significant gaps remain, particularly in the comprehensive physiotherapy management of UI across genders. While much of the existing literature focuses on female-specific approaches, male-specific interventions remain underexplored.[1112] This limited attention to UI in men underscores the need for targeted research and the development of gender-inclusive physiotherapy strategies. To date, no systematic review has thoroughly examined both the prevalence of UI and the effectiveness of physiotherapy interventions for both men and women. This review aims to bridge this gap by synthesizing existing evidence to provide a more comprehensive understanding of UI management across genders. Addressing these critical research gaps is essential for increasing awareness, improving knowledge, and enhancing the management of a condition that carries significant physical, psychological, and social consequences. Ultimately, this study seeks to tackle the challenges of underreporting and undertreatment of UI, contributing to better care and improved outcomes for those affected.

METHODOLOGY

This systematic review followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines to ensure a transparent and rigorous methodology. The study design was independently developed by two reviewers to ensure objectivity and clarity in the research framework. The conceptualization of the study and the determination of the content scope were carried out collaboratively yet independently by three reviewers. Data collection and subsequent analysis were performed by two reviewers, maintaining consistency and accuracy throughout the process. The initial drafting of the manuscript involved contributions from three reviewers, while two reviewers were responsible for refining and editing the document. Finally, a comprehensive review of the entire manuscript was conducted collectively by the three reviewers to ensure the quality, coherence, and integrity of the study. Studies published in English between January 2015 and January 2024 were included. A thorough literature search was conducted using electronic databases such as Google Scholar and PubMed, employing a combination of keywords, including prevalence, urinary incontinence, physiotherapy, stress incontinence, urge incontinence, mixed incontinence, pelvic floor muscle training, biofeedback, and electrical stimulation. Boolean operators (“AND”/”OR”) were utilized to refine search results and retrieve the most relevant publications. Study selection was based on predefined eligibility criteria. Observational studies (cross-sectional, cohort, and descriptive) were included if they reported the prevalence of UI—covering stress urinary incontinence (SUI), urge urinary incontinence (UUI), and mixed urinary incontinence (MUI)—in males and/or females, had a sample size of at least 50 participants, and included individuals aged 18 years or older. Abstracts were excluded. For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), inclusion criteria were structured according to the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, and Study Design), as detailed in Table 1.

PICO- guidelines

Data extracted from prevalence studies included author(s), year of publication, country, study type, study population, sample size, participant age, prevalence rates, predisposing risk factors, and quality assessment. UI prevalence was examined for different subtypes (SUI, UUI, and MUI). For RCTs, extracted data included author(s), year, sample size, participant age, intervention details, outcome measures, study duration, results, and QoL. To assess the quality of prevalence studies, the NOS was used, which evaluates studies based on selection, comparability, and outcome domains, assigning a maximum of nine points. Studies were categorized as having a high (≥8 points), moderate (5–7 points), or low (<5 points) risk of bias.[24] For RCTs, methodological quality was assessed using the Jadad scale, which scores trials from 0 to 5 based on randomization, blinding, and description of withdrawals. Trial scoring ≥3 was considered to have adequate methodological quality.[25]

RESULTS

A total of 2,426 studies were identified through the initial search, including 1,400 prevalence studies and 1,026 RCTs. After removing 158 duplicate studies, the remaining studies were screened based on their titles and abstracts, leading to the exclusion of 1,860 studies. This left 408 studies for a detailed full-text review. Following a thorough evaluation, 370 studies were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 38 studies were deemed eligible and included in the systematic review [Figure 1]. All selected studies underwent a methodological quality assessment, with results presented in Tables 2 and 5.

Description of the available studies

The summary of the included studies is presented in Tables 2-4 (prevalence studies and RCTs). A total of 27 prevalence studies, comprising 23,690 participants aged 18 to 96 years, were analyzed. Of these, 18 studies focused on females, six included both genders, and three were specific to males. Most prevalence studies followed a cross-sectional design and were conducted in various settings, including population-based (n = 7), community-based (n = 4), health center-based (n = 7), hospital-based (n = 5), clinic-based (n = 3), and institution-based (n = 1) environments. Maximum studies reported UI as the “involuntary loss or leakage of urine.”

For RCTs, 11 studies were included, involving a total of 2,102 female participants, with sample sizes ranging from 30 to 625. Notably, no RCTs assessing the physiotherapy management of UI in the general male population were found during this period. The available RCTs focused exclusively on males with specific conditions, such as post-prostatectomy UI or cystic fibrosis, both of which were part of our exclusion criteria.

Prevalence and types of UI studied

The reported prevalence of UI across studies from different countries varied significantly, ranging from 3.8% to 100% [Table 2]. A considerable variation in prevalence rates was observed across populations. Among females, UI prevalence ranged between 12% and 100%,[2627] while in males, it was reported as high as 75.5%[27] and as low as 14.2%.[28] Seventeen studies investigated the prevalence of different UI subtypes, including SUI, UUI, and MUI. Two studies reported prevalence data for SUI and UUI but did not specify the prevalence of MUI, while another, which exclusively focused on SUI, did not specify the data on UUI and MUI. Additionally, one study presented findings for SUI and MUI but did not provide separate prevalence rates for UUI. Six studies did not specify the individual prevalence of UI subtypes. Most studies identified SUI as the most prevalent subtype among females, whereas UUI was the most reported type in males [Table 3]. Several studies also explored predisposing risk factors for UI, which are summarized in Table 2.

RCTs and types of intervention

All the available RCTs focused exclusively on female participants. In most of these RCTs (n = 5), the intervention period lasted 12 weeks [Table 4]. The longest study duration among the included RCTs was 24 months,[29] while the shortest lasted 5 weeks.[30] Urinary leakage and muscle strength were the most assessed outcome measures across the studies. The interventions examined varied and included PFMT, a combination of PFMT and abdominal muscle therapy, biofeedback, surface electromyography (EMG), postural education, pelvic floor muscle education, and video-based educational programs [Table 4].

Quality assessment

The quality assessment of the included prevalence studies based on NOS: 10 were classified as high quality, 13 as moderate quality, and four as low quality. Notably, all cohort studies demonstrated high methodological quality [Table 2]. Based on the Jadad scale, 8 RCTs were rated as high quality (scoring 3 out of 5), while three were considered low quality (scoring 2 out of 5). None of the included RCTs were double-blinded [Table 5].

DISCUSSION

The results of this review show that UI is a very common condition that affects millions of men and women, and physiotherapy interventions play an important role in the conservative management of UI.

Prevalence of UI across continents

The prevalence of UI varies significantly across populations, influenced by factors such as age, gender, cultural differences, and study methodologies. SUI is the most common among females, particularly those aged 35–44 years, while UUI is more prevalent in males. MUI is frequently reported in older adults.

Europe

UI is most prevalent in Europe, with Lithuania reporting the highest rates.[27] In the Netherlands, UI affects 49% of females and 22.6% of males between the ages of 18 and 85.[31] In Russia, nearly 48% of individuals aged 65 and older experience UI.[3233] Similarly, in Germany, 48.3% of women are affected, while Denmark reports a prevalence of 46.4% among females.[34] In the United Kingdom, approximately 40% of women over the age of 21 suffer from UI.[35]

Asia

UI prevalence varies across Asian countries. In China, UI was reported in 56.3% of females and 35% of males,[36] while in Turkey, 54.5% of women and 32.9% of men were affected.[37] In Korea, 45.8% of women aged 30 years and older experienced UI.[38] Similarly, in Taiwan, 17.3% of men aged 40 and above reported UI.[39] In India, UI is widely prevalent among females aged 30 to 90 years,[26404142] SUI specifically affecting 55.3% of women aged 18 and older.[43] In Oman, 34.5% of women experienced UI,[44] whereas in Malaysia and Iran, the prevalence among women was 39% and 24.9%, respectively.[4546]

Africa

UI prevalence in Egypt was notably high, affecting 50% of elderly men[47] and 80% of women.[48] In Nigeria, 32.5% of women experienced UI.[49]

North America

In Mexico, UI was reported in 14% of women.[50] According to a study by Helfand et al.,[51] 32% of males aged 18 years and older in the United States experienced UI.

South America

In Brazil, UI prevalence was recorded at 14.2% in men and 28.2% in women.[52] SUI was reported in 36.5% of Brazilian women.[53] Variations in UI prevalence can be attributed to differences in study populations, cultural influences, and the definitions of UI used in research. Despite regional differences, the prevalence of UI tends to increase with age, with elderly populations exhibiting significantly higher rates.

RISK FACTORS OF URINARY INCONTINENCE

Age and gender

Age remains a significant factor, with higher prevalence rates observed in elderly populations.[2731363752] Aging leads to anatomical and physiological changes in the urogenital system, including muscle and axon degeneration, reduced bladder capacity, increased detrusor activity, and decreased detrusor contractile strength, making women more prone to UI. In men, prostate enlargement with age can cause urinary retention. Menopause significantly increases the risk of UUI, with the loss of estrogen weakening pelvic muscles, causing vaginal atrophy, and reducing supportive tissues around the urethra.[27323738]

Parity and mode of delivery

Obstetric factors are reported to have an important role in the development of UI. In the studies, it is stated that vaginal delivery increases the risk of UI.[26273134404454] Khan et al.,[40] 2015 observed that 41% of women reported for UI who had vaginal deliveries. 26% of the participants who had a history of cesarean section reported for UI.[46] Pregnancy and delivery risk factors, such as two or more childbirths, vaginal childbirth method, and rupture of the perineum could be the explanation of why SUI occurs at a younger age.[2734404254] Birth giving the first child at an age less than 20 years, vaginal delivery of a large baby with an episiotomy and more than three pregnancies are SUI risk factors.[2742]

Body mass index and obesity

Any case causing chronic increase of intra-abdominal pressure poses a risk for development and exacerbation of UI. Obesity is stated to be a predisposing factor for SUI.[31343540444655] Many epidemiological studies indicated that increased body mass index (BMI) was a risk factor for UI, and women with BMI greater than 30 kg/m2 are two times more prone to UI than the normal weight women.[3435404446495055] Linde et al.[31] did not find any correlation between UI in men and BMI.

Smoking and chronic constipation

Frequent smoking causes cough in smokers, destruction in PFMs, increase in intraabdominal pressure, and deterioration of collagen synthesis.[395055] In the literature, UI prevalence of smokers is reported to be higher than non-smokers.[40] Constipation is another proposed risk factor of UI in both males and females. Constipation is suggested to contribute to pelvic muscle dysfunction by causing damage to pelvic nerves due to prolonged tension.[313744454647495455]

Urinary tract infection and enlarged prostate

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is frequently observed in women. Studies reported that UTI is a risk factor for UI, and a significant relation was found between them.[4255] None of the studies reported the incidence of UTI in males. Enlarged prostate can result in UUI as it causes abnormal bladder contraction.[313747]

Co-morbidities and neurological disorders

Zaki et al.[47] identified several comorbid conditions significantly associated with UI in men, including congestive heart failure, diabetes mellitus, osteoarthritis, and chronic kidney disease. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, which increases abdominal pressure during coughing, was also linked to SUI.[34374244] In women, UI was significantly associated with hypertension, osteoarthritis, diabetes, and vaginal prolapse.[3134354446505455] Additionally, neurological disorders such as stroke were identified as major risk factors for UUI.[324852]

Drugs

The use of some drugs and UI have been reported by many studies. Drugs could cause incontinence by lowering bladder outlet resistance and/or by increasing intravesical pressure, which disrupts the normal pressure relationship between the bladder and urethra and leads to urinary leakage. Other possibilities include disturbances of the central nervous control of voiding or an overproduction of urine.[46] Increased risk of UI among men using diuretics, antidepressants, and laxatives was also seen.[47]

Pelvic surgery

In males, radical prostatectomy or transurethral resection of the prostate can lead to SUI. The main pathophysiology of SUI in men is due to the dysfunction of the urethral sphincter complex or change in the urethral axis. The prevalence of UI increases after prostate surgery.[3137] Vaginal hysterectomies increased the risk of incontinence in females, while abdominal hysterectomies did not.[31]

Socioeconomic and educational challenges

Socioeconomic and educational factors play a crucial role in the prevalence, diagnosis, treatment, and management of this condition. Individuals with lower educational attainment may not recognize UI as a medical condition or may feel embarrassed to discuss it with healthcare providers. Research suggests that inadequate health literacy is associated with delayed healthcare-seeking behavior, contributing to increased severity and complications of UI.[637415456] Studies indicate that rural and underserved areas may lack specialized healthcare professionals, such as pelvic health physiotherapists, resulting in inadequate incontinence care for women.[144]

PHYSIOTHERAPY INTERVENTIONS

PFMT was found to be an effective and commonly used physiotherapy intervention for treating UI in this review. It was included in 10 out of 11 RCTs [Table 4]. PFMT with biofeedback has shown better results in some studies,[2957] though Hagen et al.[29] reported no significant additional benefit of biofeedback over PFMT alone after 24 months. PFMT reduces UI symptoms in women aged 52–80 after six weeks of training.[58] However, small sample sizes and lack of follow-up limit generalizability. Tosun et al.[59] found UI symptoms resolved in 94.1% of patients after 12 weeks of PFMT. Group exercise programs with home exercises were also successful in reducing UI in older women in Bangladesh, improving QoL.[60] Similarly, group-based PFMT was found noninferior to individual PFMT at one year for treating UI in older women.[57]

The effectiveness of a standardized UI physiotherapy program was seen in older Chinese women of more than 65 years of age with stress, urge, or MUI. The intervention in this study was given for 12 weeks. This could optimize the effect of the neural adaptation (recruitment of efficient motor units and frequency of excitation) and muscle hypertrophy and thus decrease the symptoms of UI. The combination of PFMT and bladder training has shown better results and improved the QoL.[61] The sample size of this study was small (n = 55), follow-up was not done, and a convenience sampling method was used, which might not be truly representative of the whole population suffering from UI.

A study compared the effectiveness of supervised PFMT game-therapy with an unsupervised protocol in women over 45 with UI. Game-therapy, involving PFM contractions during virtual games for five weeks, significantly improved incontinence symptoms and PFM function, including power and endurance.[30] Both groups showed improvement, but supervised training yielded better results. However, the study had a small sample size (n = 36) and no follow-up to assess long-term effects. Another study compared supervised versus home-based PFMT over 24 weeks for managing UI in women. Supervised training was more effective, as home exercises alone failed to achieve comparable results. This study also had a small sample size and no long-term follow-up.[62]

An RCT evaluated the effectiveness of physical therapy for UI in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis. A 12-week PFMT program using EMG biofeedback resulted in a 75% reduction in weekly leakage episodes, with improvements maintained at 1-year follow-up. In contrast, no improvement was observed in the control group. Abnormal spine position in osteoporosis, which increases intra-abdominal pressure, improved with PFMT.[63] The study had a small sample size (n = 48), highlighting the need for larger trials. Another study showed stabilization exercises targeting PFMs improved stress UI and chronic low back pain, likely through coactivation of the transverse abdominis and PFMs.[64] The 12-week intervention also had a small sample size, limiting generalizability. In an RCT with women unable to contract their PFMs voluntarily, vaginal palpation with or without posterior pelvic tilt was more effective than electrical stimulation or no treatment for UI. Vaginal palpation provided proprioceptive feedback, facilitating correct PFM contraction, and posterior pelvic tilt increased PFM tonic activity. The intervention lasted eight weeks with no follow-up.[65]

To our best of knowledge, this review is among the first to include both genders in assessing the prevalence and physiotherapy management of UI. Current evidence confirms that UI is prevalent in both males and females, with a higher prevalence in females. The review highlights the need for more population-based, low-bias studies to accurately determine prevalence rates. Variability in sample sizes and definitions of UI across studies has contributed to heterogeneity in findings. Physiotherapy interventions are effective in reducing UI symptoms. However, PFMT remains the most effective and recommended first-line treatment. Many studies included small sample sizes and lacked follow-up to evaluate long-term effects. Only two studies[5763] conducted follow-ups, limiting the generalizability of results. Additionally, none of the included RCTs used double-blinding, raising concerns about methodological quality. This review underscores the need for high-quality RCTs to validate physiotherapy interventions for UI. Notably, no RCTs in males were found within the scope of the PICOS guidelines used. The limited research on male-specific physiotherapy interventions, combined with the under-reporting of UI in both genders, highlights the need for greater attention to this serious health issue. Public health initiatives should focus on increasing awareness, strengthening health education, and ensuring equitable access to healthcare services. In addition, promoting community-based rehabilitation and physiotherapy programs can enhance accessibility and help reduce disparities in care. Policymakers should support the integration of physiotherapy services, particularly PFMT programs, into standard healthcare systems as a first-line, cost-effective, and non-invasive management strategy for UI. Furthermore, governments and health authorities should encourage expanded research on the epidemiology, prevention, and management of UI to guide policy development and improve clinical practice guidelines.

Limitations

The limitation of this review is that it looked for citations only on two databases (Google Scholar and PubMed). Only free full texts were reviewed. No authors were contacted for the missing data.

CONCLUSION

UI is highly prevalent in both genders worldwide. SUI is more common in women, whereas UUI is predominant in men. Despite its widespread occurrence, more population-based prevalence studies are needed to better understand demographic variations and contributing factors. Physiotherapy-based interventions are the first-line treatments for UI. While substantial evidence supports their efficacy in female populations, further research with rigorous methodological designs is required to strengthen these findings. In contrast, the current evidence base for physiotherapy interventions in males with UI remains limited, highlighting the urgent need for high-quality RCTs to establish their effectiveness. This review identifies key risk factors for UI affecting both men and women, including aging, chronic cough, persistent constipation, obesity, diabetes, and lower socioeconomic status, such as limited income and education. Additionally, women are more susceptible to UI due to factors like pregnancy, childbirth, menopause, and parity, while prostate-related conditions are significant contributors in men. Reducing socioeconomic disparities in UI diagnosis and treatment requires a multidisciplinary, evidence-based approach. Further research is essential to guide policy reforms and develop targeted interventions that reduce UI prevalence and improve patients’ overall well-being.
