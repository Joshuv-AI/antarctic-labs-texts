Real-world treatment patterns and adherence among privately insured patients with type 2 diabetes in Saudi Arabia: a retrospective cohort study using national claims data
Authors: Shabab Alghamdi, Ibrahim Abdulrahman Aljuffali, Ahmed M AlDammas, Nebras Abu Alhamayel, Muhammed Jumaah, Mirna Matni, Joelle Barhoun, Saeed Mastour Alshahrani, Nada Alagil

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) imposes clinical and economic burdens worldwide, with the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) reporting among the highest prevalence rates in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. Despite national diabetes initiatives under Vision 2030 and recent guideline implementation, evidence on T2DM management among privately insured beneficiaries remains limited. This study aimed to provide one of the first real-world assessments of antidiabetic treatment patterns, therapy modifications, medication adherence and guideline-recommended glycaemic monitoring among privately insured patients with T2DM in KSA.DesignLongitudinal retrospective cohort study.SettingA national claims-based analysis was conducted using the National Platform for Health Information Exchange Services (NPHIES), covering privately insured beneficiaries across KSA. The study period spanned from 1 September 2022 to 31 December 2024.ParticipantsEligible participants were privately insured individuals aged ≥15 years with a diagnosis of T2DM, defined as having at least one medical claim of T2DM (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Australian Modification code E11) and continuous insurance enrolment for a minimum of 12 months (including ≥180 days pre-index and ≥180 days post-index). The index date was defined as the earliest observed antidiabetic prescription within the study window. A total of 187 797 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcomes were antidiabetic real-world treatment patterns and therapy modifications, including treatment escalation, switching, early discontinuation and medication adherence. Secondary outcomes included guideline-recommended laboratory testing of glycaemic control for haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), plasma glucose tests and oral glucose tolerance test. Claims were linked to the Council of Health Insurance’s internal product dictionary using the NPHIES product/service code to retrieve the standardised product name and activity type. Data extraction used Structured Query Language-based queries and Python transformation.ResultsPatients had a mean age of 53.7 years, while T2DM rates reached 47.4 per 1000 insured men and 43.8 per 1000 insured women. Combination therapy accounted for 29.4% of initial treatment regimens among eligible patients with T2DM. Biguanide monotherapy users exhibited switching and escalation rates of 9% and 7%, respectively, while 35% of sulfonylurea users exhibited escalation. Treatment discontinuation was observed in 22% of users of combination therapies, 22% of SGLT2 inhibitor users and 19% of sulfonylurea users. The medication possession ratio was 0.87 for insulin users. Regarding laboratory monitoring, 61% of patients had at least one glycaemic test 6 months prior to therapy initiation. Laboratory testing reached 73% following therapy initiation.ConclusionThe Saudi experience suggests that integrated national claims platforms, supported by standardised coding and e-prescribing, can play a critical role in advancing real-world evidence generation and informing diabetes management strategies in KSA and across MENA health systems.Keywords: DIABETES & ENDOCRINOLOGY, Disease Management, Drug Utilization, EPIDEMIOLOGIC STUDIES, HEALTH SERVICES ADMINISTRATION & MANAGEMENT, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Diabetes is one of the leading non-communicable diseases globally and continues to impose substantial challenges on healthcare systems.1 With more than 95% of diabetes cases classified as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), its global burden has increased by nearly 200% between 1990 and 2021.1 2 The complications of T2DM result from poor glycaemic control and accelerated disease progression, including nephropathy, retinopathy, peripheral neuropathy and cardiovascular disease, which collectively contribute to significant economic strain on healthcare systems.3 These complications, alongside the costs of antihyperglycaemic therapies, hospitalisation and hypoglycaemic events, increase healthcare expenditures even in emerging markets.4–6

The Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region has experienced a rapid rise in obesity driven by urbanisation, sedentary behaviours and unhealthy dietary patterns.7 8 The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) reports one of the highest prevalence rates of both diabetes and obesity in the region.8 9 The consequences of T2DM extend beyond its clinical manifestations, exerting a measurable negative impact on patients’ quality of life and creating a growing economic burden on households and health systems.10 11 In response, KSA has implemented national prevention and early detection initiatives, including the Saudi National Diabetes Center, which developed a strategic plan aligned with Vision 2030 and informed by national and international guidelines.12

In recent years, advanced antidiabetic therapies, particularly sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), have gained prominence due to demonstrated benefits in glycaemic control, weight reduction (limited to GLP-1 RAs) and cardiovascular risk reduction.13–15 However, these therapies are significantly more costly than traditional treatments, and their uptake in the private insurance sector may diverge from public-sector utilisation patterns due to differences in reimbursement frameworks, formulary structures and prescriber preferences.16 Although insurer-level spending data is limited, available claims analyses have shown that medication costs constitute one of the largest components of insurance reimbursements for patients with T2DM.17 18

In KSA, medication cost remains a key determinant of treatment selection among patients, prescribers and policymakers.19 The Council of Health Insurance (CHI), an independent governmental entity in KSA, assures employer compliance and adaptation of health insurance coverage for their employees, including defining beneficiaries’ eligibility and coverage criteria.17 Recently, CHI introduced a revised essential benefits package aimed at improving patient access to treatment. The package applies at the prescription level and includes tiered co-payment requirements by drug category, with generic prescriptions subject to 0%–20% coinsurance (capped at SAR 30 per prescription) and prescriptions for branded medications with generic alternatives subject to coinsurance of up to 50%.20 Despite national efforts to control T2DM and improve medication access, limited national-level evidence in KSA currently describes antidiabetic prescribing trends, treatment modifications, adherence patterns or laboratory monitoring practices within the Saudi private insurance population. This gap is significant, as real-world treatment behaviour in the insured sector directly influences patient outcomes, healthcare spending and formulary decision-making. Without such data, policymakers and clinicians cannot adequately evaluate quality of care or identify opportunities to optimise diabetes management.

This study aimed to provide one of the first real-world assessments of antidiabetic treatment patterns, therapy modifications, medication adherence and guideline-recommended glycaemic monitoring among privately insured patients with T2DM in KSA using a national claims analysis.

Methodology

The guideline for REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely collected health Data, specific to pharmacoepidemiological research was used as guidance in reporting this study.21

Study design and data source

A longitudinal retrospective cohort study design was conducted using health insurance claims data from the National Platform for Health Information Exchange Services (NPHIES). NPHIES is a national unified digital infrastructure designed to standardise the exchange of electronic claims (eClaims) between healthcare providers and payers.22 This system captures healthcare utilisation for more than 13 million privately insured beneficiaries across KSA.23

NPHIES captures the full range of admitted and non-admitted claim categories across all KSA regions: institutional (inpatient admissions), professional (outpatient consultations and services), pharmacy (prescription medications), dental (procedures) and vision (optical services). All claims incorporate standardised diagnostic classification using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Australian Modification (ICD-10-AM).

Setting

NPHIES data recorded across KSA between 1 September 2022 and 31 December 2024 were included. For each patient, the observation structure included a pre-index period of at least 180 days to capture baseline claims history and a post-index period of at least 180 days to assess early treatment patterns (figure 1) . The index date was defined as the earliest observed antidiabetic prescription in NPHIES after at least 180 days of pre-index observable enrolment, with eligible index dates restricted to 1 March 2023 through 30 June 2024. This restriction ensured that all included patients had sufficient observable claims history before and after the index prescription. Patients were therefore classified as observed treatment initiators within the available NPHIES claims history, rather than confirmed lifetime first-time users.

Population inclusion criteria

Within the 12 months observation window (pre-post index periods), patients were included in the study if they met the following criteria:

At least one claim with an ICD-10-AM code E11 (T2DM) recorded in any diagnosis field (principal, secondary, admitting or discharge diagnosis) during the 12-month observation window.

At least one claim with an ICD-10-AM code E11 (T2DM) recorded in any diagnosis field (principal, secondary, admitting or discharge diagnosis) during the 12-month observation window.

Continuous enrolment in the NPHIES database for at least 180 days before and 180 days after the index date.

At least one prescription claim for an antidiabetic medication during the study period.

At least one prescription claim for an antidiabetic medication during the study period.

Each patient contributed as a single record to the study cohort. Patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded from the study cohort.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study.

Variables classifications

Antidiabetic medications and mapping

Pharmacy claim lines were enriched using the most updated drug information from the Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA) Human Drug List (December 2024), enabling retrieval of Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes, scientific names, strengths, package sizes and public reference prices. Antidiabetic medications were identified using ATC group A10, and drugs were assigned to their respective therapeutic classes as detailed in online supplemental tables 1 and 2.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic data on age and sex were extracted from the index claim to provide a baseline description of the study population. Sex was categorised as male or female as recorded in NPHIES. Glycaemic monitoring was evaluated using laboratory claims within the 180-day pre-index and 180-day post-index windows. Laboratory measures of interest corresponded to guideline-recommended assessments of glycaemic control and were identified using procedure codes for haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), plasma glucose tests and the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). For each test and time window, the presence of at least one recorded test was determined, and the findings were reported as the number and percentage of the total cohort tested at baseline and during the observation period. The use of more than one diagnostic test within the same period is consistent with clinical practice.24 Notably, the analysis was limited to the presence or absence of laboratory testing; numeric measurements were excluded as they are submitted as PDF files within NPHIES rather than as standardised coded fields, which limits their feasibility for analysis. Laboratory monitoring was evaluated as a descriptive outcome reflecting utilisation of guideline-recommended glycaemic testing and was not modelled as a determinant, mediator or confounder of treatment-related outcomes.

Data measurement

Beyond the structured demographic and clinical claim-level information, several key measures were derived. These include treatment patterns, therapy modifications and adherence to glucose-lowering medications as measured by the observed medication possession ratio (MPR) under price-validated defined daily dose assumptions.

Initial treatment regimen classification

Initial therapy was characterised by evaluating all antidiabetic medications dispensed within the first 30 days (index date+30 days). The index class was thus defined as the diabetes class dispensed on the index date.

Patients were categorised by the number of distinct index date pharmacological classes dispensed, as ‘monotherapy’, ‘dual’, ‘triple’ or ‘quadruple or higher’ therapy (at least four classes). Fixed-dose combinations were thus counted according to the number of constituent classes. Treatment initiation patterns were further reported by therapeutic class, scientific name and route of administration.

Post-initiation treatment patterns

Post-initiation treatment modifications were assessed among ‘monotherapy’ initiators during the 180-day post-index period. Any non-index drug class dispensed within the 180-day window was defined as the ‘first add-on date’, and a 90-day refill window after the ‘first add-on date’ distinguished treatment outcomes.

Patients who have initiated a new non-index drug class within the 180-day window, and who did not refill the index drug class within 90-days after the ‘first add-on date’, were considered to have ‘switched’ therapy.

Patients who have initiated a new non-index drug class within the 180-day window, and who did refill the index drug class within 90-days after the ‘first add-on date’, were considered to have ‘escalated’ therapy (add-on therapy).

If no non-index class was initiated within the 180-day observation window, patients were considered to have remained on monotherapy.

Early discontinuation of therapy

Every diabetes class that was first filled within the first 30 days (index date+30 days) was evaluated. Each index class was considered as a separate initiation. Patients with at least two refills of the index class within 90 days of treatment initiation were classified as ‘consistent starters’.

Patients who refilled the index drug class after the 180-day observation window, or who have no subsequent index-class fill despite having at least 180 days of remaining observation time, were considered to be ‘early discontinuers’ of therapy. Cases lacking sufficient follow-up to determine a 180-day gap were right-censored.

The percentage of class-specific discontinuation rates was calculated as the number of early discontinuers divided by the number of consistent starters.

Medication adherence

MPR is the most commonly used measure of medication adherence among ongoing users, excluding early discontinuers. It is calculated by dividing the total days’ supply of medication obtained by the patient by the number of days in the observation period.25

In this study, MPR was used to assess medication adherence over a 180-day observation window, with a minimum of two refills required for inclusion. Given incomplete information on dispensed quantity and prescribed dosing regimens within NPHIES, a price-validated fill methodology was applied: each fill’s paid amount was validated against the SFDA reference price, and only fill prices within 20% of the reference price were included. Days of therapy were then estimated using the product’s defined daily dose (DDD), applying fixed-dose combination or the World Health Organication DDD for single-ingredient agents.26 Adherence is thus measured as observed MPR under price-validated DDD assumptions throughout this manuscript. Because dosing flexibility (eg, individual insulin titration regimens), pack sizes and pricing structures differ across antidiabetic classes, observed MPR values were interpreted descriptively and were not treated as directly comparable adherence estimates across medication classes.

Patient-level MPR was calculated as the ratio of days covered by dispensed medication to 180-day observation window. Population-level MPR was derived by dividing total covered days across all patients by the sum of all patient-level observation days. An MPR threshold of ≥80% was applied to classify patients as adherent, with those below this threshold classified as non-adherent.27–29

Bias

This study is subject to potential selection bias, as inclusion was limited to privately insured individuals with continuous enrolment in the NPHIES database, which may under-represent patients with intermittent coverage or those receiving care solely under public coverage. Misclassification bias may have occurred due to reliance on administrative claims data for identifying T2DM diagnoses, medication exposure and treatment modifications, as claims reflect coded and dispensed services rather than clinically confirmed diagnoses or actual medication use. Additionally, information bias from the absence or exclusion of granular clinical data in claims-based analyses, including laboratory values, measures of disease severity and prescribed dosing regimens, limits the ability to fully characterise patients’ clinical status and assess treatment appropriateness. A conceptual Directed Acyclic Graph illustrating the main measured and unmeasured factors relevant to treatment selection and descriptive outcomes is provided in online supplemental figure 1. Finally, residual left truncation and prevalent-user misclassification may persist despite the application of a 180-day pre-index observable period, as prior antidiabetic exposure before a patient’s observable history within NPHIES cannot be completely excluded. This may have resulted in underestimation of discontinuation rates, particularly for long-established antidiabetic therapies. The requirement for at least 180 days of post-index observable follow-up means that treatment-pattern outcomes apply only to patients who remained observable for the required follow-up period. Patients who died, disenrolled, lost coverage or otherwise became unobservable shortly after treatment initiation may therefore be under-represented. Because these events cannot be fully distinguished from true treatment discontinuation in claims data, early discontinuation may be underestimated, particularly among older or clinically complex patients.

Study sample size

As this study is descriptive, the 187 797 sample size was not prespecified but was determined by the availability of all patient records meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria for T2D within the defined study period.

Data extraction, analysis and validation

Data extraction was performed using Structured Query Language (SQL) within CHI’s secure data enclave, with parameterised queries applied to ensure consistent and reproducible results. The primary unit of analysis was the claim line, encompassing both accepted and rejected claims. Records were subsequently aggregated to the encounter or prescription course level where appropriate. Data transformations were performed using Python (Pandas), and all SQL and Python scripts were maintained under version control, with run logs and output checksum hashes retained to support traceability and reproducibility.

Records were linked deterministically within CHI’s data warehouse using existing unique identifiers present in the claims extract (eg, patient_key for patient-level de-duplication and cohort construction and claim_request_identifier to group services within the same submission). Claims were linked to CHI’s internal product dictionary using the NPHIES product/service code to retrieve the standardised product name and activity type. Medication records were then mapped to SFDA reference data primarily via Global Trade Item Number (GTIN) using the NPHIES product/service code, with a secondary fallback using the NPHIES display name (trade name) when GTIN-based matching was not available. Mapping quality was evaluated by quantifying mapping coverage (GTIN vs trade-name fallback), monitoring unmapped products and targeted review of high-frequency unmapped or ambiguous items.

Comprehensive quality checks were applied to ensure analytical integrity, including duplicate detection, missingness profiling for key variables, plausibility and range checks for key fields (eg, dates and costs) and date-logic validation within the study window. The results were reported as counts and percentages for categorical variables and as mean, median and SD for continuous variables. No inferential hypotheses were specified a priori, and accordingly no p values were reported.

Investigators were granted read-only access to the de-identified claims extract within the secure data enclave under role-based permissions and an approved data-use agreement. No direct patient identifiers were available, and only aggregate outputs were exported in accordance with CHI disclosure controls.

Privacy and data security

All claims’ data were fully de-identified prior to access, and analyses were conducted within CHI’s secure data enclave under an approved data-use agreement. Data management procedures adhered to the Saudi Data and Artificial Intelligence Authority policies and CHI’s internal data governance standards.30 All work was conducted under formal approval from CHI’s Data Governance Committee, and all analysts were bound by strict confidentiality and data-security agreements.

Data availability statement

The de-identified individual-level NPHIES claims data used in this study is not publicly available in compliance with CHI data-governance, privacy and data-use agreement restrictions. Aggregate results supporting the findings are presented in the article and online supplemental materials.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 187 797 patients with T2DM satisfied the inclusion/exclusion criteria and were available in the database for the pre-specified study time frame and were included in the study cohort (figure 2). The cohort was predominantly male (76%), with 3% missing or unspecified sex. Because the sex distribution of the cohort may reflect that of the underlying privately insured population, sex-specific T2DM rates were also assessed. Rates were found to be similar among males and females (47.4 vs 43.8 per 1000 insured persons), suggesting that the higher proportion of males in the cohort likely reflects the male predominance of the insured population rather than meaningful sex-based differences in T2DM prevalence.

Initial treatment regimen patterns

Treatment was initiated with oral antidiabetic medications in 84.2% of patients (n=175 436), insulin-based regimens in 12.6% (n=26 280) and other injectable therapies, predominantly GLP-1 RAs, in 3.2% (n=6735) of patients in this cohort. Regarding initial therapy intensity during the first 30 days, approximately one-third of patients received monotherapy (31%), while nearly equal proportions received dual therapy (29%). Triple therapy was observed in 25% of patients and 14% received regimens consisting of four or more antidiabetic agents (figure 2).

At the drug level, metformin hydrochloride was prescribed in 16.3% of the sample (n=51 090). Among sulfonylurea users, glimepiride was prescribed to 40 560 patients (12.9%) and gliclazide to 31 704 patients (10.1%) (table 2).

Post-initiation treatment patterns

Patterns of post-initiation therapy modification varied across antidiabetic drug classes (figure 4). Patients initiating biguanides demonstrated 9% switching and 7% escalation rates. Patients on sulfonylureas and thiazolidinediones showed therapeutic variability: among sulfonylurea initiators, 35% escalated therapy while 9% switched to a different class. Thiazolidinedione users demonstrated a similar pattern, with 23% escalating and 20% switching therapy.

SGLT2 inhibitor initiators demonstrated relatively balanced treatment patterns, with 12% switching and 22% escalating therapies. Initiators of GLP-1 RAs and insulins also showed 15% escalation for both classes respectively. Switching occurred in 12% of GLP-1 RA users and 8% of insulin users. Among glucosidase inhibitor users, only 33% remained on the same therapy, with 44% switching and 22% escalating.

Early discontinuation of therapy

Varied early discontinuation rates were observed during the first 6 months of therapy (table 3). GLP-1 RAs users demonstrated a discontinuation rate of 37% (n=3855 of 10 536 initiators), and insulin initiators showed a rate of 31% (n=8071 of 25 629). Discontinuation was observed in 30% of patients receiving DPP-4 inhibitors and 29% of those receiving biguanides.

Discussion

This study leveraged NPHIES claims data to characterise real-world treatment patterns, therapy modifications, and medication adherence among privately insured patients with T2DM in KSA, providing one of the first national-scale, claims-based assessments of antidiabetic utilisation in this population. Patients with T2DM in this cohort had a mean age of 53.7 years, somewhat younger than those reported in comparable settings such as Kuwait (56.3 years) and the United Arab Emirates (55.7 years), though direct comparisons are constrained by differences in study design and data sources.31 32 Users of oral antidiabetic agents accounted for 84.2% of treatment initiations observed in this cohort and users of insulins accounted for 12.6% of treatment initiations. Pre-initiation laboratory monitoring was documented in 61% of the cohort and increased to 73% following therapy initiation, with a greater proportion receiving multiple tests, indicating alignment with guideline-recommended follow-up. 27% of patients had no documented laboratory testing during the follow-up period, potentially indicating a remaining care gap. Importantly, these findings are descriptive, and no causal relationship can be established between therapy initiation and post-initiation laboratory testing, as clinical data were not accessible or evaluated. The absence of pre-initiation laboratory testing in patients may also reflect incomplete capture within insurance claims rather than deviation from recommended standards of care, as patients may have undergone testing through out-of-pocket or public sector channels not recorded in NPHIES. Metformin and sulfonylureas collectively accounted for 39% of all initial prescriptions in this cohort, while combination of oral antidiabetic agents were initiated in 29.4% of patients.

Prescribing patterns from the NPHIES are consistent with the latest Saudi Diabetes Clinical Practice Guidelines (SDCPG), although a high prevalence of oral combination therapies may reflect inadequate glycaemic control, necessitating treatment intensification among this pool of patients. The observed use of oral antidiabetic therapies as initial treatment (84.2%) is consistent with the SDCPG, which recommend oral agents, particularly metformin, as the preferred first-line pharmacologic treatment for most patients with T2DM, unless contraindicated. Newer agents, specifically SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs, represented a small share of treatment initiations in this cohort, despite their favourable benefits under the SDCPG, likely reflecting gradual adoption of these therapies within privately insured beneficiaries.33 These findings should be interpreted with caution, as guideline recommendations are inherently patient-centred and informed by factors not captured within claims databases, including laboratory parameters, disease severity, comorbidities, patient preferences and physician clinical judgement.

Comparison to existing literature

The treatment patterns identified in this study are broadly consistent with international real-world evidence, with notable contextual differences. In Canada, the initiation of oral combination therapies has increased following the introduction of newer drug classes, which is in line with the use of combination regimens observed in NPHIES claims (29.4% of initial prescriptions).34 In the Netherlands, patients with T2DM over the age of 45 exhibited treatment modification patterns that resembled those observed in our cohort, with only 8.2% transitioning from biguanides to sulfonylureas over 6 years, while insulin was commonly introduced as add-on therapy.35 Similarly, within the present cohort, transitions among patients from metformin to alternative oral agents were relatively uncommon, and insulin use was most often observed in combination with existing therapies. With respect to adherence, evidence from the USA has demonstrated high MPR among patients using SGLT2 inhibitors compared with those on sulfonylureas, reflecting a growing preference for newer agents with more favourable adherence and tolerability profiles.36 Although direct statistical comparisons between drug classes were beyond the scope of the present descriptive study, SGLT2 inhibitor use was observed alongside relatively low discontinuation rates (22%) compared with other commonly prescribed therapies.

Real-world evidence on T2DM treatment patterns in KSA remains limited, complicating direct national comparisons, however some evidence from diverse settings exists. A study using data from a government-funded healthcare system reported findings similar to ours, including low thiazolidinediones utilisation and modest but increasing prescription rates for SGLT2 inhibitors following their formulary introduction.33 A multicentre KSA study further identified DPP-4 inhibitors and sulfonylureas as the most frequently prescribed add-on therapies overall, with recent increases in the prescriptions of GLP-1 RAs and SGLT-2 inhibitors as add-ons when compared with previous years.37 Such trends are beginning to manifest in NPHIES claims and are expected to become more pronounced as these agents gain broader formulary inclusion and clinician familiarity.

Observed switching or escalation patterns among metformin users are consistent with real-world evidence reporting sustained metformin use over time.38 However, as this analysis is purely descriptive and did not evaluate comparative effectiveness or reasons for treatment changes, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of superior durability relative to other therapies. Similarly, claims-based utilisation of sulfonylureas, despite established associations with hypoglycaemia risk and secondary failure, suggests that prescribing patterns in KSA may be influenced by factors beyond clinical guidelines, including cost, formulary coverage, prescriber familiarity and patient preference. The observed intake of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs in this cohort likely reflects the interplay of formulary availability, reimbursement policies, clinician preferences, patient factors and evolving guideline recommendations within real-world practice settings. Further research should consider prescriber education, formulary incentives for guideline-concordant prescribing and structured quality-improvement initiatives at the payer and health system level.

These findings identify several opportunities for future research and health-system evaluation. First, continued monitoring of prescribing patterns through NPHIES may help characterise the uptake of guideline-recommended therapies over time. Second, the utility of NPHIES as a pharmaco-epidemiological tool could be enhanced by improved documentation of glycaemic monitoring data and follow-up. Implementing validation checks at the point of claim submission, alongside harmonisation of diagnosis, medication and laboratory codes across e-prescribing systems, laboratories and insurance claims platforms, would improve data completeness and reporting accuracy. Third, strategies to support medication adherence, particularly for patients initiating complex combination regimens or insulin therapy, warrant prioritisation.

Strengths and limitations

This study leveraged the NPHIES database as its primary data source, a national infrastructure covering more than 13 million privately insured beneficiaries in KSA and representing a unique real-world evidence database for pharmacoepidemiologic research. The standardised NPHIES data structure, enriched by linkage to the SFDA Human Drug List, enabled detailed classification of antidiabetic agents by therapeutic class and active substance, supporting granular longitudinal analyses of therapy modification. The use of symmetric 180-day pre-index and post-index observation windows, with continuous enrolment requirements, strengthens the temporal validity of treatment pattern characterisation.

Several important limitations must be acknowledged. First, claims data capture healthcare utilisation rather than clinical intent, confirmed diagnoses, or actual medication consumption, introducing risk of misclassification bias. Second, laboratory results in NPHIES are predominantly submitted as PDF documents rather than standardised coded values, precluding extraction of numeric clinical measures and were not available for adjustment in this analysis. Consequently, observed differences in treatment patterns, adherence measures, discontinuation, switching and therapy intensification across medication classes should not be interpreted as comparative treatment effects, as these findings may be influenced by residual confounding by indication and other unmeasured patient-related, provider-related and disease-related factors. Although diagnoses and comorbidities can be analytically derived from longitudinal claims, these were outside the scope of the current analysis. Third, data lag, the incomplete capture of services or medications not claimed through insurance, and the variability in ICD-10-AM coding and procedure coding across providers may affect diagnostic accuracy and the linkage between prescriptions and clinical indications. The study’s 1 year observation period may also be considered suboptimal for chronic conditions such as T2DM. Fourth, residual left truncation may have resulted in prevalent-user misclassification despite the 180-day pre-index observable period, potentially underestimating treatment discontinuation and early treatment modification, particularly for long-established antidiabetic therapies.

With respect to adherence, MPR estimates are constrained by the absence of structured days-supply, dispensed quantity and prescribed dosing information in NPHIES. Although the ±20% price-validation was applied to reduce implausible fill estimates, the price-validated DDD method may still introduce drug-class-specific measurement error, particularly for therapies with variable dosing or heterogeneous pack sizes. The definitions of therapy switching and escalation, while consistent with validated approaches in the literature, were derived algorithmically from claims data and may not fully capture clinical intent, potentially resulting in residual misclassification of treatment modification events.

Finally, the findings are restricted to the privately insured population in KSA and may not be generalisable to patients receiving care solely under public coverage or those with intermittent insurance, who may differ in disease severity, socioeconomic status and access to care. The male predominance in the analytic cohort should also be interpreted in the context of the privately insured population structure in KSA, which may overrepresent employed male beneficiaries. The requirement for ≥180 days of post-index observable follow-up may also have under-represented patients with early death, disenrollment or loss of coverage.

Conclusion

This study provides real-world evidence on antidiabetic treatment patterns, therapy modifications, medication adherence and glycaemic laboratory monitoring among privately insured patients with T2DM in KSA using NPHIES claims data. The findings highlight opportunities to align prescribing practices with contemporary guidelines, strengthen laboratory monitoring and documentation and implement strategies to support persistent, evidence-based therapy. Collectively, they also demonstrate the value of NPHIES as a national platform for monitoring chronic disease management and identifying care gaps that can inform policy, formulary design and quality-improvement initiatives in KSA.
