The effect of an educational program on Hookah smoking among female university students: applying the integrated model of theory of planned behavior and prototype willingness model
Authors: Zahra Khalilzadeh-Farsangi, Zahra Arab Borzu, Fereshteh Najafi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of hookah smoking among females, including university students, is increasing and is considered a high-risk behavior; therefore, designing and implementing effective interventions is crucial. This study aimed to determine the effect of an educational program based on an integrated model of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and the Prototype Willingness Model (PWM) on hookah smoking among female university students.MethodsA quasi-experimental study was conducted on 100 female university students residing in dormitories from October 2024 to January 2025. Four dormitories were randomly assigned to intervention and control groups. Students were recruited through convenience sampling, proportionate to the population of each dormitory. Based on the integrated model, the educational program was delivered to the intervention group in four sessions. Data were collected using a demographic information form and a self-developed questionnaire to assess the integrated model constructs at baseline and three months post-intervention. Data were analyzed using t-tests, chi-square tests, and the Mann–Whitney U test (SPSS v26).ResultsScores for positive attitude towards hookah smoking (P = 0.01), subjective norms (P = 0.04), behavioral willingness (P = 0.02), prototype favorability (P = 0.03), and perceived behavioral control (P = 0.02) significantly decreased in the intervention group. Behavioral intention, however, significantly increased (P = 0.03). No significant differences were observed in prototype similarity (P = 0.81). Although the prevalence of hookah users after the intervention did not differ significantly between the two groups (P = 0.41), the frequency of hookah use (P = 0.02) and the duration of each smoking session (p = 0.03) were significantly different between the groups.ConclusionThe integrated model-based educational intervention led to significant reductions in both the frequency of hookah use and the duration of each smoking session. This model can be employed as an innovative and practical approach to modify students’ behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08170-y.

Background

Hookah smoking, also known as waterpipe or shisha, has emerged as a growing public health concern worldwide [1, 2]. In recent years, hookah smoking has become less stigmatized and is increasingly accepted as a common recreational activity among young people [3, 4]. In a systematic review, the prevalence of hookah use among adolescents and young adults aged 10–24 years was reported as 18.2% [5]. Prevalence varies by country, with 60% of Nigerian youth reported as users [6], and 22–27% in Iran [7, 8].

Hookah use is influenced by knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes toward smoking, as well as social factors such as social acceptance and having friends or family members who smoke [9, 10]. Hookah smoking is increasingly popular among young adults, particularly university students [11, 12]. The prevalence of hookah use among university students has been reported as 32.7%, and 46.3% in Turkey and India, respectively [13, 14]. In Iran, the lifetime prevalence of hookah smoking among university students is estimated at 25% [15]. The increase in consumption is especially significant among young women, particularly college students [16]. El Shahawy et al. (2020) reported that 10.3% of female students compared to 8.5% of male students were hookah users in the United States [17]. Jeihooni et al. (2018) found that 19.4% of female students in Iran had a history of hookah use and this trend is rising among female students living in dormitories [18].

Dormitories, as a shared living space, can significantly influence students’ behavioral habits [19]. Dormitory lifestyle, separation from family, peer pressure, easy access to hookah, lack of healthy recreational activities, and misconceptions about its relative safety are among the main factors contributing to the inclination toward hookah smoking [19, 20].

Hookah smoking among females has been associated with cervical cancer, menstrual disorders, premature menopause, primary and secondary infertility, ectopic pregnancy, abortion, as well as maternal and fetal risks during pregnancy [21, 22]. However, females often tend to underestimate the harmful effects of hookah smoking [23]. Therefore, interventions should focus on modifying attitudes, addressing social influences that encourage hookah use, and increasing awareness of its specific harmful components [24].

The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), as a well-established model for predicting health-related behaviors, emphasizes components such as attitude, subjective norms, intention, and perceived behavioral control, providing a suitable framework for development of educational interventions [25]. Multiple studies have confirmed the effectiveness of this theory in predicting behaviors such as hookah smoking [26, 27]. Nonetheless, the Prototype Willingness Model (PWM) appears more effective for behaviors that frequently occur impulsively, rapidly, and subconsciously [28, 29]. This model emphasizes youth decision-making by considering behavioral intentions, willingness, and the influence of social prototypes [30]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the PWM performs satisfactorily in explaining high-risk behaviors such as cigarette and hookah use among adolescents and young adults [31, 32]. Therefore, integrating the TPB and the PWM can provide a coherent framework for understanding and intervening in high-risk behaviors [33, 34]. Caputo et al. (2020) reported that the integrated model was more successful in predicting adolescent alcohol use than single models [35]. Similarly, Jou & Hsu (2025) emphasized the effectiveness of the integrated model in predicting risky driving behaviors [36]. However, there is a lack of evidence regarding the effectiveness of the integrated model on hookah use, especially among females. In Iran, most studies have focused on male students and employed only single theoretical models [37]. The present study aimed to investigate the effect of an educational program on hookah smoking among female university students.

Methods

Study design, setting and sample size

This quasi-experimental, two-group study with a pretest-posttest design was conducted from October 2024 to January 2025 on female university students residing in four dormitories at a medical university in southeastern Iran. Based on a 95% confidence level and 80% statistical power, a sample size of 34 participants was calculated [37]. To enhance the precision of the study and increase its statistical power to 97% while maintaining a 95% confidence level, the sample size was adjusted to 42 participants. Considering a potential 20% dropout rate, 50 participants were ultimately enrolled in each group.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were willingness to participate in the study and having at least two academic semesters remaining before graduation. Absence from more than one session, participation in similar educational programs, and unwillingness to continue participation were considered as exclusion criteria.

Instruments

Data were collected using a demographic information form, and a self-developed questionnaire based on the integrated model of TPB and PWM. The demographic information form included items on age, age at first use (if they have ever smoked hookah), education level, field of study, marital status, and perceived family economic status.

The self-developed questionnaire was used to measure integrated model variables (attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, prototype favorability, prototype similarity, behavioral willingness, behavioral intention, and hookah smoking behavior). The 47-item questionnaire was developed based on prior tools assessing hookah consumption using the TPB and the PWM [38, 39] (Supplemental File 1). It was evaluated for content validity by seven public health nursing experts using a four-point rating scale (1 = not relevant to 4 = extremely relevant). A content validity index (CVI) value ≥ 0.78 was considered adequate [40]. The findings confirmed content validity with a CVI = 0.86. Moreover, confirmatory factor analysis was used to evaluate the construct validity of the scale. The results indicated an excellent model fit (χ²/df = 2.815, CFI = 0.959, TLI = 0.955, RMSEA = 0.055) (Supplemental File 2).

The reliability of the scale was examined using the stability (test-retest) and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha coefficient). Test-retest reliability over a two-week interval with 30 female students was r = 0.83. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was 0.91. The alpha coefficients for its subscales were as follows. These students did not participate in the study.

Attitude

A total of 11 questions were used, along with a five-option Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Agree) to 5 (Strongly Disagree), with a score range of 11–55. For example, the statement " Hookah use is enjoyable for me” was included. Higher scores indicate a less favorable attitude among students toward hookah use. The scale showed excellent reliability (α = 0.95).

Subjective norms

A total of 6 questions were used, along with a five-option Likert scale ranging from “Always” to “Never”, with a score range of 6–30. Three questions addressed the perspectives of family members or friends regarding students’ hookah use (“I should never use it” to “I should use it”), while the remaining three questions evaluated their family or friends’ influence on their decision-making(“Always” to “Never”). The reliability was satisfactory (α = 0.89).

Perceived behavioral control

A total of 10 questions were used, with a five-option Likert scale ranging from “Always” to “Never”, with a score range of 10–50. For example, “If I have access to hookah, I will smoke it.” Higher scores indicated lower self-control regarding hookah consumption. The measure was reliable (α = 0.96).

Prototype perceptions

Participants were first provided with a description of the female hookah smoker prototype. This prototype is influenced by social media, narratives from peers, and occasional visits to hookah lounges in her hometown.Afterwards, they assessed how similar they felt to the prototype and evaluated its favorability. The prototype is described in the questionnaire (Supplemental File 1).

Prototype favorability was assessed using five items. Participants rated their favorability toward female hookah smokers, who were perceived as exhibiting traits such as intelligence, inexperience, confidence, independence, and self-centeredness. Responses were ranked using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “Very much” to “Never”. Higher score indicated a more positive favorability regarding hookah smokers. The Cronbach’s alpha indicated excellent reliability (α = 0.90).

Prototype similarity was measured using five items assessing respondents’ perceived similarity to female hookah smokers. Participants indicated whether the following characteristics of hookah smokers—intelligence, inexperience, confidence, independence, and self-centeredness—described them. Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from “Very much” to “Never,” with higher scores reflecting greater perceived similarity to female hookah smokers. The measure was reliable (α = 0.93).

Behavioral willingness

This section involved a scenario in which participants imagined being among friends who use hookah and were persuading them to try it. The likelihood of the participant’s response to this scenario was assessed through four possible reactions. For example, “Take the hose and smoke two or three puffs.” Responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘Never’ to ‘Very much’. Scores ranged from 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating greater willingness to smoke hookah at the suggestion of a friend. The Cronbach’s alpha indicated good reliability (α = 0.92).

Behavioral intention

A total of three questions were used, along with a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Very much” to “Never” with a score range of 3–15. For example, “It is likely that I will smoke hookah in the next month.” Higher scores indicated greater participants inclination toward hookah consumption. The reliability was satisfactory (α = 0.88).

Hookah smoking behavior

Hookah smoking was assessed using three questions: [1] ‘Have you smoked hookah in the past month?’ (Yes/No); [2] ‘On average, how often have you smoked hookah in the past month?’ (4-point Likert scale ranging from ‘Every day’ to ‘Rarely’); and [3] ‘On average, how long does each hookah session typically last?’ (4-point Likert scale ranging from ‘Less than 10 minutes’ to ‘More than 40 minutes’). Responses to all three questions were summarized using frequency distributions. The scale demonstrated good reliability (α = 0.94).

Data collection

In the present study, there were no missing data, and the response rate was 100%. The educational intervention was delivered to the intervention group through four face-to-face sessions, each lasting 60 to 70 min, held in the dormitories. These sessions were conducted in groups of 15 to 18 participants using interactive methods such as group discussion, brainstorming, role-playing, slide presentations, and educational videos.

The session content was designed based on an initial needs assessment and aligned with the constructs of the integrated model (Fig. 2), covering topics such as raising awareness about the adverse effects of hookah, correcting misconceptions, training skills to resist social pressure, analyzing the role of environmental factors and social norms in hookah use, and examining factors influencing behavioral decisions (Table 1) [41–43]. The content validity—regarding adequacy and alignment with the study objectives—was confirmed by three experts. The educational sessions were delivered by a community health nurse with a master’s degree, under the supervision of a faculty member holding a PhD in community health nursing. At the end of the program, participants received an educational booklet derived from the session content. Three months post-intervention, a posttest was conducted in both groups. The full educational content was subsequently made available to the control group.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics were used to report mean values, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Inferential statistics included paired t-tests for within-group comparisons, independent t-tests for between-group comparisons, chi-square tests for comparing the frequencies of demographic variables and hookah smoking, and the Mann–Whitney U test for the average duration of hookah smoking. All analyses were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Table 3 presents the mean scores of the integrated model’s constructs before and three months after intervention in both groups. Independent t-tests indicated no significant differences between the two groups in mean scores of all constructs before the intervention. Additionally, behavioral intention to avoid hookah smoking significantly increased (P = 0.03). However, no significant differences were observed in prototype similarity (P = 0.81).

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the effect of an educational intervention based on the integrated model of the TPB and the PWM on hookah smoking among female university students residing in dormitories. According to the results, the model-based educational intervention significantly reduced positive attitudes toward hookah smoking in the intervention group, whereas no significant change was observed in the control group. This finding aligns with the results of studies conducted by Khani-Jeihooni et al. (2020) and Shahabi et al.(2024) [38, 44]. Common features among these studies included the application of behavioral theories, the use of active and interactive educational methods, and the design of content tailored to the target audience’s characteristics. These factors played a crucial role in enhancing cognitive engagement, revising misconceptions, and consequently modifying attitudes, thereby contributing to intervention effectiveness. In contrast, the findings of Joveini et al. (2020) were not consistent with those of the present study [37]. Compared to the study by Joveini et al., which primarily involved male students who used hookah, the present study also included non-users. This broader inclusion may account for a higher likelihood of positive attitudes among participants. Attitudes are shaped by various factors, including knowledge, prior experiences, and personal beliefs [45]. Furthermore, while Joveini’s follow-up period was limited to one month, the current study extended to three months. Given that attitudinal shifts typically require more time to emerge, this longer interval may partially explain the observed differences in outcomes.

The second construct, subjective norms encouraging hookah use, significantly decreased in the intervention group following the educational intervention. Gimenez et al. (2023) and Bashirian et al. (2019) also emphasized the effectiveness of behavioral theory-based education in reducing social pressure and reconstructing incorrect norms related to tobacco use [46, 47]. Additionally, previous studies have confirmed the critical role of family and peer groups in shaping attitudes and behaviors related to hookah use [48, 49]. Overall, the design of the present intervention, which focused on revising individuals’ perceptions of social norms and reducing the legitimacy of hookah use, significantly decreased social pressure among female students. Life in Iranian university dormitories, particularly for female students, is influenced by a set of gender norms, cultural restrictions, and supervisory structures. Gender segregation, restrictions on freedom of movement, and limitations on interactions with the opposite sex are some of the policies enforced in these settings. Such environments can reinforce feelings of social exclusion, lack of autonomy, and limited social experiences among female students, especially when their gender identity or lifestyle does not align with prevailing norms [50].

Another notable finding of the study was the significant increase in behavioral intention to avoid hookah smoking in the intervention group. In contrast, no significant differences between groups were observed prior to the intervention. Asiedua et al. (2025) and Khani-Jeihooni et al. (2020) also reported that educational interventions based on behavioral theories resulted in increased intention to prevent or avoid hookah use among students [12, 38]. Furthermore, the relationship between behavioral intention and tobacco use has been confirmed as a predictive factor in several studies [26, 51].Therefore, integrating key components such as attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control into the design of educational interventions can improve behavioral intentions and reduce tobacco consumption. Conversely, Angeli et al. (2024) found that a smoking cessation educational program did not significantly impact smoking intention among students [52]. While their study focused on school-aged adolescents, an age characterized by heightened emotions and increased propensity for high-risk behaviors, the present study targeted university students in medical-related fields. Consequently, these students’ greater awareness and understanding of risky behaviors, such as hookah use, may differ substantially from that of younger adolescents.

The educational intervention led to a significant reduction in perceived behavioral control related to hookah use in the intervention group. This finding is consistent with previous studies that highlighted the impact of education on reducing perceived risk and dependence on hookah [27, 53]. In the present study, using active and interactive methods in student groups played a crucial role in enhancing cognitive and behavioral skills, thereby improving perceived behavioral control. However, Bashirian et al. (2023) and Asiedua et al.(2025) reported that their interventions did not lead to changes in perceived behavioral control [12, 54]. This difference may stem from their use of a single theoretical model in their studies, compared to the integrated model applied in the present study. Furthermore, variations in gender, and the socio-cultural backgrounds of participants could also play a role. Academic stress, separation from family, peer pressure, strict dormitory regulations, and restrictive norms for women transform dormitory life into an unattractive and monotonous environment with rigid rules. Male students, in contrast, are not subject to these restrictions. Hookah smoking is prohibited in female dormitories, unlike in male dormitories. This disparity, combined with other factors, may increase female students’ inclination toward hookah use or their perception of it as a social norm. Thus, hookah use among female dormitory residents is influenced by a complex interplay of cultural and social factors that must be carefully considered when designing and implementing prevention or cessation strategies.

In this study, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of prototype similarity before and after the intervention. In other words, whether or not they smoked hookah, participants did not perceive themselves as personally similar to hookah users. This finding is consistent with previous studies [55, 56]. Conversely, the results indicated a significant decrease in prototype faviorability following the intervention. This finding is consistent with numerous studies which demonstrated that reducing the social attractiveness of hookah smoking can effectively change youths’ attitudes [54, 56, 57]. Prototype favorability reflects students’ evaluations of the personality traits of hookah users, whereas prototype similarity refers to female students’ perceptions of how much they resemble those users. Social unacceptability surrounding hookah smoking among university students, may explain why female students were reluctant to identify themselves as similar to hookah smokers, even if they themselves smoked. As an educated and socially respected group, students may be hesitant to associate themselves with hookah users. On the other hand, given that all constructs were assessed through self-report measures, it is possible that students did perceive similarities, but were reluctant to report it.

The intervention also led to a significant reduction in behavioral willingness to engage in hookah smoking, aligning with findings from several previous studies [56, 57]. The PWM, emphasizing mental imagery and affective responses to behavior, seeks to reduce susceptibility to hookah smoking by reconstructing these initial prototypes.

Regarding the behavioral pattern of hookah smoking, despite significant reductions in the mean frequency and duration of smoking in the intervention group, the decrease in prevalence of hookah users was not statistically significant. This finding is similar to those reported by Jehi et al. (2024) and Aghdam et al. (2021) [58, 59]. It appears that the persistence of hookah smoking behavior, along with environmental factors such as peer pressure and easy accessibility, necessitates longer-term and multidimensional interventions. In contrast, The findings of a study showed significant reduction in alcohol use, which may be attributed to differences in target populations such as age, gender, type of substance or intervention types [60]. Specifically, cultural and motivational differences in response to educational programs may influence outcomes, particularly between male and female students. Nevertheless, the reductions in frequency and duration of hookah use observed in the present study are consistent with findings from Shahabi et al. (2024) and Khani-Jeihooni et al. (2020), both of whom emphasized the effectiveness of cognitive-behavioral theories [38, 44]. This concordance suggests that emphasizing attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control can modify behavioral patterns and promote more informed decision-making.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling within dormitories may have introduced self-selection bias, as some participants may have been reluctant to be identified as hookah smokers, while others were more willing to share their experiences. In addition, dormitories assigned to the intervention and control groups may have shared overlapping social networks, which raises the possibility of cross-group influence. These factors, along with the restriction of participants to dormitory residents at the University of Medical Sciences, limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly to females outside of medical fields or non-dormitory settings.

Second, the reliance on self-reported data may have led to underreporting of hookah use, despite conducting the sessions in a supportive and non-judgmental environment. Notably, no sample attrition occurred during the present study. To minimize self-report bias and strengthen accuracy, future studies may consider incorporating biochemical validation methods, such as cotinine testing. Saliva testing for cotinine detection is considered a reliable method for assessing tobacco use [61].

Finally, the effectiveness of the intervention should be assessed over longer periods. Longitudinal studies could provide greater insight into the sustained impact of educational interventions on hookah use. Factors such as accessibility and social acceptance are deeply rooted in cultural structures and macro-level policies; therefore, they are less likely to be influenced by short-term educational interventions focused primarily on individual-level dimensions.

Conclusion

Based on the integrated model of the TPB and the PWM, the findings demonstrated that the educational intervention led to significant reductions in positive attitudes toward hookah smoking, perceived behavioral control, prototype favorability, behavioral willingness, and the frequency and duration of hookah use. However, changes in the prevalence of hookah users and prototype similarity were not statistically significant. These results suggest that educational interventions should focus on reducing the social attractiveness of hookah use, reinforcing negative attitudes toward it, and providing positive alternative role models. Incorporating social support and maintaining educational efforts over longer periods may further enhance the effectiveness of such interventions in modifying hookah-related behaviors. The findings suggest that interventions based on integrated models, rather than single models, may more effectively target multiple constructs and produce better outcomes for high-risk behaviors.
