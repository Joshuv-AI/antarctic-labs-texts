Noise Resilience and Robust Convergence Guarantees for the Variational Quantum Eigensolver
Mirko Legnini, Julian Berberich

Abstract
Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) are a class of hybrid quantum-classical algorithms that leverage on classical optimization tools to find the optimal parameters for a parameterized quantum circuit. One relevant application of VQAs is the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE), which aims at steering the output of the quantum circuit to the ground state of a certain Hamiltonian. Recent works have provided global convergence guarantees for VQEs under suitable local surjectivity and smoothness hypotheses, but little has been done in characterizing convergence of these algorithms when the underlying quantum circuit is affected by noise. In this work, we characterize the effect of different coherent and incoherent noise processes on the optimal parameters and the optimal cost of the VQE, and we study their influence on the convergence guarantees of the algorithm. Our work provides novel theoretical insight into the behavior of parameterized quantum circuits. Furthermore, we accompany our results with numerical simulations implemented via Pennylane.



I
Introduction

Over the last few years quantum technologies have been evolving at a steadfast pace, leading to significant improvements in hardware capabilities.
Currently available quantum computers are commonly referred to as Near Intermediate Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices
[
29
]
.
The main limitations of these devices are the number of physical qubits, currently in the hundreds, and the sensitivity to noise.
These limitations render large scale applications (e.g.
[
31
]
) hard to perform, and the research community is putting significant effort into finding applications that can provide near-term quantum advantage
[
21
,
1
]
.
Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs)
[
9
]
are one widely studied approach to this end.
In this hybrid approach, the idea is to build a parameterized quantum circuit and employ a classical iterative optimization routine to find the set of optimal parameters that minimizes a cost function encoding the problem.
VQAs have applications, among others, in quantum chemistry
[
12
,
16
]
, combinatorial optimization problems
[
13
]
, and linear algebra
[
8
]
.
Despite their widespread success, a main obstacle to the application of VQAs is the complexity of the underlying optimization problem. It has been shown that finding a global minimizer for the VQA cost is, in general, an NP-hard non-convex problem
[
7
]
.
Noticeably, the structure of VQAs is very similar to that of quantum optimal control problems
[
24
,
5
]
. Quantum optimal control landscapes have been widely studied
[
10
]
, and have already been related with the
optimization landscape of VQAs
[
15
,
18
]
. Conditions for convergence to a global minimum are however still unclear. Results under suitable convexity assumptions can be found in
[
17
,
32
]
.
Furthermore, the benefits of overparameterization have been highlighted via a dynamical Lie Algebra-based approach
[
23
]
. Recently,
[
35
]
proposed global convergence guarantees for the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) based on local surjectivity.
The VQE is a commonly utilized VQA
[
28
,
33
]
, where the cost function is chosen as a quantum measurement with respect to some observable
O
, and the goal of the parameterized circuit is to steer the system to its ground state.

One reason why VQAs are a good fit to NISQ hardware is their inherent robustness to coherent errors. In particular, it can be shown that, for systematic gate biases,
the error introduced by the biases can be automatically corrected by the algorithm by converging to a different parameter vector, as long as the error respects the circuit symmetries
[
25
]
.
Robustness of VQAs against specific rotation errors and its links to generalizability in quantum machine learning has been studied in
[
4
,
3
]
.
A different angle to look at robustness of VQAs is understanding how the optimal parameters change due to noise. For some applications, e.g. Variational Quantum Compiling (VQC)
[
20
]
, we are more interested in the set of parameters
to which we converge than in the optimal cost. The case of VQC was studied in
[
30
]
, where they found that
optimal parameters remain unchanged for specific error classes.
The question of how general errors affect VQEs optimal parameters remains largely unanswered.

The contribution of this work is twofold. First, we derive upper bounds for the distance between the optimal parameters of a nominal VQE and the corresponding noise-perturbed case, second, we study its noisy convergence properties.
In order to derive upper bounds, we define a ”perturbation level” for both coherent and incoherent noise processes. We then show that the
optimal parameter distance grows at most polynomially with the perturbation level.
We further show that, for locally surjective parameterized architectures, the error bound grows linearly with the perturbation level.
This approach can be applied to a much wider variety of noise models and algorithms than existing works on parameter resilience.
Additionally, we show that some noise classes do not affect the convergence properties of the algorithm.
We therefore state conditions that ensure robust convergence to a neighborhood of the nominal optimal parameter set.
Last, we discuss additional results for the specific cases of depolarization and coherent control errors.

II-A

Variational Quantum Algorithms

A general quantum circuit can be represented as a sequential application of unitaries

\displaystyle U=\prod_{j=1}^{L}U_{j},

(1)

where
U_{j}=e^{-iH_{j}}
for a suitable Hermitian generator
H_{j}\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}
.

In VQAs, we consider optimization problems of the form

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{\theta}\in\Theta}\ell(\mathbf{\theta})=\mathrm{Tr}[OU(\mathbf{\theta})\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\theta})],

(2)

where
\rho_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}
is an easy-to-prepare
d
-dimensional input state,
O\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}
an observable, and
the parameter space
\Theta
can be, for example,
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, or the
p
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{p}
.

U(\mathbf{\theta})
is a parameterization of
U
as expressed in (
1
).
We call
\Theta^{*}\subset\Theta
the set of globally optimal parameter values.

Typically, the unitaries are represented as

\displaystyle U_{j}(\mathbf{\theta}_{j})=\prod_{k=1}^{p_{j}}e^{-i\theta_{j,k}H_{j,k}}

(3)

with
H_{j,k}\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}
for
k=1,...,p_{j}
being the set of Hermitian generators for the
j
-th layer. Examples of architectures that satisfy this structure are QAOA
[
13
]
, the hardware-efficient ansatz
[
19
]
and the Variational Hamiltonian ansatz
[
37
]
.
Another possible structure for the layers is

\displaystyle U_{j}(\mathbf{\theta}_{j})=e^{-iA_{j}(\mathbf{\theta_{j}})}

(4)

\displaystyle A_{j}(\mathbf{\theta}_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{p_{j}}\theta_{j,k}H_{j,k},

(5)

This takes the name of
SU(N)
ansatz, as
for
p_{j}\geq N
and suitable choice of
\{H_{j,k}|k=1,\dots,p_{j}\}
this structure generates the special unitary group of dimension
N

[
36
]
.

Throughout this paper, we assume without loss of generality that
\min_{\mathbf{\theta}\in\Theta}\ell(\mathbf{\theta})=0
.
In case the minimum is a different value
\ell^{*}
, we can always shift the cost function as
\ell^{\prime}(\mathbf{\theta})=\ell(\theta)-\ell^{*}
for our analysis.

For compactness of representation, we define
\rho(\mathbf{\theta})=U(\mathbf{\theta})\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\theta})
.
It is convenient to consider the cost function as the composition of the map
U:\Theta\rightarrow SU(d)
and a cost functional
L:SU(d)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
In view of this, the partial derivatives of (
2
) can be represented as
[
35
]

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\ell(\theta)=\braket{\text{grad\,}L[U(\mathbf{\theta})],\Omega_{j}(\mathbf{\theta})},

(6)

with

\displaystyle\text{grad\,}L[U(\theta)]=[O,\rho(\mathbf{\theta})]\text{, and}

(7)

\displaystyle\Omega_{j}(\mathbf{\theta})=U^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\theta})\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}U(\mathbf{\theta}).

(8)

Here,
\text{grad\,}L[U(\mathbf{\theta})]
is the Riemannian gradient of the cost functional
L
and
\Omega_{j}(\mathbf{\theta})
the rate of change of the unitary with respect to the variation of the parameter.
Both vectors are elements of the tangent space at the identity of
SU(d)
.

In this work, we will focus on locally surjective parameterized unitaries. We start by giving a definition of local surjectivity.

Definition 1
.

A parameterized unitary transformation
U(\mathbf{\theta})
is said to be locally surjective if

\displaystyle\mathrm{span}\{\Omega_{j}(\mathbf{\theta})|j=1,\dots,p\}=\mathfrak{su}(d),\forall\mathbf{\theta}\in\Theta.

(9)

In other words, local surjectivity guarantees that it is always possible to move along any direction of the special unitary group manifold by varying some parameter
\theta_{j}
.
Locally surjective unitary transformations have been constructed in
[
35
]
.

It is important to remark that the solution to the optimization problem (
2
) is in general non-unique. This could be due both to a degenerate ground state of the observable
O

or to the non-injectivity of the Ansatz.
A relevant distinction to characterize optimal solutions for VQEs is whether the cost
\ell(\theta)
is a Morse-Bott function, which is defined as follows
[
26
, Def 2.6.1]
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a smooth manifold and
f:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function. Then
f
is said to be
Morse-Bott, if:

1.

The set of critical points of
f
is a disjoint union of connected, smooth submanifolds
\mathcal{M}_{k}
such that

f
is constant on each component, and

2.

for each
p\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
the kernel of the Hessian of
f
at
p
is exactly the tangent space
T_{p}\mathcal{M}_{k}
at
p\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
.

This can be interpreted as a function whose critical points are found in manifolds and whose local behavior is quadratic in directions
transverse to the critical set itself. In this sense, it can be interpreted as a relaxation of isolated minima with positive-definite Hessian.

II-B

Quantum Noise

In the following, we introduce the noise sources we deal with in this work.
We start by defining coherent errors.

Given an ideal quantum circuit (
1
), a coherent error
U_{\mathrm{e},j}=e^{-iH_{\mathrm{e},j}}
is a unitary operator such that
the resulting, noise-affected quantum circuit reads

\displaystyle\tilde{U}=\prod_{j}^{L}\tilde{U_{j}}=\prod_{i}^{L}U_{j}U_{\mathrm{e},j}.

(10)

The generators
H_{\mathrm{e},j}
are in general not known, and can be different for each layer
j
. It is however assumed that they are elements of a known set
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{e},j}
.
For example,
U_{\mathrm{e},j}
could be a single-qubit Pauli rotation with unknown but bounded angle.

We proceed by introducing incoherent quantum errors.
An ideal quantum circuit (
1
) affected by incoherent errors can be described by means of the quantum operation

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{L}\circ\mathcal{E}_{L-1}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{E}_{1},

(11)

such that each layer can be described as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{j}(\rho)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}E_{k}U_{j}\rho U^{\dagger}_{j}E^{\dagger}_{k},

(12)

for some set of evolution operators
E_{k}
. Quantum operations can model, for example, Pauli channels, depolarization and amplitude damping. An overview of incoherent errors can be found in
[
27
, Chapter 8]
.

III
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we derive upper bounds for the distance between the nominal and noisy optimal parameters for a VQE and we study noisy convergence properties.
In subsection III.A, we introduce the perturbed observable VQE problem and derive upper bounds on
the distance between any optimal parameter of the perturbed problem and the optimal parameter set of the
nominal problem, and we comment on implications for convergence.
In subsection III.B, we focus on coherent noise. We show that VQEs under coherent noise can be interpreted as noise-free VQEs with perturbed observables, such that the results from Section III.A apply.
We derive an analogous result for incoherent noise in subsection III.C.
In subsection III.D, we show that, in case of depolarization noise, the two optimal decision variables coincide.
We additionally prove a tighter bound on parameters for coherent control errors that can be used to inform robust VQE design.

III-A

Perturbed observable

In the following, we consider the perturbed VQE

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\tilde{\ell}(\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}[(O+\epsilon\tilde{O}(\theta))U(\mathbf{\theta})\rho_{0}U(\mathbf{\theta})^{\dagger}]

(13)

with perturbation observable
\tilde{O}(\theta)\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}
, which may depend on
\theta
, and we define
\epsilon>0
as the perturbation level.
We call
\tilde{\theta}_{\epsilon}^{*}
any solution of (
13
) .
In this setup, we can formulate our main result.

Theorem 1
.

Let (
2
) have a compact parameter space
\Theta
and suppose there exists
M_{1},M_{2}>0
such that
||\tilde{O}(\theta)||<M_{1}
and
||\nabla\tilde{O}(\theta)||<M_{2}
for each
\theta\in\Theta
.
Then there exist constants
C,\bar{\epsilon}>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}({\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon},\Theta^{*}})\leq C\epsilon^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\mathrm{,if}\,\epsilon<\bar{\epsilon}.

(14)

Furthermore, if
U(\theta)
is locally surjective,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}({\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon},\Theta^{*}})\leq C\epsilon.

(15)

Proof.

The proof is structured in two steps.
The first step of the proof is showing that
\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon}
is in a neighborhood of
\Theta^{*}
for
\epsilon
sufficiently small.
The second step provides the convergence rate.

Step 1 - Continuity bound:

We start by defining a sequence
\{\epsilon_{k}\}_{k}
such that
\epsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0
.
We then define the set of optimal parameters
\tilde{\Theta}_{\epsilon_{k}}^{*}
for the perturbed cost
\tilde{\ell(\theta)}

for a specific element of the sequence
\epsilon_{k}
via

\displaystyle\tilde{\Theta}_{\epsilon_{k}}^{*}=\mathrm{arg\,min\,\,}\ell(\theta)+\epsilon_{k}g(\theta),

(16)

with
g(\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{O}(\theta)U(\theta)\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\theta)]
.
We call an arbitrary element in this set
\tilde{\theta}_{\epsilon_{k}}^{*}\in\tilde{\Theta}_{\epsilon_{k}}^{*}
.
Let
\theta^{*}\in\Theta^{*}_{0}
be an arbitrary optimal parameter for the nominal cost
\ell(\theta)
.
By definition of optimality, we have

\displaystyle\ell(\tilde{\theta}_{\epsilon_{k}}^{*})+\epsilon_{k}g(\tilde{\theta}_{\epsilon_{k}}^{*})\leq\ell(\theta^{*})+\epsilon_{k}g(\theta^{*}).

(17)

By observing that
\ell(\theta^{*})=0
and rearranging the terms, we get

\displaystyle\ell(\tilde{\theta}_{\epsilon_{k}}^{*})\leq\epsilon_{k}(g(\theta^{*})-g(\tilde{\theta}_{\epsilon_{k}}^{*}))

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{k}(||g(\theta^{*})||+||-g(\tilde{\theta}_{\epsilon_{k}}^{*})||)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{k}(||\tilde{O}(\theta^{*})||+||\tilde{O}(\theta^{*})||)

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon_{k}M_{1},

(18)

where we used the fact that
\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{O}(\theta)U(\theta)\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\theta)]\leq||\tilde{O}(\theta)||
.

Taking the limit we get

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\ell(\tilde{\theta}_{\epsilon_{k}}^{*})\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}2\epsilon_{k}M_{1}=0.

(19)

Compactness of
\Theta
guarantees the existence of a limit point
\bar{\theta}\in\Theta
for the sequence
\{\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon_{k}}\}_{k}
.
With this fact and by continuity of
\ell
we can state that
\ell(\bar{\theta})=0
, and therefore
\bar{\theta}\in\Theta^{*}_{0}
.

This shows that
\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon}
approaches the set
\Theta^{*}_{0}
asymptotically as
\epsilon
decreases.
Formally,

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathrm{dist}(\tilde{\theta}_{\epsilon_{k}}^{*},\Theta^{*})=0.

(20)

This means that, for
\epsilon
small enough,
\tilde{\theta}_{\epsilon}^{*}
lies arbitrarily close to
\Theta^{*}
.

Step 2 - Convergence rate:
In this step, we derive the convergence rate.
By the first order optimality condition, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\ell(\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon})=-\epsilon\nabla g(\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon}).

(21)

Since
\ell
is a real analytic function, we can now use the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz inequalities.
In particular
[
22
, Corollary 2]
states that there exist constants
K>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle||\nabla\ell(\tilde{\theta}^{*})||\geq{K}\mathrm{dist}({\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon},\Theta^{*}})^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}

(22)

holds if
||\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon}-\bar{\theta}||<r_{0}
, with
\bar{\theta}\in\Theta^{*}
, that is if
\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon}
is in a neighborhood of
\Theta^{*}
.
By (
20
), for each
r
we can always find
\epsilon_{r}
such that
\mathrm{dist}(\tilde{\theta}_{\epsilon_{r}}^{*},\Theta^{*})<r
.
We define
\bar{\epsilon}=\epsilon_{r_{0}}
.
If
\epsilon<\bar{\epsilon}
, we can manipulate (
22
) to get

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}({\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon},\Theta^{*}})\leq(\frac{1}{K}||\nabla\ell(\tilde{\theta}^{*})||^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}).

(23)

Then, substituting (
21
) in (
23
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}({\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon},\Theta^{*}})\leq(\frac{1}{K}||-\epsilon\nabla g(\tilde{\theta}^{*})||)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=(\frac{1}{K}||\nabla g(\tilde{\theta}^{*})||)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\epsilon^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq(\frac{1}{K}(2\sqrt{L}||H_{\max}||\,M_{1}+M_{2}))^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\epsilon^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=C\epsilon^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}},

(24)

Where we used the facts that
||\nabla g(\theta)||\leq 2\sqrt{L}||H_{\max}||\,||\tilde{O}(\theta)||\penalty\ +\penalty\ M_{2},
and
||\tilde{O}(\theta)||\leq M_{1}
.
By
||H_{\max}||
we indicate the maximum Hamiltonian norm
||H_{\max}||=\max_{j}||H_{j}||
.
This proves the first statement.

To prove the second statement,
[
35
, Appendix C]
proves that, if
U(\theta)
is locally surjective,
\ell(\theta)
is a Morse-Bott function. This guarantees
that the exponent
\alpha=1/2

[
14
]
. The result then follows from (
24
).
∎

Visual representation of the set of unperturbed optimal parameters
\Theta^{*}
and the set
\tilde{\Theta}^{*}_{\epsilon}

In theorem 1 we guarantee a threshold for the error in the parameter space
\mathrm{dist}({\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon},\Theta^{*}})
for small perturbations.
We do so by providing an upper bound on the variation of the cost function
\ell(\theta^{*}_{\epsilon})
,
and using a continuity argument and compactness of the parameter set
to show that the perturbed optimal parameter lies in a neighborhood of the nominal optimal parameter set.
The situation is illustrated in Figure
1
.
Note that the assumption that
||\tilde{O}(\theta)||
and
||\nabla\tilde{O}(\theta)||
are bounded holds for arbitrary VQEs affected by general coherent errors or incoherent errors (the latter subject to conditions discussed in Section III.C).
We then show that, for small perturbations, we can derive a polynomial scaling

\mathrm{dist}({\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\epsilon},\Theta^{*}})\leq C\epsilon^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}
.

The proof relies on Łojasiewicz inequalities, which are used to characterize the behavior of analytic functions close to critical sets
and are widely employed in optimization theory
[
11
]
.
The Łojasiewicz exponent
\alpha
in (
22
) gives information on the conditioning of a generic (possibly non-convex) optimization problem near the set of minimizers. In particular, it is related to the exponent of the polynomial growth of the function around its minimum.
In the nondegenerate case (the cost function having an isolated minimum with a positive definite Hessian or satisfying a quadratic growth condition around optimal values) the landscape is locally strongly convex with
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
.
Theorem 1 shows that in this case perturbations of the cost induce proportional perturbations on the optimal parameters.
On the other hand, if the optimization landscape is flat around the optimal parameter set
\theta^{*}
, we have that
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
. In this case, it follows that
{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}<1
,
which implies that the slope of
\epsilon^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}
at 0 increases. Therefore the sensitivity of errors in the parameter space to perturbations is higher.

We showed that the optimal value of the perturbed problem differs from the nominal one with an upper bound that increases with the perturbation level.
Next, we show that convergence properties from the noise-free VQE transfer to the noisy one,
if the perturbation observable is constant over
\theta
.
Sufficient conditions to guarantee avoidance of local minima are given in
[
35
, Theorem 1]
. This result states that, if
U(\theta)
is surjective and locally surjective,
the parameters of a VQE either converge to a global minimum or diverge.
For perturbed VQEs, we can state the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Let
U(\theta)
be a surjective and locally surjective map used as a VQE ansatz to solve (
13
).
Let
\tilde{O}(\theta)=\tilde{O}
be constant. Then for almost all initial conditions
\theta_{0}
the algorithm either diverges
or converges to a ground state of
O+\epsilon\tilde{O}
.

Proof.

The partial derivatives of (
13
) read

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\ell(\theta)=\braket{\text{grad\,}\tilde{L}[U(\mathbf{\theta})],\Omega_{j}(\mathbf{\theta})}+\mathrm{Tr}[\rho(\theta)\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\tilde{O}(\theta)]

\displaystyle=\braket{\text{grad\,}\tilde{L}[U(\mathbf{\theta})],\Omega_{j}(\mathbf{\theta})},

(25)

where

\displaystyle\text{grad\,}\tilde{L}[U(\theta)]=[O+\epsilon\tilde{O},\rho(\mathbf{\theta})]\text{,}

(26)

and
\Omega_{j}(\theta)
is defined as in the nominal case and we used the fact that
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\tilde{O}(\theta)=0
for each
\theta
.
The result follows applying
[
35
, Theorem 1]
.
∎

Corollary 1 shows that perturbations on the observable that are not dependent on the circuit parameters do not affect convergence properties of the VQE.
In the next two subsections, we will derive a way to represent noise processes acting on the VQE as perturbations on the observable.
In combination with the contents of subsections III.A, III.B and III.C, Corollary 1 shows that a noise process acting on the last layer of a VQE recovers the same convergence properties as the nominal one.

III-B

Coherent noise

In this subsection, we introduce our results concerning robustness of the VQE under coherent errors.
In the general case, a coherent error-perturbed VQE reads

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\tilde{\ell}(\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}[O\tilde{U}(\mathbf{\theta,\eta})(\rho_{0})\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\theta,\eta})],

(27)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(\mathbf{\theta,\eta})=\prod_{j=1}^{L}\tilde{U}_{j}(\theta_{j},\eta_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{L}U_{\rm{e},j}(\eta_{j})U_{j}(\theta_{j}),

(28)

with
U_{\rm{e},j}(\eta_{j})=e^{-i\eta_{j}H_{\mathrm{e},j}}
for some Hermitian generator
H_{\mathrm{e},j}\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}
,
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
and
U(\theta_{j})
is the nominal parameterized gate as described in subsection II.A.
In other words, this error models the action of an unknown unitary gate after each layer of the VQA.
We say that a perturbed problem is
associated
to a nominal problem (
2
) if it has the same observable
O
and unitary ansatz
U(\theta)
.
In the following, we show that an error-perturbed VQE can be represented as a perturbed observable problem such that the results from subsection III.A are applicable.

Theorem 2
.

Problem (
27
) is equivalent, up to first order approximation in
\eta
, to (
13
), with
\epsilon=||\eta||_{\infty}
and a suitably defined
\tilde{O}(\theta)
which is bounded and has bounded gradient.

By saying that two optimization problems are equivalent, we mean that they have the same cost
\ell(\theta)
up to scaling or offset.
The precise form of
\tilde{O}(\theta)
is given below.

Theorem 2 will be proven via two intermediate Lemmas below. To this end, we start by focusing on a problem with the structure

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\tilde{\ell}(\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}[OU_{\mathrm{e}}(\eta,\theta){U}(\mathbf{\theta})(\rho_{0}){U}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\theta})U^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{e}}(\eta,\theta)],

(29)

with
U_{\mathrm{e}}(\eta,\theta)=\prod_{j=1}^{L}U_{\mathrm{e},j}(\eta_{j},\theta)
, and
U_{\mathrm{e},j}(\eta_{j},\theta)=e^{-i\eta_{j}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{e},j}(\theta)}
.
Note that here the coherent errors depend on the algorithm parameters
\theta
.
First, we show that this problem can be approximated by a perturbed observable problem, and then we prove that any VQE affected by coherent errors
can be represented in this fashion.

Lemma 1
.

Problem (
29
) is equivalent to (
13
) up to first order approximation in
\eta
, with
\epsilon=||\eta||_{\infty}
and

\tilde{O}(\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\frac{\eta_{j}}{||\eta||_{\infty}}[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{e},j}(\theta),O]
.

Proof.

First, we show that (
29
) is equivalent to (
2
) with a rotated observable.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[OU_{\mathrm{e}}(\eta,\theta){U}(\mathbf{\theta})(\rho_{0}){U}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\theta})U^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{e}}(\eta,\theta)]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[U^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{e}}(\eta,\theta)OU_{\mathrm{e}}(\eta,\theta){U}(\mathbf{\theta})(\rho_{0}){U}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\theta})]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{O}(\eta,\theta)(\rho_{0}){U}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\theta})],

(30)

defining
\hat{O}(\eta,\theta)=U^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{e}}(\eta,\theta)OU_{\mathrm{e}}(\eta,\theta)
.
Taking a first-order approximation of
\hat{O}(\eta,\theta)
in
\eta
we get:

\displaystyle\hat{O}(\eta,\theta)=O+\sum_{j=1}^{L}\eta_{j}[H_{\mathrm{e},j},O]+\mathcal{O}(||\eta||^{2})

\displaystyle=O+||\eta||_{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\frac{\eta_{j}}{||\eta||_{\infty}}[H_{\mathrm{e},j},O]+\mathcal{O}(||\eta||^{2}),

(31)

and we define
\epsilon=||\eta||_{\infty}
.
This proves our statement.
∎

Next, we show that any VQE affected by coherent errors can be rewritten in the form (
29
).
We note that a strategy similar to Lemma 2 was pursued in
[
2
]
for the robustness analysis of generic quantum circuits.
In order to show this, we provide the following definitions:

\displaystyle V_{k,j}(\theta)=\prod_{m=j}^{k}U_{m}(\theta_{m})\text{, for }0<j<k\leq L,\text{ and }

(32)

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{k,j}(\theta,\eta)=\prod_{m=j}^{k}\tilde{U}_{m}(\theta_{m},\eta_{m})\text{, for }0<j<k\leq L.

(33)

It remains to prove that (
27
) can be rewritten as (
29
).
We can do this by exploiting the interaction picture.

Lemma 2
.

Problem (
27
) is equivalent to (
29
) with

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{e},j}(\theta)=V_{L,j}(\theta)H_{\mathrm{e},j}V_{L,j}^{\dagger}(\theta)
.

Proof.

To prove the lemma, we show how noise on one intermediate layer can be pushed onto the observable. The statement follows by iteratively repeating the procedure
over the circuit.
It is possible to show that a circuit
\tilde{U}(\theta,\eta)
with a perturbation acting up to the
j
-th layer can be represented as

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(\theta,\eta)=V_{L,j}(\theta)U_{\mathrm{e,j}}(\eta_{j})\tilde{V}_{j,1}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{e,j}}(\eta_{j},\theta)V_{L,j}(\theta)\tilde{V}_{j,1}(\theta),

(34)

with
\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{e,j}}(\eta_{j},\theta)=V^{\dagger}_{L,j}(\theta)U_{\mathrm{e,j}}(\eta_{j})V_{L,j}(\theta)
.
By Taylor expansion of the matrix exponential, it is easy to check that

\displaystyle V^{\dagger}_{L,j}(\theta)\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{e,j}}(\eta_{j},\theta)V_{L,j}(\theta)=e^{-i\eta_{j}V^{\dagger}_{L,j}(\theta)H_{\mathrm{e,j}}V_{L,j}(\theta)}.

(35)

Defining
\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{e},j}=V^{\dagger}_{L,j}(\theta)H_{\mathrm{e,j}}V_{L,j}(\theta)
,

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(\theta,\eta)=e^{-i\eta_{j}\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{e},j}}U(\theta).

(36)

In case noise is present on each layer, we repeat the procedure for each layer iteratively and our statement is proven.
∎

It remains to show that
\tilde{O}(\theta)
is bounded. This is easily done by checking that it is a finite sum of bounded elements.
The same holds for
\nabla\tilde{O}(\theta)
.
It follows that the hypotheses on
\tilde{O}(\theta)
for Theorem 1 hold for VQEs affected by general coherent errors.

III-C

Incoherent noise

In this subsection, we introduce our results concerning robustness of the VQE under incoherent errors.
We consider incoherent error-perturbed VQEs structured as

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\tilde{\ell}(\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}[O\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\rho_{0})],

(37)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\rho_{0})=\mathcal{E}_{L,\mathbf{\theta}_{L}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{L-1,\mathbf{\theta}_{L-1}}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{E}_{1,\mathbf{\theta}_{1}}(\rho_{0})\text{,}

(38)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{j,\mathbf{\theta}_{j}}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{j}(U_{j}(\mathbf{\theta_{j}})\rho U_{j}(\mathbf{\theta_{j}})^{\dagger})\text{, and }

(39)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{j}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+p\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}E_{k}\rho E_{k}^{\dagger},

(40)

with Kraus operators
E_{k}
with norm
||E_{k}||\leq 1
for each
k
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}=1
.
This quantum operation models the action of incoherent noise after each layer. The only restriction on the noise is that it must leave the input density operator
\rho
unaltered with probability
1-p
.
We proceed with the same approach as subsection III.B. First we show that quantum channels on the output of the circuit can be interpreted as perturbations on the observable, then we
show how quantum channels can be represented as acting on the output.
We begin by stating our main result:

Theorem 3
.

Problem (
37
) is equivalent to (
13
) up to a constant scaling factor of the cost.

We again prove the result via two intermediate lemmas. To this end, we first consider the perturbed VQE

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\tilde{\ell}(\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}[O\mathcal{E}_{L,\theta}(U(\mathbf{\theta})\rho_{0}U(\mathbf{\theta})^{\dagger})],

(41)

with
\mathcal{E_{\theta}(\rho)}=(1-p)\rho+p\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}E_{k}(\theta)\rho E_{k}^{\dagger}(\theta)
,
E_{k}(\theta)
being a Kraus operator with norm
||E_{k}||\leq 1
, possibly depending on the parameters, and
\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}=1
.
First, we show that the noise affecting this problem is equivalent to a perturbation on the observable.

Lemma 3
.

Problem (
41
) is equivalent, up to a scaling factor, to (
13
) with
\epsilon=\frac{p}{1-p}
and

\tilde{O}(\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}E_{k}^{\dagger}(\theta)OE_{k}(\theta)
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the following steps

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}(\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}[O\mathcal{E}(U(\theta)\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\theta))]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[O((1-p)U(\theta)\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\theta)+

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad p\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}E_{k}(\theta)U(\theta)\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\theta)E_{k}^{\dagger}(\theta))]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[((1-p)O+p\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}E_{k}^{\dagger}(\theta)OE_{k}(\theta))U(\theta)\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\theta)]

\displaystyle=(1-p)\mathrm{Tr}[(O+\epsilon\tilde{O}(\theta))U(\theta)\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\theta)],

(42)

where we define
\epsilon=\frac{p}{1-p}
as the perturbation level,

\tilde{O}(\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}E_{k}^{\dagger}(\theta)OE_{k}(\theta)
.
Not that the scaling
1-p
does not affect the optimal decision variable.
∎

Lemma 3 shows that it is possible to represent a parameter-dependent quantum operation acting on the output of the quantum circuit
U(\theta)
as a perturbed observable.
In the following, we show that (
41
) can model any incoherent noise process described by (
37
).

We can state the following lemma:

Lemma 4
.

Problem (
37
) is equivalent to (
41
) with

p=1-\prod_{j}(1-p_{j})
and suitably defined operators
\tilde{E}_{j,k}(\theta)
.

Proof.

The proof can be decomposed in two steps.
First, we show that noise acting on any intermediate layer can be seen as a different quantum operation acting on the
output layer.
Second, we show how to combine nested quantum operations into a single one.
Combining this two results and using them iteratively for each layer, one proofs he lemma.

Itn order to show that mid circuit noises can be represented as a different noise process on the output,
we start defining

\displaystyle\rho_{j}(\theta)=\mathcal{E}_{j-1,\mathbf{\theta}_{j-1}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{j-2,\mathbf{\theta}_{j-2}}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{E}_{1,\mathbf{\theta}_{1}}(\rho_{0}),

(43)

as the (perturbed) density operator at the
j
-th layer of the circuit.
Using this, we can define a VQE affected by incoherent noise up to the
j
-th layer as

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\tilde{\ell}(\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}[OV_{L,j}(\theta)\mathcal{E}_{j}(\rho_{j}(\theta))V_{L,j}^{\dagger}(\theta)],

(44)

we can derive the following

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[OV_{L,j}(\theta)\mathcal{E}_{j}(\rho_{j}(\theta))V_{L,j}^{\dagger}(\theta)]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[OV_{L,j}(\theta)((1-p)\rho_{j}(\theta)+

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad p\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}E_{k}(\theta)\rho_{j}(\theta)E^{\dagger}_{k}(\theta))V_{L,j}^{\dagger}(\theta)]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[O((1-p)V_{L,j}(\theta)\rho_{j}(\theta)V_{L,j}^{\dagger}(\theta)+

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad p\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}V_{L,j}(\theta)E_{k}(\theta)\rho_{j}(\theta)E^{\dagger}_{k}(\theta)V_{L,j}^{\dagger}(\theta))]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[O((1-p)V_{L,j}(\theta)\rho_{j}(\theta)V_{L,j}^{\dagger}(\theta)+

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad p\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}\tilde{E}_{j,k}(\theta)V_{L,j}(\theta)\rho_{j}(\theta)V_{L,j}^{\dagger}(\theta)\tilde{E}^{\dagger}_{j,k}(\theta))]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[O\tilde{\mathcal{E}_{\theta}}(V_{L,j}(\theta)\rho_{j}(\theta)V_{L,j}^{\dagger}(\theta))]

(45)

with
\tilde{E}_{j,k}(\theta)=V_{L,j}(\theta)E_{k}(\theta)V_{L,j}^{\dagger}(\theta)
.

For the second step, we use the well-known fact that the composition of quantum operations is itself a quantum operation.
To derive the error probability
p=1-\prod_{j}(1-p_{j})
it is sufficient to observe that the probability that the input density operator
\rho

remains unaltered in all the quantum operations is
q=\prod_{j}(1-p_{j})
. It follows immediately that the error probability
p=1-q
.
By combining the two results and iterating for each layer we get a structure that matches (
41
).
∎

Lemma 4 can also be used to characterize how the perturbation level changes with increasing circuit depth. Indeed,
assuming constant error probability
p
for each layer, one gets
\epsilon(L)=\frac{1-(1-p)^{L}}{(1-p)^{L}}=(1-p)^{-L}-1
.
This suggests an exponential scaling of the perturbation level with respect to the circuit depth. This can be compared to the scaling
of coherent errors, which is instead linear in
L
.

III-D

Special cases

In this subsection, we discuss specific cases for which we can derive stronger results.

Coherent Control errors
Coherent control errors are multiplicative errors that model imprecisions in the application of the control pulses.
This can happen, for example, in case of miscalibration.
A parameterized unitary
U_{j}(\theta_{j})
affected by a coherent control error is modeled as a unitary

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{j}(\theta_{j},\eta_{j})=e^{-i\theta H_{j}}e^{-i\eta_{j}\theta_{j}H_{j}}=e^{-i(1+\eta_{j})\theta_{j}H_{j}}.

(46)

Since, for this class of errors, the noisy optimal parameters are just a scaling of the nominal ones, it is easy to show that

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\eta,j}=\frac{1}{1+\eta_{j}}\theta^{*}_{j},

(47)

where by
\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\eta,j}
we indicate the
j
-th component of the noisy VQE optimal parameter
\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\eta}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle||\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\eta}-\theta^{*}||\leq||\eta||_{\infty}||\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{\eta}||.

(48)

This is a tight bound that holds globally in the parameter space
\Theta
.
Additionally, from this bound one can see that L2 regularization of the parameter vector
\theta
as proposed in
[
3
]
improves perturbation bounds on optimal parameters as well.

Depolarization Noise
A statement stronger than the bound given by Theorem 1 can be made for the specific case of depolarization noise.
We can define depolarization noise as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{DN}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+{p}I.

(49)

In this case, we can state that

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}^{*}\in\Theta^{*},

(50)

which means that, regardless of the intensity, the optimal parameters for VQEs affected by depolarization noise are the same as the nominal optimal parameters.

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathrm{arg\,min\,\,}_{\theta\in\Theta}\tilde{\ell}(\theta)=\mathrm{arg\,min\,\,}\mathrm{Tr}[O\mathcal{E}(U(\mathbf{\theta})\rho_{0}U(\mathbf{\theta})^{\dagger})]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{arg\,min\,\,}(1-p)\mathrm{Tr}[OU(\mathbf{\theta})\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\theta})]+pTr[O]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{arg\,min\,\,}(1-p)\mathrm{Tr}[OU(\mathbf{\theta})\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\theta})]\in\Theta^{*}

(51)

∎

The result highlights that, since depolarization corresponds essentially to a shrinking of the Bloch sphere, it does not affect the correct rotation for the state. It has to be noted, however, that although it does not affect asymptotic convergence depolarization noise
gives rise to barren plateaus
[
34
]
, thus representing a major obstacle for VQA training.

V
Conclusions and future works

V-A

Conclusions

In this work, we investigated the robustness of optimal parameters for VQEs with respect to quantum noise.
We proved that, if the observable is perturbed, the perturbation on the optimal parameter scales proportionally to the
perturbation level, as long as the perturbation level is small.
We then showed that this formulation includes both general coherent errors and a wide class of incoherent errors.
We proved that, as long as the perturbation observable does not depend on the VQE parameters, the same convergence guarantees as the
nominal VQE can be recovered. Further, we have provided numerical evidence that supports our results.

V-B

Future Works

There are many possible extensions to this work. While Theorem 1 assumed a compact parameter space, studying the non-compact case is practically relevant
and numerical evidence supports the possibility of generalizing our results to this case.
This work could also be extended to other classes of VQAs, potentially including to Quantum Machine Learning applications, where the interest is mainly in the optimal parameters rather than the optimal cost.
Last, the derived analysis results could be used to design a synthesis procedure to obtain inherently robust VQEs.
