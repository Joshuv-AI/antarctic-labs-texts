Tandem Kinase WTK1 Triggers Waves of Transcriptional Reprogramming Underlying Wheat Resistance to Stripe Rust
Authors: Liubov Govta, Nikolai Govta, Evgenii Potapenko, Hanan Sela, Gitta Coaker, Tzion Fahima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTandem kinases are emerging regulators of plant immunity, but the downstream defense programs they activate remain poorly understood. Stripe rust, caused by Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Pst), is a major constraint on global wheat production. The Yr15 resistance gene, derived from wild emmer wheat, encodes the tandem kinase protein WTK1. To define the temporal basis of this resistance, we analysed resistant and susceptible near‐isogenic lines across a 7‐day infection time course using histopathology, ROS phenotyping, RNA sequencing, co‐expression network analysis, and qRT‐PCR validation. WTK1‐mediated resistance limited fungal colonization, enhanced ROS accumulation, and triggered extensive transcriptional reprogramming. We identified 2509 pathogen‐responsive differentially expressed genes, including 1530 genes upregulated in the resistant line, revealing an early phase enriched for ROS and Ca2+ signaling, autophagy‐related processes, and metacaspases consistent with hypersensitive cell death observed in pathogen‐invaded cells. This was followed by later defense reinforcement and metabolic reprogramming in later stages of infection, including pathogenesis‐related proteins, cell‐wall reinforcement, and phytoalexin biosynthesis, consistent with the confinement of Pst to localized necrotic lesions. Our results show that WTK1 triggers coordinated waves of transcriptional reprogramming and co‐expression network responses during infection, providing insight into tandem kinase‐mediated immunity and a framework for improving durable stripe rust resistance in wheat.

Introduction

The ability to rapidly and selectively reprogram transcription is fundamental to the activation of effective plant immune responses to microbial infection. Through evolution, plants have developed sophisticated regulatory networks to precisely control transcription in response to the behavior and virulence strategies of invading pathogens (Tsuda and Somssich 2015; Sørensen et al. 2016). The plant immune system perceives pathogen‐associated molecules and triggers defense activation through interconnected genetic signaling networks. When pathogens are recognized at the cell surface, pattern recognition receptor (PRR)‐triggered immunity (PTI) is initiated. This layer of defense relies on receptor kinases (RKs) and receptor proteins (RPs) that detect pathogen‐derived or host‐derived molecular signatures (Andersen et al. 2018). These surface receptors perceive pathogen‐, damage‐, and microbe‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs, DAMPs, and MAMPs, respectively) as well as specific pathogen‐encoded effectors, and transmit signals by activating their cytoplasmic kinase domains or associated kinases, thereby initiating downstream immune signaling cascades (Goff and Ramonell 2007; Tang et al. 2017; Dievart et al. 2020; Hailemariam et al. 2024; Huang and Joosten 2024). In response to cytoplasmic virulence effectors, plants have developed a specific detection mechanism known as effector‐triggered immunity (ETI), resulting in a robust plant defense response. This mechanism is activated via intracellular receptors (encoded by resistance (R) genes), mainly through nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeat (NLR) receptors.

Recent findings have revealed overlap between immune signaling pathways mediated by surface‐localized PRRs and intracellular NLRs, indicating extensive crosstalk between these receptor classes (Ngou et al. 2021; Yuan et al. 2021). Thus, PTI and ETI lead to similar downstream defense responses, while the timing, amplitude, and duration of responses differ (Lolle et al. 2020). The primary responses include production of ROS, cell wall modification, closure of stomata, expression of genes responsible for defense responses (e.g., antimicrobial enzymes, defensins, phytoalexins), as well as hypersensitive response (HR) (Li et al. 2025). HR is a crucial plant defense mechanism that helps plants survive biotrophic pathogen invasions (Durrant and Dong 2004; Balint‐Kurti 2019). This response is marked by the presence of dead, dark cells at the site of pathogen entry and is mainly mediated by NLRs, which detect pathogen effectors and initiate coordinated immune signaling (Balint‐Kurti 2019; Noman et al. 2020; Locci and Parker 2024). This helps to limit the spread of the pathogen and ultimately leads to systemic acquired resistance (Balint‐Kurti 2019). However, despite significant progress, the molecular mechanisms that fine‐tune pathogen‐specific immune responses remain only partially understood.

In cereals like wheat ( Triticum aestivum ), stripe rust, caused by Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Pst), is among the most destructive diseases worldwide, posing a continual threat to food security through recurring epidemics. Host resistance is a fundamental to sustainable disease‐management strategies. Models describing ETI highlight the role of intracellular NLR receptors in recognizing pathogens and activation of downstream transcriptional reprogramming. However, recent discoveries indicate that immune signaling in wheat is not solely mediated by NLRs (Zhao et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2019; Förderer et al. 2022; Li et al. 2025). Tandem kinase proteins (TKPs) are a large and evolutionarily diverse family of immune sensors. Previous studies demonstrate that some of TKPs act as immune receptors by binding cognate pathogen effectors and then partnering with helper NLRs to transduce downstream responses (Chen et al. 2025; Li et al. 2024; Lu et al. 2025; Sung et al. 2025). This arrangement expands the repertoire of defense mechanisms enabled by their unique tandem kinase domain architecture (Klymiuk et al. 2018, 2021; Fahima and Coaker 2023). Members of this family have been shown to confer resistance to fungal pathogens in monocots (Li et al. 2025; Reveguk et al. 2025). Moreover, computational analyses identified 2683 TKPs distributed across 104 plant species, demonstrating that TKPs are widespread throughout the plant kingdom and not restricted to the Triticeae lineage (Reveguk et al. 2025).

One member of the TKP family is WHEAT TANDEM KINASE 1 (WTK1), encoded by the Yr15 locus derived from wild emmer wheat ( Triticum dicoccoides ) (Klymiuk et al. 2018). Yr15 confers wide‐spectrum and durable resistance against more than 2000 Pst isolates (van Silfhout 1989; Sharma‐Poudyal et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2017; Ali et al. 2017). Structurally, WTK1 consists of a functional kinase fused to a degenerate pseudokinase; both domains are required for resistance, indicating an unconventional activation mechanism. Since the cloning of Yr15, over 10 tandem kinase R genes have been identified in Triticeae (Li et al. 2025), establishing tandem kinases as an important new class of disease R genes that has captured considerable interest from both breeders and researchers. However, the downstream processes mobilized by TKPs, such as WTK1, remain poorly understood.

Klymiuk et al. (2018) demonstrated that WTK1 confers resistance to stripe rust via a post‐haustoria mechanism, indicative of cytoplasmic signaling that triggered the observed HR‐cell death response. They further proposed a model explaining the molecular function of TKPs, such as WTK1 (Klymiuk et al. 2018, 2021). Direct interaction of TKPs with fungal pathogen effectors has been demonstrated for other TKP pathosystems, namely Sr62TK and RWT4 (Chen et al. 2025; Sung et al. 2025), supporting the decoy model proposed by Klymiuk et al. (2018).

Understanding the immune response mechanisms activated by WTK1 during wheat stripe rust infection could provide valuable insights into resistance pathways in this non‐model organism. Next‐generation sequencing, particularly RNA‐Seq, has revolutionized the study of plant‐pathogen interactions by providing comprehensive insights into the transcriptional dynamics of both host and pathogen during infection (Dobon et al. 2016; Poretti et al. 2021; Dorostkar et al. 2022). Previous wheat transcriptome studies have mainly focused on NLR‐mediated resistance, revealing the activation of multiple immune pathways (Sørensen et al. 2016). The downstream signaling events triggered by WTK1 activation remain incompletely understood. Elucidating these responses will advance our understanding of the molecular basis of tandem kinase resistance and inform strategies for incorporating WTK1 into breeding pipelines for durable stripe rust resistance.

In this study, we employed RNA‐Seq to investigate gene expression dynamics in resistant and susceptible near‐isogenic lines (NILs) over a seven‐day period following inoculation with Pst. This time‐course analysis enabled us to track the waves of transcriptional reprogramming and capture the temporal patterns of WTK1‐associated defense responses from early to late stages of infection. Through integrated bioinformatic analyses, we reconstructed potential signaling networks and identified potential candidate components of the broader WTK1‐dependent resistance network activated during Pst infection. Our results indicate that WTK1 likely orchestrates a dynamic immune cascade, transitioning from early oxidative stress and ROS homeostasis to a broad defense program involving metabolic reprogramming, hormone signaling, and cell wall fortification, and ultimately engaging autophagy and localized hypersensitive cell death of the invaded cells to restrict pathogen growth.

Materials and Methods

To elucidate how the tandem kinase‐pseudokinase WTK1 mediates defense against Pst, we analyzed phenotypic responses and transcriptional dynamics over a seven‐day infection period in resistant (Kronos R) and susceptible (Kronos S) wheat transgenic NILs that differ only in the presence of WTK1. The integrative experimental design, encompassing phenotypic, histopathological, and transcriptomic analyses, is illustrated in Figure 1 .

Plant Materials, Growth Conditions, Pst Inoculation and Tissue Sampling

The primary objective of this study was to elucidate the molecular mechanisms activated by WTK1 in response to stripe rust infection. To this end, we compared the susceptible wheat ( Triticum aestivum ) cultivar Kronos (hereafter, Kronos S) with its resistant NIL carrying WTK1 (Kronos+WTK1, hereafter, Kronos R) to characterize WTK1‐dependent changes in gene expression. Kronos R was generated by Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation of Kronos S using a WTK1 genomic construct driven by its native promoter and regulatory elements (Klymiuk et al. 2018). Consequently, the two NILs (Kronos R and Kronos S) are genetically identical, differing only by the presence of the Yr15 R gene. Homozygous transgenic Kronos R plants were tested and confirmed to exhibit stable resistance to Pst through the T4 generation.

The experimental design involved inoculating both the resistant Kronos R and susceptible Kronos S wheat lines with Pst, followed by leaf tissue sampling at 24‐h intervals from 0 to 7 days post‐infection (dpi). Seedlings were sown and grown under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod, with daytime and nighttime temperatures of 15°C and 10°C, respectively. At the second‐leaf stage, seedlings were inoculated with fresh Pst spores (10 mg/mL) suspended in Soltrol 170 mineral oil (Govta et al. 2026). The Pst isolate #5006 avirulent on Yr15 was used for NIL inoculation. Pst#5006 is prevalent in Israel and is virulent to at least eight known yellow rust resistance genes (Yr2, Yr6, Yr7, Yr9, Yr22, Yr23, YrSD, YrHVII) (Cheng et al. 2010). Following inoculation, plants were transferred to a dew chamber (PERCIVAL SCIENTIFIC, Czech Republic) for 24 h at 100% relative humidity, including 16 h at 10°C in the dark, followed by 8 h under light at 15°C. Thereafter, plants were moved to a growth chamber (CONVIRON) and maintained under a long‐day photoperiod (16 h light at 15°C, 150 μmol m−2 s−1, and 8 h dark at 10°C, 70% RH). Leaf samples were collected daily at 9 a.m. at 0, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, and 168 h post‐inoculation (hpi). For each sample, 100 mg leaf tissue was placed into RNAlater (Sigma‐Aldrich, R0901) and stored at −20°C until further processing. Five biological replicates were collected at each time point for subsequent RNA extraction, cDNA library preparation, and RNA‐Seq on the Illumina platform.

Assessment of Macroscopic Resistance and Susceptible Components

Each leaf segment was imaged using a stereo microscope (ZEISS Stemi 305) equipped with an integrated 1.2‐megapixel color camera. Images were acquired at 8 × magnification and processed with ZEN imaging software (version 3.0, blue edition, Zeiss).

Histopathological Characterization of Pst‐Wheat Interactions

Histopathological analysis of wheat‐Pst interactions was performed by fluorescence microscopy to visualize fungal structures stained with wheat germ agglutinin conjugated to FITC (WGA‐FITC; Sigma Aldrich, L4895), following established protocols (Dawson et al. 2015; Klymiuk et al. 2018). Second‐leaf segments (10 cm) from Kronos S and Kronos R plants were collected at 9:00 a.m. and 9:00 p.m. over a 1–7 dpi time course, with three biological replicates per time point. Microscopic observations were carried out using an inverted Leica DMi8 microscope equipped with a FITC filter cube (excitation 460–500 nm; emission 512–542 nm) and operated in fluorescence (FLUO) mode.

The extent of fungal colonization was quantified from fluorescence micrographs of infected leaf tissues over the 7‐day period. One representative high‐resolution micrograph was captured per biological replicate (leaf segment). Image analysis to determine the fungal colonization area was performed using the R‐based package ‘pliman’ (TiagoOlivoto/pliman 1.0.0, Olivoto 2023). To ensure accurate segmentation of the restricted fungal structures in resistant tissues and the expanding colonies in susceptible tissues, a custom color palette was generated in GIMP (v2.10.32, Oliver et al. 2006; https://www.gimp.org/). This palette distinguished fluorescence‐positive pixels from the background by manually sampling representative regions, which were then supplied to “pliman” for automated segmentation of WGA‐FITC fluorescence. Pathogen coverage was expressed as the percentage of fluorescence‐positive area relative to the total leaf area in each image. All quantitative outputs were exported as CSV files for downstream statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses of phenotypic data were performed in R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team 2023). Stripe rust symptoms and resistance responses were quantified using the “pliman” package. Descriptive statistics and one‐ and two‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc tests, were conducted using the “rstatix” package (Kassambara 2021, https://cloud.r‐project.org/package=rstatix). Bar plots displaying the results, including p values from the post hoc tests, were generated using the “ggpubr” package (Kassambara 2018; https://CRAN.R‐project.org/package=ggpubr).

Total RNA Extraction, Library Preparation and Sequencing

Total RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing were performed by Novogene (UK) Company Limited. RNA‐Seq libraries were prepared following the manufacturer's protocols and sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq platform using paired‐end 150 bp reads (PE150), targeting a Q30 score ≥ 80%. Raw data quality was assessed with FastQC v0.11.8 (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Adapter removal, barcode trimming, and quality filtering were conducted using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. 2014) with standard parameters to remove residual adapter sequences and low‐quality bases.

Analysis of Sequencing Data

Because the RNA‐Seq dataset comprised a mixture of wheat and Pst transcripts, we first removed pathogen‐derived reads prior to quantifying wheat gene expression. Raw reads were pre‐aligned to the Pst reference genome (isolate 134E16A + 17 + 33+; Schwessinger et al. 2022) using STAR v2.6.1d (Dobin et al. 2013), and all reads mapping to the pathogen genome were discarded. The unmapped reads were subsequently aligned to the durum wheat reference genome (Svevo v1.55, Maccaferri et al. 2019; https://ftp.ensemblgenomes.ebi.ac.uk/pub/plants/release‐55/gff3/triticum_turgidum/). Gene‐level read counts were generated with feature Counts (Liao et al. 2014; https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btt656) using default parameters.

Differential Gene Expression Analysis

Prior to differential expression analysis, data quality was assessed and normalized using the “NormExpression” R package with the TU normalization factor (pre_ratio = 0.5, lower_trim = 0.05, upper_trim = 0.65) (Wu et al. 2019). Data structure was examined by principal component analysis (PCA; Figure S1), and sample relationships were evaluated using Pearson correlation matrices visualized as a heat map (Figure S2). Differential expression across time points was analyzed using the moanin workflow (Varoquaux and Purdom 2020). The DE_timepoints function was used to identify genes showing significant pairwise differences, applying thresholds of q value < 0.05 and log2 fold change |LFC| ≥ 2.0 (Storey and Tibshirani 2003) to focus on genes with robust and biologically meaningful transcriptional responses. To capture temporal expression dynamics and genotype‐specific trajectories, the DE_timecourse function was applied, which fits spline‐based models and performs log‐likelihood ratio tests on the entire mean expression functions of each differentially expressed gene (DEG) (Varoquaux and Purdom 2020). To interpret the large number of DEGs, we clustered genes according to their temporal expression patterns after inoculation. Clustering was performed using spline‐based k‐means (splines_kmeans function, “moanin” package). Cluster stability was evaluated using within‐cluster sum of squares (WCSS) across multiple k values, and cluster quality was assessed using both the elbow method (WCSS) and the silhouette method (similarity measures) (Figure S3a,b). Since “moanin” does not automatically select the optimal number of clusters, these metrics guided the choice of k = 20, which provided an appropriate balance between resolution and biological interpretability. Cluster centroids and temporal trajectories were visualized using the plot_splines_data function (Varoquaux and Purdom 2020).

Functional Annotation, Gene Ontology Enrichment, and KEGG Pathway Analysis

Functional annotation of DEGs was performed using the Triticeae‐GeneTribe database (Chen et al. 2020). Gene descriptions were retrieved from the UniProt (Coudert et al. 2023), and Gene Ontology (GO) terms were obtained through EnsemblPlants (Harrison et al. 2024; https://plants.ensembl.org/). GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the “topGO” R‐package (Alexa and Rahnenführer 2009), with a false discovery rate (FDR)‐corrected significance threshold of q < 0.05. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis (Wixon and Kell 2000) was carried out using the KOBAS 3.0 web server (Kanehisa et al. 2007; http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/). Enriched KEGG pathways were identified using FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05.

Weighted Gene co‐Expression Network Analysis

Weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed on the Kronos S and Kronos R datasets. DEGs were first identified using ‘DESeq2’ to compare gene expression between the two genotypes at each time point and DEGs with p ≤ 0.05 were retained for the network construction. Co‐expression networks were built using the ‘WGCNA’ package v1.47 in R (Langfelder and Horvath 2008). To determine an appropriate soft‐thresholding power, we evaluated the scale‐free topology fit index and mean connectivity across a series of candidate powers (Figure S4). The scale‐free topology criterion (R 2 ≥ 0.9) was achieved at power 10, accompanied by the expected reduction in mean connectivity. Based on these diagnostics, networks were constructed using settings such as a soft‐thresholding power of 10, an unsigned topological overlap matrix (TOM), a merge cut height of 0.25, and a minimum module size of 30. An adjacency matrix was first generated from pairwise gene expression correlations and transformed into a TOM. Gene modules were then identified using hierarchical clustering followed by dynamic tree cutting. Module eigengenes were used for sample clustering and to evaluate correlations between modules and genotypes. Other parameters were kept at their default settings. For all 43,743 genes, we calculated total connectivity, intramodular connectivity (function soft connectivity), eigengene‐based connectivity (kME, i.e., module membership) and the associated kME p values. Genes that showed significant correlations between module eigengene and genotype and exhibited high intramodular connectivity were considered trait‐associated. Putative hub genes were defined as those with the highest connectivity (high intramodular connectivity and |kME|) within their respective modules.

ROS Assays

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation in Pst infection‐related structures was assessed using 2′,7′‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA), a broad‐spectrum ROS‐sensitive fluorogenic probe that reacts with several commonly produced ROS (Hu et al. 2022). A 10 μM H2DCFDA working solution was prepared in ethanol and used for fluorescence staining. Leaf segments (10‐cm sections of the second leaf) from inoculated and mock‐inoculated Kronos S and Kronos R plants were collected daily from 1 to 7 dpi at 9:00 a.m., with three biological replicates per time point. Each segment was cut into ~1.5 cm pieces and incubated in 1.5 mL of staining solution in 2 mL Eppendorf tubes for 20 min in the dark at room temperature. Following staining, samples were mounted on glass slides and imaged under a Leica DMi8 microscope (Leica Microsystems) equipped with a FITC filter set (Ex: 460–500 nm; Dc: 505 nm; Em: 512–542 nm) in fluorescence (FLUO) mode. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using the image‐analysis workflow described in the section “Histopathological characterization of Pst‐wheat interactions.” Statistical differences in ROS accumulation between Kronos S and Kronos R across time points were evaluated by two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc tests.

Quantitative Reverse Transcriptase PCR Analysis

The RNA samples used for quantitative reverse transcriptase PCR (qRT‐PCR) validation were identical to those used for RNA‐Seq. First‐strand cDNA was synthesized using the qScript Flex cDNA Synthesis Kit (Quanta Biosciences). The qRT‐PCR was performed on a StepOne Plus Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using the cycling conditions described in Klymiuk et al. (2018): 95°C for 20 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 3 s and 60°C for 30 s. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1. Each qRT‐PCR reaction mixture had a total volume of 10 μL, containing 5 μL Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), 2.5 μL diluted cDNA, and 300 nM of each primer. Primer efficiencies were determined using four serial tenfold dilutions (1:1, 1:10, 1:100, 1:1000) performed in triplicate, and all primer pairs showed amplification efficiencies greater than 95%. Transcript abundance is expressed as linearized fold change relative to ACTIN levels, calculated using the 2^(−∆CT) formula (Schmittgen and Livak 2008). The baseline transcript levels of ACTIN were empirically verified to be highly stable and showed no statistically significant variance across genotypes or infection time points throughout the study. To assess concordance between qRT‐PCR and RNA‐Seq measurements, Pearson correlation (cor. test function, ‘stats’ package, base R) coefficients were calculated between the two methods for six selected genes across six time points under infection conditions, based on the relative expression from five biological replicates.

Results

Phenotypic Characterization of Resistant and Susceptible Kronos NILs Following Pst Inoculation

Resistant (Kronos R) and susceptible (Kronos S) NILs were inoculated with Pst isolate #5006 and monitored over 0–168 hpi time course. Fungal penetration into host cells was detected in both NILs at one dpi (Figure 2; Table S2). From 24 to 96 hpi, there was little increase in fungal biomass accumulation in either NIL. However, from 120 hpi onward, fungal growth increased exponentially in Kronos S, whereas it remained strongly restricted in Kronos R (p < 0.001; Figure 2g; Tables S2 and S3). Notably, there was no significant difference in fungal colonization area across the whole time course in Kronos R (from 24 to 168 hpi). Accordingly, a significant genotype × time interaction was detected for fungal coverage area (p < 0.001; Figure 2g; Tables S2 and S3).

ROS accumulation, quantified by H2DCFDA staining, was initially low in both NILs at 24 hpi but increased around stomata by 48 hpi (Figure 2c,f,i; Tables S4 and S5). Divergence between the genotypes became evident at 72 hpi, when Kronos R accumulated significantly more ROS than Kronos S (p < 0.001). ROS levels in Kronos R peaked at 120 hpi and remained elevated at 168 hpi, whereas Kronos S showed only a weak late response. Together, these results indicate that WTK1‐mediated resistance involves a rapid and sustained ROS burst that restricts fungal development, while the susceptible NIL fails to mount an effective oxidative defense.

Defining the Transcriptome Responses of Durum NILs to Stripe Rust Infection

Given the contrasting phenotypic and ROS responses between Kronos R and Kronos S, we next examined the transcriptional programs underlying WTK1‐mediated resistance. To this end, we performed RNA‐Seq on susceptible (Kronos S) and resistant (Kronos R) NILs following inoculation with Pst. The second leaves were collected at 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, and 168 hpi, thereby capturing both early and late stages of responses. In total, 60 cDNA libraries were sequenced, yielding ~700 Gb of sequence data, with an average of 38.2 million (31.9–42.3 million) paired‐end 150 bp clean reads per library (Table S6). As expected, the dataset contained both wheat and fungal transcripts. To focus on host responses, we removed reads aligning to the Pst reference genome (Schwessinger et al. 2022) prior to analysis. The remaining reads were mapped to the durum wheat reference genome (cv. Svevo, Maccaferri et al. 2019), with a mean mapping rate of 97.2% ± 0.4%. This high‐quality dataset provided a robust foundation for subsequent differential expression analysis between the resistant and susceptible NILs.

Identification of Genes Differentially Expressed Upon Pst‐Inoculation in the Resistant Line Kronos R Compared to Its Susceptible NIL Kronos S

To capture genes with complex, non‐linear expression dynamics, we applied a spline‐based model to the time‐course RNA‐Seq dataset and evaluated global expression trajectories between the resistant (Kronos R) and susceptible (Kronos S) NILs using a log‐ratio likelihood ratio test (Varoquaux and Purdom 2020). Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed at q < 0.05 with |LFC| > 2.0 in at least one genotype comparison at one or more time points. A total of 2509 DEGs were identified between Kronos R and Kronos S. Clustering DEGs by expression trajectories identified 20 distinct clusters (Figure S5), of which 13 clusters comprising 1530 genes showed higher expression in Kronos R (Figure 3). The 13 Kronos R‐enriched clusters were further organized into three temporal response categories: Group I comprised clusters with transient, time‐specific peaks (24 hpi: C5, C7; 48 hpi: C12; 72 hpi: C6; 96 hpi: C3, C16, C17; 168 hpi: C19; Figure 3a). Group II included clusters with low expression at 24 hpi followed by sustained high expression at later time points (C2, C4, C15; Figure 3b). Group III contained clusters with bimodal peaks at 48 and 120 hpi (C13, C20; Figure 3c). Together, these dynamics suggest that WTK1‐mediated resistance involves both rapid, transient transcriptional reprogramming and longer‐term activation of defense‐associated gene expression.

Analysis of DEGs With Higher Expression in the Resistant NIL Kronos R

We next focused on the 1530 DEGs that exhibited higher expression in the resistant NIL Kronos R compared with Kronos S, representing candidate components of the WTK1‐mediated response to Pst infection. Functional characterization was performed using GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses. Because wheat gene annotation remains incomplete, functional assignments were inferred primarily from sequence similarity to UniProt entries and annotated genomes of durum wheat ( Triticum durum cv. Svevo), bread wheat ( T. aestivum ), Arabidopsis thaliana , and rice ( Oryza sativa L.).

GO enrichment revealed extensive transcriptional reprogramming throughout the seven‐day time course with significant terms enriched in molecular function (MF; Figure S6), biological process (BP; Figure S7), and cellular component (CC; Figure S8). Enrichment profiles differed among expression clusters, consistent with functional specialization within the resistance program and suggesting a combination of conserved defense modules and wheat‐specific features. To resolve the temporal structure of WTK1‐mediated resistance, we summarized enriched functional categories among Kronos R—upregulated DEGs across the time course (Figure 4a). During the early phase (24–48 hpi), enrichment was dominated by processes linked to ROS production, cytoskeleton organization, protein degradation, and membrane transport, consistent with the early ROS burst (Figure 2h). The intermediate phase (72–96 hpi) showed strong enrichment for transcriptional regulation, signal transduction, and metabolic adjustments, coinciding with sustained ROS accumulation and reduced fungal growth (Figure 2e,h). In the late phase (120–168 hpi), enrichment shifted toward hormone‐mediated signaling, secondary metabolism, and stress tolerance pathways, reflecting long‐term stabilization of defense responses and containment of pathogen biomass (Figure 2g).

Across the entire time course, metabolism and biosynthesis consistently represented the largest categories, peaking at 96 hpi, while transport, membrane organization, and molecular recognition were prominent between 48 and 96 hpi (Figures 4 and S6–S8). Transcriptional regulation and protein processing/degradation increased after 48 hpi, highlighting extensive remodeling of gene expression and protein turnover. Signal transduction peaked at 96 hpi, integrating upstream cues with transcriptional and post‐transcriptional regulation but declined by 168 hpi as cellular homeostasis was restored. Together, these results demonstrate that WTK1‐mediated resistance is governed by a temporally coordinated defense program, integrating early pathogen recognition, sustained signaling, and late‐stage stabilization to achieve effective protection against Pst.

KEGG enrichment analysis using the KOBAS platform identified significantly enriched pathways among DEGs with higher expression in Kronos R (Table S7). Group II genes were enriched in 30 pathways and Group III in four pathways, while Group I exhibited time‐dependent enrichment (24, 48, 72, 96, and 168 hpi: 5, 2, 2, 23, and 5 pathways, respectively). Enriched pathways spanned metabolism and transport, genetic and environmental information processing, cellular processes, and signaling. The most represented categories included metabolic pathways (39 genes), ubiquitin‐mediated proteolysis (10 genes), autophagy (7 genes), carbon metabolism (7 genes), and the spliceosome (5 genes), highlighting coordinated regulation of metabolism, protein turnover, and RNA processing during the resistance response.

Co‐Expression Network Analysis of Wheat Resistance to Stripe Rust

To further dissect the transcriptional programs underlying WTK1‐mediated resistance, we performed WGCNA on the time‐course RNA‐Seq dataset. Co‐expression networks were constructed separately for the resistant and susceptible NILs, and gene modules were correlated with resistance‐associated phenotypic and infection‐related traits. Conserved modules were then identified between NILs, and intra‐module hub genes were prioritized for downstream functional annotation. Using DEGs from Kronos R following Pst inoculation, we constructed a wheat‐Pst co‐expression network that was resolved into eleven color‐coded modules (Figures 5 and S4b).

Differentially expressed genes were partitioned into 11 co‐expression modules, each designated by color label. Rows represent modules, with gene counts shown at the right. Columns correspond to sampling time points for Kronos S (KI) and Kronos R (WI) at 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, and 168 hpi. The heatmap displays correlations between module eigengenes and genotype × time combinations. Significance codes: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

The Turquoise module contained the largest number of genes (672), whereas the Purple module was the smallest, with 30 DEGs (Figure 5). Among all modules, the Green, Brown, and Purple modules showed strong positive correlations with the resistant NIL Kronos R, but negative correlations with the susceptible Kronos S, while the Grey module displayed the opposite trend (Figure 5). At 24 hpi, both genotypes correlated with the Green module (Kronos S: R = 0.24, p < 0.05; Kronos R: R = 0.73, p < 0.05), which contained 299 Pst‐induced genes. In Kronos S, this correlation declined at later stages (to R = −0.01, p < 0.05), whereas in Kronos R it dropped at 72 hpi (R = −0.13, p < 0.05) but increased again at later time points (up to R = 0.22, p < 0.01). GO enrichment analysis indicated that Green module genes were primarily associated with carbohydrate metabolism (Figure S9), a key process underlying pathogen defense. Genes were also linked to ROS metabolism, particularly the glyoxylate cycle, and their encoded proteins were enriched in extracellular and boundary‐associated compartments, consistent with a role in reinforcing the cell wall during resistance (Molina et al. 2024).

KEGG pathway analysis revealed that genes in the Green module were predominantly associated with core metabolic pathways, including secondary metabolite biosynthesis (osa01110), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (osa00940), amino acid degradation (valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation; osa00280), carbohydrate metabolism (glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism; osa00630), lipid metabolism (glycerolipid and peroxisomal pathways; osa00561), and diterpenoid biosynthesis (osa00904) (Figure S9). These pathways are central to the production of defense‐related metabolites and signaling molecules that enhance resistance to pathogen attack (Thakur et al. 2021). In addition, Green module genes were enriched in cellular processes such as protein processing, endocytosis, and MAPK signaling, underscoring their role in coordinating metabolic and signaling responses during defense. The co‐expression network (Figure S9c) comprised 25 genes (14 unique due to wheat polyploidy), with 13 encoding regulators of stress responses, signaling, metabolism, and protein transport, highlighting their central contribution to WTK1‐mediated resistance. Together, these results suggest that the Green module functions as a metabolic‐signaling hub that reinforces cell wall integrity and promotes stress adaptation.

The Brown module comprised 228 genes and showed a stronger correlation with the resistant NIL Kronos R (R = 0.5, p < 0.05 at 48 hpi) compared to the susceptible Kronos S (R = −0.06 at 48 hpi) (Figure S10). DEGs in this module were enriched for processes related to cellular homeostasis, response to stimuli, developmental regulation, lignin biosynthesis, and secondary metabolism, including phenylpropanoid and flavonoid pathways. Proteins encoded by Brown module genes were mainly localized to the plasma membrane and involved in calcium ion binding and transmembrane transport. KEGG analysis highlighted enrichment in amino acid metabolism, cell wall organization, stress responses, and redox homeostasis, with cellular transport being the most represented category (7 genes). The co‐expression network of the Brown module (Figure S10c) contained 72 genes, including 28 encoding regulators of stress responses. Together, these findings suggest that the Brown module contributes to WTK1‐mediated resistance by reinforcing cell wall integrity through lignin and phenylpropanoid metabolism, while simultaneously maintaining redox balance and stress adaptation during Pst infection.

The Purple module comprised 30 genes that were consistently upregulated in the resistant NIL Kronos R across all time points (Figure S11). DEGs were enriched in inositol and alcohol metabolism, cytoskeleton organization, and signaling processes, including the ethylene‐activated signaling pathway. The co‐expression network (Figure S11c) identified 24 hub genes associated with stress signaling and cytoskeletal dynamics. Importantly, the enrichment of ethylene signaling and cytoskeletal remodeling in the Purple module complements the metabolic and ROS‐related functions of the Green module, as well as the lignin and phenylpropanoid pathways of the Brown module. Together, these modules suggest an integrated defense program in which ethylene and cytoskeleton‐mediated signaling (Purple) initiates and coordinates responses that promote ROS metabolism and cell wall reinforcement (Green and Brown), thereby stabilizing WTK1‐mediated resistance against Pst.

Cross‐Validation of WTK1‐Dependent DEGs by Clustering and Network Analysis

To strengthen confidence in the transcriptional signatures associated with WTK1‐mediated resistance, we cross‐validated DEGs obtained from global transcriptome clustering (clusters C1‐C20) with those derived from network‐level perspectives of WGCNA modules (Brown, Green, Purple). This comparison provided a consolidated view of the defense programs activated by WTK1 during Pst infection (Figure S8). Grouping the data by gene revealed clear functional categories with strong convergence between the two approaches. Several genes were consistently identified by both methods, underscoring their central roles in WTK1‐mediated resistance.

Early Signaling Cascades

Transcriptome analysis revealed robust induction of MAPK cascade members in resistant Kronos R plants, including MAPKKK3, MKK4, MKK9, and SIS8 (C6, C2, C3, and C19) (Figure S8). Histidine kinases (HK3, HK4) and multiple receptor‐like kinases (including CRK family, EIX2, and RLK7) were also enriched, consistent with rapid activation of stress‐ and pathogen‐associated signaling pathways (Figure S8). Notably, these patterns were most evident in time‐resolved expression clusters, which captured transient, time‐specific peaks of kinase activation that are likely to be diluted in module‐level co‐expression summaries.

ROS‐Ca2+ Interplay

Genes encoding NADPH oxidases (RBOHB, RBOHH) were expressed alongside Ca2+ influx channels (CNGC17, TPC1, CSC1), annexins (ANN4, ANN5), and CaM‐like proteins (CML43, CML46). Global clustering highlighted the temporal induction of ROS producers and Ca2+ channels during the early oxidative burst, whereas WGCNA grouped Ca2+ sensors and annexins into stable co‐expression modules (Figure S8). The complementary application of these methods shows both when ROS‐Ca2+ signaling is triggered and how it is sustained through network‐level regulation.

Transcriptional and Metabolic Reprogramming

Several transcription factor families were consistently upregulated, including WRKY50/51/65, NAC92/68/48, and ethylene‐responsive regulators (ERF060, ERF118, RAP2‐3; Figure S8). Lipid metabolism genes (PLP2, PLA1, PLDZETA1, PLC2/6; Figure S8) were activated across both approaches, underscoring their role as shared messengers linking signaling, metabolism, and defense. While clustering resolved transient peaks of ethylene‐ and WRKY‐family expression, WGCNA revealed broader modules (e.g., Green and Pink modules; Figure S8) enriched for transcription factors and lipid enzymes, suggesting coordinated regulation across multiple time points.

Execution of HR and PCD

Key genes associated with HR were strongly represented. RGA2, RGA4, and RGA5 (NLRs) were repeatedly detected across both analyses, while HIR1 (HYPERSENSITIVE‐INDUCED RESPONSE PROTEIN, Green module) and MCA1 (METACASPASE‐1, Brown module), marking the execution phase of cell death, were identified with WGCNA (Figure S8). VPE (VACUOLAR‐PROCESSING ENZYME, C19) and autophagy components (ATG7, ATG18D, ATG9, etc.; C2, C4, C13, C15, C20; Figure S8) further emphasized cellular remodeling during HR. The dual application of clustering and network analysis was particularly informative here: clustering resolved the early transcriptional induction of PCD executors, while WGCNA placed them within modules of co‐expressed defense genes, demonstrating how HR is embedded within a broader defense network.

Together, these results demonstrate that WTK1‐mediated resistance reflects a temporally dynamic yet network‐integrated defense program, in which early signaling cascades and transcriptional bursts are embedded within co‐regulated modules that sustain HR and programmed cell death.

Validation of RNA‐Seq Results by qRT‐PCR

We selected six homologous genes involved in defense response pathways to validate the transcriptome results using qRT‐PCR during stripe rust infection. These genes were EDR1 (ENHANCED DISEASE RESISTANCE 1), PLP2 (PATATIN‐LIKE PROTEIN 2), RGA4 (DISEASE RESISTANCE PROTEIN RGA4), TPC1 (TWO‐PORE CALCIUM CHANNEL PROTEIN 1), WRKY50 (WRKY TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR 50), and MCA1 (METACASPASE‐1). For all six genes, qRT‐PCR profiles were consistent with the RNA‐seq trends (Figure 6), supporting the reliability of the transcriptome dataset and indicating activation of these defense‐associated genes in the WTK1‐mediated response to Pst infection.

Discussion

Stripe rust remains a major constraint on global wheat production because most cultivars are highly susceptible and can incur substantial yield losses. Harnessing natural R genes from wild relatives offers a sustainable strategy for disease control, and Yr15, which encodes the tandem kinase‐pseudokinase WTK1, is a prominent example (Klymiuk et al. 2018). Despite their agronomic importance, the mechanisms by which tandem kinases mediate defense remain incompletely understood. In this study, analysis of the wheat‐WTK1‐Pst interaction revealed that WTK1‐triggered resistance in the resistant NIL Kronos R is associated with extensive transcriptional reprogramming.

The current spatiotemporal resolution of the wheat–Pst pathosystem remains limited, hindering detailed dissection of infection mechanisms. Bulk transcriptomic approaches obscure cellular heterogeneity, making it difficult to distinguish responses in invaded cells from those in adjacent non‐invaded tissue. Because Pst invasion occurs asynchronously, mechanisms underlying local HR, PTI‐ and ETI‐like outputs, and systemic signaling overlap in time and space, complicating mechanistic resolution. Despite these challenges, our time‐course and network analyses identified major waves of transcriptional reprogramming and the co‐expression networks associated with WTK1‐mediated stripe rust resistance, including an early phase marked by ROS accumulation and HR (24–48 hpi), an intermediate phase enriched for PTI‐like outputs (72–96 hpi), and a late phase associated with hormone‐mediated systemic defense signaling (120–168 hpi).

Although WTK1‐mediated resistance was dominated by coordinated waves of transcriptional upregulation, transcriptional repression may also contribute to the overall immune response. In this study, we focused primarily on upregulated DEGs because these genes best captured the active defense programs associated with WTK1 activation, including ROS and Ca2+ signaling, defense‐related metabolism, cell‐wall reinforcement, and HR‐associated processes. Nevertheless, downregulated transcripts may represent an additional layer of regulation, including the suppression of photosynthesis, growth‐related processes, or primary metabolism during infection. Such repression is consistent with a defense–growth trade‐off, in which resources are redirected toward immune responses and away from host pathways that may support biotrophic pathogen development. A detailed functional analysis of downregulated genes was beyond the scope of this study, but this transcript class may provide useful candidates for future investigation. Our dataset further indicates that WTK1‐triggered resistance overlaps substantially with ETI‐associated outputs. Recent studies in the WTK3/RWT4, Sr62, and Lr41 pathosystems have shown that these tandem kinase proteins function as pathogen sensors that require non‐canonical helper NLRs to execute immune signaling (Chen et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2025; Lemmer et al. 2026). Upon activation by their cognate kinases, these helper NLRs assemble into Ca2+‐permeable channels essential for cell death induction (Chen et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2025). This “kinase‐sensor/NLR‐executor” architecture highlights a conserved modular theme in plant immunity, conceptually analogous to the resistosome‐based signaling in Arabidopsis (Wang et al. 2019).

Our results are consistent with the possibility that WTK1 may use a similar receptor‐executor setup as described for other TKPs (Sung et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2025; Lemmer et al. 2026). Although the non‐synchronous invasion of Pst haustoria into mesophyll cells (Lorrain et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2020) may blur the resolution of discrete “waves” of transcriptional responses, the overall expression profile is consistent with a model in which WTK1 likely operates in cooperation with an associated NLR (Lu et al. 2025). Consistent with this framework, transcripts induced during WTK1‐mediated resistance encompass NLRs, Ca2+/ROS signaling components, MAPK cascade programmed cell death (PCD) regulators, proteases, vacuolar trafficking factors, and autophagy‐related proteins.

WTK1 (Yr15) encodes a tandem kinase‐pseudokinase protein with a unique WAK‐like/RLCK_8‐derived architecture (Klymiuk et al. 2018). Although direct structural data are not yet available, TKPs are generally thought to be maintained in a low‐activity resting state in the absence of pathogen challenge (Figure 7). Recent studies on other tandem kinase systems provide useful frameworks for understanding these data (Chen et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2025; Lemmer et al. 2026). The results here suggest that WTK1‐mediated resistance may involve a broader immune signaling module. However, this study did not directly examine the receptor composition or the sequence of early signaling events. Like other tandem kinase systems, the current model proposes that effector perception activates WTK1. This activation may recruit a helper NLR, linking HR in invaded cells to defense activation in neighboring non‐invaded cells (Chen et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2025; Lemmer et al. 2026). This framework aligns with our transcriptome data and resistosome‐like signaling models (Bi et al. 2021; Förderer et al. 2022; Klymiuk et al. 2021). However, it remains hypothetical until the cognate effector and a cooperating NLR partner are identified (Figure 7). Perception of a cognate effector may trigger conformational and signaling changes. These changes could be associated with WTK1 activation. Whether these early events involve recruitment of helper NLRs or assembly of higher‐order immune complexes remains an open question. Future genetic, biochemical, and structural studies are needed to resolve this.

Our data further indicate that WTK1‐mediated resistance is executed through a coordinated network integrating ROS, calcium signaling, and PCD pathways. Reactive oxygen species are well established as central components of plant immunity (Camejo et al. 2016; Ali et al. 2023; Bhar et al. 2023; Khan et al. 2023), functioning both as direct antimicrobial factors and as secondary messengers that amplify defense signaling and transcriptional responses (Shetty et al. 2008; Juan et al. 2021; Wei et al. 2025). Consistent with this paradigm, ROS accumulation was substantially higher in Kronos R than in Kronos S, with a pronounced increase at 120 hpi, as quantified by H2DCFDA fluorescence intensity analysis (Figure 2i). This pattern aligns with our RNA‐Seq results showing early and sustained induction of ROS‐associated pathways from 48 hpi onward (Figures S7 and S10). While the fluorogenic probe H2DCFDA functions as a broad‐spectrum indicator of total intracellular ROS accumulation, the molecular identity of this oxidative burst is further elucidated by transcriptomic data. The highly synchronized upregulation of the NADPH oxidase subunits RBOHB and RBOHH suggests that WTK1‐mediated early defense may involve increased production of superoxide (O2˙−), followed by accumulation of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). This interpretation is consistent with, but not directly proven by, the fluorescence observed in the histopathological assays. Together, the elevated ROS phenotype and the transcriptional upregulation of ROS‐generating and ROS‐scavenging enzymes and defense‐associated transcription factors (including WRKY and NAC families) support the presence of a strong oxidative burst that likely primes PCD and linked defenses in the resistant NIL (Table S8).

Complementing the ROS program, our data indicate robust activation of calcium signaling, consistent with models in which resistosome‐like channels and downstream Ca2+‐responsive kinases and transcriptional regulators (e.g., WRKYs, CAMTAs, CBP60s) couple early effector perception to metacaspase activation and HR execution (Iqbal et al. 2020; Ren et al. 2021; Li et al. 2024). In the framework proposed here (Figure 7; Table S8), WTK1 may cooperate with an associated NLR to modulate Ca2+ influx through a resistosome‐like pore, analogous to emerging tandem kinase‐NLR systems such as WTN1 (Lu et al. 2025) and Sr62NLR (Chen et al. 2025). In line with this hypothesis, we detected induction of multiple candidate Ca2+‐permeable channels (Taneja and Upadhyay 2021), including CNGC17, GLR2.7, GLR3.4, ANN4, ANN5, TPC1, and CSC1, together with Ca2+‐dependent protein kinases (e.g., CPK3) and Ca2+‐responsive transcriptional regulators (WRKYs, CAMTA1/2, and CBP60B/C) that are widely implicated in defense amplification and PCD (Figure 7 ; Table S8).

Downstream of Ca2+/ROS signaling, the WTK1 module appears to engage a protease‐centered PCD machinery. We observed upregulation of proteases linked to immune‐associated cell death, including metacaspases, vacuolar processing enzymes, and additional defense‐related proteases known to coordinate localized collapse and restrict pathogen spread (van Doorn et al. 2011; Mondal et al. 2021; Pei et al. 2024) (Figure 7 ; Table S8). Notably, the Ca2+ dependence of several metacaspases (e.g., AtMC4) provides a mechanistic bridge whereby Ca2+ fluxes can directly couple early signaling to the execution phase of HR and, potentially, autophagy‐associated remodeling (Tsiatsiani et al. 2011; Minina et al. 2014, 2017; Bozhkov 2018). Collectively, these patterns position Ca2+ as a central integrator in WTK1‐mediated immunity, linking pathogen perception to transcriptional rewiring and controlled cell‐death outputs (Figure 7 ; Bozhkov et al. 2005; Minina et al. 2013, 2014; Thor 2019; Ren et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2024).

Autophagy and vacuolar trafficking further modulate these death‐associated responses. The induction of vacuolar processing enzymes, metacaspases, and autophagy‐related genes indicates that WTK1 signaling facilitates vacuolar remodeling and HR‐associated cellular reorganization in infected cells, consistent with our previous confocal observations (Klymiuk et al. 2018; Figure 7 ; Table S8). Consistent with engagement of the endomembrane system, we observed late induction of vacuolar sorting factors such as VPS11 (cluster C4; peak at 120 hpi) and the vacuolar sorting receptor VSR55 (cluster C20; peak at 120 hpi) (Figure 7; Table S8), pointing to retromer/SNX‐linked trafficking and receptor recycling that may enhance vacuolar delivery of defense‐related cargo during infection (Castro and Rodriguez‐Furlan 2026; Hara‐Nishimura and Hatsugai 2011; Hatsugai et al. 2015; Tan et al. 2017; Zhu et al. 2025).

Induction of multiple phospholipase (PL) genes was also detected, suggesting that these enzymes generate lipid messengers that may amplify WTK1 signaling. Lipid messengers, such as phosphatidic acid (PA), are proposed to enhance RBOH‐dependent ROS production, thereby strengthening the oxidative burst that supports HR and defense gene activation (Mehta et al. 2021). In our dataset, a broad set of PL families was activated, including PLAs (PLA1‐II7, PLA1, PLIP2, PLAIIA), PLCs (PLC2, PLC6), and PLDs (PLDZETA1, PLD1) (Figure 7; Table S8). PLC‐derived second messengers (diacylglycerol and inositol phosphates) and PLD‐derived PA can integrate with Ca2+ and ROS pathways (Balla 2013; Xie et al. 2025). Under the PA‐ROS‐SA conceptual model, PA promotes RBOHB/H activation, enhancing ROS production and supporting downstream salicylic acid‐associated signaling that contributes to systemic resistance (Zhang and Xiao 2015; Sun and Zhang 2024). Thus, lipid signaling is a plausible biochemical route by which WTK1‐triggered recognition is translated into durable local and systemic immune states (Figure 7).

Our results establish a spatiotemporal framework for WTK1‐associated immune reprogramming during wheat–Pst interaction and identify candidate downstream pathways for future mechanistic dissection. In summary, WTK1‐mediated resistance involves a coordinated network that integrates Ca2+/ROS signaling, transcriptional reprogramming, protease‐driven hypersensitive response, lipid signaling, and autophagy with vacuolar trafficking. It is proposed that WTK1 functions within a modular immune complex, likely in association with an NLR protein, to couple pathogen recognition with localized containment and broader defense activation in wheat. A limitation of this study is the lack of direct resolution of the receptor architecture upstream of the observed defense outputs. While recent research in related tandem kinase systems has implicated NLR‐associated modules, the experimental design here focused on defining the downstream phenotypic and transcriptional programs associated with WTK1 activation, rather than testing NLR requirement through genetic or biochemical approaches. Therefore, the mechanistic involvement of additional receptor partners remains a hypothesis for future investigation.
