Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a Lagrange multiplier approach to construct linearly implicit energy-preserving schemes of arbitrary order for general Hamiltonian PDEs. Unlike the widely used auxiliary variable methods, this novel approach does not require the nonlinear part of the energy to be bounded from below, thereby offering broader applicability. Moreover, this approach preserves the original energy exactly at both the continuous and discrete levels, as opposed to a modified energy preserved by the auxiliary variable methods. Rigorous proofs are provided for the energy conservation and numerical accuracy of all derived schemes. The trade-off for these advantages is the need to solve a nonlinear algebraic equation to determine the Lagrange multiplier. Nevertheless, numerical experiments show that the associated computational cost is generally not dominant, indicating that the new schemes retain computational efficiency comparable to the auxiliary variable-based schemes. Numerical results demonstrate the efficiency, accuracy, and structure-preserving properties of the proposed schemes.

Introduction

Many important conservation models, including the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, the sine-Gordon equation, and the Korteweg-de Vries equation, can be reformulated as Hamiltonian systems
[
26
]
. In this paper, we consider Hamiltonian PDEs of the form

z_{t}=\mathcal{S}\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta z},

(1)

where
\mathcal{S}
is a skew-adjoint operator independent of the solution variable
z(x,t)
, with
(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A most intuitive property of (
1
) is the conservation of energy, meaning that the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is invariant under the continuous flow, i.e.,

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(z)=0,\quad\mathcal{H}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\big(z,\mathcal{L}z\big)+\big(N(z),1\big),

(2)

where
\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta z}=\mathcal{L}z+N^{\prime}(z)
,
\mathcal{L}
is a symmetric non-negative linear operator,
N(z)
denotes the nonlinear energy density, and
(\cdot,\cdot)
represents the standard
L^{2}
inner product on
\Omega
. Nowadays, numerical schemes that preserve a discrete analogue of (
2
) are often preferred over non-conservative methods, especially for long-time simulations.

Fully implicit schemes have served as a cornerstone in the development of energy-preserving methods and continue to be of fundamental importance. For problems with quadratic energy functionals, symplectic Runge-Kutta (RK) methods
[
21
]
are naturally energy-preserving and can achieve arbitrary accuracy by increasing the number of stages. For more general forms of energy, discrete gradient methods
[
31
]
provide a systematic framework for constructing energy-preserving schemes. Among these, the averaged vector field (AVF) method
[
32
]
and its extensions
[
12
,
36
]
are particularly prominent. These methods were later generalized within the discrete variational derivative (DVD) framework
[
18
]
. Driven by the growing demand for high-precision simulations, high-order methods have attracted significant attentions. Examples include high-order AVF schemes
[
22
,
28
]
, time finite element methods
[
2
,
35
]
, and Hamiltonian boundary value methods (HBVMs)
[
5
,
7
,
9
]
. In particular, HBVMs have been employed as spectral methods for efficiently solving some classes of Hamiltonian PDEs
[
6
]
. Despite these advances, all aforementioned methods are fully implicit and require solving nonlinear systems. This often results in high computational costs, especially in large-scale simulations or for problems with strong nonlinearities. Additional challenges arise from the need for efficient nonlinear solvers
[
8
]
. These aspects can limit their practicality in computationally intensive settings, which motivates ongoing research into more efficient alternatives.

Linearly implicit energy-preserving schemes provide an effective alternative to the above problems. These schemes not only preserve the energy but also enable the efficient computation by requiring only solving linear systems at each time step. For systems with polynomial energies, the multiple DVD method
[
30
]
and the polarization approach
[
16
]
have been proposed. However, no general methodology is available for conserving arbitrary forms of energy until the advent of auxiliary variable methods, which include the scalar auxiliary variable (SAV)
[
34
]
and the invariant energy quadratization (IEQ)
[
37
]
methods. Although originally developed for phase field models, they have been successfully extended to various conservative systems, including Hamiltonian systems (
1
)
[
3
,
10
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
38
]
. These two methods share similar ideas in constructing linear schemes. To facilitate a comparative study with the Lagrange multiplier approach, we present a sketch of the SAV method. Let
F(z)=\big(N(z),1\big)
, the SAV method introduces an additional variable
r(t)=\sqrt{F(z)+c_{0}}
, under the restriction that
F(z)
is bounded from below. The constant
c_{0}
is selected to ensure that
F(z)+c_{0}>0
. Then, the system (
1
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} z_{t}&=\mathcal{S}\big(\mathcal{L}z+\frac{N^{\prime}(z)}{\sqrt{F(z)+c_{0}}}r\big)\\
r_{t}&=\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{N^{\prime}(z)}{\sqrt{F(z)+c_{0}}},z_{t}\big).\end{aligned}

(3)

The system (
3
) satisfies a modified energy conservation law

\frac{d}{dt}~\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big(z,\mathcal{L}z\big)+r^{2}\Big)=0.

(4)

The SAV and IEQ methods transform the original energy (
2
) into a simple quadratic form, thereby simplifying the treatment of the nonlinear term. Then, a second-order linearly implicit energy-preserving scheme reads

\displaystyle\frac{z^{n+1}-z^{n}}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}\big(\mathcal{L}z^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{N^{\prime}(\tilde{z}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})}{\sqrt{F(\tilde{z}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})+c_{0}}}r^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big),

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{r^{n+1}-r^{n}}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{N^{\prime}(\tilde{z}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})}{\sqrt{F(\tilde{z}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})+c_{0}}},\frac{z^{n+1}-z^{n}}{\Delta t}\big),

where
\Delta t
is the time step,
z^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{z^{n}+z^{n+1}}{2}
and
\tilde{z}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{3z^{n}-z^{n-1}}{2}
. The scheme (
5
) is named SAV-CN. It is not difficult to prove that SAV-CN is energy-preserving in the sense that

\mathcal{H}^{n+1}=\mathcal{H}^{n},\quad\mathcal{H}^{n}=\frac{1}{2}\big(z^{n},\mathcal{L}z^{n}\big)+\big(r^{n}\big)^{2}.

(6)

Note that
r^{2}\neq F(z)
when
c_{0}\neq 0
, the modified energy (
4
) differs from the original energy (
2
). Consequently, the scheme (
5
) does not preserve the original energy. Moreover, implementing (
5
) numerically requires solving a linear system with variable coefficients, which in turn relies on nonlinear iterations and increases computational costs. Higher-order linear schemes
[
1
,
4
,
17
,
27
,
29
]
can be constructed using symplectic RK methods and high-order extrapolation formulas. Although both the SAV and IEQ methods lead to linear schemes, the calculation of the solution variable and the auxiliary variable in the resulting schemes can not be decoupled. Then, extra nonlinear iterations must be applied previously to obtain the solution variable, which would become more complicated for high-order schemes.

Recently, supplementary variable methods
[
20
]
have been developed to construct linear schemes for general energies by adding extra terms to the original vector field to enforce the conservation of energy. In this paper, inspired by
[
15
]
, we adopt an alternative approach wherein the Lagrange multiplier is multiplied directly to the nonlinear term without introducing extra terms, resulting in a modified system that is structurally simpler. The remarkable features are given as

i.

The Lagrange multiplier is not based on any restrictions, so it is applicable to general Hamiltonian PDEs.

ii.

In both continuous and discrete cases, the new approach conserves the original energy (
2
), as opposed to a modified energy in the auxiliary variable methods.

iii.

We present a novel class of energy-preserving schemes that are computationally efficient and capable of arbitrary order accuracy. Although solving for the Lagrange multiplier is necessary, its computational cost is shown to be negligible.

iv.

The methodology of this paper can be simply generalized to other conservative or dissipative systems.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section
2
details the main results, starting with the Lagrange multiplier approach and a simple second-order linear scheme. Then, we develop high-order linearly implicit energy-preserving schemes via a prediction-correction strategy. This is followed by rigorous analyses of the original energy conservation and numerical accuracy for all proposed schemes. In Section
3
, numerical tests are provided to illustrate the effectiveness, convergence, and structure-preserving properties of the proposed schemes. Some concluding remarks are given in Section
4
.

2
Main results

We introduce below a novel Lagrange multiplier approach for Hamiltonian systems (
1
). The multiplier is multiplied directly on the nonlinear term of the original vector field without adding additional variables and terms.

2.1
The Lagrange multiplier approach and its second-order scheme

Defining a scalar function
\lambda(t)
, the system (
1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} z_{t}&=\mathcal{S}\big(\mathcal{L}z+\lambda(t)N^{\prime}(z)\big),\\
\frac{d}{dt}F(z)&=\lambda(t)\big(N^{\prime}(z),z_{t}\big),\end{aligned}

(7)

where
F(z)=\big(N(z),1\big)
is the nonlinear part of (
2
). If we set the initial condition
\lambda(0)=1
, then the new system (
7
) is equivalent to (
1
), i.e.,
\lambda(t)\equiv 1
in (
7
).

Theorem 1
.

The system (
7
) satisfies the original energy conservation law

\frac{d}{dt}~\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big(z,\mathcal{L}z\big)+\big(\mathcal{N}(z),1\big)\Big)=0.

(8)

Proof.

Taking the inner product of the first equation in (
7
) with
\mathcal{L}z+\lambda(t)N^{\prime}(z)
and summing it with the second equation, we obtain (
8
).
∎

Note that unlike the SAV method (
3
) where
r(t)
is just an auxiliary variable,
\lambda(t)
here is to serve as a Lagrange multiplier to enforce conservation of the original energy. Furthermore,
F(z)
does not require to be bounded from below. Similar to the SAV method, we can construct efficient numerical schemes for (
7
). For example, a novel second-order scheme based on the Crank-Nicolson method is as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{z^{n+1}-z^{n}}{\Delta t}&=\mathcal{S}\big(\mathcal{L}z^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda^{n+\frac{1}{2}}N^{\prime}(\tilde{z}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})\big),\\
\frac{F(z^{n+1})-F(z^{n})}{\Delta t}&=\lambda^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big(N^{\prime}(\tilde{z}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}),\frac{z^{n+1}-z^{n}}{\Delta t}\big).\end{aligned}

(9)

Hereafter, the scheme (
9
) is named LM-CN. There is the following energy conservation.

Theorem 2
.

The scheme LM-CN (
9
) satisfies the semi-discrete original energy conservation law

\mathcal{H}^{n+1}=\mathcal{H}^{n},\quad\mathcal{H}^{n}=\frac{1}{2}\big(z^{n},\mathcal{L}z^{n}\big)+\big(N(z^{n}),1\big).

(10)

Proof.

Computing the inner product of the first equation in (
9
) with
\mathcal{L}z^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda^{n+\frac{1}{2}}N^{\prime}(\tilde{z}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})
and summing it with the second equation yields the preservation of original energy (
10
).
∎

We present an efficient method for solving the scheme LM-CN (
9
), beginning with a derivation of

z^{n+1}=p^{n}+\lambda^{n+\frac{1}{2}}q^{n},

(11)

where

p^{n}=(I-\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L})^{-1}(I+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L})z^{n},\ q^{n}=\Delta t(I-\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{S}N^{\prime}(\tilde{z}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}).

(12)

The values of
p^{n}
and
q^{n}
can be computed explicitly. Substituting (
11
) into the second equation of (
9
), we obtain

\big(N(p^{n}+\lambda^{n+\frac{1}{2}}q^{n})-N(z^{n}),1\big)=\lambda^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big(N^{\prime}(\tilde{z}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}),p^{n}+\lambda^{n+\frac{1}{2}}q^{n}-z^{n}\big).

(13)

As the equation (
13
) is a nonlinear algebraic equation, it generally admits multiple solutions. However, since
\lambda^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
approximates
1
, we can compute it efficiently using the Newton iteration with
1
as the initial value. To summarize, the scheme LM-CN (
9
) consists of the following steps:

\star

step 1:
compute
p^{n}
and
q^{n}
from (
12
);

\star

step 2:
search
\lambda^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
by solving (
13
);

\star

step 3:
obtain
z^{n+1}
by (
11
) and then proceed to the next step.

The notation
(\cdot,\cdot)
in (
13
) denotes the
L^{2}
inner product. Following spatial discretization, this is evaluated using the discrete
L^{2}
inner product
[
33
]
. Note that only the linear constant-coefficient equations need to be solved, along with the algebraic equation (
13
). Since the computational cost of determining the Lagrange multiplier is negligible compared to solving (
12
), the scheme LM-CN (
9
) is computationally efficient. Moreover, when combined with Fourier pseudo-spectral spatial discretization, the computations in (
12
) can be significantly accelerated using fast Fourier transforms.

2.2
High-order linearly implicit energy-preserving schemes

This subsection shows how to easily derive higher-order linear schemes for solving (
7
). The proposed method utilizes the Gauss collocation methods integrated with a prediction-correction framework. For this purpose, we first introduce the following high-order prediction-correction scheme.

2.2.1
The high-order prediction-correction scheme

To facilitate the presentation, we recast (
1
) in the following form

z_{t}=f_{1}(z)+f_{2}(z),

(14)

where
f_{1}(z)=\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}z
and
f_{2}(z)=\mathcal{S}N^{\prime}(z)
. Applying the
s
-stage RK method
[
21
]
with coefficients
a_{ij}
,
b_{i}
,
c_{i}
(
i,j=1,2,\cdots,s
) to (
14
) on the time interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
leads to the following scheme

\displaystyle z_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=z^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}k_{j}^{n},

(15)

\displaystyle k_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=f_{1}\big(z_{i}^{n}\big)+f_{2}\big(z_{i}^{n}\big),

\displaystyle z^{n+1}

\displaystyle=z^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}k_{i}^{n}.

Here, we allow a full matrix
(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{s}
of non-zero coefficients. It should be noted, however, that solving such a fully implicit scheme can be computationally challenging. A nonlinear iterative solver must be employed, which entails substantial computational expenses. In particular, HBVM(
s
,
s
), which is known to be equivalent to an
s
-stage Gauss method, requires the design of efficient nonlinear solvers, especially for high-dimensional problems
[
8
]
. To mitigate this, we present the following high-order prediction-correction scheme:

\star

step1 (prediction):
set
z_{i,(0)}^{n}=z^{n}
and compute
k_{i,(m+1)}^{n}
from
m=0
to
\Lambda-1
by solving

\displaystyle z_{i,(m+1)}^{n}

\displaystyle=z^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}k_{j,(m+1)}^{n},

(16)

\displaystyle k_{i,(m+1)}^{n}

\displaystyle=f_{1}\big(z_{i,(m+1)}^{n}\big)+f_{2}\big(z_{i,(m)}^{n}\big),

\star

step 2 (correction):
update
z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}
by

z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}=z^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}k_{i,(\Lambda)}^{n}.

(17)

The following lemma holds for the two RK schemes described above.

Lemma 3
.

Assuming that
z^{n+1}
and
z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}
are solutions to the schemes (
15
) and (
17
), respectively, it follows that

z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}-z^{n+1}=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\Lambda+1}\big).

(18)

Proof.

From (
15
) and (
17
), we can first obtain

z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}-z^{n+1}=\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\big(k_{i,(\Lambda)}^{n}-k_{i}^{n})=\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}(g_{1,i}^{n}+g_{2,i}^{n}),

(19)

where

g_{1,i}^{n}=f_{1}\big(z_{i,(\Lambda)}^{n})-f_{1}(z_{i}^{n}\big),\quad g_{2,i}^{n}=f_{2}\big(z_{i,(\Lambda-1)}^{n}\big)-f_{2}\big(z_{i}^{n}\big).

(20)

Note that
z_{i,(m)}^{n}
and
z_{i}^{n}
can be considered functions of the time step
\Delta t
. For
\Delta t=0
, it follows directly that

z_{i,(m)}^{n}=z_{i}^{n}=z^{n},\quad i=1,\cdots,s,~m=0,1,\cdots,\Lambda.

Differentiating the first two equations in (
15
) and (
16
) with respect to
\Delta t
and applying the chain rule yields

\frac{d^{q}}{d(\Delta t)^{q}}\big(z_{i,(m)}^{n}-z_{i}^{n}\big)\bigg|_{\Delta t=0}=0,\quad q=0,1,\cdots,m.

Here the number
q
denotes the order of derivatives. It immediately leads to

z_{i,(m)}^{n}-z_{i}^{n}=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{m+1}\big),\quad m=\Lambda-1~\mbox{or}~\Lambda.

(21)

Substituting (
21
) into (
20
) and considering the smoothness of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, we obtain the following equation via the Taylor expansion as

g_{1,i}^{n}+g_{2,i}^{n}=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\Lambda+1}\big)+\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\Lambda}\big)=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\Lambda}\big).

Combining (
19
) with the identity
\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}=1
, we have

z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}-z^{n+1}=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\Lambda+1}\big).

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4
.

Given that the underlying RK method is of order
p
, the scheme (
16
)-(
17
) possesses an order of
\min\{p,\Lambda\}
, i.e.,

z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}-z(t_{n+1})=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\min\{p,\Lambda\}+1}\big).

(22)

Proof.

If the underlying RK method is of order
p
, it holds for (
15
) that

z^{n+1}-z(t_{n+1})=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{p+1}\big).

Combining this result with (
18
) yields (
22
), which completes the proof.
∎

2.2.2
The high-order Lagrange multiplier scheme

In general, the above prediction-correction scheme (
16
)-(
17
) typically fails to conserve the energy. To address this issue, we employ it to develop a high-order Lagrange multiplier scheme. We then analyze the original energy conservation and numerical accuracy of the proposed scheme. Specifically, a high-order prediction step is applied to (
14
), followed by a high-order correction step applied to the Lagrange multiplier system (
7
). This procedure yields the high-order linear scheme as follows:

\star

step1 (prediction):
set
z_{i,(0)}^{n}=z^{n}
and compute
z_{i,(m+1)}^{n}
from
m=0
to
\Lambda-1
by solving the linear system

\displaystyle z_{i,(m+1)}^{n}

\displaystyle=z^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}k_{j,(m+1)}^{n},

(23)

\displaystyle k_{i,(m+1)}^{n}

\displaystyle=f_{1}\big(z_{i,(m+1)}^{n}\big)+f_{2}\big(z_{i,(m)}^{n}\big).

Then, we take
\tilde{z}_{i}^{n}=z_{i,(\Lambda)}^{n}
for all
i=1,\cdots,s
.

\star

step 2 (correction):
update
z^{n+1}
by

\displaystyle z_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=z^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}k_{j}^{n},

(24)

\displaystyle k_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=f_{1}\big(z_{i}^{n}\big)+\lambda^{n}f_{2}\big(\tilde{z}_{i}^{n}\big),

\displaystyle z^{n+1}

\displaystyle=z^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}k_{i}^{n},

\displaystyle F(z^{n+1})

\displaystyle=F(z^{n})+\lambda^{n}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\big(N^{\prime}(\tilde{z}_{i}^{n}),k_{i}^{n}\big).

Note that
F(z^{n})=\big(N(z^{n}),1\big)
. This scheme admits an energy conservation law of the following form.

Theorem 5
.

The scheme (
23
)-(
24
) satisfies the semi-discrete original energy conservation law

\mathcal{H}^{n+1}=\mathcal{H}^{n},\quad\mathcal{H}^{n}=\frac{1}{2}\big(z^{n},\mathcal{L}z^{n}\big)+\big(N(z^{n}),1\big).

(25)

Proof.

The third equation in (
24
) gives

\mathcal{L}z^{n+1}=\mathcal{L}z^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\mathcal{L}k_{i}^{n}.

(26)

Taking the inner product of (
26
) with
z^{n+1}
and using the symmetry of
\mathcal{L}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\big(z^{n+1},\mathcal{L}z^{n+1}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(z^{n},\mathcal{L}z^{n}\big)+2\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\big(k_{i}^{n},\mathcal{L}z^{n}\big)

(27)

\displaystyle+(\Delta t)^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{s}b_{i}b_{j}\big(k_{i}^{n},\mathcal{L}k_{j}^{n}\big).

From the first equation of (
24
), it follows that

\mathcal{L}z^{n}=\mathcal{L}z_{i}^{n}-\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}\mathcal{L}k_{j}^{n}.

Substituting this into the second term of (
27
) gives

\displaystyle\big(z^{n+1},\mathcal{L}z^{n+1}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(z^{n},\mathcal{L}z^{n}\big)+2\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\big(k_{i}^{n},\mathcal{L}z_{i}^{n}\big),

(28)

\displaystyle+(\Delta t)^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{s}\big(b_{i}b_{j}-b_{i}a_{ij}-b_{j}a_{ji}\big)\big(k_{i}^{n},\mathcal{L}k_{j}^{n}\big).

The RK symplecticity condition
[
21

b_{i}b_{j}-b_{i}a_{ij}-b_{j}a_{ji}=0,\quad i,j=1,\cdots,s,

(29)

implies the vanishing of the second row in (

) with
\mathcal{L}z_{i}^{n}+\lambda^{n}N^{\prime}(\tilde{z}_{i}^{n})
, and noting that

is skew-adjoint, we derive

\big(k_{i}^{n},\mathcal{L}z_{i}^{n}\big)+\lambda^{n}\big(k_{i}^{n},N^{\prime}(\tilde{z}_{i}^{n})\big)=0.

Inserting it into (
28

\frac{1}{2}\big(z^{n+1},\mathcal{L}z^{n+1}\big)=\frac{1}{2}\big(z^{n},\mathcal{L}z^{n}\big)-\lambda^{n}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\big(k_{i}^{n},N^{\prime}(\tilde{z}_{i}^{n})\big).

Together with the fourth equation in (
24
), this result leads to (
25
), thus completing the proof.
∎

23
)-(
24
) efficiently. For each

\textbf{z}_{(m+1)}^{n}=\mathcal{C}^{-1}\big(\textbf{z}^{n}+\Delta tA\textbf{f}_{2,(m)}^{n}\big),

where
\mathcal{C}=I_{s}-\Delta t\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}A
,

denotes the

identity matrix,
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{s}
is the RK matrix, and the
s
-dimensional column vectors

\displaystyle\textbf{z}^{n}

\displaystyle=\big(z^{n},z^{n},\cdots,z^{n}\big)^{T},

\displaystyle\textbf{z}_{(m+1)}^{n}

\displaystyle=\big(z_{1,(m+1)}^{n},\cdots,z_{s,(m+1)}^{n}\big)^{T},

\displaystyle\textbf{f}_{2,(m)}^{n}

\displaystyle=\big(f_{2}(z_{1,(m)}^{n}),\cdots,f_{2}(z_{s,(m)}^{n})\big)^{T}.

After completing the prediction step (

\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}=\textbf{z}_{(\Lambda)}^{n},

\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}=\big(\tilde{z}_{1}^{n},\cdots,\tilde{z}_{s}^{n}\big)^{T}

\textbf{k}^{n}=L_{1}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{1}^{n},

L_{1}^{n}=\mathcal{C}^{-1}f_{1}(\textbf{z}^{n}),\quad R_{1}^{n}=\mathcal{C}^{-1}f_{2}(\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}),\quad\textbf{k}^{n}=\big(k_{1}^{n},\cdots,k_{s}^{n}\big)^{T}.

(31)

Then, the final numerical solution is

z^{n+1}=L_{2}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{2}^{n},

(32)

where

L_{2}^{n}=z^{n}+\Delta t\textbf{b}L_{1}^{n},\quad R_{2}^{n}=\Delta t\textbf{b}R_{1}^{n},\quad\textbf{b}=\big(b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{s}\big).

(33)

We can employ the Newton iteration with an initial value
1
to solve the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda^{n}
from the algebraic equation

\big(N(L_{2}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{2}^{n})-N(z^{n}),1\big)-\lambda^{n}\Delta t\textbf{b}\big(N^{\prime}(\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}),L_{1}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{1}^{n}\big)=0.

(34)

In summary, the scheme (
23
)-(
24
) can be implemented in the following steps:

\star

step 1:
obtain
\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}
from (
30
);

\star

step 2:
compute
L_{1}^{n}
,
R_{1}^{n}
and
L_{2}^{n}
,
R_{2}^{n}
from (
31
) and (
33
);

\star

step 3:
search
\lambda^{n}
by solving (
34
);

\star

step 4:
get
z^{n+1}
by (
32
) and then proceed to the next step.

In the first two steps, this scheme requires solving only linear systems with constant coefficients. When combined with pseudo-spectral spatial discretization, these solution process can be accelerated using fast Fourier transform. Subsequent numerical tests show that the computational cost of the Lagrange multiplier is negligible, so the calculation of the proposed scheme is efficient.

2.3
Convergence of the Lagrange multiplier scheme

This subsection is devoted to a convergence analysis of the Lagrange multiplier scheme. Following the work of
[
13
,
14
]
, we gives the following theorem.

Theorem 6
.

If
\big(N^{\prime}(z^{n}),\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta z}(z^{n})\big)\neq 0
, there exists a constant
\Delta t^{*}
such that, for all
\Delta t\in(0,\Delta t^{*}]
, the algebraic equation (
34
) locally defines a unique solution
\lambda^{n}=\lambda^{n}(\Delta t)
in a neighborhood of
1
. Furthermore, if the underlying RK method is of order
p
, then the scheme (
23
)-(
24
) is of order
min
{
p,
\Lambda
}
, i.e.,

z^{n+1}-z(t_{n+1})=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\min\{p,\Lambda\}+1}\big).

(35)

Proof.

Note that
\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}
,
L_{1}^{n}
,
R_{1}^{n}
,
L_{2}^{n}
and
R_{2}^{n}
in (
34
) can be regarded as functions of
\Delta t
. It follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}

\displaystyle=\textbf{z}^{n},\quad\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}L_{1}^{n}=f_{1}(\textbf{z}^{n}),\quad\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}R_{1}^{n}=f_{2}(\textbf{z}^{n}),

(36)

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}L_{2}^{n}

\displaystyle=z^{n},\quad\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}R_{2}^{n}=0.

From (
34
), we introduce

\lambda^{n}=\frac{\big(N(L_{2}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{2}^{n})-N(z^{n}),1\big)}{\Delta t\textbf{b}\big(N^{\prime}(\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}),L_{1}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{1}^{n}\big)}:=\varphi(\lambda^{n},\Delta t).

(37)

Using the L’Hôpital’s rule, along with the antisymmetry of
\mathcal{S}
and (
36
), we derive

\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\lambda^{n}=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\varphi(\lambda^{n},\Delta t)=\frac{\big(N^{\prime}(z^{n}),f_{1}(z^{n})\big)}{\big(N^{\prime}(z^{n}),f_{1}(z^{n})\big)}=1,

(38)

where
\big(N^{\prime}(z^{n}),f_{1}(z^{n})\big)=\big(N^{\prime}(z^{n}),\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta z}(z^{n})\big)\neq 0
. Analogous calculations show that

\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial\varphi(\lambda^{n},\Delta t)}{\partial\lambda^{n}}=\frac{0}{\big(N^{\prime}(z^{n}),f_{1}(z^{n})\big)}=0.

This equation confirms a contraction property. Therefore, by the fixed-point theorem, for a sufficiently small
\Delta t^{*}
, (
37
) possesses a unique solution
\lambda^{n}=\lambda^{n}(\Delta t)
near
1
for all
\Delta t\in(0,\Delta t^{*}]
. Subsequently, we can compute

\lambda^{n}-1=\frac{\big(N(L_{2}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{2}^{n})-N(z^{n}),1\big)-\Delta t\textbf{b}\big(N^{\prime}(\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}),L_{1}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{1}^{n}\big)}{\Delta t\textbf{b}\big(N^{\prime}(\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}),L_{1}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{1}^{n}\big)}:=\frac{\mu(\Delta t)}{\nu(\Delta t)}.

(39)

Combining (
36
), (
38
), and the identity
\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}=1
, we obtain

\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\textbf{b}\big(N^{\prime}(\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}),L_{1}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{1}^{n}\big)=\big(N^{\prime}(z^{n}),f_{1}(z^{n})\big)\neq 0.

This result implies that

\nu(\Delta t)=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t).

Then, we define
z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}
as the solution of

\displaystyle\hat{z}_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=z^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}\hat{k}_{j}^{n},

\displaystyle\hat{k}_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=f_{1}\big(\hat{z}_{i}^{n}\big)+f_{2}\big(\tilde{z}_{i}^{n}\big),

\displaystyle z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=z^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\hat{k}_{i}^{n},

where
\tilde{z}_{i}^{n}=z_{i,(\Lambda)}^{n}
is computed from (
23
). According to Theorem
4
, if the underlying RK method is of order
p
, it follows that

\displaystyle z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}-z(t_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\min\{p,\Lambda\}+1}\big),

(40)

\displaystyle F\big(z(t_{n+1})\big)-\big(F(z^{n})+\Delta t\textbf{b}\big(N^{\prime}(\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}),\hat{\textbf{k}}^{n}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\min\{p,\Lambda\}+1}\big),

where
\hat{\textbf{k}}^{n}=\big(\hat{k}_{1}^{n},\cdots,\hat{k}_{s}^{n}\big)^{T}
, and we have
z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}=L_{2}^{n}+R_{2}^{n}
and
\hat{\textbf{k}}^{n}=L_{1}^{n}+R_{1}^{n}
. Using the Taylor expansion and the first equation in (
40
), we get

F\big(z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}\big)-F\big(z(t_{n+1})\big)=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\min\{p,\Lambda\}+1}\big).

With (
38
), the above equations imply that as
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
,

\displaystyle\mu(\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\big(N(L_{2}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{2}^{n})-N(z^{n}),1\big)-\Delta t\textbf{b}\big(N^{\prime}(\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}),L_{1}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{1}^{n}\big),

\displaystyle=F\big(z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}\big)-F\big(z(t_{n+1})\big)+F\big(z(t_{n+1})\big)-\big(F(z^{n})+\Delta t\textbf{b}\big(N^{\prime}(\tilde{\textbf{z}}^{n}),\hat{\textbf{k}}^{n}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\min\{p,\Lambda\}+1}\big).

From (
39
), we then have

\lambda^{n}-1=\frac{\mu(\Delta t)}{\nu(\Delta t)}=\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta t)^{\min\{p,\Lambda\}}\big).

(41)

Thus, the accuracy of the scheme (
23
)-(
24
) can be obtained from

\displaystyle z^{n+1}-z(t_{n+1})

\displaystyle=L_{2}^{n}+\lambda^{n}R_{2}^{n}-z(t_{n+1})

\displaystyle=L_{2}^{n}+R_{2}^{n}-z(t_{n+1})+(\lambda^{n}-1)R_{2}^{n},

\displaystyle=z_{(\Lambda)}^{n+1}-z(t_{n+1})+(\lambda^{n}-1)\Delta t\textbf{b}R_{1}^{n}.

In the limit as
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
, the equations (
36
), (
40
) and (
41
) collectively yield (
35
), which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 7
.

Theorem
6
requires that
\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta z}
not be orthogonal to
N^{\prime}(z)
, this condition that is generically satisfied. Moreover, viewing the scheme (
23
)-(
24
) as a high-order generalization of the scheme LM-CN (
9
), this theorem also implies the second-order convergence of the latter.

Finally, we employ the fourth- and sixth-order Gauss methods for numerical tests due to their excellent stability and high accuracy. The corresponding Butcher tableaux
[
21
]
are displayed as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c|cc}\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}&\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}&\frac{1}{4}\\
\hline\cr&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\end{array}~\mbox{and}~\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{15}}{10}&\frac{5}{36}&\frac{2}{9}-\frac{\sqrt{15}}{15}&\frac{5}{36}-\frac{\sqrt{15}}{30}\\
\frac{1}{2}&\frac{5}{36}+\frac{\sqrt{15}}{24}&\frac{2}{9}&\frac{5}{36}-\frac{\sqrt{15}}{24}\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{15}}{10}&\frac{5}{36}+\frac{\sqrt{15}}{30}&\frac{2}{9}+\frac{\sqrt{15}}{15}&\frac{5}{36}\\
\hline\cr&\frac{5}{18}&\frac{4}{9}&\frac{5}{18}\end{array}.

We refer to the high-order Lagrange multiplier scheme (
23
)-(
24
) as LM-GAUSS. As the Gauss methods are symplectic, they satisfy the RK symplecticity condition (
29
) in the proof of Theorem
5
. The diagonally implicit symplectic RK methods also serve as a promising alternative, capable of producing favorable numerical results. These methods will not be further explored in this paper. In summary, LM-GAUSS constitutes a novel class of linear energy-preserving schemes that can achieve arbitrary accuracy by increasing the RK stages.

4
Conclusions

In this paper, we present a novel framework for constructing linearly implicit energy-preserving schemes of arbitrary order for Hamiltonian PDEs. The methodology integrates the newly developed Lagrange multiplier approach with symplectic Runge-Kutta methods and a prediction-correction strategy, enabling exact preservation of the original energy in both continuous and discrete settings. The resulting numerical schemes only require solving linear equations with constant coefficients. When combined with the Fourier pseudo-spectral spatial discretization, their computational efficiency is further improved via fast Fourier transforms. We provide rigorous proofs of the energy conservation and numerical accuracy for all schemes. Finally, numerical experiments on three representative Hamiltonian PDEs demonstrate the superior behaviors of the proposed schemes.
