“ Yeah Right! ” - Do LLMs Exhibit Multimodal Feature Transfer? Thanks: Work done while at Symbl.AI.
Benjamin Reichman, Kartik Talamadupula

Abstract
Human communication is a multifaceted and multimodal skill. Communication requires an understanding of both the surface-level textual content and the connotative intent of a piece of communication. In humans, learning to go beyond the surface level starts by learning communicative intent in speech. Once humans acquire these skills in spoken communication, they transfer those skills to written communication. In this paper, we assess the ability of speech+text models and text models trained with special emphasis on human-to-human conversations to make this multimodal transfer of skill. We specifically test these models on their ability to detect covert deceptive communication. We find that with no special prompting speech+text LLMs have an advantage over unimodal LLMs in performing this task. Likewise, we find that human-to-human conversation-trained LLMs are also advantaged in this skill.



1
Introduction

Human communication is pregnant with meaning. The words of a text or speech are just the surface-level understanding of what someone is trying to communicate. The connotation of a communication can be entirely different from its denotation. In spoken language communication, non-verbal cues form a majority of what is being communicated
Mehrabian et al. (1971)
. With text communications, the sender modifies the text to convey a certain tone either through word choice, punctuation, or emojis. The receiver has to pick up on each of these signals in order to reconstruct the full intended message.

In this work, we focus on deceptive communications to test our hypothesis about multimodal transfer in large language models. There are two main classes of deceptive communications. The first – overt deception – includes any lie or misdirection. In these communications the connotation and denotation of what is being said are congruent, and the overall message is constructed to mislead and deceive. The second – covert deception – occurs when the connotation and denotation of a message are incongruent and the person listening or reading has to pick up on that. There are multiple types of covert deception. In this paper, we investigate three types in detail:

1.

Sarcasm
, which is used to mock a person or idea by taking a tone that conveys that what is being said or read should be taken for its opposite.

e.g. "Having massive watermarks over videos really enhances the experience…"

2.

Irony
, is a communication pattern that uses the contrast between expectation and reality to convey a message.

e.g. "The professor’s son was a high school dropout."

3.

Condescension
, which uses pleasant-sounding words to convey a patronizing tone (e.g. “bless your heart”).

e.g. "Sometimes, it’s better not to reveal your stupidity publicly."

Each of these covert deception behaviors is difficult to detect in a text-only medium. Though these behaviors sometimes have a common “text template”, it is difficult to detect them solely by relying on these templates. In spoken language, these behaviors are usually accompanied by voice modulation and other changes to prosody, signals that do not exist in text.

Children through their development learn how to modulate speech to convey different emotions, sentiments, and even deceptive behaviors like sarcasm. They are able to pick up on sarcasm from as young of an age as eight or nine
Creusere (1999)
. However, they are not able to identify this communicative behavior without cues from verbal intonation, indicating that identifying these communication patterns is first learned through spoken communication
Capelli et al. (1990)
. Later, they learn to transfer these skills to interpreting written language.

Large Language Models (LLMs) – in a few short years – have changed the field of natural language processing. They have made great strides in sentiment analysis and emotion detection. However, the way most LLMs learn and approach language is at odds with how humans learn language. This paper explores whether the features of multimodal LLMs trained with the speech or conversation modalities transfer to the text-only (language) modality. We focus on context-free covert deceptive communication detection as this is a task that can be challenging for both humans and machines in the (unimodal) text-only modality.

2
LLMs and Deceptive Communication

LLMs up until recently learned language solely through processing vast amounts of text. Through this process, they can learn a lot about human language and explicit human knowledge. Additionally, they can acquire a semblance of human emotional knowledge through this process. The models are helped by the fact that a lot of emotive communication follows certain templates, allowing them to pick up on these emotions. However, since emotive communication is fundamentally multi-modal, it is difficult for these models to learn them just from text.

Covert deceptive emotive communication is especially difficult to pick up just from text, as the purpose of this mode of communication is to smuggle in a connotation that is entirely at odds with the text’s denotation. This can be illustrated by how a simple phrase such as “that’s great” can be said. This phrase can be said genuinely after hearing some great news. In that case, no specific syllable is stressed. It can also be said sarcastically after hearing some bad news. In that case, many would put an inflection on the “gr” sound. Through learning language initially through speech, one can walk through this exercise and understand the intention behind both possible ways of saying “that’s great”. However, a model trained solely on text (language) would have a much harder time stepping through this exercise. This simple example of sarcastic communication is context-dependent. However, deceptive communication behaviors like sarcasm and irony can exist in context-free states (e.g. "You’re right, it’s the first week of winter! It will definitely get warmer in a couple weeks for our first playoff game."). In these cases, being able to hear the text in our mind is a great advantage for humans, but is something that LLMs are unable to do.

LLMs trained on either text+speech data or text with a special emphasis on human conversations may be able to navigate the connotation versus denotation problem better. These models are learning language features in a way that is closer to how humans learn these features. Humans learn language through interaction, speech, and dialogue, and then through reading. LLMs that are trained on the speech modality, like GPT-4o, may learn features that help them pick up on covert deceptive communication, as that is the native modality of such communications. LLMs that are fine-tuned on conversation data may also better learn the textual patterns of these deceptive communication techniques
Jawale et al. (2024)
, as such patterns may be more highly represented in dialogue than in standard written text. However, it is not yet known whether learning these features in the speech or conversation modalities transfers to the text modality.

Covert Deceptive Communication Detection

3.1
Methods

This paper focuses on three covert deceptive communication behaviors: sarcasm, irony, and condescension. We used subcomponents of the Big-Bench dataset
bench authors (2023)
to assess a model’s ability to detect each behavior as they are high-quality datasets with a much lower likelihood of having been in a model’s pretraining set. For condescension, we used the TalkDown dataset
Wang and Potts (2019)
, which is included in Big-Bench. For irony, Big-Bench’s Irony Identification dataset was used
bench authors (2023)
. For sarcasm, Big-Bench’s Snarks dataset was used
bench authors (2023)
. Both Irony Identification and Talkdown are binary classification datasets. Big-Bench’s Snarks dataset is a multiple choice dataset, where one choice is a non-sarcastic statement and the other choice is a minimally-edited version of the statement that is sarcastic. The dataset was used in both the intended fashion and converted into a binary classification dataset. "Snark1" in this paper indicates the multiple-choice Snark dataset and "Snark2" indicates the binary classification version of the dataset. We also created a general deception dataset which is a combination of the other three datasets. In this dataset, the model only needs to identify if a statement is an example of a deceptive communication behavior, without identifying the specific behavior itself. Appendix
A
provides positive and negative examples of each type of deceptive communication from the datasets used.

To investigate multimodal feature transfer for covert deception detection, we compare two separate pairs of models. The first pair is GPT-4-Turbo and GPT-4o
Achiam et al. (2023)
: the former is a multimodal model trained with text and image inputs; the latter is trained on text, image, and audio inputs. The second pair is Llama-2-70B-chat
Touvron et al. (2023)
and Llama-2-70B-conversational. The former is trained with internet-scale text data. The latter is a fine-tuned version of the former, with a fine-tuning dataset that heavily features human-to-human conversation data. An analysis of a comparable model and dataset can be found in
Jawale et al. (2024)
. All of the models were set to have a temperature of
0
so that the abilities of the models could be tested with minimal random interference and maximum reproducibility.

For each model, we tried two distinct types of prompting in four different ways: (1) A
basic prompt
where the model is just asked to classify if a communication is either sarcastic, ironic, condescending, or deceptive depending on the dataset; and (2) A
speech/conversational prompt
(depending on the model pair) which tries to invoke the speech or conversational features of the model for better classification. Both prompts were tried in the zero-shot, few-shot, zero-shot chain-of-thought, and few-shot chain-of-thought configurations. For the exact wording of each prompt see the released code.

shows the average performance difference between prompting GPT-4o and GPT-4-Turbo with a generic sentiment analysis prompt versus a prompt meant to emphasize the model’s speech features. The results show that emphasizing the model’s speech features on average hurts the model’s accuracy, precision, and F1-score across all tasks. There are cases where recall is significantly improved by using a prompt that requires the model to think about how the text would sound. These performance differences are observed for both GPT-4o and GPT-4-Turbo, indicating that this difference may not come from the speech features of GPT-4o, but through textual features that are common to both models.

Thus these results are merely indicative of the hypothesis that multi-modal feature transfer is at work. Related Work

Speech and Language Models.
Beyond GPT-4o, there are a variety of recent multimodal models that combine speech and text processing. Some models focus on combining the two modalities using prefix-tuning, like Pengi
Deshmukh et al. (2023)
and Qwen-Audio
Chu et al. (2023)
. SpeechGPT discretizes speech into tokens and fine-tunes a text model to perform next-token prediction with this expanded vocabulary; the model is then instruction-tuned with text-speech pairs
Zhang et al. (2023a)
. LTU uses Whisper embeddings from across the network with Whisper outputs to train a frozen Llama model with LoRA-Adapters to add the speech modality
Gong et al. (2023)
. Salmonn uses a Q-former network with LORA to adapt an LLM to be able to accept audio input
Tang et al. (2024)
.

Sentiment Analysis.
There is a plethora of work on sentiment analysis and emotion recognition. Some of these efforts focus on detecting sentiment using unimodal data and unimodal models
Li et al. (2022)
;
Bulla et al. (2023)
;
Deng et al. (2023)
;
Yin et al. (2020)
;
Wang et al. (2023)
;
Zhang et al. (2023b)
. Other work uses purpose-built models to do multimodal emotion recognition such as speech emotion recognition
Makiuchi et al. (2021)
;
Siriwardhana et al. (2020)
;
Chu et al. (2022)
;
Pepino et al. (2021)
;
Dutta and Ganapathy (2023)
. Another set of work adds the vision modality to help with multimodal sentiment analysis
Wang et al. (2017)
;
Luo et al. (2021)
;
Han et al. (2021)
;
Mao et al. (2022)
. However, to our knowledge, there is no prior work that investigates how models that learned multimodal features in speech+text or conversation+text translate these features to an unimodal sentiment analysis task.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we begin the investigation of whether large multimodal models use multimodal features when processing data in a single modality. We focus on the problem of deceptive communication detection as humans use both speech and language signals even when presented with just the text modality.
We focus on models with two modality mixtures: speech+text, and text+human-to-human conversations. We find that with basic prompting speech+text models and human-to-human conversations trained models outperform their unimodal counterparts, indicating that multimodal feature use for unimodal inputs does occur in these models. Future work will explore trained approaches that explicitly take advantage of multimodal features to improve accuracy on unimodal tasks.

6
Limitations

This work has two key limitations. First, the speech+text model used is closed-source with no available architectural or dataset information. It is difficult to know if the performance differences that are reported stem from the addition of a modality, or from a difference in how the model was trained or the data used during training. The second limitation of this study is the datasets used. Some of the datasets, like the irony dataset, are quite small. Furthermore, though the datasets on Big-Bench are of higher quality, there are still instances (identified during manual data review) where the authors found disagreements with the ground truth labels provided by the dataset. This is because sentiment detection in text, unlike other tasks (e.g. QA), can be subjective, and therefore subject to reader discretion.

7
Ethics Statements

The work presents an analysis of multimodal feature transfer. We are not aware, however, of the full set of data that the models (GPT-4o, GPT-4-turbo, Llama-70b-chat) were trained on, and thus are not able to evaluate the potential biases in their data and thus the downstream models. Additionally, there is the potential for wrong identification of emotions and sentiments, leading to misclassification in downstream systems that rely on the sentiment analysis work presented here.
