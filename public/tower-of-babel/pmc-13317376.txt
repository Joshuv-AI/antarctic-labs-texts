Evaluation of large language models in cardiovascular surgery: a comparative study of board-level clinical question answering and generation
Authors: Mehmet Inanc Yesilkaya, Uzeyir Yilmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge language models (LLMs) are increasingly being explored in surgical training and clinical knowledge assessment. Although these models have demonstrated promising performance in standardized examinations, their performance in highly specialized fields such as cardiovascular surgery remains insufficiently investigated. This study aimed to evaluate the performance of current large language models in answering and generating board-level cardiovascular surgery questions reflecting guideline-based clinical reasoning.MethodsIn this cross-sectional evaluation study, three large language models (ChatGPT-5.1, Gemini 3, and DeepSeek v3.2) were evaluated in two stages. In the first stage, the models answered 150 multiple-choice questions developed and validated by five cardiovascular surgery specialists using a Delphi process, designed to reflect the content scope and difficulty level of the American Board of Thoracic Surgery certification examination. Accuracy rates and pairwise comparisons were analyzed using the McNemar test. In the second stage, model-generated questions were evaluated by expert cardiovascular surgeons in terms of medical accuracy, clinical relevance, exam-level appropriateness, error type, and difficulty level. Statistical analyses included Spearman correlation, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and chi-square analysis.ResultsThe models demonstrated comparable accuracy rates (ChatGPT 80.7%; Gemini 78.7%; DeepSeek 82.0%), with no statistically significant differences between them. Question difficulty level was not associated with model accuracy. Error distribution differed significantly between models (χ² = 8.1; p = 0.02), with Gemini demonstrating the highest rate of valid question generation and DeepSeek showing a higher rate of major errors. A significant positive correlation was observed between model- and expert-assigned difficulty levels.ConclusionsCurrent large language models demonstrate strong performance in board-level cardiovascular surgery knowledge assessment. However, the presence of major errors and variability in difficulty calibration, together with known limitations in clinical reasoning, indicate that these systems should be used cautiously as supportive tools in surgical training and knowledge assessment rather than as substitutes for clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04251-1.Keywords: Large language models, Artificial intelligence, Cardiovascular surgery, Surgical training, Clinical reasoning, Board examination

Background

Recent developments in artificial intelligence-based large language models have led to new applications in the field of medicine. These models have the capacity to understand human language, establish context, and generate content similar to that produced by humans [1]. They are systems trained using large information sources such as extensive internet-based text datasets, academic publications, and books [2]. Their increasing accessibility on a global scale has prompted research into their potential applications in areas such as clinical decision support processes, big data analysis, and drug development [3–5].

Furthermore, the use of large language models in medical education is also increasing. In this context, the models’ clinical question-solving performance [6] and their capacity for question generation and educational material preparation [7] have been evaluated in various studies. A study by Gilson et al. showed that ChatGPT could correctly answer more than 60% of the questions on the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) [8]. Similarly, in their study comparing the USMLE performance of GPT models, Brin et al. reported that the GPT-4 model achieved high accuracy rates in certain exam formats [9]. These findings indicate that large language models can demonstrate remarkable performance in standardized exams that assess general medical knowledge.

However, studies evaluating the performance of large language models in highly specialized surgical fields such as cardiovascular surgery are still limited. Furthermore, the majority of studies in the existing literature focus on question-answering performance, and the performance of generating exam-level questions in the field of cardiovascular surgery has not been sufficiently investigated. This situation points to a significant knowledge gap, particularly in terms of surgical education and exam preparation processes.

This study aims to evaluate the question-solving and question-generating performance of widely used AI-based large language models at a cardiovascular surgery board-level examination standard, with questions designed to reflect the content scope and difficulty level of the American Board of Thoracic Surgery (ABTS) certification examination, and to contribute to the existing knowledge gap in the literature.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a two-phase cross-sectional evaluation study to assess the performance of large language models in the context of a cardiovascular surgery board-level examination. Three current artificial intelligence models—ChatGPT-5.1, Gemini 3, and DeepSeek v3.2—were evaluated in terms of both question solving (Phase I) and new question generation (Phase II) tasks. ChatGPT-5.1 (OpenAI), Gemini 3 (Google), and DeepSeek v3.2 were accessed via their official web interfaces using default configurations available to users at the time of the study. No additional parameters (e.g., temperature adjustment, system prompts, or reasoning modes) were modified. All interactions were conducted in November 2025 to ensure temporal consistency.

No patient data or identifiable personal data were used in this study. Expert evaluations were conducted anonymously. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and did not require ethical committee approval.

Development of the question set and expert validation

For this study, 150 multiple-choice questions were developed by five cardiovascular surgery specialists, designed to reflect the content scope and difficulty level of the ABTS certification examination written component. The scope of the questions was defined as adult cardiac surgery, congenital cardiac surgery, and intensive care topics. The questions were evaluated by experts using the Delphi method [10] for content validity and accuracy. The expert panel consisted of experienced cardiovascular surgeons actively involved in clinical practice and surgical training. In this three-round process, the experts evaluated the questions in terms of accuracy, logical consistency, subject relevance, and difficulty level. Items with disagreements were revised, and consensus was reached for all questions. Thus, 150 high-quality multiple-choice questions were created, reflecting a cardiovascular surgery board-level examination standard. Answer options were distributed across positions (A–D) during question development to reduce positional bias; however, this was not formally controlled or statistically evaluated, and residual bias cannot be excluded.

All models were used with default settings, without fine-tuning. Responses were recorded without any processing.

Phase I: Question solving

In this phase, the question-solving performance of artificial intelligence models was evaluated. Each model answered 150 expert-approved questions. Each model was given the following standardized command at the beginning of the session:

I will ask you questions designed to reflect the content scope and difficulty level of a cardiovascular surgery board-level certification examination. Each question has four options, only one of which is correct. Please write your answer as a single letter (A, B, C, or D). Do not provide any additional explanation.

The prompting strategy was intentionally designed to simulate real-world examination conditions, where candidates are required to provide final answers without explicit reasoning. Therefore, advanced prompting techniques such as chain-of-thought or extended reasoning were not utilized.

The questions were presented to the models in groups of 10. Each question was presented in full text with four options labeled A–D. The model responses were recorded as generated. Responses were considered correct if they matched the expert-approved answers and incorrect if they did not match. The question response times were recorded with a stopwatch for each set of 10 questions. The total response time for 150 questions was calculated to determine the number of questions per minute (QPM) solved. Time measurement was based on the time elapsed until the model response appeared on the screen. To standardize the queries, they were performed at similar times for each model using the same network connection.

Phase II: Question generation

In this phase, the models’ ability to generate new board-style questions in the field of cardiovascular surgery was evaluated. Each model was given the following standardized command at the beginning of the session:

Generate 10 original multiple-choice questions designed to reflect the content scope and difficulty level of a cardiovascular surgery board-level certification examination. Each question should have four options and only one correct answer. Specify the correct answer without additional explanation. Also assign a difficulty level between 1 (easy) and 4 (very difficult) for each question. Ensure that the difficulty distribution of the questions is balanced.

To enable each model to generate 150 questions, this command was run 15 times consecutively. The generated questions were recorded as generated. A total of 450 questions obtained from the three models were submitted for evaluation by expert cardiovascular surgeons. Similarly, no additional contextual information or guidance was provided during question generation, allowing evaluation of the models’ baseline knowledge and generative capabilities.

Evaluation of AI-generated questions by experts

A total of 450 questions generated by the models during Phase II were submitted for evaluation by five cardiovascular surgery experts. The experts evaluated each question in terms of medical accuracy, suitability for the exam level, clinical applicability, and the accuracy of the answers provided and the assigned difficulty levels. The evaluations were performed using the Delphi consensus method. According to this method, in the first round, the experts independently reviewed all questions according to the specified criteria. As a result of the review, questions containing major and minor errors were identified. Incorrect answer keys or statements contrary to medical facts were considered major errors, while statement omissions or grammar errors that did not affect the answer were considered minor errors. In addition, experts assigned a difficulty level between 1 and 4 to each question.

Experts reconvened to discuss questions for which there was disagreement regarding the results obtained in the first round. The validity of the questions, their difficulty levels, and the accuracy of the identified major and minor errors were evaluated. In the third and final round, all questions were reviewed again and classified as majorly flawed, minorly flawed, or valid. This three-step method ensured that the experts reached consensus.

The criteria to be used to evaluate the performance of the models in the study were defined at the outset. In Phase I, the models’ question-solving ability was evaluated by the percentage of correct answers, and their speed was evaluated by the number of questions solved per minute. Additionally, correct response rates were calculated according to difficulty level. In Phase II, the questions generated by the models were reviewed by experts for accuracy, presence of major and minor errors, and appropriateness of difficulty levels. These criteria were used as the primary evaluation variables in the comparative statistical analysis between the three models.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Within Phase I (question-answering performance), the responses provided by each model were coded as correct (1) or incorrect (0). Since the same questions were answered by all models, question-based accuracy comparisons between model pairs were performed using the McNemar test. The relationship between question difficulty level (1–4) and accuracy was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation.

In Phase II (question generation), the relationship between the difficulty levels assigned by the models for the 150 questions generated by each model and the difficulty level assignments made by the experts was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation, and systematic differences between model and expert assignments were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The generated questions were classified as valid, minor error, or major error. The distributions of error types across models were compared using the Pearson chi-square test, and pairwise comparisons for valid question rates were performed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, where appropriate.

In terms of validity, no statistically significant differences were found in pairwise comparisons between models (all p > 0.05). The higher number of major errors observed in DeepSeek contributed to the overall chi-square significance.

A statistically significant positive correlation was found between the difficulty levels assigned by the models and those determined by the experts for all three models. ChatGPT showed a moderate correlation (Spearman’s ρ = 0.409, p < 0.001), DeepSeek showed a moderate-to-strong correlation (ρ = 0.487, p < 0.001), and Gemini showed the highest agreement with expert evaluations (ρ = 0.723, p < 0.001).

The paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test showed statistically significant differences between AI and expert difficulty scores for ChatGPT (Z = − 5.667; p < 0.001) and DeepSeek (Z = − 8.692; p < 0.001). No significant difference was detected for Gemini (Z = − 1.588; p = 0.112).

Discussion

This study evaluated the performance of three current large language models in answering questions at the level of a cardiovascular surgery board exam and generating new questions at the same level. The findings show that all three models achieved similar and high accuracy rates on expert-approved questions. It is noteworthy that the models’ accuracy was not significantly affected by question difficulty level and that they were able to generate a high proportion of valid questions. However, while their overall success rates were similar, some differences were observed between the models in terms of question difficulty calibration and major error rates. Although the ability of large language models to answer multiple-choice questions has been explored in prior studies, this component of our study serves as a useful benchmark to contextualize and compare question-generation performance.

The effects of large language models on medical education and exam performance have been evaluated in an increasing number of studies in recent years [11–13]. Our current findings are generally consistent with previous studies reporting that GPT-based models can achieve passing levels on standardized exams such as the USMLE [8, 11]. However, in highly specific fields such as cardiovascular surgery, studies examining both the question-solving and question-generation performance of large language models remain limited. In this regard, our study is thought to contribute to the existing knowledge gap. Unlike prior studies that primarily relied on established question banks such as SESATS [14], our study utilized a newly developed and expert-validated question set, allowing for greater control over content scope, difficulty calibration, and clinical relevance. The present study extends existing literature by integrating both question-answering and question-generation performance within a single comprehensive framework, providing a more comprehensive assessment of large language model capabilities in a specialty-specific context.

However, it cannot be said that the performance achieved directly reflects clinical reasoning ability. This is most likely because the models access information patterns in the training data rather than performing clinical reasoning [15]. This finding demonstrates that there is a significant difference between the capacity of artificial intelligence systems to generate correct answers and the processes involved in interpreting the clinical context and assuming responsibility for decisions. The clinical decision-making process is multidimensional and requires experience, contextual assessment, and clinical judgment. Therefore, large language models should be considered tools that support clinical decision-makers rather than independent decision-makers [16, 17]. In addition, recent studies have evaluated the performance of large language models in clinical reasoning and clinically oriented scenarios, highlighting both their potential and their current limitations [8, 15].

One of the important findings of our study is that there is no significant correlation between question difficulty level and model accuracy. While it is expected that success rates decrease as questions become more difficult in exams with human participants, no such relationship has been observed in artificial intelligence models. This finding can be interpreted as models using probabilistic information matching when responding to questions rather than performing human-like cognitive difficulty assessment [18].

One of the significant contributions of our study is that it evaluates not only question-solving performance but also question-generating performance, which is limited in the literature. The fact that all models can generate highly valid exam questions suggests that large language models can be used as supportive tools in preparing educational materials, developing question banks, and exam preparation processes [19]. The evaluation of question-generation performance represents a particularly relevant and practical aspect of this study, given its potential applications in medical education, such as developing question banks and training materials.

In addition to overall validity, an analysis of the distribution of generated questions revealed a consistent pattern across models, with a predominant focus on adult cardiac surgery topics and relatively limited representation of congenital cardiac surgery domains. This imbalance may reflect underlying biases in training data or differences in the representation of subspecialty knowledge within large language models. From an educational perspective, this finding highlights the importance of expert oversight when utilizing AI-generated content, particularly to ensure balanced coverage across all relevant domains of cardiovascular surgery.

Furthermore, rather than merely demonstrating the ability of models to generate questions, this study provides a structured evaluation of the quality, clinical validity, and error patterns of generated content, offering a more systematic assessment compared to prior descriptive reports.

However, the higher error rates in some models indicate that content generated by artificial intelligence should not be used directly and must undergo expert review [20].

Our study found that some large language models produced results closer to experts’ difficulty assessments than other models. This suggests that the models can reflect the exam structure and question style to a certain extent and may have a limited capacity for adaptation. However, the statistically significant differences between AI and expert assessments indicate that current models still have limitations in consistently determining question difficulty levels [21].

From an educational perspective, the ability of large language models to generate responses at high speed and achieve acceptable accuracy rates suggests that they could be used as auxiliary tools in exam preparation, theoretical knowledge review, and learning processes [19]. However, it should be noted that uncontrolled use may lead to incorrect results due to the potential to occasionally produce major errors and the inability to always accurately reflect the clinical context [22]. Although the question set was designed to reflect board-level difficulty, the inclusion of more complex, multi-step clinical reasoning scenarios may further enhance the ability to distinguish performance differences between models.

The main strengths of this study are that the question set was prepared by cardiovascular surgery specialists and validated using the Delphi method, that question-solving and question-generating performances were evaluated together, and that a multi-model comparison was performed.

This study provides a structured, expert-validated, and specialty-specific evaluation of large language model performance, offering clinically meaningful insights for cardiovascular surgery education.

Our study has some limitations. First, the study was based on a single board exam format, and the analysis was performed on a question set consisting of a limited number of questions. Therefore, it is not possible to draw a definitive conclusion that similar results will be obtained in different exam structures. In addition, the fact that the evaluations were made based on exam questions may not fully reflect the complex nature of real clinical decision-making processes. Furthermore, since performance differences may arise with updates to artificial intelligence models over time, the findings represent a specific time period.

Although all questions were newly developed and validated by experts, it cannot be completely excluded that large language models may have been exposed to similar concepts or question styles during training on large-scale datasets, representing an inherent limitation of AI-based evaluation studies. The question set also did not include image-based questions, which are commonly used in board examinations and may limit the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, while the questions included clinically oriented scenarios requiring applied knowledge and decision-making, the study did not incorporate sequential or case-based linkage questions involving multi-step clinical reasoning, which may better reflect real-world decision-making processes.

Beyond these limitations, future studies may incorporate blinded evaluations in which experts attempt to distinguish between human- and AI-generated questions, providing further insight into the realism and educational applicability of AI-generated content.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that current large language models can achieve consistently high accuracy in both answering and generating questions at the level of the cardiovascular surgery board examination. These findings suggest that large language models can perform at an acceptable level not only in general medical knowledge but also in highly specialized surgical domains. However, given limitations such as the presence of major errors, variability in difficulty calibration, and the inability to fully capture clinical reasoning processes, these systems should not be considered independent decision-makers in clinical practice.

Current evidence indicates that the most appropriate application of large language models is as supportive tools in medical education. When used under expert supervision, they may contribute meaningfully to board examination preparation, theoretical knowledge review, and the development of educational materials. Future studies incorporating larger datasets, diverse specialties, and real-world clinical scenarios will be essential to further define the role of large language models in cardiovascular surgery education and their potential use in decision-support contexts.
