Systemic analysis of rural teacher attrition in china: a hybrid DEMATEL-ISM approach to multidimensional drivers and policy implications
Authors: Yan Ma, LongShiyu Zhou, Juhua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRural teacher attrition in China presents a complex systemic challenge impacting educational equity and rural revitalization, necessitating multidimensional intervention strategies. This study employs an integrated DEMATEL-ISM methodology to model hierarchical interactions among 27 attrition determinants across individual, organizational, and societal dimensions. Key results indicate: (1) School size (Di = 6.303), geographical location (Di = 6.001), and compensation (Di = 5.918) dominate as causal factors, while professional identity (Ci = 6.401) and belongingness (Ci = 6.397) exhibit highest susceptibility; (2) A three-tiered structure emerges—surface-level urban-oriented preferences directly trigger attrition, intermediary factors (e.g., job burnout, resource disparities) mediate effects, and root-level institutional deficiencies (teaching environments/administrative systems) drive systemic causality through psychological and spatial pathways. We suggest that sustainable retention likely requires tripartite policy integration: optimizing material support systems, reconstructing professional identity through cultural-ecological rehabilitation, and implementing stratified interventions targeting institutional, psychological, and behavioral dimensions to disrupt attrition cascades.Keywords: Rural teachers, Attrition factors, DEMATEL-ISM, Rural educationSubject terms: Development studies, Geography, Geography, Psychology, Psychology, Sociology

Introduction

Rural teachers serve as a cornerstone of China’s rural revitalization strategy. The development of this workforce directly impacts the quality of rural education and the sustainable development of rural areas. In 2018, the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China and the State Council issued the Opinions on Comprehensively Deepening the Reform of the Teacher Workforce in the New Era, establishing a framework for enhancing rural teachers’ capacity. In 2020, the Ministry of Education and five other departments further emphasized advancing high-efficiency reforms and high-quality development of rural teachers in the Opinions on Strengthening the Development of the Teacher Workforce in the New Era. Nevertheless, constrained by the urban-rural dual structure and imbalanced allocation of educational resources, China’s rural teaching force has long grappled with the exodus of educators—particularly high-performing ones—a phenomenon termed “rural teachers leave, and excellent rural teachers leave even more”1. A survey of 39,470 rural teachers in Province H revealed an annual outflow of approximately 14,000 educators2. This sustained brain drain not only widens the urban-rural education quality gap but also traps rural schools in an ecological dilemma: teacher shortages -- declining enrollment -- deteriorating educational quality. Addressing rural teacher retention is thus imperative for narrowing urban-rural disparities and ensuring human capital sustainability in rural revitalization.

Many scholars have explored the causes of rural teacher attrition. For example, based on the Survival-Relationship-Growth (ERG) theory, Zhu Yanfei3 found that local rural teachers chose to leave their hometowns in pursuit of better wages and professional development. Other scholars have investigated this phenomenon from a cultural-environmental perspective, identifying a shortage of spiritual/cultural resources, a lack of opportunities for personal value realization, and low occupational identity as key drivers of attrition4. However, research has paid limited attention to the causal pathways and interactive mechanisms among these factors, neglecting the interdependencies between multidimensional elements such as livelihood security, career development, and social support. To address this gap, this study innovatively applies the DEMATEL-ISM method to quantify the importance of influencing factors, construct a hierarchical model, and analyze their structural relationships and interaction pathways. This approach aims to provide theoretical foundations and policy guidance for enhancing rural teacher retention.

Literature review

Factors contributing to the attrition of rural teachers

Individual factors in rural teacher attrition

Research has found that individual factors affecting the attrition of rural teachers include both objective factors such as age, gender, and family circumstances, as well as subjective factors such as emotions and value judgments. The specific analysis is as follows:

From an identity perspective, Miao Xuejie5 found that family dynamics significantly influence rural teachers’ relocation decisions within close-knit relational contexts. Parental affirmation of teaching’s intrinsic value supports young professionals’ career choices and provides positive reinforcement. Conversely, when family needs conflict with rural teaching demands, these relationships exert varying pressures on career transitions. A survey of rural teachers in Guangdong’s impoverished areas revealed significant dis-parities in retention willingness based on experience years, professional titles, and employment status. Specifically, on-payroll teachers with higher titles, longer tenure, and lower educational attainment showed stronger retention commitment6. Rural teachers’ core mission—cultivating virtue and holistic student development—often clashes with evaluation systems prioritizing “test scores” and “admission rates.” This instrumental treatment of teachers as academic-output tools fuels occupational burnout, diminishes professional fulfillment, and traps them in mobility cycles7,8. Applying Lewin’s field theory, Yang Jin9 identified how stereotypes of rural “backwardness” and unique school challenges erode professional identity, sustaining low morale and reinforcing attrition motives10. Supporting this, a Jilin Province survey of 452 teachers confirmed that workplace belonging strongly correlates with retention. “Matching” denotes the alignment between teachers’ (abilities, educational philosophy, values, communication style) and organizational requirements/culture. The study indicates that higher matching levels increase organizational comfort and reduce resignation likelihood11.

Organizational factors in rural teacher attrition

Research on school organizational factors influencing rural teacher attrition indicates that administrative practices, student demographics, school climate, teaching conditions, and compensation all impact turnover. Crucially, salaries and benefits remain the most significant factor driving attrition; improvements in compensation effectively enhance retention12,13. Zou Wei14 identified through extensive literature analysis that attrition willingness varies by school size, descending sequentially from large to small to medium-sized institutions. Work environment is another critical determinant. Rural schools typically face challenges including outdated teaching facilities, substandard living conditions, and transportation barriers. Obsolete equipment under-mines teaching efficacy and fosters negative work attitudes15,16. Geographic isolation further compounds attrition17: a survey of 650 rural teachers revealed 41.2% cited transportation inconvenience, while 40.1% attributed potential departure to remote location18. Student enrollment patterns also contribute to attrition. Sparsely populated mountainous areas often have under-enrolled schools with significant academic ability disparities, impeding teachers’ sense of accomplishment19,20. Teacher value derives not only from educational fulfillment but also career advancement prospects. Rural teachers face dis-proportionate barriers in obtaining professional titles due to lower evaluation quotas than urban schools, creating promotion gaps. This indirectly fuels attrition through tenure in-security and stalled career progression21. Through in-depth interviews with rural primary teachers, Tao Xia22 found that resignations result from combined effects of Herzberg’s dual-factor theory (hygiene-motivation). Incomplete management systems, inadequate compensation, and limited promotion opportunities drive severe attrition23. Rural teachers must navigate multiple relational dimensions—teacher-student, colleague, parent-school, and leadership dynamics—with satisfaction in these relationships directly influencing attrition intention13. Practical constraints further compel rural teachers to handle non-instructional tasks alongside teaching duties, creating excessive workloads. Research confirms that attrition intention rises proportionally with workload increases24. Organizational climate also critically impacts turnover: Zhu Xiuhong25 demonstrated through per-son-environment fit theory that positive school atmospheres significantly reduce young teachers’ mobility intentions .

Environmental factors in rural teacher attrition

Beyond individual and school organizational characteristics, social environmental factors also drive rural teacher attrition. Existing research identifies work environment, social influences, professional prestige, and social status as primary determinants of attrition intention. Of these, social status proves pivotal26: it significantly enhances young rural teachers’ retention intentions, whereas work pressure significantly reduces them27. Studies indicate that many equate rural teaching with professional inadequacy. Such societal biases exert substantial pressure on young teachers, as evaluations of teacher competence frequently correlate with workplace location—undermining commitment to rural education28. Moreover, the broader societal shift toward urbanization critically influences attrition. Recent research reveals increasingly market-driven motives behind teacher mobility, with flows demonstrating pronounced “urbanization” and “profit-seeking” tendencies. Collectively, this forms an upward mobility pattern driven by rural disengagement29,30. Research indicates that regional economic conditions significantly influence teacher attrition. Disparities in economic development create corresponding variations in teachers’ social status and working conditions. When comparing their socioeconomic standing with other professions, teachers avoid regions where their status is relatively higher, thereby accelerating attrition31. As socialized individuals, rural teachers seek self-actualization alongside improved living standards and economic benefits. In practice, however, limited developmental and educational resources in rural areas widen the urban-rural income gap, motivating rural teachers to pursue urban relocation for better resources32. The rural environment constitutes the foundational ecosystem for teacher retention, determining their sustainable development capacity33. Although rural infrastructure has improved, systemic developmental delays persist. Deficiencies in housing, transportation, healthcare, and cultural/recreational facilities remain primary drivers of attrition20.

Theoretical foundations of rural teacher attrition

Push-pull theory

Push-Pull Theory is a classic migration framework originating from demography and economic geography, designed to explain spatial population mobility. Its core premise is that migration decisions result from the combined effects of “push factors” in the place of origin and “pull factors” at the destination34. Push factors refer to negative conditions in the origin that compel people to leave. These typically include unsatisfactory living and development conditions such as poverty, unemployment, resource scarcity, environmental degradation, and lack of advancement opportunities. Pull factors denote positive attributes in the destination that attract people, often opportunities and conditions that improve living standards—such as income, family reunification, and better employment prospects. In the context of this study, “push factors” primarily refer to negative elements within the rural work and living environment, specifically including: harsh teaching conditions, remote geographical locations, and limited opportunities for advancement. Conversely, “pull factors” denote the more attractive, superior conditions found in urban areas, most directly manifested in teachers’ preferences for urban migration. Therefore, the exodus of rural teachers can be viewed as a result of the combined influence of rural “push factors” and urban “pull factors.”

Resource conservation theory

Resource conservation theory is a social psychological theory proposed by Hobfoll to explain individuals’ psychological and behavioral responses when facing stress and environmental demands. This theory posits that individuals possess a fundamental motivation to acquire, retain, and protect resources they value35. These resources encompass material resources (e.g., compensation), conditional resources (e.g., job security), personal attribute resources (e.g., self-efficacy, occupational identity), and energy resources (e.g., time, energy). The core argument of this theory is that when individuals perceive a persistent threat of losing key resources and find their efforts fail to replenish these resources effectively, they experience significant psychological stress and adopt defensive behaviors to prevent further resource depletion36. The work environment of rural teachers inherently constitutes a setting prone to resource depletion. On one hand, heavy workloads and challenging commutes continuously deplete teachers’ energy reserves. On the other, obstructed career advancement pathways undermine their sense of professional accomplishment. Over time, this erodes the most fundamental psychological resources of rural teachers, such as professional identity and occupational belonging. When these psychological resources are exhausted and compensatory resources (e.g., salary increases, organizational support) remain chronically absent, resignation becomes a protective strategy for teachers to safeguard their mental health and sense of self-worth.

Materials and methods

Identification of attrition factors

Rural teacher attrition exhibits multidimensional complexity, with causes rooted in the interplay of individual characteristics, organizational environments, and social structures. To systematically construct an attrition factor framework, this study employed the Delphi method following literature analysis. Ten former rural teachers with firsthand experience were surveyed to identify influencing factors. The Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W = 0.511, *p* < 0.001) confirmed strong expert consensus. Ultimately, a three-dimensional framework was established: (1) Individual Dimension (8 factors): occupational traits, family relationships, job burnout, teaching motivation, professional identity, occupational belonging, educational philosophy, self-efficacy; (2) Organizational Dimension (13 factors): compensation, school size, teaching environment, geographic lo-cation, student enrollment, promotion systems, management policies, evaluation mechanisms, professional development, leadership style, interpersonal dynamics, workload, organizational climate; (3) Social Dimension (6 factors): social status, occupational stigma, urban migration preference, regional economic level, resource disparities, living conditions. see Table 1 for details.

Research process

DEMATEL analysis phase

The DEMATEL (Decision-Making Trial and Evaluation Laboratory) method is a systems analysis tool that utilizes expert knowledge to analyze interdependent factors in complex systems. Its core involves constructing and analyzing a direct influence matrix to identify causal relationships and interaction intensities among system elements37. The DEMATEL procedure comprises four steps:

Step 1

Experts conduct pairwise comparisons of factors to determine mutual influence levels, yielding the direct influence matrix B.

Step 2

Normalize matrix B to obtain the normalized influence matrix R.

Step 3

Compute the comprehensive influence matrix T.

where I denotes the identity matrix.

Step 4: Calculate for each factor: Influence degree (Di), Affected degree (Ci), Centrality degree (Mi), Causality degree (Ri).

ISM analysis phase

ISM (Interpretive Structural Modeling) is a systematic method for analyzing complex socio-technical systems. It enables decision-makers to understand system structures by mapping elements and their interrelationships graphically38. This study applies ISM to hierarchically classify factors influencing rural teacher attrition through the following steps:

Step 1

Introduce threshold λ to convert the comprehensive influence matrix T into an adjacency matrix A. Matrix A is added to identity matrix I, followed by iterative Boolean multiplication until matrix stabilization, yielding the reachability matrix F.

Step 2

Determine the reachable set R and the preceding set Q.

The reachable set R refers to the set of elements that can influence other elements, i.e., the set where the numbers are 1 when arranged by row; the preceding set Q refers to the set of elements that influence the current element, i.e., the set where the numbers are 1 when arranged by column.

Step 3

Perform hierarchical extraction where S = R ∩ Q.

Step 4

Construct a recursive hierarchical model.

Data collection

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol (involving anonymous expert scoring and collecting no private data) was reviewed by the Academic Committee of the College of Computer and Information Science at Chongqing Normal University and was granted an ethical exemption because it posed no more than minimal risk to participants. All participating experts provided their consent after being informed of the nature of the research. This exemption has been officially documented and filed with the University’s Office of Scientific Research.

Regarding the DEMATEL expert panel size, prior established methodologies (e.g., Wang Linxiu39 demonstrate that 5–7 experts are sufficient to yield valid results. Accordingly, five specialists in rural education research were invited based on the following criteria: (1) holding the title of associate professor or higher (or equivalent academic honors) within prefecture-level cities or higher administrative regions; (2) having at least 10 years of experience in rural education research or practice; and (3) having previously served as a principal investigator on relevant research projects. Utilizing their expertise, these experts scored paired factors on a 5-point scale (0–4) to assess mutual influence intensity, with the scale comprising the linguistic variables: no influence (0 points), very weak influence (1 point), weak influence (2 points), strong influence (3 points), and very strong influence (4 points).

Hierarchical structure via ISM

Based on DEMATEL, the study adopted the principle of causal significance screening to eliminate passive factors with low centrality and negative causality in the third quad-rant of Fig. 2, i.e., secondary factors that have a weak impact on the system or are primarily controlled by other variables, and retained core variables with significant direct effects or strong causal driving forces. As a result, a total of 18 factors were screened and the comprehensive influence matrix T was reconstructed. According to formulas (8) and (9), a threshold λ is introduced into the comprehensive influence matrix T to convert it into an adjacency matrix A. Generally speaking, selecting λ based solely on expert experience lacks objectivity. This study employs the sum of the mean and standard deviation from the statistical distribution as the λ value, a method that effectively reduces subjective influence40. The mean α of all influence factors in the comprehensive influence matrix T is calculated to be 0.21, the standard deviation β is 0.02, and the threshold λ is 0.23. Through a series of matrix operations, the reachability matrix F was obtained, fol-lowed by the reachability set R, antecedent set Q, and common set S. Hierarchical division was performed according to formula (10), and the multi-level hierarchical structure model diagram of the factors influencing the attrition of the rural teacher workforce was finally plotted (see Fig. 3).

Multi-level hierarchical structural model of rural teacher attrition determinants.

In the hierarchical relationship model diagram of factors influencing rural teacher attrition, solid arrows represent the influence pathways and interaction mechanisms between factors, while dashed lines indicate different hierarchical levels, thereby clarifying the relationships among factors within the system of factors influencing rural teacher attrition. Overall, the hierarchical relationship model of factors influencing rural teacher attrition is divided into four layers. Among these, our model identifies occupational traits of rural teachers, alongside school size, teaching environment, geographical location, management policies, and evaluation mechanisms, as the most fundamental causes. Our model positions society’s urban migration preference as the most direct factor influencing rural teacher attrition. Factors that indirectly influence the attrition of the rural teacher workforce include individual teachers’ educational philosophies, job burnout, motivations for teaching, professional identity, and occupational belonging; organizational-level factors such as promotion systems, compensation, and workloads; and societal-level factors such as resource disparities and living conditions. By analyzing the relationships and interactions among these factors, this study aims to clarify the underlying mechanisms driving rural teacher attrition and identify pathways and strategies to incentivize rural teachers to remain in their communities.

Discussion

DEMATEL-based analysis

Analysis of influence and affected degrees

Influence (Di) quantifies the comprehensive influence of a particular factor on other factors. The higher the Di value, the stronger the role of that factor as a causal variable within the system, making it a key trigger or driver of rural teacher attrition. The analysis results show that school size (F10, Di = 6.303) has the highest influence among all factors, highlighting its foundational role in rural teacher retention decisions. The size of a school directly relates to core issues such as resource allocation, workload, and development opportunities, profoundly influencing teachers’ overall work experience and career prospects. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 1, the top ten factors in terms of influence are all concentrated in the teacher individual dimension (e.g., F6 occupation belonging, F3 job burnout, F8 self-efficacy, F1 occupation traits, F7 educational philosophy) and the school organizational dimension (e.g., F10 school size, F12 geographical location, F9 compensation, F14 pro-motion systems, F11 teaching environment). This distribution pattern clearly indicates that the tendency of rural teachers to leave is primarily shaped by two core forces: first, teachers’ individual subjective perceptions, psychological states, and professional values; second, the objective environment, resource conditions, and management systems of the schools where they work. The school organizational dimension appears to constitute a significant external push for rural teacher attrition, while the teacher individual dimension strongly influences the internal resilience and response mechanisms.

The degree of affected (Ci) measures the overall extent to which a particular factor is affected by other factors in the system. The higher the Ci value, the stronger the sensitivity of that factor as a result variable, making it more likely to become the “focus” of dynamic changes in the system or the ultimate manifestation of loss risk. Factors with higher in-fluence include: professional identity (F5, Ci = 6.401), occupation belonging (F6, Ci = 6.397), self-efficacy (F8, Ci = 6.331), job burnout (F3, Ci = 6.281), teaching motivation (F4, Ci = 5.994), workload (F20, Ci = 5.919), urban migration preference (F24, Ci = 5.794), compensation (F9, Ci = 5.692), promotion systems (F14, Ci = 5.569), and resource disparities (F26, Ci = 5.377). It is worth noting that professional identity (F5), occupation belonging (F6), self-efficacy (F8), and job burnout (F3) all belong to the dimension of individual psychological capital among teachers. The high influence of these factors strongly suggests that the attrition process of rural teachers exhibits significant “psychological exhaustion” characteristics. These factors do not exist in isolation but are highly susceptible to multiple pressures and erosion from other aspects of the individual (such as motivation and ideology) and the school organizational environment (such as workload, resource gaps, promotion difficulties, and “urbanization” caused by geographical location). This finding aligns with the Resource Conservation Theory36. This theory posits that when individuals perceive a sustained depletion of their key resources (such as a sense of identity, belonging, and efficacy within psychological capital), coupled with a lack of effective mechanisms for replenishing or compensating for these losses (such as improvements in salary and benefits, enhanced promotion opportunities, reduced workloads, or strengthened resource support), individuals ultimately tend to terminate further resource depletion by resigning from their positions. accumulated burnout), and lacks effective mechanisms for resource replenishment or compensation (such as improved salary and benefits, enhanced promotion opportunities, reduced workloads, or strengthened resource support), individuals will ultimately tend to resign to halt further resource loss.

Analysis of centrality and causality degrees

Centrality (Mi = Di + Ci) measures the overall importance of a factor within the causal network. A high centrality value indicates that the factor is a core hub in the system’s dynamic evolution, and changes in its state can have widespread and significant impacts on the phenomenon of rural teacher attrition. The analysis shows that the top ten key hub factors in terms of centrality are, in order: occupation belonging (F6, Mi = 12.182), job burnout (F3, Mi = 12.005), self-efficacy (F8, Mi = 11.988), professional identity (F5, Mi = 11.859), compensation (F9, Mi = 11.610), teaching motivation (F4, Mi = 11.320), promotion systems (F14, Mi = 11.266), workload (F20, Mi = 11.033), school size (F10, Mi = 11.015), and urban migration preference (F24, Mi = 10.986). This result further confirms and reinforces the findings of the previous influence/affectedness analysis: individual psychological capital (F6 occupational belonging, F3 job burnout, F8 self-efficacy, F5 professional identity) and core economic and career development factors (F9 compensation, F14 promotion systems) of rural teachers play an extremely critical role in the issue of attrition. Not only do these factors have significant influence (high Di) or are highly susceptible to influence (high Ci), but more importantly, their comprehensive hub status (high Mi) means they serve as the “focal points” and “amplifiers” within the entire network of attrition-related factors. Changes involving these factors are likely to have widespread ripple effects throughout the entire system.

The causality index (Ri = Di - Ci) is used to clarify the causal attributes of factors and distinguish between causal factors and result factors in the system. Causal factors (Ri > 0) play the role of “driving forces” in the system. The larger the positive value of Ri, the stronger the active driving force of the factor on the system, making it an ideal intervention point. This study identified 14 causal factors. Among them, geographical location (F12, Ri = 1.667), school size (F10, Ri = 1.591), evaluation mechanisms (F16, Ri = 0.603), and management policies (F15, Ri = 0.569) are the most influential driving factors. Notably, all these key driving factors belong to the school organizational dimension. This indicates that the objective environment and institutional arrangements at the school level (such as resource accessibility and living convenience determined by the school’s geographical location, re-source allocation and work atmosphere influenced by school size, fairness and incentivization of evaluation methods, and the scientific and humanistic nature of management systems) are the underlying causes triggering or inhibiting the dynamic chain of rural teacher attrition. Optimizing these factors can more effectively intervene in the attrition process from its source. Result factors (Ri < 0) typically play the role of system “responders.” The larger the absolute value of the negative Ri, the more sensitive the factor is to external changes (especially the influence of causal factors). This study identified 13 out-come factors. Among them, professional identity (F5, Ri = -0.942), workload (F20, Ri = -0.806), self-efficacy (F8, Ri = -0.675), and teaching motivation (F4, Ri = -0.669) are the most sensitive key outcome factors. Combined with their high centrality (Mi) and high influence (Ci) characteristics, these factors (especially psychological capital elements such as F5 professional identity, F8 self-efficacy, and F4 motivation to teach) clearly outline the path of resource depletion for rural teachers under adverse environmental pressures: Adverse factors at the school organizational level (such as remote geographical location, resource scarcity, unreasonable evaluation or management) first exacerbate practical pressures such as F20 workloads, which in turn continuously deplete teachers’ core psychological resources such as F5 professional identity, F8 self-efficacy, and F4 teaching motivation (high Ci, Ri < 0). This finding further validates the resource conservation theory, which posits that the sustained depletion of key psychological resources without compensation (e.g., through improved salaries, promotions, or management systems) is the direct psychological driver of resignation behavior.

ISM-based analysis

Root-level foundational factors

The fundamental factors are located at the L1 layer of the hierarchical model, acting directly or indirectly through multiple pathways on intermediate factors and ultimately influencing the surface factors leading to the attrition of rural teacher teams. Fundamental factors are the key determinants of rural teacher attrition, primarily composed of individual teachers’ professional characteristics and factors under school organizational indicators. Issues such as limited school size, outdated teaching environments, geographically marginalized locations, rigid management policies, and single-dimensional evaluation methods fundamentally stem from structural contradictions caused by imbalanced distribution of educational resources and outdated management models. Limited school size restricts teachers’ professional development opportunities, thereby affecting rural teacher staffing and title evaluations. Combined with spatial isolation resulting from geographical disadvantages, these factors exacerbate the phenomenon of rural teacher attrition.

Intermediary indirect factors

Intermediate indirect factors (L2–L3) play a pivotal role in the factors influencing the attrition of rural teachers. These factors are closely linked to direct factors, driving changes in direct factors while also being influenced by fundamental factors. They constitute an important link in the attrition of rural teachers, with diverse constituent elements spanning three levels: individual teachers, school organizations, and society. Under the pressure transmitted from fundamental factors, the interaction between individual psycho-logical mechanisms and organizational support systems forms the intermediate hub of rural teacher attrition. Rural teachers face the cumulative burden of excessive teaching and non-teaching tasks, leading to a vicious cycle of emotional exhaustion and occupational burnout. Discrepancies in educational philosophy further weaken professional identity and sense of belonging. The absence of organizational support systems, such as blocked promotion pathways, urban-rural wage disparities, and relatively backward re-source and living conditions, leads to low self-efficacy among rural teachers, thereby ex-acerbating teacher attrition.

Surface-level direct factors

The direct factor (L4 level) is the “urban migration preference” under social indicators, which is the direct factor influencing the attrition of rural teachers. However, this factor does not independently influence the attrition of rural teachers but rather exerts its influence in conjunction with indirect and fundamental factors. School size constraints and geographical marginalization leading to spatial isolation result in occupational burnout, low occupational identity, and a sense of belonging among rural teachers. As professional burnout and identity crises continue to ferment at the individual psychological level, the absence of organizational support (such as imbalanced salaries and promotions) and disparities in social resources jointly exacerbate teachers’ feelings of resource disparity, leading to survival anxiety and amplifying their perception of the resource gap between urban and rural areas. This aligns with push-pull theory. Specifically, root causes and indirect factors at Levels L1 to L3 collectively form a powerful “push” system at the rural end: harsh teaching environments, rigid management systems, depleted psychological capital, imbalanced compensation, and scarce development resources continuously push teachers toward resignation. Simultaneously, China’s rapid urbanization has shaped a potent “pull” field within societal perceptions and individual aspirations—a yearning for superior urban educational resources, career prospects, quality of life, and cultural atmosphere. This manifests directly as “urban migration preference” (F24). Under this dual pressure, rural teachers ultimately choose to resign and flood into cities.

Reflections from an international comparative perspective

The multi-level model of rural teacher attrition constructed in this study, while rooted in China’s unique urban-rural dual structure and sociocultural context, reveals core mechanisms that resonate profoundly with global research findings on teacher attrition. For instance, studies on teacher shortages in the United States indicate that high attrition rates—rather than insufficient training—are the primary cause of staffing pressures41. Underlying factors include low compensation, challenging working conditions, inadequate teaching preparation, and insufficient administrative support from schools. Similarly, European Commission research emphasizes that salary increases, improved working conditions, career development, and professional support can effectively enhance the attractiveness of the teaching profession42. However, an international comparative perspective also highlights the distinctiveness of the Chinese context. First, “school size” and “geographic location” emerge as root-cause drivers in this study, reflecting China’s vast territory and macroeconomic backdrop of uneven regional development. Second, during China’s rapid urbanization phase, the “preference for urban migration” functions as a potent socio-psychological pull, potentially far stronger than in developed nations that have entered stable urbanization stages. Finally, China’s unique staffing quota and professional title evaluation systems create distinctive upward pull forces at the institutional level, incentivizing rural teachers to migrate to cities to achieve institutional identity transformation.

Research implications

The issue of rural teacher attrition is essentially the result of multiple institutional deficiencies and cultural imbalances. This study systematically analyzes and reveals the three-dimensional mechanisms that influence rural teacher retention, providing new in-sights into solving this problem. Based on the findings, achieving stable development of the rural teacher workforce requires the construction of multidimensional, systematic solutions.

Improve the staffing and compensation system: strengthen the material foundation

At the institutional guarantee level, it is currently urgent to break through structural bottlenecks such as staffing, professional titles, and salaries. Research data shows that school size (Di = 6.303) and compensation (Di = 5.918) are key factors affecting teachers’ retention. This requires the establishment of a more flexible staffing management system, the implementation of a provincial-level coordinated “county-managed, school-hired mechanism”, and a focus on resource allocation towards remote areas. At the same time, the reform of professional title evaluation should be deepened, with a dedicated promotion channel set up for rural teachers, with years of service as a hard indicator, and the implementation of a “targeted evaluation and targeted use” policy. In terms of treatment guarantee, a compound salary system including basic salary, living subsidies, and performance rewards should be established, with subsidies for teachers in remote areas in-creased, and supporting benefits such as turnover housing and medical green channels improved, to fundamentally solve teachers’ worries.

Rebuilding professional identity: cultivating endogenous motivation

The cultivation of professional identity is the endogenous driving force for stabilizing the teaching staff. The research found that professional identity (Ci = 6.401) and professional belonging (Ci = 6.397) are the core factors in resisting the risk of attrition. This requires us to redefine the role positioning of rural teachers. We should not only strengthen their identity as education professionals, but also enhance their professional sense of achievement through establishing learning communities for urban and rural teachers and developing characteristic local courses. It is also necessary to expand its social function as a cultural inheritor, incorporate teachers into the rural governance system, and endow “new local sages” with social expectations43. The practice of the “Wheat Field Watch Plan” project in Jiaxing City, Zhejiang Province, shows that through media publicity of typical deeds and professional training, teachers’ professional identity and pride can be effectively enhanced.

Reconstructing the cultural ecosystem: breaking the urban-rural dual dilemma

Reconstructing the educational and cultural ecosystem in rural areas is the fundamental solution to breaking the tendency of “flowing to cities”. Research shows that under the influence of the urban-rural dual structure, geographical location (Di = 6.001) has be-come an important obstacle restricting teachers’ retention. This requires us to take cultural empowerment as the core, build a distinctive educational ecosystem, promote the construction of “one school, one brand”, and deeply integrate local elements such as farming culture and traditional handicrafts into the curriculum system. At the same time, it is necessary to innovate the “school-community” linkage mechanism. By organizing activities such as local study Tours and cultural festivals, the bond between teachers and the community can be enhanced. The Lingjia Town Central Primary School in Zhong District, Neijiang City, Sichuan Province, has helped rural teachers deeply integrate into rural life through three major sections: “Entering the Countryside, Listening to the Local Accent, and Immersing in the Local Charm”. As a result, the school’s teacher turnover rate has dropped from 18.04% in 2013 to 2.85% in 2024, and the familiarity of new teachers with local culture has risen from 21.15% to 63.14%.

Implementing tiered interventions: Building systemic solutions

Based on the systematic characteristics of the influencing factors, it is recommended to adopt a hierarchical intervention strategy. For deep-seated institutional obstacles, the focus should be on improving the management of staffing and the guarantee of benefits. For the problem of job burnout among middle-level managers, it is necessary to alleviate it through reducing burdens and increasing efficiency as well as psychological counseling. At the surface selection level, it is necessary to establish an honor and recognition system as well as localized development channels. This approach of implementing policies at different levels not only captures the essence of the problem but also reflects the targeted nature of the countermeasures, providing a scientific basis for formulating differentiated retention policies. It is worth noting that these measures need to be advanced in a coordinated manner and form a synergy. Any unilateral improvement is unlikely to achieve lasting results. The establishment of a three-in-one support system of institutional guarantee, identity recognition and cultural ecology is posited as a crucial strategy for achieving the long-term stable development of the rural teacher team.

Research limitations

This study utilized the DEMATEL-ISM method to analyze the influencing factors of rural teacher attrition and constructed a hierarchical structure model of the factors of rural teacher attrition, providing a systematic theoretical framework and policy implications for understanding and intervening in this issue. However, this study still has the following limitations. Firstly, at the methodological level, this study is based on the DEMATEL expert scoring method. Although it ensures the professionalism and representativeness of the expert group members and passed the consistency test (Kendall W = 0.511, p < 0.001), this method is difficult to completely avoid the bias caused by subjective judgment. Additionally, while the number of experts involved in scoring (5) meets basic methodological requirements, the sample size remains relatively limited. Although our expert panel possesses extensive knowledge of China’s rural education context, forming a larger and more diverse group—such as one encompassing experts from different geographic backgrounds and with direct experience across various school types—could capture a broader range of perspectives, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the research findings. Secondly, in terms of group heterogeneity, this study did not conduct a differential analysis of different subgroups. In fact, there may be some differences in the dominant attrition factors among teachers of different generations (such as veteran teachers and special post teachers), different subjects, and different genders. However, the conclusion of this study is more inclined to reflect the macro mechanism at the overall level.

Given the aforementioned limitations, future research can be improved in the following ways: First, expand the size and enhance the diversity of the expert panel. Second, conduct empirical tests on the structural model and its associated relationships proposed in this study through large-scale questionnaire surveys and statistical methods such as structural equation modeling, using a representative sample of rural teachers. Comparative analyses across groups and regions can also be conducted to enhance the model’s explanatory power and policy applicability.
