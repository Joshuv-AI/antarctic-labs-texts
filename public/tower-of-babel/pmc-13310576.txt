Substrate elasticity controls fibroblast motility on non-oxidized PDMS under weak adhesion
Authors: Arata Nagai, Kyotaro Kanazashi, Hiromu Kuwabara, Yuuta Moriyama, Toshiyuki Mitsui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiac fibroblasts play a pivotal role in heart tissue dynamics, responding to mechanical and interfacial cues in their microenvironment. This study examines how substrate stiffness and surface condition influence the motility and morphology of chicken embryonic cardiac fibroblasts cultured on collagen-coated polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrates with approximate elastic moduli of ~10 kPa and ~400 kPa, representing mechanically soft and stiff conditions, respectively. The PDMS surfaces were used without plasma or UV/ozone treatment, retaining their native hydrophobic character without surface oxidation. This absence of surface activation resulted in weak cell-substrate adhesion, leading to delayed spreading and frequent partial detachment during migration. Using time-lapse phase-contrast microscopy, we observed irregular motility cycles characterized by contraction-driven retraction, followed by partial detachment and re-adhesion. On soft substrates, fibroblasts exhibited longer motility cycles and left trailing edges upon retraction, while on stiff substrates, cells remained rounded and displayed less polarized motion. Quantitative analysis revealed that the duration of static phases and the degree of cell elongation varied with substrate stiffness but were also strongly influenced by adhesion instability due to unoxidized PDMS surfaces. These findings clarify the roles of substrate stiffness and surface adhesion in regulating fibroblast motility, and demonstrate that non-oxidized PDMS surfaces—though less adhesive—can preserve a rounded, less-spread cell morphology. Rather than promoting full spreading, such conditions may maintain fibroblasts in a mechanically less engaged or undifferentiated state. This work provides insight into how surface condition and mechanics together shape fibroblast behavior, with implications for mechanobiology and in vitro model design using non-activated biomaterials.

Significance

Fibroblast behavior is widely interpreted through the lens of substrate stiffness, yet many in vitro studies overlook how surface adhesion modulates cellular responses under weakly adhesive conditions. Using non-oxidized PDMS that preserves native hydrophobicity, we show that cardiac fibroblast motility is not dictated by stiffness alone but by the interplay between mechanics and unstable adhesion. Cells undergo irregular contraction–detachment cycles, altered polarization, and delayed spreading that differ fundamentally from behaviors observed on conventionally treated substrates. These findings highlight interfacial chemistry and provide guidance for designing physiologically relevant biomaterial platforms and interpreting fibroblast responses in vitro.

Introduction

The intricate dynamics of cellular behavior in response to the physical environment have become a focal point in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine [1]. In the heart, fibroblasts—often overshadowed by cardiomyocytes—play a pivotal role in tissue maintenance and repair by responding to mechanical cues [2]. Under physiological conditions, cardiac fibroblasts regulate extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling to support normal development and wound healing [3,4]. However, in pathological states, prolonged mechanical stress can induce fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition, leading to fibrosis and adverse cardiac remodeling, ultimately contributing to heart disease [2,5].

Recent advances in bioengineering emphasize the importance of soft materials that replicate in vivo microenvironments for studying cell behavior in vitro [6,7]. Among these materiatttls, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and hydrogels with tunable stiffness are widely used to investigate mechanobiological responses. Adjusting substrate stiffness, topography, and tension allows researchers to analyze how cells adapt their migration, proliferation, and differentiation [8,9]. In particular, single-cell studies suggest that motility is largely governed by ECM stiffness and external mechanical factors [10,11]. Adhesion strength also plays a critical role, further illustrating how mechanical interactions regulate cell behavior [12].

Cellular responses to external forces vary with both cell type and ECM mechanics [13]. For instance, neuronal cells migrate more efficiently on soft substrates (~50 kPa), while fibroblasts and muscle cells display optimal motility on stiffer matrices (10–100 kPa), representative of fibrotic and cardiac tissues [14–16]. In fibroblasts, adhesion is additionally modulated by the density and type of ECM protein coatings such as fibronectin or collagen, even when substrate stiffness remains unchanged [17,18]. These observations reveal that the intricate interplay between substrate properties, adhesion dynamics, and cell morphology in regulating motility patterns.

PDMS is a versatile platform for mechanobiology due to its tunable stiffness and controllable surface properties [19]. Its mechanical flexibility allows it to mimic a wide range of soft tissues, making it particularly suitable for preserving fibroblast phenotypes in vitro [20–23]. However, most studies employ PDMS surfaces activated by plasma or UV/ozone treatment to enhance hydrophilicity and promote ECM protein adsorption [19,24]. These treatments increase adhesion by introducing polar functional groups and enhancing protein binding, which facilitates focal adhesion formation and cell spreading. While such surface activation is widely used, it alters not only adhesion strength but also interfacial chemistry, protein adsorption dynamics, and potentially downstream signaling pathways [19,24]. As a result, it becomes difficult to isolate the pure effect of substrate stiffness from adhesion-mediated effects.

In contrast, PDMS without surface oxidation retains its native hydrophobic character, resulting in reduced ECM adsorption and weaker cell–substrate adhesion [25,26]. In this study, we intentionally focus on this weak-adhesion regime to investigate how insufficient adhesion modulates fibroblast motility independently of conventional surface activation treatments.

To investigate how reduced adhesion influences fibroblast motility, we used collagen-coated PDMS substrates with two distinct elastic moduli, soft (~10 kPa) and stiff (~400 kPa), representing relatively soft and stiff mechanical conditions, respectively [10,27,28]. Notably, the PDMS surfaces were not subjected to plasma or UV/ozone treatment prior to collagen coating. As a result, fibroblast adhesion remained weak, leading to limited spreading and partial detachment. These conditions allowed us to isolate the mechanical effects of stiffness while exploring how insufficient adhesion shapes migration dynamics. These values were adopted from representative literature reports for the same PDMS formulations and preparation conditions, and are used here to indicate relative mechanical contrast rather than absolute calibrated stiffness values. It should be noted that UV/ozone-treated PDMS represents a distinct adhesion regime with stronger and more stable cell attachment. Therefore, such conditions are not treated as a direct control in this study but rather as a complementary system that will be investigated in future work.

Using time-lapse microscopy, we tracked isolated single cells over 24 hours and quantified temporal changes in displacement, morphology, and motility cycles. Single-cell migration typically follows a cycle of extension, static elongation, and retraction [12]. Although the molecular pathways underlying these transitions are well described [29–31], the biophysical coordination of cell shape and displacement during each phase remains less understood. Here, we observed that weak adhesion modulates post-retraction shape and polarity, which in turn govern the directionality and speed of subsequent migration. Our findings provide insight into how adhesion and stiffness jointly modulate fibroblast behavior, informing biomaterial design for tissue remodeling and fibrosis modeling. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for uncovering the mechanobiological pathways that regulate fibroblast behavior and their potential implications for tissue remodeling and fibrosis.

Materials and methods

Preparation of substrates

To observe cell dynamics in environments with different Young’s moduli, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) was chosen as the substrate, and Sylgard-184 (Dow Corning, Midland, MI, USA; Silicone Elastomer Kit, Cat. #2646340) and DOWSILTM CY 52-276 A&B (Dow Corning Toray, Tokyo, Japan, Cat. #2624028/2624052) were selected as the liquid silicones.

To make a hard substrate by Sylgard-184, a 200 mg aliquot of pre-polymer base and a 20 mg aliquot of crosslinking curing agent were mixed. A 15 mg portion of this mixture was evenly spread on a 10 mm diameter No.0 coverslip (P35G-0-10-C, MatTek) in a 35 mm dish and cured at 80°C for 30 minutes. The Young’s modulus was not measured in this study; instead, we adopted a representative value of stiff (~400 kPa) from Fitzgerald et al., [32] who reported this range for Sylgard-184 prepared under the same formulation and curing conditions. Reported values for Sylgard-184 typically vary by 10–20%, reflecting differences in curing schedule and test method.

To make a soft substrate, a 100 mg aliquot each of CY52-276A and CY52-276B were mixed. A 15 mg portion of this mixture was spread on a 10 mm diameter coverslip (P35G-0-10-C, MatTek) in a 35 mm dish and cured at room temperature for 2 days. The Young’s modulus was not measured in this study; instead, we adopted a representative value of soft (~10 kPa) from Zhang et al., [33] who reported ~10.6±0.8 kPa by spherical nanoindentation for CY52-276 prepared under identical conditions (1:1 ratio, room-temperature curing).

Notably, no plasma or UV/ozone treatment was applied, preserving the native hydrophobic character of the silicone and avoiding surface oxidation. For collagen coating, each PDMS surface was treated with 300 μL of 0.3 mg/mL collagen I-C (Nitta Gelatin, Osaka, Japan; Cat. #631-00771) and incubated at room temperature for 4 h. After incubation, the coating solution was removed, and the surfaces were gently rinsed with 1× PBS (Nissui Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan; Cat. #5913) without allowing them to dry.

It should be noted that the Young’s modulus of Sylgard-184 and related PDMS formulations reported in the literature can differ by up to two orders of magnitude for nominally similar preparations, largely due to differences in curing conditions and the measurement method employed [33,34]. Therefore, the modulus values reported here should be regarded as representative estimates within this expected uncertainty.

Cell culture

Primary cardiac cells, consisting mainly of cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts, were isolated from the embryonic ventricles of 7-day-old Japanese Momiji chicken embryos at Hamburger–Hamilton stage 31 to 33. (Yamagishism Jikkenchi, Mie, Japan). The blood vessels were removed and the heart tissue was cut into ~1 mm3 pieces using pruning shears and incubated in 1 mL of 3.5% trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. #15090046) at room temperature for 10 minutes. Once the cells had detached, two volumes of pre-warmed complete growth medium were added to inactivate the trypsin. The medium was then gently aspirated and dispensed up and down with a pipette seven times to resuspend the cells, taking care to avoid excessive force that could damage them. To further minimize shear stress, a 21 G needle (TERUMO, Tokyo, Japan; Cat. #NN-2138R), with an inner diameter of approximately 0.51 mm, was used during aspiration. The suspension was subjected to two rounds of centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 5 minutes each. The pellet was resuspended and pipetted again, then passed through a 100 μm cell strainer (Corning, NY, USA; Cat. #353360) to remove undissociated tissue fragments. The resulting cell suspension was aliquoted and seeded at low density onto the prepared substrates. Cells were maintained at 37°C in humidified air containing 5% CO2 and cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. #10565018) supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan; Cat. # 168-23191), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Biowest, Nuaillé, France; Cat. #S1400-500). To promote isotropic cell adhesion, cultures were placed on a rocking shaker for 18 hours, followed by a 3-hour resting period before imaging was initiated. All animal experiments were approved by the Life Science Committee of Aoyama Gakuin University.

Time-lapse observation and analysis of cell morphological transitions during the motility cycle

Images were captured using a phase-contrast microscope (BZ-X810, KEYENCE) equipped with a 4× objective lens (UPlanFl, N.A. 0.13, OLYMPUS), recorded in TIFF format at 1920×1440 pixels (covering approximately 10 mm2) at 5-minute intervals for over 24 hours. On collagen I-C–coated PDMS substrates, approximately 500 cells were present per image. Isolated fibroblasts that were not in contact with other cells were selected for analysis. Only isolated fibroblasts that did not contact other cells during the observation period were included in the quantitative analysis. Trajectories in which cells collided or overlapped with neighbors were automatically excluded to avoid confounding effects of intercellular interactions. A total of eight independent culture dishes, four for soft (~10 kPa) and four for stiff (~400 kPa), were prepared with one dish representing one independent experiment. Each experiment required more than four days, including substrate preparation, cell isolation, seeding, and 24 h of time-lapse imaging. Because of the exclusion of collision events, the number of usable trajectories was limited. Data were collected from independent imaging sessions, and the reported sample sizes (n) reflect the number of isolated fibroblasts analyzed from these independent experiments.

Cell shape and centroid positions were determined by analyzing contrast differences between the cell and the background using ImageJ. To examine the angular distribution of cell velocity, only displacement events with velocities exceeding 0.1 μm/min were included, excluding static-phase cells in which no apparent movement was observed.

Uneven illumination was first corrected by background subtraction. Cells, which appeared darker than the PDMS substrate, were segmented by intensity thresholding, and the Fill Holes operation was applied to ensure continuous regions were recognized as cell areas, even when the phase-contrast halo produced bright centers. Results

We investigated the influence of substrate elasticity on cellular morphology and motility by seeding cardiac fibroblasts onto PDMS substrates with reported elastic moduli of ~10 kPa and ~400 kPa, representing relatively soft and stiff conditions, respectively, under weak adhesion conditions. Importantly, the PDMS surfaces were used without plasma or UV/ozone treatment, preserving their native hydrophobicity and preventing surface oxidation. As a result, collagen-coated substrates exhibited reduced cell-substrate adhesion, enabling us to examine cell behavior under unstable attachment conditions. The dynamics of individual cells were recorded using time-lapse phase-contrast microscopy at 5-minute intervals over a 24-hour period, focusing on isolated cells to capture intrinsic behaviors [36]. Observations extending beyond 24 hours revealed distinct cycles of characteristic cellular motions [12,37]. Each motility cycle began with cell extension, marked by the formation of a protrusion at one end and the development of new adhesions at the leading edge as the cell elongated [29]. This was followed by a static phase, during which the cell maintained its shape and established polarity while generating increasing contractile force [30]. The cycle concluded with a retraction phase, in which the rear of the cell detached and relaxed, enabling forward movement and completing the cycle before the next began.

Motility patterns on the softer substrate

On the softer substrate, ~10 kPa, fibroblasts exhibited oscillatory behavior with coordinated back-and-forth displacements as part of a repeated motility cycle (Figs. 1A–1D). To quantitatively characterize the deformation dynamics of the cell aggregate, we define an elongation ratio as the ratio of the major axis length to the minor axis length of the aggregate. This metric captures the degree of anisotropic deformation during cyclic motion.

Based on the temporal evolution of the elongation ratio, we classify the motion into three phases: extension, static, and retraction. Specifically, the extension phase is defined as the period during which the elongation ratio increases continuously, indicating elongation of the aggregate. The static phase corresponds to intervals where the elongation ratio remains approximately constant, reflecting minimal shape change. The retraction phase is defined by a decreasing elongation ratio, indicating contraction of the aggregate. Representative elongation ratio traces and phase boundaries are shown in Fig. 1O. Notably, the elongation process occurs gradually, whereas retraction is markedly rapid and nearly instantaneous, producing sharp negative excursions in the elongation ratio. This quantitative classification allows direct correspondence between changes in elongation ratio and morphological transitions observed in the cell. Each figure (a–d) illustrates characteristic cell shape changes corresponding to the steps in the motility cycle. For example, in Group A (t=0–80 minutes), shown in Fig. 1A, the cell initiates protrusion formation (arrowhead, Fig. 1Ab) in the direction indicated by the red arrow, accompanied by an increase in elongation ratio (Fig. 1O), reflecting progression through the extension phase and elongation to 71 μm.

During the static phase, the elongation ratio reaches a plateau, and the trailing edge thins (arrowhead, Fig. 1Ac). Within 10 minutes, the cell enters the retraction phase, characterized by a decrease in elongation ratio, reducing its length to 51 μm while moving forward (Fig. 1Ad). The centroid trajectory (Fig. 1Ae) confirms directional displacement along the Y-axis, capturing one complete motility cycle. In Group B (t=80–180 minutes), as shown in Figs. 1Ba–1Bb, the cell reverses its direction, forming a new protrusion at the previously trailing edge (arrowhead). The cell elongates, as reflected by an increasing elongation ratio (Fig. 1O; Fig. 1Bc), and within 10 minutes, enters a retraction phase marked by a rapid decrease in elongation ratio, reducing its length to 36 μm while migrating back toward its original position (red arrow, Fig. 1Bd). The trajectory in Fig. 1Be confirms this back-and-forth motion.

Similar behavior was observed in Groups C and D, with each cycle consisting of an extension phase, increasing elongation ratio, a static phase (plateau), and a retraction phase (decreasing elongation ratio) (Figs. 1Ce, 1De). As a result, each complete motility cycle lasted nearly 100 minutes, with the centroid showing oscillatory displacement along a single axis (Fig. 1De). These dynamic behaviors are presented in Supplementary Movie 1. Speed analysis from centroid displacement (Fig. 1E) revealed peaks exceeding 2 μm/min during retraction phases, while speeds remained below 1 μm/min [12,31] during gradual protrusion and adhesion phases. Given that typical cellular migration speeds are under 1 μm/min [31], the rapid shrinkage observed during trailing-edge detachment constitutes a notable finding.

Motility patterns on stiffer substrate

On the stiff (~400 kPa)) PDMS substrate, fibroblasts exhibited random-like displacements, in contrast to the oscillatory behavior observed on softer substrate. As shown in image sequences a–d of Groups F through I in Figs. 1F–1I, the cells underwent repeated protrusion–retraction cycles with no clear directional regularity. Corresponding centroid trajectories (Figs. 1Fe–1Ie) further confirmed the absence of consistent directionality throughout each cycle.

For example, in Group F (t=0–50 minutes), the cell extended protrusions toward both ends (red arrows, Fig. 1Fb). During the static phase, it maintained a length of nearly 55 μm (Figs. 1Fb–1Fc), while one edge began to thin (arrowhead, Fig. 1Fc), indicating cell polarity. The duration of this static phase was shorter than that observed on softer substrate. Within 10 minutes, the cell retracted, reducing its length to 22 μm and forming a nearly circular shape (Fig. 1Fd). Similar behaviors were observed in Groups G, H, and I, where cells transitioned from brief static phases to rapid retraction. Notably, static phase durations were consistently shorter on stiffer substrate compared to the soft condition. Directional changes and random-like oscillatory activity were frequently observed, as shown in Figs. 1F–1I, with Fig. 1Ie illustrating a typical example of such motion.

Speed analysis (Fig. 1J) revealed peaks exceeding 2 μm/min during retraction phases, while speeds during protrusion remained below 1 μm/min [12,31]. These peaks corresponded to the onset of retraction phases within the motility cycle. The overall motility cycle period on the stiff substrate was estimated to be approximately 50 minutes, shorter than the nearly 100-minute cycles observed on the soft substrate. These dynamic behaviors are presented in Supplementary Movie 2.

Effect of substrate elasticity on directionality of cell displacement

To investigate how substrate elasticity influences the directionality of cell motion, cell displacement was decomposed into X and Y components, as shown in Fig. 1Ae for soft substrate and Fig. 1Fe for the stiff substrate, and analyzed in Figs. 1K, 1L and 1M, 1N, respectively. For soft substrate, Fig. 1L illustrates oscillatory displacements primarily along the Y-axis, with amplitudes reaching approximately 50 μm during retraction phases and speeds exceeding 2 μm/min. Displacement along the X-axis remained confined within ±5 μm. Conversely, on the stiff substrate, cells exhibited displacements exceeding 20 μm in both X and Y directions (Figs. 1M and 1N), reflecting a more diffusive and less aligned motion pattern.

Cell shape during the motility cycle on soft and stiff PDMS substrates

We analyzed cell morphology, including elongation ratio, circularity, and area, as shown in Figs. 1O–1Q for the soft substrate and Figs. 1R–1T for the stiff substrate. On the soft PDMS substrate, the elongation ratio exhibited periodic sharp decreases with a characteristic timescale of approximately 90 minutes (Fig. 1O). Notably, elongation increased gradually during the extension phase, whereas retraction occurred rapidly, producing abrupt negative excursions in the elongation ratio. These rapid reductions in length were synchronized with peaks in cell speed during retraction phases. Despite these dynamic shape changes, circularity remained around 0.5 (Fig. 1P), indicating that cells maintained an elongated morphology, while the cell area stabilized at approximately 1000 μm2 during static phases (Fig. 1Q).

On the stiff substrate, a similar asymmetric behavior was observed in the elongation ratio, with gradual extension followed by rapid, nearly instantaneous retraction (Fig. 1R). Retraction phases were associated with sharp decreases in elongation ratio, leading to transient rounding of the cell. Consistently, circularity increased toward unity during these phases (Fig. 1S). Concurrently, the cell area decreased to a minimum during retraction and subsequently recovered to slightly above 500 μm2 during static phases (Fig. 1T).

Statistical analyses of directionality of cell velocity and cell motility cycle on soft and stiff PDMS substrates

To quantitatively compare behavior on the two substrates, we analyzed both the directionality of cell movement and motility cycle periods. The angular distribution histograms of cell velocity during elongation and retraction phases on the soft (~10 kPa) substrate (Supplementary Fig. 1A–1D) showed pronounced directional, linear motion for isolated cells. In contrast, the angular distributions on the stiff (~400 kPa) substrate (Supplementary Fig. 1E–1H) were broadly distributed, indicating isotropic displacements.

To further quantify this difference, we calculated the bidirectional order parameter (R2) from the angular distributions. Cells on the soft (~10 kPa) substrate exhibited significantly higher R2 values (0.479±0.043, n=4) compared to those on the stiff (~400 kPa) substrate (0.128±0.047, n=4) (Supplementary Fig. 1I), indicating strong bidirectional (uniaxial) alignment of migration. In contrast, the low R2 values on the stiff substrate are consistent with isotropic, random-like movement. This difference was statistically significant (P<0.01).

With respect to the motility cycle period, cells on the soft (~10 kPa) substrate exhibited significantly longer cycles, averaging 79.64±7.53 minutes (n=19), compared to 37.01±2.47 minutes (n=72) on the stiff (~400 kPa) substrate (Supplementary Fig. 2). These values correspond to 19 isolated fibroblasts from four independent dishes on soft (~10 kPa) and 72 isolated fibroblasts from four independent dishes on stiff (~400 kPa).

Cell shape transitions and statistical analyses of motility phase durations, cell length, circularity, and area

To further investigate how substrate elasticity affects the timescales of the extension, static, and retraction phases, we analyzed cell shape deformation during a single motility cycle using 5-minute temporal resolution. Representative phase-contrast time-lapse images are shown in Figs. 2Aa–2Ae (soft, ~10 kPa) and 2Ba–2Be (stiff, ~400 kPa). On the soft (~10 kPa) substrate, fibroblasts displayed pronounced anisotropic elongation, forming a distinct fan-shaped lamellipodium at the leading edge while retaining a trailing edge (Fig. 2Aa). Retraction caused detachment of the trailing edge (arrowhead, Fig. 2Ab), resulting in forward displacement. The cell then reversed polarity by forming a new lamellipodium at the former trailing edge, enabling oscillatory motion (Figs. 2Ac–2Ad). On the stiff (~400 kPa) substrate, the lamellipodium was shorter and less defined. After retraction (arrow, Fig. 2Ba), the cell adopted a rounded shape with reduced length and directional alignment (Fig. 2Bb). As the cell elongated again (Fig. 2Bc), the extension deviated from the previous direction and initially lacked a clear lamellipodium. A slight lamellipodium formed later, leading to minor displacement (Fig. 2Bd). Retraction occurred before full elongation was achieved, resulting in a rounded morphology and diminished polarity. Phase durations were quantified using elongation ratio and cell length. The extension phase was defined from the minimum cell length to elongation, when the elongation ratio exceeded 0.1. The static phase was identified when the ratio remained between –0.06 and 0.1 for 5 minutes, and retraction began when the ratio dropped below –0.06, continuing until the next length minimum.

Figure 2C shows that the static phase was significantly prolonged on the soft (~10 kPa) substrate (57.82±1.16 min, n=4 independent culture dishes) compared to the stiff (~400 kPa) substrate (21.00±1.30 min, n=4 independent culture dishes). In contrast, extension and retraction durations showed no significant differences. This suggests that the longer motility cycle period observed on soft substrate (Supplementary Fig. 2) is primarily due to an extended static phase.

Additionally, we analyzed cell morphology at the end of the extension phase (maximum length) and at the end of the retraction phase (minimum length). Cell length (Figs. 2D, 2G), circularity (Figs. 2E, 2H), and area (Figs. 2F, 2I) were evaluated. Cells on soft substrate were longer and covered a larger area in both phases, while those on stiff were shorter and more circular. Circularity values approaching 1.0 on stiff substrate reflect a lack of polarity in shape. Notably, the difference between maximum and minimum cell length was similar on both substrates, suggesting comparable extension speeds.

These morphological and temporal differences likely result not only from substrate stiffness but also from reduced adhesion stability due to the absence of surface oxidation on the PDMS. Because no plasma or UV/ozone treatment was applied, the substrate retained its native hydrophobicity, potentially limiting the strength and persistence of cell-substrate interactions.

Discussion

Our study examined the influence of substrate elasticity on cellular morphology and motility by analyzing cardiac fibroblasts on soft and stiff substrate untreated (non-plasma-treated) PDMS substrates, which provide reduced surface adhesion [25,26]. Cells on the softer substrate exhibited coordinated oscillatory motion with pronounced elongation and distinct fan-shaped lamellipodia, whereas cells on stiff substrate displayed random-walk-like motion, reduced polarization, and shorter lamellipodia. Statistical analysis revealed that the prolonged static phase on soft substrate was the primary contributor to the longer motility cycle, in contrast to the shorter cycles on stiff substrate. Morphological differences further reflect the role of substrate elasticity: on soft substrate, cells extended more and retained polarity after retraction, while on stiff substrates, they became more circular, exhibiting a non-directional morphology. These results emphasize the essential role of substrate elasticity in shaping both cell motility and morphology under weak adhesion conditions. In conventional mechanobiology studies, PDMS substrates are often treated with UV/ozone or plasma to enhance hydrophilicity and promote protein adsorption, thereby strengthening cell adhesion [25,26]. In such systems, robust focal adhesion formation stabilizes cell spreading and typically supports persistent, directional migration. In contrast, our results demonstrate that under weak-adhesion conditions on untreated PDMS, cells exhibit intermittent detachment, reduced polarization, and transitions between oscillatory and random-walk-like migration patterns. These differences indicate that cell motility is governed not only by substrate stiffness but also by the balance between traction force generation and adhesion stability.

Our results indicate that cell motility under weak adhesion conditions is governed by a balance between intracellular traction forces and adhesion stability. Although increased substrate stiffness is generally associated with enhanced force generation and cell spreading, our data show that, when adhesion is insufficient, higher stiffness instead promotes premature detachment and limits elongation. Thus, stiffness-dependent force generation alone does not determine cell morphology or motility. Under these conditions, softer substrates (~10 kPa) permit sustained adhesion relative to the generated traction forces, enabling prolonged elongation and longer motility cycles. In contrast, on stiffer substrates (~400 kPa), increased contractile forces exceed adhesion strength, leading to early detachment, reduced elongation, and more isotropic displacement. This imbalance between traction and adhesion provides a mechanistic basis for the observed differences in motility dynamics. Together, these findings demonstrate that adhesion strength acts as a key regulatory parameter that interacts with substrate stiffness to control cell behavior. Such a regime is likely relevant in physiological contexts where adhesion is transient or spatially heterogeneous, including tissue development, wound repair, and extracellular matrix remodeling.

Swift displacement after detachment

Previous studies have suggested that cellular morphology and motility are primarily influenced by substrate adhesion and elasticity, which regulate the number and area density of focal adhesions (FAs) [8,9]. Additionally, FA lifetimes generally last tens of minutes, and the balance between FA formation and annihilation, along with internal cell tension, determines the dynamic state of the cell [23]. Notably, high-density collagen substrates enhance FA attachment to the substrate, increasing the residence time of FAK within FAs by approximately fourfold compared to low-density collagen [38]. Strong adhesion to the substrate has been suggested to enhance cell spreading [9], which in turn may suppress the cell speed. In contrast, our experiments used non-plasma-treated PDMS substrates, poor protein adsorption and cell adhesion [25,26]. Under these reduced-adhesion conditions, we observed significant shape changes and rapid detachment motion in fibroblasts. This suggests that weakened adhesion contributes to swift displacement dynamics upon detachment.

Cell shape change in motility cycle on soft and stiff PDMS substrates

Fibroblasts on stiffer substrates generally elongate more after polarization, exerting greater traction forces [39–42]. In our study, cells on the soft (~10 kPa) substrate extended to an area of approximately 1200 μm2, while those on the stiff (~400 kPa) substrate extended to about 700 μm2, with both conditions exhibiting similar circularity values (Fig. 2F). The smaller cell area observed on the stiff substrate, approximately half of the values reported in earlier studies [43], suggests that cells on the stiff (~400 kPa) substrates were unable to elongate as extensively. Instead, they transitioned prematurely into the retraction phase, reducing overall elongation. Traction force, an internal pulling force correlated with FA formation, plays a crucial role in cell shape dynamics [44]. During the process of increasing contractile force following polarization, the non-plasma-treated PDMS surface of the stiff substrate may have provided insufficient adhesion to sustain FA attachment, leading to premature detachment before maximum elongation. In contrast, on soft substrates, FA maturation may proceed more effectively, allowing cells to sustain elongation for a longer duration before retraction. Since internal traction forces are higher on stiffer substrates, the imbalance between traction and adhesion results in a more circular shape on the stiff substrate compared to the soft substrate [45].

Although we did not directly visualize focal adhesions by fluorescence microscopy, previous studies have shown that FA density and lifetime strongly correlate with cell morphology and spreading [41,44,45]. The pronounced elongation and sustained polarity observed on the soft (~10 kPa) substrate are consistent with more stable FA maturation, whereas the rapid rounding and premature detachment seen on the stiff (~400 kPa) substrate suggest unstable adhesions with shorter lifetimes. Thus, our phase-contrast observations provide indirect but reproducible evidence of altered FA dynamics under weak-adhesion conditions. Future work incorporating direct FA visualization, such as vinculin staining or live-cell FA reporters, as suggested by the reviewer, will be valuable to confirm and extend these interpretations.

This mechanical instability likely contributes to the shorter duration of the static phase on stiffer substrate, as cells detach and transition into a new motility cycle more quickly. These findings demonstrate the role of substrate elasticity in stabilizing elongation and regulating the duration of motility phases, and point to the importance of mechanical feedback in governing fibroblast migration.

Oscillatory motion vs. random-walk-like motion induced by cell shape after retraction

The directionality of migrating cells is governed by a combination of biochemical anisotropy and intracellular signaling, involving Rho-family GTPases such as Rac, Rho, and Cdc42 [46,47]. These molecules regulate protrusion and contraction in distinct regions of the cell, establishing front-rear polarity, which in turn determines the direction of movement. Rac is localized at the leading edge, where it promotes the formation of lamellipodia and actin polymerization through Arp2/3 activation, whereas Rho enhances contractility at the rear to drive retraction. Cdc42 coordinates Rac activity to maintain polarity. Fibroblasts establish such polarization more slowly than other cell types, typically taking 30–50 mins, and maintain directional persistence as long as the intracellular concentration gradients remain unchanged [48].

Although we did not directly measure molecular distributions, the distinct morphological states observed here are consistent with these known pathways. On the soft (~10 kPa) substrate, fibroblasts exhibited well-defined lamellipodia and trailing edges, behaviors consistent with stabilized Rac-driven protrusions and sustained focal adhesion maturation. By contrast, on the stiffer (~400 kPa) substrate, cells elongated bidirectionally before polarity was firmly established and then detached prematurely, producing rounded morphologies and random-walk-like displacements. Such behavior may reflect unstable adhesions combined with traction forces that exceed anchoring capacity, conditions predicted to shorten FA lifetime and disrupt polarity stabilization [49,50].

In cells that retracted on the softer and (~400 kPa) substrate, circularization after detachment required polarity to be reestablished de novo, likely contributing to random migration patterns (Supplementary Figs. 1E–H). In contrast, cells on the soft (~10 kPa) substrate often reversed polarity after the static phase, producing oscillatory, back-and-forth migration (Figs. 2Ab, 2Ae; Supplementary Fig. 2A–D). This behavior is consistent with redistribution of Rac and Rho activities, whereby Rac activity declines at the front or Rho dominance at the rear initiates polarity reversal [51]. Oscillatory migration has been described in mesenchymal cells, attributed to cyclic Rac–Rho feedback and reaction–diffusion signaling models [47,52,53]. Our results therefore suggest that substrate stiffness modulates not only adhesion stability but also the intracellular signaling dynamics that govern polarity maintenance versus reversal.

These mechanistic interpretations are inferred from cell shape and motility patterns, supported by published molecular studies, and underscore that cell morphology can serve as a proxy for underlying signaling states [46,51]. Future studies incorporating direct visualization of focal adhesions or live-cell biosensors for Rac, Rho, and Cdc42 activity will be valuable to confirm these inferences and extend our understanding of stiffness-dependent polarity regulation.

Relevance across cell types

Although our experiments were restricted to embryonic chicken fibroblasts, comparable stiffness-dependent effects have been reported in diverse mammalian cell types, including humans. For example, human mesenchymal stem cells display lineage specification and altered migration on substrates of differing elasticity [10]. Fibroblast polarization has been shown to be rigidity-dependent in mammalian cells [41], and human cardiac fibroblasts regulate collagen metabolism and profibrotic signaling in response to stiffness [20,54]. Notably, human gingival fibroblasts cultured on soft PDMS exhibited enhanced proinflammatory signaling and suppressed ECM synthesis, whereas stiffer substrates promoted ECM production and attenuated inflammatory responses [55]. Together, these findings support that stiffness-dependent regulation of migration, mechanotransduction, and inflammatory responses is a broadly conserved phenomenon. We therefore propose that the oscillatory and random-like migration cycles we report here, while demonstrated in chicken fibroblasts, are likely relevant across species and cell types.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that substrate elasticity plays a crucial role in regulating the motility and morphology of cardiac fibroblasts. By analyzing fibroblast behavior on PDMS substrates of different stiffness, we identified distinct motility patterns. Cells on the softer substrate exhibited oscillatory motion, characterized by static elongation phases, directional persistence, and periodic polarity reversals. In contrast, cells on stiffer substrate displayed random-walk-like behavior, reduced polarity, and frequent transitions into a circular shape, likely due to shorter elongation times and weaker adhesion. Notably, all experiments were conducted on PDMS surfaces without plasma oxidation. This non-treated condition results in reduced protein adsorption and cell adhesion, providing a model for evaluating motility dynamics under weaker adhesive environments. The combination of weak adhesion and substrate elasticity influenced the balance between focal adhesion maturation and internal traction forces. These findings suggest that fibroblast motility is governed by the interplay between traction forces and substrate adhesion. On the softer substrate, stable focal adhesion maturation allowed cells to sustain elongation, leading to longer motility cycles. In contrast, weaker adhesion and higher traction forces on stiffer substrate promoted premature retraction, resulting in rapid polarity loss and non-directional displacement. The transition into a circular shape following retraction further disrupted polarity, contributing to isotropic movement.

While focal adhesions and Rho-family GTPases were not directly visualized here, the observed morphological states are consistent with established Rac- and Rho-mediated polarity pathways. The oscillatory versus random-like motility cycles we describe in chicken fibroblasts likely represent conserved behaviors across mammalian fibroblasts and related mesenchymal cells, as supported by prior studies. These results therefore contribute to linking descriptive cell motion with established molecular frameworks of polarity regulation. Future work combining high-temporal-resolution imaging with focal adhesion staining or live-cell biosensors will be crucial to validate these mechanistic links and to extend the applicability of this PDMS-based model system to broader contexts in tissue remodeling, fibrosis, and biomaterial design. Finally, by deepening our understanding of fibroblast mechanobiology, these insights may inform tissue engineering, regenerative medicine, and the development of biomaterials designed to control fibroblast behavior in therapeutic applications.
