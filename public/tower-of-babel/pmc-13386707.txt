Chloroplast genomics and nuclear ribosomal ITS phylogeny reveal pronounced cytonuclear discordance in Lycoris chunxiaoensis
Authors: Shi-Yu Huang, Jia Yao, Yu-Tong Han, Yong-Chun Zhang, Xuan-Min Guang, Feng Yang, Hong-Fei Li, Yuan Tian, Fu-Yuan Zhu, Ke-Wang Xu, Liu-Yan Yang, Qing-Zhu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Lycoris chunxiaoensis is a recently described species that shares high morphological similarity with L. radiata. Resolving the exact phylogenetic position and evolutionary origin of species within the genus Lycoris is often challenged by complex evolutionary processes.ResultsWe sequenced and assembled the complete chloroplast genome of L. chunxiaoensis and conducted comparative genomic and phylogenetic analyses. The phylogenetic reconstructions revealed cytonuclear discordance: the plastid phylogeny placed L. chunxiaoensis as sister to Lycoris rosea, whereas the nuclear ribosomal ITS phylogeny clustered it with Lycoris radiata. Furthermore, karyotype and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analyses showed that L. chunxiaoensis (2n = 3x = 33) shares cytogenetic similarity with L. radiata.ConclusionsPhylogenetic analyses based on the chloroplast genome and ITS datasets revealed differences in the phylogenetic placement of L. chunxiaoensis. In the plastome-based phylogeny, L. chunxiaoensis was recovered as sister to L. rosea, whereas in the ITS-based phylogeny it was placed within the same clade as L. radiata. This incongruence between chloroplast genome and ITS sequence suggests potential cytonuclear discordance and indicates a complex evolutionary history within Lycoris.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09041-4.

Introduction

The genus Lycoris Herb. (Amaryllidaceae), holding significant ornamental and medicinal value due to their unique aesthetic appeal [1], consists of about 20 species which are distributed in the moist warm temperate woodlands of eastern Asia, as in China, Korea, Japan [2, 3]. However, the genus presents significant challenges in classification. This difficulty arises from continuous variation in morphological and physiological traits [4], combined with overlapping geographic distributions and highly homogeneous habitats [5] that facilitate frequent natural hybridization among species. Furthermore, Lycoris exhibits substantial karyological complexity, with ploidy levels spanning diploid to tetraploid forms and diverse karyotype groups, for instance A, M + T, and M + T+A [6, 7].

Given this extensive morphological diversity and complex web of interspecies relationships driven by hybridization, traditional classification relying solely on morphological traits is often insufficient to achieve a consensus [8]. In recent decades, integrating biparentally inherited nuclear genomic data (e.g., nrDNA-ITS) [9] with maternally inherited plastid DNA has emerged as a highly effective approach for reconstructing phylogenetic relationships and resolving reticulate evolution in Amaryllidaceae [10, 11].

Lycoris chunxiaoensis, a Chinese endemic species found and described by Li et al. [12], is morphologically similar to Lycoris radiata. Despite its morphological description, its phylogenetic position and evolutionary history remain unclear. The presence of overlapping phenotypic traits with closely related taxa suggests a potentially complex evolutionary background; however, this hypothesis still requires further validation based on molecular evidence.

To address this knowledge gap and clarify the phylogenetic position of this newly described species, it is essential to move beyond morphological observations. In this study, we newly sequenced and assembled the complete chloroplast genome of L. chunxiaoensis and conducted a comprehensive comparative analysis with those of seven additional Lycoris species. Furthermore, we performed phylogenetic analyses by integrating chloroplast genome data with nuclear ITS sequences. By systematically comparing organellar and nuclear genomic datasets, this study aims to resolve the taxonomic status of L. chunxiaoensis and provide insights into intrageneric classification and species delimitation within Lycoris, particularly by assessing potential cytonuclear discordance.

Materials and methods

Taxon sampling, DNA extraction, and sequencing

The materials of L. chunxiaoensis used for the experiment were identified by Yuhong Zheng (the Institute of Botany, Jiangsu Province and Chinese Academy of Sciences) under voucher number NAS000736327. The fresh leaf of L. chunxiaoensis used in this experiment was collected from its natural habitat in Chunxiao Town (China, Zhejiang Province, Ningbo City, Beilun District, Chunxiao town, on the edges of forest roads, farmlands and riverbanks, 44 m a.s.l., 29° 47’ N, 121° 52’ E), and collected for whole genome DNA extraction using a plant genomic DNA kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). Morphologically, L. chunxiaoensis is a perennial herb characterized by its relatively large bulbs, autumn-emerging leaves that are long and wide, a notably elongated peduncle, and relatively large tepals with a moderately undulate and recurved margin. Its flower color dynamically shifts from rose-red in bud to dark rose-red at anthesis, eventually developing blue tips and white margins post-anthesis (Fig. 1). Morphological comparison reveals that L. chunxiaoensis differs from the closely resembling L. radiata by its larger bulbs, longer and wider leaves, taller peduncle, larger tepals, distinct coloration, and less pronounced tepal undulation (Fig. 2). There was no endangered or protected species involved in the study, and no specific permissions were required for the sample.

In addition, total DNA was also employed to amplify ITS sequence. PCR amplification was carried out in a 20 µL reaction volume containing 10 µL of 2 × Phanta Flash Master Mix (Dye Plus) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), 1 µL of forward primer ITS-1 (10 µM; 5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGCTTAA-3′) [13], 1 µL of reverse primer ITS-2 (10 µM; 5′-AGAAGTCGTAACAAGGTTTCCGTAGG-3′), 1 µL of template DNA, and 7 µL of ddH₂O. The PCR program consisted of an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s; followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 5 s, and extension at 72 °C for 5 s; with a final extension at 72 °C for 1 min. The PCR products were then held at 4 °C. The products were sent to sequencing company (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. Shanghai, China) for Sanger sequencing. PCR amplification and sequencing were performed at least twice for each DNA template to ensure accuracy. The obtained sequences were assembled and aligned using DNASTAR software.

Chloroplast genome sequencing, assembly and annotation

For chloroplast genome sequencing, approximately 1 µg of total genomic DNA was fragmented to a target size of 300–500 bp using a Covaris M220 focused-ultrasonicator. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the TruSeq™ Nano DNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) strictly following the manufacturer’ s protocol. Briefly, the fragmented DNA underwent end repair, 3’ adenylation, and index adapter ligation, followed by library enrichment via 8 cycles of PCR amplification. The target fragments were then size-selected and recovered using a 2% Certified Low Range Ultra Agarose gel (Bio-Rad, USA). The purified libraries were accurately quantified using a TBS380 Fluorometer with PicoGreen (Invitrogen, USA) and pooled in equimolar ratios based on the target data output. Cluster generation was performed on a cBot solid-phase support, and 150 bp paired-end (PE150) sequencing was subsequently conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The chloroplast genome of L. chunxiaoensis was de novo assembled via GetOrganelle v1.7 software [14], L. radiata (NC_045077) as reference. To assess the sequencing depth and overlapping contigs, the cleaned reads were mapped to the reference plastome by Geneious R11 [15]. The Plastid Genome Annotator (PGA) was applied to annotate the chloroplast genome [16], followed by the validation of annotation adopting online GeSeq tool [17] with default parameters. The newly yielded ITS sequence and chloroplast genome of L. chunxiaoensis have been submitted to NCBI (GenBank Accession ID: PZ159264 and PX957146). Additionally, we downloaded 24 ITS sequences and 29 chloroplast genomes for the phylogenetic analysis. The names of the species and GenBank accession numbers for all samples were listed in Table S1 and Table S2.

Genome comparison

To investigate the divergence regions in Lycoris, IRscope (https://irscope.shinyapps.io/irapp/, accessed on 13 May 2026) [18] was employed to visualize the variation at the boundary junctions of eight chloroplast genome sequences in Lycoris.

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) in the chloroplast genome of L. chunxiaoensis were identified using MISA-web with the following minimum repeat thresholds: ten repeat units for mononucleotide repeats, five repeat units for dinucleotide repeats, four repeat units for trinucleotide repeats, and three repeat units for tetranucleotide, pentanucleotide, and hexanucleotide repeats. Compound SSRs were identified using default parameters implemented in MISA-web.

The mVISTA program (http://genome.lbl.gov/vista/index.shtml) [19] was used with default parameters to compare complete chloroplast genomes of Lycoris using Shuffle-LAGAN mode, with L. radiata as the reference genome.

Multiple sequence alignment of the eight Lycoris chloroplast genomes was performed using MAFFT v7.467 [20], and the sequence alignment was manually inspected to ensure positional homology and to remove ambiguously aligned regions when necessary. The curated alignment was then put into MEGA X for paired genetic distance calculation [21].

Sliding window analysis was used to calculate nucleotide diversity (Pi) in protein-coding and non-coding regions of the chloroplast genomes. Pi values were computed using DnaSP v5 [22] based on the aligned chloroplast genome sequences with a window size of 600 bp and a step size of 200 bp.

Phylogenetic analysis

To pinpoint the phylogenetic position of L. chunxiaoensis, a total of 25 ITS sequences and 30 complete chloroplast genome sequences were used to reconstruct phylogenetic trees. Eleven species from other genera within Amaryllidaceae were selected as outgroups [8, 23]. Sequence alignments were performed using MAFFT v7 [20] and manually adjusted in BioEdit [24]. After manual adjustment, the final ITS alignment was 653 bp in length, and the complete chloroplast genome alignment was 158,685 bp in length. Ambiguously aligned sites were manually inspected and excluded when necessary, and gaps were treated as missing data in the phylogenetic analyses.

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods. The ITS dataset and the complete chloroplast genome dataset were analyzed separately. For each dataset, the best-fit nucleotide substitution model was selected independently using ModelFinder implemented in IQ-TREE v2.1.1 according to the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). The ML trees were constructed using IQ-TREE v2.1.1 [25] under the best-fit substitution model (TVM + F+R2), and branch support was assessed with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. No additional partitioning strategy was applied within either dataset.

The BI analyses were performed using MrBayes v3.2.7 [26] under the GTR + F+I+G4 model, with two independent runs of four Markov chains each for 1,000,000 generations, sampling every 1,000 generations. The first 25% of sampled trees were discarded as burn-in, and posterior probabilities were used to assess node support. Convergence of the BI analyses was evaluated by examining the average standard deviation of split frequencies (ASDSF), potential scale reduction factor (PSRF), and effective sample size (ESS) values. ESS values were assessed in Tracer v1.7 [27], and values above 200 were considered indicative of adequate sampling. The analyses were considered to have reached convergence when the ASDSF value was below 0.01 and the PSRF values approached 1.0. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized using FigTree v1.4.3.

Chromosome preparation and Fluorescent in Situ Hybridization (FISH) analysis

Mitotic metaphase chromosomes were prepared from root tips according to Han et al. [28]. Root tips (1.5–2.0 cm) were treated with nitrous oxide gas at 1 MPa for 2 h, fixed in 90% acetic acid for 10 min, and enzymatically digested using 2% cellulase and 1% pectolyase at 37 °C for 45 min. After digestion, the root sections were washed once in 70% ethanol and once in 100% ethanol. Meristematic cells were isolated via vortexing in 100% ethanol and re-suspended in 90% acetic acid before being dropped onto slides. FISH was performed following the protocols of Tang et al. [29]. Probes included a telomere-specific sequence (TTTAGGGTTTAGGGTTTAGGG) labeled with Tamra (red) or 6-FAM (green) as per [30]. The 5 S rDNA clone (pTa794) and 18 S rDNA clone (9-kb fragment) were labeled with fluorescein-12-dUTP via nick-translation. Probe solution (20 ng/L in 2 × SSC and 1 × TE buffer pH 7.0) for 10 µL was used for each slide. The hybridization was performed at 42 °C overnight in a humid chamber. After hybridization, the slides were washed using 2 × SSC and then mounted with Vectashield mounting medium containing 1.5 µg/mL 4, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). The images were captured with an Olympus BX-53 microscope equipped with a DP-70 CCD camera.

Results

Karyotype and FISH analysis

Karyotype and FISH analyses revealed that the specimen is a typical autotriploid with a somatic chromosome number of 2n = 3x = 33, including 3st-, and 30 t-type chromosomes (Fig. 3). Karyotype Type is 4 A type, exhibiting high asymmetry and a complete absence of large metacentric (M) chromosomes. FISH mapping further localized 45 S rDNA signals at the terminal regions of Group 1, while 5 S rDNA signals were consistently detected at the interstitial positions of Group 8. The exclusive presence of I-type (st/t) chromosomes, coupled with the highly symmetrical distribution of rDNA loci across the three homologous set.

General feature of chloroplast genome

The chloroplast genome of L. chunxiaoensis is 158,685 bp in length, with an overall GC content of 37.75% (Table 1). It exhibits a typical conserved quadripartite structure, comprising a large single-copy (LSC) region of 86,509 bp, a small single-copy (SSC) region of 18,538 bp, and a pair of inverted repeat (IR) regions of 26,819 bp each (Fig. 4). Additionally, sequence analysis identified a total of 44 mononucleotide, 13 dinucleotide, and 1 trinucleotide repeats within the chloroplast genome.

Chloroplast Simple Sequences Repeats (cpSSRs)

We analyzed the simple sequence repeats (SSRs) in the chloroplast genomes of eight Lycoris spp. (Fig. 5). A total of five categories of SSRs (mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, and penta-nucleotide repeats) were detected across the species. Among the identified SSRs, mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant, with the A/T motif accounting for the majority of the total SSRs in all eight species. In contrast, C/G repeats were rare, being completely absent in L. squamigera. However, the AG/CT dinucleotide motif showed presence/absence variation, notably being absent in L. squamigera. Furthermore, various longer nucleotide repeats, including trinucleotide (e.g. AAT/ATT), tetranucleotide, and pentanucleotide motifs, were identified. The specific pentanucleotide motif AAATC/ATTTG was exclusively detected in L. sprengeri.

Chloroplast genomes comparison and hotspots identification

We compared the large single-copy (LSC), small single-copy (SSC), and inverted repeat (IR) junction regions across the eight Lycoris species to assess boundary expansions and contraction (Fig. 6). In L. radiata and L. incarnata, the IRb regions expanded by 37 bp toward the rps19 gene, and their IRa regions expanded by 1–3 bp toward rps19. In contrast, no expansion at the IRb/LSC boundary was observed in the other six Lycoris species. Only in both L. radiata and L. incarnata did rpl2 have duplications in the IR region, while rpl2 on the IRb region was not detected in any of the other six species. Comparison of IRb/SSC and SSC/IRa regions of ndhF, and ycf1 showed high similarity between L. radiata and L. incarnata. The ndhF spanned the IRb/SSC boundary in five species, resulting in a partial duplication of this gene, whereas it was located strictly within the SSC region in L. chunxiaoensis, L. radiata, and L. incarnata. Furthermore, the typical IR duplication of the rpl2 gene was detected in both L. radiata and L. incarnata;, the rpl2 copy in the IRb region was absent in the remaining six species. High structural similarity at the IRb/SSC and SSC/IRa junctions involving the ndhF and ycf1 genes was shared exclusively between L. radiata and L. incarnata.

To investigate the level of sequence divergence, the complete chloroplast genomes of the eight Lycoris species were aligned and compared using the mVISTA program, with L. radiata set as the reference (Fig. 7) The alignment showed that the coding regions (CDS) were highly conserved across all analyzed species. Conversely, the non-coding regions (CNS), including intergenic spacers and introns, showed lower similarity and higher variation of variation, specifically in regions such as rps16-atpA, ndhF-rpl2, and psbM-rpoB.

Phylogenetic analysis

To determine the phylogenetic position of L. chunxiaoensis, phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using the complete chloroplast genomes and the nuclear ribosomal ITS datasets. In the plastid phylogeny (Fig. 10), L. chunxiaoensis formed a strongly supported sister clade with L. rosea, with support values of BS = 99% and PP = 1.00 in maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) analyses, respectively. In the nuclear ITS‑based phylogeny (Fig. 11), L. chunxiaoensis clustered with L. radiata (BS = 97%, PP = 0.9973). The placement of L. chunxiaoensis differed between the two datasets: in the plastid tree it was sister to L. rosea, whereas in the ITS tree it was grouped with L. radiata.

Discussion

Plastome characteristics

In this study, we reported the complete chloroplast genome of L. chunxiaoensis and performed comparative analyses with other Lycoris species. The results showed that the plastome exhibited a typical quadripartite structure, consistent with previously reported Lycoris chloroplast genomes [23, 31]. In addition, the chloroplast genomes of Lycoris species were highly similar in genome size, gene order, and GC content, indicating a high level of structural conservation. Such conservation is commonly observed in plastid genomes and suggests relatively stable plastome evolution within the genus [32].

Simple Sequence Repeats (SSRs) analyses

Chloroplast simple sequence repeats (cpSSRs) typically exhibit intraspecific variation and have been utilized as molecular markers for assessing ecological and evolutionary processes [33, 34]. In this study, a total of 63–71 cpSSRs were identified across eight Lycoris species, with a predominance of A/T-rich mononucleotide repeats, which is consistent with the AT-rich nature of chloroplast genomes [11, 24]. The largely conserved distribution patterns of these cpSSRs among Lycoris species may suggest limited variation in chloroplast genomes. However, given the maternal inheritance of plastid genomes, cpSSR variation alone is insufficient to fully resolve evolutionary relationships within the genus [35].

The IR/SC boundaries of the eight plastomes

The expansion and contraction of inverted repeat (IR) regions are known to contribute to variation in chloroplast genome size [36–38]. Comparative analysis of IR/SC boundary regions revealed that the overall structure of the chloroplast genomes in Lycoris was highly conserved. Sequence variation was generally higher in the single-copy (SC) regions than in the IR regions [39, 40]. In addition, most sequence divergence was concentrated in non-coding regions. Although some variation was observed among Lycoris species, the overall structural conservation of plastomes suggests that large-scale plastome rearrangements are unlikely to be the primary source of phylogenetic discordance within the genus. Instead, such discordance may be associated with evolutionary processes not captured by plastid genomes, such as hybridization or incomplete lineage sorting.

Phylogenetic analysis and taxonomic implication

Chloroplast genomes have been widely used in plant phylogenetics due to their conserved structure and predominantly uniparental inheritance [41, 42]. However, plastid-based phylogenies do not always reflect species histories, particularly in lineages with complex evolutionary dynamics [43]. Discordance between plastid and nuclear phylogenies can arise from multiple processes, including incomplete lineage sorting (ILS), chloroplast capture and hybridization [44], and distinguishing between these alternatives is critical for accurate evolutionary inference.

In this study, phylogenetic analyses based on complete chloroplast genomes and ITS sequences recovered largely congruent topologies with moderate to strong support across most clades. Furthermore, the overall small genetic distances observed among all Lycoris species corroborate the highly conserved nature of their chloroplast genomes. Nevertheless, the phylogenetic placement of L. chunxiaoensis differed between datasets, revealing cytonuclear discordance. Specifically, in the ITS-based phylogeny (Fig. 11), L. chunxiaoensis clustered with L. radiata, suggesting a close nuclear affinity between these species.

This nuclear affinity is supported by multiple lines of evidence. Cytogenetic analyses (Fig. 3; Table 3) indicate that L. radiata possesses an A-type genome with a chromosome complement of 2n = 2x = 22 [7, 8], whereas L. chunxiaoensis exhibits a karyotype of 2n = 3x = 33, consistent with previous reports. The shared basic chromosome number and high karyotypic homology indicate a close evolutionary relationship within the A-type genome group [45]. Furthermore, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analyses have shown that these two species share identical rDNA loci distributions, reinforcing their affinity at the chromosomal level [46]. The FISH experiment revealed completely symmetrical signals on three chromosomes. As asymmetric signal distribution is often observed in allopolyploids, this symmetrical pattern is more consistent with an autotriploid constitution than with an allopolyploid origin. However, whether the autotriploid genome derives directly from L. radiata or from another closely related A‑type genome lineage cannot be determined solely from these cytogenetic data. Morphological similarities between L. chunxiaoensis and L. radiata provide additional support for this relationship. To conclude, these nuclear, cytogenetic, and morphological data consistently support a close relationship between L. chunxiaoensis and L. radiata.

In contrast, the phylogeny of chloroplast genome placed L. chunxiaoensis as sister to L. rosea, suggesting that these two species may share similar hybrid origins. However, their exact maternal progenitors remain unclear. Because the chloroplast genome is typically maternally inherited [41], it reflects only the maternal lineage and may therefore differ from the biparentally inherited nuclear genome. Under the framework of the multispecies coalescent model [47], phylogenetic discordance does not necessarily reflect true differences in species evolutionary history. As emphasized by Larson et al. [44], incomplete lineage sorting alone can generate topological incongruence among gene trees, with such conflicts typically distributed stochastically across different loci. Therefore, in the absence of additional corroborating evidence, observed cytonuclear discordance should not be directly interpreted as evidence of reticulate evolution or cytonuclear dissonance. In our study, the concordant signal across nuclear (ITS), cytogenetic, and morphological datasets consistently supports the affinity between L. chunxiaoensis and L. radiata, whereas the chloroplast genome alone supports an alternative placement. Given that we have only a single nuclear marker, we cannot statistically distinguish whether this discordance results from incomplete lineage sorting, chloroplast capture, or a combination of processes.

Among these, chloroplast capture represents a plausible explanation, whereby the chloroplast genome of L. chunxiaoensis could have been acquired from a lineage related to L. rosea through historical introgression. While this scenario is compatible with the reticulate evolutionary patterns previously proposed for the genus Lycoris [35], the current data are insufficient to directly demonstrate such processes.

Nevertheless, given the limited resolution of ITS as a single nuclear marker, we cannot conclusively distinguish between incomplete lineage sorting and introgression, nor can we robustly infer cytonuclear dissonance (i.e., genuinely distinct evolutionary histories between nuclear and plastid genomes). Future studies incorporating multiple low-copy nuclear genes, population-level sampling, and explicit tests of introgression (e.g., D-statistics) [44] will be essential to rigorously evaluate these alternative scenarios.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified clear cytonuclear discordance in L chunxiaoensis, with nuclear, cytogenetic, and morphological evidence consistently supporting a close relationship with L. radiata, while chloroplast genome analysis indicates an alternative affinity with L. rosea. This incongruence could arise from various processes, including incomplete lineage sorting under the multispecies coalescent framework or chloroplast capture. However, given that we have only a single nuclear marker (ITS), the current data are insufficient to conclusively distinguish among these alternative evolutionary scenarios. Our findings highlight the importance of integrating multiple lines of evidence in phylogenetic inference and suggest that plastid genomes alone may be inadequate for resolving species relationships in Lycoris. Further analyses based on genome-wide nuclear data will be essential to clarify the evolutionary history of this genus.
