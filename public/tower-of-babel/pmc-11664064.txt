Higher levels of plasmatic saturated fatty acid were significantly associated with liver fibrosis in HIV mono-infection: A case-control study
Authors: Cristiane Fonseca de Almeida, Wilza Arantes Ferreira Peres, Paula Simplício da Silva, Claudia Santos de Aguiar Cardoso, Michelle Morata de Andrade, Julio Castro-Alves, Marcel de Souza Borges Quintana, Marina Campos Araujo, Karla Yasmin Dias Fraga, Julliana Antunes Cormack, Ronaldo Ismerio Moreira, Sandra W Cardoso, Valdilea G Veloso, Beatriz Grinsztejn, Patricia Dias de Brito, Hugo Perazzo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between plasmatic fatty acid (FA) composition and liver fibrosis remains scarce in people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA). We aimed to evaluate the association of plasmatic FAs and liver fibrosis in HIV mono-infected individuals.MethodsThis case-control study included PLWHA with liver fibrosis (cases) and randomly selected subjects without fibrosis (controls) from the PROSPEC-HIV study (NCT02542020). Participants with viral hepatitis, abusive alcohol consumption and lipid supplements use were excluded. Liver fibrosis was defined using transient elastography (TE) by liver stiffness measurement (LSM) ≥ 7.1 kPa or ≥ 6.2 kPa with M or XL probe, respectively. All HIV mono-infected participants with liver fibrosis identified at the baseline PROSPEC-HIV visit were included. Controls (1:1) were randomly selected among those HIV mono-infected participants without liver fibrosis. Plasmatic FA profile, dietary lipid intake, anthropometric measures, and blood samples were assessed. Plasmatic fatty acid was analyzed using gas chromatography and intake of fats lipids were assessed by two 24-h dietary recall (24-HDR). Multivariate logistic regression models adjusted by age, sex at birth and duration of antiretroviral therapy (ART) were performed.ResultsA total of 142 participants (71 cases and 71 controls) [62 % female, median age = 46 (IQR, 37–53) years, 14.8 % with diabetes, median CD4 count = 655 cells/mm3, 96.5 % under ART] were included. Higher percentages of plasmatic palmitc acid (16:0) and saturated fatty acids (SFA) were observed in participants with liver fibrosis (cases) compared to those without (controls). Presence of higher percentage of plasmatic palmitc acid (16:0) was associated with an increased odds for liver fibrosis [adjusted OR = 1.23 (95%CI 1.04–1.46); p = 0.02] in multivariate models.ConclusionThis study showed the potential role of the plasmatic FA composition in the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis in PLWHA.Keywords: Fatty acid composition, Liver fibrosis, NAFLD, HIV infection

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is one of the most frequent causes of chronic liver disease worldwide due to its strong relationship with metabolic features [1,2]. NAFLD can progress to advanced liver fibrosis, especially driven by presence of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) that is characterized by lobular necro-inflammatory activity [3]. Recently, an international multi-society Delphi consensus statement have changed the terminology of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) to MASLD (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease) due to the strong relationship between this liver disease and metabolic factors [4]. Several studies from different countries/regions have reinforced that this change did not affect the validity of the evidence previously published on NAFLD [5].

The hypothesis of “multiple hits” has been proposed in the pathogenesis of NAFLD/NASH and progression to fibrosis/cirrhosis. Fat infiltration of hepatocytes (steatosis) is often referred as the “first hit” that might be followed by proinflammatory cytokine cascade activation, oxidative stress and apoptosis leading to liver fibrosis/cirrhosis (“multiple hits”) [6].

Excessive accumulation of lipids in the hepatocytes lead to lipotoxicity that has been characterized by cellular dysfunction and death [7]. Plasmatic composition of fatty acid (FA) remains a major mediator of liver steatosis and has been used as a surrogate marker of liver metabolism dysfunction. Levels of plasmatic FA reflect endogenous metabolism and are related to the diet lipids ingestion. Recent studies demonstrated controversial findings regarding the relationship of fatty liver disease with sub-types of plasmatic FA. Individuals with NAFLD and advanced liver fibrosis had a higher percentage of palmitic, stearic and oleic FA in erythrocytes compared to those without advanced fibrosis. Besides, a study that compared individuals with and without NAFLD showed that total plasmatic saturated fat and plasmatic n-6 poly-unsaturated FA (PUFA) were positively related to NAFLD, while docosaehexanoic FA (PUFA n-3) was negatively correlated with presence of NAFLD [8].

Moreover, several studies have been reporting the burden of NAFLD and/or liver fibrosis in people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) [[9], [10], [11]]. Overall, risk factors for NAFLD in PLWHA are similar to those in the general population, including obesity, insulin resistance, type-2 diabetes and dyslipidemia. However, additional mechanisms might play a role to contribute for a higher prevalence of NAFLD in PLWHA, such as higher visceral adipose tissue, long-term use of combined antiretroviral therapy (c-ART), inflammatory status and gut dysbiosis [[12], [13], [14]]. Despite the evidence of prevalence and risk factors for NAFLD and/or fibrosis in PLWHA data evaluating the relationship among those liver diseases and plasmatic levels of FA remains controversial and scarce in this population [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the relationship between levels of plasmatic FAs and liver fibrosis in people with HIV mono-infection.

Methods

Study design and population

This is a case-control study that used baseline data from the PROSPEC-HIV cohort study (NCT02542020) that has been conducted at Evandro Chagas National Institute of Infectious Diseases (INI/FIOCRUZ, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil) [14]. Overall, the PROSPEC-HIV study included adults with HIV infection from June 2015 to January 2019. For this analysis, we excluded participants with viral hepatitis co-infection defined by positive HCV-antibody or positive HBsAg; excessive alcohol consumption defined by the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) score ≥8 [19]; use of lipids supplements or missing/inconsistent data on laboratory or dietary assessment. Presence of liver fibrosis (METAVIR stage ≥ F2) was defined by liver stiffness measurement (LSM) ≥ 7.1 kPa or ≥ 6.2 kPa with probe M or XL, respectively. A sample size calculation considering averages of the plasma concentration of fatty acids, a significance level of 5 % (α = 0.05) and power of 80 % (β = 0.80) was performed. This calculation defined that 126 participants with HIV infection, 63 cases (PLWHA with liver fibrosis based on transient elastography) and 63 controls (PLWHA from without liver fibrosis) would be necessary. All participants enrolled in the PROSPEC-HIV study from June/2015 to January/2019 who had liver fibrosis were included as cases in this analysis. A total of 71 participants had liver fibrosis (based on transient elastography) considering the baseline visit of the PROSPEC-HIV study. Then, the control group (1:1) was randomly selected from PLWHA without fibrosis (n = 71) from the same PROSPEC-HIV study population. This study was approved by the Ethical Committee from INI/FIOCRUZ (IRB 32889514.4.0000.5262). All participants signed an informed consent upon enrollment in the PROSPEC-HIV study.

Clinical assessment and HIV infection history

Clinical data included age, sex at birth, self-report skin-color [20], schooling and presence of co-morbidities. Dyslipidemia, hypertension, type-2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome were defined according to the International Diabetes Federation [21]. Anthropometric measures, such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference, were measured by a trained operator. Body fat percentage was measured using a bioelectrical impedance analyzer (Biodynamics® 450, Sao Paulo, Brazil). The procedures performed are described elsewhere [22]. Data regarding HIV infection history were available at the INI/FIOCRUZ HIV cohort: (i) date of first positive HIV antibody test; (ii) date of initiation of any antiretroviral drug; (iii) dates of start and end of combined antiretroviral therapy (c-ART) and (iv) CD4 + T-lymphocyte count and HIV viral load from the closest day of the study visit.

Laboratory tests and transient elastography

Blood tests were performed in a centralized laboratory using an analyzer Dimension-RxL-Max (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostic, Illinois, USA). Enzymatic assay was used to measure liver tests, such as alkaline phosphatase (AP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT). Glucose was measured using the hexokinase method; total and high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-cholesterol and triglycerides were determined using enzymatic methods. Insulin was determined using chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) and the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated using the following formula: [fasting insulin (mIU/L)∗fasting glucose (mg/dL)]/405 [23].

Presence of liver fibrosis was defined by liver stiffness measurement (LSM) using transient elastography (TE) by FibroScan (EchoSens, Paris, France) performed by a single experienced (>2000 examinations) operator (HP), as previously described [14]. The LSM result was defined as a median of 10 valid measures and expressed in kPa. TE exams were considered as reliable for analysis if the following criteria had been met: (1) at least 10 valid measurements; (2) an interquartile range (IQR) lower than 30 % of the median of LSM, and (3) a success rate of more than 60 %. Presence of liver fibrosis (METAVIR stage F ≥ 2) was defined by LSM ≥7.1 kPa or ≥ 6.2 kPa with M or XL probe, respectively [24].

Dietary data

The dietary intake of lipids and FA were evaluated by the 24-h dietary recall (24-HDR) method in two non-consecutive days. The 24-HDR was applied using the Automated Multiple-Pass method to structure the interview and to increase the accuracy of the report [25,26]. Data of each food and/or beverage item reported by the participant were entered into a nutritional analysis software (Diet Win Professional Plus 3.0®package software), as previously described [22]. Additionally, Multiple Source Method (MSM) was used to estimate the nutrients usual intake of participants and to correct the intrapersonal dietary variability (https://msm.dife.de). Nutrients were adjusted by energy total intake using density method and were expressed as a percentage [27].

Plasmatic fatty acid composition assessment

FA contents were analyzed by gas chromatography, using Agilent Technologies 7890A CG System equipment, equipped with flame ionization detector, coupled to the EZChrom Elite CDS program (Agilent Technologies, Inc., C.A., U.S.A) at Nutritional Biochemistry Laboratory of UFRJ.

Plasma samples were submitted to lipid extraction, saponification and direct alkaline methylation by the method proposed by American Oil Chemist’ Society AOCS 2b-11 [28]. Then, the samples were injected into the gas chromatograph equipment with a flame ionization detector. The methyl esters were separated into a fused silica capillary column SP-2560 of polysiloxane bis-cyanopropyl (Sulpeco Inc., PA, USA), with a length of 100m × 0.25 μm x 0.20 μm of internal diameter whose temperature programming was established at 100 °C with an increase rate of 3 °C/minute, reaching the temperature of 140 °C. The temperatures of the injector and detector were 250 °C. Gas flows used: (Linde Gases, RJ, BR) were 0.75 mL/min for drag gas (hydrogen); 25 mL/min for auxiliary gas (nitrogen); 30 mL/min and 400 mL/min for hydrogen and synthetic air of flame, respectively. FA methylated were identified based on the comparison with the relative retention time of the pattern peaks (Nu-Chek Prep. Inc., mixture of methyl esters 463) and the quantification of each FA was performed using the internal standard C13:0 (Sigma-Aldrich) as a reference. The assay generated data on 36 fatty acids and the fatty acid composition was expressed as percentage of the total fatty acids analyzed in plasma.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported as absolute (n) and relative frequency (%) and continuous variables as median and interquartile range (IQR). We used Chi-square and Mann-Whitney tests to compare proportions and medians, respectively. Plasmatic FA were analyzed in proportion (%) of total FA and all nutrients in percentage of energy intake (E%). Direct Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) (supplementary figure) were created with assumptions on the relationship among co-variables and outcome to support our decision about the most parsimonious models for liver to avoid collinearity and select confounders [22]. Logistic multivariate regression models were performed considering plasmatic FAs as independent variables and presence/absence of liver fibrosis as outcome. The multivariate models were adjusted for age, sex and duration of c-ART according to the DAGs (model 1). The variables included in the multivariate models should met the following criteria: (1) they should be associated with the outcome (potential risk factors); (2) they should be associated with the exposure; and (3) they should not be implicated on the causal pathway from the exposure to the outcome. Additionally, in a sensitivity analysis, BMI and diabetes were included in the multivariate model to assess the association of metabolic factors in the relationship of liver fibrosis and plasmatic lipids levels (model 2). Statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.6.3 and considering p-values <0.05 as statistically significant.

Results

A total of 727 participants with HIV infection were enrolled in the PROSPEC-HIV study. Participants were excluded due to viral hepatitis coinfection (n = 95), abusive alcohol intake (n = 123), use of lipid supplement (n = 6), unreliable for liver fibrosis assessment (n = 8) or missing data of analysis laboratory (n = 6). Therefore, 489 participants were eligible for this case-control analysis. Of them, 71 participants (14.5 %) had liver fibrosis defined by TE (cases). Then, 71 individuals were randomly selected (1:1) among those 418 participants from the PROSPEC-HIV study without liver fibrosis (controls). Therefore, we analyzed data from 142 participants [71 controls and 71 cases] as described in Fig. 1. Overall, 62 % were female, the median (IQR) age was 46 (37–53) years and median BMI was 26.7 (23.3–30.1) Kg/m2; 14.8 % had type-2 diabetes, 28.2 % had hypertension and lipodystrophy was present in 17.6 % of participants. Participants had a median (IQR) duration of HIV infection of 8.5 (4.5–14.8) years, median CD4+ t-lymphocyte count of 665 (477–861) cells/m3 and 96.5 % were under c-ART (Table 1). Participants with liver fibrosis were significantly older [median age; 47 (40–56) vs 43 (IQR, 33–52) years; p = 0.009], and had higher levels of ALT, AST and GGT compared to controls. Additionally, participants with liver fibrosis had higher BMI [29.0 (23.7–32.7) vs 25.9 (23–28.3) Kg/m2; p = 0.005] and higher proportion of metabolic features, such as type-2 diabetes (22.5 % vs 7.0 %, p = 0.009) and hypertension (40.8 % vs 15.5 %, p < 0.001) when compared with controls. On the other hand, there was no differences in HIV characteristics between cases and controls (Table 1).

Plasmatic fatty acid

Table 3 reports the median plasmatic levels of fatty acids according to presence or absence of liver fibrosis. Participants with liver fibrosis had higher levels of palmitc acid (16:0) compared to those without fibrosis [23 % (IQR, 20.8–24.8) vs 21.8 % (20.8–23.1); p = 0.006]. Similarly, saturated FA were higher in cases compared to controls [31.7 % (30–33.9) vs 30.8 % (20.9–32.2); p = 0.041]. On the other hand, there was no significant difference in plasmatic levels of others FA between cases and controls.

A higher percentage of plasmatic palmitc acid (16:0) was associated with presence of liver fibrosis [OR = 1.26 (95%CI 1.06–1.49), p = 0.010] and this association has remained after adjustment for confounding factors [aOR = 1.23 (95%CI 1.04–1.46); p = 0.02]. In sensitivity analyses, BMI and/or diabetes were included in the multivariate models due to the strong correlation among liver fibrosis and metabolic factors. The association between plasmatic palmitic acid (16:00) levels and liver fibrosis remained significant when diabetes was entered in the model [OR = 1.2 (95%CI 1.0–1.43)] (Supplementary Table 1). However, this association was not observer when entering BMI alone in the model or both variables diabetes and BMI (model 2 of Table 4). Furthermore, an increase in the higher percentage of total plasmatic saturated FA was significant associated with liver fibrosis on univariate analysis [OR = 1.15 (95%CI 1–1.32); p = 0.04], but this association was not observed after adjustment for confounding factors (Table 4).

Discussion

This study highlighted the potential role of plasmatic levels of FA for developing liver fibrosis in PLWHA. In addition, the present study demonstrated a significant association of higher percentages of plasmatic saturated FA, specifically those related to palmitic acid, with presence of liver fibrosis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the one of the first studies that evaluated plasmatic FA according to presence/absence of liver fibrosis in people with HIV mono-infection. The main novelty of this study remains the evaluation of the relationship of plasmatic fatty acids with liver fibrosis, based on transient elastography, in PLWHA controlled for confounding factors in a case-control study.

Increased circulating free FAs could be a major cause of hepatic lipotoxicity that might lead to hepatocytes injury and drive the progression of NAFLD [29,30]. In present study, we showed that a higher percentage of plasmatic saturated FA was associated with presence of liver fibrosis. This result is aligned with the study from Shim et al. that evaluated plasmatic levels of FA in uninfected people with fatty liver disease detected by abdominal ultrasound [31]. Lipids, especially saturated FA, alter cellular autophagy, playing an important role mediating lipotoxic injury in NAFLD [30]. Saturated FAs might have caused proinflammatory cytokines activation and insulin resistance in an experimental studies [32,33].

Palmitic acid (C16:00), a saturated FA mostly endogenously produced, has an important role in the pathogenesis of liver injury. Palmitic acid is the first fatty acid synthesized and is also the precursor to longer fatty acids. This FA may be elongated to stearic acid (C18:00) or to longer fatty acids. Both FA C16:00 and C18:00 may be desaturated to the palmitolate (C16:1) and oleate (C18:1) forms, respectively [34,35]. Unsaturated FA such C18:01 are preferentially utilized on triglyceride synthesis, thereby protects against palmitate-induced lipotoxicity [36]. Therefore, palmitic acid might be considered a potential candidate biomarker for de novo lipogenesis [37]. Additionally, palmitic FA remains a cornerstone for inflammasome activation that can lead to hepatic stellate cells activation and production of IL-1α and IL-1β in Kupffer cells [[38], [39], [40], [41]]. Furthermore, this FA cause insulin resistance (IR) in hepatic cells by the increased of the reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the lipid accumulation, both provoke activation simultaneously of two protein kinases (JNK and PKCε), that consequently reduce glycogen synthase and activity [32]. In the present study, we demonstrated that higher plasmatic levels of palmitic FA was independently associated with the presence of liver fibrosis compared to controls. This finding is aligned with previous studies that included uninfected individuals. Cansanção et al. demonstrated that a higher percentage of palmitic FA in red blood cells was associated with liver disease progression evaluated by TE, in individuals with NAFLD in Brazil. However, those authors reported that the proportion of stearic acid, oleic acid and total MUFA was also significantly increased in participants with advanced liver fibrosis (LSM ≥7.9 kPa) [42]. In a case-control study the authors showed that patients with NASH had increased levels of several fatty acids, including, palmitic FA, compared to NAFL patients and healthy controls [43]. A previous study showed that people with biopsy-proven NASH had significantly higher plasmatic levels of palmitic acid, palmitoleic acid, oleic acid and γ-linoleic compared to those without NASH. Furthermore, higher palmitic acid levels were significantly associated with high NAFLD activity score (NAS) in analyses adjusted for the same factors and fibrosis stage [44]. Additionally, another study was demonstrated that patients with NAS ≥5 had significantly higher palmitate levels than patients with NAS ≤4 [45].

Despite several studies reporting the relationship between palmitic acid and liver fibrosis in uninfected individuals, data regarding this relation in PLWHA remains scarce. A limited sample size cross-sectional study compared FA composition in erythrocytes between 20 HIV-infected men with NAFLD, 21 uninfected men with NAFLD and 7 healthy controls (uninfected HIV and without NAFLD). This study demonstrated a higher proportion of plasmatic palmitic FA in participants HIV/NAFLD compared to controls [46]. However, discordant of our findings, those authors showed a significant lower plasmatic proportion of n-3 PUFA in participants HIV/NAFLD compared to controls. This fact might be explained because this FA might be associated with inflammatory and immunological processes in the pathogenesis of HIV infection [47]. In a recent prospective cohort study that evaluated differences in plasma fatty acids profiles between people with and without HIV infection with NAFLD was demonstrated that the plasmatic levels of FA was similar in both groups. However, those with HIV infection had lower activity of specific enlongases (ELOVL1 and ELOVL6) which had been associated with chronic inflammatory condition [48]. The relationship among obesity, diabetes, insulin resistance and liver fibrosis in PLWHA remains complex and multifactorial [49,50]. The fact that we could not observe an association between palmitic FA (C16:00), a saturated fatty acid, and liver fibrosis when adjusting the model by adding BMI can be explained by the role of this variable as a mediator in the relationship between plasmatic fatty acids (the exposure) and liver fibrosis (the outcome).

In present study, we did not observe a significant association between lipids ingestion and presence of liver fibrosis. We are aware that only 15 % of the circulating FA are derived from diet in people with NAFLD [51]. Therefore, our hypothesis remains on the fact that the profile of plasmatic FA was influenced by endogenous metabolism (de novo lipogenesis) [52]. Subjects with NAFLD often show an increase in de novo lipogenesis and is a major pathway in the pathogenesis of NAFLD and NASH. This may be an adaptive mechanism that direct lipids toward beneficial pathways to improve energy balance even in the setting of excess FA accumulation [34].

On the other hand, we reported that median usual intake of protein was higher in HIV individuals with liver fibrosis compared to controls. Soleimani et al. demonstrated that higher intake of red meat was associated with liver fibrosis in uninfected individuals [53]. This relationship can be explained by the fact that high protein intake might be associated with insulin resistance and oxidative stress [54]. The iron content of red meats can generate free radicals, that might be associated with cellular and tissue damage [55]. Moreover, a higher protein diet might potentially lead to gut microbiome dysbiosis and pro-inflammatory status. A high-protein diet affects the gut microbiome leading to a potentially pathogenic and proinflammatory microbiota profile which increases ammonia, phenols and hydrogen sulfide concentrations that induce mucosal inflammation. Indeed, the dysregulation of gut endothelial barrier function can allow translocation of bacterial components and can lead to hepatic inflammation [56].

The major limitations of our study were the cross-sectional study design hinders the possibility of drawing any conclusions on causality, the absence of a HIV-uninfected control group and the lack of liver biopsy as the reference for the presence of the liver fibrosis. We used data from the PROSPEC-HIV cohort study, which included mostly people with HIV mono-infection. It would be unethical to perform liver biopsy in people with HIV mono-infection without liver enzymes elevations. Moreover, we defined presence of liver fibrosis using TE by Fibroscan that remains as an extensive validated non-invasive tool. Another limitation would be the unbalance between cases and controls regarding BMI. However, it reflects the clinical characteristics of the population. The present analysis included all participants who had liver fibrosis at baseline visit of the PROSPEC-HIV cohort (n = 71) and controls (1:1) were randomly selected among those participants without liver fibrosis. The mains strengths of this study remain in the fact that we evaluated the relationship of plasmatic FA composition in a well-characterized sample of people with HIV mono-infection and the quality of FA composition determination. Furthermore, our final sample size (n = 142) was higher than that calculated (n = 126). Additionally, all TE exams were performed for a single experimented operator and all blood analyses were performed in a centralized laboratory.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that saturated FA, especially palmitic FA, was independently associated with liver fibrosis in people with HIV mono-infection. These results reinforce the importance of the quality of the plasmatic levels of lipids in pathogenesis of liver fibrosis in this population. The findings of the present study generate hypotheses for future personalized interventions aiming to modulate fatty acid metabolism in subjects at high-risk of liver fibrosis, such as people living with HIV. Future directions might include the use of lipid biomarkers for monitoring, dietary and pharmacological approaches for lipid modulation. Therefore, those findings emphasize the importance of nutrition in prevention and treatment of liver fibrosis in PLWHA.
