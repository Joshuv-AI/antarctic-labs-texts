Biofabricated Al2O3 nanoparticles as a recyclable heterogeneous catalyst for nicotinonitrile synthesis: antimicrobial activity, molecular docking and ADMET assessment
Authors: Prakash Kokare, Ashwini More, Nilesh Pandit, Vishvanath Ghanwat, Amol Jadhav, Aniket Sawant, Chaitali Bagade, Dipak mali, Sunanda Mahanwar, Santosh Kamble
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An efficient and environmentally benign Al2O3 nanoparticle-catalyzed protocol was developed for the synthesis of nicotinonitrile derivatives under mild reaction conditions using an ethanol–water solvent system. The Al2O3 nanoparticles were synthesized via a green approach employing Coccinia indica leaf extract and characterized by XRD, SEM, EDS, FTIR, and BET analyses. The developed catalytic protocol afforded excellent yields (88–96%) within short reaction times (18–40 min), demonstrating a broad substrate scope, high catalytic activity, and excellent recyclability. The synthesized nicotinonitrile derivatives were further evaluated for their antimicrobial activity against S. aureus, B. subtilis, P. aeruginosa, and E. coli, exhibiting promising antibacterial efficacy. Molecular docking studies against the bacterial DNA gyrase B enzyme further supported their potential antimicrobial mechanism, while in silico ADMET analysis indicated favorable drug-likeness and toxicity profiles. Overall, this study demonstrates a sustainable nanocatalytic strategy for the efficient synthesis of biologically active nicotinonitrile derivatives.

Introduction

Heterogeneous nanocatalysts have gained significant importance in organic synthesis owing to their large surface area, high catalytic efficiency, and excellent recyclability. Among various metal oxide nanomaterials, aluminium oxide (Al2O3) nanoparticles have been widely employed because of their thermal stability, non-toxic nature, low cost, and Lewis acidic properties.1–5 Their nanoscale structure provides a greater number of active sites, thereby enhancing reaction efficiency and product selectivity.6,7 Furthermore, the heterogeneous nature of Al2O3 nanoparticles enables the easy separation and reuse of the catalyst, making them highly suitable for environmentally friendly synthetic processes.8–13 Recently, the green synthesis of nanoparticles using plant extracts has attracted considerable attention as an environmentally benign alternative to conventional chemical methods. Plant extracts contain a variety of natural phytochemicals that act as reducing and stabilizing agents during nanoparticle formation. In the present study, Coccinia indica leaf extract was employed for the eco-friendly synthesis of Al2O3 nanoparticles.

Nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds are of considerable importance in medicinal and pharmaceutical chemistry because of their broad spectrum of biological activities, including antimicrobial, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties.14–20 Among these compounds, nicotinonitrile derivatives have emerged as valuable heterocyclic scaffolds with significant pharmacological and synthetic applications.21–28 The nitrile group enhances the structural and electronic properties of these molecules and also serves as a versatile functional group for the synthesis of other biologically active compounds. However, many of the reported methods for the synthesis of nicotinonitrile derivatives involve harsh reaction conditions, toxic solvents, lengthy procedures, and non-reusable catalysts.29–33 Therefore, the development of a simple, efficient, and environmentally benign synthetic approach remains highly desirable.

Considering these aspects, the present work focuses on the application of green-synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles as a recyclable heterogeneous catalyst for the synthesis of nicotinonitrile derivatives under mild reaction conditions in an ethanol–water medium. The synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles were characterized by XRD, SEM, EDS, FTIR, and BET analyses to investigate their structural and surface properties. Furthermore, the synthesized nicotinonitrile derivatives were evaluated for their antimicrobial activity against selected bacterial strains and were further investigated through molecular docking studies. Overall, this study demonstrates an environmentally benign and efficient nanocatalytic strategy for the synthesis of biologically important nicotinonitrile derivatives using a reusable Al2O3 nanocatalyst.

Materials and methods

Materials

Coccinia indica leaf extract, potassium hydroxide (KOH, 1 M, 99% purity), and aluminium nitrate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Ethanol, aromatic aldehydes, acetophenone, ammonium acetate, and other chemicals were of analytical grade and were used as received without further purification. Distilled water was used throughout the study. The hardware and software employed for the molecular docking study included a Dell computer equipped with a 12th Gen Intel® Core™ i3-12100 processor (3.30 GHz) running the Windows 11 Pro operating system, and ICM Pro software (Version 3.9-2, Molsoft LLC, USA).

Methods

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to investigate the crystalline phase, structural purity, and crystallinity of the biosynthesized aluminium oxide (Al2O3) nanoparticles using a Bruker AXS D8 Advance diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The surface morphology and microstructural features of the Al2O3 nanocatalyst were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a JEOL JSM-IT-200 instrument, while the elemental composition and uniform distribution of aluminium and oxygen were confirmed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was carried out using a Lambda Scientific FT-IR-7600 spectrometer to identify the characteristic Al–O stretching vibrations and to examine the surface functional groups originating from the phytochemical constituents of Coccinia indica leaf extract, which act as reducing and stabilizing agents during nanoparticle formation. Furthermore, the synthesized nicotinonitrile derivatives were structurally characterized by FT-IR spectroscopy (Lambda Scientific, Model FT-IR-7600). Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was performed to determine the specific surface area and porosity of the Al2O3 nanoparticles. Detailed molecular characterization of the synthesized nicotinonitrile derivatives was accomplished by recording 1H and 13C NMR spectra on a Bruker Avance 400 MHz spectrometer using deuterated chloroform (CDCl3) as the solvent.

Experimental

Preparation of leaf extract

Fresh, healthy, and mature leaves of Coccinia indica were collected directly from the natural environment and transported to the laboratory for immediate processing without prolonged storage. The leaves were thoroughly washed with distilled water, finely chopped, and homogenized using a mortar and pestle. About 20 g of the prepared leaves were heated with 100 mL of distilled water at 60–70 °C for 45 min under continuous stirring. After cooling to room temperature, the mixture was filtered through filter paper. The resulting crude aqueous extract was collected and stored at room temperature until further use in the green synthesis of Al2O3 nanoparticles. The extract was used in its crude form, without purification or individual characterization of its phytochemical constituents. A structure-based assessment of the synthetic route indicated a low potential for nitrosamine formation under the applied reaction conditions. Potential process-related impurities were considered based on the reaction pathway; however, individual impurities were not isolated or subjected to detailed toxicological characterization. Moreover, mutagenicity, genotoxicity, and immunotoxicity were not experimentally investigated in the present study.

Preparation of Al2O3 nanoparticles

In a 100 mL round-bottom flask, 10 mL of a 0.05 M aluminium nitrate precursor solution was mixed with a water–ethanol solvent system (1 : 1, v/v). To this solution, 25 mL of Coccinia indica leaf extract was added dropwise under ultrasonic conditions to minimize nanoparticle agglomeration. The aqueous leaf extract of Coccinia indica acts as both a reducing and capping agent during nanoparticle synthesis.34 Subsequently, 25 mL of 1.0 M KOH solution was added slowly to ensure complete precipitation, and the reaction mixture was subjected to ultrasonication for 2 h. The resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation, and the separated nanoparticles were calcined at 500 °C for 6 h. The overall synthesis process is illustrated in Fig. 1. The synthesis of Al2O3 nanoparticles involves a green approach using Coccinia indica leaf extract as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent. Initially, the bioactive phytochemicals present in the leaf extract interact with aluminium precursor ions and promote their reduction under the reaction conditions. The resulting nuclei gradually grow into stable Al2O3 nanoparticles, while the plant-derived metabolites prevent particle agglomeration by acting as capping agents. Continuous ultrasonication and the reaction conditions facilitate nucleation, growth, and crystallization, resulting in the formation of uniformly distributed aluminium oxide nanoparticles.

Mechanism of synthesis of Al2O3 nanoparticle.

Synthesis of nicotinonitrile

In a typical procedure, malononitrile (1.0 mmol), the appropriate aromatic aldehyde (1.0 mmol), acetophenone (1.0 mmol), and ammonium acetate (1.0 mmol) were dissolved in a 1 : 1 (v/v) water–ethanol mixture (10 mL) in a 50 mL round-bottom flask. Al2O3 nanoparticles (5 mol% with respect to malononitrile) were then added as a heterogeneous catalyst. The reaction mixture was subjected to ultrasonication at ambient temperature to improve mass transfer and promote catalytic activity. The progress of the reaction was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC). After completion of the reaction, the Al2O3 nanoparticles were separated by filtration, washed with ethanol, dried, and reused in subsequent catalytic cycles. The filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure, and the resulting crude product was purified by recrystallization from ethanol to obtain the desired nicotinonitrile derivative. The structures of the synthesized compounds were confirmed by FT-IR spectroscopy and further characterized by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the reaction proceeds through a multicomponent sequence involving acetophenone, a substituted aromatic aldehyde, malononitrile, and ammonium acetate to afford 2-amino-4,6-diarylnicotinonitrile derivatives. The Al2O3 nanoparticles facilitate the reaction by providing surface Lewis acidic and basic sites that promote the activation of the reactants. The Lewis acidic sites can interact with the carbonyl groups of the aromatic aldehyde and acetophenone, increasing the electrophilicity of the carbonyl carbon and facilitating the subsequent condensation reactions. At the same time, the basic surface sites assist in the deprotonation of malononitrile, thereby enhancing its nucleophilicity and promoting the formation of the arylidenemalononitrile intermediate.

Plausible mechanism of synthesis of nicotinonitrile.

Initially, acetophenone reacts with ammonia generated from ammonium acetate to form an enamine intermediate (I), with activation of the carbonyl group by the Al2O3 surface facilitating this transformation. Concurrently, the substituted aromatic aldehyde undergoes Knoevenagel condensation with malononitrile to generate the activated arylidenemalononitrile intermediate (II), which is promoted by the basic sites of Al2O3. The enamine intermediate (I) subsequently undergoes a Michael addition to intermediate (II), producing an open-chain intermediate. The adsorption and activation of the reactants on the Al2O3 surface facilitate this nucleophilic addition. The resulting intermediate then undergoes intramolecular cyclization through nucleophilic attack of the amino nitrogen on the cyano group, leading to the formation of a dihydropyridine ring. Finally, proton transfer followed by aromatization produces the stable 2-amino-4,6-diarylnicotinonitrile derivative. Thus, the Lewis acidic and basic sites present on the Al2O3 nanoparticles act cooperatively to activate the substrates and facilitate the key condensation, Michael addition, cyclization, and aromatization steps, resulting in efficient formation of the desired nicotinonitrile derivatives under mild reaction conditions.

Antimicrobial activity

The antibacterial activity of the synthesized nicotinonitrile derivatives was evaluated using the agar well diffusion method. An inoculum containing 106 CFU mL−1 of each bacterial culture was uniformly spread onto nutrient agar plates using a sterile spreader. Subsequently, wells of 6 mm diameter were punched into the agar medium, and each well was filled with 100 µL of the synthesized compounds. The plates were then allowed to stand at room temperature for 1 h to facilitate diffusion of the test compounds into the agar medium. Thereafter, the plates were incubated in an upright position at 37 °C for 24 h. Wells containing the same volume of ethanol served as the negative control, while the standard antibiotic streptomycin was used as the positive control. After incubation, the diameters of the zones of inhibition were measured and recorded in millimetres (mm).

Enzyme preparation

The target protein selected for this study was the bacterial DNA gyrase B enzyme, which plays a vital role in DNA replication and is a well-established antibacterial target. The three-dimensional crystal structure of DNA gyrase B in complex with a 6-fluoro-8-(methylamino)-2-oxo-1,2-dihydroquinoline derivative (PDB ID: 7C7O)34 was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. The selected crystal structure of E. coli DNA gyrase B was determined by X-ray diffraction at a resolution of 1.80 Å. The protein structure was imported into ICM Pro software for pre-processing. Non-essential components, including water molecules, duplicate chains, and co-crystallized solvent molecules (e.g., glycerol), were removed.35 The processed protein structure was then converted into an Internal Coordinate Mechanics (ICM) object, followed by the addition of hydrogen atoms and optimization of amino acid residues, including histidine, glycine, proline, and cysteine. Missing side chains were modelled to maintain the structural integrity of the protein. The co-crystallized ligand was separated from the protein and designated as the reference (native) ligand. The active binding site was defined by selecting a region within a 5 Å radius of the native ligand. A grid box was subsequently generated to encompass the identified binding cavity, thereby facilitating the docking studies. Finally, the reference ligand was extracted from the binding pocket and converted into a two-dimensional (2D) format for comparative docking analysis with the synthesized compounds.

Ligand preparation

In the present study, a set of 11 synthesized compounds was selected to evaluate their inhibitory potential against DNA gyrase (PDB ID: 7C7O) through molecular docking analysis. The reference antibacterial agent, novobiocin, was retrieved from the PubChem database. The chemical structures of all the synthesized compounds were constructed using the LigEdit module of ICM Pro software, followed by energy minimization to obtain stable conformations. The optimized structures were subsequently converted into two-dimensional (2D) representations and organized in tabular form for further analysis.36

Molecular docking study

Molecular docking was performed using the ICM docking module, with the docking parameters set to a thoroughness value of 3 and three conformations generated for each ligand. Options for sampling double-bond geometries, racemic centres, and tautomeric forms were enabled to ensure comprehensive conformational exploration. The resulting docked complexes were subsequently analysed to identify their interaction patterns and were visualized using the ICM Viewer tool. Binding affinity was evaluated based on the ICM scoring function by comparing the synthesized ligands with the reference compound. In addition, key molecular interactions, including hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and hydrophobic contacts between the ligands and the amino acid residues within the target protein, were thoroughly examined.37

In silico physicochemical, drug likeness properties and toxicity assessment

The theoretical physicochemical properties and toxicity profiles of the synthesized derivatives (1d–12d) were evaluated using Molsoft ICM Pro software. The drug-likeness of these compounds was assessed by applying established filters, including Lipinski's rule of five, Muegge's criteria, Ghose's filter, Egan's rule, and Veber's parameters. In addition, key toxicity-related properties, including nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, blood–brain barrier permeability, and LD50 values, were predicted using the same computational platform.38

Results and discussions

Characterization of Al2O3 nanoparticles

XRD

The XRD pattern of the synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles is shown in Fig. 3, recorded over a 2θ range of 10°–80°. The diffraction peaks were indexed using standard reference data (JCPDS No. 00-016-0394), confirming the crystalline nature of the synthesized material. Distinct diffraction peaks were observed at approximately 19–20°, corresponding to the (012) plane; 35–37°, assigned to the (104) plane; 37–38°, attributed to the (110) plane; 45°, corresponding to the (113) plane; 58–60°, indexed to the (116) plane; and 66–68°, corresponding to the (300) plane. These diffraction peaks are in good agreement with the characteristic reflections of aluminium oxide (Al2O3), confirming the successful formation of crystalline alumina nanoparticles. The relatively broad diffraction peaks suggest the nanocrystalline nature of the material, as peak broadening is generally associated with a small crystallite size and possible lattice strain. Furthermore, the absence of additional diffraction peaks indicates the high phase purity of the synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles. Overall, the XRD pattern confirms that the synthesized nanoparticles possess a well-defined crystalline structure consistent with standard alumina.39

XRD of Al2O3 nanoparticles.

SEM

The SEM images of the synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles, shown in Fig. 4, reveal a predominantly agglomerated morphology. The nanoparticles exhibit irregular to quasi-spherical shapes and form compact clusters in the micrometre range due to the aggregation of finer primary nanoparticles. This agglomeration can be attributed to the high surface energy of the Al2O3 nanoparticles and the strong interparticle interactions between them. The rough and uneven surface texture of the aggregates indicates the presence of nanosized crystallites, thereby confirming the nanostructured nature of the synthesized Al2O3 material.

SEM of Al2O3 nanoparticles.

EDS

The EDS spectrum of the synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles, shown in Fig. 5, exhibits prominent characteristic peaks corresponding to aluminium (Al) and oxygen (O), confirming the formation of aluminium oxide. The intense Al Kα and Kβ signals, together with the distinct O Kα peak, indicate the successful incorporation of aluminium and oxygen as the major elemental constituents. Minor signals corresponding to carbon (C) and silicon (Si) are also observed, which can be attributed to surface contamination, carbon coating, or the sample holder/substrate used during SEM-EDS analysis. No additional impurity-related peaks were detected, suggesting the high purity of the synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles.

EDS of Al2O3 nanoparticles.

FT-IR spectra

The FT-IR spectrum shown in Fig. 6 of the synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles confirms the formation of aluminum oxide. In the fingerprint region, the strong absorption bands appearing between 600 cm−1 are characteristic of Al–O and Al–O–Al stretching vibrations, which are the signature features of Al2O3.

IR spectrum of Al2O3 nanoparticles.

BET

The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm of the synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles, shown in Fig. 7, exhibits a typical Type IV isotherm with a distinct hysteresis loop, indicating a predominantly mesoporous structure. The gradual increase in nitrogen adsorption at lower relative pressures, followed by a sharp rise at higher relative pressure (P/P0 > 0.8), is attributed to capillary condensation within the mesopores. The BET-specific surface area was determined to be 72.34 m2 g−1, indicating a relatively high surface area that is favourable for surface-driven processes. The mesoporous nature of the material enhances mass transport and provides a large number of accessible active sites, making the synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles highly suitable for catalytic and adsorption-based applications.

Screening of catalyst

The catalytic methodologies summarized in Table 1 reveal substantial differences in catalytic efficiency and reaction conditions for the synthesis of nicotinonitrile derivatives. Previously reported Al2O3-based catalytic systems generally require considerably high temperatures, typically in the range of 350–420 °C, to obtain good product yields. Such elevated temperatures increase energy consumption and may compromise the overall sustainability of the synthetic process. In contrast, the present study utilizes green-synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles as a recyclable heterogeneous nanocatalyst and achieves the desired transformation under considerably milder conditions. The reaction proceeds efficiently at 65 °C and is completed within only 18 min, affording an excellent yield of 96%. Therefore, the present Al2O3 nanocatalyst offers a substantial advantage over previously reported Al2O3-based catalysts in terms of reaction temperature and reaction time, while providing a high product yield. In addition, the heterogeneous nature of the catalyst enables convenient separation and recovery, thereby enhancing its practical applicability and sustainability. Other catalytic approaches, including hybrid magnetic catalysts, surfactant-assisted aqueous systems, homogeneous acid catalysts, and metal oxide nanoparticles such as CuO and ZnO, have been developed under comparatively milder conditions; however, several of these systems require longer reaction times or present limitations associated with catalyst separation and recovery. Overall, the present green-synthesized Al2O3 nanocatalyst represents an efficient, rapid, recyclable, and energy-saving catalytic system for the sustainable synthesis of nicotinonitrile derivatives.

Choice of solvent

The influence of solvent polarity on the synthesis of nicotinonitrile derivatives was systematically investigated, and the results are summarized in Table 2. Non-polar solvents, such as toluene, resulted in poor yields and prolonged reaction times due to inadequate solvation of the reactants and limited interaction with the heterogeneous catalyst. Moderately polar solvents, including dichloromethane, acetonitrile, and ethyl acetate, showed moderate improvements; however, the reaction efficiency remained suboptimal. Polar protic solvents significantly enhanced both the reaction rate and product yield, with ethanol exhibiting better performance than methanol, likely due to its balanced polarity and greater compatibility with the reaction system. Water alone afforded only a moderate yield, which may be attributed to the limited solubility of the organic substrates. Notably, mixed ethanol–water solvent systems outperformed the individual solvents, demonstrating a synergistic effect. Among these, the ethanol–water (1 : 1, v/v) mixture proved to be the most effective, affording the highest yield (96%) within the shortest reaction time (18 min). This superior performance can be attributed to optimal reactant solvation, enhanced mass transfer, and efficient catalytic activation by the Al2O3 nanocatalyst. Accordingly, the ethanol–water (1 : 1, v/v) mixture was identified as the optimal solvent system for all subsequent reactions.

Table 3 summarizes the applicability of various aromatic aldehydes in the Al2O3 nanoparticle-catalyzed synthesis of nicotinonitrile derivatives using an ethanol–water (1 : 1, v/v) solvent system. Turnover number and turnover frequency

The Al2O3 nanoparticle catalyst demonstrated excellent catalytic efficiency in the synthesis of nicotinonitrile derivatives, as quantitatively evaluated by the turnover number (TON) and turnover frequency (TOF) values summarized in Table 4. The TON values ranged from 17 129 to 19 576, indicating that the Al2O3 catalyst is capable of promoting a large number of catalytic cycles without noticeable deactivation under the applied reaction conditions. Similarly, the TOF values ranged from 27 189 h−1 to 65 255 h−1, reflecting the rapid catalytic turnover and high reaction rates achieved during the transformation. Among the synthesized derivatives, compound 2d exhibited the highest catalytic activity, affording the maximum TON (19 576) and TOF (65 255 h−1) values (Table 4). The enhanced catalytic performance observed for this substrate may be attributed to favourable electronic effects and efficient interaction between the substrate and the active surface sites of the Al2O3 nanocatalyst, thereby facilitating effective activation of the reactants and smooth progression of the reaction pathway. The graphical representation of the TON and TOF values is shown in Fig. 8. Overall, the consistently high TON and TOF values obtained for a wide range of nicotinonitrile derivatives highlight the superior catalytic performance of the Al2O3 nanoparticles. The heterogeneous nature, operational simplicity, excellent catalytic activity, and high catalytic turnover of this system further confirm its potential as a robust, reusable, and environmentally benign catalyst for multicomponent organic syntheses in accordance with the principles of green chemistry.48

Antimicrobial activity of Al2O3 nanoparticles

The antimicrobial efficacy of the synthesized nicotinonitrile derivatives was evaluated using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as the negative control.49–51 Streptomycin was used as the standard reference antibiotic. The antibacterial activity of the synthesized compounds against S. aureus (ATCC 259), B. subtilis (ATCC 19659), P. aeruginosa (ATCC 15442), and E. coli (ATCC 16404) was assessed using the agar well diffusion method, and the results are presented in Table 5. The synthesized derivatives exhibited clear zones of inhibition, indicating their effectiveness in suppressing the growth of all the tested bacterial pathogens. The results suggest that the synthesized nicotinonitrile derivatives possess promising antimicrobial activity compared with the standard antibiotic, as well as with previously reported nanomaterials and plant-based extracts (Fig. 9 and 10). The experimental data are expressed as the mean ± standard error (SE) of three independent replicates.52

Recyclability

The recyclability of the Al2O3 nanoparticle catalyst was systematically evaluated to assess its catalytic stability and reusability during the synthesis of nicotinonitrile derivatives. After the completion of each reaction cycle, the catalyst was separated by simple filtration, thoroughly washed with ethanol and distilled water to remove any adsorbed organic residues, and dried before reuse. The recovered Al2O3 nanoparticles were subsequently employed in successive reaction cycles under identical optimized conditions. The catalyst maintained high catalytic efficiency over five consecutive cycles, with only a slight decline in product yield observed after repeated use, as shown in Fig. 16. This minimal loss in catalytic activity indicates that the active surface sites and structural framework of the Al2O3 nanoparticles remained largely intact throughout the recycling process. The observed durability further confirms the heterogeneous nature and operational robustness of the catalyst. Overall, the excellent reusability over multiple catalytic cycles, together with its simple recovery, underscores the potential of Al2O3 nanoparticles as a stable, cost-effective, and environmentally benign catalyst for the sustainable synthesis of nicotinonitrile derivatives.53

Conclusion

In conclusion, the green-synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles exhibited effective catalytic activity and good recyclability for the synthesis of nicotinonitrile derivatives under mild and environmentally favourable conditions. The developed protocol provided high product yields, short reaction times, simple operation, and convenient catalyst recovery and reuse. The synthesized derivatives also demonstrated promising antimicrobial activity, while molecular docking studies indicated favourable interactions with the DNA gyrase B enzyme. The predicted drug-likeness and toxicity profiles further supported the potential biological relevance of these compounds. However, the present investigation has some limitations. The antimicrobial assessment was performed against a limited selection of microbial strains, and the molecular docking and ADMET results are computational predictions that require experimental verification. Furthermore, the catalytic methodology was investigated using a selected range of substrates, and its applicability to a broader substrate scope remains to be established. Future investigations will therefore focus on expanding the substrate scope, elucidating the catalytic mechanism in greater detail, and validating the biological activity of the synthesized compounds through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies. Further evaluation of catalyst performance on a larger scale, extended recycling studies, and process optimization would also be valuable for assessing its practical and industrial potential. Overall, this study demonstrates the potential of green-synthesized Al2O3 nanoparticles as a sustainable heterogeneous catalyst for the preparation of potentially bioactive nicotinonitrile derivatives.
