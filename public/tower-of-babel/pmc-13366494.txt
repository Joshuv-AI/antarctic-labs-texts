Active-site recognition enables hydroxylation of a fluorine-deactivated C–H bond in 6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine
Authors: Marwa Ismail, Ayman Fakhry, Wahid Hamaway, Eman M. Elkafoury, Numrah Nisar, Tom Swift, Richard Telford, Refaat B. Hamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enzymatic C–H hydroxylation of fluorinated amino acids is challenging. The Fe(ii)/2-oxoglutarate oxygenases EcdK and SBQ2 hydroxylate the CF3-adjacent C–H bond of 6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine, with EcdK enabling preparative, (5R)-selective product formation; comparison with 5,5,5-trifluoro-l-norvaline and L92A/F209A/F291A mutagenesis shows that active-site positioning, not bond strength alone, controls reactivity.

Fluorinated amino acids occupy a privileged region of non-canonical amino-acid chemical space because fluorine can strongly influence hydrophobicity, conformational preference, acidity/basicity and metabolic stability while often preserving a close steric relationship to hydrogen.1–3 Hydroxylated amino acids are likewise valuable motifs in synthesis, chemical biology and biosynthesis, where they serve as versatile chiral building blocks and precursors to further functionalisation.4–6 By contrast, amino acids that combine both features remain much less accessible, particularly when the hydroxyl group must be introduced by direct oxidation of a C(sp3)–H bond adjacent to a trifluoromethyl group. Such a transformation presents a substantial catalytic challenge. Model bond-dissociation data show that replacing β-CH3 with β-CF3 can strengthen an adjacent alkyl C–H bond by ∼6 kcal mol−1, and β-fluorination is widely used in medicinal chemistry to modulate oxidative metabolism through combined effects on C–H activation, substrate recognition and physicochemical properties.7 Thus, hydroxylation next to CF3 places unusually stringent demands on both intrinsic C–H reactivity and active-site positioning.8–12 The value of this target motif is underscored by prior chemical syntheses of δ-(polyfluoroalkyl)-δ-hydroxy-α-amino acids, including 2-amino-6,6,6-trifluoro-5-hydroxyhexanoic acid, which have been described as analogues of 2-amino-5-hydroxyvaleric acid and glutamic acid.13 However, these routes rely on multistep chemical synthesis from fluorinated pyrone precursors, involving hydrogenation, ring opening and hydrolysis, and provide CF3-containing products as diastereomeric mixtures or racemic material rather than through direct enantioselective C–H oxidation of an l-amino-acid precursor.13 Fluorinated substituents can also reshape enzymatic C–H oxidation in other oxygenase systems; for example, engineered P450 BM3 variants oxidise fluorinated n-octanes with fluorine-dependent changes in regioselectivity, stereoselectivity and coupling efficiency, accompanied by proposed interactions between C–F/CF2 groups and aromatic π systems.14 These studies provide useful precedent for fluorine-controlled substrate positioning, but do not establish direct hydroxylation of the C–H bond adjacent to CF3 within an amino-acid scaffold. We therefore reasoned that a phylogenetically diverse panel of Fe(ii)/2-oxoglutarate-dependent leucine oxygenases might reveal whether this challenging reactivity could be accessed in a non-haem oxygenase setting.

To test this hypothesis, we assembled seven known or putative leucine oxygenases spanning fungal, cyanobacterial and actinobacterial origins: EcdK (Aspergillus rugulosus),15 SBQ2 (Aspergillus mulundensis), and SLP6 (Hyphodiscus hymeniophilus); LH (Nostoc sp. LEGE 12447) and LxaO (Nostoc sp. UHCC 0702);16 and AdeA (Streptomyces hawaiiensis)17 and MypE (Actinoplanes awajinensis).18 The panel was selected to sample enzyme diversity while preserving a shared expectation of activity on hydrophobic amino-acid substrates (partial sequence alignment in Fig. 1A; protein production and purification in Fig. S1). Because 6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine represents a stringent extension of norleucine side-chain oxidation, we first evaluated related non-fluorinated substrates: (S)-2-aminobutyric acid, l-norvaline and l-norleucine. Across this comparator series, the enzymes showed non-uniform activity, confirming that leucine oxygenases can accommodate unbranched hydrophobic amino acids but differ markedly in oxidation register and oxidation depth. Five enzymes were confirmed as l-leucine hydroxylases, giving predominantly (4R)-5-hydroxy-l-leucine in the cases of EcdK, MypE, AdeA and SBQ2, and predominantly (4S)-5-hydroxy-l-leucine in the case of LH (Fig. S2–S4). No turnover was detected for (S)-2-aminobutyric acid. On l-norvaline, four enzymes showed substantial turnover: EcdK (94%), AdeA (∼100%), MypE (∼100%) and SBQ2 (90%) (Table S13). We characterised the EcdK, AdeA and MypE products in detail to define the oxidation registers represented within the panel. EcdK produced a single detectable product assigned as (4R)-4-hydroxy-l-norvaline (Fig. 2A and Fig. S5–S7), consistent with γ-hydroxylation and high stereoselectivity and, to the best of our knowledge, representing the only reported single-step synthetic access to this isomer.19 By contrast, AdeA and MypE oxidised l-norvaline at the terminal position, consistent with C5 hydroxylation, and therefore operated on the shortest competent linear side chain examined here (Fig. S5, S8 and S9).20 This regiodivergence implies distinct substrate-placement solutions relative to the Fe(iv)O intermediate, with EcdK targeting C4 (Fig. 1B) whereas AdeA and MypE favour C5 (Fig. S9). Extending the side chain by one methylene to l-norleucine led to convergence in oxidation site but divergence in oxidation depth. Five enzymes showed near-complete substrate consumption and oxidised predominantly at C5: EcdK, SBQ2, AdeA and MypE gave mainly (5S)-5-hydroxy-l-norleucine,20 with minor formation of the (5R)-isomer, whereas LH showed the opposite stereochemical bias and favoured the (5R)-isomer21 (Fig. 2B and Fig. S5, S10, S11). EcdK, and to a lesser extent LH and SBQ2, additionally generated the corresponding C5 ketone (Fig. 2B and Fig. S10, S12), consistent with iterative oxidation following initial alcohol formation, whereas AdeA and MypE remained largely mono-oxidative under the same conditions. Intramolecular reductive amination of the crude EcdK reaction mixture with NaBH3CN at approximately pH 6 furnished the corresponding 5-methyl-l-prolines (Fig. 2B and Fig. S13–S15), confirming C5 ketone formation and showing that EcdK can access oxidation states beyond simple hydroxylation on hydrocarbon substrates.

With this baseline established, we examined 6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine as a stringent test of whether a leucine oxygenase could engage a fluorine-deactivated methylene. This substrate retains the side-chain length and amino-acid framework of l-norleucine but replaces the terminal methyl group with CF3, introducing a strong electronic perturbation at the distal end of the substrate and raising the target C5 C–H bond dissociation free energy (BDFE), as calculated using ORCA22 (Fig. 2D and Fig. S25, Tables S14, S15). Under standard Fe(ii)/2-oxoglutarate oxygenase assay conditions, productive turnover was observed only with the closely related fungal enzymes EcdK (∼95% conversion) and SBQ2 (∼68% conversion), whereas the remaining panel members gave no detectable product (Fig. 2C and Fig. S21, Table S13). Consistent with the electronic penalty inferred from the BDFE calculations, a time-limited comparison using EcdK (3 h, 100 : 1 substrate:enzyme) showed complete consumption of l-norleucine but only ∼40% conversion of 6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine under otherwise identical conditions. Thus, the fluorinated analogue is turned over more slowly, and its hydroxylation is not a general property of leucine oxygenases despite the competence of several panel members on corresponding hydrocarbon scaffolds. Instead, this reactivity maps to a restricted enzyme subspace represented by EcdK and SBQ2. Consistent with this enzyme-specific outcome, modelling of 6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine in AdeA and MypE places the target C5 hydrogens farther from the iron centre and/or in less favourable orientations for Fe(iv)O-mediated abstraction (Fig. S24).

EcdK showed the superior production and activity profile and was therefore selected for preparative-scale biotransformation. Product analysis established oxidation at C5, directly adjacent to the trifluoromethyl terminus, to give 6,6,6-trifluoro-5-hydroxy-l-norleucine (Fig. 2C and Fig. S20, S21–S23). Preparative-scale oxidation afforded two C5 diastereomers, consistent with installation of a new stereogenic centre at the site of oxidation. Structural analysis, supported by active-site modelling of the productive EcdK binding pose, enabled assignment of the major product as the (5R)-isomer, with the minor product possessing the alternative C5 configuration (Fig. 1C and Fig. S20, S22, S23).

This change in descriptor relative to the major l-norleucine-derived product does not indicate inversion of the stereochemical course of hydroxylation; rather, it arises from the change in Cahn–Ingold–Prelog priority order caused by replacing the terminal methyl group with CF3. In spatial terms, the major fluorinated and non-fluorinated products arise from oxidation of the same prochiral face, as supported by the optimised three-dimensional configurations, with the (5R)/(5S) descriptor difference reflecting only the altered CIP priority order.

To distinguish the effect of CF3-induced C–H deactivation from chain-length-dependent substrate recognition, we next examined the shorter fluorinated analogue 5,5,5-trifluoro-l-norvaline. Under the same analytical assay conditions, EcdK and SBQ2 converted this substrate only poorly, giving approximately 16% and 14% conversion, respectively (Fig. 2C and Fig. S16, Table S13). EcdK was selected for product isolation and stereochemical assignment. Scale-up, purification and NMR analysis assigned the product as 5,5,5-trifluoro-4-hydroxy-l-norvaline (Fig. S16–S20). Proton-coupled 19F NMR analysis confirmed formation of two diastereomers, with a major (4S)-diastereomer and a minor (4R)-diastereomer in an approximately 82 : 18 ratio (Fig. S20). Although this major product carries the formal (4S) descriptor, whereas the major fluorinated norleucine product is assigned as (5R), the two products arise from hydroxylation at the same substrate face; the descriptor change reflects altered CIP priority order rather than a reversal of EcdK facial selectivity. Thus, EcdK preserves its underlying facial selectivity across the fluorinated norvaline and norleucine substrates, but the shorter fluorinated substrate is processed much less efficiently. The matched norvaline comparison is particularly informative alongside the calculated C–H BDFEs. Replacing the terminal methyl group with CF3 increases the calculated target C–H BDFE in both substrate pairs, but the magnitude of this penalty does not track with conversion. The calculated increase is comparable for the l-norvaline/5,5,5-trifluoro-l-norvaline pair (∼14.0 kJ mol−1) and the l-norleucine/6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine pair (∼13.9 kJ mol−1), yet the shorter fluorinated substrate is converted only poorly while 6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine reaches high conversion (Fig. 2D and Table S15). Intrinsic C–H bond strengthening alone therefore cannot account for the observed reactivity; rather, these paired comparisons show that CF3-adjacent hydroxylation reflects the interplay between electronic deactivation and productive active-site recognition.

To probe the structural basis for this behaviour, EcdK was examined by active-site modelling. The model suggests that the CF3 group of 6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine is accommodated in a small hydrophobic pocket defined mainly by L92 and F209, with a possible contribution from V331 (Fig. 1C). This is consistent with reports that fluorinated alkyl groups can tune enzymatic C–H oxidation selectivity through interactions with hydrophobic/aromatic active-site environments, and that CF3 groups can form weak but meaningful noncovalent contacts within protein–ligand complexes,14,26 although those systems did not address Fe(ii)/2-OG oxygenase-catalysed hydroxylation of trifluoromethylated amino acids. To test the model experimentally, the pocket-forming residues L92 and F209, together with F291, were individually substituted with alanine. All three variants retained measurable activity with l-norleucine: L92A, F209A and F291A showed ∼50%, ∼40% and ∼20% of wild-type activity, respectively, by LC-MS analysis. However, their product profiles showed a higher relative proportion of the C5 ketone than wild-type EcdK, suggesting that perturbation of these residues alters the balance between mono-hydroxylation, product release and iterative oxidation. In contrast to their retained activity on l-norleucine, the variants were severely compromised in turnover of 6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine: L92A retained only ∼9% of wild-type activity, whereas F209A and F291A gave no detectable product under the assay conditions (Fig. 2C and Fig. S21, Table S13). These data point to a genuine structure–function relationship underlying the fluorinated transformation. The effects of L92A and F209A are consistent with their proposed role in shaping the hydrophobic pocket that accommodates the CF3 group, whereas the loss of fluorinated-substrate activity in F291A suggests that this residue is also important for productive substrate positioning or catalytic alignment. Together with the fluorinated norvaline comparison, these mutational data indicate that EcdK retains a conserved facial course of hydroxylation across related chain lengths, while productive turnover of CF3-containing substrates is highly sensitive to active-site fit.

Taken together, these results show that linear hydrophobic amino-acid oxidation is broadly embedded within the leucine oxygenase family, but that hydroxylation adjacent to CF3 imposes a much sharper selectivity boundary. EcdK is notable in combining robust activity on l-norvaline and l-norleucine with the capacity for both hydroxylation and overoxidation, yet productive oxidation of 6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine remains restricted to EcdK and SBQ2. From a synthetic perspective, EcdK provides direct biocatalytic access to C5-hydroxylated 6,6,6-trifluoro-l-norleucine without pre-oxidised starting materials or prefunctionalised cyclic precursors, and with clear diastereomeric bias. From a mechanistic perspective, the conserved facial course of hydroxylation, the contrasting conversions of the two fluorinated substrates despite comparable calculated C–H bond-strengthening penalties, and the fluorinated-substrate-specific mutational effects together point to a defined substrate-positioning requirement for oxidation of a fluorine-deactivated aliphatic C–H bond. These results establish the feasibility of Fe(ii)/2-OG oxygenase-catalysed fluorine-deactivated C–H oxidation and show how active-site recognition can overcome electronic deactivation in a challenging transformation.
