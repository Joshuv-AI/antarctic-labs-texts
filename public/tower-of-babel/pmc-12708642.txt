Target configuration determines how and what we learn during sensorimotor adaptation
Authors: Pamela Villavicencio, Jonathan S. Tsay, Cristina de la Malla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Motor adaptation is essential for keeping our actions well-calibrated. However, the role of training context—specifically, the configuration of targets—in shaping motor adaptation remains poorly understood. We tested this by exposing participants to a visuomotor gain perturbation under two contexts: The Extent Group, which trained with targets at two amplitudes in a fixed angular direction, and the Angular Group, which trained with targets at equal amplitude in two angular directions. Strikingly, the groups differed in how they learned: the Angular Group relied predominantly on implicit adaptation, whereas the Extent Group relied more on explicit strategies. Additionally, the two groups differed in what they learned: the Angular Group acquired a translation rule, whereas the Extent Group captured the true gain rule. These findings underscore that training context determines both the processes engaged and the representations formed, underscoring its importance in shaping both how and what we learn.

Introduction

Motor adaptation—the process of correcting movement errors through feedback and practice—is a fundamental human capacity that keeps our actions well-calibrated amid changes in the environment and the body1–3. For example, when switching to a new computer mouse with a different gain sensitivity (i.e., the mapping between hand movement and cursor displacement), the same hand movements may produce larger or smaller cursor displacements. Yet, within a short amount of time, we can rapidly adapt to restore precise control over the cursor.

Motor adaptation is known to rely on implicit and explicit learning processes. Explicit strategies enable deliberate, flexible motor corrections, while implicit learning keeps the motor system finely tuned through unconscious, automatic adjustments4–18. A defining feature of explicit strategy is its flexibility: it can be volitionally deployed or withdrawn in response to task instructions. In contrast, the hallmark of implicit learning is the presence of aftereffects—residual movement deviations that persist once the perturbation is removed, even when participants are told to abandon any strategies.

A growing body of research has characterized the distinct properties of implicit and explicit learning. For example, explicit strategies scale with different perturbation sizes, whereas implicit learning is rigid and largely insensitive to the size of the perturbation. Moreover, explicit strategies generalize broadly to new contexts, such as different target locations19,20. By contrast, implicit learning is again markedly rigid, generalizing only narrowly around the trained target location9,21–24.

Despite these well-characterized properties, it remains unknown how training context—specifically, the configuration of targets—influences the balance between implicit and explicit processes. This gap is surprising, given that motor adaptation is inherently goal-directed, and that target arrangement should critically shape the motor errors encountered, the explicit strategies considered, and ultimately the degree to which learning generalizes. While one study using standard adaptation tasks has shown that target configuration can modulate learning25, it did not disentangle the contributions of implicit and explicit processes. Target configuration may have no effect, with both systems contributing similarly across layouts, or certain configurations may preferentially recruit one system over the other.

To address this question, we conducted two reaching experiments that separated implicit from explicit contributions to motor adaptation. Specifically, participants experienced a visuomotor gain perturbation, with feedback scaled to 1.3× (Exp 1) or 0.7× (Exp 2) the actual movement amplitude (Fig. 1a). Visuomotor gain perturbations elicit strong adaptation and broad generalization across the workspace, providing the sensitivity needed to test whether target configuration modulates adaptation26–30. Participants were assigned to either the Extent Group, which trained with targets arranged at two amplitudes in a single angular direction, or the Angular Group, which trained with targets arranged at the same amplitude in two angular directions (Fig. 1b). Additionally, we included no-feedback reaches to novel untrained target locations, instructing participants to either engage (“continue your re-aiming strategy”) or disengage their explicit re-aiming strategies (“reach directly to the target without re-aiming your hand away”). This instructional manipulation allowed us to dissociate implicit from explicit adaptation and examine how each process is shaped by target configuration—revealing which system supports generalization across novel extents and directions. Together, this work deepens our understanding into how training context—specifically, the configuration of targets—determines not only how we learn, but also what we learn during motor adaptation.

Results

Experiment 1: Extent training led to less implicit adaptation and more explicit re-aiming compared to Angular training

We asked whether the spatial layout of training targets influences the relative engagement of implicit and explicit learning processes. Two groups of participants were exposed to the same visuomotor gain perturbation, in which the cursor was scaled to 1.3× the amplitude of their hand movements; that is, a 1 cm movement would result in a 1.3 cm movement of the cursor.

The groups trained with distinct target configurations: In the Extent group, participants trained with two targets along the same direction but at different amplitudes. In the Angular group, participants trained with two targets at the same amplitude but in different directions. Both groups shared one training target, positioned 11 cm from the home location, enabling a direct comparison of adaptation at an identical site. To adapt successfully, participants must decrease the amplitude of their movements to land the perturbed cursor on the target.

At the shared target, both groups rapidly adapted to the gain perturbation, reducing their movement amplitude within a few trials to bring the perturbed cursor onto the target (Fig. 2a). Adaptation was evident in both groups, reflected in a main effect of phase (F(3,120) = 134.89, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.77); post-hoc within-subjects t-tests revealed a significant difference between baseline and late adaptation (t(120) = 17.70, p < 0.0001, Dz = 3.86). However, neither group fully adapted, as reaches did not decrease enough to bring the cursor to the target (Fig. 2a; dashed line = full adaptation). Participants then entered a generalization block, reaching not only to the trained targets but also to novel, untrained targets distributed around the workspace. Adaptation at the trained target location was sustained throughout the generalization phase (baseline vs late generalization, within-subjects: t(120) = 16.77, p < 0.0001, Dz = 3.66). We found a slight increase in reach amplitude between the late adaptation and the late generalization blocks; however, this change was negligible, accounting for only 0.28 cm (13% of total learning) in the Extent group and 0.04 cm (2% of total learning) in the Angular group. To isolate implicit learning, participants were instructed to stop re-aiming and simply reach to the original 11 cm target, thereby disengaging any explicit strategies. Both groups showed robust implicit aftereffects, evidenced by sustained reductions in reach amplitude relative to baseline (Fig. 2a) a hallmark of implicit adaptation (baseline vs aftereffect, within-subjects: t(120) = 13.77, p < 0.0001, Dz = 3.01). Notably, implicit learning accounted for the majority of total adaptation in both groups: 65% in the Extent Group and 92% in the Angular Group.

There was also a marked change in reach amplitude from late generalization to aftereffect (t(120) = −3.00, p = 0.017, Dz = −0.65). This suggests that a substantial component of adaptation arose from explicit re-aiming—an adjustment that can be volitionally engaged or disengaged with instructions (Fig. 2b). Correspondingly, explicit re-aiming accounted for 35% of total learning in the Extent Group and 8% in the Angular Group.

Given that both training groups engaged implicit and explicit learning processes, we turned to our central question: Does training context differentially engage these processes? The shared target model revealed a marked interaction between Group and Phase (F(3,120) = 5.36, p = 0.0017, η2 = 0.12). First, both groups exhibited similar levels of adaptation (Extent vs Angular, between-subjects, late adaptation: t(119) = −1.46, p = 0.148, D = −0.55; late generalization: t(119) = 0.02, p = 0.98, D = 0.009). In other words, both target configurations were equally effective in driving adaptation.

Second, the Extent group exhibited reduced implicit learning (aftereffects) compared to the Angular group (Fig. 2b; Extent vs Angular, between-subjects, aftereffect: t(119) = 3.02, p = 0.003, D = 1.15). Correspondingly, the Extent group relied more on explicit strategies (Extent vs Angular, between-subjects: t(120) = 2.61, p = 0.0103, D = −0.74). In other words, the Extent group exhibited a marked drop in performance from late generalization to the aftereffect phase (Fig. 2b; Extent group, within-subjects: t(120) = −3.96, p = 0.0031, Dz = −1.22), but in contrast, this drop was absent in the Angular group (Angular group, within-subjects: t(120) = -0.28, p = 1.00, Dz = -0.09).

Together, Extent training elicited less implicit adaptation and more explicit re-aiming compared to Angular training. Indeed, the configuration of training targets determined how people learned.

Extent training promotes generalization to new amplitudes, whereas Angular training promotes generalization to new directions

We then asked whether training context influenced what people learned—that is, how the participants represented the perturbation. To test this, we fit a linear-mixed model to examine how learning generalized to novel target locations that differed in either extent (9 cm) or angular distance (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°) from the shared training target (0°).

We first examined late generalization (no feedback) to novel angular directions (45°, 90°, 135°) at a constant extent (11 cm). Consistent with prior work, both total adaptation (Fig. 2c) and implicit adaptation (Fig. 2d) declined with increasing angular distance from the trained target, whereas explicit strategy remained largely constant (Fig. 2e). Critically, the Angular group generalized more broadly than the Extent group, that is, their reaches stayed closer to the dashed line across all novel directions (Fig. 2c; between-subjects: t(69.4) = 0.95, p = 0.34, D = −0.27).

In contrast, we observed the opposite pattern for generalization to a novel extent (9 cm) at the trained direction (0°): the Extent group generalized more effectively than the Angular group. The Extent group accurately scaled their movements to the new target, whereas the Angular group overshot by 0.9 cm (Fig. 3a, b; between-subjects: t(223) = −1.88, p = 0.06, D = −0.74). Extent training reflected a gain-based representation, whereas Angular training did not

The Angular group’s poor generalization to novel extents (9 cm) prompted us to ask why. Did both groups represent the perturbation in the same way, or did they form distinct solutions? While not exhaustive, we identified three core ways participants may represent the sensorimotor solution: gain-based, translation-based, or location-based (Figs. 1a, 3a). At the untrained 9 cm extent, the predicted reach amplitude was 6.9 cm for a gain-based solution, 6.5 cm for a translation-based solution, and 8.5 cm for a location-based solution.

Strikingly, the two groups represented the perturbation differently, as indicated by a significant interaction between group and solution (solution-based model: F(3,131) = 9.55, p = 0.0000095, η2 = 0.18). The Extent group seemed to represent the perturbation veridically as a gain: participants’ reaches closely matched the gain solution (Extent group, within-subjects, observed vs gain solution: t(120) = 1.11, p = 0.95, Dz = 0.34), but exhibited significant deviations from the alternatives (Extent group, within-subject, observed vs translation: t(120) = 4.94, p = 0.0001, Dz = 1.53; observed vs location: t(120) = -11.73, p < 0.0001, Dz = −3.62). These results are shown in Fig. 3b, where the Extent group´s combined learning approximates the dashed line, and in Fig. 3c, which plots each group’s observed error relative to the expected error for the three candidate solutions. Performance aligning more closely with a given solution falls nearer to zero (dashed line).

In contrast, the Angular group showed no clear evidence of adopting a distinct representation among those considered here. Specifically, as a group, their performance deviated significantly from all predicted patterns (Angular group, within-subjects, observed vs. gain: t(120) = 7.39, p < 0.0001, Dz = 2.25; observed vs. translation: t(120) = 11.12, p < 0.0001, Dz = 3.43; observed vs. location: t(120) = −5.55, p < 0.0001, Dz = −1.71). We also examined the distribution of individual data to assess whether different participants adopted different solutions that could have obscured clear patterns at the group level. This was not the case: in both groups, Gaussian mixture modeling favored a single-cluster solution over multi-cluster alternatives (Extent: 1-cluster BIC = –203.22 vs. 2-cluster BIC = −208.17; Angular: 1-cluster BIC = –204.68 vs. 2-cluster BIC = -211.44), with smaller BIC values indicating better fit. Indeed, the difficulty of executing small-amplitude movements, combined with the similarity between gain- and translation-based solutions, likely obscured what the Angular Group was learning—prompting us to design Experiment 2 to address these limitations.

In summary, training context—specifically the spatial configuration of training targets—determines both how people learn and what they learn: Extent training relied more on explicit strategies and appeared to represent the perturbation as a gain. In contrast, Angular training engaged more implicit learning and did not use a gain-based representation, though its underlying basis remains unclear.

Experiment 2: Replicated that Extent training produced less implicit adaptation and greater explicit re-aiming than Angular training

We replicated that Extent training produced less implicit adaptation and greater explicit re-aiming than Angular training. In our previous experiment, however, we could not determine how the Angular group represented the perturbation. We suspected this ambiguity arose because the predicted reaches for different solutions—particularly translation- and gain-based—were too close (e.g., at the 9 cm target: translation = 6.46 cm; gain = 6.92 cm), making them difficult to disentangle. This challenge was further compounded by motor noise, which is especially pronounced for small movements, since scaling down to very low amplitudes is inherently difficult to execute precisely at high speeds31–34.

To address these limitations, we designed Experiment 2 with the same task structure as Experiment 1 but applied a 0.7× gain (instead of 1.3×). This required participants to increase rather than decrease their movement amplitude. Critically, this inversion provided a more sensitive test of the underlying representations by widening the separation between predicted outcomes (at the 9 cm target: gain = 12.86 cm; translation = 13.71 cm; location = 15.71 cm). It also mitigated the motor control challenges of executing very small, high-speed movements.

We replicated all key learning patterns from Experiment 1. Specifically, both groups rapidly adapted to the gain perturbation by increasing their movement amplitude within a few trials to bring the cursor to the target (Fig. 2a; shared target model main effect of phase: F(3129) = 318.81, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.88; baseline vs late adaptation within-subjects: t(129) = −25.44, p < 0.0001, Dz = −5.37). Interestingly, unlike in Experiment 1, both groups reached full adaptation (Fig. 2a; reach amplitudes approximating the dashed line). This success, absent in Experiment 1, was likely due to the larger and more easily controlled movements required by the 0.7× gain perturbation.

Moreover, both groups maintained adaptation through the late generalization phase (baseline vs late generalization within-subjects: t(129) = −27.58, p < 0.0001, Dz = −5.82), showed significant aftereffects (baseline vs aftereffect within-subjects: t(129) = −20.79, p < 0.0001, Dz = −4.38), and engaged explicit processes, as indicated by a significant drop from late generalization to aftereffect (t(129) = 6.80, p < 0.0001, Dz = 1.43). Importantly, both groups showed similar levels of total learning during late adaptation (Extent vs Angular between-subjects: t(156) = −1.11, p = 0.2671, D = −0.50) and late generalization (t(156) = -0.66, p = 0.5091, D = -0.30), indicating that both training schedules were equally effective in driving successful performance. We also saw an increase in reach amplitude from late adaptation to late generalization, which we considered negligible because it amounted to 0.4 cm (9% of total learning) in the Extent group and 0.3 cm (6% of total learning) in the Angular group. Again, implicit learning accounted for the majority of total adaptation in both groups: 68% in the Extent Group and 81% in the Angular Group.

Importantly, the groups differed in how they learned: the Extent group showed significantly smaller aftereffects than the Angular group, as seen in Fig. 2a where the Angular group maintained a greater reach amplitude in the aftereffect block compared to the Extent group (Extent vs Angular between-subjects aftereffects: t(156) = −2.63, p = 0.0094, D = −1.18). This indicates that the Extent group experienced less implicit learning than the Angular group. While both groups showed a significant drop in reach amplitude from late generalization to aftereffect (depicted as the magnitude of explicit learning in Fig. 2b; Extent: t(129) = 5.96, p < 0.0001, Dz = 1.76; Angular: t(129) = 3.68, p = 0.008, Dz = 1.11), the Extent group exhibited a trend toward a larger drop between phases (–1.40 cm) than the Angular group (–0.89 cm) (Extent vs Angular: t(129) = −1.54, p = 0.126, D = 0.38). This is also reflected in Fig. 2e, where the Extent group showed a greater contribution of explicit learning. Together, consistent with Experiment 1, Extent training resulted in less implicit adaptation and greater reliance on explicit strategies compared to Angular training.

Replicated that Extent training promotes generalization to new amplitudes, whereas Angular training promotes generalization to new directions

We also replicated the key generalization patterns from Experiment 1. We observed a peak in both total and implicit adaptation at the trained direction, with a marked decline as angular distance increased (Fig. 2c, d). In contrast, explicit adaptation showed broad generalization, remaining stable across angular distances (Fig. 2e).

Importantly, the Extent group accurately scaled their movements to the new target, whereas the Angular group overshot by 0.8 cm (Fig. 3a, b) (Extent vs Angular between-subjects: t(199) = −1.60, p = 0.11, D = −0.80). In contrast, Angular training yielded greater generalization to novel directions (Fig. 2c; Extent vs Angular between-subjects: t(68.6) = −1.55, p = 0.13, D = −0.58). These findings reinforce the principle of training-specific generalization—Extent training promotes transfer to new amplitudes, while Angular training supports transfer to new directions.

Extent training promotes gain-based learning, whereas Angular training promotes translation-based representations

To assess how participants represented the perturbation, we compared their actual reach amplitudes to the predicted amplitudes for each hypothesized solution. At the untrained 9 cm extent, the predicted reach amplitude was 12.9 cm for a gain-based solution, 13.7 cm for a translation-based solution, and 15.7 cm for a location-based solution.

Consistent with Experiment 1, we found that the two groups differed in what they learned (solution-based model interaction between group and solution: F(3,140.7) = 4.95, p = 0.0027, η2 = 0.10). The Extent group seemed to correctly represent the perturbation as a gain. Their mean error was closest to the gain solution (Fig. 3; Extent group within-subjects: t(129) = 0.41, p = 1.00, Dz = 0.12), with significant deviations from translation (t(129) = −4.35, p = 0.0007, Dz = −1.28) and location-based solutions (t(129) = −15.54, p < 0.0001, Dz = −4.58). Strikingly, the Angular Group’s performance clearly aligned with a translation-based solution (Fig. 3c; Angular group within-subjects: t(129) = 0.15, p = 1.00, Dz = 0.05), deviating significantly from gain-based (t(129) = 4.81, p = 0.0001, Dz = 1.45) and location-based solutions (t(129) = −15.54, p < 0.0001, Dz = −3.26). Together, these results demonstrate how target configuration during training can fundamentally influence the nature of what is learned.

Discussion

We asked how the training context—specifically, target configuration—shapes the implicit and explicit processes underlying sensorimotor adaptation. To test this, we exposed two groups of participants to the same visuomotor gain perturbation (Exp. 1: 1.3× gain; Exp. 2: 0.7× gain) under different training configurations: the Extent group trained with two amplitudes at a single direction, whereas the Angular group trained with two directions at a single amplitude. Critically, the groups shared one common training target, allowing direct comparison of learning at an identical site. Participants were then tested at untrained, novel generalization targets without feedback to assess how they represented the perturbation. Finally, we included trials in which participants either engaged or disengaged their re-aiming strategy, enabling us to estimate total performance (explicit + implicit), implicit learning (aftereffects, when re-aiming was disengaged), and explicit strategy use (total – implicit).

We found that training context—specifically, the spatial layout of targets—shapes both how people learn (implicit vs explicit) and what they learn (gain vs translation). This claim is supported by three key findings. First, training across different extents led to reduced implicit adaptation and greater reliance on explicit re-aiming, compared to training across different directions. Second, learning was context-specific: Extent training enhanced generalization to novel amplitudes, while Angular training enhanced generalization to novel directions. Third, the type of training influenced how participants represent the nature of the perturbation. Extent training promoted a gain-based representation, where participants learned that the visual feedback was scaled multiplicatively relative to their hand movement (e.g., reaching 10 cm moves the cursor 7 cm). In contrast, Angular training favored a translation-based representation, where participants learned to apply a fixed additive offset to their reach (e.g., always reach 2 cm beyond the target), regardless of the target amplitude.

Previous studies have shown that training contexts—such as the number of available targets23 or their spatial arrangement25— influence the degree of adaptation in response to a visuomotor rotation. Yet, these studies have not addressed the specific learning processes that drive adaptation to a visuomotor gain. This is relevant as gain-based perturbations are arguably more common than visuomotor rotations in everyday life—for example, when using an unfamiliar computer mouse, switching to a new touchscreen device, or adjusting to changes in a tool’s weight. Our study fills this gap by being the first to disentangle the implicit and explicit contributions during adaptation to a visuomotor gain and to show how these processes are shaped by training context—specifically by the spatial arrangement of targets.

Why might training context bias the engagement of implicit and explicit learning processes? We have several hypotheses: First, spatial layout may specifically modulate the implicit learning system. For example, movements constrained by extent and angle may engage different populations of sensorimotor neurons that may vary in both their capacity and rate of plasticity35,36. Our results suggest that neurons tuned to extent-based movements may adapt more slowly, leading to weaker implicit learning, whereas neurons engaged by angular-based movements adapt more rapidly, supporting greater implicit learning.

Second, spatial layout may specifically modulate explicit learning. An extent-based layout may naturally prime a gain-based rule—one that aligns with the amplitude-deviating structure of the perturbation—which participants can readily adopt to reduce error37,38. If implicit and explicit systems compete to reduce motor errors39,40, the Extent group has less residual error available to drive implicit adaptation, thereby indirectly suppressing it. In contrast, an angular layout—being incongruent with the relevant dimension for gain perturbations—may not prime extent-related rules and may evoke misleading associations (e.g., rotations), making it harder to form an effective explicit strategy.

Lastly, spatial layout may modulate general cognitive resources, which in turn impacts explicit learning. Specifically, Angular training may require greater attentional resources, as participants must simultaneously plan movement extent to compensate for the perturbation and determine direction based on target layout. This dual demand, coupled with increased uncertainty in motor planning, may hinder the discovery of a successful explicit strategy, increasing reliance on implicit learning22,41–45. In contrast, extent training isolates a single relevant dimension—movement amplitude—simplifying both strategy formation and movement execution46,47. Future studies are needed to disentangle these possibilities—whether spatial layout selectively biases one learning system or independently influences both.

Why did the Extent group accurately represent the perturbation, while the Angular group defaulted to a translation-based interpretation? Building on findings from numerical cognition showing that addition is typically easier to compute than multiplication48, we propose that in ambiguous training contexts, participants default to a simpler additive rule—translation—as their action-outcome hypothesis. In contrast, when the context provides clearer cues—as in Extent training, where participants encountered more varied data points along the dimension most diagnostic of a gain—they may override this default and adopt a more complex but accurate multiplicative, gain-based rule. This pattern is reminiscent of schema theory49 (also see ref. 3): learners may hold a repertoire of candidate schemas (e.g., hypotheses for the nature of the perturbation) ranging from simple (translation) to complex (gain) and default to simpler ones unless the training data compel adoption of a more complex representation.

Methods

Participants

42 unique participants took part in Experiment 1 (mean age = 21 years) and 47 in Experiment 2 (mean age = 26 years). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no known motor impairments, and were predominantly right-handed (right handed = 77 participants), as assessed by the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory50. Participants signed an informed consent prior to the start of the experiment and were compensated with course credit. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of two groups (Extent or Angular, see Experimental Design). This study was part of an ongoing research project approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Barcelona (IRB: 00003099).

Apparatus

Participants were seated in a dark room in front of a graphic tablet (Calcomp Drawing Board III 24240, 61 × 46 cm) that recorded the position of a hand-held stylus at 145 Hz. Stimuli were displayed at a frame rate of 75 Hz and a resolution of 1024 × 768 pixels onto a back-projection screen that was mounted parallel to and above the tablet. A half-silvered mirror was placed horizontally between the screen and the tablet, reflecting the stimuli while occluding the participant’s view of their hand. The mirror created the perception that both the hand and stimuli occupied the same plane on the tablet surface. LED lights underneath the mirror were off to occlude vision of the arm. A computer controlled the presentation of the stimuli and registered the position of the stylus. The experiment was programmed using custom code in Python.

General procedure

Participants performed quick center-out reaching movements from a gray home position (1 cm diameter) to several possible blue targets (1 cm diameter). To begin each trial, participants placed a white cursor (0.6 cm diameter), which was aligned with the stylus, on the home position located at the center of the tablet. After remaining on the home position for a random interval between 0.8 and 1 second, the home position disappeared, and a target appeared. The target was displayed for 1 second, and participants were instructed to make a quick, ballistic reaching movement and complete their reach before the target disappeared. We did not impose any constraints on movement initiation time, allowing participants to freely start their movements without restricting the use of explicit strategies6,51. To discourage online corrections, participants saw a “too slow” message for 0.75 seconds if movement time exceeded 0.5 seconds. Following a 0.3-second delay after the target vanished, the home position reappeared, signaling participants to return their hand to the center of the workspace.

Feedback during the reach was presented in one of three forms: veridical, perturbed, or absent. When feedback was present, it was in the form of a white cursor. With veridical feedback, the cursor´s position was aligned with the stylus. With perturbed feedback, the cursor´s radial displacement relative to the stylus was scaled by a visuomotor gain of either 1.3× (Exp 1) or 0.7× (Exp 2). This meant that with a 10 cm reach, participants saw the cursor end at 13 cm (Exp 1) or 7 cm (Exp 2), requiring them to shorten or extend their reach, respectively, to hit the target (Fig. 1a). The gain was applied from the stylus start position as soon as movement was initiated. When the feedback was absent, participants did not receive any visual feedback during their reach. Participants received no visual feedback as they moved back to the home position, and only received veridical feedback when they were within 1 cm of the home position. This allowed participants to reposition the stylus and initiate the next trial.

Experimental design

Participants in both experiments were assigned to one of two groups: Extent or Angular (Fig. 1b) that trained with different target layouts. For reference, 0° is defined as the direction pointing to the right from the home position. The Extent group trained with two targets at different amplitudes (7 and 11 cm) and at a single angular location, counterbalanced across participants to lie either along the cardinal (90°) or diagonal (315°) axis to control for reach biases52. The Angular group trained with two targets at different angular directions (counterbalanced across participants: cardinal, 90° and 180°; diagonal, 45° and 315°) while maintaining a fixed amplitude of 11 cm.

Note that both Extent and Angular groups trained with visual feedback on two 11 cm training targets, one of which was shared. Additionally, both groups were tested without visual feedback on a common set of 10 generalization targets, consisting of the shared training target and nine novel, untrained locations. Generalization targets were located at angular distances of 0°, 45°, 90°, 135° relative to the shared training target, at amplitudes of either 9 cm or 11 cm (Fig. 1b).

Both experiments 1 and 2 consisted of four blocks (Fig. 1c: 432 total trials; 25 minutes). baseline, adaptation, generalization, and aftereffect. Baseline consisted of 44 trials with veridical feedback to the generalization and training targets (11 total targets). Adaptation consisted of 40 trials with perturbed feedback (Exp 1: 1.3× gain; Exp 2: 0.7× gain) to the two training targets. The generalization block comprised 304 trials: 100 no-feedback trials to the generalization targets and 204 perturbed-feedback trials to training targets. Each of the 10 generalization targets was presented 10 times without feedback and randomly to minimize serial-dependence and reverse-transfer effects53–55. Each no-feedback trial was preceded by two perturbed-feedback trials to the training targets to prevent decay of learning. The aftereffect block comprised 44 no-feedback trials to all 11 targets, with instructions to cease any re-aiming and reach directly to the target—an instructional manipulation designed to isolate implicit learning. Full instructions preceding each block are detailed in Fig. 1c.

Data analysis

All data and statistical analyses were performed in R. The primary dependent variable in our analyses was the reach amplitude, defined as the straight-line distance from the stylus start position (when the target was presented) to the stylus position at the end of the reach. Target amplitude was defined as the target’s radial distance from the home position. Amplitude error was defined as the difference between the participant’s reach amplitude and the target amplitude. To account for individual biases, we baseline-corrected each participant’s reach amplitude by subtracting their mean amplitude error from the second half of baseline trials for each target. Euclidean error—the distance between the target position and the stylus endpoint at movement termination—was used as a measure of overall endpoint accuracy.

We excluded trials under the following conditions: (1) the stylus lost contact with the tablet (Exp 1: 0.45%; Exp 2: 0.82%); (2) the reach amplitude or Euclidean error deviated by more than 3 standard deviations from a 5-trial moving average (Exp 1: 0.14%; Exp 2: 0.19%), and (3) the start and/or end of the movement was not detected (Exp 1: 0.45%; Exp 2: 1.19%). Specifically to the third criterion, movement onset and termination were determined using two velocity-based criteria within the window from target onset to 1 s afterward. First, a peak velocity of at least 10 cm/s had to be detected; otherwise, the trial was classified as a non-movement. Second, movement termination was defined as the point at which velocity dropped below 10% of the trial’s peak velocity; if this threshold was not met, movement termination could not be determined. These criteria were applied on a trial-by-trial basis to account for variability across targets and participants.

In total, we removed 1.05% of trials in Experiment 1 (0–3.94% per participant). In Experiment 2, two participants were excluded for having more than 20% of trials removed in at least one block. This yielded a final sample of 45 participants (Extent group: N = 23; Angular group: N = 22), with a total of 1.47% of trials excluded from these participants (0–6.7% of trials removed per participant).

Quantifying implicit and explicit motor learning

We quantified combined, implicit, and explicit learning by comparing changes in reach amplitude across blocks. To quantify the contribution of different learning processes, participants received specific instructions prior to each block. In the baseline block, participants were instructed to reach directly to the target and land the stylus (hand) on the target. When the perturbation was first introduced (adaptation block), participants were instructed to land the cursor on the target. Since the perturbation was applied abruptly, participants likely engaged explicit strategies and consciously adjusted their reach amplitude to compensate for the perturbation56. The same instructions were repeated prior to the generalization block—emphasizing that the task was to land the cursor on the target. During this phase, explicit adjustments are accompanied by the gradual emergence of implicit learning6,15. Combined learning was reflected in performance during the generalization block—across both perturbed and no-feedback trials—since strategies were permitted.

During the aftereffect block, participants were instructed to aim directly on the target so that the stylus—not the cursor—landed on the target. Participants were instructed to disengage any re-aiming strategy and instead move their hand directly to the target. Any systematic deviation from the target was taken as evidence of implicit learning9,15,18,57–59.

We operationalized implicit learning (aftereffects) as the change in movement amplitude from baseline (veridical feedback) to aftereffect (no feedback). Baseline subtraction was used to minimize any residual reaching biases52,60, which were likely minimal given that veridical feedback was provided during baseline. In both phases, participants were instructed to reach directly to the target (pre- and post-perturbation exposure), ensuring equivalent instructions. Although the feedback type differed, prior studies have shown no difference when computing aftereffects relative to veridical- versus no-feedback baselines61 Finally, explicit learning was estimated as the difference between combined learning in the late generalization phase and performance in the aftereffect phase.

Performance was summarized across four learning phases—baseline, late adaptation, late generalization, and aftereffect—which correspond to subsets of the four experimental blocks (Fig. 1c). Baseline included the second half of trials to each target in the baseline block (22 trials). Late adaptation comprised the last 10 trials to each training target in the adaptation block (20 trials). Late generalization included the second half of the generalization block: the last 102 perturbed-feedback trials to the training targets and the last 5 no-feedback trials to each generalization target (50 no-feedback trials total). The aftereffect phase included all trials from the aftereffect block (44 trials).

Representations of the learned solution

While not exhaustive, we identified three core ways participants may represent the sensorimotor solution once learned: gain-based, translation-based, or location-based (see below; also Fig. 1a). We use the terms representation and solution interchangeably, as each representation held in mind specifies a corresponding solution in the workspace. To evaluate which account best captures behavior, we compared their distinct predictions for generalization at a novel amplitude (9 cm) in the trained direction. Importantly, this tests the representation participants acquired during perturbed feedback trials at the training targets.

1) Gain-based solution. Participants learn the visuomotor transformation itself—that visual feedback is scaled by a constant factor relative to hand movement (Exp 1: 1.3×; Exp 2: 0.7×). At the 9 cm generalization target, this predicts reaches of 6.9 cm (Exp 1) and 12.9 cm (Exp 2).

2) Translation-based solution. Participants learn the fixed spatial offset between hand and cursor that aligned the cursor with the trained target. This predicts reaches of 6.5 cm (Exp 1) and 13.7 cm (Exp 2) at the 9 cm target.

3) Location-based solution. Participants learn the absolute reach amplitude required to compensate at the trained target, applying this same amplitude regardless of the new target location. This would lead to 8.5 cm (Exp 1) and 15.7 cm (Exp 2) reaches to 9 cm targets.

Statistical modeling

To examine how training context influenced learning at the trained (shared) location, we conducted a linear mixed-effects model (R: lmer function) with learning phase (baseline, late adaptation, late generalization perturbed-feedback, aftereffect) and group (Extent, Angular) as fixed (interacting) factors, participant ID as a random factor, and mean reach amplitude as the dependent variable.

We also assessed the impact of training context on generalization to novel probe locations using a linear mixed-effects model with learning phase (baseline, late generalization no-feedback, aftereffect), group, angular distance from the trained direction (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°), and target amplitude (9, 11 cm) as fixed factors, participant ID as a random factor, and mean reach amplitude as the dependent variable.

To examine how participants represent the learned solution, we analyzed reaches during late generalization no-feedback trials in the trained direction at a novel amplitude (9 cm). We fit a linear mixed-effects model with solution (observed, gain, translation, location) and group as fixed factors, participant ID as a random factor, and the mean reach amplitude for each solution as the dependent variable. The observed solution corresponded to the reach amplitude produced by each participant, while the gain-based, translation-based and location-based solution values corresponded to the predicted reaches outlined above.

We tested for overall effects using the anova function in R and conducted post-hoc two-tailed t-tests using the emmeans function with Tukey corrections. Effect sizes are reported as η2 for fixed factors, Cohen’s Dz for within-subjects t-tests, and Cohen’s D for between-subjects t-tests.
