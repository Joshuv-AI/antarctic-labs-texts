The eHealth literacy and its sociodemographic determinants among adult inpatients in Web 3.0 context: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xinyu Feng, Jing Jiang, Mengyuan Liu, Lingyun Tian, Yinglan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rise of Web 3.0 in healthcare has made eHealth literacy essential. Patients experiencing prolonged hospital stays encounter unique challenges with digital tools. However, a critical gap remains in understanding their eHealth literacy due to the absence of assessment instruments tailored to contemporary healthcare systems.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate eHealth literacy and identify its sociodemographic determinants among adult inpatients.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 532 adult inpatients in Hunan, China, using randomized cluster sampling. Data were collected with the Adult Inpatient eHealth Literacy Scale, an instrument developed to assess multiple literacy domains relevant to digital healthcare use in the Web 3.0 context. Multiple linear stepwise regression was performed to identify factors associated with eHealth literacy.ResultsThe mean score of eHealth literacy was 141.12 ± 35.03, with a scoring rate of 64.15%. The scoring rates for traditional literacy, information literacy, media literacy, health literacy, computer literacy, and scientific literacy were 68.37%, 63.82%, 60.86%, 67.13%, 61.65%, 65.25%, respectively. Lower eHealth literacy was associated with older age, lower educational level, fewer years of electronic device use, limited experience with eHealth tools, lower perceived usefulness, higher perceived ease of use, and lower willingness to learn (P < 0.05). The final regression model explained 62.20% of the total variability.ConclusionsAdult inpatients demonstrated an overall moderate level of eHealth literacy, with notable deficiencies in media, computer, and information literacy. Tailoring eHealth tools to individuals from diverse backgrounds is essential to mitigate health disparities. Healthcare professionals are well positioned to serve as critical facilitators in supporting inpatients’ engagement with emerging digital technologies. Patient-centered training programs aimed at strengthening foundational digital and information-processing skills are warranted to promote the safe, effective, and equitable use of eHealth services and improve health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27903-2.Keywords: eHealth literacy, Inpatients, Cross-sectional study, Healthcare innovation, Web 3.0

Background

eHealth refers to the cost-effective and secure use of information and communications technologies (ICTs) to support health services, surveillance, education, knowledge exchange, and research [1]. As ICTs have rapidly evolved, digital technologies have become deeply embedded in healthcare delivery. More recently, the proliferation of artificial intelligence (AI) has further transformed how health information is created, accessed, and used. In this context, the ability of patients to effectively engage with digital health resources is important for care participation and health management. This ability was conceptualized by Norman and Skinner in 2006 as eHealth literacy (eHL), defined as “the ability to seek, find, understand, and appraise health information from electronic sources and apply the knowledge gained to addressing or solving a health problem [2].“The Lily model conceptualizes eHL as a set of six interrelated literacies: traditional literacy, information literacy, media literacy, health literacy, computer literacy, and scientific literacy [2]. These domains explain the broad range of skills required for meaningful engagement with eHealth and provide the theoretical foundation for subsequent eHL assessment. Evidence has shown that higher eHL is associated with better health management, improved treatment adherence, and lower healthcare costs, whereas limited eHL may contribute to delayed care and poorer outcomes [3–5]. However, eHL is not static but evolves alongside technological advancements and shifts in social, personal, and environmental contexts [2]. The transition from Web 1.0 (read-only) to Web 2.0 (interactive and social) and now Web 3.0 (semantic and machine-driven integration) has changed the demands placed on patients. While Web 1.0 emphasized basic information retrieval, and Web 2.0 introduced interactivity and collaboration, Web 3.0 demands higher-order skills, such as personal data management, cybersecurity, and critical appraisal of AI-generated hallucinations [6, 7].

These changes are especially relevant in hospital settings, where digital tools such as patient portals, AI-assisted services, indoor navigation systems, and generative AI applications are increasingly integrated into care [8–10]. Although these technologies may improve efficiency and access, they also create new challenges for inpatients, who are often in physically or emotionally vulnerable conditions but are expected to make health-related decisions and engage actively in care procedures. Previous research has shown that patients often turn to the internet as their first source of health information before consulting physicians, regardless of the severity of their condition [11]. However, many do not make full use of authoritative digital health resources and may lack the skills required to critically appraise information quality or safeguard personal data. Recent evidence suggests that at least 30% of text on active web pages is AI-generated, increasing the risks of exposure to unverified information [12]. Shekar et al. [13] found that people without medical background often perceived low-accuracy AI-generated responses as valid, trustworthy, and satisfactory, and were highly inclined to follow potentially harmful medical advice or seek unnecessary medical attention based on those responses. The mismatch between the large volume of inaccurate or inappropriate medical advice available online and the high level of trust placed in such information may result in misinformed decisions, misdiagnosis, and harmful consequences for inpatients, highlighting the urgent need to better understand eHL in this population.

Despite growing interest in eHL, existing studies have focused mainly on people with chronic diseases or those in outpatient settings, with limited attention to hospitalized patients in the Web 3.0 era. For example, lower self-efficacy, negative attitudes toward aging, and technophobia have been associated with lower eHL in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [14], while better eHL has been linked to greater acceptance of disease management applications among patients with type 2 diabetes [15]. However, these studies did not capture contemporary eHL demands in hospitalized settings. This gap is primarily attributed to the weakness of previous instruments which remain anchored in the Web 1.0 paradigm and fail to capture the full spectrum of eHL skills under the Web 3.0 era [16–18]. To address this gap, this study aimed to evaluate eHL and identify its sociodemographic determinants among adult inpatients using the Adult Inpatient eHealth Literacy Scale (AIPeHLS), which was previously developed and validated for this population [19]. Without addressing this gap, patients may have fewer opportunities to enhance their self-management capabilities and quality of life in increasingly digitalized care environments, which may in turn hinder efforts to optimize the overall efficiency and effectiveness of healthcare delivery systems [3, 20]. Methods

Study design, participants, and sampling

A cross-sectional study was conducted in September 2023. A randomized cluster sampling approach was employed to recruit eligible participants in a university-affiliated general hospital in Hunan, China in line with the following criteria: 1) Currently hospitalized as an inpatient and age ≥ 18 years; 2) Ability to complete the survey independently or with guidance; 3) Informed consent and voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria included: 1) Mental illness or severe cognitive impairment; 2) Acute or critical illness preventing survey completion; 3) Significant visual, auditory, or language impairments. Accounting for a 20% attrition rate and potential sampling error, the sample size was determined using the statistical formula for simple random sampling, resulting in a final required sample size of 480 participants [21].

In this calculation, Uα/2 represents the critical value of the standard normal distribution for a two-tailed test at significance level α, σ denotes the standard deviation (SD) of the variable of interest, and δ refers to the allowable margin of error, typically set as a proportion of the SD (ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 times the SD). For this study, α was set at 0.05, corresponding to Uα/2 = 1.96, and δ was specified as 0.1 times the SD [21]. Based on the results of a pilot study, σ was estimated to be 45.078 [19]. The random numbers were generated using SPSS 26.0 to select wards. Based on an average of 60 adult inpatients per ward (excluding pediatrics), nine wards were estimated to meet the required sample size. The selected wards represented a range of clinical specialties, including radiotherapy oncology, geriatric neurology, respiratory medicine, endocrinology, nephrology, skull base neurosurgery, obstetrics, breast surgery, and general urology surgery. All eligible inpatients in these wards were included in the study until a total of 60 participants per ward was reached (Fig. 1).

Measurements

Individual characteristics questionnaire

A questionnaire was designed by the research team for collecting demographic characteristics including gender, age, marital status, education level, permanent residence, occupation, per capita family income, family medical history, number of diagnosed diseases, and hospitalization frequency; as well as eHealth usage patterns, such as types of electronic devices used, daily duration of electronic device use, experience with eHealth tools, perceived usefulness, importance, and ease of use of eHealth tools.

The Adult Inpatient eHealth Literacy Scale (AIPeHLS)

This scale was developed based on the Lily Model, comprising 44 items across six dimensions: traditional literacy (6 items), information literacy (13 items), media literacy (7 items), health literacy (6 items) computer literacy (8 items), and scientific literacy (4 items). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). The total score ranges from 44 to 220, with higher scores indicating better eHL [19]. In this study, the scale demonstrated excellent reliability, with an overall Cronbach’s α of 0.97 and dimension-specific values ranging from 0.95 to 0.97. Its structural validity was also supported by satisfactory fit indices, including a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) of 0.048, a comparative fit index (CFI) of 0.957, a normed fit index (NFI) of 0.925, and an incremental fit index (IFI) of 0.957 [19].

Data collection

Participants were given a detailed explanation of the study’s objectives, risks and benefits at the beginning. They were also informed about the assessment content, instructions for completing the measures, and their right to exit at any time. Informed consent was obtained before starting the survey which required approximately 8–10 min to finish. The scales were distributed in hard copies by the first author and two trained research assistants.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The eHL scores among inpatients with different individual characteristics were compared using independent-sample t-tests, analysis of variance (ANOVA), Welch’s t-test, and Welch’s ANOVA for a univariable analysis, followed by a multivariable analysis using the multiple linear stepwise regression model (αE = 0.05, αR = 0.10) to identify sociodemographic determinants of eHL among adult inpatients.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 532 participants were involved in this study after excluding 3 incomplete and 5 invalid responses (Fig. 1). Most participants (44.12%) were aged between 31 and 60 years. 35.34% of the participants reported their first time to be hospitalized for treatment. The average number of diagnosed diseases was 3.18 ± 1.76. The majority of participants used mobile phones, while only 24.62%, 19.17%, and 8.65% of participants ever used computers, tablets, and smart wristbands, respectively. 37.78% of participants had been using electronic devices for over 10 years, and 32.52% of them used them for 1–3 h daily. 78.38% of participants ever used eHealth tools for appointment booking, followed by health records and accessing medical information. The main issues with eHealth tools were operational complexity (32.33%) and slow website loading (16.92%). Regarding selecting eHealth tools, participants prioritized information source reliability, followed by operational performance, payment, and time. More details are provided in Tables 1 and 2.

Discussion

Principal findings

Our study revealed a moderate level of eHL among adult inpatients, consistent with findings from Zheng et al. [23] This indicates that, although patients possess a certain ability to acquire, comprehend, evaluate, and apply health information from digital sources, there remains considerable room for improvement. The relatively balanced scores across six dimensions of eHL suggest no pronounced strengths or weakness in specific areas. Furthermore, our study identified seven key factors associated with eHL: age, education level, years of using electronic devices, experience with eHealth tools, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and learning willingness. These findings offer important guidance for the development of targeted interventions tailored to the varying needs of different patient populations. Overall, the study has important implications for healthcare professionals, technology developers, and policymakers seeking to improve patient engagement and health outcomes through digital health innovations.

Traditional, information, and media literacy

Within the Lily Model, traditional, information, and media literacy are categorized as analytic components that are foundational and applicable across contexts [2]. Among these, the scoring rate for the traditional literacy was the highest across six dimensions, while the media literacy got the lowest. This finding indicated that inpatients generally possess foundational abilities to understand clinical instructions, medication guides, and educational materials, but had significant deficiencies in abilities to critically analyze, question, and verify information disclosed through media sources. Moreover, the distrust of online spaces, concerns about privacy breaches, and fear of information misuse may discourage patients from engaging actively with online health discussions, such as sharing mutual support information or medical experiences on social platforms [24]. Social media’s reliance on user-generated content creates blurred boundaries between professional and amateur information, making it challenging for patients to distinguish credible sources [24]. Unverified reports, such as misinformation during disease pandemic, or hallucinations produced by generative models can impact patients’ decision-making and lead to distrust in healthcare institutions, resistance to effective medical interventions, or pursuit of unnecessary treatments [25, 26]. The moderate information literacy observed among participants further highlights challenges in searching, screening, and evaluating targeted health information. The item “When using eHealth tools, I verify the authenticity of the information obtained by consulting other sources (e.g., healthcare providers, books, newspapers)” was scored lowest, which may be attributed to two factors: First, patients may lack awareness of the importance of cross-check information from multiple sources and have limited communication with healthcare providers [11, 27]. Second, the complexity of technologies may overwhelm patients who feel challenging to proceed, intensified by information overload and variability in quality during global health crises [28]. These findings emphasized the necessity for targeted interventions aimed at improving patients’ critical appraisal skills and fostering collaborative information-seeking behaviors.

Health, computer, and scientific literacy

In contrast, health, computer, and scientific literacy are categorized as context-specific components that rely on more situation-specific skills [2]. In this study, patients demonstrated relatively high level of health literacy, indicating a reasonable understanding of their conditions and the ability to engage in basic self-care. However, the relatively low computer literacy observed reflects limited proficiency in using digital tools and adapting to new technologies. The majority of patients reported infrequent use of eHealth tools, with their primary application being limited to scheduling appointments rather than advanced health management. Barriers such as complex interfaces, technical issues, or premium features of digital tools further reduced their willingness to learn and engage with these technologies. Besides, while most patients were familiar with mobile phones, access to other devices such as computer, tablets, and wearable devices was limited, restricting their experience in obtaining benefits from more advanced eHealth service, such as sleep monitoring [29]. These findings highlighted the need for simplified digital platforms, enhanced usability of eHealth tools, and targeted training programs to improve patients’ comfort and competence in digital health environment. Patients also exhibited an acceptable level of scientific literacy, reflecting a basic understanding of the scientific basis, validity, and timeliness of eHealth tools and information. The lowest-scoring item, “I proactively follow the latest research findings related to my diseases,” suggested their low interests in exploring innovative solutions and lack of awareness regarding the significance of scientific advancements in health management. To address this gap, it is essential to empower patients with the tools and knowledge to engage with medical research while fostering partnerships with healthcare providers to bridge the divide between scientific progress and practical applications.

Sociodemographic determinants of eHealth literacy

Our findings indicated that the level of eHL among adult inpatients declined with increasing age, consistent with study by Hoogland et al. [30] This trend can be explained by several factors. First, older adults often exhibit lower familiarity with and acceptance of emerging technologies compared to younger generations. This gap could arise from challenges in operating complex technologies and navigating digital platforms, thereby limiting their experiential learning opportunities and access to updated healthcare resources [31]. Second, older patients, particularly those with limited prior exposure to the Internet and low self-efficacy to address health issues, may struggle with processing information from multiple sources [30]. Third, cognitive decline in older patients is strongly associated with lower health literacy, particularly declines in executive function and situational memory, which can impair the ability to process complex health information [32]. Xavier et al. [33] have demonstrated that proficient use of technology could help to minimize cognitive decline in older adults aged 50 to 89 years, highlighting the potential of technology practice in improving cognitive resilience in aging population to enhance their engagement with eHealth tools. As expected, patients with higher education levels exhibited significantly better eHL, which aligned with findings from Zhou et al. [34] Patients with limited formal education may face barriers in cultivating critical thinking essential to discern low-quality information, as well as in abilities to effectively apply and benefit from online resources. They are also more likely to come from disadvantaged background with resource constraints, underscoring the need for accessible, tailored interventions to ensure equitable access to digital health resources from diverse educational and socioeconomic backgrounds [35].

Perceived usefulness, a belief that using a particular system enhances work performance according to the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [36], was positively associated with eHL. This finding echoes results from Wubante et al. [37], suggesting that patients who recognize the practical benefits of eHealth tools are more motivated to engage with them. Increased perceived usefulness not only improves self-efficacy but also encourages frequent use, enabling patients to develop the skills necessary to access, evaluate, and apply digital health information effectively [38, 39]. Surprisingly, perceived ease of use, another core concept of TAM, defined as the degree to which an individual believes that using a particular system would be free of effort [36], was negatively associated with eHL in our study. In other words, patients who considered eHealth tools easier to use tended to report lower levels of eHL. This finding differs from previous study and should therefore be interpreted with caution [40]. One possible explanation is that perceived ease of use primarily reflects the user’s subjective impression of the operational simplicity of digital tools but does not necessarily indicate stronger abilities to search for, understand, critically appraise, and apply online health information. In contrast, our results also showed that years of using electronic devices and prior experience with eHealth tools were positively associated with eHL, suggesting that sustained exposure and accumulated experience may play a more important role in developing eHL. It is also possible that patients who perceive eHealth tools as highly convenient and effortless may engage with them more superficially, relying on readily accessible information instead of critically evaluating its quality. In this context, some users may be less motivated to actively expand their knowledge or refine their information evaluation skills. Therefore, although perceived ease of use may initially reduce technological fear and facilitate adoption, the development of eHL may depend more on sustained experience and active critical engagement than on ease of use alone [41, 42].

Our results further demonstrated that patients with a greater willingness to learn eHealth tools exhibited higher levels of eHL. Positive attitudes toward learning motivate individuals to take the initiative to acquire and improve relevant skills continuously. In addition, the duration of electronic device use was significantly and positively correlated with eHL, consistent with findings by Jin et al. [43] Prolonged exposure to technology enhances computer literacy and provides users with practical experience in accessing and processing health information, thereby can significantly improve patients’ computer literacy, and the experience of using them over the years improves patients’ ability to obtain and process health information, making them more confident and effective in searching and applying health information [30]. Finally, patients with prior experience using eHealth tools demonstrated significantly higher levels of eHL than those who had never used them. Specifically, those who utilized eHealth platforms for managing health records or communicating with family members and other patients online exhibited superior eHL. Moreover, our study revealed a notable gap in the promotion of digital service, with only 21.05% and 19.55% of patients being informed about eHealth tools from healthcare professionals and educational materials, respectively. This finding suggests that current channels for promoting and accessing eHealth tools may be insufficient. To address this gap, efforts should focus not only on improving the visibility and independent accessibility of eHealth tools, but also on strengthening provider engagement. In particular, integrating digital health training into routine professional education may help equip healthcare providers with the knowledge and resources needed to more effectively introduce these tools to patients and promote their safe use through better awareness of information credibility and quality.

Implications for practice and future research

Our findings underscore the critical need to prioritize improvements in media, computer, and information literacy among adult inpatients to improve their overall eHL. To enhance media literacy, educational programs should concentrate on critical thinking skills that empower patients to discern between factual and misleading content such as commercial advertisements [44]. Simultaneously, the development of AI tools should proceed cautiously, with emphasis on ethical considerations and transparency [45]. In terms of computer literacy, healthcare systems should optimize patient portals by simplifying their design and ensuring intuitive user interfaces. Providing step-by-step guidelines, instructional videos, and responsive online consultation services can significantly enhance acceptability of and engagement with digital health technologies. In addition, continuous technical support is essential to help patients overcome technical challenges, thereby enhancing their self-efficacy in using tools to address health issues [46]. Future research should examine the impact of digital interventions developed using co-design frameworks on patient satisfaction, engagement, and health outcomes. Also, analyzing how different demographic groups interact with technology through data tracking can provide valuable insights for tailoring treatment strategies to meet the needs of diverse populations. Information literacy is crucial for enabling patients to evaluate the credibility of health information sources. Regular workshops can raise awareness about cross-checking and provide guidance on evaluating authorship, publication sources, and the timeliness of information. Mental health counseling services are crucial to mitigate the psychological burden of excessive or conflicting health information, particularly during public health crises [47]. Moreover, establishing online health communities categorized by illness type can provide patients with a secure platform for peer support and professional guidance [48, 49]. These platforms can help identify psychosocial needs that may be underreported in face-to-face consultations, thereby facilitating improvements in the quality of care in the future.

The development of eHealth tools tailored to individuals from diverse sociodemographic backgrounds is essential for reducing health disparities. Providing age-appropriate skill training programs for patients with limited digital experience can accelerate skill acquisition and improve confidence in using these tools [50]. For older patients, aligning the design features with their usage habits can significantly enhance usability, such as voice guidance, larger font sizes, and simplified information presentation. Meanwhile, caregivers and social workers should be encouraged to assist elderly patients in learning new technologies. More importantly, healthcare providers play a pivotal role in bridging the gap between patients and emerging technologies. Specialized training in nursing informatics is essential to facilitate the development of innovative care solutions that benefit both patients and caregivers [51–53]. For instance, data-visualized blood pressure monitor watches enabled patients, families, and healthcare providers to intuitively understand health data and take promptly responsive actions, thereby enhancing patient safety and operational efficiency [54]. Moreover, recommending free and easily accessible services to patients particularly those with limited educational background, can help reduce inequality of healthcare resource distribution. Incorporating elevator pitches into nursing curriculum can empower nurses to effectively articulate their innovative solutions to stakeholders such as patients, senior manager, and investors, in which the communication skills among healthcare providers are highlighted to promote the adoption of eHealth tools [55].

Health education initiatives should aim to improve patients’ perceived usefulness of eHealth tools and their willingness to engage with them. By demonstrating the practical benefits of digital tools, such as monitoring sleep patterns, blood oxygen levels, or dietary habits, patients can be motivated to explore more advanced functions for health management. Iterative feedback from users can in turn inspire engineers to refine existing tools and enhance their functionality [56]. Additionally, incorporating novel, interactive features into interventions is essential to boost patients’ adherence to treatment. Gamification strategies, such as quizzes, rewards, and leaderboards, can make learning more engaging and enjoyable. Activities launched by social media, such as “Step Challenge” or “Healthy Eating Challenge,” combined with ecological momentary assessments, can encourage patients to actively use eHealth tools and share their experiences in online communities. Augmented reality technologies and AI chatbots can offer flexible, visualized instructions to patients by providing real-time feedback on vital signs, gait analysis, and muscle exertion [57]. While enhancing the usability of eHealth tools is crucial, it is equally important to educate patients on the potential risks of over-reliance and misuse of technology. Research efforts should prioritize developing guidelines for ethical AI use in healthcare and evaluating their long-term effectiveness on patients’ health outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, the representativeness of the results might be constrained because the sample was drawn exclusively from a single clinical institution in China. The applicability of the AIPeHLS across different patient populations, healthcare settings, and clinical contexts warrants further investigation to establish its external validity and support its broader use. Second, due to the cross-sectional design, the observed associations cannot be interpreted as causal. In particular, the possibility of reverse causality cannot be ruled out. Third, the eHL and related variables were assessed using self-reported measures, which may be subject to recall bias and may not fully reflect participants’ actual skills or behaviors in using eHealth tools. Future research would benefit from multi-center longitudinal designs and the inclusion of objective or behavioral measures to provide a more robust understanding of eHL and its determinants. Despite these limitations, the current study addressed a critical gap in literature, as no prior research has specifically focused on evaluating eHL among inpatient populations who must navigate increasingly complex eHealth challenges. By integrating the evolving Web 3.0 context and the rapidly advancing eHealth technologies worldwide, our work represents an innovative contribution to the field, providing a valuable foundation for future research and practice aimed at improving eHL and exploring its relationships with health outcomes in diverse inpatient settings.

Conclusion

Our study revealed a moderate level of eHL among adult inpatients, as measured by the AIPeHLS, with notable deficiencies in media, computer, and information literacy. Sociodemographic factors including age, education level, years of using electronic devices, experience with eHealth tools, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and learning willingness emerged as statistically significant determinants. This study represents the first investigation into eHL among hospitalized adults across diverse specialties, providing a robust foundation for developing tailored educational and technological strategies. As digital solutions have been increasingly integrated into healthcare systems, improving eHL among inpatients will be crucial to harmonize service provision with patients demand and promote equitable access to high-quality care.
