Startup activities and challenges implementing an integrated care model for pregnant and postpartum individuals with substance use disorders: a qualitative study
Authors: Jennifer D. Hall, Maria N. Danna, Viviane Cahen, Andrea Baron, Camille C. Cioffi, Deborah J. Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubstance use has increased among pregnant and postpartum people in the last decade, yet few pregnant individuals receive prenatal care and treatment for substance use disorder (SUD). The Nurture Oregon model aims to integrate medical care with SUD treatment and provide destigmatized care through regular visits in an integrated care setting. We studied one family medicine, one behavioral health, and two SUD treatment organizations in rural counties with high SUD rates and limited resources that were funded to implement the model.MethodsTo examine the startup and early implementation phases of Nurture Oregon, we used a prospective, observational design to appreciate the effort from multiple perspectives. We observed program development meetings, team operations, and conducted semi-structured interviews with organization leaders, team members, and community partners. We used an inductive and comparative approach to identify sites’ startup and early implementation activities and challenges.ResultsEach site started with different program elements at baseline. This influenced the model elements each organization worked on during startup and the implementation challenges they experienced. The SUD and behavioral health organizations did not fully integrate care due to difficulty developing partnerships with medical organizations; they leveraged peers and doulas to provide cohesion for patients. The family medicine site was the only site that fully implemented the model, but they experienced barriers to financially supporting their peer workforce due to licensing and reimbursement policy constraints. All sites experienced challenges collaborating with hospital labor and delivery departments, and they all took steps to address patient housing needs by connecting patients to housing resources or acquiring housing units.ConclusionsImplementing a care model to integrate medical and SUD treatment for pregnant individuals is difficult to accomplish but has the potential to make a significant difference in maternal and child health outcomes, recovery success, and prevention of foster care placement. Experiences during the startup and early implementation phases can shape the entire trajectory of a program and determine its long-term success. Our work shows the early challenges that need to be addressed to build an integrated program.

Background

In the past 20 years, the United States has seen increases in the prevalence of substance use disorder (SUD) during pregnancy [1–3]. These data likely underestimate the problem, as pregnant individuals may be afraid to report their substance use due to bias and fear of child welfare removing their child after delivery [4]. In rural areas, where stigma may be heightened and treatment options are more limited, these challenges may be amplified [5]. Despite effective treatments for SUD during pregnancy, only a small percentage of the population receives this treatment [6]. Untreated SUD during pregnancy is associated with adverse maternal and infant health outcomes and long-term consequences for the parent and infant dyad [5, 7–9].

Pregnant individuals and families experience difficulty engaging with a highly fragmented healthcare system characterized by siloed services and lack of communication [10]. SUD treatment, prenatal and postpartum care, and pediatric care for newborns require regular medical visits that may be in different locations. In addition, patients seeking these services, particularly pregnant individuals, may experience stigma, and clinicians may feel uncomfortable providing care to people with SUD who are pregnant. Furthermore, there can be long waits to access care [11]; this can be true for primary care, medication induction for methadone, and residential treatment, as there are few residential treatment sites that allow children and provide parenting support resources [4, 12, 13].

Integrated care, defined as a team of medical and behavioral health clinicians using a systematic approach to provide patient-centered care for a defined population [14], has yielded encouraging results at improving outcomes for individuals with SUD [15–17]. There’s been growing attention on programs like Kaiser Permanente’s Early Start program [18] and the Maternal Opioid Misuse [19] that aims to coordinate care for pregnant individuals with SUD. Despite evidence of these promising approaches, care in Oregon and throughout the US remains relatively siloed, requiring patients to navigate separate medical and SUD treatment systems for their healthcare.

One Medicaid Coordinated Care Organization (CCO) in Oregon (an insurer) conducted a pilot in Portland, Oregon to address this problem. Three pilot sites implemented a care model called Project Nurture that integrated prenatal and postpartum care with SUD treatment to provide destigmatized care in a single setting. Project Nurture was implemented in partnership with three large health systems, two were implemented by family medicine teams that practiced full scope medicine including perinatal care, and one was a nurse midwifery practice. An initial evaluation showed reductions in foster care placements and reports of child maltreatment within twelve months after birth and was associated with increased prenatal visits and maternal lengths-of-stay [20]. However, this evaluation did not study how the pilot sites implemented the care model.

The Oregon legislature mandated the expansion of the program [21], and Oregon Health Authority (OHA) funded five sites in rural counties with high SUD rates and limited resources to implement the model. They also funded a quality improvement organization to provide implementation support. This expansion was named Nurture Oregon. Nurture Oregon was designed to be available to patients during pregnancy, continue until one year postpartum, and include integrated medical care, pediatric care, SUD treatment, which includes access to medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD), peer recovery support, social service coordination, and an approach that was non-judgmental and trauma informed [22, 23].

Our team has been studying the implementation of Nurture Oregon since its inception in 2021. We chose to study startup and early implementation, as these early phases function as key predictors of program success, influencing whether an intervention is ever launched, and carried out [24]. Site behavior and early-stage fidelity can forecast successful program startup and shape the trajectory for later implementation [25], ultimately shaping program scalability and long-term program viability. Our prior research identifies the 14 core care team functions of this care model and the professionals that carry out these functions. We found that medical, behavioral health, and substance use treatment organizations configured their care teams differently [26]. Little is known about what is needed to implement an integrated care model that serves pregnant and postpartum individuals experiencing SUD. We describe the factors that influenced sites’ startup and early implementation activities and the challenges they experienced.

Methods

Design

To examine the startup and early implementation phases of Nurture Oregon, we used a prospective, observational design, which included observation and interview data, to appreciate the effort from multiple perspectives. The Oregon Health & Science University institutional review board approved this protocol (22112).

Sample

Five Nurture Oregon expansion sites were originally funded. One site lost funding during implementation, and four sites (one family medicine, one behavioral health, and two substance use treatment organizations) were ultimately included in the analysis. From each organization, we purposively selected team members (e.g., organization leaders, clinicians, peer recovery mentors, doulas, SUD treatment counselors, caseworkers). While each site had its own unique staffing arrangements, we aimed to speak to at least one person representing each role on each team. Nurture team members were also asked to help us identity their partners. The types of organizations that were recommended varied by site and included Medicaid payers, residential treatment centers, community organizations, and members of the implementation support team (e.g., program managers, consultants) that supported the sites during startup and implementation.

Data collection and management

Data collection was aligned with startup and early implementation phases, consistent with implementation science approaches [27]. Data collection began in January 2021 and continued until September 2023. This aligns with when the sites conducted startup activities and the point at which most sites reached a steady state of implementation. To understand early implementation, we continued to observe those meetings and conducted in-person observation visits with Sites 1–3. A visit to Site 4 was postponed due to leadership turnover. We observed team members as they delivered care and services, and we took handwritten notes that were later developed into detailed fieldnotes.

We interviewed site leaders, team members, and community partners (n = 43) about their implementation experiences. Interviews followed a guide that asked participants about their experiences implementing Nurture Oregon and explored attitudes and experiences that were informed by the consolidated framework for implementation research [28]. Interviews were recorded and professionally transcribed. Fieldnotes and interview transcripts were de-identified and entered into Atlas.ti (Version 9) for data management and analysis.

Data analysis

We designed this evaluation to collect and analyze data iteratively and in real time. This iterative approach allowed us to monitor for saturation, the point at which no new findings emerged from data collection. By following the sites as they created and implemented their programs, we were able to observe baseline characteristics, startup activities, and eventually, the barriers and challenges they experienced.

A diverse team of researchers with qualitative expertise and backgrounds in primary care, integrated care, and behavioral health (JDH, MND, VC, AB, and DJC) began by analyzing the initial data we collected: fieldnotes from observed program development meetings between each site, OHA, and the implementation support team. During analysis meetings we read these notes as a group and captured reflections from this process in analytic memos. These memos informed the initial codebook comprised of broad codes that were used to tag the text and track care model elements that teams were implementing, including medical care, peer support, and SUD counseling. As we collected more data, and introduced new data sources like interview transcripts, we expanded the code list to capture additional concepts that were emerging such as hiring and team formation activities, developing partnerships, and defining roles and workflows. When what we observed conflicted with what participants reported, we re-examined the original field notes and transcripts to ensure accurate representation, and we conducted follow-up interviews to verify or refine our understanding.

When the codes were clearly defined and used consistently among the research team, three analysts (JH, MD, AB) independently analyzed the remaining data, meeting weekly to discuss data, identify emerging findings, reconcile discrepancies and augment the codebook as needed.

Through this process we created definitions that were tailored to the sites we were studying. We defined startup as the point when organizations received funding and took steps to engage partners, and hire, train, and retrain team members in the care model. We defined early implementation as the point at which organizations completed initial hiring, built relationships with partners, and were delivering care to patients. During this early implementation phase, the sites were still refining and revising their models, which provided an opportunity to identify their implementation challenges.

We summarized preliminary findings for each site, and we conducted a subsequent analytic cycle to compare the sites, identifying where startup and early implementation activities and challenges differed and where they were consistent. We analyzed the data to identify, where possible, explanations for the variation we observed.

We describe the difficulties the SUD treatment centers and behavioral health organization (BHO) experienced integrating medical care, the barriers the family medicine practice experienced integrating behavioral health care, the challenges all organizations experienced with labor and delivery, and the ways in which the sites expanded the care model to include housing support.

Integrating medical care in behavioral health settings

Challenges partnering with medical organizations

The SUD treatment centers and the BHO experienced challenges developing partnerships with organizations to provide medical care (e.g., prenatal and postpartum care, pediatric care). These organizations successfully developed partnerships with other sectors (housing, home visiting nurses, doulas), but they were unable to develop fully integrated partnerships (i.e., where services were provided in the same space and teams collaborated and communicated as part of an integrated system) with medical organizations. The SUD treatment centers engaged in numerous attempts to form such partnerships, but experienced ongoing challenges that they shared during program implementation meetings:The site lead is having trouble engaging the large health system. She says, “I feel like I’m not making any movement. I’m willing to take help regarding this in any way.” […] She tried to meet with [health system leadership] but never got a response. She’s wondering if she should keep pushing or take a different approach? She repeatedly says she’s open to any suggestions at this point.

Peers and doulas played an important connecting role

Nurture Oregon sites that did not provide integrated care (Sites 2–4) reported leveraging peers and doulas to provide cohesion for patients. This reliance on peers to bring together a fragmented medical and behavior health system was a workaround for system redesign. At one SUD treatment site, “The doula is going to all the patient appointments and looping back with the rest of the team.” (Site 4, Program Development Meeting Fieldnotes). At the other, the peers were referred to by the team as “the glue” and were responsible for helping patients navigate dispersed services.The peer is at the apex. They are the tip of the spear for the rest of us being able to deploy services. […] The peer is the central spoke. Anybody who’s not the peer has most of their interaction with the peer on behalf of the client.

Integrating behavioral health care in medical settings

The family medicine practice was the only site that fully integrated behavioral health with prenatal and postpartum care (i.e., where an interdisciplinary team operated in the same space and functioned as a single unit with shared goals and clear communication channels) and provided a minimum of one year postpartum and pediatric care. The medical practice hired a prenatal care clinician to expand their medical team and expanded behavioral health support by hiring a peer support specialist and two behavioral health clinicians. They refined how they integrated behavioral health care for their patients and experienced ongoing challenges related to billing and financing behavioral health care. The implementation support team helped them develop team clinical care processes and workflows, which included improving scheduling and optimizing behavioral health billing.[Site 1] is still building out their scheduling process. The behavioral health clinicians are required to have 50% availability to meet the Medicaid integrated behavioral health requirement. They track warm handoffs in the EHR and use a dot phrase. The implementation support consultant is on site monthly to help with training, shadowing, building out the peer supervision role, and billing sustainably.

As a medical practice, this site was limited by the types of behavioral health services they could provide. In Oregon, Certified Alcohol and Drug Counselors (CADCs) must be employed by SUD treatment organizations. This licensing restriction limited their ability to hire a CADC, so they partnered with a SUD organization to co-locate a CADC in their clinic. Similarly, peer-support specialist services were not reimbursable in medical settings in Oregon.: “…we’re a physical health organization, and she’s certified under mental health. None of her services are billable under our codes.” (Site 1, Physician Owner Interview). To work around this challenge, Site 1 paid peers with startup funding. In addition, this practice learned that they needed more than one peer to care for their patient panel. Discussion

The Nurture Oregon sites started in developmentally different places, and the model components they had at baseline influenced their early startup and implementation experiences. We found that only one site, the family medicine practice (Site 1), was able to implement the targeted model in a single location where an interdisciplinary team communicated as part of an integrated system and collaboratively addressed patients’ medical and SUD treatment needs together. The BHO and SUD treatment centers never fully integrated medical care and had difficulty developing partnerships with medical groups and hospital systems who were unresponsive. The medical site was not without early implementation challenges; while they hired peers and a prenatal care clinician, they experienced barriers to financially supporting their new peer workforce due to licensing and reimbursement policy constraints [29–33].

Prior to startup, the BHO and SUD treatment organizations (Sites 2–4) were already providing behavioral health services and were funded by OHA to provide peer support services through a separate program [34]. However, building on this foundation resulted in the implementation of a care model that departed from the original Project Nurture model, as these organizations struggled to fully integrate medical care into their interdisciplinary teams. Managing the tension between flexibility and care model fidelity is critical for a model’s effectiveness [35–37]. While launching integrated services in rural areas has challenges [38], it can be accomplished in rural settings [39]. Barriers to partnering with medical organizations remain unresolved and may reflect a power differential between small behavioral health and SUD organizations attempting to partner with large health systems and medical groups, the limitations of an intervention that serves a small volume of patients, as well as an underestimation of the difficulties engaging medical partners [40–42].

This study echoes other research on integrated care models which found that constraints on billable services and siloed medical and behavioral health systems limit interdisciplinary integrated care [29, 43]. Our findings also align with others that found that OBGYN departments’ definition of the scope of the postpartum period (6 − 8 weeks) [44] make OBGYNs challenging partners for care models that intend to serve parent and infant dyads and provide ongoing care at least one-year postpartum. Family medicine may be a better specialty with which to collaborate due to their ability to provide continuity to dyads. Collaborating with family medicine clinicians also aligns with previous literature that indicated positive engagement in family-centered substance use treatment [45].

All sites experienced challenges collaborating with hospital labor and delivery departments to provide de-stigmatized care to patients. Even the medical site that leveraged relationships with hospitalists was unable to control care that occurred outside of their organization. This has implications for clinical and foster care outcomes and represents a key gap in the care continuum [5, 46, 47]. Support with system redesign that includes labor and delivery departments and hospitals will be critical for providing destigmatized care to this patient population.

The participating sites implemented several workarounds to address their startup and early implementation challenges. Without an established relationship with a hospital, sites worked around this issue by hiring doulas or cross training peers as doulas who were present for the births. However, greater implementation support on this important care transition is needed. Sites also used grants to fund positions that weren’t billable and leveraged their peers to provide cohesion for patients when services weren’t fully integrated. The solutions that sites utilized to overcome these challenges were instructive, but they may be inadequate to support effectiveness and sustainability of these programs.

All participating organizations added to the services they were offering at baseline, developed new partnerships with other sectors, and either added or expanded their housing support. The addition of housing support services among all the sites reflects a clear connection between safe housing and the ability to treat SUD among pregnant and parenting patients and is worthy of consideration for inclusion in the model.

There are several limitations to this study. First, there was only one medical practice in the sample, and it uniquely only served families impacted by SUD. There may be startup and implementation experiences that we missed by only studying one medical practice. Second, this study focuses on organizations in a single state, and other states may have different policy, licensing, and payment structures that limit the transferability of these findings. Third, Nurture Oregon sites began implementation during the COVID-19 pandemic which may have contributed to their challenges finding medical organizations that were ready to implement a new program. While only one site was able to implement the care model as it was originally designed, changes are common when implementing interventions and care models into practice, and there may be other approaches to achieving similar outcomes. More research is needed on these modified versions of the original Project Nurture care model and their impact on effectiveness and outcomes.

Conclusion

This article is a resource for policy makers and health system leaders who want to develop integrated models of care to serve this population. Implementing a care model to integrate medical and SUD treatment for pregnant individuals is difficult to accomplish but has the potential to make a significant difference in maternal and child health outcomes, recovery success, and prevention of foster care placement. Experiences during the startup and early implementation phases can shape the entire trajectory of a program and determine its long-term success. Our work shows the early challenges that need to be addressed to build an integrated program that supports pregnant individuals and their families.
