Successful live birth after interstitial ectopic pregnancy in a patient with Swyer syndrome following IVF: a case report
Authors: Jarosław Kopko, Alena Novikava, Marta Dąbrowska, Polina Novikava, Jarosław Niemoczyński
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSwyer syndrome (46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis) is a rare disorder of sex development characterized by a female phenotype in the absence of functional gonads. Despite ovarian failure, pregnancy can be achieved through in vitro fertilization (IVF) using donor oocytes in patients with a preserved uterus.This report describes the case of a 29-year-old woman with Swyer syndrome and prior bilateral adnexectomy who underwent IVF with donor oocytes. Following endometrial preparation in an artificial cycle, a single frozen embryo transfer was performed. The first implantation resulted in an interstitial ectopic pregnancy, which was successfully treated with methotrexate. Six months later, a subsequent IVF cycle resulted in a normally progressing intrauterine pregnancy. The pregnancy was uncomplicated and ended in the delivery of a healthy male neonate at 39 weeks of gestation by cesarean section.This case highlights both the reproductive potential of patients with Swyer syndrome using assisted reproductive technologies and suggests that early transvaginal ultrasound should be mandatory in all IVF pregnancies, regardless of adnexal status, as ectopic implantation may occur even after bilateral adnexectomy.Keywords: Swyer syndrome; 46,XY gonadal dysgenesis; In vitro fertilization (IVF); Oocyte donation; Ectopic pregnancy

Introduction

Swyer syndrome, also known as pure gonadal dysgenesis, is a rare disorder of sex development characterized by a 46,XY karyotype with a female phenotype. Diagnosis is based on clinical findings, hormonal profile, genetic testing, and histopathological assessment of gonads [1]. The condition was first described by Swyer in 1955 [2] and has an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 80,000 births [3]. Patients typically present with primary amenorrhea, delayed puberty, and normal external female genitalia. Müllerian structures, including the uterus and fallopian tubes, are present due to absent anti-Müllerian hormone production during fetal development [4]. Gonadal dysgenesis results in hypergonadotropic hypogonadism [5], [6]. Dysgenetic gonads carry a significant risk of malignancy, including gonadoblastoma (∼30%) and dysgerminoma (50–60%) [1], which justifies bilateral gonadectomy as standard management [6]. Salpingectomy is also recommended to reduce malignancy risk and potentially decrease the risk of ectopic pregnancy in future oocyte donation cycles [7].

Pregnancy is possible only via assisted reproductive techniques using donor oocytes [8]. The first successful pregnancy through in vitro fertilization (IVF) in Swyer syndrome was reported in 1988 [9], and approximately 40–50 live births have been described worldwide [8], [9], [10].

Case Presentation

A 29-year-old woman with Swyer syndrome presented for IVF with donor oocytes. She had undergone bilateral laparoscopic adnexectomy at the age of 14 due to gonadal dysgenesis. Her BMI was 19.7 kg/m2 and her family history was unremarkable. She was on long-term hormone replacement therapy. Baseline transvaginal ultrasound showed a normal uterus (34 × 37 × 35 mm). Frozen embryo transfer was planned in an artificial cycle. Endometrial preparation included estradiol (4 mg/day), followed by vaginal progesterone (800 mg/day) and oral progesterone (100 mg/day). The embryo used for transfer was a vitrified-warmed single blastocyst (day 5) derived from donor oocytes. Embryo quality was assessed as 5AA. Endometrial thickness on the day of progesterone initiation was 8 mm, with a trilaminar pattern. Frozen embryo transfer was performed under transabdominal ultrasound guidance using a soft catheter. No technical difficulties were encountered during embryo transfer. The catheter passage was smooth, and no blood or mucus was observed on withdrawal.

Ten days after transfer, serum β-hCG was 51.4 mIU/mL. Due to inadequate rise in subsequent measurements, luteal support was discontinued and withdrawal bleeding occurred. Ultrasound revealed an 18 × 14 mm vascular lesion in the right interstitial portion of the fallopian tube, consistent with interstitial ectopic pregnancy (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Serum β-hCG was 485 mIU/mL. The patient was treated with methotrexate, resulting in complete resolution and negative β-hCG.

Six months later, a single vitrified-warmed blastocyst of comparable quality was transferred in a subsequent artificial cycle using the same protocol. The embryo transfer was uneventful and performed under ultrasound guidance. β-hCG at day 10 was 152 mIU/mL with appropriate rise. Ultrasound confirmed an intrauterine gestational sac (7 mm) and yolk sac (3.5 mm). The pregnancy progressed uneventfully, resulting in delivery of a healthy male neonate at 39 weeks of gestation by cesarean section (3590 g, Apgar score 10).

Discussion

Swyer syndrome is a rare disorder of sex development with heterogeneous genetic etiology. Most cases are sporadic, although autosomal dominant, recessive, and sex-linked inheritance patterns have been described [3], [11]. SRY gene abnormalities are detected in 10–20% of cases, while most patients have an intact SRY gene [12]. Familial occurrence is uncommon (∼4%) [13]. No SRY mutation was identified in the present patient.

Diagnosis is typically made during adolescence due to primary amenorrhea [14]. Unlike androgen insensitivity syndrome, Müllerian structures are preserved due to absent anti-Müllerian hormone secretion [4], enabling pregnancy via donor oocyte IVF. Hormone replacement therapy is essential for pubertal development, uterine development, and long-term prevention of osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease [15]. Pregnancy in Swyer syndrome is possible only through assisted reproduction with donor oocytes [1], requiring adequate estrogen and progesterone supplementation due to absent ovarian function. Since the first reported case of pregnancy in Swyer syndrome, in 1988 [9], evidence remains limited to case reports and small series, with approximately 40–50 live births described worldwide [8], [9], [10]. These pregnancies are associated with increased obstetric risk, particularly hypertensive disorders [10], and cesarean section is frequently performed [16], although successful vaginal deliveries have been reported [17].

Ectopic pregnancy in Swyer syndrome is extremely rare. No previous reports of cases of interstitial ectopic pregnancy after bilateral adnexectomy could be found [8], [18], [19]. While ectopic implantation is a recognized complication of IVF, its occurrence in patients without adnexal structures remains exceptional [7], [16]. Although embryo transfer was technically uncomplicated in both cycles, the occurrence of interstitial ectopic pregnancy highlights that standard procedural factors alone may not fully account for abnormal implantation, pointing toward alternative mechanisms such as uterine contractility or microanatomical pathways.

Clinicians should be aware that the absence of fallopian tubes does not eliminate the risk of ectopic implantation after embryo transfer.

To the authors' knowledge, this is the first reported case of interstitial ectopic pregnancy in a patient with Swyer syndrome after bilateral adnexectomy, followed by a successful live birth.

Conclusion

Patients with Swyer syndrome can achieve successful pregnancy through IVF with donor oocytes. However, rare complications such as interstitial ectopic pregnancy may still occur even after bilateral adnexectomy. Early pregnancy monitoring and accurate localization of implantation are crucial for optimal outcomes.
