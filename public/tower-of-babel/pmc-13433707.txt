Topical strategies for antimicrobial delivery of peptide medicines for the management of chronic wounds
Authors: Sasikumar Murthy, Tan Yong Zhao, Vasantha Kumari Neela, Masriana Hassan, Malarvili Selvaraja, Vijayaraj Kumar Palanirajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic wounds remain difficult to manage because persistent infection, biofilm formation, and antimicrobial resistance limit the effectiveness of conventional antibiotics. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have emerged as promising therapeutic alternatives because they combine broad-spectrum membrane activity with quorum-sensing interference and immunomodulatory effects but generally impose a lower resistance burden than single-target antibiotics do. This review examines topical delivery systems designed to improve AMP stability, local bioavailability, targeted release, and residence time within the wound microenvironment. Hydrogels and films provide moist, biocompatible matrices for localized and sustained peptide release, including alginate-based systems with activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms. Nanoparticle platforms, including lipid, metallic, liposomal, and micellar systems, can protect AMPs from enzymatic degradation and generate depot-like release profiles against drug-resistant pathogens such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus. Biopolymer scaffolds, microneedles, and stimuli-responsive carriers activated by pH, reactive oxygen species, temperature, light, or ultrasound provide spatiotemporal control of AMP deployment in infected wounds. These strategies are particularly relevant to chronic diabetic wounds, where delayed healing, persistent inflammation, and biofilm burden frequently coexist. Smart design elements, including extracellular matrix-mimetic nanofibers, integrated pH and cytokine sensors, cyclic peptides, and dendrimers, further extend AMP function by linking antimicrobial activity with tissue regeneration and wound monitoring. Preclinical studies report accelerated closure, reduced inflammatory burden, and biofilm disruption, but clinical translation remains limited by manufacturing scalability, dose-dependent cytotoxicity, sterilization stability, and limited human validation. Overall, next-generation topical AMP delivery systems offer a rational route to combine infection control with regenerative wound repair while reducing the reliance on conventional antibiotics.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Wound healing is a coordinated physiological process that restores tissue integrity after acute or chronic injury. Disruption of the skin barrier by surgery, burns, infection, trauma, or dermatological disease initiates four overlapping phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodelling. During hemostasis, platelet recruitment and fibrin clot formation establish a provisional extracellular matrix that limits blood loss and provides a scaffold for subsequent cellular events. In the inflammatory phase, immune cells are recruited to clear debris and pathogens. Proliferation follows through granulation tissue formation, angiogenesis, and re-epithelialization, restoring tissue continuity. Finally, remodelling reorganizes and matures collagen fibres, increasing tensile strength and the formation of scar tissue (Fig. 1) [1].Fig. 1Visual representation of the four phases involved in wound healing. Chronic wound management is complicated by comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, obesity, aging, and immune dysfunction. Hyperglycaemia promotes protein glycation and impairs macrophage switching from a proinflammatory M1 phenotype to a reparative M2 phenotype, prolonging cytokine release and extracellular matrix destruction [2, 3]. Obesity further compromises healing by reducing vascularization in adipose tissue; localized hypoxia limits the oxygen-dependent production of the reactive oxygen species required for microbial killing and impairs fibroblast-mediated collagen deposition. Although hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha may be activated, dysfunctional angiogenesis can prevent effective repair [4]. Ageing adds another barrier because senescent cells remain metabolically active and secrete matrix metalloproteinases that degrade the growth factors required for tissue regeneration; the paracrine effects of senescent cells may also propagate inflammation to neighbouring cells. While transient senescence contributes to wound closure, sustained senescence delays repair [5]. In 2019, the incidence of chronic wounds in the United States reached 10.5 million patients, an approximately 28% increase over a five-year period [6]. Persistent microbial colonization therefore represents a central obstacle to healing because it sustains inflammation, promotes biofilm formation, and increases the risk of antimicrobial resistance [7, 8].

Conventional antibiotics have become less reliable for wound infection control, as the clinical relevance of multidrug-resistant pathogens, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA), and other extensively drug-resistant organisms, has increased. The World Health Organization has recognized wound management and antimicrobial resistance as major public health concerns because treatment failure worsens patient outcomes and narrows therapeutic options [1, 9]. These pressures necessitate the search for alternatives that combine antimicrobial action with wound-compatible delivery. AMPs are endogenous peptides, typically 10 to 60 amino acids in length, with broad antibacterial and immunomodulatory activity that make them attractive substitutes or adjuncts to conventional agents. AMP structures are typically grouped into alpha-helical, beta-sheet, extended, and hybrid classes. Clinically relevant mammalian examples include human cathelicidin LL-37 and defensins, while amphibian-derived peptides such as magainin remain important for antimicrobial research [10, 11]. Alpha-helical AMPs are amphipathic, membrane-associated peptides that can form membrane-spanning pores or disrupt gram-negative biofilms through carpet-like or toroidal pore mechanisms; for example, LL-37 exhibits activity against P. aeruginosa biofilms [12]. Beta-sheet AMPs, including defensins, contain disulfide-stabilized strands that provide protease resistance and structural rigidity [11]. Extended and loop peptides, such as indolicidin and apidaecins, remain relatively unstructured until they interact with microbial membranes or intracellular targets, where their proline-, arginine-, glycine-, or tryptophan-rich sequences support antibacterial activity [13, 14]. Hybrid AMPs combine motifs from distinct peptides to improve bacterial targeting, bioavailability, and antibiofilm activity; for example, PA2-GNU7 has targeted activity against P. aeruginosa [11].

AMP limitations

Most cationic AMPs act through electrostatic interactions with anionic bacterial membrane components, including phospholipids and lipopolysaccharides, leading to membrane destabilization and cell death [15, 16]. Their activity is clinically relevant because biofilms create dense, multi-layered barriers that reduce antimicrobial penetration and protect bacteria from host defences and antibiotics. Biofilm-embedded cells may exhibit antimicrobial resistance levels that are 10- to 1,000-fold greater than those of planktonic populations, making biofilm control central to chronic wound management [17]. Bacteria also use quorum sensing (QS) to coordinate biofilm maturation through autoinducer-mediated regulation of adhesins, extracellular polymeric substances, and virulence factors. AMPs such as LL-37 can interfere with QS by binding to receptors or transcriptional regulators [18]. They may also disrupt autoinducer signalling and reduce adhesin production, surface attachment, EPS accumulation, and transporter gene expression, thereby weakening the biofilm state [19, 20].

Because AMPs target membrane architecture and multicellular biofilm behavior rather than a single enzyme, resistance development is generally less straightforward with AMPs than with conventional antibiotics. Bacteria that resist AMPs often need altered membrane lipid composition, a change that can reduce cellular fitness [21]. The central translational challenge is therefore not only peptide discovery but also delivery: AMPs must be protected from proteolysis, retained at therapeutic concentrations, and released at the infected wound site. Hydrogels and films

Hydrogel wound dressings are attractive AMP carriers because they are biocompatible, retain moisture, and can incorporate bioactive agents for local delivery. Hydrogels typically contain 70–99% water, enabling efficient encapsulation of hydrophilic therapeutics within an interconnected polymer network. This three-dimensional matrix can also limit premature degradation by reducing enzyme access to the payload [70]. Natural polymers, including proteins and polysaccharides, and synthetic polymers such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) and polycaprolactone (PCL) are typically used to tailor hydrogel properties. Polysaccharides provide high moisture retention and tunable chemical functionality, whereas protein-based hydrogels such as collagen and gelatin offer extracellular matrix-like bioactivity [71, 72]. Collagen-hyaluronic acid hydrogels, for example, have been associated with thicker granulation tissue and improved wound repair, which is consistent with their film-forming capacity and ECM-like composition [71, 73]. Synthetic polymers may require additional design control because poorly degradable or immunologically foreign structures can compromise compatibility. Biodegradable aliphatic polyesters and copolymers, including PCL, PEG, polylactic acid, polyglycolic acid, and poly-L-lactic acid, address this limitation by undergoing hydrolytic degradation and supporting scaffold replacement without removal [74]. Hydrophobic payload delivery can be improved by incorporating moieties such as cyclodextrins, which form hydrophobic cavities and inclusion complexes that increase drug loading [75].

Combining hydrogels with AMPs is an increasingly important strategy because it enables sustained, localized peptide release while preserving a moist wound environment. Alginate-based hydrogels are widely used for this purpose because they are biodegradable, tissue-compatible, and capable of encapsulating cationic peptides. Their porous sodium alginate networks absorb exudate, reduce trauma during dressing removal, and support gradual drug release. In murine models, sodium alginate hydrogels loaded with the AMP Chol-37(F34-R) suppressed proinflammatory cytokines and showed antibacterial activity against P. aeruginosa infection, while sustained release may reduce the dosing frequency and help maintain effective local concentrations [76]. Hydrogel dressings can also be processed into films or fibres, allowing them to adapt to flat wounds or deeply infected cavities. In chronic diabetic wounds, dual-function hydrogel dressings with antibacterial and antioxidative activity accelerated repair in MRSA- or CRPA-infected models, supporting their relevance for wounds in which infection and oxidative stress coexist [77].

Supramolecular hydrogels are self-assembled polymer networks designed for reversible structural recovery.

Their function typically relies on host–guest chemistry, in which a guest molecule reversibly fits within a ring-shaped host molecule, creating strong but reversible noncovalent connections. These interactions generate a three-dimensional mesh that can shear thin during application and then rapidly reform after placement on an irregular wound surface. In practice, this allows syringeable semisolid gels to liquefy during extrusion and resolidify in situ, improving wound conformity without traumatic manipulation. Supramolecular hydrogels may also be delivered as aerosols, topical sheets, or microneedle patches. Because the porous matrix resembles an extracellular scaffold, fibroblasts can migrate from the wound edge toward the center of the gel [53, 78]. Incorporation of arginine–glycine–aspartic acid motifs can further improve fibroblast adhesion and cellular anchorage, while enzymatic degradation allows gradual replacement of the gel by collagen-rich tissue [1, 79].

Nanoparticles

Nanocarriers are widely engineered for topical drug delivery because they exhibit permeability, stability, premature degradation, and local therapeutic efficacy. With respect to AMPs, nanoparticle platforms can improve stratum corneum penetration while reducing systemic exposure through localized delivery. Particles in the 1–100 nm range provide a high surface-to-volume ratio, increasing interactions with peptide payloads, bacterial membranes, and biofilm matrices [104]. Nanoparticles can accommodate hydrophilic and lipophilic therapeutics and can prolong local drug action through their physicochemical tunability [105, 106]. In topical use, they may create a depot effect, in which the drug accumulates within deeper skin layers and is released gradually from a localized reservoir [107, 108]. These properties are relevant for AMPs because peptide therapeutics are vulnerable to enzymatic degradation, rapid clearance, and dose-dependent toxicity in sensitive or inflamed skin. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), which are formulated from endogenous lipids such as phospholipids, triglycerides, and fatty acids, can encapsulate AMPs and protect them from enzymatic breakdown, thereby improving their stability and reducing the need for high local doses. Although surfactants used to stabilize LNP dispersions may cause mild irritation at some concentrations, preclinical in vivo data generally indicate good tolerability on intact and wounded skin [109]. Metallic nanoparticles, including AgNPs, AuNPs, and CuNPs, are also frequently paired with AMPs. AgNPs provide antibiofilm activity partly through ROS generation and oxidative damage to bacterial proteins and membranes, and AMP-modified AgNPs have synergistic antibacterial and antibiofilm effects [49, 110].

AuNPs are stable and relatively nonimmunogenic, which supports their use as biomedical carriers. When conjugated with AMPs, they can improve antibacterial efficacy against drug-resistant P. aeruginosa while maintaining favourable pharmacokinetic behavior, including renal clearance with limited systemic accumulation [49, 111]. CuNPs are less costly and more accessible than silver or gold systems are, and natural stabilizing agents can improve their environmental compatibility. Nanoscale design parameters and engineering principles governing AMP delivery

Biofilm EPS architecture imposes size-dependent diffusion barriers; smaller nanoparticles generally penetrate P. aeruginosa and S. aureus biofilms more effectively than larger particles do. Reducing the nanoparticle size to less than 100 nm has been associated with improved intrabiofilm penetration, increased antimicrobial accumulation, and increased biofilm elimination [130, 131]. The surface charge is equally important. Moderately cationic nanoparticles improve electrostatic interactions with negatively charged bacterial membranes and EPS components, whereas excessive cationic charge may reduce colloidal stability in physiological fluids. pH-cleavable PEG coatings provide a compromise by restoring the cationic charge selectively within acidic infection microenvironments [131].

Encapsulation efficiency and drug loading capacity determine the achievable AMP dose at the wound site. Loading is strongly influenced by electrostatic interactions between cationic AMPs and anionic polymer matrices, so the polymer composition and charge ratio must be optimized rather than treated as formulation details. In the alginate/carboxymethyl chitosan polyelectrolyte systems, the encapsulation efficiency of the dual-complex microcapsules was 95.4%, whereas that of the alginate-only and alginate/carboxymethyl formulations was 86.3% and 68.6%, respectively [132]. These data show that balanced polymer interactions and reduced matrix porosity can substantially improve peptide retention. Solid lipid nanoparticles can further improve stability through crystalline lipid matrices that reduce hydrolytic degradation, although they may provide less loading flexibility than nanostructured lipid carriers do.

AMP release depends on polymer degradation, drug-matrix affinity, diffusion distance, swelling behavior, and cross-link density. The hydrogel swelling ratio and cross-link density strongly influence peptide release, with increased swelling generally prolonging sustained delivery over several days [133]. Stimuli-responsive systems add selectivity by linking release to the wound environment. ROS-responsive thioketal-linked nanocarriers remain relatively stable under physiological conditions but are degraded in oxidative wound microenvironments because thioketal bonds are cleaved by reactive oxygen species such as hydrogen peroxide. An et al. developed a thioketal-containing PEG hydrogel that responded to wound-site ROS concentrations of approximately 100–250 μM H₂O₂, enabling controlled payload release while maintaining stability under normal conditions [134].

Nanocarrier engineering improves AMP antibiofilm efficacy by protecting peptides, increasing biofilm penetration, and prolonging local exposure. For example, the dose required to disrupt P. aeruginosa biofilms decreased from 310 μg/mL for free LL-37 to 103 μg/mL after nanoencapsulation [135]. Metallic nanoparticles, particularly AgNPs, can further increase AMP activity by combining ROS-mediated EPS disruption with increased bacterial membrane permeability. The formulation strategy remains decisive: compared with electrostatic adsorption alone, covalent AMP‒nanoparticle conjugation generally provides greater peptide stability and sustained antimicrobial activity [136].

Clinical translation of AMP nanocarriers remains limited by manufacturing and formulation challenges. Regulatory approval requires tight control of nanoparticle size, surface charge, and encapsulation reproducibility, with low interbatch variability in the polydispersity index. In physiological environments, protein corona formation rapidly alters nanocarrier surface properties and may reduce targeting efficiency; therefore, corona-resistant coatings such as dense PEG or zwitterionic layers are often necessary. Sterilization also presents major challenges, as gamma irradiation and autoclaving can damage polymeric and lipid nanocarriers, making aseptic processing or sterile filtration the primary manufacturing options despite increased cost and scalability constraints.

Liposomes and micelles

Liposomes are spherical vesicles composed of one or more phospholipid bilayers surrounding an aqueous core. Their membrane-like architecture allows hydrophilic compounds to be loaded in the core and lipophilic compounds within the bilayer. Micelles are smaller single-layer assemblies of surfactants or block copolymers with a hydrophobic core and hydrophilic shell, making them useful for solubilizing lipophilic compounds [137–139]. Micelles are typically 5–50 nm long and depend on concentrations above the critical micelle concentration; dilution below this threshold can cause disassembly and premature payload release. Liposomes, by contrast, are typically 50–500 nm in size, and cholesterol incorporation can increase bilayer rigidity, reduce membrane fluidity, and improve colloidal stability [139–141].

Extensive investigations have been carried out on liposomes as AMP carriers. Researchers have capitalized on the bilayer structure of liposomes for membrane-mimetic delivery, which has enhanced stability and superior bactericidal potency against drug-resistant strains. A recent study on liposomes revealed that nanoliposomes with fused AMPs resisted enzymatic degradation and maintained bioactivity in murine wound infection models [142]. Moreover, a novel virus-like AMP-conjugated liposome displayed biofilm disruption and broad antibacterial activity without cytotoxicity. Cationic liposomes, engineered to resemble viruses containing AMPs with a spike-like surface and a lipid bilayer structure, exhibited enhanced fusion with bacterial membranes [143]. Another study used polymerized liposomes as carriers for AMP IG-25 and successfully demonstrated increased bacterial killing through localized peptide clustering and high potency against P. aeruginosa while maintaining its stability and low cytotoxicity [144]. Recently, an AMP-liposome conjugated system formulated for antimicrobial wound treatment for chronic wounds with biofilms was shown to improve antibiofilm action against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus compared with unformulated AMP [67].

Micelle-based AMP delivery systems for topical applications have gained significant attention in recent research. Pegylated phospholipid micelles-AMPs demonstrated targeted effects on MRSA skin infections with abscesses in murine models. After application, the abscess size and bacterial load decreased significantly compared with those of unformulated peptides, while toxicity was mitigated, and self-clustering or aggregation of the peptides was reduced [145]. Cholesterol-modified micelles loaded with AMPs demonstrated immunomodulatory effects in one study, whereby excessive inflammation in chronic wounds was alleviated. These carriers balanced proinflammatory cytokines while increasing chemokine production, preserving effective bacterial clearance [146]. Additionally, hybrid micelle–nanogel systems enabled coencapsulation of AMPs and other antibiotics, resulting in prolonged elution over multiple days and enhanced dermal retention and biofilm infiltration in ex vivo wound models. These platforms can increase bioavailability by up to fivefold, enabling reduced dosing intervals for effective topical management of chronic wounds [147].

Biopolymer Scaffolds

Biopolymer scaffolds provide localized AMP delivery with tunable encapsulation and release. Chitosan and dextran sulfate networks electrostatically trap cationic AMPs, shielding them from protease-rich wound fluid and limiting premature diffusion. Their ECM-like architecture and biomimetic design principles are discussed further in Sect. 3.4. [148, 149]. Release kinetics can be altered by the charge, hydration, and pH responsiveness of the polymer, allowing AMPs to diffuse progressively through the matrix while avoiding the high burst concentrations associated with free peptide application [148]. The porous structure of chitosan-dextran networks also supports tissue penetration and may improve the disruption of bacterial QS and EPS. Microneedles

Microneedles enable painless transdermal delivery by creating micron-scale channels through the skin with limited tissue trauma. These projections, typically 50–900 μm long, bypass cutaneous barriers that restrict hydrophilic macromolecules such as AMPs [160, 161]. Microneedle formats include solid, hollow, coated, dissolving, and hydrogel-forming systems [162]. Dissolving microneedles are particularly common because they can be fabricated from biocompatible polymers such as hyaluronic acid or polyvinylpyrrolidone [163]. After insertion, they dissolve within the epidermal or dermal layers and release encapsulated AMPs at the target site. Bacterial hyaluronidase-responsive dissolving microneedles further improve infection site specificity by enhancing penetration and release in enzyme-rich wound areas [164].

Microneedles can maintain therapeutic exposure by delivering AMPs directly into infected wound beds, which may improve their activity against biofilms in diabetic ulcers and other chronic wounds [165, 166]. In diabetic murine models, AMP delivery through microneedles combined with nanofiber dressings outperformed free AMPs in wounds infected with P. aeruginosa and MRSA biofilms [165]. Compared with topical carriers that rely on passive diffusion, microneedles physically penetrate dense biofilm layers and improve access to microbes embedded within the matrix. Stimuli-responsive and smart-design release systems

Stimuli-responsive systems improve AMP administration by linking release to pathological cues such as pH, oxidative stress, or externally applied energy. Rather than relying solely on passive diffusion, these carriers synchronize AMP deployment with infection-associated conditions, including alkaline pH shifts and elevated ROS levels [169, 170]. This approach can increase local bactericidal activity while reducing nonspecific exposure and resistance pressure. External modalities such as light and ultrasound further allow clinician-controlled dosing, supporting individualized wound care and reducing reliance on systemic antibiotics.

pH-Sensitive carriers

Healthy skin maintains a mildly acidic surface pH of 4.8–6.0 through keratinocyte secretions, supporting barrier function and microbial balance. Changes in wound pH during healing: Acute wounds may initially exceed pH 7.0 because of interstitial fluid leakage but then acidify as healing progresses. Capillary disruption promotes glycolysis, CO₂ retention, and keratinocyte activity, restoring the acid mantle [170, 171]. Chronic wounds, by contrast, often remain alkaline, with reported pH values of approximately 7.42–8.90 [171]. This alkalinization is driven by bacterial urease activity and ammonia production, neutrophil influx, protease and cytokine elevation, ECM degradation, and disturbed ionic homeostasis [1, 172]. Poor vascular supply and hypoxia further limit oxygen delivery and impair the glycolytic and repair responses needed for acidification [173]. The resulting oxygen deficit sustains inflammation, elevates ROS levels, and impairs granulation [174]. Low oxygen tension can also favour anaerobic bacterial growth and amplify urease-mediated ammonia production, reinforcing the alkaline state. When perfusion remains chronically impaired, biofilms persist, and protease activity degrades buffering proteins that normally help maintain pH homeostasis [175, 176]. Restoring or exploiting the wound pH is therefore a rational strategy for AMP delivery, provided that acute and chronic wound states are clearly distinguished.

pH-sensitive carriers use pathological pH shifts to trigger controlled AMP release. Preclinical models suggest that these systems can improve biofilm eradication and wound closure when release thresholds are matched to the wound environment. Polyacid hydrogel pumps, for example, release antibiotic payloads in infected wound models when the pH is above 6.5, making them relevant to both acute and chronic wound conditions [177]. In another study, Schiff base-cross-linked hyaluronic acid hydrogels delivered antibacterial agents under acidic conditions and accelerated the closure of MRSA-infected diabetic wounds [178]. The same principle extends to microneedles: pH-responsive microneedle formulations can direct AMPs into infected wound beds under alkaline conditions while shielding peptides from protease-rich fluid [166].

ROS-responsive systems

ROS are highly reactive oxygen-containing molecules generated by phagocyte NADPH oxidase, mitochondria, and xanthine oxidase during cellular respiration and inflammation. In acute wounds, regulated ROS production supports neutrophil chemotaxis, microbial killing, platelet activation, and neovascularization. Antioxidative enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and catalase then limit excess ROS as wounds progress toward proliferation. In chronic wounds, however, prolonged neutrophil and macrophage infiltration sustains an increase in ROS levels, whereas hypoxia-related signalling and insufficient antioxidant control promote oxidative stress. This damage to ECM components and growth factors favours biofilm persistence and delays repair [179].

ROS-responsive delivery systems exploit oxidative stress to induce AMP release, but their design must account for the damaging effects of ROS on peptides and host tissue. In vivo studies using ROS-responsive nanoparticles coloaded with AMPs and moxifloxacin showed activity against S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, and MRSA biofilms upon exposure to ubiquitinated at ROS-enriched sites after carrier cleavage [180]. Self-assembling peptide systems containing thioketal or disulfide motifs can similarly breakdown into soluble fragments under high-ROS conditions, unmasking hydrophobic AMP domains and improving bacterial membrane disruption and biofilm penetration [181].

Thermal, light, or ultrasound activation

Beyond endogenous cues, exogenous triggers provide clinician-controlled AMP release. Thermal, near-infrared (NIR), and focused ultrasound activation can deliver spatially precise dosing to infected wound regions where MDR pathogens and biofilms limit conventional therapy. AMPs themselves are constrained by short half-lives, instability, and possible nonspecific cytotoxicity; thus, encapsulation within phase-change liposomes, polymeric vesicles, disulfide-linked hydrogels, or photosensitive polymers can provide better spatiotemporal control [182]. Photothermal agents convert light into heat, damaging bacterial membranes and disrupting biofilms, whereas photodynamic agents absorb light to generate ROS that oxidize bacterial membranes, proteins, DNA, and enzymes. Both modalities have limited resistance potential because they impose physical or oxidative damage rather than single-target enzymatic inhibition. Hybrid nanocoatings that combine organic and inorganic components illustrate this approach; PU-Au-PEG nanocomposites under NIR irradiation showed broad antibacterial activity, likely supported by gold nanorod photothermal behavior and PEG-mediated hydrophilicity [183, 184].

Thermal triggers such as hyperthermia can cue thermosensitive phospholipids or lower critical solution temperature (LCST) polymers to collapse and release encapsulated material. For example, the use of thermosensitive liposomes with the LCST polymer PNIPAM allows the utilization of NIR-induced hyperthermia for controlled release and targeted delivery to specific sites [185]. More recent studies have incorporated nanomotors triggered by the NIR to self-propel into tissues and deliver drugs into desired locations. These nanomotors are loaded with the antibacterial enzyme lysostaphin and the NIR photosensitizer indocyanine green (ICG). ICG enables the system to have photothermal and photodynamic properties because of its ability to convert NIR light to heat and form ROS, which have antibacterial effects. On the other hand, lysostaphin acts by MRSA-targeting membrane hydrolysis. Heating via the NIR light creates a temperature gradient and drives the nanomotors through autonomous motion along the gradient. Self-propulsion is critical, as MRSA biofilms act as physical barriers that prevent conventional antibiotics from penetrating [186]. The combination of silver and gold nanosheets has been shown to increase the thermal sensitivity of MRSA infection treatment through photothermal action with NIR. Successful in vivo results revealed that MRSA biofilm eradication was 99.992% efficient [187]. Concerns have been raised concerning how AMPs are loaded into these temperature-responsive systems, as there are risks associated with AMP degradation when the temperature is greater than 60 °C. Photothermal systems mitigate such risks through the use of a few strategies. In general, PTT involves the use of NIR-emitting lasers to raise the temperature to 55 °C to 60 °C for a brief 5 to 10 min at once. Milder PTT aims for temperatures of 41 °C to 43 °C [188]. Short pulses of NIR bursts of 2 to 5 min allow thermally induced liposomes to release AMP LL-37, while the peptide is designed to resist up to 10 min at 60 °C and retain more than 80% bioactivity [189]. Integrating photocleavable linkers into nanomaterials can easily facilitate the spatiotemporal regulation of peptide liberation. NIR or UV light focused with lasers can be focused on specific sites with infectious tissue, activating only nanocarriers in illuminated areas while sparing healthy tissues. Confining AMP action to target biofilms or abscesses can reduce off-target cytotoxicity. Photoisomerization of secondary structures in AMPs that are light driven can also serve as another strategy in drug delivery. By switching secondary peptide structures between trans- or cis-formations, azobenzene-linked peptides can exhibit targeted action at desired locations. Ultrasound-activated drug delivery systems use acoustic energy to trigger release from carriers such as liposomes, microbubbles, micelles, and nanodroplets. These systems can improve delivery into deeper cutaneous layers through pressure oscillation, localized heating, acoustic streaming, and cavitation. Ultrasound transducers typically operate across 0.1–50 MHz and 0.01–10 MPa peak negative pressures, with lower frequencies near 1 MHz favoured for deeper penetration because tissue attenuation increases with frequency. Cavitation is central to many ultrasound-triggered systems: compressible carriers expand and contract during acoustic cycles, and microbubbles act as cavitation nuclei because their gas cores reduce the energy required for bubble oscillation and collapse. Direct ultrasound-triggered microbubble studies for AMP delivery remain limited, but antibacterial biofilm models are informative. Nitric oxide-loaded microbubbles activated by low-frequency ultrasound resulted in more than 90% biofilm dispersal and a 4- to eightfold reduction in the MIC against P. aeruginosa biofilms [194]. Targeted microbubbles also improved adherence to S. aureus biofilms by eightfold when a specific affimer protein was used [195]. Conventional microbubbles can be limited by size-dependent penetration, whereas antibiotic-loaded nanodroplets approximately 125–250 nm in size are smaller than microbubbles 350–650 nm in size and can vaporize into transient microbubbles under focused ultrasound [196]. This phase-change mechanism may improve localized transport, cavitation-mediated biofilm disruption, and payload penetration into EPS-rich matrices (Fig. 8).Fig. 8Ultrasound-triggered drug delivery using hydrogel carriers and the mechanism of inertial cavitation. Ultrasound waves applied to the hydrogel at the treatment site generate oscillating microbubbles that undergo cycles of compression and rarefaction. Once the applied ultrasonic pressure is higher than the threshold of inertial cavitation, the microbubbles expand during the rarefaction phase and subsequently collapse violently (implosion), producing localized mechanical effects that enhance drug release and penetration into the surrounding tissue [197].

Electrical and mechanically activated delivery systems also merit attention because noninvasive transdermal approaches can improve patient comfort and adherence. The main limitation of transdermal therapy is poor permeability through cutaneous layers. Haptic or electromechanical stimulation systems can be programmed to release payloads in response to mechanical deformation or electrical pulses, and touch-activated microneedle patches exemplify this direction. Electrically responsive nanofibers loaded with amoxicillin demonstrated controlled antibiotic release upon stimulation [198]. Electroporation increases permeability when high-voltage pulses of 5–25 kV/cm2 are applied, generating transient hydrophilic pores in lipid bilayers and enabling rapid molecular transport [199]. Although electroporation can increase drug permeability by orders of magnitude, skin irritation from high-voltage exposure limits clinical use. Iontophoresis, by contrast, uses mild electrical currents to drive charged molecules across the skin; antibacterial agents such as amoxicillin and cefuroxime delivered by iontophoresis achieved rapid therapeutic concentrations and sustained action in animal models [200-203].

Biomimetic designs

Biomimetic designs aim to reproduce key structural and functional features of skin so that wound dressings integrate with tissue, limit infection risk, and support AMP delivery. Nanofibrous dressings that emulate native topography improve compatibility with the wound bed while providing a dynamic matrix for sustained payload release. For AMPs, this architecture is useful because antimicrobial action must occur alongside tissue regeneration. Human skin contains stratified epidermal and dermal layers supported by aligned collagen fibres [202]. Biomimetic bilayer dressings reproduce this organization by combining a hydrophobic, bactericidal upper layer that limits moisture loss and pathogen entry with a hydrophilic lower layer that retains moisture and supports cellular infiltration. Electrospun nanofibers resemble dermal collagen and can guide fibroblast chemotaxis, ECM remodelling, and tissue integration [202, 204]. Biomimetic designs of smart wound dressings

Several biomimetic strategies have been explored to improve tissue integration and antibacterial performance. Catechol-functionalized polymers, inspired by mussel adhesion chemistry, provide durable wet adhesion in exudative wound environments and may outperform conventional dressings under moist conditions [212]. Lotus-leaf-inspired microstructures use a hydrophobic surface architecture to restrict bacterial colonization and biofilm initiation [213]. Enzyme-responsive hydrogels cross-linked with MMP-sensitive peptides can undergo localized proteolysis, enabling cell migration and matrix remodelling as healing progresses [202]. Immunomodulatory mimics incorporate components such as lysozyme, which cleaves bacterial peptidoglycan and promotes innate antimicrobial defence while modulating inflammation [214]. Decellularized ECM formulations preserve hepatotactic and proangiogenic motifs that can support neovascularization, and cost-efficient soy protein matrices have been investigated as ECM surrogates capable of activating wound repair signalling [215].

For AMP encapsulation, coaxial electrospinning of core–shell NFs allows loading of AMPs within the core structures, which allows sustained release of the initial antimicrobial action of the shell. The underlying mechanism involves simultaneously spinning two polymer solutions through a concentric nozzle, resulting in an AMP within the core encased in a protective outer shell. The shell delivers an initial antimicrobial burst upon wound contact, whereas the core enables prolonged, diffusion-controlled AMP release over days or weeks. These structures rely on structural mimicry of the bilayer skin membrane with outer epidermal layers for barrier protection paired with hydrophilic inner dermal cores for nutrient storage and controlled elution [202, 216]. Furthermore, inspired by bacterial membranes, biomimetic carriers called bacterial membrane-coated nanoparticles use these pathogen-derived structures to camouflage therapeutic agents and improve their targeting to biofilms, penetration through the ECM and evasion of host immunity for enhanced delivery in infectious wounds [217]. They work by enhancing bacterial recognition, as macrophages are naturally programmed to recognize bacterial antigens through surface receptors. Leveraging the upregulation of macrophage toll-like receptors (TLRs) activated by bacteria, S. aureus macrophage membranes, which are preactivated macrophages, develop, resulting in high levels of TLR-expressing membrane structures. They are subsequently used to coat nanoparticles encapsulated with vancomycin, creating a ROS-responsive targeted delivery system that focuses their antimicrobial action on the infected microenvironment, thereby achieving accurate localized infection eradication [218]. Additionally, macrophage membrane-coated antimicrobial peptide nanopeptides (M-AMPNP) were formulated for enhanced intravenous AMP delivery in vivo. It is possible to apply such nanocarriers transdermally [219]. Future transdermal AMP delivery via biomimetic designs is a potential candidate for use as a next-generation therapeutic. These designs promise noninvasive, sustained release of antibacterial agents while overcoming enzymatic barriers and providing superior bioavailability in wounds.

Integrated sensors

Unlike conventional methods that depend on inconsistent visual assessments, these adaptable, wireless platforms employ new technologies such as ion-sensitive transistors, aptamer-based detectors and near-field communication. Easy viewing of insights can also be provided via mobile applications; static treatments will evolve into targeted, responsive strategies that activate therapies such as antimicrobial peptide deployment as needed. This proactive approach is effective at contributing to faster recovery and reducing wound-related medical expenses [220]. Traditional assessments rely on visual inspection of wounds and infrequent swabbing, delaying interventions and risking complications such as sepsis. Integrated sensors can play a key role in addressing these challenges. Embedding these biosensors into dressings can help wirelessly track changes in the wound microenvironment and around the clock. These systems use flexible electronics, NFC/Bluetooth connectivity, and AI algorithms to deliver actionable data via apps. Sensor-integrated AMP dressings represent an emerging class of smart wound care systems that combine real-time monitoring with targeted drug delivery.

Building on the alkaline wound microenvironment described in Sect. 3.1, real-time pH monitoring provides a continuous proxy for infection severity. Compared with conventional potentiometry, ion-sensitive field-effect transistors (ISFETs) transform shifts in electrolyte ion concentration into measurable current variations, offering higher sensitivity and faster response for detecting minute pH changes in wounds [220, 221]. Temperature-sensitive sensors, also known as thermistors, are only used in clinical applications for wound monitoring, instead of triggering AMP delivery into wounds. Current designs use temperature-responsive polymers or hydrogels for AMP modulation, but thermistors serve only as passive monitoring sensors without feedback loops to trigger release. Soft, intelligent wound dressings were fabricated on stretchable styrene ethylene butylene styrene-integrated commercial negative temperature coefficient sensors (TMP112) for temperature sensing and iridium oxide electrodes for pH monitoring. TMP112 thermistors select wound temperature signals at 10 s intervals to determine whether any hyperthermia is induced by infection. Rat models revealed bacterial metabolic activity as early as 8 h post-infection. These signals are transmitted via Bluetooth and are displayed in mobile applications with live pH/temperature graphs for infection alerting, allowing clinicians to remotely monitor patient wounds. While biocompatible and conformable, the dressing lacks drug reservoirs, limiting it to monitoring rather than closed-loop AMP delivery [222]. However, another study integrated sensing technology into smart hydrogel dressings containing antibiotics, which release their payload in situ upon UV irradiation triggered by elevated wound temperatures, as detected by embedded sensors, making them temperature-responsive drug delivery systems. [223].

Fluorescence-based sensing was also incorporated in an effort to enhance the functionality of smart wound dressings loaded with AMP. Aggregation-induced emission composites are made of tetraphenylene vinyl (TPE) grafted onto polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), which is luminescent when it is in an aggregated state. This property occurs when an increased concentration of PVA-TPE induces high-intensity fluorescent emission, which serves as a signal that allows antimicrobial detection in real time. They used S. aureus as their target pathogen for detection because of its prevalence in wound sites. This design is engineered to provide information on infection conditions while allowing AMPs to be delivered in a controlled manner through grafting with hyaluronic acid [224].

Additionally, inflammatory markers are key stimuli for advanced integrated sensor systems that are engineered to detect cytokines within wound environments, enabling precise monitoring of skin healing progression. Elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha and IL-6, are associated with persistent bacterial burden and neutrophil dominance, whereas increased IL-10 expression indicates M2 macrophage polarization and tissue regeneration [225]. Researchers developed a smart wound patch incorporating an electrochemical sensor printed on a bendable polyethylene terephthalate film via a screen-printing technique. The sensor was tested under various bending conditions, demonstrating its ability to function reliably during mechanical motions in patients. Cyclic peptides and dendrimers

Cyclic peptides and dendrimers address major pharmacokinetic and immunological limitations of linear AMPs. Linear peptides are susceptible to rapid proteolysis, short half-lives, and conformational flexibility that may expose immunogenic epitopes. Cyclization constrains the peptide backbone into a rigid, protease-resistant loop, improving bacterial selectivity, membrane penetration, and host-cell tolerance. Ultrashort cyclic AMPs such as R3W4V demonstrate activity against multidrug-resistant pathogens while mimicking defensin-like stability [228]. Dendrimers provide a complementary strategy through branched, tree-like structures that present multiple AMP motifs at their periphery. This multivalent display increases the avidity of bacterial surfaces and can improve biofilm accumulation more effectively than monovalent peptides can [159]. The dendritic core can also shield payloads and support prolonged topical release. Early AMP dendrimers such as G3KL incorporated into collagen dressings with skin progenitor cells showed bactericidal activity without compromising eukaryotic viability, supporting their potential in infected burns and chronic wounds [203].

Cyclic AMPs such as DP7 and KR-12 combine protease resistance with membrane-disrupting activity, making them suitable for hydrogels and electrospun nanofibers designed for pH- or enzyme-triggered release. Enzyme-sensitive matrices can be cleaved by MMPs or bacterial proteases, releasing peptides at infected sites while limiting exposure elsewhere. In diabetic wound models, cyclic AMPs have been associated with reduced inflammation and increased collagen deposition [27, 155]. KR-12 provides an example of a feedback-compatible peptide: pore formation in bacterial membranes can acidify the local microenvironment, which may protonate matrix sensors or ionizable groups and trigger additional swelling or linker cleavage. Artificial intelligence (AI) is being increasingly used to accelerate AMP discovery and optimization by replacing part of the trial-and-error screening workflow with data-driven sequence design. Generative and predictive models can propose peptide sequences with improved antimicrobial potency, stability, and solubility and lower predicted hemolysis before synthesis. The DeepAMP framework illustrates this approach by using peptide language models trained on large protein databases such as UniProt to generate or refine AMP candidates. In one study, more than 90% of the designed AMPs inhibited gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria [261]. DeepAMP also compared designed peptides from the T1 and T2 series with ciprofloxacin, identifying candidates that reduced S. aureus resistance [261]. More recent models extend this strategy. LLAMP uses the ESM-2 protein language model and MIC-focused prediction to fine-tune candidates against specific bacterial species; it screened 5.5 million sequences and validated peptides with potency comparable to that of clinical-stage antibiotics such as pexiganan [262]. ProteoGPT mined UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot to identify antimicrobial potential in protein sequences not previously categorized as AMPs [263]. BroadAMP-GPT targets ESKAPE pathogens through multitiered screening for stability and toxicity, leading to the identification of AMP S13, which showed stability and wound-healing efficacy in a MRSA-infected murine model [264]. AIPAMPDS combines a GPT-driven sequence design with ProBERT screening and a hemoRisk estimator to assess hemocompatibility [265]. Despite this progress, AI-derived AMPs remain limited by training-data bias toward known structures and by uncertain generalizability in complex wound environments. Future AMP design will therefore require larger, wound specific, and patient-stratified databases linked to formulation and delivery outcomes, not sequence activity alone.

Discussion

Antibiofilm mechanisms

A recurring limitation in AMP treatment of chronic wound biofilms is the tendency to describe EPS only as a physical diffusion barrier. Penetration is important, but biofilm-mediated AMP resistance is also electrochemical and matrix specific. P. aeruginosa EPS is rich in anionic alginate, which can sequester cationic AMPs such as LL-37 at the biofilm periphery before they reach embedded cells [266]. Conversely, S. aureus and S. epidermidis produce cationic poly-N-acetylglucosamine, which can repel cationic AMPs while also sequestering anionic AMPs such as dermcidin. This bidirectional charge-based sequestration is a major weakness of free or unmodified topical AMP delivery. LL-37 can downregulate virulence factors controlled by the Las and Rhl quorum-sensing systems in P. aeruginosa at subminimum inhibitory concentrations, and this effect has been reported to be dose dependent and independent of direct growth inhibition [18]. However, this mechanism likely operates within a narrow concentration window. In protease-rich wounds, LL-37 hydrolysis by P. aeruginosa elastase and aureolysin may prevent sustained QS suppression inside the biofilm. Delivery systems must therefore maintain therapeutic concentrations long enough to prevent partial, subthreshold exposure that could leave the biofilm architecture intact.

AMP antibiofilm mechanisms extend beyond membrane disruption to include EPS modulation and interference with the (p)ppGpp-mediated stringent response that governs bacterial stress adaptation and persister-cell formation. Synthetic peptides 1018, DJK-5, and DJK-6 inhibit biofilm formation and eradicate mature Klebsiella pneumoniae biofilms by preventing the accumulation of the alarmone nucleotides ppGpp and pppGpp [268]. This mechanism is relevant to chronic wounds because persister cells survive antibiotic exposure through metabolic dormancy and stress-response signalling. Delivery systems that sustain AMP exposure throughout the biofilm, rather than producing brief burst release, are better positioned to suppress this refractory population.

This mechanistic insight challenges the prevailing assumption that increasing AMP hydrophobicity or cationic charge density alone will improve biofilm penetration, since EPS composition actively adapts to neutralize both charge polarities. Advanced nanocarrier strategies that temporarily mask AMP charge through pH-cleavable charge-switching coatings designed to restore cationic character only after penetrating the EPS interface represent a more mechanistically grounded approach, although the trade-off between charge masking and bacterial membrane targeting must be carefully characterized under wound-relevant pH and ionic conditions.

Wound microenvironment modulation

Alkaline pH and its consequences for AMP activity

The alkaline pH of chronic wounds, which is typically reported to range from approximately 7.4–8.9, can impair AMP activity through more than conformational destabilization. Protease activity in wound fluid changes with pH: proteases active near physiological pH may become less active during acidification, whereas other enzymes, such as cathepsin D, peak under more acidic conditions [269]. Thus, alkalinity does not simply suppress proteolysis; it can increase the availability of protease conditions that degrade peptide therapeutics in the pH range typical of chronic wounds. Many cationic AMPs also require specific ionization states of lysine and arginine residues for electrostatic engagement with bacterial membranes. Alkaline pH can alter peptide and membrane charge density, reducing membrane insertion and pore formation even when peptides survive proteolysis.

The alkaline microenvironment of chronic nonhealing wounds is associated with poor healing trajectories, particularly when the pH reaches approximately 7.15–8.9. Microbial urease activity releases ammonia and contributes to this alkalinization [270]. The resulting cycle is clinically important: bacterial colonization increases the pH, higher pH reduces AMP function and topical peptide efficacy, and reduced antimicrobial activity permits further bacterial expansion. Carriers that buffer the pH or release acidifying components therefore do more than protect the AMP payload; they modulate the wound chemistry to determine whether the delivered peptide can remain active. This coupling between carrier chemistry and wound pH should be treated as a core design variable rather than an auxiliary feature.

Oxidative stress

Elevated ROS in chronic wounds creates a design paradox. ROS-responsive nanocarriers can use oxidative stress as a release trigger, but the same environment may oxidize AMPs through methionine sulfoxide formation, tryptophan oxidation, or disulfide scrambling [271]. Multifunctional AMPs with intrinsic antioxidant and antimicrobial properties may partly resolve this conflict by regulating cytokines, stimulating keratinocyte migration, and activating epidermal growth factor receptor pathways linked to human beta-defensin-2 induction [272]. Carrier design should therefore distinguish between ROS as a trigger and ROS as a degradation pressure.

The oxidative landscape of chronic wounds also changes over time with immune activity and infection severity. P. aeruginosa can adapt to oxidative stress by upregulating antioxidant defences and lowering local ROS levels while inducing the expression of QS and biofilm genes [267]. This microbial response may weaken ROS-triggered carriers by suppressing the stimulus required for release at the site where treatment is most needed. Consequently, in vitro release thresholds based only on exogenous hydrogen peroxide are insufficient. ROS-responsive platforms should be calibrated against wound-relevant gradients and microbial counter responses.

Impaired macrophage polarization and immunomodulatory misalignment

Macrophage polarization is a key but often underdeveloped dimension of AMP therapy. During physiological healing, wounds transition from an M1 proinflammatory state to an M2 prorepair phenotype within approximately five days, which is supported by the activity of IL-4, IL-13, and IL-10. In chronic wounds, this transition fails, and M1-like macrophages persist beyond the point at which resolution should begin. Hyperglycemia, advanced glycation end products, and sustained proinflammatory signalling can lock macrophages into this phenotype [273]. This creates a barrier for AMP therapy because peptides such as LL-37 may have immunomodulatory effects through the TLR4 and formyl peptide receptor pathways, but these effects can be blunted when target cells remain fixed in a chronic inflammatory state. Antimicrobial depots that ignore this immunological context may reduce bacterial burden without restoring healing competence [274].

To overcome this, future AMP delivery platforms must evolve into immunoregulatory matrices. Rather than relying on simple peptide release, these systems should integrate synergistic therapies, such as M2-polarizing ligands or IL-4 mimetics, to actively restore macrophage balance. Given that macrophage polarization is strongly linked to epigenomic organization, which cannot be reversed by pathogen clearance alone, the integration of these immunomodulatory signals is essential for the progression of chronic, nonhealing wounds toward successful tissue repair.

Diabetic immune dysfunction

Diabetic wounds impose barriers to AMP therapy that are not fully captured by general chronic wound models. Poor healing is associated with persistent inflammation, excessive neutrophil accumulation, macrophage imbalance, and dysregulated T-cell function, reflecting an altered immune architecture rather than simple delay [275]. In this context, immune effector cells may be inflammatory yet functionally impaired. Hyperglycemia and insulin resistance sustain M1 macrophage dominance and lead to the production of neutrophils with impaired chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and bactericidal capacity [276].

Free radicals interacting with proinflammatory cytokines in diabetic wounds promote serine proteinase and MMP production, damaging the ECM components and growth factors required for repair. High-glucose conditions also prolong neutrophil activation and dysregulate apoptosis, increasing neutrophil extracellular trap formation and NETosis. NETs are relevant to AMP delivery because extracellular DNA‒histone complexes can electrostatically sequester cationic AMPs in the wound bed, similar to EPS-mediated charge trapping [277]. This is a diabetes-specific barrier that is mechanistically distinct from proteolysis or alkaline pH alone.

Reduced endogenous AMP production in diabetes further compounds this challenge. Hyperglycemia suppresses keratinocyte expression of human beta-defensin-2 and cathelicidin, resulting in exogenous AMP delivery to compensate for a weakened host peptide defense. Diabetic foot ulcers also involve peripheral neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, chronic inflammation, and impaired regeneration, creating a wound environment that is hostile to therapeutics and unable to achieve normal repair [278]. Clinical translation

Peptide instability and the proteolytic microenvironment of chronic wounds

Clinical translation of AMPs remains constrained by technical, biological, and regulatory barriers. Proteolytic instability is among the most important limitations, particularly in diabetic foot ulcers and other enzyme-rich chronic wounds. Prolonged inflammation leads to the recruitment of polymorphonuclear neutrophils that release MMP-2, MMP-8, MMP-9, and human neutrophil elastase, whereas MDR biofilms secrete bacterial proteases such as aureolysin and elastase that neutralize host defense peptides. Linear L-amino acid AMPs are vulnerable to rapid hydrolytic cleavage in this environment, limiting antibacterial and immunomodulatory action before therapeutic concentrations can be sustained. This mismatch between in vitro potency and in vivo stability has contributed to the clinical failure of peptide candidates such as pexiganan, iseganan, Neuprex, and omiganan in phase III trials [279]. Engineering strategies attempt to overcome this barrier by reducing protease recognition. Cyclization removes flexible terminal regions and constrains the backbone, D-amino acid or retro-inverse substitutions to create protease-resistant peptidomimetics, and dendritic AMP architectures provide steric protection while maintaining high local cationic charge density for membrane disruption [279].

The protein corona is another underappreciated variable in AMP nanocarrier translation. Once carriers contact protein-rich wound fluid, adsorbed biomolecules such as albumin and fibrinogen can mask engineered surface chemistry and redefine the biological identity of the carrier. For cationic nanocarriers, corona formation may neutralize the charge needed for bacterial membrane targeting and can increase the hydrodynamic diameter, reducing EPS penetration. PEGylation can limit protein adsorption but may also weaken the electrostatic interactions required for bacterial targeting. Future designs should therefore move beyond universal PEGylation toward zwitterionic coatings or biomimetic surfaces that preserve targeting under wound-relevant conditions [280].

Large-scale manufacturing, batch-to-batch reproducibility, and cost

Advanced biohybrid wound dressings face substantial manufacturing barriers because their biological and synthetic components vary in structure, processing requirements, and cost. Natural biomatrices such as porcine acellular dermal matrix (PADM) are valued for their native ECM architecture, but their preparation requires multistep decellularization, lyophilization, pulverization, enzymatic digestion, osmotic adjustment, and neutralization [281]. Donor age, health status, and tissue origin can alter fibre density, mechanical elasticity, and ligand display, making the degradation rate and drug release difficult to standardize across lots. Synthetic hydrogels offer more structural precision but may require labor-intensive synthesis and chemical cross-linkers such as glutaraldehyde, acrylic acid, or glutaric acid, which can be cytotoxic or difficult to remove from the matrix [281]. Inorganic and composite alternatives create additional scale-up problems: mesoporous bioactive glasses may be mechanically fragile; electrospinning is sensitive to variations in voltage, temperature, humidity, and flow rate; and polymer‒nanoparticle composites require tight control of degradation and ion release [282]. Highly branched systems such as poly(beta-amino ester)s add further complexity through multistep reaction chemistry and difficult topology control. Together, these requirements increase the cost of goods for cyclic peptides, dendrimers, inorganic dopants, and high-purity matrices, limiting commercial feasibility unless manufacturing is simplified and standardized [283].

Sterilization and storage stability of biohybrid systems

Sterilization and storage stability are major constraints for biohybrid AMP platforms. Autoclaving is unsuitable because heat and pressure can denature ECM proteins such as PADM and gelatin, degrade polyesters such as PLGA and PLA, and reduce peptide integrity. Ethylene oxide prevents thermal damage but diffuses slowly through porous matrices and may leave toxic residues such as ethylene chlorohydrin, necessitating prolonged outgassing. Ionizing radiation, including gamma and electron-beam sterilization, is effective against microbes but generates ROS through water radiolysis, promoting polymer chain scission and peptide backbone cleavage. These changes can affect the rheology, adhesion, and release kinetics, often increasing premature burst release [284].

Long-term biosafety and systemic toxicology of metallic nanoparticles

Metallic nanoparticles and bioactive glasses provide antimicrobial and immunomodulatory benefits, but their clinical utility depends on controlling ion release and systemic exposure. If silver, copper, zinc, or magnesium ions accumulate faster than local clearance permits, they can become cytotoxic to keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and immune cells. Transition metals may amplify ROS production, causing lipid peroxidation, protein denaturation, DNA damage, and cell death through apoptosis, necrosis, or ferroptosis. Even magnesium-based materials can cause localized toxicity when rapid degradation releases magnesium and hydroxide ions, increasing the pH above 9.0 and generating hydrogen gas that disrupts osmotic balance and wound repair [285].

Regulatory barriers

Biosensor-integrated smart dressings represent an emerging class of closed loops that combines real-time diagnostics with on-demand drug release, but their clinical deployment faces regulatory, power, and data security barriers. From a regulatory perspective, a dressing that senses pH or cytokines and autonomously triggers AMP release functions as both a diagnostic device and a drug delivery system. This dual role creates a combination-product pathway in which validation of algorithmic release thresholds remains poorly defined [286]. International requirements are similarly layered, and harmonized standards for autonomous drug-releasing sensor dressings have not yet been established [287]. Developers will need prospective clinical evidence showing that trigger thresholds are safe across diverse wound microenvironments, comorbidities, and patient populations. The power supply is another limitation: continuous sensing, wireless transmission, and actuation are difficult to sustain for weeks to months with current flexible batteries. Energy-harvesting approaches, including triboelectric nanogenerators, wound-metabolite biofuel cells, and thermoelectric modules, remain under investigation but have not yet demonstrated consistently sufficient output under realistic dressing conditions [288]. Frequent dressing changes further risk data loss, device resetting, or incomplete dose delivery. Data security adds a third barrier because wound pH, temperature, and cytokine data transmitted to mobile or cloud platforms constitute sensitive physiological information. Wireless transmission can be vulnerable to interception, and cloud storage must comply with jurisdiction-specific privacy laws such as the HIPAA and the General Data Protection Regulation [289]. Closed-loop systems must also provide auditable logs explaining when and why AMP release was triggered. Until regulatory pathways, power architecture, and security standards mature together, autonomous AMP-releasing dressings will remain difficult to translate.

Future directions

Future AMP delivery platforms should increasingly integrate antimicrobial activity with stage-specific immune modulation. Huo et al. developed a PADM hydrogel containing copper-zinc-codoped bioactive glass nanoparticles for MRSA-infected wounds [290]. Sequential ion release enabled early Cu2⁺-associated antibacterial macrophage activity and delayed Zn2⁺-associated attenuation of prolonged inflammation, supporting the transition toward an M2 reparative phenotype. This spatiotemporal pattern aligns with the inflammatory-to-repair transition that is often disrupted in chronic wounds.

Similarly, Bao et al. developed a PADM hydrogel incorporating magnesium-doped MBGs coloaded with curcumin [281]. Curcumin provided adjunct antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects during the early inflammatory stage, whereas sustained Mg2⁺ release promoted the release of proregenerative mediators, including VEGF and transforming growth factor-beta, supporting M2-like macrophage polarization and angiogenesis. Together, these studies show that controlled ion release can regulate macrophage behavior across wound-healing stages.

These findings are highly relevant to AMP-based delivery systems. While many AMP nanocarriers focus primarily on antibacterial efficacy, bioactive glass-based systems provide additional programmable immunomodulatory functions. Therefore, incorporating ions such as Mg2⁺ or Zn2⁺ into AMP delivery matrices represents a promising strategy for simultaneously controlling infection and restoring immune healing competence in chronic wounds.

The importance of stage-specific biomaterial design has also become increasingly recognized. Recent studies have demonstrated that therapeutic efficacy depends not only on total bioactive release but also on temporal synchronization with wound-phase biology [281, 290, 291]. Compared with conventional burst-release AMP formulations, sequential-release systems capable of delivering an early antibacterial response followed by later immunoregulatory signalling may therefore provide superior outcomes, particularly in diabetic wounds where hyperglycemia disrupts the normal M1-to-M2 macrophage transition. Consequently, next-generation AMP delivery platforms will likely require combined antimicrobial and immune-regulatory functionality to address both infection and impaired wound healing simultaneously.

Conclusion

Topical AMP delivery systems offer a rational strategy for chronic wound therapy by addressing infection, biofilm persistence, and antimicrobial resistance while supporting tissue repair. Conventional antibiotics are increasingly limited by MDR pathogens such as MRSA and CRPA and by biofilm-associated inflammation that delays progression through hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodelling. AMPs counter these barriers through membrane disruption, QS interference, and biofilm dispersal, and their lower resistance profile is linked to the fitness cost of bacterial membrane remodelling. Carrier systems extend this potential: hydrogels maintain moisture, protect peptides from proteases, and provide sustained release; nanoparticles improve penetration and depot formation; metallic platforms can increase ROS-mediated antibiofilm activity; and liposomes or micelles provide membrane-mimetic encapsulation for resistant biofilm environments.

Stimuli-responsive platforms increase delivery precision by exploiting wound-associated cues such as alkaline pH, elevated ROS levels, temperature changes, light exposure, and ultrasound activation. pH-sensitive hydrogels and MNs can respond to bacterial urease activity and protease-rich alkaline microenvironments, whereas ROS-cleavable systems use oxidative stress to trigger local peptide release. External triggers, including NIR-responsive nanocarriers, photocleavable linkers, and ultrasound-activated nanodroplets, may improve penetration into EPS-rich biofilms. Biomimetic bilayer nanofibers, wet-adhesive catechol materials, and enzyme-responsive scaffolds further connect antimicrobial activity with regenerative processes such as fibroblast migration, angiogenesis, and collagen deposition.

Integrated sensors extend these approaches toward closed-loop wound care. ISFETs can detect pH fluctuations associated with infection, thermistors can monitor infection-related hyperthermia, and cytokine sensors targeting IL-6 or IL-10 can provide information on inflammatory status. When combined with cyclic peptides, dendrimers, microneedles, or hydrogel reservoirs, these sensing platforms could support feedback-controlled AMP delivery that limits overuse and toxicity. However, the evidence remains predominantly preclinical, and closed-loop therapeutic claims should be interpreted cautiously until human validation is available.

Important translational barriers remain. Proteolytic instability, dose-dependent metallic nanoparticle toxicity, scalability of electrospinning and dendrimer synthesis, sterilization-related payload degradation, and limited storage stability all restrict clinical adoption. Sensor-integrated dressings additionally face wireless integration, power supply, data security, and regulatory challenges. Most current evidence comes from murine or ex vivo models, with relatively few AMP platforms entering advanced clinical testing. Future work should prioritize clinically relevant diabetic ulcer and burn models, standardized antibiofilm endpoints, scalable manufacturing, and patient-centered formats such as painless microneedles and low-cost biopolymer dressings. With these constraints addressed, topical AMP delivery systems could contribute to proactive and personalized wound care by combining infection control, regenerative support, and reduced antimicrobial resistance pressure.
