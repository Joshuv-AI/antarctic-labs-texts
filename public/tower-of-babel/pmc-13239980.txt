CIC–DUX4 Rearranged Gastric Sarcoma Confirmed by Next‐Generation Sequencing: A Case Report and Review of Literature
Authors: Hiroko Hasegawa, Atsushi Takeno, Rei Higashiura, Yuko Sakakibara, Mizuki Koba, Yumiko Hirose, Kiyoshi Mori, Motohiro Hirao, Masayuki Mano, Ryotaro Sakamori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case highlights that gastric CIC‐DUX4 rearranged sarcoma is highly aggressive and diagnostically challenging. Early comprehensive genomic profiling was essential for confirming this rare subtype. Despite transient responses to Ewing sarcoma regimens, rapid chemoresistance was observed, emphasizing the need for precise molecular diagnosis and novel therapeutic approaches.Keywords: CIC‐DUX4 rearrangement, comprehensive genomic profiling, sarcoma, stomach

Introduction

Next‐generation sequencing (NGS) testing based on a comprehensive genomic profile (CGP) is rapidly becoming standard in clinical practice for patients with various types of cancer. Recently, CGP testing has been gradually evaluated and accepted as an effective approach to improve the clinical course, provide an accurate diagnosis of sarcoma subtype, and predict prognosis with the ability to screen for a wide range of genetic aberrations in one test in clinical practice [1].

Among many types of sarcoma, sarcoma with rearrangement of CIC (a human homolog of Drosophila capicua) to DUX4 (double homeobox 4) as a result of t (4;19)(q35;q13) is a recently classified subtype of undifferentiated small round cell sarcoma with an aggressive nature showing partial morphological similarities to Ewing sarcoma [2]. Previously, we reported a case of gastric sarcoma that underwent surgical gastrectomy, diagnosed through only immunohistochemistry (IHC) testing [3]. In this report, we detail the further analysis of the CGP test from the resected specimen, which identified the presence of the CIC‐DUX4 rearrangement. Additionally, we describe the clinical course and assess the efficacy of chemotherapy in treating CIC‐DUX4 rearranged gastric sarcoma.

Case Presentation/Examination

A 48‐year‐old Japanese male presented to our department with a one‐week history of left lower abdominal pain. He had undergone total gastrectomy for a huge gastric sarcoma [3]. On admission, physical examination revealed a firm, immobile mass in the left flank with mild tenderness, along with reported pain in the left lumbosacral region. Although the patient remained afebrile, he exhibited significant anorexia due to abdominal pressure from the mass and had experienced a rapid 4 kg weight loss over the two‐month postoperative period. These clinical findings strongly suggested an aggressive intra‐abdominal recurrence.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

Abdominal CT scan showed multiple tumors with a maximum diameter of 8 × 5 cm at the hepatic hilus, left retroperitoneum, and beneath the left diaphragm (Figure 1a,b), and he was diagnosed with an intra‐abdominal recurrence of gastric sarcoma. We conducted CGP testing using FoundationOne CDx for precise diagnosis and for selecting an optimal treatment strategy. CGP analysis revealed the existence of CIC‐DUX4 rearrangement with low tumor mutational burden (0 mutations/Mb), and no other gene alterations were found. The final diagnosis of CIC‐rearranged sarcoma was made based on the overall pathological findings, including the results of immunohistochemical staining from the previous surgical specimen, as well as the CGP analysis (Figure 2), confirming the CIC‐DUX4 rearrangement. Due to the tumor's enlargement, the patient underwent palliative chemotherapy based on an Ewing sarcoma regimen because no gene mutations related to eligibility for targeted therapy in clinical trials were found. The patient was administered the vincristine, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide (VDC) regimen.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

After one course of chemotherapy, the size of the recurrent tumor mass slightly decreased (Figure 3). However, after three courses, the patient experienced rapid tumor progression (Figure 3). He received subsequent palliative chemotherapy with the ifosfamide and etoposide (IE) regimen; however, the tumor progression couldn't be stopped. As the patient's performance status gradually deteriorated, the treatment was shifted to the best supportive care. The patient died 6 months after recurrence. CIC‐rearranged sarcoma is rare and progresses rapidly, requiring early and precise diagnosis. The molecular approach by the CGP test is a valuable tool not only for understanding the genetic background but also for improving clinical management. Since there is no standard treatment, further research and accumulation of CGP data are needed to develop effective therapies.

Discussion

This was a rare case of CIC‐DUX4 rearranged gastric sarcoma, which was revealed by NGS‐based CGP testing in a 48‐year‐old male patient. CIC‐DUX4 rearranged sarcoma accounts for < 1% of all sarcomas [4]. Generally, sarcomas are most common in the extremities. Therefore, CIC‐rearranged sarcomas originating from the abdominal organs are very rare, and until now, only 2 cases have been reported from the gastric wall (Table 1). In clinical practice, reverse transcription‐polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assay is generally used as a diagnostic tool to detect genetic fusion; however, a false negative rate was reported to be approximately 20% in patients with CIC‐rearranged sarcoma [13]. Lam SW et al. reported that the NGS assay showed superior results compared with conventional techniques such as RT‐PCR and FISH [14]. Recently, genomic profiling by NGS has been beneficial as a detection tool for somatic driver mutations and also as a more precise diagnostic tool for evaluating the genetic background of tumors in clinical practice. Therefore, NGS is recommended and has recently been widely used to diagnose subtypes of sarcoma for cost‐effectiveness and specificity [1]. Until now, only 4 cases showed the results of targeted NGS analysis for CIC‐DUX4 rearranged sarcoma (Table 2), and the result of the CGP test in CIC‐DUX4 rearranged sarcoma originating from the stomach was only our case. Tumor mutation burden in CIC‐DUX4 sarcoma was low, as with previous reports [7, 11, 15, 16].

Several alternative fusion partners of the CIC gene, including NUTM1, NUTM2A, FOXO4, and LEUTX, have been identified [17]. Among these, CIC–DUX4 fusion is the most prevalent and has been reported in literature reviews to be associated with the highest rate of metastasis [18], potentially contributing to the poor prognosis observed in affected patients.

Preclinical studies have shown that the CIC–DUX4 fusion gene functions as a transcriptional activator rather than a repressor [6]. More than 90% of tumors harboring the CIC‐DUX4 fusion gene exhibit upregulation of ETV4 expression by IHC [19], and our case similarly demonstrated strong ETV4 expression [3]. High ETV4 expression has been correlated with poor disease‐specific survival and chemoresistance in various cancers, including sarcomas [20], which may explain the early recurrence and chemoresistance observed in our case.

Currently, CIC‐rearranged sarcomas are commonly treated using Ewing sarcoma regimens because of their histological similarities. However, their prognosis remains significantly worse than that of other small round cell sarcomas, mainly due to aggressive growth and poor response to chemotherapy [4].

In the present case, treatment was initiated according to the Ewing sarcoma protocol. The size of the recurrent tumor showed a slight decrease after the first course of the VDC regimen. However, after three courses, the tumor demonstrated chemoresistance and rapid regrowth, reflecting the highly aggressive nature of this disease. Additionally, no druggable genomic alterations were identified by CGP testing.
