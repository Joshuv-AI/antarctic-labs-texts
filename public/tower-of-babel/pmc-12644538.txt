Pore plate sensilla scale and distribution modulate odor capture around honey bee antennae
Authors: Derek Goulet, Brian Smith, Aaron True, John Crimaldi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsects increase fitness by extracting chemical odor information from the environment using chemoreceptors on antennae that exhibit diverse morphology across species. Honey bees have rod-like antennae with three segments. The most distal segment is the flagellum, which includes pore plates with olfactory receptor neuron dendrites that contain odor receptors. Many studies have examined flow and odor dynamics around antennal structures with simplified sensillae; however, no extant studies resolve pore plate scale fluid processes along the antennal surface. Here, we numerically modeled honey bee antennae in odorized flow fields to evaluate effects of pore plate scale and distribution on fluid drag forces and odor capture rates. For a range of wind speeds and pore plate sizes, we find that boundary layer development enhances odor fluxes preferentially to leading edge pore plate sensilla. This could explain observations of increased pore plate densities along the leading edge of honey bee antennae. Furthermore, we find an asymptotic limit on odor capture rates for a fixed antennal size for dense pore plate sensilla; these pore plates ultimately compete for a finite supply of odor. Larger antennae capture more odor but do so at an energetic cost due to increased drag forces. Our findings can guide both bio-inspired design principles of robotic olfaction and experimental efforts aimed at studying insect odor perception.Subject terms: Biological physics, Ecology, Engineering

Introduction

Insect antennae are multifunctional sensory organs that extract chemical information from the environment for tasks such as locating predators, finding mates, and foraging for food1–3. The environment around insects is comprised of air flow and odor fields, with flow conditions that range from laminar within hives to turbulent in foraging contexts. These flows transport and stretch filaments that contain volatile odor ligands4–6. Differences in flow conditions and filamentous structures control how organisms perceive odors7,8, where organisms may rely more on the spatial structure of odor while walking and the temporal structure while flying9. Insects have diverse antennal morphologies across taxa that further modify flow and odor structures to extract chemosensory information10,11. These morphologies, having evolved from environmental and selective pressures12, include long thread-like antennae in cockroaches, feather-like plumose antennae with hairs in male mosquitoes and moths, and rod-like geniculate antennae in bees. All antennae share the same three basic segments: the scape at the base attaches the antenna to the head; the pedicel contains musculature that allows for movements of the antennae; and the flagellum contains many sensory structures that support different sensory modalities.

The odor transport through fluid flows to antennal structures dictates odor capture rates. Early studies on the interactions between antennae and flow have shown that flow speeds are reduced through arrays of hairs on feather-like morphologies13. Flow penetration through these morphologies has been quantified as a function of wind speed and density of hairy arrays14. Wing flapping further redirects odor filaments toward the antennae15, for which advective fluxes in odorized flow fields bring odor near the antenna while diffusion transports odors to sensillae on the surface. Models of simplified sensory structures show that odor capture is sensitive to flight speed16, angle of hairs17, distance between hairs18, and flow induced by wing beating19. Plumose antennae contain sensory structures on long hairs branching off the flagellum, whereas geniculate antennae have olfactory sensory structures on the flagellum surface known as pore plates that may modulate odor capture differently.

Studies on geniculate antennae have shown that antennae act as a physical filter and modify spatiotemporal structures of odor filaments20. Increasing oscillation frequency of geniculate antennae initially enhances odor capture but ultimately results in oversampling of chemical information21. Despite resolving antennal-scale processes with simplified sensillae, studies have yet to examine pore plate sensilla processes that govern how sensory structures on the antennal surface actively shape local flow and odor fields during sensing. These factors can potentially affect odor detection and localization. Investigating how pore plates affect odor capture could provide insight into evolutionary adaptations and design of unique antenna morphologies in diverse environments.

We use the honey bee (Apis mellifera) as a model for understanding how interactions between antennae and surrounding odorized flow fields at the pore plate sensilla scale affect odor capture and the energetic cost of sensing (Fig. 1a), using the drag force around an antenna as a proxy for metabolic demand. Honey bees have geniculate antennae, in which the flagellum is made up of 10 or 11 segments for females and males, respectively (Fig. 1b). Different types of sensory structures along the flagellum house dendrites of sensory neurons that represent different sensory modalities. Honey bee antennal segments have pore plates, each of which houses dendrites of several olfactory receptor neurons, as well as other sensory structures thought to be mostly mechanosensory known as hairs (Fig. 1c), albeit some may be olfactory22,23. The dendrites within pore plates express olfactory receptors that bind volatile odor molecules in the insect’s environment and transduce those ligands into electrical information sent to the central nervous system24. Pore plates are oval depressions in the cuticle surface surrounded by a rim-like boundary. Inside the boundary, and distributed around the annular region of the plate, are small pores through which ligands gain access to the aqueous medium that bathes the dendrites. Ligands are picked up by binding proteins as odor reaches the fluid exposed in the pores of the pore plate, and they are transported across the aqueous medium to olfactory receptors on the dendrites of receptor neurons.

Many studies, which are too numerous to comprehensively review here, have investigated different processes along the odor source-to-olfactory receptor pipeline: this includes the transport and dispersion of odors through the environment4,6, interactions with odorized flow fields at the antennal-scale with simplified sensilla15,21, how sensilla respond to odor from a molecular and physiological basis24,25, and transduction of ligands into electrical impulses sent to the brain26,27. However, the interaction between odorized flow fields and pore plate sensilla on the antenna, which leads to transport of odor ligands to the fluid interface in the pore plate, has been less investigated despite being an essential part of the transduction process. We develop a numerical model to study key interactions between a honey bee antenna and representative odorized flow fields at the pore plate sensilla scale. We focus on evaluating effects of wind speed and pore plate scale and distribution on odor capture rates and fluid drag forces. We show that sharp gradients of concentration near the leading edge of the antenna enhance odor fluxes to pore plates placed there. Our results reveal a competition for odor between pore plate areas that bounds odor capture for a fixed antennal size. These results may be relevant to many insects with geniculate antennae such as bees, ants, or wasps. However, extending studies at the pore plate scale to different antennal morphologies such as plumose antennae can more broadly inform principles of bio-inspired robotic olfaction and may shed light on other evolutionary adaptations.

We can observe olfactory limitations by comparing odor transduction times of olfactory receptor neurons (ORNs) with diffusive timescales of odorants. ORNs on the honey bee antenna have approximately a 2 ms transduction time to incoming odor ligands26. For the highest wind speeds studied, diffusion of an odor ligand through the concentration boundary layer (right, Fig. 2b) to the pore plate would be less than 1 ms on the leading half of the antenna. Thus, a honey bee antenna should maximize pore plate area with many ORNs to avoid missing any chemical information that cannot be processed within the relatively short diffusive timescale of the odor ligands. This spatial configuration would ideally enhance odor capture rates but limit relative odor fluxes to efficiently process chemical cues in diverse environmental conditions. Reduced relative odor fluxes would additionally allow honey bees to discriminate between different odors needed for source localization38 as ORNs can effectively transduce distinct signals. Honey bee antennae have pore plate area spatial configurations that capture near the possible limit of odor transported to the pore plates with packing densities that reduce relative odor fluxes, potentially balancing transduction times of ORNs with diffusive timescales of odorants to efficiently sense.

Thus, a larger antenna with increased sensing area would capture more odor compared to a smaller antenna. However, honey bees have evolved antennal sizes to balance a range of physical tasks that have a metabolic cost such as somatosensation, locomotion, and odor-driven antennal movements.

This work illustrates that honey bees may have adapted antennal sizes that compromise some odor capture to reduce energetic cost and properly balance all physical tasks necessary for survival.

Our findings highlight the importance of studying pore plate sensilla scale processes that modulate odor capture rates in insect olfaction. Our results suggest that honey bees may have evolved antennal sizes with reduced odor capture rates compared to larger antennae to limit the metabolic cost of sensing and balance other physical demands. We further highlight that honey bees may have evolved packing densities that overcome transduction time limitations by exhibiting near maximal total odor capture rates with reduced relative fluxes of odor for a given antennal size. Densely packed pore plates on the leading edge of the antenna enhance odor capture and can guide spatial configurations of olfactory sensors on robotic platforms. This study underscores that there is a trade-off between total odor capture rates, energetic costs, and relative odor fluxes in insect olfaction that should be considered when studying odor capture of other organisms on the pore plate sensilla scale.

Discussion

We numerically modeled honey bee antennae to explore an important but under-investigated aspect of odor capture: the role of pore plate sensilla scale interactions with odorized flow fields. Our modeling approach isolated the effects of pore plate area and antennal radius on odor capture under steady flow conditions to draw meaningful insights into pore plate sensilla scale odor capture mechanisms. Pore plate areas were placed on a uniform grid along the antennal surface, which allowed us to observe the effects of pore plate location on odor capture. We demonstrated that pore plate areas on the leading edge captured up to four times more odor than those on the trailing edge, suggesting that locating pore plates on the leading edge is optimal for odor capture. These results aligned well with observations that honey bees along with other organisms with antennae have preferential placement of pore plate areas on the leading edge of the antenna34,37. Our findings shed light on the potential evolutionary advantage of this preferential placement and underscore the importance of considering pore plate area locations for experimental designs aimed at understanding the physical, physiological, and neural bases of odor reception and encoding.

We also showed that honey bee antennae have pore plate sizes with near maximal total odor capture rates for a fixed antennal radius. This configuration avoids any diminished returns on odor capture from larger pore plates that have increased competition for odor. We further found through geometrically scaled models that honey bees may have evolved antennal sizes that balance total odor capture rates with energetic costs of sensing to manage the demands of other physical tasks needed for survival. Larger antennae could increase total odor capture rates but may disrupt the finely tuned balance of other evolutionary adaptations in honey bees, such as body or wing size, by having increased drag forces that reduce performance.

As we have shown, increasing wind speeds increase total odor capture rates by reducing velocity and concentration boundary layer thicknesses, sustaining stronger gradients of odor concentration normal to the pore plates that enhance diffusive fluxes. Other mechanisms that increase the velocity of odorized flows relative to the antennal surface can also similarly enhance odor capture. For example, bees can generate relative wind speeds by flying, antennal movements associated with active sensation, or through wing beating that draws air past the antennae40. When honey bees are flying, antennae are held in a relatively fixed position to the sides of the head, with some movement driven by optic flow41. Classical forward surge and back-and-forth casting while flying help insects, including bees, locate sources of odor plumes. In these contexts, having pore plate sensilla distributed toward the leading edge37 may further aid in behavioral decisions as the direction of incoming odor can be recognized based on antennal position, potentially being an adaptive benefit for source localization. It is possible that flight speed and wing beat frequency are optimized to maximize odor sensing42 but are also important for other modalities. Flying bees also use body movement and motion parallax to regulate flight speed43, which is an important determinant of distance estimation44. Optimal speeds for olfaction might therefore interact with the need to monitor a steady optic flow.

Honey bees spend most of their lives walking in the colony when performing a variety of activities involved in nest maintenance and defense, and also while walking during foraging for floral resources or water. In these cases, and in contrast to flying, body motion does not generate much relative flow of air over the antennae. Instead, honey bees move their antennae in characteristic patterns driven by the presence or absence of odor and what they have learned about the meaning of that odor45. These active sensing movements generate flow around the antenna, which further modify the odorized flow fields and alter the perception of chemical cues depending on how the sensors are moved. Walking insects equipped with flagellar antennae are shown to perform the same antennal movement behaviors46, suggesting that many organisms may employ these active sensing schemes to modulate environmental conditions, optimizing odor capture in a range of diverse odorized flow fields.

Our work fits into a broader array of studies of the physical interactions between odorized flows and antennae in aquatic and terrestrial environments. In both water and air, odors are transmitted by environmental flows to organisms in diverse odor landscapes47. However, less diffusive odors in water and differences in fluid properties may alter effective antennal sensing schemes in aqueous versus terrestrial contexts. For example, comparative work has shown that aquatic organisms alter the structure of the odorized flow field to extract chemosensory information differently than terrestrial organisms48. These differences may be driven both by diversity in physical environments and antennal morphologies that affect pore plate sensilla scale processes.

In aquatic contexts, studies have focused on the interactions between arrays of hairs on antennae and odorized flow fields49. Many aquatic organisms extract chemical information effectively by flicking their antennae in a pattern of quick downstrokes followed by slower return strokes50,51. These movements modulate the Reynolds number and modify the boundary layers around the arrays of hairs52. The fast downstrokes reduce boundary layer thicknesses and increase leakiness, a measure of fluid flow through hairy arrays that varies with Reynolds number and hair density53,54. Conversely, slower return strokes increase the boundary layer thicknesses and reduce leakiness to give odor ligands time to diffuse to pore plates as the antenna returns to its original position55.

In addition to the flicking behaviors used by many aquatic organisms, terrestrial organisms employ other active sensing schemes such as wing-driven fanning of odorized flow past pore plates, flying, or antennal movements56. Some terrestrial species flick their antennae in a similar manner to aquatic counterparts but rely more on stronger diffusion in air to quickly transport odor across boundary layers and less on the entrainment of odor within hairy arrays57. Thus, stronger diffusion in air makes leakiness and other flow metrics that are used to predict odor capture in water a weaker predictor of odor capture in air18. Instead, the distance between hairs may better predict odor capture in air; hairs placed further apart sample larger areas with stronger diffusion transporting odor to the pore plates in less time than in water. Future efforts may need to compare pore plate sensilla scale odor capture across a wide range of antennal structures to better understand how evolutionary adaptations of diverse morphologies can influence olfaction and bio-inspired design in different environments.

Our modeling efforts on odor capture at the pore plate sensilla scale can guide design principles for bio-inspired, olfactory robotic platforms suitable for diverse environments. A robust range of olfactory sensors has been developed to detect odors using different sensing modalities58,59. If one is to optimize the performance of an array of these sensors, like current platforms used in robotic olfaction60, the placement of the sensors should be considered as their spatial configurations can significantly influence odor capture rates. This work shows that olfactory sensors positioned densely and on the leading edge of a platform would increase odor capture rates; however, densely packed sensors can also compete for available odors and modulate signals received from the environment. Just as honey bees incur metabolic costs for sensing, similar energetic costs of these advanced platforms can be calculated to determine power limitations. Additional robotic advancements could also place sensors on antennal-like platforms that utilize similar active sensing schemes as honey bees to further enhance odor capture and actively adapt to diverse environmental conditions.

Methods

Honey bee parameterization

To quantify the parameters of a honey bee, we used available scanning electron microscopy images23,28 of honey bee antennae. We used ImageJ61 to extract physical dimensions from images, where we measured antennal radius , pore plate radius , and distance between pore plates multiple times over different images and took the average of each quantity to approximate a representative honey bee antenna. We found that a honey bee has average dimensions of = 105 m, = 6.5e-6 m, and = 18.0e-6 m, where odor capture occurs in the outer 65% of pore plates. These physical parameters served as the baseline dimensions for our numerical model.

Computational simulations

We performed steady-state numerical simulations of flow and odor around honey bee antennae. We used COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2 to discretize and solve Eqs. (1) and (2) that govern the odorized flow field. Both the fluid flow and odor concentration variables were solved with a staggered scheme using the generalized minimal residual method (GMRES), an iterative method. A residual tolerance of 0.01 was set as convergence criterion. A full model parameter space is highlighted in Fig. S1.

Numerical convergence and validation

An unstructured mesh of the honey bee antenna consisted of tetrahedral and prisms for the volume elements and triangular and quadrilateral for surface elements. Domain size and mesh element size h were iterated until solutions were sufficiently converged. The mesh was refined around the antennal surface to capture regions with sharp gradients in velocity and concentration.

The model was validated against existing simulations of flow around a cylinder at low-Re numbers62–64, for which drag coefficients were compared as a function of Re and are shown in Fig. S2. Odorant mass conservation served as another proxy for validation, where total mass transport of odor into the domain was equal in magnitude to the mass transport of odor through pore plate areas and the outlet of the domain.
