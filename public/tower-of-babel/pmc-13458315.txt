Phytochemical profile and in vitro antimicrobial, antiviral, and anticancer activities of Echium angustifolium ethanolic extract
Authors: Nashaat N. Mahmoud, Alsayed E. Mekky, Ebrahim Saied, Faisal Alsenani, Hamed Alsufyani, Adnan Alharbi, Mohammad Y. Alfaifi, Ali A. Shatii, Islam Abdelhakim Seyiam, Mohammed Aufy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Echium angustifolium is traditionally valued for its therapeutic properties; yet comprehensive studies on its biological activities are limited. Aerial flowering parts were sequentially extracted with solvents of varying polarity. Extracts were analyzed for yield, phytochemical composition, and total phenolic and flavonoid contents and HPLC. LC–MS/MS analysis in negative ion mode was performed for detailed compound identification. Antioxidant activity was measured using the DPPH assay. Antibacterial activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria was evaluated via agar diffusion and MIC/MBC methods. Anti-inflammatory potential was determined in vitro through RBC membrane stabilization (hemolysis) assay. Cytotoxicity and antiviral activity were examined using MTT and cell-based assays on HepG2 and Vero cells. The molecular mechanism of anticancer activity was investigated through qPCR analysis of apoptosis-related genes (Bax, Caspase-3, Caspase-9, and Bcl-2) in HepG2 cells. Antiviral efficacy against Hepatitis A virus (HAV) and Coxsackievirus B4 (CoxB4) was further validated by RT-qPCR quantification of viral RNA copy numbers. The ethanolic extract showed the highest yield (14.23%) among the tested solvents and richest phytochemical profile, with rutin as the predominant compound. LC–MS/MS identified 24 compounds including threonic acid, hypoxanthine, indole-3-carboxylic acid, N-methyllysine, 4-methyl-2-oxovaleric acid, 4-isopropylbenzoic acid, methionine, N-2-fluorenylacetamide, arachidic acid, N-methylanthranilic acid, carbocysteine, fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, lumazine, 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide, and 3-isochromanone. It exhibited significant antioxidant activity (IC50 = 21.08 µg/mL) and antibacterial effects against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. It exhibited in vitro anti-inflammatory effects via RBC membrane stabilization (95.5% inhibition at 1000 µg/mL) and dose-dependent cytotoxicity against HepG2 cells (IC50 = 94 µg/mL), while showing low toxicity toward Vero cells (IC50 = 632.24 µg/mL). Mechanistically, qPCR analysis revealed that the extract at IC50 concentration significantly upregulated pro-apoptotic Bax (4.21-fold), Caspase-3 (5.02-fold), and Caspase-9 (4.08-fold), while downregulating anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 (0.33-fold) compared to untreated controls, confirming apoptosis induction through the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway. Antiviral assays revealed significant inhibition of HAV and Coxsackievirus B4 replication at non-toxic concentrations. RT-qPCR quantification demonstrated that the extract at MNTC (250 µg/mL) reduced HAV and CoxB4 viral RNA copies by 50.6% (from 1.22 × 106 to 6.03 × 105 copies/mL for HAV; from 2.47 × 105 to 1.22 × 105 copies/mL for CoxB4), providing direct evidence of viral inhibition. The ethanolic extract of E. angustifolium exhibited multifunctional in vitro bioactivities, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and antiviral effects. LC–MS/MS identified a diverse array of bioactive compounds, while mechanistic studies revealed apoptosis induction via the mitochondrial pathway (upregulation of Bax, Caspase-3, and Caspase-9; downregulation of Bcl-2) and direct antiviral activity through inhibition of viral replication (confirmed by RT-qPCR). These findings validate the traditional medicinal uses of E. angustifolium and highlight its potential as a promising source of bioactive compounds that warrant further in vivo and mechanistic investigations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-65619-w.

Introduction

Echium angustifolium Mill. (Boraginaceae) is a Mediterranean herb widely distributed in Egypt and traditionally used for the treatment of liver disorders, skin diseases, and inflammatory conditions1. Various species of the genus Echium have been recognized for their diverse pharmacological properties2, including analgesic, antioxidant, and antimicrobial activities, highlighting their potential as valuable sources of bioactive compounds3. Additionally, in Egypt, E. angustifolium has served as grazing material, fuel, and a folk remedy for multiple ailments4.

Phytochemical investigations of Echium species have revealed the presence of a wide range of secondary metabolites, including pyrrolizidine alkaloids, lignans, naphthoquinones, and unsaturated fatty acids5. These compounds have been associated with multiple biological activities, particularly antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anticancer effects6. These phytoconstituents have been associated with diverse pharmacological activities, such as antitumor effects of lignans, insecticidal activity of volatile oils against Aphis craccivora, analgesic and antioxidant effects, wound healing, and protective activity against ciguatoxin toxicity and snake venom-induced inflammation7. More broadly, plant-based phytochemicals have received substantial research interest worldwide. Medicinal plants continue to play a critical role in traditional healthcare, supporting approximately 80% of the global population in meeting primary health needs8. In general, plant-derived phytochemicals continue to play a crucial role in drug discovery, with a significant proportion of currently used antimicrobial and anticancer agents originating from natural products9.

Among the various classes of phytochemicals, phenolic compounds are particularly noteworthy due to their strong antioxidant potential. These compounds are widely applied in the food and pharmaceutical industries for preservation, packaging, and health-promoting purposes. Oxidative stress, primarily induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS), can damage biomolecules such as proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and carbohydrates, contributing to the pathogenesis of diseases including cancer, diabetes, arteriosclerosis, gastric ulcers, and aging10, 11. Plant-based antioxidants like tannins, flavonoids, anthocyanins, phenolic acids, and carotenoids help alleviate these effects12. Numerous studies have demonstrated that plants with higher phenolic content often exhibit stronger antioxidant activity, emphasizing the value of phenolics as key bioactive molecules13. In this context, E. angustifolium may serve as a potential source of bioactive metabolites for pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and cosmeceutical applications. Despite growing interest in its phytochemistry, the biological evaluation of whole plant extracts remains limited, with only isolated lignans tested previously against certain cancer cell lines14.

Viral infections have emerged as major public health challenges due to their diverse replication strategies, transmission routes, and the limited availability of broad-spectrum antiviral agents that act without affecting host cell function15, 16. Although synthetic antiviral drugs can suppress viral replication through different mechanisms, their application is often constrained by high cost, limited cost-effectiveness, adverse side effects, and the growing problem of viral resistance17. These limitations underscore the need for alternative therapeutic options, especially plant-derived agents, whose antiviral potential remains relatively underexplored compared to the extensive research conducted on plant-based antimicrobials18. The hepatitis A virus (HAV) and coxsackievirus B4 (CoxB4) are associated with severe viral infections of global concern. HAV is highly contagious and a major cause of acute hepatitis, which may progress to liver failure in patients with underlying liver disease, although it usually resolves spontaneously19, 20. CoxB4, a member of the enterovirus B group within the Picornaviridae family, can infect multiple tissues including the liver, pancreas, and central nervous system, and has been linked to serious complications such as spastic paralysis and pancreatic dysfunction21. Given these health impacts, evaluating the antiviral potential of E. angustifolium against HAV and CoxB4 represents an important aspect in the current investigation.

Furthermore, elucidation of the molecular mechanisms underlying the anticancer activity of medicinal plant extracts is essential for validating their therapeutic potential. The induction of apoptosis through the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway, characterized by modulation of Bcl-2 family proteins and activation of caspase cascades, represents a key mechanism by which many plant-derived compounds exert their anticancer effects22. Therefore, investigating the expression of apoptosis-related genes including pro-apoptotic Bax, Caspase-3, Caspase-9, and anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 provides critical insights into the mechanism of action. Similarly, for antiviral activity, direct quantification of viral RNA using RT-qPCR offers a more precise and reliable assessment of antiviral efficacy compared to indirect methods such as cytopathic effect observation23.

Based on the traditional medicinal use and reported phytochemical constituents of Echium angustifolium, this study aimed to characterize its phytochemical profile using HPLC and LC–MS/MS analyses and to evaluate its in vitro antioxidant, antimicrobial, antiviral, and cytotoxic activities, supported by qPCR and RT-qPCR analyses.

Material and methods

Plant material collection and authentication

In March 2024, aerial flowering parts of E. angustifolium Mill. were collected from the Agiba region, Mersa Matruh, Egypt. The species was taxonomically confirmed by Prof. Dr. Abd El-Halim Abd El-Mogali Mohamed, and a voucher specimen (CAIM-369) was deposited at the Herbarium of the Flora and Phytotaxonomy Research Department, HRI, ARC, Giza. The samples were rinsed with distilled water, shade-dried at room temperature to constant weight, and kept in sterile containers for subsequent analyses.

All plant materials used in this study were collected and handled in accordance with institutional and national guidelines. The plant was collected from a non-protected area. Echium angustifolium is not listed as a threatened species on the IUCN Red List and is not regulated under the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES).

Preparation of extracts

The powdered aerial parts of E. angustifolium (100 g) were sequentially extracted with petroleum ether, ethyl acetate, ethanol, and water using a Soxhlet apparatus, with extraction conducted below the boiling point of each solvent. The resulting extracts were filtered, concentrated under reduced pressure at ≤ 45 °C using a rotary evaporator, and stored at 4 °C in light-protected, airtight containers until further analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic diagram of successive extraction of aerial flowering parts of E. angustifol.

Phytochemical analysis

Qualitative screening of E. angustifolium extracts was conducted using established phytochemical tests to identify major secondary metabolites24–26. Quantitative determinations of total phenolics, tannins, flavonoids, and flavonols were performed using colorimetric assays based on Folin–Ciocalteu, Folin–Denis, and aluminum chloride reactions, respectively, following previously described protocols27–30, with minor modifications. Absorbance values were recorded at their respective wavelengths using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. Standard calibration curves of gallic acid, tannic acid, quercetin, and rutin were employed to quantify the corresponding contents, expressed as mg GAE/g, mg TAE/g, mg QE/g, and mg RE/g of dry extract, respectively. All assays were carried out in triplicate, and data were presented as mean ± SD.

LC–MS/MS analysis

LC–MS/MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC system coupled to an Agilent 6460 Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer with electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Separation was achieved on an Eclipse XDB-C18 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm) using a mobile phase of acetonitrile and 2% aqueous acetic acid under a gradient program at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. The injection volume was 50 µL. Mass spectrometry was performed in negative ion mode with the following parameters: capillary voltage 3.5 kV, source temperature 350 °C, desolvation gas flow 10 L/min, and nebulizer pressure 45 psi. Compound identification was based on retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and fragmentation patterns compared with reference standards and literature data31. Isotope patterns were recorded as m/z: intensity values.

Antimicrobial activity

The antibacterial activity of E. angustifolium extracts (petroleum ether, ethyl acetate, ethanol, and aqueous) was assessed using a modified agar well diffusion assay32. Standardized bacterial suspensions (0.5 McFarland; ≈1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) were spread on Mueller–Hinton agar plates, and wells (6 mm) were filled with 100 µL of each extract (100 mg/mL). Gentamicin (10 µg/disc) served as the positive control, while sterile water or MHB acted as negative controls. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h, and inhibition zones were measured in triplicate and expressed as mean ± SD.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The antibacterial potency of E. angustifolium extracts was assessed through the broth microdilution method following CLSI standards2. Serial dilutions (0.125–64 mg/mL) were prepared in Mueller–Hinton broth, and each was inoculated with ~ 5 × 105 CFU/mL of the test bacteria. After incubation at 37 °C for 18–24 h, the MIC was determined as the lowest extract concentration showing no visible growth. To establish the MBC, aliquots from non-turbid wells were subcultured on drug-free agar. Gentamicin served as the reference antibiotic, and all experiments were conducted in triplicate.

HPLC analysis

Phytochemical profiling was performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC system equipped with a diode-array detector, following a modified method of Trafkowski33. Separation was achieved on an Eclipse XDB-C18 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm) using a mobile phase of acetonitrile and 2% aqueous acetic acid under a gradient program at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. The injection volume was 50 µL, and detection was carried out at 280, 320, and 360 nm for phenolic acids and flavonoids. Samples were filtered through 0.45 µm membranes before analysis, and compound identification was based on retention times and UV spectra compared with authentic standards.

Evaluation of antioxidant activity by DPPH radical scavenging method

The antioxidant activity of the leaf extracts was assessed using the DPPH radical scavenging assay34. Extracts soluble in ethanol were mixed with a 0.1 mM DPPH solution at concentrations of 3.9–1000 μg/mL, incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. Ascorbic acid was used as a standard, and all tests were performed in triplicate. Anti-inflammatory activity

Fresh human blood was collected into heparinized tubes, and erythrocytes were isolated by centrifugation, washed with saline, and adjusted to a 40% suspension in isotonic phosphate buffer (pH 7.4)35. The membrane stabilization potential of E. angustifolium extracts was assessed using hypotonic-induced hemolysis. Extracts (100–1000 μg/mL) were incubated with erythrocyte suspensions under hypotonic and isotonic conditions, with distilled water and indomethacin (200 μg/mL) serving as negative and positive controls, respectively. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, samples were centrifuged, and absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 540 nm. Cell lines and viruses

Vero cells (African green monkey kidney) were obtained from VACSERA (Holding Company for Biological Products and Vaccines, Egypt). HepG2 human hepatocellular carcinoma cells were obtained from VACSERA (Egypt) and maintained under standard cell culture conditions. Additionally, Hepatitis A virus (HAV) and Coxsackievirus B4 (CoxB4) strains were kindly provided by the Department of Microbiology, Faculty of Medicine for Girls, Al-Azhar University, Egypt.

Cytotoxic activity

The cytotoxic effect of the ethanolic extract on HepG2 cells was evaluated using the MTT assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 105 cells/mL) and allowed to attach for 24 h. After washing, serial dilutions of the extract were added, and plates were incubated for 48 h. MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added, and formazan formation was allowed for 4 h at 37 °C. The crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 560 nm with 620 nm as reference. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) for apoptosis gene expression

Total RNA was extracted from HepG2 cells treated with the ethanolic extract of E. angustifolium at different concentrations (IC₅₀, ½ IC₅₀, and ¼ IC₅₀) for 48 h using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR® Green Master Mix (Thermo Scientific). The following gene-specific primers were used: GAPDH (forward: 5′-GAAGGTGAAGGTCGGAGTC-3′; reverse: 5′-GAAGATGGTGATGGGATTTC-3′), Bax (forward: 5′-GCCCTTTTGCTTCAGGGTTT-3′; reverse: 5′-GCCGGCGTTGTCTAAGAT-3′), Caspase-3 (forward: 5′-CCGTGGTTCATCCAGTCGCT-3′; reverse: 5′-GATGGCACAAAGCGACTGGA-3′), Caspase-9 (forward: 5′-CTGGTTCATCTCCAACCGTC-3′; reverse: 5′-GCAGGCCGATGTAGTCCTTA-3′), and Bcl-2 (forward: 5′-ATGTGTGTGGAGAGCGTCAAC-3′; reverse: 5′-ACAGTTCCACAAAGGCATCCC-3′). The PCR cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Melting curve analysis was performed to verify primer specificity. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^ − ΔΔCt^ method, with GAPDH as the internal control and untreated HepG2 cells as the calibrator37. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments.

Antiviral activity and viral RNA quantification

Cytotoxicity assessment on vero cells

The cytotoxicity of the ethanolic extract on Vero cells was evaluated using the MTT assay to determine the maximum non-toxic concentration (MNTC), defined as the highest dose maintaining ≥ 90% cell viability without morphological changes38. For antiviral testing, Vero cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 105 cells/mL) and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 24 h.

Virus preparation and infection

Hepatitis A virus (HAV) and Coxsackievirus B4 (CoxB4) strains were propagated in Vero cells. Viral titers for ₅₀ of HAV and CoxB4 were determined using the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID₅₀) method according to Reed and Muench39. Briefly, Vero cells were seeded in 96-well plates, infected with serial tenfold dilutions of virus, and incubated for 5–7 days. The cytopathic effect (CPE) was observed microscopically, and TCID₅₀ was calculated. The titers were calculated as 10⁻5·2 TCID₅₀/mL for HAV and 10⁻4·⁸ TCID₅₀/mL for CoxB4. All antiviral assays were performed at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1.

Antiviral assay

Vero cells were infected with HAV or CoxB4 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 in the presence or absence of serial dilutions of the ethanolic extract (MNTC and lower concentrations). After 1 h of adsorption at 37 °C, the inoculum was removed, and cells were washed with PBS and overlaid with maintenance medium containing the corresponding extract concentrations. After 48–72 h of incubation at 37 °C, the antiviral activity was assessed by observing CPE and measuring cell viability using the MTT assay. Viral RNA extraction and RT-qPCR quantification

Viral RNA was extracted from cell culture supernatants of infected Vero cells using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For absolute quantification of viral RNA, standard curves were generated using tenfold serial dilutions of plasmid DNA containing the target gene sequences (102 to 10⁸ copies/µL). The standard curves for HAV and CoxB4 showed linearity with R2 values of 0.996 and 0.994, respectively. Detection limits were 102 copies/µL for both viruses. RT-qPCR was performed using the One-Step RT-PCR Kit (Applied Biosystems) with specific primers for HAV and CoxB4. For HAV, the following primers were used: forward 5′-TCACCGCCGTTTGCCTAG-3′ and reverse 5′-GGAGAGCCCTGGAAGAAAG-3′41. For CoxB4, primers were: forward 5′-CCTCCGGCCCCTGAAT-3′ and reverse 5′-ACCGGATGGCCAATCCAA-3′42. Thermal cycling conditions were: 50 °C for 30 min (reverse transcription), 95 °C for 10 min (initial denaturation), followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Viral RNA copy numbers were calculated using a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of known concentrations of plasmid DNA containing the target gene sequences43. Results were expressed as copies/mL and compared to untreated virus controls.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 17. Differences among groups were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. For viral RNA quantification, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used to compare each treatment group to the corresponding virus control.

Results and discussion

The use of medicinal plants dates back to early human history, with various plant parts such as seeds, flowers, leaves, fruits, and roots employed for therapeutic purposes. Traditional knowledge has gradually evolved into structured treatments, and modern research combined with computational tools has enhanced the ability to tackle emerging diseases44. Antioxidants play a key role in protecting cells from oxidative damage linked to chronic conditions such as cancer, atherosclerosis, and neurodegenerative disorders45. Herbal extracts are natural sources of antioxidants, and are widely used as dietary supplements to reduce oxidative stress46. In this study, the antioxidant activity of E. angustifolium was evaluated using the DPPH assay, a reliable method for assessing free radical scavenging potential of plant-derived compounds.

In phytochemical studies, extractive yield serves as a key indicator of a solvent’s efficiency in isolating bioactive compounds from plant material. This parameter depends on factors such as solvent polarity, extraction technique, particle size, temperature, and duration. Typically, polar solvents such as ethanol, methanol, and water produce higher yields, owing to their strong ability to dissolve a wide range of phytochemicals such as phenolics, flavonoids, glycosides, and lipids48. Comparing extractive yields from different solvents helps optimize extraction protocols for specific phytochemical and pharmacological applications. Ethanol often provides the highest yield and antioxidant activity, while aqueous extracts also perform well, efficiently recovering hydrophilic compounds such as sugars, tannins, and phenolic acids49, 50.

In contrast, ethyl acetate gave a lower yield (4.21%) due to its semi-polar nature, although such fractions may contain metabolites with higher antimicrobial potential51. Petroleum ether produced the lowest yield (2.75%), consistent with previous reports, and typically extracts lipophilic antioxidants or pigments despite the low overall yield52. The differences in extract color reflect variations in phytochemical composition: yellowish petroleum ether extracts indicate carotenoids and lipids, green ethyl acetate extracts suggest chlorophyll derivatives and terpenoids, and brown ethanolic or dark-brown aqueous extracts are rich in phenolics, including tannins and flavonoids53. Overall, ethanol and water proved to be the most efficient solvents for maximizing extractive yield, while ethyl acetate and petroleum ether are better suited for fractionation of specific metabolite groups rather than maximizing total content.

The observed differences in phytochemical content among the extracts are consistent with the principle that solvent polarity influences the types of compounds extracted. Ethanol and aqueous extracts of E. angustifolium contained the highest levels of flavonoids, phenols, and saponins, in agreement with previous reports (Table 2). For instance, Ng et al.54 reported that ethanol and water extracts of medicinal plants tend to yield higher levels of polyphenolic compounds and flavonoids attributable to their polar characteristics and efficiency in extracting hydrophilic compounds. Similarly, Muflihah et al.55 demonstrated that ethanolic extracts of several medicinal herbs contained abundant phenolic and flavonoid compounds, which correlated strongly with antioxidant activity. In contrast, the ethyl acetate fraction exhibited moderate phytochemical richness, especially in phenols and flavonoids. This finding is in agreement with Axiotis et al.56, who showed that ethyl acetate selectively extracts medium-polar compounds, often concentrating specific bioactive metabolites such as glycosides and terpenoids but at lower overall abundance compared to ethanolic fractions. Petroleum ether extracts, as expected, contained only weak amounts of flavonoids, tannins, and phenols, consistent with its non-polar nature. Similar observations were made by Muhamad et al.57, where petroleum ether extracts yielded limited phytoconstituents, mainly restricted to lipophilic molecules such as steroids and simple phenolic derivatives. (Table 3) illustrates marked solvent-dependent variations in the phytochemical content of E. angustifolium extracts. The ethanol extract exhibited the highest levels of flavonoids (81.74 mg QE/g), flavonols (40.77 mg RTE/g), phenols (46.96 mg GAE/g), and tannins (36.92 mg TAE/g), highlighting its superior capacity to extract diverse polyphenolic compounds. The aqueous extract also showed substantial amounts of flavonoids (58.55 mg QE/g) and flavonols (32.08 mg RTE/g), while ethyl acetate and petroleum ether extracts contained moderate to low concentrations, respectively. These results align with previous studies58–60, indicating that ethanol, due to its intermediate polarity, efficiently solubilizes both hydrophilic and moderately lipophilic compounds, yielding extracts with enhanced phenolic content and associated antioxidant and antimicrobial activities. The aqueous extract also demonstrated a relatively high content of flavonoids and tannins, supporting observations by Fraga-Corral et al.61, who noted that water extracts are particularly rich in tannins and glycosidic compounds. However, aqueous solvents may sometimes co-extract sugars and proteins that dilute phenolic concentration, which explains why the ethanol extract still outperformed the aqueous one in total polyphenol content. Ethyl acetate showed intermediate results, with moderate levels of flavonoids (53.11 mg QE/g) and phenols (16.96 mg GAE/g). These findings confirm earlier report by Patyra et al.62, who reported that ethyl acetate selectively extracts medium-polar compounds, such as specific flavonoid glycosides and terpenoids, but generally yields lower total phenolics than ethanol. Petroleum ether extracts exhibited the lowest levels of all analyzed compounds, reflecting their non-polar character. Similar findings were reported by Traversier et al.63 and Altemimi et al.64, indicating that petroleum ether mainly extracts non-polar metabolites such as lipids and chlorophylls, with limited recovery of phenolics. The high phenolic and flavonoid content in ethanol and aqueous extracts has important pharmacological relevance, as polyphenols are associated with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities. HPLC analysis of the ethanolic extract of E. angustifolium

HPLC analysis of the ethanolic extract of E. angustifolium revealed seventeen polyphenolic compounds, including protocatechuic, gentisic, chlorogenic, caffeic, syringic, vanillic, sinapic, and cinnamic acids, in addition to flavonoids such as epicatechin, epicatechin gallate, rutin, quercetin, apigenin, apigenin-7-glucoside, kaempferol, and chrysin. Among the detected constituents, rutin and epicatechin were present at comparatively higher levels. Comparable phenolic and flavonoid profiles have also been documented in other medicinal plant species, including Fagonia arabica and species of the genus Dahlia66. The relatively high levels of rutin and apigenin are in agreement with previous reports describing E. angustifolium as a source of flavonoid glycosides with documented antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties67, 68. Rutin has been widely reported as an efficient free radical scavenger with protective roles against oxidative stress–related conditions69.

Deng et al.70 identified 28 phenolic and flavonoid compounds in Elaeagnus angustifolium using LC–MS/MS analysis. The results indicated that the plant is rich in phenolic compounds and could serve as a natural source of antioxidants. Arumugam et al.71 evaluated the antioxidant activity and phytochemical composition of methanolic leaf extracts of Doronicum orientale and Echium angustifolium using LC–ESI–MS/MS. D. orientale showed higher total phenolic content and stronger antioxidant activity, while E. angustifolium contained several phenolic compounds such as rosmarinic, caffeic, and p-coumaric acids. The study suggested that D. orientale methanol extract could serve as a potential natural agent against oxidative stress. Similarly, epicatechin and its gallate derivative have been previously associated with antioxidant activity and cardiometabolic health benefits, including improved glucose metabolism and vascular function72. Moderate levels of phenolic acids such as protocatechuic acid, vanillic acid, and sinapic acid were also detected. These compounds have individually been reported to exhibit anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activities73. While the presence of these phenolic constituents may partly explain the biological activities observed in the crude extract, definitive attribution cannot be made without compound isolation and mechanistic validation. The presence of these compounds, particularly those with established antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities, supports the observed biological effects of the extract. The identification of compounds such as threonic acid, hypoxanthine, and lumazine provides new insights into the phytochemical composition of this species, which has not been extensively characterized using advanced analytical techniques.

Antimicrobial activity

(Table 6 & Fig. 3) shows that E. angustifolium extracts exhibit notable antimicrobial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. The ethanol extract displayed the strongest inhibition, especially against S. aureus (18.0 ± 0.95 mm) and E. coli (17.0 ± 0.78 mm), while petroleum ether, ethyl acetate, and aqueous extracts also demonstrated activity, albeit generally lower than that of ethanol. Distilled water showed no activity, confirming that the inhibitory zones were due to phytochemicals present in the extracts. Although some extracts exhibited inhibition zones comparable to those of gentamicin against selected bacterial strains, these findings are limited to in vitro conditions and should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent clinical efficacy. These findings suggest that ethanol may extract a broader spectrum of bioactive phytochemicals, such as flavonoids, phenolics, and terpenoids, which are well-documented for their antimicrobial properties. Interestingly, the aqueous, ethyl acetate, and petroleum ether extracts also demonstrated moderate activity, indicating that bioactive compounds with variable polarity contribute collectively to the antimicrobial potential. The inhibitory zones observed for Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii suggest that the extracts are effective even against opportunistic and drug-resistant pathogens, which aligns with previous reports highlighting the antibacterial efficacy of E. angustifolium phytoconstituents. The comparable activity of some extracts to gentamycin against E. coli and S. aureus suggests that these extracts may represent promising candidates for further phytochemical isolation and preclinical investigation. These results underscore the broad-spectrum antibacterial potential of E. angustifolium extracts. Their activity against both Gram-positive (S. aureus, B. subtilis) and Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli, K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii) indicates the presence of phytochemicals capable of permeating diverse bacterial cell walls. Similar findings were reported by Imran et al.76 and Konstantinovitch et al.77, where ethanolic and aqueous extracts of medicinal plants exhibited substantial inhibition of S. aureus and E. coli, often comparable to standard antibiotics. Similarly, Mehmood et al.78 found that phenolic-rich plant extracts produced inhibition zones between 14–18 mm, which closely matches the current results. In addition, Kavaz Yüksel et al.79 demonstrated that extracts with higher flavonoid and tannin content generally exhibited stronger antibacterial activity, supporting the link between the phytochemical composition of E. angustifolium (rich in flavonoids, phenols, and tannins) and its antimicrobial effect. The moderate but consistent inhibition against K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii is particularly noteworthy, as these are opportunistic multidrug-resistant pathogens. Studies by Alam et al.80 and Kim et al.81 highlighted that plant extracts often show lower but significant inhibitory activity against resistant Gram-negative strains, suggesting a possible role as adjuvants to conventional antibiotics.

Satyanarayana et al.82 observed that methanolic extracts of C. procumbens leaves exhibited weak antibacterial activity against E. coli, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, B. subtilis, and E. faecalis, with inhibition zones of 1–12 mm at concentrations of 25–100 µg/mL. Similarly, Revathi et al.83 found that the aqueous extract exhibited very low activity against several bacterial strains at 20–50 mg/mL, with inhibition zones generally below 10 mm, except for P. vulgaris which reached 18 mm at the higher concentration. The observed antibacterial activity of E. angustifolium extracts is likely due to the synergistic effects of flavonoids, phenolic compounds, tannins, and saponins. Flavonoids target microbial cell membranes and form complexes with extracellular/soluble proteins, impairing microbial functions and inhibit nucleic acid synthesis84. Phenols cause protein denaturation and alter membrane permeability, leading to cell lysis85. Tannins bind to bacterial cell walls and enzymes, reducing their metabolic activity and growth86. Saponins interfere with cell membrane integrity by forming pores, leading to leakage of cellular contents87. This multi-target mechanism explains why the extracts were effective against diverse bacterial strains.

MIC and MBC

The MIC and MBC values (Table 7) further confirmed the antibacterial activity of all solvent fractions, with varying degrees of potency. The ethanol extract exhibited the lowest MIC (62.5 µg/mL) and MBC (125 µg/mL) values among the tested extracts against most bacterial strains, indicating comparatively stronger inhibitory activity within the extract group. Other fractions demonstrated MIC and MBC values ranging from 125–250 µg/mL, suggesting moderate antibacterial effects. Gentamicin was included as a positive control to confirm bacterial susceptibility and validate the assay. Its MIC and MBC values were not determined in this study, and therefore direct quantitative comparison with plant extracts is not possible. The absence of inhibition in the DMSO solvent control confirms that the antibacterial activity was attributable solely to the plant extracts. Overall, these findings demonstrate that E. angustifolium extracts possess measurable in vitro antibacterial activity. Nevertheless, crude extracts tested at mg/mL concentrations should not be directly compared to purified antibiotics tested at µg levels, and further bioactivity-guided fractionation and mechanistic studies are required to identify the specific active constituents. Similarly, Emami et al.88 evaluated the antibacterial activity of Elaeagnus angustifolia extracts. The methanolic extract showed the strongest effect against Staphylococcus aureus (MIC = 62.5 mg/mL; MBC = 125 mg/mL), while the ethanolic extract showed the weakest activity against Escherichia coli (MIC = 250 mg/mL; MBC = 300 mg/mL). Additionally, the study by Blonk and Cock89 showed that Pittosporum angustifolium leaf extracts possess antibacterial activity against several bacteria associated with autoimmune diseases. Methanolic, aqueous, and ethyl acetate extracts strongly inhibited Proteus mirabilis and Klebsiella pneumoniae (MIC = 26 and 57 µg/mL). Combinations with conventional antibiotics often enhanced antibacterial activity. Among 250 tested combinations, 49.6% showed additive effects, 46.8% were indifferent, while only 0.02% showed synergy and 0.02% antagonism, all involving tetracycline. Despite the encouraging in vitro antibacterial activity observed in the present study, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The extracts evaluated are crude plant extracts, and their efficacy cannot be directly compared with purified antibiotics. Further studies are required to isolate the active constituents, investigate their mechanisms of action, evaluate pharmacokinetic and toxicological profiles, and confirm efficacy in appropriate animal models before any clinical application can be considered.

Antioxidant activity

The DPPH assay demonstrated that the ethanolic extract of E. angustifolium scavenged free radicals in a concentration-dependent manner, comparable to the positive control, ascorbic acid (Fig. 4). While ascorbic acid exhibited the strongest activity (IC₅₀ = 2.52 µg/mL), the extract showed moderate antioxidant potential (IC₅₀ = 21.08 µg/mL). This effect is likely due to its abundant bioactive constituents, including phenolics, flavonoids, and tannins, which aid in neutralizing free radicals. The antioxidant activity of the ethanolic extract aligns with previous reports on Boraginaceae species. For instance, Asghari et al.90 demonstrated strong DPPH scavenging by E. amoenum ethanolic extracts (IC₅₀ = 22.8 and 17.1 μg/mL), supporting the current results. Similarly, Jin et al.91 observed moderate antioxidant activity in E. italicum flower extracts, attributed to their high phenolic and flavonoid content. When compared to synthetic or pure antioxidants such as ascorbic acid or Trolox, plant extracts generally show higher IC₅₀ values (lower potency). This is expected since plant extracts are complex mixtures of compounds, and their activity often depends on synergistic interactions rather than a single dominant antioxidant. In line with this, Nguyen et al.92 demonstrated that crude extracts of medicinal plants usually display IC₅₀ values between 20 and 50 µg/mL, which supports the results obtained in this study. Arroussi et al.93 reported that the 80% aqueous MeOH extracts of C. creticum and E. plantagineum exhibited the strongest DPPH scavenging activity, with IC₅₀ values of 9.83 and 10.59 µg/mL, respectively, comparable to ascorbic acid. In contrast, O. ficus indica showed the weakest activity (IC₅₀ = 79.41 µg/mL), while the other tested species demonstrated moderate effects. The variation between the extract and ascorbic acid reflects the difference between pure molecules with high reducing capacity (ascorbic acid) and heterogeneous mixtures (extracts) containing phenolics, flavonoids, and tannins94. Although less potent than standard antioxidants, plant-derived compounds offer additional therapeutic effects, including anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activities, enhancing their pharmacological relevance. The antioxidant effect of the ethanolic extract is primarily due to its phenolic constituents, which stabilize free radicals like DPPH• by donating hydrogen atoms or electrons, thereby terminating radical chain reactions95. Flavonoids act through metal chelation, radical scavenging, and inhibition of oxidative enzymes. Cytotoxic activity

The ethanolic extract of E. angustifolium exhibited a clear dose-dependent cytotoxic effect on HepG2 cells, with growth inhibition increasing from ~ 20% at 31.25 µg/mL to nearly 97% at 500–1000 µg/mL (Fig. 6). The IC₅₀ was approximately 94 µg/mL, indicating notable in vitro cytotoxicity. According to NCI guidelines, crude extracts with IC₅₀ values below 100 µg/mL are considered strongly cytotoxic, suggesting that E. angustifolium may contain bioactive compounds capable of suppressing hepatocellular carcinoma cell proliferation108. Several studies have reported differing cytotoxic effects of plant extracts on HepG2 cells. For instance, Ashwagandha leaf extract inhibited cell proliferation with an IC₅₀ of 153.8 µg/mL, while doxorubicin showed much stronger activity (IC₅₀ = 18.8 µM)109. Extracts of Vitis vinifera and Camellia sinensis demonstrated minimal cytotoxicity. Moringa oleifera crude extracts affected liver cell metabolic activity in a dose- and time-dependent manner, with low concentrations (50–100 mg/L) having negligible or slightly stimulatory effects, and higher concentrations (200–400 mg/L) causing significant reduction110. Cinnamon water extract and metformin also reduced HepG2 viability in a concentration- and time-dependent fashion, with notable inhibition observed above 0.125 mg/mL for the cinnamon extract, whereas metformin was cytotoxic from 6.2 mg/mL onward111. Hydroalcoholic extracts of Oxalis corniculata showed selective cytotoxicity, with IC₅₀ values of 34.49 ± 0.42 µg/mL for the crude extract and 30.25 ± 0.58 µg/mL for the ethyl acetate fraction; doxorubicin remained the most potent (IC₅₀ = 24.89 ± 0.25 µg/mL)112.Fig. 6Cytotoxic activity of the ethanolic extract of E. angustifolium on HepG2 cells, assessed by the MTT assay. The observed cytotoxicity is likely mediated through multiple mechanisms. Natural extracts are known to induce apoptosis via activation of intrinsic and extrinsic pathways, triggering caspase cascades and modulating Bcl-2 family proteins113. In addition, they can cause cell cycle arrest at various phases, disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential, and generate ROS, leading to oxidative stress in cancer cells114, 115. The dose-dependent inhibition observed in this study may reflect one or a combination of these mechanisms. Furthermore, plant-derived bioactive compounds often selectively target cancer cells while sparing normal cells, enhancing their therapeutic value. Such observations have also been reported in previous research, where flavonoids, polyphenols, and alkaloids from plant extracts showed selective cytotoxicity against HepG2 cells and other carcinoma cell lines. For example, Bhosale et al.116, demonstrated that flavonoid-rich extracts induced apoptosis through ROS-mediated pathways, while Liu et al.117, reported cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase as a key mechanism for anticancer activity.

Similarly, for CoxB4, the extract at MNTC (250 µg/mL) reduced viral RNA copies from 2.47 × 105 ± 0.09 × 105 copies/mL (virus control) to 1.22 × 105 ± 0.07 × 105 copies/mL, corresponding to a 50.6% reduction (p < 0.001). These quantitative RT-qPCR results corroborate the MTT-based antiviral activity data, confirming that the ethanolic extract of E. angustifolium directly inhibits viral replication in a dose-dependent manner. The consistency between the percentage reduction in viral RNA copies and the MTT-based antiviral protection (50.6% vs. ~ 50%) validates the reliability of the antiviral assessment.

The RT-qPCR results provide stronger evidence of antiviral activity compared to the indirect MTT assay, as they directly measure viral RNA rather than inferring antiviral effects from cell viability. The dose-dependent reduction in viral RNA copies with significant effects at higher concentrations indicates that the extract interferes with viral replication rather than merely protecting cells from viral-induced cytopathic effects. The similar patterns observed for both HAV and CoxB4 suggest a broad-spectrum antiviral potential of the extract.

The cytotoxicity evaluation on Vero cells is a key step for confirming the safety of natural extracts before antiviral or anticancer testing. In this study, the ethanolic extract of E. angustifolium exhibited an IC₅₀ of 632.24 ± 5.01 µg/mL and a MNTC of 250 µg/mL. According to U.S. NCI guidelines, extracts with IC₅₀ values above 500 µg/mL are considered to have negligible toxicity toward normal cells, indicating that this extract is relatively safe121. These findings align with previous reports on plant-derived extracts: Musarra-Pizzo et al.121 observed an IC₅₀ of 710 µg/mL for a polyphenol-rich fraction, while Ogbole et al.122 reported that a peptide fraction from Euphorbia hirta exhibited potent antiviral activity against Coxsackievirus A13, A20, and Enterovirus C99 with IC₅₀ values below 2.0 µg/mL and a selective index ≥ 81. Similarly, Ogbole et al.123 found that the methanol extract of Macaranga barteri displayed strong antiviral effects against E7 and E19, with the dichloromethane fraction showing the highest selectivity. Compared with these studies, the high IC₅₀ observed here highlights the low cytotoxicity of the extract toward mammalian kidney cells, providing a favorable safety margin for further biological assays. This reduced toxicity may result from the extract’s low interaction with essential cellular pathways in healthy cells, while its bioactive constituents preferentially affect infected or transformed cells—an advantageous trait for therapeutic applications.

The antiviral activity of the ethanolic extract of E. angustifolium demonstrated a dose-dependent inhibitory effect on both HAV and CoxB4 as determined by RT-qPCR. The most significant reduction in viral RNA copies occurred at the MNTC (250 µg/mL), achieving 50.6% inhibition for both viruses. These findings are in agreement with previous studies describing dose-dependent antiviral effects of plant-derived compounds on enteroviruses, including HAV and Coxsackie viruses121, 124. Importantly, the extract maintained minimal cytotoxicity (5–8%) at effective antiviral concentrations, consistent with earlier reports where plant extracts displayed low toxicity while preserving antiviral efficacy125. A clear dose–response relationship was observed, as increasing concentrations enhanced antiviral effects without markedly compromising cell viability. Such patterns are well-documented in studies of HAV inhibition by plant-derived extracts126.

The RT-qPCR quantification provides direct evidence of viral RNA reduction, confirming that the extract interferes with viral replication rather than merely protecting cells from viral-induced cytopathic effects. The similar inhibitory patterns observed for both HAV and CoxB4 suggest broad-spectrum antiviral potential, which may be attributed to the diverse phytochemicals identified by LC–MS/MS, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, and other bioactive compounds known for their antiviral properties127. Although the precise mechanism of action remains to be clarified, existing evidence suggests that bioactive metabolites may interfere with viral entry, inhibit replication post-entry, or directly inactivate viral particles, thereby reducing viral activity and enhancing host cell survival16, 128.

While this study provides comprehensive evidence for the in vitro bioactivities of E. angustifolium, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, although RT-qPCR quantification confirmed significant viral RNA reduction, the absence of plaque assay data for infectious virus quantification represents a limitation that should be addressed in future studies. Second, while qPCR analysis confirmed apoptosis induction at the transcriptional level, additional experiments including flow cytometry with Annexin V-FITC/PI staining and protein-level analysis (Western blot) would further validate the apoptotic pathway. Third, the phytochemical identification by LC–MS/MS, while robust, could be strengthened by HRMS and NMR analysis for absolute structural confirmation of the major compounds. Finally, the antimicrobial mechanism of action and potential synergy with conventional antibiotics warrant further investigation. Despite these limitations, the consistency of the data across multiple bioassays and the mechanistic insights provided by qPCR and RT-qPCR analyses support the multifunctional therapeutic potential of E. angustifolium.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively characterized the phytochemical composition and in vitro biological activities of Echium angustifolium ethanolic extract. HPLC and LC–MS/MS analyses confirmed the presence of diverse bioactive phytochemicals, particularly phenolic and flavonoid compounds. The extract exhibited strong antioxidant, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and selective cytotoxic activities against HepG2 cells while showing low toxicity toward normal Vero cells. Mechanistic investigations further demonstrated apoptosis induction through modulation of Bax, Caspase-3, Caspase-9, and Bcl-2 gene expression, whereas RT-qPCR confirmed significant inhibition of HAV and CoxB4 viral RNA replication. Collectively, these findings provide comprehensive in vitro evidence supporting the multifunctional biological activities of E. angustifolium and highlight its potential as a promising source of bioactive compounds. Nevertheless, further bioactivity-guided fractionation, in vivo studies, and clinical investigations are required before any therapeutic application can be established.
