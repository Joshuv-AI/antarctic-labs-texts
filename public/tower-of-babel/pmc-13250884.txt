The regulatory roles of non-coding RNAs in aerobic glycolysis and therapeutic potential in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yixu Fan, Xiang Tang, Shijiao Li, Shutong Liu, Yuan Fang, Xia Sun, Zhenyu Xue, Hao Niu, Yanwei Chen, Chunhua Dai, Rui Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly lethal malignancy with a five-year survival rate below 10%, largely due to late diagnosis and rapid metastasis. PDAC cells exhibit profound metabolic reprogramming, particularly enhanced aerobic glycolysis. Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and circular RNAs (circRNAs), have emerged as key regulators of glycolysis in PDAC, although their integrated roles and clinical relevance remain incompletely understood.Main findingsMiRNAs regulate glycolytic flux by targeting key enzymes and signaling pathways, thereby modulating glucose uptake and lactate production. LncRNAs promote glycolysis through pathways such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR, Wnt/β-catenin and p53, or by acting as competing endogenous RNAs. CircRNAs function as miRNA sponges or transcriptional regulators, contributing to metabolic adaptation and chemoresistance. Beyond glycolysis, ncRNAs also influence mitochondrial function, redox balance and tumor microenvironment remodeling. Clinically, several ncRNAs show promise as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. Therapeutic strategies targeting ncRNAs, including nano-delivery systems and RNA-based approaches, demonstrate potential for overcoming chemoresistance and enhancing treatment efficacy.ConclusionNcRNAs play central roles in regulating glycolysis and metabolic adaptation in PDAC and represent promising targets for diagnosis and therapy. Further studies are required to validate key regulatory networks and translate these findings into clinical applications.Keywords: ncRNAs, PDAC, Glycolysis, miRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs

Introduction

Although there have been continuous advances in modern medical technology and significant improvements in survival rates of many cancers, pancreatic cancer remains a deadly disease, with a poor prognosis and an increased number of pancreatic cancer-related deaths [1,2]. From the global cancer statistics 2022, the death rate of pancreatic cancer ranked sixth among cancers, representing 4.8% of all cancer deaths [3]. Moreover, the incidence of PDAC is increasing by 0.5%–1.0% per year, and it is projected to become the second-leading cause of cancer-related mortality by 2030. Approximately 60,000 new cases of PDAC are diagnosed per year, and approximately 50% of patients have advanced disease at diagnosis [4]. This is related to the unique anatomical location of the pancreas and the lack of means for early screening of pancreatic cancer. The primary treatment strategies for pancreatic cancer currently include surgery, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy. However, each of these approaches has its drawbacks, such as drug resistance and severe side effects, leading to a low five-year survival rate for advanced PDAC (approximately 8.5% based on SEER data) [5]. Therapeutic resistance in pancreatic cancer is associated with multiple factors, among which metabolic reprogramming plays a critical role. Therefore, developing novel therapeutic strategies targeting metabolic reprogramming in PDAC is of particular importance.

Aerobic glycolysis is one of the most significant metabolic characteristics of cancer cells. This is manifested by the substantial consumption of glucose and increased secretion of lactate, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect(as summarized in the Graphical Abstract) [6]. The characteristics of PDAC include an impenetrable stroma and a poorly vascularized microenvironment. Due to the stiffness of the stroma, blood vessels are compressed, leading to low perfusion and a hypovascularity tumour microenvironment [7,8]. This unique and harsh microenvironment provides PDAC with a hypoxic and nutrient-deprived setting, further reinforcing PDAC’s reliance on glycolysis. In recent years, it has been discovered that within the network of aerobic glycolysis, non-coding RNAs play a pivotal role.

NcRNAs encompass subclasses such as microRNAs (miRNAs), which are 20–22 nucleotides long, and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), spanning over 200 nucleotides, and also circular RNAs(circRNAs) [9]. NcRNAs play pivotal roles in various cellular processes, including DNA replication and chromosome maintenance, transcriptional activity, RNA processing, translation and stability, as well as protein translocation [10]. Additionally, ncRNAs play a crucial role in the glycolytic process of tumours [11]. This review summarizes the potential molecular mechanisms of ncRNA in the glycolytic process, aiming to provide a basis for exploring new targeted therapies for PDAC.

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus databases for studies published up to January 2026. The search strategy combined keywords such as ‘pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma’ OR ‘PDAC’, ‘glycolysis’, ‘aerobic glycolysis’, ‘non-coding RNA’, ‘miRNA’, ‘lncRNA’ and ‘circRNA’. Only peer-reviewed articles published in English were considered. Studies were prioritized if they provided experimental evidence in PDAC models, including in vitro, in vivo, or clinical data. Reference lists of relevant articles were further screened to ensure comprehensive coverage of the topic.

Overview of glycolysis in PDAC

PDAC is highly metastatic and is projected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality by 2030. While tumour cell growth is unchecked, the cells exist in a chronic state of nutrient deprivation and hypoxia due to vascular disturbances and desmoplastic reactions. Consequently, pancreatic cancer cells utilize metabolic reprogramming to meet their aerobic needs.

Aerobic glycolysis is one of the most significant metabolic characteristics of cancer cells. However, normal cells resort to glycolysis for their energy supply only in anoxic conditions, it seems quite peculiar, given that aerobic glycolysis is far less efficient in terms of ATP yield per mole of glucose than mitochondrial respiration. This phenomenon occurs because, despite the lower ATP yield from glycolysis compared to oxidative phosphorylation, cancer cells are capable of rapidly assimilating ATP and metabolic intermediates generated through glycolysis. This not only facilitates an accelerated energy turnover but also contributes to the establishment of an acidic microenvironment and adaptation to hypoxic conditions, which are associated with tumour progression [12–14]. This distinct phenomenon is called the ‘Warburg effect’ or ‘Aerobic Glycolysis’. Aerobic glycolysis in tumour cells contributes to their growth, metastasis, invasion and other malignant characteristics.

Characteristics of glycolysis in pancreatic cancer

A century ago, Otto Warburg’s groundbreaking discovery revealed that tumour cells could rapidly consume glucose and convert it to lactate even in the presence of oxygen (Figure 1) [15]. With advancements in genetics and molecular biology, it has been found that the metabolic reprogramming underlying the Warburg effect is driven by various oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes.

Metabolic reprogramming in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) driven by KRAS mutations and intercellular metabolic interactions.

The metabolic characteristics of tumour cells are driven by KRAS mutations, which enhance the glycolytic pathway by upregulating the expression of glucose transporters (such as GLUT1 and SGLT2) and key glycolytic enzymes (such as hexokinase 2 [HK2], phosphofructokinase 1 [PFK1] and lactate dehydrogenase A [LDHA]) (Figure 1). Additionally, KRAS promotes lactate efflux by regulating lactate transporters (like MCT4), thereby maintaining intracellular pH balance and preventing the toxic accumulation of lactate [16,17]. Further investigations have revealed that, under sustained KRAS activation, PDAC exhibits an accentuated glycolytic drive and an enhanced capacity for lactate handling, thereby establishing the foundation for downstream metabolic rerouting and intercellular metabolic crosstalk [18,19].

The intermediates of glycolysis are diverted to various biosynthetic pathways, including the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP) and the serine biosynthesis pathway (SSP). These pathways not only provide precursors for nucleotide, amino acid and lipid synthesis but also support the redox homeostasis of tumour cells (through NADPH production) and DNA methylation. For instance, the oxidative arm of the PPP generates NADPH to support reactive oxygen species (ROS) detoxification, while the non-oxidative arm promotes tumour proliferation through nucleotide synthesis [17]. The HBP participates in the glycosylation regulation of proteins and lipids by generating UDP-GlcNAc, while the SSP provides carbon sources for one-carbon metabolism, supporting DNA and histone methylation. While maintaining the glycolysis–branching network, PDAC relies more heavily on exogenous inputs and recycling pathways to sustain flux through these branches under low-glucose conditions [20].

Furthermore, PDAC cells utilize nutrient recycling pathways such as autophagy and phagocytosis to acquire additional metabolites in response to the nutrient-depleted microenvironment. Autophagy provides amino acids, fatty acids and nucleotides by degrading intracellular components (Figure 1), while phagocytosis supplements metabolic demands by engulfing extracellular macromolecules (such as proteins and lipids) [21]. KRAS signalling also promotes the activation of these pathways, for example, by enhancing macropinocytosis to obtain exogenous nutrients. Compared with other malignancies, PDAC exhibits a heightened dependence on autophagy and macropinocytosis, a trait that enables it to sustain glycolysis and downstream biosynthetic demands even under profound scarcity of accessible carbon sources [20].

Metabolic interactions within the tumour microenvironment play a crucial role in the progression of PDAC (Figure 1). For instance, the secretion and reutilization of lactate create a metabolic symbiosis between tumour cells. Hypoxic tumour cells secrete lactate via MCT4, while oxygen-rich tumour cells take up lactate through MCT1 and utilize it for the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Additionally, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) support the TCA cycle and biosynthetic needs of tumour cells by secreting metabolites such as alanine [22]. Alanine is considered a key carbon source that can directly feed mitochondrial metabolism and alleviate biosynthetic stress under glucose-restricted conditions [23].

Overall, within an extremely nutrient-poor, poorly perfused, and highly heterogeneous microenvironment, PDAC establishes a reinforced glycolysis–branching–intercellular metabolic coupling network—secured by its pronounced reliance on autophagy and macropinocytosis, the MCT4→MCT1–mediated division of labour in lactate symbiosis, and exogenous inputs such as alanine supplied by CAFs—to ensure continuity of energy production and biosynthesis (Figure 1).

KRAS mutations enhance glycolysis by upregulating glucose transporters (GLUT1 and SGLT2) and key glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PFK1, LDHA). The elevated lactate production and subsequent efflux via MCT4 maintain intracellular pH balance. PDAC cells utilize intermediates from glycolysis to feed into various biosynthetic pathways, including the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP) and serine biosynthesis pathway, supporting nucleotide, amino acid and lipid synthesis while contributing to redox homeostasis through NADPH generation. In response to nutrient deprivation, PDAC cells engage autophagy and phagocytosis to recycle nutrients, including amino acids and fatty acids. Furthermore, metabolic crosstalk between hypoxic tumour cells and oxygenated tumour cells occurs through lactate exchange mediated by MCT1 and MCT4, with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) providing essential metabolites like alanine to sustain mitochondrial metabolism. Glycolysis promotes PDAC progression

The occurrence and development of tumours often go through steps such as evading immune system surveillance, acquiring unlimited proliferative ability, forming masses, initiating invasive growth, undergoing EMT and distant metastasis (Figure 2). The extent of glycolysis is involved in the above processes of pancreatic cancer. Glycolysis is capable of producing a substantial amount of lactic acid, and tumour-derived lactate exerts a broad immunosuppressive effect, facilitating the survival of metastatic cells under immune pressure, and a significant quantity of lactic acid can be generated through glycolysis. Lactate released from glycolytic tumours can also induce the polarization of tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) into the M2 phenotype, promoting the expression of VEGF and creating a link between immune suppression and angiogenesis (Figure 2) [24].

The role and mechanisms of glycolysis in the progression and metastasis of PDAC.

After pancreatic cancer escapes from immune surveillance, glycolysis further promotes the proliferation and invasive growth of PDAC. This effect is mainly achieved by affecting tumour angiogenesis. Tumour angiogenesis is essential for the growth and dissemination of PDAC (Figure 2). The ‘angiogenic switch’ is continuously activated, with new blood vessels sprouting to provide sustenance; factors such as VEGF-A and fibroblast growth factor(FGF)play critical roles in this process [25]. Hypoxia can directly upregulate VEGF-A and can also promote angiogenesis by enhancing the glycolysis of cancer cells (Figure 2). In pancreatic cancer cells, the absence of PKM2 weakens tumour growth and angiogenesis; under hypoxic conditions, PKM2 translocates into the nucleus, regulating VEGF secretion induced by HIF-1α via the NF-κB/p65 pathway, while secreted PKM2 also promotes endothelial cell proliferation, migration and adhesion to ECM [26]. High glycolysis leads to the accumulation of metabolites like lactate, which interact with G protein-coupled receptor GPR81 to promote tumour growth and angiogenesis; the lactate-GPR81 activation further upregulates MCT1/MCT4 and CD147, enhancing endothelial cell proliferation [27]. This metabolic-receptor axis, along with lactate-induced TAM M2 polarization [24], collectively maintains the positive feedback loop of angiogenesis and immune remodelling.

In addition, glycolysis eventually leads to distant metastasis of pancreatic cancer by enhancing EMT (Figure 2). PDAC is a highly metastatic tumour, and its progression is closely associated with enhanced glycolysis. Increased glycolysis systematically boosts the invasion and metastasis capabilities by promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis and distant colonization [28]. EMT endows epithelial cells with the ability to disseminate and degrade the extracellular matrix (ECM), regulated by transcription factors such as Zeb1, Snail and Twist1, with Zeb1 having a particularly significant impact on metastasis and lesion formation [29,30]. Under hypoxic conditions, elevated glycolysis gives PDAC cells a ‘mesenchymal-like phenotype’ [31]. Numerous glycolysis-related molecules directly drive or maintain the EMT program: PGI/AMF binding to gp78 activates the invasion-metastasis cascade, downregulating E-cadherin, upregulating Snail and promoting the relocalization of RhoA/Rac1 to remodel the cytoskeleton; TGF-β1 induces the upregulation of PFKFB3, enhancing glycolysis and mediating the invasion of PANC-1 cells, or providing energy for migration by enhancing interactions with actin; high expression of ALDOA is associated with low E-cadherin expression and poor prognosis, with its silencing inhibiting proliferation and metastasis while restoring E-cadherin levels [32]. Additionally, lactate metabolism can upregulate TGF-β2 [33], further amplifying migration and EMT. ENO1, acting as a receptor for plasminogen, interacts with cytoskeletal proteins to promote metastasis; its knockdown reduces adhesion to ECM and weakens invasion [34]. Furthermore, lactate secreted by tumour cells can induce cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to secrete hyaluronic acid, remodelling the stroma early and promoting tumour progression [35].

Ultimately, glycolysis can contribute to the recurrence and spread of pancreatic cancer by influencing CTC (Figure 2). The distant colonization of CTCs necessitates overcoming barriers like tissue infiltration, immune clearance and survival in foreign sites, with their metastatic ability partially relying on mesenchymal characteristics enhanced by glycolysis [24,36,37]. TAMs can assist CTCs in traversing the pulmonary capillary wall; the survival of CTCs is highly dependent on the premetastatic and perivascular niches [37]. The immunosuppressive nature of tumour-derived lactate further ensures the survival of metastatic cells [24]. The liver is a common metastatic site for PDAC, where hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) can transdifferentiate into hepatic myofibroblasts (HMFs) under inflammatory conditions, increasing local glycolysis and awakening dormant metastatic cancer cells [38]. Moreover, cancer cells can secrete LDHA and PKM2 as extracellular activation factors to promote metastasis; metabolic-related signalling pathways, including PI3K-AKT, MAPK, HIF-1α and NF-κB, collectively support the growth, survival and stemness of metastatic cells [39]. In summary, the glycolytic phenotype of CTCs and the regulation of their microenvironment synergistically drive the distant dissemination and colonization of PDAC.

Tumour-derived lactic acid, produced through enhanced glycolysis, exerts an immunosuppressive effect, promoting the survival of circulating tumour cells (CTCs) under immune pressure. Lactate polarizes tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) into the M2 phenotype, leading to increased VEGF expression and highlighting the connection between immune suppression and angiogenesis. The ‘angiogenic switch’ is activated, with factors such as VEGF-A and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) driving tumour angiogenesis, further supported by HIF-1α-mediated glycolytic enhancement. PKM2 plays a pivotal role in mediating these processes, translocating to the nucleus under hypoxic conditions to regulate VEGF secretion. Additionally, the accumulation of lactate activates GPR81, which promotes angiogenesis and tumour growth through upregulation of key transporters (MCT1/MCT4) and CD147. Enhanced glycolysis also promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), driven by factors such as TGF-β1, Zeb1 and Snail, allowing PDAC cells to invade and metastasize more efficiently. Finally, the interplay between glycolysis and CTCs contributes to their survival and colonization in distant sites, particularly in the liver, where the microenvironment supports metastatic spread. Factors influencing tumour glycolysis

Driver genes regulating tumour glycolysis

Since the twenty-first century, molecular genetic changes within cancer cells have become a prominent area of research. It is widely recognized that metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark associated with the activation of oncogenes. The development of PDAC involves several driver genes, such as Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS), TP53, SMAD4 and CDKN2A. Among these, the key genes involved in the glycolytic process of PDAC are KRAS and TP53, with mutation rates of approximately 98% and 50%, respectively.

KRAS mutations play a significant role in the glycolysis of pancreatic cancer cells by participating in various biochemical pathways and regulatory mechanisms. Specifically, the KRASG12D mutation upregulates the SLC2A1 gene, promoting the expression of glucose transporter GLUT1 and thereby significantly increasing glucose uptake [40]. Research indicates that PFKFB3 is crucial for aerobic glycolysis in PDAC, with PFKFB3/PFK2 acting as an activating factor of KRAS mutations. Phosphorylated PFKFB3 interacts with GLUT2 within cells to enhance its expression, further facilitating glucose uptake [41]. Moreover, KRASG12D enhances the metabolic adaptability of PDAC cells in high-glucose environments by activating the MAPK and Myc signalling pathways, leading to the upregulation of various glycolytic genes, such as GLUT1, HK1, ENO1 and LDHA. Additionally, KRASG12D promotes the expression of type I cytokine receptor complexes (e.g. IL2rγ-IL4rα and IL2rγ-IL13rα1) through paracrine pathways, which further enhances glycolysis. KRAS mutations also influence glycolysis by reducing the stability of FBW7 (F-box and WD repeat domain-containing protein 7) [42]. Overexpression of FBW7 can activate the c-Myc signalling pathway and target the negative regulator of glucose metabolism, TXNIP, leading to decreased expression of glycolysis-related genes such as GLUT1, GLUT4, HK2, LDHA and LDHB. However, KRAS mutations reverse this inhibitory effect of FBW7, allowing for sustained expression of GLUT1 and promoting glycolysis [43]. Other regulatory mechanisms include the influence of KRAS mutations on NADPH levels, specifically, KRASG12D upregulates the mRNA and protein expression of Nix through activation of the MEK signalling pathway [44], which also impacts glycolysis.

P53 is involved in the regulation of glycolysis in pancreatic cancer through multiple mechanisms, thereby affecting cellular metabolism and cancer progression. Firstly, p53 can transcriptionally inhibit glycolysis-related genes, particularly 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 4 (PFKFB4), which slows down glycolysis and reduces the cancer cells’ demand for glucose [45]. Additionally, p53 suppresses glycolysis by upregulating the expression of TP53-induced glycolysis and apoptosis regulator (TIGAR), a known inhibitor of glycolysis; it accomplishes this by directly binding to the TIGAR promoter to enhance its expression [46]. Furthermore, p53 activates the transcription factor Thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP), which acts as a negative regulator of glycolysis by inhibiting the expression of GLUT1 and thereby decreasing glucose uptake [47,48]. In conditions of glucose scarcity, p53 impacts glucose metabolism through the AMPK signalling pathway, where the activation of AMPK enhances the metabolic adaptability of cells [49,50]. When p53 undergoes gain-of-function mutations (GOF mutp53), its role shifts to promoting glycolysis by enhancing GLUT1 translocation, thereby increasing glucose uptake and improving the metabolic adaptability of tumour cells. Specifically, GOF mutp53 promotes the transport of GLUT1 to the plasma membrane, consequently amplifying the Warburg effect and enhancing cancer cell survival [24,51]. Moreover, p53 modulates Sirtuin1 (SIRT1) to influence the cytoplasmic localization of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), thus facilitating glycolysis [24]. In cancer cells lacking p53, the enhancement of glycolysis supports rapid proliferation, while the absence of p53 leads to a heightened reliance on PFKFB4 function. P53 can also transcriptionally repress paraoxonase 2 (PON2), but mutant p53 elevates PON2 expression, activating GLUT1-mediated glucose transport and promoting the anti-apoptotic capabilities of cancer cells. In summary, p53 plays a critical role in the regulation of glycolysis in pancreatic cancer through these complex mechanisms, significantly influencing the survival and proliferation of cancer cells [51,52].

Glycolytic enzyme expression and activity

Indeed, it is now widely recognized that enhanced glycolysis represents a major metabolic alteration in tumours and serves as a hallmark of PDAC, characterized by the overexpression of various glycolytic enzymes [53]. These glycolytic enzymes regulate the glycolytic rate by influencing genomic stability, transcriptional regulation, and post-translational modifications.

HK2 is the first rate-limiting enzyme of glycolysis, catalyzing the phosphorylation of glucose to glucose-6-phosphate (G6P). In PDAC, the overexpression of HK2 significantly enhances glycolytic flux, thereby promoting energy supply and biosynthesis in tumour cells [54]. PFK1 (phosphofructokinase-1) and PKM2 are critical rate-limiting enzymes in glycolysis. Specifically, PFK1 catalyzes the conversion of F6P to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (F1,6BP), while PFKFB3 activates PFK1 by generating F2,6BP, thereby augmenting glycolysis [55–57]. Meanwhile, PKM2, in its low-activity state, facilitates the accumulation of glycolytic intermediates to support the biosynthetic demands of tumour cells.

Research indicates that LDHA catalyzes the reduction of pyruvate to lactate while oxidizing NADH to NAD+, sustaining glycolytic flux. Moreover, lactate acidifies the TME, promoting immune evasion and tumour metastasis [58]. Additionally, GLUT1, the primary glucose transporter in tumour cells, exhibits significant upregulation in PDAC to meet the high glucose demand of cancer cells [59].

Notably, the regulation of these key enzymes involves mutations in KRAS and TP53, further underscoring the importance of driver genes in this context.

Mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondrial dysfunction is one of the core features of metabolic reprogramming in PDAC, promoting the ‘Warburg effect’ through various mechanisms [60]. Common mtDNA mutations in PDAC, such as those in the D-loop region, impair the function of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) and reduce the efficiency of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). These mtDNA mutations can also lead to ATP insufficiency, activating the AMPK signalling pathway and promoting the expression of key glycolytic enzymes, including HK2, PKM2 and LDHA. Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction results in an imbalance in mitochondrial dynamics, accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), defects in mitophagy and disruption of the TCA cycle [61,62], all contributing to the dependence of PDAC on glycolysis.

Metabolic heterogeneity in PDAC

To avoid overgeneralization of a single PDAC metabolic program, it is important to acknowledge the metabolic heterogeneity within PDAC. Although many PDAC cells exhibit a strong glycolytic phenotype (Warburg effect), a subpopulation of cells may rely more on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), particularly under certain microenvironmental conditions or therapeutic pressures. Hypoxia, stromal interactions (e.g. with cancer-associated fibroblasts) and exposure to chemotherapy can shift the balance between glycolysis and OXPHOS. For instance, CAFs can undergo reverse Warburg effect, producing lactate that adjacent cancer cells utilize for OXPHOS. This metabolic plasticity allows PDAC cells to adapt to fluctuating nutrient availability and therapeutic insults, and should be considered when interpreting ncRNA-mediated metabolic regulation.

The interaction between immune cells and malignant cells creates the tumour microenvironment (TME), which plays a critical role in regulating tumour growth, invasion, metastasis and immune evasion. Notably, the TME of PDAC is characterized by high levels of fibrosis, immunosuppression and hypoxia, all of which support glycolysis and thereby impact tumour growth, metastasis and treatment resistance [63]. The dense fibrotic stroma in PDAC restricts blood flow, resulting in a hypoxic TME; hypoxia can stabilize HIF-1α, directly upregulating key glycolytic enzymes. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), an essential component of the TME, have been found to secrete lactate, glutamine and TGF-β, which collectively regulate glycolysis. Additionally, immune cells can compete for glucose and activate the STAT3 pathway, while the significant lactate production from PDAC glycolysis leads to an acidic environment within the TME, further stimulating HIF-1α and promoting immune evasion [64]. In summary, the TME plays a crucial role in the glycolytic processes associated with PDAC.

Regulatory mechanism of ncRNAs in glycolysis of PDAC

The ncRNAs can regulate the expression of key genes or enzymes involved in the glycolysis pathway through various cellular signalling pathways, thereby promoting the malignant development of tumours. It should be noted that although a growing number of ncRNAs have been implicated in glycolytic regulation, a substantial proportion of mechanistic evidence is derived from non-PDAC tumour models. Therefore, further studies are required to validate these findings specifically in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. MicroRNAs in the glycolysis of PDAC

MiRNAs are a family of short non-coding RNAs, typically ranging from 19 to 25 nucleotides in length, and they regulate target gene expression at both the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels instead of being translated at the protein level (Figure 3). Their expression is highly conserved and exhibits significant specificity pertaining to developmental stages and tissue types [65]. Dysregulation of miRNAs expression or function is closely associated with cancer, as miRNAs are involved in various physiological and pathological processes by modulating the expression and activity of their target genes, thereby influencing tumour development [66]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that miRNAs can regulate tumour metabolism and play a crucial role in the glycolytic processes of PDAC.

Regulation of glycolysis in PDAC by microRNAs (miRNAs) and their associated molecular pathways.

MiRNAs primarily regulate glycolysis by modulating enzymes and genes involved in the process (Figure 3). It is well established that cancer cells typically enhance glucose uptake and metabolism through the Warburg effect to acquire substantial energy, even in the presence of oxygen. MiRNAs influence glycolytic processes by regulating the expression of target genes. For instance, miR-3662 has been found to target HIF-1α dominant oxygen regulator widely present in mammals, triggering various biological events, including the activation of glycolysis and tumourigenesis. MiR-3662 inhibits aerobic glycolysis in PDAC cells via a negative feedback loop involving HIF-1α, suppressing the expression of glucose transporters (SLC2A1) and glycolytic enzymes (such as PFKP, PKM and LDHA) (Figure 3), thereby reducing metabolic activity in cancer cells. Moreover, in a gemcitabine-resistant mouse model, overexpression of miR-3662 was able to resensitize tumours to gemcitabine (GEM) treatment, reducing tumour growth, likely due to a decrease in HIF-1α levels, which is known to promote glycolysis in cancer cells [67]. Additionally, miR-889-3p and miR-185-5p also participate in the regulation of HIF-1α [68,69].

Lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), an important metabolic enzyme belonging to the 2-hydroxy oxidoreductase family, plays a crucial role in anaerobic glucose metabolism within cells. Hypoxic conditions induce the overexpression of LDHA in PDAC, shifting the metabolic pathway for ATP synthesis from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis (Figure 3). Therefore, inhibiting LDHA is considered an effective strategy for tumour treatment [70,71]. Recent studies have found that miR-4259 directly targets the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) of LDHA, suppressing its expression and reducing lactate dehydrogenase activity and lactate production, thus attenuating the glycolytic effects mediated by LDHA and tumour stemness characteristics [72]. Furthermore, miR-510-5p directly inhibits c-Myc expression by binding to the 3′ UTR of c-Myc mRNA and subsequently binding to the transcription start site (TSS) in the LDHA promoter region, thereby repressing LDHA transcription and expression [73]. MiRNAs are involved in the regulation of key enzymes in the glycolytic process; for example, functional experiments have shown that miR-30d directly targets the transcription factor RUNX1, inhibiting its transcriptional activation of key glycolytic enzymes SLC2A1 (encoding glucose transporter GLUT1) and HK1. SLC2A1 and HK1 are important enzymes in the glycolytic pathway, responsible for glucose uptake and the first step of glycolysis, respectively. By inhibiting the expression of these enzymes, miR-30d significantly reduces glucose uptake (Figure 3) [74].

Studies have indicated that genes such as miR-143, miR-4458, miR-98, miR-125a and miR-323a are considered key tumour-suppressive miRNAs, regulating glycolysis by targeting HK2 (Figure 3) [75–79]. As a critical rate-limiting enzyme in glycolysis, HK2 catalyzes the first irreversible step, with glycolytic levels significantly elevated in various tumour cells. HK2 strongly inhibits the mitochondrial functions responsible for tumour growth, survival and metastasis. Moreover, miR-124 has been found to regulate monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) to inhibit glycolytic activity in tumour cells, affecting the acid-base balance by suppressing lactate transport and thereby inhibiting cancer cell proliferation and invasion [80].

MiRNAs also influence glucose metabolism through the regulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway and the p53 pathway. MiR-21 has been shown to promote glycolysis in PDAC by targeting PTEN and activating the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway, highlighting its role in metabolic reprogramming in pancreatic cancer [81]. MiR-126 suppresses cancer cell growth by targeting the p85β subunit of PI3K [82], while miR-199a-3p blocks cell cycle progression by inhibiting mTOR [83]. MiR-181a increases lactate production and cell proliferation by inhibiting PTEN, thus upregulating AKT activity [84]. Additionally, p53 inhibits glycolysis by downregulating genes such as GLUT1, GLUT3 and HK2, while miR-661, miR-605 and miR-29 regulate the MDM2-p53 feedback loop to affect p53 stability (Figure 3) [85]. Important, p53 can reprogram glucose metabolism in tumour cells, reversing the so-called Warburg effect to reduce their oncogenic potential and suppress the formation of aggressive tumours. Recent studies have further expanded the landscape of glycolysis-regulating miRNAs in PDAC. For instance, a newly identified regulatory axis involving Circular RNA LIPH promotes pancreatic cancer glycolysis and progression through sponge miR-769-3p and interaction with GOLM1 [86]. Additionally, exosomal lncRNA LINC01268 promotes pancreatic cancer progression via the miR-217-KIF2A-PI3K/AKT axis [87]. These findings collectively underscore the diverse mechanisms by which miRNAs integrate into the glycolytic regulatory network.

MiR-3662 targets HIF-1α, leading to a decrease in the expression of key glycolytic enzymes, including PFKP, PKM and LDHA, as well as the glucose transporter SLC2A1. By inhibiting HIF-1α, miR-3662 decreases aerobic glycolysis in cancer cells. Additionally, miR-4259 directly inhibits LDHA, resulting in a reduction of lactate production and overall glycolytic activity. The role of miR-510-5p further supports this regulatory network by repressing c-Myc, which consequently decreases the transcriptional activation of LDHA. Furthermore, miR-30d acts on RUNX1, thereby limiting the expression of both SLC2A1 and HK1, which are crucial for glucose uptake and the initiation of glycolysis. Other important miRNAs, such as miR-143, miR-4458, miR-98, miR-125a and miR-323a, exert negative regulation on HK2, a key enzyme in glycolysis that catalyzes the first committed step of the pathway. In addition, these miRNAs influence critical signalling pathways, including the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and the p53 pathway, which further modulate the metabolic dynamics in PDAC cells, emphasizing the multifaceted role miRNAs play in cancer cell metabolism. LncRNAs in the glycolysis of PDAC

LncRNAs are a class of non-protein-coding transcripts that exceed 200 nucleotides in length. LncRNAs play diverse roles in physiological and pathological processes; they bind to proteins through their unique secondary structures, forming RNA-protein complexes that interact with multiple RNAs to create complex gene expression regulatory networks. Increasing evidence suggests that lncRNAs exert intricate and precise regulatory functions by acting as oncogenes or tumour suppressors. They not only regulate the proliferation, differentiation, invasion and metastasis of cancer cells but also influence metabolic reprogramming. Similar to miRNAs, lncRNAs function by modulating genes and key enzymes associated with glycolysis. Therefore, lncRNAs serve as crucial regulators in the glycolytic process of PDAC.

Research indicates that lncRNA-BX111 downregulation can promote glycolysis, invasion and metastasis of pancreatic cancer by regulating the transcription of ZEB1 (Figure 4) [88]. ZEB1 is a critical regulator of EMT, and its increased expression is closely associated with the upregulation of glycolytic enzymes [89]. Furthermore, lncRNA Sox2ot enhances the EMT and stemness characteristics of pancreatic cancer cells by competitively binding to miRNAs (the competing endogenous RNA mechanism) to regulate the expression of glycolysis-related genes. Studies have shown that high expression levels of Sox2ot can facilitate glycolytic metabolism in pancreatic cancer cells, increase lactate production and support tumour cell proliferation and migration by regulating key enzymes such as LDHA and PKM2 (Figure 4) [90].

Role of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in glycolysis and metabolic reprogramming in PDAC.

For instance, PTAR, lncRNA-PNUTS, lncRNA-ATB and H19 can regulate tumour glycolysis through ZEB1 [89,91,92]. Additionally, CCAT2 can enhance the expression of the glycolytic isoform of glutaminase (GLS) by modulating the selective splicing of metabolism-related genes, thereby increasing the metabolic capacity and invasive potential of cancer cells [93]. HIF-1α is considered a key inducer of the Warburg effect and regulates tumour metabolism by interacting with various lncRNAs (Figure 4). For instance, lncRNA-p21 has been reported to stabilize HIF-1α expression and promote glycolysis in non-PDAC models such as HeLa cells; however, direct evidence in PDAC remains limited, suggesting that further validation in pancreatic cancer is required [94,95]. PCED1B-AS1 enhances the translation of HIF-1α mRNA by binding to its 5′-UTR, promoting glycolysis and the proliferation of PDAC and glioblastoma [96,97]. HIF-1α can activate GLUT1 and LDHA, enhancing the anaerobic glycolytic pathway to promote tumour growth. Inhibiting the activity of ZEB1 and HIF-1α or related pathways may represent potential strategies for cancer therapy. In other malignancies, such as prostate and gastric cancers, lncRNAs have been shown to regulate glycolysis through c-Myc and NF-κB signalling pathways. However, whether these mechanisms are conserved in PDAC requires further investigation [98–100].

LncRNA GLS-AS has been reported to regulate glycolytic metabolism under nutritional stress conditions by inhibiting GLS expression; however, current evidence is primarily derived from non-PDAC models, and its role in pancreatic cancer requires further validation. The downregulation of GLS-AS leads to an increase in GLS activity, thereby promoting glycolytic metabolism and tumour cell growth [101,102]. Recent studies using a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) system via lentiviral vectors to silence Linc00941 revealed a significant suppression of glycolytic capacity in PANC-1 and SW1990 cell lines. Measurement of mRNA expression levels of enzymes in the glycolytic pathway showed a substantial decrease in the mRNA expression of GLUT1, HK2, PFKFB3 and LDHA [103]. LncRNA HOTAIR enhances glucose uptake by activating HK2, thereby boosting energy metabolism and growth in PDAC cells (Figure 4) [103]. Additionally, HOTAIR influences cell proliferation and metastatic potential by modulating the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway [104].

Another lncRNA, PVT1, can upregulate HIF-1α under hypoxic conditions, further activating glycolysis-related genes and promoting tumour cell growth and chemoresistance [105]. HOTTIP affects cell proliferation and apoptosis by regulating the expression of HOX genes, enhancing the invasiveness of tumour cells (Figure 4) [106]. Meanwhile, lncRNA IGFBP4-1 promotes glycolysis and lactate production through energy metabolism reprogramming, involving the upregulation or indirect stimulation of key glycolytic enzymes such as PDK1 and HK2 [107], thereby supporting rapid tumour cell proliferation and invasiveness. Metalnc9 (Linc00963) has been shown to regulate glycolysis and tumour progression in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) by stabilizing PGK1 and activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway [108]. Similarly, in colorectal cancer, FEZF1-AS1 promotes glycolysis by interacting with PKM2 [108], while AC020978 regulates both PKM2 and the HIF-1α complex, contributing to aggressive tumour phenotypes in NSCLC [109]. However, these findings are largely derived from non-PDAC malignancies, and their relevance to pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma remains to be further validated [109,110]. Emerging evidence has highlighted additional lncRNAs involved in PDAC glycolysis. For example, LINC00941 promotes glycolysis in pancreatic cancer by modulating the Hippo pathway, specifically through PP2A-mediated dephosphorylation of MST1 and subsequent nuclear localization of YAP1, which enhances transcription of GLUT1, HK2, PFKFB3 and LDHA [111]. Furthermore, the glycolytic-related CircHPCAL1 promotes the progression of pancreatic cancer via the regulation of STEAP2 axis [112].

Recent studies suggest that LINC00941 promotes protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A)-mediated dephosphorylation of mammalian STE20-like protein kinase 1 (MST1) through its interaction with MST1. This process activates the Hippo signalling pathway, thereby enhancing glycolysis. LINC00941 also promotes the nuclear localization of YAP1, enhancing the transcription of glycolysis-related genes such as GLUT1, HK2, PFKFB3 and LDHA (Figure 4) [111]. Similar to miRNAs, lncRNAs such as LINC00470 (also known as C18orf2), and AK023948 can participate in the regulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, which plays a crucial role in glycolysis during cancer (Figure 4) [113,114]. Additionally, lncRNAs like ANRIL accelerate the progression of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) via the AdipoR1/AMPK/SIRT1 pathway [115]. LINP1 influences AML progression by employing HNF4α as a transcription factor within the HNF4α/AMPK/WNT5A signalling pathway, enhancing AMPK phosphorylation [116]; AMPK is an essential cellular energy sensor activated through phosphorylation that regulates glycolysis. Moreover, lncRNAs modulate the p53 signalling pathway in various ways; for instance, MALAT1 enhances the activity of SIRT1, promoting the deacetylation of p53 and thereby inhibiting its activation of downstream target genes (Figure 4) [117]. LINK-A enhances the K48 polyubiquitination-mediated degradation of p53, reducing its stability [118]. It is worth noting that there has been limited exploration of the regulatory networks of lncRNAs in PDAC, making this review of significant reference value.

They play dual roles in tumourigenesis, acting as potential oncogenes or tumour suppressors, and influence cancer cell proliferation, differentiation, invasion and metabolic pathways. CircRNAs in the glycolysis of PDAC

CircRNAs are a distinct class of ncRNAs characterized by their covalently closed loop structure, formed by the ligation of the 3′ and 5′ ends of RNA transcripts. Unlike linear RNAs such as mRNA, circRNAs were initially considered byproducts of aberrant RNA splicing (Figure 5). However, their biogenesis is now understood to involve multiple mechanisms, including intron-pairing-driven circularization, RNA-binding protein (RBP)-mediated circularization and lariat-driven circularization [119].

Role of circRNAs in glycolysis and tumour progression in PDAC.

Functionally, circRNAs exhibit remarkable versatility. The majority act as miRNA sponges, sequestering miRNAs to modulate gene expression. They also interact with RBPs, regulate alternative splicing and transcription, influence translation, generate pseudogenes and participate in cellular transport and communication [119]. Notably, some circRNAs contain open reading frames (ORFs) that can be translated into, offering potential applications in early diagnosis and prognosis [120]. Additionally, circRNAs have been implicated in various physiological and pathological processes, including embryonic development, preeclampsia, spermatogenesis, endometrial cancer and ovarian epithelial tumours. These findings highlight their potential as therapeutic targets or biomarkers for disease [121].

The exceptional stability of circRNAs, conferred by their unique circular structure, has attracted significant scientific interest. Moreover, their regulatory role in glycolysis has emerged as a focal point in cancer research, underscoring their importance in tumour metabolism.

CircRREB1 is significantly upregulated in the tumour tissues of PDAC patients and shows a negative correlation with the five-year overall survival rate of these patients (Figure 5). Additionally, high levels of circRREB1 correlate positively with the TNM staging of the disease. CircRREB1 inhibits the binding of the tumour suppressor PTEN to PGK1, thereby preventing the dephosphorylation of PGK1 and increasing its phosphorylation at the Y324 site, which enhances glycolytic activity (Figure 5). Furthermore, circRREB1 activates the transcription of WNT7B through its interaction with YBX1, leading to the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway, further promoting glycolysis (Figure 5) [122]. Notably, hsa_circ_0008383 also facilitates glycolysis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma via the SNRPA/PPA2c/mTORC1 axis. The article highlights that hsa_circ_0008383 is highly expressed in gemcitabine-resistant PDAC cells and tumour tissues, suggesting its potential role as a biomarker for drug resistance in circRNAs (Figure 5) [123].

In PDAC, circRNAs function primarily as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), regulating glycolysis through circRNA–miRNA–mRNA interaction networks (Figure 5). Current research on circRNAs indicates that they primarily participate in tumour regulation through miRNA interactions. For instance, hsa_circ_0012634 is significantly downregulated in PDAC tissues and cells. Its overexpression can inhibit PDAC cell proliferation and glycolysis while promoting apoptosis. The underlying mechanism involves the binding of hsa_circ_0012634 to miR-147b, suppressing its expression and consequently upregulating the target gene HIPK2, whose elevated expression reduces the glycolytic capacity of PDAC cells (Figure 5) [124]. Additionally, CircSLIT2 promotes glycolysis and proliferation in PDAC cells via the miR-510-5p/c-Myc/LDHA axis [73]. Circ_0072008 enhances proliferation, migration, invasion and glycolysis of PDAC cells by regulating the miR-545-3p/SLC7A11 axis (Figure 5) [125]. Notably, hsa_circ_0008383 has been identified as a critical regulator of glycolysis in gemcitabine-resistant PDAC cells through the SNRPA/PPA2c/mTORC1 axis, highlighting its potential as both a biomarker and therapeutic target for chemoresistance [123].

NcRNAs in mitochondrial dysfunction and TME

It is well established that mitochondrial dysfunction plays a critical role in the progression of PDAC. Regarding the role of ncRNAs in mitochondria, researchers have discovered that 7S RNA—a key functional unit of ncRNA found in mammalian mitochondria—significantly regulates mitochondrial gene expression by binding to mitochondrial RNA polymerase (POLRMT), inhibiting the initiation process of transcription [126]. Although studies on ncRNAs in mitochondrial function exist, such as the observation that microRNA-33 deficiency in macrophages improves mitochondrial homeostasis and enhances autophagy [127], investigations specifically in PDAC remain relatively scarce.

Beyond direct metabolic regulation, ncRNAs exert multifaceted effects on the TME. These can be conceptually separated into three interconnected components: (a) glycolysis-driven acidosis and lactate signalling effects, (b) mitochondrial and redox consequences and (c) immune and stromal phenotypes. For instance, miR-21 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway, promoting M2 macrophage polarization and tumour growth. CircFAM13B stimulates CD8+ T cells to secrete increased IFN-γ, enhancing sensitivity to immunotherapy [128]. It is important to distinguish between mechanistic evidence (where manipulation of an ncRNA alters glycolysis and downstream phenotype) and associative evidence (based solely on correlation), as the former provides stronger support for therapeutic targeting.

Clinical applications and progress

Despite being recognized as one of the most aggressive cancers, treatment options for PDAC remain limited. GEM has been the traditional standard therapy; however, due to the heterogeneity of PDAC, resistance to this drug often develops. Although the FOLFIRINOX regimen shows better efficacy, it is only suitable for patients with a high functional status. The early diagnosis rate of PDAC is notably low, with only 13% of patients being diagnosed at a stage where the cancer is resectable; most patients present with metastatic disease, resulting in a very low five-year survival rate. Therefore, the role of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) as biomarkers and their potential as therapeutic targets have significant clinical implications.

From a translational readiness perspective, ncRNA candidates can be stratified into three categories. (1) Biomarker-ready candidates: miR-21 and circ-ASH2L have demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for early PDAC diagnosis in retrospective cohorts, and lncRNA UCA1 overexpression is associated with shortened survival, supporting their utility as prognostic markers [129–131]. (2) Target-ready candidates: LINC02432 promotes gemcitabine resistance by adsorbing miR-98-5p and relieving HK2 inhibition, while circACTR2 mitigates resistance by sponging miR-221-3p to relieve PTEN suppression and inhibit PI3K/AKT signalling [78,132]. (3) Exploratory candidates: numerous other ncRNAs have shown regulatory effects in preclinical models but require further validation.

Key practical barriers must be addressed before clinical translation. Delivery challenges include the need for tumour-specific targeting and efficient systemic delivery of ncRNA modulators; the dense PDAC stroma further limits tumour penetration. Molecular stability issues arise from the susceptibility of RNA-based therapeutics to nuclease degradation. Off-target effects and unintended immune activation remain concerns, as ncRNAs can regulate multiple targets. The types of studies needed to address these issues include: prospective cohort validation for candidate biomarkers, standardized biospecimen handling protocols and orthotopic preclinical models that recapitulate the human PDAC stroma and vasculature.

Promising preclinical advances are underway. The nano-drug miR-21i@HA-Gem-SS-P12 significantly suppresses PDAC progression by concurrently inhibiting miR-21 and delivering gemcitabine [128]. The bioflavonoid apigenin enhances anti-tumour effects by regulating glycolysis-related immune responses through inhibition of the miR-155/SHIP-1 axis [129]. Additionally, combining ncRNAs with immunotherapy represents a promising direction; for example, lncRNA NEAT1 knockout enhances immune checkpoint inhibitor efficacy (e.g. PD-1/PD-L1) through glycolysis modulation [130]. Recent studies have also conjugated gemcitabine to tumour-suppressive microRNAs (miR-15a and miR-194), demonstrating that Gem-miR-15a exhibits superior tumour inhibitory capabilities compared to gemcitabine alone in both in vitro and in vivo studies [133].

Research has demonstrated that miR-21 and circ-ASH2L exhibit high sensitivity and specificity for the early diagnosis of PDAC [129,130]. Studies have found that the overexpression of lncRNA UCA1 is associated with a shortened survival period in patients, suggesting the potential role of ncRNAs as prognostic markers [131]. Extensive research has also explored the involvement of ncRNAs in tumour treatment resistance. For instance, LINC02432 promotes PDAC resistance, particularly to gemcitabine, by adsorbing miR-98-5p and releasing its inhibition on HK2 [78]. Similarly, circACTR2 mitigates gemcitabine resistance by adsorbing miR-3-221p, thereby alleviating its suppression of PTEN and inhibiting the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway [132]. This indicates that ncRNAs have the potential to serve as glycolytic targets. Notably, the nano-drug miR-21i@HA-Gem-SS-P12 significantly suppresses PDAC progression by concurrently inhibiting miR-21 and GEM [134]. Additionally, the bioflavonoid apigenin (API) enhances anti-tumour effects by regulating glycolysis-related immune responses through the inhibition of the miR-155/SHIP-1 axis [135]. Furthermore, the combination of ncRNAs with immunotherapy represents a promising direction for future research; for example, the knockout of lncRNA NEAT1 can enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as PD-1/PD-L1) through modulation of glycolysis [136].

In recent years, extensive research has focused on the role of ncRNAs in the drug resistance of PDAC. For instance, LINC01134 interacts with the tumour suppressor microRNA miR-497-5p in PDAC cells, promoting GEM resistance via the WNT5A/WNT pathway [137]. Additionally, CircRPS29 enhances the expression of TRIM29 through competitive interaction with miR-770-5p, subsequently activating the MEK/ERK signalling pathway to promote GEM resistance in PDAC [138]. Moreover, CAFs play a crucial role in GEM resistance within PDAC. Research indicates that CircFARP1 enables CAFs to enhance gemcitabine resistance in pancreatic cancer through the LIF/STAT3 axis [139]. Excitingly, a recent study developed Gem-miR-15a and Gem-miR-194 by conjugating GEM to tumour-suppressive microRNAs (miR-15a and miR-194). Subsequent experiments demonstrated that GEM-conjugated miRNAs are more effective than GEM alone, particularly Gem-miR-15a, which exhibited significant tumour inhibitory capabilities in both in vitro and in vivo studies [133].

Translational challenges and clinical readiness of ncRNA-based strategies. Although numerous ncRNAs have been identified as regulators of aerobic glycolysis in PDAC, their clinical translation remains at different stages. Some ncRNAs, such as miR-21, have demonstrated potential as diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers, supported by validation in patient samples. In contrast, many other ncRNAs, including lncRNAs and circRNAs, are still primarily investigated in preclinical models and lack robust clinical evidence.

Importantly, several challenges limit the clinical application of ncRNA-based therapies. First, the efficient and targeted delivery of ncRNAs remains a major obstacle, particularly due to the dense stromal microenvironment characteristic of PDAC. Second, issues related to molecular stability, off-target effects and potential immune activation need to be carefully addressed. Third, the metabolic heterogeneity of PDAC further complicates therapeutic targeting, as different tumour cell populations may exhibit distinct metabolic dependencies.

Future studies should focus on large-scale clinical validation, standardized detection methods and the development of safe and efficient delivery systems. Additionally, integrating ncRNA-based strategies with existing therapies, such as chemotherapy and immunotherapy, may offer new opportunities for improving treatment outcomes in PDAC.

Future perspectives and conclusion

The development and progression of PDAC is a complex physiological process. Due to its high malignancy, PDAC poses significant challenges in clinical practice; however, current technological methods face difficulties in early detection, intervention and treatment. One of the prominent changes in tumour cells during metabolic processes is the increased rate of glycolysis, which meets the high energy and nutritional demands required for rapid tumour cell growth. This biological characteristic change also leads to adaptive alterations in tumour cell phenotypes, including tolerance to low glucose and hypoxic environments, as well as enhanced proliferation, migration and invasion capabilities. While these cellular phenotype changes provide new entry points for the development of clinical anti-cancer drugs, they also present new challenges. Various tumour genes and signalling pathways regulate glycolytic enzymes to influence glycolysis, but due to the high heterogeneity of PDAC, the mechanisms of key enzymes and their complex interactions with signalling pathways have not been well studied.

The process of glycolysis requires the participation of numerous factors. Research has shown that ncRNAs, including miRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs and 7S RNA can regulate glycolysis directly or indirectly from different angles, demonstrating the importance of ncRNAs in the regulation of tumour glycolytic processes. We have discovered that the expression of specific ncRNAs in PDAC can predict the biological characteristics of the tumour and its potential outcomes and prognosis. On the other hand, ncRNAs may also become targets for tumour therapy. Unfortunately, despite many studies demonstrating the mechanisms by which ncRNAs are involved in the regulation of glycolysis, there are still few applications of ncRNA regulation of glycolysis in clinical anti-tumour therapy, with most still in the clinical trial phase.

In summary, ncRNAs play significant roles in the aerobic glycolysis of PDAC, regulating the activity and levels of specific enzymes and acting as transcriptional activators to modulate the expression of metabolism-related genes. Furthermore, addressing how to ensure the precise targeting of ncRNAs, select appropriate combination therapy strategies and cope with the potential for ncRNAs to confer drug resistance or reduce resistance in tumour cells are challenges that need to be solved in the future. Our current research focus should be on confirming and elucidating the potential of ncRNAs as molecular biomarkers in the aerobic glycolysis of PDAC. Clarifying the mechanisms of ncRNAs in the aerobic glycolysis of PDAC will provide new insights for PDAC research and offer new strategies for clinical treatment.

In summary, ncRNAs play significant roles in the aerobic glycolysis of PDAC, regulating the activity and levels of specific enzymes and acting as transcriptional activators to modulate the expression of metabolism-related genes. Future research should focus on mapping the integrated ncRNA-glycolysis-signalling network, validating key regulatory nodes with mechanistic rigour and developing multi-target tumour-specific delivery strategies that overcome stromal barriers. Addressing how to ensure precise targeting of ncRNAs, selecting appropriate combination therapy strategies and managing the potential for ncRNAs to either confer or overcome drug resistance are challenges that need to be solved in the future.
