Airway immunometabolic responses during pulmonary bacterial and viral infections
Authors: Ridhima Wadhwa, Eric Tang, Lok Yin Roy Wong, Tania Wong Fok Lung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYAirway infections caused by viral and bacterial pathogens pose a significant threat to human health, with the COVID-19 pandemic serving as a stark reminder of their detrimental impact. This review explores the critical role of metabolism in determining the outcome of respiratory infections. It covers fundamental concepts in immunometabolism and details how common pathogens exploit the host metabolism to dysregulate immune responses or evade immune clearance. We further consider how immune-signaling metabolites can directly drive pathogen evolution, emphasizing the importance of a better understanding of host-pathogen metabolic interactions in developing effective new therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Over the last decade, metabolism has gained renewed attention for its critical contribution to immune cell function, resulting in the emergence of the rapidly expanding field of immunometabolism. While initial research primarily focused on metabolic disorders such as obesity and cancer,1 the field has increasingly recognized the crucial role of metabolism in infection.2–6 Early studies demonstrated that macrophages stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a major immunostimulant from Gram-negative bacteria, exhibit a metabolic shift reminiscent of the Warburg effect in cancer cells.7 This metabolic reprogramming is characterized by an increase in aerobic glycolysis (glucose metabolism to lactate even in the presence of oxygen) and a decrease in mitochondrial activity. Such changes result in the accumulation of metabolites that act as signaling molecules, driving the inflammatory function of the macrophages. Investigations employing live bacterial infection models have revealed that bacterial pathogens can subvert the host metabolism to evade immune clearance.8–11 These findings highlight the contribution of bacterial metabolism itself, which is highly adaptable to the microenvironment, in influencing host metabolic changes and demonstrate the complexity of metabolic regulation of immunity.

The COVID-19 pandemic, which claimed 18 million deaths globally between 2020 and 2021,12 has shifted our general perspective of infections, revealing their significant metabolic aspect. Heightened susceptibility to severe COVID-19 in patients with metabolic dysfunctions, coupled with the observation that severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection induces or exacerbates metabolic disorders, provided compelling evidence for the intricate metabolic-immune connection. Prior to the development of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, several metabolism-modulating drugs—including statins—were investigated as potential therapeutic options, further highlighting the critical role of metabolism in the context of infectious diseases. This review will delve into the metabolic responses induced by prevalent bacterial and viral pathogens in the airway and their impact on immunity.

METABOLIC CHANGES AND IMMUNE-SIGNALING METABOLITES

Traditionally, metabolites have been considered the building blocks of energy production and macromolecule synthesis, passively accumulating according to the cell’s metabolic demands. Subsequent seminal studies have fundamentally changed this concept by demonstrating that metabolites actively regulate immune-signaling processes rather than serving merely as byproducts of cellular activity.13–16 Macrophages and other immune cells adopt an inflammatory phenotype by increasing aerobic glycolysis in cancer settings and during infections.7,17 Much of the foundational work on macrophage metabolism, particularly after LPS stimulation, has depicted accompanying breaks in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle that downregulate mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and result in the accumulation of the metabolites, succinate, citrate, fumarate, and itaconate. Disruption of the TCA cycle enables excess citrate to enter anabolic pathways that promote inflammation.7 Similarly, succinate and fumarate act as pro-inflammatory metabolites. Itaconate exhibits early pro-inflammatory effects18–20 but later adopts anti-inflammatory functions, mitigating excessive tissue damage and promoting tolerance to NLRP3 inflammasome activation.21 Nonetheless, rapid deviations from canonical metabolic reprogramming can occur in response to changes in the microenvironment, underscoring the highly dynamic and adaptable nature of cellular metabolism.

More recently, studies have shown that bacteria and viruses exploit the host metabolism to establish persistent infection or chronic sequelae (after viral clearance). Herein, we will explore the mechanisms underlying immunometabolic manipulation by clinically relevant pulmonary pathogens. We will begin with a brief overview of how key TCA cycle metabolites direct the immune response (Figure 1), which has been extensively reviewed elsewhere.22,23 We focus on macrophages, monocytes, and neutrophils, the dominant innate immune cells early in infection, which play a key role in influencing the ensuing adaptive immune response. While other immune cells, such as dendritic cells (DCs) and T cells, also undergo metabolic reprogramming, we narrow our scope to emphasize core immunometabolic mechanisms. This avoids the added complexity of cell-intrinsic programs, which are often overridden by microenvironmental cues. Macrophages exhibit a spectrum of activation states, with metabolic programs supporting a range of pro- and anti-inflammatory functions rather than fitting a strict M1/M2 dichotomy. This metabolic flexibility is key to rapidly influencing disease resistance—the ability of the host to eliminate pathogens through the induction of inflammation—and disease tolerance—the capacity to limit tissue damage and preserve host survival at the expense of pathogen clearance.24

Pro-inflammatory immune response

Succinate

Succinate functions as a pro-inflammatory signaling molecule through various mechanisms. Upon LPS stimulation, succinate accumulates primarily through glutamine-dependent anaplerosis (glutaminolysis) and stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), inducing glycolysis25 and pro-inflammatory interleukin (IL)-1β production.7,26 Simultaneously, HIF-1α promotes the production of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1), which phosphorylates and suppresses pyruvate dehydrogenase E1α (PDH-E1α), inactivating the TCA cycle and OXPHOS.27 These metabolic changes mirror the Warburg effect in cancer cells. Succinate further exacerbates IL-1β production in macrophages through both an autocrine and paracrine manner through surface-expressed succinate receptor 1 (SUCNR1; also called GPR91). Endogenously generated succinate is released, binding to SUCNR1 on the same or nearby cells to amplify IL-1β production.28 IL-1β itself upregulates the expression of SUCNR1, reinforcing the cytokine production cycle.

Fumarate

Fumarate also builds up in LPS-stimulated macrophages and promotes pro-inflammatory signaling.29 This increase is due to the decreased expression of fumarate hydratase (FH) and the increased expression of argininosuccinate synthase 1 (ASS1) that fuels the aspartate-argininosuccinate shunt. FH inhibition (i.e., fumarate accumulation) suppresses the expression of anti-inflammatory IL-10 but increases tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interferon (IFN)β production. The rise in IFNβ production is driven by fumarate-induced mitochondrial damage, triggering the release of mtRNA and the activation of the host RNA sensors retinoic-inducible gene I (RIG-I) and melanoma-differentiation-associated gene 5 (MDA5).

Excessive or unregulated fumarate can contribute to pathology. Fumarate has recently been shown to irreversibly modify (Michael-like addition termed succination) Parkin, inhibiting its E3 ligase activity.30 By disrupting the PINK1-Parkin-mediated mitophagy pathway, which is essential for the clearance of damaged mitochondria, fumarate may contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction associated with the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease. FH suppression contributes to the pathogenesis of other diseases with a strong metabolic or inflammatory component, including systemic lupus erythematosus, multiple sclerosis, hereditary leiomyomatosis, and renal cell cancers.29,31,32 As such, while fumarate can mediate inflammatory responses, its accumulation must be tightly regulated to prevent pathological outcomes.

Citrate

Downregulation of isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) following LPS stimulation increases citrate in activated immune cells. Citrate is exported to the cytosol from the mitochondria via the citrate carrier (CIC), also known as solute carrier family 25 member 1 (Slc25a1).33 There, it is converted to acetyl-coenzyme A (CoA) and oxaloacetate. Oxaloacetate can serve as a source of NADPH, which is essential for NADPH oxidase and inducible nitric oxide (NO) synthase (iNOS) to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) and NO, respectively. Acetyl-CoA promotes protein acetylation and the expression of inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, acetyl-CoA can be converted by acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) into malonyl-CoA, which is crucial for fatty acid synthesis and the production of inflammatory mediators.34,35

Itaconate (both pro- and anti-inflammatory properties)

Accumulation of itaconate in activated macrophages occurs through defined metabolic pathways; mitochondrial aconitase 2 (ACO2) converts citrate to cis-aconitate, which is then decarboxylated by cis-aconitate decarboxylase 1 (Acod1), encoded by immune-responsive gene 1 (Irg1), to produce itaconate. Itaconate is an immunomodulatory metabolite that functions as a mild anti-inflammatory/antioxidative agent through multiple mechanisms as well as an inducer of type I IFN (IFN-I) production.18,20

Given its structural similarity to succinate, itaconate competes with it for succinate dehydrogenase (SDH; also called respiratory complex II) binding.36,37 This interaction blocks both the oxidation of succinate to fumarate and the generation of mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) production at complex I by the reverse electron transport (RET) chain. Thus, despite the accumulation of succinate, itaconate-mediated SDH inhibition reduces oxidative stress, HIF-1α stabilization, and IL-1β transcription. These findings suggest that the direction or efficiency of the electron transport chain (ETC), rather than succinate accumulation, may be the primary driver of the HIF-1α-IL-1β axis. Other ways through which itaconate dampens inflammation include the inhibition of glycolysis through the covalent modification (Michael-like addition termed itaconation) of several glycolytic enzymes.38 This effect was identified using iTalk, a derivatized itaconate analog probe, which inhibited glucose uptake in LPS-stimulated macrophages to a similar extent as 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI; esterified itaconate derivative), though to a lesser degree than endogenous itaconate.38 While iTalk is a powerful tool for identifying itaconate targets, findings from this analog probe—like those from itaconate derivatives—require validation under physiological conditions, either in the presence of endogenous itaconate or using Irg1-deficient systems. Indeed, itaconate derivatives do not always replicate the bona fide functions of endogenous itaconate, as their enhanced electrophilicity can introduce additional, non-physiological effects.39 Itaconate also activates nuclear factor E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) and a transcriptional anti-inflammatory/antioxidative program by modifying Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1).40 Perhaps most physiologically relevant—and with more pronounced effects than the earlier, milder anti-inflammatory phenotypes—itaconate that accumulates over time specifically inhibits late-stage NLRP3 inflammasome activation. This occurs through the covalent modification of gasdermin D.21

More recently, itaconate has been shown to promote IFNβ production by inducing the release of double-stranded mtRNA through the VDAC1 pore. This pro-inflammatory function, triggered by SDH inhibition and succinate accumulation,20 mirrors the mechanism by which fumarate stimulates IFN-I signaling.29 However, an independent study demonstrates that itaconate also promotes IFNβ production through an SDH-independent pathway.18 Specifically, itaconate inhibits the mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme peroxiredoxin 5 (PRDX5), independently of itaconation (covalent modification), resulting in increased ROS generation and subsequent induction of IFNβ. The above findings highlight the context- and timing-dependent effects of itaconate.

Anti-inflammatory immune response

Glutaminolysis (conversion of glutamine to glutamate and α-ketoglutarate) and fatty acid oxidation (FAO; or β-oxidation) typically direct anti-inflammatory signaling, such as in immunosuppressive macrophages (formerly referred to as M2 or M2-like) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs).41–44 Both pathways fuel the TCA cycle, OXPHOS, and ROS production. Glutaminolysis skews macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype through the production of α-ketoglutarate.41 Activation of the enzyme Jumonji-domain-containing 3 (Jmjd3) by α-ketoglutarate results in the demethylation of H3K27 on the promoters of genes associated with anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization, relieving their repression.41 Additionally, α-ketoglutarate inhibits inflammatory nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling.45 Anti-inflammatory macrophages contribute to the resolution of inflammation and to tissue repair through anti-inflammatory factors, such as arginase 1, IL-10, and TGF-β.46

The exact mechanisms through which FAO promotes anti-inflammatory macrophage or MDSC polarization remain incompletely understood. MDSCs, which are best characterized for their role in tumor progression, rely on fatty acid uptake and FAO to support their immunosuppressive function. MDSCs inhibit T cell activity and proliferation by depleting essential amino acids, such as arginine, or producing ROS, which disrupt the T cell receptor (TCR)-major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-peptide interaction.47–49 Their immunosuppressive role is also linked to the production of itaconate.50 Notably, itaconate derived from myeloid cells has been shown to enhance FAO in non-myeloid cells, boosting its immunomodulatory signaling.51 Pharmacological inhibition of FAO with etomoxir reduces the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs in mouse tumor models and significantly slows tumor growth in a T cell-dependent manner.43

Together, the above metabolites and biochemical pathways demonstrate the intricate link between metabolism and immunity, which is further complicated by pathogens during infection. While the metabolic pathways that drive inflammation have been increasingly well characterized, those that actively suppress immune activation remain comparatively less defined, highlighting a critical area of ongoing investigation in the field of immunometabolism. Infection with pathogens provides a valuable model to study these regulatory mechanisms.

IMMUNOMETABOLIC SUBVERSION BY COMMON PULMONARY BACTERIAL PATHOGENS

Unlike viruses, bacteria are live microorganisms with their own highly versatile metabolic program. While initial studies using LPS as a proxy for bacterial stimulation have deciphered crucial immunometabolic routes, we are now delving deeper into how live pathogens introduce an additional layer of complexity. For example, live Klebsiella pneumoniae stimulates a unique immunometabolic response compared to the heat-killed bacteria or purified LPS in an established mouse pneumonia model.11 A more recent study showed that live Escherichia coli provided an alternative nutrient source upon macrophage phagocytosis and stimulated a distinct immunometabolic response to LPS stimulation or killed bacteria.52 Degradation of live bacteria directed carbon atoms and amino acids into several host metabolic pathways, such as glutathione and itaconate biosynthesis, and supported the bioenergetic needs of macrophages. The microbial metabolic recycling was regulated by the host energy sensor, mechanistic target of rapamycin complex C1 (mTORC1). In contrast, dead bacteria were sensed via their derived cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), which sustained the macrophage intracellular AMP pool, activated the kinase, AMPK, and inhibited mTORC1. Here, we will discuss the metabolic strategies employed by the prevalent bacterial pathogens Staphylococcus aureus (Gram positive), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Gram negative), and K. pneumoniae (Gram negative), to establish persistent respiratory infection.

S. AUREUS EXPLOITS IMMUNOMETABOLITES TO CAUSE PERSISTENT PNEUMONIA

Despite lacking LPS, S. aureus stimulates host glycolysis and the production of itaconate in the airway by competing with host cells for glucose, its preferred carbon source.10 This metabolic competition induces mitochondrial stress and the generation of ROS, which subsequently trigger itaconate synthesis (Figure 2A). Whereas macrophages are the primary source of itaconate during Gram-negative infections, neutrophils predominantly produce it in response to S. aureus.9 Itaconate blocks glycolysis and the oxidative burst in neutrophils, impairing their survival and blunting their ability to kill staphylococci. The blocking of this critical host defense presumably occurs because of the covalent modification of NADPH oxidase by itaconate.38 In a mouse pneumonia model, neutrophil Irg1 expression (i.e., itaconate production) protects the lung from an exuberant inflammatory response but compromises bacterial killing. Itaconate also inhibits S. aureus glycolysis, which is essential for its growth and survival (Figure 2A). Hence, itaconate exerts strong metabolic pressure on staphylococci, forcing the selection of strains that redirect carbon flux to support extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) synthesis and biofilm formation.10 Unlike other pathogens such as Salmonella and P. aeruginosa, S. aureus lacks the enzymes that degrade itaconate. Therefore, S. aureus develops adaptive changes to itaconate and further exploits it to evade the innate immune response and persist in the host.

S. aureus also adapts to the immunometabolite fumarate, a glycolytic inhibitor that shares structural similarity with itaconate.53 This adaptation is mediated by the S. aureus enzyme FH (FumC), which catalyzes the conversion of fumarate to malate and promotes the incorporation of carbon derived from fumarate into anabolic pathways, including gluconeogenesis and hexosamine biosynthesis.53 This metabolic rerouting enhances the bioenergetic fitness of S. aureus, thereby improving its physiology and pathogenicity. Interestingly, itaconate appears to potentiate FumC activity, synergistically amplifying fumarate catabolism in S. aureus. Although host-derived FH contributes to airway fumarate homeostasis, chronic pulmonary infections—such as those observed in patients with cystic fibrosis (CF)—are characterized by elevated levels of fumarate.53 In this fumarate-rich environment, S. aureus leverages its own FumC to exploit the excess metabolite, gaining a selective advantage. Strikingly, an ~10,000-fold upregulation of fumC expression has also been observed in S. aureus CF isolates,54 indicating a strong selective pressure for fumarate metabolism in the chronically infected airway. Thus, the accumulation of fumarate in persistent lung infections, coupled with the high conservation of fumC with minimal mutational divergence in clinical S. aureus isolates, highlights the critical role of FumC in sustaining long-term pulmonary infections.

In a mouse model of prosthetic joint infection, the metabolite lactate produced by S. aureus was shown to inhibit histone deacetylase 11 (HDAC11), causing unchecked HDAC6 activity. This resulted in increased histone 3 acetylation at the Il-10 promoter of innate immune cells and enhanced IL-10 production.8 Whether staphylococcal lactate-mediated epigenetic changes also occur in the context of pneumonia has not been ascertained. Interestingly, the mitochondria have been recently shown to sense S. aureus-derived lactate, stimulating neutrophil extracellular trap release (NETosis) for bacterial killing.55 It will be of considerable interest to investigate whether lung-adapted S. aureus strains can evade this mitochondrial sensing mechanism and its associated bactericidal response.

P. AERUGINOSA INDUCES HOST METABOLIC REPROGRAMMING AND ADAPTS TO SUCCINATE

The pathogenesis of P. aeruginosa infection involves LPS as a major driver of host metabolic reprogramming, initiating a brisk and destructive inflammatory response. Upon stimulation of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) by LPS, macrophages elevate glycolysis and downregulate OXPHOS. As a result, succinate accumulates and induces glycolysis, IL-1β production, and immunopathology.56 Of note, succinate is the preferred carbon source for P. aeruginosa, owing to a hierarchical nutrient preference controlled by carbon catabolite repression.57 Thus, P. aeruginosa utilizes the abundant supply of succinate in the inflamed airway for growth, further damaging the lungs (Figure 2B). The continuous assimilation of succinate by the bacteria fuels their oxidative metabolism at the bacterial cell membrane and their own production of the metabolic byproducts, ROS, selecting for isolates that bypass aerobic respiration58 (Figure 2B). Instead, the ROS-adapted isolates direct their metabolic flux toward the glyoxylate shunt and Entner-Doudoroff pathway (alternative to glycolysis in prokaryotes) to produce EPSs (e.g., alginate) and biofilms for protection against oxidative stress. This metabolic flexibility allows P. aeruginosa to exploit the inflamed airway environment early during infection and transition to a more sessile, protected state that supports long-term persistence.

P. AERUGINOSA FAVORS DISEASE TOLERANCE THROUGH HOST PRODUCTION OF KETONE BODIES AND ITACONATE

Host-adapted strains of P. aeruginosa promote disease tolerance by inducing host metabolic programs that preserve tissue integrity while allowing bacterial persistence.59,60 Airway infection with alginate-producing P. aeruginosa isolates that have reduced LPS surface display and toxin production induces hepatic ketogenesis and glutaminolysis in the airway.59,60 As a result, antioxidative ketones produced in the liver are transported to the lungs through the bloodstream. The host-adapted isolates fail to activate host glycolysis and the production of tissue-damaging cytokines in the lungs. Instead, these strains induce host OXPHOS and itaconate production in the airway, pathways that preserve lung tissue bioenergetics and integrity.59,60 The induction of these pathways prioritizes host survival at the expense of pathogen burden. P. aeruginosa adapts to airway itaconate and ketones by further reducing LPS surface display and forming biofilms, perpetuating a continuous cycle of bacterial adaptation for co-existence in the host (Figure 2B). Thus, the environment in the infected lung supports both pathoadaptive changes in the bacteria and the maintenance of respiratory integrity via OXPHOS. P. aeruginosa can also catabolize the bactericidal metabolite itaconate using the enzymes itaconate CoA transferase (Ict), itaconyl-CoA hydratase (Ich), and (S)-citramalyl-CoA lyase (Ccl).61 The end products, acetyl-CoA and pyruvate, are directed into pathways that sustain bacterial growth, biofilm formation, and chronic lung colonization.61 Hence, these findings corroborate the importance of metabolic interactions between host and bacterial cells in determining the outcome of the infection.

K. PNEUMONIAE STIMULATES HOST OXPHOS AND ITACONATE PRODUCTION TO PROMOTE INDOLENT PULMONARY INFECTION

K. pneumoniae is broadly classified into hypervirulent and classical pathotypes based primarily on clinical presentation. Classical strains, often multidrug resistant, drive indolent (subacute but persistent) healthcare-associated infections, including ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), by eliciting a maladaptive immune response characterized by anti-inflammatory macrophages and MDSCs.11,62,63 In contrast, hypervirulent strains cause acute community-acquired infections, such as metastatic liver abscesses, and are typically antibiotic susceptible. The host metabolic response to these strains plays a prominent role in determining whether the disease outcome is one of resistance or tolerance.11

The distinct host metabolic response to classical K. pneumoniae promotes an immunosuppressive profile compared to the response elicited by the hypervirulent strains, heat-killed bacteria, or purified LPS.11 This is characterized by an increase in host metabolic pathways, such as glutaminolysis and FAO, that fuel OXPHOS and mtROS production (Figure 2C). The activation of glutaminolysis and FAO in murine lungs infected with bacterial mutants lacking these pathways confirmed that such metabolic routes were predominantly host derived rather than bacterial. Furthermore, mice infected with classical K. pneumoniae displayed an increase in the urea cycle; the metabolism of ketones, arginine, and aspartate; and the production of antioxidants, such as itaconate and glutathione, in the lungs. Pharmacological or genetic inhibition of glutaminolysis/FAO and itaconate production resulted in an acute airway infection. Exacerbated inflammation and lung tissue damage were observed, which resulted in the failure of the host to contain the bacterial burden. These findings demonstrate that despite expressing LPS, which is expected to induce the canonical glycolytic/inflammatory response, classical K. pneumoniae predominantly stimulates mitochondrial activity, generating an airway milieu that promotes disease tolerance.

ROS DRIVE K. PNEUMONIAE ADAPTATION FOR SURVIVAL

In addition to fostering an immune response that facilitates disease tolerance, OXPHOS-driven mtROS produced during infection inadvertently promotes K. pneumoniae adaptation by upregulating the type VI secretion system (T6SS)11 (Figure 2C). While the T6SS is primarily known for delivering effector proteins directly into bacterial or eukaryotic target cells—either to eliminate competitors or subvert host functions64—it is increasingly recognized as a sensor of environmental stress that enhances bacterial survival. The latter function may be particularly relevant in low-microbiome-biomass environments, such as the lungs. In other bacterial species, several T6SS-secreted effector proteins, such as catalases and zincophores, are released in a contact-independent manner to mitigate oxidative stress.65–67 The T6SS is similarly essential for K. pneumoniae survival in the oxidant-rich airway, although its precise mechanism of action remains unclear. Notably, the K. pneumoniae T6SS effector VgrG4 has been shown to target mitochondria,68 raising the possibility that the T6SS not only responds to host immunometabolic shifts but also directly modulates host metabolism.

IMMUNOMETABOLIC RESPONSES INDUCED BY VIRAL INFECTIONS

Viruses are obligate intracellular organisms that require host cells for mediating the viral life cycle. Numerous studies have shown that viruses hijack host cellular processes to establish an optimal microenvironment for replication, assembly, and propagation. These processes, which include the host metabolism, contribute to pathogenesis. Using the coronavirus and influenza virus as examples, we will summarize the current knowledge on the metabolic changes induced in the airway by clinically relevant viruses and their influence on immunity.

CORONAVIRUS-INFECTION-INDUCED GLYCOLYSIS FUELS INFLAMMATION AND IMMUNOPATHOLOGY

Three highly pathogenic human coronaviruses (hCoVs), namely, SARS-CoV, Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), and SARS-CoV-2, were identified in 2002, 2012, and 2019, respectively.69–72 A key feature of pathogenic hCoV infection is the induction of exuberant inflammatory responses in infected individuals that contribute to severe disease.73–78 This starkly contrasts with common-cold hCoVs, including hCoV-OC43, hCoV-HKU1, hCoV-NL63, and hCoV-229E, which predominantly cause mild upper respiratory tract infection.79 Most studies have focused on comparing immune cell populations in the infected lungs. However, the fundamental host changes that trigger these immune responses remain largely elusive. Since metabolic processes tightly regulate immunity, understanding the host metabolic changes stimulated during CoV infection may significantly improve our understanding of CoV pathogenesis. Reports indicating that CoV infection induces host metabolic reprogramming add credence to this proposition.80–82

SARS-CoV-2 infection upregulates glycolysis,83,84 a common viral strategy that exploits the increased production of energy and biosynthetic precursors by host cells to support viral replication. Previous studies have shown that pharmacological inhibition of glycolysis reduces viral replication, while its enhancement leads to increased viral replication.84,85 Therefore, glycolysis seemingly plays an important role in coronavirus replication, providing macromolecule precursors to sustain the viral life cycle during infection.

Importantly, glycolysis polarizes macrophages to an inflammatory phenotype, providing a metabolic link to the observed inflammatory responses to hCoV infection. The mechanisms driving the SARS-CoV-2-mediated increase in glycolysis are not completely understood. One possible way is through the induction of hypoxic conditions following infection. Patients with severe COVID-19 develop low oxygen saturation that correlates with mortality.86 This is likely contributed by the death of alveolar type 1 (AT1) and type 2 (AT2) cells, impairing gaseous exchange during infection.87 Hypoxic conditions activate the host transcription factor, HIF-1, promoting glycolysis in cells to maintain energy production.88 A recent study showed that SARS-CoV-2 induces the production of mtROS in human monocytes through RET, which also stabilizes HIF-184 (Figure 3). Activation of HIF-1 signaling resulted in the elevated expression of the SARS-CoV-2 receptor angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), with a concomitant increase in abortive virus replication and the production of the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IFNα, -β, and -λ in monocytes.84 The increased expression of cytokines likely contributes to the heightened inflammatory responses observed in patients with severe COVID-19. Thus, SARS-CoV-2-induced glycolysis not only supports viral replication but also amplifies the inflammatory response, which impacts disease severity.

CORONAVIRUS INFECTION MODULATES OXIDATIVE METABOLISM

The increase in aerobic glycolysis during metabolic reprogramming is typically accompanied by a decrease in OXPHOS. Proteomic analyses of infected monocytes indicate that SARS-CoV-2 infection impairs energy generation through the TCA cycle and OXPHOS (Figure 3). In addition, the metabolic reprogramming in SARS-CoV-2 monocytes was associated with T cell exhaustion and dysfunction, as well as epithelial cell death. These events were likely mediated by monocyte-derived metabolites, suggesting that metabolic changes in one cell type influence immune responses in other cell types, thus having widespread and systemic effects on the overall host immune defense. These findings were supported by an independent study demonstrating that SARS-CoV-2 inhibition of OXPHOS generates mtROS. These changes contributed to severe disease in transgenic mice expressing human ACE2 (K18-hACE2).82 Notably, Guarnieri et al. showed that the associated pathology could be mitigated through mitochondria-targeted antioxidants. However, a separate report demonstrated that activation of HIF-1 signaling leads to the inhibition of SARS-CoV-2 infection and the mitigation of subsequent pathological outcomes in the golden Syrian hamster model.89 Diminished levels of infectious virus were observed in the upper and lower airway with less injury in ciliated epithelial cells upon pharmacological activation of HIF-1.89 These studies highlight the contrasting role of HIF-1 in SARS-CoV-2 pathogenesis in different animal models, prompting further detailed studies to characterize the role of specific host metabolic pathways in mediating different immune responses (protective versus pathogenic) using different CoV infection models.

CoV UPREGULATION OF HOST LIPID SYNTHESIS PROMOTES PATHOGENESIS

In addition to glycolysis and OXPHOS, lipid metabolism is crucial for efficient viral replication and pathogenesis. CoVs remodel lipid membranes in infected host cells to form double-membrane vesicles (DMVs), which compartmentalize the viral replication machinery from the intracellular environment.90 The formation of DMVs sequesters and increases the local concentration of biomolecules available for replication, transcription, and translation of viral products. More importantly, viral RNA and replication intermediates are immunogenic. Therefore, DMVs shield these immunogenic moieties to minimize host sensing of viral RNA by intracellular TLRs and the cytoplasmic RNA sensors RIG-I and MDA5.91,92 Furthermore, viruses harness host lipid metabolism, utilizing host lipids to promote viral replication and assembly.

SARS-CoV-2 and MERS-CoV both enhance host lipid synthesis by inducing the expression of genes responsible for fatty acid and cholesterol biosynthesis.93,94 Infection with MERS-CoV upregulates the expression of host metabolic genes encoding the major lipogenic enzymes ACC, fatty acid synthase (FAS), and HMG-CoA synthase (HMGCS). This is initiated by the binding of sterol regulatory element (SRE)-binding protein (SREBP) to SREs in the promoter region of the target genes.93 Similarly, SARS-CoV-2 promotes lipid droplet formation through the activation of SREBP in infected cells.94 Pharmacological inhibition or genetic knockdown of SREBP resulted in diminished MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 replication, respectively,93,94 demonstrating the importance of lipid biogenesis in CoV replication. A meta-analysis encompassing 19 retrospective studies and over 395,000 patients found that prior use of statins—cholesterol-lowering drugs that competitively inhibit HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR), a key enzyme in lipid biosynthesis—was associated with a lower risk of severe COVID-19 and mortality.95 However, contrasting findings from other studies caution that statin treatment may not improve clinical outcomes,96 highlighting the need for further research.

In a related aspect of lipid metabolism, functional inhibitors of acid sphingomyelinase (FIASMAs)—such as fluoxetine, fluovaxamine, and amitriptyline, which are typically used as antidepressants—highlight the role of ceramides (lipids) in SARS-CoV-2 pathogenesis. A multi-center study on hospitalized patients with severe COVID-19 found that FIASMA treatment reduced the risk of intubation or death and that this effect was not limited to any specific class of FIASMAs.97 Beyond viral control, fluoxetine has recently been shown to stimulate IL-10-dependent metabolic protection against sepsis-induced lethality.98 Overall, these findings indicate that modulation of lipid metabolism may influence both viral replication and immunity.

Building on the role of lipid metabolism in shaping immune responses during CoV infection, one key pathway involves PLA2G2D, a phospholipase whose expression increases in the lungs with age.99,100 This drives the increased production of the lipid mediator prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) in the airway during CoV infection. While PGD2 reduces chronic low-grade inflammation associated with aging (inflammaging), it has been shown to contribute to severe disease in aged animals infected with SARS-CoV or SARS-CoV-2.99,100 This finding aligns with the well-established observation that age is a significant risk factor for severe CoV infection.101 Mechanistically, high levels of PGD2 were associated with the reduced migration of lung DCs to draining lymph nodes and a weaker CD8+ T cell response in the airway, worsening the disease outcomes. Consistently, PLA2G2D−/− or PTGDR−/− (PGD2-receptor-deficient) mice exhibited improved SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 clearance and survival upon infection. In contrast, immune memory and protection against secondary MERS-CoV challenge were impaired in the absence of PLA2G2D and PGD2 production.102 Thus, these studies highlight the complex and divergent roles of lipid metabolism in modulating immune responses during CoV infection—impairing antiviral defenses during the acute phase of SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 infection while supporting the development of protective immune memory in response to MERS-CoV.

METABOLIC SUBVERSION BY CoV ACCESSORY PROTEINS MAY ALTER THE OUTCOME OF INFECTIONS

While numerous studies have shown that CoV infection can disrupt host metabolic pathways, the physiological relevance and precise underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Since successful pathogens evade immune responses by subverting host signaling networks, it is likely that these CoVs also exploit the host metabolism to facilitate immune evasion. CoVs encode a positive-strand RNA genome that is directly translated into polyproteins 1a (Pp1a) and 1ab (Pp1ab). These polyproteins are proteolytically cleaved into individual non-structural proteins (Nsps). The Nsps assemble into the viral replication-transcription complex (RTC), which facilitates the expression of structural proteins necessary for virion formation, as well as accessory proteins that modulate pathogenesis. Among these accessory proteins, orthologous proteins 9b of SARS-CoV-2 and 8b of MERS-CoV both antagonize IFN-I production.103,104 However, despite their shared inhibition of IFN-I, they have contrasting effects on disease outcomes, suggesting additional, yet-to-be-defined functions.105 Protein 9b of SARS-CoV-2, which is encoded by an open reading frame (ORF) that overlaps with the structural nucleocapsid (N) gene, promotes inflammatory pathology in the host. In contrast, protein 8b of MERS-CoV limits exacerbated disease. Deletion of protein 9b from SARS-CoV-2 resulted in attenuated disease in K18-hACE2 mice. Similarly, C57BL/6 mice infected with a mouse-adapted strain of SARS-CoV-2 lacking protein 9b displayed improved outcomes with lower viral burden in the airway compared to those infected with the wild-type (WT) strain.99,105,106 However, mice infected with MERS-CoV lacking protein 8b developed significantly more severe disease, as reflected by a substantial increase in weight loss and mortality and a higher viral burden in the lungs.

SARS-CoV-2 protein 9b disrupts the interaction between the mitochondrial membrane protein translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 70 (TOM70) and mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS) protein107 to suppress IFN-I production.108 TOM70 is not only critical for antiviral signaling but is also essential for mitochondrial respiration.109 The interaction between SARS-CoV-2 protein 9b and TOM70107,110 suggests that protein 9b may impair OXPHOS, further skewing the infected cell toward glycolysis. Supporting this idea, ectopic expression of protein 9b in host cells leads to the depletion of mitochondrial proteins,111 implicating it in the modulation of host cellular energy production. Consistently, independent studies have shown that SARS-CoV-2 infection alters mitochondrial gene expression and reduces mitochondrial respiration.82,112 In contrast, while MERS-CoV protein 8b is an ortholog of SARS-CoV-2 protein 9b, it shares low amino acid sequence similarity and displays variations in mitochondrial colocalization. These differences suggest possible divergence in mitochondrial interactions, which could contribute to distinct effects on host metabolism and immune responses. Nonetheless, further studies are needed to clarify whether these differences translate into distinct effects on mitochondrial function and immune modulation.

IMMUNOMETABOLIC DYSFUNCTION AND SECONDARY BACTERIAL INFECTIONS IN SEVERE CoV DISEASE

While excessive inflammation (cytokine storm) has been proposed as a contributor to severe COVID-19 and mortality, other hypotheses have been put forward, including failed immune responses113,114 and secondary bacterial infections.115 COVID-19 patients exhibited significant depletion of circulating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, along with functional impairments in T cells and monocytes that produced lower levels of effector cytokines.113 In the same study, approximately one in four COVID-19 patients showed a modest increase in IL-6 levels without corresponding elevations in other common pro-inflammatory cytokines. The hypo-responsiveness of T cells was supported by another study that attributed the blunting of CD4+ T cell effector function during severe COVID-19 to impaired ketogenesis (production of ketone bodies from fatty acids) and OXPHOS. CD8+ T cell dysregulation was also observed during long COVID (post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection [PASC]), with these cells expressing the exhaustion markers PD1 and CTLA4.114

Beyond impairing antiviral immunity, the altered metabolic milieu during SARS-CoV-2 infection may also increase susceptibility to secondary bacterial infections, which have emerged as a major contributor to COVID-19 mortality. Using a machine-learning approach on a cohort of 585 patients, Gao et al. found that unsuccessful treatment of VAP—often caused by secondary infections—was strongly associated with fatal outcomes.115 Such infections, particularly with ESKAPE bacterial pathogens (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter species), were frequently observed during the pandemic.116,117 Notably, SARS-CoV-2 infection appears to sensitize hosts to secondary infection, even with S. aureus strains of low intrinsic virulence,117 though the underlying mechanistic basis remains unclear. Collectively, these findings underscore the possibility that virus-induced immune dysfunction not only weakens host antiviral defenses but also creates an environment permissive of secondary bacterial infections, compounding disease severity.

INFLUENZA VIRUS INFECTION ALTERS THE HOST METABOLISM

Influenza, another clinically relevant respiratory virus, also rewires host metabolic pathways to support viral replication and shape immune responses. However, the literature on the immunometabolic responses to influenza is comparatively more heterogeneous than that on SARS-CoV-2. This variability likely reflects differences in viral strains, host systems, and experimental conditions rather than a lack of underlying biological significance. To maintain clarity and focus, we provide a concise overview of influenza-related findings, highlighting the common metabolic hall-marks—such as enhanced glycolysis and reduced mitochondrial OXPHOS—that mirror those observed in SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Previous studies have shown that glycolysis is upregulated upon infection with the influenza virus to sustain viral replication.118–121 Important enzymes catalyzing glycolysis are upregulated after influenza virus infection in human epithelial cells and mouse lungs (Figure 3). In particular, the expression of hexokinase 2 (HK2), converting glucose to glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) and other glycolytic enzymes, including pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), PDK3, and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), is elevated after influenza infection.122,123 Consistently, HIF-1α expression is induced following infection.121 Blocking glycolysis with 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) suppresses virus replication.123 Consistently, the pharmacological inhibition of HIF-1 signaling diminished influenza replication.122,123 Of note, influenza virus proteins polymerase basic 2 (PB2) and non-structural protein 1 (NS1) induce mitochondrial fragmentation and ROS production.124–126 In addition, G6P dehydrogenase (G6PD) levels are reduced after infection, indicating impaired activation of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) that produces NADPH, a critical reducing agent. G6PD downregulation leads to decreased Nrf2, a major antioxidative transcriptional regulator, which in turn results in increased oxidative stress and viral replication, hyperinflammation, and severe disease.127

Furthermore, influenza infection stimulates glutaminolysis and fatty acid synthesis, which are important for generating nucleotides and lipids for the viral life cycle.120,128 These observations demonstrate that the influenza virus hijacks the host metabolism to complete its viral life cycle. However, the relative contribution of each host metabolic pathway to the immune response remains to be thoroughly investigated, preferably in vivo, given the glycolytic bias of cancer cell lines and the lack of a recapitulated metabolic milieu influenced by both immune and stromal cells in typical tissue culture models.

SECONDARY BACTERIAL INFECTIONS IN SEVERE INFLUENZA INFECTION

Patients infected with the influenza virus have a high risk of secondary bacterial infection, particularly with S. aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, or Streptococcus pyogenes.129–131 The heightened risk of secondary infection is typically associated with virus-induced epithelial barrier damage, which increases bacterial entry.131 Influenza infection induces IFN production, which triggers PANoptosis—a form of programmed cell death.132 This is attributed to the activation of ZBP1 by the influenza viral nucleoprotein (NP) and polymerase subunit PB1.133 PANoptosis disrupts the epithelial barrier integrity, facilitating secondary bacterial infection.

Apart from epithelial barrier disruption, immune dysregulation following viral infection also promotes secondary infections, as evidenced by enhanced bacterial infections despite largely intact epithelial barriers.134,135 Previous studies showed that impaired alveolar macrophage phagocytic functions contribute to dysregulated immune responses, impeding the clearance of the secondary infection. While IFNγ aids in the stimulation of specific anti-influenza immunity, it suppresses innate defenses against certain bacterial pathogens in the lungs.136,137 Mice lacking IFNγ signaling exhibited enhanced bacterial clearance following influenza infection compared to WT mice.136 IFNγ induced after influenza infection inhibited the expression of macrophage receptor with collagenous structure (MARCO), a scavenger receptor for phagocytosing pneumococci, on the cell surface of alveolar macrophages.136,138 Overall, these observations suggest that both physical damage and immune dysregulation of the airway contribute to enhanced secondary infection. The metabolic basis for these changes remains an important area for future investigation.

CONCLUSIONS

Pathogens have evolved sophisticated strategies to subvert a multitude of host processes. The role of traditional virulence factors in suppressing/evading immune-signaling pathways and establishing colonization is extensively documented. There is a growing recognition that pathogens also harness host metabolic processes, significantly impacting the outcome of the infection. Notably, by interacting with host cells at the metabolic level, pathogens can modulate immune responses in ways that enhance their own survival. In retrospect, perhaps this should not be surprising, given the long-standing knowledge of the remarkable metabolic versatility of microorganisms, which microbiologists have historically leveraged for microbial identification. Even viruses, despite lacking the cellular machinery of bacteria and parasites, hijack the host metabolism for their own replication and pathogenesis.

The lung microenvironment undergoes dynamic alterations during infection, driven by the pathogen, immune, and stromal cells and their subsequent release of metabolites. This milieu often dictates the nature of the immune response. It is increasingly recognized that pathogens can manipulate the airway metabolic response to promote an immune response tolerant of infection. This immune-evasion strategy mirrors how tumor cells promote a microenvironment conducive to their proliferation. In addition, many immunometabolites that accumulate in the lungs during infection have bactericidal properties, such as itaconate and fumarate. As a result, they inadvertently exert selective pressure on the pathogens, selecting for variants that are better optimized to survive in the harsh airway environment.

The complexity of respiratory infections underscores the critical role of a finely tuned inflammatory response, a concept brought sharply into focus by the COVID-19 pandemic. Insufficient inflammation hinders pathogen clearance, while excessive inflammation leads to immunopathology, ultimately also impeding effective pathogen elimination. Inspired by the success of immunotherapies targeting metabolic pathways in various cancers, researchers are exploring similar approaches for infectious diseases. Promising results during the COVID-19 pandemic, including dietary interventions139 and the use of repurposed US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved metabolic drugs,140 highlight the potential of immunometabolic strategies. However, further investigation is critical to enhance our mechanistic understanding of immunometabolic regulation. Equally important is evaluating the impact of metabolic therapies or metabolites on pathogens, heeding the lessons learned from the development of antibiotic resistance.

In the last 5 years, the field of immunometabolism in the context of infections has witnessed a period of exciting growth, which is only projected to increase. Studies on metabolic changes during infection are resurging, focusing on both cellular and organismal levels. The development of cutting-edge tools enables researchers to assess metabolic and immune responses with greater precision. These advances have fostered collaborations among microbiologists, biochemists, immunologists, and clinicians, uniting efforts to delineate metabolic pathways that regulate immune responses and can be targeted in broad clinical settings. Such multidisciplinary work will likely herald a new era in our approach to infectious and inflammatory disease management.
