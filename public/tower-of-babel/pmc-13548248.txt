Bridging the digital pathology divide: reciprocal learning between high-resource and low-resource settings
Authors: Kamran M Mirza, Arvind Rao, Jessica A Baker, Robert K Parker, Shane C Quinonez, Priscilla Njenga, Mansoor Saleh, Ulysses GJ Balis, Akbar K Waljee, Shahin Sayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDigital pathology (DP) represents a major advance in global health, integrating digital whole-slide imaging, telepathology and clinical-grade artificial intelligence into cohesive workflows that can improve diagnostic accuracy and educational access. Realising these gains, however, depends on local infrastructure, governance, reimbursement and workforce capacity. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated DP uptake in some settings, but adoption remains highly uneven between high-resource settings (HRS) and low-resource settings (LRS), reflecting unequal infrastructure, workforce, policy and financing. This analysis contends that reciprocal innovation, a model of ongoing two-way learning between HRS and LRS that is distinct from one-way technology transfer, can accelerate DP adoption by leveraging the strengths of each environment. Drawing on examples from HRS and LRS, we examine use cases in education and clinical practice, the adoption barriers that persist and policy mechanisms required for sustainable scaling. While HRS contribute regulatory frameworks, validation standards and mentorship, LRS offer innovations in cost efficiency, workflow resilience and open-source adaptation that can inform more sustainable models globally. Neither HRS nor LRS are homogeneous entities. Resource availability also varies substantially within each category: HRS may contain poorly resourced institutions, while some institutions in LRS may have comparatively greater resources but still face broader system-level infrastructure constraints. Ensuring access to those in LRS requires targeted investment, harmonised standards and mutual learning across resource levels. DP has the potential to foster bidirectional partnerships to democratise access to timely, accurate and patient-centred pathology services across all resource settings.Keywords: Telemedicine, Global Health, Health Services Accessibility

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Digital pathology scale-up should be guided by sustainable financing, harmonised standards, workforce development, ethical artificial intelligence governance and partnerships that support mutual learning rather than one-way technology transfer. These approaches could expand equitable access to timely, accurate pathology services while strengthening education, clinical diagnostics and research capacity across resource settings.

Introduction

Digital pathology (DP) has reshaped how pathologists work, teach and share knowledge.1 By integrating whole-slide imaging (WSI), telepathology and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted workflows (figure 1), DP improves diagnostic accuracy, efficiency and cost containment. Studies confirm high concordance with traditional microscopy in addition to advancements in reproducibility, turnaround time and electronic medical record (EMR) integration.2 This perspective offers a narrative synthesis of the literature and case examples, rather than a systematic review, to frame DP implementation through the lens of reciprocal innovation.

Pillars of digital pathology. The three foundational components of digital pathology: whole-slide imaging, which enables high-resolution scanning of glass slides into digital format; telepathology, which supports transmission of digital images for remote consultation; and AI-assisted digital pathology workflows, which integrate technologies such as cloud computing and artificial intelligence into practice. AI, artificial intelligence.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, remote workflows became essential, resulting in accelerated adoption in some settings.3,4 Professional bodies such as the College of American Pathologists, the Royal College of Pathologists, the Royal College of Pathologists of Australasia and the Digital Pathology Association have issued DP validation guidelines, firmly establishing DP’s role in care and education.5–10 However, a global divide persists with high-resource settings (HRS) moving towards fully digital pathology workflows, while low-resource settings (LRS) face significant barriers related to start-up costs, infrastructure and policy.11,12 Throughout this manuscript, we use HRS and LRS to describe differences in resources and system capacity. While LRS often overlap with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), these categories are not synonymous, as resource availability can vary substantially within countries and across institutions.

Here we consider lessons learnt from HRS that may guide DP adoption across resource settings, while also highlighting opportunities for reciprocal learning. While prior perspectives have described many of the challenges associated with DP implementation, particularly in LRS,12 less attention has been given to how innovations in these environments can inform practice in more resourced contexts. We therefore frame DP adoption through the lens of reciprocal innovation: an ongoing, bidirectional exchange in which innovations developed in one resource setting are actively adapted and reapplied in the other, distinguishing this model from one-way technology transfer or informal collaboration. In this model, innovations from LRS such as hub-and-spoke networks and low-cost digital microscopes also inform more sustainable systems in HRS, while HRS contribute validation frameworks, regulatory standards and subspecialty expertise (figure 2).

HRS contribute validation frameworks, subspecialty consultation and open-access training and AI resources, while LRS contribute frugal technologies, rare and advanced-stage case diversity and resilience-oriented workflows. These exchanges converge across three shared domains: education and workforce development, clinical care and research and AI. This analysis draws on peer-reviewed literature, professional society guidelines and policy documents identified through targeted searches of PubMed and Google Scholar, supplemented by reference mining of key publications and professional society websites. Sources were selected to illustrate the breadth of DP implementation across resource settings rather than to provide an exhaustive review, with case studies included where they offered transferable lessons across HRS and LRS.

Digital pathology use cases

The promise of digital pathology for educational purposes

DP improves pathology education by replacing limited glass-slide collections with accessible digital libraries.13 Traditionally, pathology education relied on physical slides, restricting access to diverse cases. Virtual microscopy and cloud-based repositories have expanded this access, especially in LRS, where subspecialty cases and high-quality training materials are scarce.14,15

During the COVID-19 pandemic, DP ensured uninterrupted teaching and virtual rotations.3 In HRS, DP supports competency-based medical education (CBME) through standardised, objective assessments and AI-assisted feedback. In LRS, international partnerships provide curated slide repositories and mentorship, exposing trainees to rare and complex cases as well as guided expertise.14–16

Virtual tumour boards exemplify DP’s educational value in real time by fostering reciprocal dialogue and shared decision-making among clinicians and trainees across institutions. Global mentorship programmes pairing trainees in under-resourced areas with experienced pathologists further enhance training sustainability.15

Integrating AI into education is increasingly essential, as it enables trainees to interpret algorithm outputs, identify model biases and collaborate with developers to create tools that augment, rather than disrupt clinical decision-making. Pathologists also play a foundational role in AI development itself, as expert annotation of training data, including defining regions of interest, classifying cell types and quantifying diagnostic features, remains essential to supervised and semi-supervised learning approaches.17

Overall, DP has revolutionised education by making training more accessible, interactive and competency-driven, especially in resource-limited settings. With appropriate AI integration and reciprocal mentorship, it continues to strengthen capacity in both HRS and LRS.

Expanding clinical practice through digital pathology

Beyond education, DP is also reshaping clinical practice through remote consultation, second opinions and real-time collaboration across institutions. In HRS, fully digitised pathology laboratories integrate AI within DP workflows for triage and reporting, improving diagnostic turnaround times and reducing diagnostic variability.12,17–19 Virtual tumour boards extend these benefits globally by linking multidisciplinary teams across institutions.18,20

In LRS, telepathology can bridge critical expertise gaps. Low-cost digital microscopy and hub-and-spoke teleconsultation models in LRS settings illustrate streamlined workflows that HRS institutions can adapt for outreach clinics and satellite networks, reinforcing reciprocal learning.21 Studies demonstrate a high concordance in cytopathology and haematology using such low-cost imaging solutions.22 These cost-conscious innovations also inform scalable approaches in HRS, underscoring the value in reciprocal learning.18

Overall, DP bridges geographic and resource gaps for clinical care by enhancing diagnostic precision and workflow efficiency and expanding access to pathology expertise regardless of geography. Beyond strengthening laboratory processes, DP ultimately enhances patient care by improving turnaround times, increasing diagnostic consistency and supporting more timely clinical decision-making.17,18 Because clinicians rely on accurate, prompt histological diagnoses to guide therapeutic management, successful DP adoption requires active clinician engagement and advocacy to ensure that these workflow improvements translate into coordinated, patient-centred care across multidisciplinary teams.

Case studies: success stories and learned lessons from LRS and HRS

Case studies illustrate DP’s adaptability across the globe. In HRS, the UK’s National Health Service (NHS) has scaled national initiatives such as the National Pathology Imaging Co-operative and PathLAKE to improve workflow efficiency, diagnostic accuracy and AI-readiness. Several NHS trusts have fully transitioned to digital workflows enabling remote reporting, review and AI-driven triaging, resulting in reductions in turnaround times.2,23–25 In the USA, centres have implemented large-scale DP programmes enabling remote consultations and second opinions. For example, Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center has demonstrated safe and effective remote sign-out using WSI26 while Mayo Clinic has revealed an enterprise-wide transition to digital workflows plan.26,27

In LRS, telepathology networks in sub-Saharan Africa connect local pathologists with international experts where workforce shortages are severe. For example, in Kenya, there are approximately 0.13 pathologists per 100 000 people, making it one of the lowest globally.28 These collaborations also benefit HRS partners through exposure to advanced-stage and rare cases more frequently encountered in LRS, where differences in disease burden and stage at presentation shape case mix.29 Importantly, DP has advanced research capacity in settings where molecular diagnostics are unaffordable.11 By enabling digital slide sharing and image-based analytics, institutions can participate in collaborative research, contribute unique datasets and study disease patterns without requiring costly molecular infrastructure.

Operationalising reciprocal innovation across education, clinical care and research

Collaborative partnerships between HRS and LRS are central to translating the promise of DP into sustained improvements for education, clinical diagnostics and research.11 Moving beyond pilot projects, these collaborations operationalise reciprocal innovation by aligning technological infrastructure, human capacity and governance structures across resource contexts.

Education and workforce development

Virtual teaching networks using WSI and cloud-based repositories enable trainees in LRS to access curated slide libraries and CBME curricula.14,15 Initiatives such as the Open Pathology Education Network provide asynchronous access to diagnostically diverse cases while supporting mentorship from subspecialty-trained pathologists in HRS, addressing shortages in case diversity and expertise.15

Structured tele-education initiatives operationalise these approaches.15 In Nigeria and Ethiopia, virtual mentorship adapted from Project-ECHO delivers continuous, case-based learning.30 In Kenya and South Africa, partnerships with HRS institutions support access to WSI repositories and standardised competency assessments aligned with accreditation requirements.15 These exchanges are bidirectional as HRS trainees and faculty gain exposure to advanced-stage disease and uncommon pathologies more frequently encountered in LMIC settings, while LRS institutions benefit from subspecialty expertise and validation frameworks.11

Clinical diagnostics and quality assurance

Synchronous and asynchronous telepathology networks function as diagnostic backbones linking laboratories in LRS with subspecialty expertise in HRS supporting primary diagnosis, frozen section consultation, second opinions and multidisciplinary tumour boards.2,18 Hub-and-spoke models have proven particularly effective.11 At the Butaro Cancer Center in Rwanda, static-image telepathology across 953 cases over a 9-month period demonstrated greater than 95% concordance with conventional histology review,31 while WSI-based collaborations in Nigeria and Ghana support complex oncological case review and external quality assurance.11

These implementations reduce diagnostic turnaround time and improve interobserver concordance.2,18 At the same time, workflow innovations developed in LRS, including staged image acquisition and bandwidth prioritisation, offer transferable lessons for outreach models in HRS.21

Translational research and AI development

Beyond education and clinical diagnostics, HRS-LRS partnerships are also foundational to advancing translational research and computational pathology. Shared, de-identified WSI repositories enable multicentre cohort studies and provide the data diversity required for robust AI development.29,32 Consortia such as the Multinational African Digital Cancer Pathology network exemplify this approach through centralised review, data harmonisation and standardised annotation.29

Aligning practice with global diagnostic policy

Collectively, these educational, clinical and research activities align closely with the recommendations of the Lancet Commission on Diagnostics, which emphasises strengthening diagnostic networks, investing in digital infrastructure and leveraging partnerships to expand equitable access.11 Embedding reciprocal innovation into routine workflows translates global policy priorities into measurable improvements in training quality, diagnostic performance and research equity.11

Despite progress, the adoption of DP remains uneven globally, including within individual countries and institutions rather than only between them. Barriers are multifactorial, shaped by financial, technological, infrastructure, cultural and policy contexts (table 1). Understanding these differences and overlaps is essential for building reciprocal learning models that strengthen global practice.

Financial and technological barriers in LRS

Infrastructure and technology limitations remain among the largest barriers. DP relies on electricity, bandwidth and skilled personnel, yet many LRS experience power instability and poor wireless connectivity. Proprietary platforms often cannot integrate with existing laboratory information systems (LIS), if any exist, or with hospital infrastructure. The cost of WSI scanners (US$100 000 to US$300 000), cloud storage and system maintenance is prohibitive. Without long-term funding strategies, institutions often rely on short-term grants or donations, making sustainability programmes challenging to implement.11,12 The initial concern that DP might threaten job security has largely been replaced by recognition that trained pathologists remain central to DP workflows and that the transition will generate new competencies and professional roles.11

While low-cost scanners and basic connectivity solutions provide feasible entry points, equipment with slow scanning speeds, poor optical performance or limited bandwidth can become major bottlenecks, delaying image transfer and undermining diagnostic reliability.11,33

Cultural and policy barriers in HRS

Even in better resourced environments, pathologists may resist transitioning from glass to digital slides, despite strong evidence of concordance with traditional methods. Concerns over workflow disruption, diagnostic validity and institutional reluctance to invest in DP amid competing budget priorities continue to slow adoption.11

Regulatory oversight as well as restrictively stringent privacy and data protection laws, while essential, adds another significant layer of complexity. In the USA, Food and Drug Administration (FDA) validation studies are required before WSI can be used for primary diagnosis while in Europe, In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR) standards mandate extensive conformity assessments. These processes ensure quality, safety and data protection but also delay implementation.

Compliance with data protection frameworks, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act in the USA and the General Data Protection Regulation in Europe, and emerging privacy and data protection acts in LMICs, safeguard patient confidentiality and data integrity but increase regulatory and financial burdens. Additionally, cross-border telepathology requires documented data-transfer agreements, de-identification procedures and alignment with national data-protection laws.3,11

Lack of standardisation and global interoperability

Unlike radiology, where Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) enables consistent image sharing and cross-platform compatibility, WSI formats remain fragmented across proprietary vendor formats, restricting interoperability. Donated scanners in LRS often lack compatibility with existing tools, leading to inefficiencies. Integration with LIS and EMR is also limited, especially where legacy systems are closed or poorly aligned with pathology workflows.3,11

AI brings additional complexity as algorithms trained in HRS may underperform in LRS due to differences in pre-analytical processing, staining, scanner types and disease profiles. Lack of standardised validation protocols further undermines reproducibility. Datasets from LMICs that may be rich in advanced-stage and rare diseases remain underused in model training, risking bias and inequity in model development.12,32 Emerging approaches such as federated learning, which enables collaborative model training across institutions without centralised sensitive data sharing and multi-institutional efforts to build more representative training datasets, may help address these disparities.34 Neither setting has clear frameworks for dealing with AI uncertainty, emphasising the need for global standards to promote transparency, reliability and equitable performance.32,35

Effective AI governance also requires distinguishing four interrelated activities: technical validation (algorithm performance and reproducibility), clinical oversight (pathologist review and accountability), regulatory approval (FDA, IVDR and equivalent national bodies) and real-world workflow integration (day-to-day use, exception handling and audit). Failures in one domain can compromise the others.35,36

Enhancing metalearning from reciprocal failure cases

In addition to showcasing success stories, it is equally important to build global registries of AI and DP failures, especially those arising in LRS that reflect unanticipated real-world constraints (eg, power fluctuations, inadequate processing before samples reach the pathology department, non-standard slide prep, variable staining or rare pathogens).11,35,37–39 These registries could form the foundation for meta-learning by AI developers in HRS, allowing future models to learn from context-specific failure modes and become more generalisable. Shared failure casebooks between HRS and LRS institutions could be instrumental in improving safety, reliability and mutual trust.36,37

Taken together, these barriers differ in how readily they can be resolved. Interoperability gaps, workforce upskilling and aspects of clinician confidence in AI-supported workflows may be more readily addressed in the near term through open-source tooling, structured mentoring, transparent validation and shared learning from implementation outputs. Reimbursement pathways, national regulatory harmonisation and long-term infrastructure financing are more structurally entrenched and will require sustained policy commitment across multiple stakeholders.

Strategies for bridging the digital pathology gap

The strategies that follow are organised around four interdependent domains: infrastructure, workforce development, governance and sustainability. Progress in any single domain is difficult to sustain without parallel investment in the others.

Affordable and scalable solutions for LRS

In LRS, long-term sustainability depends on affordable, context-appropriate innovations. Pragmatic strategies developed in LRS, such as offline access, staged scanning, batching and lean quality control, demonstrate how institutions can operate under bandwidth and power constraints. These approaches also offer lessons for HRS as they develop business continuity plans during digital transitions.11

Entry-level technologies, such as modular light microscopes with digital cameras or smartphone attachments, offer a practical entry into digital workflows. These allow institutions to digitise slides without the prohibitive investment associated with high-end WSI scanners, while encouraging local innovation where creative engineering often fills resource gaps.11 Cloud-enabled telepathology platforms reduce barriers by providing flexible, subscription-based access to digital storage and case sharing, avoiding the need for costly in-house servers.3

Open-source, vendor-neutral software platforms such as QuPath, Cytomine and OpenSlide are equally critical. These tools eliminate licensing fees and can be adapted by local information technology teams, reducing costs and empowering institutions to manage their own digital ecosystems.40–42 Embedding modular trust features like model audit logs and uncertainty visualisations can further help users gauge when to trust or question AI outputs in uncertain environments.35,37,39 In practice, open-source solutions also encourage international collaboration: institutions in HRS can contribute code improvements and technical support, while those in LRS can adapt and stress-test the tools under conditions of limited bandwidth and unstable power. This reciprocal learning accelerates software robustness and ensures that tools are broadly applicable across diverse contexts.11

Infrastructure innovations can also be scaled cost-effectively. Cloud storage subsidised through academic or non-governmental organisation (NGO) partnerships offers secure, compliant hosting, while hybrid models combining local servers with asynchronous cloud uploads are well-suited to regions with intermittent connectivity. These models, pioneered in LRS to cope with instability, can also serve as resilience templates for HRS facing cyberattacks.11,15

Affordable solutions extend beyond technology to human capacity, especially in the context where AI and DP introduce a need for new competencies and work streams that introduce new professional opportunities. Train-the-trainer models, virtual mentorship and shared repositories of annotated slides allow institutions to scale expertise alongside hardware. This underscores that cost-effective DP depends not only on equipment but on a holistic ecosystem of tools, people and partnerships. The reciprocal value is evident: strategies for efficiency and resilience from LRS can inform sustainability reforms in HRS, where rising healthcare costs demand innovation.11,15

Importantly, future-ready DP platforms, particularly those deployed in under-resourced settings, should embed AI agents that offer not only diagnostic support but also trust calibration interfaces. Visual uncertainty indicators, disagreement flags and multimodel consensus layers where outputs are compared, ranked or explained in plain language, ensure clinicians are not left uncertain about AI reliability, especially in high-stakes cases.29,35,37

Building collaborative networks between institutions

Collaborative training between HRS and LRS, or LRS to LRS using ‘train-the-trainer’ models, is vital for bridging the gap in DP adoption. To ensure LRS fully leverages DP for education and clinical practice, structured training programmes must focus on knowledge transfer, skill development and sustainable mentorship models.11,15

Virtual mentorship and telepathology networks are highly effective. Real-time consultations, case discussions and specialist guidance from HRS improve diagnostic accuracy in LRS, especially in complex areas like oncologic pathology, haematopathology and molecular pathology.11 Programmes like the Project ECHO Global Health Initiative have successfully implemented virtual mentoring models, connecting pathologists in LRS with global experts, enhancing both clinical decision-making and professional growth.30 Reciprocal learning occurs when institutions in LMIC settings contribute rare and diverse cases, while HRS partners provide subspecialty expertise and validation frameworks. Joint case reviews can capture lessons in both directions, balancing LRS-driven approaches to prioritisation under resource constraints with HRS frameworks for validation and audit.11

Integrating DP into pathology residency and fellowships through HRS partnerships is another approach to capacity building. Many LRS institutions lack access to high-quality glass slide sets for pathology education. Virtual microscopy, digital slide libraries and AI-enabled learning modules provide scalable educational alternatives. HRS can support LRS by sharing open-access DP libraries, offering online courses and embedding DP into competency-based curricula. Interactive formats, like case reviews, slide reviews and interactive assessments, have proven effective in boosting pathology trainees’ diagnostic skills. Training should also cultivate DP champions in LRS by focusing on leadership, advocacy and strategic planning for institutional transformation.11,15,26

Short-term workshops and exchange programmes complement virtual models by providing hands-on exposure to digital workflows and accelerating skills transfer across institutions. Initiatives from the Royal College of Pathologists and the International Academy of Pathology exemplify successful short-term exchanges that facilitate such skills transfer.6,11,15,43

For sustainability, HRS and LRS institutions must co-develop context-specific DP curricula covering technical skills, workflow integration, data security and regulatory compliance. Programmes in areas with limited internet access can adapt by using offline modules or social media-based platforms. Standardised evaluation frameworks that incorporate structured assessments, feedback collection and metrics are essential to measure impact and ensure long-term skill retention.11,15,19

Collaborative training programmes are crucial for global DP equity. By investing in mentorship, education and leadership, HRS and LRS institutions can build a digitally empowered pathology workforce. Importantly, lessons from these collaborations should extend beyond pathology departments, as non-pathologists also benefit from DP-enabled dialogue and shared decision-making.11,15

Policy recommendations for equitable adoption

The institutional commitment and policy environment will ultimately determine whether DP becomes a routine component of diagnostic care. Governments and regulatory bodies play a pivotal role in enabling DP adoption through infrastructure investment and creating legal frameworks. Individual institutions play a key role in driving DP adoption and informing broader policy uptake. In HRS, regulatory oversight has focused on rigorous validation, patient safety and compliance with frameworks such as the FDA in the USA or IVDR in the European Union. While these mechanisms ensure quality, they can also delay implementation and increase costs. In LRS, by contrast, the absence of national standards has resulted in fragmented pilot projects, reliance on donor-driven initiatives and inconsistent regulatory oversight, undermining sustainability and long-term integration into health systems.11

Overcoming these disparities requires harmonised yet flexible policies. International bodies such as the WHO and professional organisations like the College of American Pathologists and the Royal College of Pathologists can help establish shared validation protocols, vendor-neutral data standards and interoperability guidelines. One concrete example is the work of DICOM Working Group 26, the pathology-focused working group of the DICOM Standards Committee, which developed standards for representing and exchanging WSIs. This effort illustrates how multistakeholder technical standards can support vendor-neutral interoperability across DP platforms.44 Global frameworks must also be adaptable, allowing countries to tailor implementation according to local resources and health priorities. Reciprocal learning is especially valuable where HRS contribute experience in building robust regulatory systems and LRS provides lessons in flexibility, frugality and innovation under constraint, which are insights that can help streamline overly complex processes in wealthier countries.11

Policy also needs to address reimbursement and long-term sustainability. Without clear payment models, institutions hesitate to invest in DP. Establishing reimbursement pathways for digital workflows, including remote consultations and AI-assisted diagnostics, aligns incentives with innovation.45,46 Governments should consider policies that promote public–private partnerships that leverage industry expertise and capital while safeguarding equitable access. Examples from LRS, such as the government-NGO-academic partnership model at the Butaro Cancer Center in Rwanda, demonstrate that cost-sharing agreements between ministries of health, academic institutions and international NGOs, can result in sustained adoption.11,45,46

Finally, policy frameworks must incorporate education and workforce development. Mandating DP in medical curricula, continuing education and accreditation ensures investments in technology are matched by investments in human capital. In both HRS and LRS, this alignment prevents obsolescence and maximises returns. DP will move from scattered initiatives to a true global standard only when policy links technology, financing, interoperability and training.15

Limitations

This analysis has several limitations. As a narrative synthesis rather than a systematic review, source identification was not exhaustive and may favour published successes over unpublished or unsuccessful implementations. Quantitative outcome data remain limited for some LRS case examples, constraining direct comparison across settings. Finally, the pace of change in AI-assisted DP means that specific tools and platforms referenced here may evolve quickly.

Conclusion

DP has the potential to make diagnostic services more accessible and equitable worldwide. Realising this potential depends on local infrastructure, governance, reimbursement and workforce capacity. Building on prior work describing barriers to adoption, this analysis has framed implementation as a two-way exchange between HRS and LRS rather than a one-directional transfer of technology. Equitable adoption requires sustainable funding, interoperability standards and trust-centred AI. As a concrete next step, institutions across resource settings should use table 2 to identify implementation gaps and develop partnerships that address those gaps in both directions. Above all, DP should support, rather than replace, dialogue between pathologists and clinicians so that geography no longer determines access to timely, accurate and patient-centred care.
