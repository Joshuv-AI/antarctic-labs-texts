Regulatory role of phosphatidylcholine metabolic reprogramming in colorectal cancer progression and neutrophil interactions
Authors: Fu Cheng, Luji Huang, Yuanhong Peng, Lina Luo, Lin Zhong, Yongle Ju, Yan Lu, Manzhao Ouyang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryColorectal cancer (CRC) remains a significant global health challenge due to its high incidence, mortality, and persistent issues with drug resistance, recurrence, and metastasis. Lipid metabolism reprogramming—a hallmark of CRC—plays a critical role in tumor progression. Mendelian randomization analysis established a causal relationship between phosphatidylcholine (PC) metabolism and CRC. Integrative analyses of transcriptomic and lipidomic data demonstrated that elevated PC levels are linked to enhanced CRC proliferation, metastasis, drug resistance, and increased tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) infiltration. Machine-learning-based diagnostic and prognostic models underscore the clinical significance of PC-metabolism-related genes, identifying ATP10A and DBI as central dysregulated genes. ATP10A, markedly overexpressed in CRC, drives tumor growth, metastasis, and chemoresistance by upregulating TAN-related chemokines and TGF-β, whereas DBI appears protective. Collectively, these findings define PC metabolism as a potential therapeutic target and provide insight for developing combination strategies to mitigate CRC progression and improve outcomes.Subject areas: molecular biology, cell biology, cancer

Introduction

According to data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer, colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide, accounting for 9.6% of all cancer cases, and ranks as the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally.1,2 Current treatment strategies for CRC primarily rely on multimodal approaches centered on surgery, including neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. While these combination therapies have improved patient outcomes to some degree, significant challenges such as drug resistance, metastasis, recurrence, and limited therapeutic applicability continue to hinder their efficacy. In recent years, the widespread adoption of high-fat, high-sugar dietary patterns in modern societies, combined with the increasing prevalence of obesity, has been accompanied by a steady rise in CRC incidence.3 Moreover, lipid metabolism reprogramming has been identified as a critical metabolic alteration in CRC, closely associated with these clinical challenges.4 Consequently, identifying novel and reliable biomarkers related to lipid metabolism, as well as developing therapeutic targets through lipid metabolism research, represents promising strategies to address these clinical obstacles.

Research has demonstrated that during the malignant transformation of intestinal epithelial cells, significant alterations in lipid metabolism occur, which promote malignancy, distant metastasis, the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and the development of drug resistance. For example, lipid mediators such as lipoxins, prostaglandin E2, and resolvins have been shown to modulate angiogenesis, influence the composition of stromal cells, and regulate immune cell function, thereby impacting tumor progression and the efficacy of chemotherapy and immunotherapy.5,6,7 However, current research on the role of specific lipid molecules in CRC is limited. Among the various lipid classifications, phosphatidylcholine (PC) is the most abundant lipid in cellular membranes and plays a vital role in maintaining membrane structure and fluidity. Furthermore, PC is actively involved in processes such as signal transduction, lipid metabolism, and the regulation of membrane protein function.8 Consequently, PC metabolism reprogramming has garnered significant attention as a critical component of lipid metabolism reprogramming, particularly in its implications for CRC development and progression. Nevertheless, current studies on PC and CRC primarily focus on plasma biomarkers, and the underlying causal relationship between PC metabolism and CRC remains unclear.9,10 Thus, PC metabolism reprogramming represents a promising research avenue in CRC diagnosis and treatment, offering the potential to uncover novel therapeutic approaches and concepts for managing this disease.

Emerging research suggests that lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), a derivative of PC, plays a regulatory role in neutrophil movement and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps in non-cancer diseases.11 Neutrophils, the most abundant myeloid cells in the human bloodstream, are critical for immune defense against infections and for the activation and regulation of immunity.12 However, due to their short lifespan, terminal differentiation, and lack of proliferative capacity, neutrophils have historically been underexplored in the context of tumors. In recent years, tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) have gained increasing attention for their functional diversity and plasticity within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Studies have shown that the generation, recruitment, and survival of neutrophils are driven by CXC chemokines, inflammatory cytokines (such as interleukin-17 [IL-17] and interleukin-1β [IL-1β]), complement component C5a, and integrins, which are secreted by tumor cells and macrophages.13,14 Once recruited, TANs can differentiate into antitumor (N1)15 or pro-tumor (N2)16 phenotypes under the influence of interferon-β (IFN-β) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), or transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), respectively. The N1-type TANs demonstrate antitumor activity by inducing tumor cell death and inhibiting metastasis through direct cellular contact, reactive oxygen species, nitric oxide, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand.17,18 Furthermore, N1-TANs enhance antitumor T cell responses via antigen presentation.19 In contrast, N2-type TANs promote tumor progression by facilitating genetic instability, angiogenesis, metastasis, and tumor cell proliferation, while simultaneously suppressing T cell activation. These pro-tumor effects are mediated through the secretion of factors like matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9), neutrophil elastase (NE), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA), and the expression of immune suppressive ligands such as programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1).20,21 The abundance of TANs has been correlated with poor responses to conventional treatment modalities such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and ICB therapies.22,23 As such, elucidating the interactions between PC metabolism and TANs within the CRC TME may offer novel insights into combination therapies targeting PC metabolism or TANs.

This study identified a causal relationship between PC levels and CRC risk through Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. Integrated analyses of bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data, together with targeted lipidomics, revealed that PC abundance is significantly elevated in CRC and is closely associated with CRC initiation, progression, drug resistance, and TANs infiltration. To assess the clinical utility of PC-metabolism-related genes (PC-MRGs) in CRC diagnosis and treatment, machine-learning-based diagnostic and prognostic models were developed. ATP10A and DBI were identified as key genes, both of which are significantly upregulated in CRC. ATP10A promotes CRC proliferation and metastasis and is associated with TAN infiltration, while DBI exhibits the opposite effect. Furthermore, ATP10A is closely linked to CRC treatment sensitivity. This study underscores the pivotal regulatory role of PC metabolism in CRC and TANs, exploring their interactions and mechanisms, and providing novel targets and theoretical insights for combined targeting of PC metabolism or TANs in CRC therapy, offering potential strategies to overcome drug resistance.

Results

Mendelian randomization analysis reveals causal relationships between phosphatidylcholine and CRC development

We performed a two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis to evaluate the causal effects of 179 lipid species on CRC risk. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method identified 33 lipids (3 sterols, 25 glycerophospholipids, 2 sphingolipids, and 3 glycerolipids) associated with CRC risk, including 19 protective and 14 risk factors (Figures S1 and S2). Eleven lipids were not significant when using Bayesian weighted Mendelian randomization. After applying a false discovery rate correction to the IVW P-values (Data S1), 7 lipid molecules remained significantly associated with CRC risk (Figure 1). These associations were consistently supported by MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode analyses, without evidence of heterogeneity (IVW/MR-Egger) or pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO). Reverse MR analysis ruled out bidirectional causality (Data S1), and validation using FinnGen GWAS data (FinnGen: R10_C3_COLORECTAL_EXALLC) confirmed the findings (Figure 1). Overall, in conclusion, by integrating multiple MR analytical methods and statistical validations, we confirmed that 7 lipid molecules have a significant causal association with CRC risk, with PC exhibiting a particularly notable association compared with other lipids.

The abundance of phosphatidylcholine is significantly increased in CRC cancer tissues

To further elucidate the changes in PC metabolism activity in CRC, we investigated PC metabolism alterations in CRC using both bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data. In bulk RNA sequencing analyzed by gene set variation analysis (GSVA), PC synthesis and modification pathways were significantly upregulated, whereas PC degradation and transport pathways were downregulated in tumors (Data S2 and S3, Figure 2A). Single-cell analysis with the miloR algorithm revealed increased epithelial cells, macrophages, and neutrophils in cancer tissues (Figures 2B, 2C, and S3). To further dissect PC metabolism changes at the single-cell level, epithelial cells were classified into “Epithelial Cells (Normal)” and “Epithelial Cells (Tumor)” groups using copy number variation (CNV) analysis (Figure 2D), and AUCell confirmed that Epithelial Cells (Tumor) exhibited enhanced PC synthesis/modification and reduced PC degradation/transport activity (Data S4, Figure 2E).

Targeted lipidomic profiling of paired cancerous and adjacent normal tissues from CRC patients identified 979 lipid metabolites (Data S5, Figure S4). According to LIPID MAPS classification, 97 distinct PC species were detected, ranking second among all lipid classes (Figure 3A). Applying criteria of p < 0.05, VIP >1, and |log2FC| > 1, we found 24 PC species differentially expressed, with 21 upregulated and 3 downregulated in tumors, indicating a considerable increase in PC abundance in CRC (Data S5, Figures 3B–3D). These results, consistent with our bioinformatics analysis, support a crucial role for altered PC metabolism in CRC progression. Moreover, network analysis revealed a close association between elevated PCs and long-chain fatty acids, including PC(14:0-14:0), PC(14:0-18:0), and PC(16:0-16:1) (Figure S4).

Increased synthesis of phosphatidylcholine is significantly associated with the development and progression of CRC and increased infiltration of N2-type neutrophils

We quantified PC biosynthesis pathway activity for each patient using GSVA and divided samples into high- and low-activity groups based on the median. Differential expression analysis identified 921 genes with significant differences (p < 0.05, logFC >0.263) (Data S3, Figure S5A). Gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed that altered PC biosynthesis was strongly associated with CRC onset, Epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), metastasis, and drug resistance through pathways such as MAPK, Wnt, TNF, NF-κB, apoptosis, ECM disassembly, and stem cell proliferation. Moreover, changes in PC activity correlated with immunosuppressive states, notably affecting neutrophil migration, chemotaxis, activation, IL-8 production, and TGF-β signaling. At the molecular function level, enrichment was observed for ECM components, chemokine activities, and enzymes involved in lipid metabolism (Data S3, Figures 4A–4C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further connected PC biosynthesis with Wnt, NF-κB, TNF, TGF-β, chemokine signaling, and Th17 cell differentiation (Data S3, Figure 4D). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed that increased PC biosynthesis was positively associated with pathways promoting CRC progression, drug resistance, and neutrophil recruitment in the TME (Data S3, Figures 4E–4H), underscoring its critical role in CRC tumorigenesis.

We assessed immune infiltration in CRC tumors using several algorithms and found that elevated PC synthesis was consistently associated with higher neutrophil infiltration (Data S6, Figures 5A–5G). In these patients, expression of neutrophil chemokines and regulatory genes (CXCL8, CXCL5, CXCL6, CXCL12, CXCR2, CXCR4, and ALOX5) was significantly increased (Data S7, Figure 5H). Moreover, markers for N2 polarization, including IL10, TGF-β, as well as effector molecules MMP9, IL4R, IL6, CD274, S100A8, and S100A9, were upregulated (Figure 5I). CellChat analysis revealed that malignant epithelial cells with high PC biosynthesis activity (Epithelial Cells (Tumor)_high) exhibited enhanced signal transmission and reception, particularly in interactions with neutrophils, via pathways such as CXCL, GALECTIN, GAS, GDF, MIF, MK, PARs, and PDGF (Data S4, Figures 6A–6D and S5). Notably, CXCL, GALECTIN, MIF, MK, and PDGF pathways were strongly linked to neutrophil recruitment and regulation (Figure S6). Transcription factor analysis further showed increased activity of SMAD3, CREB3, KLF2, CEBPG, and SREBF1 in Epithelial Cells (Tumor)_high compared with their low-activity counterparts (Data S4, Figures 6E and 6F). Overall, these results suggest that enhanced PC biosynthesis promotes tumor progression by modulating neutrophil recruitment and polarization, offering potential therapeutic targets.

Exploring the diagnostic and therapeutic value of PC-MRGs in CRC through machine learning

We evaluated the diagnostic capacity of PC-MRGs for CRC using 107 combinations from 11 machine learning algorithms based on 88 PC metabolism-related genes. Model performance (area under the curve [AUC], accuracy, recall, and F1 score) was compared (Data S8, Figures 7A and S7–S10). Although the Stepglm[both]+LDA model had the highest AUC (training: 0.987; average: 0.972), the Stepglm[both]+SVM model achieved the highest F1 score (0.983; average: 0.935). Calibration curves and clinical decision curves in both the training (GEO: GSE87211) and validation (GEO: GSE44076) sets confirmed that Stepglm[both]+SVM was consistently robust with a higher net benefit (Figure 7B). Moreover, receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis of individual model genes in GEO: GSE87211 demonstrated strong diagnostic performance for ABHD3 (AUC: 0.961), CAPN2 (AUC: 0.923), CDS1 (AUC: 0.895), and ABCB1 (AUC: 0.859) (Figure 7C). Thus, we consider Stepglm[both]+SVM as the optimal diagnostic model.

We evaluated the link between PC metabolism and CRC prognosis using five machine learning algorithms for gene selection, with candidate genes incorporated into multivariate Cox regression models. The LASSO-based model achieved the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) alongside the highest C-index and AUC (Data S9, Figures 8A–8C), and was therefore adopted as the final prognostic model. This nomogram, comprising 13 genes (LIPG, ATP10A, PNPLA7, PNPLA8, PLA2G6, PLA2G4D, PLA2G2E, PLA2G2D, PLA2G1B, DBI, PCYT1B, LPCAT4, LPCAT2), was further integrated with clinical parameters (gender, age, tumor stage) to create a composite nomogram that robustly predicted survival across training, test, and validation datasets, as confirmed by time-dependent ROC and calibration curves, as well as Kaplan-Meier analysis (Figures 8D–8G). Risk scores correlated significantly with primary tumor site, stage, metastasis, recurrence, and MMR deficiency (Data S10, Figure S11). Functional enrichment (GO, KEGG, GSEA) indicated that high-risk scores were strongly associated with tumorigenesis, ECM remodeling, treatment resistance, and neutrophil chemotaxis (Data S7, Figure S12), while immune infiltration analyses revealed increased neutrophil presence, elevated chemokine expression, and upregulation of N2-type neutrophil markers in high-risk patients (Data S6, Figures S13 and S14). Finally, individual gene analysis identified ATP10A as most strongly associated with poor prognosis, whereas DBI appeared protective (Figure S15).

ATP10A and DBI are significantly highly expressed in CRC and are associated with the proliferation and metastasis of CRC

Overlapping the best diagnostic and prognostic gene sets, ATP10A and DBI emerged as key regulators (Figure S15). Clinical correlations showed ATP10A expression positively correlated with T, N, and M stages and was elevated in recurrent patients, indicating its role as a risk factor (Data S10, Figures 9A–9D). In contrast, DBI was significantly downregulated in advanced stages, recurrence, and in dMMR compared with pMMR patients (Data S10, Figures 9E–9J). qRT-PCR analysis of 48 paired CRC and adjacent normal tissues confirmed that both genes were overexpressed in tumors—with ATP10A showing a more pronounced increase (Data S11, Figure 9K–9L). Functional assays (scratch wound, colony formation, CCK-8, and EdU assays) using ATP10A and DBI knockdown in SW620 and DLD-1 cells revealed that suppressing ATP10A markedly inhibited migration and proliferation, while DBI knockdown enhanced these activities (Figures 10, 11, and 12, Data S12). These findings suggest that ATP10A and DBI exhibit opposing roles in CRC progression, with ATP10A functioning as an oncogene and DBI potentially acting as a tumor suppressor. Further exploration of their molecular mechanisms may provide novel insights into CRC pathogenesis and therapeutic strategies.

ATP10A is associated with increased infiltration of N2-type neutrophils

To assess the relationship between ATP10A and DBI expression and immune infiltration, we analyzed their average expression levels in relation to TME features. Our analysis revealed that ATP10A expression is robustly and positively correlated with neutrophil infiltration across multiple immune algorithms, and it is also significantly associated with neutrophil chemokine receptors and N2-type neutrophil biomarkers (Data S6, Figures S16 and S17). In contrast, DBI expression showed a significant negative correlation with these neutrophil markers. To validate these observations, we compared the expression levels of neutrophil-associated chemokines—including IL-8, CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, CXCL5, CXCL6, and CXCL12—and the key N2 polarization regulator TGF-β among four experimental groups (WT, NC, KO-1, and KO-2) in SW620 and DLD-1 cell lines (Data S12, Figure S18). Knockdown of ATP10A markedly reduced the expression of IL-8, TGF-β1, CXCL2, CXCL6, and CXCL12, whereas DBI knockdown led to a significant upregulation of IL-8, TGF-β1, and CXCL2 (Data S11, Figure 13). Meanwhile, we examined the expression levels of key enzymes involved in PC metabolism (CHKA, PCYT1A, and PLD1) in ATP10A- and DBI-knockdown cell lines. The results demonstrated that silencing ATP10A led to a concomitant reduction in the expression of the core enzymes CHKA and PCYT1A, which are essential for PC biosynthesis (Figure S19). These results indicate that ATP10A and DBI, two central genes in PC metabolism, have opposing roles in modulating neutrophil recruitment and N2 polarization in the TME, highlighting their potential as key molecular targets for immune modulation in CRC.

ATP10A is closely associated with the drug treatment of CRC

Using the Estimate algorithm, we found that ATP10A expression positively correlates with higher Immune, Stromal, and Estimate Scores, and inversely with tumor purity (Data S13, Figures S20A and S20B). In contrast, DBI shows the opposite trend, suggesting that a high-ATP10A TME is more complex and may promote CRC cell proliferation, metastasis, and drug resistance. Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) analysis further revealed that ATP10A is positively associated with overall TIDE, Dysfunction, and Exclusion scores (Data S13, Figures S20C–S20E), implying that patients with high ATP10A might benefit less from immune therapy, whereas DBI’s negative correlation with these scores (Figures S20F–S20H) indicates a potentially better immunotherapeutic response. We also evaluated the association between these genes and drug sensitivity. High ATP10A expression correlates with resistance to chemotherapeutic agents such as fluorouracil, gemcitabine, methotrexate, and paclitaxel, as well as targeted therapies including COL-3, lapatinib, ibrutinib, gefitinib, and afatinib, while simultaneously conferring sensitivity to dasatinib, sunitinib, TG-100-115 (a PI3K inhibitor), and PLX-4032 (a BRAF V600E inhibitor) (Data S13, Figure 14A). Conversely, high DBI expression is linked with increased sensitivity to lapatinib, afatinib, gefitinib, erlotinib, C6-ceramide, MI-1, and PF-543 (a SphK1 inhibitor), but with resistance to dasatinib, bleomycin A2, and XL765 (a PI3K/mTOR inhibitor) (Figure 14B). Moreover, a high prognostic model risk score—paralleling high ATP10A expression—correlates with resistance to fluorouracil, gemcitabine, paclitaxel, lapatinib, and gefitinib, and sensitivity to dasatinib and TG-100-115 (Data S13, Figure S21). These findings suggest that ATP10A may drive drug resistance through PC metabolic reprogramming in CRC, warranting further investigation.

Discussion

Although limited prior studies have suggested associations between PC metabolism and CRC, the specific roles and mechanistic underpinnings remained poorly characterized.24,25 In this study, we found that PC metabolism plays a crucial role in the initiation, progression, metastasis, and drug resistance of CRC. Furthermore, we discovered that PC metabolism—and its metabolic gene ATP10A—regulates neutrophil infiltration and function, which may represent a key mechanism underlying its pro-tumorigenic effects. These findings provide novel insights and a theoretical basis for the development and clinical application of therapies targeting PC metabolism or TANs, thereby contributing to the optimization of combination treatment strategies for CRC.

PC is the most abundant phospholipid in cell membranes; however, its causal relationship with CRC remains previously unclear. Andrew et al. reported that elevated circulating PC levels were associated with an increased risk of CRC in men,10 as revealed by plasma metabolomics sequencing. In contrast, two studies on European populations found a negative correlation between PC levels and CRC risk.26,27 Additionally, a study focusing on female CRC precursors identified that the specific PC species C36:3 is associated with a reduced risk of adenomas.9 In this study, we employed MR analysis to determine that PC is the lipid molecule most closely associated with CRC risk. Furthermore, we identified 5 PC molecules that are causally linked to an increased risk of CRC, whereas 1 PC molecule exhibited a protective association. Further bioinformatics analysis and lipidomic sequencing confirmed that PC abundance is significantly elevated in CRC. With respect to CRC treatment, alterations in PC metabolism have been implicated in tumor resistance to therapy.25 For example, oxaliplatin treatment has been shown to significantly increase PC levels in CRC cells.24 Additionally, lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 2 (LPCAT2), a key enzyme involved in PC synthesis, enhances CRC resistance to chemotherapeutic agents, including 5-fluorouracil and oxaliplatin, by promoting lipid droplet formation.28 Consistent with these findings, our study found that high prognostic model risk score is significantly positively correlated with resistance to multiple chemotherapeutic drugs, such as fluorouracil, oxaliplatin, gemcitabine, paclitaxel, and docetaxel, as well as targeted therapy agents, including lapatinib, afatinib, ibrutinib, erlotinib, gefitinib, and Bax channel blockers. Pathway enrichment analysis further revealed that enhanced PC metabolism is positively associated with pro-tumor signaling pathways, such as MAPK, PI3K, NF-κB, and TGF-β, as well as stem cell proliferation, extracellular matrix remodeling, inhibition of apoptosis, and angiogenesis. These findings may elucidate the mechanisms by which PC promotes resistance in CRC. In conclusion, these results highlight the importance of PC metabolism in CRC progression and resistance, emphasizing the need for further research to uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying these processes. Such studies could provide valuable insights for identifying novel therapeutic targets to overcome drug resistance in CRC.

Research has demonstrated that the increase in neutrophils in CRC is closely associated with malignant phenotypes and predicts poor prognosis in patients.29 Chemokines play an indispensable role in recruiting neutrophils within the TME. Of these, IL-8 is the most potent chemokine for neutrophil recruitment and activation within the TME. Upon binding to CXCR1 and CXCR2 on neutrophils, CXCL8 mediates the activation of the MAPK, PLC, and PI3K pathways via G-protein-coupled receptors, leading to neutrophil chemotaxis and effector functions.30,31 Moreover, the CXCL1/8-CXCR2 axis is a key driver in recruiting TANs, thereby promoting CRC progression.32 Additionally, IL-17 plays a pivotal role in neutrophil production, infiltration, and survival. For instance, studies have shown that γδT cells producing IL-17 (γδT17 cells) aid in the accumulation and survival of immunosuppressive TANs.33 Pathway enrichment analysis in this study revealed a positive correlation between PC, chemokine activity and signaling, the production of IL-8, and Th17 cell differentiation. This correlation is positively linked to neutrophil chemotaxis, migration, and activation. Furthermore, single-cell communication analyses indicate that epithelial cells with high PC activity secrete elevated levels of CXCL chemokines. PC biosynthesis activity and ATP10A expression were also positively associated with chemokines essential for neutrophil recruitment. An important regulator of TANs polarization toward the N2 phenotype is TGF-β, which is released by tumor epithelial cells to induce the production of immunosuppressive TANs, thereby facilitating tumor growth and liver metastasis.34 Our findings indicate that PC and ATP10A are positively correlated with TGF-β expression in cancer cells. This positive correlation extends to key molecules associated with the tumor-promoting effects of N2-type neutrophils, including MMP9, CD274 (PD-L1), S100A8, S100A9, and IL-10. Furthermore, molecular analyses focusing on transcription factors revealed that enhanced PC activity is accompanied by a significant increase in SMAD3 activity, a critical transcription factor downstream of the TGF-β signaling pathway. SMAD3 plays a central role in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and immunity, including its critical regulatory influence on neutrophil polarization toward the N2 phenotype.35 Currently, TANs are considered a promising target for antitumor therapies.36 However, their precise roles in CRC progression, metastasis, and drug resistance remain incompletely understood. This underscores the importance of further investigating the mechanisms by which PC and its metabolic gene ATP10A promote the infiltration of TANs in the TME via chemokines such as CXCL8 and IL-17, while driving their polarization to the N2 phenotype via TGF-β. These mechanisms represent a research direction with significant clinical translational value, warranting further validation through future studies.

In this study, we systematically elucidated the pivotal role of PC in CRC initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance through integrative multi-omics analyses. Moreover, by leveraging PC-related genes in combination with machine learning approaches, we established robust models for early CRC diagnosis and prognosis prediction. Notably, our findings suggest a potential interaction between the PC metabolic gene ATP10A and neutrophils, highlighting a promising direction for translational research that may facilitate the development of novel therapeutic strategies against CRC. Importantly, PC metabolic reprogramming is not exclusive to CRC; aberrant lipid metabolism has also been implicated in hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer, and lung cancer, where it contributes to tumor invasiveness, immune suppression, and drug resistance.37,38,39,40 Therefore, the present findings not only establish a foundation for further elucidation of metabolism-immunity interactions in CRC but also offer valuable perspectives for the development of precision therapeutic strategies across multiple cancer types.

Limitations of the study

Despite the strengths of our study—including the integration of multiple public databases, incorporation of single-cell transcriptomic data, and complementary validation using clinical specimens and cell-based experiments—certain limitations remain. Specifically, the absence of further validation in gene knockout animal models, three-dimensional culture systems, and patient-derived xenograft models under complex microenvironmental conditions constrains the robustness and generalizability of our conclusions. At the same time, some issues remain unresolved. For example, although DBI has been suggested to exert protective effects, its expression is elevated in CRC, which appears to be a paradoxical finding. In recent years, accumulating evidence has indicated that, while traditional models of tumorigenesis emphasize the activation/amplification of oncogenes and the inactivation/deletion of tumor suppressor genes as the primary driving forces, cancer progression is in fact a dynamic and highly complex nonlinear process. Within this context, many genes play dual roles, functioning either as oncogenes or as tumor suppressors, and such genes are often referred to as “double agents.” This raises the possibility that DBI may exert a more complex and dynamic role in the progression of CRC—potentially acting as a context-dependent double agent gene—or that its upregulation reflects a stress-induced and compensatory response of the host during tumor progression. Future investigations integrating cutting-edge technologies such as spatial transcriptomics, proteomics, and epigenomics will enable a more comprehensive exploration of the spatial colocalization and crosstalk between PC metabolism and neutrophils in CRC tissues. Such efforts are expected to clarify the mechanisms by which PC metabolic reprogramming drives tumor progression and immune modulation. In addition, longitudinal and prospective cohort studies, complemented by real-time monitoring of PC levels in patients’ biofluids, may provide critical insights into the temporal and causal relationship between PC metabolism and tumor dynamics, thereby reinforcing its potential clinical utility.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture

The SW620 cell line (Servicebio, STCC10812P) and the DLD-1 cell line (Shanghai Jinyuan Biotechnology, JY056) were cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator (Esco, Singapore). SW620 cells were maintained in L15 medium (BIOSPECIES, Bios-0009) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Daxi, DX-1002), 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 100 IU/mL penicillin (Gibco, 15140122). In contrast, DLD-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, C11875500BT) containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 100 IU/mL penicillin. All cell-based experiments were performed using cells in the logarithmic growth phase.

Patient tissue samples

The collection and use of human specimens were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University (Shunde First People’s Hospital, Foshan) (Approval No. KYLS20240912). A total of 48 CRC patients were enrolled in this study (30 males and 18 females), and both CRC tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues were collected. All 48 paired samples were subjected to quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) to validate the differential expression of key genes. Among them, tissue samples from six patients (4 males and 2 females) were selected for broad-targeted lipidomic analysis to assess the abundance of PC. Of the enrolled subjects, 19 were younger than 65 years, and 29 were 65 years or older. No significant differences in ATP10A or DBI gene expression were observed with respect to age or sex. All specimens were obtained from pathologically confirmed CRC patients who underwent colorectal resection at Shunde Hospital between 2023 and 2025. Tissue samples were immediately snap-frozen after surgical removal and stored at −80 °C or in liquid nitrogen until further analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and none of the patients had received radiotherapy or chemotherapy prior to surgery. Detailed clinical information is provided in Table S2.

Method details

Mendelian randomization study

We leveraged the most comprehensive genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary data on CRC risk available, using a discovery dataset (GWAS: GCST012879)43 of 32,072 Europeans (19,948 cases and 12,124 controls) and a validation dataset from the FinnGen database (FinnGen: R10_C3_COLORECTAL_EXALLC)44 comprising 321,040 Europeans (6,847 cases and 314,193 controls). For the exposure, we used GWAS lipidome data from 7,174 Europeans that profiled 179 lipid molecules, including triglycerides, sterol esters, sphingolipids, phosphatidylinositols, phosphatidylethanolamines, phosphatidylcholines, diglycerides, cholesterol, and ceramides (GWAS: GCST90277238-GCST90277416).45

The workflow for the Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis is illustrated in Figure S1. Instrumental variables (IVs) were selected based on three criteria: (1) Genome-wide significance thresholds of P < 5 × 10-6 for forward MR analyses (lipid species as exposures) and P < 5 × 10-8 for reverse MR analyses (CRC as exposure); (2) Pruning for linkage disequilibrium (LD) using the R package Plinkbinr (v0.0.0.9000) with an LD threshold of r2 < 0.001 within a 10,000 kb window, referencing the 1000 Genomes Project; (3) Calculation of the proportion of variance explained (PVE) for each SNP as PVE = 2 × EAF × (1 − EAF) × β2, and exclusion of weak IVs with F-statistics ≤ 10, where F = [PVE × (n − 1 − k)] / [(1 − PVE) × k]. Two-sample MR (TSMR) analyses were performed to explore causal associations between 179 lipid species and CRC. The primary methods were inverse variance weighted (IVW, fixed-effects model) and Bayesian Weighted Mendelian Randomization (BWMR) . IVW serves as the standard MR approach, while BWMR integrates Bayesian modeling to account for pleiotropy and weak instruments via outlier detection and adjustment. Robustness was assessed through multiple sensitivity tests. Heterogeneity among SNPs was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test under both IVW and MR-Egger models; when P < 0.05, a random-effects model was applied. MR-PRESSO was used to detect and correct for horizontal pleiotropy through its global, outlier, and distortion tests, followed by reassessment with the MR-PRESSO global and MR-Egger intercept tests. If pleiotropy persisted, causal estimates from MR-Egger were adopted. To control for multiple testing, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to IVW-derived P-values. Additional validation was conducted using MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods. Replication analyses were performed using the R10_C3_COLORECTAL_EXALLC dataset from the Finnish database. Reverse MR analyses were further conducted with CRC as the exposure and lipid species as outcomes to assess potential bidirectional causality. All analyses were implemented using the TwoSampleMR (v0.5.6) and BWMR (v0.1.1) R packages.

Our Mendelian randomization analyses were conducted using a custom-built R package, MR_EASY, designed specifically for handling GWAS data and implementing multiple MR methods. MR_EASY integrates approaches such as Inverse Variance Weighted, Bayesian Weighted MR, MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode, and provides tailored functions for data preprocessing and analysis (e.g. process_gwas_data, perform_mr_analysis, and perform_re_mr_analysis). Code, example datasets, and comprehensive usage instructions are publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/qifengle4/MR_EASY.git). The results of the Mendelian randomization analyses are summarized in Data S1, and the GWAS datasets used for the analyses can be accessed through the GWAS Catalog and FinnGen databases.

Bulk-transcriptome data source and processing for CRC

We obtained bulk RNA sequencing counts and corresponding phenotypic annotations from the UCSC Xena: TCGA TARGET GTEX cohort via UCSC Xena,42 where datasets were preprocessed through an official pipeline to remove batch effects. Colorectal samples were then filtered so that each sample had expression values ≥1 in at least 9,000 genes and each gene was expressed in at least 370 samples, yielding data for 382 CRC tissues and 354 normal tissues. Additionally, we integrated RNA sequencing data from 16 CRC datasets available in the GEO database, specifically including GEO: GSE10950,46 GSE21510,47 GSE22598,48 GSE24514,49 GSE25071,50 GSE32323,51 GSE35279,52 GSE37364,53 GSE41328,54 GSE44076,55 GSE50746,56 GSE110223,57 GSE87211,58 GSE39582,73 GSE17536,60 and GSE1753761 (Data S2). To ensure consistency across platforms and to mitigate batch effects arising from differing experimental conditions, we applied the Combat function from the ‘limma’ package. This approach utilizes an empirical Bayesian framework to balance technical variability while preserving true biological variation.74

Single-cell sequencing data source and processing for CRC

We retrieved the single-cell RNA-seq dataset from GEO (GEO: GSE132465), which includes 23 CRC tissue samples and 10 adjacent normal samples.62 Quality control was performed using Seurat (v4.4.0)65 with the criteria: nFeature_RNA >500 and <7000, percent.mt <10, percent.HB <5, and nCount_RNA <100,000 (Figures S2A and S2B), yielding 53,250 cells for analysis. Data normalization and standardization were carried out using SCTransform with regression of mitochondrial gene expression (percent.mt), and 3,000 highly variable genes were selected. Principal component analysis was then conducted, and based on an ElbowPlot, the first 18 PCs were chosen for downstream analyses (Figure S2C). Batch effects across patient samples were mitigated using the Harmony algorithm (v1.2.0).66 Unsupervised clustering was performed using FindNeighbors and FindClusters with a resolution of 1.0, resulting in 33 clusters. For visualization, UMAP was employed to reduce the data dimensions, providing an intuitive display of cell distributions (Figure S2). Clusters were manually annotated according to established cell marker genes (detailed in Table S1), and differences in cell type distributions between CRC tissues and adjacent normal tissues were further analyzed using the miloR package (v2.0.0).67 The results of single-cell transcriptomic data quality control, annotation, and differential analysis are summarized in Data S4.

The copy number variation (CNV) algorithm identifies malignant epithelial cells

Epithelial cells were classified based on tissue origin into two categories: “Epithelial Cells (Normal)” and “Epithelial Cells (Tumor)”. To identify malignant epithelial cells, we utilized the InferCNV package (v1.20.0) to compute CNV scores for both groups (Figure S2F). Fibroblasts and endothelial cells from normal tissues served as reference cells to establish a baseline genomic profile, and 1,000 randomly selected CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from normal tissues were added as internal references. This comprehensive approach ensured accurate benchmarking against non-neoplastic states, facilitating precise identification of malignant epithelial cells (Data S4).

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) and area under the curve cell (AUCell) assess the activity of the phosphatidylcholine metabolic pathway

Fourteen gene sets related to phosphatidylcholine metabolic pathways were retrieved from MSigDB75 (Data S3), and after removing duplicates, we established a panel of 88 unique genes. We then applied the GSVA algorithm68 to RNA sequencing count data from TCGA and GTEx to quantify pathway activity in CRC and normal tissues. GSVA normalizes gene expression across samples and computes enrichment scores, facilitating the comparison of pathway activities across different groups (Data S3). At the single-cell level, we used the AUCell algorithm70 on epithelial cell populations (“Epithelial Cells (Normal)” and “Epithelial Cells (Tumor)”) to assess the activity of these PC-related gene sets (Data S4). AUCell ranks gene expression within each cell to calculate an AUC score that reflects overall gene set activity, with higher values indicating increased pathway activation.

Broad-target lipidomics

To verify the abundance changes of PC in CRC, we collected CRC tissues and adjacent normal tissues from six pairs of CRC patients, and performed wide-target lipidomics sequencing based on multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) (Cosmos Wisdom). The tissue samples were extracted using a two-phase solvent (MTBE (HPLC grade, CNW) / methanol (LC-MS grade, CNW) / water), and the extracted lipid molecules were analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (Jasper HPLC) coupled with triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (UPLC-QQQ-MS, AB Sciex 4500/6500), detecting and analyzing the lipid molecules listed through MRM scan mode. Based on the raw files generated by the mass spectrometer, the following data analyses were conducted: (1) Data extraction, using Skyline software to complete the extraction and integration of chromatographic peaks; (2) Data preprocessing, including missing value filtering, missing value imputation, data normalization, etc.; (3) Data quality control, evaluating the stability of the instrument and method through a series of analyses on PQC (pooled quality control samples) and SST (System suitability test) samples; (4) Data analysis, including unsupervised and supervised analyses of different treatment groups, screening of differential lipid molecules, statistical analysis of differential lipid molecules, and presentation of results for differential lipid molecules; (5) Pathway and functional analysis, revealing changes in related biological functions through pathway enrichment analysis of differential lipid molecules. Data from the broad-target lipidomics sequencing and analysis are summarized in Data S5.

Differential analysis and pathway enrichment analysis

Batch effects across GEO: GSE39582, GSE17536, and GSE17537 were removed using Combat from limma, and the corrected datasets were merged to obtain transcriptomic profiles from 798 CRC samples. The activity of the PC biosynthetic pathway (GOBP_REGULATION_OF_PHOSPHATIDYLCHOLINE_BIOSYNTHETIC_PROCESS) was quantified per patient using GSVA,68 and patients were stratified into high- and low-activity groups based on the median score. Differential gene expression analysis was then performed with limma, comparing groups defined by pathway activity or prognostic model risk scores. Genes were considered differentially expressed at P < 0.05 and |logFC| > 0.263. Enrichment analyses (Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and GSEA) were conducted using clusterProfiler76 with the c5.all.v2024.1.Hs.entrez.gmt dataset as reference, with GO and KEGG analyses focusing on significant genes, and GSEA applied to all genes. Results were visualized using ggplot2 and GseaVis (v0.1.0). The results are summarized in Data S4.

Analysis of the immune microenvironment in CRC

Immune cell infiltration in 798 CRC patients was quantified using the ssGSEA algorithm (28 immune features from TISIDB,77 29 immune features from PMC631092878) from the GSVA package (v1.52.0) and multiple tools from the IBOR package (v0.99.0), including CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT_ABS, MCPcounter, quanTiseq, and xCell.79 These analyses enabled comparisons among groups based on PC biosynthesis pathway activity, prognostic risk scores, and ATP10A/DBI expression levels (Data S6). In addition, we compared levels of neutrophil-associated chemokines and receptors (CXCL8, CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, CXCL5, CXCL6, CXCL12, CXCR1, CXCR2, CXCR4, ALOX5) and N2-type TANs markers (IL10, TGFβ1–3, MMP9, IL4R, IL6, PD-L1, S100A8, S100A9) across these groupings (Data S7). For single-cell data, a threshold of 0.060 for PC biosynthesis activity was determined using AUCell_exploreThresholds, categorizing epithelial cells into high (Epithelial Cells(Tumor)_high) and low (Epithelial Cells(Tumor)_low) activity groups. Intercellular communication was then inferred with CellChat (v1.6.1) by analyzing “Secreted Signaling”. Following data subsetting, identification of overexpressed genes, ligand–receptor interaction analysis, network correction, and communication probability calculation, significant communication pairs were identified. Communication at the pathway level was further evaluated using computeCommunProbPathway and aggregateNet, while centrality analysis (netAnalysis_computeCentrality) quantified the roles of signal senders and receivers (Data S4).

pyScenic transcription factor analysis

We employed pySCENIC70,71 to analyze transcription factors (TFs) as follows: (1) Expression matrices from Epithelial Cells (Tumor)_high and _low groups were extracted and converted into Loom format; (2) TF-target relationships were inferred with GRNBoost2 based on co-expression patterns; (3) These relationships were validated by cisTarget motif enrichment analysis using a motif ranking database and TF motif annotation table; (4) Regulatory activity scores for each TF were assessed with AUCell; (5) The specificity of the TF network was quantified by calculating the Residual Sum of Squares (RSS) (Data S4).

Diagnostic model of PC-MRGs in CRC

To construct a robust clinical diagnostic model for CRC, we applied 11 machine learning algorithms—Elastic Net, Lasso, Ridge regression, Stepwise GLM, SVM, LDA, glmBoost, plsRglm, RF, XGBoost, and Naive Bayes—which were combined into 107 algorithm variations (Table S3).80 Parameter optimization and model evaluation were performed via 10-fold cross-validation on standardized datasets to mitigate size bias. The GEO: GSE87211 dataset (203 CRC and 160 normal samples) was used for training, while model robustness was tested using multiple validation cohorts (including GEO: GSE10950, GSE110223, GSE21510, GSE22598, GSE24514, GSE25071, GSE32323, GSE35279, GSE37364, GSE41328, GSE44076, GSE50746, and GSE39582). Performance metrics included AUC, accuracy, recall, and F1 score, with AUC and F1 score considered most reliable (Data S8). The optimal model was selected based on calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) in both the training set (GEO: GSE87211) and the primary validation dataset (GEO: GSE44076, comprising 98 CRC and 50 normal samples). The GEO: GSE44076 dataset, which includes 98 CRC tissue samples and 50 normal mucosa samples, was selected as the primary validation dataset due to its optimal representation of real-world diagnostic scenarios. ROC curves generated from GEO: GSE87211 further evaluated the diagnostic value of the model’s gene features (Data S8).

Prognostic model of PC-MRGs in CRC

Merged datasets from GEO: GSE39582, GSE17536, and GSE17537 were batch-corrected and further filtered to remove missing values and samples with zero survival time. These data were then randomly split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%) using a fixed seed (Seed = 1792), with GEO: GSE87211 serving as an independent validation set. Focusing on PC-MRGs expression, feature selection was performed using five machine learning methods—univariate Cox regression, LASSO regression, XGBoost, gradient boosting (GBM), and random forests—and the significant variables were subsequently incorporated into multivariate Cox regression models. The stepAIC function, based on the Akaike Information Criterion, was employed for automatic variable selection to define the optimal model formula. Models were evaluated in the training set using AIC, C-index, and AUC metrics, which led to the selection of the LASSO-Cox model as the best performer (Data S9). A predictive nomogram was then developed for clinical decision-making. The model’s predictive performance was validated by calculating risk scores for patients in the training, test, and independent validation sets; time-dependent ROC curves (1, 3, and 5 years), calibration curves, and Kaplan–Meier survival analyses further confirmed its reliability and generalizability. Additionally, correlations between risk scores and clinical indicators were examined (Data S10). Moreover, differential, enrichment, and immune infiltration analyses were performed based on the prognostic model’s risk score-based grouping (Data S9).

Establishment of knockdown cell lines

The genomic sequences for ATP10A and DBI were retrieved from the NCBI database. sgRNAs were designed using the Benchling platform (https://benchling.com/editor) to ensure low specificity scores and minimal off-target effects, with their sequences listed in Table S5. The sgRNAs were synthesized by Beijing Qinke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., cloned into the lentiCRISPR v2 plasmid, and co-transfected with psPAX2 and VSV-G into HEK-293T cells using PEI 40K. After 24 and 48 hours, viral supernatants were collected and used to infect SW620 and DLD-1 cells, which were further incubated for 24 hours. Puromycin (2 μg/mL) was then applied until all wild-type cells were eliminated. Finally, RNA and protein were extracted and the knockout efficiency was validated by Real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting (WB).

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR

We used RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan) to reverse transcribe primers from CRC tissue or cells (SW620, DLD-1), utilizing the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) and the QuantStudio™ 5 real-time fluorescence PCR system (Thermo Fisher, USA) for cDNA amplification. β-actin was used as an internal control. For tissue samples, we employed the -ΔCt method to detect the expression of related genes and compared their expression differences between cancerous tissues and adjacent non-cancerous tissues; for cell samples, we used the 2ˆ-ΔΔCt method to assess the expression of related genes and then compared the expression differences between different treatment groups. Each experiment was repeated three times. The primer sequences are Table S4. All qRT-PCR results are summarized in Data S11.

Western blot

Total cellular proteins were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Beyotime, China). The protein samples were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto methanol-activated polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Germany) using a wet transfer method. The membranes were blocked at room temperature for 2–2.5 h with PBST containing 5% nonfat milk to prevent nonspecific binding. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2–2.5 h. Finally, the protein bands were visualized using the Omni-ECL™ Femto Light Chemiluminescence Kit (Yazyme, China) and imaged with a Bio-Rad imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). The anti-ATP10A antibody was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, and all other antibodies were obtained from Proteintech.

Cell scratch assay

CRC cells (SW620 and DLD-1) were divided into three experimental groups: wild-type (WT), negative control (NC; transfected with an empty plasmid), and knockdown (KO). Horizontal lines were evenly drawn on the underside of a 6-well plate using a marker, with a spacing of 1–1.5 cm between each line, ensuring that each well was intersected by at least three lines. Approximately 5 × 105 CRC cells from the WT, NC, and KO groups were seeded into the wells. Using a medium-sized pipette tip, scratches were made perpendicular to the horizontal lines on the underside of the plate. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was then used to wash away detached cells, after which fresh culture medium was added to each well. The plate was placed back into the incubator, and photographs were captured at 0 and 24 hours for subsequent analysis. Wound healing areas were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54), and the healing rate was subsequently calculated. Each experiment was performed in three independent biological replicates. Statistical analysis was conducted, and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Significance levels were denoted as follows: P < 0.05 (∗), P < 0.01 (∗∗), P < 0.001 (∗∗∗), and P < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗); “ns” indicates no statistically significant difference.

Colony formation assay

To assess colony formation, cells from the WT, NC, and KO groups were seeded at a density of 3,000 cells per well in a 6-well plate during their logarithmic growth phase. The cells were monitored daily, and the culture medium was refreshed every three days. The assay was terminated when the majority of individual colonies contained more than 50 cells. After the culture medium was aspirated and the wells were washed with PBS, 1.5 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde was added to each well to fix the cells for 20 minutes at room temperature. Subsequently, 1.5 mL of 0.1% crystal violet staining solution was added to each well, and the cells were stained for another 20 minutes. The wells were then rinsed thoroughly with running water and allowed to air dry. Once dried, the plate was inverted onto a blank A4 sheet of paper, photographed. The number of colonies was subsequently quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54). Each experiment was performed in three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted, and differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. Statistical significance was indicated as follows: P < 0.05 (∗), P < 0.01 (∗∗), P < 0.001 (∗∗∗), and P < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗); “ns” indicates no statistically significant difference.

5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

Cells from the WT, NC, and KO groups were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The next day, cell proliferation was measured using the EdU-488 Cell Proliferation Detection Kit (Beyotime, C0071S). Briefly, a 2× EdU solution (20 μM; prepared by diluting 10 mM EdU at a 500:1 ratio in culture medium) was added (2 mL per well) and incubated for 4 hours at 37 °C. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10–15 minutes. Next, the Click reaction solution was applied and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 minutes, followed by nuclear staining with 1:1000 diluted Hoechst 33342 in PBS (1 mL per well) for 10 minutes in the dark. Finally, fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope, detecting signals from Azide 488 and Hoechst 33342. Cell counts and the proportion of proliferating cells were determined using ImageJ software (version 1.54). All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted, and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Significance levels were indicated as follows: P < 0.05 (∗), P < 0.01 (∗∗), P < 0.001 (∗∗∗), and P < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗); “ns” denotes no statistically significant difference.

Cell counting kit-8

According to the WT/NC/KO grouping, after cell counting, 5000 cells in 100 μl were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate, with 3 replicate wells set up for each group. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator, and 200 μl of PBS was added around the perimeter of the 96-well plate. Once the cells stabilized, measurements were taken at 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours. A CCK-8 premix solution (complete culture medium: CCK8 reagent = 9:1) was added at 100 μl per well to the 96-well plate, and after 2.5 hours of dark incubation in the cell culture incubator, a multifunctional microplate reader (Berthold, Germany) was used to measure the absorbance at 450 nm for each well (Data S12). Statistical analyses were conducted, and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Significance levels were indicated as follows: P < 0.05 (∗), P < 0.01 (∗∗), P < 0.001 (∗∗∗), and P < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗); “ns” denotes no statistically significant difference.

Analysis related to pharmacotherapy

The Estimate algorithm was employed to assess immune, stromal, and overall tumor microenvironment scores, as well as tumor purity. The Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) score predicts cancer patient response to PD-1/CTLA-4 inhibitors by quantifying immune dysfunction and exclusion. We applied the TIDE platform (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) to a standardized expression matrix from 798 CRC patients to compute Immune Dysfunction, Immune Exclusion, and overall TIDE scores. In parallel, we used the oncoPredict package to develop drug response models from Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal 2 (CTRP2) data (545 drugs across 829 cell lines), yielding drug sensitivity scores for each patient (with higher scores indicating resistance and lower scores denoting sensitivity). Drug sensitivity comparisons were then made by grouping patients according to prognostic model risk scores, ATP10A expression, and DBI expression levels (Data S13).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as the median (center line) with boxes indicating the interquartile range (IQR; 25th–75th percentile) or as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), as appropriate. Statistical comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, unpaired or paired Student’s t-test, and one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as applicable. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r). All statistical analyses and figure generation were carried out using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) or R software (versions 4.2.3 and 4.4.1). Statistical significance was defined as follows: P < 0.05 (∗), P < 0.01 ( ), P < 0.001 ( ∗), and P < 0.0001 ( ∗∗∗∗); “ns”, “NA”, or “NS” indicates no statistically significant difference.
