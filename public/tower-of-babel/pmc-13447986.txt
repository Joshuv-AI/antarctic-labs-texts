Bone histological evidence for posture‐related load transmission in the talpid pectoral girdle
Authors: Daichi Nakai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pectoral girdle of terrestrial tetrapods functions as a critical interface for transmitting loads between the trunk and forelimbs. While mammals with a parasagittal forelimb posture mainly rely on the scapula as the principal element for load bearing, talpids (Talpidae) exhibit a markedly specialised pectoral girdle related to a hyper‐abducted forelimb posture. In talpids, the humero‐clavicular articulation plays an important functional role, suggesting that load‐transmission mechanisms within the pectoral girdle may differ from those of typical terrestrial mammals. However, because the pectoral girdle of talpids is highly developed as a fossorial adaptation, it is difficult to infer the mechanical environments experienced by each skeletal element from external morphology alone, and the mechanical roles of the pectoral girdle elements are poorly understood. To examine whether posture‐related reorganisation of load‐transmission pathways is reflected in bone microstructure, collagen fibre orientation weighted mean greyscale level (CFO‐WMGL) and cortical bone robusticity (relative robusticity indicator: RRI) in the clavicle and scapula were evaluated in fossorial talpids (Mogera imaizumii, Mogera wogura and Urotrichus talpoides) and compared with those of the parasagittal outgroup Suncus murinus. Talpids exhibited high RRI values in both the clavicle and scapula, indicating overall mechanical reinforcement of the pectoral girdle. In contrast, CFO‐WMGL showed element‐specific differences among taxa. In Mogera, CFO‐WMGL values were consistently higher in the clavicle than in the scapula, suggesting that the clavicle played a relatively greater load‐bearing role within the pectoral girdle. Conversely, S. murinus exhibited histological characters consistent with the scapula functioning as the principal element for load bearing. These distinct histological patterns indicate that the pectoral girdle is not a mechanically uniform structure, but rather an integrated functional system in which each skeletal element performs distinct mechanical roles. These findings suggest that the evolution of the forelimb posture in talpids was accompanied by a reorganisation of load‐transmission pathways within the pectoral girdle, involving a relative increase in the load‐bearing role of the clavicle compared with the scapula. This study demonstrates that bone histological characters can provide insights into skeletal function that are not apparent from external morphology alone. This approach may offer a useful framework for reconstructing load‐transmission mechanisms in both extant and extinct tetrapods and for investigating how the mechanical demands imposed on each skeletal element have changed during evolution. Future comparative studies across a broader range of taxa will be essential for evaluating the generality of these relationships and for advancing our understanding of the mechanical reorganisation of the vertebrate musculoskeletal system throughout evolutionary history.Keywords: collagen fibre orientation, forelimb posture, functional histology, pectoral girdle, Talpidae

INTRODUCTION

In terrestrial tetrapods, the pectoral girdle has evolved under the common mechanical constraint of supporting body weight on land. The forelimbs not only sustain loads derived from trunk weight but also receive ground reaction forces (GRFs) generated through contact with the substrate (Biewener, 1989, 1990; Blob & Biewener, 2001; Carrier et al., 2006; Kawano & Blob, 2022; McElroy et al., 2014). Since these loads are transmitted through the pectoral girdle, the girdle is subjected to distinct mechanical environments depending on forelimb posture and locomotor mode. Throughout tetrapod evolutionary history, multiple lineages independently underwent transitions from sprawling to parasagittal postures, accompanied by substantial modifications of the skeletal elements and associated muscles of the pectoral girdle (Bishop & Pierce, 2024; Brocklehurst et al., 2022; Fahn‐Lai et al., 2020; Hohn‐Schulte et al., 2013; Kemp, 2005; Luo, 2015; Polly, 2007; Preuschoft et al., 2022). In mammals with the parasagittal posture, the scapula functions as the primary load‐bearing element within the pectoral girdle, whereas the clavicle tends to be reduced or lost, especially in cursorial taxa (e.g. de Souza Junior et al., 2020). However, some mammalian taxa exhibit forelimb postures and pectoral morphology deviating from the parasagittal condition. For example, monotremes with the sprawling posture have a rigid pectoral girdle on the ventral side composed of the clavicle, interclavicle and coracoid (Gambaryan et al., 2015). These morphological differences suggest that evolutionary changes in forelimb posture were accompanied by shifts in load‐transmission patterns and functional partitioning among pectoral girdle elements (Polly, 2007).

Talpids (Talpidae, Mammalia) are a clade characterised by markedly specialised forelimbs and pectoral girdles (Campbell, 1939; Kistin, 1929; Reed, 1951; Sánchez‐Villagra et al., 2004; Whidden, 2000). Fully fossorial talpids (Talpinae) adopt a hyper‐abducted posture in which the humeri are strongly abducted, and locomote by alternately placing the forelimbs cranially (Figure 1; Lin et al., 2019b; Wada et al., 2021). This posture is closely related to the digging behaviour, as talpids burrow tunnels by pushing substrate laterally away from the body using humeral rotation mainly generated by m. teres major (Gambaryan et al., 2002; Lin et al., 2019a; Rose et al., 2013; Yalden, 1966). The skeletal elements of the talpid pectoral girdle show substantial modifications associated with this posture and digging behaviour (Campbell, 1939; Kistin, 1929; Reed, 1951): the clavicle is markedly shortened and articulates broadly with the proximal humerus. Furthermore, the humero‐clavicular articulation functions as the effective rotational centre of the pectoral girdle. On the contrary, the scapula is positioned dorsally on the thorax and oriented along the cranio‐caudal axis. The glenoid cavity is elongated cranially; consequently, the shoulder joint is located cranial to the first rib. These morphological modifications have traditionally been interpreted as an adaptation for the digging behaviour of talpids. However, they may also reflect a distinct load‐transmission pattern within the pectoral girdle compared with the parasagittal posture.

In mammals with the parasagittal posture, m. serratus ventralis functions as a principal antigravity muscle and plays a major role in transmitting loads from the trunk to the pectoral girdle (Carrier et al., 2006). This muscle originates from the cranial ribs and inserts along the medial border of the scapula. In most mammals, the scapula is positioned on the lateral side of the thorax where m. serratus ventralis originates (Fujiwara et al., 2009), placing the glenoid cavity near the muscle insertion. Consequently, the scapula is predicted to experience compressive loading because it lies between the forelimb receiving the GRF and the trunk suspended by m. serratus ventralis (Figure 1a; Polly, 2007).

In talpids, however, such a load‐transmission mechanism is unlikely to operate in the same manner because of their specialised scapular morphology. The ventral trunk participates directly in body support (Reed, 1951), and the forelimbs may only intermittently function in body‐weight support. Nevertheless, during locomotion, talpids alternately contact the ground with the radial side of the manus (Lin et al., 2019b), suggesting that the forelimbs likely contribute to body support. When the forelimbs are positioned laterally in relation to the trunk, the distance between the GRF and the joint centre increases, generating a larger rotational moment at the joint (Blob & Biewener, 2001). Under the hyper‐abducted posture of talpids, abduction moments are therefore predicted to increase particularly around the humero‐clavicular articulation, potentially counteracted by adductor muscles (e.g. m. pectoralis). Moreover, both forelimbs receive medially directed reaction forces during digging driven by humeral long‐axis rotation (Gambaryan et al., 2002; Kistin, 1929; Rose et al., 2013). Under these conditions, the clavicle is predicted to play a greater role in transmitting compressive loads (Figure 1b). Such shifts in load‐transmission patterns within the pectoral girdle suggest functional modifications associated with postural evolution in talpids. However, in taxa highly specialised for the fossorial lifestyle, where skeletal morphology is markedly modified by increased robustness and expanded muscle attachments, it is challenging to infer specific mechanical roles solely from external morphology. In such cases, functional inference based on bone internal structures reflecting the loading regime may be informative.

Histological characters of bone provide information useful for understanding mechanical adaptation to loading environments (e.g. Curry, 2002). Cortical cross‐sectional area is often adopted as an indicator of structural strength (Biewener, 1982; Kilbourne & Hutchinson, 2019; Stock & Shaw, 2007). Increases in cross‐sectional area primarily reflect increases in cortical thickness, which contribute to stress reduction under equivalent loading conditions (Etienne et al., 2024). However, cortical cross‐sectional area does not necessarily reflect the directional properties of in vivo loading (Demes et al., 1998; Lieberman et al., 2004; Meier et al., 2013; Patel et al., 2013).

By contrast, bone matrix exhibits mechanical anisotropy determined by collagen fibre orientation (CFO: Ascenzi & Bonucci, 1967, 1968; Ascenzi et al., 2003; Hasegawa et al., 1994; Skedros et al., 2006, 2024; Stockhausen et al., 2021). When thin sections perpendicular to the long axis of the bone are observed under polarised light microscopy, birefringence intensity varies according to CFO (Boyde & Riggs, 1990; Bromage et al., 2003): Transversely oriented fibres exhibit strong birefringence (i.e. bright pixels), whereas longitudinally oriented fibres exhibit weak birefringence (i.e. dark pixels). Recent studies have shown that CFO primarily reflects the predominant strain mode habitually experienced by bone tissue rather than strain magnitude (Skedros, 2026; Skedros et al., 2026; Takano et al., 1999). This relationship has been examined extensively in the ungulate calcaneus, where regional variation in CFO, osteon morphology and material properties corresponds closely to regional tensile and compressive strain environments under repetitive uniaxial bending (see Skedros, 2026 and references therein). However, studies of other skeletal elements have reported CFO patterns that cannot be fully explained by directly measured or predicted loading environments (Martin et al., 1996; McMahon et al., 1995; Skedros et al., 2026; Warshaw et al., 2017), indicating that CFO reflects adaptation to complex local strain environments rather than a simple tension‐versus‐compression relationship. Because cortical bone is mechanically less resistant to tensile and shear loading than to compression, collagen fibres are hypothesised to be preferentially organised to enhance resistance to complex multiaxial strain environments habitually experienced by bone tissue rather than to any individual loading mode (McMahon et al., 1995; Skedros et al., 2026; Skedros & Doutré, 2019).

Nevertheless, CFO remains a reliable indicator of habitual mechanical loading, as demonstrated by its correspondence with locomotor behaviour (Kalmey & Lovejoy, 2002; Riggs, Lanyon, & Boyde, 1993; Riggs, Vaughan, et al., 1993; Skedros et al., 2007, 2019) and with directly measured in vivo strain distributions (e.g. walking dogs [Takano et al., 1999]; brachiating gibbons [Swartz et al., 1989; Bromage et al., 2003]; flying bats [Swartz et al., 1992; Hieronymus et al., 2024]). Thus, CFO provides information on loading history that cannot be inferred from external bone morphology alone. Accordingly, integrating CFO with cross‐sectional robusticity may allow functional partitioning among skeletal elements to be evaluated in terms of both the predominant local strain mode and the relative loading magnitude within the pectoral girdle. Based on the mechanical models described above, CFO patterns consistent with predominantly compressive strain modes and greater cortical cross‐sectional areas are predicted in the scapula under the parasagittal posture and in the clavicle under the hyper‐abducted posture.

The aim of this study was to test how loading patterns within the pectoral girdle vary among taxa with different forelimb postures and to clarify how modifications of load‐transmission mechanisms associated with postural evolution are reflected in the histological characters of pectoral girdle bones. I investigated the clavicle and scapula, the principal skeletal elements of the mammalian pectoral girdle, across talpid species and an outgroup species used as a control for comparative analyses of CFO within the bone matrix and cortical robusticity.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Some of the specimens used in this study were the same individuals examined by Nakai and Yokohata (2024): I chose fossorial talpids exhibiting the hyper‐abducted posture, Mogera imaizumii (n = 6) and Mogera wogura (n = 8), as well as the semi‐fossorial species Urotrichus talpoides (n = 6), which exhibits a similar forelimb posture. As a terrestrial outgroup phylogenetically close to Talpidae and exhibiting the parasagittal posture, Suncus murinus (Soricidae: n = 5) was selected (Riskin et al., 2016). The Mogera specimens were preserved in the laboratory of Prof. Yokohata under dark and well‐ventilated conditions. Among the Urotrichus specimens, four individuals had been stored in 70% ethanol after collection in the laboratory of Prof. Yokohata. Considering the potential effects of fluid preservation on bone microstructure, two additional specimens stored frozen at −30°C were included. The Suncus specimens had also been stored frozen at −30°C after death.

The clavicle and scapula were used for analysis as skeletal elements involved in body‐weight support. Additionally, the femur was selected as a control element because it functions as a principal weight‐bearing bone and has commonly been used as a scaling standard in allometric analyses (Alexander et al., 1979; Biewener, 1982; Doube et al., 2009; Stock & Shaw, 2007). Exact age and sex information were unavailable for the specimens; however, to minimise ontogenetic effects, only individuals in which the epiphyses of the limb bones were completely fused to the diaphysis were examined. From each individual, only the better‐preserved side was examined. Detailed specimen information, including specimen number, skeletal element analysed, laterality and bone length, is listed in Table S1.

Thin section preparation

For the clavicle and femur, transverse sections perpendicular to the long axis of the bones were obtained at the midshaft region (Figure 2). For the scapula, the scapular neck, which is expected to experience substantial loading, was selected as the sampling region. Transverse sections perpendicular to the long axis of the scapular spine were prepared at the midpoint between the glenoid cavity and the distal end of the scapular spine. However, in talpids, the scapular spine is positioned distally, making it difficult to obtain sections from a directly corresponding scapular neck region. Therefore, in talpid scapulae, the proximal portion of the acromion process was preferred as the sampling region (Figure 2d). Because this region is morphologically inferred to function as a stress‐concentration area, it was treated as a region functionally corresponding to the scapular neck.

Bone thin sections were prepared following the protocol of Nakai and Boyde (2024). After removal of soft tissues, the undecalcified samples were embedded in epoxy resin (Devcon ET; ITW Performance Polymers, Japan), and were cut into approximately 1‐mm‐thick sections using a low‐speed diamond saw (Refine Saw Lo; Refine Tec Ltd., Japan). The sections were immersed in Quadrol (N,N,N′,N′‐tetrakis(2‐hydroxypropyl)ethylenediamine) for 1 week to remove blood pigments and subsequently rinsed with xylene. The acquired sections were progressively ground and polished under water using a polisher (LaboPol‐4; Struers, Denmark) with P1000, P2500 and P4000 abrasive papers. While thickness was repeatedly measured using a micrometre (0–25 mm; accuracy 0.001 mm; MDC‐25MXT, Mitutoyo Co. Ltd., Japan), final section thickness was adjusted to 150 ± 10 μm. The embedding resin was removed using dichloromethane for 1–2 days. Finally, the thin sections were mounted with coverslips using DPX as the mounting medium.

Image acquisition

Bone thin sections were imaged using a linear polarising microscope (Eclipse E200, Nikon Co., Japan) under both plane‐polarised and crossed‐polarised conditions. All images were obtained under the same illumination settings in each polarised condition, respectively. Each thin section was photographed at ×4 magnification (resolution: 0.56 μm/pixel), and sequential images covering the entire cortical region were acquired by moving the mechanical stage. The sequential images were reconstructed into complete cross‐sectional montages using ImageJ (Fiji v1.53t; Schindelin et al., 2012).

Images obtained under crossed‐polarised light were converted into 8‐bit greyscale images using Adobe Photoshop CS6. Background regions were removed by referencing the corresponding plane‐polarised images overlaid as separate layers, and only cortical bone regions were extracted as regions of interest. Pixel frequencies corresponding to each greyscale value (0–254) of the cortical bone regions were obtained using ImageJ. Because ImageJ treated transparent background as white pixels (greyscale value 255), the pixel frequency corresponding to greyscale value 255 was separately obtained in Photoshop and integrated with the ImageJ‐derived histogram to reconstruct a complete greyscale histogram ranging from 0 to 255.

In previous collagen fibre orientation weighted mean greyscale level (CFO‐WMGL) studies, low greyscale values associated with porous spaces have been excluded from analyses (e.g. Skedros et al., 1996, 2009, 2013, 2026). However, examination of greyscale levels 0–5 in the dataset of this study indicated that these pixels rarely corresponded to vascular canals or other porous spaces and were largely distributed within cortical bone regions (Figure S1). Therefore, in this study, these greyscale levels were retained to avoid potential loss of birefringence‐related information. Relatively large pores filled with epoxy resin were excluded during region of interest selection based on the corresponding plane‐polarised images.

Analysis of CFO and relative robusticity indicator (RRI)

The relative patterns of CFO within the cortical bone were quantified from polarised light images. Greyscale values in polarised light images reflect birefringence intensity derived from CFO; however, these values are also influenced by imaging conditions, illumination settings and section thickness (Bromage et al., 2003; Georgiadis et al., 2016). In this study, all images were acquired under the same imaging conditions to minimise methodological variation among specimens. Absolute angular calibration using a calcified tendon sample was not performed (Spiesz et al., 2011). Accordingly, CFO measurements in this study were interpreted not as absolute orientation angles but as a relative indicator for comparing CFO among specimens and skeletal elements.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R ver. 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria). For CFO‐WMGL and RRI, descriptive statistics for each group (species × skeletal element) included sample size (n), mean, standard deviation (SD) and median.

Prior to group comparisons, normality of data distribution within each group was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed because of its suitability for assessing normality in small‐sample sizes (Shapiro & Wilk, 1965). The results generally supported normality in most groups, and few marked deviations from normality were observed in the Q–Q plots (Figures S2 and S3; Table S2). Accordingly, parametric tests were employed in subsequent analyses.

To evaluate interspecific differences in CFO‐WMGL and RRI, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted separately for each skeletal element, followed by the Tukey–Kramer post hoc test. All tests were two‐tailed, and statistical significance was set at 5% (p < 0.05).

To evaluate the robustness of group comparisons under small‐sample conditions, sensitivity analyses were performed to identify influential observations. For each skeletal element, linear models with species as the explanatory variable were constructed, and Cook's distance was calculated. Specimens with Cook's distance values exceeding 4/n were identified as influential observations (Cook, 1977). To assess whether the observed intergroup differences were robust to the removal of influential observations, one‐way ANOVA and Tukey–Kramer post hoc tests were repeated after excluding these specimens.

RESULTS

Interspecific variation in CFO‐WMGL

Comparisons among species showed clear interspecific differences in CFO‐WMGL across skeletal elements (Figure 3). In the clavicle, M. imaizumii exhibited the highest CFO‐WMGL values (mean ± SD = 57.09 ± 18.93), followed by M. wogura (39.44 ± 12.35), U. talpoides (28.80 ± 6.57) and S. murinus (21.30 ± 5.02: Table 1a). In contrast, the scapula exhibited the opposite pattern: S. murinus exhibited the highest values (58.64 ± 15.36), followed by U. talpoides (27.38 ± 9.97), whereas the two Mogera species exhibited markedly lower values (M. imaizumii: 9.21 ± 3.93; M. wogura: 10.08 ± 5.79). In the femur, interspecific differences were relatively small, and all groups exhibited broadly similar values.

Interspecific variation in RRI

In RRI, comparisons among species also showed clear interspecific differences (Figure 5). In the clavicle, the two Mogera species exhibited high RRI values (M. imaizumii: 2.68 ± 0.64; M. wogura: 2.58 ± 0.60), followed by U. talpoides (0.87 ± 0.15) and S. murinus (0.17 ± 0.02: Table 1b). A similar pattern was observed in the scapula, where the two Mogera species also exhibited relatively high values (M. imaizumii: 1.24 ± 0.12; M. wogura: 1.30 ± 0.09), followed by U. talpoides (0.94 ± 0.12) and S. murinus (0.89 ± 0.29).

Sensitivity analysis after removal of influential observations

Influential observations based on Cook's distance were identified in several specimens (Table S3). Reanalysis after excluding these specimens showed that the overall patterns of interspecific differences in CFO‐WMGL and RRI were generally maintained (Tables 2 and 3). Significant differences in the clavicle and scapula remained detectable after exclusion, indicating that the principal statistical conclusions were not strongly dependent on influential observations. However, in clavicular CFO‐WMGL, the significance of post hoc comparisons changed between M. imaizumii and M. wogura and between M. wogura and S. murinus (Table 3a,b). Additionally, for femoral CFO‐WMGL, significant differences were not detected before the removal of influential observations, whereas a significant difference between M. imaizumii and U. talpoides emerged after exclusion (p = 0.04: Table 3b). In the RRI analysis, a change in significance was also observed for the clavicular comparison between U. talpoides and S. murinus (Table 3c,d). A significant difference between U. talpoides and S. murinus emerged after exclusion (p < 0.0001: Table 3d). However, the significant differences detected in the statistical tests with the full dataset did not disappear in either the CFO‐WMGL or RRI analyses.

DISCUSSION

Functional partitioning within the pectoral girdle revealed by CFO and RRI

This study evaluated histological evidence indicating that skeletal elements that compose the pectoral girdle experience different mechanical demands among species and among skeletal elements. Bone internal structure is formed not only by mechanical adaptation but also by phylogenetic constraints and ossification patterns (Galea et al., 2021; Warshaw et al., 2017). Nevertheless, interspecific differences were detected even within homologous skeletal elements. In contrast, the result of the femur did not indicate interspecific variation as pronounced as that observed in the pectoral girdle elements (Figure 3; Table 3). These results support the interpretation that the histological differences observed in the pectoral girdle are not solely determined by systemic bone histological properties or developmental constraints, but instead reflect functional adaptation to the specific mechanical environment of the pectoral girdle.

RRI is interpreted as an indicator reflecting structural robusticity related to load‐bearing capacity, whereas CFO‐WMGL is interpreted as reflecting loading history associated with predominant strain environments. In Mogera, high RRI values were observed in both the clavicle and scapula, indicating that the entire pectoral girdle has acquired substantial structural robusticity. In fossorial mammals, digging against resistant substrate imposes considerable mechanical loads on the forelimb skeleton, resulting in shortening and thickening of bones or modifications of cross‐sectional geometry against bending in specific directions (Kilbourne & Hutchinson, 2019; Meier et al., 2013; Montoya‐Sanhueza & Chinsamy, 2017). Particularly in fossorial talpids, the forelimbs function as the main digging apparatus and generate markedly high out‐forces (Rose et al., 2013), implying that substrate reaction forces act on the forelimbs and pectoral girdle. Therefore, the overall increase in pectoral girdle robusticity can reasonably be interpreted as structural reinforcement related to fossorial adaptation.

In contrast to RRI, CFO patterns indicated species‐specific bias between the clavicle and scapula. CFO is considered to develop in accordance with the mechanical loading acting on the bone (Suda et al., 1999), and some previous studies have supported correspondence between CFO patterns and predominant strain modes, including compression, tension and shear (e.g. Bromage et al., 2003; Hieronymus et al., 2024; Kalmey & Lovejoy, 2002; Riggs, Lanyon, & Boyde, 1993; Skedros et al., 2007). However, under complex local strain environments resulting from the superposition of multiple loading regimes, CFO may preferentially reflect multiaxial strain conditions, possibly because cortical bone is mechanically less resistant to tensile and shear loading than to compression (McMahon et al., 1995; Skedros, 2026; Takano et al., 1999). In the absence of evidence for complex loading regimes, transversely oriented collagen fibres may reasonably indicate a predominantly compressive strain environment. Therefore, although the inter‐element differences in CFO‐WMGL observed in this study likely reflect differences in predominant local strain environments among skeletal elements, interpretation of the predominant loading regimes requires careful consideration in the context of the mechanical models proposed for forelimb posture.

Furthermore, comparisons between the clavicle and scapula exhibited similar inter‐element biases in both CFO and RRI (Figures 4 and 6). This finding suggests that structural reinforcement within the pectoral girdle did not occur as a uniform increase in bone mass, but rather developed in association with load‐transmission pathways concentrated in specific skeletal elements. In particular, the species‐specific bias observed between the clavicle and scapula suggests that differences in internal load‐transmission pathways within the pectoral girdle are associated with forelimb posture and/or digging behaviour.

Interspecific differences in mechanical loading within the pectoral girdle

The scapula of Suncus and the clavicle of Mogera exhibited histological patterns consistent with the mechanical models of forelimb posture proposed in the Introduction. In Suncus, relatively high CFO‐WMGL and RRI values were observed in the scapula, whereas in Mogera these values were increased in the clavicle (Figures 4 and 6). In mammals with the parasagittal posture, m. serratus ventralis functions as a main muscle for supporting loads transmitted from the forelimb to the trunk, and the scapula serves as a major element transmitting compressive loading from the forelimb to the thorax. Indeed, adaptive reinforcement related to body‐weight support has been reported in the thoracic ribs where m. serratus ventralis originates (Fujiwara et al., 2009). The high scapular CFO‐WMGL and RRI values observed in Suncus are consistent with the predominant local strain environment expected from this load‐transmission pathway. In contrast, in Mogera, which exhibits the hyper‐abducted posture, the clavicle appears to experience a predominantly compressive local strain environment in which compressive components predominate. Finite‐element analyses of Tiktaalik under sprawling weight‐support conditions showed concentration of compressive stress in the ventral pectoral girdle and forelimb–trunk articulation regions during asymmetrical weight support (Hohn‐Schulte et al., 2013). The high clavicular CFO‐WMGL values observed in Mogera are consistent with the predominantly compressive local strain environment expected from this stress‐concentration model involving the ventral pectoral girdle. Furthermore, laterally positioned forelimbs increase rotational moments around the shoulder joint (Blob & Biewener, 2001). In Mogera, electromyographic studies have shown activation of the m. pectoralis during stance phase of locomotion (Wada et al., 2021), suggesting that this muscle may stabilise the ventral pectoral girdle against abduction moments generated by the GRF. These observations suggest that the clavicle functions as a load‐bearing element during locomotion, where a predominantly compressive local strain environment may result from multiple mechanical loading regimes involving both compression and muscle‐induced bending.

In contrast, the clavicle of Suncus and the scapula of Mogera exhibited relatively low CFO‐WMGL and RRI values (Figures 4 and 6). Under the current interpretation that collagen fibres reflect the predominant strain mode habitually experienced by bone tissue rather than a simple compression‐versus‐tension relationship, longitudinally dominant CFO is generally interpreted as indicating adaptation to complex multiaxial strain environments, particularly those involving bending and tensile components, rather than purely compression‐dominated strain environments. Although the scapula of Mogera exhibited relatively high RRI values, its CFO pattern showed longitudinally oriented collagen fibres. This result suggests that, despite experiencing substantial mechanical loading, the scapula is not primarily subjected to simple compression‐dominated strain environments. In talpids, the scapula serves as the origin for digging‐related muscles, such as m. teres major and m. triceps brachii (Campbell, 1939), and strong contraction of these muscles may generate multidirectional bending loads. Therefore, compared with mammals exhibiting the parasagittal posture, the scapula of Mogera may function less as a body‐weight‐supporting compressive element and more as a skeletal element adapted to complex multiaxial loading generated during digging.

This study alone cannot clearly distinguish whether the loading experienced by the clavicle in talpids is primarily derived from locomotion or digging behaviour. The clavicle of Mogera broadly articulates with the proximal humerus and receives medially directed reaction forces (i.e. compressive loading) generated from both forelimbs by the digging stroke without mediation through the thorax (Kistin, 1929). Thus, digging behaviour alone could plausibly account for increased mechanical demands on the clavicle. However, relatively low CFO‐WMGL values were observed in M. wogura despite its remarkable fossorial specialisation (Figure 3; Table 1). This result suggests that clavicular loading in talpids cannot be explained solely by bilateral compressive loading generated by digging. Considering the discussion in the section ‘Functional Partitioning Within the Pectoral Girdle Revealed by CFO and RRI’, the clavicle may experience multiple strain regimes, including bending as well as compressive components, generated through muscular activity and stabilisation of the pectoral girdle during locomotion. Therefore, the histological characters observed in the clavicle should be interpreted not as the result of either posture or digging independently, but rather as the cumulative result of complex mechanical loading associated with both posture and digging behaviour.

Interestingly, Urotrichus exhibited an intermediate pattern between Suncus and Mogera in both CFO‐WMGL and RRI, with statistically significant differences particularly observed in the scapula (Figures 4 and 6). Urotrichus exhibits the hyper‐abducted posture while maintaining a semi‐fossorial lifestyle involving both surface and subterranean activity (Abe & Yokohata, 1998). Compared with Mogera, Urotrichus burrows in mechanically less resistant substrates consisting of fallen leaves and humus layers (Abe & Yokohata, 1998; Nakai & Yokohata, 2024), which is consistent with its intermediate RRI values. Furthermore, although anatomical studies remain limited, Urotrichus and Neurotrichus (Neurotrichini, Talpidae), whose morphology is similar to that of Urotrichus, have been observed walking with the entire manus placed directly on the ground (Ishii, 2010; Nowak, 1999), suggesting that they may adopt a less extremely abducted forelimb posture during terrestrial locomotion compared with Mogera. Importantly, intermediate CFO‐WMGL patterns should not be interpreted simply as representing an intermediate magnitude of loading, but rather as reflecting differences in predominant strain mode (Skedros et al., 2026; Takano et al., 1999). Although Mogera and Urotrichus differ in the compactness of the substrates in which they dig, the intermediate CFO‐WMGL pattern in Urotrichus should not be interpreted simply as reflecting a reduced magnitude of predominantly mediolateral compressive strain generated by digging. Therefore, the mechanical environment of the pectoral girdle in Urotrichus may reflect not only relatively reduced frequency or magnitude of digging‐related loading, but also a local strain environment associated with a locomotor mode distinct from those of both Suncus and Mogera.

This study demonstrates that histological characters can provide insight into load‐transmission patterns within the pectoral girdle related to differences in forelimb posture and locomotor behaviour. Especially, comparative analyses of cross‐sectional bone properties, including CFO, may be useful for investigating not only extant mammals but also taxa exhibiting the sprawling posture or highly specialised pectoral girdle functions (e.g. turtles: Mayerl et al., 2019). Because this study was restricted to comparisons among extant small insectivorous mammals with relatively limited phylogenetic disparity, broader comparative analyses encompassing a wider diversity of postural and locomotor adaptations will be necessary to evaluate the generality of this approach.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, the evaluation of CFO patterns was based on greyscale values from polarised light images and therefore represents a relative indicator of CFO. Because CFO‐WMGL was quantified as a single weighted‐mean metric, the resulting values do not represent absolute orientation angles of collagen fibres and should instead be interpreted as a relative indicator of orientation. Furthermore, images were acquired using linear polarised light microscopy, under which birefringence intensity varies according to the relative angle between CFO and the polarisation axis, with extinction occurring at specific angles (Bromage et al., 2003). Owing to this imaging condition, birefringence may be underestimated in regions near extinction positions. However, because all specimens were imaged under identical conditions and aligned in the same orientation, the relative comparability of interspecific trends is maintained.

Second, completely homologous sampling locations could not be standardised across species for scapular thin sections. In S. murinus, transverse sections were obtained from the scapular neck corresponding to the midpoint between the glenoid cavity and the distal end of the scapular spine. In contrast, in Mogera, morphological specialisation of the scapula prevented sampling from an equivalent region, and sections were instead obtained from the proximal portion of the acromion region, which was inferred to function as a stress‐concentration area. Consequently, interspecific comparisons of the scapula may have been influenced by local differences in mechanical environment and microstructural organisation among sampling regions. This issue should therefore be considered when interpreting scapular CFO and RRI patterns.

Because preparation of bone thin sections required destructive sampling, the number of available specimens was limited, constraining statistical power. Nevertheless, significant differences were detected in the interspecific comparisons, and the overall trends were generally maintained after exclusion of influential observations, suggesting that the major conclusions of this study are reasonably robust. In addition, preservation conditions differed among some specimens. In particular, the Urotrichus sample included both ethanol‐preserved and frozen specimens. However, no marked differences related to preservation condition were detected (Table S1), and these differences were therefore considered unlikely to critically affect the principal conclusions of this study.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates that bone histological characters can reveal functional differentiation among skeletal elements within the mammalian pectoral girdle. By integrating CFO (CFO‐WMGL) and RRI, distinct mechanical features were identified in the clavicle and scapula that varied according to forelimb posture and fossorial behaviour. Fossorial talpids exhibited an overall increase in pectoral girdle robusticity, whereas the material anisotropy of the bone matrix markedly differed between the clavicle and scapula. In particular, high CFO‐WMGL values in the clavicle suggest an enhanced role in load bearing associated with the hyper‐abducted forelimb posture. In contrast, Suncus, which adopts the parasagittal posture, exhibited histological characters consistent with the scapula functioning as the principal element for load bearing.

These findings support the interpretation that the evolution of forelimb posture in talpids was accompanied by a reorganisation of load‐transmission pathways within the pectoral girdle. Rather than functioning as a mechanically homogeneous structure, the pectoral girdle can be understood as an integrated system in which each skeletal element performs distinct mechanical roles. The intermediate histological patterns observed in Urotrichus further suggest that variation in forelimb posture and locomotor behaviour is associated with corresponding changes in loading regimes within the pectoral girdle.

This study also demonstrates that bone histology can provide insights into skeletal function that are difficult to infer solely from external morphology. Because histological characters preserve information regarding habitual mechanical environments, this approach may offer a useful framework for reconstructing load‐transmission mechanisms in both extant and extinct tetrapods and for investigating how the mechanical demands imposed on each skeletal element change during evolution. Future comparative studies across a broader range of taxa are necessary to evaluate the generality of these relationships and to deepen our understanding of how the mechanical organisation of the vertebrate musculoskeletal system has evolved throughout evolutionary history.
