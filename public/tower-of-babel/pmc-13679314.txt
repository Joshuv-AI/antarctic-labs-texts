Social and Structural Determinants of Chronic Pain Among Migrant Workers: A Scoping Review Using an Intersectionality Framework
Authors: Lise Dassieu, Jacqueline Schneider, Manon Choinière, Nataly Espinoza-Suarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDue to structural inequities, many migrant workers face adverse health outcomes potentially exposing them to long-term pain issues. However, the extent of current knowledge on chronic pain (>3 months) in this population is unclear. As labor migration policies evolve toward less protective measures, it is essential to gain insights into the experiences and drivers of chronic pain among migrant workers.AimsDrawing on the theoretical framework of intersectionality, this scoping review aimed to synthesize existing knowledge on chronic pain among migrant workers in Canada and the United States. More precisely, we examined evidence related to epidemiology, treatments, lived experiences, and the role of social and structural determinants.MethodsThis review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews. A search of four databases was followed by a screening and extraction procedure involving four researchers. Relevant findings were reported through quantified and narrative syntheses.ResultsThis scoping review highlights a major gap in understanding pain chronicity among migrant workers. Nine studies met the eligibility criteria. Most studies have focused on occupational health outcomes. Definitions of chronic pain are inconsistent. Only one qualitative study examined migrant workers’ pain trajectories beyond workplace settings. Social and structural determinants were underscored in all studies, including poor working conditions, socioeconomic precarity, unfavorable labor market structure, restrictive immigration policies, racism and discrimination, and gendered inequities.ConclusionsThis review invites researchers and policymakers to move beyond occupational health approaches to fully appraise and tackle the intersecting structural and political drivers exposing migrant workers to lifelong experiences of chronic pain.

Introduction

Background

This scoping review examines chronic pain among migrant workers in Canada and the United States (US) using an intersectional lens. The United Nations defines a migrant worker as “A person who is to be engaged, is engaged or has been engaged in a remunerated activity in a State of which he or she is not a national.”1 In Canada, the number of migrant workers has been rising steadily in recent years,2,3 representing an important driver of economic growth, and contributing to fill critical staff shortages in sectors such as health care, construction, and agriculture.4 However, as the political and economic situations are becoming less favorable, the Canadian government has recently implemented significant restrictions on temporary foreign worker programs and permanent economic immigration programs.5–8 Many experts have expressed concern that these changes may lead to a deterioration in migrant workers’ living conditions in the country, as a significant number of them may become undocumented, and those enrolled in the most restrictive labor migration programs (e.g., employer-specific permits, low-wage stream) may have very limited possibilities to legally stay and work in Canada.9,10 In the US, the situation has been evolving quickly in recent years toward antiimmigration policies and enforcement-based approaches toward undocumented migrants, which has highly reinforced the socioeconomic precarity of migrant workers employed in nonspecialized and/or low-wage jobs.11

Living and working in their host countries may significantly impact migrant workers’ bodies. Previous studies have highlighted multiple adverse health outcomes facing migrant workers, especially those employed in low-wage positions and manual job settings characterized by unsafe working conditions that can lead to frequent injuries or accidents.12–19 Precarious housing conditions have also been described among migrant workers, especially in the agricultural sector.20 Migrant workers employed in lowerwage jobs are also likely to face multiple barriers to accessing health care and medical insurance.13,16,20–23 Acute episodes of work-related pain and injuries are well documented among migrant workers in a wide range of industries including construction, agriculture, housekeeping, and manufacturing.13,16–18 This may suggest that due to their often precarious working and living conditions, as well as structural barriers to health care, migrant workers in these industries might be particularly at risk of developing chronic pain, i.e., pain persisting or recurring for longer than 3 months.24 However, to date, the extent of current knowledge about chronic pain among migrant workers is unclear. In fact, there is no synthesis of the existing literature in terms of epidemiology, treatments, and lived experiences of chronic pain in this population. It is also unclear to what extent existing literature has explored the influence of social determinants (e.g., living and working conditions) and structural determinants (e.g., systemic oppressions, policy contexts) on migrant workers’ chronic pain. A scoping review on these issues could help to establish an agenda for future research and policies aimed at advancing equity and quality in the management of chronic pain for migrant workers. An intersectionality-based theoretical framework appears particularly helpful to analyze these issues.

Theoretical framework

This scoping review is grounded in an intersectional approach to social and structural determinants of health. Social determinants refer to the conditions in which individuals live and develop, and which may affect their health. This includes, for example, socioeconomic, housing, and working conditions.25 Structural determinants refer to the deeper roots of social health inequities, embedded in societal, institutional, and political contexts.26,27 The intersectional approach, as theorized by Patricia Hill Collins,28,29 analyzes the coconstruction of multiple systems of oppression historically based on social positions (e.g., racism, colonialism, economic exploitation, gender inequities, ableism, etc.). These systems of oppression can affect the framing of policies in different domains such as employment, immigration, and health, thereby reinforcing the injustices experienced by migrant workers, who often face multiple sources of structural oppression and inequities. Intersectionality was previously utilized in several studies analyzing the experiences of migrant workers across different statuses and industries,30–32 including their health, well-being, and access to health care.33,34 The relevance of the intersectional framework is also increasingly recognized in chronic pain research.35,36 In this scoping review, intersectionality was used as an analytic framework to interpret the findings and discuss how different social and structural determinants interplay in migrant workers’ chronic pain outcomes and experiences. By drawing on intersectionality, we intend to examine the ways in which social and structural determinants of chronic pain are mutually constituted, rather than conceptualizing them as separate and independent influences.

Objectives

The primary objective of this scoping review was to identify and synthesize existing knowledge on chronic pain among migrant workers in Canada and the US. More specifically, the domains of interest were as follows: (1) chronic pain epidemiology (i.e., prevalence and characteristics), (2) chronic pain treatment (e.g., treatment access and approaches, health care providers’ perspectives), (3) lived experiences of migrant workers with chronic pain, and (4) the role played by social and structural determinants in influencing the three above domains. Moreover, this scoping review intends to outline knowledge gaps in this area to inform the development of a future research and policy agenda. A scoping review design appears relevant to address our objectives, as this type of review typically allows to “assess the potential size and scope of available research literature” and “identify the nature and extent of research evidence.”37

To fulfil our objectives, this scoping review was based on a general research question and four specific research questions. The general research question guided the literature search and selection, while the four specific research questions guided the extraction and synthesis of identified evidence. The general research question was: Based on the existing recent scientific literature, what is known about chronic pain among migrant workers in Canada and the US?

The specific research questions were as follows:

1. What is known about the epidemiology of chronic pain among migrant workers in Canada and the US?

2. What is known about the treatment of chronic pain among migrant workers Canada and the US?

3. What is known about the lived experiences of migrant workers with chronic pain in Canada and the US?

4. What is known about the influence of social and structural determinants on chronic pain epidemiology, treatment, and experiences among migrant workers in Canada and the US?

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews38 and reported based on the Prisma Extension for Scoping Reviews Checklist (PRISMAScR).39 The JBI methodology was developed as an extension of the Arksey and O’Malley approach,40 offering more detailed and structured guidance.38 The JBI methodology facilitates the clarification of the review scope using the Population, Concept, Context (PCC) model, the integration of a theoretical framework, and the use of standardized reporting guidelines (PRISMAScR). The scoping review protocol was not published but is provided as supplementary material for transparency.

Eligibility criteria

Population: migrant workers

In this study, we used the United Nations’ definition of migrant workers cited above,1 which includes all migrants who work outside of their country of citizenship, independently from their immigration status. This definition includes undocumented migrant workers. Only studies with adult migrant workers were included, as we consider that the issue of child work goes beyond the scope of this review. To be eligible, references had to explicitly refer to participants as “workers” in their title or abstract.

Concept: chronic pain

We used the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) Classification of Chronic Pain for ICD-11, which defines chronic pain as “pain that lasts or recurs for longer than 3 months.”24

Context: Canada and the US in contemporary times

Research conducted in Canada and/or the US was eligible. More precisely, this includes research conducted in one or more Canadian or US provinces, territories, states, regions, counties, or towns. International studies where data from Canada or the US was not reported separately were excluded. As our research team is based in Canada, our primary interest was to assess the scope of existing literature in the Canadian context. However, while designing our search strategies, it appeared that Canadian evidence was likely extremely limited. The review scope was therefore broadened to the US, given that this country shares a border with Canada and both are high-income countries situated in North America.

In Canada, the majority of migrant workers enter the country with a temporary work permit allowing them to fulfil occupational duties for a limited period of time. Canadian work permits fall under two main programs with different conditions and implications for migrant workers.41,42 First, the temporary foreign worker program is intended to fill labor shortages. Work permits in this program are usually employer-specific and the program is divided into high-wage and low-wage streams, as well as specific streams for agricultural workers, home care workers, and highly specialized workers (designated as “global talent stream”). The low-wage and agricultural streams lead to very limited possibilities of obtaining permanent residence, contrary to the other streams.41,42 Second, the International Mobility Program includes labor migration streams for which employers do not need to prove a labor shortage. Several streams in this program offer open work permits allowing to work for any employer, with pathways to permanent residence for workers employed in specialized jobs.41,42

In the US, labor migration is organized into multiple programs, with the “H” program encompassing labor shortage fillings for occupations requiring specialized knowledge (H-1), agricultural workers (H-2A), and nonagricultural workers who do not fall under the H-1 category (H-2B).43 The H-2A and H-2B visas are less likely to result in permanent residence and are associated with more precarious conditions for workers and their families. Other US labor migration programs are mostly intended to facilitate international mobility of specialized workers from countries with specific agreements, or in domains requiring specific skills. All temporary migration programs in the US appear to be employer-specific.43 The main characteristics of labor migration programs in both countries are detailed in Table 1.

Moreover, not all migrant workers fall under these programs, as they may also have other immigration statuses allowing them to work in their host countries (e.g., permanent resident, refugee, asylum-claimant), or be undocumented. It is worth noting that undocumented migrant workers represent a particularly vulnerable group in terms of access to rights and exposure to labor exploitation.

As outlined in Table 1, it is important to acknowledge that labor migration programs, responses to undocumented migration, and health policies differ between the two countries, which leads to different implications for migrant workers’ daily living, service access, and potentially chronic pain. In terms of time boundaries, this scoping review was limited to research conducted from 2010 onwards to account for the contemporary situation of migrant workers with chronic pain.

Types of evidence sources: peer-reviewed literature

To be eligible, references had to be published in peer-reviewed journals. This comprised primary studies using qualitative, quantitative or mixed methods, literature reviews and commentaries or editorials. Gray literature was considered out of the scope of this review given that our objective was to explore the state of peer-reviewed academic knowledge to identify gaps and future research priorities. Research published in English or French (the two official languages in Canada) were considered for inclusion. All the authors are fluent in French.

Search and selection strategies

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed with the help of a senior librarian from the principal investigator’s research center. It was based on two main concepts: migrant workers and chronic pain. The proposed keywords for the first concept (migrant workers) were adapted from the methods reported in a scoping review on the health of migrant workers in Canada.15 The proposed keywords for the second concept (chronic pain) were adapted from the search strategy employed in a review of chronic pain care recommendations previously conducted by two members of our research team (LD, MC).44 The following databases were searched: APA PsycInfo, Pubmed, CINAHL, and Embase. The reference lists of selected articles were also examined to find additional relevant sources. The detailed search strategy is available in appendix as supplementary material.

Screening procedures

Retrieved references were firstly screened based on titles and abstracts and secondly based on the full texts of potentially relevant articles. All titles and abstracts were screened by three members of the research team (LD, JS, and MC). Then, all full texts were double-screened by two members of the research team: the principal investigator (LD) screened all full texts while each of the co-investigators (JS, MC, and NES) double-screened 33% of the selected sample. All researchers documented their choices in a standardized Microsoft Excel document. The final percent agreement was 91.5% at the title and abstract stage, and 75.5% at the full text stage. At each step, disagreements were discussed with the four team members to reach consensus. In addition, to ensure appropriateness and consistent application of the selection criteria across the researchers, the criteria were pilot tested with 50 references, and clarifications were collectively added to the criteria when necessary. Inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The research team comprised a sociologist (LD), an anthropologist (JS), a senior chronic pain epidemiologist (MC), and a family physician completing a PhD in health sciences (NES). LD is an early-career researcher whose research program explores structural inequities at the intersection of chronic pain, immigration, and professional trajectories. She is involved in several ongoing research projects with migrant workers and people seeking asylum. JS is an early-career immigration specialist who has conducted several studies exploring the lived experiences of migrant women, including their work experiences. MC is a senior pain specialist with strong expertise with injured workers. NES’s research focuses on cancer pain experiences among Latin-American immigrants in Canada, encompassing economic and employment-related consequences of their pain conditions. In addition, three members of the research team have personal experience of immigration. Our diverse backgrounds and specializations provided interdisciplinary and complementary insights into the references, which reinforced our reflexivity during the discussion and decision-making process. Our complementary perspectives and collective approach to decision-making reinforced the rigor and validity of screening decisions by reducing risks of individual selection bias.

Operational definitions: methodological choices regarding pain chronicity

During the screening process, the research team made several methodological choices in order to adequately identify chronic pain in the references. Indeed, surprisingly, none of the retrieved articles used the official definition of chronic pain (pain duration > 3 months). Therefore, the research team collectively made the decision to assess this criterion through measures or reports of pain duration and chronicity available in the abstracts and/or full texts. Studies were included when a duration equivalent to more than 3 months was identifiable through quantitative measures or qualitative reports (e.g., longitudinal studies reporting repeated measures of pain during a period longer than 3 months, studies where participants reported daily pain during a period equivalent to more than 3 months, qualitative interview studies with participants describing long-lasting pain experiences). Studies which explicitly referred to chronic pain (e.g., participants self-reporting chronic pain or self-defining as living with chronic pain) were also included even when information on the exact pain duration was lacking. However, studies that did not refer to chronicity were excluded (e.g., studies measuring pain experienced while performing a specific job duty), as were those which explicitly used a timeframe inferior to 3 months to define chronic pain (e.g., a study defining chronic pain as pain lasting 6 weeks or more). The extreme discrepancy in chronic pain definitions appeared as a notable characteristic of the retrieved articles. Extraction and analysis

Extraction

Evidence from selected articles was extracted using a standardized grid developed in Microsoft Excel. All members of the research team contributed to the extraction process. The following data were extracted: authors, title, year of publication, journal, country of study, study design and methods, immigration status of participants, definition of chronic pain used, industry or occupational context, and findings relevant to the four specific research questions (chronic pain epidemiology, treatment, lived experiences, social and structural determinants). Additional notes were recorded to capture contextual information, critical considerations regarding methods and concepts, or observations not otherwise reflected in the extraction fields. To ensure consistency and completeness, all extracted data were subsequently reviewed by the principal investigator.

Descriptive quantitative data

We used descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) to summarize key characteristics of the selected references in terms of countries, year of publication, methods, immigration status of participants, industries or occupational contexts, and definitions of chronic pain. The extent to which each study addressed the four review questions was also quantified to characterize the distribution of evidence across thematic areas.

Narrative synthesis

We developed a narrative synthesis summarizing relevant findings identified in the included studies. The synthesis was structured according to the four research questions and focused on identifying patterns, convergences, divergences, and gaps in the literature. The analytic framework of intersectionality was used to interpret and discuss the findings. During the narrative synthesis of social and structural determinants, we paid particular attention to the extent to which studies referred to broader systems of power and policy-level contexts. Furthermore, our intersectionality-informed interpretive approach enabled us to discuss how different social and structural determinants (e.g., migration status, gender, etc.) can interact to shape the chronic pain outcomes and experiences reported across the studies. The narrative synthesis was led by the principal investigator, drawing on the extracted data contributed by all coauthors, and was iteratively refined through team revisions.

Narrative synthesis

Chronic pain epidemiology

The most commonly studied epidemiological outcome was chronic pain prevalence. However, the variables used to measure it varied significantly across articles, depending on the authors’ primary interests and objectives. Pain intensity was also examined by two articles.51,52

In Kearney et al.’s study49, 52.4% of migrant tree-trimmers reported everyday work-related pain in the last 12 months. In Mirpuri et al.’s study,46 the prevalence of self-reported chronic pain in a sample of migrant taxi drivers was 24% in New York City (US) and 39% in Toronto (Canada). Arcury et al.’s longitudinal study among migrant manual workers50 investigated the prevalence of three painful occupational health conditions: epicondylitis, rotator cuff syndrome, and low back pain. In their sample, nine participants received an epicondylitis diagnosis at baseline (4% of the total sample). Among these, three participants (33%) still presented this condition at the 1-year follow-up and were considered chronic cases. Thirty participants were diagnosed with rotator cuff syndrome at baseline (12% of the total sample), and ten of them (33%) still chronically presented this condition after 1 year. Regarding low back pain, 43 participants (17% of the sample) were diagnosed at baseline and 10 of them (23%) were considered chronic at the 1-year follow-up. This suggests that although these conditions were relatively rare in the study sample, an important proportion of them (one in four to one in three) became chronic.

Panikkar and Barrett54 indicated that 77% of the dairy farmworkers in their study reported musculoskeletal conditions, primarily back or neck pain resulting from carrying heavy loads or performing repetitive movements. Although the authors did not report any quantitative data on pain duration, their qualitative interviews indicated that workers could endure this pain “for years.”54 In Arcury et al.’s cross-sectional survey among migrant farmworkers51, 40% of participants reported severe musculoskeletal discomfort during the current agricultural season. Although measured, the frequency and duration of pain were not reported in this article. Based on the same sample of migrant farmworkers, Sandberg et al.52 provided data on pain intensity and frequency during the agricultural season. Mean musculoskeletal pain intensity was rated 1.35/4 (±2.09). The authors noted that 5% of participants reported weekly or daily pain that was described as moderate to unbearable in at least one body part during the entire season. In their sample, women were more likely than men to report high-intensity pain.

Chronic pain prevention and treatment

The authors’ investigations of chronic pain treatment focused on chronic pain prevention,50 workplace interventions to reduce pain,53 and self-management of pain.48

Two studies focused on improving occupational conditions to prevent or reduce chronic pain. Arcury et al.50 provided several occupational health recommendations to prevent chronic musculoskeletal problems among migrant manual workers. These included implementing ergonomic changes in workplaces, as well as ensuring that workers can take frequent breaks. The authors recommended that these changes be implemented at a large scale through policies and workplace regulations. In the same vein, Wang et al.53 tested the effects of an ergonomy-based intervention (i.e., adjustments in chairs, tables, etc.) aimed at reducing pain over 4 months for migrant sewing-machine operators. The authors reported a reduction in pain intensity during the first month and a small increase during the following months of the intervention. Reduced pain intensity was associated with occupational factors such as longer rest periods and lower perceived physical workload.

In their qualitative study, Ramos et al.48 focused on migrant workers’ perspectives on chronic pain management. They described various self-management strategies used by migrant meatpacking workers to cope with their musculoskeletal pain, including massage, heat/ice application, topical ointments, and over-the-counter medications like acetaminophen or ibuprofen. Some participants noted that these techniques were not always helpful to relieve pain in a context where they needed to continue performing painful tasks at work. Furthermore, many participants felt powerless regarding the possibility of preventing work-related injuries and chronic pain. For example, one participant stated, “I don’t think there is any way for us to take care of ourselves,” and another believed that “At the end we are all going to end up getting hurt.”48 In such contexts, pain management appeared to become less about relief and more about endurance.

The results reported in these three studies outline that preventing and managing chronic pain requires appropriate working conditions that need to be ensured by employers and workplace policies,50 and on which migrant workers themselves often have low control.48

Lived experiences of chronic pain

Two qualitative studies analyzed lived experiences of chronic pain among migrant workers.

Ramos et al.’s48 contribution to this theme was strictly focused on workplace experiences. These authors noted that many of their participants reported chronic pain conditions caused by their duties at work (e.g., arthritis, bursitis, tendonitis, neuropathic pain). Their qualitative analyses highlighted the powerlessness of migrant workers in preventing health issues, as well as their mistrust regarding the ability of their employers to provide help with injuries and pain. The authors also outlined migrant meatpacking workers’ frustration regarding not being taken seriously when seeking help at the workplace. These experiences are tightly linked with social determinants of health and power dynamics with employers, as we develop in the next subsection of the results.

Chavez and Altman’s study47 took some distance from workplace situations to explore the lived experiences of Mexican migrant roofers facing long-term consequences of work injuries, years after returning to their country of origin. They noted that an important proportion of their participants were healthy when they came to work in the US, and returned to Mexico with injuries causing chronic pain. For example, some participants still experienced difficulties walking due to their pain several years after leaving the US. The authors noted that chronicity also materialized through scars that participants used as symbols to demonstrate that they survived dangerous working conditions, considering themselves as “war veterans.” Furthermore, this study described the persisting psychological trauma resulting from witnessing colleagues die or suffer severe injuries at work. This article is the only one in our sample that draws attention to the long-term life trajectories of migrant workers, rather than considering solely their workplace situations. The authors highlighted that being a migrant worker can lead to pain issues persisting long after the job in the host country came to an end.

Social and structural determinants influencing chronic pain

Social determinants

Inadequate working conditions were the most frequently examined social determinant that may put migrant workers at risk for chronic pain. These poor working conditions refer to the characteristics of the job that make migrant workers’ daily duties particularly unsafe. Ramos et al. emphasized that it is insufficient to focus solely on workers’ respect of safety measures and use of personal protective equipment.48 In fact, working conditions encompass multiple realities which mostly belong to the employer’s responsibility, such as long hours,46,53,54 insufficient break time or days off,50,53,54 uncomfortable postures,50,53,54 presence of hazards in the workplace,49,54 insufficent health and safety measures,49,51,54 heavy workload and/or stressful tasks,51,53,54 absence of duty adaptations when injuries occur,48 high expectations of productivity from employers,48,53 and workplace isolation from health services with few options for transportation.49,54 Inadequate housing conditions offered to migrant workers by their employers were also mentioned in several studies,47,54 although authors did not directly relate them to chronic pain. In addition, strong power imbalance with employers and job precarity appeared to increase risks of complications, as many workers avoided disclosing pain, seeking help, or complaining about insufficient workplace safety, due to threats of job termination.48,49,54

In addition, Kearney et al. highlighted the impacts of migrant workers’ precarious socioeconomic conditions, with participants unable to take time off for economic reasons, and lacking money to use health care services.49 Several articles outlined that migrant workers are often offered low-wage jobs without health insurance benefits, which renders access to health care more challenging.46,54

Structural determinants

The selected studies described several structural determinants influencing the onset, experience, and treatment of chronic pain among migrant workers. These include labor market structure, immigration programs and policies, racism and discrimination, as well as gender norms and inequities.

Several authors mentioned subcontracting as a core element of labor market structure that exposed migrant workers to precarious and unsafe working conditions, with increased risks of developing chronic pain.47,49 In fact, subcontracting increased migrant workers’ structural vulnerability by diluting the employer’s responsibility regarding workplace safety into a chain of intermediary companies.47,49 Labor market structure was described as particularly unfavorable to women and undocumented migrant workers, who had less job opportunities and were forced to accept jobs posing more risks, or keep working while in pain.47,48 Due to the unfavorable labor market structure, many migrant workers were relegated to non-unionized jobs with few employment rights to protect them against exploitation and dangerous working conditions.49,54 In the case of agricultural migrant workers, Arcury et al. outlined the insufficient labor policies and norms in this particular industry, exposing migrant farmworkers to heightened health risks with less legal protections.51

Precarious immigration statuses and racial inequities were analyzed in several articles. Some studies showed that immigration policies reinforced power imbalances between migrant workers and their employers, forcing migrants to continue working despite their pain problems, as they would no longer be allowed to stay in the host country if they lost their job.48,49 Undocumented migrant workers may also avoid using health care services due to risks of racial profiling and deportation.49,54 Furthermore, in Mirpuri et al.’s study, experiences of racism and immigration-related discrimination were significantly associated with the presence of chronic pain for migrant taxi drivers.46 Other studies noted that experiences of racism could lead to avoidance of healthcare services54 and mistrust toward workplace health resources.48 Moreover, language barriers could result in migrant workers being unable to get information about their rights in the workplace, and to use legal resources to obtain compensation in case of long-term disability.48,54

Finally, gender and traditional masculinity norms were outlined by Chavez and Altman, as they encouraged migrant roofers to take more risks at work, forgo safety measures, and minimize the intensity and impacts of pain.47These authors described the links between migration, high-risk job, and masculinity among their study participants, with labor migration considered as a rite of passage for young men.47 Other articles highlighted structural inequities experienced by women.48,52 Women had less available job opportunities, which made them more vulnerable to precarious and unsafe working conditions.48 Furthermore, female migrant farmworkers seemed more likely than men to experience high-intensity pain.52

Discussion

In a context of limited available literature, this scoping review offered a preliminary overview of the current state of knowledge on chronic pain among migrant workers in Canada and the US in terms of epidemiology, treatment and prevention, lived experiences, as well as social and structural determinants. Our work highlighted significant gaps and areas that warrant further investigation. The low number of studies included in this review (n = 9, drawing on eight participant samples), as well as their geographic concentration in a small number of US regions and states (e.g., North Carolina), attest to a significant shortcoming in current research literature, especially in the Canadian context, which was examined in only one retrieved article. Our results draw attention on two main issues: (1) a major research gap in investigating pain chronicity when it comes to migrant workers and (2) the importance of addressing intersecting structural and political determinants that may render migrant workers particularly vulnerable to chronic pain. Beyond documenting gaps, our findings suggest the need for a reconceptualization of how chronic pain is defined, measured, interpreted, and dealt with in the context of precarious labor migration.

The blind spot of pain chronicity in the context of temporary labor migration

One important observation stemming from this review is the low number of studies meeting the inclusion criterion of pain chronicity (duration > 3 months, or equivalent). In fact, many studies were excluded because they did not contain any information about pain duration, as most of them focused solely on painful symptoms experienced at work. This suggests a blind spot in existing research, where the health of migrant workers seems to be considered primarily in reference to their ability to work in the host country during a limited period of time. We found only one study that explored the evolution of migrant workers’ pain on the long term and its impacts on their life trajectories beyond the workplace.47 Chronic pain among migrant workers appears to be implicitly treated as a temporary impairment relevant only insofar as it affects immediate productivity, rather than as a long-term health condition with lasting personal, social, and economic consequences.

The blind spot of chronicity is salient if we consider the rare statistical data on chronic pain epidemiology in this population. Several studies that were excluded from this review provided data on present pain55 or pain experienced in the previous week or month,56,57 without considering longer-term experiences. One excluded study58 defined chronic pain as pain lasting 6 weeks or longer, which illustrates the dominant short-term focus of existing research with migrant workers. In the same vein, one included study assessed pain experienced during the current agricultural season,51,52 limiting its scope to occupational health and short-term outcomes.

As a result, epidemiological estimates of chronic pain among migrant workers remain fragmented and should be interpreted with caution. In the included studies, the prevalence of chronic pain among migrant workers varied between 23% and 52.4%. However, meaningful comparison between studies and with general population estimates is precluded by inconsistencies in the ways chronic pain was defined and assessed. The wide variability in prevalence estimates observed across studies may reflect not only differences in populations and industries but also inconsistent definitions of chronic pain and heterogeneous measurement approaches. This heterogeneity limits the usefulness of existing data for long-term pain surveillance and public health planning.

Moreover, only one article examined pain intensity, reporting relatively high rates, especially among women farmworkers.52 None of the included studies provided epidemiological data on pain interference on daily activities, despite this indicator being an important predictor of physical function,59,60 mental health outcomes,61 and mortality risks62 associated with chronic pain. In order to shift away from a short-term and workplace-centered focus, it appears essential to develop further research investigating chronic pain epidemiology among migrant workers using clearer conceptualization (e.g., the official IASP definition of chronic pain) and proven measurement tools (e.g., the Brief Pain Inventory63) to adequately estimate chronic pain prevalence, severity, and interference in this population beyond the workplace.

Studies examining the prevention and treatment of chronic pain among migrant workers were also largely focused on workplace settings, with occupational health research representing the dominant approach. This narrow focus led researchers to recommend mostly workplace prevention and pain management measures (e.g., ergonomic changes). While such measures may provide short-term relief, they may be partly disconnected from the potential long-term consequences of workplace injuries and the complex daily health care needs of migrant workers living with chronic pain. The focus on occupational health may result in placing the analysis in a perpetual present, which hinders consideration of the chronic nature of pain and of how it is embedded in the life courses of migrant workers.

Chavez and Altman’s qualitative study encourages an understanding of chronic pain experienced by migrant workers that goes beyond the context of workplace exposure. This study is the only one in our sample which investigated migrant workers’ lived experiences with chronic pain after returning to their countries of origin.47 The authors highlighted the persisting physical, psychological, and social consequences of chronic pain issues developed by their participants while working in the US. They demonstrated how chronic pain extends beyond employment periods and host countries, shaping long-term life trajectories after return migration.

Chavez and Altman’s findings also bring attention to the fact that chronic pain conditions developed in host countries may ultimately fall under the responsibility of healthcare systems in migrants’ home countries. Return or repatriation of migrant workers to their country of origin for medical reasons appears to be frequent in both the US and Canada,64–66 which may likely carry an additional economic and social burden to countries of the Global South. It is plausible that pain chronicity remains a blind spot in current research precisely because it is largely invisible to the health systems and societies of the countries conducting this research. Western countries such as the US and Canada are often not the ones managing the long-term pain issues subsequent to migrants’ work experiences on their territory. This underscores the need for Canadian and US pain research to attend to transnational trajectories, return migration with disability, and the long-term social and psychological impacts of occupational pain on migrant workers, dimensions that appear mostly absent from the current literature.

Addressing the intersecting structural and political drivers of migrant workers’ chronic pain

Importantly, all the nine articles in this review discussed the influence of one or more social or structural determinants in migrant workers’ chronic pain outcomes and experiences. In line with the existing body of scientific evidence documenting the social dimensions of chronic pain and related inequities,35,36,67–73 our findings suggest that chronic pain among migrant workers is a socially and structurally produced condition. Using an intersectionality-informed lens on the retrieved evidence enables us to posit that several intersecting structural determinants may often play a central role in the onset and chronization of pain among migrant workers, including labor market structure,47,49,51,54 immigration policies,48,49,54 structural racism,46,48,54 and gendered inequities.47,48,52 These structural determinants may lead to combined effects and should not be considered in isolation, as it is essential to adopt an intersectional lens in order to properly understand how several interrelated systems of oppression influence migrant workers’ chronic pain experiences.29,35 Indeed, intertwined structural factors may concur to reinforce power imbalances with employers, constrain pain disclosure and management, and delay access to care, therefore potentially increasing risks of pain chronicity.

As stated in the results, inadequate working conditions, a social determinant of health, were the primary focus of most studies adopting an occupational health approach. Several studies recommended the implementation of workplace-related changes to reduce chronic pain risks.50,53 One study highlighted the relative inefficacy of individual coping strategies, such as self-management techniques, in preventing and relieving chronic pain in contexts of job precarity where workers have little control over work pace and task allocation.48 This reveals a potential mismatch between the locus of responsibility implied by individual-focused measures and the structural inequities faced by many migrant workers in low-wage, precarious jobs, and manual work settings. Indeed, to better understand the social grounding of migrant workers’ chronic pain issues, it may be relevant to contextualize workplace-related factors in the broader political contexts and structural power dynamics that made them possible, especially in countries where rules and laws are supposed to equally protect workers’ rights. For example, the omnipresence of subcontracting in many industries was highlighted in several articles as an important element that rendered migrant workers more vulnerable to exploitation and unsafe working conditions.47,49 Some other research already highlighted that subcontracting can lead to increased health risks in the general population.74 Subcontracting and non-unionized labor arrangements may further dilute accountability for pain prevention and management, allowing hazardous conditions to persist without clear responsibility. In this sense, chronic pain among migrant workers might be understood as a predictable outcome of structural inequities. In Chavez and Altman’s findings, the metaphor of migrant workers as “war veterans”47 powerfully reframes chronic pain as the embodied consequence of structural violence, rather than as an unfortunate occupational outcome. These findings encourage a shift away from framing migrant workers’ chronic pain as a failure of individual self-care or a result of some specific employers’ misconduct, and toward understanding it as a consequence of inequitable labor and migration systems. Framing chronic pain as a manifestation of social injustice aligns with growing literature on structural violence, health inequities, and pain as a human rights issue.35,36,75–77

Restrictive immigration policies appear central to understand migrant workers’ chronic pain experiences as they define the conditions these workers have to fulfil in order to legally stay and work in the host countries. This review suggests that migrant workers’ precarious statuses contribute to structurally weaken their situation, exposing them to health risks, low access to care, and lack of consideration of their rights (e.g., for disability compensation). In the US, the increasing emphasis on repression, violence, and racial profiling in recent policy approaches geared toward undocumented migrants appears highly concerning in terms of structural injustice and vulnerability of migrant workers. In Canada, close work permits (i.e., work permits allowing the person to work for one specific employer only) are regularly called out by advocacy groups, community organizations, and researchers, as they reinforce migrant workers’ structural vulnerability and dependency toward their employer.19,78,79 This has recently led the special rapporteur of the United Nations to alert about risks of “modern slavery” for migrant workers in Canada.80 Amnesty International also described the history of temporary migration programs as rooted in “the legacy of slavery” and racialized policies.81 This illustrates how much immigration policies and working conditions appear tightly intertwined with historically constructed oppressions such as structural racism.

Our intersectionality framework provided a useful lens through which to interpret our findings in terms of intermingling gendered and racialized dimensions of chronic pain among migrant workers. Although several studies mentioned more than one of these structural determinants, their intersecting effects were rarely discussed. Our work informed by intersectionality suggests that gendered and racialized inequities may structure labor market and migration realities. Experiences of racism and discrimination can further exacerbate the burden of chronic pain by limiting access to care, fostering mistrust toward health and workplace institutions, and reinforcing the concealment of pain from employers and compensation boards.48,54 One study described how masculinity norms, including men’s traditional role as income providers, encouraged risk-taking behaviors and valued resistance to pain, potentially further limiting help-seeking opportunities.47 This may leave male migrant workers particularly likely to experience dangerous working conditions leading to severe long-term pain issues.47 Several studies reported that women faced occupational segregation exposing them to higher pain intensity, and had fewer options on the job market, which often led them to low-wage, undeclared, and unsafe jobs.48,52,82 Other research showed that women also tend to have more precarious immigration statuses than men.83 These intertwined dynamics call for more intersectional approaches to research, health care, and policymaking that account for gendered inequities, racism, migration and labor policies altogether.

Implications for research, policy, and practice

This review suggests the importance of moving beyond occupational health research and developing studies of chronic pain among migrant workers in different disciplines such as epidemiology, social sciences, or health services and policy research. Chronic pain should also be investigated in migrant worker populations with diverse backgrounds, job types, and statuses, and in sectors with a predominantly female workforce. In addition, it appears urgent to further develop knowledge outside the US, particularly in the Canadian context, given that research conducted in the US may not be transferable to Canada. Developing further research in Canada would be helpful to provide insights into the situations of migrant workers with chronic pain in a political and social environment that differs from that of the US. An examination of gray literature, which was excluded from this review given its focus on exploring academic knowledge, may also be a useful next step in further delineating future priorities for research and action. To broaden the research scope, it also appears critical to involve migrant workers living with chronic pain in the definition of research priorities, designs, and objectives. Using participatory and collaborative approaches could help to address blind spots and conduct research that is better aligned with migrant workers’ realities, priorities, and social experiences of chronic pain across their life course.

Moreover, our work suggests that in order to develop and implement evidence-based health policies and programs helping migrant workers with chronic pain, it is crucial to gather more robust quantitative and qualitative data on this issue. As long as chronic pain will be inaccurately measured, documented, and understood in this population, it may not be considered as a public health issue to be addressed by tailored policies and programs in the host countries. Furthermore, to inform equity-oriented policies and practices, developing future empirical studies using an intersectionality approach84 will be essential to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how intertwined social and structural determinants can differentially affect migrant workers’ experiences of chronic pain. To help tackle structural determinants of chronic pain among migrant workers, it also appears crucial to ensure that labor norms and policies are effective to protect their rights and are correctly enforced by employers. Immigration policies and programs geared at reducing migrant workers’ structural dependency toward employers could help to reduce imbalanced power dynamics leading to exploitation and long-term health consequences such as chronic pain.

Finally, this review is intended to help raising health and social service practitioners’ awareness of the importance of assessing and treating chronic pain among migrant workers, and of considering the social and structural determinants that influence their health condition and daily living experiences in the host countries. In this intent, educating pain care practitioners in structural competency,85 an approach aimed at equipping providers to attend to the structural inequities that may influence migrant patients’ health behaviors, as well as extending their knowledge of immigration-related barriers,86 may be promising avenues in order to support them in offering tailored responses.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review filled an important research gap by exploring the state of knowledge on chronic pain among migrant workers in the US and Canada. Our analysis highlighted the risks of chronic pain often carried by the legal status and daily living conditions of migrant workers. In doing so, we brought into the foreground the intersecting social and structural determinants that influence migrant workers’ pain experiences. However, like any study, our work has some limitations. First, the literature search was limited to the contexts of the US and Canada, which did not allow us to encompass different situations of migrant workers, especially those who migrate to countries of the Global South and follow migration dynamics that are very different from South-to-North migration. Second, the heterogeneity in definitions of chronic pain across studies led us to refine the operationalization of the related eligibility criterion during the selection process, consistent with the iterative and exploratory nature of scoping reviews. Although the review was originally guided by an a priori protocol with a standard definition of chronic pain, it was not possible to follow this protocol strictly due to the variability of the literature. As a result, some studies may have been included or excluded differently had alternative operational definitions been adopted. The definitional variability of chronic pain also limited the comparability of findings across the included studies. Finally, as this is not a systematic review, our approach did not include a methodological appraisal. However, although the quality of the data was not systematically evaluated, we adopted a critical approach allowing to highlight gaps and weaknesses in the literature, especially the insufficient conceptualization and analysis of pain chronicity, and the overemphasis on workplace factors rather than structural determinants.

Conclusion

This scoping review highlighted that there is still a long way to go to fully understand the impacts of chronic pain on the lives of migrant workers. Future research efforts should be geared at moving away from a short-term occupational health focus to broader approaches allowing a meaningful investigation pain chronicity among migrant workers and its social dimensions. It also appears essential to address the intersecting social and structural determinants of migrant workers’ chronic pain through equity-oriented health, labor, and immigration policies.
