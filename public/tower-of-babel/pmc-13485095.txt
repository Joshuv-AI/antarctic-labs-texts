Increased neuro-ophthalmic manifestations of herpes zoster infection incidence: A 14-year cohort study from a tertiary medical center
Authors: Ella Nissan, Eden Abergel, Iris Ben Bassat Mizrachi, Ruth Huna-Baron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:This study aimed to evaluate the spectrum and incidence of neuro-ophthalmic complications of herpes zoster (HZ) over 14 years at a large tertiary medical center.Design:Retrospective cohort study.Methods:The study included patients with a confirmed diagnosis of HZ who presented to the neuro-ophthalmology clinic with cranial nerve 3/4/6 involvement, optic disc edema, abnormal cerebrospinal fluid, or imaging findings suggestive of meningoencephalitis. Demographic, clinical, imaging, and outcome data were manually collected from electronic medical records. For comparative analysis, data were aggregated into two periods: 2006–2012 and 2013–2020.Results:Seventeen patients with neuro-ophthalmic complications were identified. The mean age at presentation was 67 years, and 76% were male. Autoimmune disease was present in 35% of patients, malignant disease in 29%, and 24% were receiving immunosuppressive therapy at HZ onset. Only one patient had documented prior Varicella zoster vaccination. Neuro-ophthalmic manifestations included visual field defect (53%), diplopia (53%), ptosis (47%), and post-herpetic neuralgia (41%). At the end of follow-up, 18% achieved complete recovery, 65% partial improvement, and 12% showed no improvement. The vast majority of patients (82%) presented in 2013–2020, with the incidence increasing from 0.1% of new clinic patients in 2006–2012 to 0.3% in 2013–2020 (P < 0.05).Conclusion:Neuro-ophthalmic manifestations of HZ appear to be increasing, predominantly affect older males, and frequently result in incomplete recovery. Limited neuroimaging findings highlight the primacy of clinical assessment. Larger prospective studies are needed to clarify risk factors and improve management.

Herpes zoster (HZ) is a common infectious condition caused by the reactivation of varicella-zoster virus (VZV). The ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (V1) involved in HZ, commonly referred to as herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO), accounts for 4–20% of the cases. Common ocular manifestations of HZO include conjunctivitis, keratitis, iritis, and uveitis.[12]

Less commonly, a few patients present with neuro-ophthalmic manifestations of HZ, such as optic neuropathy, acute retinal necrosis or progressive outer retinal necrosis, orbitopathy, cranial neuropathy (isolated or multiple), and central nervous system (CNS) manifestations.[34] While several theories have been raised, the underlying mechanism of these complications is still unclear.

In accordance with its scarcity, only a few case reports and case series describe the clinical sequelae of neuro-ophthalmic manifestations in patients with HZ.[56] These neuro-ophthalmic complications, though less frequent than anterior segment involvement in HZO, represent a spectrum of potentially severe conditions that can lead to significant and sometimes permanent visual impairment, debilitating pain such as post-herpetic neuralgia affecting ocular or periocular structures, and disabling cranial nerve palsies. A comprehensive understanding of these manifestations, including their typical presentations, associated risk factors, and prognostic indicators, is crucial for early recognition, appropriate therapeutic intervention, and effective patient counseling. However, it remains incompletely defined in larger patient series.

Additionally, as the incidence of HZ has increased over the last few decades,[78] and some reports indicate an increased incidence of HZO,[9] the literature lacks data regarding the trend of neuro-ophthalmic manifestations of HZ.

We aimed to assess the neuro-ophthalmic manifestations of patients in a 14-year cohort at a large tertiary medical center.

Methods

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRB) under approval number: 7858-20.

Data were retrospectively collected from the electronic medical records (EMRs) of the neuro-ophthalmic unit in a large tertiary medical center. All patients diagnosed with HZ between 2006 and 2020 visited the hospital’s neuro-ophthalmic clinic and had one of the following: 1. Cranial nerve (CN) 3, 4, or 6 disorder, or a combination of them; 2. Optic disc edema; 3. Evidence of abnormal spinal fluid; 4. Clinical or imaging signs of meningoencephalitis. Patients without a clear diagnosis of HZ were excluded. Neuro-ophthalmic manifestations defined as any neurological complication involving optic neuropathy, post-herpetic ophthalmic neuralgia, visual and nonvisual cranial neuropathy and orbitopathy, and other central nervous system (CNS)-related manifestations.

Data on patients’ demography (at first visit), year of diagnosis, disease sequelae, symptomatology, comorbidities, laboratory results, imaging data, treatment, and outcome extracted from the patients’ EMRs.

The outcome was assessed at the last follow-up visit. The outcome was defined as one of the following: “Without improvement” – no change of the neuro-ophthalmological symptoms and findings at the last follow-up; “Complete recovery” – all the neuro-ophthalmological manifestation resolved at the last follow-up; “Partial improvement” – partial resolution of neuro-ophthalmological manifestations at the last follow-up; “Unknown” – the patient does not have an available follow-up. Imaging results were collected from the neuro-radiology report, and only relevant findings were noted—any intraorbital or cranial nerve-related (e.g., cavernous sinus, orbital apex, superior orbital fissure, brain stem) lesion.

Due to the relatively low number of patients with neuro-ophthalmic manifestations, we aggregated data from two separate timeframes: 2006–2012 and 2013–2020, for comparative analysis of earlier versus later periods. Data on the total number of patients seen each year in the clinic were collected. Microsoft Excel 2016 was used for data analysis, and the tabulated results were organized in structured tables. Chi-squared test was used to compare the frequency in the two timeframes.

Results

We identified 17 patients with neuro-ophthalmological complications from 2006 through 2020 in our neuro-ophthalmic clinic. The mean age at presentation was 67 years (range 30–80 years). Thirteen (76%) of the patients were men. The cohort’s median follow-up time was 1.7 years (IQR, 1.3–4.5 years). Six (35%) patients had an autoimmune disease, including three patients with hypothyroidism. Five (29%) patients had a background or a current malignancy, and four (24%) were treated with immunosuppressive medications during the HZ onset. Only one patient had a documented Varicella zoster vaccination previously to the herpetic active disease [Table 1].

Fifteen (88%) patients had available neuro-imaging reports, 14 (93%) patients underwent computerized tomography (CT) scans, and 12 (71%) patients underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans. Most CT scans (%) have not shown any relevant findings, and the most common findings were bilateral optic complex enlargement (2/16, 12.5%) and ipsilateral recuts muscle enlargement (2/16, 12.5%). Five (5/12, 42%) MRI scans have not demonstrated any relevant findings, and the most common findings were orbital fat infiltration (2/12, 17%) and ipsilateral rectus muscle enhancement (2/12, 17%) [Table 2].

Eight patients (47%) were treated with intravenous acyclovir. Three patients (18%) were free of neuro-ophthalmological manifestations at the end of the follow-up, 11 (65%) had partial improvement, 2 (12%) had no improvement at all, and one patient did not have any follow-up visit [Table 2].

The vast majority of the patients in our cohort presented during 2013–2020, while the total number of new patients during this period has not increased in the same proportion. In 2006–2012, only three patients presented with neuro-ophthalmic complications of HZ out of 2957 new patients in the neuro-ophthalmic clinic (0.1% of the new patients). In the period of 2013–2020, a significantly higher number of patients with neuro-ophthalmic complications of HZ were admitted to the neuro-ophthalmic clinic, 14 out of 4232 patients (0.3% of the new patients), P < 0.047. All patients in the 2006–2012 period were males; their mean age at diagnosis was 56 years, while 72% of the patients in 2013–2020 were males, and the mean age was 66 years.

Discussion

This study describes a 14-year cohort of patients with neuro-ophthalmological manifestations of HZO from a large tertiary medical center. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest cohort study describing the course of patients with neuro-ophthalmological manifestations of HZ.

We identified an increased incidence of these cases in our clinic, which might be associated with similar reports of increasing incidence of HZO.[78910] Moreover, 2020 was unusual, with the highest number of annual cases, with almost 1% of all new cases in the clinic admitted due to neuro-ophthalmological manifestation of HZ, far more than any previous year. This exceptional number of cases in this specific year may be attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic. This global event possibly led to stress among patients, a known risk factor of HZ, alongside COVID-19 infection itself.[1112] However, even after excluding this abnormal year, the last 4 years before 2020 (2016–2019) had more than doubled the number of cases than all the years since 2006 (8 cases vs. 3 cases, respectively).

While one study demonstrated no change in the mean age of HZ incidence,[7] two studies found a decline in the age of presentation of HZO during the last years, which was speculated to be secondary to the introduction of the varicella vaccine.[910] However, the current study indicates an increased mean age of presentation between the two defined periods. Additionally, most patients in this cohort were males (76%), while the literature presents higher rates of females among HZ and HZO cases.[1013] However, this current study’s findings match other studies that demonstrated male predominance among patients with CNS manifestations of HZ.[141516] These age and gender differences between HZ, HZO, and HZ neuro-ophthalmic manifestations should be evaluated in further study.

The presence of underlying comorbidities and immunosuppression in a significant portion of our cohort underscores the well-established role of compromised immunity in VZV reactivation and the development of more severe HZ complications.[13] The fact that only one patient in our cohort had a documented prior VZV vaccination is also noteworthy. Since the recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV, Shingrix) was available during the study time, the cohort’s low vaccination rate might indicate evidence for possible high effectiveness of the vaccine, which reflected in disease incidence mainly among nonvaccinated patients, or rather low rate of vaccination in the population, and potential missed opportunity for prevention.

This study is also the first to present the time interval between the first symptom of HZ and the onset of the first neuro-ophthalmic manifestation in a cohort study. We found a median latency of 10 days from the initial HZ symptom to the first neuro-ophthalmic complication, but this period was highly variable, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 0–31 days. The full range observed in our cohort extended from concurrent onset (0 days) to as late as 90 days after the first HZ symptoms appeared. This wide variability is clinically significant and indicates that neuro-ophthalmic involvement can be an immediate presenting feature of HZ, but it can also manifest as a delayed sequela weeks to months after the initial infection.

Optic nerve-related symptoms and imaging findings were present in 10 (59%) patients. They included optic disc edema, vision loss, RAPD, visual field defects, dyschromatopsia, along with CT findings of optic complex enlargement, increased fluid around the optic nerve, and bilateral optic nerve sheath enlargement, and MRI findings of bilateral optic nerve sheath enlargement, and optic nerve enhancement. Optic neuropathy is a recognized, albeit less common, complication of HZ,[3] and the spectrum of findings in our cohort is consistent with previous reports. This study also presents contralateral optic nerve involvement which has been reported previously,[1718] suggesting mechanisms beyond direct contiguous spread of the virus, possibly involving autoimmune responses.

CN3-related symptoms and imaging findings were also present in 10 (59%) patients. They included ptosis, anisocoria, CN3 Palsy, Adie’s tonic pupil, fixed dilated pupil, mydriasis, sluggish pupil response, MRI findings of medial rectus muscle enhancement and edema, and CT findings of medial rectus muscle enlargement. CN6 involvement was less common, and CN4 palsy was the least common cranial nerve involved. These cranial nerve frequencies are similar to previous studies.[1419]

Only 3 (18%) patients in the current study demonstrated complete recovery. This lower rate of complete recovery indicates HZ with neuro-ophthalmological manifestations as a disease that causes long-term disability in the vast majority of patients. Physicians should be aware of this prognosis and deliberate it with patients. The mean age of patients with complete recovery was 55.0 years old, much lower than the mean cohort age, suggesting lower age as a predictor for better outcomes. This finding is strengthened by recent systematic review that indicated lower age as a predictor for complete recovery of ophthalmoplegia.[20]

The relatively few findings in the imaging modalities and the fact that these findings did not predict better outcomes might suggest that imaging might have a less crucial role in evaluating these patients.

This study is subject to several limitations, primarily stemming from its retrospective, single-center design, which may limit the generalizability of findings and introduce the potential for information bias from reliance on existing electronic medical records. Furthermore, the relatively small cohort size of 17 patients, while the largest described for this specific condition, restricts statistical power and the ability to draw robust conclusions regarding risk factors or prognostic indicators. Finally, a contributory role of the underlying malignancy or immunosuppressive state in the five affected patients cannot be definitively excluded in this retrospective setting. Nevertheless, the ipsilateral dermatomal distribution, short temporal course, absence of imaging or clinical evidence of active primary malignancy, and CSF VZV PCR positivity in a subset of patients support HZ as the etiology of the neuro-ophthalmic manifestations. Furthermore, treatments for these conditions typically induce immunosuppression, predisposing to VZV reactivation, rather than causing direct neuro-ophthalmic toxicity.[2122]

Conclusion

This 14-year retrospective study identified an apparent increasing incidence of neuro-ophthalmic manifestations of Herpes Zoster at our tertiary center, predominantly affecting older males and resulting in a diverse range of complications, including optic neuropathy and cranial nerve palsies. Crucially, these manifestations frequently lead to incomplete recovery and long-term disability, underscoring the serious nature of this condition and the need for heightened physician awareness and candid patient counseling regarding prognosis. Notably, neuroimaging often revealed a lack of significant relevant findings or nonspecific changes that did not correlate with outcomes, emphasizing the paramount importance of clinical assessment in these cases. Future prospective, multicenter research with larger cohorts is essential to validate these trends, elucidate risk and prognostic factors, and optimize management strategies.
