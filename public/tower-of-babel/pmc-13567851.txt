Contemporary Comparison between the Hemodialysis Reliable Outflow Device and Femoral Arteriovenous Graft
Authors: Hind Anan, Amaan Rahman, Elonay Yehualashet, Jacob Weineberg, Theodore H. Yuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

Central venous occlusion (CVO) affects 10% of all patients on dialysis and predialysis patients1 and typically leads to arteriovenous (AV) access malfunction.2 The 2019 Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative guidelines consider it reasonable to use a long-term tunneled dialysis catheter (TDC) in uncorrectable occlusions.3 However, given the morbidity associated with TDCs, femoral arteriovenous graft (fAVG) and hemodialysis reliable outflow (HeRO) graft (HeRO, Merit Medical Systems, Inc., UT) can be considered as alternatives when traditional upper extremity AV accesses have been exhausted, and the patient is expected to live more than 1 year. According to the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative and the European Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines, there is no clear preference between fAVG and HeRO graft, although fAVG has been associated with increased complication rates, and many patients express strong preferences in terms of avoiding exposure of the thigh during dialysis treatments. The choice between the two depends on the patient's preferences, characteristics, and the surgeon's expertise.3,4

HeRO graft is a completely subcutaneous access system. Its arterial component consists of an expanded polytetrafluoroethylene graft (6 mm inner diameter) surgically anastomosed to an arterial inflow source. This graft is attached to the venous outflow component by a titanium connector. The outflow component is a silicone tube with braided nitinol (5 mm inner diameter) that traverses through occlusive lesions in the central venous system. It is cut to an appropriate length so that the venous outflow is in the right atrium, which makes it optimal for patients with CVO.5 This system has shown comparable patency, adequacy of dialysis, and complication rates with those of conventional AV grafts.6

In the United States, the HeRO system was approved in 2008 by the Food and Drug Administration for catheter-dependent patients who are not candidates for upper extremity access, with the first HeRO graft implantation performed in May 2008.7 In Europe, the HeRO graft received full Conformité Européenne mark approval in 2013.8

Previously, fAVG was the standard approach for patients with upper extremity CVOs. fAVGs demonstrate similar patency rates to upper extremity grafts.9,10 However, they are associated with high infection rates10 and independently predict increased mortality in patients on dialysis.11 The aim of this study is to provide a contemporary comparison between HeRO graft and fAVG in terms of patency, complications, and mortality.

Methods

Study Design

We performed a retrospective study of consecutive fAVG and HeRO graft placed between January 2016 and July 2023 at the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center, which is a multihospital health care system, with all surgeries performed by board-certified or board-eligible vascular surgeons. Patients were identified using the local Vascular Quality Initiative (VQI) database and supplemented by a review of surgeons' case logs. All patients undergoing either of the two procedures were included. The choice of fAVG or HeRO graft versus traditional upper extremity AV access was at the discretion of the operating surgeon, incorporating patient preferences and surgeon experience. Typically, fAVG and HeRO graft were considered after all conventional upper extremity access avenues were exhausted.

After obtaining approval of the Institutional Board Review of the University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine, medical history, demographics, and operative and postoperative details were obtained using a combination of VQI data and hospital-based electronic medical records. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting observational studies.

Outcomes

Outcomes of interest include primary, assisted primary, and secondary patency as outlined in the Society for Vascular Surgery‑recommended standards for reporting12 and the Clinical Practice Guidelines of the European Society for Vascular Surgery.4 Functional patency was defined as the interval from the time of TDC removal or first use for dialysis until access abandonment. Other outcomes include (1) successful cannulation, defined as reliable cannulation with two needles13; (2) the number of interventions needed before successful cannulation; (3) graft removal because of infection; and (4) all-cause mortality.

Statistical Analyses

Patients' demographics and characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Parametric and nonparametric tests were used to compare the groups where appropriate. For patency and mortality, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was used, adjusting for baseline characteristics that were significantly different on bivariate analysis. To assess the proportional hazards assumption of the Cox regression model, Schoenfeld residual test was used. Graphs were truncated at 36 months, including only data points where the SEM was below 10%. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline Characteristic

During the study period, 114 AV accesses were identified for inclusion in the study: 68 HeRO grafts and 46 fAVG in 105 unique patients. Patients' demographics and comorbidities are summarized in Table 1. Patients who received HeRO grafts were older (66.1 versus 57.7 years) and had higher body mass index (BMI; 31.8 versus 27.9 kg/m2). They were also more likely to have a history of atrial fibrillation (56% versus 20%). On the other hand, patients who received fAVGs had significantly more previous failed AV accesses (mean 2.4 versus 1.4, P < 0.001; Table 1).

Mortality

Thirty-day mortality occurred in two patients (5%) receiving fAVG as opposed to no patients from the HeRO group (P = 0.07). At 12, 24, and 36 months, all-cause mortality was 22%, 43%, and 54% in the HeRO group and 18%, 29%, and 39% in the fAVG group, respectively. This was not statistically significant on Kaplan–Meier univariate analysis (Figure 1). On Cox regression, adjusted for age, BMI, history of atrial fibrillation, and history of previous access, access type was also not a predictor of mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.92; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.44 to 1.96; P = 0.83).

Patency

Patients were followed for a median of 9.7 months (IQR, 2.8–21.6 months). The ability to use the access for HD was higher in the HeRO group (94% versus 83%; P = 0.049). Most of them were suitable for cannulation without intervention, except for seven patients (four HeRO versus three fAVG; P = 0.12).

Kaplan–Meier analyses were performed for primary (Figure 2), primary-assisted (Figure 3), and secondary (Figure 4) patencies. Although fAVG appeared to have higher patency at 12, 24, and 36 months in all three graphs, on an unadjusted basis, the log-rank test did not demonstrate that these findings were statistically different. At 12, 24, and 36 months, the primary patency rates were 35%, 12%, and 5% for the HeRO graft, respectively. For fAVG, primary patency was 37% at 12 months and 17% at 24 and 36 months. Secondary patency at 12, 24, and 36 months was 58%, 45%, and 30% for the HeRO graft and 68%, 54%, and 54% for fAVG, respectively. Cox regression, adjusted for age, BMI, history of atrial fibrillation, and history of previous access, demonstrated that primary patency, primary-assisted, and secondary patencies were similar in both groups with a trend toward fAVG having better patency (aHR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.43 to 1.23; P = 0.23; aHR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.35 to 1.2; P = 0.17; and aHR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.28 to 1.19; P = 0.14; respectively).

Complications and Reinterventions

Six (9%) patients from the HeRO group and 10 (22%) from the fAVG group underwent graft removal because of infection (P = 0.05). According to the Kaplan–Meier analysis, 78% of patients who received fAVG remained free from explantation due to infection at 6 months as compared with 95% in the HeRO group (P = 0.12; Figure 6). No further graft explantations occurred in the fAVG group for the remainder of the study period. By contrast, the HeRO group decreased to 85% infection-free survival at 36 months. On Cox regression, there was a trend toward fAVG having higher risk of infection, but that was NS (aHR, 1.51; 95% CI, 0.39 to 5.79; P = 0.55).

Explanation as a result of graft infection in fAVG versus HeRO graft.

Four (6%) patients from the HeRO group and five from the fAVG group required reintervention for steal syndrome (P = 0.33; Table 3). Two fAVG patients underwent access ligation and two others had leg amputations for steal, compared with none in the HeRO group (P = 0.01).

Both groups had similar reintervention rates per person-year, with incidence rates of 1.06 for HeRO grafts and 0.94 for fAVGs (P = 0.61; Table 3). Most procedures were thrombectomies and angioplasties.

Discussion

For patients who have exhausted AV access options in the upper extremities, guidelines suggest that both lower extremity AV graft and HeRO graft are reasonable options. Our study compares these two types of AV accesses in a large cohort of contemporary patients in terms of mortality, patency, and complications.

Previously, our institution published an early experience with the HeRO grafts in 2013, which reported a primary patency of 11% and secondary patency of 32% at 1-year follow-up.14 The current study demonstrates a markedly improved 1-year rate with 35% and 58% for primary patency and secondary patency, respectively. We believe this is because of the accumulated expertise in identifying patients most likely to benefit from a HeRO graft and overall familiarity with the device.

In the current study, the HeRO group exhibited a higher likelihood of obtaining functional patency compared with the fAVG group. However, primary and secondary patency rates of 5% and 30% at 3 years, respectively, compared to 17% and 54% for the fAVG group were not statistically significant, after adjustment using Cox regression. Steerman et al. reported similar findings, with fAVG showing higher patency rates, albeit not statistically significant, at 6 months.15 In line with this trend, Brownie et al. reported that the fAVG had maintained a significantly better patency rate with primary and secondary rates of 6% and 70% in fAVG compared with 4% and 12% in HeRO graft at 3 years, respectively.16 Similar results were seen in a recent meta-analysis.10 On the other hand, Kudlaty et al. observed a nonsignificant trend toward better patency in the HeRO group at 2 years, with secondary patency rates of 38% for HeRO and 19% for fAVG.17 In addition, the wide variation in reported fAVG patency rates remains unexplained because these studies did not address the underlying causes of patency loss. This variation may be attributed to the heterogeneity of graft types and techniques used in fAVG. Our secondary patency rates for both HeRO and fAVG are consistent with most of the previously published literature.10,18,19

We found no significant difference in long-term mortality between HeRO and fAVGs. This was similar to the outcome reported by Steerman et al.15 In their study, they reported a 33% mortality rate after HeRO graft placement and 20% after fAVG placement at 1 year. In comparison, we found 1-year mortality rates of 22% and 18% in the HeRO and fAVG groups, respectively.

A major concern commonly associated with fAVG is graft infection. In our study, 95% of patients with HeRO grafts remained free from infection at 6 months, compared with 78% in the fAVG group. Although the fAVG group showed a higher infection rate, the difference was not statistically significant. This finding is consistent with the outcomes reported in previous studies.15–17 The reported rate of graft infection in the literature varies significantly,20,21 mainly because of variation in the follow-up period. A VQI-based study reported an infection rate of 10% in fAVG at 6 months, compared with 22% in the present study.20 The lower fAVG infection rate observed in their study could potentially be due to underreporting, given the nature of the large dataset used, which may have lacked granular patient-level data, unlike our study. Furthermore, our infection rates are consistent with those reported in other published meta-analyses.10,18

Similarly, the incidence of steal was comparable between the HeRO and fAVG groups in our study, as well as in other comparative studies.15–17 However, the consequences in the fAVG group were more severe, with four patients requiring leg amputation or access ligation compared with none in the HeRO group.

In addition to HeRO grafts and fAVGs, another potential option for patients requiring vascular access is the transposed femoral vein autogenous AV fistula (superficial femoral artery-transposed femoral vein [SFA-tFV]). However, this approach is not performed in our practice because of the high risk of wound complications associated with the procedure. Landeheim et al. compared SFA-tFV with HeRO grafts and found that although SFA-tFV demonstrated superior patency rates at 1 year (79% versus 30%), it was associated with significantly higher rates of hematoma or seroma formation (37% versus 5%) and delayed wound healing (17% versus 3%).22 Another multicenter study reported a 10% incidence of compartment syndrome and 24% rate of wound complications among patients undergoing vein transposition. Given these clinical concerns, we do not consider this approach a viable option in our practice.23 In terms of limitations, our study is retrospective and nonrandomized. Patients who were selected to undergo HeRO grafts were older and had more comorbidities. Although not specifically studied, many patients prefer upper extremity access because of perceived concerns around lack of dignity associated with exposure of the thigh during thrice-weekly dialysis sessions, adding to possible selection bias in terms of the patients selected for femoral AV graft placement. In the femoral graft group, there was heterogeneity in surgical techniques in terms of choice of geometry and conduit material, and the choice of access type depended on the expertise of the 14 different surgeons who performed the procedures. On the other hand, this study features a relatively large sample size and long follow-up, and it is more recent than other comparative studies, which were conducted soon after HeRO approval.

HeRO and fAVGs provide alternative hemodialysis access options for patients who have exhausted all traditional upper extremity AV accesses because of CVO. In this contemporary series comparing HeRO and fAVG, the likelihood of obtaining functional patency is higher after HeRO, although long-term patency rates were similar. The use of the HeRO graft in the upper extremity is a reasonable option for patients, preserving lower extremity AV access once the upper extremities have been fully exhausted.
