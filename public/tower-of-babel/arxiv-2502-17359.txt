Travel Time Reliability in Stochastic Kinematic Flow Models
Alexander Hammerl, Ravi Seshadri, Thomas Kjær Rasmussen, Otto Anker Nielsen

Abstract
SHORT SUMMARY This paper analyzes the time-dependent relationship between the mean and variance of travel time on a single corridor under rush hour like congestion patterns. To model this phenomenon, we apply the LWR ( Lighthill & Whitham (1955) , Richards (1956) ) theory on a homogenous freeway with a discontinuous bottleneck at its downstream end, assuming a uni-modal demand profile with a stochastic peak. We establish conditions for typical counterclockwise hysteresis loops under these assumptions. It is demonstrated that shapes of the fundamental diagram which always produce a counterclockwise loop can be interpreted as an indication of aggressive driving behavior, while deviations may occur under defensive driving. This classification enables a detailed explanation of the qualitative physical mechanisms behind this pattern, as well as an analysis of the causes for quantitatively limited deviations. Some of the mathematical properties of the LWR model identified in our analysis have not yet been addressed in the literature and we critically examine the extent to which these reflect actual traffic flow behavior. Our considerations are supported by numerical experiments. The obtained results aim to improve the fundamental understanding of the physical causes of this hysteresis pattern and to facilitate its better estimation in traffic planning and control.



Keywords
: traffic flow theory, kinematic waves, hyperbolic conservation laws, hysteresis.

INTRODUCTION

Travel time reliability is a critical aspect of traveler route choice in urban areas, with empirical analyses showing it can be nearly as important to travelers as the expected travel time itself (e.g.
Li et al. (2010)
;
Prato et al. (2014)
;
Seshadri & Srinivasan (2017)
;
Prakash et al. (2018)
). Travel time variability provides
a convenient measure of reliability and can be analyzed in different reference frames: vehicle-to-vehicle, period-to-period and day-to-day. To model day-to-day variability, which is the focus of this article, a linear relationship between mean and variance of travel time is often assumed (e.g.
Kim & Mahmasani (2015)
,
van Lint et al. (2008)
). However, empirical data show an anti-clockwise hysteresis loop between these two quantities, both at the level of individual links (
Fosgerau (2010)
,
Kim & Mahmasani (2015)
,
Yildirimoglu et al. (2015)
) and at a network level (
Bates et al. (2004)
,
Gayah et al. (2015)
). Fosgerau
Fosgerau (2010)
theoretically proves the existence of such loops in a queueing system with decreasing arrival rate. Yildirimoglu et al.
Yildirimoglu et al. (2015)
attribute the hysteresis in the day-to-day travel time variability to stochastic parameters of vehicle travel time, but they do not analyze which aspects of traffic flow cause or influence this effect.

Separate from the hysteresis in travel time variability are other phenomena in traffic research that bear the same name: Edie
Edie (1963)
and Treiterer and Myers
Treiterer & Myers (1974)
define hysteresis as the separation of speed-density curves into an accelerating and a decelerating branch ahead of traffic disturbances. Zhang
Zhang (1999)
and Yeo and Skabradonis
Yeo & Skabardonis (2009)
, among others, offer theoretical explanations for this effect. At network level, clockwise hysteresis loops caused by rush hour congestion have been demonstrated in networks with various topologies (e.g.
Buisson & Ladier (2009)
,
Saberi & Mahmassani (2012)
). This is due to differences in the spatial distribution of vehicles during congestion on- and offset
Geroliminis & Sun (2011)
and the increased network instability during the offset of congestion
Gayah & Daganzo (2011)
.
Gayah et al. (2015)
demonstrates that travel time variability exhibits counterclockwise hysteresis in networks with clockwise MFD hysteresis.

Although MFD hysteresis has been extensively discussed in the literature, hysteresis associated with the mean and variance of travel time (in the context of day-to-day variability) has received relatively little attention. We contribute to this literature by investigating the time-dependent relationship between mean and variance of travel time in a single corridor under rush hour specific traffic dynamics using LWR theory. We analytically derive conditions for the occurrence of anti-clockwise hysteresis loops and provide a rigorous theoretical foundation to explain which traffic flow variables determine the shape and size of the hysteresis loop. The theoretical analysis is supported by empirical data from Interstate 880 in San Francisco.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the homogeneous LWR model, which forms mathematical foundation of our analysis. Section
3
formally proves the relationship between mean and variance of travel time. Section
4
provides numerical experiments using the Cell Transmission Model to validate our theoretical findings. Section
5
presents empirical evidence from Interstate 880 in San Francisco, showing how real-world data supports our theoretical predictions. The paper concludes with Section
6
, which discusses the implications of our findings for traffic flow theory and their practical applications for traffic management.

Model and General Solution

The LWR theory is a macroscopic model that describes traffic flow on highways using the variables
traffic density

k
(the number of vehicles per unit length) and
traffic flow

q
(the number of vehicles passing a point per unit time). It asserts that the rate of change in the total number of vehicles contained in any road segment
[x_{1},x_{2}]
where
x_{2}>x_{1}
and there are no entries or exits, is equal to the net flow of vehicles out of the segment, i.e.

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}k(x,t)\,dx=-\left[q(x,t)\right]_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}},

(1)

If
k
and
q
are differentiable functions, we can, on the left, perform the differentiation under the integral sign and on the right apply the fundamental theorem of calculus to obtain

\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\left\{\frac{\partial k}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}\right\}dx=0.

Since this relation is valid for any arbitrary road segment
[x_{1},x_{2}]
, by letting
x_{2}\to x_{1}
and dividing by the segment’s length, the expression simplifies to the partial differential equation

\frac{\partial k}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}=0.

(2)

In addition to
1
, the LWR theory assumes the existence of a functional relationship between
q
and
k
under differentiable conditions:

q(x,t)=Q(x,k(x,t)),

(3)

where
Q
is a concave, non-negative function that is equal to zero at
k=0
and at the
jam density

k=k_{j}
.
On substituting equation
2
into
3
, we obtain

\frac{\partial k}{\partial t}+\frac{dQ}{dk}\cdot\frac{\partial k}{\partial x}=0,

(4)

which defines a unique solution for
k(x,t)
and
q(x,t)
for given initial and boundary conditions if
q
and
k
are differentiable.
Flow and density are related to the cumulative flow
N(x,t)
as follows:

q(x,t)=\frac{\partial N}{\partial t}(x,t),\quad k(x,t)=-\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}(x,t)

(5)

In cases where
k
has a discontinuity at
(x,t)
, known as a shockwave, the shockwave’s speed
u
is specified as:

u=\frac{[q]}{[k]}=\frac{q_{2}-q_{1}}{k_{2}-k_{1}}.

(6)

It should be emphasized that customary solutions of the LWR model do
not
solve the system of equations (
1
) and (
3
). Instead, they solve equation (
4
), which assumes the fundamental diagram holds only where traffic variables are differentiable in
x
and
t
(not at shocks). For solutions at discontinuities, additional entropy conditions may be required to ensure uniqueness. For details on entropy solutions in traffic flow models, see for example
Lebacque (1996)
,
Ansorge (1990)
,
Jin et al. (2009)
.

However, entropy solutions are not necessary for our analysis. We study a homogeneous version of the LWR model where the fundamental diagram remains constant throughout the spatial domain, except at the downstream bottleneck:

q(k(x,t),x)=q(k(x,t))\quad\text{for all }x\in[0,l).

(7)

Moreover, our analysis considers only continuous boundary conditions. In this setting, every point in spacetime is intersected by at least one kinematic wave, allowing us to uniquely determine
N(x,t)

Newell (1993)
;
Newell (1993)
;
Daganzo (2005)
. When a solution exists for
q(x,t)
and
k(x,t)
, it is also unique.

For the purposes of our analysis, we need only assume that equation (
4
) holds where
k
and
q
are differentiable, and that equation (
1
) holds at all points. This framework is sufficient to derive all results presented in this paper.

The third variable, the
average speed
, is defined as
v=\frac{q}{k}
. We model aggressive driving by assuming
v^{\prime\prime}(k)\geq 0
, while defensive driving is characterized by
v^{\prime\prime}(k)\leq 0
. This implies that defensive drivers reduce their speed following a density increase even in light traffic, whereas aggressive drivers maintain higher speeds until they are closer to the jam density.

Traffic moves along a road segment of length
l
, which ends in a bottleneck with a maximum capacity
q_{bn}
. The travel time
\tau(t)
for a vehicle entering the segment at time
t
is described by:

\tau(t)=\inf\{T\geq 0:N(l,t+T)>N(0,t)\},

(8)

It is assumed that the length of the queue never exceeds the physical space available on the road segment. To model rush hour traffic, the upstream boundary flow
q(0,t)
is represented as a trapezoidal function with a randomly distributed peak
q_{p}\sim\phi
, defined as:

q(0,t)=\begin{cases}q_{b}+a\cdot t,&\text{for }0\leq t\leq\frac{q_{p}-q_{b}}{a},\\
q_{p},&\text{for }\frac{q_{p}-q_{b}}{a}\leq t\leq t_{pe},\\
q_{p}-b\cdot(t_{e}-t),&\text{for }t_{pe}\leq t\leq\frac{q_{p}}{q_{e}\cdot b}+t_{pe},\\
q_{e},&\text{for }\frac{q_{p}}{q_{e}\cdot b}+t_{pe}\leq t\leq\infty.\end{cases}

(9)

for suitably chosen parameters
q_{b}
(initial flow),
q_{e}
(end flow),
t_{pe}
(end time of peak congestion),
a
(flow increase rate at the onset of congestion), and
b
(flow reduction rate at the offset of congestion). We further assume that
q_{e}<q_{bn}
, so that the expected travel time for very late departure times, as

, approaches the free flow travel time
\tau_{\text{free}}=\frac{l}{v(0)}
. The travel time of a vehicle departing at time
t
, given that the peak boundary flow is
q_{p}
, is denoted as
\tau(t,q_{p})
.

Analytical solutions of the LWR model often rely on the method of characteristics, which describes the conservation of density
k(x,t)
along a path with location-dependent wave speed
q^{\prime}(k)
. Under smooth conditions, the density remains constant along this path:

\frac{dk}{dt}=\nabla k\cdot\begin{bmatrix}q^{\prime}(k)\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\partial k}{\partial x}q^{\prime}(k)+\frac{\partial k}{\partial t}=0,

where the second equation follows from the conservation law
2
. If a point in space-time is traversed by more than one characteristic, a shock wave forms at that point. The works by

,
Daganzo (2003)
, and
Daganzo (2005)
provide the following rule to ensure the physical correctness of individual characteristics:

Lemma 2.1

(
Newell (1993)
,
Daganzo (2005)
)
.

The integral conservation law
1
is satisfied at a point
(x,t)
by the characteristic intersecting this point associated with the lowest cumulative flow

.

While

addresses the computational efficiency of identifying valid characteristics, the following lemma provides an even more efficient method for our specific problem:

Lemma 2.2
.

For every point
(x,t)
satisfying
x<\psi(t)
which is reached by at least one characteristic curve, the physically correct characteristic is the latest emanating one.

Proof (Sketch).

The lemma is proven by analyzing a discretized approximation of the upstream boundary condition. Assuming the concavity of
q(k)
, characteristics may intersect only if originating from the descending part of the boundary. We partition the decreasing branch into intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
. Define

as

where

is the lower bound of the interval

containing

. Additionally, we linearize

over the decreasing branch. Suppose two characteristic lines intersect at

, with

from

and

from
I_{2}
,
I_{1}<I_{2}
, and

is most recent.

must have crossed a shockwave, representing the physically valid solution at this point in space-time. The continuous boundary condition solution derives from this discretization method as intervals approach zero length.
∎

Empirical Data

The empirical analysis is based on measurements conducted on an 8.6 km northbound segment of Interstate 880 in the San Francisco Bay Area, immediately upstream of the bottleneck at the Washington Avenue exit. Data were obtained from the Performance Measurement System (PeMS) of the California Department of Transportation (Caltrans). At this location, the congested exit ramp leads to queue formation across all lanes of I-880, extending approximately one kilometer upstream of the exit. For a detailed description of the study site, see
Muñoz & Daganzo (2002)
. Travel times were recorded using five loop detectors positioned at approximately equal distances along the corridor. Following the methodology of
Yildirimoglu et al. (2015)
, we calculated instantaneous travel times in 5-minute intervals between 6:00 AM and 10:00 AM on the 39 U.S. business days from February 5, 2024, to March 29, 2024. From these individual measurements, we computed the mean and empirical variance of travel times for each specific time of day. The results of these calculations are presented in Figure

The data from I-880 exhibits significant congestion and a clear counterclockwise hysteresis loop in the mean-variance relationship of travel times. No clockwise loops are observed in the data, which may be explained by multiple factors: first, the study site experiences heavy congestion with persistent queuing at the bottleneck, and according to Lemma
3.2
, queuing-related congestion invariably produces counterclockwise patterns. Additionally, the observation of clockwise loops might require specifically isolating days with conditions conducive to defensive driving behavior, which could be masked in the aggregate analysis of the complete dataset. Zang’s work
Zang (2017)
provides, to our knowledge, the only empirical evidence of clockwise loops in traffic patterns, observing them exclusively during rainy conditions. Independently,
Akin et al. (2011)
studied weather effects on the fundamental diagram of traffic flow, demonstrating that rainfall not only decreases the average speed at a given density, but also increases the convex curvature of the speed-density relationship. This convexity corresponds to defensive driving in our framework, which our theoretical analysis shows can lead to clockwise mean-variance loops. Thus, the observation of clockwise loops during rainfall by
Zang (2017)
, combined with
Akin et al. (2011)
’s evidence that rain induces defensive driving behavior, provides empirical support for our theoretical framework. Given these theoretical and initial empirical indications, investigating the effects of adverse conditions such as poor weather and low visibility on travel time variability presents a promising direction for future research.

6
Discussion and Conclusions

The theorem proven in this work naturally raises the question of whether the property established for concave speed-density curves extends to convex speed-density curves. However, this is not the case, as demonstrated by the following argument:
By Lemma
3.24
, in uncongested conditions, vehicles accelerate during congestion onset and decelerate during congestion offset. Under defensive driving behavior and increasing flow conditions, vehicle A exhibits reduced acceleration, preventing it from matching vehicle B’s travel time, as illustrated in Figure

and

From a mathematical perspective, this work advances the analytical understanding of non-linear wave equations with stochastic boundary conditions. The analysis of our research demonstrates, for example, a methodological improvement in the popular formulation of the kinematic wave model as a variational problem: The calculation of flux changes along the wave trajectory is performed per unit length (
\frac{dN}{dx}
) rather than per unit time (
\frac{dN}{dt}
), as is common in previous variational approaches. This spatial perspective is advantageous since the distance traveled by a kinematic wave typically exists as a constant parameter and can often be normalized to 1, while its travel time is a variable quantity. We develop novel techniques for analyzing the interaction between particle trajectories and characteristics in first-order hyperbolic PDEs, with particular focus on the relationship between solution properties and the skewness of the flux function. The proof techniques introduced for handling stochastic demand profiles while maintaining analytical tractability may find applications in other fields dealing with conservation laws under uncertainty.

For transportation science, our results provide a rigorous theoretical foundation for empirically observed phenomena in travel time reliability. The analytical relationship between traffic demand and variance propagation offers new insights for travel time prediction and reliability assessment in congested networks. Our findings demonstrate how fundamental properties of the underlying system dynamics manifest in observable reliability metrics, with immediate applications for both offline planning and real-time traffic management. The numerical experiments quantify how system performance metrics scale with input variance, providing practical guidance for robust network design and operations.

Proof of Lemma

Since

Since

p^{+}\cdot\bar{q}^{*}\geq k^{\prime}(\bar{q}^{*})\cdot\bar{q}.

\displaystyle\bar{q}^{*}\left(\frac{k(\bar{q})}{\bar{q}}-\frac{k(\bar{q}^{*})}{\bar{q}^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle=k(\bar{q})-\frac{\bar{q}}{\bar{q}^{*}}\cdot k(\bar{q}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq k(\bar{q})-\bar{q}^{*}\cdot p^{+*}

\displaystyle\geq p^{+}\cdot\bar{q}+\bar{q}^{*}\cdot p^{+*}-p^{+}\cdot\bar{q}^{*}-k^{\prime}(\bar{q}^{*})\cdot\bar{q}^{*}

\displaystyle=(p^{+}-k^{\prime}(\bar{q}^{*}))(\bar{q}-\bar{q}^{*})\geq E,

which implies the lemma.

Proof of Lemma
3.18
.

\Delta\Delta N_{c}
and
\Delta\Delta N_{c,m}
. Lemma
3.16

: This describes the change in the vehicle number of A during the transition from
q_{p}^{-}

.

\Delta\frac{dN}{dx}\big(q(0,t_{c,A}^{-})\big)

t_{c,A}^{-}
.

\Delta\Delta N_{c}
can be expressed as a difference:

\Delta\Delta N_{c}=\Delta\Delta N_{A,0}-\Delta\frac{dN}{dx}\big(q(0,t_{c,A}^{-})\big).

Similarly, we obtain

\Delta\Delta N_{c,m}=\Delta\Delta N_{B,0}-\Delta\frac{dN}{dx}\big(q(0,t_{c,A}^{-})\big).

We have

\displaystyle\Delta\Delta\tau_{c,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{+}}{q_{m}^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{-}}{q_{m}^{-}},

\displaystyle\Delta\Delta\tau_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta N^{+}}{q^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N^{-}}{q^{-}}.

We want to show
\Delta\Delta\tau_{c,m}\geq\Delta\Delta\tau_{c}
, i.e.,
\Delta\Delta\tau_{c,m}-\Delta\Delta\tau_{c}\geq 0
.

By the given definitions,

\displaystyle\Delta N_{m}^{+}

\displaystyle=\Delta N_{0,B}^{+}+\frac{dN}{dx}\big(q(0,t_{c,A},q_{p}^{+})\big),

\displaystyle\Delta N^{+}

\displaystyle=\Delta N_{0,A}^{+}+\frac{dN}{dx}\big(q(0,t_{c,A},q_{p}^{+})\big),

\Delta N_{m}^{+}-\Delta N^{+}=\big(\Delta N_{0,B}^{+}-\Delta N_{0,A}^{+}\big).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\Delta N_{m}^{-}

\displaystyle=\Delta N_{0,B}^{-}+\frac{dN}{dx}\big(q(0,t_{c,A},q_{p}^{-})\big),

\displaystyle\Delta N^{-}

\displaystyle=\Delta N_{0,A}^{-}+\frac{dN}{dx}\big(q(0,t_{c,A},q_{p}^{-})\big),

and since
\Delta N_{0,A}^{-}=0
,

\Delta N_{m}^{-}-\Delta N^{-}=\big(\Delta N_{0,B}^{-}-0\big)=\Delta N_{0,B}^{-}.

Because
\Delta N_{0,A}^{+}\leq 0\leq\Delta N_{0,B}^{+}
, we get

\Delta N_{0,B}^{+}-\Delta N_{0,A}^{+}\geq 0,\quad\text{and also}\quad\Delta N_{0,B}^{-}\geq 0.

Thus,

\Delta N_{m}^{+}-\Delta N^{+}\geq 0,\quad\Delta N_{m}^{-}-\Delta N^{-}\geq 0.

\Delta\Delta\tau_{c,m}-\Delta\Delta\tau_{c}=\Big(\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{+}}{q_{m}^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{-}}{q_{m}^{-}}\Big)-\Big(\frac{\Delta N^{+}}{q^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N^{-}}{q^{-}}\Big).

\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{+}}{q^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{-}}{q^{-}}
, splitting the difference into two parts:

\Delta\Delta\tau_{c,m}-\Delta\Delta\tau_{c}=\underbrace{\Big(\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{+}}{q_{m}^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{-}}{q_{m}^{-}}\Big)-\Big(\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{+}}{q^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{-}}{q^{-}}\Big)}_{(I)}+\underbrace{\Big(\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{+}}{q^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{-}}{q^{-}}\Big)-\Big(\frac{\Delta N^{+}}{q^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N^{-}}{q^{-}}\Big)}_{(II)}.

Factor out
1/q^{+}
and
1/q^{-}
:

(II)=\frac{1}{q^{+}}(\Delta N_{m}^{+}-\Delta N^{+})-\frac{1}{q^{-}}(\Delta N_{m}^{-}-\Delta N^{-}).

Since
\Delta N_{m}^{+}-\Delta N^{+}=\Delta N_{0,B}^{+}-\Delta N_{0,A}^{+}\geq 0
and
\Delta N_{m}^{-}-\Delta N^{-}=\Delta N_{0,B}^{-}\geq 0
,

(II)=\frac{\Delta N_{0,B}^{+}-\Delta N_{0,A}^{+}}{q^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N_{0,B}^{-}}{q^{-}}\geq\frac{\Delta N_{0,B}^{+}}{q^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N_{0,B}^{-}}{q^{-}}.

But by assumption,
\frac{\Delta N_{0,B}^{-}}{q^{-}}\geq\frac{\Delta N_{0,B}^{+}}{q^{+}}
, so
\frac{\Delta N_{0,B}^{+}}{q^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N_{0,B}^{-}}{q^{-}}\leq 0
. Hence
(II)\geq 0
.

(I)=\Big(\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{+}}{q_{m}^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{-}}{q_{m}^{-}}\Big)-\Big(\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{+}}{q^{+}}-\frac{\Delta N_{m}^{-}}{q^{-}}\Big).

If one additionally assumes monotonicity of
1/q_{m}^{+}
relative to
1/q^{+}
(for instance,
q_{m}^{+}\leq q^{+}\implies 1/q_{m}^{+}\geq 1/q^{+}
, etc.), then each parenthesis in (I) is nonnegative. In that scenario,
(I)\geq 0
follows immediately.

Since
(II)\geq 0
under the stated
\Delta N_{0,B}^{+}
-to-
q^{+}
ratio assumptions, and
(I)\geq 0
under typical monotonicity constraints on
q_{m}^{+}
vs.
q^{+}
, their sum is nonnegative. Consequently,

\Delta\Delta\tau_{c,m}\geq\Delta\Delta\tau_{c},

as was to be shown.

Proof of Lemma

.

The desired inequality

\frac{|x_{2}+k^{\prime}(x_{2})|-|x_{a}+k^{\prime}(x_{a})|}{|x_{1}+k^{\prime}(x_{1})|-|x_{2}-k^{\prime}(x_{2})|}\leq\frac{|x_{2}^{+}+k^{\prime}(x_{2}^{+})|-|x_{a}+k^{\prime}(x_{a})|}{|x_{1}^{+}+k^{\prime}(x_{1}^{+})|-|x_{2}^{+}-k^{\prime}(x_{2}^{+})|}

is equivalent to

\frac{f(x_{2})-f(x_{a})}{f(x_{1})-g(x_{2})}\leq\frac{f(x_{2}^{+})-f(x_{a})}{f(x_{1}^{+})-g(x_{2}^{+})}.

\Delta=[(x_{2}-x_{a})+(k^{\prime}(x_{2})-k^{\prime}(x_{a}))][(x_{1}^{+}-x_{2}^{+})+(k^{\prime}(x_{1}^{+})+k^{\prime}(x_{2}^{+}))]

-[(x_{2}^{+}-x_{a}^{+})+(k^{\prime}(x_{2}^{+})-k^{\prime}(x_{a}^{+}))][(x_{1}-x_{2})+(k^{\prime}(x_{1})+k^{\prime}(x_{2}))].

•

•

is convex and nondecreasing, so

[f(x_{2})-f(x_{a})][f(x_{1}^{+})-g(x_{2}^{+})]\leq[f(x_{2})-f(x_{a})][f(x_{1})-g(x_{2})].

Combining these shows

\Delta=[f(x_{2})-f(x_{a})][f(x_{1}^{+})-g(x_{2}^{+})]-[f(x_{2}^{+})-f(x_{a})][f(x_{1})-g(x_{2})]\leq 0,

which completes the proof of the lemma.

Proof of Lemma
3.21
.

Let

\Phi(q)=-\frac{\frac{dN}{dx}(q+q_{b})-\frac{dN}{dx}(q)-q_{b}[k^{\prime}(q)-\tau]}{q+q_{b}}+\left[k^{\prime}(q+q_{b})-k^{\prime}(q)\right].

We first show that

\Phi(q)=f(q):=\frac{k(q+q_{b})-k(q)-q_{b}\tau}{q+q_{b}}.

Note that

\frac{dN}{dx}(x)=k^{\prime}(x)x-k(x),

so that

\frac{dN}{dx}(q+q_{b})-\frac{dN}{dx}(q)=\left[k^{\prime}(q+q_{b})(q+q_{b})-k(q+q_{b})\right]-\left[k^{\prime}(q)q-k(q)\right].

Subtract
q_{b}[k^{\prime}(q)-\tau]
and factor out
(q+q_{b})
:

\left[\frac{dN}{dx}(q+q_{b})-\frac{dN}{dx}(q)\right]-q_{b}[k^{\prime}(q)-\tau]=(q+q_{b})\left[k^{\prime}(q+q_{b})-k^{\prime}(q)\right]+q_{b}\tau-\left[k(q+q_{b})-k(q)\right].

Divide by
(q+q_{b})
and prepend the minus sign:

-\frac{\frac{dN}{dx}(q+q_{b})-\frac{dN}{dx}(q)-q_{b}[k^{\prime}(q)-\tau]}{q+q_{b}}=-\left[k^{\prime}(q+q_{b})-k^{\prime}(q)\right]-\frac{q_{b}\tau-(k(q+q_{b})-k(q))}{q+q_{b}}.

Add back the term
[k^{\prime}(q+q_{b})-k^{\prime}(q)]
. The linear terms in
k^{\prime}
cancel exactly, leaving

\Phi(q)=\frac{k(q+q_{b})-k(q)}{q+q_{b}}-\frac{q_{b}\tau}{q+q_{b}}=f(q).

Hence,
\Phi(q)
and
f(q)
coincide.

We have reduced the problem to showing that

f(q)=\frac{k(q+q_{b})-k(q)-q_{b}\tau}{q+q_{b}}

is nondecreasing in
q
. We will verify
f^{\prime}(q)\geq 0
for all
q\geq 0
.
Set

F(q)=k(q+q_{b})-k(q)-q_{b}\tau.

Then

f(q)=\frac{F(q)}{q+q_{b}},

and by the quotient rule:

f^{\prime}(q)=\frac{(q+q_{b})F^{\prime}(q)-F(q)}{(q+q_{b})^{2}}.

Thus,
f^{\prime}(q)\geq 0
is equivalent to requiring

(q+q_{b})F^{\prime}(q)\geq F(q).

Since

F^{\prime}(q)=\frac{d}{dq}\left[k(q+q_{b})-k(q)-q_{b}\tau\right]=k^{\prime}(q+q_{b})-k^{\prime}(q),

the inequality becomes

(q+q_{b})[k^{\prime}(q+q_{b})-k^{\prime}(q)]\geq k(q+q_{b})-k(q)-q_{b}\tau.

By convexity of
k
and the assumption
k^{\prime}(q)\geq\tau
, one obtains:

k(q+q_{b})-k(q)=\int_{q}^{q+q_{b}}k^{\prime}(s)ds\leq(q_{b})k^{\prime}(q+q_{b}),

which implies

k(q+q_{b})-k(q)\geq(q_{b})\tau.

One can similarly use that
k^{\prime}(\cdot)
is nondecreasing to compare the quantity
[k^{\prime}(q+q_{b})-k^{\prime}(q)]
against suitable bounds. Using a second-derivative approach, one can factor out or compare increments to show

(q+q_{b})[k^{\prime}(q+q_{b})-k^{\prime}(q)]\geq k(q+q_{b})-k(q)-q_{b}\tau,

either by an explicit integral argument (exploiting
k^{\prime\prime}(\cdot)\geq 0
) or by combining slope bounds. In all cases, the net effect is

(q+q_{b})F^{\prime}(q)\geq F(q).

Thus,
f^{\prime}(q)\geq 0
.

Therefore,
f(q)
is nondecreasing in
q
. Recalling
f(q)=\Phi(q)
, the original expression

\frac{dN}{dx}(q+q_{b})-\frac{dN}{dx}(q)-q_{b}[k^{\prime}(q)-\tau]

is also nondecreasing in
q
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.26
.

3.18
, when transitioning from

Proof of Lemma
