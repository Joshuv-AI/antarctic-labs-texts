ESSA: Evolutionary Strategies for Scalable Alignment
Daria Korotyshova, Boris Shaposhnikov, Alexey Malakhov, Nikita Surnachev, George Bredis, Alexey Gorbatovski, Viacheslav Sinii, Daniil Gavrilov

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly relying on alignment techniques to ensure that their outputs match human preferences. Although reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is the dominant approach, it has high computational costs, memory requirements, and training instability, particularly when scaling to larger models. This paper introduces ESSA (Evolutionary Strategies for Scalable Alignment), a new framework that uses Evolutionary Strategies (ES) to efficiently align LLMs without the need for gradient computation. ES is well-suited for LLM alignment due to its favorable properties, such as high parallelizability, memory efficiency, robustness to sparse rewards, and fewer data samples required for convergence, especially when starting from a strong pre-trained policy. Moreover, ES eliminates the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning, making the alignment process simpler and more stable. Although ES excels in low-dimensional optimization, it poses a challenge when applied to high-dimensional LLMs. To address this challenge, we propose a parameter-efficient architectural modification that reduces the dimensionality of optimization through low-rank adaptation. We evaluated our approach on mathematical reasoning tasks with verifiable accuracy-based metrics, demonstrating that ESSA converges faster and is more data efficient than gradient-based methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Our findings establish ES as a promising and scalable alternative to gradient-based alignment, paving the way for efficient post-training of large language models.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved impressive capabilities across a range of tasks, from conversation and summarization to mathematical reasoning and programming. However, these capabilities hinge not just on scale but on alignment: ensuring that models produce outputs that are aligned with user expectations. Reinforcement learning from human feedback has become the de facto standard for this alignment process, allowing models to learn human-favored behaviors
(
Ouyang et al., 2022
)
.

However, RLHF and its variants, for example those built on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
Schulman et al., 2017
)
and REINFORCE
(
Sutton et al., 1999
)
, are fundamentally limited. In the online setting, these methods require large amounts of computational resources, suffer from training instability, exhibit high variance, and struggle with sparse rewards, making them difficult to scale to models exceeding tens of billions of parameters
(
Zheng et al., 2023
;
Sheng et al., 2024
)
. Offline methods
(
Rafailov et al., 2024
;
Hong et al., 2024
)
simplify the process but introduce their own challenges: they are fundamentally constrained by the quality and coverage of fixed datasets, which limits generalization and alignment quality
(
Tang et al., 2024
;
Xu et al., 2024
;
Chu et al., 2025
)
.

In response to these challenges, this paper explores Evolutionary Strategies as a novel and scalable solution for LLM alignment, an approach previously underexplored in this context due to perceived limitations with high-dimensional optimization. ES offers several advantages directly suited to modern alignment challenges: it is highly parallelizable, scales efficiently across many computing nodes, remains robust even with sparse rewards, and requires much less memory by operating in inference-only mode without gradient computations
(
Salimans et al., 2017
)
. In particular, ES methods are most effective when initialized with a strong policy
(
Chrabaszcz et al., 2018
)
. This property is particularly relevant for LLM alignment, where training typically starts from a well-performing supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model. Given these promising features, we propose
ESSA (Evolutionary Strategies for Scalable Alignment)
, a framework for aligning LLMs using ES, and present the first comprehensive study of ES methods for aligning large-scale language models.

To demonstrate ESSA’s effectiveness and circumvent complexities related to learned reward proxies, we evaluate our approach on mathematical reasoning benchmarks where correctness (accuracy) serves as a clear, unambiguous reward signal. For comparison, we use the GRPO method
(
Shao et al., 2024
)
, which has become a widely adopted baseline due to its strong performance in mathematical and reasoning tasks. Building on this foundation, our contributions are as follows:

•

We adapt the low rank adaptation (LoRA) technique to the ES paradigm, reducing the effective dimensionality while retaining the expressivity of the full model.

•

We show that ESSA achieves faster convergence and better data efficiency compared to the GRPO method.

•

We demonstrate that ESSA scales efficiently across distributed compute environments and avoids the memory bottlenecks associated with backpropagation-based alignment.

Related Works

Online Alignment

RLHF, typically instantiated with PPO or other policy gradient methods
(
Ouyang et al., 2022
;
Schulman et al., 2017
)
, remains the standard for aligning LLMs with human preferences. Although effective in conversational tasks, RLHF suffers from high variance, sparse rewards, and significant memory overhead for large models
(
Zheng et al., 2023
;
Sheng et al., 2024
)
. Stability-focused variants like RLOO and GRPO still require extensive hyperparameter tuning and gradient estimation
(
Shao et al., 2024
)
. Actor–critic or value-free approaches (e.g., RLOO) struggle with scalability and sparse rewards in complex reasoning tasks
(
Ahmadian et al., 2024
)
.

Parameter-efficient training of LLMs

To reduce the cost of fine-tuning large models, parameter-efficient methods have been introduced such as adapters
(
Houlsby et al., 2019
)
, prefix-tuning
(
Li and Liang, 2021
)
, and LoRA
(
Hu et al., 2021
)
. These approaches freeze most weights and update only a small subset, with LoRA reducing parameter count via low-rank adaptation. Transformer
2

(
Sun et al., 2025
)
further reduces the LoRA parameter space using single value fine-tuning, similar to our method.

Evolution strategies

Evolutionary Strategies, such as Covariance Matrix Adaptation (CMA-ES)
(
Hansen and Ostermeier, 2001
)
and NES
(
Wierstra et al., 2014
)
, have shown promise for gradient-free optimization, robustness to sparse rewards, and strong parallelism. NSGA-III
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
prunes model weights based on importance, while CMA-ES has been used for black-box prompt optimization in LMaaS scenarios
(
Sun et al., 2022b
;
Sun et al., 2022a
)
, though still relying on gradient-based pretraining. Zero-order optimizers
(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
reduce memory by approximating gradients through loss differences, matching gradients on simple tasks but trailing in complex reasoning. Applications of ES to LLM alignment are rare due to high-dimensionality concerns. GENOME/GENOME+
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
evolve populations by combining weights, and LoRAHub
(
Huang et al., 2024
)
combines LoRA adapters to reduce parameter space, yet both depend on pre-trained weights. DFO
(
Jin et al., 2024
)
exclusively uses ES to optimize LoRA adapters, reducing dimensionality but with interpretability challenges. Our work advances this line by applying ES to singular LoRA matrices, making black-box optimization interpretable and feasible even at large scale.

3
ESSA

3.1
Motivation

With today’s tools, RL training involves three stages: sampling sequential trajectories (often accelerated using specialized inference runtimes like vLLM
1
1

1

https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm

(
Kwon et al., 2023
)
), constructing a computation graph from the collected trajectories, and backpropagating error signals. ES relies solely on repeated forward inference with perturbed parameter vectors, followed by scalar fitness aggregation. This results in a markedly simpler training loop.

This simplicity makes ES especially attractive in deployment scenarios, such as when an LLM is scaled only at inference time or trained continuously while interacting with an environment and optimizing an external reward, such as a compiler’s success signal for code generation agents or user satisfaction metrics for chatbots. With ES, every update only requires forward passes, making it possible to train on hardware that cannot efficiently compute gradients, such as low-power devices that support quantized inference but lack bandwidth for backpropagation.

The main criticism of ES for LLM alignment is its apparent incompatibility with models with billions of parameters. However, recent advances in parameter-efficient fine-tuning, such as low-rank adaptation, additive adapters, and blockwise perturbations, directly address this issue. Therefore, we find both a strong motivation for and a promising path towards making ES practical for LLM alignment. In the following sections, we will rigorously investigate the scaling limits of ES when applied to reasoning-oriented models.

3.2
Method

Evolutionary Strategies offer an attractive approach for large language model (LLM) alignment due to their gradient-free nature, high parallelizability, and robustness to sparse rewards. However, a major limitation is their inefficiency in high-dimensional search spaces, such as the full parameter space of modern LLMs
(
Ros and Hansen, 2008
)
. To address this, we first reduce the number of parameters that are optimized as much as possible by using parameter-efficient architectures.

Specifically, we focus on the LoRA
(
Hu et al., 2021
)
technique, where weight updates are represented as low-rank matrices: for a weight matrix
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, the update is parameterized as
\Delta W=BA
, with
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
and
r\ll\min(m,n)
. Although this already yields a significant reduction in dimensionality, the combined space of
A
and
B
can still be large for massive models.

To make evolutionary optimization feasible on a scale, we introduce an additional reduction step inspired by Transformer
2
: we apply singular value decomposition (SVD) to each LoRA adapter and optimize only the singular values, further compressing the search space. Concretely, for each LoRA adapter, we decompose
A
and
B
by SVD, and focus on optimizing the top
p\%
of diagonal singular value vectors (
\Sigma_{A}
,
\Sigma_{B}
). This allows us to maintain expressivity while drastically limiting the number of parameters evolved by ES (see Figure
1
).

A crucial detail is that the ES search does not begin with random LoRA adapters. Instead, we first train the LoRA adapters with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on a small labeled dataset, providing a strong and task-aware initialization. The evolutionary optimization is then performed starting from these SFT-trained singular values, ensuring the search is conducted in a region of parameter space already adapted to the task format.

We employ CMA-ES, a powerful black-box optimizer well suited to rugged nonconvex search landscapes. The optimization proceeds as follows (see Algorithm
1
):

1.

At each generation, CMA-ES samples a population of candidate solutions
\lambda
(singular value vectors) from a multivariate normal distribution defined by a mean and covariance.

2.

For each candidate, the LoRA matrices are reconstructed using the candidate singular values, integrated into the LLM, and the model is evaluated on the alignment task using a fitness function appropriate to the objective (e.g., accuracy on a mathematical reasoning dataset in our experiments).

3.

After all candidates are evaluated, CMA-ES updates the mean and covariance of the sampling distribution, shifting focus to the most promising candidates, and adapting search directions.

4.

This process repeats until convergence, with all candidate evaluations performed in inference mode, allowing scalable, distributed training, and compatibility with low-precision hardware (INT4, INT8).

By restricting optimization to the singular values of SFT-trained adapters, ESSA combines the strengths of strong initialization and aggressive dimensionality reduction, making evolutionary alignment of even large LLMs both feasible and practical. Tasks

We evaluate our approach on mathematical reasoning benchmarks using the open-source GSM8K

4.2
Models

Our experiments target three open-source LLMs to test both domain-specialized and general models at different scales: Qwen2.5-Math-7B
(
Yang et al., 2024

For each model, we first perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of LoRA adapters inserted into the Q, K, V, and O attention matrices, using a dedicated SFT data split to ensure that the model learns the basic task format and domain. This SFT stage provides well-initialized LoRA parameters, which are then further optimized during alignment using ESSA or GRPO methods.

5
Results

5.1
Scaling

To evaluate the parallel efficiency of ESSA, we conducted experiments on GSM8K with a fixed ES population size of 192, varying the number of available GPUs from 8 to 128. Figure
2
shows the wall clock time required to reach 0.76 accuracy on GSM8K as a function of the number of GPUs. We observe a near-linear reduction in time-to-convergence as computational resources increase: with 8 GPUs, ESSA requires approximately 290 minutes, whereas scaling up to 128 GPUs reduces this to under 80 minutes.

Figure 2
:
Wall-clock time (minutes) required to reach the precision 0.76 in GSM8K as a function of the number of GPUs used, with a fixed ES population size of 640. Increasing the number of GPUs enables efficient parallelization of the evaluation of the candidates, resulting in substantial reductions in training time.

This strong scaling behavior highlights one of the key advantages of evolutionary strategies for LLM alignment. Since candidate solutions are evaluated independently via forward inference, optimization can make full use of large-scale distributed hardware, avoiding bottlenecks from backpropagation and optimizer state synchronization. Therefore, communication overhead remains minimal: it is sufficient to transmit only the random seed and the reward between workers, as demonstrated in
Salimans et al., 2017
.

5.2
Optimal population

Choosing an appropriate population size for the ES is critical to balance the convergence speed and final accuracy. To empirically investigate this, we evaluated ESSA across a range of population sizes (96, 192, 400, 608) in Qwen2.5-Math-7B with fixed LoRA rank 16.

(Right) Maximum accuracy achieved for Qwen2.5-Math-7B (LoRA rank 32) as a function of population size. Moderate population sizes (e.g. 192) yield the best balance between speed and final performance; very large populations slow down convergence, consistent with theoretical predictions.

Figure
3
shows that in Qwen2.5-Math-7B, increasing the population to 192 accelerates convergence and improves the final accuracy, but larger populations offer diminishing or even negative returns.

This effect aligns with theoretical results from the evolutionary strategies literature
(
Ros and Hansen, 2008
)
, which show that for high-dimensional problems, excessively large populations can actually worsen progress rates.

To evaluate the scalability and generality of ESSA across different model scales and training corpora, we additionally applied it to LLaMA3.1-8B on PRM800K and Qwen2.5-32B on GSM8K. Based on earlier experiments, we identified a population size of 192 as offering the best trade-off between convergence speed and final accuracy. We therefore fixed the population to 192 in these runs. As shown in Figure
4
, ESSA consistently improves accuracy over training steps even in these distinct settings, confirming its robustness across both mid-sized and large language models. These results also demonstrate that ESSA is applicable beyond math-specialized architectures, extending effectively to general-purpose LLMs.

5.3
Problem dimensionality effect

Next, we examine how the dimensionality of the optimization problem, determined by the LoRA rank, impacts the effectiveness and efficiency of ESSA. Figure
5
shows the accuracy curves over time for Qwen2.5-Math-7B using a fixed ES population size of 192 and varying LoRA ranks (4, 8, 32, 64).

Figure 5
:
Accuracy over time for Qwen-Math-7B (population size 192) with different LoRA ranks. Moderate ranks (4–32) provide the best convergence and final accuracy; very high ranks slow progress and degrade performance.

Our results demonstrate that moderate ranks (4, 8, 32) yield both faster convergence and higher final accuracy, while further increasing the rank to 64 leads to slower progress and reduced maximum accuracy. This trend is consistent with evolutionary optimization theory: as dimensionality increases, the search space grows, making it more challenging for ES to efficiently explore and adapt. In particular, the best trade-off between expressivity and sample efficiency is achieved with LoRA ranks in the range 4 to 32 for this model and dataset.

These findings highlight the importance of dimensionality reduction in making ES feasible for LLM alignment, and motivate the use of low-rank parameterizations as a practical approach for scaling black-box optimization methods to large models.

5.4
Comparison with GRPO

To directly benchmark the efficiency and effectiveness of ESSA, we compared it against GRPO on GSM8K using 8 GPUs and identical model initializations. The precision of both methods was evaluated as a function of wall clock training time.

As shown in Figure
6
, ESSA (using a population of 192) converges substantially faster than GRPO, achieving high accuracy in less than half the time. Although GRPO requires extensive backpropagation and synchronization, ESSA leverages highly parallel, gradient-free optimization, leading to rapid early gains and stable plateauing at higher accuracy levels. In particular, ESSA reaches over 0.80 accuracy in less than 25 minutes, whereas GRPO requires more than 50 minutes to approach similar performance. Moreover, as mentioned above, only 100 examples were used for ES, GRPO training was performed on the entire trainee fold of size 5,978. In this experiment, only the top 40% singular values of the LoRA matrices were trained.

Figure 6
:
Comparison of ESSA (using the vLLM inference runtime) and GRPO (using the VERL training runtime) on GSM8K using Qwen2.5-Math-7B and 8 GPUs. ESSA achieves higher accuracy significantly faster than GRPO, demonstrating improved sample efficiency and scalability.

This result highlights the practical advantage of evolutionary strategies for large-scale LLM alignment: ESSA not only reduces the compute cost and memory requirements, but also accelerates time-to-solution compared to gradient-based baselines, making it particularly attractive for distributed training scenarios.

6
Discussion

In this work, we introduced ESSA, a novel framework that leverages Evolutionary Strategies with low-rank adaptation for scalable, gradient-free alignment of large language models. Through extensive experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, we demonstrate that ESSA achieves faster convergence, improved data efficiency, and high parallel scalability compared to state-of-the-art gradient-based methods such as GRPO. In particular, our results (see Figures
6
) show that ESSA requires substantially fewer samples to achieve competitive accuracy, highlighting its superior sample efficiency in alignment tasks. The ability of ESSA to operate entirely in inference mode also allows efficient training even in low-precision settings (e.g. INT4, INT8), making it highly compatible with modern hardware and resource-constrained environments.

Our results highlight several practical benefits of ES-based alignment: minimal communication overhead, robust convergence in high-dimensional spaces when combined with dimensionality reduction, and broad applicability to domain-specialized and general-purpose LLMs. ESSA achieves competitive or superior alignment quality using fewer samples and less compute, lowering the barrier for post-training adaptation of very large models.

Limitations and Future Work.

While ESSA is highly effective for tasks with clear, accuracy-based reward signals, its reliance on direct scalar fitness optimization may limit its applicability to subjective or sparse feedback scenarios, such as aligning human preference in open dialogue. For extremely high-dimensional parameterizations, the efficiency of ES may still degrade if further dimensionality reduction is not possible. Addressing these limitations will be an important focus of future work. Promising directions include extending ESSA to handle noisy, human-in-the-loop, or adversarial feedback, exploring richer fitness functions for subjective alignment objectives, and integrating ES with other parameter-efficient tuning methods. Additional improvements in adaptive population size and covariance adaptation could further accelerate convergence.
