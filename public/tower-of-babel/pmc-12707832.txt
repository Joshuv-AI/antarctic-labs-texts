Effect of Dokha/Midwakh Tobacco Smoking as a Risk Factor of Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) Along the Persian Gulf Nations
Authors: Mohammad-Rafi Bazrafshan, Omid Barghi, Esmaeil Kavi, Negar Shahkarami
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Since ancient times, Arab-speaking Bedouins and sailors have been consumers of dokha.[1] Dokha (meaning dizziness in Arabic) is a tobacco mixture smoked in a pipe called a midwakh. This mixture includes tobacco along with various types of bark, plant leaves, spices, dried flowers, and dried fruit. Dokha is known for its very high nicotine content with the amount of nicotine in dokha being five times higher than in regular cigarettes.[2] The traditional midwakh pipe used in Arab countries is a small instrument with a small bowl that holds about 0.5 grams of dokha. Each use of the midwakh allows the tobacco to be completely burned and refilled with just two inhalations.[3] The use of tobacco products has increased dramatically in recent decades, with nearly 36 percent of global tobacco users now useing smokeless tobacco products. The results of Crooks and Wolff, research showed that 72.1% of high school students in Dubai had consumed tobacco during their lifetime, and among intermittent tobacco users, 54.8% believed that dokha was the most popular type of tobacco.[4] Owing to the high activity of people in the southern province of Fars in the midwakh market, this product is more common in the southern cities of Fars than in other cities.[5] With the prevalence of midwakh use in the Gulf region, its popularity may spread among migrant populations in the West and subsequently to indigenous populations. Additionally, many websites selling dokha and midwakh are based in the United States of America and Britain. While it is currently, difficult to definitely comment on the emergence of midwakh, it is evident that its popularity in the West is increasing.[2] There is substantial evidence of dokha’s impact on health, raising concerns among health officials that dokha could replace cigarettes in society.[6] Dokha users often have the impression that dokha consumption is less harmful than cigarettes.[7] Some of the most important complications of dokha on the health of consumers are increased systolic blood pressure and increased heart rate.[8910] Preliminary research has shown that dokha has acute effects on blood pressure similar to those of cigarettes.[3] Numerous studies have shown that exposure to nicotine during adolescence or childhood causes harmful effects on the brain and leads to impaired lung function and development.[8111213] In 2020, in the United States of America, more than 2,800 cases of lung injury related to the consumption of smokeless tobacco products were hospitalized, affecting individuals between the ages of 13 and 85 years.[14] Tobacco use affects all stages of atherosclerosis including endothelial dysfunction, acute clinical events, inflammation, bleeding disorders, increased coronary artery blood flow, and a higher risk of thrombosis. The incidence of CVD increases during hospitalization, ultimately leading to higher mortality rates.[15] With the projected 171% increase in coronary mortality in the Middle East between 1990 and 2020, the growth rate of coronary mortality is alarming. This rapid growth in coronary artery disease can be attributed to changes in behavior and lifestyle.[16] Tobacco causes significant damage to the body, particularly to the cardiovascular system, through various mechanisms. Carbon monoxide and nicotine damage the inner membrane of the heart, thereby increasing the strain on the heart.[17] There has been tremendous growth in the incidence of ischemic heart disease in the Middle East and North Africa over the past 30 years.[18] One study showed that the highest risk occurrence of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) was associated with the consumption of tobacco products. This is despite the fact that 12-30% of men in the Gulf region use midwakh.[19] A larger study of UAE nationals found that midwakh accounted for 15% of all tobacco products used.[120] In a study on patients with cardiovascular disease, almost half of the patients were smokers (46%).[21] In the Middle East, three-quarters of participants had at least one uncontrolled risk factor, such as high blood pressure, highlighting the need for improved preventive care. Following enhancements in the quality of care, Qatar saw a 50% reduction in deaths from acute coronary syndrome over a 20-year period, achieved through adherence to guidance recommendations.[2223] According to a study of individuals who experienced myocardial infarction for the first time, tobacco users were 31 times more likely to be re-hospitalized than non-tobacco users.[24] The leading public health challenge over the next 25 years is to address and control addressing and controlling atherosclerotic disease and its risk factors.[16] There is growing concern about the use of alternative tobacco products (ATPs). With dramatic changes in the demographics of Europe and North America, the use of ATPs, including midwakh and hookah, may increase due to migration patterns and demographic changes. To prevent an abundance of ATPs, the United States and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) have proposed new regulations, including banning the sale of these products to individuals under the age of 18 and disclosing the ingredients for these products.[22526]

Despite the uncertainty in midwakh consumers’ motives, understanding the factors that may play a role in increasing their use of midwakh is essential for the development of appropriate prevention, stoppage, and policy interventions. Focas groups can provide valuable insight into the behavior of these users by systematically evaluating their knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, risk perceptions, communication patterns, preferences, and influences. It is also important to examine the cultural dimensions of the use of midwakh, including tradition, family, and social norms. Midwakh sales websites are spreading misinformation about midwakh consumption. Addressing this misinformation through targeted health education and policy actions is recommended. The use of midwakh may have significant implications for public health programs, policy initiatives, and global tobacco control efforts. Therefore, policies and interventions that promote the prevention of tobacco use, the creation of smoke-free environments, and quitting smoking and tobacco products should be important components of national and international efforts to improve population health and well-being.
