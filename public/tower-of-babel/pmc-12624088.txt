Quantitative analysis of psoriatic nail disease using optical coherence tomography
Authors: Panqiu Jiang, Li Tan, Yujie Mao, Qiao Zhou, Fan Cui, Pinghe Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To evaluate the clinical utility of optical coherence tomography (OCT) in the assessment of psoriatic nail disease, focusing on its ability to detect early structural changes, quantify lesion severity, and monitor treatment outcomes. Patients with psoriasis (PsO) or psoriatic arthritis (PsA) presenting with nail involvement were enrolled between May 2022 and September 2023. A swept-source OCT system (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.3 \, \mu \text {m}$$\end{document}, 5 mm depth) was used to image all fingernails. Cross-sectional nail structures were analyzed, and grayscale values were quantified to assess nail integrity. Paired-sample t-tests were applied to compare grayscale values before and after disease progression or treatment (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P < 0.05$$\end{document}). OCT imaging revealed structural abnormalities such as nail plate separation, thinning, depressions, and low-reflectivity regions that were not visible to the naked eye. During disease progression, grayscale values significantly decreased, reflecting loss of nail integrity. Following 4 months of treatment, grayscale values increased by 6–10 units, indicating recovery of nail structure. OCT provided objective measurements that correlated with clinical improvement and offered greater sensitivity than visual inspection. OCT is a non-invasive, high-resolution imaging technique that enables early detection and quantitative monitoring of psoriatic nail disease. Grayscale analysis provides an objective metric for evaluating disease severity and treatment response, supporting OCT as a valuable tool for diagnosis, follow-up, and clinical decision-making in PsO and PsA patients.

Introduction

Nail disease is a common clinical manifestation in patients with psoriasis (PsO) and psoriatic arthritis (PsA). Clinically, nail changes can be broadly classified into matrix-related and bed-related alterations, each reflecting different underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Matrix-related changes include pitting, leukonychia, red spots in the lunula, and nail plate crumbling, which arise due to abnormal keratinization and inflammation in the nail matrix. In contrast, bed-related changes involve onycholysis, oil-drop (salmon) patches, subungual hyperkeratosis, and splinter hemorrhages, which are linked to inflammatory and proliferative processes in the nail bed. Other non-specific findings such as transverse ridging, longitudinal striations, and nail shedding may also occur1. The incidence of nail disease in PsA patients approaches 80-90%2, while in psoriasis patients without arthritis, it is 46%3. The nail unit is intimately connected to the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint through the extensor tendon enthesis, and inflammation at this site–known as enthesitis–is thought to play a central role in the initiation of PsA. Early nail changes such as pitting, onycholysis, and subungual hyperkeratosis may thus reflect subclinical enthesitis and precede overt joint symptoms. Recognizing nail psoriasis as an early indicator of PsA has significant clinical implications: it allows clinicians to identify high-risk patients, initiate early monitoring, and implement timely treatment strategies to prevent irreversible joint damage. Currently, there is a significant gap in reliable diagnostic techniques for evaluating nail disease in PsO and PsA in clinical settings. Physicians often rely on visual assessments to evaluate the severity of nail and skin damage, which may not adequately capture the complex pathological changes in the nail bed, nail matrix, and skin.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is an advanced imaging technique that uses low-coherence light interferometry. This method detects reflected or backscattered light at various depths within a medium, enabling cross-sectional imaging of the sample4. OCT has been widely used in clinical diagnosis, particularly in ophthalmology5 and cardiology, where it is employed for imaging coronary arteries6–8. OCT allows for the measurement and imaging of the stratum corneum, epidermis, dermis, and blood vessels of human skin, detecting changes caused by pigmentation, inflammation, edema, and erythema. This capability is crucial for the early detection and treatment of skin diseases9–12. Furthermore, OCT is effective in clearly delineating the stratum corneum, epidermis, and dermis, enabling precise measurement of their scattering coefficients13. Polarization-sensitive optical coherence tomography (PS-OCT) has also been used to measure the polarization parameters of human nails14. Additionally, OCT angiography (OCTA) has emerged as a promising tool for imaging retinal microvasculature in psoriasis patients, showing potential for diagnosing PsA and distinguishing it from PsO and hand osteoarthritis (OA)15–17.

OCT is a valuable tool for detecting changes in nails, facilitating the identification of nail pigmentation and morphological alterations in the nail bed18. It is also useful for the differential diagnosis of subungual hematomas and melanocytic lesions19, as well as for assessing morphological and structural changes in diseased toenails20. Spectral domain OCT (SD-OCT) has been used to study the impact of progressive nail art on nail plates in vivo, revealing that glitter nail polish can obscure OCT imaging of the nail plate and nail bed21,22. Furthermore, differential polarization parameters have been employed to reveal anisotropic structural features beneath human fingernails23. OCT is also recognized for its ability to delineate structural and vascular features specific to nail psoriasis24–26. Studies have shown that OCT images can accurately reflect microstructural changes in nail lesions, offering higher resolution than ultrasound27–30. Despite these advancements, further research is needed to validate the use of OCT in quantifying the optical properties of psoriatic nail tissues and integrating image analysis to assess the extent and characteristics of lesions.

Nail lesions are prominent indicators of PsO and PsA, making the monitoring of nail conditions crucial for assessing, surveilling, and treating these disorders. This study investigates the potential of OCT as a non-invasive imaging technique for examining nail lesions in PsO and PsA patients, aiming to facilitate timely diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment. The results demonstrate that OCT technology can clearly visualize both healthy nail sublayers and typical nail lesions. Many patients with PsO or PsA exhibit nail abnormalities at early stages of the disease, and OCT imaging, through grayscale value changes and image analysis, provides valuable clinical insights for early diagnosis. This study confirms that OCT technology is not only useful for diagnosing nail lesions but also serves as an effective tool for monitoring and detecting changes in nail condition before and after treatment in PsO and PsA patients.

Experimental setup

Study population

This study included patients diagnosed with clinical psoriasis (PsO) and psoriatic arthritis (PsA) at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital. Participants were recruited between May 2022 and September 2023, with ages ranging from 18 to 75 years as shown in the Table 1. Inclusion criteria included patients with clinically or pathologically confirmed nail psoriasis who still had nail involvement and negative fungal microscopy for nail lesions. Exclusion criteria included patients with a history of nail psoriasis without skin lesions, those with concurrent nail lichen planus, nail tinea, traumatic nail injury, or other nail-related diseases, those wearing artificial nails, as well as individuals without independent decision-making abilities or those unable to cooperate with the examination. No vulnerable or underprivileged individuals were included in the study. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, affirming their willingness to participate. OCT imaging protocol

In this experiment, a swept-source optical coherence tomography (OCT) system operating at 1.3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m was used to capture nail images. The OCT system employed is similar to the one described in reference31. The swept-source used in the system is the HSL-20 model from Santec, with a central wavelength of approximately 1304.36 nm and a bandwidth of 107.92 nm. This system features a swept source with a built-in K-clock, offering an imaging depth of 5 mm, which meets the necessary requirements for detection. The optical power illuminating the patient is approximately 4 mW. The lateral scanning range can be controlled by the system.

Image analysis

This study is non-interventional, involving only optical testing, with a scanning time of less than 2 minutes, ensuring no impact on patient treatment or safety. For all participants, imaging of 10 fingernails was performed using the OCT system, with the detection positions determined by the physician to obtain cross-sectional images of the nails. Simultaneously, actual photographs of the nails were taken for comparison. The acquired OCT images are 656 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 1024 grayscale images. The average grayscale value of each column (i.e., the 656 grayscale values) was calculated to obtain the column grayscale value. The average column grayscale values from the fingertip to the nail root were computed, and these values were then averaged to determine the overall average grayscale value of the nail plate.

Image quantification and safety considerations

Region of interest (ROI) definition and segmentation

For each fingernail scan, the nail plate region was manually delineated by a trained dermatologist using the lateral boundaries where the nail plate signal sharply dropped to background levels and the proximal/distal edges visible in the cross-sectional OCT image. Each B-scan image (656 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 1024 pixels) was divided into 1024 vertical columns. The mean grayscale value of each column was computed, and the column values within the nail plate ROI were averaged to yield the overall mean grayscale intensity of the nail plate. Regions containing surrounding skin, cuticle, or air gaps were excluded from the ROI.

Intensity normalization

To minimize inter-scan intensity variation caused by minor alignment differences or system power fluctuations, all OCT images were normalized prior to analysis. Specifically, grayscale intensity values were linearly rescaled using the background signal intensity (air region) as zero and the brightest pixel in the image as 255 (8-bit scale). This ensured that differences in measured intensity reflected structural variations rather than acquisition variability.

Handling inter-scan variability

All scans were performed by the same experienced operator under standardized lighting and positioning conditions. Patients were instructed to keep their fingers relaxed and stationary, and the nail plate was aligned perpendicular to the OCT probe. If motion artifacts, defocus, or incomplete nail coverage occurred, the scan was immediately repeated. Fewer than 5% of images were excluded for low quality and were replaced by repeat scans acquired during the same visit.

Safety note

The swept-source OCT system operated at 1.3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m with an incident optical power of approximately 4 mW on the nail surface, which is well below the ANSI Z136.1 maximum permissible exposure (MPE) limit for skin in this wavelength range. No adverse effects (e.g., discomfort, thermal sensation, or tissue reaction) were reported by participants during or after imaging.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative variables were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test prior to performing paired-sample t-tests. Only data that met the assumption of normal distribution were analyzed with parametric tests. For each paired-sample t-test, Cohen’s dz was calculated as an effect size, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), to quantify the magnitude and precision of the observed differences. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P < 0.05$$\end{document} considered statistically significant. Bonferroni correction was applied when multiple paired comparisons were conducted (adjusted significance threshold = 0.0167).

The association between OCT grayscale values and NAPSI scores was evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document}). The 95% confidence interval (CI) of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document} was estimated using a bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap method with 5000 resamples.

For instance, nail thinning and the formation of cavities within the nail plate can lead to a significant decrease in grayscale values. Discussion and conclusion

This study demonstrated the powerful capability of Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) in capturing high-resolution cross- sectional images of human fingernails in various stages of damage. These images provided detailed insights into nail thickness, the degree of structural damage, and internal morphological changes, enabling precise visualization of nail plate separation from the nail bed. Through the quantitative analysis of grayscale values in OCT images, the optical properties of nail tissues can be assessed, allowing for the characterization of lesion severity and progression. For instance, in the early stages of psoriasis (PsO) and psoriatic arthritis (PsA), many lesions that are invisible to the naked eye can be identified through subtle abnormalities, such as nail plate thinning, low-reflectivity regions, and interlayer separations detected by OCT imaging. The Nail Psoriasis Severity Index (NAPSI) is widely used in clinical practice to score nail involvement, but it remains subjective and may miss subtle structural changes. Our results suggest that OCT grayscale values correlate with NAPSI trends, thereby providing an objective, reproducible metric that complements conventional clinical scoring. This provides a scientific foundation for monitoring disease progression and assessing treatment efficacy. Given its high-resolution imaging and real-time visualization capabilities, OCT has emerged as a valuable diagnostic tool for psoriatic nail lesions, offering critical support for therapeutic decision-making and clinical management.

At present, the application of OCT technology in the diagnosis and study of psoriatic nails remains in an exploratory phase, particularly in quantitative analyses of lesions at varying severities. However, this study, through grayscale value analysis and image interpretation, revealed distinct cross-sectional characteristics between healthy and diseased nails. In OCT imaging, grayscale intensity reflects the optical scattering properties of tissues. Healthy nails exhibit a relatively uniform keratin structure with consistent refractive indices, resulting in higher and more homogeneous grayscale values. In contrast, psoriatic nail changes–such as parakeratosis, subungual hyperkeratosis, thinning, or cavitation–disrupt tissue organization and alter optical scattering, producing heterogeneous or reduced grayscale intensities. Previous dermatological OCT studies have shown that changes in brightness correlate with structural abnormalities, including keratin thickening, edema, and inflammatory infiltration9,20. Thus, grayscale analysis provides a quantifiable surrogate for nail plate integrity and can serve as an objective marker of disease severity and treatment response.

This study also confirmed, through four months of dynamic observation, that grayscale value analysis plays a central role in visualizing the structural differences between healthy and diseased nails in psoriasis patients. Particularly in early-stage PsO and PsA, OCT imaging effectively detected subclinical lesions, such as thinning of the nail plate, low-reflectivity areas, and interlayer separation, which were invisible to the naked eye. These findings underscore the sensitivity of OCT as a tool for early screening and prevention. Through quantitative grayscale analysis, OCT provides detailed assessments of nail plate thickness, internal consistency, and the structural integrity of the nail plate-nail bed connection. During treatment, significant recovery was reflected by a 6 to 10 unit increase in average grayscale values, a reduction in low-reflectivity areas, and an improvement in the uniformity of the nail plate structure. These results confirm that OCT not only facilitates early diagnosis of psoriatic nails but also enables precise monitoring of disease progression and treatment response. Compared to conventional imaging techniques, OCT offers significant advantages, including real-time imaging, non-invasiveness, and high resolution, which open new possibilities for the clinical management of psoriatic nails.

In addition to OCT, high-frequency ultrasound of the nail bed has emerged as a valuable tool for evaluating psoriatic nail disease32,33. Ultrasound provides clear visualization of the nail plate, nail bed, nail matrix, and even the underlying phalanx, allowing clinicians to assess tissue thickness, vascular changes, and the presence of subungual hyperkeratosis. Its advantages include rapid acquisition, wide availability, and ease of use in routine practice. However, ultrasound may be less sensitive than OCT in detecting subtle and superficial changes, such as early thinning of the nail plate or small intra-plate cavities. Moreover, the need for proper gel coupling and probe positioning can introduce variability in results. By comparison, OCT offers higher resolution imaging of superficial structures, making it particularly suitable for detecting early or subclinical nail alterations. Taken together, both ultrasound and OCT provide complementary insights, and integrating these modalities may enhance the comprehensive evaluation of psoriatic nail involvement.

Histopathological analysis remains the gold standard for diagnosing nail psoriasis, as it allows direct visualization of keratinocyte changes, parakeratosis, and inflammatory infiltrates34. However, biopsies are invasive, technically challenging in the nail unit, and often avoided due to pain, risk of scarring, and cosmetic concerns. OCT offers a significant advantage in this context by providing real-time, non-invasive imaging that correlates well with histological findings, thus reducing the need for invasive procedures in many cases.

Several studies have underscored the relevance of OCT in skin and nail diseases. Aydin et al. and Abignano et al. demonstrated that OCT can accurately reflect microstructural changes in psoriatic nails and may even serve as a biomarker for treatment response28,29. Other reports confirm that OCT outperforms ultrasound in detecting early superficial changes, whereas ultrasound is superior in evaluating deeper structures. These findings are consistent with our results, which show that OCT effectively detected nail plate thinning, low-reflectivity regions, and interlayer separations during disease progression and recovery after treatment.

Clinically, the importance of nail involvement extends beyond cosmetic concerns. Nail psoriasis is strongly associated with psoriatic arthritis (PsA), and changes in the nail matrix and nail bed reflect underlying pathological processes such as enthesitis at the distal interphalangeal joint. Thus, accurate imaging of nail lesions has implications not only for dermatological evaluation but also for the early detection and monitoring of joint disease. By demonstrating that OCT can capture subtle structural changes and quantify recovery with treatment, this study adds to the growing body of evidence supporting OCT as a valuable adjunct to clinical and rheumatological practice.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size limited the statistical power, particularly in subgroup analyses of PsO versus PsA patients. Second, we did not include a control group of healthy individuals, which would have provided a stronger baseline for comparison of grayscale values and OCT structural features. Future studies should incorporate healthy controls to establish normative reference ranges. Third, although grayscale analysis provides a quantitative approach, the selection of regions of interest within OCT images may introduce some degree of subjectivity. Standardization of image acquisition and automated region selection using artificial intelligence may help minimize this variability. Finally, while OCT demonstrates clear advantages for detecting superficial structural changes, its limited penetration depth restricts the evaluation of deeper tissues compared with ultrasound. Addressing these limitations in future research will strengthen the robustness and clinical applicability of OCT-based assessments of psoriatic nail disease.

Future research should focus on several key areas, including the development of simplified OCT systems to reduce manufacturing and maintenance costs, thereby facilitating broader clinical adoption. Additionally, advancements in deep learning-based OCT image analysis could enable automated identification of nail abnormalities and quantitative assessment of disease severity, further enhancing the diagnostic utility of OCT technology in dermatology.
