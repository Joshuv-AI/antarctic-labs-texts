Health-related quality of life among healthcare workers: a comparative analysis using regression, conditional tree and forests
Authors: Fatemeh Rezaei chegini, Mozhgan Seif, Mohebat Vali, Haleh Ghaem, Seyed Jalil Masoumi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConsidering the potential importance of health care workers (HCWs) in maintaining and improving the health of society, we decided to investigate the factors affecting the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of HCWs using machine learning.MethodsThis study is a cross-sectional, population-based study that used baseline data from the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences’ Employees’ Cohort (SUMSEC), which consisted of 7073 individuals aged 20 to 70 years. To more accurately identify determinants of HRQoL, we applied multiple linear regression along with some machine learning algorithms, including conditional tree, conditional forest, and random forest. Then, the fit of these methods was compared using mean square error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), R2, and Pearson correlation coefficient.ResultsOn the test dataset, the multiple linear regression and conditional forest methods showed similar performance and produced more reliable predictions, with higher correlations and R² values, and lower MSE and RMSE than the random forest and decision tree methods. Moreover, the most important factors affecting the HRQoL of HCWs were sleep quality, underlying diseases, sex, and education.ConclusionsIn our study, multiple linear regression and conditional forest performed equally well. Therefore, the associations between predictor variables and HRQoL were likely simple. In addition, demographic, clinical, and socioeconomic factors influence the HRQoL of HCWs. Recognizing and addressing these factors through targeted interventions and supportive policies can help improve the overall well-being and resilience of the HCWs.Keywords: Health-related quality of life, Health care workers, Conditional tree, Conditional forest, Random forest

Background

Health care workers (HCWs) are the backbone of any health care system [1]. HCWs play a vital role in maintaining and promoting public health as well as training future generations of medical professionals [2]. The individual health status and life satisfaction of HCWs affect not only their own health-related quality of life (HRQoL) but also their professional abilities to meet healthcare needs, provide necessary facilities to the public, and ultimately the quality of the services they provide [3, 4]. HRQoL is a multidimensional construct that includes an individual’s subjective assessment of physical, mental, and social well-being in relation to health [5]. HRQoL is both a predictor and an outcome and is indeed a good measure of overall health [6–8]. Numerous studies have been conducted in this field around the world. They have shown that multiple factors can negatively influence the HRQoL of HCWs [9–16]. These include sociodemographic variables such as sex, marital status, and educational attainment [7, 11]; work-related characteristics such as professional role and long working hours [12], as well as clinically relevant factors like chronic illness, Sleep quality, and psychological stress [9, 10, 13]. While these studies have advanced our understanding of the determinants of HRQoL, most have taken a narrow view, focusing on specific clinical professions, particularly nurses [14–16], physicians [17, 18] or teaching-administrative staff [19, 20], and often overlooking the diverse array of roles that sustain medical science institutions [21]. Moreover, the methodological approaches in most previous studies have relied on traditional statistical methods [3, 16, 22], which may struggle to capture the complex and nonlinear associations among factors influencing HRQoL. In contrast, machine learning approaches can method these intricate patterns more effectively and make more accurate predictions [23]. Unlike regression models that assume linear relationships between variables, algorithms such as decision tree, random forest (Rforest), and conditional forest (Cforest) can automatically detect nonlinear interactions without having to specify them in advance [24]. These methods can also provide measures of variable importance, information that can be hidden in regression analysis. Therefore, this study was conducted by adopting a more comprehensive scope, including clinical, administrative, and educational staff, and using machine learning algorithms, including conditional inference tree (Ctree), Rforest, and Cforest to identify factors influencing HRQoL in HCWs.

Methods

Introducing the SUMSEC and study design

This study is a cross-sectional, population-based study that used baseline data from the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences’ Employee’s Cohort (SUMSEC), which consisted of 7073 individuals aged 20 to 70 years. SUMSEC is part of the PERSIAN Cohort and has been operating since August 2018 [25]. All HCWs, whether permanent, contract, temporary, or outsourced, were invited to participate in this study at a clinic equipped for this purpose [26].

Independent variables and data collection process

We used the variables of the SUMSEC study questionnaires to predict the factors affecting the HRQoL of HCWs. These independent variables include demographic variables (such as age, sex, marital status, education, etc.), socioeconomic status (SES), employment status (permanent, contract, temporary, and outsourced), job position (educational and administrative staff, healthcare staff, and support services staff), shift work, history of underlying diseases (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and hepatitis), other indicators including blood pressure, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), waist circumference, HDL cholesterol and triglyceride, physical activity, sleep quality, smoking, and alcohol status.

SES was calculated using the propensity score method. Propensity scores are widely known in epidemiological research for their ability to reduce bias by balancing observed covariates between groups [27]. This variable was calculated by considering the type of ownership, the size of the residential unit, the number of people living in the house, the number of bedrooms, access to a bathroom and toilet, the use of a mobile phone, laptop, or computer, the Internet, the type of vehicle and its price, the number of non-textbooks read, the number of domestic and international trips, and the presence of appliances such as a washing machine, dishwasher, vacuum cleaner, and television at home. Then, the SES variable calculated using the propensity score was classified into 5 levels (very low, low, medium, high and very high). Clinical indicators such as blood pressure were created according to the definition of the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [28]. Central obesity was characterized by > 94 cm (men) or > 80 cm (women). FPG levels > 100 mg/dL or a prior diagnosis of type 2 diabetes indicate impaired glucose regulation. HDL cholesterol was considered low at < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women, or if the individual was receiving treatment for low HDL-C. Triglyceride levels > 150 mg/dL or specific treatment for this lipid abnormality, were used as a marker of lipid abnormality. Blood pressure was regarded as elevated with systolic values ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic values ≥ 85 mmHg, or if the participant had a history of hypertension under pharmacologic management. The underlying diseases variable was constructed to include individuals with any self-reported chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, cancer, chronic pulmonary diseases, thyroid disorders, hepatitis, and kidney diseases, excluding diseases previously defined under the IDF criteria. Shift work was classified as regular (at certain hours on all days), rotating (some days working day and other days working night), and irregular (unpredictable working hours depending on the conditions and workload).

Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Long Form (IPAQ-LF). This instrument assesses physical activity in four domains (occupational, transportation, household, and leisure) and, based on the IPAQ scoring protocol, participants were classified into low, moderate, and high levels of physical activity [29, 30]. Sleep quality was measured using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), which has been validated and reliable in Iran. This questionnaire examines individuals’ attitudes about sleep quality over the past four weeks in 7 components. The components include: the individual’s general description of sleep quality, delay in falling asleep, duration of useful sleep, sleep adequacy, sleep disorders, amount of sleeping medication used, and morning performance (defined as problems experienced by the individual during the day due to poor sleep). Its scoring is such that the minimum and maximum scores considered for each component range from 0 (no problem) to 3 (very serious problem). Finally, the scores for each component are added together and converted into an overall score (0 to 21). Based on the overall score, individuals are divided into two groups: those whose overall score is higher than 5 have poor sleep quality, and those whose overall score is lower than 5 have good and adequate sleep quality [31]. The protocol of this study was approved by the ethics committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SBMU.RETECH.REC.1400.713) according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all individuals for the use of their data for research.

Measurement of the dependent variable

As mentioned, HRQoL is our dependent variable, which was measured using the World Health Organization Quality of Life – Brief Version (WHOQOL-BREF), which has also been validated and reliable in Iran [32]. This questionnaire consists of 26 questions and has 4 domains (physical, psychological, social relationships, and social environment). This tool was measured through face-to-face interviews with participants, where respondents provided their answers using a five-point Likert scale. The overall score of the questionnaire was obtained by summing all the questions in the questionnaire ranging from 36 to 130, with a higher score indicating a higher HRQoL [33].

Statistical analysis

In this study, our goal was to identify the factors affecting the HRQoL of HCWs using the linear regression model that was used in previous studies and to use machine learning algorithm methods such as Ctree, Cforest, and Rforest that are capable of investigating nonlinear and complex relationships.

Multiple linear regression. Multiple linear regression is used to examine the association of a normal quantitative variable with multiple independent variables [34]. Before fitting the multiple linear regression model, we evaluated potential multicollinearity between clinical predictor variables, including blood pressure and other clinically relevant measures. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values were calculated, and all variables demonstrated acceptable collinearity, with VIF values below 2 [35].

Conditional decision tree. Ctree is a statistical test-based decision tree algorithm that operates within the framework of conditional inference. When fitting a C-tree, the conditional distribution of statistics that measure the association between the response variable and the predictor variables is driven. Ctree first tests whether there is a statistical association between the response variable and the predictors using permutation tests. The variable with the smallest adjusted p-value is selected as the split variable, and the data is split accordingly. This process continues recursively until there is no statistically significant association or the nodes and tree depth reach a predetermined stopping threshold [36].

Conditional and Random Forests. Rforest and Cforest are among the most widely used ensemble learning methods that combine a large number of decision trees to improve prediction performance. The key difference between these two algorithms is the type of decision tree they use. Rforest is built on CART trees (Classification and Regression Trees), and Cforest is built on Ctree. An important parameter that affects the construction of trees for both methods is the number of candidate variables to split at each node. As suggested by Breiman, for regression trees, usually one-third of the total predictors are randomly selected, and for classification trees, the square root of the number of variables is selected. The output of a forest is usually reported as the importance of the variables, and in this study, the Cutler-Breiman VIMP permutation was used. This method calculates the effect of each variable using two different scenario: (1) a forest built using real data and (2) a forest grown with real data but permuted on the variable of interest. The importance of the variable is calculated from the difference in the prediction accuracy of the two forests. The greater this difference, the greater the influence of the variable of interest on the prediction [36–38].

In this study, the number of split candidates for each tree was considered to be one-third of the total number of predictors, but in practice, due to the low ratio of the number of variables to observations, all variables were considered as candidates in each split process. Also, 1000 trees were considered for each forest. To evaluate the performance of the methods, the data was also divided into two parts, training and testing, with a ratio of 70 to 30. The training set was used to train the methods. In fact, the methods are fitted on the training set; in other words, they learn from these data. Then, the performance of these methods was measured using the test set. Finally, the goodness of fit of the methods was evaluated by calculating and then comparing mean square error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), R2, and Pearson correlation coefficient between the predicted and observed values. We used RMSE because the unit of measurement of the error is the same as the unit of measurement of the observations.

Statistical analyses of this study were performed using Stata version 11 and R software version 4.2.0. The packages used are for variable selection in the “stepwise” linear regression model using the “MASS” package and machine learning methods through a partitioning algorithm consisting of a Ctree using the “Party” package, for drawing the Ctree shape from the “ggparty” package, Cforest using the “Party” package, and Rforest using the “randomforestSRC” package. P-values less than 0.05 were also considered statistically significant.

Results

Multiple linear regression model

This study was conducted on 7,073 participants included at the baseline of the SUMSEC. The mean ± SD of their HRQoL was 95.85 ± 11.47. Table 1 shows the factors associated with the HRQoL of HCWs using the linear regression statistical model. As can be seen, the quality of poor sleep decreases the HRQoL by 7.11 score (p-value < 0.001). HCWs with an underlying diseases (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and hepatitis) had a HRQoL score 3.14 units lower than healthy people (p-value < 0.001). Women constituted 57% of the population, and their HCWs were 1.81 score lower than men (p-value < 0.001). Education and SES were another factor related to the HRQoL, with HCWs with higher education having a 0.70 score and higher SES had 1.23 score higher HRQoL (p-value < 0.001). In addition to demographic and psychological factors, clinical factors such as low HDL cholesterol were also related to the HRQoL (p-value = 0.013). Low HDL cholesterol reduces the HRQoL by 0.64 score. Although nearly 20% of HCWs were obese, and obese individuals had a lower mean HRQoL score than normal- weight individuals (94.68 ± 11.48 vs. 96.13 ± 11.36), central obesity was not significantly associated with HRQoL (p-value = 0.121). We found that HCWs with irregular shifts had, on average, a better HRQoL than HCWs with regular, rotating shifts (97.08 ± 11.83 vs. 96.13 ± 11.59, 95.34 ± 11.23). According to the results of multiple linear regression, HCWs who used smoking had a 2.04 lower HRQoL score (p-value < 0.001). Only 5% of HCWs used alcohol; they had a 1.28 score lower HRQoL (p-value = 0.029).

Decision tree algorithm

A Ctree was also used to examine the factors affecting the HRQoL of HCWs (Fig. 1). The Ctree identified sleep quality as the most important predictor of HRQoL (p-value < 0.001). Participants with good sleep quality were further divided into next nodes based on the presence or absence of underlying diseases (p-value < 0.001). Other related factors in order of importance are smoking status, education, sex, high blood pressure, central obesity, physical activity, and SES. As can be seen in Fig. 1, HCWs with the highest (127) and lowest (39) HRQoL scores are located at nodes 12 and 23, respectively.

Conditional inference tree grown on predictors of HRQoL adapted from HCWs. At each terminal node, the box plot shows the distribution of HRQoL

Conditional forest algorithm

To better understand the predictors of HRQoL, we used Cforest to rank variables based on their importance. The results showed that the most important variable predicting the HRQoL is sleep quality, and in addition, underlying diseases (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and hepatitis), sex, and education were also identified as important variables. In fact, about 80% of the correct predictions were due to these four variables. Other predictors, in order of importance, were smoking status, SES, age, high blood pressure, high triglycerides, alcohol status, elevated FPG, physical activity, low HDL cholesterol, and central obesity (Fig. 2).

Random forest algorithm

In the Rforest method, as in the Cforest, sleep quality, underlying diseases (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and hepatitis), sex, and education were still identified as the most important predictors of HRQoL. Although their importance was estimated to be lower than in the Cforest method and was about 46%. According to Fig. 3, other predictors in order of importance are as follows: high blood pressure, alcohol status, smoking status, elevated FPG, high triglycerides, central obesity, physical activity, low HDL cholesterol, SES, and age. All important variables identified by the Rforest were also identified as significant by the Cforest.

Comparison of methods

As mentioned earlier, we have used MSE, RMSE, R2, and Pearson’s correlation coefficient to evaluate the accuracy and goodness of fit of different analysis methods in predicting the HRQoL and its related factors. As shown in Table 2, all indicators confirm each other. In the train set, the MSE and RMSE of Rforest are the lowest, while in the test set, the multiple linear regression is the lowest. To compare the methods based on the Pearson correlation coefficient, it has been shown that all the correlations between the observed and predicted values are significant with all methods and in both test and train sets. Based on the Pearson correlation coefficient, in the train set, Rforest has the highest value, and in the test set, multiple linear regression has the highest correlation between observed and predicted values ​​with small differences. The R2, like the other three indices, has the highest value in the training data and the highest value in the test data of the multiple linear regression. It is important to mention that the Rforest has many differences in the test and train sets, or in other words, this method is over-fitted in the train set. These results show that the random forest method may have learned the training data and overfit on the training data, and when faced with new data such as the test data set, it does not have the ability to perform the same, and in fact, its generalizability is reduced. In contrast, the Cforest showed more stable performance in both sets, probably because it uses statistical methods for split that help prevent overfitting. Moreover, the comparable performance of multiple linear regression suggests that the underlying associations between predictors and HRQoL in this dataset are approximately linear, allowing simpler methods to perform competitively with more complex ensemble methods. In the initial investigation based on both MSE and Pearson correlation coefficient in the test set, it can be concluded that multiple linear regression provides more accurate performance compared to machine learning methods. Although the test comparing the Pearson correlation coefficient from regression (0.38) and the correlation coefficient from Cforest (0.371) showed no statistically significant difference (p-value = 0.73), Similarly, the comparison of the MSE from regression (0.404) and the MSE from Cforest (0.540) in 2122 observations of the test data was not significant (p-value = 0.98), it can therefore be concluded that there is no significant difference in the goodness of fit between the multiple linear regression and Cforest methods.

Discussion

In this population-based study of the SUMSEC, which was conducted with the aim of investigating the factors affecting the HRQoL of HCWs using multiple linear regression model and machine learning algorithms Ctree, Rforest, and Cforest, we identified several determinants of HRQoL. In all methods, sleep quality, underlying diseases (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and hepatitis), sex, education, smoking status, SES, age, blood pressure, high triglycerides, alcohol status, elevated FPG, physical activity, low HDL cholesterol, and central obesity were associated with HRQoL.

The sleep quality variable was identified by all four methods (linear regression, Ctree and Rforest and Cforest) as the first and most important variable affecting the HRQoL of HCWs. Poor sleep quality can compromise both personal health and patient care by reducing cognitive function and increasing burnout [39]. Therefore, implementing sleep quality interventions such as reducing night shifts, providing rest areas at work, and providing sleep hygiene education could have beneficial effects on HRQoL and work performance of HCWs [40]. These findings are also consistent with previous research showing that poor sleep quality can reduce HRQoL in various populations, including healthcare teaching staff [40–43]. Since this is a cross-sectional study, it may be HRQoL that strongly and clearly affects the sleep quality of individuals, or it may be that sleep quality is a proxy for the HRQoL of individuals. Some studies have shown that people who experience poor HRQoL for various reasons may also suffer from reduced sleep quality [44, 45].

Underlying diseases (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and hepatitis) ranked second, indicating the importance of managing chronic diseases. This result is consistent with existing studies showing that chronic diseases affect work performance and HRQoL [46–48]. Chronic disease management, such as regular screenings and targeted interventions for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, can help reduce the incidence of chronic diseases and improve HRQoL [49]. After chronic diseases, sociodemographic factors such as gender, education, and SES status also ranked high in the importance of variables in all four methods. These findings may be due to the influence of gender differences and the role of SES factors on HRQoL. For example, women tend to report lower HRQoL scores in epidemiological studies, which may be because women have a higher threshold for what constitutes an HRQoL than men and therefore are more likely than men to perceive their lives as poor and give them lower scores [50]. Continuing education of HCWs, especially women, can increase the likelihood of informed choices about health and ultimately enhance personal growth and HRQoL. SES, as a multidimensional indicator, has a direct and indirect impact on HRQoL. Individuals with higher economic status have higher and better access to health services and a healthy lifestyle [51, 52]. In addition to these variables, education, as a factor that enables individuals to make better health decisions, has been associated with higher HRQoL, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [51, 53]. Behavioral factors, including smoking and alcohol status, were negatively associated with HRQoL, which reaffirms the importance of behavioral health and workplace health programs. smoking and alcohol status are major risk factors for cardiovascular disease and mental disorders, and their association with HRQoL has been shown in studies [54, 55].

Clinical factors such as high blood pressure, elevated FPG, high triglycerides, and low HDL cholesterol were also identified as factors affecting HRQoL, which is consistent with previous studies [56–58]. These factors increase the incidence of chronic diseases and ultimately reduce HRQoL [56]. These findings suggest that managing metabolic factors can directly improve HRQoL. These findings suggest that workplace health programs focusing on nutrition, physical activity, and metabolic health monitoring may be very beneficial in maintaining a healthy workforce.

In our study, multiple linear regression and Cforest methods performed equally well on the test data. Both methods represented the associations between predictor variables and the response variable well. Existing studies have shown that Cforest can outperform multiple linear regression, especially when the number of independent variables is large and the associations are nonlinear [59]. In such situations, because linear regression may suffer from problems such as multicollinearity when the number of independent variables increases or is only capable of linear relationships, machine learning algorithms with more flexibility such as parameter tuning and the ability to learn nonlinear relationships can better identify hidden patterns. In some cases, multiple linear regression studies have shown relative superiority over machine learning methods due to their simplicity, ability to adjust more precisely and, in cases where there are strong and known predictor variables [60, 61].

In this study, although our data were large (7073 participants), the associations between variables were likely to be mostly simple, and the association between independent variables and the response variable was not very complex. This may have caused a simpler method such as linear regression to perform equally well with Cforest. In general, our results show that the choice of method strongly depends on the nature of the data and the associations between variables. Given that the associations between the independent variable and the response do not seem to be particularly complex in our study, the use of other machine learning algorithms that are usually used to find complex associations is not recommended in this context, but it is suggested to use multivariate methods to increase the power of analysis and due to the correlation between the dimensions of HRQoL.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study was conducted using baseline data from the SUMSEC study, with a large sample size (7073 participants). The use of new data analysis methods, such as machine learning algorithms, increased the accuracy of the findings and provided an innovative approach to health research. However, the dependence of the data on self-reported HRQoL questionnaires creates the possibility of response bias. In addition, the voluntary nature of participation may have resulted in self-selection bias. Other limitations of this study include the omission of certain variables, such as nutrition and psychological status. So the generalizability of the findings should be considered with caution.

Conclusion

In our study, multiple linear regression and Cforest methods performed equally well and outperformed the Ctree and Rforest methods. Therefore, the associations between predictor variables and HRQoL were likely simple and not complex. Factors identified that influence the HRQoL of HCWs include: clinical and demographic factors such as sleep quality, underlying diseases (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and hepatitis), and sex. These findings highlight the importance of targeted workplace wellness programs aimed at improving sleep health and chronic disease management among HCWs. Policymakers should prioritize interventions that modify these factors to enhance well-being and resilience in the healthcare workforce. Future studies should continue to examine factors such as how organizational culture and workload impact HRQoL to guide more effective interventions in the future.
