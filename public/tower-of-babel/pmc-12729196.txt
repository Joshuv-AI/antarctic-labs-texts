Air pollution promotes the onset and progression of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome: a nationwide prospective cohort study
Authors: Chen Gui, Zhuotao Xiao, Mohan Li, Junwei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAir pollution and cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome have emerged as major global public health challenges. However, limited evidence exists on the association between air pollution exposure and CKM incidence and progression.MethodWe analyzed data from participants across CKM stages 0–5 enrolled in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. Ambient air pollutant concentrations were obtained from the China High Air Pollutants database. Binary logistic regression and weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression models were applied to assess the impacts of individual and combined air pollutants on CKM incidence. Cumulative exposure models were employed to study the long-term effects of pollutants on CKM progression. CKM stages were modeled as ordered categorical outcomes using proportional odds models to examine the association between pollutant levels and CKM progression.ResultsA total of 4,026 individuals were included in the main analysis. Each interquartile range increase in particulate matter with aerodynamic diameters ≤ 1 μm (PM1; OR = 1.008, 95% CI: 1.001–1.015, p < 0.05), ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5; OR = 1.009, 95% CI: 1.002–1.015, p < 0.01 ), ≤ 10 μm (PM10; OR = 1.010, 95% CI: 1.004–1.016, p < 0.01), nitrogen dioxide (NO2; OR = 1.011, 95% CI:1.004–1.018, p < 0.01), and ozone (O3; OR = 1.014, 95% CI: 1.006–1.021, p < 0.01) was significantly associated with increased CKM risk. The combined effect of multiple pollutants, reflected by the WQS index, was associated with a 77.7% higher risk of CKM incidence (OR = 1.777, 95% CI: 1.143–2.762, p < 0.05). PM10 (OR = 1.160, 95% CI: 1.059–1.270, p < 0.05) and sulfur dioxide (SO2; OR = 1.122, 95% CI: 1.018–1.236, p < 0.05) were significant contributors to CKM progression. In the cumulative effect analysis of 2,146 individuals, 5-year cumulative PM10 exposure was positively associated with CKM progression (OR = 1.027, 95% CI: 1.001–1.055, p < 0.05).ConclusionAir pollution is a significant environmental determinant of both CKM incidence and progression. Introduction

Air pollution is a leading risk factor for all-cause mortality, contributing to an estimated 4.14 million deaths worldwide in 2019 [1] and accounting for 8% of total disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2021 [2]. The detrimental influence of air pollution is particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income countries, where rapid urbanization and industrialization drive persistently high levels of air pollution exposure. Middle-aged and older adults face disproportionate risks owing to cumulative pollutant exposure and age-related declines in physiological resilience [3]. Among all air pollutants, particulate matter with aerodynamic diameters ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5) warrants particular concern, since over 90% of the population resides in areas where PM2.5 concentrations exceed the World Health Organization Air Quality Guideline (10 µg/m³) [4]. Long-term PM2.5 exposure contributes to approximately 3.5 million cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related deaths annually [5], 7 million cases of chronic kidney disease (CKD), and up to 20% of the type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) worldwide [6, 7]. Other air pollutants including PM1, PM10, sulfur dioxide (SO2), ozone (O3), and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) also challenge environmental health status, though their biological pathways are less well established [8–10]. Nonetheless, emerging evidence points to shared mechanisms, such as oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and metabolic dysregulation, through which diverse air pollutants may contribute to multi-organ injury [11, 12].

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a complex systemic disorder characterized by intricate pathophysiological interactions among cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic systems, ultimately leading to multi-organ impairment and substantially heightened CVD risks [13–16]. Clinically, CKM syndrome manifests initially with subtle metabolic changes and can progress to severe cardiovascular and kidney complications. The 2023 American Heart Association (AHA) scientific statement emphasized the importance of detecting and managing CKM stages 0–3 to prevent CVD through lifestyle modification, and physical activity (PA) is an established and evidence-based intervention with pronounced preventive effects against CKM [17–19]. However, the statement has not considered environmental determinants such as air pollution, despite their potential to affect CKM incidence and progression. Given that CKM syndrome is a recently proposed construct, its relationship with environmental risk factors remains largely unexplored, underscoring an important scientific gap.

China offers a critical setting to examine these relationships. With 1.4 billion people, the country is experiencing rapid population aging and increasing prevalence of T2DM, CVD, and CKD, contributing to a growing but underrecognized CKM burden. Additionally, severe air pollution linked to rapid industrial development may further exacerbate CKM progression [20]. This convergence makes China a natural laboratory for assessing whether ambient pollutants accelerate CKM progression in middle-aged and older adults. Given these dual challenges, our study aims to investigate the association between ambient air pollutants and CKM syndrome among Chinese adults. We further evaluate whether improvements in air quality can mitigate the adverse effects of air pollution on CKM syndrome. This study will provide evidence-based insights to inform CKM prevention and management strategies in high-pollution environments, which may help reduce disease burden and healthcare costs in China by limiting CKM progression. Additionally, we explored the role of PA in CKM incidence and progression within the context of air pollution exposure.

Methods

Study population

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), an interview-based database of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years collected between 2011 and 2012 [21]. The original cohort included 10,257 households across 450 communities in 150 counties (districts), covering 28 provinces. CHARLS employed a multi-stage, stratified, and probability-proportional-to-size sampling design, with biennial follow-up surveys. The study was approved by Peking University’s Biomedical Ethics Review Committee (IRB00001052-11015), with written informed consent obtained from all participants.

For the present analysis, we utilized follow-up data derived from the 2015 wave, which included complete assessments of CKM syndrome. Eligible participants were those aged ≥ 45 years with complete records on CKM status, air pollutant concentrations, and relevant covariates. We excluded individuals with missing data for any of these variables, as well as those individuals < 45 years. To assess the cumulative effects of air pollutants on CKM stage transitions, we further restricted the sample to participants with complete CKM records for both 2011 and 2015 and excluded those residing in areas with missing air pollution data during that period. Definition of CKM syndrome stages

The CKM syndrome was defined and staged according to the AHA Presidential Advisory Statement (Fig. 1B) [17]: (1) Stage 0: Absence of CKM risk factors (normal weight, glucose, blood pressure, lipids, and kidney function; no evidence of subclinical or clinical CVD). (2) Stage 1: Presence of excess adiposity (identified by weight or abdominal obesity and/or dysfunctional adiposity and reflected by impaired glucose tolerance or hyperglycemia). (3) Stage 2: Presence of one or more metabolic risk factors including hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, and/or moderate- to high-risk CKD. (4) Stage 3: Subclinical CVD in the presence of CKM risk factors overlapping with high-risk CKD, or high predicted risk using the CKM risk calculator. (5) Stage 4: Clinical CVD, including coronary heart disease, heart failure, or stroke.

Air pollutants assessment

Daily concentrations of PM1, PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, and O3 were sourced from the China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) database (https://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html). The CHAP database is generated using a sophisticated space-time extremely randomized trees model that integrates meteorological data, land use characteristics, emission inventories, and demographic information [22, 23]. Predictions are available at a spatial resolution of 1 × 1 km for PM1, PM2.5, PM10, and O3, and at 10 × 10 km for NO2 and SO2. Validation using ten-fold cross-validation demonstrated high accuracy (R² = 0.80–0.92) [22, 23], the 2015 city-level distribution of air pollutants was shown in supplementary Fig. 1.

City-level average exposure was assigned to participants based on residential location. For the main analysis, we used 1-year average pollutant concentrations for the survey year to represent current exposure. For cumulative exposure analyses (2011–2015), daily pollutant concentrations were summed for each city and participant based on the method described by CHAP database. To enhance the interpretability of the effect sizes, pollutant concentration and cumulative concentration were standardized by dividing them by their interquartile range (IQR), respectively [24].

Physical activity (PA) assessment

PA was assessed using a modified International Physical Activity Questionnaire, where participants reported the weekly frequency (days/week), duration, and intensity (vigorous, moderate, or mild) of activities lasting ≥ 10 min. Activity duration was categorized as: (1) < 30 min/day; (2) 30–119 min/day; (3) 120–239 min/day; and (4) ≥ 240 min/day. Median values were assigned to each category (0, 20, 75, 180, and 240 min, respectively) following established methodology [25–27].

Weekly PA intensity was expressed as metabolic equivalent (MET), a standardized unit used to estimate the amount of energy expended during physical activity, relative to resting energy expenditure: 8.0 for vigorous, 4.0 for moderate, and 3.3 for mild activity. The total weekly PA volume (MET-minutes/week) was calculated as: (8.0 × vigorous PA time) + (4.0 × moderate PA time) + (3.3 × mild PA time).

Covariates

To adjust for potential confounding factors, covariates included demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and clinical variables. Demographic variables included age, sex (male/female), residence (urban/rural), region (eastern/central/western China), and marital status (married with spouse, married without spouse, single/divorced/widowed). Socioeconomic variables included education level (elementary school or below/middle school or above). Health behavioral factors consisted of smoking status, drinking status, and household fuel type. Additionally, clinical variables included body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), fasting blood glucose (FBG), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP/DBP), and C-reactive protein (CRP).

Statistics

Categorical variables were presented as counts (n) and percentages (%). All continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed in their original metric without categorization to preserve statistical power. Pollutant concentrations were scaled by their IQR.

Binary logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between individual air pollutants and CKM incidence (presence/absence), with odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated. For assessing the cumulative effects of air pollutants on CKM stage progression by binary logistic regression models, we treated CKM stage transitions as a binary outcome; no progression was defined as no change in CKM stage between 2011 and 2015, and the progression was defined as higher CKM stage in 2015 compared to 2011. Three hierarchical models were constructed: (1) Model 1 included the pollutant concentration only; (2) Model 2 adjusted for demographic variables (age, BMI, sex, residence, education level, and marital status, regional classification); and (3) Model 3 further incorporated behavioral and clinical variables (smoking, alcohol consumption, TG, LDL, HDL, FBG, CRP, and SBP/DBP). Sensitivity analyses were conducted using restricted cubic splines (RCS) to assess potential nonlinear relationships.

Weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression was applied to evaluate combined effects of multiple air pollutants on CKM syndrome. Covariates were adjusted as in the binary logistic regression models.

To examine the association between pollutant levels and CKM staging progression, CKM stages were modeled as ordered categorical outcomes using proportional odds models.

The Karlson-Holm-Breen method was employed to quantify mediation effects of PA (METs) on air pollutants and CKM syndrome, which addresses the scaling heterogeneity problem in nonlinear probability models [28]. Effect modification was tested via stratified proportional odds models with interaction terms.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.0.3). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparative analyses using CKM stage 0 as the reference group demonstrated that participants with advanced CKM stages showed elevated levels of TG, LDL, FBG, SBP, DBP, and CRP, alongside decreased HDL and MET values. These findings confirmed the metabolic and cardiovascular profiles associated with different CKM stages within our study population. Intriguingly, environmental exposure assessment indicated that participants in advanced CKM stages were consistently exposed to higher residential concentrations of all measured air pollutants (PM1, PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, and O3) compared with those in stage 0.

Air pollutants contribute to CKM incidence

Binary logistic regression models identified all six air pollutants as significant predictors of CKM incidence, with or without covariate adjustment (Fig. 2). In both unadjusted and adjusted models, PM1 (OR = 1.008, 95% CI: 1.001–1.015, p < 0.05), PM2.5 (OR = 1.009, 95% CI: 1.002–1.015, p < 0.01), PM10 (OR = 1.010, 95% CI: 1.004–1.016, p < 0.01), NO2 (OR = 1.011, 95% CI:1.004–1.018, p < 0.01), and O3 (OR = 1.014, 95% CI: 1.006–1.021, p < 0.01) showed statistically significant positive associations with CKM incidence. SO2 was significant in Models 1 (OR = 1.007, 95% CI: 1.001–1.013, p < 0.05) and Model 2 (OR = 1.009, 95% CI: 1.002–1.015, p < 0.01) but turned insignificant after full covariate adjustment (OR = 1.006, 95% CI: 0.999–1.013, p = 0.085). RCS sensitivity analyses indicated non-linear relationships with NO2 and CKM syndrome (supplementary Fig. 2), and the sensitivity analyses excluding the covariates which have been integral to CKM’s definition and stratification showed the same trend of incidence risk (supplementary Table 2).

Combined and cumulative effects of air pollutants enhance the risk of CKM

WQS regression analysis revealed a significant positive association between multiple air pollutants and CKM risk across both unadjusted and adjusted models (Fig. 3). In fully adjusted models, each level increase in the WQS index was associated with a 77.7% higher CKM risk (OR = 1.777, 95% CI: 1.143–2.762, p = 0.033), Fig. 3A. NO2 emerged as the primary contributor (52.9%) of the total weight, followed by PM10 (33.5%), with the remaining pollutants (PM1, PM2.5, SO2, and O3) contributing minimally to the overall effect (< 7.4% for each), Fig. 3B.

Five-year cumulative exposure analyses demonstrated that only PM10 was significantly associated with CKM progression (OR = 1.027, 95% CI: 1.001–1.055, p = 0.046) after full covariate adjustments (Fig. 4). However, cumulative exposures to other pollutants showed no significant associations with CKM risk.

Air pollutants promote CKM progression while PA reduces the risk

Proportional odds ordinal logistic regression showed that PM2.5, PM10, and SO2 were associated with CKM progression (Fig. 5). In unadjusted models, only PM10 demonstrated significant association with CKM progression (OR = 1.096, 95% CI: 1.009–1.191, p < 0.05). After adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and clinical variables, the association even strengthened (OR = 1.160, 95% CI: 1.059–1.270, p < 0.01), indicating a 16.0% increased risk of CKM progression per IQR increase in PM10 exposure. Adjusted models also identified SO2 as significantly associated with CKM progression (OR = 1.122, 95% CI: 1.018–1.236, p < 0.05). PM2.5 approached statistical significance in adjusted model (OR = 1.092, 95% CI: 0.995–1.198, p = 0.063), whereas NO2, O3, and PM1 showed no significant associations in either model. Conversely, in both models, each IQR increase in PA (MET) was significantly associated with a 10.5% reduction in the risk of CKM progression. The sensitivity analyses excluding the covariates which have been integral to CKM’s definition and stratification showed the same trend of progression risk (supplementary Table 3). However, mediation effect and modification analyses of physical activity did not reach significance (supplementary Fig. 3; supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

This study unveiled the association between air pollutants exposure and the incidence and progression of CKM syndrome in middle-aged and older adults. Specifically, PM2.5, PM10, and SO2 were identified as risk factors that may accelerate the progression of CKM syndrome from early to advanced stages, while PA was independently associated with a lower CKM risk.

Increasing air pollutant concentrations contribute to both CKM incidence and progression. In the early stages, excess adiposity leads to chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance, forming a pathological cycle that promotes CKM progression [29–31]. Air pollutants can exacerbate these processes. Importantly, preclinical studies have shown that air pollutants exposure elevates circulating inflammatory markers, upregulates oxidative stress-related gene expression, and exacerbates insulin resistance in animal models, paralleling key pathways observed in human CKM pathogenesis [32–36]. Long-term exposure to PM2.5 is remarkably linked to higher risks of myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, and even cardiovascular mortality [5, 37–39]. Meta-analyses and large cohort studies consistently report that increases in PM10, PM2.5, and NO2 are linked to elevated CVD and metabolic risks including obesity, T2DM, and CKD [6, 40–43]. In this study, we observed an approximately 1% increase in CKM incidence and progression per IQR elevation in pollutant concentrations. While modest at the individual level, these effects are substantial at the population level, particularly given China’s large population and high ambient pollution. These findings underscore the importance of air pollution as an environmental risk factor for CKM, with significant implications for public health and disease prevention strategies.

Cumulative and combined exposure to air pollutants further elevated CKM risk. PM10 represents a complex mixture of particles with diameters up to 10 micrometers, containing biologically active components such as ultrafine particles, transition metals, and endotoxins, which induce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress similar to PM2.5 [44–46]. Long-term exposure to PM10 is associated with elevated TG levels, potentially promoting hyperlipidemia or obesity, which could be the early contributor for CKM incidence. Using the cumulative concentrations of air pollutants from 2011 to 2015 to represent long-term exposure to air pollution. We found that long-term exposure to PM10 significantly accelerated CKM progression. To examine the combined effects of multiple air pollutants on CKM syndrome, we employed WQS, which standardizes pollutants through IQR and combines them into a single index. We estimated the weight of each pollutant through regression analysis, and the WQS score was calculated by summing the products of pollutant weights and their IQR. In our study, WQS indices ranged from 0 to 3, with each 1-unit increase associated with a 77% higher CKM risk, significantly greater than the impact of single air pollutants on CKM syndrome. This reflects both the cumulative impact and synergistic interactions among multiple air pollutants [47, 48].

Lifestyle modifications, especially PA, remain a cornerstone for preventing CKM progression. CKM syndrome at stage 1 is characterized by obesity, which triggers inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance, key drivers of disease progression. Our findings indicated that moderate PA reduced the risk of CKM progression by 10.5%, likely through its benefits in weight management and metabolic improvement [49–51]. However, exposure to high pollutant levels during exercise may attenuate these benefits. For example, vigorous outdoor activity in high-ozone environments has been linked to a 50% higher diabetes incidence compared with sedentary or moderate activity [52–54]. Our mediation analysis found that PA provides only marginal protection against CKM progression related to PM10, PM2.5, and SO2, with p-values approaching but not reaching statistical significance, and no interaction effects were found in all kinds of air pollutants. Future studies with larger sample sizes, refined models, and better control of confounders are needed to clarify these effects and deepen our understanding of how PA may mitigate the impact of air pollution on CKM syndrome.

Limitation

Several limitations should be noted. First, although we established associations between air pollutant exposure and CKM incidence and progression, randomized controlled trials are challenging in this context, and causal inference remains limited. Longitudinal cohort studies, instrumental variable techniques, and machine learning may offer feasible alternatives to better explore causality. Secondly, the study population was derived from a Chinese cohort with a relatively modest sample size, and the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups requires further validation. Thirdly, the retrospective design of our study may introduce recall bias and limit the ability to establish causal relationships, and the questionnaire-based data may introduce reporting bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of exposure assessments and self-reported data. Finally, using regional average air pollution exposure levels to represent individual exposure within the population may lead to bias, as it may not capture specific individual-level variability.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that air pollution may be a significant environmental factor influencing both the incidence and progression of CKM syndrome. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating air quality improvement into CKM prevention strategies as part of public health policy. Additionally, PA remains an effective strategy for preventing CKM syndrome, although its protective effects might diminish in the context of air pollution. Consequently, further research is needed to quantify the impact of PA on the association between air pollution and CKM syndrome, and to evaluate the potential benefits of incorporating exercise guidance into clinical recommendations.
