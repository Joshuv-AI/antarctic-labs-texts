A netnographic analysis of online active commentaries on Malaysia’s tobacco generation end game
Authors: Noor Asilati Abdul Raob, Nawwal Alwani Mohd Radzi, Mohamad Haniki Nik Mohamed, Budi Aslinie Md Sabri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Generation End Game (GEG) policy in Malaysia aimed at establishing a smoke-free generation, and the announcement has since sparked significant public response. This study analysed Malaysian active commenters' perceptions expressed in social media comments on online news articles concerning the Generation End Game (GEG) policy, from the initial announcement of the implementation plan to its eventual removal from the Control of Smoking Products for Public Health Bill 2023.MethodsThis cross-sectional study employed qualitative and quantitative methods and used an inductive approach and thematic content analysis to analyse online commentaries on news articles published on popular online news portals from January 2022 to January 2024. Data was collected by downloading the commentaries onto Microsoft Word and importing them into NVivo.ResultsA total of 7450 commentaries were analysed across three distinct timeframes: the initial announcement, the official announcement and the period following the removal of the GEG provision from the Bill. The analysis revealed that support for the policy accounted for 43.0%, 70.4%, and 67.9% of commentaries across the respective timeframes, while opposition was noted with 57.0%, 29.6%, and 32.1% respectively. ConclusionsAlthough initial reactions to the GEG policy were largely negative, the increase in support following the official announcement and subsequent policy changes suggests that transparent communication, political developments, and public discourse significantly influence the policy’s acceptance. These findings highlight the dynamic nature of active commenters' opinions in response to policy developments and underscore the importance of engaging the public early and consistently when introducing major health policies.Keywords: Tobacco control, Tobacco endgame, Tobacco policy, Generation end game

Introduction

The Generation End Game (GEG) policy was introduced by the Malaysian government in January 2022 as a strategic measure to eliminate tobacco use by prohibiting the sale of tobacco and vape products to individuals born in 2007 and thereafter. This bold initiative, part of the original Control of Tobacco Product and Smoking Bill 2022, sought to establish a “tobacco-free generation” and reduce national smoking prevalence to below 5% by 2040 [1]. The Generation End Game (GEG) provision was initially included in the Control of Tobacco Product and Smoking Bill 2022, which was tabled in the Dewan Rakyat but stalled following the dissolution of the 14th Parliament. The Bill was later reintroduced as the Control of Smoking Products for Public Health Bill 2023 on 12 June 2023, with several amendments [2, 3]. The plan was anticipated to have a significant impact on the prevention and control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NCD) and further protect future generations from any harm and risks caused by smoking [4].

In November 2023, the GEG provision was excluded from the Control of Smoking Products for Public Health Bill after the Attorney General’s Chambers (AGC) cautioned that it could face constitutional challenges under Article 8 of the Federal Constitution [5]. The timeline for the GEG in Malaysia is shown in Table 1. Although aggressive and ambitious, the Generation End Game policy represents an innovative and forward-looking public health measure. If implemented effectively, it holds the potential to substantially reduce smoking prevalence and curb the growing vaping epidemic among adolescents. While the GEG policy gained substantial support for its potential to curb tobacco initiation and reduce long-term health burdens, it also faced criticism concerning feasibility, ethics, enforcement, and socio-economic consequences. It raised complex issues surrounding individual rights, intergenerational fairness, cultural norms, and commercial interests [6]. Despite its exclusion from legislation, the GEG continues to shape public health discourse and provides a foundation for revisiting generational tobacco bans through evidence-based, adaptive policy-making.

The Generation End Game (GEG) policy received significant media attention, particularly from newspapers that have increasingly shifted to digital platforms. This transition reflects global trends, with around 40% of internet users accessing news through online newspapers [7]. The media plays a crucial role in shaping public opinion on policies, particularly tobacco control, by framing discourse and contextualising public health issues. Policy coverage articles published on online media platforms serve as valuable sources for identifying supportive and opposing narratives, as well as the challenges encountered by stakeholders in advancing tobacco control agendas. Studies have employed content analysis to examine media coverage of health policies [8, 9]. Effective media engagement is essential, as such policies often rely on strong public support [10]. Gathering public feedback is vital in order to align health policies and services with community needs. Analysing content from news media offers a timely, cost-effective alternative to traditional methods while providing valuable public insights [11, 12]. Online newspapers have a chance to cover a wide range of topics regarding the GEG and be explored from various angles, either from the health or economic side. Thus, it also has the capacity to educate readers and create public opinion about the GEG.

Addressing public concerns and preferences is crucial for formulating and implementing effective public health strategies. The media undoubtedly have a substantial influence on public opinion [13]. McCombs and Shaw’s agenda-setting theory elucidates how mass media influences the political agenda by determining the extent and prominence of specific issues in media coverage. It further affects the issues that captivate the public’s perception. Framing theory proposes that the media establish frames that influence how those issues are portrayed and influence subjects on the political agenda [14, 15]. Media portrayals influence audience members’ construction of their individual-level frames, which, in turn, shape their attitudes toward the problem, the cause, and the proposed solution. The public’s attention to an issue has been shown to correlate with media coverage of that issue, and media frames have been demonstrated to influence recipients’ evaluations and decision-making. Mass media coverage influences which issues the public is aware of and their attitudes towards those issues through agenda-setting and framing processes. Since public perceptions are primarily shaped by what they see in the media, advocates and policymakers in the field of public health must be aware of how the media frames specific topics. An essential stage in problem framing is identifying the stakeholders affected by the issue and who will be influenced by any proposed solutions.

The GEG policy has been a controversial and divisive issue in Malaysia, sparking strong opinions and debates across multiple platforms. Previous studies have explored perceptions of the GEG among adolescents and experts, offering valuable insights into awareness, knowledge, and policy acceptability [6, 16]. However, there has been no research examining how the GEG has been represented in the media or how public sentiment has evolved across its policy milestones. In particular, the role of online narratives and digital commentaries in shaping trust, legitimacy, and acceptance of this unprecedented policy has remained unexplored. This gap is critical because media discourse and public opinion play a central role in influencing policy support and implementation. By adopting a netnographic approach, this study addresses this limitation by systematically analysing online news coverage and active commenters, thereby providing novel insights into the sociocultural, political, and governance factors that shaped the contestation and eventual removal of the GEG provision. The objective of this study was to analyse the Malaysian active commenters perceptions commenting on the online news articles in social media regarding the GEG policy after the announcement towards the plan of action.

Methods

This study employed an exploratory qualitative netnographic approach focused on identifying dominant frames and discourses surrounding the GEG policy. The analysis was designed to capture patterns of online narratives and the findings are therefore presented as reflective of discourse within the sampled online environments. The aim of this research is to provide an in-depth analysis of active online commenter’s views on this topic, and the findings will provide an in-depth exploration of views surrounding the GEG in Malaysia. Analysis of readers’ comments has previously been used to assess opinions on the acceptability of other potentially controversial public health interventions, with findings similar to those from other forms of qualitative data and suggests that using reader comments can produce findings that are transferable to different data sources [11].

Ethical

This study adhered to the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) guidelines for responsible research using publicly available online data, ensuring anonymity, confidentiality, and appropriate aggregation of findings.

Overview

Online news articles and the accompanying online commentaries from the readers in the posting were collected over a two-year period from January 2022 to January 2024, corresponding with key phases of the Generation End Game (GEG) policy progression and eventual discontinuation. The dataset was categorised into three distinct timeframes based on the timeline of the GEG policy announcement to capture evolving public discourse; (a) initial announcement of the GEG policy implementation plan from January 2022 until May 2023 (T1), (b) official announcement of the GEG policy implementation plan from June 2023 until November 2023 (T2) and (c) after the GEG provision was dropped from the Control of Smoking Products for Public Health Bill 2023 (T3). The segmentation into three timeframes was guided by distinct policy events that reshaped both media coverage and public discourse on the GEG. T1 corresponded to the initial public proposal of the GEG, when the policy idea first entered public debate. T2 aligned with the official tabling and announcement of the Control of Smoking Products for Public Health Bill 2023, which elevated the GEG into the formal legislative arena and generated peak salience. T3 reflected the removal of the GEG provision from the bill. These cut-offs were predetermined, policy-driven milestones selected to reflect authentic changes in the discourse associated with critical phases of the policy process. These timeframes were selected to capture discursive dynamics at moments of heightened media attention and political debate. The theory influenced the analytic design of the study by informing the selection of timeframes for analysis, focusing on three critical junctures in the policy process: announcement, parliamentary tabling, and removal of the GEG provision when framing struggles were most visible in media discourse. This theoretical anchoring ensured consistency between the study design and its interpretive framework.

Data collection

Two coders independently screened all comments, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa, which indicated substantial agreement (κ = 0.78). The data collection began by identifying Malaysia’s major online news portals, as listed on Mediapod.com, as shown in Table 2. Searches were conducted on each platform using keywords such as “generation end game” and “GEG,” including translated Malay terms via Google Translate. Relevant news articles and accompanying public comments discussing the GEG policy from its initial announcement to two months after its removal from the Control of Smoking Products for Public Health Bill were included. Relevant articles were captured using the NCapture (version 1.0.290.0; QSR International) browser extension and imported into NVivo (version 14; QSR International) for analysis. Reader comments were compiled in Microsoft Excel and similarly imported. An inductive thematic content analysis was conducted with NVivo software, which facilitated data management and coding.

Data were analysed using NVivo software through an iterative thematic analysis process. Initial codes were inductively generated from the data through line-by-line reading of commentaries, which were then organised into a preliminary codebook. The codebook was refined through repeated cycles of coding, comparison, and discussion among the research team, ensuring consistency and coherence across categories. These codes were subsequently consolidated into broader themes that captured the dominant frames and discursive patterns in public commentary. Coding consistency was ensured through independent coding by multiple researchers and consensus discussions to resolve discrepancies. An inter-rater reliability test (Cohen’s kappa) conducted on a sample indicated substantial agreement, strengthening rigour. Reflexive practices were also applied throughout to acknowledge and mitigate potential interpretive bias, thereby enhancing transparency and credibility.

Content analysis

Thematic analysis involved familiarisation with the dataset, followed by the identification and categorisation of recurring themes to structure the qualitative findings [17, 18]. Themes and subthemes were formulated and categorised according to the examined measures. All online comments were independently coded in full by two researchers (NAAR, NAMR). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, with input from a third researcher (BAMS). Recurring metaphors reflecting shared meanings were identified during the cyclical analysis of news and commentaries and used to develop themes, guided by Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis approach [17]. Articles were also content analysed and coded according to the most frequently presented argument in the article. Each framing argument was categorised as either in favour of, against or neutral towards the GEG. Simple logistic regression was used to examine any association between the slant of the online news articles on the GEG policy and the online reader commentaries.

Although qualitative content analysis is generally descriptive, this study employed a pragmatic mixed-elements approach. Themes were generated inductively during the qualitative phase, which highlighted potential subgroup variations. These themes then guided a complementary quantitative exploration, whereby inferential statistics were applied to examine subgroup differences and patterns across demographic and contextual variables. This integration was not intended to transform the qualitative data into purely numerical form, but rather to enhance interpretative depth, triangulate findings, and strengthen the overall trustworthiness of the study. Such an approach is consistent with established guidance on mixed-methods integration, which emphasises the value of using qualitative insights to inform subsequent quantitative analyses. Guided by a pragmatic paradigm, this study treated interpretive and positivist approaches as complementary, using each to enrich the other. Researchers’ backgrounds in public health may have shaped interpretation, though reflexive practices and team-based coding were used to mitigate bias and enhance trustworthiness.

The analysis primarily employed an inductive approach. This allowed themes to emerge directly from the data, with theory serving as an orienting lens that helped structure the categorisation of codes and interpret the resulting themes.

Consistent with the framing approach described in the Methods, the commentaries were categorised into distinct frames: supportive, opposed, opposed but proposed alternatives, or not directly applicable. These frames reflect how different actors constructed the Generation End Game in public discourse, and they provided the basis for subsequent interpretation within the theoretical lens. A total of 7450 commentaries were analysed in this study, and 3,775 (50.7%) were included as they directly addressed the GEG policy. The remaining 3,675 (49.3%) were excluded as “not applicable,” consisting of unrelated political debates, personal attacks or harsh language, spam/duplicate content, and emoji-only or link-only responses. The exclusion ensured the analysis remained focused, reliable, and representative of substantive discussions related to the policy. In the initial phase (T1), active commenters’ discourse appeared divided, with 43.0% of commentaries expressing support and 47.2% voicing opposition. Supportive frames emphasised public health, while opposition focused on enforcement feasibility, personal freedom, black markets, and policy effectiveness.

Overall, the findings reveal a dynamic shift from early polarisation toward growing support, with framing arguments becoming more refined and advocacy-oriented over time. This progression underscores the influence of policy communication and media framing in shaping active commenters’ perceptions and highlights the potential for evolving consensus in the face of contested public health legislation.

Interestingly, some commentaries that rejected the GEG policy nevertheless advanced alternative approaches to curb tobacco use, reflecting the ambivalence within active commenters’ discourse. A total of 8.2% comments suggested that the government should implement a total ban on the sale and manufacture of tobacco products. In comparison, 2.0% of the commentaries emphasised preventive measures such as increasing investment in awareness and education rather than punitive approaches. These positions illustrate that the rejection of the GEG did not necessarily equate to opposition to tobacco control, but instead reveal contradictory and overlapping perspectives, where support for tobacco reduction coexisted with disagreement over the policy mechanism.

The synthesis highlights that the active commenters’ discourse was not simply divided into support and opposition, but characterised by contradictions and ambivalence. Many Malaysians simultaneously acknowledged the public health rationale of the GEG while expressing doubts about enforcement, equality, or political integrity. An endeavour to balance conflicting logics of individual rights, economic interests, political trust, and health protection is reflected in this ambivalence. By highlighting these conflicts, the analysis demonstrates that active commenters’ opinion is a disputed negotiation of values in response to an unprecedented health policy and cannot be reduced to binary categories. Discussion

This study is guided by the understanding that active commenters’ sentiment toward health policies is shaped not only by media framing but also by broader processes described in health communication and policy implementation literature. In particular, agenda-setting theory highlights how media emphasis on certain issues can influence the salience of those issues among the active commenters, helping to explain the strong reaction during the official announcement phase when policy visibility was at its peak. Similarly, policy diffusion theory suggests that novel and ambitious policies, such as the GEG, often encounter heightened scrutiny and contestation as they represent significant departures from established norms; this provides context for the polarised active commenters’ responses observed. From a health behaviour perspective, models such as the Health Belief Model underscore how perceptions of risk, autonomy, and social norms influence acceptance or resistance to health interventions. Connecting our findings to these frameworks helps to situate the evolving active commenters’ sentiment on the GEG within established theories of communication, policy adoption, and behaviour change, thereby enhancing the study’s contribution to understanding the dynamics of public health policy implementation [20, 21].

In our three-window design, the GEG’s reception reflects classic policy-diffusion dynamics: at peak salience (T2), article slant aligned more strongly with comment sentiment, evidence of salience-driven learning; contestation drew on negative lesson-drawing from New Zealand’s repeal, while domestic filters, legal or constitutional objections, enforcement capacity and illicit-trade risks, and trust deficits shaped feasibility. In terms HBM, GEG raises perceived barriers to initiation, but weak retailer systems and illicit markets blunt those barriers; perceived benefits are contested by rights or equity objections and mistrust, and self-efficacy requires skills-based education and accessible cessation support.

The high volume of news articles and comments related to GEG policy suggests that it is a newsworthy and controversial topic that generates strong, active commenters’ opinions. A study has shown that participation in posting online comments can help build a sense of community. It may be that some online platforms have become popular conduits for critics of the government and government policies to voice their opinions [22].

Although the thematic analysis revealed distinct domains such as health benefits, economic considerations, enforcement challenges, personal freedoms, and trust, these were not always neatly separated in active commenters’ discourse. Instead, commentaries frequently displayed contradictory or ambivalent positions. Some commenters endorsed the GEG as a necessary intervention to protect adolescents but simultaneously criticised it as impractical to enforce or discriminatory across generations. Similarly, individuals who expressed support for government health initiatives often paired this with frustration about political inconsistency, suggesting underlying mistrust even among supporters.

Such contradictions underscore that an active commenter’s opinion is rarely monolithic. It is rather shaped by competing cultural logics between health protection and personal freedom, between economic cost and moral responsibility, and between political trust and suspicion of vested interests. Recognising these ambivalences strengthens our interpretation by showing that shifts in active commenters’ support were not only about acceptance or rejection but also about how Malaysians attempted to reconcile conflicting values and priorities when confronted with an unprecedented tobacco control policy [23].

Relationship between health and tobacco use

In Malaysia, tobacco consumption has been identified as a leading cause of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), contributing to significant expenditures in medical treatments and loss of productivity due to morbidity and premature mortality [24]. Implementing the GEG policy could reduce smoking-related health issues in future generations, thereby lowering healthcare demand and alleviating pressure on public health systems.

Reducing smoking rates can enhance economic productivity, as smokers typically have higher absenteeism and lower work performance compared to non-smokers [25]. The GEG-driven reduction in tobacco use could yield economic benefits by promoting a healthier workforce, reducing productivity losses, and lowering rates of early retirement from smoking-related illnesses. In addition to national economic gains through improved workforce productivity, reduced smoking prevalence also benefits individuals and households by lowering tobacco-related spending. This enables better allocation of resources toward essential needs, further contributing to overall economic development [26].

International evidence shows that comprehensive tobacco control policies yield substantial long-term economic benefits. Experiences from countries such as Australia and New Zealand show not only significant reductions in smoking prevalence but also associated declines in healthcare expenditures and improvements in population health outcomes. However, it is noteworthy that despite these successes, both countries ultimately abandoned their generational tobacco ban proposals due to political, legal, and contextual challenges, underscoring the complexity of sustaining such ambitious policies [27].

The tobacco industry has historically contributed to tax revenues and employment in Malaysia. The GEG policy could lead to declining production levels, reduced investments, and potential job losses, particularly for individuals involved in tobacco cultivation, processing, and retail distribution [24]. Perceived economic disruption has been one of the major factors contributing to opposition against the GEG policy. Retailers, particularly small businesses and convenience stores, may experience economic setbacks, as tobacco products often contribute significantly to revenue and customer flow [28]. Currently, tobacco taxation provides a substantial income stream for the Malaysian government, which is used to fund public health initiatives and other national programs [29].

Support for the GEG policy is also grounded in Islamic values, reinforcing its public health rationale. This harmful substance poses a risk not only to those who smoke but also to those who are exposed to secondhand smoke. Numerous Muslim scholars from various regions globally have recently made public statements regarding smoking-related issues, particularly those that affect Muslims. Islam places significant importance on maintaining good health as part of fulfilling one’s religious and social obligations, and the prohibition of harmful substances, including tobacco, is widely supported by Islamic teachings. As smoking is deemed haram due to its harmful effects both to the individual and others, religious authorities such as Malaysia’s Fatwa Committee and the UAE’s Ministry of Justice have declared it prohibited. Through preventing adolescents from initiating tobacco use, the GEG aligns with both public health objectives and Islamic teachings that emphasise the avoidance of self-harm and the protection of community well-being [30, 31].

The tobacco industry contributes significantly to Malaysia’s excise revenue, supporting public expenditure. A decline in tobacco consumption, as envisioned by the GEG policy, would lead to reduced excise tax revenues, necessitating alternative revenue streams to compensate for the shortfall. A study by Oxford Economics forecasts a potential reduction of RM346 million in Gross Domestic Product (GDP) by 2040, accompanied by the loss of over 2,700 jobs within the formal economy. The analysis anticipates an annual tax revenue loss of RM1.2 billion attributable to the proliferation of illicit trade resulting from the policy. The tobacco industry’s impact on Malaysia’s economy is significant, with the sales value of manufactured tobacco products reaching approximately RM4 billion in 2023 [32].

In Malaysia, the direct costs of smoking were approximated at 3.83 billion ringgit in healthcare expenditures for the treatment of tobacco-related disorders, including heart disease, COPD, and lung cancer. This figure represents 0.7% of Malaysia’s gross domestic product (GDP) and 26.1% of the budget of the Ministry of Health Malaysia [33]. The prevalence of smoking is not decreasing significantly, which is persistently increasing healthcare expenditures in the country. If the GEG policy is in place, it can reduce smoking prevalence and further decrease government healthcare expenditure in the future [34, 35]. Any policy targeting only the legal market for tobacco and vaping products, including GEG, will exert a restricted influence on total consumption. And it may risk further exacerbating the illicit market [36].

Several commentators have endorsed this perspective, viewing tobacco use as a major driver of non-communicable diseases and supporting the Generation End Game (GEG) as a public health intervention. Nonetheless, commentary on the GEG policy remains divided, with both supportive and opposing views observed. In order to address this lack of consensus, it is essential to critically examine the ethical, policy, political, economic, and international factors influencing the implementation of the GEG policy.

This finding suggests that individuals’ perceptions of the causes of health and tobacco use, whether viewed as complex or straightforward, may significantly influence their acceptance of the policy. The implications highlight the need for government efforts to enhance public awareness and education, thereby enabling individuals to form more informed opinions and judgments regarding newly implemented policies.

Conflict between public health and personal freedom

Some have perceived the introduction of the GEG policy as creating a conflict between public health and personal freedom of choice. The GEG policy prohibits individuals born after a particular year from purchasing tobacco products indefinitely, effectively creating a lifelong legal distinction between generations. Some argued that this differential treatment could be challenged as a violation of equality rights under Article 8(1) of the Federal Constitution, which states that “all persons are equal before the law and entitled to equal protection of the law”. One argument was that the policy has no constitutional rights violation as public health considerations justify the policy’s legal standing [37, 38]. The policy has been criticised as conflicting with liberal democratic principles by limiting individual autonomy. However, studies argue that smoking is not a fully autonomous act, as nicotine addiction, cognitive biases, and trauma-related psychosocial factors impair rational decision-making [39].

The debate over individual freedoms versus public health protection is central to discussions surrounding the GEG policy, with critics asserting that rather than imposing an outright ban, governments should focus on education and regulation measures that empower individuals to make informed choices about their health. This rights-based argument has fostered opposition to the GEG policy, with some perceiving it as an excessive expansion of regulatory authority that could extend to other lifestyle behaviours [40].

The opposition to Malaysia’s Generation End Game (GEG) policy has primarily centred on concerns regarding enforcement challenges, with critics framing their arguments around the feasibility and practicality of implementing such a large-scale tobacco control initiative. A key concern raised by opponents is the complexity of monitoring and regulating the sale and distribution of tobacco and nicotine products across different retail platforms, including online markets and cross-border smuggling [41]. The potential for increased illicit trade in tobacco products has been highlighted, with detractors suggesting that strict prohibitions could inadvertently fuel the black market, thereby undermining the intended public health benefits of the policy [42]. Given the existing challenges in enforcing tobacco control measures, critics argue that the GEG policy may excessively burden law enforcement agencies, regulatory bodies, and retailers, requiring significant resources for compliance monitoring and legal enforcement. Another major challenge, as framed by critics, is the issue of age verification and the long-term sustainability of enforcing a generational tobacco ban.

In contrast to existing age-based restrictions that apply universally to all adults below a certain threshold, the GEG policy introduces a permanent age-based prohibition, necessitating that enforcement authorities would need to consistently distinguish between individuals who are legally permitted to purchase tobacco products and those who are not. This requirement raises logistical concerns, as it demands a reliable system for age verification, which could potentially result in operational inefficiencies, especially in informal and small-scale retail environments. The possibility of unintentional non-compliance among retailers due to difficulties distinguishing eligible and non-eligible consumers has also been identified as a significant obstacle to the effective enforcement of the policy. Moreover, concerns have been raised about potential ethical and legal implications, such as the disproportionate targeting of marginalised communities during enforcement actions, potentially exacerbating social inequalities in public health interventions [43, 44].

It is therefore essential for policymakers to consider the potential for the GEG policy to have a positive impact on health equity, and to specify its primary purpose and frame it accordingly, in order to mitigate the potential for lobbying by opposing parties.

Trust issues

Another theme that emerged from the commentaries was a lack of trust in the government, politicians, tobacco industries and public health experts. Many commenters argued that it was the responsibility of the government and authorities to ban the tobacco industry, rather than the removal of the GEG provision from the Bill. A study has also suggested that tobacco use is a societal problem, and it is the government’s responsibility to create a healthy environment [45].

The tobacco industry has been consistently criticised and viewed with public scepticism, primarily due to its perceived resistance to supporting government-led efforts to reduce the burden of non-communicable diseases [46, 47]. This concern is driven by the perception that the industry prioritises its commercial interests over public health and disseminates misleading or incomplete information regarding the health risks associated with its products [48]. The implications of this mistrust are substantial, as it may hinder the effective implementation of public health policies and diminish public support for such initiatives. To mitigate these concerns, public health authorities should prioritise education and outreach efforts to raise awareness about the adverse health effects associated with tobacco use.

The denial of the well-established association between smoking and major diseases may indicate limited public awareness, which can undermine support for the Generation End Game (GEG) policy [49]. Evidence suggests that increased knowledge is linked to lower smoking prevalence and greater acceptance of tobacco control measures, underscoring the critical role of comprehensive health education in fostering informed attitudes and enhancing the effectiveness of such policies [50, 51].

Critics contend that implementing a generational tobacco ban infringes upon individual autonomy and the right to access legally available products. In New Zealand, a proposed generational ban, scheduled for implementation in July 2024, was repealed following a change in government. The incoming conservative coalition cited concerns related to personal freedoms as the reason for the repeal. Similarly, in Malaysia, the Generation End Game (GEG) policy encountered comparable challenges, including constitutional objections raised by the Attorney General, which ultimately led to the policy’s withdrawal [52].

The absence of a definitive enforcement mechanism, coupled with resource constraints in monitoring compliance, further complicates the policy’s viability. Malaysia’s enforcement agencies are already stretched thin in tackling the existing illicit tobacco trade and may struggle to allocate additional resources for a prolonged generational ban [53, 54].

The removal of the GEG provision has deepened public distrust in the government, raising concerns about accountability and transparency. While the state is expected to protect public health through regulation, the retraction has prompted speculation about industry influence and lobbying pressures. Enhancing transparency and adopting a more inclusive policy-making process are essential to restoring public confidence and reaffirming a commitment to tobacco control [55, 56].

Although this study focused on Malaysia, the findings resonate with international experiences such as New Zealand’s generational ban policy, which was initially legislated but later repealed. In both contexts, public discourse reflected tensions between strong public health framing and counter-frames emphasising individual autonomy, economic disruption, and political feasibility. Similarly, Singapore’s centralised governance has enabled the consistent enforcement of strict tobacco controls, while Finland and the UK illustrate how deeply ingrained anti-smoking norms and public trust in government facilitate acceptance of restrictive measures. In Malaysia, however, strong tobacco industry influence, entrenched smoking cultures, and socio-political resistance pose significant obstacles. Nonetheless, our observation that public support increased following the official policy announcement and persisted even after the GEG was withdrawn mirrors the New Zealand experience, where public support remained high despite political reversal. These comparative insights highlight that while active commenters’ sentiment towards tobacco endgame policies may be favourable, political dynamics and competing frames often determine legislative outcomes. Sustained communication, advocacy, and contextually adapted strategies are therefore essential to bridge the gap between public support and successful policy implementation.

However, a number of lessons are still applicable, including the necessity of proactive public framing prior to parliamentary debates, the strategic use of reliable community communicators, and the importance of open, data-driven communication in combating misinformation. However, contextual modification is still necessary, especially to account for Malaysia’s multiethnic sensitivities, decentralised enforcement systems, and the influence of religion on moral discourses around smoking and self-harm. The research advances a more sophisticated understanding of how local rephrasing of global endgame aspirations is necessary to maintain legitimacy and public trust by placing Malaysia’s experience in relation to these international antecedents.

Relationship between news articles and online commentaries’ slant

Over the last decade, the news media landscape has undergone significant transformations as digital platforms have created unprecedented competition for traditional media. It reflects a broader trend of convergence in the media industries, marked by cross-platform content accessibility, diminished organisational control over output and distribution, enhanced user ownership, reduced entry barriers, and heightened engagement and interactivity between consumers and content creators. Online news platforms often allow users to submit comments on news articles. The characteristics of online news facilitate user engagement with the news provider and fellow readers of the site. In optimal scenarios, user engagement may lead to an online discourse regarding current events [57].

This study, particularly in T1 and T3 as in Table 4, showed that most online reader comments’ slants were negative, even though the articles were written in a positive slant. It may be due to the fact that comments were more likely to be unfavourable when the issues in the articles did not favour the reader. It is in line with studies by Houston et al. that indicate that comments’ qualitative components may lead to a perception of bias in the online news articles on which those comments are posted. Furthermore, unfavourable remarks made about an article in the news make it seem less credible to other users [58, 59].

While in the T2 timeline, most of the online reader comments’ slants were positive, equivalent to the articles written in a positive slant. A study revealed that people responded more positively to news articles featuring a positive slant. The findings align with other research suggesting that whether people react to news content positively or negatively is highly dependent on the topic itself [60].

The observed increase in those who support the GEG policy following the official announcement, and its persistence even after policy withdrawal, highlights the critical role of timing and continuity in policy communication. Future tobacco control initiatives would benefit from aligning communication strategies with key policy milestones to maximise visibility and salience, while sustaining consistent messaging thereafter to reinforce normative acceptance. Engaging trusted health professionals and community leaders, alongside ongoing monitoring of public sentiment, may further consolidate support and mitigate the effects of political or legislative setbacks.

The observed shifts in active commenters sentiment appeared to align with major government communication milestones. However, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence of direct causality. The netnographic design captures discourse patterns within online news platforms and commentaries, but it does not account for broader influences, such as traditional media coverage, public opinion polls, or offline discussions, that may also have shaped perceptions. Intrinsically, the findings should be understood as reflective of how sentiment was expressed within the studied online environments, rather than as definitive evidence of causal relationships. This more cautious interpretation highlights the need for future research that employs triangulation with surveys or broader media analyses to validate and extend the identified patterns.

These findings underscore the importance of effective communication framing and strategic timing for policymakers. Support can be increased by making announcements during times of high public interest; however, maintaining this momentum may require regular follow-up communications, reaffirmation from reliable sources, and alignment with broader public health narratives. Furthermore, the fact that support persisted even after the policy was withdrawn suggests that if positive sentiments are generated, they may endure, laying the groundwork for the future reinstatement of comparable policies. It presents an opportunity for proponents of tobacco control to view communication as an integral part of the policy-making process, rather than merely an adjunct to it.

Regression analysis was used to complement, not contradict, the qualitative insights. The themes identified through thematic analysis highlighted possible subgroup differences, and regression provided a way to test these patterns systematically. By quantifying associations between demographic factors and the themes, the regression results added clarity and depth, reinforcing the qualitative findings rather than replacing them.

Limitations

This study involved multiple comparisons across time periods and categories, which raises the potential for Type I error. As our analyses were primarily exploratory, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and future confirmatory studies may benefit from applying statistical adjustments for multiple testing. In addition, some confidence intervals were relatively wide, reflecting limited precision in certain subgroups with smaller sample sizes. These estimates, while suggestive of meaningful trends, carry a degree of uncertainty that warrants cautious interpretation.

The observed shifts in active commenters’ sentiment appeared to align with major government communication milestones. However, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence of direct causality. The netnographic design captures discourse patterns within online news platforms and commentaries, but it does not account for wider influences such as traditional media coverage, public opinion polls, or offline discussions that may also have shaped perceptions. As such, the findings should be understood as reflective of how sentiment was expressed within the studied online environments, rather than as definitive evidence of causal relationships. This more cautious interpretation underscores the need for future research employing triangulation with surveys or broader media analyses to validate and extend the patterns identified.

This study may also be subject to temporal bias. Commenting behaviours are likely to have shifted across the three policy windows examined, influenced by changes in media coverage, political developments, and levels of public attention. Consequently, variations observed between timeframes may reflect fluctuations in online engagement as much as substantive shifts in opinion. While the design intentionally captured these distinct moments to illustrate discursive dynamics, the findings should not be interpreted as fixed measures of public sentiment over time but rather as reflections of evolving online discourse within specific contextual moments.

The exclusion of less substantive comments, such as off-topic debates, personal attacks, and emoji-only or link-only responses, may have introduced a bias toward more engaged or polarised voices. This potential skew is an inherent limitation of online discourse analysis, where active participation is typically unevenly distributed across users. Nonetheless, given the study’s aim to understand the framing and contestation of the GEG in the active online commenters’ sphere, capturing these more engaged voices remains valuable for illustrating the dynamics of public debate.

A further limitation is that this study focuses only on active commenters, who represent a self-selected subset of online users. As such, the dataset may disproportionately capture the views of more engaged or polarised individuals, while underrepresenting passive readers or those with moderate perspectives. The findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting discursive dynamics among active participants rather than the general population.

It is important to acknowledge that the demographic profile of individuals who actively post comments on online news platforms may not reflect that of the general Malaysian population. Prior research suggests that online commenters are often younger, male, politically engaged, and more likely to hold strong or polarised opinions. As this study drew exclusively on commentary from online news sites, the findings should therefore be interpreted as indicative of discourse among active online participants.

Another limitation of this study is the absence of sensitivity analyses to control for potential confounding factors. Differences in readership demographics across news platforms, temporal variations in commenting behaviour, and the presence of repeat commenters may have influenced the distribution and emphasis of themes. Future research should therefore incorporate sensitivity analyses to examine the stability of results and to provide a more robust understanding of how online public sentiment toward tobacco control policies may vary by context and over time and could extend this work by examining the motivational functions of language within online discourse, such as expressions of readiness for change, ambivalence, or resistance.

Conclusion

Findings from this component provided rich insights into the evolving active online commenters sentiment, revealing both alignment and dissonance with media framing across the three policy phases. In the initial phase (T1), active commenters responses were largely critical of the GEG policy, often highlighting doubts about enforceability and questioning the practicality of implementing a generational tobacco ban. This contrasted sharply with the media’s optimistic tone, reflecting a disconnect between expert-driven narratives and active commenters perception. As the policy progressed into the official announcement stage (T2), commentaries became more balanced with supporters citing public health benefits, while opponents raised concerns about individual autonomy and legal rights. By the time the policy was removed (T3), a notable shift occurred: public reactions were largely critical of the removal, with many commenters expressing disappointment and framing it as a loss of governmental commitment to health protection. Nevertheless, those who supported the policy’s withdrawal continued to cite concerns over personal freedom and enforceability. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating public concerns into health policy-making. While the media played a strong role in health promotion, public narratives revealed the need for greater engagement, education, and trust-building to secure policy legitimacy and acceptance.
