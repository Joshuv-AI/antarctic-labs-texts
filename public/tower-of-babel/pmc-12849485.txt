Prevalence of sickle cell disease and sickle cell traits among children and adolescents in Nigeria: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Amudalat Issa, Olayinka Rasheed Ibrahim, Rasheedat M Ibraheem, Aisha F Lawal, Mariam Abdulbaki, Mohammed Baba AbdulKadir, Biodun Sulyman Alabi, Kolade Samuel Ernest
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSickle cell disease (SCD) is one of the most common genetic disorders in the world, with Nigeria believed to have the highest burden. Despite the high burden in the country, the true prevalence of SCD and its main driver—the sickle cell trait (SCT)—is unknown, which is important for planning and resource allocation. Hence, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of the prevalence of SCD, SCT, and associated factors in Nigerian children and adolescents.MethodsUsing the PRISMA guidelines [ID: CRD42024556354], we systematically search the following major databases: AJOL, Google Scholar, PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science collections from January 1950 to October 2024 on Nigerian children and adolescents with SCD and/or SCT. The quality of the studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale adapted for cross-sectional studies. We performed a cumulative meta-analysis to estimate the pooled prevalence and associated factors for SCD and SCT.ResultsThirty studies, comprising 211,938 participants, were included in the final analysis after stepwise screening. The pooled prevalences of SCD and SCT were 4.0% (95% CI 3.0 to 6.0, I2 = 99.6%) and 21.0% (95% CI 20.0 to 23.0, I2 = 90.5%), respectively. The northwestern geopolitical zone had the highest pooled prevalence of SCD (7.0%; 95% CI 3.0 to 11.0; I2 = 98.9%) and SCT (23.0%; 95% CI 19.0 to 28.0%, I2 = 54.2%). The southeastern region had the lowest pooled prevalence of SCD (2.0%, 95% CI 0.3 to 3.0%, I2 = 96.5), whereas the southwestern region had the lowest pooled prevalence of SCT (19.0%, 95% CI 14.0 to 25.0%, I2 = 92.2). There was no relationship between sex, socioeconomic status, and SCD.ConclusionThe pooled prevalence of SCD and SCT among children and adolescents was high, with the highest burden occurring in the northwestern geopolitical zone in Nigeria. In addition, there was no relationship between sex, socioeconomic class, and prevalence of SCD.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO ID: CRD42024556354.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-03029-1.Keywords: Sickle cell disease, Sickle cell trait, Prevalence, Children, Adolescents, Nigeria

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is the most common genetic blood disorder with significant impacts on the lives of millions of affected individuals and their families [1, 2]. With an estimated eight million affected individuals, SCD affects all regions of the world with varying burdens and impacts [1]. The recent estimate of the global burden of SCD represents a 41.0% increase in the prevalence of SCD between 2010 and 2021, partly attributable to the increase in population dynamics [1, 2]. The greater burden of SCD, including its main driver—sickle cell traits (SCT)—disproportionately affects sub-Saharan Africa, where its impact is profound and multifaceted, encompassing a complex interplay of medical, social, economic, and cultural factors [3]. Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for 5.7 million global cases of SCD and 50 to 90% of related deaths among children with SCD without access to comprehensive healthcare [1, 4]. In addition, a greater variation exists in the documented prevalence of SCD within the sub-regions. For instance, the prevalence of SCD in East Africa ranges from 0.8% among infants in Kenya [5] to 3.0% in Western Uganda [6]. In the West African sub-region, the estimated prevalence of SCT was 20–30%, far higher than the pooled prevalence of 5.9% reported in a recent systematic review in India [7]. Of the estimated 400,000 babies born in Africa with SCD, Nigeria contributed the highest burden with an estimated 150,000 babies, making SCD a disease of significant priority in Nigeria [8, 9]. In Nigeria, researchers have also documented varying prevalence rates across different geopolitical zones, ranging from 0.3 to 5.0% [10–12]. For instance, a recent community-based investigation spanning 29 primary healthcare centers in the northcentral and northwest regions indicated a SCD prevalence of 3.2% and 2.7%, respectively [13]. In contrast, in southeastern Nigeria, the hospital prevalence of SCD is estimated to be 5.0% [12].

Although studies on the prevalence of SCD among children and adolescents in Nigeria have been conducted across various subpopulations and settings, such as hospitals and communities with significant variations, the actual national prevalence remains unknown, hampering adequate planning and resource allocation [12, 14]. In addition, an important driver of the high burden of SCD is the presumed high rate of the sickle cell trait (SCT), with the exact burden also unknown, despite various efforts to reduce the burden of both SCD and SCT in Nigeria. Hence, there is a need to identify the pooled prevalence nationwide and establish regional burdens and associated factors. We therefore raised the following research questions: What is the pooled prevalence of sickle cell disease (SCD) and sickle cell trait (SCT) among Nigerian children and adolescents? Is there a regional variation in the prevalence of SCD and SCT among children and adolescents across the six geopolitical zones in Nigeria? What is the relationship between SCD and sociodemographic factors (sex and socioeconomic status) in Nigeria? Is there a difference between the community- and hospital-based prevalence of SCD in Nigeria? Therefore, this study aimed to document the pooled national and regional prevalence rates and the sociodemographic factors related to SCD and SCT among Nigerian children and adolescents through a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Materials and methods

This study was prepared and written based on the PRISM (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) checklist 2020 (Supplementary material 1) [15].

Protocol and registration

The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (Prospero ID: CRD42024556354) before the commencement of this review and was published in the Nigerian Journal of Pediatrics [16].

Operational definition

Sickle cell disease was defined as an individual who inherited an abnormal hemoglobin S and another abnormal hemoglobin, which may include abnormal hemoglobin S, C, and S–beta-thalassemia [17].

Sickle cell anemia was defined as a condition in which an individual inherits two abnormal hemoglobin S (HbS) genes, resulting in a homozygous SS genotype, which is a type of sickle cell disease (SCD) [17].

Sickle cell trait was defined as an individual who inherited one normal hemoglobin A and one abnormal sickle cell gene hemoglobin, with resultant AS genotype [17].

Socioeconomic class was based on the methods described by Oyedeji, which were derived from the average of the highest educational attainment and occupational class, with cumulative scores sub-classified into upper, middle, and lower classes [18].

Information sources and search

The search used Medical Subject Headings [MeSH] terms, keywords, and entry words across the following databases: AJOL, Google Scholar, PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search was conducted using the following words or phrases: “sickle cell disease OR sickle cell anemia OR sickle cell trait” AND “Nigeria” AND “newborns OR infants OR children OR adolescents” AND “associated factors OR sex OR socioeconomic status OR sociodemographic” AND “(SCD OR SCA OR SCT)” across the database (Supplementary Table S2). The search period included all studies published from January 1950 to October 2024.

Selection process

After searching, we exported all the search literature into the Rayyan software, where we removed duplicates. Two independent and blinded reviewers [AI and ORI] screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts of the eligible studies, which formed the basis for selecting studies for inclusion in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Where there was disagreement in the decision between the two reviewers, a third reviewer (MBA; a senior member of the research team) served as a tiebreaker. The authors of eligible studies with missing data were contacted via email and given adequate time to respond. Articles whose authors did not respond within a given timeframe were excluded.

Data collection process

We used the Rayyan systematic review screening tool and Microsoft Excel to extract the data. The extracted data included the first author’s name, year of publication, total number of study samples, number of events (SCD/SCT), location of study/geopolitical region, study design, study population (age groups), study settings (hospital or community-based study), sample size, sex, diagnostic method, and socioeconomic class. This review was reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow charts of the study

Study of risk of bias assessment and methodological quality assessment

The risk of bias was minimized by allowing the principal investigator and co-investigator to perform different screening stages. The two reviewers were blinded to each other by using a screening tool. A third reviewer, acting as a tiebreaker, resolved the conflicts between the paired reviewers. A manual snowballing search for relevant studies was conducted by reviewing the references of selected studies and exploring the grey literature.

We used the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale [NOS] adapted for cross-sectional studies to rate the quality of each included article [22]. In brief, the adapted tool consists of three domains: the first domain assesses the selection of the study participant with a graded maximum of five points; the second domain assesses comparability, including consideration of confounders, and has a maximum of two points; and the third domain addresses the outcomes and statistical tests with a maximum of three points. The NOS provides a cumulative maximum score of 10 points, which is further classified as high quality for scores of 7 and above, moderate quality for scores between 5 and 6, and low quality for scores below 5.

Statistical analysis

The extracted data [Microsoft Excel spreadsheet] was analyzed using MedCalc version 23 [statistical software package for biomedical research] and ‘Meta’ and ‘Metafor’ packages of R software (version 4.5.1), R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. Due to anticipated heterogeneity, we used a random-effects model to measure the effect size of pooled prevalence, 95% confidence interval (CI) of SCD, and SCT. Similarly, random effect models were used to perform subgroup analysis of geopolitical regions, sex, age group, socioeconomic status, study methods, study settings, and diagnostic methods. We used forest plots to visually display each study’s prevalence estimates, their 95% confidence intervals, and the pooled summary estimates. Each forest plot also included weights for the common and random-effects models and predictive intervals [SCD and SCT]. To assess publication bias, we plotted the funnel plot of all included studies and further tested using Egger’s test for statistical significance. In addition, we carried out the ‘Trim-and-Fill’ to estimate the number of studies required to achieve symmetry of the funnel plot and re-calculated the pooled prevalence using the hypothetical studies. We performed I2, tau2 (τ2), and Q statistics on the pooled prevalence to assess the statistical heterogeneity. We considered I2 values between 50 and 75%, and above 75% as moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively [23]. Sensitivity analyses using a leave-one-out approach were performed to examine the impact of each included study and the impact of outliers on the stability of the pooled prevalence of SCD and SCT.

We applied a mixed-effects meta-regression model to explore the potential sources of heterogeneity in the pooled SCD prevalence. The moderators tested included years of studies, grouped into before 2000, 2001 to 2019, and 2020 to 2024; study setting (community-based vs. hospital-based—inpatients, outpatients, or both); age groups (newborns, infants, under-fives, adolescents, all ages); and methods of diagnosis (electrophoresis, high-performance liquid chromatography [HPLC], isoelectric focusing). Sex and socioeconomic classes were dropped from the meta-regression model because of missing data from the extracted studies. The model checked whether these factors significantly affected the differences in reported prevalence across studies, using an R2 statistic to summarize the level of variation. The model coefficients, confidence intervals, and p-values were reported. For all levels of statistical significance, we set a p-value of < 0.05.

Results

Study selection

We conducted a search between September and October 2024. The first search of the seven databases identified 26,815 articles. Another 10 articles were found by searching the references of the included articles and the grey literature, giving a cumulative total of 26,825 articles. These were screened step-by-step and reported using the PRISMA guidelines (Fig. 1). Finally, 30 articles that reported the prevalence of SCD and SCT were included in our review.

Overview of included studies

This review included 30 studies from all six geopolitical zones in Nigeria that met the inclusion criteria. The southwest had the highest representation, with seven articles (23.3%), while the southeast had six articles (20.0%); northwest (four studies, 13.3%), south-south (four studies, 13.3%), and north-central regions (five studies, 16.7%) (Table 1).

Study characteristics

The review participants ranged from newborns to adolescents and the study sample sizes ranged from 78 to 72,333 children across a combined population of 211,938 children and adolescents. Twenty-eight studies reported the prevalence of SCD with or without SCT, and twenty-one studies documented the prevalence of SCT. Four [13.3%] of the 30 studies were newborn screenings, two were conducted among infants [6.7%], and 19 [63.3%] included all age groups (Table 1). Twenty-seven [66.7%] of the studies were cross-sectional. Most studies were hospital-based [n = 23, 76.7%], and five [16.7%] were community-based. The diagnostic methods included electrophoresis (12, 40.0%), high-performance liquid chromatography [6, 20.0%], isoelectric focusing (1, 3.3%), and 10 [33.3%] studies did not state the diagnostic methods (Table 1).

Risk of bias assessment for the studies

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale adapted for cross-sectional studies to rate the quality of each included article [22]. All included studies, except one [moderate quality], were rated as high-quality studies (Table 1).

The pooled prevalence of SCD and SCT among Nigerian children

Using the random-effects model, the pooled prevalence of SCD in Nigeria was 4.0% [95% CI 3.0 to 6.0%]. However, the prevalence in the included studies ranged from 0.0 to 21.0% (Fig. 2) with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 99.6%, τ2 = 0.0014). The pooled prevalence of SCT was 21.0% (95% CI 20.0 to 23.0%, I2 = 90.5%,τ2 = 0.0012), with significant heterogeneity (Fig. 3).

Regional variation in the prevalence of sickle cell disease and sickle cell traits among children across geopolitical zones in Nigeria

Using the random effects model, the highest pooled prevalence of SCD was in the northwestern geopolitical zone of Nigeria, with a pooled prevalence of 7.0% [95% CI 3.0 to 11.0%, I2 = 98.9%, τ2 = 0.023], followed by the southwest, with a pooled prevalence of 5.0% (95% CI 2.0 to 8.0%, I2 = 96.3%, τ2 = 0.0017]). In comparison, the lowest regional pooled prevalence of SCD was observed in the southeast (2.0%, 95% CI 0.0 to 3.0%, I2 = 96.5%, τ2 = 0.0039) (Supplementary Fig. S1). The highest pooled prevalence of SCT was 23.0% (95% CI 19.0 to 28.0%, I2 = 54.2%, τ2 = 0.0011) in northwestern Nigeria. In comparison, the lowest pooled prevalence of 19.0% (95% CI 14.0 to 25.0, I2 = 92.2%, τ2 = 0.005) occurred in the southwestern region, while the southeast and south-south had pooled prevalences of 22.0% each (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Subgroup analysis

Prevalence of sickle cell disease based on the sex among children and adolescents in Nigeria

The pooled prevalence of SCD among males was 2.0% (95% CI 0.0 to 3%, I2 = 95.9%, τ2 = 0.002). In females, the pooled prevalence of SCD was 2.0% (95% CI 1.0 to 3.0%, I2 = 94.8%, τ2 = 0.0003) (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. S3). In the comparative analysis, the odds of SCD among males were comparable with those of females (Random-effects OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.81 to 1.28, I2 = 0.0%, τ2 = 0.0000, Q = 3.04) (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Prevalence of SCD based on the age groups among the children and adolescents in Nigeria

The pooled prevalence of studies that combined all age groups was 6.0% (95% CI 4.0 to 8.0%, I2 = 99.3%, τ2 = 0.002). Among children under-five, the pooled prevalence of SCD was 2.0% (95% CI 1.0 to 3.0%, I2 = 82.7%, τ2 = 0.0001), while among the newborns, the pooled prevalence was 1% (95% CI 0.0 to 3.0%, I2 = 85.5%, τ2 = 0.0001) (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. S5).

Prevalence of SCD based on the socioeconomic class among the children and adolescents in Nigeria

The pooled prevalence of SCD was 2.0% (95% CI 0.0 to 7.0%, I2 = 85.9%, τ2 = 0.001) among the lower socioeconomic classes. Similarly, the pooled prevalence rates among the upper and middle socioeconomic classes were 2.0% and 2.0%, respectively. There was no significant relationship between the various socioeconomic indices and SCD prevalence (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Prevalence of SCD based on the study settings among the children and adolescents in Nigeria

Based on the study settings, the highest pooled prevalence of 9.0% (95% CI 6.0 to 13.0%, I2 = 99.4%, τ2 = 0.0019) occurred among the inpatients, while the lowest pooled prevalence of 2.0% (95% CI 1.0 to 3.0%, I2 = 96.5%, τ2 = 0.0003) occurred among the outpatients. The pooled community prevalence of SCD was 3.0% (95% CI 1.0 to 4.0%; I2 = 87.3%, τ2 = 0.0002) (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. S8).

Prevalence of SCD based on the design among the children and adolescents in Nigeria

The pooled prevalence among the cross-sectional studies was 3.0% (95% CI 2.0 to 3.0%, I2 = 88.9%, τ2 = 0.0004), while the pooled prevalence among the retrospective studies was 7.0% (95% CI 4.0 to 10.0%, I2 = 99.88%, τ2 = 0.0025) (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. S9).

Pooled prevalence of SCD based on diagnostic methods among Nigerian children and adolescents

Based on the diagnostic methods, electrophoresis had a pooled prevalence of 3.0% (95% CI 1.0 to 4.0%, I2 = 86.0%, τ2 = 0.0004), while HPLC had a pooled prevalence of 2.0% (95% CI 1.0 to 3.0%, I2 = 87.4%, τ2 = < 0.0001) (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. S10).

Sensitivity analysis

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine the influence of individual studies on the pooled prevalence estimate. The results showed minimal changes in the pooled prevalence when each study was omitted in turn, with estimates ranging from 2.9% [Ibraheem et al. [47], Isezuo et al. [29]] to 3.2% [David et al. [33]], and overlapping confidence intervals between 2.1 and 4.7% (Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Fig. 11). This consistency indicates that no single study had an undue impact on the meta-analysis findings, thus supporting the overall robustness of the pooled prevalence estimate for SCD. Similarly, a leave-one-out sensitivity for SCT studies showed a stable pooled prevalence ranging from 21.1% [Idowu et al. [36]] to 22.1% [Amodu et al. [35]] with overlapping confidence between 19.5% and 23.3% (Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Fig. S12). These findings demonstrate that no single study exerted an undue influence on the pooled estimate, indicating the robustness and reliability of the meta-analysis.

Heterogeneity and publication bias

We applied a funnel plot and Egger’s test to assess publication bias. The funnel plot showed publication bias for studies included in the pooled prevalence of SCD (Fig. 4), which was confirmed by Egger’s test [intercept − 8.28, 95% CI − 14.15 to − 2.40, p = 0.008]. After applying the ‘Trim-and-Fill’ method, four studies were imputed to address potential funnel plot asymmetry. The adjusted pooled prevalence estimate was 4.0%, with a 95% CI of 2.7% to 5.9%, which remained similar to the original estimate of 4.0%. This finding suggests that publication bias is unlikely to substantially influence the overall findings (Supplementary Fig. S13). However, the heterogeneity persists with an I2 of 99.5% with a large Q of 6270.23, suggesting significant heterogeneity among the studies. Therefore, while the trim-and-fill suggests the pooled prevalence is relatively robust, the considerable heterogeneity warrants cautious interpretation. In contrast, the studies included for SCT showed no considerable publication bias [Egger’s test-intercept − 0.409, 95% CI − 2.633 to 1.815, p = 0.795] (Fig. 5).

Meta-regression

The mixed-effects meta-regression (with multiple moderators) showed that studies conducted before 2000 reported significantly lower prevalence estimates than those conducted during 2000–2019 (estimate = − 0.816, p = 0.004). Similarly, studies classified as hospital-based (both outpatient and inpatient settings combined) had significantly lower prevalence estimates than community-based studies (estimate = − 1.0483, p = 0.010). Regarding diagnostic methods, studies that employed isoelectric focusing reported substantially lower prevalence estimates (estimate = − 2.8321, p < 0.001) than the reference diagnostic method [electrophoresis], while studies using HPLC showed a non-significant trend towards lower prevalence (estimate = − 0.8053, p = 0.120). Other variables, including the target age group categories, did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that the age distribution of the study population was not a significant source of heterogeneity in the reported prevalence (Supplementary Table S4). The overall model explained a substantial portion of the heterogeneity, with an estimated R2 of 88.2%, indicating that the moderators collectively accounted for a large proportion of the observed between-study variability. The test of moderators was statistically significant (QM(df = 12) = 124.5, p < 0.0001), indicating that these covariates jointly explained the significant variability in SCD prevalence. Nonetheless, residual heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 93.2%), suggesting that other unmeasured factors may have contributed to differences among studies.

Discussion

Sickle cell disease (SCD) constitutes a significant public health burden in Nigeria, but its prevalence remains uncertain. This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized data from 30 studies involving children and adolescents across all six geopolitical zones in Nigeria. This review provides pooled prevalence estimates for SCD and SCT, highlighting substantial variations across regions, age groups, diagnostic methods, study settings, and study designs.

Pooled prevalence of SCD and SCT

The pooled prevalence of SCD was 4.0%, which is higher than that reported in most individual studies in Nigeria. The prevalence across individual studies varied widely from 0.0 to 21.0%, with significant heterogeneity likely influenced by methodological differences, including study population, setting (hospital vs. community), design, and diagnostic methods used. Notably, the pooled prevalence of 4.0% exceeds the commonly cited 1–3% prevalence in Nigeria [24, 49]. In addition, five studies reported a high prevalence of SCD (10–20.5%) [28, 29, 36, 37, 47], which was far above the pooled prevalence in this study. The higher rates were found by Ibraheem et al. [47], Isezuo et al. [29], and Yiltok et al. [28] could be because the studies were conducted in hospitals, which are more likely to have many SCD patients with different complications. In addition, the exceptionally high prevalence (21.0%) reported by Thanni et al. [37] could be due to a small sample size (78), resulting in an erroneously high prevalence value, which is difficult to generalize.

Compared with global data, the pooled prevalence of SCD (4.0%) was substantially higher than the estimated 1.17% in a recent systematic review in India [7]. In addition, the pooled prevalence of 2.0% among under-fives far exceeded 0.11% from global estimates among similar age groups [50]. This prevalence is also higher than that reported in most sub-Saharan African countries—Kenya [5], the Democratic Republic of Congo [51], and Ghana [52]—further affirming the significant burden of SCD in Nigeria. A bit of concern was the sub-analysis of the years of the studies that shows a higher prevalence from 2001 compared with earlier years. While these findings are in keeping with a recent analysis of the ‘global burden of sickle cell disease’, the findings highlighted the need for the country to critically appraise the primary drivers of the rising burden of SCD in Nigeria and institute proactive measures to curb the rising trends [1].

The pooled prevalence of SCT was 21.0%, within the reported prevalence of 20–30% within the sub-Saharan region, consistent with earlier reports of a high carrier rate of the sickle cell gene in the country [53]. Although the sensitivity analysis showed that no single study had a major impact, it is important to note that studies by Idowu et al. [36] and Lema et al. [14] found very high prevalence rates of over 35%, likely because they had small sample sizes of 85 and 100, respectively. The high carrier prevalence observed in this study suggests the need for targeted interventions, such as premarital counseling, neonatal screening programs, and genetic counseling, to mitigate the burden of SCD in Nigeria. Furthermore, the SCT prevalence observed in this study falls within the reported prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa [10–40%] [3]. It is also three to four times higher than the pooled prevalence estimates in India [7] and other global reports [50, 54], reflecting the influence of malaria-endemic regions where the sickle cell mutation confers a survival advantage. Genetic and sociocultural factors, such as consanguineous marriages in Nigerian-specific areas, may also contribute to these differences. The implication of one out of every five children and adolescents in this study having SCT demonstrated the need for proactive measures, such as premarital screening, as it remains a driver of SCD among the population.

Regional variation in the prevalence of SCD/SCT across the geopolitical zones in Nigeria

The highest pooled prevalence of SCD was recorded in the northwestern zone (7.0%), almost twice the national pooled prevalence and three and a half times higher than that in the southeastern zone (2.0%). These findings highlight regional disparities likely influenced by genetic, environmental, and healthcare-related factors. Concurrently, the northwest also had the highest pooled prevalence of SCT, partly explaining the higher SCD burden in the region. Sociocultural factors, such as consanguineous marriages, increase the likelihood of recessive genetic disorders and contribute to the observed regional differences [55]. Other factors, including awareness levels and access to diagnostic and treatment resources, may also play a significant role. Regional variations have also been documented in the Middle East [Saudi Arabia] [56], East Africa [Uganda] [56], and India [7, 54], most of which are attributable to sociocultural practices similar to those in northern Nigeria.

With country-limited resources, addressing these disparities requires region-specific public health intervention. Areas with a higher prevalence, such as the northwest, should be prioritized for genetic counseling, premarital screening, and neonatal screening initiatives. Conversely, regions with a lower prevalence, such as the southeast, should focus on sustaining and expanding healthcare services to maintain or reduce the disease burden.

SCD prevalence based on gender, socioeconomic class, and study settings

Five studies synthesized for the prevalence of SCD based on sex did not reveal differences in the burden in both males and females. This observation concurs with a systematic analysis of the “global burden of disease” on sickle cell disease [1] and a recent systematic review and meta-analysis in India [7]. Similarly, a population-based study of 735 rural school children from central India also showed no sex differences in the burden of SCD [57]. The observation of no sex differences among Nigerian children and adolescents is not unexpected, as the mode of inheritance of SCD is via the Mendelian pattern of autosomal recessive, which is not sex-linked [58]. The comparable burden in males and females demonstrated in this study suggests the need for Nigeria to strive to provide optimal care and support for all children and adolescents with sickle cell disease, irrespective of gender.

We did not observe any relationship between the prevalence of SCD and socioeconomic status. This finding requires cautious interpretation because the synthesis included only two studies in the meta-analysis. While studies have demonstrated the impact of socioeconomic factors on SCD outcomes, only a few have explored the relationship between the prevalence of SCD and socioeconomic status [59]. On a comparative note, a study by Derman et al. [60] that included 1339 patients with SCD demonstrated a significant burden among those of low socioeconomic status. In contrast, a population-based study in central Gabon demonstrated no relationship between the prevalence of SCD and socioeconomic status [61]. Limited by two studies, there is a need to evaluate further the role of socioeconomic status among Nigerian children and adolescents regarding the prevalence of SCD. Future studies should include socioeconomic indices in SCD studies to ensure robust data analysis that could guide decisions in allocating available limited resources.

This study also shows that the pooled prevalence of SCD is higher in the hospital-based studies than in the community-based studies. This finding is similar to a recent systematic review and analysis conducted in India [7]. The observations from this study that show a higher burden in hospital settings is expected because of selection bias, in which symptomatic children with SCD seek medical attention because of various complications. In contrast, community-based studies recruit broader, more representative, and larger population samples, usually with a large number of apparently healthy children. These findings imply the need to report the study settings, especially when the burden reported for SCD is higher than expected, and ensure proper interpretation. Confirming the significant burden in the hospital setting calls for a dedicated service for SCD with adequate support, especially at various Nigerian tertiary and secondary health facilities. This will ensure optimal care for the affected children and adolescents when they present at these health facilities.

Quality of the studies, publication bias, and heterogeneity

Overall, the quality of the included studies was rated as good, with 29 out of 30 rated as high quality, further supporting the reliability of our findings [22]. However, the significant findings of high heterogeneity are worth highlighting, indicating significant variations in the studies that are not explained by chance. This is not unexpected considering that more than two-thirds of the studies included were cross-sectional studies, which are more sensitive to contextual variation [62]. The high heterogeneity may reflect population characteristics (e.g., age, sex, region, and socioeconomic status), diagnostic criteria methods, study settings (hospital-based vs. community-based), and year of study. Despite the high heterogeneity observed in our analysis, the stability of the pooled prevalence after the ‘Trim-and-Fill’ of the funnels suggested the robustness of our findings and accurate reflections of the burden of SCD in Nigeria. Indeed, the consistency of the pooled estimates when each study was omitted demonstrates that the meta-analysis results are robust and enhance confidence in the pooled results’ validity for both SCD and SCT studies. To further address the high heterogeneity, we applied a meta-regression model, which revealed that a substantial proportion of this heterogeneity (R2 = 88%) was attributable to the year of study, diagnostic methods, and study settings. This finding is not unexpected given the population dynamics over the study period, the accuracy of various diagnostic methods, and the potential selection bias related to the study settings.

Implications for public health policy, clinical, and research implications

Public health policy

The high burden of SCD, which is as high as 7% in northwestern Nigeria, indicates the need for the urgent implementation of universal screening of newborns in the country. Universal screening has been demonstrated to be feasible in Nigeria and will ensure the early enrollment of confirmed cases and close monitoring to mitigate poor outcomes [26]. The regional variation from this study also demonstrates the need to prioritize northwestern and southwestern Nigeria, where a significant burden of SCD exists regarding public health intervention, including universal newborn screening, community education, awareness campaigns on SCD and SCT, treatment, and other supportive care. Notwithstanding, health awareness campaigns across geopolitical zones and appropriate legislation to determine genotypes among intending couples will also be crucial for the nation to stem rising SCD trends.

Clinical practice implications

The findings of this study show a significant burden of SCD among hospital-based studies and suggest the need to strengthen and improve access for children and adolescents with SCD [63]. Healthcare workers also need a high index of suspicion to allow prompt early diagnosis and referral to specialized centers for SCD for optimal care and improve outcome. The SCD centers across the six geopolitical zones in Nigeria should ensure that care is extended beyond the vicinity of their facilities [9]. This will ensure sick children and adolescents with SCD will have improved access to quality care, affordable, and ultimately improved outcomes.

Research implications

While the burden of SCT appears stable, the higher prevalence of SCD highlights the need to explore the drivers of SCD in Nigeria. Exploring the drivers of SCD in Nigeria would allow for targeted interventions in preventive measures and ultimately reduce the burden on the country. Most of the studies in this study are cross-sectional and suggest the need for cohort and longitudinal studies to measure the impact of SCD on Nigerian children and adolescents. The limited data on socioeconomics also suggests the need to carry out more studies on the roles of socioeconomic class in the study, as we are careful not to make a definitive conclusion on a pooled analysis with two datasets.

Future research

The significant burden of SCD with regional variations demonstrated in this study suggests the need for the country to urgently understudy the main drivers of the burden of SCD and design appropriate interventions in the short- and long-term with goals and timelines to reduce its burden. This will ensure that, while care is being offered to those with the disease, the burden is progressively reduced with a reduction in the number of new cases, ultimately improving the country's SCD/SCT indices.

Study limitations

Some studies reported extreme prevalence values of 0.0% for SCD, which could have resulted from small sample sizes, misclassifications, or limitations in diagnostic accuracy [10, 33, 42]. A few sub-analyses, such as socioeconomic class, have only two studies that could have affected the outcomes. In addition, the language search for this study was limited to English, with the possibility of missing studies published in other languages. However, we expect this to be minimal, if any, because Nigeria’s official language is English. In addition, we could not carry out a subgroup analysis [rural vs. urban], which would have allowed us to further explore other sources of heterogeneity owing to the non-availability of such data from the source literature. Another limitation of our studies is the inclusion of studies with diagnostic methods that are not clearly stated. These studies were included because they emanated from tertiary centers and were retrospective, with data retrieved from the archives of the SCD database.

Conclusion

This review underscores the significant burden of SCD in Nigeria, which has a higher-than-expected prevalence and the highest burden occurring in the northwestern geopolitical zone in Nigeria. In addition, there was no relationship between sex, socioeconomic class, and prevalence of SCD. These findings emphasize the need for region-specific, culturally sensitive interventions to mitigate the impact of the disease. Additionally, the significant differences between studies highlight the need for consistent diagnostic procedures and a national hemoglobinopathy registry to help accurately track the disease and guide policy choices. Addressing these gaps would improve SCD management and reduce the public health burden in Nigeria.
