A semi-replicating VSV (srVSV)-based platform for developing broad-spectrum mucosal vaccines against influenza A viruses
Authors: Xianmiao Ye, Junnan Lu, Zirong Han, Zhishan Fan, Xinru Hu, Lichuang Jiao, Lisha Deng, Wenming Liu, Junqi Liu, Weiqi Pan, Ling Chen, Liqiang Feng, Caijun Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Influenza A viruses (IAVs) are significant respiratory pathogens characterized by high mutation rates and frequent genetic reassortments, underscoring the need for vaccines that can induce robust and broadly protective mucosal immunity. While replication-competent vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) vectors have the potential to elicit mucosal immunity, their neurovirulence raises significant safety concerns. Herein, we report that a semi-replicating VSV (srVSV) vector, composed of one VSV with the glycoprotein (G) gene deleted (rVSVΔG) and another with the L gene deleted (rVSVΔL), has improved safety. Using srVSV, we constructed a monovalent vaccine (srVSV-N1), expressing the neuraminidase 1 (N1) of IAV. A single intranasal dose of srVSV-N1 elicited both systemic and mucosal immune responses against N1, and provided sterilizing immunity against homologous influenza virus. We further generated a bivalent IAV vaccine (srVSV-N1/N2), co-expressing N1 and N2. A single intranasal dose of srVSV-N1/N2 conferred 80% protection against heterologous IAVs (H1N1 and H3N2). Notably, low-dose priming immunization followed by a high-dose boost with srVSV-N1/N2 fully protected mice against lethal heterologous IAV challenges. These findings demonstrate the potential of the srVSV platform for developing mucosal vaccines against IAVs and other respiratory viruses.

Introduction

Influenza A viruses (IAVs) are highly contagious respiratory pathogens responsible for seasonal epidemics and periodic pandemics, posing a major threat to global public health. According to estimates by the World Health Organization (WHO), seasonal IAV infections cause approximately one billion cases annually, with 3–5 million severe illnesses and 290,000 to 650,000 deaths (WHO, accessed May 2025). Notably, co-circulation of IAVs with other respiratory viruses, such as respiratory syncytial virus, may exacerbate the epidemics (Bajema et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2021). IAV infection may awaken dormant disseminated cancer cells in the lung, leading to metastatic lesions (Chia et al., 2025). While vaccination remains the most cost-effective control strategy, current influenza vaccines are limited by low or variable effectiveness, especially when there is a mismatch between the vaccine and circulating strains (Sandbulte et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2017). Developing IAV vaccines capable of eliciting broadly protective immunity remains a public health priority.

Current IAV vaccines predominantly target the hemagglutinin (HA) protein to induce neutralizing antibody responses, because HA mediates viral attachment to host sialic acid receptors and the subsequent fusion with endosomal membranes. However, rapid antigenic drift and shift, primarily occurring in the HA head domain, can significantly reduce vaccine efficacy (Sandbulte et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2017). The neuraminidase (NA) protein, another glycoprotein present on the surface of IAV, may serve as a promising antigen target. NA plays essential roles during IAV infection by cleaving the sialic acid receptors, facilitating the release of progeny virions, and preventing HA-mediated aggregation. NA-specific antibodies can block the shedding and in vivo dissemination of progeny virions (Kirkpatrick Roubidoux et al., 2022). NA-specific CD8+ T cells directly kill IAV-infected cells and control viral infection, whereas NA-specific CD4+ T cells assist B cells in producing high-quality antibodies and promote the development and expansion of memory CD8+ T cells (Lu et al., 2021; Cullen et al., 2019; Li et al., 2026; Deng et al., 2026). Importantly, NA evolves more slowly than HA and is relatively more conserved across diverse IAV strains. Broadly cross-reactive anti-NA antibodies have been identified (Lei et al., 2023; Lederhofer et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Lv et al., 2025). Several NA-based vaccines have been shown to induce cross-protective antibodies and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses against IAVs (Madsen et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2024; Pascha et al., 2024; Han et al., 2024, Han et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025). Thus, targeting NA for the development of broad-spectrum IAV vaccines is a viable strategy.

Respiratory mucosal immunity is the first line of defense against IAV infection. However, current IAV vaccines rely mainly on intramuscular administration, which typically fails to induce mucosal immunity (Leonard et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2023). Recombinant viral vectors have the potential to deliver antigens directly to the airways, thereby inducing mucosal immunity (Wang et al., 2021; Feng et al., 2021, 2022). Replication-competent vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) vector is among such platforms (Sownthirarajan et al., 2025; Malherbe et al., 2021; Ao et al., 2022). Intranasal immunization with a VSV-vectored SARS-CoV-2 vaccine provides better protection compared to intramuscular immunization (Li et al., 2021). Secretory IgA (sIgA) antibodies, a hallmark of mucosal immunity, show higher neutralizing potency than serum IgG or IgA antibodies (Chen et al., 2024). Replication-competent VSV vectors have demonstrated good safety when administered intramuscularly, as evidenced by the FDA-approved Ebola virus vaccine, rVSVΔG-ZEBOV-GP (Anderson and Coller, 2024). However, neurotoxicity concerns arise with intranasal administration of replication-competent VSV vectors (Clarke et al., 2014; Johnson et al., 2007), as VSV may spread to brain regions by traveling along olfactory nerve axons to olfactory bulb (Reiss et al., 1998). In this context, semi-replicating VSV (srVSV), composed of two defective VSV that can complement the functions of each other, may offer improved safety profiles. srVSV replicates successfully only when both defective VSVs enter the same cell. Otherwise, infection by either defective VSV is abortive (Muik et al., 2012).

In this study, we established a srVSV-based vaccine platform by combining one VSV with the glycoprotein (G) gene deleted (rVSVΔG) and another VSV with the L gene deleted (rVSVΔL). We inserted expression cassettes encoding the neuraminidase 1 (N1) or neuraminidase 2 (N2) of IAV into srVSV to generate a monovalent vaccine, srVSV-N1 and a bivalent vaccine, srVSV-N1/N2. Using mouse models, we investigated the in vivo dissemination, immunogenicity, and protective efficacy of these srVSV-based vaccines against IAV infection. We found that srVSV-N1/N2 provided complete protection against lethal infection with heterologous IAV strains using a strategy of low-dose intranasal prime followed by a high-dose boost strategy.

Results

srVSV shows reduced infectivity in brain tissues compared to wtVSV

It has been shown that intranasally administered VSV may spread into the brain and infect neurons, astrocytes, and microglia, raising concerns about using replication-competent VSV for intranasal vaccine delivery (Moseman et al., 2020). To mitigate the neurovirulence of replication-competent VSV, we constructed rVSVΔG-GFP by replacing the G gene with the GFP gene and rVSVΔL-mCh by replacing the L gene with the mCherry gene. We rescued rVSVΔG-GFP and rVSVΔL-mCh separately by co-transfecting the genomic plasmids and helper plasmids into BHK-T7 cells. After co-propagation of rVSVΔG-GFP and rVSVΔL-mCh in Vero cells, we successfully obtained srVSV (Fig. 1A). In Vero cells that are deficient in type I interferon (IFN) pathways, the plaques formed by srVSV were larger than those by wtVSV (Fig. 1B and C). However, in A549 cells that are competent to produce type I IFNs, the plaques formed by srVSV were significantly smaller than those by wtVSV (Fig. 1B and C), implying that srVSV was more sensitive to type I IFN responses compared to wtVSV. In Vero cells, the titers of srVSV were approximately 50 times lower than wtVSV at 48 h post infection (p.i) (Fig. 1D). In contrast, in cell lines derived from mouse (LLC cells) or human (A549 and Calu-3 cells) airway tracts, the peak titers of srVSV were 2–3 logs lower than wtVSV (Fig. 1E–G). To test the pathogenicity of srVSV following an intranasal administration, we challenged 7-week-old female BALB/c mice with 2.0 × 105 plaque-forming units (PFUs) srVSV or wtVSV (Fig. 1H). Mice treated with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) were used as healthy controls. srVSV caused only a slight and transient loss of body weight during days 2–6 p.i., similar to wtVSV (Fig. 1I). Notably, we did not detect any live srVSV virions in the lung tissues, nor any pathological changes (Fig. 1J and L), suggesting that the replication of srVSV is suppressed in the lung. In contrast, we detected high titers of wtVSV (6.7 × 106 PFU/g) in the lung tissues at 1 day p.i. but not at 3 or 7 days, as well as alveolar wall thickening and inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 1J and L), suggesting that wtVSV experiences transient replication in the lungs. Similarly, srVSV infection did not generate detectable brain viral loads, whereas wtVSV infection led to high brain viral loads (∼3.5 × 105 PFU/g and ∼1.3 × 105 PFU/g at 1 and 3 days p.i., respectively), albeit no inflammatory infiltration or pathological alterations in the brain tissues of mice receiving either virus (Fig. 1K and M). This result suggests that, unlike wtVSV which spreads into the brain after intranasal inoculation, srVSV has limited replication capacity in the lung and cannot effectively spread into the brain.

To test the pathogenicity in immunodeficient mice, interferon-α receptor knock-out (IFNAR−/−) C57BL/6 mice were intranasally infected with a single low (1.0 × 103 PFU) or high (1.0 × 104 PFU) dose of either srVSV or wtVSV. Mice treated with PBS were used as healthy controls. The body weight and survival rates were monitored daily (Fig. 2A). A high dose of wtVSV caused rapid body weight loss and 100% mortality before 3 days p.i. (Fig. 2B and C). In contrast, the same dose of srVSV caused mouse death more lately than wtVSV. Notably, a low dose of wtVSV caused severe body weight loss and 100% mortality, similar to a high dose of srVSV, but a low dose of srVSV caused only moderate body weight loss and half of mice survived (Fig. 2B and C). Generation and characterization of srVSV-N1

To test the feasibility of srVSV as a vaccine vector for respiratory viruses, we inserted the codon-optimized N1 sequence of IAV CA/09 (H1N1pdm) into rVSVΔG (Fig. 3A). The resultant rVSVΔG-N1 and rVSVΔL-mCh could be separately rescued in BHK-T7 cells, and then co-propagated in Vero cells to obtain srVSV-N1 (Fig. 3A). We retained the native signal peptide and transmembrane domain of N1 to facilitate its transport to cell surface after translation (Barman et al., 2004). Theoretically, rVSVΔG-N1 undergoes a single-round infection without the G protein, and thus only the initially infected cells express N1 due to the absence of progeny virions. However, in the case of srVSV-N1, rVSVΔL-mCh provides G proteins in trans, which enable the replication of rVSVΔG-N1 and thus enhance the expression of N1 in the infected cells. To test this hypothesis, we infected Vero cells with srVSV-N1 or rVSVΔG-N1. We detected significantly higher expression of N1 in cells infected by srVSV-N1 compared to cells infected by rVSVΔG-N1 (Fig. 3B and C). Consistently, we detected significantly higher NA activities in the culture media of cells infected by srVSV-N1 compared to those infected by rVSVΔG-N1 (Fig. 3D), suggesting that at least a proportion of N1 proteins were released to the culture media via lytic virus infection. Notably, we observed the presence of N1 on the surface of infected cells, whereas mCh was mostly localized in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3E). srVSV-N1 elicits systemic and mucosal immune responses in mice

To test the immunogenicity of srVSV-N1, we intranasally immunized 7-week-old female BALB/c mice once with srVSV (2.0 × 105 PFU), rVSVΔG-N1 (1.0 × 106 PFU), or an either low (2.0 × 104 PFU) or high (2.0 × 105 PFU) dose of srVSV-N1. At 4 weeks after immunization, we examined the antibody and cell-mediated immune (CMI) responses (Fig. 4A). Immunization with srVSV-N1 or rVSVΔG-N1 but not srVSV induced anti-N1 IgG antibodies in the sera. Notably, the titers in mice receiving a low dose of srVSV-N1 were comparable to those in mice receiving a 50-fold higher dose of rVSVΔG-N1 (geometric mean titer, GMT: 2171 versus 1782 for low-dose srVSV-N1 and rVSVΔG-N1, respectively). A high dose of srVSV-N1 elicited even higher titers of anti-N1 IgG antibodies (GMT: 5521) (Fig. 4B). To assess the quality of immunization-induced antibody responses, we performed enzyme-linked lectin assay (ELLA) to measure the NA inhibition (NAI) activities in the immune sera. The NAI activities in mice receiving a high dose of srVSV-N1 were slightly higher (but not significantly) than those in mice receiving the low dose, and both were significantly higher than those in mice receiving rVSVΔG-N1 (Fig. 4C), suggesting that srVSV-N1 elicits anti-N1 antibody responses with higher quality than does rVSVΔG-N1.

We next determined the polarization of Th1/Th2 responses by testing the N1-specific IgG1/IgG2a ratios. The IgG1/IgG2a ratios of anti-N1 antibodies were mostly below 0.5 in mice receiving rVSVΔG-N1, indicating a Th1-biased response (Fig. 4D). These ratios were approximately to or slightly higher than 0.5 but below 1.0 in mice receiving srVSV-N1, indicating a more balanced Th1/Th2 response. Thus, unlike rVSVΔG-N1 that elicits a mild and Th1-biased response, srVSV-N1 elicits a robust and balanced Th1/Th2 immune response.

To determine whether the immunization also induces CMI responses, we performed ELISpot assays using lymphocytes from mouse spleens. Upon stimulation with N1 peptide pools, we detected IFN-γ-secreting cells in the spleens of mice receiving srVSV-N1 and rVSVΔG-N1 but not in those receiving empty srVSV (Fig. 4E). Unexpectedly, a low dose of srVSV-N1 induced higher N1-specific CMI responses than the high dose. Thus, srVSV-N1 is much better than rVSVΔG-N1 in inducing antigen-specific CMI responses.

Given the pivotal roles of mucosal immunity in preventing IAV infection (Matsuda et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2024), we examined the antigen-specific sIgA antibodies in mouse bronchoalveolar lavage fluids (BALFs). All the mice receiving srVSV-N1 generated N1-specific sIgA antibodies in the BALFs in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4F). However, rVSVΔG-N1 did not induce detectable N1-specific sIgA antibodies, similar to srVSV (Fig. 4F). This result suggests that srVSV-N1 is also much better than rVSVΔG-N1 in inducing respiratory mucosal immunity. Considering the replication features of these two types of vaccines, the induction of antigen-specific sIgA antibodies may rely on a productive replication of VSV vectors after inoculation.

srVSV-N1 completely protects against homologous IAV infection

To assess the protective efficacy of srVSV-N1, we intranasally challenged the immunized mice with an antigenically matched IAV CA/09 (H1N1pdm) strain at 5 × LD50 (median lethal dose) per mouse at 4 weeks after immunization (Fig. 5A). All the mice receiving srVSV-N1, even at the low dose, survived and showed no obvious loss of body weight (Fig. 5B and C). The mice receiving rVSVΔG-N1 also survived, but showed significant loss of body weight from day 4 to day 8 and gradually recovered thereafter. In contrast, all the mice receiving srVSV showed severe loss of body weight and succumbed to infection (Fig. 5B and C). We then examined the viral loads in the lung tissues at 5 days after challenge. In mice receiving srVSV-N1, at either low or high dose, we did not detect any live viral particles, indicating complete protection. We also examined the pathological changes of lung tissues by H&E staining at 5 days after challenge. The lung tissues of mice receiving srVSV-N1 at either low or high dose showed no infiltration of inflammatory cells or obvious thickening of alveolar walls (Fig. 5E). The lung tissues of mice receiving rVSVΔG-N1 showed mild perivascular lymphocytic infiltration and bronchial wall thickening. In contrast, the lung tissues of mice receiving srVSV showed severe interstitial hyperplasia, dense lymphocytic infiltration around alveoli, and alveolar space obliteration (Fig. 5E). Consistently, at this time point, we did not observe the expression of IAV NP in the lung tissues of mice receiving srVSV-N1 (Fig. 5F), suggesting that the immunization effectively suppresses the replication of invading IAV. We detected scattered NP-positive cells in mice receiving rVSVΔG-N1, suggesting that in these mice the invading IAV undergoes productive replication. There were large amounts of NP-positive cells in the alveolar regions of mice receiving srVSV (Fig. 5F). Together, srVSV-N1 immunization, even at a low dose, confers much better protection against homologous IAV infection than does rVSVΔG-N1.

srVSV-N1/N2 effectively protects against heterologous IAV infection

An ideal IAV vaccine should provide broad protection against both H1N1 and H3N2 subtypes. To achieve this, we developed a bivalent IAV vaccine, named srVSV-N1/N2, consisting of rVSVΔG-N1 and rVSVΔL-N2 that expresses a tetramerized head of NA protein derived from A/HongKong/45/2019 (H3N2) (Fig. 6A). In Vero cells infected with srVSV-N1/N2, we detected the expression of both N1 and N2 proteins (Fig. 6B). We intranasally immunized 7-week-old female BALB/c mice once with srVSV (2.0 × 105 PFU), rVSVΔG-N1 (1.0 × 106 PFU), a low or high dose of srVSV-N1/N2 (2.0 × 104 or 2.0 × 105 PFU, respectively) (Fig. 6C). At 3 weeks after immunization, we detected IgG antibodies against the N1 of A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (PR8; H1N1) in all mice receiving the high dose of srVSV-N1/N2 (GMT: 437; range: 128–804) and in 4 out of 5 mice receiving the low dose (GMT: 101; range: 30–200) (Fig. 6D). The antibody titers were significantly higher in the high-dose group compared to the low-dose group. Similarly, srVSV-N1/N2 immunization also elicited IgG antibodies against the N2 protein of A/Aichi/2/1968 (X-31; H3N2), with GMTs of 182 (range: 30–402) and 132 (range: 30–205) for the high and low dose, respectively (Fig. 6E). In contrast, rVSVΔG-N1 induced IgG antibodies against PR8 N1 but not X-31 N2 in only 2 out of 5 mice (Fig. 6D and E). A single high dose of srVSV-N1/N2 conferred 80% protection against PR8 infection, with moderate loss of body weight (<10%) observed. Only one mouse in this group reached humane endpoint criteria and was euthanized (Fig. 6F and G). A low dose of srVSV-N1/N2 also offered some level of protection, resulting in one surviving mouse in this group. However, rVSVΔG-N1 showed no obvious protection against PR8 infection. All the mice in this group succumbed to infection, similar to those receiving srVSV (Fig. 6F and G). Notably, srVSV-N1/N2 immunization also significantly protected against X-31 infection, achieving 80% and 60% survival rates in the high- and low-dose groups, respectively (Fig. 6H and I). Conversely, neither rVSVΔG-N1 nor srVSV provided any observable protection against X-31 infection. Hence, srVSV-N1/N2 confers much greater protection against heterologous IAV infection compared to rVSVΔG-N1.

A prime-boost strategy further improves the protective effects of srVSV-N1/N2

To further improve the protective effects of srVSV-N1/N2, we explored a low-dose prime and high-dose boost strategy (Fig. 7A). A low-dose prime provides limited antigen, which may increase the selection stringency of germinal center B cells for high affinities, thereby enhancing the generation of high-quality antibodies after the boosting immunization (Garg et al., 2021). To test this hypothesis, we intranasally immunized 7-week-old female BALB/c mice with 1.0 × 104 PFU of srVSV or srVSV-N1/N2. Three weeks later, we gave the mice an intranasal boost with 2.0 × 105 PFU of the respective vaccines. At 4 weeks after the final immunization, we challenged the mice with PR8 or X-31 strain, similarly as described above. In this context, srVSV-N1/N2 induced high titers of IgG antibodies against N1 (GMT: 1642; range: 697–2811) and N2 (GMT: 510; range: 207–765) (Fig. 7B and C). Notably, srVSV-N1/N2 fully protected against PR8 infection, with no significant loss of body weight and 100% survival rates observed in the challenged mice (Fig. 7D and F). srVSV-N1/N2 also effectively protected against X-31 infection. The immunized mice showed only slight loss of body weight after X-31 challenge and all survived (Fig. 7E and G). In contrast, all mice receiving srVSV showed severe loss of body weight and gradually died after challenge with either PR8 or X-31. These results suggest that it is feasible to improve the cross-protective effects of srVSV-N1/N2 by adding a low-dose priming immunization. We also observed a strong correlation between the titers of anti-NA IgG antibodies and the protective effects against weight loss caused by heterologous IAV infection (Fig. 7H and I), supporting that srVSV-N1/N2 elicits high-quality, cross-reactive anti-NA antibody responses, thereby providing effective protection against heterologous IAVs. We also collected the lung tissues for H&E analysis at day 5 post-challenge. The lungs of mice immunized with srVSV-N1/N2 showed no inflammatory cell infiltration after challenge with PR8 and only mild inflammatory cell infiltration after challenge with X-31. In contrast, significant inflammatory cell infiltration and obvious bronchial wall thickening were observed in the lungs of mice immunized with srVSV (Fig. 7J and K). Discussion

It remains a high priority to develop a broadly protective vaccine against seasonal and pandemic IAVs. To achieve this, powerful genetic vectors and optimized antigen design are both desirable for provoking mucosal immunity in upper airway tracts, the initial sites for IAVs infection. srVSV presents a promising candidate. srVSV effectively infects G62 and U87 human glioblastoma cells, but no neurotoxicity is observed by an intracranial injection in mice (Muik et al., 2012). We show that srVSV maintains replication capacity but has reduced infectivity in brain tissues after an intranasal inoculation (Fig. 1). Even in immunodeficient mice, the safety of srVSV was significantly improved compared with wtVSV (Fig. 2). Both components of srVSV can deliver IAV antigens and confer higher expression than does rVSVΔG, a single-round vector due to the lack of G protein (Fig. 3). We find that srVSV vectors, expressing antigens derived from IAVs, can induce both systemic and mucosal immunity (Fig. 4). A single-dose monovalent vaccine srVSV-N1 immunization completely protects against homologous IAV infection (Fig. 5), whereas two doses of srVSV-N1/N2 completely protect against lethal challenge by heterologous H1N1 and H3N2 (Fig. 7). To our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt to utilize srVSV as a vaccine vector, demonstrating its safety and potency for vaccines against IAVs and other respiratory viruses.

There have been at least two types of recombinant VSV vectors explored for vaccine development. Replication-competent VSV vectors, of which the native G protein is usually replaced with target antigens that can initiate the infection cycle, have shown great immunogenicity and protective efficacy in multiple vaccines such as those against Ebola, SARS-CoV-2 and IAVs (Sownthirarajan et al., 2025; Malherbe et al., 2021; Anderson and Coller, 2024). However, application of such vectors in immuno-deficient individuals might cause safety concerns. Single-round VSV vectors, usually defective in one or more VSV genes essential for various infection stages, also have been used to develop vaccines against viral infections (Cooper et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2024; Walz et al., 2018; Adeleke et al., 2025), but the immunogenicity is variable in animal models. VSV∗ΔG (NAPR8), a G-deleted VSV vector harboring the PR8 NA, induced NA-specific IgG antibodies but not CMI responses in mice (Walz et al., 2018), similar to our observations (Fig. 4). Another G-deleted VSV vector pseudotyped by the S protein of SARS-CoV-2 and the NA protein of IAV elicited protective and durable immunity against both viruses in mice (Adeleke et al., 2025), but the dose was relative high (30 μg total protein per mouse, equivalent to ∼6.0 × 1010 viral particles). In this regard, srVSV is a promising alternative with advantages. srVSV is replication-competent but attenuated both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The safety concerns regarding the neurotoxicity of replication-competent VSV vectors are at least partially resolved, while the expression of target antigens is superior over replication-incompetent VSV vectors (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). The efficient propagation of srVSV in Vero cells, an approved and widely used cell line, facilitates a large-scale production. Moreover, srVSV is flexible for further optimization, such as rearranging the location of antigen-encoding cassettes to elevate their expression (Hinzman et al., 2002), and replacing native G protein with the counterparts from other viruses to alter the tropism (Betancourt et al., 2015).

Most proteins encoded by IAV have been tested as antigens for a universal vaccine. Native HA protein is not an ideal choice, because the constant mutations on the globular head leads to antigenic drift (Sandbulte et al., 2011). Efforts have been therefore taken to focus the immune responses on epitopes conserved among IAV strains, for instance, by using a computationally optimized stem region of HA instead of the highly variable head (Impagliazzo et al., 2015; Yassine et al., 2015). NA protein has also been tested, as subunit vaccines or expressed by genetic vectors. Our results, along with those of other studies (Yue et al., 2024; Pascha et al., 2024; Leonard et al., 2024), strongly support the feasibility of NA as a target antigen (Figs. 5–7). A low dose of srVSV-N1 induced comparable anti-N1 IgG antibodies compared to a 50-fold higher dose of rVSVΔG-N1, but only the former achieved a complete protection against homologous IAV challenge (Fig. 4, Fig. 5), implying that sIgA antibodies and CMI responses, detectable in mice receiving srVSV-N1 but not in those receiving rVSVΔG-N1, contribute greatly to the protection. This is consistent to previous findings that mucosal sIgA are more important than serum IgG in inhibiting respiratory virus infection (Chen et al., 2024).

SrVSV-N1/N2 showed effectively protection against lethal infection by heterologous IAVs PR8 and X-31 (Fig. 7), but the protection against PR8 seemed to be greater than that against X-31, although the similarity of PR8 NA with the vaccine strain (CA/09H1N1pdm, 81%) is lower than that of X-31 NA with the vaccine strain (A/HongKong/45/2019H3N2, 85%). This might be attributed to the difference in antigen design. The N1 antigen, in its native sequence and structure, is present on the surface of infected host cells, released to extracellular compartments (Fig. 3), and incorporated into VSV envelope (Adeleke et al., 2025). The N2 antigen, however, only maintains the head domain, and thus is mostly secreted to head compartments after translation. Naturally tetrameric NA may have better immunogenicity than the globular head domain tetramerized artificially. In addition, the localization of N1 and N2 in srVSV genome, the original G and L gene loci respectively, may affect their expression, given that the transcription of VSV genes has a 3' → 5′ gradient (Hinzman et al., 2002). Actually, we have constructed a new srVSV-N1/N2 that carried the coding sequence for a full-length N2 protein, but this virus was difficult to rescue (data not shown). Future work may focus on re-arranging the localization of N2 to rescue the refined srVSV-N1/N2, and co-delivering multiple NA antigens or designed mosaic NA, in order to elicit broader and more balanced antibody responses against diverse influenza viruses.

A srVSV-based vaccine may have advantages over live-attenuated IAV vaccines (LAIVs). LAIVs, based on either cold-adaptive mutations or down-regulations of one or more IAV proteins, have the potential to induce both systemic and mucosal immunity (Liu et al., 2025; Jang et al., 2012), but it is difficult to adjust their antigen composition to optimize the immune responses. Fully functional HA protein, essential for the propagation of LAIVs in either embryonic eggs or cells lines, strongly competes with NA and other antigens for both antibody and T-cell responses (Johansson and Kilbourne, 1996). Additionally, the immunogenicity of LAIVs may be impaired by pre-existing anti-IAV immunity. A transient while effective infection is required for LAIVs to provoke significant immune responses, but may be suppressed if there has been antibodies or T cells reactive to the vaccine strains (Lobby et al., 2024). Moreover, LAIVs may undergo adaptive mutations during production in eggs or cell lines. These mutations, particularly in the HA protein, can lead to an alteration in immunogenicity or an antigenic mismatch (Nakowitsch et al., 2011). An IAV vaccine based on srVSV may bypass these issues. Antigen composition are compatible with rational design by incorporating other protective and conserved antigens such as HA2, matrix protein M1, NP, and ion channel protein M2 (Wang et al., 2025). The relative expression levels of these antigens can be manipulated by adjusting their locations in VSV genome (Hinzman et al., 2002). Antigen sequences may largely remain stable during propagation due to the absence of selection pressure (Schnell et al., 1996).

The srVSV vector may be superior over other commonly used vectors such as adenovirus (Ad) and modified vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA) vectors (de Vries and Rimmelzwaan, 2016). Pre-existing immunity against human Ad5 is usually high in populations, which may significantly compromise vaccine immunogenicity (Barouch et al., 2011). By contrast, VSV is a bovine-derived virus with minimal pre-existing immunity in humans. MVA vectors carrying IAV NP (MVA-NP) primarily induces T-cell responses but does not elicit neutralizing antibodies. MVA-NP fails to provide effective protection by a single dose (Dégano et al., 1999). A heterologous prime-boost regimen using Ad5-NP + M1 followed by MVA-NP + M1 cannot protect against low dose (25 PFU) PR8 virus challenge (Lambe et al., 2013). Notably, it has been reported that the MVA-HA + NP vaccine undergoes genomic recombination with naturally occurring cowpox virus (CPXV) (Diaz-Cánova et al., 2024). This raises significant safety concerns, particularly with the recent global outbreak of monkeypox virus. Therefore, srVSV adds to the promising tools for developing universal IAV vaccines.

One limitation of our study is the lack of direct comparisons between replication-competent VSV vectors and srVSV, which can more comprehensively support the advantages of srVSV. However, one previous study showed that a single-cycle rVSVΔG-EnVG, expressing the HIV envelope protein, elicited antibody and T cell responses at comparable levels compared to a replication-competent rVSV-EnVG (Publicover et al., 2005). Our results showed that srVSV-N1, inoculated intranasally, elicited higher titers of IgG and NAI antibodies than the single-cycle rVSVΔG-N1 (Fig. 4), supporting the good immunogenicity of srVSV vectors. Given that wtVSV but not srVSV disseminated into brain after an intranasal infection, and replication-competent VSV vectors but not srVSV have potential neurotoxicity (Johnson et al., 2007; Muik et al., 2012), srVSV may balance the safety and efficacy compared to replication-competent VSV vectors.

Unexpectedly, we observed that a high dose of srVSV-N1 preferentially induced antibody responses, whereas a low-dose immunization induced lower antibodies but higher CMI responses (Fig. 4). A similar phenomenon has been reported in SARS-CoV-2 vaccine in animal models and human recipients (Rappaport et al., 2022; Sadoff et al., 2021). Given that VSV vectors are generally potent activators for innate immunity such as Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7)-mediated pathways, a high-dose srVSV-N1 immunization may trigger a hyper-activation of TLR7-expressing plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), leading to the massive production of IFN-α/β (Saitoh et al., 2017; Diebold et al., 2004). This may drive plasmablast differentiation and enhance antibody class-switching, while failing to activate T cell expansion (Swanson et al., 2010). A high antigen load, as the case of a high-dose immunization, may promote MHC-II presentation (Andersen et al., 2019). However, the detailed mechanism underlying this phenomenon needs to be delineated in future studies.

Conclusions

In summary, this “proof-of-concept” study demonstrates that srVSV-vectored IAV vaccine expressing the NA antigens can induce broadly protective immunity by an intranasal administration, providing a feasible and potent platform for the development of universal IAV vaccines.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

African green monkey kidney Vero cells (ATCC, No. CCL81), human lung adenocarcinoma A549 cells (ATCC, No. CCL-185), Madin-Darby canine kidney MDCK cells (ATCC, No. CCL-34), Lewis lung carcinoma LLC cells (ATCC, No. CRL-1642), baby hamster kidney BHK-T7 cells expressing T7 polymerase (kindly provided by Professor Li Yu from Harbin Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell Bio, Montevideo, Uruguay). Human lung adenocarcinoma Calu-3 cells (ATCC, No. HTB-55) were cultured in Advanced MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and NAA.

Viruses

Influenza virus A/California/04/2009 [CA/09 (H1N1pdm)], A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (PR8; H1N1), and A/Aichi/2/1968 (X-31; H3N2) were propagated in 9-day-old SPF embryonated chicken eggs incubated at 37 °C for 72 h. Allantoic fluids were harvested and centrifuged at 134,000 ×g in a vertical ultracentrifuge at 4 °C for 2 h to precipitate virus particles. IAVs were purified by sucrose density gradient ultracentrifugation, re-suspended in PBS containing 0.3% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich), and stored at −80 °C. The titers of all IAVs were determined by plaque-forming assays in MDCK cells.

Plasmid construction and virus preparation

Plasmid pVSV-GFP, harboring the expression cassette for green fluorescent protein (GFP) between G and L genes of vesicular stomatitis Indiana virus strain (GeneBank No. NC_001560.1), was used to rescue rVSV-GFP (hereafter termed wtVSV). Plasmid pVSVΔG-GFP with the G gene deleted from pVSV-GFP was used to rescue rVSVΔG-GFP. pVSVΔL-mCh was constructed by replacing the fragment covering the GFP and L genes with a fragment covering the mCherry cassette. wtVSV was rescued by co-transfecting pVSV-GFP with helper plasmids encoding the N, P, and L proteins into BHK-T7 cells. rVSVΔG-GFP and rVSVΔL-mCh were rescued individually by co-transfecting pVSVΔG-GFP or pVSVΔL-mCh with helper plasmids encoding the N, P, G and L proteins into BHK-T7 cells. srVSV-GFP/mCh (hereafter termed srVSV) was then obtained by co-propagating rVSVΔG-GFP and rVSVΔL-mCh in Vero cells.

To construct srVSV-N1 that expresses the neuraminidase 1 (N1) of IAV and the reporter gene mCherry (mCh), the coding sequences for N1 of IAV strain CA/09 (H1N1pdm) (GenBank No. MN371610.1) were optimized according to mammalian codon usage and synthesized. N1 sequence was used to replace the GFP gene in pVSVΔG-GFP to obtain plasmid pVSVΔG-N1. To construct srVSV-N1/N2 that expresses N1 and N2 of IAVs, the coding sequence for the head domain (83–469aa) of N2 derived from IAV A/Hong Kong/45/2019 (H3N2) was optimized and synthesized. N2 sequence, fused with the tetramerization domain of measles virus nucleoprotein at the N-terminus (Strohmeier et al., 2021), was used to replace the mCherry gene in pVSVΔL-mCh to obtain pVSVΔL-N2. rVSVΔG-N1 and rVSVΔL-N2 were rescued individually in BHK-T7 cells as mentioned above. Subsequently, srVSV-N1 and srVSV-N1/N2 were obtained by co-propagating rVSVΔG-N1 with either rVSVΔL-mCh or rVSVΔL-N2 in Vero cells. All the viruses were concentrated using a Concentration Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and purified by sucrose density gradient centrifugation. Finally, purified viruses were titrated by plaque-forming assays and stored at −80 °C.

Plaque-forming assay

Viral titers were measured using plaque-forming assays as described previously (Ye et al., 2021). In brief, virus stocks or tissue homogenates were serially diluted and added onto ∼100% confluent Vero or A549 cells in 6-well plates. After incubation at 37 °C for 1.5 h, the culture media were discarded, and the cells were overlaid with 1 × MEM medium supplemented with 2% FBS and 0.6% low-melting-point agarose. After 1, 2, or 3 days of incubation at 37 °C, cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet. Viral titers were determined as plaque-forming units (PFU) per mL. Images of representative wells were captured, and the areas of plaques were also calculated.

Growth curves

The Vero, LLC, A549, or Calu-3 cells in 96-well plates were infected with wtVSV or srVSV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.001. After 1.5 h, the culture media were removed and added 2% FBS DMEM. At 2, 6, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h post-infection (p.i.), the culture supernatants were harvested and detected by plaque-forming assays.

Immunofluorescence assay

Vero cells in 24-well plates were infected with 0.001 MOI srVSV, rVSVΔG-N1, or srVSV-N1. After 36 h, culture supernatants were discarded. Cells were then fixed and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, and blocked with PBS containing 5% BSA. Subsequently, the anti-N1 polyclonal antibody (R&D, Minneapolis, USA) and anti-mCh monoclonal antibody (proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) were incubated into cells. After thoroughly washing, The CF™ 488A-conjugated donkey anti-sheep IgG (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were added into cells and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The mages were captured using the 3D Histech digital slide scanner (Pannoramic MIDI, Budapest, Hungary).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

For cell-based ELISA, Vero cells were seeded into 96-well plates and infected with srVSV, rVSVΔG-N1, and srVSV-N1 at an MOI of 0.001. At 36 h p.i., the culture media were removed. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS, and blocked with PBST (PBS + 0.05% Tween-20) containing 5% skimmed milk. Anti-N1 antibody were 3-fold serially diluted (starting at 1:100), added to the plates and incubated for 2 h. Subsequently, HRP-labelled rabbit anti-sheep IgG (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) were added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The plates were developed using TMB/E substrate (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), and the reactions were terminated with 1 M H2SO4. Finally, absorbance (OD450) was measured using an Epoch2 Microplate Reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Area under the curve (AUC) was calculated, with the average value of negative control wells as the baseline.

Antigen-coated ELISA was performed as previously described (Yin et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). In brief, ELISA plates were coated with N1 or N2 proteins (1 μg/mL) at 4 °C overnight and blocked. Mouse sera or bronchoalveolar lavage fluids (BALFs) were 5-fold serially diluted (starting at 1:30 or 1:50), added into the coated plates, and incubated for 2 h. After thoroughly washing, the plates were added with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-Mouse IgG1 or IgG2a (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The following steps were performed as described above. The titers of IgG, IgG1 and IgG2a were calculated as the reciprocal of the highest dilutions at which the OD450 values were equal to or higher than the cutoff value (2.1-fold of the mean values from negative control wells).

Enzyme-linked lectin assay (ELLA)

Vero cells in 96-well plates were infected with srVSV, rVSVΔG-N1, or srVSV-N1 at 0.01 MOI. Culture supernatants were harvested at 36 h p.i. Ninety six-well plates were coated with 2.5 μg/well fetuin (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h. After being washed with PBST, plates were added with 100 μL supernatants and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h, followed by incubation with 5 μg/mL HRP-conjugated peanut agglutinin lectin (PNA-HRP) (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 2 h (light-protected). The development, termination, and OD450 readings are similar as ELISA.

To determine the NA inhibition activities (NAI) of immune sera, 96-well plates were coated with 2.5 μg/well fetuin and blocked with 3% BSA. Heat-inactivated mouse sera were 2-fold serially diluted with DPBS (0.05% Tween-20, CaCl2, and MgCl2), mixed with equal volume of CA/09 (H1N1pdm) virus (150 PFU), and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, 100 μL of the mixtures were added to the plates and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. PNA-HRP incubation, development, and OD450 reading were performed as described above. Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated using nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism 9.5 software.

Western-blot analysis

Western-blot analysis was performed as described previously (Ye et al., 2021). In brief, Vero cells in 6 cm dishes were infected with srVSV or srVSV-N1/N2 at 0.001 MOI. At 36 h p.i., cell lysates were harvested and subjected to SDS-PAGE. After transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk in PBST and incubated with anti-N1 or anti-N2 polyclonal antibody (Sino Biological, Beijing, China) at 4 °C overnight. Finally, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-sheep IgG antibody or an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody and developed using HRP substrate.

Animal experiments

Female BALB/c mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. and housed at the Animal Center of Guangzhou Institutes of Biomedicine and Health (GIBH). Immunization and challenge assays were conducted under Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2) conditions.

To test the infectivity of srVSV, 7-week-old female BALB/c mice were intranasally challenged with 2.0 × 105 PFU of srVSV or wtVSV. Mice receiving an equal volume of PBS served as controls. Body weight and mortality were monitored daily. At day 1, 3, or 7 post-infection, a subset of mice in each group were euthanized. Lung and brain tissues were harvested and subjected to infectious virus titration or histopathological analysis. To evaluate the pathogenicity of srVSV in immunodeficient mice, 8-week-old male IFNAR−/− C57BL/6 mice were intranasally infected with 1.0 × 104 or 1.0 × 103 PFU of srVSV and wtVSV, respectively. Mice inoculated with PBS served as the healthy control. Body weight and survival rate were monitored daily.

To test the immunogenicity of srVSV vaccines, 7-week-old female BALB/c mice were intranasally immunized once with 2.0 × 104 or 2.0 × 105 PFU of srVSV-N1, or 1.0 × 106 PFU of rVSVΔG-N1. Mice receiving 2.0 × 105 PFU of srVSV empty were used as controls. Four weeks later, mouse sera, BALFs and spleens were collected.

To test the protective efficacy of srVSV-N1, mice that had been immunized as mentioned above were intranasally challenged with 50 μL of 5 × LD50 of CA/09 (H1N1pdm) at day 28 post-immunization. Body weight and mortality were monitored daily. Five days later, lung tissues were collected for histopathological analysis. To assess the protective efficacy of srVSV-N1/N2, mice were immunized and challenged as described above. Another group of mice were intranasally primed with a low dose (1.0 × 104 PFU) and boosted with a high dose (2.0 × 105 PFU) of srVSV-N1/N2 at a 3-week interval. Four weeks after the final immunization, mice were challenged with 5 × LD50 PR8 (H1N1) or X-31 (H3N2), and body weight and mortality were monitored for 14 days. Five days after infection, lung tissues were harvested for histopathological analysis.

ELISpot assay

ELISpot assay was performed as previously described (Li et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2026). In brief, ELISpot plates were coated with anti–IFN–γ capture antibodies (U-CyTech, Utrecht, the Netherlands) at 4 °C overnight. Splenic lymphocytes were isolated using mouse lymphocyte separation medium (Dakewe, Shenzhen, China) according to manufactory's protocol, seeded into the plates, and stimulated with peptide pools corresponding to N1 of CA/09 (H1N1pdm) (2 μg/mL; GenScript, Nanjing, China). DMSO and ConA (10 μg/mL) were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. After incubation in 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 24 h, plates were added with a biotinylated detection antibody (U-CyTech, Utrecht, the Netherlands) and incubated at 4 °C for 16 h. Subsequently, plates were added with streptavidin-alkaline phosphatase (BD, Franklin Lakes, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Finally, NBT/BCIP substrates (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, USA) was added. Spots were counted using an automated ELISpot reader (Bioreader 4000, BIOSYS, Karben, Germany).

Histopathological analysis

Lung tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin at room temperature (RT) for 48 h and transferred into 70% ethanol. After graded ethanol dehydration, tissues were embedded in paraffin blocks. Next, tissues were sectioned into 5-μm-thick slices. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and stained with hematoxylin for 10 min followed by eosin staining for 3 min. Subsequently, slices were consecutively incubated in ethanol (70% for 20 s, 90% for 20 s, and 100% for 2 min) and then in xylene for 5 min. Finally, images are captured and analyzed using a digital slide scanning and analysis system (3D Histech, Pannoramic MIDI, Budapest, Hungary).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis

Paraffin-embedded lung sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated as described above. Antigen retrieval was performed in sodium citrate buffer, pH 6.0 (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Slides were washed with PBS (pH 7.4), placed in a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution, and incubated at RT for 25 min. After being blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min, slides were incubated with pan influenza A NP antibody at a 1:200 dilution (Sino Biological, Beijing, China) and incubated in a humidified chamber at 4 °C overnight. Following a thorough washing, HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were added. After incubation at RT for 50 min, DAB staining substrates (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) were applied. Slides were then counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and clarified by sequential immersing in ethanol (75%, 85%, and 100%, for 5 min each) and in n-butanol and xylene for 5 min. Images were captured and analyzed using the 3D Histech digital slide scanning and analysis system (Pannoramic MIDI, Budapest, Hungary).

Data analysis and statistics

Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired, two-tailed Student's t-test, and survival rates were performed using the Kaplan-Meier. Other comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Correlation analysis was performed by Pearson's Correlation. Statistical analysis was calculated using GraphPad Prism version 9.5 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Data are presented as the means ± standard error (SD). P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data graphs were constructed using GraphPad Prism 9.5. Figures were created using Adobe Illustrator 2021 (Adobe Systems Inc, San Jose, CA, USA) and PowerPoint 2013 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA).
