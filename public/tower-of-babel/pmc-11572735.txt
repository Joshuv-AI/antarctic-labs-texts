Clinical trials for Lennox–Gastaut syndrome: Challenges and priorities
Authors: Juliet K Knowles, Aaron E L Warren, Ismail S Mohamed, Carl E Stafstrom, Hyun Yong Koh, Debopam Samanta, Renée A Shellhaas, Gita Gupta, Tracy Dixon‐Salazar, Linh Tran, Sonal Bhatia, Jane M McCabe, Anup D Patel, Zachary M Grinspan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: Lennox‐Gastaut syndrome (LGS) is a severe, childhood‐onset epilepsy that is typically refractory to treatment. We surveyed the current landscape of LGS treatment, aiming to identify challenges to the development of efficacious therapies, and to articulate corresponding priorities toward clinical trials that improve outcomes. Methods: The LGS Special Interest Group of the Pediatric Epilepsy Research Consortium integrated evidence from the literature and expert opinion, into a narrative review. Results: We provide an overview of approved and emerging medical, dietary, surgical and neuromodulation approaches for LGS. We note that quality of care could be improved by standardizing LGS treatment based on expert consensus and empirical data. Whereas LGS natural history is incompletely understood, prospective studies and use of large retrospective datasets to understand LGS across the lifespan would enable clinical trials that address these dynamics. Recent discoveries related to LGS pathophysiology should enable development of disease‐modifying therapies, which are currently lacking. Finally, clinical trials have focused chiefly on seizures involving “drops,” but should incorporate additional patient‐centered outcomes, using emerging measures adapted to people with LGS. Interpretation: Clinicians and researchers should enact these priorities, with the goal of patient‐centered clinical trials that are tailored to LGS pathophysiology and natural history.

Introduction

Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS) is a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) that has been widely recognized for decades. In 1950, Lennox and Davis described a constellation of co‐occurring seizure types and characteristic electroencephalogram (EEG) abnormalities; 1 Gastaut further described the syndrome as “certainly not any particular disease, but rather a mode of reaction of the child's brain to a chronic cerebral aggression (of whatever nature).” 2 In 2024, LGS is similarly understood as a secondary brain network disorder. The syndrome begins in children with heterogenous etiologies (structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, and other causes). 3 , 4 Over time, through mechanisms that remain poorly understood, these differing etiologies provoke a convergent pattern of brain network dysfunction (Fig. 1), and associated clinical features. These features, as defined by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), include multiple seizure types (particularly tonic, tonic–clonic, atonic, and atypical absence seizures); neurodevelopmental delay, regression, or arrest; and distinctive EEG findings (slow‐spike‐and‐wave, SSW, and generalized paroxysmal fast activity, GPFA). 5 Taken together, the heterogenous etiologies and the convergent phenotypes of LGS point to the need for precision therapeutic approaches that address specific etiologies and approaches that more broadly address secondary network dysfunction. Yet, effective treatment remains elusive. Five percent of children with epilepsy have LGS; yet LGS accounts for half of healthcare spending on pediatric epilepsy in the United States. 6 , 7 The cost of LGS for affected individuals, families, and caregivers is substantial, including adverse effects on emotional, social, and financial well‐being. 4 , 8

This narrative review reflects monthly discussions of the authorship group during 2023–2024 (14 pediatric epileptologists/clinicians, scientists, and patient advocates from the LGS Special Interest Group of the Pediatric Epilepsy Research Consortium), focused on the need for treatments with greater efficacy. We surveyed the current landscape of LGS therapies and identified key challenges and corresponding priorities for improved clinical trials leading to more effective treatments. In this review, we provide an overview of medical, dietary, surgical, and neuromodulation treatments for LGS, focusing particularly on new/emerging approaches and how they might be integrated into standard treatment. We then outline three broad challenges and corresponding priorities for clinical trial readiness. First, relatively little is known about how LGS begins and evolves over the lifespan, and whether disease trajectories differ among LGS etiologies. This knowledge gap hinders the development of preventative and/or precision therapies for LGS, and the assessment of treatment effects across the lifespan. Ultimately, patient registries are needed for prospective natural history data, but knowledge of LGS natural history can be expedited with analysis of large electronic health record data. Second, there is a need for disease‐modifying therapies based on mechanistic understanding. The differing etiologies and convergent secondary network features of LGS point to the simultaneous need for precision therapeutics (that address specific etiologies) and more general approaches (that address secondary network dysfunction). Finally, there is a mismatch between clinical trial endpoints and concerns articulated by families/caregivers of people with LGS. We discuss innovative efforts to adapt clinical trial endpoints and quantitative instruments for patient‐centered clinical trials.

Overview of Established and Emerging Therapies for LGS

Medication and dietary therapy

Expert consensus indicates that valproic acid is the first‐line therapy for LGS. 9 In addition to valproic acid, medications with specific FDA‐approved indications for adjunctive therapy in LGS include felbamate, lamotrigine, topiramate, rufinamide, clobazam, fenfluramine, and cannabidiol. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Clinical trials for LGS have increased in number. Three major trials conducted in the last 10 years (for cannabidiol and fenfluramine, each resulting in statistically significant decreases in seizure burden, and for soticlestat, resulting in a nonsignificant numerical difference in seizures involving drops) are summarized in Table 1. For specific genetic causes of LGS, two new therapies have been approved, ganaxolone for CDKL5‐defiency disorder 18 , 19 and trofenitide for Rett syndrome. 20 , 21 22 Newly approved medical therapies can now be integrated into improved treatment algorithms for LGS. In addition, trials are ongoing for LP352, a selective 5‐HT2c receptor agonist (NCT05626634), and carisbamate, a member of the carbamate family that also includes felbamate and cenobamate (NCT05219617).

Corticosteroid therapy and other hormonal and immunomodulatory agents are used by some centers for refractory seizures in LGS, including intermittent and continuous regimens with a variety of agents (e.g., dexamethasone, 23 , 24 prednisolone, 25 , 26 methylprednisolone, 24 , 27 , 28 , 29 ACTH, 30 and intravenous immunoglobulin 31 ). These retrospective, single‐center experiences often report short‐term response rates greater than 50%, though high rates of relapse. The ketogenic diet is also an important modality in managing medication‐refractory epilepsy and LGS. 9 Multiple single‐institution studies have indicated therapeutic efficacy of the ketogenic diet similar to first‐line medications, with 30–50% of patients experiencing a >50% seizure reduction after 6 or more months of treatment. 32 , 33 , 34 Single institution studies found similar efficacy for the modified Atkins diet 35 and low glycemic index diet. 36

Resective surgery and corpus callosotomy

Surgery is often considered a last resort, but recent data demonstrate that resective surgery can help substantial numbers of people with LGS. Neurosurgical removal of epileptogenic brain tissue is an effective treatment for selected individuals with drug‐resistant epilepsy, 37 but it is underutilized or delayed for people with LGS. 38 , 39 , 40 A recent meta‐analysis 41 found that 76% (105/138) of people with LGS undergoing resective surgery (ranging from lesionectomies to hemispherectomies) had ≥50% seizure reduction at ≥2 years follow‐up. Taken together, results from the four studies cited with regard to resective surgery in this meta‐analysis indicate that 47% (65/138) became seizure‐free. 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 Of note, these rates of seizure reduction/freedom are substantially better than those reported for medications routinely used for LGS. 9 If a seizure focus is not identified, as in the 25% of people with unknown cause of LGS, corpus callosotomy can be an effective alternative. The same meta‐analysis found that 66% (193/291) of people with LGS undergoing corpus callosotomy had a “worthwhile” (typically ≥50%) seizure reduction at ≥2 years follow‐up. 41 In another study, seizure freedom (without discontinuation of anti‐seizure medications (ASMs)) was seen in 19% (14/74; ≥4 years follow‐up) of patients with LGS undergoing corpus callosotomy. 47 Both resective surgery and callosotomy produced modest improvements in various measures of quality of life, behavior, and cognition (including improved attention span, memory, and intelligence quotient [IQ] scores); no changes in these measures, and occasionally declines, were also reported. 41 A combined approach (resection followed by callosotomy, or vice versa) may be performed when seizures continue unabated after the first surgery 41 , 42 or when a unilateral seizure focus becomes apparent after callosotomy, providing a target for resective surgery. 48

Neuromodulation

Knowledge of shared brain network mechanisms is beginning to guide neuromodulation for LGS. To date, the most widely studied approaches for LGS are vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) and deep brain stimulation (DBS), which deliver continuous or duty‐cycling electrical stimulation to modulate abnormal activity causing seizures. VNS has been studied in LGS since the mid‐1990s 49 and remains the most common clinical use of neuromodulation. Meta‐analyses 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 estimate that 50–58% of people with LGS have ≥50% seizure reduction after ≥1 year of VNS, with a small fraction (~5%) 50 achieving seizure freedom. VNS has higher efficiency in younger people and people with shorter epilepsy duration. 54

There is also growing evidence to use DBS for LGS, targeting the thalamic centromedian nucleus (CM‐DBS). Early studies including small, placebo‐controlled pilot studies, 55 or larger unblinded, uncontrolled trials 56 , 57 found promising anti‐seizure effects of CM‐DBS, with some people experiencing prolonged seizure freedom. Most recently, the efficacy of duty‐cycling, bilateral CM‐DBS was evaluated in a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial (Electrical Stimulation of Thalamus for Epilepsy of Lennox‐Gastaut phenotype [ESTEL]). 58 Relative to control, CM‐DBS did not significantly reduce diary‐recorded seizures (the study's primary endpoint) but did significantly reduce EEG‐recorded seizures after 3 months. Overall treatment responder rates (with responder defined as ≥50% seizure reduction) were 50% using diaries and 59% using EEG. 58 These benefits may increase with longer duration of CM‐DBS. 59 A posttrial analysis of the ESTEL data found that seizure outcomes were better when the stimulation site included the anterior and lateral subregion of the centromedian nucleus (similar to earlier findings from unblinded studies) 56 , 60 and were best when stimulation extended into the adjacent ventral lateral nucleus posterior subdivision (also termed the ventral intermediate nucleus or Vim). 61 Seizure outcomes were also better in people for whom the stimulation site was more strongly connected to areas of abnormal GPFA‐related BOLD activation 61 identified by EEG‐fMRI. 62 This raises the potential of tailoring DBS to modulate the secondary epileptic network, for example using results of an individual's EEG‐fMRI and anatomical scans to choose stimulation targets or parameters. Other emerging neuromodulation approaches include focused ultrasound, 63 transcranial magnetic stimulation, 64 and implantable drug delivery devices. 65

Although originally intended for focal epilepsy, closed‐loop/responsive neurostimulation (RNS) 66 is being investigated as a treatment option for generalized epilepsy, including in two ongoing clinical trials in idiopathic generalized epilepsy (NCT05147571) and LGS (NCT05339126). In one ongoing trial (NCT05339126), people undergo bilateral implantation of two RNS devices, each connected to a depth lead targeting the centromedian nucleus and a strip lead targeting the premotor frontal cortex. 67 The goals are to allow more flexible stimulation and detection strategies, and to provide broader modulation of the LGS epileptic network , than is possible with thalamus‐only stimulation.

Optimizing and standardizing treatment of LGS

Given newly approved medical therapies and recent data on the effectiveness of surgical and neuromodulation approaches, updated treatment algorithms are needed to integrate these findings into standard clinical practice. We propose updated international expert consensus as a first step toward defining treatment guidelines for LGS, for example, using a modified Delphi approach. In 2017, an expert panel of five epileptologists recommended valproic acid as a first‐line therapy, followed by adjunctive treatment with lamotrigine, then rufinamide. 9 The panel also appropriately emphasized early consideration of nondrug therapies such as the ketogenic diet, neuromodulation with VNS, and “palliative” surgery (corpus callosotomy). 9 A subsequent expert panel recommended conceptualizing medical treatment in tiers as follows: Tier 1 (valproate, clobazam, lamotrigine, rufinamide, topiramate, and cannabidiol), Tier 2 (levetiracetam, perampanel, and zonisamide), and Tier 3 (felbamate, lacosamide, brivaracetam, and cenobamate). 11 In light of new knowledge and conceptual understanding of LGS, updated, international consensus guidelines should incorporate new therapies (cannabidiol and fenfluramine); emphasize when surgical, dietary or other precision therapies are indicated; take age into account (e.g., child vs. adult); account for treatment availability in high‐ and low‐income communities; account for the perspectives and lived experience of people with LGS and their families; and treatment goals should balance quality of life, seizure burden, and anti‐seizure treatment side effects. Given the data summarized above, it will be important to consider surgical and neuromodulation approaches earlier in the treatment course of LGS.

Beyond clinical consensus, direct comparisons of different treatments will be valuable to form evidence‐based treatment algorithms. For example, clinical trials should help to determine which people with LGS are most likely to benefit from medical therapy versus resective surgery, callosotomy, or neuromodulation; the comparative efficacy of different surgical approaches; and the efficacy of medical and surgical therapy for the spectrum of nonseizure outcomes (such as cognition, communication, behaviors, sleep, and quality of life). Empirical studies are needed to optimize the use of dietary therapies, corticosteroids, other hormonal and immunomodulatory agents, and neuromodulation approaches for LGS.

Persistent challenges and corresponding priorities

Despite the options for LGS treatment outlined above, seizures and comorbidities remain refractory in most cases. Our group identified key, persistent challenges that influence how clinical trials for LGS are conducted, and that hinder progress toward improved outcomes. We articulated priorities to address those challenges. As explained in the sections that follow, these challenges include, (1) the natural history of LGS, from its evolution in childhood to adulthood, remains poorly understood; (2) novel treatments targeting pathogenic disease mechanisms rather than symptoms are needed; and (3) there is a need for a greater range of clinical trial endpoints that are patient‐centered and tailored to LGS.

Defining LGS natural history for optimized clinical trials

Defining LGS natural history for optimized clinical trials

Challenge: Lack of natural history data in LGS

LGS usually arises (clinically evolves) in children between 3 and 5 years old, often with a prior history of seizures, developmental delay, or other neurological abnormalities. 10 One retrospective study found that the average duration from the first seizure to LGS diagnosis is 2 years. 68 LGS persists throughout life, and LGS manifestations including characteristic seizure types and EEG findings may shift in adulthood. 11 The early signs/risk factors for LGS evolution, and the subsequent dynamics of LGS from childhood into adulthood remain to be defined in detail. Furthermore, multiple etiologies give rise to LGS, and clinical subgroups may have differing prognoses; this too remains to be defined. These important dynamics have not been factored into clinical trial design. While seizures and EEG findings are the most well‐described aspects of LGS, affected individuals and caregivers prioritize other disease manifestations—such as sleep disruption, impaired communication, and dysregulated behavior. These aspects of the syndrome have not been well quantified and thus are not typically included in clinical trial endpoints. As demonstrated by Table 1, clinical trials for LGS have chiefly targeted “drop seizures” in people who are in later stages of the disease (which may be better described as “seizures associated with a drop,” given that different seizure types can converge on similar semiologies involving loss of tone). Establishing a more comprehensive natural history for LGS will require a registry for prospective research. Recent research also highlights the power of large retrospective databases using electronic medical records. Both prospective and retrospective natural histories require consensus for diagnostic criteria and clinical investigations.

Priority: Develop registries to enable prospective research on LGS natural history

Unlike other forms of epilepsy such as Tuberous Sclerosis Complex, 69 at this time, there is no dedicated registry, biorepository, or data repository to study LGS in individuals from multiple centers in the United States. Development and implementation of such a registry have been articulated as a key priority for patient advocacy groups, such as the LGS Foundation (lgsfoundation.org). An LGS registry would facilitate the quantification of disease phenotypes over the lifespan and the comparison of etiological subgroups.

Of course, a registry comprised of people who already have LGS would not enable a prospective study of early LGS evolution. Therefore, we argue that natural history studies focused on children at risk of LGS will be critical. For development of preventative treatment, a systematic study is needed to define “pre” LGS: a state of high risk for evolution to LGS, before ILAE criteria manifest in full. Pre‐LGS might be defined by quantitative EEG findings, like biomarkers identified for other forms of epilepsy, 70 , 71 seizure types, genetics, imaging, or a combination of these factors. Children with a history of Infantile Epileptic Spasms Syndrome (IESS) could enable the study of pre‐LGS. IESS includes developmental arrest/regression, hypsarrhythmia on EEG, and epileptic spasms in clusters. 72 30–50% of children with IESS later develop LGS. 73 , 74 Prospective studies of children with IESS could help to identify specific risk factors for IESS to LGS progression, providing critical information for preventative treatment trials for LGS.

Priority: Leverage the electronic medical record for LGS research

Recent work on STXBP1‐related disorders demonstrates the value of large‐scale, retrospective, electronic clinical data for rare diseases. Computational tools such as the human phenotype ontology were applied to multicenter medical records and published cohorts of individuals with STXBP1 to enable large‐scale genotype–phenotype correlation, mapping of disease trajectories, and description of therapeutic effectiveness. 75 , 76 , 77 Similar tools could be applied to LGS to rapidly advance understanding of natural history.

A major hurdle to studying LGS with retrospective electronic records is the lack of consistent diagnosis. LGS has an ICD‐10 code, G40.81, yet neither this code nor narrative documentation of the diagnosis is used consistently in clinical practice. One single institution study found that 80% of children who meet clinical criteria for LGS did not have a documented diagnosis of LGS. 73 Variable documentation of key criteria, like tonic seizures or SSW on EEG is also problematic. 78 , 79 This variability in LGS diagnosis, including omission or overuse of the LGS diagnosis, 80 or failure to document key LGS features, prevents efficient identification of affected individuals through electronic medical record searches—that is, via text search or via search for the LGS ICD‐10 code. 79 , 81 Inconsistent, though overlapping, definitions of LGS over decades have likely added to confusion in diagnosis.

Whereas variability in documentation of the LGS diagnosis hindered the use of electronic medical records, machine learning methods have shown promise in identifying LGS in health insurance claims data, 79 and checklists can improve the accuracy of confirmatory chart review. 78 “Computable phenotypes” can be developed for LGS 82 : that is, algorithms employing search terms (e.g., other than the ICD‐10 code for LGS) empirically determined to accurately identify individuals with LGS. Once validated, computable phenotypes can be employed without the need for in‐depth chart review. A Patient‐Centered Outcome Research Institute (PCORI)‐funded study (NCT05374824) is developing and validating computable phenotypes for LGS using electronic health record data.

Priority: Develop consistency in diagnosis, documentation, and clinical investigation

Considering recent ILAE criteria clarifying the diagnosis of LGS, clinicians can be encouraged to practice consistent and specific documentation of the key clinical and EEG features of LGS (e.g., document SSW and GPFA in EEG reports, explicitly classify seizures as “tonic” or “atypical absence” when applicable) and to apply the formal diagnosis of LGS including the ICD‐10 code where appropriate. Adoption of standardized electronic health record forms for clinical encounters 83 and EEG reports 84 may help align busy clinicians' desire for efficient workflows with the benefits of robust documentation. These benefits include both clinical domains (i.e., identifying children for epilepsy diets, devices, and surgeries) as well as research domains (i.e., surveillance and epidemiology, observational comparative effectiveness studies, and enrollment in clinical trials). It is important to document both the etiology of the seizures and the LGS diagnosis and to share this information with families.

2Targeting both convergent and distinct mechanisms of LGS

Targeting both convergent and distinct mechanisms of LGS

Challenge: Lack of mechanistic, disease‐modifying therapies for LGS

Retrospective studies and clinical trials have suggested that >90% of children with LGS have persistent seizures. 4 , 85 Medical treatments are primarily empirical and symptomatic. Greater understanding of neurophysiological, cellular, and molecular bases for LGS can define targets for disease‐modifying therapies. In that vein, we highlight recent progress including the source localization mapping of LGS EEG biomarkers and the identification of convergent brain network dysfunction which may be targeted with neuromodulation. We also have an increased understanding of genetic causes for seizures that evolved to LGS. We discuss priorities to translate these discoveries into new therapeutics, enabling clinical trials for precision and/or disease‐modifying treatments. “Bespoke” therapies precisely tailored to specific etiologies may not reverse secondary network disruption in LGS. Targeting both specific etiologies and common secondary network dysfunction may be necessary and synergistic.

Priority: Target common secondary network dysfunction in LGS

The last decade of research identified specific brain networks that are pathologically activated in people with LGS, regardless of etiology. Studies employing simultaneous functional MRI, which measures blood‐oxygen‐level‐dependent (BOLD) signal changes, and EEG (EEG‐fMRI) support Gastaut's early observations that multiple precipitants can converge on LGS. The EEG findings characteristic of LGS, GPFA, and SSW involve secondary epileptic networks that can be visualized with fMRI. 3 , 86 GPFA correlates with BOLD signal increases across diffuse areas of frontal and parietal “association” (i.e., nonprimary) cortex in addition to the thalamus, caudate, anterior putamen, cerebellum, and pontine brainstem (Fig. 2A). 62 , 86 , 87 In contrast, SSW correlates with mixed BOLD signal changes, 86 , 88 including prominent decreases in areas of the “default‐mode network” (including posterior cingulate, precuneus, angular gyrus, and medial prefrontal cortex). 89 Inhibition of this network may contribute to impaired awareness during prolonged runs of SSW that occur during atypical absence seizures (Fig. 2B). These EEG‐fMRI patterns are similar between children and adults 62 and across people with distinct causes of LGS (e.g., lesions in various cortical locations), 62 , 86 indicating that convergent brain networks generate GPFA and SSW patterns. In effect, the inciting etiology generates epileptiform activity, disturbing underlying network activity; then the network activity itself becomes self‐sustaining, in a characteristic pattern independent of the etiology. 90 As described in the preceding section, neuromodulation approaches that address secondary network dysfunction are approved or in clinical trials. In future studies, it will be important to understand how secondary network dysfunction may be prevented or reversed, and whether there are critical windows for intervention.

Priority: Develop precision therapies to target specific etiologies of LGS

As noted in previous sections, LGS may evolve following a range of brain insults, including structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, and others. 3 , 4 In addition to addressing secondary network mechanisms, it stands to reason that addressing the primary mechanism will be critical. To illustrate this point, we will discuss one domain in which precision therapies are becoming increasingly possible: genetic epilepsy. The cause of LGS is unknown (cannot be attributed to structural/acquired etiologies) in 25–35% of children with LGS, 91 suggestive of genetic etiologies. Indeed, numerous genes have now been associated with seizures that later evolve to LGS. Landmark studies by the Epi4K/EPGP and EuroEPINOMICS consortia cumulatively performed exome sequencing of 356 trios (parents and probands with infantile spasms or LGS) and identified >400 de novo genetic variants in probands. 92 , 93 Genes appearing in multiple probands included: SCN1A, STXBP1, GABRB3, CDKL5, SCN8A, SCN2A, ALG13, DNM1, HDAC4, GABBR2, FASN, GNAO1, TTN, KCNQ2, PI3KAP1, RANGAP1, SLC35A2, and RYR3. 92 , 93 Since those studies, the number of genes associated with LGS has further increased.

The diagnostic yield of genetic testing is high in DEEs such as LGS. Recent estimates for the yield of exome sequencing for DEEs/epilepsy of unknown etiology range from 29% in a systematic review 94 to 45% in a meta‐analysis. 95 Yields are as high as 83% in selected cohorts such as neonatal epileptic encephalopathy. 96 The yield of genetic testing for LGS is similarly high. Whole‐genome sequencing in a cohort of 30 adults with LGS and other DEEs 97 with previously negative or inconclusive genetic testing found nine individuals (32%) with pathogenic/likely pathogenic single nucleotide variants, tandem repeats or copy number variants. Similarly, exome sequencing of 17 Korean families with LGS of unknown etiology revealed 14 pathogenic variants in neuronal‐, LGS‐ or epilepsy‐related genes. 98 Most LGS‐causing genetic variants arise de novo, similar to other DEEs. 99 Such findings have led to practice guidelines from ILAE that highlight the importance of genetic testing for LGS, 5 and a recommendation from the National Society of Genetic Counselors to provide genetic testing for all individuals with epilepsy of unknown etiology, 100 particularly exome/genome sequencing or multi‐gene panels (>25 genes).

Increasing rates of genetic testing for epilepsy, including for LGS, can provide key insights for pathogenesis and treatment. Table 2 lists examples of genes that have been associated with LGS, with related potential precision therapies that are in preclinical or clinical development. Multiple genes found to cause LGS are also associated with other DEEs, epilepsy subtypes, and neurodevelopmental disorders. As highlighted in Table 2, genetic etiologies can point toward precision (and potentially disease‐modifying) therapeutics. It is a high priority, therefore, to continue preclinical to clinical development of pharmacological and gene‐based precision approaches, such as repurposed drugs, antisense oligonucleotides, and gene therapies.

Genetic diagnoses have also enabled the development of new experimental disease models. Previous models with features resembling LGS, such as atypical absence seizures, included mice and rats treated with the cholesterol biosynthesis inhibitor, AY9944 117 ; mice with deletion (knockout) of one copy of the GABA‐A receptor ß3 subunit 118 ; and transgenic mice with overexpression of the GABA‐B α1 subunit. 119 More recently, mice with genetic mutations that mirror point mutations observed in humans with LGS have been created—for example, mice bearing knock‐in mutations in the GABA‐A receptor β3 subunit, D120N 120 and N238D. 92 , 121 These mice recapitulated key aspects of LGS including progressive atypical absence, tonic and other seizure types, thalamocortical hypersynchrony, and cognitive deficits. Seizures in these models were further validated as disease biomarkers by their responsiveness to anti‐seizure medications that are frequently used in clinical practice. Additional mouse models of LGS‐like DEE, with severe generalized epilepsy and markedly abnormal EEG backgrounds, now include mice with a knock‐in variant, G379R in Kcnb1. 122 Newly developed mouse models (Table 3) can help to clarify the pathophysiology of LGS—for example, by connecting specific molecular and neurophysiological mechanisms with secondary network dysfunction. This understanding has the potential to identify novel therapeutic targets and key time points for prevention and reversal. 127 Mouse models can also be used for preclinical testing of potential precision therapeutics, 128 , 129 and to understand the biological basis and potential treatment of nonseizure outcomes, particularly those prioritized by LGS families. Examples include impaired cognition in specific domains, as demonstrated in each of the models, and sleep–wake cycling, which is understudied in mouse models of LGS (Table 3), yet known to be disrupted in children with LGS. 130 Although we have focused this discussion on mouse models, given the ease of genetic manipulation and the recent publication of mouse models, zebrafish present additional opportunities for high‐throughput phenotyping and drug screening, 131 and larger animal models of epilepsy, such as rabbits, pigs, and dogs, may better recapitulate some aspects of human brain networks and disease. 132

Challenge: Need for tailored, patient‐centered endpoints in clinical trials

In clinical trials for LGS, efficacy is typically measured in terms of seizure burden, especially caregiver‐reported seizures associated with a “drop.” Nonseizure outcomes are infrequently assessed in LGS clinical trials, yet there is evidence that nonseizure aspects contribute substantially to the burden of disease. An unpublished LGS Foundation survey collected information from 413 caregivers or affected individuals from 17 countries. 133 The top concerns raised by respondents included (1) seizures; (2) developmental delay, especially inability to communicate effectively; (3) difficult‐to‐manage behaviors, particularly aggression; (4) impaired mobility; (5) sleep disturbances; (6) medical costs, and (7) social isolation. In a published study, about half of the parents of 26 children with LGS reported their children could not communicate well with them, and 60–80% of the children reportedly had some degree of gross or fine motor impairment. About 30% were partially or fully fed by gastrostomy tube. 134 Similarly, another survey conducted in collaboration with the LGS Foundation found that the vast majority of caregivers (>90%) agreed that it is important for healthcare providers to assess quality of life, behavior and communication, whereas less than half expressed that these domains were adequately assessed in healthcare encounters. 135 In this section, we discuss recent progress toward quantifying patient‐centered, nonseizure outcomes in children with DEEs such as LGS, and a priority for greater incorporation of nonseizure outcomes, such as behavior and sleep, into clinical trials for LGS.

Priority: Development of fit‐for‐purpose assessments of neurodevelopment for DEEs

It is a priority to measure nonseizure outcomes such as neurodevelopment in clinical trials for DEEs, yet there are potential pitfalls in applying standard instruments to measure such endpoints. 136 For example, when neurodevelopment was quantified with the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales in children with SCN2A‐related DEE, standardized scores were 2–3 standard deviations below the mean. Over time, standardized measures of neurodevelopment misleadingly appeared to decline due to the slow rate of increase in raw scores relative to neurotypical children. Even when raw scores were used, a substantial proportion of children clustered at the minimum possible score, indicating a floor effect. 138

In light of this mismatch between clinical outcome assessment and phenotype, there is a clear need for fit‐for‐purpose clinical outcome assessments that are scaled to capture meaningful dynamics in moderate to severe neurodevelopmental impairment. 136 Some investigators have designed disease‐specific scales for particular DEEs, for example, the CDKL5 Deficiency Disorder Severity Assessment 139 and the Rett Syndrome Behavior Questionnaire. 140 , 141 An alternative approach to disease‐specific scales is the repurposing of clinical outcome assessments typically administered to younger children. Recent studies applied the Adaptive Behavior Assessment System‐3 (typically only used for children 0–5 years old) and other measures developed for young children (e.g., Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers) to quantify motor and communicative function in older children or adults with DEEs including LGS. 137 , 142 These studies found that the median age‐equivalent for motor function was 20.5 months and for communication skill was ~12–16 months (well below chronological age); minimal floor or ceiling effects were observed. 137 , 142 Similarly, another study used the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development to track neurodevelopment in older children with Angelman Syndrome. 143 The ongoing Inchstone Project 144 seeks to systematically develop clinical outcome assessments that reliably detect meaningful functional change in children with DEEs.

Priority: Assess patient‐centered outcomes in clinical trials for LGS

Fit‐for‐purpose clinical outcome assessments of neurodevelopment should be incorporated into future clinical trials for LGS. 136 Additional patient‐centered domains should also be considered, including those nonseizure endpoints prioritized by families and caregivers listed above (e.g., communication, behavior, sleep), 133 as well as direct measures of quality of life.

However, these patient‐centered outcomes have been understudied, hindering their inclusion as quantitative endpoints in clinical trials. For example, disordered sleep is reported by caregivers as one of the most concerning and disruptive features of the disease. 133 Disordered sleep may be either a consequence or cause of nocturnal seizures, overall seizure burden, and/or comorbidities such as disruptive behaviors. Clinical assessment of circadian rhythm disorders, sleep‐disordered breathing, and behavioral sleep disorders are required. 130 , 145 Diagnosis and treatment of sleep‐disordered breathing in particular holds promise as an approach to improve both behavior and seizure burden for people with LGS. 146 , 147 These knowledge gaps should urgently be addressed and sleep‐related endpoints should be incorporated into clinical trials. Meanwhile, assessment for sleep disorders may be warranted as part of standard care for people with LGS. Importantly, improving the sleep of people with LGS may improve both their quality of life 148 and the quality of life of their caregivers. 149 Rigorous and systematic approaches should be similarly applied to measure other nonseizure aspects of LGS, such as behavioral challenges, to enable their inclusion as quantitative endpoints in clinical trials.

Conclusion

LGS is regularly described as a severe, “catastrophic,” medically refractory form of epilepsy, in which care is primarily palliative and disability is lifelong. Yet, we argue that recent advances may be leveraged for clinical trials that lead to transformative treatments. Optimization and standardization of medical, dietary, surgical and neuromodulation treatment modalities, based on expert consensus and empirical evidence, would likely improve quality of care for LGS substantially. Natural history and biomarker research can be expedited by standardizing clinical documentation, with an LGS registry and through analysis of electronic medical records in aggregate. Therapies should be developed to target specific genetic and other etiologies, in conjunction with treatment approaches that address the secondary network disorder that gives rise to shared electroclinical manifestations of LGS. In future clinical trials, inclusion of patient‐centered outcomes, including non‐seizure outcomes, is required, and these outcomes should be measured with fit‐for‐purpose approaches. Clinicians, scientists, and patient advocacy groups should continue working in concert toward these ends, with the overarching goal of transforming LGS from a “catastrophic illness,” to a preventable, reversible, and definitively treatable syndrome.
