When the Flag Identifies the Territory But Not the Mechanism
Authors: Tsuneaki Kenzaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Acute allergic coronary syndromes remain underrecognized, perhaps owing to their ability to mimic classic ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Deb et al1 describe a compelling case in which a patient developed severe chest pain and the South African Flag sign immediately after iodinated contrast administration. Based solely on the electrocardiogram (ECG), the presentation appeared consistent with a high-lateral STEMI. However, urgent coronary angiography revealed no obstructive coronary artery disease. This striking discordance highlights the diagnostic challenge posed by Kounis syndrome and reinforces the importance of integrating clinical context with ECG interpretation.

The South African Flag sign—ST-segment elevation in leads I, augmented vector left, and V2—is a visually memorable teaching pattern that typically suggests occlusion of the first diagonal branch. It is widely used to help clinicians recognize high-lateral myocardial infarction. However, this ECG pattern identifies where ischemia occurs, not why it occurs. This distinction represents the central educational message of the present case. Even highly characteristic ECG patterns cannot determine the underlying mechanism of ischemia. The ECG is a map of the myocardial territory involved, not a diagnosis of its etiology.

Kounis syndrome, also known as allergic acute coronary syndrome, is characterized by mast cell activation triggered by allergens such as contrast media, antibiotics, or insect stings. The release of histamine, leukotrienes, and platelet-activating factor can provoke intense coronary vasospasm, leading to myocardial ischemia. In type I Kounis syndrome, vasospasm occurs in angiographically normal coronary arteries, producing ECG changes that may be indistinguishable from those elicited by STEMI. As demonstrated in this case, clinicians cannot differentiate STEMI, vasospasm, or allergic coronary syndromes based on ECG alone. The temporal relationship to allergen exposure, the presence of any accompanying hypersensitivity features, and the rapid evolution of ECG changes may provide important diagnostic clues.

This case also complements previous observations regarding the protean presentations of Kounis syndrome.1 In our prior report,2 we described a young woman with type I Kounis syndrome who presented without chest pain, illustrating that allergic acute coronary syndrome may escape recognition when clinicians rely on symptoms alone. The present case represents the opposite diagnostic pitfall: the symptoms and ECG strongly suggested STEMI, yet the presumed underlying mechanism was allergic coronary vasospasm. Together, these reports highlight 2 contrasting diagnostic hazards:•Kounis syndrome may masquerade as an uncomplicated allergic reaction when chest pain is absent.•Kounis syndrome may appear to be a classic STEMI when the ECG findings are convincing.

These complementary scenarios emphasize the need to integrate clinical history, symptom timing, allergic manifestations, and coronary anatomy when evaluating acute chest pain. Reliance on any single element—symptoms, ECG findings, or biomarkers—may lead to misdiagnosis.

A notable feature of the present case is that contrast exposure triggered the event, yet repeat contrast administration was required for emergent coronary angiography. Although it may appear paradoxical that repeated administration of the initiating agent was required for diagnosis and management, withholding angiography would have risked missing true occlusive STEMI. Deb et al appropriately proceeded with angiography using corticosteroids and antihistamines, with careful hemodynamic monitoring and minimal contrast volume. This pragmatic approach conveys an important message: even when Kounis syndrome is suspected, emergent coronary angiography may still be performed safely by modifying the suspected contrast agent and applying appropriate precautions.3

The diagnostic evaluation of suspected Kounis syndrome continues to evolve. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging may help differentiate allergic coronary vasospasm from myocarditis or other causes of myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA).4 Serum tryptase and immunoglobulin E testing may support the diagnosis of an allergic mechanism, although these tests are not always available in the acute setting.5 Ultimately, clinical priorities often follow a “treat first, define later” strategy. Early recognition of allergic coronary syndromes may help guide subsequent evaluation and prevent recurrent events.

The present case also underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating acute coronary syndromes.1 Although STEMI remains the most time-sensitive diagnosis, alternative mechanisms such as vasospasm, myocarditis, Takotsubo syndrome, and MINOCA must be considered once occlusive coronary disease is excluded. The rapid resolution of ECG changes and the absence of obstructive coronary disease strongly support a diagnosis of transient vasospasm in this case. Nevertheless, the lack of cardiac magnetic resonance imaging represents a limitation, as myocarditis and other MINOCA etiologies cannot be definitively excluded. This reinforces the need for comprehensive follow-up evaluation when initial findings are atypical.

Perhaps the most enduring lesson from this case is that ECG patterns reveal where ischemia occurs but not why it occurs. A striking ECG should accelerate clinical decision-making, yet it must not replace clinical reasoning. In this patient, the South African Flag sign correctly identified the ischemic territory but concealed the true mechanism. Recognizing this distinction may prevent both delayed reperfusion and missed allergic coronary syndromes. As the spectrum of MINOCA and allergic coronary syndromes continues to expand, clinicians must remain vigilant for presentations that challenge traditional diagnostic frameworks.

The report by Deb et al1 enriches our understanding of Kounis syndrome and provides valuable insights for clinicians who encounter acute chest pain in the context of contrast exposure or other allergic triggers. Their careful diagnostic approach and thoughtful management offer practical guidance for navigating this complex and often deceptive clinical entity.
