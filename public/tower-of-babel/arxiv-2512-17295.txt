An Iconic Heavy Hitter Algorithm Made Private
Rayne Holland

Abstract
Identifying heavy hitters in data streams is a fundamental problem with widespread applications in modern analytics systems. These streams are often derived from sensitive user activity, making update-level privacy guarantees necessary. While recent work has adapted the classical heavy hitter algorithm Misra-Gries to satisfy differential privacy in the streaming model, the privatization of other heavy hitter algorithms with better empirical utility is absent. Under this observation, we present the first differentially private variant of the SpaceSaving algorithm, which, in the non-private setting, is regarded as the state-of-the-art in practice. Our construction post-processes a non-private SpaceSaving summary by injecting asymptotically optimal noise and applying a carefully calibrated selection rule that suppresses unstable labels. This yields strong privacy guarantees while preserving the empirical advantages of SpaceSaving. Second, we introduce a generic method for extracting heavy hitters from any differentially private frequency oracle in the data stream model. The method requires only 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ) \mathcal{O}(k) additional memory, where k k is the number of heavy items, and provides a mechanism for safely releasing item identities from noisy frequency estimates. This yields an efficient, plug-and-play approach for private heavy hitter recovery from linear sketches. Finally, we conduct an experimental evaluation on synthetic and real-world datasets. Across a wide range of privacy parameters and space budgets, our method provides superior utility to the existing differentially private Misra–Gries algorithm. Our results demonstrate that the empirical superiority of SpaceSaving survives privatization and that efficient, practical heavy hitter identification is achievable under strong differential privacy guarantees.



1 Introduction

Identifying the most frequent elements of a dataset (commonly referred to as “heavy hitters”) is a cornerstone problem in data analysis
6
.
In modern systems, this task often arises on massive data streams, where items arrive continuously and must be processed online under strict memory and time constraints.
Notable techniques include counter-based methods
13
;
14
and linear sketches
5
;
7
.
However, a defining characteristic of such streams is that they are frequently derived from sensitive user activity
18
, and the classic techniques do not provide update-level privacy.

Recent work seeks to retrofit these algorithms with
differential privacy
(
\mathsf{DP}
), aiming for minimal overhead and limited impact on utility
2
;
12
;
15
;
20
.
At a high level,
\mathsf{DP}
ensures that the outcome of an analysis is stable under small changes to the underlying dataset, thereby limiting what can be inferred about any single update.
This line of inquiry commonly adopts the
single-observation
streaming model: the stream is processed in one pass and a single
\mathsf{DP}
summary is released.
This setting aligns with widely deployed analytics pipelines that aggregate large volumes of data and publish sanitized summaries under a fixed privacy budget.

From an algorithmic perspective, enforcing
\mathsf{DP}
for heavy hitters exposes two distinct challenges.
First, noise must be added to protect individual updates without overwhelming the frequency signal of genuinely heavy items.
Second, the algorithm must report item identities, not just noisy frequency estimates, without leaking information about rare or isolated labels.
Prior work has addressed these challenges along two complementary lines.
One line focuses on the counter-based method
Misra-Gries
(
\mathsf{MisraGries}
), which explicitly maintain candidate items and their counts
2
;
4
;
12
.
The other centers on utilizing
\mathsf{DP}
linear sketches as frequency oracle within a (labor intensive) heavy hitter search algorithm
1
.

Building on initial work by Chen et al.
4
, Lebeda and Tetek adapted the
\mathsf{MisraGries}
algorithm for private release with asymptotically optimal noise error.
This is significant as the authors note that “In practice, non-private approximate histograms are
often computed using the Misra-Gries sketch”.
However, empirically, the counter-based method SpaceSaving (
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
)
14
almost universally outperforms
\mathsf{MisraGries}
(and linear sketches) in utility.
This empirical dominance raises the natural question as to whether
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
can be adapted to satisfy
\mathsf{DP}
in the single-observation streaming model.
In response, we provide the first method to release
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
under
\mathsf{DP}
and demonstrate that it retains its empirical dominance in practice.

In contrast, linear sketches have been adapted to admit
\mathsf{DP}
frequency estimates
15
;
20
with strong utility.
However, uncritically turning those estimates into released labels is not de facto differentially private.
The challenge is reporting frequent items without leaking the presence or absence of isolated labels across similar streams.
A naive approach is to iterate across the entire input domain, a prohibitive computational effort in most settings.
To overcome this issue, Bassily et al. provide a more efficient search method for finding heavy hitters that utilizes a linear sketch as a
\mathsf{DP}
frequency oracle
1
in post-processing.
However, this approach still requires significant computational effort and memory requirements that exceed allocations that are standard in the data stream model of computation.
To overcome this issue,
building on observations from the
\mathsf{DP}
release of
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
, we provide a generic method for extracting heavy hitters from any
\mathsf{DP}
frequency oracle at an additional memory cost of
\mathcal{O}(k)
, where
k
is the number of heavy items in the stream.

1.1 Our contributions.

In providing novel methods for privatizing existing non-private methods for identifying heavy hitters, our contributions can be summarized as follows.

•

We design the first
\mathsf{DP}
variant of
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
tailored to the single-observation model.
Our construction processes the output of a non-private
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
by injecting calibrated noise and applying a selection rule that suppresses privacy leakage events.
The calibrated noise added is asymptotically optimal and the algorithm retains the performance of non-private
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
.

•

We provide a general method to output frequent items from
any

\mathsf{DP}
frequency oracle in the single-observation model.
The method wraps the oracle with a label-private selection procedure that suppresses unstable labels and releases only items whose noisy estimates exceed a
\mathsf{DP}
-calibrated threshold.
The additional memory cost is proportional to the number of heavy items and can be immediately utilized with existing
\mathsf{DP}
linear sketches.

•

We perform experimental study on synthetic and real-world datasets, comparing our private heavy hitter algorithms against the state-of-the-art
\mathsf{DP}

\mathsf{MisraGries}
.
Across a wide range of privacy parameters and space budgets, we show that
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
achieves superior utility while matching
\mathsf{MisraGries}
in memory and throughput.
This demonstrates the empirical advantages of
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
survive privatization.

1.2 Algorithm overview.

A central challenge in privatizing counter-based streaming algorithms is that the component data structures are used to track frequent items.
The tracked item sets explicitly contain
IDs
, and these labels can leak sensitive update-level information if the output is not processed correctly.
At the heart of our approach is a strategy of characterizing the
differences between the tracked sets on neighboring streams
, which lets us separate unstable labels from stable ones and process releases in a
\mathsf{DP}
-safe manner.
Neighboring streams
X,X^{\prime}
are defined as streams that differ in one update (
X=X^{\prime}\cup\{x\}
).

Operationally, our
\mathsf{DP}

\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
runs a non-private
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
summary with a slightly expanded capacity to facilitate isolating low-count, unstable labels.
Based on our characterization of tracked set differences on neighboring streams, at release time, we add independent Laplace noise to the counters and apply a single threshold that both (i) suppresses labels that can differ across neighboring inputs and (ii) retains true heavy hitters with high probability.
The expanded counter capacity allows us to set thresholds that target tracked labels at risk of breaching privacy without eliminating true heavy hitters and, thus, impeding algorithm recall.

In addition, we extend these ideas to any frequency oracle with provable error guarantees.
We show that the same threshold selection principle can be adapted to any oracle with provable error guarantees (what we term the error envelope).
This enables a plug-and-play solution for
\mathsf{DP}
linear sketches.

2 Preliminaries

2.1 Privacy

\mathsf{DP}
ensures that the inclusion or exclusion of any individual in a dataset has a minimal and bounded impact on the output of a mechanism.
Two streams
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{T}\}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}=\{x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{T}^{\prime}\}
are
neighboring
,
denoted
\mathcal{X}\sim\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
,
if they differ in one element.
Formally,
\mathcal{X}\sim\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
if there exists a unique
i
such that

x_{i}\neq x_{i}^{\prime}
.
The following definition of
\mathsf{DP}
is adapted from Dwork and Roth
8
.

Definition 1

(
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-Differential Privacy)
.

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}^{*}
be neighboring input streams (i.e., differing in at most one element). A randomized mechanism
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy under a given observation model if for all measurable sets of outputs
Z\subseteq\mathtt{support}(\mathcal{M})
, it holds that:

\mathds{P}[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X})\in Z]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathds{P}[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})\in Z]+\delta.

In the single-observation model, the mechanism processes the stream once and releases a single
\mathsf{DP}
output after the final stream update has been observed.
In this model, neighboring streams differ by the addition or removal of a single update.

A common method for outputting
\mathsf{DP}
functions is the following.

Lemma 1

(Laplace Mechanism for Vector Valued Functions)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a function with
\ell_{1}
-sensitivity at most
1
, that is, for any pair of neighboring inputs
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\|f(X)-f(X^{\prime})\|_{1}\leq 1.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and define the randomized mechanism

\mathcal{M}(X):=f(X)+(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}),

where each
Z_{i}\sim\mathtt{Laplace}(1/\varepsilon)
independently. Then
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
(\varepsilon,0)
-
\mathsf{DP}
.

2.2 Heavy Hitter Detection

We consider a data-stream model in which a sequence of items
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{t}\}
from a universe
\mathcal{U}
arrive in one pass.
For each item
y\in\mathcal{U}
, its true frequency is

f_{y}=\sum_{x_{j}\in X}\mathrm{1}\{x_{j}=y\},

The frequency vector is
\mathbf{f}=(f_{y})_{y\in\mathcal{U}}
.
Two canonical problems are:

1.

Frequency estimation: for any query item
y
, return
\widehat{f}_{y}
satisfying
\widehat{f}_{y}\leq f_{y}\leq\widehat{f}_{y}+\eta t
for some parameter
\eta>0
.

2.

Heavy-hitter detection: given thresholds
k
and
\eta
, return a set
H\subseteq\mathcal{U}
such that every item
y
with
f_{y}\geq n/k
belongs to
H
and no item
z
with
f_{z}\leq(1/k-\eta)n
is included.

Streaming algorithms must use space sublinear in the stream lenth and universe size and process each update quickly.

Counter-based methods maintain a small table of size
\mathcal{O}(k)
of item-counter pairs.
On each stream update, they adjust counters so that one obtains error guarantees on both frequency estimation and heavy-hitter recovery.
These methods are attractive for their simplicity and deterministic error bounds.

3.1.1
Misra Gries

The
\mathsf{MisraGries}
algorithm maintains at most
k-1

(\text{item},\text{counter})
pairs.
For each incoming item, if it is already present in the table
\mathcal{T}
, its counter is incremented.
If it is not present and a counter has value zero, that counter is assigned to the new item with count set to 1.
If all
k-1
counters are occupied by distinct items, all counters are decremented by 1.
A simple grouping argument shows that any item appearing more than
n/k
times in the stream is guaranteed to be stored in the table when the algorithm terminates.
The process is formalized in Algorithm
1
.

To privatize a counter-based algorithm it is not sufficient to inject noise into the counts of the tracked items.
This is because the IDs of the tracked items can differ on neighboring inputs, allowing an adversary to easily distinguish the streams based on the output of the algorithm.
This notion is formalized in the following result by Lebeda and Tetek
12
, which describes the observed outcomes when a
\mathsf{MisraGries}
sketch processes neighboring input streams.

Lemma 2

(
12
)
.

Let
X=X^{\prime}\cup\{x\}
.
Let
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{C})\leftarrow\mathtt{MG}(X,k)
and
(\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\leftarrow\mathtt{MG}(X^{\prime},k)
denote the outputs of the Misra Gries algorithm on inputs
X
and
X^{\prime}
.
Then
|\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}|\geq k-2
; for all
y\notin\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
,
C[y]\leq 1
and
C^{\prime}[y]\leq 1
; and exactly one of the following is true:

(1)

\exists i\in\mathcal{T}
, such that
C[i]=C^{\prime}[i]+1
, and
\forall j\neq i:C[j]=C^{\prime}[j]
.

(2)

\forall i\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}:C[i]=C^{\prime}[i]-1
, and
C^{\prime}[j]=0
for
j\notin\mathcal{T}^{\prime}

The lemma states that there can be at most 2 elements that are in
\mathcal{T}
, but not in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, and vice versa.
These elements are referred to as
isolated elements
, and if they appear in the final output, an adversary can always tell if an output was generated by
X
or
X^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, the count of an isolated element must always be at most 1.
Therefore, we can use a thresholding trick to suppress isolated elements from the output with high probability.
Once isolated elements are suppressed, and appropriate noise is added to item counts, tracked items with noisy counts above the threshold can be released.

3.1.2
Space Saving

The
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
algorithm maintains
k
counters to track frequent items.
When an item
x
arrives, if it is already present in the table, its counter is incremented.
If it is not present and a free counter exists, the item is inserted with count set to 1.
If all
k
counters are occupied, the item with the minimum count
c_{\min}
is replaced by
i
, and its counter is set to
C[x]=c_{\min}+1
.
When multiple items observe the minimum count ties can be broken arbitrarily.
However, to enable privatization, we enforce the rule that the item that most recently occurred is evicted.
The process is formalized in Algorithm
2
.
This procedure guarantees that for every item, the estimated frequency satisfies the following bound.

Lemma 3

(Additive Error of the
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
)
.

Let the
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
algorithm process a stream of length
T
while maintaining
k
counters.
For each item
i
with true frequency
f_{i}
and estimated frequency

\widehat{f}_{i}
, the following holds:

f_{i}\leq\widehat{f}_{i}\leq f_{i}+\frac{T}{k}.

3.2 Linear Sketches

Linear sketches enable approximate frequency queries, in small memory, by hashing items to a compact collection of shared counters.
We focus on the well known Count-min Sketch (
\mathsf{CountMinSketch}
)
7
and Count Sketch (
\mathsf{CountSketch}
)
5
variants.
While they share a similar structure, they differ in their update and query procedures and offer different error trade-offs.

3.2.1
Count-Min Sketch

The
\mathsf{CountMinSketch}
uses
d
hash functions
h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow[w]
to maintain a
d\times w
matrix of counters
C\in(\mathrm{R}^{+})^{d\times w}
.
For each incoming item
x\in\mathcal{U}
the update rule is:

C[i,h_{i}(x)]\leftarrow C[i,h_{i}(x)]+1,\quad\text{for all }i\in[d].

To estimate the frequency of
x
,
\mathsf{CountMinSketch}
returns
\hat{f}_{x}=\min_{i\in[d]}C[i,h_{i}(x)]
.
This estimator is biased upwards due to hash collisions with other items but offers strong probabilistic error guarantees.

Lemma 4

(Approximation error for
\mathsf{CountMinSketch}

7
)
.

For
x\in\mathcal{U}
and any
\beta,\eta\in(0,1)
, the estimation error of Count Min Sketch with
d=\lceil\log(1/\beta)\rceil
repetitions and table size
w=\lceil 2/\eta\rceil
satisfies,

\displaystyle\mathds{P}[\hat{f}_{x}-f_{x}>\eta F_{1}]\leq\beta,

where
F_{1}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{U}}f_{y}
.

3.2.2
Count Sketch

The
\mathsf{CountSketch}
modifies the
\mathsf{CountMinSketch}
by incorporating randomized signs to reduce bias.
In addition to the hash functions
h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow[w]
, it uses a second family of hash functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\{-1,+1\}
that assign random signs.
For each
x\in\mathcal{U}
, the update rule is:

C[i,h_{i}(x)]\leftarrow C[i,h_{i}(x)]+g_{i}(x),\quad\text{for all }i\in[d].

To estimate the frequency of
x
,
\mathsf{CountSketch}
returns:

\hat{f}_{x}=\mathrm{median}_{i\in[d]}\left(g_{i}(x)\cdot C[i,h_{i}(x)]\right).

This estimator is unbiased and has error bounded in terms of the second moment of the frequency vector.

Lemma 5

(Approximation error for
\mathsf{CountSketch}

5
)
.

For
x\in[n]
and any
\beta,\eta\in(0,1)
, the estimation error of Count Sketch with
d=\lceil\ln(1/\beta)\rceil
and table width
\lceil w=3/\eta^{2}\rceil
satisfies:

\Pr\left[|\hat{f}_{x}-f_{x}|>\eta\sqrt{F_{2}/w}\right]\leq\beta,

where
F_{2}=\sum_{y\in[n]}f_{y}^{2}
is the second moment of the frequency vector.

4.1 SpaceSaving on Neighboring Streams

Before proceeding, we provide some useful notation.
Let
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{T}\}

4.2 Privacy

, where

:

Example of a transition from
(\mathsf{S4})
to
(\mathsf{S3})
when an element

C[y]=\min_{x\in\mathcal{T}}C[x]+1
, arrives in the stream.

The technical foundation for privatizing
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
is captured by Corollary
1
,
which offers two pivotal insights into the algorithm’s stability under neighboring inputs.
First, consider the outputs

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T},C)

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathsf{SS}(X,k)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}^{\prime},C^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathsf{SS}(X^{\prime},k),

where
X^{\prime}
is derived from
X
by the removal of a single element.
Corollary
1
asserts that among the items common to both tracked sets, that is,
x\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, there exists at most one for which the counters differ, and this discrepancy is bounded by a single increment.

Second, it guarantees that items outside the intersection are associated with low counts, specifically bounded above by the minimum counter in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
plus one.
These properties together imply that the output of
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
is inherently stable, and thus amenable to privatization via two mechanisms: (1) obscuring the small count differences among shared items, and (2) suppressing isolated items whose counts fall below a threshold.

The first mechanism is realized by injecting Laplace noise into the counters of tracked items. Since the sensitivity of the counters restricted to
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is at most one, Lemma
1
ensures that adding independent noise drawn from
\mathtt{Laplace}(1/\varepsilon)
suffices to achieve differential privacy.

The second step can be achieved by applying a threshold test that suppresses items with low count.
Let
\gamma
denote the maximum plausible deviation introduced by
\mathtt{Laplace}
noise.
In
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
, for a register of length
k
, the minimum count is at most
|X|/k
.
Therefore, it would be sufficient to set the threshold to
|X|/k+1+\gamma
.
However, as our task is to detect items whose frequency is at least
|X|/k
(heavy hitters), this introduces a tension.
Notably, overly aggressive suppression risks excluding valid heavy hitters that are permissible to report.

To reconcile privacy with utility, we initialize a non-private
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
data structure with an expanded capacity
\tilde{k}>k
.
This adjustment ensures that items with minimal counts are unlikely to be heavy hitters, thereby allowing suppression without sacrificing recall.
This strategy is formalized in Algorithm
3
.
We now prove the privacy properties.

define

\mathsf{DPSS}(X,k,\tilde{k},\varepsilon,\delta)

1

\gamma\leftarrow\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\!\left(\tfrac{2}{\delta}\right)

2

\mathcal{T},C\leftarrow\mathsf{SS}(X,\tilde{k})

3

\hat{\mathcal{T}},\hat{C}\leftarrow\varnothing

4

\tau\leftarrow\max\{|X|/k,|X|/\tilde{k}+1+\gamma\}

5

for

x\in\mathcal{T}

do

6

\hat{c}_{x}\leftarrow C[x]+\mathtt{Laplace}(1/\varepsilon)

7

if

\hat{c}_{x}>\tau

then

8

\hat{\mathcal{T}}\leftarrow\hat{\mathcal{T}}\cup\{x\}

9

\hat{C}[x]\leftarrow\hat{c}_{x}

10

return

\hat{\mathcal{T}},\hat{C}

11

Algorithm 3

Differentially Private
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}

Theorem 1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, Algorithm
3
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differenitally private.

Proof.

For any fixed set of input parameters
(k,\tilde{k})
define

\mathcal{A}(X)=\mathsf{DPSS}(X,k,\tilde{k},\varepsilon,\delta).

We prove that for any pair of neighboring inputs
X,X^{\prime}
(where
X^{\prime}
is obtained by removing a single element from
X
), and any measurable subset
S
of outputs,

\Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(X)\in S\,]\;\leq\;e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(X^{\prime})\in S\,]\;+\;\delta.

Define the “stable selection” event
E
as the event that no item in the symmetric difference

U:=\mathcal{T}\triangle\mathcal{T}^{\prime}

exceeds the threshold in either run.
That is,

E\;:=\;\bigl[\,\forall u\in U\cap\mathcal{T}:\;C[u]+Z_{u}\leq\tau\ \text{ and }\ \forall u\in U\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}:\;C^{\prime}[u]+Z^{\prime}_{u}\leq\tau\,\bigr],

where
Z_{u}
is the Laplace noise.
As
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
is composed of
\tilde{k}
counters that sum to
|X|
, the minimum count is at most
|X|/\tilde{k}
.
Therefore, by Corollary
1
and Lemma
3
,
|U|\leq 2
and each such
u\in U
has
true
count at most
|X|/\tilde{k}+1
.
Since
\tau\geq|X|/\tilde{k}+1+\gamma
, the margin from the minimum count to the threshold is at least
\gamma
for every such
u\in U
.
For Laplace noise
Z\sim\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)
we have the standard tail bound

\Pr[\,Z\geq\gamma\,]=\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-\varepsilon\gamma}.

Applying a union bound over at most two unstable labels and two independent runs yields

\Pr[\,\neg E\,]\;\leq\;2\cdot 2\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\,e^{-\varepsilon\gamma}\;=\;2\,e^{-\varepsilon\gamma}\;\leq\;\delta,

where the last inequality uses
\gamma=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\!\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)
.

Conditioned on the event
E
,
the released index sets
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}
are contained in
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
Let
m:=|\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}|\geq\tilde{k}-2
, and fix an arbitrary but common ordering of the labels in
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
Define the vector-valued function

F(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

as the ordered list of common counters

F(X):=(\,C[x]\,)_{x\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}},

and similarly
F(X^{\prime}):=(\,C^{\prime}[x]\,)_{x\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
.
By Corollary
1
,
F(X)
and
F(X^{\prime})
differ by at most
1
in a single coordinate and are equal elsewhere.
Thus,

\|F(X)-F(X^{\prime})\|_{1}\leq 1.

Let
Z=(Z_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
and
Z^{\prime}=(Z^{\prime}_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
be noise vectors with
Z_{x},Z^{\prime}_{x}\sim\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)
, independent across coordinates.
Then, by Lemma
1
, for all measurable
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\Pr[\,F(X)+Z\in A\,]\;\leq\;e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\,F(X^{\prime})+Z^{\prime}\in A\,].

(1)

Now define the (deterministic) post-processing map
\Phi

that keeps precisely those coordinates of
F(X)+Z
whose value exceeds
\tau
, together with their labels.
Conditioned on
E
,
by the post-processing property of differential privacy, applying
\Phi
preserves Inequality (
1
).
That is, for all measurable
S,

\Pr[\,\Phi(F(X)+Z)\in S\mid E\,]\;\leq\;e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\,\Phi(F(X^{\prime})+Z^{\prime})\in S\mid E\,].

(2)

Putting everything together,
using (
2
),
\Pr[E]\leq 1
, and
\Pr[\neg E]\leq\delta
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(X)\in S\,]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(X)\in S\mid E\,]\,\Pr[E]

\displaystyle+\Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(X)\in S\mid\neg E\,]\,\Pr[\neg E]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(X^{\prime})\in S\mid E\,]\,\Pr[E]

\displaystyle+\Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(X)\in S\mid\neg E\,]\,\Pr[\neg E]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(X^{\prime})\in S\,\mid E]\Pr[E]\;+\;\Pr[\neg E]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(X^{\prime})\in S\,]\;+\;\Pr[\neg E]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(X^{\prime})\in S\,]\;+\;\delta

The same analysis holds when exchanging the roles of
X
and
X^{\prime}
.
Thus, Algorithm
3
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy.
∎

4.3 Performance and Utility

Algorithm
3
inherits the efficiency of the underlying
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
algorithm, offering strong performance guarantees in both time and space.
Using an implementation based on hash tables and doubly linked lists Algorithm
3
can be implemented with
O(\tilde{k})
space complexity and can process each stream update in
O(1)
time
6
.
This ensures that the algorithm scales gracefully with high-throughput data streams.

In practice, for large streams, the minimum counter value
|X|/\tilde{k}
dominates the noise parameter
\gamma=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)
.
Consequently, it is sufficient to set
\tilde{k}=O(k)
without compromising utility.
Thus, the additional space does not come at an asymptotical cost.

For example, consider a stream of length
|X|=2^{28}
, with
k=512
, and privacy parameters
\varepsilon=0.1
and
\delta=10^{-3}
.
We subsequently choose
\tilde{k}>k
to ensure that the suppression threshold does not exclude true heavy hitters.
Recall the threshold used by Algorithm
3
:

\tau\;=\;\max\Bigl\{\,|X|/k,\;|X|/\tilde{k}+1+\gamma\,\Bigr\},\quad\text{where}\quad\gamma\;=\;\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\!\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right).

To ensure
\tau=|X|/k
, so heavy hitters are not suppressed, we require

\frac{|X|}{\tilde{k}}+1+\gamma\leq\frac{|X|}{k}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\tilde{k}\geq\frac{|X|}{\frac{|X|}{k}-(1+\gamma)}.

Plugging in values, with
\gamma=89.87
, we get that it is sufficient to set
\tilde{k}
to 513.

These observations are summarized in the following result.

Theorem 2
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, let
\gamma=\varepsilon^{-1}\ln(2\delta^{-1})
.
Suppose the input stream
X
and target capacity
k
satisfy
\;|X|/(2k)>\gamma\;
.
Then Algorithm
3
can be implemented with
\mathcal{O}(k)
words of memory and
\mathcal{O}(1)
update time.
Moreover, for any item
x\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}
with true frequency
f_{x}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
the released estimate
\hat{C}[x]
obeys

f_{x}\;-\;\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\;\leq\;\hat{C}[x]\;\leq\;f_{x}+\frac{|X|}{2k}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right).

In addition, any item
y
with
f_{y}>|X|/k
is released (that is,
y\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}
) with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

Set
\tilde{k}=2k
and run
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
with capacity
\tilde{k}
.
This uses
\mathcal{O}(\tilde{k})=\mathcal{O}(k)
words and
\mathcal{O}(1)
update time.
Let
C[\cdot]
be the terminal (non-private) counters and add independent noise
\eta_{x}\sim\mathrm{Laplace}(1/\varepsilon)
.
Release only labels with
C[x]+\eta_{x}>\tau
, where

\tau\;=\;\frac{|X|}{\tilde{k}}\;+\;\gamma\;=\;\frac{|X|}{2k}\;+\;\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\!\Big(\frac{2}{\delta}\Big).

By deterministic guarantee of
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
(Lemma
3
),

f_{x}\;\leq\;C[x]\;\leq\;f_{x}+\frac{|X|}{2k}\qquad\text{for all items }x.

For
\eta_{x}\sim\mathrm{Laplace}(1/\varepsilon)
we have

\Pr[|\eta_{x}|>t]=e^{-\varepsilon t}
.
Taking
t=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln(1/\delta)
gives, with probability
\geq 1-\delta
,

f_{x}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\!\Big(\frac{1}{\delta}\Big)\;\leq\;C[x]+\eta_{x}\;\leq\;f_{x}+\frac{|X|}{2k}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\!\Big(\frac{1}{\delta}\Big),

which matches the stated bounds.

If
f_{y}>|X|/k
, then
C[y]\geq f_{y}\geq|X|/k
, so

C[y]-\tau\;\geq\;\frac{|X|}{k}-\Big(\frac{|X|}{2k}+\gamma\Big)=\frac{|X|}{2k}-\gamma\;>\;0

by the premise
|X|/(2k)>\gamma
.
Therefore
y
fails the threshold only if
\eta_{y}<-(|X|/(2k)-\gamma)
, which occurs with probability

\leq\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-\varepsilon(|X|/(2k)-\gamma)}\leq\delta/2.

Combining with the suppression bound yields

5 Frequent Items with a Frequency Oracle

Linear sketches with
\mathsf{DP}

A simple way to bypass central server processing of frequency oracles would be to track a compact set of candidates online, akin to counter-based approaches.
However, this strategy is not automatically label-private.
Privacy leakage can occur, for neighboring streams

y
appears as a tracked item in
X

X^{\prime}

We now formalize the concept of suppressing of unstable labels on neighboring streams.

Lemma 13

(Suppression of unstable labels)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a data structure that estimates item frequency of stream arrival
x
at time
t

\gamma_{1}(t)\ \geq\ \tilde{f}_{t}(x)-f_{t}(x)\ \geq\ \gamma_{2}(t).

4

Fix

Proof.

Since

-th order statistic.
The maximum value of the

7 Conclusion

We introduced new methods for privatizing heavy hitter algorithms in the data-stream model of computation.
This includes the first
\mathsf{DP}
variant of
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
and a generic wrapper that converts any
\mathsf{DP}
frequency oracle into a label-private heavy-hitter mechanism.
Both constructions combine calibrated noise with a single threshold tied to the error envelope, preserving the efficiency of their non-private counterparts.

Our experiments confirm that
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
continues to dominate under differential privacy, achieving near-perfect precision and recall with minimal overhead.

\mathsf{MisraGries}
and
\mathsf{SpaceSaving}
largely match the empirical performance of their non-private versions.
Linear sketches remain close to their non-private behavior but incur a small penalty from deeper tables required to guarantee envelopes.
Linear sketches nonetheless offer advantages such as deletions.
A promising direction for future work is to further tighten sketch error envelopes.
