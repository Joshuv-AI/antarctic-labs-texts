Spatio-temporal infection and diversity indices of gastrointestinal helminths in Xenopus species from Lake Victoria Basin, Kenya
Authors: Kiki M A Ochieng, Nehemiah M Rindoria, George N Morara, Wilmien J Luus-Powell, Willem J Smit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractXenopus frogs (Pipidae) are aquatic anurans widely distributed across Africa, where they play important ecological, biomedical, and cultural roles. However, populations are increasingly threatened by a range of stressors, including habitat degradation, climate change, and parasitic infections. This study investigated spatio-temporal infection and diversity indices of gastrointestinal helminths in Xenopus borealis and X. victorianus from the Lake Victoria Basin, Kenya. A total of 480 individuals (240 per species) were collected between June 2024 and January 2025 from selected streams and ponds using baited traps and seine nets. Spatio-temporal variations in environmental conditions were recorded using a YSI multiparameter meter. Standard parasitological techniques were employed for the recovery, fixation, and identification of parasites. Three helminth genera were recorded: the intestinal cestode Cephalochlamys sp., and two nematodes, Contracaecum sp. (intestinal) and Paracamallanus sp. (stomach). Prevalence ranged from 23% to 90%, and mean intensity from 1.0 to 8.3, depending on parasite taxon and host species. Nematodes were the most prevalent and diverse group across all localities. Diversity metrics, including Shannon-Wiener, Margalef richness, Berger-Parker dominance, and Simpson’s index, showed variation between hosts and sampling sites. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed no significant spatial differences in infection indices (p > 0.05), but temporal differences were significant (p < 0.05) for Contracaecum infections in X. borealis and Paracamallanus in X. victorianus. These findings provide the first baseline data on helminth fauna from Xenopus species in Kenya and support the need for molecular analyses to clarify taxonomy and better understand host-parasite-environment interactions.Keywords: Anura, Pipidae, Diversity indices, Helminths, Infection indices

Introduction

Amphibians are species of global significance in many aspects, such as pest control, food source, medicinal value, cultural value, scientific explorations, and indicators of environmental changes (Hocking and Babbitt 2014). However, about 41% of the amphibian species face threats of extinction due to habitat degradation, diseases, and impacts of climate change, among several other factors (Stuart et al. 2004; Collins and Crump 2009; Hayes et al. 2010; Kassie et al. 2023). To some extent, the nature of amphibians’ sensitive bodies increases their vulnerability to the natural and anthropogenic stressors. Accordingly, the percentage of threatened amphibians could be substantially underestimated because approximately 22.5% of their global species populations are recorded by the International Union for the Conservation of Nature (IUCN) as being data deficient (Hoffmann et al. 2010).

Anura, encompassing families of frogs and toads, is one of the three extant orders of class Amphibia, and currently the largest with more than 7000 species of frogs and toads (Zweifel and Cash 2021). The order has a worldwide distribution pattern, with 80% of the species living in tropical habitats (Zweifel and Cash 2021). Anuran species are generally considered good environmental indicators of habitat quality and ecosystem status because of their highly permeable bodies that are sensitive to environmental variations (Hocking and Babbitt 2014; Tagar et al. 2019). Hence, the diversity and geographical distribution patterns of anuran species are significantly influenced by not only the abiotic and biotic factors (Wells 2007; Oda et al. 2016), but also the evolutionary processes that allow them to adapt to varying habitat conditions (Kabanze et al. 2023).

Out of 49 families of anurans, Pipidae comprises a primitive but highly specialised species of frogs, which are largely aquatic with higher distributions in the southern hemisphere. In this family, three species of the genus Xenopus have been recorded in various regions of Kenya (Spawls et al. 2006). Xenopus borealis Parker, 1936, and Xenopus victorianus Ahl, 1924, are widely distributed in the Lake Victoria Basin (LVB), whereas Xenopus muelleri (Peters, 1844) is predominantly a coastal species in Kenya. However, extensive ecological studies on these species are still scant in the country. Although X. borealis and X. victorianus are commonly found in ponds and wetlands associated with fisheries and aquaculture, their host-parasite interactions remain poorly documented (Mbokane et al. 2020). Furthermore, knowledge of parasite diversity and dynamics in anuran populations across Eastern Africa is still limited, leaving a significant gap in the literature. Host–parasite interactions are complex ecological processes, and understanding them is essential for elucidating the dynamics of aquatic infectious diseases in diverse ecosystems. Parasitological studies of Xenopus frogs are therefore important not only for conservation and management but also for assessing potential risks of parasite transmission, whether through invasive dispersal or consumption by predatory fish species.

Therefore, this study sought to address this gap by documenting the taxonomy, diversity, and spatio-temporal infection indices of helminth parasites in two widely distributed anurans - the Northern Clawed Frog (X. borealis) and the Lake Victoria Clawed Frog (X. victorianus) - within the Lake Victoria Basin, Kenya.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in the LVB, Kenya, encompassing the eight counties of Kakamega, Vihiga, Busia, Kisumu, Migori, Homa Bay, Kisii, and Siaya (Fig. 1). This basin forms part of the greater Lake Victoria drainage system, characterised by an intricate network of rivers, wetlands, and freshwater ecosystems that support diverse aquatic and terrestrial biodiversity (Mugo et al. 2020). The region experiences a tropical climate with bimodal rainfall patterns, influencing seasonal hydrological dynamics and habitat variability. These environmental conditions, combined with increasing anthropogenic activities such as agriculture, fishing, and urbanization, contribute to ecological shifts and habitat fragmentations that may influence ecological interactions between different organisms (Nyamweya et al. 2020). The study area comprises a range of aquatic habitats, including permanent wetlands, seasonal ponds, and aquaculture ponds.

Field sampling

Physico-chemical parameters

Sampling was conducted in three sessions-between June 2024 and January 2025-representing the wet, moderate and dry seasons. Water depth, dissolved oxygen (DO), temperature, total dissolved solids (TDS), and pH were the main physico-chemical parameters assessed to characterize host habitat conditions across all sampling points and periods. Depth was measured from the bottom of the water body to the surface using a weighted manila rope and a meter rule graduated in centimetres. Dissolved oxygen, temperature, TDS, and pH were measured using a YSI Professional Plus multiparameter meter (Model: QUATRO ISE-ISE-DO-COND 17L100213, USA). The average of these parameters was calculated from the three sampling sessions for pH; the values were first converted to hydrogen ion concentrations (Davis et al. 1995), averaged, and converted back to pH.

Collection of Xenopus frogs

A total of 240 host specimens of each species (X. borealis and X. victorianus) were collected in accordance with epidemiological survey guidelines (Corsin et al. 2009). Given that the study sites comprised relatively small ponds and wetlands, a conservative sampling strategy was adopted. Specifically, a target of 30 frogs per site was set, distributed evenly across three sampling periods (10 individuals per period). These sample sizes align with the thresholds suggested by Jovani and Tella (2006), who noted that 3–8 hosts may be considered minimal, 15–20 moderate, and 30–75 relatively high, with 15 individuals often serving as a reasonable compromise. This methodological approach therefore balanced practicality with statistical rigor while remaining within widely accepted ranges for epidemiological studies. Sample sizes between 30 and 150 individuals are generally sufficient to maintain statistical confidence levels of 95–99% (Corsin et al. 2009; de Blas et al. 2020), and are recognized by the World Organization for Animal Health (de Blas et al. 2020). Parasitological examinations

Live specimens were first examined on-site for ectoparasites and then transported to Kisii University Biology laboratory in aerated tanks filled with water from the respective sampling sites. Specimens were euthanized via spinal severance. Post-mortem dissections were performed under a stereo microscope; key organs including the gastrointestinal tract, liver, lungs, and musculature were examined for parasites. Parasite identification was conducted using taxonomic keys from Moravec (2019) for nematodes and Scholz et al. (2021) for cestodes. Helminth identification was restricted to the genus level because proper resources for detailed morphological and molecular identification were not available. This conservative approach was taken to avoid misidentification and ensure taxonomic accuracy. Parasite specimens were preserved in 70% ethanol for morphological examination, with some material retained in 96% ethanol for future identifications to the species level using molecular techniques.

Data analysis

Infection variation and correlation analyses

Infection levels (prevalence and mean intensity) of parasites in anuran hosts were determined according to Bush et al. (1997). Water quality parameters and parasite mean intensities were analysed as continuous variables, whereas parasite prevalence was treated as non-continuous data. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were initially tested using the Sharpiro-Wilk and Levene’s test. Both assumptions were violated in the raw data. Therefore, the data was log-transformed for ANOVA test. Since replicate samples were not available, the assumptions for using full factorial ANOVA could not be satisfied. So, two-way ANOVA without replication was applied to evaluate the effects of site and sampling period on water quality parameters and parasite mean intensities. This approach was considered appropriate given the constraints of the sampling design, where ethical and logistical limitations restricted the collection of larger replicate datasets. For the analysis of spatial and temporal differences in parasite prevalence, Fisher’s Exact test (expanded Chi-Square function) was employed. The correlation between water quality parameters and parasite taxa prevalence was determined using a non-parametric correlation test - Spearman’s rank (rho). Pooled data sets for the parasite taxa were used and assumed that there was no host effect on the correlation. These statistical analyses were conducted using PAST (Paleontological Statistics Software Package for Education and Data Analysis, version 4.03; Hammer et al. 2001) and the statistical significance was assessed at 95% confidence level (α = 0.05).

Sampling power analysis

Because baseline data on parasite prevalence was not available prior to fieldwork, a post-hoc minimum detectable effect (MDE) analysis was conducted to retrospectively assess the study’s ability to detect differences in infection metrics among counties. For the two Xenopus species MDEs were calculated for each of three parasite taxa by pairwise comparison between the counties with the least and highest recorded prevalence. The calculation was done using fixed sample sizes of 30 host specimen, and pooled prevalence that were based on the number of hosts infected, assuming a two-tailed significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power of 80%. Results

Spatio-temporal variability of physico-chemical parameters

The study areas were characterized by remarkable variations in the physico-chemical parameters investigated. In the sampled ponds and wetlands, water depths are shown in Figs. 2A, B, ranging from 1.3 m to 2.2 m, with an overall mean of 1.69 ± 0.29 m. Sampling points in Kisumu, Vihiga, and Kisii counties had relatively deeper water bodies, sustained by prolonged wet spells. These counties received comparatively high rainfall, influenced by their altitudes, sparsely distributed naturalized forestland, and the water retention capacity of local soils. During the dry period, all sampling sites experienced a notable decline in water levels. Spatial and temporal variations in water depth across the sites and sampling periods were statistically significant (F7, 14 = 15.451, p = 0.001; F2, 14 = 22.586, p = 0.001).

Variation in water depths (mean ± SE) across (A) sampling sites (n = 8) and (B) hydrological periods (n = 3) in eight counties within Lake Victoria Basin, Kenya

The four physico-chemical parameters, DO, temperature, TDS, and pH, varied across sampling sites and over the sampling period (Fig. 3). Dissolved oxygen concentrations ranged from 1.4 to 7.9 mg/L. Two-way ANOVA revealed statistically significant spatial and temporal differences in DO levels, with a significant effect of location (F7, 14 = 22.805, p < 0.001) and sampling period (F2, 14 = 6.515, p < 0.001). Water temperature ranged from 19.8 to 29.1 °C and was significantly influenced by both location (F7, 14 = 4.770, p < 0.001) and sampling period (F2, 14 = 17.447, p < 0.001). pH values ranged from 6.36 to 8.05, showing a significant spatial variation across sites (F7, 14 = 12.596, p < 0.001) but no significant change over time. Similarly, TDS values ranged from 96.5 to 251.6 ppm, with significant differences observed among sites, but not across sampling periods.

Helminth infection indices

Three taxa of endohelminths were recorded in both X. borealis and X. victorianus. These were broadly categorized as cestodes (Cephalochlamys sp.) and nematodes (Contracaecum sp., Paracamallanus sp.). The prevalence and mean intensities of these parasites varied between the two host species (Figs. 4A and 5B). Paracamallanus sp. exhibited the highest prevalence and mean intensity in both hosts (Figs. 4A and 5B). Notably, in X. victorianus, Paracamallanus sp. had a higher prevalence (85%) and mean intensity (8.3) during the dry season (Figs. 4B and 5B, respectively) compared to X. borealis. In X. borealis, the prevalence of Paracamallanus sp. remained relatively stable, ranging from 75% to 77.5%, indicating minimal seasonal influence on parasite load in this host. In contrast, Contracaecum sp. and Cephalochlamys sp. were less prevalent overall. Temporally, parasite prevalence was highest during the wet season across all taxa, compared to the moderate and dry seasons. Spatially, infestation levels were the highest in Kakamega, Vihiga, Kisii, and Kisumu counties, a pattern observed consistently for both host species.

Paracamallanus sp. dominated the parasite communities at all locations. The Shannon-Wiener diversity index (H′) ranged from 0.71 to 0.91 in X. borealis and from 0.62 to 1.01 in X. victorianus. Temporal patterns mirrored spatial trends, with parasite counts ranging from 326 to 750 across the hydrological periods (Table 5). Parasitic communities exhibited low diversity throughout the wet, moderate, and dry seasons. Discussion

The World Organization for Animal Health (OIE) provides guidelines on the use of generic sample sizes to detect parasitic infections in aquatic hosts. These guidelines corroborate some of the commonly used methods for sample size determination, such as those proposed by Cannon and Roe (1982). These guidelines suggest sampling up to 150 individuals for low prevalence (~ 2%), 60 for prevalence above 5%, and 30 for prevalence above 10%. Shvydka et al. (2018) further supported these thresholds, showing that samples larger than 25 hosts minimize bias, whereas fewer than 10 provide unreliable estimates. Similarly, de Blas et al. (2020) confirmed the validity of such generic sample sizes for parasitological and epidemiological studies, particularly in aquatic systems where host numbers are uncertain. In this study, a total of 240 individuals per host species were examined, which is reasonably adequate for reliable estimation of parasite prevalence and mean infection intensity.

Because host sacrifice was required to obtain parasite data, the sample sizes were deliberately kept ethically conservative to safeguard the welfare of the target anuran species. Despite these ethical constraints, the post-hoc MDE analysis indicated that the study retained sufficient statistical power to detect meaningful differences in prevalence across counties. Several of the observed differences in parasite prevalence between sites and host species exceeded the calculated MDE thresholds for both Xenopus borealis and X. victorianus. This finding implies that the selected sample sizes were adequate to detect effects at the prespecified significance level (α = 0.05) and statistical power (1 – β = 0.80). In other words, the sampling strategy achieved a balance between ethical considerations and analytical rigor, ensuring that the reported prevalence differences are unlikely to be limited by insufficient power. Overall, these findings support the robustness of the study design and provide confidence in the reliability of the prevalence comparisons made across the surveyed counties.

Parasitic burdens in host populations are often unpredictable and unevenly distributed (Tinsley et al. 2020). In this study, significant variation in habitat conditions was observed across sites and seasons. Among the measured variables, water depth, TDS, DO, pH, and temperature were the most influential in distinguishing habitat characteristics. These physico-chemical parameters can substantially influence the invasion success of parasite species, their virulence, and host resistance. For example, changes in water depth can affect infection levels by altering host behaviour or modifying host-parasite interaction dynamics. Periods of water level recession, especially during dry spells, may cause wetlands and ponds to heat up rapidly or dry out, leading to host aggregation and increased contact between infected and susceptible individuals or infected intermediate hosts.

Whilst the results of this study showed no direct correlation between water depth and infection levels, other water quality parameters, particularly DO and pH, were strongly associated with the prevalence of Contracaecum sp. and Paracamallanus sp. These findings support the notion that variations in water depth can indirectly influence water quality by altering light penetration regimes, eutrophication, and oxygen dynamics in the host’s environment, thereby favouring certain parasites (Budria 2017). Moreover, the observed negative correlation between DO levels and parasite prevalence suggests that low oxygen concentrations may increase host susceptibility to parasitism, potentially due to increased energy expenditure on air breathing at the expense of immune function (De Padova et al. 2024). Conversely, higher DO levels appear to suppress parasite infectivity, a pattern consistent with the findings of Korma (2022). Although temperature is a key factor influencing parasite development, infection, and virulence (Schampera et al. 2022), the modest temperature differences observed across the study sites did not show a significant correlation with the prevalence of the three parasite taxa. It is worth noting that in anurans, immune function is generally temperature-dependent (Lima et al. 2022), which may in turn affect host resistance to infection. Although the results found a few significant correlations between water quality parameters and parasite prevalence, the effect of host species in these water bodies were not tested within the scope of this study. However, host species can influence parasite distribution through physiological compatibility, immune defences, and ecological behaviour (Poulin 2007).

No major spatial or temporal differences in parasite infection indices were observed in the two Xenopus species. In both hosts, parasite fauna was dominated by Paracamallanus sp., which exhibited higher prevalence and mean intensity than Contracaecum sp. and Cephalochlamys sp. This finding aligns with the study by Mbokane et al. (2020), who reported Paracamallanus sp. as the most abundant parasites in related pipid frog species. Notably, a pronounced increase in the prevalence of Paracamallanus sp. was observed in X. victorianus during the dry season, while the prevalence in X. borealis remained relatively stable. This seasonal spike may be attributed to changes in host behaviour and water availability; during dry spells, frogs tend to congregate in shrinking water bodies, increasing the likelihood of contact with infective larvae. Variations in host immunity between species and across seasons may also contribute to these differences. This infection pattern suggests that Paracamallanus sp. have a well-adapted life cycle that, combined with frequent host exposure, can reach high infection intensities.

In contrast, the lower prevalence and mean intensities of Contracaecum sp. may be explained by the role of amphibians as paratenic hosts for anisakid nematodes, limiting their development and abundance in these species. The highest infection levels were recorded in Kakamega, Kisumu, and Kisii, indicating spatial clustering likely driven by habitat heterogeneity and biodiversity, which influence parasite transmission dynamics (Fearon et al. 2023; Solórzano-García et al. 2023). These spatial differences may also reflect habitat variability and fragmentation resulting from anthropogenic activities, such as agricultural expansion and urban pollution, which can affect parasite loads by disrupting larval development, altering environmental conditions, and impacting the availability of intermediate invertebrate hosts.

The consistent dominance of Paracamallanus sp. in both host species investigated in this study suggests that they may be generalist parasites, characterized by broad ecological tolerance and high transmission efficiency. In X. borealis, parasite diversity remained relatively stable throughout the study period, indicating an even distribution within the parasite community. In contrast, X. victorianus exhibited a decline in parasite diversity and an increased dominance of Paracamallanus sp. during the dry season, suggesting reduced community evenness and the potential competitive exclusion of other parasite taxa by camallanids. Specific data and information on parasite prevalence in Kenyan Xenopus populations are currently limited. However, studies in similar regions (such as Mbokane et al. 2020; Schoeman et al. 2020) suggest that X. borealis and X. victorianus in Kenya could be infected by a variety of parasites, including monogeneans and digeneans. The current study reports two helminth phyla grossly observed at the genus level. Therefore, further research on the three taxa using advanced techniques such as scanning electron microscopy and molecular approaches would be important for their identification to the species level. Furthermore, future exploration of the parasite communities infecting Xenopus populations in Kenya could enhance our understanding of their impacts and inform conservation and management strategies.
