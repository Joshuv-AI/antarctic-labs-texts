Finite mixture representations of zero-&- N N -inflated distributions for count-compositional data
André F. B. Menezes, Andrew C. Parnell, Keefe Murphy

Abstract
We provide novel probabilistic portrayals of two multivariate models designed to handle zero-inflation in count-compositional data. We develop a new unifying framework that represents both as finite mixture distributions. One of these distributions, based on Dirichlet-multinomial components, has been studied before, but has not yet been properly characterised as a sampling distribution of the counts. The other, based on multinomial components, is a new contribution. Using our finite mixture representations enables us to derive key statistical properties, including moments, marginal distributions, and special cases for both distributions. We develop enhanced Bayesian inference schemes with efficient Gibbs sampling updates, wherever possible, for parameters and auxiliary variables, demonstrating improvements over existing methods in the literature. We conduct simulation studies to evaluate the efficiency of the Bayesian inference procedures and to illustrate the practical utility of the proposed distributions.



1
Introduction

The excess of zeros in count-compositional data occurs when one or more categories have a larger number of observed
zeros than expected under common statistical distributions, such as the multinomial and Dirichlet-multinomial.
The primary complication in multivariate count-compositional settings, compared to univariate cases,
is that the excess zeros can occur in a single category or across multiple categories. When zero-inflation occurs in all but one category, this gives rise to the related phenomenon of
N
-inflation.

We discuss two multivariate probability distributions designed to address the prevalence of excess zeros
in count-compositional data. We introduce a unifying framework that represents these models as finite mixtures.
Specifically, we derive a novel zero-&-
N
-inflated multinomial (ZANIM) distribution,
which is based on multinomial mixture components, and extend this approach to
the zero-&-
N
-inflated Dirichlet-multinomial (ZANIDM).
Although ZANIDM was first introduced by
Koslovsky 2023
, under the name ZIDM, it was described only through a stochastic representation via a mixture distribution on the count probabilities. We fully characterise ZANIDM as a sampling distribution on the counts capable of simultaneously modelling both zero-and-
N
-inflation and overdispersion using Dirichlet-multinomial mixture components.

Our paper is structured as follows. We derive the finite mixture representations of both distributions in Section
2
.
These representations facilitate the derivation of some key theoretical properties of the distributions in Section
3
. We propose Bayesian frameworks for model inference in Section
4
. In particular, for ZANIDM, we note that it is possible to improve the efficiency of the MCMC algorithm by
marginalising out a latent variable, which was not done by
Koslovsky 2023
. We present two simulation studies in Section
5
which i) compare different MCMC
algorithms for inferring the parameters of ZANIDM and ii) illustrate the practical utility of both distributions
when dealing with zero-inflation in count-compositional data. We conclude with a brief discussion in Section
6
. Additional details on associated derivations and inference schemes for both distributions are deferred to the Appendices. We also provide further results in the Supplementary Material. For now, we begin by describing some related proposals.

1.1
Related work

Many extensions of the multinomial distribution have been proposed in the literature,
most of which aim to address extra variation relative to the its inherent limitations,
particularly its negative covariance structure. Notable examples include the
Dirichlet-multinomial
[
2
]
, the finite mixture of multinomials

[
3
]
, and the Conway-Maxwell-multinomial distribution
[
4
,
5
]
. In contrast, there are relatively few
extensions of the
multinomial or other
count-compositional
distributions which address the issue of excess zeros. Key contributions in this area include the following:

Diallo et al. 2018
, who studied a specific case of zero-inflation in the multinomial distribution;
Tang and Chen 2018
, who
introduced the zero-inflated generalised Dirichlet-multinomial by modifying the Beta stick-breaking representation
of the generalised Dirichlet distribution
[
8
]
to incorporate a zero-augmented Beta distribution; and

Tuyl 2019
, who proposed a spike-and-slab prior for the multinomial probability parameter,
assigning a positive probability mass to zero.

More recently,
Zeng et al. 2023
proposed the zero-inflated logistic normal multinomial,
while
Koslovsky 2023
introduced a zero-inflated extension of the Dirichlet-multinomial,
leveraging its gamma representation to incorporate zero-inflation into the count probabilities.
Notably, both of these recent works focus on modifying the latent space rather than the sampling distribution of the
counts, and we note some similarities in their derivations. However,
Zeng et al. 2023
and
Koslovsky 2023
primarily focus on modelling data from human microbiome studies, without
emphasising the theoretical properties of their models.
Our paper addresses this gap by: (i) presenting a unified framework for both models,
reformulating them in terms of their unconditional representation without latent variables,
(ii) leveraging this framework to derive key statistical properties for both models, and
(iii) discussing Bayesian inference using their latent structures while proposing improvements to the sampling scheme for the ZANIDM distribution.

2
Derivation of the distributions

In what follows, we shall assume that

\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})
denotes a
d
-dimensional random vector of
count compositions, where
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
represents the observed
data.

2.1
Zero-&-
N
-inflated multinomial distribution

A well-known probability distribution for describing count-compositional data is
the multinomial distribution, whose probability mass function (PMF) is given by

\Pr[\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y};\bm{\theta}]=\binom{N}{y_{1}\dots y_{d}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{j}^{y_{j}},\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathbf{y}\in\Omega_{d,N},

(1)

where
\bm{\Omega}_{d,N}=\left\{\mathbf{y}\in(0,1,\ldots,N)^{d};\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,y_{j}=N\right\}
is the sample space,
represented by a discrete simplex space
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d})
, with
\theta_{j}\geq 0

denoting the probability of occurrence of category
j
, subject to the constraint
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\theta_{j}=1
.
Here,
N
is a known constant denoting the number of trials.
Within this paradigm, we shall consider the parameterisation

\theta_{j}=\lambda_{j}/\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k},

where
\lambda_{j}
is a measure of the relative importance of category
j
;
when it is normalised, it gives the probability of category
j
, i.e.,
\theta_{j}
.

A well-known data-augmentation (see, e.g.,
[
11
]
), which leads to conditional independence for the category-specific multinomial parameters, uses the latent variable

(\phi\mid\bm{\lambda},\mathbf{y})\sim\operatorname{Gamma}\left[N,\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\lambda_{j}\right],

(2)

which yields the following joint PMF for
\mathbf{Y}
and
\phi
:

\Pr[\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y},\phi;\bm{\lambda}]=\dfrac{N!\,\phi^{N-1}}{\Gamma(N)}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\dfrac{\lambda_{j}^{y_{j}}e^{-\lambda_{j}\,\phi}}{y_{j}!}\right],

(3)

where the marginal PMF of
\mathbf{y}
follows that of Equation (
1
).
Note that the augmented likelihood factors into independent terms for each
\lambda_{j}
.
The same likelihood, up to a multiplicative constant, can be obtained via the Poisson-multinomial
transformation of
Baker 1994
.
For an arbitrary category
j
, the likelihood contribution of an observation takes a ‘Poisson-type’ form, i.e.,

p(y_{j},\phi\mid\lambda_{j})\propto\dfrac{\lambda_{j}^{y_{j}}\,e^{-\lambda_{j}\,\phi}}{y_{j}!},\quad y_{j}=0,1,2,\ldots,N.

(4)

The excess of zeros in multinomial count data may be structural in nature. A reasonable way to address this using the augmented likelihood given in Equation (
3
) is to introduce additional parameters to account for zero-inflation with respect to each category.
Specifically, we can consider a mixture-type model by adjusting the ‘Poisson-type’ form in Equation
(
4
) to include a zero-inflation parameter.
Thus, the likelihood contribution for category
j
adopts a ‘ZI-Poisson-type’ form, i.e.,

p(y_{j},\phi\mid\lambda_{j},\zeta_{j})\propto\zeta_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{0}\left(y_{j}\right)+\left(1-\zeta_{j}\right)\,\dfrac{\lambda_{j}^{y_{j}}\,e^{-\lambda_{j}\,\phi}}{y_{j}!},\,\quad y_{j}=0,1,2,\ldots,N,

(5)

where
\zeta_{j}\in[0,1]
denotes the probability of zero-inflation
of category
j
, which we henceforth refer to as the ‘excess of zero parameter’,
and
\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{j})
is the usual indicator function
\mathds{1}(y_{j}=0)
,
which evaluates to
1
if
y_{j}=0
or
0
otherwise. It is important to note
that
Y_{j}
may still be
0
even if
\zeta_{j}=0
. We refer to such zeros as
‘sampling zeros’, in contrast to ‘structural zeros’.

By plugging Equation (
5
) into
Equation (
3
), we have that

\Pr[\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y},\phi;\bm{\lambda},\bm{\zeta}]=\dfrac{N!\,\phi^{N-1}}{\Gamma(N)}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\zeta_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{j})+(1-\zeta_{j})\,\dfrac{\lambda_{j}^{y_{j}}\,e^{-\lambda_{j}\,\phi}}{y_{j}!}\right].

(6)

We now aim to marginalise out the latent variable
\phi
from Equation
(
6
) and ensure that the function

\int\Pr[\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y},\phi;\bm{\lambda},\bm{\zeta}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi

will be a proper PMF.
We state our main result concerning the PMF of this new distribution, named the zero-&-
N
-inflated multinomial (ZANIM) distribution, after first introducing some notation that will help to represent ZANIM as a finite mixture.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{K}=\{\mathcal{K}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\};1\leq\lvert\mathcal{K}\rvert\leq d-2\}

represent the set of all subsets
\mathcal{K}
of
\{1,\ldots,d\}
with cardinality between
1
and
d-2
, such that the subsets
\mathcal{K}
gather categories with counts of zero, excluding the cases where exactly
d
and
d-1
categories are zero-inflated.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\bm{\zeta}=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{d})
, with
\zeta_{j}\in[0,1]
, and let

\eta_{d}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,(1-\zeta_{j})
,

\eta_{0}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,\zeta_{j}
, and

\eta_{N}^{(j)}=(1-\zeta_{j})\,\prod_{k\colon k\neq j}\zeta_{k}
, for
j=1\ldots,d
.
We define

\eta_{\mathcal{K}}=\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\zeta_{k}\,\prod_{j\notin\mathcal{K}}(1-\zeta_{j})
for each
\mathcal{K}\in\mathfrak{K}
and let
\bm{\eta}_{\mathfrak{K}}=\{\eta_{\mathcal{K}};\mathcal{K}\in\mathfrak{K}\}
denote the set of such terms.
The full set of mixture weights

\bm{\eta}=\{\eta_{d},\eta_{0},\eta_{N}^{(1)},\ldots,\eta_{N}^{(d)},\bm{\eta}_{\mathfrak{K}}\}
are functions of the
\bm{\zeta}
parameters and sum to one as required.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\delta_{\mathbf{0}_{d}}(\cdot)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\delta_{0}(\cdot)
, where
\delta_{x}(\cdot)
, is the Dirac measure which places a point mass at
x
. If a random variable follows
X\sim\delta_{x}(\cdot)
, then its distribution function is
F_{X}(a)=\Pr[X\leq a]=1
if
a\geq x
and
0
otherwise.

Proposition 2.1
.

The zero-&-
N
-inflated multinomial distribution, denoted by

\mathbf{Y}\sim\operatorname{ZANIM}_{d}[N,\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta}]
, is a finite mixture
distribution of
2^{d}
components with PMF given by

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y};\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta}]

\displaystyle=\eta_{d}\,\binom{N}{y_{1}\dots y_{d}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{j}^{y_{j}}

(7)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\eta_{N}^{(j)}\left[\mathds{1}_{0}\left(\sum_{k\colon k\neq j}y_{k}\right)\,\right]

(8)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+\sum_{\mathcal{K}\in\mathfrak{K}}\eta_{\mathcal{K}}\left[\mathds{1}_{0}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}y_{k}\right)\binom{N}{\{y_{j}\}_{j\notin\mathcal{K}}}\prod_{j\notin\mathcal{K}}\left(\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{K}}\right)^{y_{j}}\right]

(9)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+\eta_{0}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{j})\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathbf{y}\in\bm{\Omega}_{d,N}^{0},

(10)

where

\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d})
, with
\theta_{j}\geq 0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{j}=1
,

\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{K}}=\theta_{j}/(1-\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{K}}\,\theta_{\ell})
, and

\bm{\zeta}=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{d})
, with

\zeta_{j}\in[0,1]
.
The mixture weights
\bm{\eta}
are functions of
\bm{\zeta}
(see Definition
2.2
).

Proof.

See
A
for details.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

In Proposition
2.1
,
\bm{\Omega}_{d,N}^{0}=\bm{\Omega}_{d,N}\cup\mathbf{0}_{d}
is an expansion of the multinomial support, which accounts for the case where
y_{j}=0\>\forall\>j=1,\ldots,d
. Although simultaneously observing zero counts for all categories may be a rarity in practice, the addition of the associated component in Equation (
10
) is necessary to ensure the validity of the PMF.

It is clear that ZANIM is a finite mixture of multinomials, along with two degenerate distributions for the cases where all counts are zero, corresponding to Equation (
10
), and where all but one category are zero, corresponding to Equation (
8
). Equation (
7
) is a multinomial with
d
categories,
N
trials, and probabilities
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d})
. Equation (
9
) represents multinomials with
N
trials and probabilities
\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{K}}
for all categories
j\notin\mathcal{K}
. We note, however, that a given
\mathbf{y}_{i}=\{y_{i1},\ldots,y_{id}\}
can belong to as few as one and at most
2^{d-1}
components, given the presence of indicator functions in the above PMF. Obviously, if there are no zeros in the given
\mathbf{y}_{i}
, then we just evaluate the purely multinomial component in Equation (
7
). In the special case where the entire
\mathbf{y}_{i}
consists of zeros, we evaluate only the purely degenerate component in Equation (
10
).
Otherwise, when
\mathbf{y}_{i}
contains both zero and non-zero counts, we require the evaluation of
2^{q}
components, where
q\in[1,d-1]
denotes the number of observed zeros, including the purely multinomial component, the reduced multinomial components, and (when
q=d-1
exactly) the corresponding
N
-inflated component. This simplifies the calculation of the likelihood by obviating the need to evaluate all
2^{d}
components and highlighting how few components need to be evaluated when the number of zeros is low.

Proposition 2.2
.

If
\mathbf{Y}\sim\operatorname{ZANIM}_{d}[N,\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta}]
, then
\mathbf{Y}
has
the stochastic representation:

\mathbf{Y}\sim\eta_{0}\,\delta_{\mathbf{0}_{d}}(\cdot)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\eta^{(j)}_{N}\,\mathbf{E}_{d}^{(j)}+\eta_{d}\,\operatorname{Multinomial}_{d}[N,\bm{\theta}]+\sum_{\mathcal{K}\in\mathfrak{K}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{K}}\,\operatorname{Multinomial}_{d}[N,\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\mathcal{K}}],

(11)

where
\mathbf{E}_{d}^{(j)}=(\delta_{N}(Y_{1}),\ldots,\delta_{0}(Y_{j-1}),\delta_{N}(Y_{j}),\delta_{0}(Y_{j+1}),\ldots,\delta_{0}(Y_{d}))

denotes a vector of length
d
with Dirac mass at
N
in the
j
-th entry and Dirac masses at zero elsewhere, and

\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\mathcal{K}}
reflects the fact that the entries of
\{\theta_{1}^{\mathcal{K}},\ldots,\theta_{d}^{\mathcal{K}}\}

are zero for all
j\in\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

Follows by identifying each mixture component in the PMF of ZANIM.
∎

Furthermore, we note that the mixture components in ZANIM are generated from a set of independent Bernoulli random variables, which leads to an alternative stochastic representation.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
\mathbf{Y}\sim\operatorname{ZANIM}_{d}[N,\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta}]
, then
\mathbf{Y}
has
the stochastic representation:

\displaystyle z_{j}

\displaystyle\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}[1-\zeta_{j}],\quad j=1,\ldots,d

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Y}\mid N,\bm{\theta},z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})

\displaystyle\sim\begin{cases}\delta_{\mathbf{0}_{d}}(\cdot)&\textrm{if}\>z_{j}=0\>\forall j\\
\operatorname{Multinomial}_{d}\left[N,z_{1}\dfrac{\theta_{1}}{1-s},\ldots,z_{d}\dfrac{\theta_{d}}{1-s}\right]&\textrm{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
s=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(1-z_{k})\theta_{k}
.

Proof.

Follows from the fact that the mixture weights defined in Definition
2.2
are products of Bernoulli probabilities.
∎

From the two stochastic representations above, we can easily generate values from ZANIM.
Obviously, the representation in Proposition
2.3

is more efficient.
We note that this representation has similarities to the zero-inflated logistic normal multinomial model of
Zeng et al. 2023
. However, in their model they do not consider the case where
z_{j}=0\>\forall\>j
,
which has been studied in a specific space-time application by
Douwes-Schultz et al. 2024
.

2.2
Zero-&-
N
-inflated Dirichlet-multinomial distribution

Leveraging the hierarchical representation of the Dirichlet-multinomial distribution (henceforth DM) through the compounding of the multinomial and
Dirichlet distributions,
Koslovsky 2023
introduced the zero-inflated Dirichlet-multinomial model (ZIDM).
We note that
Koslovsky 2023
provides a latent stochastic representation but not the marginal distribution.
We now derive a probabilistic representation of this model. By later expressing ZIDM as a finite mixture, we conjecture that a more appropriate name would be the zero-&-
N
-inflated Dirichlet-multinomial (ZANIDM) distribution.

Definition 2.4
.

A random vector
\mathbf{Y}
is said to have a ZANIDM distribution if
it has the following stochastic representation:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\sim\operatorname{Multinomial}[N,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d}]

\displaystyle(\lambda_{j}\mid z_{j},\alpha_{j})

\displaystyle\sim(1-z_{j})\,\delta_{0}(\cdot)+z_{j}\operatorname{Gamma}[\alpha_{j},1]

\displaystyle(z_{j}\mid\zeta_{j})

\displaystyle\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}[1-\zeta_{j}],\quad j=1,\ldots,d,

where
\theta_{j}=\lambda_{j}/\sum_{k=1}^{d}\,\lambda_{k}
. In brief,
\mathbf{Y}\sim\operatorname{ZANIDM}_{d}[N,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\zeta}]
,
where the parameters are:
N
, the number of trials,

\bm{\zeta}=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{d})
, s.t.
\zeta_{j}\in[0,1]
is the
excess of zero parameter of category
j
, and

\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
are the concentration parameters with
\alpha_{j}>0
.

Proposition 2.4
.

If
\mathbf{Y}\sim\operatorname{ZANIDM}_{d}[N,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\zeta}]
, then
\mathbf{Y}

is a finite mixture of
2^{d}
components with PMF given by

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y};\bm{\alpha},\bm{\zeta}]

\displaystyle=\eta_{d}\,\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha_{s})\,\Gamma(N+1)}{\Gamma(N+\alpha_{s})}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,\left[\dfrac{\Gamma(y_{j}+\alpha_{j})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})\,\Gamma(y_{j}+1)}\right]

(12)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\eta_{N}^{(j)}\left[\mathds{1}_{0}\left(\sum_{k\colon k\neq j}y_{k}\right)\,\right]

(13)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+\sum_{\mathcal{K}\in\mathfrak{K}}\eta_{\mathcal{K}}\left[\mathds{1}_{0}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}y_{i}\right)\right]\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha^{\mathcal{K}}_{s})\,\Gamma(N+1)}{\Gamma(N+\alpha^{\mathcal{K}}_{s})}\prod_{j\notin\mathcal{K}}\left[\dfrac{\Gamma(y_{j}+\alpha_{j})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})\,\Gamma(y_{j}+1)}\right]

(14)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+\eta_{0}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{j})\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathbf{y}\in\bm{\Omega}_{d,N}^{0},

(15)

where
\alpha_{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}
,

\alpha^{\mathcal{K}}_{s}=\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{K}}\,\alpha_{j}
, and the mixture weights

\bm{\eta}
are as given in Definition
2.2
.

Proof.

Marginalising out the latent variables
z_{j}
and
\lambda_{j}
in Definition
2.4

and accounting for the fact that
y_{j}=0
when
\lambda_{j}=0\,(z_{j}=1)
yields the desired result.
See
B
for details.
∎

Recasting ZIDM as ZANIDM under our finite mixture model framework enables a fuller characterisation of the distribution which highlights, in particular, that the PMF also incorporates degenerate components to capture
N
-inflation. Notably, these components in Equation (
13
), as well as the case where
(y_{1}=\ldots=y_{d})=\mathbf{0}_{d}
in Equation (
15
), are identical to their ZANIM counterparts in Equations (
8
) and (
10
), respectively. However, the remaining component distributions differ from ZANIM in that Equation (
12
) is a DM distribution and Equation (
14
) represents DM distributions of reduced dimension, in contrast to the multinomial distribution in Equation (
7
) and sets of reduced multinomial distributions in Equation (

) under ZANIM.
Similarly, the finite mixture stochastic representation for ZANIDM is obtained by appropriately replacing the multinomial distributions in Proposition
2.2
with DM distributions.

Proposition 2.5
.

If
\mathbf{Y}\sim\operatorname{ZANIDM}_{d}[N,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\zeta}]
, then
\mathbf{Y}
has
the stochastic representation:

\mathbf{Y}\sim\eta_{0}\,\delta_{\mathbf{0}_{d}}(\cdot)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\eta^{(j)}_{N}\,\mathbf{E}_{d}^{(j)}+\eta_{d}\,\operatorname{DM}[N,\bm{\alpha}]+\sum_{\mathcal{K}\in\mathfrak{K}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{K}}\,\operatorname{DM}[N,\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{\mathcal{K}}],

(16)

where
\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{\mathcal{K}}
has empty entries in
\bm{\alpha}
for all
j\in\mathcal{K}
.

Follows by identifying each mixture component in the PMF of ZANIDM.

Remark 2.2
.

Although ZANIM and ZANIDM have
2^{d}
components, each distribution has only
2d
parameters, since the component weights are functions of
\bm{\zeta}
and the
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{\alpha}
parameters fully determine the non-degenerate components under ZANIM and ZANIDM, respectively.

Remark 2.3
.

The DM distribution arises from compounding the multinomial distribution with a probability vector which follows a Dirichlet distribution.
The concentration of its random success probabilities around their mean is governed by
\alpha_{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}
.
This variability diminishes as
\alpha_{s}\rightarrow\infty
while keeping the
proportions
\alpha_{j}/\alpha_{s}
constant. Consequently, the Dirichlet distribution collapses to a degenerate distribution and the DM tends toward a multinomial distribution with fixed probabilities.
Hence, we can relate the ZANIM and ZANIDM distributions by noting that, subject to keeping the corresponding parameters constant,
the DM component in ZANIDM approaches a multinomial distribution with parameters
\alpha_{j}/\alpha_{s}
as
\alpha_{s}\rightarrow\infty
, while the reduced DM components tend toward their multinomial counterparts with parameters
\alpha_{j}/\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{K}}
as
\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{K}}\rightarrow\infty
. The remaining degenerate components are already common to both distributions.

3
Properties of ZANIM and ZANIDM

We outline some basic probabilistic properties of the ZANIM and ZANIDM distributions.
Notably, the properties that follow are consequences of the fact that both the
ZANIM and ZANIDM distributions can be seen as finite mixture distributions.
As described in Propositions
2.1
and
2.4
, both models have
K=2^{d}
mixture components and the corresponding mixture weights
\bm{\eta}
are functions of the excess of zero parameters
\bm{\zeta}
(see Definition
2.2
).

3.1
Marginal distribution of
Y_{j}

When
\mathbf{Y}
follows either the ZANIM or ZANIDM distribution, whose respective PMFs are given in Equations
(
7
)–(
10
) and
(
12
)–(
15
), the marginal PMF of the
j
-th element is itself a mixture.
Under both distributions, obtaining the marginal PMF of
Y_{j}
involves summing over the support of all other variables in
\mathbf{Y}
while fixing
Y_{j}=k
.
We use the well-known results that the multinomial and DM distributions have binomial and beta-binomial marginals, respectively, and introduce the following set notation to help write explicit formulas for the marginal PMFs.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{j}=\{\mathcal{S}_{j}\subseteq\{1,\ldots j-1,j+1\ldots,d\};1\leq\lvert\mathcal{S}_{j}\rvert\leq d-2\}
represent the set of all subsets
\mathcal{S}_{j}
of
\{1,\ldots,d\}\setminus\{j\}

with cardinality between
1
and
d-2
for a given category
j
, such that the subsets
\mathcal{S}_{j}
are obtained by excluding the cases which contain the
j
-th index from the subsets
\mathcal{K}
described in Definition
2.1
.
Note that
\lvert\mathfrak{S}_{j}\rvert=2^{d-1}-2
.

Performing the required summation over each mixture component in the ZANIM and ZANIDM PMFs is
reasonably straightforward.
For ZANIM, the ‘non-zero’ component in
Equation (
7
)
and the ‘reduced dimension’ components in
Equation (
9
)
yield weighted binomial distributions, with weights given by
\eta_{d}
and
\smash{\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}=\prod_{k\in\mathcal{S}_{j}}\zeta_{k}\prod_{j\notin\mathcal{S}_{j}}(1-\zeta_{j})}
, respectively. The corresponding ZANIDM components in Equations (
12
) and
(
14
), yield similarly weighted beta-binomial distributions.
Recall that the remaining components are common to both distributions.
Firstly, regarding the
N
-inflated components in Equations (
8
)
and (
13
), we note that
marginalising the
N
-inflation component corresponding to the
j
-th category contributes a degenerate mass at
N
with probability
\smash{\eta_{N}^{(j)}}
, while the remaining
N
-inflated components each contribute a degenerate mass at
0
with probability

\smash{\eta_{N}^{(k)}}
, for
k\neq j
. Secondly, the purely degenerate components in Equations (
10
) and (
15
) also contribute a degenerate mass at
0
with probability
\eta_{0}
. Therefore, the marginal distribution of
Y_{j}
is degenerate at
0
with probability
\eta_{0}+\sum_{k\neq j}\eta_{N}^{(k)}

\zeta_{j}
. Finally, we obtain the marginal PMF of

under both distributions by combining the contributions of the associated marginal mixture components.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
\mathbf{Y}\sim\operatorname{ZANIM}_{d}[N,\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta}]
, then the marginal distribution of
Y_{j}
is

\displaystyle\Pr[Y_{j}=k]=\begin{cases}\zeta_{j}+\eta_{d}\,\left(1-\theta_{j}\right)^{N}+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\,\in\,\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\big(1-\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\big)^{N}&\textrm{if}\quad k=0\\

\eta_{d}\,p_{B}(k;N,\theta_{j})+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\,\in\,\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,p_{B}\big(k;N,\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\big)&\textrm{if}\quad k=1,\,\ldots,N-1,\end{cases}

where
p_{B}(k;N,\theta)
denotes the binomial PMF
and
\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}=\theta_{j}/(1-\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{j}}\theta_{\ell})
.

Proof.

Follows directly from the concept of a marginal distribution, using Definition
3.1
.

Proposition 3.2
.

If
\mathbf{Y}\sim\operatorname{ZANIDM}_{d}[N,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\zeta}]

Y_{j}

\displaystyle\Pr[Y_{j}=k]=\begin{cases}\zeta_{j}+\eta_{d}\,\dfrac{\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{j},N+\alpha^{(j)}_{s})}{\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{j},\alpha^{(j)}_{s})}+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\,\in\,\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\dfrac{\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{j},N+\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}})}{\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}})}&\textrm{if}\quad k=0\\

is the Beta function.
Similarly,
\smash{\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}=\sum_{k\notin\mathcal{S}_{j}}\alpha_{k}}
where
k\notin\mathcal{S}_{j}
implies the indices in
\{1,\ldots,d\}\setminus\{j\}
that are not in
\mathcal{S}_{j}
.

Proof.

Follows directly from the concept of a marginal distribution, using Definition
3.1
.

3.2
Moments

We briefly review a generic result that will be used for the derivations.
Let
\bm{\tau}=\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{K}\}
be a set of mixture weights, such that
\tau_{k}
reflects a generic indexed mixture component
without regard to whether that component relates to any particular case of zero-inflation,
N
-inflation, or otherwise.
Let
W
denote a discrete random variable indicating the mixture component, i.e.,
taking values in
\{1,\ldots,K\}
with corresponding probabilities
\bm{\tau}
.
Then, the expected value of
g(\mathbf{Y})
can be expressed as

\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{Y})]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tau_{k}\,\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{Y})\mid W=k]
. Thus, since both distributions can be expressed as multivariate finite mixture distributions
with
K=2^{d}
components, we can easily obtain their moments. We recall that the component distributions are either degenerate random vectors (at
\delta_{\mathbf{0}_{d}}
or
\mathbf{E}_{d}^{(j)}
), or multinomial/DM
random vectors (including some of reduced dimension); see the stochastic representations in
Equations (
11
) and (
16
).

Definition 3.2
.

By analogy with the sets
\mathfrak{K}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{j}
described in Definitions
2.1
and
3.1
, respectively, let
\mathfrak{R}_{jh}=\{\mathcal{R}_{jh}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,j-1,j+1,\ldots,h-1,h+1,\ldots,d\};1\leq\lvert\mathcal{R}_{jh}\rvert\leq d-2\}
represent the set of all subsets
\mathcal{R}_{jh}
of
\{1,\ldots,d\}\setminus\{j,h\}
for a given pair of categories
j\neq h
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}\sim\operatorname{ZANIM}_{d}[N,\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta}]
,
then the mean and variance of the
j
-th entry of the random vector
\mathbf{Y}
, i.e.,
the random variable
Y_{j}
, are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}]

\displaystyle=N\Big(\eta^{(j)}_{N}+\eta_{d}\,\theta_{j}+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\Big)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}[Y_{j}]

\displaystyle=\eta_{N}^{(j)}\,N^{2}+\eta_{d}\,N\theta_{j}\left(1+\theta_{j}(N-1)\right)+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,N\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\left(1+\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}(N-1)\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~-N^{2}\Big(\eta^{(j)}_{N}+\eta_{d}\,\theta_{j}+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\Big)^{2}.

The covariance between the random variables
(Y_{j},Y_{h})\>\forall\>j\neq h
of the random vector
\mathbf{Y}
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}[Y_{j},Y_{h}]

\displaystyle=N(N-1)\,\Big(\eta_{d}\,\theta_{j}\,\theta_{h}+\sum_{\mathcal{R}_{jh}\in\,\mathfrak{R}_{jh}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{R}_{jh}}\,\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{R}_{jh}}\,\theta_{h}^{\mathcal{R}_{jh}}\Big)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~-N^{2}\Big(\eta^{(j)}_{N}+\eta_{d}\,\theta_{j}+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\Big)\Big(\eta^{(h)}_{N}+\eta_{d}\,\theta_{h}+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{h}\in\mathfrak{S}_{h}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{h}}\,\theta_{h}^{\mathcal{S}_{h}}\Big).

Proof.

Follows from the moment properties of finite mixture distributions.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}\sim\operatorname{ZANIDM}_{d}[N,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\zeta}]
,
then the mean and variance of the
j
-th entry of the random vector
\mathbf{Y}
, i.e.,
the random variable
Y_{j}
, are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}]

\displaystyle=N\bigg(\eta^{(j)}_{N}+\eta_{d}\,\dfrac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{s}}+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\dfrac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Y_{j}]

\displaystyle=\eta_{N}^{(j)}\,N^{2}+\eta_{d}\bigg(\dfrac{N\,\alpha_{j}[N(1+\alpha_{j})+\alpha_{s}^{(j)}]}{(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{s}^{(j)})\,(1+\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{s}^{(j)})}\bigg)+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\bigg(\dfrac{N\,\alpha_{j}[N(1+\alpha_{j})+\alpha_{s}^{{\mathcal{S}_{j}}}]}{(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{s}^{{\mathcal{S}_{j}}})\,(1+\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{s}^{{\mathcal{S}_{j}}})}\bigg)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~-N^{2}\bigg(\eta^{(j)}_{N}+\eta_{d}\,\dfrac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{s}}+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\dfrac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}}\bigg)^{2}.

(Y_{j},Y_{h})\>\forall\>j\neq h

\mathbf{Y}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+N\sum_{\mathcal{R}_{jh}\in\,\mathfrak{R}_{jh}}\eta_{\mathcal{R}_{jh}}\,\dfrac{\alpha_{j}\alpha_{h}}{\big(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{h}+\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{R}_{jh}}\big)^{2}}\bigg[N-\dfrac{N+\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{h}+\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{R}_{jh}}}{1+\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{h}+\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{R}_{jh}}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~-N^{2}\bigg(\eta^{(j)}_{N}+\eta_{d}\,\dfrac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{s}}+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\dfrac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}}\bigg)\times\bigg(\eta^{(h)}_{N}+\eta_{d}\,\dfrac{\alpha_{h}}{\alpha_{s}}+\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{h}\in\mathfrak{S}_{h}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{h}}\,\dfrac{\alpha_{h}}{\alpha_{h}+\alpha_{s}^{\mathcal{S}_{h}}}\bigg).

Proof.

Follows from the moment properties of finite mixture distributions.

4
Bayesian inference for ZANIM and ZANIDM

We develop Bayesian inference frameworks for estimating the parameters of the ZANIM and ZANIDM distributions.
Inference for ZANIM and ZANIDM is based on the likelihood (or log-likelihood) functions defined in Equations
(
7
)–(
10
) and
(
12
)–(
15
), respectively.
These functions involve complex mixture likelihoods where the mixing proportion parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}
depend on
the zero-inflation parameters
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{d}
. As the dimension
d
increases, computing the likelihood
becomes computationally intensive.
To address this, we explore the stochastic representations of the distributions and consider
data augmentation strategies, simplifying the posterior distributions and enabling efficient sampling. In each case, we assume access to an i.i.d. random sample of size
n
denoted by

\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n})
, where
\mathbf{y}_{i}=(y_{i1},\ldots,y_{id})
.
We allow the number of trials, a fixed and known parameter given by
N_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{ij}
, to be observation-specific, such that
\mathbf{Y}_{i}\sim\operatorname{ZANIM}_{d}[N_{i},\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta}]
or
\mathbf{Y}_{i}\sim\operatorname{ZANIDM}_{d}[N_{i},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\zeta}]
.

4.1
ZANIM

Inference for the ZANIM parameters
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{\zeta}
is based on the
the stochastic representation given in Proposition
2.3
. In the Supplementary Material, we show that augmenting ZANIM
with the latent variables
z_{j}
and
(\phi\mid z_{j})\sim\operatorname{Gamma}[N,\sum_{j=1}^{d},\lambda_{j}\,z_{j}]
enables recovery of the zero-inflated
augmented likelihood in Equation (
6
) from which
ZANIM was derived, and give the full MCMC algorithm.
Based on a random sample
\mathbf{y}
, the further augmented ZANIM likelihood using the latent variables

\bm{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\ldots\phi_{n})
and
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{z}_{i},\ldots\mathbf{z}_{n})
, with

\mathbf{z}_{i}=(z_{i1},\ldots,z_{id})
, is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{y},\bm{\phi},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left\{\left[(1-z_{ij})\zeta_{j}+z_{ij}(1-\zeta_{j})e^{-\phi\lambda_{j}}\right]^{\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{ij})}\times\left[z_{ij}(1-\zeta_{j})\lambda_{j}^{y_{ij}}e^{-\phi\lambda_{j}}\right]^{1-\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{ij})}\right\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left\{\zeta_{j}^{n-t_{j}}(1-\zeta_{j})^{t_{j}}\times\lambda_{j}^{r_{j}}e^{-s_{j}\,\lambda_{j}}\right\},

where

t_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,z_{ij},r_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{ij}\,z_{ij}
, and

s_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}\,z_{ij}
play the role of conditional sufficient statistics for category
j
.
We can now see that the augmented likelihood factors into a product of beta and gamma terms,
and that the category-specific parameters are independent. This implies that inference procedures
can be performed independently for each category. Thus, we can consider a joint prior for

(\zeta_{j},\lambda_{j})
as a product of two independent priors which exhibit conjugacy properties, i.e,

\zeta_{j}\sim\operatorname{Beta}[a_{j},b_{j}]
and

\lambda_{j}\sim\operatorname{Gamma}[c_{j},d_{j}],

for
j=1,\ldots,d
.
Thus, the full conditional distributions of
\zeta_{j}
and
\lambda_{j}

given the augmented data
(\mathbf{y},\bm{\phi},\mathbf{z})
are

\displaystyle(\zeta_{j}\mid\mathbf{y},\bm{\phi},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle\sim\operatorname{Beta}[n-t_{j}+a_{j},t_{j}+b_{j}]

(17)

\displaystyle(\lambda_{j}\mid\mathbf{y},\bm{\phi},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle\sim\operatorname{Gamma}[r_{j}+c_{j},s_{j}+d_{j}].

(18)

Finally, we note that the full conditional distribution of
z_{ij}
is given by

(z_{ij}\mid y_{ij},\phi_{i},\lambda_{j},\zeta_{j})\sim\begin{cases}\operatorname{Bernoulli}\left[\dfrac{(1-\zeta_{j})e^{-\phi_{i}\lambda_{j}}}{\zeta_{j}+(1-\zeta_{j})e^{-\phi_{i}\lambda_{j}}}\right]&\textrm{if}\>y_{ij}=0\\
1&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(19)

4.2
ZANIDM

As per
Koslovsky 2023
, we exploit the stochastic representation of ZANIDM given in Definition
2.4
.
We further introduce the latent variables

(\phi_{i}\mid\mathbf{y}_{i},\bm{\lambda}_{i})\sim\operatorname{Gamma}\left[N_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\lambda_{ij}\right],\>i=1,\ldots,n,\quad\text{where}~N_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{ij}.

Given the latent variables
\lambda_{ij},z_{ij}
, and
\phi_{i}
, along with the observed
vector
\mathbf{y}_{i}
, the augmented likelihood of the
i
-th observation factors into
d
independent terms, as per ZANIM, as follows

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y}_{i},\bm{\lambda}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i},\phi_{i}\mid\bm{\alpha},\bm{\zeta})

\displaystyle=p(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\bm{\lambda}_{i})\,p(\phi_{i}\mid\mathbf{y}_{i},\bm{\lambda}_{i})\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[p(\lambda_{ij}\mid z_{ij},\alpha_{j})\,p(z_{ij}\mid\zeta_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\phi_{i}^{N_{i}-1}}{\Gamma(N_{i})}\binom{N}{y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[(1-\zeta_{j})^{z_{ij}}\,\zeta_{j}^{1-z_{ij}}\,(1-z_{ij})^{\mathds{1}_{0}(\lambda_{ij})}\,z_{ij}^{1-\mathds{1}_{0}(\lambda_{ij})}\right]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~\times\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\,\left(\dfrac{\lambda_{ij}^{y_{ij}+\alpha_{j}-1}e^{-\lambda_{ij}(1+\phi_{i})}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}\right)^{1-\mathds{1}_{0}(\lambda_{ij})}\right].

Thus, the inference over the parameters
\bm{\zeta}
and
\bm{\alpha}
can be performed independently.

Koslovsky 2023
proposed a method to perform Bayesian inference when the parameters
\bm{\alpha}
and

\bm{\zeta}
can depend on covariates (which we do not consider here), which relies on so-called
‘expand and contract’ moves (effectively transdimensional Metropolis-Hastings (MH) steps) to jointly update the
latent variables
z_{ij}
and
\lambda_{ij}
. We propose an alternative collapsed Gibbs sampling approach that improves the efficiency by enabling fast conjugate updates for both quantities. By avoiding joint updates, we obviate the need to change the dimension of the parameter space as the MCMC algorithm proceeds.
Specifically, we note that it is
easy to obtain the distribution of

z_{ij}
unconditional on
\lambda_{ij}
, then update
\lambda_{ij}
conditional on
z_{ij}
. We sketch our proposals below, but provide more details in the Supplementary Material, including the full MCMC algorithm.

We first discuss the updates of
\lambda_{ij}
and
z_{ij}
.
Note that the joint distribution of
\lambda_{ij}
and
z_{ij}
given the observed data
y_{ij}
and
the latent variable
\phi_{i}
is

p(\lambda_{ij},z_{ij}\mid y_{ij},\phi_{i})\propto(1-\zeta_{j})^{z_{ij}}\,\zeta_{j}^{1-z_{ij}}\left[(1-z_{ij})\,\delta_{0}(\lambda_{ij})+z_{ij}\,\dfrac{\lambda_{ij}^{y_{ij}+\alpha_{j}-1}e^{-\lambda_{ij}(1+\phi_{i})}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}\right].

An easy away to avoid the complicated expand and contract approach of
Koslovsky 2023
when updating

\lambda_{ij}
and
z_{ij}
is to take advantage of the marginalisation of the joint distribution

p(\lambda_{ij},z_{ij}\mid y_{ij},\phi_{i})
over
\lambda_{ij}
.
In doing so, we obtain

p(z_{ij}\mid y_{ij},\phi_{i})\propto(1-z_{ij})\,\zeta_{j}+z_{ij}\,(1-\zeta_{j})\dfrac{(1+\phi_{i})^{-(y_{ij}+\alpha_{j})}\Gamma(y_{ij}+\alpha_{j})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}.

For a given
j
,
z_{ij}=1
with probability
1
when
y_{ij}>0
. Conversely, when
y_{ij}=0
, we have that

p(z_{ij}\mid y_{ij}=0,\phi_{i})\propto\left[(1-\zeta_{j})(1+\phi_{i})^{-\alpha_{j}}\right]^{z_{ij}}\zeta_{j}^{1-z_{ij}}.

Therefore, the collapsed conditional distribution of
z_{ij}
is

(z_{ij}\mid y_{ij},\phi_{i})\sim\begin{cases}\operatorname{Bernoulli}\left[\dfrac{(1-\zeta_{j})(1+\phi_{i})^{-\alpha_{j}}}{\zeta_{j}+(1-\zeta_{j})(1+\phi_{i})^{-\alpha_{j}}}\right]&\textrm{if}\>y_{ij}=0\\
1&\textrm{otherwise},\end{cases}

(20)

and the full conditional distribution of
\lambda_{ij}
is

(\lambda_{ij}\mid y_{ij},z_{ij},\phi_{i})\sim\begin{cases}\operatorname{Gamma}[\alpha_{j}+y_{ij},1+\phi_{i}]&\textrm{if}\quad z_{ij}=1\\
0&\textrm{if}\quad z_{ij}=0,\end{cases}

(21)

which is recognisable as a zero-augmented gamma distribution. Thus, straightforward Gibbs updates are available for both
\lambda_{ij}
and
z_{ij}
, without requiring the joint expand and contract updates performed by
Koslovsky 2023
.

As regards the parameters
\zeta_{j}
and
\alpha_{j}
, their full conditional distributions are given by

\displaystyle p(\zeta_{j}\mid\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\bm{\phi})

\displaystyle\propto(1-\zeta_{j})^{t_{j}}\,\zeta_{j}^{n-t_{j}}\times p(\zeta_{j})

\displaystyle p(\alpha_{j}\mid\mathbf{y},\bm{\lambda},\mathbf{z},\bm{\phi})

\displaystyle\propto\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})^{t_{j}}}\,\exp\bigg[\alpha_{j}\,\sum_{i\colon\lambda_{ij}>0}\log\lambda_{ij}\bigg]\times p(\alpha_{j}),

(22)

where
t_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{ij}
.
For
\zeta_{j}
, we clearly have the kernel of a Bernoulli distribution; assuming its conjugate prior,
\zeta_{j}\sim\operatorname{Beta}[a_{j},b_{j}]
,
the full conditional distribution for
\zeta_{j}
is

(\zeta_{j}\mid\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\bm{\phi})\sim\operatorname{Beta}[n-t_{j}+a_{j},t_{j}+b_{j}].

(23)

However, the full conditional distribution of
\alpha_{j}
is not analytically tractable.
We consider and evaluate the performance of three approaches. The first assumes a gamma prior for

\alpha_{j}\sim\operatorname{Gamma}[c_{j},d_{j}]
in conjunction with Equation (
22
), resulting in the full conditional target

\pi(\alpha_{j})\coloneqq p(\alpha_{j}\mid\mathbf{y},\bm{\lambda},\mathbf{z},\bm{\phi})\times p(\alpha_{j})\propto\dfrac{\alpha_{j}^{c_{j}-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})^{t_{j}}}\exp\left[-\alpha_{j}\,\left(d_{j}-\sum_{i\colon\lambda_{ij}>0}\log\lambda_{ij}\right)\right].

(24)

We then apply a data augmentation scheme proposed by
Hamura et al. 2023
, which performs a MH step
with independent power-truncated-normal (PTN) proposals.

The second and third approaches consider the re-parameterisation
\log\alpha_{j}=\beta_{j}

and assume a Normal prior,
\beta_{j}\sim\operatorname{Normal}[m_{j},s_{j}^{2}]
,
resulting in the full conditional target

\pi(\beta_{j})\propto\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(e^{\beta_{j}})^{t_{j}}}\exp\left[e^{\beta_{j}}\sum_{i\colon\lambda_{ij}>0}\log\left(\lambda_{ij}\right)+\beta_{j}\,s_{j}^{-2}(m_{j}-0.5\beta_{j})\right].

(25)

To sample from
\pi(\beta_{j})
, we consider two well-known general schemes; a MH algorithm where the proposals
follow a Gaussian random walk, as used by
Koslovsky 2023
, and slice sampling with the stepping-out and shrinkage procedures as proposed by

Neal 2003
. Note that our use of random walk MH still differs from
Koslovsky 2023
by virtue of our novel updates for
\lambda_{ij}
and
z_{ij}
. Further details on all proposed sampling schemes for
\bm{\alpha}
are provided in the Supplementary Material.

6
Discussion

The main contributions of this paper have been the novel probabilistic insights provided for the ZANIM and ZANIDM distributions, which are
suitable for addressing zero-inflation in count-compositional data.
We provided a probabilistic characterisation of ZANIDM, which was first proposed by
Koslovsky 2023
, and introduced the more parsimonious ZANIM.
We demonstrated that both distributions belong to a unifying framework and can be represented as finite mixtures.
We derived their key properties, including moments and the corresponding marginal distributions.
We showed that the distributions can accommodate overdispersion and positive correlations, which can be attributed
to their mixture structure and zero-inflation properties.

We subsequently developed Bayesian inference frameworks for both distributions. Specifically, for ZANIDM, we
showed through simulation studies that our collapsed Gibbs sampling approach for updating the latent parameters is more efficient than the
algorithm of
Koslovsky 2023
. Our extensive simulations also showed that both distributions are effective when
data exhibit zero-inflation across multiple categories.
It is worth noting that if exclusively non-zero counts are observed in one or more categories, or if contextual information gives sufficient reason to believe that the observed zeros for a given
Y_{j}
are not structural in nature, we can simplify the distributions by removing the corresponding zero-inflation parameters
\zeta_{j}
from the model.

Our unifying framework that characterises both distributions as finite mixtures can be extended to incorporate other component distributions suitable for modelling count-compositional data, e.g., the Conway-Maxwell-multinomial distribution
[
4
,
5
]
.
In doing so, novel zero-&-
N
-inflated counterparts for such distributions could be developed, though inference would remain a challenge. Compositional distributions that handle proportions, such as the Dirichlet distribution, could also be extended in a similar fashion.

Another interesting avenue for future research would be to explore non-parametric regression approaches for incorporating covariates into the category-specific parameters of both distributions. For ZANIM, this would extend the approach of
Zeng et al. 2023
, who allow only the success probabilities, and not the zero-inflation parameters, to depend on covariates. For ZANIDM, this would provide additional flexibility over the log-linear and logistic regressions employed by
Koslovsky 2023
for the
\alpha_{j}
and
\zeta_{j}
parameters, respectively. We also stress that, even in regression settings, our Gibbs updates of
\lambda_{ij}
and
z_{ij}
under ZANIDM would still be advantageous.

Overall, we envisage that the novel theoretical insights we provide for ZANIM and ZANIDM will be of interest to researchers and applied practitioners working with either distribution or with zero-inflated multivariate
data more broadly.

Appendix A
Derivation of the ZANIM PMF

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

The goal is to marginalise out the latent variable
\phi
from Equation (
6
)
and ensure that the function
\int\Pr[\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y},\phi;\bm{\lambda},\bm{\zeta}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi

will be a proper PMF.
We shall denote
\Pr[\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y},\phi;\bm{\lambda},\bm{\zeta}]=p(\mathbf{y},\phi)
, for brevity.
We begin with

p(\mathbf{y},\phi)=\dfrac{N!\,\phi^{N-1}}{\Gamma(N)}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\zeta_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{j})+(1-\zeta_{j})\,\dfrac{\lambda_{j}^{y_{j}}\,e^{-\lambda_{j}\,\phi}}{y_{j}!}\right],

and note that the product will have
2^{d}
terms, as a consequence of the binomial theorem.
However, due to the indicator function
\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{j})
, we can simplify some terms.
We shall consider four different groups of terms, corresponding to the four types of mixture component in ZANIM.

\bullet
Standard multinomial
\colon

\displaystyle\int p(\mathbf{y},\phi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi

\displaystyle=\int\dfrac{N!\,\phi^{N-1}}{\Gamma(N)}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[(1-\zeta_{j})\,\dfrac{\lambda_{j}^{y_{j}}\,e^{-\lambda_{j}\,\phi}}{y_{j}!}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\binom{N}{y_{1}\dots y_{d}}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,(1-\zeta_{j})\,\lambda_{j}^{y_{j}}\right]\int\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(N)}\,\phi^{N-1}\,e^{-\phi\,\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\lambda_{j}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\binom{N}{y_{1}\dots y_{d}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,(1-\zeta_{j})\,\left(\dfrac{\lambda_{j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}}\right)^{y_{j}}=\displaystyle\binom{N}{y_{1}\dots y_{d}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,(1-\zeta_{j})\,\theta_{j}^{y_{j}}.

\bullet

\delta_{\mathbf{0}_{d}}
component
\colon

\displaystyle\int p(\mathbf{y},\phi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,\zeta_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{j})\right]\,N!\,\int\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(N)}\,\phi^{N-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,\zeta_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{j})\right]\,N!\,\int\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(N)}\,\phi_{i}^{N-1}\,e^{-0\,\phi}\,0^{N-N}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,\zeta_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{j})\right]\,0!\,0^{0}\int\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(N)}\,\phi^{N-1}\,e^{-0\,\phi}\,0^{N}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi,

since
N=0
when
y_{j}=0\>\forall\>j
, such that
N!=0!=1
, and
0^{0}=1
, by convention.
The integral above is an abstract representation of a
\operatorname{Gamma}[0,0]
distribution,
which in practice is not well-defined.
Here, we shall adopt the convention that
\operatorname{Gamma}[0,0]\overset{d}{=}\delta_{0}(\cdot)
. Under this convention, the distribution is a point-mass at
0
with probability
1
, whose integral evaluates to
1
. Thus,

\int p(\mathbf{y},\phi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\,\zeta_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{0}(y_{j}).

\bullet

\mathbf{E}_{d}^{(j)}
components
\colon

We have
d
terms with this constraint, which can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\int p(\mathbf{y},\phi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\{\mathds{1}_{0}\left(\sum_{k\colon k\neq j}y_{k}\right)(1-\zeta_{j})\prod_{k\colon k\neq j}\zeta_{k}\int\dfrac{N!\,\phi^{N-1}}{\Gamma(N)}\dfrac{\lambda_{j}^{y_{j}}\,e^{-\lambda_{j}\phi}}{y_{j}!}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\{\mathds{1}_{0}\left(\sum_{k\colon k\neq j}y_{k}\right)(1-\zeta_{j})\prod_{k\colon k\neq j}\zeta_{k}\,\dfrac{\lambda_{j}^{y_{j}}\,N!}{y_{j}!}\int\dfrac{e^{-\lambda_{j}\phi}\phi^{N-1}}{\Gamma(N)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\{\mathds{1}_{0}\left(\sum_{k\colon k\neq j}y_{k}\right)(1-\zeta_{j})\prod_{k\colon k\neq j}\zeta_{k}\right\},

where the simplification comes from the fact that
N=y_{j}

when
y_{j}>0
and
y_{k}=0

\forall\>k\neq j
.

\bullet
Reduced multinomials
\colon

The remaining terms represent cases where at most
d-2
categories exhibit zero-inflation. To write the
2^{d}-d-2
such terms compactly, we define the corresponding set
\mathfrak{K}
as follows. Let
\mathfrak{K}=\{\mathcal{K}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\};1\leq\lvert\mathcal{K}\rvert\leq d-2\}
.
Then,
\mathfrak{K}
consists of mutually exclusive sets
\mathcal{K}
, where
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
with
1\leq\lvert\mathcal{K}\rvert\leq d-2
, i.e., all subsets
\mathcal{K}
of the category indices
\{1,\ldots,d\}
with
cardinality between
1
and

\lambda_{j}=0

Next, we note that we can marginalise out

p(z_{ij}\mid\zeta_{j})=(1-\zeta_{j})^{z_{ij}}\zeta_{j}^{1-z_{ij}}
.
Then, from the augmented likelihood given in Section
4.2
, the full joint distribution of
\lambda_{ij}
and
z_{ij}
given the observed data
y_{ij}
is

\displaystyle p(\lambda_{ij},z_{ij}\mid y_{ij},\phi_{i})

\displaystyle\propto(1-\zeta_{j})^{z_{ij}}\,\zeta_{j}^{1-z_{ij}}(1-z_{ij})^{\mathds{1}_{0}(\lambda_{ij})}\,z_{ij}^{1-\mathds{1}_{0}(\lambda_{ij})}\times\left(\dfrac{\lambda_{ij}^{y_{ij}+\alpha_{j}-1}e^{-\lambda_{ij}(1+\phi_{i})}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}\right)^{1-\mathds{1}_{0}(\lambda_{ij})}

\displaystyle\propto(1-\zeta_{j})^{z_{ij}}\,\zeta_{j}^{1-z_{ij}}\left[(1-z_{ij})\,\delta_{0}(\lambda_{ij})+z_{ij}\,\dfrac{\lambda_{ij}^{y_{ij}+\alpha_{j}-1}e^{-\lambda_{ij}(1+\phi_{i})}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}\right].

The marginalisation of the joint distribution
p(\lambda_{ij},z_{ij}\mid y_{ij},\phi_{i})
with respect to

\lambda_{ij}
is given by

\displaystyle p(z_{ij}\mid y_{ij},\phi_{i})

\displaystyle=\int p(\lambda_{ij},z_{ij}\mid y_{ij},\phi_{i})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda_{ij}

\displaystyle\propto(1-\zeta_{j})^{z_{ij}}\,\zeta_{j}^{1-z_{ij}}\int\left[(1-z_{ij})\,\delta_{0}(\lambda_{ij})+z_{ij}\,\dfrac{\lambda_{ij}^{y_{ij}+\alpha_{j}-1}e^{-\lambda_{ij}(1+\phi_{i})}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda_{ij}

\displaystyle\propto(1-\zeta_{j})^{z_{ij}}\,\zeta_{j}^{1-z_{ij}}\left[(1-z_{ij})\int\delta_{0}(\lambda_{ij})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda_{ij}+z_{ij}\,\int\dfrac{\lambda_{ij}^{y_{ij}+\alpha_{j}-1}e^{-\lambda_{ij}(1+\phi_{i})}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle\propto(1-z_{ij})\zeta_{j}+z_{ij}(1-\zeta_{j})\dfrac{(1+\phi_{i})^{-(y_{ij}+\alpha_{j})}\Gamma(y_{ij}+\alpha_{j})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}.

When
y_{ij}>0
, we know that
z_{ij}=1
almost surely.
Conversely, when
y_{ij}=0
, we have that

p(z_{ij}\mid y_{ij}=0,\phi_{i})\propto\left[(1-\zeta_{j})(1+\phi_{i})^{-\alpha_{j}}\right]^{z_{ij}}\zeta_{j}^{1-z_{ij}}
. Since
z_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
, it is easy to obtain the normalising constant and write the distribution of
(z_{ij}\mid y_{ij},\phi_{i})
as per Equation (
20
).
On the other hand, the distribution of
(\lambda_{ij}\mid y_{ij},z_{ij},\phi_{i})
, which yields Equation (
21
) when normalised, is

p(\lambda_{ij}\mid y_{ij},z_{ij},\phi_{i})\propto(1-z_{ij})\,\delta_{0}(\lambda_{ij})+z_{ij}\,\lambda_{ij}^{y_{ij}+\alpha_{j}-1}e^{-\lambda_{ij}(1+\phi_{i})}.

Finally, we recall that we consider several approaches in Section
4.2
for updating
\alpha_{j}
, whose full conditional distribution is given in Equation (
22
). Two of these approaches — namely, MH with a Gaussian random walk and the slice sampler of
Neal 2003
— work by updating
\beta_{j}
according to the re-parameterisation
\log\alpha_{j}=\beta_{j}
and the prior
\beta_{j}\sim\operatorname{Normal}[m_{j},s_{j}^{2}]
. As these approaches are quite standard, we do not describe them further here. Instead, we provide some details on the data augmentation strategies proposed by
Hamura et al. 2023
, who present a general scheme for cases where the parameter of interest appears as the argument of a gamma function, as occurs with the
\bm{\alpha}
parameter in the Dirichlet, Dirichlet-multinomial, and indeed ZANIDM distributions. Recall that under the prior
\alpha_{j}\sim\operatorname{Gamma}[c_{j},d_{j}]
,
where the category-specific hyper-parameters
c_{j}
and
d_{j}
are known, our target
\pi(\alpha_{j})
, given by Equation (
24
), is not straightforward to sample from. The main idea of
Hamura et al. 2023
is to introduce auxiliary variables, such that the target can be approximated by proposing from an independent power-truncated-normal (PTN) distribution and evaluating a simple MH step. These strategies lead to a three-step process, which we adapt to ZANIDM as follows below.

\bullet
First step
\colon
Beta data augmentation for dealing with the term
\Gamma(\alpha_{j})^{-t_{j}}
in Equation
(
24
).

Consider
\rho_{kj}\sim\operatorname{Beta}[\alpha_{j}+(k-1)/t_{j},(t_{j}-k+1)/t_{j}],~k=2,\ldots,t_{j}
.
Then, the target, conditional now on
the auxiliary variables
\bm{\rho}_{j}=(\rho_{2j},\ldots,\rho_{t_{j}})
, is given by

\displaystyle\pi(\alpha_{j}\mid\bm{\rho}_{j})

\displaystyle\propto\alpha_{j}^{c_{j}+t_{j}-1/2}\,\exp\left[-\alpha_{j}\,\left(d_{j}-\sum_{i\colon\lambda_{ij}>0}\log\lambda_{ij}-\sum_{k=2}^{t_{j}}\log\rho_{kj}-t_{j}\right)\right]\times\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{j}^{t_{j}\alpha_{j}}}\,C(\alpha_{j}),

where
C(\alpha_{j})=\dfrac{(t_{j}\alpha_{j})^{t_{j}\alpha_{j}-1/2}}{\Gamma(t_{j}\alpha_{j})e^{t_{j}\alpha_{j}}}
.

\bullet
Second step
\colon
Gamma data augmentation for dealing with the term
1/\alpha_{j}^{t_{j}\alpha_{j}}
.

By introducing the auxiliary variable

w_{j}\sim\operatorname{Gamma}[t_{j}\alpha_{j},t_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2}]
and defining
p_{j}^{\star}=t_{j}+c_{j}
,
a_{j}^{\star}=t_{j}w_{j}
,
and
b_{j}^{\star}=t_{j}\log w_{j}+2t_{j}+\sum_{i\colon\lambda_{ij}>0}\log\lambda_{ij}+\sum_{k=2}^{t_{j}}\log\rho_{jk}-d_{j}
, we obtain

\pi(\alpha_{j}\mid\bm{\rho}_{j},w_{j})\propto\alpha_{j}^{p_{j}^{\star}-1}\exp\left[-a_{j}^{\star}\,\alpha_{j}^{2}+b_{j}^{\star}\,\alpha_{j}\right]C(\alpha_{j})^{2}.

(S.2)

\bullet
Third step
\colon
Metropolis-Hastings with independent PTN proposals.

The target in Equation (
S.2
), now conditioned on the
auxiliary variables
\bm{\rho}_{j}
and
w_{j}
, can be written as

\pi(\alpha_{j}\mid\bm{\rho}_{j},w_{j})\propto f_{\operatorname{PTN}}(\alpha_{j};p_{j}^{\star},a_{j}^{\star},b_{j}^{\star})C(\alpha_{j})^{2},

where
f_{\operatorname{PTN}}(x;p,a,b)
denotes the probability density function of a PTN random variable
3
3

3

If
X\sim\operatorname{PTN}[p,a,b]
, then
f(x)\propto x^{p-1}\,e^{-a\,x^{2}+b\,x}
for
x,a,p>0
and
b\neq 0
.
. Following
Hamura et al. 2023
, we consider independent PTN proposals with the same parameters, i.e.,

\smash{\alpha_{j}^{(t)}\sim\operatorname{PTN}[p_{j}^{\star},a_{j}^{\star},b_{j}^{\star}]}
.
Then, due to the proposals being independent and of the same form as the target, the MH acceptance probability to move from
\alpha_{j}^{(t-1)}
to
\alpha_{j}^{(t)}
simplifies to
\min\{1,C(\alpha_{j}^{(t)})^{2}/C(\alpha_{j}^{(t-1)})^{2}\}
.
As shown by
Hamura et al. 2023
, the factor
C(\alpha_{j})
is almost constant when
\alpha_{j}
is not extremely small, and the acceptance probability is close to
1
.

Moment generating functions via mixture properties

We can find the moment generating function (MGF) for both distributions using the moment properties of mixtures with
g(\mathbf{Y})=e^{\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{Y}}
.
By identifying the component-specific distributions based on our novel stochastic representations of
ZANIM and ZANIDM in terms of finite mixtures,
we note that the first two terms
\delta_{\mathbf{0}_{d}}(\cdot)
and
\mathbf{E}_{d}^{(j)}
are degenerate random vectors, while the
remaining terms follow multinomial and DM distributions, respectively. Thus,

M_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{t})=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{Y}}]=\eta_{0}\,M_{\delta_{\mathbf{0}_{d}}(\cdot)}(\mathbf{t})+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\eta_{N}^{(j)}\,M_{\mathbf{E}_{d}^{(j)}}(\mathbf{t})+\eta_{d}\,M_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{t})+\sum_{\mathcal{K}\in\mathfrak{K}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{K}}\,M_{\mathbf{X}^{\mathcal{K}}}(\mathbf{t}),

(S.3)

where
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})
, and the random vectors
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\mathcal{K}}
have
multinomial
or DM distributions with
appropriate dimension and parameters. Expanding the sum in Equation (
S.3
) with the
MGFs of the corresponding components
trivially yields the ZANIM MGF as follows

M_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{t})=\eta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\eta_{N}^{(j)}\,\exp\left(N\,t_{j}\right)+\eta_{d}\,\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\theta_{j}\,\exp\left(t_{j}\right)\right)^{N}+\sum_{\mathcal{K}\in\mathfrak{K}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{K}}\,\left(\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{K}}\,\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{K}}\,\exp\left(t_{j}\right)\right)^{N}.

By way of verification, we have that the first partial derivative of
M_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{t})
w.r.t.
t_{j}
is

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial M_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{t})}{\partial\,t_{j}}

\displaystyle=N\,\eta_{N}^{(j)}\,\exp\left(N\,t_{j}\right)+N\,\eta_{d}\,\theta_{j}\,\exp\left(t_{j}\right)\,\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\theta_{j}\,\exp\left(t_{j}\right)\right)^{N-1}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+N\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{j}}\,\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\exp(t_{j})\left[\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\exp(t_{j})\right]^{N-1},

where the partial derivative of the last term w.r.t.
t_{j}
vanishes for sets outside the defined
\mathfrak{S}_{j}
. As both
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\theta_{j}=1
and
\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{S}_{j}}\,\theta_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{j}}=1
,
it is trivial to show that the derived expression for
\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}]
is recovered by setting
t_{j}=0
here. Using the usual argumentation for MGFs also recovers
\mathrm{Var}[Y_{j}]
under ZANIM and yields similar results for ZANIDM.
