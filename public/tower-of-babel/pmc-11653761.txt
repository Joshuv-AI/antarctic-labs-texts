Is medical school culture conducive to women’s academic success? a survey on faculty perceptions and experiences of gender equity
Authors: Shefaly Shorey, Yunn-Hwen Gan, Maleena Suppiah Cavert, Sophia Archuleta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn academic medicine, the lack of gender equity hinders the career progression of women academics. This can often affect scientific productivity and widen the gender gap that exists in this field. As such, this study aimed to understand how full-time faculty members in academic medicine perceive the climate for women academics and identify target areas for interventions supporting gender equity.MethodsThis study adopted an embedded mixed-methods design. Data collection was done from November 2022 to January 2023, at the National University of Singapore (NUS) School of Medicine. The participants comprised academic clinicians and non-clinician faculty members who were either currently employed full-time at the NUS School of Medicine or had recently resigned. They completed an adapted version of the Culture Conducive to Women’s Academic Success (CCWAS) questionnaire, which assessed their perceptions of the faculty climate across four domains: equal access, work-life balance, freedom from gender bias, and supportive leadership. Quantitative findings were complemented by qualitative data gathered through open-ended survey questions. ANOVA and t-test were used to analyse the quantitative data, and content analysis was used to analyse the qualitative data.ResultsCompared to female participants (M = 140.0, SD = 57.9), male participants perceived greater gender equity (M = 158.7, SD = 62.9). Personal experiences of gender inequity were described by some female participants. Participants also raised various suggestions to improve work-life balance for both male and female faculty members, mitigate structural gender bias and increasing support for women faculty.ConclusionsFemale faculty members tend to have a less favourable view of the working climate for women in academic medicine, compared to their male counterparts. This might be attributed to differences in awareness of gender inequity and understanding of gender bias. Findings of this study provided further insight into the perceptions of faculty members regarding workplace gender equity and interventions that can be implemented to support them in their career.Trial registrationEthical approval was obtained from the National University of Singapore Institutional Review Board (NUS-IRB-2022-325).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06470-3.Keywords: Women, Equity, Academic, Medicine, Gender equality

Background

The gender gap existing in the field of academic medicine is not a novel concern, having been extensively discussed in past research [1–4]. This lack of gender equity has been viewed as an obstacle hindering women’s success in academic medicine over the past several decades, affecting their progression in academic ranks, leadership opportunities, and other career advancements [5]. Specifically, women tend to be paid lower, granted less funding, and promoted more slowly compared to their male counterparts [3]. A closer look at the gender distribution within medicine faculties in various schools showed that despite a drastic increase in women’s enrolment into medical school during the late 1900s, it was not followed by a commensurate increase in women faculty members [6]. The journey of women in academia may also be plagued by workplace hostility, microaggressions, and insufficient institutional support [7]. They are evaluated more harshly and are more likely to experience sexual harassment [7, 8]. Additionally, they are often pressured to take on service roles like committee work, which are undervalued but demand much time and effort [7].

Besides overt discrimination and negative treatment of women, some have attributed the gender gap in academic medicine to unconscious gender bias [5]. Burke and colleagues [4] found that anonymising training program applications for junior doctors improved gender balance. A study conducted by Salles et al. [9] discovered that healthcare professionals tend to have implicit gender bias, where they associate women with family and men with careers. Such automatic associations contribute to the gender disparity present in leadership roles in academic medicine. The lack of role models in the field thus reduces the attractiveness of an academic career to females. This is consistent with the findings of González-Pérez, Mateos de Cabo, and Sáinz [10] who investigated the impact of introducing female role models in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) careers on female students’ beliefs. Their study demonstrated that exposure to these role models was significantly associated with increased aspirations among students to pursue STEM fields. Within the Asian context, cultural beliefs regarding the role of women as homemakers, submission of women to men and the inappropriateness of women taking on leadership positions affect women’s pursuit of careers in academia [11, 12]. The time commitment demanded by the academic profession also resulted in structural limitations for women, who are often expected to take on household responsibilities. Some of these gender expectations and stereotypes may be internalized, and cause females to adjust their career goals and aspirations accordingly [11].

All these factors contribute to a poor work climate that deters and hinders women from achieving higher academic ranks within the medicine faculty. Despite being sufficiently capable and qualified, the self-efficacy of women is lowered when they do not observe other women holding higher positions in their careers [13]. The gender bias present in academia is thus an obstacle to scientific productivity [14]. According to Llorens and colleagues [14], key structural and cultural changes are needed to tackle this issue properly. To implement such changes, it is important to further understand how individuals in academic medicine perceive the climate for women’s academics and to identify target areas for interventions supporting greater gender equity.

Methods

Study objectives

This study aimed to explore how full-time academic faculty at the National University of Singapore (NUS) School of Medicine perceive the climate for women academics. It also aimed to compare the perceptions and experiences of gender equity among faculty, based on characteristics such as gender, faculty status, parental status, and years in practice. According to QS rankings, NUS is recognized as one of the top universities globally [15]. It fosters a distinctive academic environment characterized by rigorous research, a strong global orientation, and a focus on experiential, cross-disciplinary education [16]. Like any world-class institution, NUS may pose a competitive environment, that may present unique challenges for female academics who must navigate demanding professional expectations while balancing family and career commitments [17]. This makes NUS an ideal setting for exploring the experiences and needs of women in competitive academic environments, especially given the university’s leadership’s interest in investing in resources to better support its academics.

Study design

The study adopted an embedded mixed-methods design [18], where the primary focus was to collect data via a self-report questionnaire to assess faculty climate for women academics across four domains: equal access, work-life balance, freedom from gender bias, and supportive leadership. Quantitative findings were complemented by qualitative data gathered through open-ended survey questions.

Eligibility criteria

Clinicians and non-clinician faculty with academic appointments who were currently working full-time or had recently resigned from the NUS School of Medicine were included in this study. To participate in the study, faculty members had to be above 21 years old. Faculty members who were working part-time, had adjunct appointments or other types of academic appointments were excluded from this study.

Outcome measures

An adapted English version of the Culture Conducive to Women’s Academic Success (CCWAS) questionnaire [19, 20] was administered to participants meeting the eligibility criteria (see Additional File 1). The CCWAS includes 45 items across four domains: (1) equal access, (2) support for work-life balance, (3) freedom from gender bias, (4) supportive leadership. Domain 1 measured the extent to which female faculty have equal access to resources contributing to career progression, compared to men. Domain 2 looked at how much female faculty are supported to balance their work and family responsibilities, so as to attain personal and professional success. Domain 3 assessed the extent to which female faculty were comfortable voicing out their concerns regarding gender biases. Finally, Domain 4 examined the level of support that the department leaders provide to contribute to the careers of female faculty. The questionnaire was piloted in April 2022 among 10 academics (5 men and 5 women) holding appointments at other local medical schools. These individuals did not participate in the subsequent survey. The instruments were revised based on the pilot feedback. Examples include increasing the weekly working hours from 44 to 60 h to align with the local norm and incorporating items that guide funding or grant applications. This was to reflect local practice and include components of academic medicine that were not examined in the original questionnaire.

The final questionnaire consisted of 55 items across the four domains, answered on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The scores ranged from 19 to 95 for Domain 1, 11 to 55 for Domain 2, 3 to 15 for Domain 3, and 12 to 60 for Domain 4. The Cronbach alpha was 0.969, showing high internal consistency. Higher scores on the CCWAS indicate the working culture is more conducive to women’s academic career development. Two open-ended questions were also included, for participants to suggest interventions that could improve gender equity of the faculty and provide additional comments on gender equity. These two questions made up the qualitative component of this study. Demographic data of the participants were also collected.

Data collection

E-mail invitations with electronic links were sent to eligible participants via SurveyMonkey from July to November 2022. Paper-based surveys were sent to non-responders via the school’s department meetings and collected from December 2022 to January 2023. The surveys were provided in English language only. A study coordinator randomly assigned a unique identifier to each participant, but this information was not shared with the study team, ensuring anonymity. Participants would use these identifiers to access the online data collection platform or enter the identifiers on the paper survey form before completing the survey questions. A random sample of 10% of all manually entered surveys was reviewed for data input accuracy.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 24 (SPSS-24) was used to analyse the quantitative data. Descriptive statistics were presented as n (%) for categorical variables. The CCWAS total scores and respective domain scores were computed. Domain means were compared across demographic characteristics such as gender, age, years of practice, role, academic appointment and parental status using ANOVA. Results were significant if p-value was less than 0.05, and all tests were two-tailed.

Qualitative analysis

Content analysis was used to analyse the qualitative data [21, 22]. Meaning units were regarded in the form of the participants’ responses to each open-ended question. After familiarising the participants’ responses, two independent coders (C.J.S and S.S) began labelling the responses using codes by identifying possible patterns or similarities [21]. Each response was scrutinized for key themes related to the two open-ended questions asked: opinions on gender equity and suggestions to improve gender equity [22]. Following the initial coding process, the coders engaged in comprehensive discussions to address and resolve any discrepancies or differences in interpretation. When the initial codes were established, they were organised into broader subcategories and categories. The frequency of each sub-category was also tallied to quantify the occurrence of ideas that emerged based on the participants’ responses. To ensure consistency and rigor, multiple meetings and discussions were held among all research team members to reach a consensus on the codes and categories derived. This iterative process not only strengthened the credibility of the analysis but also deepened our understanding of the identified categories and subcategories, providing a more nuanced interpretation of the qualitative data.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the National University of Singapore Institutional Review Board (NUS-IRB-2022-325). Before responding to the questionnaires, all participants were informed about the study details and procedures that participation is strictly voluntary, and that they have the right to withdraw from the study at any point without consequences. Data were coded and anonymised.

A total of 132 out of 628 eligible faculty members responded to the online survey, of which 55 were male, 76 were female and one was transgender. The response rate was 31% among women faculty and 14% among men faculty. The demographic characteristics of participants are presented in Table 1. Most of the participants were still working in the NUS School of Medicine (n = 130, 98.5%) at the time of the survey. A large proportion of the participants were also parents (n = 85, 64.4%).

The overall mean total score on the CCWAS was 148.3 (SD = 60.5). It was found that men had higher total scores (M = 158.7, SD = 62.9) compared to females (M = 140.0, SD = 57.9), although this difference was not significant (p = .081). Scores on the four domains of CCWAS

Scores on each domain of the CCWAS were compared between female and male faculty members. The one response from the transgender participant was excluded from this analysis to protect their privacy, as they could be identified if their gender was known within the faculty. There were no significant gender differences in CCWAS scores for Domain 1 (M = 73.09, 95% CI [64.42, 81.76] vs. M = 63.0, 95% CI [56.49, 69.51]; p = .06) and 2 (M = 36.11, 95% CI [33.63, 38.59] vs. M = 34.05, 95% CI [31.88, 36.23]; p = .218), although the mean scores of male participants were higher than those of female participants. Differences in scores between male and female faculty members were statistically significant for Domain 3 (M = 9.51, 95% CI [8.65, 10.37] vs. M = 8.45, 95% CI [7.83, 9.06]; p = .041) and Domain 4 (M = 50.0, 95% CI [47.1, 52.90] vs. M = 44.46, 95% CI [42.07, 46.85]; p = .004). This indicates that male faculty tended to perceive that women faculty experience more freedom from gender bias and have more supportive leadership than women faculty themselves.

Results comparing domain scores across years of practice found statistical significance for Domain 1 (F(4,120) = 2.499, p = .046) and 3 (F(4,120) = 2.587, p = .040). Those who had been working for a period of between 10 and 15 years scored significantly lower for Domain 1, when compared with those who worked for less than two years (Mean difference=-31.2, 95% CI -31.4 to -3.60, p = .014), between 5 and 10 years (Mean difference=-17.5, 95% CI -28.8 to -2.19, p = .023) and more than 15 years (Mean difference=-15.5, 95% CI -28.8 to -2.19, p = .023). Faculty members who had been working for 5 to 10 years had scored significantly higher on Domain 3, compared with those who had been working for 10 to 15 years (Mean difference = 2.20, 95% CI − 0.239 to 2.35, p = .002). No significant differences were found for the remaining subgroups.

General comments on climate

This theme explored the beliefs and views that faculty members held with regard to gender inequity within the School of Medicine. Two categories were generated from the 25 responses. However, 10 responses were excluded as they were not relevant to the question. Out of the remaining 15 responses, 11 were from female faculty members and four were from male faculty members. Most of these responses came from faculty members who had more than 15 years working in the School of Medicine (n = 6), while none of the responses came from faculty members who had been working for less than 2 years.

General perception of climate for women

Some participants (n = 5) shared sentiments that there was an overall lack of gender equity, making the School of Medicine climate unfavourable for women. They felt that traditional expectations of gender roles made it difficult for women to focus on both work and family responsibilities. It was pointed out that salary differences between male and female faculty members exhibit differential treatment between genders. One participant also commented that it is difficult to change the implicit gender biases that exist in many settings.

Personal experiences

A few participants cited instances where they personally observed or experienced gender inequity (n = 7). All of them had been working in the School of Medicine for more than five years. Two female participants heard unpleasant remarks from male colleagues that indicated biases against their gender and the fact that they had family-commitments. Another male participant shared that his wife had experienced overt discrimination, where she was openly told that she could not hold higher positions due to her gender. On the other hand, two male participants expressed that they had not observed any gender biases against female colleagues. Nonetheless, they recognize that these biases do exist and have heard stories from their female colleagues who have had such experiences. At the same time, some felt that gender equity within the School of Medicine has improved over the years due to stricter implementation of regulations against gender discrimination and harassment.

Suggested improvements

The first theme consisted of four categories outlining the suggestions that participants had put forth to improve gender equity or improve the working climate for women in the School of Medicine. Out of the 132 participants, 33 of them responded to this question, of which 13 were male, 16 were female and one was transgender. Among the respondents, only one participant had been working in the School of Medicine for less than two years. The majority of the suggestions were contributed by those who had been working for five to 10 years (n = 11) or more than 15 years (n = 12).

Changes to working arrangements

Many faculty members (n = 10) suggested implementing flexible working arrangements like allowing for work-from-home or adjustable working hours, to allow them to manage caregiving responsibilities at home better. They also wanted to draw a clearer line between work and personal life, by having less events or meetings taking place after office hours and not be expected to respond to emails after work.

Encourage balanced distribution of care-giving responsibilities

Some of the faculty members (n = 6) felt that gender equity could be improved by increasing men’s involvement at home. This can come in the form of longer paternity leave. It was also suggested that faculty members holding leadership positions advocate for gender equity by emphasising equal distribution of caregiving responsibilities between men and women. Additionally, faculty members with children wished to have access to more parent-friendly facilities like nursing rooms and childcare services.

Recognizing and mitigating structural gender bias

Overall, participants proposed four main ways to manage structural gender bias in the school (n = 12). Firstly, to offer opportunities for faculty members to initiate action by setting up feedback portals to raise concerns regarding gender inequity. Secondly, increase the awareness of gender inequity within the School of Medicine. This could come in the form of promoting open discussion of gender biases and increasing transparency of salary scales. Thirdly, ensuring equal access to resources and opportunities, such as by setting gender equity as a priority target for departments. Lastly, structural gender bias can be mitigated by ensuring fairness in work distribution and performance assessment. Some participants felt that not all the work given to them was considered in their performance assessment. This led to feelings of unfairness among those who have been allocated menial tasks, especially women faculty as they felt that men were less likely to be given these responsibilities.

Support for women faculty

Many participants (n = 10) felt that more support could be provided to women faculty members to reduce the impact of gender inequity and improve their career progression. Mentorship programs and workshops were suggested to address concerns that are unique to women working in the School of Medicine, as well as provide career advice. It was also mentioned that greater visibility of the achievements of women faculty could serve as inspiration for other women faculty members. Furthermore, some felt that interventions could be implemented to offset the disadvantages that women experience due to gender inequity. Examples include adjusting the tenure track for faculty members who are taking maternity leave and providing women-only grants. These suggestions were offered by an equal number of male and female faculty members.

Discussion

This mixed-methods study examined the perceptions of full-time faculty members in the School of Medicine on how favourable the working climate is for female faculty members in Singapore. It was found that male participants perceived greater gender equity compared to female participants. This was consistent with previous research on gender equity in academic settings [19, 23]. Female faculty members had significantly lower scores on domains assessing gender bias and leadership compared to male faculty members.

Based on the CCWAS scores, male faculty members generally had a more positive perception of how favourable their faculty culture is for women, than the female faculty members themselves, although this result was not significant. The qualitative findings of this study corroborate this, as women faculty members cited experiences with gender bias or even discrimination at work, while some of the male faculty members felt that gender equity was attained as they have not observed instances that indicated otherwise. Similar findings were also found in Ruzycki and colleagues’ [19] study. Through interviews with male physicians, Ruzycki et al. [19] found that they had little understanding of the challenges faced by their female counterparts who were also mothers. They were also not fully aware of the support, if any, provided by the department to assist these mothers. Horta & Tang [24] conducted interviews with Chinese academics, revealing that most male academics believed that the system was egalitarian and felt that female academics were treated fairly. While the Chinese female academics did not report much overt discrimination, they experienced subtle gender discrimination and biases that potentially affected their career progression. Our findings highlight differences in the perceptions of male and female academics, the prevalence of male power in academia, and micropolitical practices that (re)produce gender inequality. This could indicate a possible lack of awareness among male faculty members regarding the gender inequity that their female counterparts face [19]. Nonetheless, some of the male respondents in this study acknowledged that gender inequity does exist to some degree within the faculty, as their female colleagues previously confided in them about their experiences. It is important that both male and female faculty are engaged in discussions surrounding workplace gender inequality, so that perspectives can be shared and understood [25]. This would help to develop initiatives that can drive positive change in academic settings.

It was also suggested that implicit gender bias perpetuates the contrast in perceptions between genders, where males are unaware that certain comments might communicate prejudices against females or be offensive to them [23]. A study conducted in Spain and the United Kingdom found that men and women in academia have differing beliefs about the gender gap in their workplace [26]. Therefore, further research might be needed to obtain a more in-depth understanding of the differences in how men and women perceive gender biases. Providing a definition of gender inequity and giving examples to show the ways it can manifest can help future participants provide a more objective view of how favourable their workplace climate is to women.

Those who had worked in academic medicine for over 10 to 15 years scored lower on Domain 1 (Equal access) compared with other faculty members who had been working for a different duration. Faculty members who have worked for less than two years also had the highest mean scores for Domain 1. It is possible that participants who were less experienced with working in academic medicine encountered fewer situations involving unequal access to opportunities and guidance compared to their more experienced colleagues. These participants were also less likely to respond to the open-ended questions when completing the survey. Current literature has found that faculty members with more experience, particularly those in leadership positions, tend to experience more structural biases and are thus more aware of them [27]. Hence, majority of the participants who raised issues regarding structural gender biases and suggested ways to provide support for women faculty were those who had been working in academic medicine for more than 5 years.

Faculty members who were working for about five to 10 years had higher scores on Domain 3 (freedom from gender bias) than the other groups, especially when compared to those who had been working for approximately 10 to 15 years. This meant that they felt more comfortable than other respondents raising concerns regarding gender biases in the workplace. Responses to the open-ended survey also revealed that some faculty members recognised the importance of raising awareness and encourage discussions regarding this topic so as to better respond to instances of gender inequity. However, more information is required to understand how the duration of employment in academic medicine may affect their scores on Domain 3. Zawadzki and colleagues [28] pointed out that delivering information regarding gender inequity can elicit reactance, where individuals feel negative emotions and view the information as a threat to their freedom or to an existing situation they feel comfortable in and resist changing. Additionally, when people do not see gender inequity as a significant problem, they tend to feel that attempts to fix these issues are unnecessary [29]. Hence, Zawadzki and colleagues [28] explained that to reduce reactance, it is important to convey information regarding gender inequity in a way to promote feelings of self-efficacy. In doing so, it leads individuals to believe that they have the knowledge and skills to address the issue of gender inequality. Experiential learning interventions have proven to be an effective approach to raising awareness about gender inequity in the workplace while minimizing resistance. One such example is the Workshop Activity for Gender Equity Simulation, Academic Version (WAGES-Academic) [28] which serves as a brief intervention to educate participants on workplace sexism and explore strategies for reducing gender bias. Future research aimed at reducing gender inequity may benefit from incorporating experiential learning methods to enhance effectiveness. Additionally, qualitative studies using semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions should be conducted to gain deeper insights into factors that encourage female faculty members to voice their concerns about workplace gender inequity.

Beyond addressing gender inequity directly, some faculty members in this study suggested modifications to current working arrangements. If implemented, these changes could serve as interventions that benefit all faculty members. A recent review highlights the potential for such adjustments to positively impact workplace dynamics and overall faculty well-being [30]. The review highlighted that flexible work arrangements are linked to improved physical health and reduced workplace absenteeism. However, studies conducted during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic revealed that the impacts of working from home are more nuanced and complex [31, 32]. This was present in Singapore as well, where work-from-home arrangements had led to changes in work-family balances within households [33]. Despite the possible increase in productivity and reduction in work-related stress, bringing work home can lead to family-work role blurring and compromise familial relationships at home [32]. As such, it is important to consider both benefits and pitfalls when deciding how flexible working arrangements should be to maximise the positive impact on work productivity as well as work-life balance. While it can be difficult to improve distribution of family-related responsibilities on an institutional level, faculty members in this study felt that increasing the duration of paternal leave would help to improve fathers’ involvement at home. This was found to be true by Petts and Knoester [34], where fathers tend to engage more in caretaking of infants when they have longer periods of leave. Providing facilities like nursing rooms and corporate childcare can also help faculty members who are parents to feel more included and supported [35].

It was also suggested that more support be provided to women faculty members in the form of mentorship, career talks and grants. Stadler and colleagues [2] highlighted similar methods to improve gender equity in Singapore, encouraging women faculty to prioritize their career progression through professional development programmes and mentorship. According to Farkas and colleagues [36], mentorship programs designed for women in academic medicine are often well-received. They also promote retention of female faculty members. Having more opportunities to receive more career-related advice from female mentors who can relate to their needs better would thus benefit female faculty members. For this to materialize, the proportion of female leaders in academic medicine would need to increase as well. Therefore, career talks and schemes to level the playing field for women can serve as avenues to help female faculty members to enhance their career progression in the meantime.

Limitations

This study has limitations. As the questionnaire was disseminated to all full-time faculty members in the School of Medicine, non-response bias may be present. Furthermore, less than half of the participants responded to the open-ended questions, and most of the respondents were female. Thus, the views of certain individuals might be over-represented, undermining the generalisability of this study’s results. Future studies should aim to gather more detailed qualitative data through individual interviews or focus group discussions to gain deeper insights into the complexities of the female academic experience in Singapore. Additionally, future research should explore the experiences of male academics to develop a more comprehensive understanding of medical school culture. It was also challenging to interpret results on the CCWAS, due to a lack of criterion-referenced scores. Hence, the findings must be interpreted based on how they compare to other populations, which might not be an optimal gauge of the climate. Additionally, many qualitative responses were excluded from the content analysis as they were too brief or vague to be meaningful. Future studies may consider using face-to-face interviews to reduce non-response bias while improving the depth of qualitative data obtained. This may also help to ensure the collection of more robust data.

Conclusion

This mixed-methods study demonstrated that compared to females, male faculty members had a more positive impression of how favourable their working climate is for fellow female colleagues. Possible reasons for this include a lack of awareness of gender inequity and varying beliefs in what constitutes as gender bias. Interventions were suggested to improve gender equity and distribution of caregiving responsibilities, though existing studies raised various points of consideration when developing such programs. Overall, this study has provided an insight into the perceptions of working climate for Singaporean women in the context of academic medicine, and the areas in which these individuals hope to receive further support in to feel that gender equity is achieved.
