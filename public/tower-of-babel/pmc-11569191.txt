Evaluation of a compact cone beam CT concept with high image fidelity for point-of-care brain imaging
Authors: Uttam Pyakurel, Ryan Sabounchi, Mohamed Eldib, Farhang Bayat, Hien Phan, Cem Altunbas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCone beam computed tomography (CBCT) has potential advantages for developing portable, cost-effective point-of-care CT systems for intracranial imaging, such as early stroke diagnosis, hemorrhage detection, and intraoperative navigation. However, large volume imaging with flat panel detector based CBCT significantly increases the scattered radiation fluence which reduces its image quality and utility. To address these issues, a compact CBCT concept with enhanced image quality was investigated for intracranial imaging. The new system features a novel antiscatter collimator and data correction method to address the challenges in imaging large volumes with CBCT. A benchtop CBCT prototype was constructed. Imaging studies with anthropomorphic phantoms showed that soft tissue visualization, Hounsfield Unit (HU) accuracy, contrast, and spatial resolution increased significantly with the proposed CBCT concept, and they were comparable to the values measured in the gold standard multidetector-row CT (MDCT) images. Contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) in CBCT images was within 12–31% of the CNR in MDCT images. These findings indicate that a compact CBCT system integrated with effective scatter suppression techniques may have increased utility in the context of brain imaging, and the proposed approach may enable the development of point-of-care CT systems for head imaging based on flat panel detector based CBCT technology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-79874-2.Keywords: Compact CBCT, Point of care CT system, Scatter mitigation, Antiscatter grid, Image quality enhancement in CBCTSubject terms: Brain imaging, Computed tomography

Introduction

Point-of-care CT systems based on multidetector-row CT (MDCT) technology has been use in variety of brain imaging applications1–3. One of the most notable use cases is the use of CT systems mounted in mobile units for early diagnosis and treatment of stroke, where nonenhanced head CT scans are acquired4. A patient diagnosed with ischemic stroke may be eligible for administration of tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) on site, an approach significantly improving outcomes after stroke5–7. Another use case is the nonenhanced CT imaging for triaging patients with possible traumatic brain injury8, which is sensitive for diagnosing conditions that necessitate neurosurgical interventions9. Point-of-care CT scanners have also been used for neurosurgical navigation, such as during electrode placement for Deep Brain Stimulation10,11, postoperative monitoring of patients after neurosurgical procedures, and bedside imaging in intensive care units12,13.

While MDCT-based point-of-care CTs demonstrated the clinical benefits of portable CT imaging, a flat panel detector (FPD) based CBCT system may potentially offer reduced cost, reduced system weight, and improved mobility due to utilization of widely available, large area FPDs in CBCT14,15. Because the field of view can be made large enough to scan whole, or large section of brain, with only one gantry rotation. Another potential advantage of CBCT approach is in perfusion CT scans CT scans, where cranio-caudal field of view in each sequential CT scan is desired to reach 3–5 cm16,17. Current portable CT systems based on MDCT technology have smaller cranio-caudal field of view, in the order of 1–3 cm, which may challenge their utility in perfusion CT imaging2.

In the last two decades, CBCT systems with FPDs have emerged as an important imaging tool in variety of imaging applications18,19, such as dentistry20–22, orthopedics23,24, and image-guided surgery25. CBCT scanners developed for such applications often employ a compact geometry, where source-to-image receptor distance is less than 100 cm, reducing system weight and improving portability.

Even though a compact and portable CBCT system may have potential for point-of-care brain imaging applications, MDCT technology is favored over FPD-based CBCT in soft tissue imaging in general, due to its higher image quality1–3,26. FPD-based CBCT has inferior soft tissue visualization and CT number accuracy. Thus, most CBCT systems are aimed towards bony anatomy imaging applications, such as maxillofacial imaging, implantology, image guidance for pedicle screw placement27–29.

One of the primary reasons behind CBCT’s poor image quality is the increased intensity of scattered radiation. In addition to large X-ray cone angles that increase the scatter intensity, shorter source-to-imager distance in a compact CBCT geometry reduces the air gap between the patient and the detector, increasing the scatter intensity in projections and compromising the image quality30–33.

Numerous hardware-based scatter rejection and correction solutions have been proposed to mitigate the detrimental effects of scatter34. Among these, 2D antiscatter grid (2D-ASG) has emerged as one of the effective solutions to address the scatter problem. 2D-ASGs have been used MDCT systems35–37, but they are a relatively new concept in FPD based CBCT systems. We demonstrated that 2D-ASGs can provide substantially better scatter suppression and improvement in image quality when compared to conventional ASGs and physics model-based scatter correction methods in CBCT systems aimed towards image guided radiation therapy31,38–41.

In this work, the concept of compact and portable CBCT for brain imaging is revisited with an alternative scatter suppression approach. In contrast to prior works on compact brain CBCT imaging14,15, a 2D-ASG based scatter mitigation method was employed in a benchtop compact CBCT prototype under investigation.

In prior works, the role of 2D-ASG was evaluated in the context of CBCT guided radiotherapy and by using CBCT systems with a source to imager distance of 150 cm31,41–44. In the envisioned compact CBCT system for brain imaging, source to imager distance will be shorter to achieve small footprint and portability. Reduced air gap between the patient and the imager alters the distribution and intensity of scattered X-rays incident on the 2D-ASG, when compared to scatter observed in CBCT systems for radiotherapy. Change in scatter fluence characteristics may interfere with correction of residual scatter transmitted through the 2D-ASG. Moreover, experiments and phantoms used in prior studies do not depict the imaging scenarios anticipated in brain imaging with a compact CBCT system.

Hence, we developed a focused 2D-ASG prototype for a CBCT system with short source to imager distance and implemented a previously developed residual scatter correction method. Subsequently, we examined the image quality of the proposed compact head CBCT approach through experiments relevant to brain imaging and benchmarked the image quality with respect to the standard MDCT images.

Materials and methods

Emulation of compact head CBCT on a benchtop imaging system

To study the image quality characteristics of the envisioned head CBCT (Fig. 1), a CBCT system was constructed on a benchtop, which includes a rotating anode X-ray tube (Dunlee DA1094, Philips, Netherlands), a flat panel detector (RF4343 F4, Thales, France) on a 3-axis motion stage. CBCT gantry rotation was emulated with a phantom placed on a rotation stage. Source-to-imager distance (SID) and source-to-object distance (SOD) were 750 mm and 500 mm, respectively. The FPD has 16 bit digitization, active area is 426 × 426 mm2 and the pixel size is 0.148 mm. The effective pixel size at the center of rotation is 0.098 mm. The FPD has a Cesium Iodide (CsI) scintillator, with a thickness of 0.6 mm. The tube was powered by an EPS 80RF high voltage generator (EMD Technologies, Canada).

(a) Photo of the compact head CBCT benchtop setup. (b, c) 2D-ASG prototype. It is 40 cm long, has a grid pitch of 1.6 mm, a height of 18 mm, and a wall thickness of 0.1 mm.

The 2D-ASG prototype was developed, additively manufactured from tungsten by using Direct Metal Laser Sintering process. The 2D-ASG has focused septa which is designed for the CBCT system with 75 cm SID. The 2D-ASG has a 2D array of focused square holes separated by 0.1 mm thick septa, grid pitch of 1.6 mm, and a grid ratio of 12. The 2D-ASG prototype (Fig. 1c) is 400 mm long and 27 mm wide. The weight of the 2D-ASG prototype was 516 g.

To estimate and correct the residual scatter, the Grid-Based Signal Intensity Modulation and Scatter Sampling (GSS) method was employed39,42,45. While the GSS method was described in prior publications, it is summarized below for completeness. The GSS method utilizes the 2D-ASG itself as a scatter measurement device. The radiopaque footprint of 2D-ASG’s septa reduces the signal in detector pixels residing underneath the septa, i.e., wall shadows, whereas as pixels residing in 2D-ASG’s grid holes, introducing a periodic variation in signal intensity. When scatter is present, the relative amplitude of signal variation between wall shadows and adjacent grid holes changes as a function of residual scatter intensity. This scatter intensity dependent signal variation was used to estimate the scatter intensity in projections.

Scatter reduction performance of the 2D-ASG was validated before performing CBCT imaging studies. To validate the scatter estimation from the GSS method, scatter transmission by the 2D-ASG was also measured using the standard beam stop method31. An acrylic cylinder with a diameter of 20 cm and height of 20 cm was placed on the rotary stage, and a 6 mm thick tungsten bar was placed horizontally between the phantom and the X-ray tube as the beam stop. Two sets of projections were acquired, one with and the other without the tungsten bar, using the same X-ray technique parameters as the CBCT scans. The scatter signal, S, was measured in square regions of interest (ROIs) at the Tungsten bar’s shadow in the projections, whereas total signal consisting of scatter and primary, T, was measured in projections acquired without the tungsten bar. CBCT imaging experiments

CBCT scans were acquired with three different commercially available phantoms: Catphan 504 (Phantom Laboratory NY, USA), Angiographic CT Head Phantom (Kyoto Kagaku Co. Ltd., Japan), and a printed phantom generated from a clinical CT scan of a patient with arteriovenous malformation (AVM) (PhantomX, Germany), referred to as AVM phantom. All the relevant guidelines and regulations regarding the use of patient data during the manufacturing of the phantom were handled by the phantom manufacturer. Each CBCT scan was acquired at 120 kV and at 115 mAs. The FPD was operated at 2 × 2 binning mode, yielding a pixel size of 0.296 mm in projections. The half-value layer (HVL) of the beam was 7.08 mm of Al. The X-ray field of view (FOV) was 16 cm in the axial direction and 21 cm in the transverse direction at the isocenter. However, only the axial CBCT slices from the central 2 cm were analyzed, as the 2D-ASG covered the central 2 cm section of the imager to reduce prototyping costs. Images were reconstructed using the filtered back projection method46. Two sets of CBCT images were acquired using identical image acquisition parameters, one without 2D-ASG and the other with the 2D-ASG Images acquired with the 2D-ASG CBCT were further corrected for residual scatter by using the GSS method. Acquired projections were further binned by using 7 × 3 binning along the axis of rotation and transverse direction, respectively. Dead pixels were corrected and the standard flat field correction was applied to suppress pixel-to-pixel gain variations and grid wall shadows41,47. Images were reconstructed by using the Filtered Backprojection method46,48. The CBCT images without scatter mitigation are referred to as “CBCT before correction”, and the images with 2D-ASG and GSS based scatter mitigation are called “CBCT after correction” in the rest of the text.

The dose was measured using the CTDI head phantom per ACR guidelines49. The CTDIvol was 30 mGy per CBCT scan. To benchmark image quality, same phantoms were scanned using a Siemens Definition AS Open 20 MDCT scanner at 120 kV. The collimation width of the MDCT scanner is 16 × 1.2 mm. The CTDIvol of MDCT scans was measured the same way as for CBCT and was also 30 mGy. Both CBCT and MDCT scans were reconstructed at a voxel size of 0.48 × 0.48 × 2 mm³. The MDCT scanner reconstruction filter was Head-Regular-Sharpness Level 38 (Hf38s) for the head CT protocol, while the CBCT images were reconstructed by using the Hamming filter with relative cutoff frequency at 1.

Evaluation of image quality

For image quality comparisons, CBCT and MDCT image sets of each phantom were first coregistered using a rigid registration method. Besides qualitative evaluations, contrast, noise, and contrast to noise ratio (CNR) were compared among CBCT and MDCT images. To compare shading artifacts, HU nonuniformity was calculated in the uniform tissue section of the phantoms.

Except for water, HU values for a given tissue type may not be identical in CBCT and MDCT images, because of the differences in X-ray spectra between the two systems. However, HU values are expected to be comparable. Therefore, HU values for brain parenchyma, craniospinal fluid, iodine containing vessels, and different material inserts in Catphan were compared in CBCT and MDCT images.

Blurring and HU profiles at anatomical structure boundaries were also evaluated. Ideally, appearance of structure boundaries is desired to be comparable in high fidelity CBCT and MDCT images. Finally, structure similarity of CBCT and MDCT images were evaluated by using the structural similarity index measure (SSIM).

Discussion

This work was focused on the image quality investigations of a compact CBCT concept aimed primarily towards brain imaging. While flat panel detector-based compact CBCT systems dedicated to head imaging have been previously investigated15, the unique aspect of our work is the development and implementation of 2D-ASG based scatter mitigation methods in compact head CBCT.

As demonstrated in our experiments, scatter rejection by the 2D-ASG and further correction of residual contaminant signal with the GSS method plays an important role in the improvement of image quality in compact head CBCT. In prior studies, conventional, radiographic 1D ASGs have also been investigated for head CBCT imaging. These studies have shown that the radiographic ASGs provided a small improvement in CNR or even reduced CNR, concluding that the use of radiographic 1D ASGs may not provide significant image quality improvement in a compact CBCT geometry33,50–52. This challenge with radiographic ASGs is likely due to their relatively low primary transmission and relatively low scatter rejection performance. A radiographic ASG transmits about 70% of the primary X-rays to the image receptor, leading to an increase in noise. Moreover, radiographic ASGs stop 75–80% of scattered X-rays, 20–25% of scatter is still transmitted to the image receptor53. Whereas, in this work, an average primary transmission fraction of 84% and scatter transmission fraction of 6% have been achieved when using a 2D-ASG in compact head CBCT geometry. The GSS method further eliminates the residual scatter. As a combined result, substantial improvement in CNR, HU accuracy, and image artifacts were observed with the proposed approach. Specifically, CNR was improved by more than a factor of 2 on average.

Comparisons indicate that the compact head CBCT system’s image quality approaches the image quality of MDCT in head phantom images. While CNR was less in the CBCT images, these differences between CBCT and MDCT can be further reduced by further improvement of image denoising algorithms in the CBCT system. Currently, a dedicated method to denoise images has not been implemented in the compact CBCT system. Even though effort was put into matching the spatial resolution properties of the CBCT and MDCT images, CBCT images had slightly higher spatial resolution as indicated by the bar pattern phantom images. Thus, the spatial resolution of CBCT images can be reduced to better match the spatial resolution of MDCT, which may also improve CNR in CBCT images.

Over the years, numerous hardware based scatter suppression methods have been developed, such as antiscatter grids (ASG)54,55, bow tie filters that reduce scatter to primary ratio30,56, beam blocker arrays to estimate the scatter57,58, and optimization of air gap between the imaged object and the detector59. However, these methods do not sufficiently suppress scatter, or in the case of beam blocker arrays, they can be challenging to implement in clinical CBCT systems60. Another solution is to use physics-based scatter estimation and correction methods such as scatter kernel superposition34,61,62. In our preliminary evaluations, we observed that such methods may over- or underestimate the scatter43,63.

There are also recent advances in using Deep Learning methods to estimate and correct scatter64–66. Deep Learning based methods can be adapted more easily to the proposed compact CBCT system, as patient-to-patient variations in head anatomy are considered small, which may ease the implementation of Deep Learning based scatter correction. However, the performance of Deep Learning methods varies based on the differences between the training data set and the actual imaging conditions due to factors such as the position of head within the field of view, variations in image/projection noise, tubes and masks within the field of view, and other foreign objects in the brain (such as shunts to drain cerebrospinal fluid). Such factors may affect the accuracy of Deep scatter estimation. Although relatively small, patient-to-patient variations in head size should also be evaluated in the context of Deep Learning based scatter estimation accuracy. Another point to consider is the noise. 2D-ASG reduces the noise associated with scatter. If scatter is not reduced by an antiscatter grid, but solely corrected by a scatter correction method, the image noise would increase further after scatter correction. Thus, the effect of noise increase on the image quality needs to be investigated. In future studies, a comparison of Deep Learning based scatter correction and 2D-ASG based approach can be performed under identical conditions and in the context of compact head CBCT to assess the benefits of both approaches. Besides Depp Learning bases methods, there are other computational methods based on Monte Carlo-based scatter estimation; however, they come with longer computation times67–70.

The use of 2D-ASG in a CBCT system requires robust suppression of grid wall shadows in projections to avoid grid-induced artifacts in CBCT images. A conventional flat field, or gain correction, method can potentially correct the X-ray fluence variations caused by grid wall shadows in projections. However, such methods work suboptimally due to the presence of scatter in CBCT projections. Flat field calibration projections are typically acquired without an object in the X-ray beam, where additive scatter signal is absent. Therefore, presence of additive scatter signal during CBCT projections introduces an additive bias in grid wall shadows and cannot be fully corrected by flat field correction, causing ring artifacts in CBCT images42,45. Therefore, the GSS method for residual scatter correction is instrumental in correction of the grid wall shadows. First, the residual scatter in projections is corrected by the GSS method and subsequently, flat field correction is applied to projections. This approach successfully suppresses the grid wall shadows in projections and ring artifacts in CBCT images42,45. Moreover, pixel binning of projections in the preprocessing of raw data acts as a low pass filter and suppresses remaining grid induced artifacts.

Accurate tissue density representation is a major challenge of CBCT for brain imaging. Hemorrhages exhibit a relatively small HU difference compared to brain tissue71,72. Likewise, iodine contrast HU values in contrast-enhanced CT images are expected to be comparable in MDCT and CBCT scans 73,74. For example, iodine uptake variations in perfusion CT scans can help to visualize blood flow maps and diagnose infarcts. Accurate tissue and density representation is also essential for using Artificial Intelligence (AI) based image analysis and diagnostic tools, which are developed and trained using MDCT images. While this work did not investigate the performance of these tools, one would expect that comparable image quality between MDCT and CBCT images may potentially allow or ease the implementation of such AI-based tools for images acquired with point-of-care CBCT systems. In this work, HU values measured in brain parenchyma, craniospinal fluid, and iodine using the proposed CBCT approach were found to be close to those obtained from MDCT images. Whereas HU value for higher atomic number or density materials, such as iodine, was higher in CBCT images. While the exact reason for such differences requires further investigation, they can be in part due to differences in X-ray spectra between the MDCT and CBCT systems. Another reason could be scatter under- or overestimation in the MDCT and CBCT systems.

Several artifacts were observed in compact CBCT images. First, the circular artifact in the center of the images was caused by the junction of two grid submodules in the 2D-ASG prototype. This can be addressed by fabricating a monolithic 2D-ASG. The other artifact was the shading artifacts in the phantom-air boundary. This is caused by a combination of the narrow FOV of the X-ray tube collimator and the image lag. In-plane collimation width was only 21 cm at the isocenter due to the design limits of the tube collimator, which caused the phantom-air boundary to be in the beam penumbra in a subset of projections in a CBCT scan, causing artifacts. These artifacts can be addressed by using a collimator with a larger FOV and implementing image lag correction in future works.

Primary transmission of the 2D-ASG was measured without a protective cover on the grid. A thin carbon fiber plate on the 2D-ASG can protect the grid but will also reduce the primary transmission. Based on our preliminary experiments, a 1.2 mm thick carbon-fiber plate would reduce primary transmission by less than 4%. 2D-ASG’s weight is another factor to consider in the implementation of large area 2D-ASGs. We expect that the weight of a large 2D-ASG can be in the order of 3–4 kg. We envision that the proposed compact CBCT concept to have a ring gantry, which may reduce the gantry flex issues arising from the increased weight of the grid-detector assembly.

Besides scatter, there are other factors that degrade the image quality of flat panel detector based CBCT, when compared to MDCT systems. The digitization of flat panel detectors is typically 16 bits or less. Whereas MDCT detectors employ 20 bits or more for digitization, which reduces discretization errors. Scintillators in flat panel detectors have about 60% X-ray absorption efficiency, which increases quantum noise. Scintillators in MDCT detectors have more than 90% X-ray absorption efficiency. In addition, amorphous flat panel detectors have higher electronic noise and more image lag which contribute to image quality degradation in CBCT images75–78. Besides FPD, there are other preprocessing steps that impact image quality, such as beam hardening correction, image lag correction, correction of artifacts due to off-focal radiation, and post-reconstruction ring artifact correction. Such correction methods were not implemented in this current work, as this work was focused on the suppression of scatter. Such data correction methods will be implemented in the future.

There are several limitations of this work. First, the 2D-ASG width was 27 mm wide in the cranio-caudal direction to reduce prototyping challenges and cost. Larger 2D-ASGs that cover the full active area of the image sensor should be fabricated to further investigate the clinical utility of the proposed approach. Second, this work demonstrates the relative improvement in the compact CBCT’s image quality by using static phantoms. The diagnostic accuracy of the proposed system remains to be investigated in more realistic imaging scenarios such as the diagnosis of hemorrhage with the presence of potential head motion during CBCT scans, or dynamic imaging of iodine contrast in perfusion CT scans.
