Oxidative Stress–Induced Age-Related Bullous Keratopathy in Corneal Endothelial Cells With Lipid Droplets and Vacuoles
Authors: Hiromi Onouchi, Hiroyuki Yamasaki, Rikuto Toda, Toshiyasu Taniguchi, Takahiro Suzuki, Yasuyuki Suzuki, Naoaki Ishii, Takamasa Ishii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWe previously demonstrated that intracellular oxidative stress reduces corneal endothelial cells in Tet-mev-1 mice that genetically overproduce mitochondrial superoxide anions. This study aimed to elucidate the physiological processes underlying oxidative stress–induced corneal endothelial cell loss.MethodsPrimary cultures of mouse corneal endothelial cells were established. Intracellular lipid droplets were detected using Lipi-Green, and autophagosomes in live cells were visualized using DAPGreen. Autophagy was assessed by immunostaining and Western blot analysis using microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3). Furthermore, autophagic flux was evaluated by LC3 and p62/SQSTM1 levels with bafilomycin A1. DNA damage was assessed by γH2AX pSer139 immunostaining.ResultsCorneal endothelial cells formed a paving stone–like monolayer and expressed endothelial markers. Tet-mev-1 cells, which were mitochondrial oxidative stress–overproduced mouse cells, exhibited senescent-like cells with abnormal lipid droplet accumulation, vacuolation, and DNA damage compared with wild-type cells. Quantitative analyses confirmed increased lipid droplets, autophagosomes, DNA damage, and vacuolated cells. In young Tet-mev-1 cells, autophagy-related responses were relatively preserved under oxidative stress conditions. In contrast, aged Tet-mev-1 cells showed attenuated responses to lysosomal inhibition, suggesting a decline in autophagic degradation capacity with premature aging by oxidative stress.ConclusionsIntracellular oxidative stress is associated with enhanced autophagic activity in young corneal endothelial cells, whereas progressive endothelial cell loss with aging is accompanied by alterations in autophagy-related processes. This shift contributes to the accumulation of lipid droplets, vacuolations, and DNA damage. These findings suggest that maintenance of lipid homeostasis through autophagy-related pathways may play an important role in delaying age-related corneal endothelial dysfunction.

The majority of endogenous oxidative stress is caused by mitochondrial electron transfer system–generated superoxide (O2·−).1–3 Although O2·− generated from cells contributes to homeostasis—including immune response mechanisms, cell proliferation, and differentiation—excessive production as a by-product of energy metabolism is converted into more toxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) that can cause malignant tumors, neurodegenerative diseases, and lifestyle-related diseases.4–8

The eye is exposed to ultraviolet light from the cornea to the retina, making it one of the organs most susceptible to external oxidative stress. Recently, oxidative stress has been increasingly implicated in blindness-causing diseases, such as glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, and age-related macular degeneration.2,9–11

The cornea is located on the ocular surface and is easily affected by external environments. Prolonged exposure to ultraviolet rays and atmospheric oxygen makes it more susceptible to oxidative stress than other tissues.12 Although dry eye and pterygium develop in association with oxidative stress,13–17 the relationship between oxidative stress and corneal endothelial cell loss, as well as the detailed molecular mechanisms, remains unclear.

Corneal endothelial cells do not regenerate after injury; instead, the damaged area is compensated for by the enlargement of surrounding endothelial cells.18 These cells are essential for maintaining corneal transparency, and when cell density falls below a critical threshold, corneal edema occurs, resulting in bullous keratopathy.19 Endogenous oxidative stress, resulting from excessive mitochondrial oxidative stress generation, induces corneal endothelial cell loss and dry eye in Tet-mev-1 mice.20 Mitochondrial oxidative stress has been previously demonstrated in Tet-mev-1 mice, including increased superoxide (O2·−) production and carbonylated protein accumulation by the complex II–ubiquinone electron transport defect, supporting the relevance of this model.20 In this study, we aimed to elucidate the physiological mechanisms underlying endogenous oxidative stress–induced corneal endothelial cell loss under a primary culture system in vitro using Tet-mev-1 mouse cornea.

Methods

Mice

Tet-mev-1 transgenic mice on a C57BL/6 background were generated as previously described.21 Genotyping was performed using polymerase chain reaction and Southern blotting.21 All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The mice were continuously exposed to doxycycline (Dox; 0.4 mg/mL with 0.05 mg/mL saccharine in drinking water; Sigma-Aldrich Japan, Tokyo, Japan) throughout prenatal and postnatal development.21

Cell Culture

The enucleated eyes were sectioned at the limbus using specialized scissors and forceps under a biomicroscope (SPZ-50FTM; Carton Optical Industries, Tokyo, Japan) to separate the corneas. The corneas were dissected using four radial cuts, mounted, and placed in a collagen type I–coated 12-well plate (Costar; Corning, Corning, NY, USA) with the endothelial side down. They were soaked in OptiMEM-I (#51985; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Canoga Park, CA, USA) or Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (08460-95; NACALAI TESQUE, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (#10082139; Thermo Fisher Scientific), gentamicin 50 µg/mL (#15710072; Thermo Fisher Scientific), amino acids, epidermal growth factor (5 ng/mL) (#01–107; Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), recombinant fibroblast growth factor (0.2 ng/mL; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan), nerve growth factor (5 ng/mL; CEDARLANE Laboratories, Burlington, Ontario, Canada), recombinant mouse transforming growth factor β (0.5 ng/mL; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), Y-27632 (5 ng/mL; Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA), insulin (50 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), Doxycycline Hyclate (1 µg/mL; Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan), 0.08% chondroitin sulfate (#G6737; Sigma-Aldrich), and antibiotic/antimycotic solution diluted 1:100 (#15240062; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The medium was adjusted to prevent tissue floating. The culture medium was gradually added daily while confirming stable tissue adhesion to the plate.

Western Blot Analysis and Autophagic Flux Assay

For autophagic flux analysis, cultured corneal endothelial cells were treated with bafilomycin A1 (100 nM) for 24 hours prior to sample collection. After treatment, the cells were washed twice with 0.15 M NaCl and incubated on ice in 10% trichloroacetic acid for 30 minutes. Fixed cells were collected after peeling with Cell Lifter (Corning, Corning, NY, USA), centrifuged, and cleared by centrifugation (13,000 rpm, 3 min). The supernatants used as cell lysates (containing 60 µg of protein) were solubilized by boiling after adding 2× Ez Apply (AE-1430; ATTO, Tokyo, Japan) and directly subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride ClearBlot membrane (ATTO) using a semi-dry blotting machine WSE-4125 (ATTO). To block nonspecific protein binding, membranes were treated for 1 hour at 20°C to 25°C with 0.1% Tween 20 and 5% nonfat dried milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) for analyzing microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) (1:500–1:2000; 14600-1-AP; Proteintech Group, IL, USA), p62/SQSTM1 (1:1000; 18420-1-AP; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), and anti–β actin (1:5000–1:10,000; GTX109639; GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA). The membranes were treated with antibodies against LC3 (detecting both LC3-I and LC3-II), p62/SQSTM1, or anti–β actin antibodies in TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 and 5% nonfat dried milk at room temperature (RT) for 1 hour. Actin was used as an internal control protein for loading normalization of the quantification analysis. The membranes were washed with antibody dilution buffer for 30 minutes. Subsequently, they were treated with the ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection System (Amersham Biosciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA) after treatment with secondary antibodies of anti–rabbit–IgG–horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (#7074P2; Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA) or SC-2357; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), anti–mouse-IgG-HRP (#7076S; Cell Signaling Technology) in TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 hour at RT. Chemiluminescent signals were visualized using an ImageQuant LAS 500 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Quantitative densitometric analysis of LC3-I, LC3-II, and p62 was performed using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

Immunofluorescence Staining

The cells were fixed for 20 minutes at RT with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized at RT for 10 minutes using 0.1% Triton X-100. Between each step, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline. Subsequently, 3% bovine serum albumin was used to block the nonspecific adsorption of antibodies for 30 minutes, followed by incubation with β-catenin (GTX101435; GeneTex), LC3 (14600-1-AP; Proteintech Group), N-cadherin (66219-1-Ig; Proteintech Group), ZO-1 (ab216880; Abcam Limited, Cambridge, UK), Ki-67 (sc-23900; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) (1:100 dilution), and phosphorylated histone H2AX (γH2AX pSer139) (NB100-384; Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA) (1:500 dilution) for 8 hours at 4°C. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488–coupled secondary antibodies (1:1000 dilution) for 1 hour at RT in the dark. Finally, images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope (NIS-Elements; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Autophagy Detection

A 12-well cell culture plate was used as described previously. After removing the culture medium, the cells were washed once with fresh culture medium. Subsequently, 0.1 to 0.5 µmol/L DAPGreen working solution (Fujifilm, Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) and NucBlue were added and incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes. After incubation, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed twice with the culture medium. The medium was replaced with a fresh culture medium. After 1 to 2 hours, images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (NIS-Elements; Nikon).

Quantitative Image Analysis

Quantitative analysis of fluorescence images was performed using ImageJ software. For Lipi-Green analysis, images were converted to 8-bit grayscale and thresholded using a fixed threshold applied consistently across all samples. Lipi-Green–positive puncta were quantified using the “Analyze Particles” function, and the number of puncta per field was calculated. For DAPGreen analysis, the area fraction of DAPGreen-positive signal was measured using threshold-based segmentation, and the percentage of the positive area (% area) was calculated. To evaluate vacuolation, cells exhibiting clear intracellular vacuole–like structures were manually counted, and the proportion of vacuolated cells was calculated as a percentage of total cells per field. To evaluate DNA damage using γH2AX pSer139 immunostaining, γH2AX pSer139 foci were manually counted, and the proportion of DNA-damaged cells, which included over 15 foci of γH2AX pSer139 per cell, was calculated as the percentage of total cells per field. All quantifications were performed using at least three to six independent fields per group.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical measurements of the biochemical assay and Western blot were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc comparisons and a two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Establishment of a Primary Culture System for Mouse Corneal Endothelial Cells

First, we established an in vitro culture system for the mouse corneal endothelial cells. The endothelial cell side of the mouse corneal tissue was attached to a plate and immersed in culture medium, enabling the successful outgrowth of endothelial cells (Fig. 1A). The cells formed a pavement-like monolayer and expressed the cell adhesion molecules ZO-1 (green) and N-cadherin (red), confirming their endothelial nature (Fig. 1B).

Appearance of Extracellular Fatty or Lipid Droplets Around Swollen Corneal Endothelial Cells

During prolonged culture, cell death granules with abnormal lipid accumulation were prominently observed (Fig. 2A, black arrows). Immunostaining with Ki67 (Fig. 2B, white arrows) revealed that the cells in the peripheral area of the tissue were proliferative, whereas the cells in parts of the outer ring were nonproliferative and began to hypertrophy into differentiated endothelial cells. To support these observations, the proportion of vacuolated cells was quantified. The percentage of vacuolated cells was significantly higher in Tet-mev-1 mice compared to wild-type (WT: C57BL/6j) mice (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, corneal endothelial cells derived from Tet-mev-1 mice showed increased senescent-like cells (Figs. 2D) with vacuolation and increased traces of cell death compared with WT cells (Fig. 2D). The aged (18-month-old) mice showed reduced cell growth consistent with age-related functional decline.

Increase in LC3-II–Positive Autophagosome in Young Adults and the Age-Dependent Changes in Autophagic Flux in Tet-mev-1 Corneal Endothelial Cells Compared to Age-Matched Controls

Figure 3A and 3B illustrate immunofluorescence images and Western blot analysis using the LC3 antibody. Young (3-month-old) Tet-mev-1 mice demonstrated higher LC3-II levels than those in age-matched WT mice (P = 0.046). However, in old mice, quantitative analysis demonstrated that WT and Tet-mev-1 mice were comparable. Additionally, Tet-mev-1 mice showed a higher number of cells with abnormal intracellular vacuoles than WT mice (Fig. 3A, red arrows). To further determine whether LC3-II accumulation reflected enhanced autophagic activity or impaired degradation, autophagic flux experiments were performed using bafilomycin A1. Then, the levels of LC3-I, LC3-II, and p62/SQSTM1 were evaluated by Western blot analysis (Fig. 3C). In young WT and Tet-mev-1 corneal endothelial cells under the inhibition of vacuolar-type H+-ATPase (V-ATPase) by bafilomycin A1, LC3-I and p62 levels were significantly increased, which caused corresponding changes in a tendency toward increased LC3-II accumulation. These results suggested that autophagic flux was normally enhanced under oxidative stress in young Tet-mev-1 corneal endothelial cells. In contrast, in aged Tet-mev-1 cells, these responses appeared to be attenuated with moderately decreased levels of p62 and LC3. Although these differences did not reach statistical significance, the observed patterns suggest age-dependent alterations in autophagy-related responses in Tet-mev-1 corneal endothelial cells.

Discussion

In this study, we established an in vitro primary culture system for mouse corneal endothelial cells that enabled the analysis of the effects of oxidative stress and autophagy. In Tet-mev-1 mice with increased oxidative stress, corneal endothelial cells exhibited abnormal lipid droplet accumulation accompanied by enhanced autophagy in young and old mice. During prolonged culture, we observed cell death granules with fatty droplet-like alterations, indicating oxidative stress–induced cellular damage. Lipi-Green is known to preferentially detect neutral lipids; therefore, the observed fluorescent signals likely reflect the accumulation of neutral lipid droplets. However, in the context of oxidative stress, such as in Tet-mev-1 mice, it is possible that these lipid droplets also contain oxidized lipid components. Further biochemical or ultrastructural analyses would be required to determine the precise composition of these lipid structures. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that the accumulation of extracellular fatty droplets, likely resulting from the exocytosis of enlarged, swollen corneal endothelial cells, is associated with disrupted autophagy. Inadequate autophagy accumulates intracellular lipid droplets, such as autophagosomes, and its impairment may contribute to vacuole accumulation and subsequent cellular dysfunction.22,23

Our findings indicate that autophagy—responsible for degrading lipid droplets in healthy cells—plays a crucial role in preventing the accumulation of oxidized cellular components, such as carbonylated proteins and peroxidized products that arise because of oxidative stress.20 In this study, LC3-I, LC3-II, and p62/SQSTM1 were analyzed in combination, together with the response to lysosomal inhibition. These integrated findings suggest that young Tet-mev-1 corneal endothelial cells preserved autophagy-related responses under oxidative stress, whereas aged Tet-mev-1 cells exhibited a tendency toward attenuated responses, indicating age-related alterations in autophagy-related processes. In young Tet-mev-1 mice, autophagy may mitigate the effects of oxidative stress by eliminating lipid droplets. However, this process is compromised by aging, as evidenced by enlarged and swollen cells with the accumulation of lipid droplets and vacuoles in older Tet-mev-1 mice (Fig. 4). Autophagy failure in these older animals likely exacerbates oxidative damage, resulting in corneal endothelial cell loss.20

Figure 5 illustrates our hypothesis regarding the mechanism underlying oxidative stress–induced corneal endothelial cell depletion. In young Tet-mev-1 mice, the overproduction of mitochondrial O2·− causes the accumulation of lipid droplets, including oxidized cellular components, within corneal endothelial cells. These lipid droplets, a characteristic of oxidative stress–induced cellular damage, were accompanied by an increase in autophagic vesicles, such as LC3-II–positive autophagosomes. This suggests that autophagy remains functional in young animals and plays a crucial role in mitigating the effects of oxidative stress. Lipophagy, involving activated autolysosome or peroxisome functions, can be a selective autophagic process that degrades excess lipid droplets, preventing their accumulation and subsequent cellular damage.22,23 In our study, this process may contribute to the normal cell size and density in young Tet-mev-1 mice, as evidenced by enhanced autophagy induction and suppressed cell hypertrophy. This aligns with previous studies demonstrating that autophagy or lipophagy can protect cells from oxidative stress by eliminating toxic lipid accumulation.24–27

Schematic representation of the mechanisms of corneal endothelial cell depletion because of oxidative stress.

Autophagy-related responses in young Tet-mev-1 mice appeared sufficient to handle increased oxidative stress without causing significant damage. The ability of young animals to manage ROS through the autophagic degradation of lipid droplets may explain why endothelial cell survival and function were maintained in these animals despite mitochondrial ROS. Oxidative stress can damage cellular components, such as lipids, proteins, and DNA,4,20,28 and efficient autophagic responses are crucial for protecting cells under such stress.22,29 These findings support the protective role of autophagy-related processes in young corneal endothelial cells exposed to chronic intracellular oxidative stress.

In contrast, old Tet-mev-1 mice exhibited different responses. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed an increased number of autophagosomes in the corneal endothelial cells. However, the ability to autophagically clear lipid droplets appeared to be reduced, resulting in their accumulation and associated cell hypertrophy. These findings suggest age-dependent alterations in autophagy-related activity that may contribute to increased oxidative stress, further damaging corneal endothelial cells and resulting in cell loss. These changes may reflect an age-related decline in autophagic efficiency with age, a process widely implicated in the aging of various tissues and organs.29–31 Our findings align with other studies demonstrating that autophagic dysfunction is a characteristic of aging, causing the accumulation of cellular debris and facilitating age-related degenerative diseases.32–36

The inability to degrade excess lipids in aged animals likely enhances ROS accumulation, resulting in severe cellular damage. Because corneal endothelial cells are nonregenerative, their loss from oxidative damage is irreversible and may lead to conditions, such as bullous keratopathy, that significantly affect vision.37–39

Our study highlights the age-related decline in the regenerative capacity of corneal endothelial cells. In young Tet-mev-1 mice, oxidative stress may be mitigated, at least in part, by autophagy-related processes, enabling endothelial cell survival and maintaining transparency. However, in older Tet-mev-1 mice, reduced capacity to clear lipid droplets and subsequent accumulation of oxidative damage may contribute to decreased endothelial cell numbers. These findings suggest that age-related oxidative stress, compounded by altered autophagy-related processes, may contribute to cellular degeneration and disease progression with abnormally accumulated autophagosomes.40 Although oxidative stress is a natural by-product of mitochondrial activity, the failure of autophagic systems in aging tissues enhances oxidative damage, facilitating the onset of various age-related diseases, including corneal endothelial dysfunction.

Conclusions

Our findings offer valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying corneal endothelial cell loss, specifically the role of autophagy in managing oxidative stress. In young animals, autophagy may help prevent oxidative damage by clearing lipid droplets, whereas in older animals, impaired or dysregulated autophagy with abnormally accumulated autophagosomes and vacuoles may contribute to increased oxidative stress and cell loss. These results indicate that therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing autophagic processes, specifically autolysosomal or peroxisomal functions, can offer potential treatments for age-related corneal endothelial dysfunction and other oxidative stress–related degenerative diseases.
