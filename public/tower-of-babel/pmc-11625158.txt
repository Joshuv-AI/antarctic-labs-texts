Genetic and epigenetic alterations in aging and rejuvenation of human
Authors: Kyunghyuk Park, Min Chul Jeon, Dakyung Lee, Jong-Il Kim, Sun-Wha Im
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
All the information essential for life is encoded within our genome and epigenome, which orchestrates diverse cellular states spatially and temporally. In particular, the epigenome interacts with internal and external stimuli, encoding and preserving cellular experiences, and it serves as the regulatory base of the transcriptome across diverse cell types. The emergence of single-cell transcriptomic and epigenomic data collection has revealed unique omics signatures in diverse tissues, highlighting cellular heterogeneity. Recent research has documented age-related epigenetic changes at the single-cell level, alongside the validation of cellular rejuvenation through partial reprogramming, which involves simultaneous epigenetic modifications. These dynamic shifts, primarily fueled by stem cell plasticity, have catalyzed significant interest and cross-disciplinary research endeavors. This review explores the genomic and epigenomic alterations with aging, elucidating their reciprocal interactions. Additionally, it seeks to discuss the evolving landscape of rejuvenation research, with a particular emphasis on dissecting stem cell behavior through the lens of single-cell analysis. Moreover, it proposes potential research methodologies for future studies.

INTRODUCTION

Aging, the inevitable process of growing older, is intricately linked to an increased susceptibility to a myriad of diseases, including cancers, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, dementia, and more. The field of aging research has gained significant momentum, particularly with recent breakthroughs in understanding the molecular underpinnings of aging and groundbreaking experiments demonstrating rejuvenation in mice (Ji et al., 2023, Yi and Kim, 2020). Central to aging are several key hallmarks, as Lopez-Otin et al. outlined in 2013 and 2023 (Lopez-Otin et al., 2013, 2023). These include genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, impaired macroautophagy, deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, chronic inflammation, and dysbiosis. These hallmarks are not isolated but interconnected, creating a complex web of cause-and-effect relationships.

Maintaining tissue homeostasis relies significantly on the intricate balance stem cells maintain, which possess proliferative capacity and the ability to differentiate into specialized cell types. A thorough investigation into the diverse stem cell populations across various organs is imperative to understand aging comprehensively. This inquiry should elucidate mechanisms governing the equilibrium between quiescence and proliferation within these stem cells and their intricate interactions with the microenvironment. Moreover, an in-depth analysis of the evolving cellular phenotypes of stem cells across different tissues during aging holds promise for shedding light on disease-related pathological processes.

Cellular reprogramming using a set of factors known as OSKM (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc), discovered by Yamanaka, can reset the epigenetic patterns established during development. This process allows for the transformation of differentiated cells into pluripotent stem cells, capable of developing into various cell types (Takahashi and Yamanaka, 2006). Remarkably, this reprogramming also reverses some signs of aging. However, while this approach shows promise in laboratory settings, introducing these reprogramming factors into living organisms has demonstrated adverse effects in mice. It can lead to the formation of tumors called teratomas and even death (Abad et al., 2013, Ohnishi et al., 2014). Alternatively, partial reprogramming, achieved through transient expression of these factors, has shown potential benefits. This partial reprogramming can rejuvenate cells' physiological functions while preserving their original identity (Sarkar et al., 2020). Recent studies have demonstrated that partial reprogramming exhibited rejuvenating effects in various tissues such as the kidney, skin, pancreas, liver, spleen, and blood in mice at the organismal level. The rejuvenation observed entailed a reversal of the epigenetic clock, a mathematical model estimating cellular and tissue age by analyzing changes in methylation values at specific CpG sites in the genome. Furthermore, a significant decrease was noted in the expression of genes associated with inflammation, senescence, stress response, and related metabolomic changes. These findings underscore the close relationship between epigenetic changes and the aging process (Artegiani et al., 2020, Browder et al., 2022, Chondronasiou et al., 2022, Yang et al., 2023a).

Recent advancements in single-cell joint multimodal omics, capable of concurrently capturing transcriptomic and epigenomic information, have progressed rapidly (Baysoy et al., 2023, Park et al., 2022, Vandereyken et al., 2023). This novel approach provides a comprehensive understanding of gene regulation in accordance with the diverse cellular states and types found within tissues. Analysis of single-cell transcriptomes and epigenomes across different age groups has unveiled age-associated modifications in cellular phenotypes and provided insights into age-related regulatory changes across various tissues. Additionally, spatial omics technologies have emerged, facilitating the investigation of the stem cell niche environment (Baysoy et al., 2023, Vandereyken et al., 2023). Integrating single-cell multi-omic data with spatial information across different age groups promises to elucidate tissue-level age-related changes at unprecedented resolution. However, further data collection across a spectrum of tissues and diseases is imperative to understand these dynamics comprehensively.

Successful rejuvenation experiments in model organisms, such as mice, hint at the potential for rejuvenation in human cells or tissues. Human two-dimensional cell lines and organoids derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) or adult stem cells stand out as promising options for studying human aging (Kim et al., 2020, Lancaster and Knoblich, 2014). Organoids, in particular, are gaining traction as a preferred model system for investigating aging and rejuvenation due to their amenability to genetic manipulation, exposure to experimental compounds, and capacity to differentiate into various cell types.

This review will describe the genetic and epigenetic aging-related alterations and their interplay. Specifically, we will explore findings from single-cell experiments centered on stem cells, pivotal for tissue balance, alongside recent breakthroughs in rejuvenation research. Lastly, we will examine novel experimental techniques facilitating more profound investigations into aging and rejuvenation.

A typical age-related mutation is the transformation of cytosine into thymine, specifically when 5-methylcytosine at a CpG site undergoes deamination (Sassa et al., 2016). The prevalence of CpG sites varies, leading to a higher mutation rate in coding regions than in noncoding regions (Subramanian and Kumar, 2003). Age-related mutations are not only the result of replication errors by DNA polymerases but also occur due to inaccuracies in the nonhomologous end-joining (NHEJ) process, which repairs DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs). While base substitutions or small insertions and deletions (indels) are more frequent, copy number alterations and structural variations, which include large indels or rearrangements, also become more familiar with age (Jaiswal and Ebert, 2019).

Clonal Hematopoiesis of Indeterminate Potential

Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is characterized by the state of a clonally expanded hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) with oncogenic somatic mutations. These cells comprise more than 4% of the peripheral blood, yet no associated hematologic malignancy exists. If the mutation is heterozygous, this 4% presence equates to a variant allele frequency of 2%. CHIP prevalence increases with age, particularly after 50, and is found in over 10% of individuals over 70 (Jaiswal and Ebert, 2019). Common mutations associated with CHIP involve the loss of function in the epigenetic regulators DNMT3A, de novo DNA methyltransferase, and TET2, demethylase (Genovese et al., 2014a, Jaiswal and Ebert, 2019). These genes are responsible for the methylation and demethylation of DNA, respectively. The reason why these loss-of-function mutations are selected with aging, particularly in older adults, and how they contribute to clonal expansion is not fully understood. However, experimental evidence offers some insight. Studies using mouse models have shown that the impairment of either DNMT3A or TET2 leads to increased self-renewal capabilities, delayed differentiation, and a bias toward myeloid lineage in HSCs (Challen et al., 2011, Cole et al., 2017, Ko et al., 2011). In DNMT3A or TET2 knockout mouse models, or in humans with CHIP carrying loss-of-function variants in these 2 genes, DNMT3A loss resulted in a decrease in methylated regions across the genome, whereas TET2 loss resulted in an increase in methylated regions (Uddin et al., 2022). These methylation changes are not entirely random across the genome but are instead concentrated in different functional regions depending on whether DNMT3A or TET2 is lost. Assuming that DNA methylation regulates the expression of nearby genes, one study analyzed methylome data from individuals with DNMT3A or TET2-mutated CHIP. The study suggests that loss of either DNMT3A or TET2 may enhance HSC self-renewal capabilities, with DNMT3A loss upregulating genes involved in HSC activity and TET2 loss downregulating genes necessary for HSC differentiation (Uddin et al., 2022).

DNMT3A/TET2 Mutation Increases Genomic Instability

The loss of function in DNMT3A and TET2 is linked to increased genomic instability. The K562 cell line, derived from a human chronic myeloid leukemia case, has demonstrated this. When K562 cells possess a frameshift mutation that yielded truncated DNMT3A lacking catalytically active C-terminal domain, they display a compromised DNA damage response and significant genomic instability, which includes alterations in chromosome number and structure, as well as the presence of dicentric chromosomes (Banaszak et al., 2018). Patients with myeloid neoplasia with mutations in TET2 show approximately double the number of single-nucleotide variations compared to those with the normal TET2 gene. Furthermore, mutations in TET2 knockout HEK293 cells and TET2 knockdown HeLa cells are more than twentyfold higher (Jiang et al., 2022). The study authors have connected the loss of TET2 function with genomic instability, noting that TET2, particularly near CpG sites, is involved in DNA repair by associating with MSH6 to form the MMR complex.

DNMT3A and TET2 may influence different DNA repair mechanisms at sites of DNA DSBs. TET2 deficiency has been shown to impair homologous recombination by reducing BRCA1 levels and to affect DNA-dependent protein kinase–mediated NHEJ by diminishing DNA ligase 4. Similarly, DNMT3A deficiency has been associated with decreased PARP1 (poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1)-mediated alternative NHEJ due to reduced PARP1 levels (Maifrede et al., 2021). Further experiments with mouse embryonic stem and epithelial cell–like cells that lack DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B (triple knockout) or TET1, TET2, and TET3 (triple knockout) have revealed that DNMT3A and TET2 not only impact histone modifications but also affect chromatin accessibility and interactions (Charlton et al., 2020).

Somatic Mutations in Morphological Normal Tissues

Somatic mutations are not exclusive to blood but accumulate in other morphologically normal tissues with age. However, mutations commonly seen in conditions like CHIP have not been widely reported in these tissues. An extensive analysis of 1,737 micro biopsies from 9 different organs (including the esophagus, cardia, stomach, duodenum, rectum, colon, pancreas, liver, and bronchus) taken from 5 individuals over 80 years old revealed that mutations were least frequent in the pancreatic parenchyma and most frequent in the liver (Li et al., 2021b). The esophagus was found to have a high abundance of potential cancer driver genes, with specific mutations such as NOTCH1 (notch receptor 1) and TP53 (tumor protein p53) in the esophagus, MUC6 (mucin 6) in the cardia and stomach, KMT2D (lysine methyltransferase 2D) in the liver, ERBB3 (erb-b2 receptor tyrosine kinase 3) in the rectum, and SMARCA4 (SWI/SNF related BAF chromatin remodeling complex subunit ATPase 4) in the duodenum. Copy number alterations were particularly prevalent in the esophagus and cardia. The normal liver tissue from 5 individuals showed an accumulation rate of 33 somatic mutations per year per diploid genome. This result aligns with previous findings, and no recurrently mutated genes were observed (Brunner et al., 2019b). Tissues like the bladder and lungs, which are continuously exposed to chemical carcinogens, and the skin, which is exposed to ultraviolet light, tend to develop cancers with high mutational burdens. While cancer tissues have been more extensively studied, there has been some research into somatic mutations in these normal tissues. For instance, mutations in epigenetic regulators KMT2D and KDM6A (lysine demethylase 6A) were identified in the normal urothelium of the bladder and ureters (Lawson et al., 2020, Li et al., 2020). The number of somatic mutations in nonsmokers' proximal bronchial basal cells increased with age. Yet, no specific genes were significantly associated with lung cancer or pan–cancer driver gene lists (Huang et al., 2022). Interestingly, while most normal tissues exhibit mutations distinct from their cancerous counterparts, studies on the eyelid epidermis exposed to sunlight showed that normal skin can harbor mutations in genes like NOTCH1/2, FAT1, and TP53, which are also common in squamous cell carcinoma (Martincorena et al., 2015).

Epigenetic Change With Age

DNA Methylation and Its Role

DNA methylation, a biochemical process, entails adding a methyl group (−CH3) to the fifth carbon of the cytosine base (Table 1). Predominantly occurring at CpG dinucleotides in humans, DNA methylation manifests in non-CpG contexts within pluripotent cells and the brain (He and Ecker, 2015). This epigenetic modification is integral to a comprehensive system present in most animals, including humans, encompassing the establishment, removal, maintenance, and recognition of methylcytosine (de Mendoza et al., 2020, Zemach and Zilberman, 2010). Furthermore, DNA methylation plays a pivotal role in determining cell fate and is intricately linked to various diseases (Berdasco and Esteller, 2011, Kim and Costello, 2017, Lee et al., 2014, Locke et al., 2019, Nishizawa et al., 2016, Salas et al., 2018), underscoring its extensive influence on gene regulation and cellular lineage.

Cell-type-specific methylation patterns predominantly localize to enhancers and binding sites for tissue-specific transcriptional regulators, highlighting the specificity of this epigenetic mark (Loyfer et al., 2023). Given the longevity of DNA methylation compared with other epigenetic markers once established (Bintu et al., 2016) and its cell-type-specific patterning in gene regulatory regions (Loyfer et al., 2023), DNA methylation is instrumental in sculpting lineage-specific epigenetic landscapes, guiding the differentiation pathways of stem cells across various organs and cell type. Crucially, both de novo methylation and demethylation processes are actively engaged during embryonic development (Hajkova, 2011, Reik et al., 2001), with the proper balance of these processes being essential for differentiation; any disruption could result in the maintenance of pluripotency instead (Dawlaty et al., 2014, Tsumura et al., 2006).

DNA Methylation With Aging

Over the years, extensive research has elucidated the complex and multifaceted relationship between DNA methylation patterns and the aging process, revealing significant changes in methylation as individuals age. These changes are characterized by both hypomethylation, commonly observed in repetitive DNA sequences leading to genomic instability, and hypermethylation at specific CpG islands associated with gene promoters, resulting in gene silencing in adult tissues (Bell et al., 2012, Bollati et al., 2009, Christensen et al., 2009, Fraga and Esteller, 2007, Horvath et al., 2012, Jung and Pfeifer, 2015, Munoz-Najar and Sedivy, 2011, Murgatroyd et al., 2010, Rakyan et al., 2010).

The development of epigenetic clocks, particularly the Horvath clock, has played a pivotal role in accurately estimating biological age based on DNA methylation patterns, offering insights into an individual's rate of biological aging (Horvath and Raj, 2018). These epigenetic clocks have demonstrated a strong correlation between discrepancies in biological and chronological age and the prevalence of age-related diseases and mortality, underscoring the significant impact of DNA methylation on the aging process (Marioni et al., 2015).

Among various biological age estimators, including telomere length, transcriptomic-based, proteomic-based, and metabolomic-based estimators, as well as composite biomarkers, the epigenetic clock has garnered the most attention due to its accuracy and consistency (Horvath and Raj, 2018). Environmental and lifestyle factors, such as diet, exercise, and exposure to pollutants, are crucial in influencing DNA methylation patterns over time, potentially contributing to the aging process and the onset of age-related diseases (Johnson et al., 2012). This comprehensive understanding opens new avenues for interventions to modify epigenetic marks to promote healthy aging and longevity, including lifestyle modifications, pharmacological interventions (Barzilai et al., 2016), and advanced gene editing techniques (Lu et al., 2020). Advancements in aging and disease markers using DNA methylation information are accelerating with the development of various analytical methods. Using large-scale genetic data through epigenome-wide Mendelian randomization, a newly developed DNA epigenetic clock separately identifies and measures CpG sites associated with adverse outcomes, including mortality, and those linked to beneficial adaptations (Ying et al., 2024). Like this study, future research will primarily focus on detailed mechanisms behind methylation changes associated with aging.

Histone Modifications and Its Role

Within the nucleus, a nucleosome is crafted as 147 DNA base pairs that wrap around a histone core, the chromatin's structural essence. This core comprises an octet of histone proteins—H2A, H2B, H3, and H4, paired respectively. Research has consistently shown that the amino-terminal tails of these histones are subject to chemical modifications. Histone modifications are crucial regulators of gene expression, acting as molecular markers that can either promote or repress transcription (Bannister and Kouzarides, 2011, Millan-Zambrano et al., 2022). These modifications, including methylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination, occur on the histone protein tails and alter the chromatin structure, affecting gene accessibility. For instance, adding methyl groups (methylation) can either activate or repress gene expression depending on the specific amino acid residues modified on the histones. Acetylation, generally associated with transcriptional activation, loosens the chromatin structure, allowing transcription factors to access DNA.

The interplay between different histone modifications, often described by the “histone code,” can dictate the transcriptional outcome of a gene. For example, trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4me3) is commonly found near the promoters of actively transcribed genes, while trimethylation at lysine 27 (H3K27me3) is associated with gene repression. The dynamic and reversible nature of histone modifications provides a responsive system to regulate gene expression in accordance with cellular signals and environmental conditions, playing a pivotal role in processes such as development, differentiation, and stress response (Lawrence et al., 2016). Dysregulation of histone modifications can lead to aberrant gene expression patterns, contributing to the development of various diseases, including cancer and neurodegenerative disorders (Park et al., 2022, Sodersten et al., 2018, Yuan et al., 2016).

Histone Modifications With Aging

Histone modifications have been implicated in the aging process in mammals. Genetic, environmental, epigenetic, and stochastic factors influence a time-dependent functional decline as organisms age. Among these, epigenetic alterations, particularly histone modifications with DNA methylation, are of significant interest because they are potentially reversible. For instance, changes in histone acetylation patterns associate with age-related gene expression changes and cellular senescence (Wang et al., 2018).

Histone acetyltransferases, reversed by histone deacetylases, are crucial for DNA damage response and repair. Specific acetylation patterns can either facilitate or hinder access to DNA for repair proteins. The balance of acetylation and deacetylation influences the choice of DNA DSB repair mechanisms (Miller et al., 2010, Tang et al., 2013, Tjeertes et al., 2009). Histone deacetylases, linked to longevity, plays a significant role in maintaining genome stability and epigenetic balance, particularly in aging and age-related diseases like Alzheimer's, where histone acetylation patterns are disrupted, affecting gene expression related to neuronal function and disease pathology (Lu et al., 2014, Nativio et al., 2018). H3K27me and H3K9me are involved in aging and cellular senescence, influencing genome stability and the inflammatory state of cells (Soto-Palma et al., 2022, Yang and Sen, 2018). Research has shown that global redistribution of histone modifications occurs over time, affecting intergenic regions and transcription start sites. This research suggests that the epigenetic landscape of an organism becomes altered as it ages, which could contribute to the age-related decline in physiological function. Histone modifications exhibit varying implications with aging. For instance, certain histone marks, such as H3K9ac and H4K16ac, associated with active gene expression, may undergo global increases, while repressive marks like H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 might experience overall decreases during aging.

Conversely, the active mark H3K36me3 tends to decrease with age (Yi and Kim, 2020). Moreover, comparing various age-related profiles of histone H3 marks unveils a significant global redistribution of histone H3 modifications over time in mice. This redistribution is notably observed in intergenic regions and proximal to transcription start sites. Intriguingly, interventions such as caloric restriction, which is known to retard aging, have been found to reduce the extent of histone modification changes, indicating that lifestyle factors can influence the rate of epigenetic aging (Hillje et al., 2022).

Chromatin Remodeling, Laminin-Associated Domains, and Topologically Associating Domains With Age

Chromatin remodeling is a dynamic process that alters chromatin structure to regulate gene expression, involving the repositioning or restructuring of nucleosomes. Laminin-associated domains are large genomic regions that interface with the nuclear lamina and are generally transcriptionally inactive, influencing the genome's spatial organization. Topologically associating domains are self-interacting chromosomal regions that enable regulatory elements, like enhancers and promoters, to come into proximity and modulate gene expression. The interplay between these elements is crucial for maintaining genomic stability and orchestrating developmental processes, with implications for aging and disease (Gonzalez-Sandoval and Gasser, 2016, van Steensel and Belmont, 2017). Senescent cells exhibit notable epigenetic modifications during aging, particularly in 3D genome organization. Studies utilizing Chromosome Conformation Capture (3C) techniques have elucidated genomic rearrangements associated with aging (Liu et al., 2022). Specifically, repositioning laminin-associated domains toward the nuclear periphery strongly correlates with changes in nuclear compartments observed in aging stem cells (Liu et al., 2022). Additionally, comprehensive investigations into topologically associating domains alterations during aging have been conducted across various cell types, including human umbilical vein endothelial cells, human fibroblasts, and human mesenchymal stem cells. Genes involved in DNA conformation maintenance are suppressed upon senescence across all cell types. Nuclear depletion of the abundant proteins of the high-mobility group B 2 protein occurs early on the path to senescence (Zirkel et al., 2018).

Interplay Between the Genomic and Epigenomic Alterations and Their Accumulation With Age

The intricate interplay between genomic and epigenomic alterations establishes a persistent cellular memory that significantly impacts multiple aging hallmarks (Fig. 1). Accumulating somatic mutations in epigenetic regulators, chromatin remodelers, and DNA repair genes over time leads to widespread epigenetic modifications, too, thereby influencing transcriptional dynamics and cellular identity. Notably, DNA DSBs are closely associated with aging, occurring at a rate of 10 to 50 per cell per day (Tian et al., 2019, Vilenchik and Knudson, 2003). However, the precise impact and mechanism of DSBs on cellular dysfunction remain poorly understood. Recent studies indicate that DSBs, even without genetic mutations, can induce epigenomic changes contributing to aging (Yang et al., 2023a). Single-Cell Study of Aging

To decipher the intricate cellular phenotypes characteristic of aging and the influence of epigenetic modifications on cellular senescence, a comprehensive perspective is needed—one that considers the interplay of genetic backgrounds, epigenetic landscapes, and environmental influences on cellular longevity and functionality. Single-cell transcriptomic and epigenomic experiments provide this level of detail (Carter and Zhao, 2021, Kelsey et al., 2017, Park et al., 2022, Svensson et al., 2018), particularly for stem cells, which are crucial for tissue maintenance, balancing proliferation and differentiation, and repairing injury. Understanding the changes in these cells with age enhances our grasp of aging mechanisms (Levra Levron and Donati, 2023).

The study of aging at the single-cell level reveals how chromatin accessibility patterns evolve from fetal stages to adulthood, as demonstrated in an expanded analysis across 222 cell types (Zhang et al., 2021). It highlights the transformation of chromatin landscapes in various cell types and their impact on cell function and development. For instance, in skeletal myocytes, regulatory elements active during fetal development change in adult cells, reflecting their different functions at each life stage.

Specialized cells, like those in the retina and ovaries, exhibit particular susceptibility to age-induced stress and inflammation, leading to a decline in defensive mechanisms and loss of cellular identity (Wang et al., 2021). This decline is underscored by variable DNA methylation patterns, which may explain the uneven nature of cellular aging. Stem cells also display increased genetic instability and epigenetic alterations over time, contributing to aberrant gene activity (Argelaguet et al., 2019, Hernando-Herraez et al., 2019).

Cells adapt to stress in diverse ways, as seen in pancreatic β-cells and cochlear cells, which accumulate protein aggregates and experience increased apoptosis. In β-cells, this leads to impaired insulin secretion, while in cochlear cells, it can result in hearing loss—both conditions are ripe for therapeutic intervention (Li et al., 2021a, Sun et al., 2023).

Environmental interventions, such as caloric restriction, have been shown to slow the immune system's aging trajectory, notably reducing inflammation propensity (Ma et al., 2020). Mutant mouse embryos highlight the critical role of DNA methylation disruptions in cellular aging, particularly affecting gene silencing and activation crucial for cell differentiation (Clark et al., 2022). Additionally, aging is linked with increased chromatin accessibility and activation of LINE1 elements, indicative of age-related epigenetic changes (Zhang et al., 2022).

Epithelial stem cells' remarkable plasticity to respond to damage and inflammation is compromised with age due to accumulated genetic and epigenomic instability (Levra Levron and Donati, 2023). Hair follicle stem cells demonstrate adaptability by accumulating epigenetic memories that influence their response to environmental stimuli, affecting their function and skin barrier integrity (Gonzales et al., 2021). The AgeAnno database has been instrumental in identifying genes associated with aging and elucidating cellular and molecular changes across tissues, highlighting the phenomenon of increased “genetic noise” in aging cells (Huang et al., 2023, Mohammed et al., 2017).

These findings underscore that a sophisticated interplay of genetic and epigenetic factors orchestrates cellular aging. The diverse and nuanced aging process presents a spectrum of changes that offer potential targets for interventions aimed at mitigating the effects of aging.

Stem Cell Aging

Comprehending stem cell aging is vital for understanding age-related tissue degeneration and disease progression. Young and healthy stem cells typically maintain tissue balance by alternating between quiescence and proliferation, ready to replace damaged tissue when necessary (Fig. 2). The young stem cell pool has high clonal diversity and makes a balanced pool of differentiated cells with low plasticity (Fig. 2). Therefore, the tissue functions correctly. However, age-related genetic and epigenetic alterations can disrupt these processes. Stem Cell Pool Reduction

Age-related changes in the myometrium, the muscle layer of the uterus, were explored using single-cell and nuclei RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics (Punzon-Jimenez et al., 2024). The study reveals 23 distinct cell types in the myometrium, including those involved in contraction and vascular function, as well as immune-regulating fibroblasts. As people age, research indicates a decrease in the number of contractile cells in blood vessels. Moreover, there is a noted reduction in the expression of ion channels in smooth muscle cells, coupled with the absence of calcitonin receptor signaling. This signaling pathway is essential for keeping muscle stem cells in a quiescent state, maintaining the muscle stem cells pool (Yamaguchi et al., 2015).

Stem Cell Diversity Exhaustion and Biased Lineage

Previously, long-term ex vivo studies showed the heterogeneity of HSCs (Wilkinson et al., 2019). Besides, with an enhanced single-cell lineage tracing method using mitochondrial DNA mutations, allowing simultaneous tracking of transcriptional states and chromatin accessibility, Weng et al. (2024) delineate the clonal structure of HSCs, revealing stable, functional differences and reduced diversity with age. In mice, the mutation of either DNMT3A or TET2 leads to increased self-renewal capabilities, delayed differentiation, and a bias toward myeloid lineage (Challen et al., 2011, Cole et al., 2017, Ko et al., 2011). Remarkably, the targeted depletion of myeloid HSC in aged mice via antibody-mediated mechanisms rejuvenates hallmark characteristics of a more youthful immunity. It includes the augmentation of common lymphocyte progenitors, naive T cells, and B cells, concomitant with the attenuation of age-associated indicators of immune senescence (Ross et al., 2024). Age-related changes in brain cell dynamics have been thoroughly examined, uncovering notable declines in the proliferation capability of neural progenitor cells and the differentiation capacity of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, alongside increased proliferation rates in reactive microglia among mice and decreased oligodendrogenesis in the cerebellum has been observed in humans (Ament et al., 2023). Human intestinal organoids derived from patients with Cronkhite-Canada syndrome (CCS) exhibited notable characteristics of biased lineage (Suppinger et al., 2023). These organoids, called CCS human intestinal organoids, demonstrated a marked increase in cell proliferation and a significant rise in serotonin-producing enteroendocrine cells. These distinct features were evident in the organoids and mirrored in tissue biopsies from CCS patients. The study further established a clear link between the heightened presence of enteroendocrine cells and the increased proliferation rates observed in these organoids.

Stem Cell Memory Changes With Aging

The study shows that after acute inflammation, pancreatic epithelial cells undergo lasting changes in gene expression and epigenetic makeup, a process termed “epithelial memory” (Del Poggetto et al., 2021). This memory enables the cells to more effectively reactivate a cellular transformation process (acinar-to-ductal metaplasia) during future inflammations, reducing tissue damage. Interestingly, the study suggests that specific mutations in the KRAS gene, which cause a permanent state of acinar-to-ductal metaplasia, might protect against damage from repeated pancreatic inflammation. These findings indicate a complex relationship between inflammation, genetic mutations, and epigenetic shifts in the progression to pancreatic cancer, underscoring how previous inflammatory episodes can prepare epithelial cells for future challenges, including the development of cancer. Specific stem cell populations, distant from the original injury, give rise to long-lasting wound memory progenitors. These cells, which have not participated in the initial healing, become preactivated through priming and maintain this state at the chromatin and transcriptional level. The established memory leads to enhanced wound repair. However, it also has long-term harmful consequences, such as exacerbating tumorigenesis, indicating a complex interplay between wound repair and cancer risk (Levra Levron and Donati, 2023).

In summary, gathering and analyzing the diverse behaviors of stem cells across different ages is crucial for understanding the intricate nature of stem cell aging and its implications for tissue function and disease susceptibility. The alterations in stem or progenitor cells with abnormal phenotypes observed in aged tissues are likely influenced by genetic and epigenetic alterations. Analyzing the single-cell epigenome of various stem cells across ages can elucidate the crucial age-related epigenetic characteristics associated with cellular phenotype.

Sirtuin in DNA Repair and Aging

Sirtuins (SIRT), specifically SIRT6, are integral to metabolism, DNA repair, and inflammation management, which are all processes connected to aging and neurodegenerative diseases (Bhatt and Tiwari, 2023). Mice deficient in SIRT6 experience reduced lifespan and other symptoms of aging, worsened by increased LINE1 activity and subsequent inflammation. Treatments targeting LINE1 activity can alleviate these symptoms and extend lifespan, indicating that both LINE1 activity and inflammation are significant in aging and could be targets for antiaging therapies (Simon et al., 2019). Additionally, the effectiveness of DNA repair mechanisms, particularly those repairing DSBs, correlates with longevity across rodent species and is significantly affected by the functionality of the SIRT6 protein. Variations in SIRT6 protein among species with different lifespans are linked to specific amino acids, suggesting evolutionary optimization of this protein concerning aging. This insight can inform the development of new antiaging treatments (Tian et al., 2019).

Blood Exchange

Diluting aged blood plasma can improve tissue function and cognitive performance in aged mice, suggesting that aging can be modulated by adding youthful factors and removing aging factors (Mehdipour et al., 2020). For example, GDF11 in young blood induces vascular remodeling, increases neurogenesis, and improves olfactory discrimination (Katsimpardi et al., 2014) and oxytocin in plasma rejuvenates skeletal muscle stem cells in mice (Elabd et al., 2014). Tet2, a DNA demethylase, decreases with age in mouse hippocampi but increases following blood exchange via parabiosis (Gontier et al., 2018). Therapeutic plasma exchange shows promise for enhancing elderly health. Single-cell transcriptomic studies indicate young blood exposure can rejuvenate stem cells across various tissues (Ma et al., 2022, Rebo et al., 2016). Proteins like TIMP2 in human cord plasma have also been found to reverse age-related cognitive decline (Castellano et al., 2017). Given their role in age-related bone diseases, rejuvenating mesenchymal stem cells may be vital for addressing skeletal aging (Cheng et al., 2023a).

Activation of Autophagy

Studies have shown that activating autophagy, either through genetic manipulation or through natural compounds like spermidine, fisetin, citrate, nicotinamide riboside, and alpha-ketoglutarate can lead to enhance regeneration and lifespan extension (Brakedal et al., 2022, Castoldi et al., 2020, Dalla Bella et al., 2021, Demidenko et al., 2021, Dong et al., 2021, Fan et al., 2021, Fernandez et al., 2018, Madrigal-Matute et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2022, Yousefzadeh et al., 2018). The research also delves into the potential of autophagy, the unfolded protein response, and the circadian alignment of caloric restriction in combating age-related decline (Acosta-Rodriguez et al., 2022, Spadaro et al., 2022).

Metabolic Interventions and Others

Metabolic interventions, including the modulation of mitochondrial function (Chee et al., 2021, Tavallaie et al., 2020, Zhang et al., 2020), have been linked to improved healthspan and, in some cases, reversal of biological aging markers. In humans, multicomponent supplements containing Vitamin B3, Vitamin C, Vitamin D, Omega 3 fish oils, Resveratrol, Olive fruit phenols, and Astaxanthin show possible benefits in individuals with an accelerated epigenetic age and inflammation (McGee et al., 2024). Heat shock proteins and polyamines are being explored for their roles in delaying senescence and enhancing cognitive function (Bobkova et al., 2015, Eisenberg et al., 2016). Senolytics, which selectively remove aging cells, are showing promising results in animal models and human clinical trials, particularly in age-related diseases like idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and diabetic kidney disease (Hickson et al., 2020, Justice et al., 2019). Fecal microbiota transplantation from younger to older mice has improved gut health and cognitive function, highlighting the gut-brain axis's role in aging (Barcena et al., 2019, Stebegg et al., 2019, Xu et al., 2022). Senescent retinal pigment epithelium cells induced by doxorubicin were treated with ABT-263, an inhibitor of Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL. ABT-263 selectively removed the senescent cells, leading to structural and functional recovery of the retina and partial restoration of retinal pigment epithelium cell proliferation (Ryu et al., 2023).

Finally, emerging research into cellular reprogramming suggests that enhancing the body's inherent regenerative capabilities may offer new avenues for rejuvenation therapies.

Rejuvenation

Partial Reprogramming for Rejuvenation

Partial reprogramming refers to a process in which cells are induced to revert to a state that is closer to pluripotency but not to a fully pluripotent state, as in the generation of iPSCs. Partial reprogramming is achieved by transiently expressing the reprogramming factors—Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc (OSKM)—for a limited period or at a reduced level. Myc is typically excluded because it is an oncogene (Dhanasekaran et al., 2021) that shortens the lifespan of mice (Hofmann et al., 2015) and is not essential for initiating cellular reprogramming (Rand et al., 2018). Unlike full reprogramming, partial reprogramming aims to rejuvenate cells and reset their biological age without erasing their specialized cell identity. It leverages the cells' ability to renew and repair themselves, thus potentially reversing age-related phenotypes and improving function in aged tissues. This method could reduce or eliminate the risk of teratoma formation, which is a concern with the full reprogramming used to create iPSCs (Lehmann et al., 2019, Puri and Wagner, 2023, Singh and Zhakupova, 2022). Partial reprogramming rejuvenates aging cells, delays age-related phenotypes, and extends lifespans in mouse models. The treatment duration dictates the extent of rejuvenation, with more prolonged treatment proving more efficacious. Rejuvenation manifests as reversing the epigenetic clock and reducing genes associated with inflammation and stress, implying a potential for safely and effectively preventing age-related changes (Browder et al., 2022). Epigenetic noise accumulation is a suggested cause of aging, and strategies to restore youthful gene expression patterns show promise in enhancing tissue function. For instance, targeted expression of reprogramming factors in the retina has restored vision by reverting DNA methylation patterns and activating axon regeneration. This result indicates that tissues retain a blueprint for youthfulness that can be activated for functional improvement (Lu et al., 2020). Beyond the eye, partial reprogramming has improved older mice's recovery from metabolic and muscular diseases (Ocampo et al., 2016). In brain trauma, reprogramming has converted reactive glial cells into functional neurons within the cortex, suggesting a novel regenerative approach without external cell manipulation (Gao et al., 2016). Similarly, inducing reprogramming factors in heart tissue has promoted cardiac function recovery post injury by driving cardiomyocytes back into the cell cycle, akin to fetal heart cells' regenerative properties (Chen et al., 2021). It also can enhance tissue repair and reduce fibrosis and scar formation in mice without causing tumors (Doeser et al., 2018). Additionally, this approach induces dedifferentiation in intestinal epithelial cells, leading to the emergence of cells that assist in tissue regeneration. This process also alters prostaglandin synthesis pathways, suggesting a novel method for promoting injury-free intestinal regeneration (Kim et al., 2023). These advances point to partial reprogramming as a promising strategy for human tissue regeneration and antiaging interventions (Gaspar-Silva et al., 2023, Huyghe et al., 2023, Ji et al., 2023).

Furthermore, another concept of partial cellular reprogramming, exemplified by chemical Epigenetic Programming of Old Cell Health, offers a promising avenue for reversing the transcriptomic age of cells without OSKM (Yang et al., 2023b) Utilizing transcription-based aging clocks and nucleocytoplasmic compartmentalization assays, novel chemical cocktails such as VC6TF (comprising Valproic acid, CHIR-99021, E-616452, Tranylcypromine, and Forskolin) and C6NYSA (containing CHIR-99021, E-616452, TTNPB, Y-27632, Smoothened agonist, and ABT-869) have demonstrated the ability to rejuvenate cells by restoring a youthful genome-wide transcript profile and reversing transcriptomic aging. These findings suggest a potential therapeutic strategy for combating age-related cellular decline and associated pathologies, both genetic and chemical means. While partial reprogramming is not exclusive to chemical cocktails, small molecules alone are also recognized for their capacity. GSK3β, a pivotal serine/threonine kinase, profoundly influences neurodevelopmental processes, including neurogenesis, morphological alterations, cell polarization, and migration. Notably, the GSK3β inhibitor CHIR-99021, administered at low doses (1 μM), significantly enhances cell survival, neural progenitor cell proliferation, and neuronal migration (Delepine et al., 2021). Moreover, a novel intestinal organoid culture system consisting of 8 key components, notably including VPA, EPZ6438, LDN193189, and R-Spondin 1 conditioned medium, has been developed to mimic gut epithelium regeneration, resulting in the formation of hyperplastic crypts post injury (Qu et al., 2021). This innovative model facilitated the identification of distinct regenerative stem cell populations within hyperorganoids. In particular, VPA and EPZ6438 were identified as essential components for epigenome reprogramming and regeneration in hyperorganoids, functioning by epigenetically regulating YAP signaling. Here, VPA and EPZ6438 promote the regenerative response in the gut following damage in vivo. In vivo, administration of 1-carbon metabolites derived from metabolic pathways for the synthesis of DNA, polyamines, amino acids, creatine, and phospholipids also accelerated regeneration after muscle injury in both young and old mice (Hernandez-Benitez et al., 2024).

A clear understanding of how rejuvenation or regeneration occurs in different tissues is necessary, and research that accurately elucidates the mechanisms of partial reprogramming can expand our fundamental knowledge of cell biology. For example, experiments that activate the cell cycle in quiescent, nondividing stem cells through partial reprogramming to induce regeneration can help identify cis-regulatory elements and pathways that play a crucial role in distinguishing between the quiescent and proliferating states of stem cells through single-cell omics. Additionally, comparing the reprogramming potential of cells across different tissues by determining through lineage tracing whether rejuvenation occurs via dedifferentiation or stem cell reprogramming is valuable foundational knowledge. Such studies can be conducted using genetically manipulable systems like model organisms or human organoids.

Lifespan Extension of Partial Reprogramming

While partial reprogramming can rejuvenate aging signs in mice, the lifespan extension observed was modest. The primary benefit of partial reprogramming was in tissue rejuvenation rather than lifespan extension (Ocampo et al., 2016). In addition, senolytic, mTOR inhibitor (rapamycin) and NAD+ precursors can have a more profound effect on lifespan than partial reprogramming (Blagosklonny, 2019, Zhu et al., 2015). A key reason of less effectiveness in extending lifespan compared with senolytics or metabolic modulation might be the complexity of cellular rejuvenation within the organism-wide. Interestingly, combining partial reprogramming with senolytics synergistically extends lifespan (Kaur et al., 2022). This therapy simultaneously replacing lost stem cells and removing senescent cells. Detailed studies on the mechanisms of partial reprogramming, combined with various in vivo trials on combination therapies, could further enhance the utility of partial reprogramming.

Rejuvenation and Epigenetics

Fundamentally, during development, there is a widespread reprogramming of epigenetic marks across the genome, and the spatial and temporal epigenome serves as a critical indicator of cell identity, playing a pivotal role in normal development (Morgan et al., 2005, Singh et al., 2023, Wilkinson et al., 2023). Furthermore, reprogramming induced by factors like OSKM alters nearly all epigenetic features. This alteration includes rapid changes in chromatin accessibility during reprogramming, accompanied by subsequent transcriptional activity. Additionally, regions of the genome that become accessible during reprogramming are demethylated (Schwarz et al., 2018). Moreover, genome-wide shifts in euchromatic histone modification, such as H3K4me2, occur across numerous genomic loci, affecting a substantial portion of promoters and enhancers associated with pluripotency or developmental regulation (Koche et al., 2011).

Indeed, rejuvenation by partial reprogramming for axon regeneration and vision hinges on the presence of DNA demethylases TET1 and TET2. This result underscores the notion that mammalian tissues preserve a repository of youthful epigenetic cues partially encoded through DNA methylation (Lu et al., 2020). Partial reprogramming also improves cellular aging, resetting transcriptomic and epigenetic clocks. It diminishes the inflammatory characteristics in chondrocytes and reinstates the youthful regenerative capacity in aged human muscle stem cells, all while preserving cellular identity (Sarkar et al., 2020). The rejuvenating effects were tied to a reversal of the epigenetic clock and shifts in metabolic and transcriptomic profiles involving a decrease in the expression of genes linked to inflammation, senescence, and stress response pathways (Browder et al., 2022). DNA methylation patterns were also reversed, typically seen with aging in the pancreas, liver, spleen, and blood. Furthermore, specific serum metabolites and aging-related methylation patterns returned to youthful levels following transient reprogramming (Chondronasiou et al., 2022).

In addition, tranylcypromine, a known inhibitor of LSD1, has shown promising implications in impeding tumor development (Huang et al., 2017). LSD1, an enzyme responsible for chromatin modification through histone demethylation, is frequently overexpressed in various cancers. Inhibition of LSD1 by tranylcypromine leads to an increase in H3K4me2 levels, consequently hindering cancer cell proliferation and progression. This result elucidates the potential therapeutic role of tranylcypromine in combating cancer through epigenetic regulation.

These findings suggest a profound association between rejuvenation and epigenetics, highlighting the intricate role of epigenetic modifications in cellular aging and regeneration processes. The observed reversal of aging-related epigenetic alterations underscores the potential of epigenetic interventions in promoting tissue rejuvenation. However, further research is necessary to elucidate the mechanisms underlying these rejuvenating effects and explore their therapeutic implications for age-related diseases.

Proposed Research on Epigenetic Regulation in Aging and Rejuvenation

Single-Cell Epigenomics

Through single-cell omics technologies, we have observed a diverse array of cell types and states to an extent previously unattainable (Svensson et al., 2018). It enabled various studies of cell types and states within tissues, leading to the accumulation of substantial archives (Ament et al., 2023, Li et al., 2022, Regev et al., 2017, Shi et al., 2023). However, transcriptomics describes the cellular state, a cell molecular phenotype. Single-cell epigenomic information is necessary to study the regulatory basis of transcriptomes (Fig. 3). Single-cell epigenomic technologies have rapidly advanced in line with these needs, with various developments centered around techniques like single-cell Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin (Buenrostro et al., 2013, Preissl et al., 2023, Schwartzman and Tanay, 2015). Recently, methods utilizing antibody-conjugated transposases have enabled the targeting of diverse histone modifications and specific proteins, thus facilitating the acquisition of diverse single-cell epigenomic information (Bartosovic and Castelo-Branco, 2023, Bartosovic et al., 2021, Kaya-Okur et al., 2019, Stuart et al., 2023). Additionally, methods for profiling DNA methylation and 3D genome structures at the single-cell level have been developed, revealing the diversity of single-cell methylomes (Harada et al., 2019, Kim and Lee, 2023, Lee et al., 2019, Luo et al., 2017). Above all, the development of multimodal approaches enabling simultaneous profiling of transcriptomic and epigenomic information in the same cell has made it possible to study detailed gene regulatory mechanisms at the single-cell level (Baysoy et al., 2023, Park et al., 2022, Vandereyken et al., 2023). Understanding how epigenetic changes occur at the single-cell level during rejuvenation processes and which cis-regulatory regions undergo epigenetic changes provides crucial insights into aging and cellular fate alterations. Organoid Technology

Organoids derived from aged tissues serve as powerful models for studying age-related cellular and tissue changes. These models maintain the intrinsic characteristics of aging, such as genomic instability and epigenetic alterations. For instance, organoids generated from aged human mammary epithelial cells have shown that cells from older donors frequently adopt a luminal-like phenotype, with increased expression of estrogen receptor α, which suggests that aging influences cancer subtype development (Lee et al., 2015). Moreover, age-related modifications in the extracellular matrix, such as glycation, can enhance invasiveness and reduce epithelial differentiation in organoid models, indicating that extracellular matrix aging contributes to cancer progression (Rodriguez-Teja et al., 2015). Caloric restriction is well-known for its antiaging effects and has been extensively studied using organoid models. Yilmaz et al. (2012) showed that intestinal stem cells (ISCs) from calorie-restricted mice exhibited enhanced organoid formation and size, suggesting that caloric restriction positively influences the stem cell niche and promotes tissue homeostasis during aging (Yilmaz et al., 2012). Nalapareddy et al. (2017) found that aged ISCs exhibited deficient Wnt signaling, which impaired their ability to form organoids. Supplementation with exogenous Wnt3a was shown to restore organoid formation and growth in these aged ISCs, highlighting the critical role of the niche environment in stem cell aging (Nalapareddy et al., 2017). The study used intestinal organoids to model alcohol-induced damage, showing that alcohol upregulates CYP2E1, increasing oxidative stress, and suggests targeting specific miRNAs to reduce this injury (Mun et al., 2024). Furthermore, senescent fibroblasts release the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, including protein tyrosine kinase 7, which impairs cell differentiation in mouse intestinal organoids by activating noncanonical Wnt/Ca2+ signaling, secretory cells such as goblet, enteroendocrine, and Tuft cells, and stem cells were reduced (Yun et al., 2023). Organoids differentiate from stem cells into various differentiated cells. Therefore, changes in the proportion of cell types and changes in each cell type during aging-rejuvenation can be observed (Fig. 3). Additionally, because they grow in vitro, genetic manipulation and chemical treatment, including Yamanaka factors, are possible. Although the media composition to enhance stem cell ability may differ from in vivo conditions, the advantage lies in the ability to observe detailed changes in stem cells, which are difficult to observe at the single-cell level in vivo due to their limited numbers.

The limitation of the organoid system is that it cannot replicate the niche environment. To overcome the limitations of organoid model systems, which cannot observe the niche environment excluding stem cells and epithelial cell types (Fig. 3), combining them with spatial transcriptomic methods is recommended. Spatial transcriptomics, a rapidly evolved method, provides spatial information on cell types and states by simultaneously observing hundreds of genes (Cheng et al., 2023b, Rao et al., 2021, Tian et al., 2023, Williams et al., 2022). Information on various tissue environments, including the immune system, can be obtained. By acquiring single-cell transcriptomic-epigenomic details on stem cells and cell differentiation at the level of organoids and obtaining information on human tissue environment changes during aging through spatial transcriptomics, it will be possible to understand cellular-level changes during aging. Furthermore, compared with accumulated aging data, experiments on rejuvenation conducted through organoid model systems will allow for a detailed understanding of how rejuvenation induces cellular changes. This analysis enables rejuvenation research on human cells, ultimately leading to attempts to improve human health through the knowledge of aging.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the field of aging research has seen significant advancements, particularly with the breakthrough of rejuvenation research via partial reprogramming. The interplay between somatic mutations, such as DNA DSBs, mutations of epigenetic regulators, and epigenomic alterations during aging, contributes to cellular dysfunction and age-related phenotypes. In particular, stem cell aging is pivotal for tissue homeostasis, and understanding their behavior through single-cell multimodal epigenomic analysis is crucial regarding cell plasticity. Partial reprogramming, notably, holds promise in reversing age-related phenotypes by resetting the epigenome. Utilizing single-cell omics technologies and organoid model systems provides valuable insights into cellular diversity, epigenetic modifications, and tissue-level changes during aging and rejuvenation. Continued advancements in methodologies and interdisciplinary collaborations will further illuminate the mechanisms underlying aging and rejuvenation, offering potential interventions to promote healthy aging and combat age-related diseases.
