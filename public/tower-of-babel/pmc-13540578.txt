Electroacupuncture Attenuates Neuroinflammation in PCPA-Induced Insomnia Rats by Suppressing the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB p65 Pathway and M1 Microglial Activation
Authors: Meng Zhang, Lian Liu, Tian Tan, Lijuan He, Bingqing Hu, Sixian Li, Mengya Jiang, Peng Deng, Qingjia Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsomnia is closely associated with neuroinflammation, yet the therapeutic mechanism of electroacupuncture (EA) remains unclear. This animal study investigated whether EA attenuates hypothalamic neuroinflammation in para-chlorophenylalanine (PCPA)-induced insomnia rats by suppressing the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB p65 pathway and M1 microglial activation, using TAK-242 (HY-11109), a selective TLR4 inhibitor, as a positive control.MethodsForty Sprague-Dawley rats (20 male, 20 female, sex-balanced across groups) were randomly allocated to Control, Model, EA, and TAK-242 groups (n=10 per group). Insomnia was induced in the model, EA, and TAK-242 groups by intraperitoneal injection of PCPA (500mg/kg) for 2 consecutive days. The TAK-242 group received daily intraperitoneal injection of TAK-242 (3 mg/kg). The EA group received acupuncture at Baihui (GV20), bilateral Benshen (GB13), Shenmen (HT7) and Sanyinjiao (SP6), with ipsilateral Shenmen (HT7) and Sanyinjiao (SP6) connected to an electroacupuncture device using a 2 Hz continuous wavefor 20 minutes daily over 7 days. Sleep latency and total sleep duration were recorded via the pentobarbital sodium righting reflex test. Serum interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) levels were assessed via the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA); Hypothalamic microglial M1 polarization was assessed by Iba-1/CD86 immunofluorescence co-localization. Protein and mRNA levels of TLR4, MyD88, NF-κB p65, TNF-α, and IL-6 were quantified by Western blot and RT-qPCR, respectively. All outcome assessments were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.ResultsCompared with the model group, both electroacupuncture and TAK-242 significantly shortened sleep latency and prolonged total sleep time (P < 0.01), and reduced serum levels of TNF-α and IL-6 (P < 0.01). Both interventions also attenuated CD86 fluorescence intensity (P < 0.01) and Iba-1/CD86 co-expression (P < 0.05), and down-regulated the protein and mRNA expression of TNF-α, IL-6, TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB p65 in the hypothalamus (P < 0.01). The effects of electroacupuncture and TAK-242 on sleep parameters, CD86 fluorescence intensity, and Iba-1/CD86 co-expression were comparable, whereas TAK-242 exerted a more pronounced inhibitory effect than electroacupuncture on the expression of inflammatory cytokines and pathway-related proteins in the serum and hypothalamus (P < 0.05, P < 0.01).ConclusionElectroacupuncture effectively improved sleep disturbances in PCPA-induced insomnia rats. The underlying mechanism may be associated with inhibition of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB p65 signaling pathway, suppression of M1 microglial activation, and downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby partly alleviating hypothalamic neuroinflammation.Keywords: electroacupuncture, insomnia, microglia, inflammation

Introduction

Insomnia is a common sleep disorder characterized primarily by difficulties falling asleep, sleep maintenance disturbances, and early morning awakenings. It is often accompanied by impaired daytime function and has become the second most common psychiatric disorder,1,2 With the accelerated pace of modern life and rising stress levels, the global prevalence of insomnia continues to climb, constituting a significant public health issue. A meta-analysis published in 2025 reported an insomnia prevalence of 12.4% in the general population.3 According to a meta-analysis published in 《Sleep Medicine Reviews》, involving over 260,000 individuals, approximately 16.2% of adults meet the diagnostic criteria for insomnia disorder, with about 7.9% suffering from severe insomnia.4 Chronic insomnia not only significantly reduces quality of life and occupational functioning but is also closely associated with an increased risk of various chronic diseases, including immune dysfunction, metabolic abnormalities, and neurodegenerative diseases.5–9

Recent research indicates that inflammatory mechanisms play a crucial role in the occurrence and development of insomnia.10–13 Patients with insomnia often exhibit a state of systemic and central inflammation, characterized by elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the periphery and central nervous system, as well as enhanced neuroinflammation mediated by microglial activation.14–16 These inflammatory mediators can interfere with the function of sleep-wake regulatory centers, such as the hypothalamus, thalamic reticular nucleus, and tuberomammillary nucleus,15 disrupt neurotransmitter balance and normal sleep architecture17–19 and create a vicious cycle where insomnia and inflammation mutually reinforce each other. Specifically, sleep disturbance can activate the innate immune system and promote systemic inflammation, which in turn compromises the blood-brain barrier and facilitates central neuroinflammation; the resulting neuroinflammatory state further impairs sleep-regulatory neural circuits, thereby perpetuating and exacerbating insomnia.12,16 Therefore, modulating neuroinflammation is considered a potential strategy for intervening in insomnia and its related complications.

As the primary immune cells in the central nervous system, microglia play a key role in insomnia-related neuroinflammatory processes.20 Under pathological conditions, microglia can undergo classical (M1) activation, characterized by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, and alternative (M2) activation, which is associated with anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects. Excessive or sustained M1 polarization drives neuroinflammation and can disrupt neuronal function in sleep-wake regulatory centers. Experimental studies have shown that inhibiting M1 microglial activation helps alleviate neuroinflammation and improve sleep.16,21,22

Multiple insomnia animal models have been established, including environmental intervention, chronic restraint stress, and pharmacological induction. Among them, the para-chlorophenylalanine (PCPA)-induced model is the most extensively utilized and has been thoroughly validated in our previous experiments.23 PCPA depletes central 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) by inhibiting tryptophan hydroxylase, accompanied by elevated dopamine (DA) levels in the amygdala and posterior hypothalamus, thereby disrupting the sleep–wake cycle and reliably producing an insomnia-like phenotype. Moreover, 5-HT can drive the transcription and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines via the NLRP3-GSDMDpathway, consequently triggering neuroinflammation.24 As the pivotal center for integrating sleep–wake regulation, the hypothalamus, when studied with this model, allows concurrent investigation of sleep-related parameters and the underlying neuroinflammatory mechanisms. Therefore, the present study utilized a PCPA-induced rat insomnia model and targeted the hypothalamus as the brain region of interest for subsequent analysis.

Acupuncture, as a non-pharmacological intervention, has demonstrated favorable clinical efficacy in improving sleep quality.25,26 According to traditional Chinese medicine theory, the core pathogenesis of insomnia lies in the disharmony between yin and yang; therefore, acupuncture treatment aims to restore this balance and calm the mind. Based on the “Tiao Shu An Shen” (regulating the pivot and tranquilizing the mind) academic concept proposed by Professor Wu Qingming and our team’s previous research, we continued to select Baihui (GV20), Benshen (GB13), Shenmen (HT7), and Sanyinjiao (SP6) as the acupoint prescription. GV20 and GB13, both located on the head, are believed to regulate mental and spirit activities; HT7, the yuan-source point of the Heart meridian, is specifically indicated for calming the mind; and SP6, the intersection point of the three yin meridians, harmonizes the yin. An international evidence-based clinical practice guideline integrating multidisciplinary consensus recommends 2 Hz continuous-wave electroacupuncture for insomnia and identifies GV20, HT7, and SP6 as core acupoints for this condition.27 Furthermore, neuroimaging and functional connectivity studies have confirmed that acupuncture at GV20, HT7, and SP6 can remodel functional brain networks and modulate signaling pathways related to neuroinflammation and sleep–wake regulation.2 Together, these theoretical foundations and evidence-based findings provide a solid rationale for the acupoint selection and electroacupuncture protocol employed in the present study.

Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), an important pattern recognition receptor expressed on microglia, can signal through the myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88)-dependent pathway to activate nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), promoting the transcription and release of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6, thus forming a positive feedback loop of neuroinflammation. Our preliminary research also confirmed that electroacupuncture can reduce inflammatory cytokine levels in the hypothalamus of insomnia rats and suppress abnormal microglial activation.23 However, whether this effect is related to the modulation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB p65 signaling pathway requires further investigation. To specifically interrogate this pathway, we included a TAK-242 (HY-11109) group (3 mg/kg, i.p.), a dose previously shown to achieve effective central TLR4 blockade in ischemic models.28

Therefore, this study aims to test the following hypothesis: The mechanism by which electroacupuncture improves PCPA-induced insomnia involves inhibiting the excessive activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB p65 signaling pathway, thereby suppressing M1 microglial activation and ultimately reducing hypothalamic neuroinflammation. We systematically validate this hypothesis using multi-dimensional indicators to provide a solid molecular immunology foundation for electroacupuncture in treating insomnia.

Material and Methods

Research Object

Forty Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (20 males and 20 females) with initial body weights ranging from 160 to 180 g were used in this study. All animals were provided by the Experimental Animal Center of Hunan University of Chinese Medicine (Production license No.: SYXK (Xiang) 2019–0009). The rats were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) barrier environment under controlled conditions: temperature 24±2°C, humidity 50%±10%, and a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle. Food and water were available ad libitum throughout the study. During the 7-day acclimatization period, all rats were gently handled daily for 5 min to minimize handling stress during subsequent procedures.

Main Reagents And Instruments

Reagents

P-chlorophenylalanine, TLR4 inhibitor (TAK-242, HY-11109) (MCE Bio); Pentobarbital sodium (Sigma Aldrich, USA); rat IL-6 and TNF-α ELISA detection kits (Jiangsu Feiya Biotechnology Co., Ltd.); Column-based WB-specific animal cell and tissue total protein extraction kit (RS0024), ready-to-use mouse/rabbit universal secondary antibody (Immunoway Company); primary antibodies: rabbit/mouse TNF-α, IL-6, NF-κB p65, MyD88, TLR4, ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba-1), CD86 (Affinity Company); β-actin primary antibody (1:5000, Affinity Company).

Instruments

Disposable acupuncture needles (0.25mm × 25mm, Suzhou Acupuncture and Moxibustion Supplies Co., Ltd.); Electroacupuncture therapeutic apparatus (Huatuo SDZ - IV, Suzhou acupuncture and moxibustion Supplies Co., Ltd.); Ultrasonic machine (F-010S, Shenzhen Fuyang Technology Co., LtD.); Fluorescence Imaging System (LICOR Company); Fluorescence Quantitative PCR Instrument (Thermo, USA); Electrophoresis System (Bio-Rad Company); Imaging System, Upright Fluorescence Microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Animal Grouping and Model Preparation

After 7 days of adaptation, 40 rats were randomly divided into blank control group (n = 10, 5 females and 5 males) and model reserve group (n = 30, 15 females and 15 males) using a computer-generated random number table. Allocation concealment was ensured by having an independent researcher prepare the coded group assignments. The rat model of insomnia was prepared by intraperitoneal injection of PCPA29: Briefly, 5% NaHCO3 solution was slowly added to normal saline until the pH reached 7–8, then PCPA was dissolved, followed by the addition of gum arabic to form a suspension. Rats in the PCPA treatment group received PCPA (500 mg/kg, 10 mL/kg) intraperitoneally at 8:00–9:00 AM once daily for 2 consecutive days. The Control group received an equal volume of normal saline. Twelve hours after the last injection, behavioral changes were observed, and a pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, i.p.) righting reflex test was performed to confirm model establishment. Compared with the control group, the PCPA-treated rats exhibited restlessness, disrupted circadian rhythm, increased reactivity to stimuli, and significantly prolonged sleep latency (P < 0.01) with shortened sleep duration (P < 0.01). All rats in the model reserve group were successfully modeled; no animals were excluded.

Following model validation, the 30 modeled rats were re-randomized into three groups (n = 10 per group) using a random number table with balanced sex distribution: model group (5 females, 5 males), EA group (5 females, 5 males), and TAK-242 group (5 females, 5 males). The sample size of 10 per group was determined based on our preliminary data on sleep duration (effect size d ≈ 1.5, power = 0.8, α = 0.05) and in accordance with the reduction principle of animal ethics.

Methods of Intervention

All interventions were performed during the light phase (8:00 AM–12:00 PM) to standardize circadian influences. The experimenters performing the interventions were not involved in outcome assessments.

EA Group

Starting on the third day after the first PCPA injection (i.e, after model confirmation), rats received EA treatment. Acupoints were selected according to Experimental Acupuncture30 and the rat acupoint atlas by Hua Xingbang:31 Baihui (GV20) was needled subcutaneously at a 15° angle to a depth of 1 mm; bilateral Benshen (GB13) were needled at a 45°angle to a depth of 3 mm; bilateral Shenmen (HT7) and bilateral Sanyinjiao (SP6) were needled perpendicularly to a depth of 2 mm. The ipsilateral HT7 was connected to the positive electrode and SP6 to the negative electrode of the EA apparatus. Continuous wave mode at 2 Hz was used, and the intensity was adjusted to evoke mild local muscle twitches. The treatment lasted 20 min daily for 7 consecutive days. Concurrently, rats received an intraperitoneal injection of normal saline (3 mL/kg) once daily for 7 days.

TAK-242 Group

Rats received an intraperitoneal injection of TAK-242 (3 mg/kg, 3 mL/kg) once daily for 7 days. During the injection period, animals were restrained for 20 min daily, matching the handling time of the EA group.

Model Group

Rats underwent daily restraint for 20 min over 7 days without EA stimulation and received an intraperitoneal injection of normal saline (3 mL/kg) once daily for 7 days.

Control Group

Rats were not subjected to any intervention but were handled similarly during daily health checks.

Anesthesia and Euthanasia

The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Hunan University of Traditional Chinese Medicine Animal Ethics Committee (Approval No.: LL2022110809). At the end of the experiment (24 h after the last intervention), rats were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, i.p), and the depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of a toe-pinch reflex. Rats were then euthanized by cervical dislocation, performed by trained personnel in accordance with American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) guidelines. Death was confirmed by cessation of heartbeat and respiration. Tissues were harvested immediately thereafter.

Observation Indicators and Testing Methods

General Condition

Mental state, coat color, irritability, daytime activity, and body weight were recorded daily at the same time. Body weight gain was calculated as the difference between the current and previous measurements.

Sleep Latency and Total Sleep Duration

The pentobarbital sodium righting reflex test was used to evaluate sleep parameters. At two time points—after model establishment (to confirm model) and 24 h after the final intervention—rats were injected with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, i.p). Sleep latency (time from injection to loss of righting reflex) and total sleep duration (time from loss to return of righting reflex) were recorded by an observer blinded to group allocation.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta under deep anesthesia and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Serum levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All samples were assayed in duplicate, and the operator was blinded to group identity.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Frozen hypothalamic sections (20 μm thick) were prepared. From each rat, 4 serial coronal sections containing the hypothalamus (bregma −1.80 mm to −3.80 mm) were collected. After antigen retrieval, blocking, and permeabilization, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Iba-1 (1:300) and CD86 (1:300). Following PBS washes, sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies and counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a slide scanner, and for each section, 3 non-overlapping fields within the hypothalamus were randomly selected for analysis. The average fluorescence intensity of Iba-1/CD86 co-localization was quantified using ImageJ 1.53q by a researcher blinded to the experimental groups. The final value for each animal represents the mean of 12 measurements (4 sections × 3 fields).

Western Blot Assay

Hypothalamic tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer, and protein concentration was determined. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (200 mA, 90 min). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 60 min and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against TNF-α (1:1000), IL-6 (1:1000), TLR4 (1:1000), MyD88 (1:1000), NF-κB p65 (1:1000), and β-actin (1:5000). After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000, 37°C, 1 h), protein bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence. Gray-scale values were measured with ImageJ and normalized to β-actin. The experiment was repeated three times, and all gel analyses were performed by a blinded investigator.

Real-Time Fluorescence Quantitative (RT-PCR) Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from hypothalamic tissue using TRIzol reagent and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green master mix with the following protocol: 95°C for 10 min, then 40 cycles of 95°C for 10s and 60°C for 30s. Relative gene expression was calculated by the 2^−ΔΔCt method with β-actin as the internal control. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Each sample was run in triplicate, and the experimenter was blinded to group assignments.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.5. Normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed by Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Because both male and female rats were included with a balanced design, primary outcomes (sleep parameters, ELISA, immunofluorescence, Western blot, and RT-qPCR data) were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with group and sex as fixed factors. When a significant interaction was detected, simple main effects were examined. If no interaction was found, main effects of group were reported, followed by post hoc comparisons using the LSD test (equal variances) or Tamhane’s T3 test (unequal variances). For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. A two-tailed P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Body Weight

Body weight analysis revealed that the body weights of rats in the Model, TAK-242, and EA groups were significantly lower than those in the Control group (P<0.01). After the intervention, weight gain in the EA and inhibitor groups was significantly greater than that in the model group (P<0.01), whereas no significant difference was observed between the EA and inhibitor groups (P>0.05) (Figure 1A).

Electroacupuncture Improves Insomnia-Like Behavior in PCPA-Treated Rats

After model establishment, PCPA-treated rats exhibited disrupted circadian rhythms, heightened photosensitivity, frequent aggressive behaviors, exaggerated responses to restraint, and dry, lackluster fur. Following the 7-day intervention, both the electroacupuncture (EA) and TAK-242 (inhibitor) groups showed behavioral improvements, including enhanced emotional stability, restored fur glossiness, and reduced aggressive behaviors, whereas the model group remained hyperreactive.

The pentobarbital sodium righting reflex test quantified sleep latency and total sleep duration. As shown in Figure 1B and C, the model group displayed significantly prolonged sleep latency and shortened total sleep duration compared with the control group (P<0.01), confirming the validity of the insomnia model. Both the EA group and the inhibitor group showed significantly reduced sleep latency and increased sleep duration relative to the model group (P<0.01). Notably, there was no statistically significant difference between the EA and inhibitor groups for either parameter (P>0.05).

Electroacupuncture Reduces Serum Inflammatory Cytokines

Serum concentrations of TNF-α and IL-6 were measured by ELISA. The model group exhibited markedly elevated levels of both cytokines compared with the control group (P < 0.01). Both EA and TAK-242 interventions significantly reduced TNF-α and IL-6 levels relative to the model group (P < 0.01). These results indicate that both EA and TAK-242 effectively suppressed the release of inflammatory cytokines, although the effect of EA was weaker than that of TAK-242 (P < 0.05, Figure 1D and E).

Electroacupuncture Suppresses M1 Microglial Activation in the Hypothalamus

Immunofluorescence co-labeling of Iba-1 (pan-microglial marker) and CD86 (M1 phenotype marker) was performed to assess microglial activation status in the hypothalamus. The model group exhibited a significant increase in CD86 fluorescence intensity and Iba-1/CD86 co-localization compared with the control group (P < 0.01, P < 0.05). Both EA and inhibitor treatments significantly decreased CD86 expression and Iba-1/CD86 co-expression compared with the model group (P < 0.01, P < 0.05). Importantly, the reduction achieved by EA was comparable to that of the inhibitor; no statistically significant difference was found between these two groups (P > 0.05). Representative immunofluorescence images are presented in Figure 1F and G. For optimal visual confirmation of the quantified differences, brightness and contrast were uniformly adjusted across all panels without selective enhancement.

Electroacupuncture Downregulates the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB p65 Pathway at the Protein and mRNA Levels

RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that the mRNA expression of TNF-α, IL-6, TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB p65 was significantly elevated in the model group compared with controls (P < 0.01). Both EA and inhibitor interventions significantly suppressed the transcription of these genes relative to the model group (P < 0.01). The TAK-242 group showed a significantly greater reduction in the mRNA levels of these molecules than the EA group (P < 0.01; Figure 2A). Similarly, Western blot analysis of hypothalamic tissue revealed that, relative to the control group, the model group had significantly upregulated protein expression of TNF-α, IL-6, TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB p65 (P < 0.01). Both EA and TAK-242 significantly reduced the protein levels of all these molecules compared with the model group (P < 0.01). Notably, the TAK-242 group exhibited a more pronounced downregulation of these proteins than the EA group, with statistically significant differences observed for all targets (P < 0.01). Representative blots and quantitative analysis are shown in Figure 2B and C. These findings indicate that, while both interventions effectively modulated the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB p65 pathway, TAK-242 exerted a stronger inhibitory effect than EA at both the protein and transcriptional levels under the present experimental conditions.

Summary of Multi-Level Evidence

Collectively, these findings provide converging behavioral, serological, histological, and molecular evidence that electroacupuncture, like the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242, effectively ameliorates insomnia-like symptoms and neuroinflammation in PCPA-treated rats, associated with the suppression of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB p65 signaling pathway and M1 microglial activation.

Discussion

Currently, insomnia has become a global public health challenge, with its treatment primarily falling into two categories: pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. While pharmacological treatments can improve sleep in the short term, long-term use may lead to side effects such as drug dependence, increased tolerance, residual daytime effects, and cognitive impairment, along with a high relapse rate after discontinuation.32,33 Non-pharmacological treatments, such as cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), are recommended as first-line therapies. However, they face limitations in implementation, including longer treatment durations, insufficient professional resources, and varying patient compliance, making widespread adoption challenging.34 Therefore, exploring safe, effective, and mechanistically clear non-pharmacological treatments holds significant clinical value.

This study systematically evaluated the effects of EA on sleep behavior and the TLR4/MyD88/NF‑κB p65 signaling pathway, as well as associated neuroinflammation, in the hypothalamus of PCPA‑induced insomnia rats. Behavioral, serological, histological, and molecular evidence consistently demonstrates that EA can effectively ameliorate sleep disturbances in this model, and that this effect is associated with the suppression of M1 microglial‑driven neuroinflammation.

The behavioral observations revealed that PCPA-treated rats exhibited circadian rhythm disruption and hyperirritability, manifested as prolonged sleep latency and shortened total sleep duration, thereby validating the model and providing a reliable phenotypic basis for subsequent mechanistic investigation.

At the molecular level, ELISA revealed that the pro‑inflammatory cytokines TNF‑α and IL‑6 were significantly elevated in the serum of model rats, confirming a systemic inflammatory state. More importantly, direct analysis of hypothalamic tissue demonstrated a consistent upregulation of TNF‑α and IL‑6 at both the protein and mRNA levels, providing converging evidence for local neuroinflammation within the sleep‑regulatory centers. These findings align with the concept that insomnia can trigger an “inflammatory storm”.15 The concordance between peripheral cytokine levels and central tissue expression suggests that systemic inflammation may, in part, mirror the neuroinflammatory processes occurring in the hypothalamus. However, it must be emphasized that the Western blot and RT‑qPCR data from hypothalamic homogenates offer a more direct and region‑specific assessment of central inflammation than serum ELISA, as they are not confounded by the restrictive nature of the blood–brain barrier or by cytokine production from peripheral immune cells. The parallel elevation at the transcriptional and translational levels further underscores the robust activation of the local innate immune response. Sustained upregulation of these inflammatory mediators can directly disrupt the function of the hypothalamus and other sleep‑wake regulatory nuclei, potentially contributing to the chronicity of insomnia and related emotional and cognitive disturbances.35,36

Our data further demonstrate that the hypothalamic inflammatory response is tightly linked to activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB p65 pathway. TLR4, a key pattern recognition receptor on microglia, is activated under insomnia-associated stress and signals through the adaptor MyD88 to trigger NF-κB nuclear translocation, driving the transcription of TNF-α, IL-6, and other pro-inflammatory factors. These findings are consistent with prior research on sleep-deprivation-induced microglial activation,37 collectively indicating that sleep disruption can act as an intrinsic stressor that directly initiates central neuroimmune cascades.

Immunofluorescence staining showed a marked increase in the co-expression of Iba-1-positive microglia and CD86, an M1 phenotype marker, in the hypothalamus of model rats. This indicates that insomnia drives microglia toward a pro-inflammatory M1 activation state. Activated M1 microglia, as the principal executors of central neuroinflammation, release substantial quantities of the aforementioned cytokines, thereby creating and perpetuating a local inflammatory microenvironment. Thus, M1 microglial activation represents a critical cellular event in the pathological progression of insomnia.

Notably, electroacupuncture demonstrated effects similar to those of a TLR4-specific inhibitor in improving sleep quality and suppressing neuroinflammation. This finding, from a pharmacological perspective, suggests that modulating the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κBp65 pathway may be one of the key mechanisms through which electroacupuncture exerts its therapeutic effects. Based on these observations, we have preliminarily outlined a potential signaling pathway: insomnia-induced stress activates TLR4 in hypothalamic microglia, triggering the MyD88/NF-κB p65 downstream signaling cascade. This activation promotes the polarization of microglia toward the M1 phenotype, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6. These factors disrupt the homeostasis of the sleep-wake regulatory center and result in persistent sleep structure disturbances. Electroacupuncture may intervene at the upstream stages of this pathway, thereby inhibiting the amplification of inflammatory signals.

The novelty of this work resides in the integration of the traditional Chinese medicine theory of insomnia—rooted in yin-yang imbalance—with the modern neuroimmunological TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB p65 signaling axis. By providing converging behavioral, cellular, and molecular evidence within a single experimental framework that includes a pharmacological comparator, this study offers experimental support for the intrinsic links among acupuncture, immune regulation, and sleep improvement. However, the specific neuroimmune targets of EA remain to be fully defined.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study providesconvergingbehavioral, serological, histological, and molecular evidence that electroacupuncture effectively ameliorates insomnia-like phenotypes in PCPA-induced rats, an effectattributable tosuppression of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB p65 signaling cascade, inhibition of M1 microglial polarization, andconsequentattenuation of hypothalamic neuroinflammation. Notably, electroacupunctureachieved comparable efficacyto the selective TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 in improving sleep parameters and reducing microglial activation, althoughTAK-242 exerted a more pronounced inhibitory effect on downstream inflammatory cytokine expression. Collectively, these findings deepen our understanding of the neuroinflammatory mechanismsunderpinninginsomnia andfurnisha solid molecular immunological basis for the clinical application of electroacupuncture as a non-pharmacological intervention.

Nevertheless, several limitationswarrant considerationwhen interpreting these results. The lack of a sham electroacupuncture control group precludesdefinitive dissociationof specific effects from non-specific factors, including needle insertion, skin stimulation, and restraint stress. Moreover, the use ofonly a single electroacupuncture protocol and one dose of TAK-242 leaves dose–response and parameter–response relationships unexplored. In addition, while our dataconfirmsuppression of the MyD88-dependent branch, the potential involvement of the TRIF-dependent arm of TLR4 signalingremains to be determined, and the absence of M2 microglial markerspreventsa comprehensive assessment of M1/M2 polarization balance. Although both male and female rats were included with balanced distribution, the sample size per sex was insufficient for robust sex-stratified analyses; furthermore, the lack of polysomnography and in vivo calcium imaging limits objective evaluation of sleep architecture and real-time neuroimmune dynamics.

Looking forward, severalavenuesmerit priority in future research. First, the application of TRIF-knockout or pharmacological intervention models would help clarify whether electroacupuncture also modulates the TRIF-dependent arm of TLR4 signaling. Second, simultaneous quantification of both M1 and M2 markers is essential to determine whether electroacupuncture actively shifts microglial polarization toward a neuroprotective phenotype. Third, upstream regulatory pathways governing TLR4 expression or activity—such as microRNA-mediated regulation, post-translational modifications, or endogenous ligand release—should be explored toidentifythe molecular nodes targeted by electroacupuncture. Fourth, incorporating polysomnography and fiber photometry or miniaturized calcium imaging in freely behaving animals would enable objective, real-time assessment of sleep stage transitions and neuronal activity within sleep–wake circuits, thereby bridging the gap between molecular events and network dynamics. Finally, future studies with larger sample sizes and adequate sex stratification are warranted to examine potential sex differences in electroacupuncture responsiveness, which mayguidepersonalized therapeutic strategies.
