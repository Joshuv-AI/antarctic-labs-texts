Patient perspectives on low back pain treatment in primary care: a qualitative study of hopes, expectations, and experiences
Authors: Simon Dyrløv Madsen, Mette Jensen Stochkendahl, Lars Morsø, Merethe Kirstine Andersen, Elisabeth Assing Hvidt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients’ hopes and expectations for low back pain treatment influence their consultation experiences and treatment outcomes. These hopes and expectations may evolve over time, potentially leading to a shift in what patients consider important before and after a consultation. Understanding the distinction between hopes and expectations, and how they evolve is important for improving patient care. This study explored patients’ hopes and expectations prior to LBP consultations and examined how these were reflected in their post-consultation experiences.MethodsWe employed a qualitative design consisting of individual semi-structured pre- and post-consultation interviews with patients seeking care for low back pain from general practitioners, physiotherapists, and chiropractors in Denmark. A convenience sample of 18 patients (10 females and 8 males) aged between 28 and 79 years were interviewed about their hopes and expectations before a consultation and their experiences immediately following the consultation. Data were analysed employing Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis.ResultsTwo themes with five subthemes were developed: (1) “Something needs to be done,” which included subthemes related to life disruption, expectations of clinical assessment, and the clinicians as experts; and (2) “Experiences and emotional responses to the interaction with the clinician”, highlighting the importance of trust and the personal attributes of clinicians. Patients initially sought urgent help and clarity regarding their condition but shifted their focus post-consultation to the relational dynamics and emotional engagement experienced during interactions with clinicians.ConclusionsThis study contributes to the understanding of how patients’ hopes and expectations regarding low back pain consultations evolve, shifting from a focus on clinical actions and outcomes to valuing interpersonal relationships and emotional support from clinicians. Recognising these shifts can enhance clinician-patient interactions and improve overall patient satisfaction and treatment outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-024-08116-3.

Background

When patients seek care for low back pain (LBP) or other musculoskeletal pain conditions, their hopes, expectations, and experiences shape their perceived treatment effects [1–4] and impact patient-reported outcomes [5]. For example, positive recovery expectations are associated with improved outcomes in both chiropractic [3] and physiotherapy settings [6].

Hopes and expectations are both associated with positive outcomes, and the terms are often used interchangeably. However, despite being closely related, they are separate constructs; hopes represent what patients prefer, while expectations reflect what they believe is likely to happen [7]. The distinction is important, as hope has been shown to act as a central catalyst for recovery [8]. Specifically, for LBP, restoring patients’ hopes can improve prognostic and behaviour-related factors, such as reducing fear of LBP, enhancing understanding of the mind-body connection, and encouraging self-pacing [9]. Additionally, fostering hope through positive communication during consultations can help reduce pain in LBP patients [8, 10]. These examples highlight that hopes and expectations are dynamic constructs that can evolve and be modified during the clinical encounter [7].

When neither hopes nor expectations are met during consultations, patients are more likely to have a negative experience [11]. Patients with LBP often struggle with unpredictable pain fluctuations and seek help to better understand and gain control over their pain [12, 13]. As a result, they are often disappointed when a clear explanation of the pain’s cause is not or cannot be provided [11]. The relational dynamics between patient and clinician, particularly supportive communication, can positively influence patient satisfaction and experiences related to pain, disability, and quality of life [14–16]. However, patients and their clinicians may adhere to different models of illness (e.g., biomedical vs. biopsychosocial) and have differing treatment goals (e.g., reducing pain vs. improving daily functioning), which can lead to negative patient experiences.

Previous quantitative and qualitative studies have primarily focused on patient experiences after LBP consultations, but findings have been extrapolated and interpreted as representing patients’ expectations or needs before such consultations [17–19]. While learning from patient experiences is important for improving clinical practice and patient outcomes, the absence of pre-consultation perspectives may limit our understanding of what patients need. By considering patients’ initial hopes and expectations, and how they evolve as a result of consultation experiences, we foster the opportunity to identify key components of patients’ needs and we may reveal valuable insights into the shaping of patient experiences that would otherwise go unnoticed. Therefore, this study explored patients’ hopes and expectations prior to LBP consultations and examined how these are reflected in their post-consultation experiences.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative design with an interpretive approach was chosen, seeking to understand the patients’ reasoning, underlying motivations and progressions of patients’ hopes and expectations. To explore this, interviews with patients were conducted before and after LBP consultations with a general practitioner (GP), physiotherapist, or chiropractor. The study adhered to the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) principles [20].

Setting and participants

In Denmark, patients with LBP pain have three public primary healthcare entry points, namely via GPs, physiotherapists, and chiropractors. GPs are free of charge, while consultations with physiotherapists and chiropractors involve out-of-pocket expenses for the patient. The types of treatment provided by the three professions vary considerably both within and across the professions [21].

Sampling and recruitment

The study was conducted from January to September 2022 following COVID-19 lockdowns, limiting access to patients with LBP and clinicians. Therefore, we used a convenience sampling technique with eligibility criteria: adults (+ 18 years) with non-specific LBP of any duration. We asked two GPs (one clinic), three physiotherapists (two clinics), and three chiropractors (two clinics) and their administrative staff from our professional networks to identify and facilitate contact with eligible patients who scheduled an LBP consultation in the respective clinics, but otherwise to manage the patients habitually. The principal investigator contacted interested patients by phone and provided information about the aim and content of the study, which they consented to participate in. We used the principle of information power to inform the sample size, which is guided by the broadness of the aim, participants’ characteristics, applied theory, quality of dialogue and analytic strategy. Fewer informants were needed due to the narrow study aim and longitudinal exploration [22].

Data generation

Methods for data generation included individual pre- and post-consultation interviews with patients, researchers’ field notes, and reflective notes related to immediate impressions of the interviews. The interviews were supported by an adaptive semi-structured interview guide developed for this study (Additional file 1) and were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Before consultations, the patients were interviewed about their hopes and expectations before the consultation and the reason behind their care-seeking. After consultations, they were asked to describe their experiences with the consultation. To ensure privacy and non-interference by clinical staff members, interviews were conducted over the phone at a preferred time for the patient. The clinicians were instructed to manage patients habitually.

SDM, a male chiropractor, PhD candidate, and primary investigator, conducted the interviews. SDM had clinical experience and was familiar with the clinical settings, jargon, and healthcare system. Patients were unknown to the principal investigator, who was introduced to patients as a researcher. The co-authors supported the study design, interview guide development, discussions about preconceptions, and provided feedback on interview technique, transcripts, and coding. The author group represented diverse professional backgrounds (chiropractors, a physiotherapist with a master of public health, a GP and a sociologist) and had clinical and/or qualitative research experience.

Ethical considerations

According to Danish legislation regarding medical research, interview studies are exempt from ethical approval. The Regional Ethics Committee of Southern Denmark confirmed that ethical approval was not required (file no. 20222000-11). Patients gave informed, oral consent over the phone to participate before the telephone interviews commenced. Subsequently, written patient consent was obtained at the clinics before the consultation, where patients were also given an opt-out option. The authorised legal department at the University of Southern Denmark approved data collection, processing, and storage (ID #11.226) following the European Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Theme 1. Something needs to be done

Subtheme 1a: Life disruption leads to a hope for urgent help

Before consultations, some patients were hoping that the clinician would ‘do something’. The hopes were motivated by a desperation for action prompted by painful experiences, a desire to return to everyday life and a longing for diagnostic closure. For most patients, care-seeking was triggered by experiencing severe back pain described by some patients as ‘worse than ever’ or feelings that ‘something is wrong’. The pain was impeding the patients in their daily activities and depriving them of sleep to the extent that they could no longer endure. Some had unsuccessfully tried to manage the situation by themselves using previously successful self-management strategies like taking walks or waiting it out. As time passed without improvements, their increasing hope for help fuelled their need for action.Ehm, it’s simply because I thought, this needs to be taken care of. Some treatment is needed to… for me to be human, right, and not live on pain medication to make it through the day. [P6, physiotherapy]

Ehm, it’s simply because I thought, this needs to be taken care of. Some treatment is needed to… for me to be human, right, and not live on pain medication to make it through the day. [P6, physiotherapy]

Patients’ accounts highlighted the profound impact of LBP, both physically, as it limited daily activities, and psychologically, leaving some mentally drained and pushed to their limits. This amplified their sense of urgency and hope that a solution could be found.

Some patients related their present care-seeking situation to previous negative consultations in which their expectations of finding the cause of pain, guidance or timely treatment had not been met.I think, sometimes, they close it [diagnostic procedures] down too quickly, like it is too expensive to refer you onwards in the system, like, that’s how you could sometimes feel. [P12, chiropractic]It might be better in 2 weeks, but it might be even worse and make the situation harder than what was really necessary to begin with. [P17, general practice]

I think, sometimes, they close it [diagnostic procedures] down too quickly, like it is too expensive to refer you onwards in the system, like, that’s how you could sometimes feel. [P12, chiropractic]

It might be better in 2 weeks, but it might be even worse and make the situation harder than what was really necessary to begin with. [P17, general practice]

In the two cases presented here, both patients were given a tentative diagnosis with no affirmative explanation about their condition, no follow-up appointments and, consequently, inadequate reassurance about their condition. Both patients were left distressed and anxious about the neglect of timely care and the worsening of their situation, as well as feeling frustrated and dismissed by ‘the system’. Feeling that ‘nothing had been done’ and being left to their device compelled patients to seek care elsewhere in the hope of finding urgent help.And I probably haven’t been insisting enough, so I have searched for my own path so to speak, to get some help [P12, chiropractic].

And I probably haven’t been insisting enough, so I have searched for my own path so to speak, to get some help [P12, chiropractic].

Experiencing feelings of abandonment and neglect resulting from previous healthcare consultations that had come to nothing seemed to cause patients to lower their expectations for receiving help. When feeling left to their own device, realistic expectations waned, and only hope remained.

Subtheme 1b: Hope for clarity drives expectations of a clinical assessment

Most patients prioritised knowledge of ‘what can be done’ over a diagnosis, whereas some felt that knowing ‘what it is’ was essential. Generally, patients were confident that visual and manual assessment of their backs would establish their diagnosis. Further, repeated assessments to monitor symptoms and treatment progress were also expected in ongoing treatment trajectories.And normally, they can actually see… and sometimes it’s not even where you feel the pain. I’m kind of hoping that we get a look at how it all looks with/in my back. If there is anything completely tight/tense or just a pinched nerve or something. [P16, chiropractic]

And normally, they can actually see… and sometimes it’s not even where you feel the pain. I’m kind of hoping that we get a look at how it all looks with/in my back. If there is anything completely tight/tense or just a pinched nerve or something. [P16, chiropractic]

The above excerpt also exemplifies that some patients were expecting the pain to be related to structural impairments that were palpable for the clinicians. Other patients were hopeful that their clinician could see or touch their pain to validate their situation by looking and touching their backs.The expectation is also that she can see that something is wrong […] that it’s not all imaginary, but that she can see (that) there is something, or feel something. It’s also nice to know that you don’t just…THINK that there is something [wrong], and then they [the clinician] can’t find anything. [P1, chiropractic]

Some trusted GPs as the first choice to rule out particular diagnoses: “I was at my own GP first because I was afraid it was a slipped disc or arthritis in the hip” [P6, physiotherapy]. Others expressed that their expectations of the GPs’ diagnostic competencies were not always met.So before, when I’ve had something with my feet or back or something, I’ve never gotten the impression that the doctor knew awfully much about it, like in detail. [P11, chiropractic]

Another patient with recurrent LBP and temporary pain relief from treatments lacked trust in the diagnostic skills of primary care clinicians and expected referral to diagnostic imaging and ‘specialist care’. When previous care had not adequately met the expectations of the patient, she continued to seek clarity.But I hope she [the GP] will refer me to an x-ray because my physio has asked for it, and then I think I would like to see some back specialists, who could like see if they could do something about it. [P18, general practice]

But I hope she [the GP] will refer me to an x-ray because my physio has asked for it, and then I think I would like to see some back specialists, who could like see if they could do something about it. [P18, general practice]

While the label ‘specialist’ in some interviews was linked with secondary care and diagnostic imaging, the patients expressed various perceptions of what expertise and competency encompass. While hopes of a diagnosis were a prerequisite for some patients, the following subtheme illustrates deep trust in clinicians’ treatment competencies and expectations of recovery, introducing a contrasting perception of specialists.

Subtheme 1c: Patients expect clinicians to be experts and provide a solution

Some patients consulting physiotherapists and chiropractors expected to immediately receive ‘hands-on’ treatment, including ‘cracking the back’, ‘releasing tensions’, or massage for pain resolution. These patients motivated their expectations by previous positive experiences with manual therapy or linked physical symptoms like a ‘stiff’ spine in need of ‘loosening’ with physical treatment. Self-management interventions or exercise instructions were less often mentioned, primarily as side notes. For one patient, the frustration from failed preventive efforts and self-management attempts led to a conviction that some manual treatment was needed.I just think it is really unfair. Why should I get back pain when I’m so good at doing exercises […] so I think there should be some physical treatment from some therapist because I think I do so much myself. [P8, physiotherapy]

I just think it is really unfair. Why should I get back pain when I’m so good at doing exercises […] so I think there should be some physical treatment from some therapist because I think I do so much myself. [P8, physiotherapy]

Others did not have specific expectations regarding what their treatment should entail but described how they relied on the clinician, or so-called specialists or experts, to decide the appropriate means to alleviate their symptoms.They are the specialists in that sort of thing, right? So that is why I want to find a chiropractor and have it fixed […] I don’t expect anything. I just expect he can fix me. [P15, chiropractic]

They are the specialists in that sort of thing, right? So that is why I want to find a chiropractor and have it fixed […] I don’t expect anything. I just expect he can fix me. [P15, chiropractic]

The patient’s statement exemplifies an understanding of LBP as a biomedical condition that a clinician could resolve, but otherwise, minimal intention to participate in healthcare decisions. When asked to reflect on what the clinician should do to ‘fix him’, the patient said: “But it is for him to decide how to get it fixed” [P15, chiropractic]. The excerpt is complemented by other patients expecting the clinician to decide what treatment should entail.

Theme 2. Experiences and emotional responses to the interaction with the clinician

The patients’ accounts of their post-consultation experiences centred around how the interaction with the clinicians made them feel and the bodily sensation of the clinician’s manual examination. This highlighted a shift of perspectives towards attaching importance to relational aspects of clinicians’ emotional and personal engagement during consultations.

Subtheme 2a. Trust in the clinician arises from thoroughness and acknowledgement of the patient

Regardless of the type of clinician sought, the interviewed patients’ experiences were predominantly positive. Many accounts revolved around the physical examination and their carefulness and thoroughness.It’s the thoroughness in his examination of my body. That is, he really gets to the bottom of this, completely, and keeps trying. [P8, physiotherapy]

It’s the thoroughness in his examination of my body. That is, he really gets to the bottom of this, completely, and keeps trying. [P8, physiotherapy]

The patients often contrasted their experience with previous consultations in which the clinician had been less meticulous. The patients interpreted this as a lack of engagement in their situation or lack of acknowledgement. Thoroughness was particularly noted concerning situations when the clinician had ‘worked their way to the bottom of the pain’ by using a variety of examination approaches. Patients emphasised that the clinician had visually inspected their back and used hands-on techniques to identify sore or painful spots. They attached significance to the connection between their bodily pain and how clinicians could apply manual pressure on the tissue and ‘find their pain’. It was reassuring for the patients that the clinician knew ‘where to look’ and recognised their problem: ”[…] she believes in what I say and actually examines me” [P17, general practice]. In continuation, another patient described how being heard and understood was reassuring.When I’m telling what I have noticed, she knows what it is. So, she is good at putting something into action that matches that and works with what is left to work with […] it’s like she is able to read it. [P11, chiropractic]

When I’m telling what I have noticed, she knows what it is. So, she is good at putting something into action that matches that and works with what is left to work with […] it’s like she is able to read it. [P11, chiropractic]

The clinicians’ acknowledgement of the patient’s problem instilled trust in the patients. Especially when the clinician could reproduce the pain by manual examination, the patients described the clinicians as competent, professional and ‘somebody who knows where to find the problem’. This made patients experience the clinician as ‘dedicated’, ‘passionate’, and ‘someone who does a good job’. Further, the clinician’s acknowledgement and confirmation of the patient’s understanding reinforced the patient’s perception of the clinicians as knowledgeable and competent.I think I have a really good sense of [PT1’s] uhm, her professionalism, and… she is good at putting things right… in terms of what I have been thinking […] she seems very, very competent. [P9, physiotherapy]

I think I have a really good sense of [PT1’s] uhm, her professionalism, and… she is good at putting things right… in terms of what I have been thinking […] she seems very, very competent. [P9, physiotherapy]

Subtheme 2b. The personhood of the clinician is key

Patients with positive experiences attached several positive features to the clinician, such as being ‘kind, ‘open-minded’, ‘listening, ‘understanding’, ‘humorous’, ‘easy to talk to’, and ‘forthcoming’, all of which contributed to a relaxing atmosphere during the consultation.We have never seen each other before. Yet, well, it was almost like we knew each other, right. He certainly understands how to make people relax, by the way he is. [P13, chiropractic]

We have never seen each other before. Yet, well, it was almost like we knew each other, right. He certainly understands how to make people relax, by the way he is. [P13, chiropractic]

Other patients noticed how eye contact and the clinician’s approach made them feel like more than ‘just a number’.I don’t just get in and be put on the examination couch like in an assembly line. She is figuring out what is specifically going on today […] she listens, and then we work together, and she asks me during… is it too hard or is it too soft, or should I go deeper. So, in a way, I think it is a collaboration. [P10, physiotherapy]

I don’t just get in and be put on the examination couch like in an assembly line. She is figuring out what is specifically going on today […] she listens, and then we work together, and she asks me during… is it too hard or is it too soft, or should I go deeper. So, in a way, I think it is a collaboration. [P10, physiotherapy]

Some patients related the present experience with earlier negative experiences of feeling left out of the process or the clinician conducting treatment without including the patient. For example, one patient wanted to avoid a specific clinician because of an earlier episode where the patient had experienced the clinician as pushy and hard-handed: “The problem wasn’t the treatment as such. It was really more the person” [P7, physiotherapy].

When patients trusted and valued the clinician’s assessment, most patients described feeling reassured by the information provided regarding LBP and trusting that the pain was not a sign of serious illness. For example, a patient described how the clinician’s calmness and optimism were reassuring and instilled hope that there would be a solution to the problem: “I get the sense that we will get this under control” [P11, chiropractic]. However, for a few patients, the positive connection with the clinician and the information provided was not enough to reassure them.Right now, I’m still feeling insecure, will this get better or not, even though he [PT2] was really nice telling me several times that ‘you will get well’. [P8, physiotherapy]

Right now, I’m still feeling insecure, will this get better or not, even though he [PT2] was really nice telling me several times that ‘you will get well’. [P8, physiotherapy]

Thus, even though some trust was established relatively quickly during that one consultation, longer time or more consultations may be needed to overcome one’s distress and feel reassured.

Discussion

Summary of findings

While patients’ hopes and expectations centred around wanting clinicians to act, making sense of the LBP situation and seeking pain resolution, the patients’ perspective shifted after the consultations toward attaching importance to the clinicians’ personal attributes and interactional competencies, valorising being taken seriously and recognised as individuals. Before consultations, the patients’ hopes and expectations were motivated by the disabling impact of LBP on daily life. Many sought clarity of their symptoms and what could be done to alleviate them, make sense of the future implications of their condition, and, through that, establish new hopes and expectations for treatment outcomes. Most patients expected and trusted the clinician to act and alleviate their pain. However, for some patients, earlier negative experiences lowered expectations while still being hopeful for immediate actions. Experiences following consultations were predominantly positive. Examining the patient’s back and reproducing pain by touch were pivotal in building rapport with the patient. Furthermore, feelings of trust were helped by positive clinician traits and interaction abilities.

Comparison with other literature

The patients in this study sought care due to an urgent need for something to be done; a desperation brought on by their LBP disrupting their daily life. This finding aligns well with general care-seeking patterns, showing that pain intensity is only slightly associated with care-seeking, whereas stronger associations are observed for high levels of disability [24] and poor sleep [25]. According to Leung et al.’s framework of hopes and expectations [7], the sense of urgency may be brought on by the uncertainty of the situation and the patients’ hope for clarity. Also reflected in other qualitative studies, the perceived urgency of the situation and the need for an answer may not be related to the diagnosis per se (although this is how patients speak of it) but rather to the patient’s desire to make sense of the situation and reducing uncertainty regarding life aspects [26, 27]. Our findings may be interpreted similarly in that gaining knowledge about one’s condition is not a singular goal. Still, it is an important stepping stone for understanding prognosis and managing hopes and expectations about their future health and prospective treatment outcomes. The findings also lean into the expectation model by Laferton et al. [28] and the concepts of personal control and personalised outcome expectancy (behaviour expectations). They argue that illness beliefs about the cause, the expected symptom duration (timeline), and the anticipated consequences are critical to grasp if their condition can be self-managed (personal control) or managed by treatment (treatment control). Pain is persistent or recurrent for most patients with LBP, and self-management is increasingly considered foundational in LBP treatment [29]. Therefore, providing information about the prognosis, optimising behaviour-related expectations (e.g., regarding physical activity or exercise), and setting realistic goals about activities of daily living may be more beneficial than what a structural diagnosis offers. Understanding what LBP is and learning how to manage it may be a longer journey for some patients and require repeated information and reassurance, as shown in this study. Still, unfortunately, this happens only to a limited extent beyond initial visits in Danish primary healthcare settings [21].

The pre-consultation interviews showed that many patients relied heavily on the clinicians as experts, and some did not expect shared clinical decision-making. Some had expectations of what could be done but little or no desire to be involved in the more specific decision-making. Similar findings have been described by Stenner et al. [30] when patients are prescribed exercises. Patients were less inclined to participate in decisions regarding exercise type as it was perceived as a minor decision but also because they were not provided with alternatives [30]. Evidence suggests that patients are more likely to adhere to treatment plans and lifestyle changes and experience increased satisfaction when involved or choose their level of involvement in decisions regarding their health [31]. Further, timely follow-up and supervision may optimise conditions for improved clinical outcomes [32]. In this study, however, those refraining from decision-making may challenge clinicians in ensuring patient autonomy, patient involvement, and shared decision-making. This may contribute to clinician dependence and low self-efficacy, which is associated with poorer chances of pain reduction and improved quality of life [33].

Our findings show that patients’ perspectives on what is important for them and what they find likely to happen (their hopes and expectations, respectively) change from before to after the consultations; hopes and expectations were focused on actions and outcomes, while experiences were tied to the interpersonal process, patient values and perceived personal connection with the clinician. Therefore, clinicians’ enquiry and awareness of these changes may help them interpret patient expectations and address unspoken relational and acknowledgement needs through dialogue. Addressing and discussing patients’ hopes and expectations allows clinicians to tailor care to meet patients’ needs, clarify any misconceptions about the treatment or recovery process, enhance trust and increase patient compliance. Taken together, this helps provide more person-centred care.

Our findings align with previous studies on patient-clinician relationships, showing that patients appreciate when their clinician is empathetic, kind, humorous, and a good listener and communicator [17, 34–36]. Additionally, Rossettini et al. [31] described various characteristics of the relationship between clinicians and patients, such as being respected as an individual and the need for mutuality and consultive relationships that enhanced the patients’ sense of connection with their clinician. Miciak et al. [35] found that being present, receptive, genuine, and committed is foundational for clinicians in establishing therapeutic relationships. These findings resonate with our results, but these were not expressed as a conscious or spoken hope or expectation, as they were mainly expressed in the post-consultation interviews. We have identified only one other study that has explored both expectations before and experiences of LBP treatment [37]. Within a 6-month timeframe, Unsgaard-Tøndel et al. shows similar shifts in patients’ perspectives from outcome-related hopes and expectations to experiences centred on relational aspects. Our study adds to their finding by indicating that these shifts begin early on and in the initial consultations. Similar to our results, other authors have shown that patients with LBP often express modest expectations and frame hopes as potentially unrealistic outcomes, which can be interpreted as reflective of uncertainty regarding outcomes [38]. Therefore, when patients express little or unrealistic hope, it may be a cue for clinicians to assess or address patient uncertainty [37–39].

For many of our patients, both expectations and experiences of ‘getting to the core of the problem’ entailed a thorough visual and hands-on examination of their back and that the clinician was able to see or feel the pain. The careful examination and resulting pain experience seem to work in two directions. Firstly, the patient feels acknowledged and validated when the treatment is based on an action-response interaction and when it is both patient-centred and collaborative. The manual assessment is an integrated part of the clinical examination for most clinicians who see patients with LBP [40]. Miciak et al. [41] have described the patient’s body as a pivot point in establishing a connection between the patient and a physiotherapist. Conversely, we argue that the examination may also magnify a patient’s perception of pain as observable and palpable or confirm unhelpful beliefs at odds with current clinical understandings of LBP about a simple and causal biomedical condition. This is in potential conflict with Miciak et al. describing touch as respecting the patients’ bodily experiences and helping the patient become aware of and (re)connect to their bodies without detaching the body from the psychological and social aspects of the patient [41]. Although physical touch can be a means for clinicians to establish connections with patients, clinical findings from manual examinations have limited clinical and prognostic value for patients [42]. Therefore, clinicians may have a balancing act not to over-emphasise physical assessments and findings.

Strength and limitations

The strength of this study includes findings established across three clinician groups in primary care, across different patients with variations in LBP characteristics (duration, cause, pain intensity), including first-time and follow-up consultations. This strengthens the transferability of results to other settings with similar organisations and patient populations. It is possible that patients who came for follow-up consultations based their answers regarding hopes and expectations on recent experiences. However, the interviewer focused the questions on the present consultation to counter this. The study’s limitations include the gate-keeping sampling strategy where we cannot exclude that the clinicians or clinic staff selectively contacted patients with positive attitudes, resulting in selection bias. This may especially apply to patients undergoing a longer course of treatment sessions. Patients’ experiences were predominantly positive, while several had had negative healthcare experiences before this study. This paradox may be related to social desirability bias but may also reflect that negative experiences arise at other times in treatment trajectories, such as at discharge without pain resolution or clarity of the situation. Finally, we cannot exclude the possibility that paying a fee for healthcare services may change the perspectives of the patients both before and after consultations.

We included interviews with 18 patients. Given the study’s narrow aim and the specificity from the patients’ first-hand accounts, fewer interviews were needed, but the lack of applied theory and relatively short dialogues would require more interviews [21]. Based on these considerations, we believe the sample size and level of detail ensured sufficient information for the study. The study group consisted of a multidisciplinary group of experienced researchers (MJS, LM, MKA, and EAH) with clinical experience (MJS, LM and MKA). The researchers held various perspectives on clinical practice, which contributed to discussions in relation to the analysis at several meetings throughout the study.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the understanding of how patients’ hopes and expectations regarding low back pain consultations evolve, shifting from a focus on clinical actions and outcomes to valuing interpersonal relationships and emotional support from clinicians. Recognising these shifts can enhance clinician-patient interactions and improve overall patient satisfaction and treatment outcomes.
