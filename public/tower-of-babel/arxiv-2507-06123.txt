Abstract
. We consider autonomous Hamiltonian systems and present an algorithm to compute at the same time partially hyperbolic invariant tori (whiskered tori), as well as high-order expansions of their stable and unstable manifolds. Such whiskered tori have been shown to be important for transport phenomena in phase space. For instance, by following their invariant manifolds one could obtain zero-cost trajectories in space mission design. We present in detail the case when the (un)stable directions are one-dimensional. The strategy to compute tori and their invariant manifolds is based on the parameterization method. We formulate a functional equation for a parameterization of both the torus and its whiskers expressing that they are invariant. This equation is naturally discretized in Fourier-Taylor series or, equivalently, in a grid of Taylor series. Using a return map, we are reduced to study functions of n − 1 n-1 variables where n n is the number of degrees of freedom (the phase space is 2 ​ n 2n dimensional). Then, we implement a Newton-like method that converges quadratically. They key advantage of our approach is that, using geometric identities coming from the Hamiltonian nature of the problem, the algorithm has small storage requirements and a low operation count per step which is highly efficient. The simultaneous computation of the torus and the whiskers improves the efficiency and the stability of the algorithm. We present implementations and extensive numerical experiments in the Circular Restricted Three Body Problem.



1.
Introduction

Both in theory and applications, it is of great interest to
identify the asymptotic behavior of orbits of dynamical
systems—specially when the orbits approach invariant sets. Such
asymptotic convergence is described by the stable and unstable
manifolds of hyperbolic objects, e.g., fixed points, periodic
orbits, or whiskered tori. A torus is said to be whiskered if its
motion is a rotation and, associated to each of its points, there exists
directions that contract or expand exponentially under the
linearization of the dynamics. These are the stable and unstable bundles.
More importantly (as a mathematical consequence of
the existence of contracting bundles) there are stable and
unstable manifolds that contract or expand exponentially under
the full dynamics. The intersection of these manifolds can lead
to interesting dynamics.

In modern times,
[
Arn64
,
AA68
]
showed that
one can generate large scale motions in phase space
by having chains of whiskered tori so that the unstable
manifold of one intersects the stable manifold of the next.
Such sequences are called transition chains.
In
[
Arn63
]
it is conjectured with good evidence,
that transition chains happen often in celestial mechanics and
that this phenomena is in fact the main mechanism for diffusive
orbits. Even if other mechanisms for global instability have
been discovered, whiskered tori—specially those generated
by resonances
[
Tre89
]
—provide very complicated maneuvers.
Rigorous proofs of existence of whiskered tori
and several or their properties (including the existence of
(un)stable manifolds) were established
in
[
Gra74
,
Zeh76
]
.

The pioneering works
[
GLMS01
,
GSLM01
,
GJSM01a
,
GJSM01b
]
marked
significant progress in the application of advanced numerical
dynamical systems techniques to celestial mechanics.
These books focus on libration point missions for which the
Restricted Three Body problem is the basic model. Dynamical
connections between hyperbolic objects serve as zero-fuel
trajectories.

One of the motivations of this work is to enable the construction
of detailed maps of whiskered tori and their invariant manifolds
in regions of interest. This is based on developing
very efficient algorithms. With this goal in mind, we are interested
in computing accurately not only tori and their whiskers
but also the tangents. This makes it possible to
develop Newton methods to compute connections between invariant
tori or to deploy control algorithms in their neighborhood.
We consider only tori for which the stable/unstable directions
are 1-dimensional. This is the case most often considered in
celestial mechanics as in
[
Arn63
]
. Of course, we hope to
return to this problem and develop methods to compute invariant
tori with higher dimensional stable/unstable manifolds as well as
methods for the computation of whiskers in quasi-periodic
systems. Such methods can be useful in more realistic models in
celestial mechanics such as those of
[
GMM02
]
or
within the framework of
[
SPH24
]
.

The goal of this paper is then to develop and implement a method
for the simultaneous computation of parameterizations of
invariant tori and their whiskers by means of
Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser schemes. In this work, we only describe
the algorithms and implementations. We postpone for future
work theoretical studies of convergence and a-posteriori
theorems.

Following
[
HM21
,
FMHM24a
,
FMHM24b
]
, we make use of time-
T
map
reductions, which allows one to work with functions of one fewer
variable than when working directly with the vector field. This
improves the efficiency of the calculations since it mitigates
the
curse of dimensionality
. It is also natural to
separate the calculation along the flow and the transversal
directions. One reason is that the torus remains very
differentiable along the flow and the algorithms for numerical
integration have been studied for several centuries and are well
understood. Another reason to separate the propagation along the
flow from other computations is that integration is very
parallelizable.

The method described here is based on a Newton-like iteration
that computes simultaneously high order Fourier-Taylor expansions
for tori and their whiskers (as well as the normal contraction).
The simultaneous computation leads to some acceleration because
improving the invariance equation for whiskers improves the
computation of tori. Compared with methods that compute
first the torus and then the whiskers
order by order, see e.g.
[
KAdlL22
]
,
we note that each iteration of our method—if the
torus is computed exactly—doubles the order the whiskers
are computed exactly. The numerical errors at all orders are
corrected at each step of the iterative procedure.

We use identities coming from Hamiltonian geometry to simplify
the iterative step into substeps that are diagonal either in
Fourier representation or in grid representation. The result is
a highly efficient algorithm that does not require tuning.
Another improvement that we have
implemented is that, to take care of instabilities in the
propagation, we set up a multiple shooting approach.

The method we present is extremely efficient.
It is quadratically convergent as a Newton method
but does not need to store a matrix (much less to invert it).
If we discretize the torus and whiskers using
N
coefficients,
the algorithm only requires
{\mathcal{O}}(N)
storage and a step
only requires
{\mathcal{O}}(N\log N)
operations.
This is to be contrasted with conventional Newton methods (see,
e.g.,
[
Oli16
,
GM01
]
), which require
{\mathcal{O}}(N^{2})
storage and

{\mathcal{O}}(N^{3})
operations, as is the case for approaches that
discretize the invariance equation using collocation points or
Fourier series and apply a standard Newton method.
The paper
[
HdlL07
]
contains a comparison between
large matrix methods and fast methods based on reducibility
(both reducibility due to geometric identities as here
and reducibility obtained through numerical computation).

In summary, the method presented here satisfies:

•

The method is quadratically convergent as a Newton method. That is,
the error after applying one step is comparable to the square of
the error before starting.

•

In a system with
n
degrees of freedom, we need to deal
with functions of
n-1
variables.
Note that the cost of representing a function grows
exponentially with the number of variables but only linearly
with the dimension of the range.

•

The storage requirements are small (no matrices needed).
If parameterizations are discretized in
N
Fourier-Taylor or grid-Taylor
coefficients, the storage needed is
{\mathcal{O}}(N)
.

•

The operation count for one step of the iterative procedure is

{\mathcal{O}}(N\log N)
.
The iterative step breaks into substeps which
require only
{\mathcal{O}}(N)
operations (either in Fourier representation
or in grid representation). Then, the
{\mathcal{O}}(N\log N)
operation
count comes from switching representations with the FFT.
In practice the FFT is well optimized even in hardware, so that
it takes even less time than the theoretical bound.

•

Its implementation is straightforward. The algorithm for one iterative step
breaks into 11 steps, see Algorithm
4.1
, each of
which consists of natural operations and require few
computational parameters. Many of these steps can be
implemented in a single instruction in modern programming
languages.

•

It is backed up by
a-posteriori
theorems which
show it is reliable provided some (explicit) non-degeneracy
conditions are met.

•

The algorithm obtains information not only of the invariant
objects but also of their derivatives, making it possible to
develop Newton methods to compute intersections or to devise
control methods for space mission design. Such developments are
not carried out in this paper.

The basic idea for the fast Newton-like iteration is that we use
geometric identities to devise a method that only involves
operations in objects that are of size
{\mathcal{O}}(N)
. We observe that
the geometric properties of the map imply that there is an
explicit frame where the linearization of the motion on the
whisker is an upper triangular matrix. This has been used for
invariant tori, but we now observe that this applies also for
whiskers. We note, however that methods based on parameterization
methods (that are not as fast as those presented here)
but that work without leveraging geometry also exist

[
CH17b
,
CH17a
]
.

Once a local representation of the whiskers is computed, one can
use the flow in the future and in the past to get a global manifold.
Given this goal, it is worth to obtain an accurate local
representation in a
fundamental domain
which
we want to be as large as possible. The algorithm we present has
few computational parameters. Mainly the
number of coefficients used and a scale parameter whose theory
is studied in Section
4.1
, which affects the
numerical stability of the computations. In our numerical
runs, we typically make a preliminary calculation that allows to
fine tune the parameters and then, we make longer runs with the
optimized parameters. The algorithm can run largely unattended
in a variety of problems.

We would like to note that a Newton method leads immediately to
continuation strategies on any parameter. Take the solution at a
value of a parameter as the starting point for the Newton method
for a slightly different value. Here, we present details about a
particularly efficient continuation method (including
extrapolations) along the period. This is equivalent to
conducting continuations along the energy. In the study of
continuation of solutions, we point out that the a-posteriori
result shows that the continuation can proceed until the torus
fails to satisfy some of the geometric conditions of the theorem,
which indicates breakdown.

Regarding implementations, we have applied our techniques in the
CRTBP where we computed a large set of non-resonant

2-
dimensional invariant tori and their stable/unstable
manifolds studying the fundamental domain of the
parameterizations. As expected, the methods are
extremely fast and for a fairly large number of coefficients,
the calculations for realistic models such as
the CRTBP are in the order of seconds in a commercial machine of
today without any sophisticated fine tuning. See Table

2.
Setting

We assume all objects to be sufficiently
smooth—even real analytic. In this paper we
concentrate on the algorithms and postpone precise formulations
of regularities (which are essential to study convergence using
KAM theory) to a theoretical paper.

2.1.
Geometric structures

We will be working in an Euclidean space (more suitable for numerical
analysis). All the geometric objects will be expressed as matrices
and the geometric identities used become matrix identities.

We assume we have an exact symplectic form

\bm{\omega}={\rm d}\bm{\alpha}
on an open set
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
,
where
\bm{\alpha}
is the Liouville 1-form, endowing
U
with an
exact symplectic structure. Let
\Omega:U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2n\times 2n}

be the matrix representation of
\bm{\omega}
. Since
\bm{\omega}
is exact,
the matrix representation of the symplectic form satisfies

\Omega(z)={\rm D}a(z)^{\!\top\!}-{\rm D}a(z),

where
a(z)^{\!\top\!}
is the matrix representation of
\bm{\alpha}
at

z
.
Then, for any
z\in U
and
u,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}_{z}(u,v)=u^{\!\top\!}\Omega(z)v,

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}_{z}(u)=a(z)^{\!\top\!}u=u^{\!\top\!}a(z).

Let
\bm{J}
be an almost complex structure on
U

compatible with the symplectic form and with matrix representation

J:U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
. That is,
J
is
anti-involutive and symplectic. In coordinates, these properties
read

J(z)^{2}=-I_{2n},\quad J(z)^{\!\top\!}\Omega(z)J(z)=\Omega(z).

The almost complex structure and the symplectic form induce a
Riemannian metric
\bm{g}
on
U
with matrix representation

G:U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
constructed as
G(z)=-\Omega(z)J(z)
.

It is well known that one can arrange to have
a compatible triple of symplectic form, almost complex
structure, and metric
(\bm{\omega},\bm{J},\bm{g})
such that
for all
u,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
and
z\in U
, the compatible
triple satisfies

\bm{\omega}_{z}(u,v)=\bm{g}_{z}(u,\bm{J}_{z}v).

Note that any two elements of the triple determine
the other one
[
Ber01
]
.
Generally, the metric
\bm{g}
corresponding to
the two previously made choices is not the standard metric.
Nonetheless, for the numerical explorations, we use the standard
symplectic form and the standard metric.

The use of an almost complex structure is not strictly
necessary but it leads to significant simplifications and
numerical advantages. Some of the properties that the matrix
representations satisfy are the following

\displaystyle\Omega(z)

\displaystyle=G(z)J(z),

\displaystyle\ \Omega(z)^{\!\top\!}

\displaystyle=-\Omega(z),

\displaystyle J(z)^{\!\top\!}

\displaystyle=-J(z),

\displaystyle G(z)^{\!\top\!}

\displaystyle=G(z).

For any smooth function
H:U\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we construct a
Hamiltonian vector field
X:U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
as

(1)

X(z):=\Omega(z)^{-1}{\rm D}H(z)^{\!\top\!}.

Let
\phi:{\mathcal{D}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}\times U\to U
denote the flow for the
vector field
X
. We will use the standard notation

\phi(t,z)=\phi_{t}(z)
. For fixed
t
, the diffeomorphism

\phi_{t}
is exact symplectic—that is,
\phi_{t}
preserves the
symplectic form as

\phi_{t}^{*}\bm{\omega}=\bm{\omega},

and the 1-form
\phi_{t}^{*}\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}
is exact for some smooth
primitive function, see
[
Zeh76
,
HM21
]
.

In this work, we encounter functions defined in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}

For every

3.
Whiskered tori

Let us consider
(d+1)
-dimensional invariant tori

\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}\subset U
, where
d=n-2
, with frequency vector

\hat{\omega}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
. For a suitable
T
, we can look for a
codimension
-1
torus
{\mathcal{K}}\subset\hat{\mathcal{K}}
—invariant under
a time-
T
map—that generates
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
. See
e.g.
[
HM21
,
FMHM24a
]
. First, let us define

\hat{\omega}:=\tfrac{1}{T}(\omega,1)
, and assume
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
to

Then, we look for parameterizations
K:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to U

that conjugate the dynamics in
{\mathcal{K}}=K({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
under

to a rigid in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
with rotation vector

\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}K-K{\!\>\circ\!\>}R_{\omega}=0,

where the map

defines the dynamics in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and is
given by:

\displaystyle R_{\omega}:\quad{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\longmapsto\theta+\omega.

An invariant torus
{\mathcal{K}}
is whiskered if it has directions that
contract exponentially fast in the future and in the past under
iteration of the differential of the map. These directions are the
invariant bundles of the torus and together with the tangent to
the torus and its symplectic conjugate, span the tangent bundle
of
U
restricted to
{\mathcal{K}}
. Tangent to the exponentially contracting
directions, the torus has attached invariant manifolds that converge
exponentially fast to the torus—the whiskers.

To make the definition of whiskered tori precise, let us
define the
transfer matrix

M:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
defined on the graph of
K

M(\theta)={{\rm D}_{z}}\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}K(\theta).

In order to study the dynamics under iteration of

M^{(n)}=M{\!\>\circ\!\>}R_{\omega}^{{\!\>\circ\!\>}(n-1)}\dots M{\!\>\circ\!\>}R_{\omega}M,

where

R_{\omega}^{{\!\>\circ\!\>}n}:=\underbrace{R_{\omega}{\!\>\circ\!\>}\cdots{\!\>\circ\!\>}R_{\omega}}_{n\rm\ times}.

Observe that
M
satisfies the cocycle relation

M^{(n+m)}=M^{(n)}{\!\>\circ\!\>}R_{\omega}^{{\!\>\circ\!\>}m}M^{(m)},

which we can use to define
M^{(-n)}
as

M^{(-n)}=\left(M^{(n)}{\!\>\circ\!\>}R_{\omega}^{{\!\>\circ\!\>}(-n)}\right)^{-1},

where

R_{\omega}^{{\!\>\circ\!\>}(-n)}:=\underbrace{R_{-\omega}{\!\>\circ\!\>}\cdots{\!\>\circ\!\>}R_{-\omega}}_{n\rm\ times}.

\displaystyle v\in E^{u}_{K(\theta)}\Longleftrightarrow\quad|M^{(n)}(v)|\leq C\mu_{1}^{-n}|v|\quad

\displaystyle n<0,

\displaystyle v\in E^{c}_{K(\theta)}\Longleftrightarrow\quad|M^{(n)}(v)|\leq C\mu_{2}^{|n|}|v|\quad

\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{Z}.

In our case, we can take

arbitrarily close to
1
, taking

sufficiently large. As it is well known, the tangent vectors
in the torus may grow polynomially with the number of iterations.

3.1.
Invariance equation for whiskers

We are interested in an invariant manifold
\hat{\mathcal{W}}\subset U
, containing
the rotational torus
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
and its associated rank
-1

whisker (that is whiskers for which the dimension of the stable and
unstable direction is
1
). As in
the previous section, we consider a codimension
-1
manifold

{\mathcal{W}}\subset\hat{\mathcal{W}}
, invariant under the time
-T
map, that
generates
\hat{\mathcal{W}}
. In particular, we have that
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\mathcal{W}}
.

We look for a parameterization

W:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to U
such that under the map
\phi_{T}
, the
parameterization conjugates the dynamics on the torus to a rigid rotation
and the dynamics on the whisker to a constant contraction.
The value of the contraction is one of the unknowns of the procedure.

A completely analogous argument works for the unstable direction
using the inverse dynamics
\phi_{-T}
. Therefore, in what follows, we
assume
|{\lambda}|<1
. The conjugacy condition translates into

W
satisfying the invariance equation

(8)

\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}W-W{\!\>\circ\!\>}{R_{\omega}^{\lambda}}\hskip 1.0pt=0,

where the map

\displaystyle{R_{\omega}^{\lambda}}\hskip 1.0pt:\quad{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle(\theta,s)

\displaystyle\longmapsto(\theta+\omega,\lambda s)

represents the internal dynamics of
{\mathcal{W}}={W}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}})

under
\phi_{T}
. Note that at
s=0
, we recover the invariance
equation for
{\mathcal{K}}
which is parameterized by
K(\theta):=W(\theta,0)
.

We emphasize that, in (
8
) the unknowns are both
W
and
\lambda
.
We consider
\omega
and
T
fixed. The iterative step will involve
adjusting both
{W}
and
\lambda
.

If we differentiate the invariance equation
(
8
), we obtain

(9)

\left({\rm D}\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}W\right){\rm D}W=\left({\rm D}W{\!\>\circ\!\>}{R_{\omega}^{\lambda}}\hskip 1.0pt\right){\rm D}{R_{\omega}^{\lambda}}\hskip 1.0pt,

where

{\rm D}{R_{\omega}^{\lambda}}\hskip 1.0pt=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}I_{d}&\\
\hline\cr&\lambda\end{array}\right).

This expression reveals that the invariance equation for
{\mathcal{W}}

carries a bundle invariant under
{\rm D}\phi_{T}
: the tangent bundle
of
{\mathcal{W}}
. In what follows,
for the sake of notational simplicity, we will avoid the use of
parenthesis in formulas as in (
9
), where composition
precedes products.

Remark 3.4
.

Solutions to (
8
) are not unique—we can
scale the coordinate
s
by some scaling factor
\rho

and obtain the reparameterization

W^{\rho}(\theta,s):=W(\theta,\rho s)
. There is also a lack of
uniqueness in the parameterization of the torus. If
{W}
is a
solution of (
8
), for any
\alpha\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

{W}^{\alpha,\tau}(\theta,s):=\phi_{\tau}{\!\>\circ\!\>}{W}(\theta+\alpha,s)
is also a
solution.

Note that all these parameterizations give the same torus
but there is freedom in the choice of scale and origin of angle in
the reference manifold domain of the parameterization.
To obtain a Newton method with locally unique solutions,
we will chose some normalization conditions that fix the origin
of coordinates in
\theta
and the scale in
s
.
All these normalization conditions are equivalent for the mathematics,
but some of them lead to algorithms that are less sensitive to round-off error.
We will specify the normalization choices in
4.1
.

Remark 3.5
.

It is possible to find a parameterization
{W}_{1}:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to T_{{\mathcal{K}}}U

of the invariant bundles
{W}_{1}
by looking for a solution
of the following invariance equation

(10)

{\rm D}\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}K\,{W}_{1}-{W}_{1}{\!\>\circ\!\>}R_{\omega}\lambda=0,

see
[
HM21
]
. The bundle
{W}_{1}
can be considered as a first
order approximation of
{\mathcal{W}}
.

Remark 3.6
.

If the vector field
X
is reversible, as in the CRTBP,
we can obtain parameterizations of the unstable whisker from that
of the stable whisker. More specifically, if there exists an
involution
\varrho:{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
satisfying

X{\!\>\circ\!\>}\varrho=-{\rm D}\varrho X,

then we can obtain a parameterization of the unstable whisker, say
\tilde{W}
, from
that of the stable whisker, and vice versa, as
\tilde{W}=\varrho{\!\>\circ\!\>}{W}
.
Note that the parameterization given by
{W}(\theta,0)
is
different from the parameterization given by
\tilde{W}(\theta,0)
.
Nonetheless, both
{W}(\theta,0)
and
\tilde{W}(\theta,0)
give the
same torus
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
, see the beginning of Section
3
.

Remark 3.7
.

We can consider (
8
) also as including a normal form
for the dynamics in the stable whisker.
In contrast to the Birkhoff normal form developed in a neighborhood
of the torus, the normal form on the stable whisker
does not have any Birkhoff invariants. Therefore, the formalism used
here can be used rather generally.

Remark 3.8
.

Equation (
8
) involves only functions of
the dimension of the stable manifold. It
nevertheless, together with (
9
) and the adapted frame
constructed in the next section, gives approximate control of
what happens in a neighborhood of the stable manifold
with a good accuracy.
Using a full normal form around the torus produces higher
accuracy for the transitions but a full normal forms requires
using functions of a number of variables equal to the dimension
of the phase space.

Remark 3.9
.

The formalism used in this paper generalizes to higher
dimensional (un)stable manifolds,
but some new phenomena appear. If the motion has real
\lambda_{i}
,
there could be resonances (the product of some
\lambda_{i}^{\prime}s
is
another). Such resonances require only a small change and is not a stable phenomenon in
the sense that, if they are present in a system, they could be
destroyed by a small perturbation. However if some of the
\lambda_{i}
are
a complex conjugate pair, there appear extra small divisors (the first Melnikov
condition) and a new formalism is needed. The difficulties are
larger if one allows elliptic directions.
Some algorithms for elliptic tori based on other methods have been developed in

[
JV97
,
LV11
,
CFH25
]

We hope to come back to these problems and develop algorithms
similar to this paper’s to develop
simultaneous computations of tori and normal forms.

3

\langle\xi^{2}_{1}\rangle
are free.
This underdeterminacy reflects the freedom in the choice of

and in the choice of the first order approximation of

, i.e.,
the freedom in the scaling of the invariant bundle of

, see

. A simple

and

\langle\xi^{2}_{1}\rangle=0
.
Then, we can solve for

and

as described in

. Lastly,
we obtain the corrected parameterization
and the corrected rate of contraction as
\bar{W}=W+P\xi
and

\bar{\lambda}=\lambda+{\Delta\lambda}
, respectively.

Remark 4.1
.

As already mentioned, there is certain freedom in the
parameterization of
W
—we can
scale the coordinate
s
by some scaling factor
\rho

and obtain the reparameterization

W^{\rho}(\theta,s)=W(\theta,\rho s)
. Using the
Fourier-Taylor representation of Section
2.2
,
the coefficients of
{W}^{\rho}
are

{{W}^{\rho}}_{j}(\theta)=\rho^{j}{W}_{j}(\theta)
.

All such parameterizations are mathematically equivalent but the
scaling can have effects on the numerics. If one chooses scaling factors
such that coefficients grow or decrease to zero too fast with the order,
the algorithm might be affected by round-off error.
In order to avoid this, we can
take
\rho=1/r
, where
r
is an estimate of the radius of convergence
of the series in
s
that we can obtain with a root test.

Remark 4.2
.

Suppose we represent
{W}
as a Fourier-Taylor
series, see Section
2.2
, and assume we have,
for instance, the torus and the bundle. That
is, we have the terms
{W}_{0}
and
{W}_{1}
.
Since the iterative procedure is quadratically
convergent, after
it
iterations we have

2^{it+1}-1
correct terms for
{W}
.

4.2.
Continuation with respect to
T

A Newton method gives rise to a continuation strategy.
The most elementary is taking a well computed zero for
a value of the parameter as an initial guess of
the algorithm to compute a zero for an slightly different value,
but one can also use extrapolations, etc. Note that the a-posteriori
theorem guarantees that these continuation methods will progress
until parameter values where the hypothesis of the a-posteriori theorem
do not hold, which indicates breakdown of the tori.

In this section, we discuss a particularly efficient continuation
method on the parameter
T

This is equivalent to multiplying the frequency of the torus
which is the usual KAM method of exploring what happens in energy
surfaces. The continuation in the parameter
T
is significantly
better behaved that continuation on other parameters since we
can obtain explicit extrapolation formulas and reuse many of
the calculations.

Assume we have a
\phi_{T}

4.3.
Fundamental domain

Assume we have a parameterization
W
given by a truncated
Fourier-Taylor series at order
N_{T}
. The series for
W
will only be
valid in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times\mathcal{D}
for some open interval

\mathcal{D}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
—we refer to
{\mathcal{D}}
as the fundamental
domain of
{W}
.
Let us consider the following norm for the error (
22
)

(41)

\|E\|_{{\mathcal{A}}}:=\sup_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\theta&\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\\
s&\in{\mathcal{A}}\crcr}}}\|E(\theta,s)\|_{\infty},

where
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then, we can define
{\mathcal{D}}
as the largest interval where

(42)

\|E\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}<\varepsilon,

holds for some threshold
\varepsilon>0
small. If we proceed formally,
we can construct a Fourier-Taylor series for
E
of the form

(43)

E(\theta,s)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}E_{j}(\theta)s^{j}.

Then, using the definition (
41
), we have the inequality

\|E\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}=\sup_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\theta&\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\\
s&\in{\mathcal{D}}\crcr}}}\|\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}E_{j}(\theta)s^{j}\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\sup_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\theta&\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\\
s&\in{\mathcal{D}}\crcr}}}\|E_{j}(\theta)s^{j}\|_{\infty}\right)

and if we define

(44)

\|E_{j}\|:=\sup_{\theta\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\|E_{j}(\theta)\|_{\infty},\quad D:=\sup_{s\in{\mathcal{D}}}|s|,

we can estimate the fundamental domain by requiring

(45)

\|E\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}\|E_{j}\|D^{j}+\sum_{j=N_{T}+1}^{\infty}\|E_{j}\|D^{j}<\varepsilon.

Observe that if we want (
45
) to hold,
necessarily for any
k

(46)

\|{E_{k}}\|D^{k}<\varepsilon.

Let us now inspect which term contributes the most in the sums of
(
45
) so we can obtain a useful bound for
D
from
(
46
).

Observe that
E_{j}
, for
j\leq N_{T}
, should be small since these are
the terms that we control with our iterative procedure. The terms
for
j>N_{T}
are terms of order
j
of
\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}{W}
since we do
not have terms of order higher than
N_{T}
for
{W}
. Consequently,
we assume that the term that contributes the most in
(
45
) is in the infinite sum.
Note that the radius of convergence for
the series (
43
), say
\tilde{D}
, satisfies

D\leq\tilde{D}
. Furthermore,
E
is analytic so we can bound
each
E_{j}
using Cauchy estimates by

\|{E_{j}}\|\leq\frac{C}{\tilde{D}^{j}},

for some constant
C
. Consequently, in the infinite sum, we can
bound each term by

C\left(\frac{D}{\tilde{D}}\right)^{j},

from where we see that the largest upper bound corresponds to the
term
j=N_{T}+1
.
We can then obtain the following necessary condition for
D

(47)

D<\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\|E_{N_{T}+1}\|}\right)^{\frac{1}{N_{T}+1}},

from where we estimate the fundamental domain as
{\mathcal{D}}=[-D,D]
.

Alternatively, for some grid of values
\{\theta_{l}\}_{l\in{\rm I}}
,
where
{\rm I}
is some index set, and some
\delta>0
small
we can estimate
\|E\|_{{\mathcal{A}}}
numerically as

(48)

\|E\|_{{\mathcal{A}}}\approx\max_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr l&\in{\rm I}\\
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}&\delta\cap{\mathcal{A}}\crcr}}}\left(\|\phi_{T}\big(W(\theta_{l},s)\big)-W(\theta_{l}+\omega,\lambda s)\|_{\infty}\right).

To estimate the fundamental domain, let us define

k=\max\{l\in{\mathbb{N}}:\|{E}\|_{[-l\delta,l\delta]}<\varepsilon\},

where the norm is approximated with (
48
). Then,
we simply obtain
{\mathcal{D}}
as
{\mathcal{D}}=[-k\delta,k\delta]
.

5.
Computational considerations

In this section, we provide some insights into the
computational implementation of the methods discussed in Section

4
.

5.1.
Function representations

The algorithms of the previous section for the computation of
Newton and continuation steps are written in terms of scalar,
vector, and matrix valued functions defined in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
.
In view of the expressions for the solutions of cohomological
equations (Section
2.3
), a natural representation for
these functions is as Fourier-Taylor series as given in
(
2
)-(
3
). In a computer, truncated
versions of such series can be stored.

For functions
f:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
(resp.
f:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
,

f:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell\times m}
) and some finite multi-index set

{\mathcal{I}}
with elements in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, we denote
Fourier
representations
of
f
by

f\sim\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}\hat{f}_{k}e^{\bm{i}2\pi k\theta},

which is given by the finite set of coefficients
\{\hat{f}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}
.

As it is usual in the numerical implementation of the
parameterization method in KAM-like contexts (see

[
HCF+16
]
for an overview), it is convenient to work
also with a grid representation of the function
f
. For some
finite multi-index set
{\rm I}
with elements in
{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
, we also
consider representations of
f
of the following form

f\sim\{f_{l}\}_{l\in{\rm I}},

where
f_{l}=f(\theta_{l})
and
\{\theta_{l}\}_{l\in{\rm I}}
is an
equally spaced grid of values in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
indexed by
{\rm I}
. Let
N_{F}
be the
cardinality of the set
\{f_{l}\}_{l\in{\rm I}}
, i.e., the number of
elements of the grid of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. Then, the

\{\hat{f}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}
coefficients can be obtained
from the set
\{f_{l}\}_{l\in{\rm I}}
through the Discrete Fourier
Transform (DFT) and vice-versa through the inverse DFT—at a
computational cost of
O(N_{F}\log N_{F})
operations. We refer to the
set of values
\{f_{l}\}_{l\in{\rm I}}
as a
grid
representation
of the function
f
.

Remark 5.1
.

We remark that the Fourier coefficients used
in the description and the DFT coefficients used
in the implementation are not the same.
Nonetheless, there is a close routine connection, see

[
HM21
,
Hen79
]
, and this is just a minor bookkeeping detail.

For functions
f:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

(resp.
f:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
,

f:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell\times m}
)
and some positive natural order

N_{T}
, we consider
Fourier-Taylor
representations of
f
of the
following form

(49)

f\sim\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}f_{j}(\theta)s^{j}\sim\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}\hat{f}_{jk}e^{\bm{i}2\pi k\theta}s^{j}.

Numerically, functions are represented by the finite set of coefficients

\{\hat{f}_{jk}\}\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr&0\leq j\leq N_{T}\\
&k\in{\mathcal{I}}\crcr}}
.
It will be convenient to also work with a grid representation of
the
f_{j}
coefficients in (
49
).
We will also consider representations of
f
of the following form

f\sim\{f_{jl}\}\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr&0\leq j\leq N_{T}\\
&l\in{\rm I}\crcr}},

where
f_{jl}=f_{j}(\theta_{l})
and
\{\theta_{l}\}_{l\in{\rm I}}

is an equally spaced grid of values in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.
We refer to the set of coefficients
\{f_{jl}\}\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr&0\leq j\leq N_{T}\\
&l\in{\rm I}\crcr}}
as a
grid-Taylor
representation of
f
.
Such representation is very well suited for the computation of
product of functions with variables in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
and for
the (numerical) application of the flow and its differential to
such functions. That is, for the numerical computation of

\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}f
and
({\rm D}\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}f)g
for fixed
T
and
f,g:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
.
The computation of the flow applied to Taylor polynomials
is often called Jet Transport.

5.2.
Jet Transport

In the iterative scheme of Section
4.1
, it is
necessary to compute Fourier-Taylor series for the flow and the
variational equations applied to functions defined in

{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
. That is, we need to compute the
coefficients
\phi_{j},\Phi_{j}:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
of
the following Fourier-Taylor representations

(50)

\displaystyle\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}{W}\sim\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}\phi_{j}(\theta)s^{j},

(51)

\displaystyle\left({\rm D}\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}{W}\right)u\sim\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}\Phi_{j}(\theta)s^{j},

for some arbitrary
u:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
.

We note that propagating polynomials
up to high order is the same as computing the derivatives of
an evolution. This is a standard numerical problem
called
Jet Transport
—which has attracted interest
in celestial mechanics since it enters in several other aspects such
as computations of normal forms, bifurcations, etc. Several
systematic approaches and surveys can be found in

[
AFV+09
,
PP15
,
PPMG15
,
GJJC+23
]
.

The Jet Transport algorithm we use is based on taking a
standard integrator of ODE’s and overload the arithmetic
operations involved in evaluating a step with operations on polynomials.
We think of the operations as the representative of derivatives.
For the applications to the invariance equation, we use the grid-Taylor
representation and compute the flow map of the jet on each point.
For the CRTBP, all the operations involved are
arithmetic, but it is well known that there are fast implementations of
many other functions
(\sin,\penalty\ \cos,\penalty\ \exp,\penalty\ \log,
elliptic
functions, etc). Some comments on our actual implementation of
the Jet Transport technique can be found in Section

5.3
.

Let us elaborate on our approach. Assume we have grid-Taylor representations of

{W}
and
u
, i.e, we have
\{W_{jl}\}\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr&0\leq j\leq N_{T}\\
&l\in{\rm I}\crcr}}
and
\{u_{jl}\}\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr&0\leq j\leq N_{T}\\
&l\in{\rm I}\crcr}}
.
Recall that the Fourier-Taylor representations

\displaystyle{W}

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}{W}_{j}(\theta)s^{j},

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}u_{j}(\theta)s^{j},

are related to the grid-Taylor ones by

\displaystyle{W}_{jl}

\displaystyle={W}_{j}(\theta_{l}),

\displaystyle u_{jl}

\displaystyle=u_{j}(\theta_{l}),

where
{W}_{jl},u_{jl}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, and
\{\theta_{l}\}_{l\in{\rm I}}
is an equally spaced grid of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
indexed by

{\rm I}
. Let us define the truncated Taylor series of
{W},u

associated to each grid point as

\displaystyle{W}_{l}(s)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}W_{jl}s^{j},

\displaystyle u_{l}(s)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}u_{jl}s^{j}.

The Jet Transport technique allows the propagation of

W_{l}
and
u_{l}
through the flow in order to
obtain truncated Taylor series of
\phi_{T}\circ W
and

({\rm D}\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}{W})u
associated to each grid point as

\displaystyle\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}{W}_{l}\sim\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}\phi_{jl}s^{j},

\displaystyle\left({\rm D}\phi_{T}{\!\>\circ\!\>}{W}_{l}\right)u_{l}\sim\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}\Phi_{jl}s^{j},

for all
l\in{\rm I}
. Therefore, we obtain

\{\phi_{jl}\}\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr&0\leq j\leq N_{T}\\
&l\in{\rm I}\crcr}}
and

\{\Phi_{jl}\}\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr&0\leq j\leq N_{T}\\
&l\in{\rm I}\crcr}}
.
That is, we obtain grid-Taylor representations for
(
50
) and (
51
). We can recover a
Fourier-Taylor representation with the inverse DFT.

5.3.
Comments on implementations

Throughout this section, assume

d=1
—which is the case for the numerical experiments of
Section
6
—and that we have the following
Fourier-Taylor representation

(52)

{W}(\theta,s)\sim\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}{W}_{j}(\theta)s^{j}\sim\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}\hat{W}_{jk}e^{\bm{i}2\pi k\theta}s^{j},

for some finite order
N_{T}
and a finite multi-index set

{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

In the following we describe some elementary techniques
we have found useful.

5.3.1.
Low pass filter

We follow
[
HM21
,
FMHM24a
]
and implement
a low-pass filter after each iterative step of Algorithm

4.1
. This is, for some filtering factor

r_{f}\in[\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2})
, we set to zero the
coefficients
\hat{W}_{jk}
for

|{k}|>r_{f}\cdot N_{F}
.
In order to decide the number of coefficients for the Fourier
series, i.e.,
N_{F}
, we examine the exponential decay
since our objects are real analytic.
In particular, for any
{W}_{i}
, we compute
the tail as

t(W_{j})=\sum_{|{k}|>r_{t}\cdot N_{F}}\|\hat{W}_{jk}\|_{\infty},

where
r_{t}<r_{f}
is some tail factor. Then, we can decide to
increase
N_{F}
if we detect the tails do not decrease
sufficiently fast. See algorithm
5.1
.

5.3.2.
Choice of scale in the
s
parameter

According to Remark
4.1
, we can scale
the series in the coordinate
s
by its estimated radius of
convergence for the sake of normalization. A possibility is to run
algorithm
4.1
twice: the first to estimate the
radius of convergence and the second, after the scaling has been
done. However, if we are interested in high order
parameterizations, say up to a maximum order
N_{T_{max}}
, estimating the
radius of convergence after performing algorithm

4.1
to order
N_{T_{max}}
might not give good
estimates. For this reason, we run algorithm
4.1

first to order
N_{T_{i}}<N_{T_{max}}
. Then we estimate the radius of convergence
of the parameterization and scale the series. Then, we iterate at
order
N_{T_{i+1}}>N_{T_{i}}
until the radius of convergence is close to 1
or until
N_{T_{i}}=N_{T_{max}}
. See algorithm
5.1
.

5.3.3.
Multiple Shooting

To mitigate the instability of hyperbolicity in the propagation,
we follow

[
HM21
,
FMHM24a
]
and implement a multiple-shooting strategy.
For some positive integer
m
,
we look for whiskered tori
\{{\mathcal{W}}^{i}\}_{i=0}^{m-1}
and
parameterizations
\{{W}^{i}\}_{i=0}^{m-1}
, where

{W}^{i}:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to U
, such that the following system of
functional equations is satisfied

(53)

\phi_{T_{m}}{\!\>\circ\!\>}{W}^{i}-{W}^{i+1}{\!\>\circ\!\>}{R}_{\omega_{m}}^{\lambda_{m}}=0,\quad\text{for}\penalty\ i=0,\dots,m-1,

where the superscript of
W
is defined modulo
m
,
T_{m}:=T/m,\penalty\ \omega_{m}:=\omega/m,
and
\lambda_{m}:=\lambda^{1/m}
.
Assuming
|{\lambda}|<1
, we choose
m

such that the expansion rate
|{\lambda}|^{\frac{-1}{m}}
is small
enough. For the sake of simplicity, we have described the methodology
for
m=1
—the generalization to
m>1
, albeit tedious,
presents no additional complications.

5.3.4.
Implementation of jet transport

Our Jet Transport implementation substitutes the floating point
operations of a
RKF78
integrator by a simpler yet similar
version of the truncated formal power series manipulator
described in Chapter 2 of
[
HCF+16
]
; which is publicly
available. It does so by means of a
C++
wrapper that overloads
the arithmetic operators of a
RKF78 C
routine with jet
operations. A subtle point is that the step control of the
RKF78
algorithm involves propagation by two methods and
comparing the two results. The comparison of two polynomials
requires choosing a norm in the space of polynomials. It seems to
be not a critical choice. In our implementation, the step size
is Fehlbergh’s but taking into account all the coefficients of
the jet using the
\ell_{1}
norm. This is justified by the fact
that, assuming analyticity, a calculation shows that the error in
all the coefficients of the jet is of the order of the numerical
integrator in the step size. Reference
[
GJJC+23
]
shows
actually more: the jet of coefficients obtained through, e.g.,
Taylor and Runge-Kutta methods is the same (up to rounding
errors) as the one that would be obtained by integrating the
higher order variational equations up to the order of the jets.

5.3.5.
Parallelization

Jet Transport (which profiling shows is the most time consuming
part of the algorithm) is very easy to parallelize since the integration of
an orbit can be made independently of the integration of another.
Hence, the most computationally intensive part of our algorithm can
be efficiently parallelized, which we do with
OpenMP
.
Note that the approach generalizes straightforwardly to
overloading to polynomials in two or more variables, which is
what will be needed in the computation of higher dimensional
whiskers.

6.
Numerical experiments

We apply our algorithms in the Hamiltonian vector field of the
Circular Restricted Three Body Problem (CRTBP). That is, we have
a Hamiltonian system describing the motion of a massless particle
under the influence of the gravitational attraction of of two
massive bodies, known as primaries, moving in circular orbits
around their common barycentre. Let
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
denote the
masses of the primaries and let us also define the mass parameter

\mu:=\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}
. We can define a rotating frame, also
known as a synodic frame, such that the position of the primaries
is fixed in
x_{1}
axis, see e.g.
[
Sze67
]
. For a
suitable rescaling in space and time, the coordinates of the
primaries are then
(\mu,0,0)
and
(\mu-1,0,0)
and their period
of revolution is
2\pi
. The motion of the infinitesimal body is
then described by autonomous Hamiltonian

H(x,p)=\frac{1}{2}(p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}+p_{3}^{2})-x_{1}p_{2}+x_{2}p_{1}-\frac{1-\mu}{r_{1}}-\frac{\mu}{r_{2}},

where
x,p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,
r_{1}^{2}:=(x_{1}-\mu)^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}
, and

r_{2}^{2}:=(x_{1}-\mu+1)^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}
.

The CRTBP has five fixed points denoted by
L_{i}
with

i=1,2,\dots,5
. We focus on the
L_{1}
point which is the one
located between the primaries. In terms of stability, the
L_{1}

point is of type center
\times
center
\times
saddle. That is,

{\rm Spec}{\rm D}X(L_{1})=\{\bm{i}2\pi\omega_{p}^{0},-\bm{i}2\pi\omega_{p}^{0},\bm{i}2\pi\omega_{v}^{0},-\bm{i}2\pi\omega_{v}^{0},\lambda^{0},-\lambda^{0}\},

for
\omega_{p}^{0},\omega_{v}^{0},\lambda^{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
. Consequently,
L_{1}

has a
4-
dimensional center manifold. Lyapunov’s center theorem,
see
[
MHO09
,
SM95
]
, guarantees the
existence of two families of periodic orbits contained in the
center manifold: the vertical and the planar Lyapunov families.
Note that there exist several other families of periodic orbits,
see e.g.
[
DDP03
]
, but we limit our case to the
classical Lyapunov families.
The center manifold carries quasi-periodic motion defined by

2-
dimensional invariant tori around periodic orbits with a
central part. The quasi-periodic orbits around the Lyapunov
orbits are commonly known as Lissajous orbits—these orbits
correspond to partially hyperbolic invariant tori with rank
-1

whiskers which will be our case study. More specifically, we
focus on the family of Lissajous tori around the vertical
Lyapunov family.

6.1.
The Lissajous family

In order to identify each torus of the Lissajous family, we
follow
[
GM01
,
HM21
]
and define the rotation number,
denoted by
\rho
, as follows. Let

\hat{\omega}=(\omega_{p},\omega_{v})
denote the frequencies of any

2
-dimensional torus
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
in the Lissajous family
and let
h
denote the value of the Hamiltonian for any point

p\in\hat{\mathcal{K}}
.
If we denote by
h^{0}
the Hamiltonian of the
L_{1}
point, we take

\hat{\omega}=(\omega_{p},\omega_{v})
such that

\omega_{p}\rightarrow\omega_{p}^{0}
and

\omega_{v}\rightarrow\omega_{v}^{0}
when
h\rightarrow h^{0}
. Then, we
define the rotation number as

\rho:=\frac{\omega_{p}}{\omega_{v}}-1.

Each torus of the Lissajous family can be uniquely identified by
their value of
h
and
\rho

For the computation of the Lissajous family of whiskered tori we
have two main options: to perform continuations of families for
fixed

4.2
or to compute tori
from approximate solutions of (
8

iterative scheme of Section
4.1

approach, we need to have approximate parameterizations. To this
end, we follow the approach of
[

and compute
parameterizations of invariant tori and their invariant bundles
for the Lissajous family—that is, we have the terms
W_{0}

and
W_{1}
the Fourier-Taylor expansion of

The tori obtained are included in
