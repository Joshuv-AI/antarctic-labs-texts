A founder variant in TANGO2 p.(Leu148Trp) in ten Palestinian families: clinical characterization and haplotype analysis of TANGO2 deficiency disorder
Authors: Christina Canavati, Motee Ashhab, Grace Rabie, Lina Hreimat, Hanin Shatrit, Lara Kamal, Fouad Zahdeh, Dania Dajani, Osama Atawneh, Mohammed Ali Alsharha, Nadirah Damseh, Bassam Abu-Libdeh, Imad Dweikat, Nader Handal, Moien Kanaan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTANGO2 deficiency disorder (TDD) is a rare autosomal recessive condition characterized by recurrent metabolic crises, rhabdomyolysis, encephalopathy, seizures, intellectual disability, and life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias.MethodsHere, we describe 16 affected individuals from 10 consanguineous Palestinian families harboring a shared homozygous missense variant in TANGO2 (NM_152906.7:c.443T > G; NP_690870.3:p.(Leu148Trp)). Exome sequencing identified the variant in three probands and targeted Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation in all remaining families. The variant is absent from population databases and is predicted to be deleterious by multiple in silico tools. Microsatellite marker analysis was performed using five short tandem repeat (STR) markers spanning the TANGO2 locus.ResultsClinical presentation was heterogeneous, including developmental delay, recurrent encephalopathy, rhabdomyolysis, seizures, and cardiac involvement. Microsatellite marker analysis revealed a conserved ancestral haplotype flanking the TANGO2 locus in all genotyped affected individuals, supporting a founder effect in this population. Supportive management including coenzyme Q10 and B-complex vitamins (B-100) was commonly used; consistent with prior reports, some families described reduced frequency and/or severity of spells following supplementation.ConclusionsThese findings provide strong clinical, genetic, and haplotype evidence supporting reclassification of the TANGO2 p.(Leu148Trp) variant as likely pathogenic and highlight the importance of early molecular diagnosis and targeted screening strategies in high-consanguinity populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-026-02407-5.

Introduction

TANGO2 deficiency disorder (TDD, OMIM #616878 [1]) is a rare and often devastating autosomal recessive disease that disrupts the lives of children and families through unpredictable episodes of metabolic collapse, seizures, developmental regression, and life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias [2, 3]. In many cases, the disorder remains unrecognized until a catastrophic event, such as sudden cardiac arrest or coma, forces an urgent search for answers [2, 4].

Although the underlying biology of the TANGO2 gene is still not fully understood, emerging evidence suggests it plays a critical role in mitochondrial function and protein trafficking between the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus [4–6]. Mutations in this gene can trigger severe metabolic crises, typically during stressors such as illness or fasting, and lead to complications like rhabdomyolysis, encephalopathy, hypotonia, and prolonged QT intervals [7–9]. In addition to life-threatening events, many patients experience recurrent ‘TANGO2 spells’, episodes of sudden disorientation, ataxia, and dysarthria, that interfere with daily life and development [10, 11].

Our study was inspired by the lived experiences of families from Doura, a town in the Hebron region of Palestine, who had long struggled with unexplained and recurrent medical episodes in their children. Through exome sequencing of three probands and subsequent targeted Sanger validation, we identified the same homozygous missense variant in exon 6 of TANGO2 (NM_152906.7:c.443T > G; NP_690870.3:p.(Leu148Trp)) in 16 affected individuals from 10 consanguineous families. Haplotype analysis confirmed a shared homozygous block flanking the variant, consistent with a founder mutation.

This finding is especially significant in the context of the Palestinian population, where consanguineous marriage remains culturally prevalent, particularly in southern regions such as Hebron. Reported consanguinity rates in some Palestinian communities exceed 40% [12–14], a factor that substantially increases the burden of autosomal recessive disorders and offers a unique opportunity to identify founder mutations and better understand rare diseases at a population level [15, 16].

Here, we not only establish the pathogenicity of the TANGO2 c.443T > G p.(Leu148Trp) variant and propose its reclassification from a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) to likely pathogenic, but also highlight the importance of early genetic diagnosis and culturally tailored screening approaches in high-consanguinity populations. Importantly, supportive management with coenzyme Q10 and B-complex vitamins (B-100), previously reported in TANGO2 deficiency, was commonly used in our cohort; several families reported reduced frequency and/or severity of spells following supplementation [2, 11, 17]. Our study expands the clinical and genetic characterization of TANGO2 deficiency in an underrepresented population and supports early molecular diagnosis and targeted screening in high-consanguinity settings.

Materials and methods

Study subjects and ethical considerations

Families were recruited through clinical referrals to Caritas Baby Hospital (Bethlehem, Palestine), and Makassed Hospital in East Jerusalem, Palestine, where initial evaluations of suspected metabolic or neurodevelopmental disorders were conducted. Medical histories were obtained through structured family interviews (Supplementary File 1) and review of clinical records. Families with recurrent cases of unexplained episodes of rhabdomyolysis, metabolic crises, or neurodevelopmental regression were referred to Bethlehem University for genetic testing and follow-up.

In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, all participants’ parents or legal guardians gave their informed written consent. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Bethlehem University reviewed and approved the study protocol. Genetic testing, including exome sequencing and targeted Sanger validation, was performed at the Hereditary Research Laboratory, Bethlehem University.

Exome sequencing and variant identification

Exome sequencing (ES) was initially performed on three affected individuals from unrelated families (Family A: II-1, Family B: III-3, and Family D: II-3; see Fig. 1) to identify the underlying genetic cause. For the remaining families (C and E–J), targeted Sanger sequencing was employed to screen for the candidate variant identified through ES and to perform segregation analysis. Sanger sequencing was carried out using an ABI 3130 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems®). The primers used for amplification of the TANGO2 c.443T > G p.(Leu148Trp) variant at GRCh37: NC_000022.10:g.20043528T > G were: Forward 5′-TGGCCACAAAGTCATCTCAG-3′ and Reverse 5′-TGCAAGCTCCTGACCTGG-3′.

ES was conducted at the Hereditary Research Laboratory, Bethlehem University, using the Illumina® NextSeq™ 500 Sequencing System. DNA libraries were prepared with the Nextera™ Flex for Enrichment Prep Kit. Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA). Preprocessing included removal of PCR duplicates, indel realignment, and base quality recalibration using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK).

Variant annotation was performed using ANNOVAR with reference to multiple databases, including gnomAD [18], AF PopMax, and a local Palestinian in-house dataset. Variants were filtered based on the following criteria: minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1% in gnomAD and ExAC, functional effect (nonsynonymous, nonsense, frameshift, or splice-site), and predicted pathogenicity by in silico tools such as PolyPhen-2 [19], AlphaMissense [20], and REVEL [21]. In addition, ES was independently performed for Family G, individual III-1, at Arcensus Diagnostics (Germany).

Copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed on exome sequencing data from all three probands using XHMM (eXome-Hidden Markov Model) software to exclude potential deletions at the TANGO2 locus [22]. No pathogenic deletions or duplications were detected across the exomes. Specifically, read depth analysis at the TANGO2 locus demonstrated normal diploid coverage, excluding underlying copy number variants.

Haplotype analysis utilizing microsatellite markers

For this study, we focused on five short tandem repeat (STR) markers situated in proximity to the TANGO2 p.(Leu148Trp) locus (see Figs. 1 and 2). STR markers were selected and primer pairs were designed using the Hemi-NeSTR online tool. Primers were chosen to generate amplicons suitable for capillary electrophoresis (typically ~ 100–300 bp) and to target a unique flanking sequence around each repeat; final primer sequences and genomic coordinates are provided in Table S1. Each polymerase chain reaction (PCR) product was combined with 0.25 µl of GeneScan™ 500 ROX™ Dye, and the resulting mixtures were subjected to capillary electrophoresis on an Applied Biosystems 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To analyze the electropherograms produced by the 3500, we employed Gene Mapper software (Version 5.2) for genotype calling and extraction. The construction of haplotypes was performed manually.

Protein structure analysis

The crystal structure of the human TANGO2 (Protein Data Bank [PDB]: 9BWA, 1.70 Å resolution) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank. Structural visualization and in silico mutagenesis were performed using PyMOL (version 3.1.0, Schrödinger, LLC) [23]. The p.Leu148Trp mutation was introduced using PyMOL’s mutagenesis wizard, and steric clashes were identified as interatomic distances < 2.2 Å between heavy atoms of the mutant residue and surrounding residues. Neighboring residues were defined as those within 5 Å of position 148. Thermodynamic stability predictions were obtained using DynaMut2 (http://biosig.unimelb.edu.au/dynamut2/) [24], a machine learning-based predictor of protein stability changes upon mutation. DynaMut2 integrates structural information with graph-based signatures to predict the change in Gibbs free energy (ΔΔG) upon mutation, where negative values indicate destabilization.

Results

Clinical features of patients with TANGO2-related metabolic encephalopathy

We evaluated 16 affected individuals from 10 consanguineous Palestinian families, all homozygous for the TANGO2 NM_152906.7:c.443T > G; NP_690870.3:p.(Leu148Trp) variant (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1). The variant was predicted to be deleterious, with a PolyPhen-2 score of 1.0 and an AlphaMissense pathogenicity score of 0.9345, both supporting a damaging effect on protein function; notably, the variant is absent from population databases, including gnomAD v2.1.1 and v3.1.2 (last accessed December, 2025). In our Palestinian in-house exome database (1,552 individuals), two heterozygous carriers of p.Leu148Trp were identified, corresponding to an allele frequency of 0.064%. Using American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) criteria together with ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation (SVI) recommendations for autosomal recessive disorders, the variant fulfills PM2 (absence from population databases), PM3 (homozygous occurrence in affected individuals with a recessive disorder), PP1_Strong (extensive segregation across 10 unrelated families with 16 affected homozygous individuals and founder haplotype confirmation), PP3 (multiple computational and structural analyses supporting a deleterious effect), and PP4 (highly specific phenotype consistent with TANGO2 deficiency disorder). Taken together with the shared founder haplotype and structural evidence supporting protein destabilization, these findings support classification of p.(Leu148Trp) as likely pathogenic. Four of the affected family members were deceased (FC.IV-1, FE.II-5 [CVS sample], FF.II-2, FJ.II-2), and therefore complete clinical data could not be obtained for these individuals. Consanguinity was confirmed in 9/16 patients, and a positive family history was reported in most cases. The majority of patients presented during infancy or early childhood, with a median age of onset around 2–3 years; one individual remained undiagnosed until age 11 (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1).

Neurological and developmental features

Psychomotor delay was observed in all patients with available data, with frequent delays in both motor and language and social milestones. Intellectual disability ranged from mild to moderate. Several individuals exhibited psychomotor regression or dysarthria during acute episodes. Delayed walking, gait abnormality, and frequent falls were common. Learning difficulties and attention deficit and hyperactivity symptoms were reported in a subset (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1).

Neurological examination often revealed episodic weakness in 11 patients, dystonia (6/12), and occasionally spasticity or hyperreflexia (4/12). Seizure-like episodes were documented in three patients, typically focal atonic events with head drops or ocular deviations, although Electroencephalogram (EEG) findings were frequently normal. Recurrent TANGO2 spells were a hallmark feature in all patients with available data. The patients experienced repeated episodes of altered consciousness, sometimes with coma-like presentations (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1).

Metabolic manifestations

Acute metabolic crises were reported in nearly all patients, often triggered by fasting or intercurrent illness. These were characterized by hypoglycemia (6/12), rhabdomyolysis with markedly elevated creatine phosphokinase (CPK) (up to > 200,000 U/L), and encephalopathy with altered consciousness. Laboratory evaluations frequently showed lactic acidosis (7/12) and hyperammonemia (4/12). Abnormalities in acylcarnitine profiles were detected in one patient, while urine organic acids were variably reported. Oromotor dysfunction was noted in nine patients, particularly during spells, with several exhibiting excessive salivation (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1).

Systemic involvement

Cardiac manifestations ranged from normal findings to arrhythmias and cardiomyopathy. Prolonged QTc was observed in one patient, while one patient died at age 8 due to cardiac complications. Musculoskeletal anomalies were rare but included hip dysplasia in one patient. Endocrine and immunological abnormalities were occasionally observed, including mild hypothyroidism in one patient and recurrent infections in three. Ocular features such as nystagmus, myopia, strabismus, or optic disk pallor were reported in five patients (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1).

Neuroimaging

Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) findings were largely unremarkable. The only patient with a clear abnormality was Family G, III-1, who showed a prominent pineal gland (10 mm) and mild inflammatory mucosal thickening in both maxillary sinuses. This individual also underwent exome sequencing, which identified a heterozygous variant of uncertain significance (VUS) in SCN2A NM_001040142.2:c.5051 A > T; NP_001035232.1:p.(Tyr1684Phe), a gene associated with episodic ataxia. Therefore, it is unclear whether the MRI abnormality and some neurological features in this case are attributable to TANGO2 deficiency or the SCN2A variant (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1).

Treatment and outcomes

Most patients were treated with a combination of L-carnitine, B-complex vitamins, and coenzyme Q10. Clinical outcomes were variable: several patients stabilized on supplements and are currently doing well, while others continue to experience significant morbidity from recurrent spells and metabolic decompensation (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1).

Microsatellite analysis identifies a shared founder haplotype in TANGO2-related metabolic encephalopathy

To assess whether the recurrent c.443T > G p.(Leu148Trp) variant in TANGO2 gene originated from a common ancestor, we performed haplotype analysis using five microsatellite markers - TANGM3, TANGM4, TANGM6, TANGM11, and TANGM17 - spanning approximately 2.5 Mb around the TANGO2 locus on chromosome 22 (Figs. 1 and 3, Table S1).

STR genotyping was performed on 27 individuals from the ten families, including 9 of the 16 affected individuals with TANGO2-related metabolic encephalopathy and 18 unaffected relatives. As shown in Figs. 1 and 3, all genotyped affected individuals (n = 9) shared an identical homozygous core haplotype across four contiguous microsatellite markers spanning the TANGO2 locus, defined by the allele combination 229–157–170–191 (TANGM3–TANGM4–TANGM6–TANGM11). Most affected individuals also carried the same allele at the distal marker TANGM17 (161); however, variation at TANGM17 was observed in a subset, consistent with recombination outside the core disease-associated haplotype. The five markers span approximately ~ 2.5 Mb around the TANGO2 variant (~ 1.1 Mb upstream and ~ 1.4 Mb downstream), and the minimal shared region of homozygosity encompassing the p.(Leu148Trp) locus extends at least from TANGM3 to TANGM11.

While some unaffected relatives carried one copy of the founder haplotype (i.e., were heterozygous), their second allele displayed high variability, and none were homozygous for the same conserved block observed in affected individuals. This pattern reflects increased background haplotype diversity on the wild-type allele and reinforces the disease-associated block’s specificity (Figs. 1 and 3).

The sharing of this conserved homozygous haplotype in affected individuals across eight independently ascertained families supports the existence of a founder effect. These findings indicate that the p.(Leu148Trp) variant likely originated from a single ancestral mutation, segregating with a tightly linked ancestral haplotype in the Hebron region of Palestine.

Structural impact of the p.Leu148Trp variant

To assess the molecular consequences of the p.Leu148Trp substitution, we analyzed the recently solved crystal structure of human TANGO2 (PDB: 9BWA, 1.70 Å resolution). Leucine 148 is located in a highly conserved region and is deeply buried within the hydrophobic core of the protein (Supplementary Figure S1A, C). Structural analysis indicated that the residue occupies a tightly constrained pocket surrounded by multiple hydrophobic residues, including Phe6, Phe8, Ile122, Ile134, Ile146, Val147, Pro150, Tyr153, and Leu160.

In silico mutagenesis indicated that substitution of leucine with tryptophan introduces a substantially larger aromatic side chain, representing a ~ 36.5% increase in side-chain volume (167 Å³ → 228 Å³, based on published amino acid volume estimates). Structural modeling suggests that the bulky tryptophan indole ring is unlikely to be accommodated within the constrained hydrophobic pocket, leading to predicted steric clashes (interatomic distances < 2.2 Å) with surrounding residues, particularly Ile146 and Tyr153 (Supplementary Figure S1B, D).

Thermodynamic stability analysis using DynaMut2 predicted a destabilizing change in Gibbs free energy (ΔΔG = -2.25 kcal/mol), consistent with the observed structural disruption. These findings provide plausible structural mechanistic evidence supporting the pathogenicity of p.Leu148Trp and are consistent with computational predictions (PolyPhen-2: 1.0, AlphaMissense: 0.9345) and the severe clinical phenotype observed in our cohort.

Discussion

Our study reports a homozygous missense variant in TANGO2 c.443T > G; p.(Leu148Trp) identified in 16 individuals across 10 consanguineous Palestinian families from the Hebron region. This variant co-segregated with disease phenotype, was absent in healthy population databases, and lies in a functionally conserved domain of the protein. These findings, supported by bioinformatic predictions and population data, provide strong evidence for reclassifying the variant from a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) to likely pathogenic.

Since the initial description of TANGO2 deficiency in 2016, over 100 individuals have been reported worldwide [2, 3, 9]. Yet, TDD remains underdiagnosed, in part due to its highly variable phenotype, which spans episodic rhabdomyolysis, developmental delay, seizures, encephalopathy, and life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias [10, 11]. Our cohort mirrored this heterogeneity; some patients initially presented with seizures and global developmental delay, while others showed primarily muscle-related symptoms, including hypotonia and elevated CPK levels. These findings reinforce the notion that TDD should be considered in any patient with recurrent encephalopathy or unexplained metabolic crises, even in the absence of cardiac involvement [25].

The identification of a shared haplotype block around the p.(Leu148Trp) variant is consistent with a founder mutation, an observation supported by the high rates of consanguinity and endogamy within Palestinian communities, particularly in Hebron [14]. This demographic structure offers a unique opportunity to identify and validate founder mutations, as seen in other rare disorders emerging from similarly structured populations [26, 27]. Our findings underline the importance of community-based genetic studies in high-consanguinity settings, where rare recessive disorders may not be rare at all, but rather hidden by a lack of awareness and diagnostic access [28].

While our study presents compelling clinical-genetic evidence establishing p.(Leu148Trp) as pathogenic, direct functional characterization of this specific variant remains to be performed. TANGO2’s biological roles have been increasingly elucidated through functional studies in patient-derived cells, including demonstrations of absent mitochondrial proteins in fibroblast-derived mitochondrial extracts, involvement in ER–Golgi trafficking, and regulation of autophagy [4–6, 29]. Understanding precisely how the p.(Leu148Trp) substitution disrupts these molecular functions would provide mechanistic insights that could inform targeted therapeutic strategies and refine clinical management.

Consistent with prior reports, several families in our cohort described clinical improvement following coenzyme Q10 and B-complex vitamin supplementation [2, 11, 30]. Additionally, recent evidence suggests folate supplementation may represent a promising therapeutic avenue, particularly for preventing ventricular arrhythmias in TANGO2 deficiency [31]. While the precise mechanism of action is not fully understood, both supplements play plausible roles in maintaining cellular metabolism: coenzyme Q10 functions as an essential electron carrier in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, thereby improving energy homeostasis, while B-complex vitamins serve as cofactors for multiple enzymatic reactions, including those involved in amino acid and neurotransmitter metabolism [32, 33]. Thus, their beneficial effect is likely indirect, by enhancing general metabolic resilience, rather than specifically correcting the underlying TANGO2 defect. Nevertheless, the consistent clinical response provides supportive evidence for the pathogenicity of the p.(Leu148Trp) variant. These observations highlight the need for proactive management, including metabolic crisis prevention, cardiac monitoring, and tailored nutritional support.

Finally, this study illustrates the diagnostic power of integrated genomic and clinical approaches, particularly when applied in the context of underrepresented populations. In a setting where genetic diagnostics are not routinely accessible, the identification of a single shared mutation among multiple affected families has profound implications: it enables cascade testing, empowers early intervention, and opens the door for population-specific screening strategies. We urge the inclusion of founder variants like p.(Leu148Trp) in local and regional gene panels for epilepsy, rhabdomyolysis, and sudden unexplained childhood collapse.

In conclusion, our findings expand the known phenotypic and geographic spectrum of TANGO2 deficiency and highlight the importance of conducting genetic studies in diverse populations. They also speak to the resilience of families who, despite years of uncertainty, participated in this research with the hope of bringing clarity, not only for their children, but for others still undiagnosed.
