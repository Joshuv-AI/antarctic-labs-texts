Faster Logconcave Sampling from a Cold Start in High Dimension
Yunbum Kook, Santosh S. Vempala

Abstract
We present a faster algorithm to generate a warm start for sampling an arbitrary logconcave density specified by an evaluation oracle, leading to the first sub-cubic sampling algorithms for inputs in (near-)isotropic position. A long line of prior work incurred a warm-start penalty of at least linear in the dimension, hitting a cubic barrier, even for the special case of uniform sampling from convex bodies. Our improvement relies on two key ingredients of independent interest. (1) We show how to sample given a warm start in weaker notions of distance, in particular q q -Rényi divergence for q = 𝒪 ~ ​ ( 1 ) q=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1) , whereas previous analyses required stringent ∞ \infty -Rényi divergence (with the exception of Hit-and-Run, whose known mixing time is higher). This marks the first improvement in the required warmness since Lovász and Simonovits (1991). (2) We refine and generalize the log-Sobolev inequality of Lee and Vempala (2018), originally established for isotropic logconcave distributions in terms of the diameter of the support, to logconcave distributions in terms of a geometric average of the support diameter and the largest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix.

Introduction

Sampling from high-dimensional distributions is a fundamental problem
that arises frequently in differential privacy
[
MT07
,
HT10
,
Mir17
]
,
scientific computing
[
CV16
,
KLSV22
]
, and system
biology
[
T+13
,
HCT+17
]
. It has broad applications,
ranging from volume estimation of convex bodies and integration of
high-dimensional functions to Bayesian inference and stochastic optimization.

In this paper, we focus on the problem of sampling arbitrary logconcave
functions given access to an evaluation oracle. An important special
case of this problem, which captures many of its challenges and has
provided the gateway to efficient algorithms, is uniform sampling
from a convex body given by a membership oracle (Definition
1.11
).
We introduce new ideas and analyses first for uniform sampling, and
later extend them to general logconcave sampling. To put our results
in context, we begin with a brief account of the history of sampling
and the crucial role of warm starts.

A brief history of sampling and warmth.

In high-dimensional regimes, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) (or
random
walk
) serves as the primary general approach. At its core, MCMC leverages
a transition kernel designed to explore a space in a way that, after
sufficiently many iterations, the resulting sample has distribution
close to a desired one. The efficiency of an MCMC algorithm is quantified
by its query complexity: given target accuracy
\varepsilon>0
, query complexity
measures the number of oracle queries required to generate a sample
whose law is
\varepsilon
-close to the target distribution, measured by
metrics such as total variation (
\mathsf{TV}
) distance or
\chi^{2}
-divergence
(see §
1.3
for definitions).

Dyer, Frieze, and Kannan
[
DFK91
]
proposed the first algorithm
for uniformly sampling a convex body
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
in polynomial
time, the central ingredient in the first polynomial time (randomized)
algorithm for volume estimation. They used a walk on a sufficiently
fine grid in a suitably “smoothened” body and bounded its
conductance

(i.e., conditional escape probability) from below by an inverse polynomial
in the dimension
n
and the Euclidean diameter
D
of the body.

Following this, Lovász and Simonovits
[
Lov90
,
LS90
]

introduced the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
, where each proposed step is a uniform random
point in a ball of fixed radius around the current point, and is accepted
if the proposal is in the body. They developed a general framework
for sampling in continuous domains, bounded the conductance via
isoperimetry

of the target distribution, and introduced
s
-conductance
,
which lower-bounds the conductance of the Markov chain for subsets
of measure at least
s>0
. Since the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
can have bad starts (e.g.,
near the corner of a convex body), this notion led to a mixing rate
of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
from a
warm start
(i.e.,
M=\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\pi_{0}}\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}/\mathrm{d}\pi\lesssim 1

for initial
\pi_{0}
and target
\pi
), where the mixing rate depends
polynomially on
M
. In words, the warm start refers to an initial
distribution already close to a target in this sense. Hereafter, we
use the
q
-Rényi divergence (
\mathcal{R}_{q}
) for
q\in(1,\infty]

to quantify the warmness (see §
1.3
; recall the monotonicity
of
\mathcal{R}_{q}
in
q
, so
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warm is stronger than

\mathcal{R}_{2}
-warm for instance).

To achieve such an
M
-warm start, they used a basic annealing procedure—starting
from a unit ball contained in
\mathcal{K}
and gradually growing the ball
truncated to
\mathcal{K}
; each distribution in the sequence provides an

\mathcal{O}(1)
-warm start to the next one. In their analysis, the dependence
on the distance
\varepsilon
to the target distribution is also inverse
polynomial,
\operatorname{poly}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}
. We note that in their paper
and in all subsequent work till very recently
[
KZ25
,
KV25
]
,
even though the initial distribution has bounded pointwise distance
(i.e., in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
), the final distribution has weaker guarantees
(in
\chi^{2}
or
\mathsf{TV}
).

The next major improvement came in
[
KLS97
]
which introduced
the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
, an analytical tool to overcome some challenges
of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
. It directly uses conductance (rather than
s
-conductance),
so its mixing rate achieves
\log\nicefrac{{M}}{{\varepsilon}}
, log-dependence
on both the warmness and the target distance. However, its implementation
uses rejection sampling, so its query complexity scales as
\operatorname{poly}M
.
They used the annealing approach in earlier work to generate a warm
start.

Nevertheless, from an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-warm start in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
, they
achieved an improved
n^{3}
-complexity of sampling an isotropic
convex body. In a companion paper
[
KLS95
]
, directly motivated
by the mixing rate of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
in the same setup, they posited the
Kannan-Lovász-Simonovits (KLS) conjecture, which has since become
a central and unifying problem for asymptotic convex geometry and
functional analysis. Following a long line of progress, the current
best bound is
\mathcal{O}(\log^{1/2}n)

[
Kla23
]
, which in particular
implies a mixing rate of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})
for the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
in an isotropic
convex body from an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-warm start.

So far, the mixing rate was analyzed based on Cheeger or Poincaré
inequalities, which have, at best, logarithmic dependence on the warmness
parameter. Kannan, Lovász, and Montenegro
[
LK99
,
KLM06
]

introduced
blocking conductance
to improve the analysis of
the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
. They established a
logarithmic Cheeger inequality

for convex bodies, improving the mixing rate to
n^{3}
for isotropic
convex bodies
without
a warm start. While this does not yield
an algorithmic improvement (as the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
has to ultimately be implemented
with the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
and rejection sampling), this framework has turned
out to be useful for the analysis of Markov chains.

In a parallel line of work, Lovász and Vempala
[
LV06b
]

analyzed the
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
sampler
[
Smi84
]
, showing a mixing
rate of
n^{2}D^{2}
for a convex body with diameter
D
under a
weaker warm start, namely in
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-divergence (i.e.,
M_{2}=\lVert\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}/\mathrm{d}\pi\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}
)
with a logarithmic dependence on
M_{2}
. This was later extended
by
[
LV06a
]
to logconcave distributions and has remained the
only rapidly mixing Markov chain from any start for general logconcave
distributions. It played a crucial role in an improved volume algorithm

[
LV06c
]
. However, unlike the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
, the KLS conjecture
does not imply a faster mixing rate for
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
even in isotropic
convex bodies.

In 2015, using the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
, Cousins and Vempala
[
CV15
]

showed how to sample a well-rounded convex body (a condition weaker
than isotropy) with
n^{3}
queries via a new annealing scheme called

\mathsf{Gaussian\ cooling}
, without any warm start assumption. It uses a sequence of Gaussians
of increasing variance restricted to the convex body, each providing
a warm start to the next. This is the current state-of-the-art and
was recently extended to arbitrary logconcave densities for sampling
(and integration) while preserving the same complexity as the special
case of convex bodies
[
KV25
]
. Going below
n^{3}
has
remained an open problem, even after the near-resolution of the KLS
conjecture.

There is, however, one other development that raises the possibility
of going below cubic time. In 2018, Lee and Vempala
[
LV24
]

established logarithmic Sobolev inequality for isotropic logconcave
distributions, improving the log-Sobolev isoperimetric constant from

\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
to tight
\mathcal{O}(D)
. This leads to an improved
n^{2.5}
-mixing
of the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
in an isotropic convex body from any start. Again, since
the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
is a conceptual process, it does not give an algorithmic
improvement.

The leads us to the main motivating question of this paper.

Question
.

Is there an algorithm of sub-cubic complexity to sample a convex body
in near-isotropic position? Can the sub-cubic complexity of the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}

be realized algorithmically?

Besides uniform sampling (and general logconcave sampling), a special
case of considerable interest is sampling a truncated Gaussian, i.e.,
a standard Gaussian distribution restricted to an arbitrary convex
body containing the origin.

The above question opens up a set of concrete challenges, which are
also interesting in their own right. We discuss them in more detail
below. To give a quick preview, we mention briefly that our main result
is an affirmative answer to this main motivating question.

Uniform sampling from a warm start.

[
LS90
]
connected the convergence rate of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}

to the
Cheeger constant

C_{\mathsf{Ch}}(\pi)
defined as follows: for
a probability measure
\pi
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and Euclidean distance
d
,

C_{\mathsf{Ch},d}(\pi):=\inf_{S:\pi(S)\leq\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\pi(\partial S)}{\pi(S)}\,,

where
S
is any open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with smooth boundary,
\pi(\partial S):=\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\pi(S_{\varepsilon})-\pi(S)}{\varepsilon}
,
and
S_{\varepsilon}=\{x:d(x,S)\leq\varepsilon\}
. The
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
requires
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}C_{\mathsf{Ch},d}^{-2}(\pi))

queries in expectation to reach a distribution that is
\varepsilon
-close
to the target uniform distribution
\pi
over a convex body in
\mathsf{TV}
-distance,
starting from a
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warm start. We use the notation

\mathsf{Ball\ walk}:\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\to\mathsf{TV}
to indicate the required input warmness
and output metric. Significant breakthroughs have refined the Cheeger
bound
[
PW60
,
KLS95
,
Eld13
,
LV24
,
Che21
,
KL22
,
Kla23
]
,
leading to the current best bound
C_{\mathsf{Ch},d}^{-2}(\pi)\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log n

by Klartag
[
Kla23
]
, where
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert
denotes the
largest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix of
\pi
.

A different sampler
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}:\mathcal{R}_{2}\to\mathcal{R}_{2}
, studied in
[
Lov99
,
LV06b
]
,
was shown to mix using
n^{2}D^{2}C_{\mathsf{Ch},d_{\mathcal{K}}}^{-2}(\pi)
queries,
where
d_{\mathcal{K}}
is the cross-ratio distance. Unlike the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
, since

C_{\mathsf{Ch},d_{\mathcal{K}}}\leq 1
is tight, progress on the KLS conjecture does
not lead to an improvement in its complexity. Since
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert<D^{2}
,
the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
has a better bound from a
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warm start.

Recently, Kook, Vempala, and Zhang
[
KVZ24
]
introduced
\mathsf{\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}}

(equivalently, the proximal sampler
[
LST21
]
for uniform
distributions, referred to here as
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
), a refinement of the

\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
. Its convergence rate can be directly related to the
Poincaré
constant
for a target
\pi
:

Definition 1.1
.

A probability measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies a
Poincaré
inequality
with constant
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)
if for any locally
Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\operatorname{var}_{\pi}f\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]\,,

(
\mathsf{PI}
)

where
\operatorname{var}_{\pi}f=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[|f-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f|^{2}]
.

In general, the Poincaré inequality is implied by the log-Sobolev
inequality.

Definition 1.2
.

A probability measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies a
logarithmic
Sobolev inequality
with constant
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)
if for any
locally Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi}(f^{2})\leq 2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]\,,

(
\mathsf{LSI}
)

where
\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi}(f^{2}):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f^{2}\log f^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f^{2}]\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f^{2}]
.

\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
achieves a query complexity of
qn^{2}C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)
to generate
a sample with
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-guarantee, starting from a
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warmness
(i.e.,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}:\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\to\mathcal{R}_{q}
under (

PI

)).
This guarantee can be viewed as a right generalization of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}

to
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-divergence, since for logconcave probability measures

\pi
,

\frac{1}{4}\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{Ch},d}^{-2}(\pi)}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)}\leq 9\,,

where the first inequality is due to Cheeger
[

]
,
and the second follows from the Buser–Ledoux inequality
[

,
Led04
]
.
Then, Kook and Zhang

provided a query complexity
of

for

-guarantees from a

(i.e.,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}:\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\to\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
under (

LSI

)),
where

holds for any logconcave distribution
with finite support of diameter

. Even though its output guarantee
is stronger than that of the

, it still has the same bottleneck,
requiring a rather stringent

-warmness. A natural
question arises from these observations.

Question
.

Can we combine the better isoperimetric bounds of
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
(e.g.,

) with the relaxed warmness requirements of

(e.g.,

\mathcal{R}_{2}
-warmness)?

Our

in §

demonstrates that

\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
requires only

-warmness for

,

to obtain a sample with
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-guarantee while retaining a

PI

\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}:\mathcal{R}_{c}\to\mathcal{R}_{2}

under (

to

Moreover, we show analogous improvements for the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
for truncated

\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}
(referred to as

\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
to

Warm-start generation by annealing.

Our discussion naturally prompts the question of how to quickly generate
a warm start. The main idea is
annealing

[
DFK91
,
KLS97
,
LV06c
,
CV18
,
KZ25
]
.
It involves constructing a sequence
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
of distributions
gradually approaching the target
\pi
, such that
(i)

\mu_{1}

is easy to sample from (e.g., Gaussians),
(ii)

\mu_{m}
is close
to the target
\pi
, and
(iii)
a current distribution
\mu_{i}

provides a warm start to the next one
\mu_{i+1}
.

Lovász and Vempala
[
LV06c
]
proposed an annealing
scheme maintaining
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-warmness, with
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
used to transition
across annealing distributions. Due to the approximate nature of the
distributions obtained during annealing, their analysis uses a coupling
argument to account for this issue, providing guarantees for final
samples in
\mathsf{TV}
-distance. Hence, combined with
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
, when
R^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}]
,
their final sampling algorithm has query complexity of
n^{3}R^{2}

for
\mathsf{TV}
-guarantees.

\mathsf{Gaussian\ cooling}

[
CV18
]
uses a sequence of truncated Gaussians

\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}
, increasing the variance
\sigma^{2}
from

n^{-1}
to
R^{2}
according to a predetermined schedule,
\sigma^{2}\leftarrow\sigma^{2}(1+\nicefrac{{\sigma^{2}}}{{R^{2}}})
.
This scheme relays stronger
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warmness, thus being
more ‘conservative’ than the Lovász–Vempala scheme, but benefits
from the faster mixing of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
(for truncated Gaussian) compared
to
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
.
\mathsf{Gaussian\ cooling}
has complexity of
n^{2}(n\vee R^{2})
with
\mathsf{TV}
-distance
guarantees. Roughly, doubling
\sigma^{2}
takes
R^{2}/\sigma^{2}

phases, and with the complexity per phase of
n^{2}\sigma^{2}
, this
gives the overall claimed complexity. Later, Kook and Zhang
[
KZ25
]

refined this scheme by replacing the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
with
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
for Gaussian
sampling, achieving final guarantees in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-divergence
with the same complexity.

Now that we have the sampler for truncated Gaussian and uniform distributions
with
\mathcal{R}_{c}
-warmness for
c=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
, we are naturally led
to accelerate an annealing schedule of
\sigma^{2}
in
\mathsf{Gaussian\ cooling}
, with
the Lovász–Vempala scheme in mind. This would make it sufficient
to maintain only
\mathcal{R}_{c}
-warmness instead of stringent
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warmness,
while still leveraging the faster mixing of
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
. This relaxation
allows us to update
\sigma^{2}
more
rapidly
by
\sigma^{2}\leftarrow\sigma^{2}(1+\nicefrac{{\sigma}}{{R}})

(§
1.2.3
). Doubling
\sigma^{2}
now
requires roughly
R/\sigma
annealing phases, and the query complexity
improves to
n^{2}R\sigma
, compared to
n^{2}R^{2}
. Unfortunately,
since the annealing procedure must continue until
\sigma^{2}\asymp R^{2}
,
this seemingly promising approach yields no overall complexity gain.

Log-Sobolev inequality for strongly logconcave distributions.

This leads us to the question how to sample
\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}

faster. One possibility is to develop an improved (

LSI

)
if possible. There are at least two distinct approaches to bounding

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}
for a strongly logconcave density with support of diameter

D
. The first, which yields a bound
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}})\leq\sigma^{2}
,
directly leverages strong logconcavity and can be established by multiple
ways, e.g., Caffarelli’s contraction theorem
[
Caf00
]

or the Bakry–Émery condition
[
BGL14
]
. The second
is of the form
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}})\lesssim D^{2}

[
FK99
]
,
independent of strong logconcavity and arises from finite diameter
of the distribution. The latter was improved to
\mathcal{O}(D)

for
isotropic
logconcave distributions
[
LV24
]
.

In fact, our current understanding still remains incomplete. To illustrate
this, consider the uniform distribution
\pi
over an isotropic convex
body, which has diameter at most
n+1

[
KLS95
]
. Here, the
strong logconcavity-based bound gives
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{n^{2}})\leq n^{2}
,
while the second method, through the Holley–Stroock perturbation
principle (Lemma
1.15
) and the
\mathcal{O}(D)
-bound
above, implies a better bound of
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{n^{2}})\lesssim C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim n
.
This gap highlights the following question.

Question
.

Is there a better bound on
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}
for strongly logconcave distributions
with compact support?

Our
Result 2
provides a new bound of the form
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}})\lesssim D\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}
,
valid for all
\sigma^{2}>0
and logconcave distributions
\pi

supported on diameter-
D
domains. In the example above, our bound
yields
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}})\lesssim n
for
any

\sigma^{2}
.

Back to warm-start generation.

With this new machinery in hand, we return to the warm-start generation
problem. It is now apparent that our improved LSI bound should have
direct algorithmic implications. In the Gaussian-annealing method,
we may assume that the diameter is of order
R
by a suitable preprocessing,
and thus once we reach
\sigma^{2}\gtrsim R\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}
,
sampling from
\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}
can be
accelerated

due to the faster mixing rate of
n^{2}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}})\leq n^{2}R\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}
.
The complexity for doubling
\sigma^{2}
before reaching this accelerated
phase is bounded by
n^{2}R\sigma\lesssim n^{2}R^{3/2}\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/4}
,
and similarly, subsequent doublings within the accelerated phase also
incur at most the same complexity. Therefore, we establish a faster
sampling algorithm with total query complexity of
n^{2}R^{3/2}\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/4}

(
Result 3
) (note
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\leq R^{2}
). Notably, when

\pi
is near-isotropic, this translates to an
n^{2.75}
-complexity
sampling algorithm, breaking the previous cubic bound first established
in
[
CV15
,
CV18
]
. For the special case of sampling
a standard Gaussian restricted to an arbitrary convex body, the complexity
is
n^{2.5}
, again improving on the previous best cubic bound
[
CV14
]
.

Extension to logconcave distributions.

Our approach can be extended to general logconcave distributions
under a well-defined function oracle. Although the extension is conceptually
similar, it involves some technical complications related to handling
general convex potentials.

The complexity of logconcave sampling under this zeroth-order oracle
has been studied using similar tools, notably by the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}

[
LS93
]
,

\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}

[
LV06a
,
LV07
]
, and the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}

[
KV25
]
.
In
[
KV25
]
, sampling from
\pi
is reduced to sampling
from an exponential distribution:

\bar{\pi}(x,t)\propto\exp(-nt)\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}(x,t)\quad\text{for the covex set }\mathcal{K}:=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}:V(x)\leq nt\}\,.

To facilitate warm-start generation, they proposed
\mathsf{Tilted\ Gaussian\ cooling}
that uses
“tilted” Gaussians defined as

1.2
Technical overview

Here we discuss detailed outlines of our approach and the main proofs.

1.2.1
Sampling under weaker warmness

\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
under relaxed warmness.

Analysis of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
has hitherto assumed
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warmness

[
LS90
]
. Unlike
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
, when the walk is near a corner
of a convex body (e.g., the tip of a cone), most steps become wasted
and make no progress, because the probability of finding another point
within the intersection of the step ball and the convex body can be
exponentially small. However, if the initial distribution is already
close to the uniform distribution
\pi
in the
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-sense,
then the chance of stepping into such low-probability regions is small,
and thus the total expected wasted queries can still be bounded.

Precisely, in the analysis of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
with step size
\delta

(specifically, the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
) in
[
KLS97
]
, each step samples
uniformly from
B_{\delta}(x)\cap\mathcal{K}
with proposal uniform in
B_{\delta}(x)
,
so the success probability—called the
local conductance
—is

\ell(x)=\frac{\operatorname{vol}\bigl(B_{\delta}(x)\cap\mathcal{K}\bigr)}{\operatorname{vol}\bigl(B_{\delta}(x)\bigr)}\,.

Its stationary distribution
\pi^{\mathsf{SW}}
is proportional to
\ell
,
so if the initial distribution is already at stationarity, then the
expected number of trials per step is
\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\mathsf{SW}}}\ell^{-1}=\mathcal{O}(1)
,
provided that the body contains a unit ball and the step size is
\delta\lesssim n^{-1/2}
.

Relaxing the warmness requirement is challenging. Suppose one starts
from a general initial distribution
\mu
and attempts a change of
reference measure, say, through a Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\frac{1}{\ell}\bigr)^{2}\leq\frac{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\ell^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\ell\,\mathrm{d}x}\cdot\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ \ \ }\,}{\mathrm{d}\pi^{\mathsf{SW}}}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\pi^{\mathsf{SW}})}^{2}=\exp\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{2}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi^{\mathsf{SW}})\bigr)\,\frac{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\ell^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\ell\,\mathrm{d}x}\,.

Even if
\mu
and
\pi
are close in
\mathcal{R}_{2}
, it is unclear
how to bound
\int_{\mathcal{K}}\ell^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}x
, and thus previous work has
always assumed
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warmness to replace
\mu
by
\pi^{\mathsf{SW}}

without introducing extra
\ell^{-\alpha}
into
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\ell^{-1}
.

Proximal sampler.

Before introducing
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
, we briefly discuss the proximal sampler
[
LST21
,
CCSW22
,
FYC23
,
Wib25
]
,
which has been analyzed in
well-conditioned
settings, where

-\log\pi^{X}
is
\alpha
-strongly convex and
\beta
-smooth in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Such
uniform
regularity allows rejection sampling to
implement the second step
x_{i+1}\sim\pi^{X|Y=y_{i+1}}
efficiently,
requiring only
\mathcal{O}(1)
queries for
any

y_{i+1}
.

However, constrained settings (such as uniform sampling over a convex
body
\mathcal{K}
) pose new challenges. For instance, for the uniform target

\pi^{X}\propto\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}
, one can readily derive that
\pi^{Y}(y)=\ell(y)/\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}

and
\pi^{X|Y=y}\propto\mathcal{N}(y,hI_{n})|_{\mathcal{K}}
, where
\ell(y)=\mathbb{P}_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(y,hI_{n})}(Z\in\mathcal{K})

serves as a Gaussian version of the local conductance. Even at stationarity

\pi^{X}
, the expected per-step complexity of rejection sampling
with proposal
\mathcal{N}(y,hI_{n})
becomes infinite, as
\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\ell^{-1}

is unbounded.

To address this,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}

[
KVZ24
]
introduces a threshold
parameter
N
: the algorithm declares failure if the rejection sampling
exceeds
N
attempts. Under a step size
h\asymp n^{-2}
, they identify
an
essential domain
as
\mathcal{K}_{t/n}=\mathcal{K}+B_{t/n}
, and show that

\pi^{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{t/n})\lesssim e^{-t^{2}}
. For an
M_{\infty}
-warm

\mu
, the expected number of trials can be bounded by carefully
choosing parameters
t,N
. For
\delta=t/n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu*\gamma_{h}}\bigl[\frac{1}{\ell}\wedge N\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq M_{\infty}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\bigl[\frac{1}{\ell}\wedge N\bigr]\leq M_{\infty}\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}}{\ell}+N\pi^{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\mathcal{K}_{\delta})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=M_{\infty}\bigl(\frac{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}+N\pi^{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\mathcal{K}_{\delta})\bigr)\lesssim M_{\infty}(e^{t}+Ne^{-t^{2}})\,,

ensuring exponentially small failure probability as well. Nonetheless,
similar to the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
, applying Cauchy–Schwarz to relax
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warmness
fails, as
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ell^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}x
still appears in the analysis.

Improved analysis under a relaxed warmness.

Our approach demonstrates the first successful relaxation of
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warmness.
We avoid such pointwise closeness by carefully using the
(p,q)
-Hölder
inequality and a refined decomposition of
\mathbb{E}_{\mu*\gamma_{h}}[\ell^{-1}\wedge N]

into three regions: (
\mathsf{A}
) high local conductance in the essential
domain, (
\mathsf{B}
) low local conductance in the essential domain,
and (
\mathsf{C}
) non-essential domain: for
(\cdot)_{h}:=(\cdot)*\gamma_{h}

below,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}\bigl[\frac{1}{\ell}\wedge N\bigr]=\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell\geq N^{-p}]}\cdot+\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<N^{-p}]}\cdot+\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\cdot=:\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}+\mathsf{C}\,.

We then bound the integrand simply by
N
in
\mathsf{B}
and
\mathsf{C}
,
and change the reference measure from
\mu_{h}
to
\pi_{h}
in

\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
, defining
M_{q}=\lVert\nicefrac{{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}}{{\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}}}\rVert_{L^{q}(\pi_{h})}

and using the
(p,q)
-Hölder with parameters
p=1+\alpha^{-1}
,

q=1+\alpha
for some
\alpha\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell\geq N^{-p}]}\frac{1}{\ell^{p}}\wedge N^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}\Bigr)^{1/p}\,M_{q}\leq\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell\geq N^{-p}]}\frac{1}{\ell^{p}}\,\frac{\ell}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\Bigr)^{1/p}\,M_{q}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell\geq N^{-p}]}\frac{1}{\ell^{1/\alpha}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\Bigr)^{1/p}\,M_{q}\leq M_{q}N^{1/\alpha}\,\bigl(\frac{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\bigr)^{1/p}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}

\displaystyle\leq N\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<N^{-p}]}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}\leq NM_{q}\,\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<N^{-p}]}\frac{\ell}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\Bigr)^{1/p}\leq M_{q}\bigl(\frac{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\bigr)^{1/p}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}

\displaystyle\leq N\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}\leq NM_{2}\bigl(\pi_{h}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})\bigr)^{1/2}\,.

Selecting
\alpha=\log N
ensures
N^{1/\alpha}=1
, so our bounds
depend only on
\pi_{h}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})
and volume ratios. With
proper choices of
h
,
\delta
, and
N
, the per-step complexity
can be bounded under
\mathcal{R}_{1+\log N}
-warmness.

1.2.2
Improved LSI for strongly logconcave distributions with compact support

We now provide insights and proofs for two results: (1) a new bound
on the log-Sobolev constant,
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim D\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}
,
and (2) for a logconcave distribution
\pi
,
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\gamma_{h}\rVert\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert

whenever
h\gtrsim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\lVert\cdot\rVert
.
These results, combined with the classical Bakry–Émery condition,
yield our key bound: for strongly logconcave distributions supported
within diameter
D
,
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{h})\lesssim_{\log}D\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}

for any
h>0
. We believe each of these results is of independent
interest.

(1) Functional inequalities:
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim_{\log}D\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}
.

This result interpolates known bounds for (

LSI

):
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim D

(isotropic case) and
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim D^{2}
(general case). Unlike
(

PI

), the interpolation via affine transformations does
not work well for (

LSI

). Precisely, let
X\sim\pi
with

\Sigma=\operatorname{cov}\pi
,
Y:=\Sigma^{-1/2}X
, and
\nu:=\operatorname{law}Y
. Then,

\nu
is isotropic logconcave. For
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)
the largest Poincaré
constant of
n
-dimensional isotropic logconcave distributions and
for locally Lipschitz
f
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}_{\pi}f

\displaystyle=\operatorname{var}_{\nu}(f\circ\Sigma^{1/2})\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\lVert\nabla(f\circ\Sigma^{1/2})\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\lVert\Sigma\rVert\lVert\nabla f\circ\Sigma^{1/2}\rVert^{2}]\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]\,,

which implies
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert
. Similarly,
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(n)\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert

holds (see §
3.1.1
). However,
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(n)
can be
as large as
D\asymp n

[
LV24
]
, this LSI bound is unsatisfactory
unlike the (

PI

) case.

Inspired by
[
KL24
]
, we present a simple approach leveraging
a deep result established by Milman
[
Mil10
]

that
Gaussian concentration
and (

LSI

) are equivalent
for logconcave measures. Recall that a concentration function (Definition
3.5
)
of a probability measure
\pi
is defined as

\alpha_{\pi}(r)=\sup_{E:\pi(E)\geq 1/2}\pi(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash E_{r})\quad\text{for all }r\geq 0\,.

Gaussian concentration with constant
C_{\mathsf{Gauss}}(\pi)
refers to
\alpha_{\pi}(r)\leq 2\exp(-r^{2}/C_{\mathsf{Gauss}}(\pi))

while exponential concentration with constant
C_{\mathsf{exp}}(\pi)
refers
to
\alpha_{\pi}(r)\leq 2\exp(-r/C_{\mathsf{exp}}(\pi))
. There are two known
results on exponential concentration of logconcave measures. The first
one,
C_{\mathsf{exp}}^{2}(\pi)\lesssim C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)
, is classical and holds under
(

\alpha_{\pi}(r)\leq 2\exp(-c\min\{r/\lambda^{1/2},r^{2}/\lambda\log^{2}n\})

\lambda=\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert
and universal constant

by Bizeul
[
Biz24
]
through Eldan’s stochastic localization
(SL)
[
Eld13
]
.

Since the diameter of support is

\alpha_{\pi}(r)=0

r/D\leq 1
otherwise. Then, we have
\alpha_{\pi}(r)\leq 2\exp(-\nicefrac{{r}}{{cC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\pi)}})\leq 2\exp(-\nicefrac{{r^{2}}}{{cDC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\pi)}})
.
Due to Milman’s result on equivalence,
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim DC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\pi)\lesssim D\lambda^{1/2}\log^{1/2}n
.
Similarly, when using Bizeul’s concentration, we can obtain
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim\max\{D\lambda^{1/2},\lambda\log^{2}n\}
.
Taking minimum with the bound of
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim D^{2}
and using

\lambda\leq D^{2}
, we can conclude that
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim\max\{D\lambda^{1/2},D^{2}\wedge\lambda\log^{2}n\}\leq D^{2}\lambda^{1/2}\log n

See Remark
3.9
on a comparison between these
two LSI bounds.

We present another proof through SL, specifically a simplified version
from

(see §

which was our first approach in an earlier version.

(2) Convex geometry:
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\gamma_{h}\rVert\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert
.

Consider the isotropic uniform distribution
\pi
over a convex body
for illustration. We have
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\gamma_{h}\rVert\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi\gamma_{h})\leq h
,
where the first inequality follows from (

PI

), and the second
from the Brascamp–Lieb (or Lichnerowicz) inequality. Thus,
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\gamma_{h}\rVert\leq 1

for
h\leq 1
. As we increase
h
, this pushes the mass toward the
boundary
\partial\mathcal{K}
, likely boosting covariance. On the other hand,
for large
h\gtrsim n^{2}
, the measure
\pi\gamma_{h}
becomes
a
\Theta(1)
-perturbation of
\pi
, since the support of
\pi

has diameter at most
n+1
(due to isotropy). Thus,
\pi\gamma_{h}

is
\mathcal{O}(1)
-close to
\pi
in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
, and thus
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\gamma_{h}\rVert\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert=1
.
Therefore, it is plausible to conjecture that
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\gamma_{h}\rVert\lesssim 1

for
all

h
.

We show this conjecture for
h\gtrsim\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\|\cdot\|\asymp n^{1/2}
,
much smaller than
n^{2}
. To prove this, we bound
\mathbb{E}_{\pi\gamma_{h}}[(X\cdot v)^{2}]

for any unit vector
v
. Rewriting

\mathbb{E}_{\pi\gamma_{h}}[(X\cdot v)^{2}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(X\cdot v)^{2}\,e^{-\lVert X\rVert^{2}/2h}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}e^{-\lVert X\rVert^{2}/2h}}\,,

we aim to upper-bound the numerator and lower-bound the denominator,
both approximately by
e^{-n/2h}
. To this end, we examine which
regions mainly contribute to each expectation, relying on concentration
properties of
\pi
only.

Recall isotropic logconcave distributions concentrate in a thin shell
of radius
n^{1/2}
and width
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)

[
Gua24
]

(conjectured to be
\mathcal{O}(1)
by the well-known
thin-shell conjecture

[
ABP03
,
BK03
]
). For the thin-shell (
\mathsf{S}
),
inner (
\mathsf{I}
), and outer (
\mathsf{O}
) regions, we argue
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Den.}:

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{\pi}e^{-\lVert X\rVert^{2}/2h}\geq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\inf_{\mathsf{S}}e^{-\lVert X\rVert^{2}/2h}\gtrsim e^{-n/2h}\,\pi(\mathsf{S})\gtrsim e^{-n/2h}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Num.}:

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(X\cdot v)^{2}e^{-\lVert X\rVert^{2}/2h}]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(\cdot)\times\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{I}}]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(\cdot)\times\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(\cdot)\times\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{O}}]\,.

As for the numerator, the inner part’s contribution is negligible
due to the small ball probabilities
[
DP10
,
Biz25
]
,
while the outer region’s contribution is exponentially small since
the Gaussian weight is as small as
e^{-n/2h}
. Thus, the essential
contribution comes from the thin-shell, yielding

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(X\cdot v)^{2}e^{-\lVert X\rVert^{2}/2h}]\lesssim e^{-n/2h}\,\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot v)^{2}]\leq e^{-n/2h}\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert=e^{-n/2h}\,.

For general logconcave distributions, we use Lipschitz concentration
under (

PI

), given as

\pi(\lVert X\rVert-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\lVert X\rVert\geq t)\leq 3\exp\bigl(-t/C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\pi)\bigr)\,,

and the known bound
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log n
. We then
apply the co-area formula and integration by parts to control contributions
from the inner and outer parts.

1.2.3
Faster sampling algorithm

We derive a faster sampling algorithm for uniform distributions over
convex bodies, with query complexity
n^{2}R^{3/2}\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/4}
,
which is
n^{2.75}
for nearly isotropic ones, the first sub-cubic
bound.

Algorithm.

Our algorithm (§
4.2.1
) follows the annealing
approach from
\mathsf{Gaussian\ cooling}
; it also utilizes truncated Gaussian
\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}}

for annealing, increasing
\sigma^{2}
from
n^{-1}
up to
R^{2}
,
with some key differences. First, we eliminate the initial annealing
by
\sigma^{2}\leftarrow\sigma^{2}(1+n^{-1/2})
. Second, we accelerate
it through a faster multiplicative update by
1+\nicefrac{{\sigma}}{{q^{1/2}R}}

with
q=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
, instead of the slower update by
1+\nicefrac{{\sigma^{2}}}{{R^{2}}}
.
Third, once
\sigma^{2}
surpasses
R\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}
(i.e.,
the geometric mean between
R^{2}(\geq\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{cov}\pi)
and
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert
),
our algorithm benefits from a faster mixing of
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
due to
our new LSI bound.

Complexity.

To bound the total complexity, we need a
c
-Rényi divergence
bound for
c=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
between neighboring annealing distributions.
We establish in Lemma
4.5
that
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}}\mathbin{\|}\gamma_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}|_{\mathcal{K}})\leq qR^{2}\alpha^{2}/\sigma^{2}

for
q>1
, which generalizes the known result for
q=2

[
CV18
, Lemma 7.8]
.
This bound justifies our multiplicative update with
\alpha=\nicefrac{{\sigma}}{{q^{1/2}R}}
.
Setting
q=c
, we ensure that
\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}}
and
\gamma_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}|_{\mathcal{K}}

remain close in
\mathcal{R}_{c}
, enabling sampling by
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
with
provable guarantees.

Each
\sigma^{2}
-doubling requires at most
q^{1/2}R/\sigma
phases,
with each phase run by
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
incurring
n^{2}\sigma^{2}
queries.
Thus, each doubling has total complexity of
q^{1/2}n^{2}R\sigma
.
Given
\mathcal{O}(\log nR)
doubling phases, the total query complexity up
to
\sigma^{2}\approx R\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}
is bounded by
q^{1/2}n^{2}R^{3/2}\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/4}
.
Beyond
\sigma^{2}\gtrsim R\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}
,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
requires

n^{2}R\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}
queries thanks to our improved
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}
.
Thus, each subsequent doubling also contributes
q^{1/2}n^{2}R^{3/2}\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/4}

as well. The final transition from
\gamma_{R^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}}

to
\pi
via
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
requires
n^{2}\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert
queries.
Therefore, the total complexity is
n^{2}R^{3/2}\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/4}
.

One subtle point we have brushed over is the approximate nature of

\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
. Specifically, when sampling from
\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}}
,
it actually outputs a sample
X_{*}
satisfying
\mathcal{R}_{2}(\operatorname{law}X_{*}\mathbin{\|}\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}})\leq\varepsilon
,
thus
slightly off
from
\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}}
. Nonetheless,
we have pretended that the output is distributed as
\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}}
.
We can readily address this issue by using the triangle inequality
for
\mathsf{TV}
-distance. Let
m
be the total number of phases during
annealing,
\gamma_{i}
the target distribution for
i\in[m]
, and

P_{i}
the Markov kernel defined by
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
with suitable step
size. Then, the actual distribution of a sample at each phase
i

is
\hat{\gamma}_{i}:=\gamma_{0}P_{1}\cdots P_{i}=\hat{\gamma}_{i-1}P_{i}
.
Using the triangle inequality and data-processing inequality,

\lVert\hat{\gamma}_{i}-\gamma_{i}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\lVert\hat{\gamma}_{i-1}P_{i}-\gamma_{i-1}P_{i}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}+\lVert\gamma_{i-1}P_{i}-\gamma_{i}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\lVert\hat{\gamma}_{i-1}-\gamma_{i-1}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}+\varepsilon\,,

and induction leads to
\lVert\hat{\gamma}_{i}-\gamma_{i}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq m\varepsilon
.
Since the total complexity scales as
\operatorname{polylog}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}
,
by replacing
\varepsilon\leftarrow\varepsilon/m
, we can achieve the
desired accuracy without increasing complexity significantly.

1.2.4
Extension to general logconcave distributions

We now extend our developments thus far to general logconcave distributions,
under a well-defined function oracle. The overall strategy parallels
our approach for uniform sampling: (1) sampling under weaker warmness,

Logconcave sampling.

2.1
Uniform sampling

We recall the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
for the uniform distribution over a convex body

\mathcal{K}
(denoted as
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
here), referred to as
\mathsf{\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}}
in
[
KVZ24
]
.
Given a step size
h
, a preset threshold
N
, and the uniform target

\pi^{X}(x)\propto\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}(x)
, one iteration consists of

•

[Forward]
y_{i+1}\sim\pi^{Y|X=x_{i}}=\mathcal{N}(x_{i},hI_{n})
.

•

[Backward]
x_{i+1}\sim\pi^{X|Y=y_{i+1}}=\mathcal{N}(y_{i+1},hI_{n})|_{\mathcal{K}}\propto\exp(-\frac{1}{2h}\,\lVert\cdot-y_{i+1}\rVert^{2})\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot)
.

–

This is implemented by repeating
x_{i+1}\sim\gamma_{h}(\cdot-y_{i+1})

until
x_{i+1}\in\mathcal{K}
. If the number of attempts in this iteration
exceeds
N
, then we declare
Failure
.

We recall that
\pi^{Y}=\pi^{X}*\gamma_{h}=:\pi_{h}
.

One notable advantage of this sampler is that it separates
(i)

the convergence-rate analysis and
(ii)
the query-complexity analysis
of the backward step. The first question is how many iterations —
each consisting of a successful execution of both the forward and
backward steps — are required to achieve a target accuracy in a
desired metric. The second is how many queries are needed to successfully
implement the rejection sampling in each backward step. As a result,
the total query complexity is simply the product of these two quantities.

2.1.1
Mixing analysis

Previous work on the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
established its convergence rate under
various assumptions on the target, including (

PI

) and (

LSI

).
It was first proven for smooth densities
[
CCSW22
, Theorem 3 and 4]

and extended to distributions truncated to convex constraints
[
KVZ24
, Lemma 22]
.
For rigorous treatments of these types of strong data-processing
inequalities, we refer readers to
[
KO25
]
.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
CCSW22
, Theorem 3 and 4]
)
.

Assume that a probability measure
\pi
is absolutely continuous
with respect to the Lebesgue measure over a convex body
\mathcal{K}
, and
let
P
denote the Markov kernel of the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
. If
\pi
satisfies
(

LSI

) with constant
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}
, then for any
q\geq 1
and
distribution
\mu
with
\mu\ll\pi
,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu P\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\frac{\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)}{(1+h/C_{\mathsf{LSI}})^{2/q}}\,.

(2.1)

If
\pi
satisfies (

PI

) with constant
C_{\mathsf{PI}}
, then

\chi^{2}(\mu P\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\frac{\chi^{2}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)}{(1+h/C_{\mathsf{PI}})^{2}}\,.

(2.2)

Thus, the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
mixes in roughly
qh^{-1}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\log\frac{\mathcal{R}_{q}}{\varepsilon}

and
h^{-1}C_{\mathsf{PI}}\log\frac{\chi^{2}}{\varepsilon}
iterations under (

LSI

)
and (

PI

), respectively. Since any logconcave distribution

\pi
satisfies (

PI

), and the current best bound
[
Kla23
]

gives

\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log n\,,

\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
requires no more than
h^{-1}\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log n\log\frac{\chi^{2}(\pi_{0}\mathbin{\|}\pi)}{\varepsilon}

iterations to achieve
\chi^{2}(\pi_{0}P^{n}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\varepsilon
.

2.1.2
Complexity of the backward step

Under the relaxed warmness condition, there is no change in the mixing
analysis, but we must refine previous analysis of the backward step.
Here, we show that both the failure probability and the expected query
complexity (i.e., the expected number of trials until the first success
of rejection sampling) can be moderately bounded under weaker warmness.
In analysis, we can assume that
x_{0}=0
by translation without
loss of generality.

As briefly sketched in §
1.2
, the
essential
domain
of
\pi^{Y}=\pi_{h}
can be identified as
\mathcal{K}_{\delta}=\mathcal{K}+B_{\delta}
:

Lemma 2.4

(
[
KVZ24
, Lemma 26]
)
.

For a convex body
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

containing
B_{1}(0)
,

\pi^{Y}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2h}+\delta n\bigr)\,.

By taking
\delta=\frac{t}{n},\,h=\frac{c}{n^{2}}
for some
c,t>0
,
the measure of the non-essential part is bounded as

\pi^{Y}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{2c}+t\bigr)\,.

We now proceed to analyze the failure probability (i.e., the probability
of hitting the threshold at least once before mixing) and the expected
number of trials under wearer warmness,
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})=\mathcal{O}(1)

with
q\geq 2
. While the former can be bounded straightforwardly
using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the latter requires a more
delicate analysis as seen shortly.

(1) Failure probability.

Recall that

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)=\frac{1}{q-1}\,\log\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}^{q}=\frac{1}{q-1}\,\log\bigl(\chi^{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)+1\bigr)\,.

Suppose we run
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
with initial distribution
\mu^{X}\ll\pi^{X}
.
Denoting
\mu_{h}:=\mu^{X}*\gamma_{h}
, we can bound the failure
probability of one iteration as

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{2N}]}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{2N}]}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\,,

where the first inequality follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
and the second follows from the data-processing inequality (e.g.,
the DPI for the Rényi divergence, Lemma
1.14
). To bound

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{2N}]
, we adapt the analysis in
[
KVZ24
]
.
For
M_{2}=\lVert\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\pi\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}
, we decompose

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1-\ell)^{2N}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}=\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\cdot+\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell\geq N^{-1}\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)]}\cdot+\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<N^{-1}\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)]}\cdot=:\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}+\mathsf{C}\,.

Using
\pi^{Y}=\pi_{h}=\ell/\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}
when bounding
\mathsf{C}
below,

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle\leq\pi^{Y}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{2c}+t\bigr)\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{[\ell\geq N^{-1}\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)]}\exp(-2\ell N)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}\leq\bigl(\frac{\eta}{3kM_{2}}\bigr)^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<N^{-1}\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)]}\frac{\ell(y)}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)}{N}\,\frac{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\leq\frac{e^{t}}{N}\,\log\frac{3kM_{2}}{\eta}\,.

Here, we used
\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\subset\operatorname{vol}((1+\delta)\,\mathcal{K})=(1+\delta)^{n}\,\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}\leq e^{t}\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}

(due to
B_{1}(0)\subset\mathcal{K}
).

Let
Z:=\frac{16kM_{2}}{\eta}
,
c=\frac{\log\log Z}{2\log Z}
,

t=\sqrt{8}\log\log Z
, and
N=Z^{2}\log^{4}Z
. Under these choices,
we have
\frac{t^{2}}{2c}-t\geq\frac{t^{2}}{4c}
, which is equivalent
to
t\geq 4c
. Hence,
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{2N}]\leq(\frac{\eta}{kM_{2}})^{2}
.
Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]\leq M_{2}\,\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{2N}]\bigr)^{1/2}\leq\frac{\eta}{k}\,,

which implies that the total failure probability across
k
iterations
is at most
\eta
.

(2) Complexity of the backward step.

Let
p=1+\frac{1}{\alpha}
and
q=1+\alpha
with
\alpha=\log N
.
Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}\bigl[\frac{1}{\ell}\wedge N\bigr]=\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell\geq N^{-p}]}\cdot+\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<N^{-p}]}\cdot+\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\cdot=:\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}+\mathsf{C}\,.

Using the
(p,q)
-Hölder below,

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell\geq N^{-p}]}\frac{1}{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}\leq\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell\geq N^{-p}]}\frac{1}{\ell^{p}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}\Bigr)^{1/p}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\pi_{h})}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell\geq N^{-p}]}\frac{1}{\ell^{p-1}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\Bigr)^{1/p}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\pi_{h})}\leq N^{1/\alpha}\bigl(\frac{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\bigr)^{1/p}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}\leq e\,\frac{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}

\displaystyle\leq N\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<N^{-p}]}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}\leq N\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<N^{-p}]}\frac{\ell}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\Bigr)^{1/p}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\pi_{h})}\leq\frac{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}

\displaystyle\leq N\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}\leq N\sqrt{\pi_{h}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}\leq N\sqrt{\pi_{h}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\,.

Putting these together,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}\bigl[\frac{1}{\ell}\wedge N\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq 4\,\frac{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}+N\sqrt{\pi_{h}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq 4e^{t}M_{q}+N\exp\bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{4c}+\frac{t}{2}\bigr)M_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4M_{q}\log^{\sqrt{8}}Z+Z^{2}\log^{4}Z\times\exp\bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{8c}\bigr)\,M_{2}\leq 5M_{q}\log^{4}Z\,,

where we used
M_{2}\leq M_{q}=\lVert\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\pi\rVert_{L^{q}(\pi)}
(i.e.,
the monotonicity of
L^{q}
-norm). Therefore, we only need
M_{q}
-warmness
with

q=1+\log N\leq 2\log(Z^{2}\log^{4}Z)\leq 12\log\frac{16kM_{2}}{\eta}\,.

Combining these mixing and per-step analyses, we prove the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

By (
2.2
),

\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
can ensure
\chi^{2}(\mu_{k}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\varepsilon
after
iterating

k\gtrsim\bigl(h^{-1}C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\vee 1\bigr)\log\frac{M_{2}}{\varepsilon}

times. Under the choice of
h=\frac{c}{n^{2}}=\frac{\log\log Z}{2n^{2}\log Z}

with
Z=\frac{16kM_{2}}{\eta}
, the required number
k
of iterations
must satisfy an inequality of the form

k\gtrsim A\log^{2}(Bk)\,,

which can be fulfilled if
k\gtrsim A\log^{2}(AB)
. Therefore, the
required number of iterations is of order

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\log^{3}\frac{M_{2}}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log^{3}\frac{M_{2}}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)\,.

Since
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{k}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)
by the
DPI (Lemma
1.14
), the complexity of the backward step is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(M_{q})
for any iteration. Multiplying this by the required
number of iterations, the total expected number of queries becomes

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(M_{q}n^{2}\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log^{7}\frac{M_{2}}{\varepsilon\eta}\bigr)\,.

This completes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
∎

2.2
Truncated Gaussian sampling

In this section, we similarly relax a warmness condition of the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}

for truncated Gaussian distributions
[
KZ25
]
(referred to
here as
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
) from
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
to
\mathcal{R}_{c}
for small

c=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
.

In the same setting as the previous section, let
\pi
denote the
uniform distribution over
\mathcal{K}
, and define
\mu^{X}:=\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\propto\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{n})|_{\mathcal{K}}
.
For
\tau:=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{h+\sigma^{2}}<1
,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
for
\mu^{X}

alternates between

•

[Forward]
y_{i+1}\sim\mu^{Y|X=x_{i}}=\mathcal{N}(x_{i},hI_{n})
.

•

[Backward]
x_{i+1}\sim\mu^{X|Y=y_{i+1}}=\mathcal{N}(\tau y_{i+1},\tau hI_{n})|_{\mathcal{K}}

This backward step is implemented via rejection sampling using the
proposal
\mathcal{N}(\tau y_{i+1},\tau hI_{n})
. If the number of trials
in a given iteration exceeds
N
, then declare
Failure
.

Regarding the mixing rate, it follows from
[
KZ25
, Lemma 21]

or (

) that
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
achieves
\varepsilon
-distance
in

after iterating

k\gtrsim h^{-1}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu^{X})\log\frac{\mathcal{R}_{2}}{\varepsilon}\asymp h^{-1}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu^{X})\log\frac{\log M_{2}}{\varepsilon}\,.

Preliminaries.

We now analyze the failure probability and expected query complexity
of the backward step under relaxed warmness. Recall from
[
KZ25
]

that for
y_{\tau}:=\tau y
and
\mu_{h}:=\mu^{Y}=\mu^{X}*\gamma_{h}
,
the density is given by

\mu_{h}(y)=\frac{\tau^{n/2}\,\ell(y)\exp(-\frac{1}{2\tau\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert y_{\tau}\rVert^{2})}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x}\,.

To proceed, we will make use of two helper lemmas.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
KZ25
, Lemma 22]
)
.

For a convex body
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing
B_{1}(0)
, let
R=(1+\frac{h}{\sigma^{2}})\,\mathcal{K}_{\delta}=\tau^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}
.
If
\delta\geq hn
, then

\mu^{Y}(R^{c})\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2h}+\delta n+hn^{2}\bigr)\,.

For
h=\frac{c}{n^{2}}
and
\delta=\frac{c+t}{n}
for parameter

c,t>0
, we can satisfy
\delta\geq hn
. Another is the following:

Lemma 2.6

(
[
KZ25
, Lemma 24]
)
.

Let

\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex body containing a unit ball
B_{1}(0)
.
For
\tau=\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{h+\sigma^{2}}
and

,

\tau^{-n/2}\int_{\mathcal{K}_{s}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\tau\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert z\rVert^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z\leq 2\exp(hn^{2}+sn)\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert z\rVert^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z\,.

(1) Failure probability.

Just as in the analysis of
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
, we adapt the analysis of
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}

from
[
KZ25
]
. For an initial distribution
\nu\ll\mu
,
the failure probability can be bounded using the Cauchy–Schwarz
as follows:

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{2N}]}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=:M_{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{2N}]}\,.

Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1-\ell)^{2N}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}=\int_{R^{c}}\cdot+\int_{R\cap[\ell\geq N^{-1}\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)]}\cdot+\int_{R\cap[\ell<N^{-1}\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)]}\cdot=:\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}+\mathsf{C}\,,

where in
(i)
below, by using change of variables and Lemma

,

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle\leq\mu^{Y}(R^{c})\underset{\text{Lemma \ref{lem:gaussian-effective}}}{\leq}\exp\bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{2c}+\frac{3c}{2}\bigr)\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{[\ell\geq N^{-1}\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)]}\exp(-2\ell N)\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}\leq\bigl(\frac{\eta}{3kM_{2}}\bigr)^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{R\cap[\ell<N^{-1}\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)]}\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}=\int_{R\cap[\ell<N^{-1}\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)]}\frac{\tau^{n/2}\ell(y)\exp(-\frac{1}{2\tau\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert y_{\tau}\rVert^{2})}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert z\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}z}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)}{N}\,\frac{\int_{R}\tau^{n/2}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\tau\sigma^{2}}\lVert y_{\tau}\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\lVert z\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}z}\underset{(i)}{\leq}\frac{\log(3kM_{2}/\eta)}{N}\,2\exp(hn^{2}+\delta n)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2e^{2c+t}}{N}\log\frac{3kM_{2}}{\eta}\,,

For
Z=\frac{16kM_{2}}{\eta}
, we choose
c=\frac{\log\log Z}{10\log Z}
,

t=\log\log Z
, and
N=Z^{2}\log^{3}Z
, under which each final bound
is bounded by

. Therefore, the failure
probability per iteration is
\eta/k
.

(2) Complexity of the backward step.

Similar to the uniform-sampling case, let
p=1+\alpha^{-1}
and
q=1+\alpha

with
\alpha=\log N
. Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{h}}\bigl[\frac{1}{\ell}\wedge N\bigr]=\int_{R\cap[\ell\geq N^{-p}]}\cdot+\int_{R\cap[\ell<N^{-p}]}\cdot+\int_{R^{c}}\cdot=:\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}+\mathsf{C}\,,

where

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\int_{R\cap[\ell\geq N^{-p}]}\frac{1}{\ell^{p}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}\Bigr)^{1/p}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\mu)}=M_{q}\Bigl(\frac{\int_{R\cap[\ell\geq N^{-p}]}\ell^{p-1}(y)\,\tau^{n/2}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\tau\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert y_{\tau}\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x}\Bigr)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq N^{1/\alpha}M_{q}\,\Bigl(\frac{\int_{R}\tau^{n/2}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\tau\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert y_{\tau}\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x}\Bigr)^{1/p}\leq 2eM_{q}\exp(hn^{2}+\delta n)\leq 6M_{q}e^{2c+t}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}

\displaystyle\leq N\int_{R\cap[\ell<N^{-p}]}\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{h}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}\leq N\,\Bigl(\int_{R\cap[\ell<N^{-p}]}\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}\Bigr)^{1/p}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\mu)}

\displaystyle\leq NM_{q}\Bigl(\frac{\int_{R\cap[\ell<N^{-p}]}\tau^{n/2}\,\ell^{p}(y)\exp(-\frac{1}{2\tau\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert y_{\tau}\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x}\Bigr)^{1/p}\leq M_{q}\Bigl(\frac{\int_{R}\tau^{n/2}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\tau\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert y_{\tau}\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x}\Bigr)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq 2M_{q}e^{2c+t}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}

\displaystyle\leq N\int_{R^{c}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{h}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}\leq NM_{2}\sqrt{\mu_{h}(R^{c})}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{h}}\bigl[\frac{1}{\ell}\wedge N\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq M_{q}\,\Bigl(8e^{2c+t}+N\exp\bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{4c}+\frac{3c}{4}\bigr)\Bigr)\leq M_{q}\,\Bigl(8e^{2c+t}+N\exp\bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{8c}\bigr)\Bigr)\,

\displaystyle\leq M_{q}\,(2e\log Z+e\log^{3}Z)\leq 10M_{q}\log^{3}Z\,.

We now prove the main theorem for this section.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

By (
2.1
),

\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
achieves
\mathcal{R}_{2}(\nu_{k}\mathbin{\|}\mu)\leq\varepsilon
after

k\gtrsim\bigl(h^{-1}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu)\vee 1\bigr)\log\frac{\log M_{2}}{\varepsilon}

iterations. Under the choice of
h=\frac{1}{10n^{2}\log Z}
with

Z=\frac{16kM_{2}}{\eta}
, it suffices to run
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
for

k=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu)\log^{3}\frac{M_{2}}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)

iterations. Therefore, the total expected number of queries over
k

iterations is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(M_{q}n^{2}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu)\log^{6}\frac{M_{2}}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)\,,

where
q=1+\log N\leq 6\log\frac{16kM_{2}}{\eta}
.

In general, we can use the bound of
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu)=C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}})\leq\sigma^{2}

[
BGL14
]
. In this case, the
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
requires
k=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}\sigma^{2}\log^{3}\frac{M_{2}}{\eta\varepsilon})

iterations, with total query complexity of

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(M_{q}n^{2}\sigma^{2}\log^{6}\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)\,.

When
\sigma^{2}\gtrsim D\lambda^{1/2}\log^{2}n\log^{2}\nicefrac{{D^{2}}}{{\lambda}}

for
\lambda=\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert
, we can use the improved bound,
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu^{X})\lesssim D\lambda^{1/2}\log n

(which will be proven by Theorem
3.1
and
3.2

in §
A
). In this case,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}

suffices to iterate
k=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}D\lambda^{1/2}\log^{3}\frac{M_{2}}{\eta\varepsilon})

times, with total query complexity of

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(M_{q}n^{2}D\lambda^{1/2}\log^{6}\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

3
Improved logarithmic Sobolev constants

Throughout this section,
\pi
denotes a logconcave probability measure
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with first moment
R:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\lVert\cdot\rVert
, covariance
matrix
\Sigma:=\operatorname{cov}\pi
, and operator norm
\lambda:=\lVert\Sigma\rVert
.
For
t>0
, we use
\pi\gamma_{t}
to denote the
t^{-1}
-strongly
logconcave distribution obtained by weighting
\pi
with a Gaussian

\gamma_{t}
(i.e.,
\mathrm{d}(\pi\gamma_{t})(x)\propto\pi(x)\,\gamma_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
).
We also denote
\Sigma_{t}:=\operatorname{cov}\pi\gamma_{t}
and
\lambda_{t}:=\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert
.

We begin by proving a bound on
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)
for logconcave
\pi

supported with diameter
D>0
, which interpolates the
D
-bound
for isotropic logconcave distributions
[
LV24
]
and the classical

D^{2}
-bound for general logconcave distributions
[
FK99
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Restatement of Theorem
1.5
)
.

For a logconcave distribution
\pi
with support of diameter
D>0

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim\max\{D\lambda^{1/2},D^{2}\wedge\lambda\log^{2}n\}\,.

It also holds that
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim DC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\pi)\lesssim D\lambda^{1/2}\log^{1/2}n
.

Our second result addresses how a Gaussian factor affects the largest
eigenvalue of the covariance matrix of a logconcave distribution.

Theorem 3.2

(Restatement of Theorem
1.6
)
.

For a logconcave distribution
\pi
with
R=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\lVert\cdot\rVert

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
\sigma^{2}\gtrsim R\lambda^{1/2}\log^{2}n\log^{2}\frac{R^{2}}{\lambda}
,
then

\lambda_{\sigma^{2}}\lesssim\lambda\,.

Combining these two together with the classical result
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}})\leq\sigma^{2}
,
we obtain the following bound for strongly logconcave probability
measures with compact support.

Corollary 3.3

(Restatement of Corollary
1.7
)
.

Let
\pi
be a logconcave distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with support of
diameter
D>0
. Then, for any
\sigma^{2}>0
,

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}})\lesssim D\lambda^{1/2}\log^{2}n\log^{2}\frac{D}{\lambda^{1/2}}\,.

3.1
Log-Sobolev constant for logconcave distributions with compact support

It follows from the curvature-dimension condition
[
BGL14
]

that
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\gamma_{h}|_{\mathcal{K}})\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\gamma_{h})=h
(i.e., truncating
a strongly logconcave measure to a convex set does not worsen the
LSI constant), but how about the other natural possible inequality

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\gamma_{h}|_{\mathcal{K}})\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}})
(i.e., Multiplying
a radially symmetric Gaussian does not increase the LSI constant)?

Why do we care about this? To motivate this question, suppose
\pi\propto\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}

is an isotropic logconcave distribution with support of diameter
\Theta(n)
,
so
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim n

[
LV24
, Theorem 49]
. Now consider

h\asymp n^{2}
. Then, the distribution
\gamma_{h}|_{\mathcal{K}}
is a

\Theta(1)
-perturbation to
\pi
, so by the Holley–Stroock perturbation
principle, we obtain
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\gamma_{h}|_{\mathcal{K}})\lesssim C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim n
.
Note that the straightforward bound
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\gamma_{h}|_{\mathcal{K}})\leq h

only yields an
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
-bound. This suggests that as
h
increases
from
n
to
n^{2}
, if the LSI constant
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\gamma_{h}|_{\mathcal{K}})

initially increases, it must start to decrease somewhere to satisfy

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\gamma_{n^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}})\lesssim n
. This naturally leads us
to ask if
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\gamma_{h}|_{\mathcal{K}})\lesssim n
for
all

h>0
.

This question can be phrased more generally as follows.

Question 3.4
.

For any logconcave distribution
\pi

with compact support, and any
h>0
, do we have
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{h})\lesssim C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\,?

We may first ask whether
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{h})
remains close to
the known bound on
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)
. In §
3.3
,
we show that this is indeed the case. Before proceeding, however,
we mention what is currently known about
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)
.

The classical upper bound for the LSI constant of any logconcave distribution

\pi
with compact support of diameter
D>0
is
\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
, while
for isotropic logconcave distributions, a stronger bound
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim D

holds. In the context of (

PI

), the known bound
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\lambda\log n

nearly interpolates the
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
bound for isotropic cases and

\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
for general cases (noting that
\lambda\leq D^{2}
). This
motivates the following mathematical question, which we will address
in this section.

Question
.

What is a general bound on (

LSI

) that interpolates those
two known bounds on (

LSI

) for logconcave distributions with
compact support?

3.1.1
A naïve approach: Interpolation via a Lipschitz map

As sketched earlier in §
1.2
, one justification
for focusing on the Poincaré constant of
isotropic
logconcave
distributions is via the affine map
T:x\mapsto\Sigma^{1/2}x
, which
has Lipschitzness
\lVert\Sigma\rVert^{1/2}
.

This approach does not work well for (

LSI

). Indeed, let

\nu:=(T^{-1})_{\#}\pi
be the pushforward of
\pi
under the inverse
affine map, so that
\nu
is isotropic. For any test function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi}[f^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Ent}_{\nu}[(f\circ\Sigma^{1/2})^{2}]\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\lVert\nabla(f\circ\Sigma^{1/2})\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}\rVert^{2}\lVert\nabla f\circ\Sigma^{1/2}\rVert^{2}]\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]\,,

which implies that

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert\,.

This bound has several downsides. First, the best-known bound for
isotropic logconcave distributions is
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)\lesssim D
, and
this
cannot be
improved in general
[
LV24
, Lemma 55]
.
Moreover, isotropy implies
D\geq\sqrt{n}
, so we cannot hope for
a better general bound on
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)
than
\sqrt{n}
. Second,
since
\lVert\Sigma\rVert=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(v^{\mathsf{T}}(X-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}X))^{2}]\leq D^{2}
,
it is unclear how the RHS of the inequality could even recover the
classical
\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
bound for
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)
. Thus, this affine
reduction fails to interpolate the known two bounds and motivates
the need for a more intrinsic analysis.

3.1.2
A better approach via Gaussian concentration

We prove the following two bounds presented in Theorem
3.1
:

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim\min\bigl\{DC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\pi),\max\{D\lambda^{1/2},D^{2}\wedge\lambda\log^{2}n\}\bigr\}\,.

Inspired by
[
KL24
, §8]
, we present a simple proof using
a known equivalence between Gaussian concentration and (

LSI

)
for logconcave measures.

We first define a concentration function of a measure.

Definition 3.5

(Concentration)
.

For a probability measure

\pi
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, consider the concentration function of
\pi

defined as

\alpha_{\pi}(r)=\sup_{S:\pi(S)\geq 1/2}\pi(S^{c})\,.

The measure
\pi
is said to satisfy
exponential concentration

with constant
C_{\mathsf{exp}}(\pi)
if

\alpha_{\pi}(r)\leq 2\exp\bigl(-\frac{r}{C_{\mathsf{exp}}(\pi)}\bigr)\quad\text{for all }r\geq 0\,,

and is said to satisfy
Gaussian concentration
with constant

C_{\mathsf{Gauss}}(\pi)
if

\alpha_{\pi}(r)\leq 2\exp\bigl(-\frac{r^{2}}{C_{\mathsf{Gauss}}(\pi)}\bigr)\quad\text{for all }r\geq 0\,.

Milman
[
Mil10
]
established that for a logconcave
distribution, exponential concentration is equivalent to (

PI

),
and Gaussian concentration is equivalent to (

LSI

).

Theorem 3.6
.

For any logconcave probability
measure
\pi
, it holds that
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\asymp C_{\mathsf{Gauss}}(\pi)
.

To use this result, we recall two known results on exponential concentration.
The first is a standard result on exponential concentration under
(

PI

). Precisely, this is a combination of concentration
of Lipschitz functions (around its median or mean)
[
BGL14
, §4.4.3]

and its equivalence with exponential concentration.

Theorem 3.7
.

For a probability measure
\pi

with
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)<\infty
, there exists a universal constant
c>0

such that

\alpha_{\pi}(r)\leq 2\exp\bigl(-\frac{r}{cC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\pi)}\bigr)\,.

There is another bound established by
[
Biz24
, Theorem 1.1]

using Eldan’s stochastic localization (see another proof based on
this technique in §
A
).

Theorem 3.8
.

For any logconcave probability measure

\pi
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a universal constant
c>0
such
that

\alpha_{\pi}(r)\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-c\min\bigl(\frac{r}{\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}},\frac{r^{2}}{\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log^{2}n}\bigr)\Bigr)\,.

We now prove the desired LSI bound in a simple way by combining these
two bounds.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Since the diameter
of the support is
D
, it clearly holds that
\alpha_{\pi}(r)=0

for
r>D
. For
r\leq D
, using
r/D\leq 1
and letting
\lambda=\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert
,
by Theorem
3.8
,

\alpha_{\pi}(r)\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-c\min\bigl(\frac{r^{2}}{D\lambda^{1/2}},\frac{r^{2}}{\lambda\log^{2}n}\bigr)\Bigr)=2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{cr^{2}}{D\lambda^{1/2}\vee\lambda\log^{2}n}\Bigr)\,.

By Theorem
3.6
, since
\pi
is logconcave,

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\asymp C_{\mathsf{Gauss}}(\pi)\lesssim D\lambda^{1/2}\vee\lambda\log^{2}n\,.

Combining with
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim D^{2}
, we can deduce that

\displaystyle C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)

\displaystyle\lesssim D^{2}\wedge(D\lambda^{1/2}\vee\lambda\log^{2}n)=\max\{D\lambda^{1/2}\wedge D^{2},D^{2}\wedge\lambda\log^{2}n\}

\displaystyle=\max\{D\lambda^{1/2},D^{2}\wedge\lambda\log^{2}n\}\,.

Regarding the second bound, it follows from Theorem
3.7

that for some universal constant
c>0
,

\alpha_{\pi}(r)\leq 2\exp\bigl(-\frac{r}{cC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\pi)}\bigr).

Using a similar argument above, we have

\alpha_{\pi}(r)\leq 2\exp\bigl(-\frac{r^{2}}{cDC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\pi)}\bigr)\,.

Thus, by the equivalence between (

LSI

) and Gaussian concentration
(Theorem
3.6
),

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\asymp C_{\mathsf{Gauss}}(\pi)\lesssim DC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\pi)\leq D\lambda^{1/2}\log^{1/2}n\,,

where the last inequality follows from
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\lambda\log n
.
∎

Remark 3.9

(Comparison of two bounds)
.

The first bound
successfully interpolates the
\mathcal{O}(D)
-bound of isotropic logconcave
distributions and
\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
-bound of general logconcave ones. However,
it cannot attain
\mathcal{O}(D\lambda^{1/2})
even if the KLS conjecture is
true. On the other hand, the second bound almost interpolates those
two bounds (up to
\log^{1/2}n
), while it attains
\mathcal{O}(D\lambda^{1/2})

when the KLS conjecture is true.

3.2
Covariance of strongly logconcave distributions

We have just shown that
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim D\lambda^{1/2}
and
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{h})\lesssim D\lambda_{h}^{1/2}

(ignoring logarithmic factors). How does this new bound compare to
the previous two bounds of
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{h})\leq h\wedge\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
,
from strong logconcavity and from the support diameter respectively?

A standard upper bound on the operator norm
\lambda_{h}
can be obtained
via

\lambda_{h}\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi\gamma_{h})\leq h\,,

where the second inequality follows from the Brascamp–Lieb inequality.
Plugging this into our bound yields
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{h})\lesssim Dh^{1/2}
,
which is the geometric mean of the previous two bounds (
h
and
D^{2}
),
and making no essential gain.

However, for sufficiently large
h
, one can expect that
\pi\gamma_{h}

is closer to
\pi
, suggesting that
\lVert\Sigma_{h}\rVert
may be comparable
to
\lVert\Sigma\rVert
. In fact, when
h\geq D^{2}
, the distribution

\pi\gamma_{h}
becomes a
\Theta(1)
-perturbation of
\pi
, and
the Holley–Stroock perturbation principle gives
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi\gamma_{h})\lesssim C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim D\lambda^{1/2}
.
If this bound is true for
any

h
, and particularly when

\pi
is isotropic, then this would already yield a substantial gain,
since
D\lambda^{1/2}=D
is smaller than the naïve
h
-bound, which
only gives
\Omega(D^{2})
.

Our main goal at this point is to show
\lambda_{h}\lesssim\lambda
for

h\gtrsim\lambda^{1/2}RC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{2}(n)\log^{2}\nicefrac{{R^{2}}}{{\lambda}}
(Theorem
3.2
),
where
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)
is the largest possible Poincaré constant of
n
-dimensional
isotropic logconcave distributions. Since
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\lesssim\log n

[
Kla23
]
, a sufficient condition is
h\gtrsim\lambda^{1/2}R\log^{2}n\log^{2}\nicefrac{{R^{2}}}{{\lambda}}
.
We remark that the logarithmic factor in the guarantee can be further
improved by using a thin-shell estimate instead of
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)
and
tightening some computations in the proof.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

We start off by scaling
the coordinate system via
T:x\mapsto\lambda^{-1/2}x
, so that
\nu:=T_{\#}\pi

satisfies
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\nu\rVert=1
. We show that if
\eta\gtrsim\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\lVert Y\rVert\log^{2}n\log^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\lVert Y\rVert
,
then
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\nu_{\eta}\rVert\lesssim 1
for
\nu_{\eta}=\nu\gamma_{\eta}
.
Then, by scaling back through
T^{-1}
, we can deduce the main claim
in the theorem (due to
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\lVert Y\rVert=\lambda^{-1/2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\lVert X\rVert

and
h=\eta\lambda
). Throughout the proof, we use
c
and
c_{1}

to denote positive universal constants.

By rotating the coordinate system, it suffices to bound that for
\mu
,
the barycenter of
\nu
,

Q:=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\eta}}[(Y-\mu)_{1}^{2}]\lesssim 1\,.

To this end, we instead bound

Q=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[(Y-\mu)_{1}^{2}\,\exp(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta})\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[\exp(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta})\bigr]}=:\frac{N}{D}\,,

where
N
and
D
refer to the numerator and denominator, respectively.

Recall the classical Lipschitz concentration that for
1
-Lipschitz

f
: for any
t\geq 0
,

\nu(f-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}f\geq t)\leq 3\exp\bigl(-\frac{t}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\nu)}\bigr)\,.

(3.1)

Let
R:=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}(\geq 1)
and
C_{\mathsf{PI}}:=C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)
. Noting

C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu)\lesssim C_{\mathsf{PI}}
, and taking
f(\cdot)=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}

and
t=c_{1}C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}\log R
, we have that

\nu(\lVert Y\rVert\in[R\pm t])\geq 1-6\exp(-c_{2}\log R)\,,

where
[R\pm t]:=[R-t,R+t]
. Hence, the thin-shell
\mathcal{S}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lVert y\rVert\in[R\pm t]\}

takes up more than a half of
\nu
-measure by taking
c_{1}
large
enough. Since
\eta\gtrsim RC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{2}\log^{2}R\gtrsim t\,(R\vee t)=Rt\vee t^{2}
,

D\geq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\bigr]\geq\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}+2Rt+t^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\nu(\mathcal{S})\gtrsim\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,.

As for the upper bound on the numerator
N
, we decompose
N
as
follows:

N=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[(Y-\mu)_{1}^{2}\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\bigr]}_{=:\mathsf{(I)}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[(Y-\mu)_{1}^{2}\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}\bigr]}_{=:\mathsf{(II)}}\,.

For
(\mathsf{I)}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[(Y-\mu)_{1}^{2}\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}+\frac{Rt}{\eta}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[(Y-\mu)_{1}^{2}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\bigr]\lesssim\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(Y-\mu)_{1}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,.

For
\mathsf{(II)}
, since
\lVert\mu\rVert\leq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\lVert\cdot\rVert=R
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[(Y-\mu)_{1}^{2}\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[(Y-\mu)_{1}^{2}\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,(\mathds{1}_{\{\lVert Y\rVert\leq R-t\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{\lVert Y\rVert\geq R+t\}})\bigr]

\displaystyle\lesssim R^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\{\lVert Y\rVert\leq R-t\}}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[(R^{2}+\lVert Y\rVert^{2})\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\{\lVert Y\rVert\geq R+t\}}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq R^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\{\lVert Y\rVert\leq R-t\}}\bigr]+2\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[\lVert Y\rVert^{2}\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\{\lVert Y\rVert\geq R+t\}}\bigr]\,.

By the co-area formula, for the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\{\lVert Y\rVert\leq R-t\}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R-t}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}}\underbrace{\int_{\partial B_{r}}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)}_{=:\nu_{n-1}(\partial B_{r})}\,\mathrm{d}r\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigl[\lVert Y\rVert^{2}\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert Y\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\{\lVert Y\rVert\geq R+t\}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\int_{R+t}^{\infty}r^{2}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}}\,\nu_{n-1}(\partial B_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\,.

Using integration by parts for the first expectation,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R-t}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}}\,\nu_{n-1}(\partial B_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=[e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}}\,\nu(B_{r})]_{0}^{R-t}+\int_{0}^{R-t}\frac{r}{\eta}\,e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}}\,\nu(B_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{eq:PI-Lips-concent}}{\leq}3\exp\bigl(-\frac{(R-t)^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{t}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)+\eta^{-1}\int_{0}^{R-t}re^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}}\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{R-r}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\lesssim\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\exp(-c_{1}\log R)+\frac{R}{\eta}\int_{t}^{R}\exp\bigl(-\frac{(R-r)^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{r}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,R^{-c_{1}}+\frac{R}{\eta}\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\int_{t}^{R}\exp\bigl(\frac{Rr}{\eta}-\frac{r}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r\,.

Using
\eta\gtrsim Rt
and taking
c_{1}
large enough,

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{R}\exp\bigl(\frac{Rr}{\eta}-\frac{r}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t}^{R}\exp\Bigl(r\,\bigl(\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}-\frac{1}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r\leq\int_{t}^{R}\exp\bigl(-\frac{r}{2C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}\exp\bigl(-\frac{t}{2C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\leq 2C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}R^{-c_{1}/2}\,.

Substituting these back to above, for large enough
c_{1}>0
, we
obtain that

\int_{0}^{R-t}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}}\,\nu_{n-1}(B_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\bigl(R^{-c_{1}}+\frac{2RC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}R^{-c_{1}/2}}{\eta}\bigr)\lesssim R^{-c_{1}/2}\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,.

We can bound the second expectation in a similar way:

\displaystyle\int_{R+t}^{\infty}r^{2}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}}\,\nu_{n-1}(\partial B_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\bigl[r^{2}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}}\,\nu(B_{r})\bigr]_{R+t}^{\infty}+\int_{R+t}^{\infty}\bigl(\frac{r^{3}}{\eta}-2r\bigr)\,e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}}\,\nu(B_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\lesssim\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{2}\exp\bigl(-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}-\frac{r-R}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)+\eta^{-1}\int_{R+t}^{\infty}r^{3}\exp\bigl(-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}-\frac{r-R}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\eta^{-1}\int_{t}^{\infty}(r+R)^{3}\exp\bigl(-\frac{(r+R)^{2}}{2\eta}-\frac{r}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq 4\eta^{-1}\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\int_{t}^{\infty}(r^{3}+R^{3})\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{r}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r\,.

We can bound each integral as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}R^{3}\exp\bigl(-\frac{r}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=R^{3}C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}\exp\bigl(-\frac{t}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\leq R^{3-c_{1}}C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}\,,

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}r^{3}\exp\bigl(-\frac{r}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{\lesssim}t^{3}C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}\exp\bigl(-\frac{t}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}\bigr)\leq t^{3}R^{-c_{1}}C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}\,,

where in
(i)
we used properties of the incomplete Gamma function
to compute
\int_{a}^{\infty}x^{3}e^{-x/b}\,\mathrm{d}x=6b^{4}e^{-a/b}\,(1+\frac{a}{b}+\frac{a^{2}}{2b^{2}}+\frac{a^{3}}{6b^{3}})
.
Thus,

\int_{R+t}^{\infty}r^{2}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\eta}}\,\nu_{n-1}(\partial B_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\lesssim\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,\frac{(R^{3-c_{1}}+t^{3}R^{-c_{1}})\,C_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}}{\eta}\lesssim\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,.

Therefore, for large enough
c_{1}>0
,

\mathsf{II}\lesssim R^{2}\times R^{-c_{1}/2}\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)+\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\lesssim\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,,

and thus

N=\mathsf{I}+\mathsf{II}\lesssim\exp\bigl(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta}\bigr)\,.

Combining all previous bounds,

Q=\frac{N}{D}\lesssim 1\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

4.1
Rényi divergence of annealing distributions

Since we have mixing guarantees under
\mathcal{R}_{c}
-warmness for
c=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
,
we can design and leverage a faster annealing scheme with provable
guarantees. In this section, we extend existing results on the closeness
of consecutive annealing distributions to the setting of
q
-Rényi
divergence.

The first type: fixed rate annealing.

Previous work used that for logconcave
e^{-V}
and
\alpha=n^{-1/2}
,
the distributions
\exp(-V)
and
\exp(-(1+\alpha)V)
are
\mathcal{O}(1)
-close
in
\chi^{2}
-divergence (i.e.,
\mathcal{R}_{2}
)
[
LV06c
,
KV06
]
.
We generalize this result to
q
-Rényi divergence (or equivalently,
the relative
L^{q}
-norm).

Lemma 4.4

(Rényi version of universal annealing)
.

Let
\mathrm{d}\nu\propto e^{-V}\,\mathrm{d}x
and
\mathrm{d}\mu\propto e^{-(1+\alpha)\,V}\,\mathrm{d}x

be logconcave probability measures over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For
q>1
and
\delta>0

such that
1-q\delta>0
,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\leq\begin{cases}\frac{qn\alpha^{2}}{2}&\text{if }\alpha\geq 0\,,\\
\frac{qn\alpha^{2}}{1-q\delta}&\text{if }\alpha\in[-\delta/2,0]\,.\end{cases}

In particular,
\alpha=(qn)^{-1/2}
yields an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-bound on
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)
,
and
\alpha=-(16q\,(q\vee n))^{-1/2}
also yields an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-bound.

Proof.

Let us define

F(s):=\int e^{-sV(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

We recall from
[
KV06
, Lemma 3.2]
that
s\mapsto s^{n}F(s)

is logconcave in
s
.

For
q>1
, we compute the
L^{q}
-warmness of
\mu
with respect
to
\nu
:

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\nu)}^{q}

\displaystyle=\int\bigl(\frac{\mu}{\nu}\bigr)^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\int\exp\bigl(-q\,(1+\alpha)\,V(x)+(q-1)\,V(x)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x\times\frac{F^{q-1}(1)}{F^{q}(1+\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\frac{F(1+q\alpha)\,F^{q-1}(1)}{F^{q}(1+\alpha)}=\Bigl(\frac{(1+\alpha)^{n}}{(1+q\alpha)^{n/q}}\,\frac{(1+q\alpha)^{n/q}F^{1/q}(1+q\alpha)\,F^{1-q^{-1}}(1)}{(1+\alpha)^{n}F(1+\alpha)}\Bigr)^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(\frac{(1+\alpha)^{q}}{1+q\alpha}\bigr)^{n}\,,

where the last inequality follows from logconcavity of
s^{n}F(s)

in
s
.

We now claim that for
\alpha\geq 0
,

\frac{(1+\alpha)^{q}}{1+q\alpha}\leq\exp\bigl(\frac{q\,(q-1)}{2}\,\alpha^{2}\bigr)\,.

For
\alpha\geq 0
, let us define

g(\alpha):=\frac{q\,(q-1)}{2}\,\alpha^{2}-q\log(1+\alpha)+\log(1+q\alpha)\,.

We can compute that
g(0)=0
and

g^{\prime}(\alpha)=(q-1)\,q\alpha-\frac{q}{1+\alpha}+\frac{q}{1+q\alpha}=\frac{\alpha^{2}q\,(q-1)(\alpha q+q+1)}{(1+\alpha)(\alpha q+1)}\geq 0\,,

from which the claim follows.

We also show that for
\alpha\in[-\delta/2,0]
with
1-q\delta>0
,

\frac{(1+\alpha)^{q}}{1+q\alpha}\leq\exp\bigl(\frac{q\,(q-1)}{2\,(1-q\delta)}\,\alpha^{2}\bigr)\,.

In a similarly way, we define and compute that

\displaystyle g(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\frac{q\,(q-1)}{2\,(1-q\delta)}\,\alpha^{2}-q\log(1+\alpha)+\log(1+q\alpha)\,,

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{q-1}{1-q\delta}\,q\alpha-\frac{q}{1+\alpha}+\frac{q}{1+q\alpha}=\alpha q\,(q-1)\,\bigl(\frac{1}{1-q\delta}-\frac{1}{(1+\alpha)\,(1+q\alpha)}\bigr)\leq 0\,,

from which the claim follows.

Using each bound for
\alpha\geq 0
and
\alpha\in[-\delta/2,0]
,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)=\frac{1}{q-1}\,\log\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\nu)}^{q}\leq\begin{cases}\frac{qn\alpha^{2}}{2}&\text{if }\alpha\geq 0\,,\\
\frac{qn\alpha^{2}}{1-q\delta}&\text{if }\alpha\in[-\delta/2,0]\,.\end{cases}

This completes the proof.
∎

The second type: accelerated annealing.

In this annealing, the change in the annealing parameter depends on
the variance of the current distribution. A bound of this type was
previously established for
\chi^{2}
-divergence in
[
CV18
, Lemma 7.8]
.

We show that this bound also extends to
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-divergence, enabling
control over the closeness of annealing steps under Rényi-divergence.
In particular, this allows us to multiply the variance by a factor
of
1+\sigma/R
in each step.

Lemma 4.5

(Rényi version of accelerated annealing)
.

Let
\mu
be a logconcave probability density in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with support
of diameter
R>0
. Then, for
q>1
and
\sigma^{2}>0
,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\mathbin{\|}\mu\gamma_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)})\leq\frac{qR^{2}\alpha^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,.

In particular,
\alpha\lesssim\sigma/(q^{1/2}R)
yields an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-bound
on the
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-divergence.

Proof.

We define

\mu_{\sigma^{2}}(x):=\mu\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\,,\qquad F(s):=\int\mu(x)\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+\alpha+s}{2\sigma^{2}\,(1+\alpha)}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2}\bigr)\,,\qquad G(s):=\log F(s)\,.

Then,

\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\sigma^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\mu_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)})}^{q}=\frac{F^{q-1}(-\alpha)\,F\bigl((q-1)\,\alpha\bigr)}{F^{q}(0)}\,,

and

\displaystyle\log\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\sigma^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\mu_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)})}^{q}

\displaystyle=(q-1)\,G(-\alpha)+G\bigl((q-1)\,\alpha\bigr)-q\,G(0)

\displaystyle=(q-1)\,\bigl(G(-\alpha)-G(0)\bigr)+G\bigl((q-1)\,\alpha\bigr)-G(0)

\displaystyle=-(q-1)\int_{0}^{\alpha}G^{\prime}(-t)\,\mathrm{d}t+(q-1)\int_{0}^{\alpha}G^{\prime}\bigl((q-1)\,t\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=(q-1)\int_{0}^{\alpha}\Bigl(G^{\prime}\bigl((q-1)\,t\bigr)-G^{\prime}(-t)\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=(q-1)\int_{0}^{\alpha}\int_{-t}^{(q-1)\,t}G^{\prime\prime}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}t\,.

For the strongly logconcave distribution
\nu_{u}(x)\propto\mu(x)\exp(-\frac{1+\alpha+u}{2\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})
,
we have

\displaystyle G^{\prime\prime}(u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}\,(1+\alpha)}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}\Bigl(\frac{\int\lVert x\rVert^{2}\mu(x)\exp(-\frac{1+\alpha+u}{2\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})}{\int\mu(x)\exp(-\frac{1+\alpha+u}{2\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\sigma^{4}\,(1+\alpha)^{2}}\,\Bigl[\frac{\int\lVert x\rVert^{4}\mu(x)\exp(-\frac{1+\alpha+u}{2\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})}{\int\mu(x)\exp(-\frac{1+\alpha+u}{2\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})}-\Bigl(\frac{\int\lVert x\rVert^{2}\mu(x)\exp(-\frac{1+\alpha+u}{2\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})}{\int\mu(x)\exp(-\frac{1+\alpha+u}{2\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2})}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\sigma^{4}\,(1+\alpha)^{2}}\operatorname{var}_{\nu_{u}}(\lVert X\rVert^{2})\underset{\eqref{eq:pi}}{\leq}\frac{1}{4\sigma^{4}\,(1+\alpha)^{2}}\,C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu_{u})\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{u}}[\lVert X\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R^{2}}{\sigma^{2}\,(1+\alpha)(1+\alpha+u)}\,,

where the last line follows from
\sup_{\nu_{u}}\lVert X\rVert\leq R
and

C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu_{u})\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}{1+\alpha+u}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\mathbin{\|}\mu\gamma_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)})=\frac{1}{q-1}\,\log\,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{q}}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R^{2}}{\sigma^{2}\,(1+\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\int_{-t}^{(q-1)\,t}\frac{1}{1+\alpha+u}\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}}{\sigma^{2}\,(1+\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\log\frac{1+\alpha+(q-1)\,t}{1+\alpha-t}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}}{\sigma^{2}\,(1+\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\log\bigl(1+\frac{qt}{1+\alpha-t}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{qR^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{t}{1+\alpha-t}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{qR^{2}\alpha^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

Similar to previous work, we follow an annealing approach based on

\mathsf{Gaussian\ cooling}

Algorithm

We can truncate
\mathcal{K}

2

Proposition 4.6

(
[
KZ25

)
.

Given

target
distribution. This was justified in §
1.2

by the argument based on the triangle inequality for
\mathsf{TV}
-distance.

Let

•

Initialization (

–

Rejection sampling to sample from

.

•

Termination

.

4.2.2
Analysis

To obtain a provable guarantee on the final query complexity, we should
specify the Rényi parameter
q
. While this is somewhat subtle
since parameters are dependent to each other, we walk through how
to set all necessary parameters without incurring any logical gaps.

Choice of parameters.

For
q\geq 2
, our algorithm guarantees
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-warmness between
any pair of consecutive distributions (i.e.,
M_{2}\lesssim 1
). The
number
m
of inner phases is bounded as

m\leq\frac{q^{1/2}D}{\sigma_{0}}\log nD\leq q^{1/2}n^{1/2}D\log nD=:m_{\max}(q)\,.

Also, by Theorem
2.2
, the number of iterations
required for each inner phase is at most

k\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}D^{2}\log^{3}\frac{m_{\max}^{2}(q)}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}D^{2}\log^{3}\frac{q}{\eta\varepsilon})=:k_{\max}(q)\,.

To ensure the query complexity bound of
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
, it suffices to
choose
q
such that

q\gtrsim\log\frac{qnD}{\eta\varepsilon}\Bigl(\gtrsim\log\frac{k_{\max}(q)\,m_{\max}(q)}{\eta}\gtrsim 6\log\frac{16kmM_{2}}{\eta}\Bigr)\,,

to obtain a query complexity bound for
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
via the monotonicity

\sigma^{2}\in[n^{-1},1]
, we need at most
(qn)^{1/2}

inner phases to double the initial
\sigma^{2}
. Any pair of consecutive
distributions is
\mathcal{O}(1)
-close in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
by Lemma
4.4
.
By Theorem
5.3
, each inner phase then
requires

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}\log^{3}\frac{m_{\max}^{2}}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}\log^{3}\frac{D}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)

evaluation queries in expectation. Therefore, Phase I uses

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}\log^{3}\frac{D}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)\times(qn)^{1/2}\times\log n=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{5/2}\log^{4}\frac{D}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)

evaluation queries in expectation. The final failure probability is
immediate from the union bound, while the final
\mathsf{TV}
-guarantee follows
from the triangle inequality as in §
1.2
.
∎

Lemma 5.14

(Phase II,
\rho
-annealing)
.

With probability at least
1-\eta
,
Phase II started at the output of Phase I returns a sample whose law

\nu
satisfies

\lVert\nu-\bar{\pi}\gamma\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\varepsilon\,,

using
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}(n^{1/2}\vee D)\log^{4}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\eta\varepsilon}})

evaluation queries in total.

Proof.

At initialization, the two distributions are
\mathcal{O}(1)
-close in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}

since
\bar{\mathcal{K}}
has diameter of
\mathcal{O}(1)
in the
t
-direction.
Hence, the query complexity of
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{ann}}
is simply
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}\log^{3}\nicefrac{{D}}{{\eta\varepsilon}})
.

The outer annealing, which updates
\sigma_{i}^{2}
and
\rho_{i}

simultaneously, happens at most
(qn)^{1/2}\log n
times. For each
outer annealing, the second form of Lemma
4.4

with
\alpha=-\gamma\,(1+\gamma)^{-1}
for
\gamma=(16q\,(q\vee n))^{-1/2}

implies that

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{i}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{i+1})=\mathcal{O}(1)\,.

Then, the outer annealing via
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{ann}}
requires
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}\log^{3}\nicefrac{{D}}{{\eta\varepsilon}})

queries in expectation.

This outer annealing is immediately followed by the inner annealing,
where it increases
\sigma^{2}
from
(1+(16q\,(q\vee n))^{-1/2})^{-1}

to
1
. Under the update of
\sigma^{2}\leftarrow\sigma^{2}(1+\frac{\sigma}{q^{1/2}D})
,
there would be at most

\frac{q^{1/2}D}{\sigma}\times\frac{1}{\bigl(16q\,(q\vee n)\bigr)^{1/2}}\leq\frac{D}{(q\vee n)^{1/2}}

inner-annealing phases, and each annealing via
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{ann}}
also takes

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}\log^{3}\nicefrac{{D}}{{\eta\varepsilon}})
queries.

Putting these together, the total query complexity throughout the

\rho
-annealing is

\bigl(16q\,(q\vee n)\bigr)^{1/2}\log n\times\Bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}\log^{3}\frac{D}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)+\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}\log^{3}\frac{D}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)\times\frac{D}{(q\vee n)^{1/2}}\Bigr)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}(n^{1/2}\vee D)\log^{4}\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.15

(Phase III,
\sigma^{2}
-annealing)
.

For
\lambda=\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi^{X}\rVert
,
with probability at least
1-\eta
, Phase III started at the output
of Phase II returns a sample whose law
\nu
satisfies

\lVert\nu-\pi\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\varepsilon\,,

using
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}D^{3/2}(\lambda^{1/4}\vee 1)\log^{4}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\eta\varepsilon}}\log\nicefrac{{D^{2}}}{{\lambda}})

evaluation queries in expectation.

Proof.

For any given
\sigma^{2}\in[1,D^{2}]
, its doubling requires at
most
q^{1/2}D/\sigma
phases. Any consecutive distributions are

\mathcal{O}(1)
-close in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
(Lemma
4.5
),
so
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{ann}}
requires
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}\sigma^{2}\log^{3}\nicefrac{{D}}{{\eta\varepsilon}})

queries. Thus, one doubling takes

\frac{q^{1/2}D}{\sigma}\times\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}\sigma^{2}\log^{3}\frac{D}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}D^{3/2}(\lambda^{1/4}\vee 1)\log^{4}\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon}\log\frac{D^{2}}{\lambda}\bigr)

until
\sigma^{2}\lesssim D\,(\lambda^{1/2}\vee 1)\log^{2}n\log^{2}\nicefrac{{D^{2}}}{{\lambda}}
.

When
\sigma^{2}
exceeds this threshold,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{ann}}
requires
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}D\,(\lambda^{1/2}\vee 1)\log^{3}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\eta\varepsilon}})

queries in expectation. Hence, one doubling in this regime takes

\frac{q^{1/2}D}{\sigma}\times\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}D\,(\lambda^{1/2}\vee 1)\log^{3}\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}D^{3/2}(\lambda^{1/4}\vee 1)\log^{4}\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)\,.

At termination, we note that

\frac{\bar{\pi}\gamma_{D^{2}}}{\pi}=\frac{\bar{\pi}\gamma_{D^{2}}}{\bar{\pi}}\,\frac{\bar{\pi}}{\pi}\leq\bigl(\pi(\bar{\mathcal{K}})\bigr)^{-1}\leq 2\,.

By Theorem
5.2
,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{exp}}
requires
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}(\lambda\vee 1)\log^{3}\frac{D}{\eta\varepsilon})

queries in expectation. Adding these up, the total query complexity
is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(n^{2}D^{3/2}(\lambda^{1/4}\vee 1)\log^{4}\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon}\log\frac{D^{2}}{\lambda}\bigr)

as claimed.
∎

Combining the previous three lemmas together, we can establish the
query complexity of sampling from a logconcave distribution as claimed
in Theorem
5.1
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

We simply add up
the query complexities in the previous three lemmas, with
D\asymp R
.
That is,

\displaystyle n^{5/2}\log^{4}\frac{R}{\eta\varepsilon}+n^{2}(n^{1/2}\vee R)\log^{4}\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon}+n^{2}R^{3/2}(\lambda^{1/4}\vee 1)\log^{4}\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon}\log\frac{D^{2}}{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\Bigl(n^{2}\max\bigl\{n^{1/2},R^{3/2}(\lambda^{1/4}\vee 1)\bigr\}\log^{5}\frac{R\lambda^{-1/2}}{\eta\varepsilon}\Bigr)\,.

When
\pi^{X}
is further near-isotropic, the claim follows from

R\lesssim n^{1/2}
and
\lambda\asymp 1
.
∎
