A Synthesis of the Best Evidence for Rhubarb Administration in the Treatment of Acute Pancreatitis in China
Authors: Li-jun Han, Xiao-wen Pan, Jia Zhu, Jia-yu Qin, Hui-li He, Hong Chen, Di Wang, Hui-ren Zhuang, Li-li Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAcute pancreatitis has complex causes, and traditional Chinese medicine plays a significant role in its treatment. However, there is a lack of standardized protocols and a comprehensive best evidence summary for rhubarb administration in acute pancreatitis, leading to inconsistent clinical practice. This study aimed to retrieve and summarize the best evidence on the administration of rhubarb in the treatment of acute pancreatitis and to provide a theoretical reference for the development of clinical practice.MethodsA systematic search was performed from database inception to 31 October, 2025 across multiple electronic databases and guideline repositories, including the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, the US Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, Cochrane Library, PubMed, EMbase, China biomedical, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP. Eligible evidence comprised clinical practice guidelines, expert consensus statements, and systematic reviews focusing on rhubarb administration in adult patients with acute pancreatitis. The methodological quality of included studies was independently evaluated using the AGREE II, JBI, and AMSTAR 2.0 tools.ResultsA total of 15 documents were included, including 3 clinical guidelines, 3 expert consensus, and 9 systematic evaluations. Twenty-seven pieces of evidence related to rhubarb administration in patients with acute pancreatitis were extracted and summarized into four themes: mechanism of action, feasibility, evaluation, and clinical implementation.ConclusionHealthcare providers may apply the best evidence for rhubarb administration in acute pancreatitis to improve intestinal function recovery. However, recommendations should be interpreted cautiously, as all evidence comes from Chinese studies with limited generalizability and some low-level findings. This study is in accordance with the evidence summary reporting specifications of the Fudan University Center for the Evidence-based Nursing, and the registration number is “ES20246947”.Keywords: acute pancreatitis, rhubarb, Chinese medicine nursing, evidence summary, best practice, Rheum palmatum

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is an acute inflammatory disorder of the pancreas. It results from the excessive activity of pancreatic digestive enzymes, leading to a series of pathological changes in the pancreas and surrounding tissues, such as edema and necrosis. The global annual incidence of AP is 33.74 patients per 100,000 person-years, causing 1–60 deaths per 100,000 person-years1–3 and approximately 20% of AP patients develop moderately severe acute pancreatitis (MSAP) or severe acute pancreatitis (SAP).4–6 SAP is associated with acute gastrointestinal injury (AGI), including impaired gastrointestinal mucosa, gastrointestinal dysfunction, paralytic intestinal obstruction, and intestinal bleeding. AGI is not only the initial manifestation of SAP but also a contributing factor to extra-organ damage affecting the heart, lungs, and brain. This cascade can trigger the incidence of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), with a 20–30% mortality rate, which poses a significant threat to health.7,8 The grade of acute gastrointestinal injury serves as an independent predictor of death in patients with SAP.9,10 Protecting intestinal function is essential for preventing secondary complications and bowel-derived sepsis.11

The integration of Traditional Chinese Medicine ((TCM) with Western medicine has emerged as a primary treatment approach for acute pancreatitis, with TCM playing a significant role. TCM should be actively employed as early as possible, using a combination of traditional Chinese medicinal practices tailored to the syndrome differentiation of the condition, supplemented by acupuncture, external treatments, and acupoint injections.8,10 Rhubarb (Rheum palmatum L., Chinese rhubarb), a commonly used Chinese herbal remedy. (In this study, we focus on the application of raw rhubarb). It is known for its diverse effects, including promoting diarrhea, alleviating heat, clearing heat, and detoxifying the body. Research has demonstrated that single-taste rhubarb exerts protective effects on pancreatic cells during the early phases of SAP. Commonly utilized TCM formulations, such as the Dachengqi Decoction, Chaihu Decoction, and Qingpan Soup, include rhubarb as an ingredient. These formulations aim to enhance gastrointestinal motility, mitigate inflammatory responses, improve microcirculatory disorders, and prevent organ dysfunction.12

In recent years, many scholars have found that rhubarb treatment plays a significant role in the recovery of gastrointestinal function in patients with acute pancreatitis.13,14 Several studies15–18 have pointed out that rhubarb can effectively help patients relieve clinical symptoms, reduce their pain, improve serological indicators and inflammatory factor levels, and there are no obvious adverse reactions. However, there is a lack of unified standards for rhubarb administration in AP, including inconsistent administration routes, timing, dosage, and monitoring indicators, which lead to unstable and inconsistent therapeutic effects. Furthermore, current evidence predominantly focuses on the efficacy validation of individual intervention components, lacking a comprehensive and updated best-evidence summary for the holistic system of rhubarb treatment for acute pancreatitis. This is the specific research gap that the present evidence summary aims to address.

This deficiency hinders the standardized selection and individualized implementation of this complex intervention protocol in clinical practice, thereby increasing the uncertainty in clinical decision-making.

Notably, rhubarb administration for AP is defined as a complex intervention in line with the Medical Research Council/National Institute for Health Research (MRC/NIHR) framework. This intervention involves multiple components (oral, nasogastric/jejunal tube, enema), multiple implementation links (drug preparation, administration, efficacy evaluation, safety monitoring), and requires individualized adjustment based on disease severity and gastrointestinal function status. Therefore, it is a multi-dimensional, and adaptive intervention that fully meets the definition of complex interventions in the updated MRC/NIHR framework. In 2024, Kathryn Skivington and others reported that a new framework was jointly commissioned by the Medical Research Council and the National Institute for Health Research.19 This new framework comprehensively integrates the latest theoretical and methodological advancements, along with the requirement to maximize research efficiency, utilization, and impact. Complex intervention research can be categorized into three phases: the development or identification of interventions, feasibility assessment and evaluation design, and intervention assessment and impactful implementation. This new framework provides a systematic and adaptive roadmap for researchers engaged in complex intervention studies. And it ensures that interventions are not only effective but also scalable and sustainable in real-world settings. It should also be emphasized that nearly all available evidence on rhubarb for AP is derived from Chinese. This regional and ethnic characteristic limits the direct generalizability of the evidence to other populations and healthcare systems, and such applicability must be carefully evaluated before implementation. Therefore, the aim of this study was to provide an evidence-based practice summary through a systematic search, evaluation, and summary of relevant evidence for rhubarb administration in patients with acute pancreatitis at home and abroad.

Methods

Problem Establishment

In order to summarize evidence-based practice for rhubarb administration, according to the PIPOST model,20 the clinical problems of rhubarb administration in the treatment of AP should be raised first as the evidence-based problem.

The Population (P) is the target population, and in this study, it refers to patients with acute pancreatitis. Intervention (I) refers to raw rhubarb administration. Profession (P) is the evidence practitioner, which refers to the medical staff. Outcome (O), the outcome measure, refers to the time for abdominal pain relief and abdominal distension disappearance, the time for intestinal function recovery, the time of first defecation, the average days of hospital stay, the acute physiology and chronic health status II (APACHE II) score after treatment, the recovery time of blood/urinary amylase, the inflammatory index C-reactive protein (CRP), and procalcitonin (PTC). Setting (S) refers to the site of evidence application, mainly a medical institution. Type of evidence (T) refers to the evidence resource type, such as guidelines, systematic reviews, expert consensus, randomized controlled trials (RCT), etc.

The included articles were limited to those published or electronically published online, from the inception of the databases until October 31, 2025. This study was approved by Fudan University’s Evidence-based Nursing Center (project registration number ES20246947).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria for Evidence Retrieval

The inclusion criteria: ① Study subjects were patients with acute pancreatitis aged 18 years or older; ② Interventions for rhubarb administration in acute pancreatitis patients, such as evaluation, route, usage, and observation; ③ Literature type: best clinical practice, expert consensus, clinical decision-making, clinical practice guidelines, systematic reviews, and evidence summary; ④ The full text of the literature can be obtained; ⑤ The published language is Chinese or English.

Exclusion criteria: ① The literature is translated, interpreted, or non-up-to-date; ② The full text and incomplete information cannot be obtained; ③ Repeated publication.

Literature Screening

Articles were imported into EndNote, and duplicate articles were removed. Two researchers who had attended evidence-based medicine courses independently screened the literature. In the initial screening process, the titles, abstracts, and keywords in the literature were reviewed, and an overall examination of the full text was carried out to assess the quality of the rescreened articles. If there was any controversy regarding the inclusion of specific studies during the evaluation process, the final decision on inclusion was made by a third expert proficient in evidence-based medicine.

Quality Evaluation

In this study, we employed appropriate tools to evaluate various types of studies. The relevant guidelines were evaluated using the AGREE II.22 The scoring criteria encompass 23 entries, including six domains: scope and purpose, participants, rigor, clarity, applicability, and independence. In the AGREE II scoring system, the ratings for each item range from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The standardized score for each dimension was calculated using the following formula: [(actually assessed score-assessed lowest score)/(assessed highest score-assessed lowest score)] × 100% 23. The guidelines are classified into three levels: Level A is recommended if six or more items score above 60%. If more than three items score above 30% while other items score below 60%, Level B is recommended after amendment. Level C is assigned if more than three areas score below 30%.23

Expert consensus was evaluated using the Australian JBI Evidence-Based Health Consensus Evaluation Criteria (2016 version),24 which includes six evaluation domains: the source of opinion, the author’s influence in the field, the perspectives of relevant stakeholders, the logical coherence of the view, references to other literature, and the consistency of the view with previous articles. The results for each evaluated item were categorized as “Yes”, “No”, “Not Clear”, or “Not Applicable”. A multidimensional system evaluation tool (Assessment of Multiple Systematic Reviews, AMSTAR) was used for systematic quality evaluation.23 The assessment tool encompasses 16 entries, of which 7 are key items. If none or only one unqualified non-critical entry was assessed, it was considered to be of high quality; if there was one or more unqualified non-critical entries, it was considered to be of medium quality; if there was only one unqualified key entry, it was considered to be of low quality; and if there were more than one unqualified key entry, it was regarded as extremely low quality.

Quality evaluation of the included studies was independently conducted by two researchers with backgrounds in evidence-based medicine. The study team made the final decisions based on this evaluation. In instances where evidence from different sources presented conflicting conclusions, the principles adhered to in this study prioritized evidence-based literature, followed by high-quality evidence and the most recently published authoritative literature.25

The Extraction and Summary of Evidence

The included studies were individually reviewed by two researchers. Relevant evidence was extracted, integrated, and evaluated, and then verified by a third researcher. The extracted evidence addresses evidence-based questions and includes details such as topic, content, source, study type, and publication date. All of these details are summarized according to thematic categories. In this process, the following principles of evidence integration should be followed: If the integrated content is consistent, the evidence with the same content should be summarized in a clear, concise, and understandable manner. If the integrated content is complementary, the complementary evidence should be integrated into complete evidence according to the language logical relationship. If the integrated content conflicts, evidence-based literature, high-quality evidence, and the latest published evidence are preferred. When evidence is graded by the JBI pre-graded evidence and evidence recommendation level system (2014 version),26 the evidence level is divided into 1 to 5 according to different study types (level 1 is the highest level and 5 is the lowest level).

In accordance with the FAME framework, which evaluates evidence based on feasibility (F), appropriateness (A), meaningfulness (M), and effectiveness (E). A panel of seven experts was convened. The panel included one chief physician in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), three head nurses, and three clinical nurses. The expert panel determined the strength of recommendations by comprehensively evaluating the integration level, evidence grade, and source of the evidence. Strong recommendations were classified as Grade A, general recommendations as Grade B, and weak recommendations as Grade C.

Summary and Description of Evidences

This study summarizes the evidence regarding rhubarb administration in patients with acute pancreatitis. The new framework for the development and evaluation of complex interventions, jointly commissioned by the Medical Research Council and the National Institute for Health Research, was summarized into four themes: pharmacological mechanisms, feasibility (safety), assessment (effectiveness), and clinical implementation, ultimately resulting in 27 recommendations (Table 5). The key synthesized evidence indicates that Rheum palmatum contains components such as emodin and rhein, which can inhibit the release of inflammatory factors, reduce pancreatic damage, protect the intestinal barrier, and decrease bacterial translocation. Clinical administration (via nasogastric tube or enema) is safe and feasible, with simple preparation and low cost. Early use of Rhubarb can reduce intra-abdominal pressure, alleviate abdominal pain and distension, promote the recovery of gastrointestinal function, and improve organ function. It is recommended to administer rhubarb within 24–48 hours of onset, with rhubarb being administered last. Maintain standardized dosages, administer in stages, and monitor electrolyte levels. Discontinue the treatment upon symptom relief. It is contraindicated in cases of gastrointestinal bleeding/perforation. Combination with external application of mirabilite further improves prognosis.

Discussion

This study indicated that rhubarb had demonstrated favorable therapeutic effects in improving gastrointestinal function, reducing inflammatory responses, and preventing organ dysfunction in patients with acute pancreatitis. However, all existing included studies derived from China, and international guidelines rarely mentioned the clinical use of traditional Chinese medicine and rhubarb for acute pancreatitis. The methodological quality of most systematic reviews was moderate, and the original studies lacked large-sample, multicenter randomized controlled trials. Therefore, the recommendations proposed herein were applicable only to specific research contexts and serve merely as provisional reference guidelines.

Standardized Use of Rhubarb Ensures Safety in the Treatment of Acute Pancreatitis

Rhubarb had demonstrated significant feasibility and safety in the treatment of acute pancreatitis. Various administration routes (gastric fistula tubes, nasogastric tubes, and high-residue enemas) could meet the needs of different patients. Studies31,32 indicated that under the physician’s guidance, individualized treatment plans tailored to the patient’s condition, along with strict control of dosage and processing standards, could significantly reduce the incidence of adverse reactions and ensure medication safety.

In clinical practice, the choice of administration route should be based on the patient’s gastrointestinal function and disease severity. For mild AP patients with relatively intact gastrointestinal function, gastrostomy was preferred because of its simple operation.33 For severe cases or those with severe gastrointestinal paralysis, nasogastric tube administration was recommended to enhance the therapeutic efficacy.34 Studies27,35 demonstrated that administering rhubarb preparations via a nasogastric tube offered significant advantages, particularly in cases of severe acute pancreatitis complicated by severe gastrointestinal paralysis. Both methods could achieve the treatment goals while ensuring safety.

Postoperative pancreatitis was a common complication of endoscopic retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). High-residue enemas allowed rhubarb preparations to act directly on the upper colon, exerting anti-inflammatory effects, inhibiting pancreatic enzyme activation, and regulating the intestinal microecology through mucosal absorption. Simultaneously, its laxative effect promoted the elimination of intestinal toxins and metabolic waste and reduced the production of gut-derived inflammatory factors, thereby blocking the occurrence and progression of PEP. Clinical research data36–38 indicated that a high-residue enema with rhubarb after ERCP significantly reduced the incidence of PEP, shortened the postoperative gastrointestinal recovery time, and had a low incidence of adverse reactions with mild severity. It was important to note that enema procedures should strictly adhere to aseptic principles, with the temperature and speed of the enema solution controlled, to avoid intestinal irritation due to improper operation and further ensure medication safety.

Assessing the Efficacy of Medications is Crucial in the Treatment of Acute Pancreatitis

For AP patients in the acute phase, early intervention with Traditional Chinese Medicine, mainly composed of rhubarb, along with conventional therapy, could significantly reduce the risk of organ failure, which represented one of the core manifestations of its efficacy. Early application of rhubarb preparations (eg, Dachengqi Decoction) could markedly decrease intra-abdominal pressure, promote the recovery of gastrointestinal function, and effectively relieve symptoms such as abdominal pain and bloating.39–42

Early enteral nutrition was a critical intervention in the treatment of AP. However, some patients might experience intolerance (eg, nausea, vomiting, or worsening bloating) to standalone enteral nutrition due to the incomplete recovery of gastrointestinal function, which would affect the efficacy of nutritional support.43 Rhubarb could create favorable conditions for enteral nutrition by repairing the intestinal mucosal barrier, promoting gastrointestinal motility, and regulating the balance of intestinal microbiota.44

Studies42,45,46 demonstrated that patients receiving rhubarb combined with early enteral nutrition had a significantly lower incidence of nutritional intolerance. A faster recovery of nutritional parameters such as serum albumin and prealbumin, as well as improvements in intestinal barrier function-related indicators (eg, D-lactate and diamine oxidase), was also achieved. Enhanced nutritional efficacy ultimately led to an improved overall patient prognosis and a reduced hospitalization duration.

Furthermore, the lungs were among the most vulnerable organs in patients with AP. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) was a common and severe complication during the acute phase of AP, closely associated with inflammation-mediated parenchymal damage and disruption of the alveolar capillary barrier.47 Rheum palmatum and its active components (such as emodin and rhein) could mitigate pulmonary injury by inhibiting the release of inflammatory factors (eg, TNF-α and IL-6), reducing inflammatory infiltration in lung tissue, and modulating oxidative stress responses to preserve the integrity of the alveolar capillary barrier.48 Clinical studies demonstrated that AP patients treated with Rheum palmatum exhibited a lower incidence of ARDS, a faster improvement in the arterial oxygen partial pressure/insufflated oxygen concentration (PaO2/FiO2) ratio, and a reduced risk of pulmonary infection.31,49 The efficacy of rhubarb in the treatment of AP had been validated by extensive clinical practice and research, and its effects could be assessed in multiple dimensions, including organ function protection, symptom relief, and enhanced nutritional efficacy.

Clinical Administration of Rhubarb is a Critical Safeguard for the Treatment of Acute Pancreatitis

The timing of administration, processing requirements, administration methods, dosage, treatment course, and adverse reaction monitoring of rhubarb should be precisely controlled.

The clinical recommendation for initiating enteral nutrition was within 24 or 48 h after the onset of acute pancreatitis.50,51 The administration of rhubarb could commence simultaneously with the initiation of enteral nutrition.50,51 The core advantage of this concurrent intervention strategy lied in leveraging the regulatory effects of rhubarb on gastrointestinal function to facilitate the smooth implementation of enteral nutrition. The active component of raw rhubarb was anthraquinone, which was heat-sensitive. Therefore, the decoction process must strictly adhere to the “post-addition” principle, meaning that rhubarb should be added 10 min after the completion of the decoction and before the herbs removed from the pot. This approach maximized the protection of anthraquinone components from heat-induced degradation, thereby ensuring optimal therapeutic efficacy.31,52

Oral administration or nasogastric feeding was the most common route of rhubarb administration, with standardized procedures and dosage adjustments tailored to individual patient conditions. Specific adjustments should be made based on the patient’s tolerance, relief of abdominal pain and distension, and bowel movement status to avoid prolonged treatment that may lead to excessive laxative effects. Rhubarb enema was indicated for patients with severe gastrointestinal dysfunction or intolerance to oral/nasogastric feeding and required strict adherence to standardized procedures and dosage control.40 Clinically, compound decoctions containing rhubarb, such as the Dachengqi or Dachaihu decoction, might be selected for enema administration based on syndrome differentiation to achieve precise syndrome-based treatment. During enema administration, positional adjustments (eg, left lateral decubitus position) should be made to ensure adequate retention of the medicinal solution in the colon, enhance local absorption, relieve heat, and eliminate endotoxins.

Based on the pathological characteristics of AP at different disease stages, a staged drug administration strategy should be adopted.53 During the systemic acute phase (within 2 weeks of onset), administered the first decoction of rhubarb-based herbal formulations via nasogastric or jejunal tube infusion. When using the second decoction for enema administration, it achieved dual intervention of “oral + local” to rapidly control inflammatory responses and improved intestinal qi stagnation.39 The dosage and frequency of administration should be adjusted according to the severity of abdominal pain, distension, and defecation status. Upon entering the systemic infection phase (2 weeks after onset), the therapeutic focus shifted to controlling the infection and protecting organ function. It was recommended that rhubarb-based herbal formulations be continued via nasogastric or jejunal tube infusion, with the frequency of administration flexibly adjusted based on infection control and gastrointestinal function status, to avoid excessive purgation that may compromise the body’s vital signs.11,27

For patients with significant abdominal pain and distension accompanied by intestinal paralysis, a multimodal strategy was recommended.39,40,54 Clinically, a combination of methods such as nasogastric or oral administration of TCM, enema, external application of TCM, and acupoint injection can be employed. These approaches synergistically enhanced gastrointestinal motility, alleviated intestinal paralysis, and reduced pain. This combined intervention model targeted multiple mechanisms and was particularly suitable for severe cases of intestinal paralysis in critically ill patients, where conventional treatments were ineffective, significantly improving the efficiency of gastrointestinal function recovery.

Safety monitoring was a critical component of the clinical administration of rhubarb, focusing on monitoring dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, and internal homeostasis. During administration, it was essential to strictly record patients’ fluid intake and output to assess potential dehydration risks. Regular monitoring of blood electrolytes (eg, potassium, sodium, and chloride) and acid-base balance parameters was required to promptly identify and correct electrolyte disturbances, thus preventing the disruption of internal homeostasis and the exacerbation of organ dysfunction caused by excessive laxative effects.

Concurrently, the principle of “discontinuation upon symptom resolution” must be adhered to, especially in elderly patients with reduced tolerance. As excessive laxative use may impair vital energy (zhengqi) and lead to constitutional weakness and compromised immune function. In cases of severe complications such as gastrointestinal bleeding or perforation during treatment, it is imperative to immediately discontinue rhubarb preparations, followed by prompt targeted therapeutic interventions.

Limitations

This study retrieved and summarized the best evidence on the nursing practice of rhubarb administration in patients with acute pancreatitis. However, there are a few limitations. First, all articles were from China. Therefore, there is no evidence for the application of rhubarb in other races, and the evidence may lack some generalizability. Second, some evidence is of poor quality, which may affect the quality of this evidence-based practice summary. Third, the different processing and administration methods of rhubarb may affect the medication effect, but relevant high-quality studies have not yet been reported, which may affect the comprehensiveness and completeness of this study. Future studies can expand the application of rhubarb administration in patients with AP from other countries and obtain more sources and evidence on the effects of rhubarb administration in patients with AP.

Conclusion

This study strictly followed the evidence-based pathway. Based on the framework of the guideline for development and evaluation of complex interventions, it summarized 27 pieces of recommended evidence from four themes—drug mechanism of action, feasibility (safety), assessment (effectiveness), and clinical implementation. Evidence indicated that rhubarb could be utilized as an adjunctive therapeutic agent in the clinical management of acute pancreatitis. Rhubarb effectively mitigated inflammatory responses, protected pancreatic and intestinal functions, alleviated abdominal discomfort symptoms, and demonstrated favorable safety profiles. Key research evidence demonstrated that standardized administration, early initiation of treatment within 24 to 48 hours of onset, and combination with enteral nutritional support yielded optimal therapeutic outcomes. However, all current evidence regarding acute pancreatitis originated from China, and international guidelines as well as clinical decision-making documents do not address the application of TCM therapies. Therefore, the recommendations of this study are applicable only to the clinical context of China; the study is constrained by regional clinical practice standards, population characteristics, and evidence quality. Caution is required when extending these findings to other countries or regions. Therefore, international, multicenter, large-sample, high-quality studies are needed to further validate the global applicability of rhubarb in treating acute pancreatitis.
