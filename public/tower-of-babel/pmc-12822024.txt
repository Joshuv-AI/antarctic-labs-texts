Mechanistic study of HIF-1α-mediated regulation of the Notch1 pathway in promoting radioresistance in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma: a comprehensive analysis based on bioinformatics and functional experiments
Authors: Ruizhi Bao, Junhua Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiotherapy remains a cornerstone in the treatment of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), yet resistance to irradiation significantly impairs therapeutic outcomes. Hypoxia within the tumor microenvironment is a known contributor to radioresistance, largely mediated by hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α). However, the downstream molecular mechanisms remain incompletely defined. This study aimed to investigate whether HIF-1α promotes radioresistance in HNSCC through activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway.MethodsTranscriptomic data from TCGA-HNSC were analyzed to identify differentially expressed genes between radiosensitive (n = 251) and radioresistant (n = 35) groups. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and Pearson correlation assessed pathway involvement. In vitro experiments using CAL27 and FaDu cell lines were conducted under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. HIF1A was silenced via shRNA, and Notch1 was overexpressed for rescue experiments. Clonogenic survival, γ-H2AX immunofluorescence, EdU incorporation, and apoptosis (Annexin V/PI) were measured post-irradiation (2 Gy).ResultsHIF1A and Notch1 pathway genes were upregulated in radioresistant tumors (p < 0.05). Hypoxia significantly increased HIF1A, NOTCH1, DLL4, and HES1 expression in vitro (p < 0.001). HIF1A knockdown reduced clonogenic survival (P < 0.001), increased γ-H2AX foci (p < 0.001), suppressed proliferation (p < 0.001), and elevated apoptosis (p < 0.001). Notch1 overexpression partially reversed these effects (all p < 0.01).ConclusionOur findings suggest that HIF-1α promotes radiotherapy resistance in HNSCC by activating the Notch1 signaling axis. Targeting this pathway may enhance radiosensitivity and provide a new strategy for overcoming hypoxia-driven resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15428-7.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) ranks among the six most prevalent cancers worldwide, with over 900,000 new cases and 450,000 deaths annually according to GLOBOCAN 2020 statistics [1]. Despite advances in multimodal treatment—including surgery, chemotherapy, and especially radiotherapy—long-term prognosis remains unsatisfactory, with 5-year survival rates below 50% for locally advanced cases [2]. Radiotherapy plays a pivotal role in the management of HNSCC, but intrinsic and acquired radioresistance significantly limit its efficacy, contributing to local recurrence and poor clinical outcomes [3].

One of the major biological contributors to radioresistance is the tumor hypoxic microenvironment, which occurs in up to 60–70% of HNSCC lesions [4]. Hypoxia not only promotes malignant progression but also directly diminishes radiotherapy-induced DNA damage by reducing oxygen-dependent free radical formation [5]. Hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α), a master transcriptional regulator under low oxygen conditions, orchestrates a broad spectrum of adaptive responses including angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, anti-apoptosis, and stemness maintenance [6]. Increasing evidence has implicated HIF-1α in the development of resistance to radiotherapy across various tumor types including glioblastoma, cervical cancer, and HNSCC [7]. However, the downstream molecular mechanisms by which HIF-1α confers radioresistance in HNSCC remain incompletely defined.

Among the putative downstream pathways regulated by HIF-1α, the Notch signaling pathway has gained attention due to its roles in cell fate determination, maintenance of cancer stem cell properties, and DNA damage repair [8]. Notch1 activation is frequently observed in HNSCC tissues and correlates with poor therapeutic response [9]. Moreover, emerging data suggest that hypoxia may promote Notch signaling via HIF-1α-mediated transcriptional regulation of its ligands and receptors, such as Delta-like ligand 4 (DLL4) and Notch1 itself [10]. Nevertheless, whether HIF-1α drives Notch1 activation in the context of radioresistance in HNSCC has not yet been systematically investigated.

In this study, we aim to elucidate the regulatory relationship between HIF-1α and the Notch1 signaling axis in HNSCC and to explore how this interaction contributes to radiotherapy resistance. By integrating bioinformatic profiling of patient data and functional validation using in vitro cellular models, we seek to uncover a potential hypoxia-driven mechanism underlying therapeutic failure in head and neck cancers.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

Transcriptomic and clinical data for patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) were retrieved from the TCGA-HNSC cohort via the Genomic Data Commons portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) [10]. To focus on mechanisms of radiotherapy resistance, only cases with documented radiotherapy and clear therapeutic outcomes were included. Based on clinical response—categorized as complete/partial response or no measurable disease (radiosensitive, n = 251) versus progressive or persistent disease (radioresistant, n = 35)—a total of 286 cases were analyzed. Raw RNA-seq data in fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) were converted to transcripts per million (TPM) for normalization. Only protein-coding genes were retained for downstream analyses.

Differential expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between radiosensitive and radioresistant groups were identified using the limma package in R. To enhance biological interpretability, genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change (|logFC|) > 0.585 were considered significant. Hypoxia- and Notch-related genes—including HIF1A, NOTCH1, DLL4, HES1, and HEY1—were specifically highlighted for further investigation.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the clusterProfiler package to assess pathway-level changes associated with HIF1A expression. All genes were ranked by signal-to-noise ratio, and enrichment scores were computed against the c2.cp.kegg.v7.0.symbols.gmt gene set from MSigDB, using 1,000 permutations. Significantly enriched pathways were defined by adjusted p-values < 0.05. Focus was placed on hypoxia- and cancer-related processes, including Notch signaling, DNA repair, apoptosis, and stemness signatures.

Correlation analysis

To explore co-expression relationships between HIF1A and Notch pathway components, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated across all samples using normalized TPM values. Genes with |r|> 0.3 and p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly correlated. Particular focus was given to the expression correlation between HIF1A and key Notch signaling genes, including NOTCH1, DLL4, HES1, and HEY1.

Co-expression network construction

Significantly correlated genes were used to construct a co-expression network centered on HIF1A. The network included HIF1A and its top positively associated Notch signaling partners. Node–edge relationships were mapped and visualized using Cytoscape (v3.9.1). Edge thickness reflected correlation strength, and key nodes were highlighted to illustrate the HIF1A-Notch axis.

Hypoxia induction in HNSCC cells

Two human head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cell lines, CAL27 (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, China) and FaDu (ATCC, USA), were employed to assess the hypoxia-induced expression of HIF-1α and Notch signaling components [11]. Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ExCell Bio, China), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator.

For hypoxia induction, both cell lines were exposed to either normoxic conditions (21% O₂) or hypoxic conditions (1% O₂) for 24 h. Hypoxia was achieved using a tri-gas incubator (MCO-5 M-PA, PHCbi, Japan). Following treatment, total RNA and protein were extracted to evaluate the expression of HIF1A, NOTCH1, DLL4, and HES1 via quantitative PCR and Western blotting.

Functional experiments in CAL27 cells

All functional experiments in this section were performed using CAL27 human head and neck squamous cell carcinoma cells (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ExCell Bio, China), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂.

Plasmid-based knockdown and overexpression experiments were conducted using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) in Opti-MEM reduced serum medium (Gibco, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. All shRNA and overexpression plasmids were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Hypoxic conditions (1% O₂, 5% CO₂) were established using a tri-gas incubator (MCO-5 M-PA, PHCbi, Japan), and X-ray irradiationwas delivered using a clinical linear accelerator (Elekta Precise, UK).

HIF1A knockdown

CAL27 cells were used to assess the effect of HIF1A silencing under normoxia. Three groups were established: Control, untransfected control; sh-NC, cells transfected with non-targeting shRNA (sh-NC); and sh-HIF1A, cells transfected with HIF1A-specific shRNA (sh-HIF1A). All shRNA plasmids were obtained from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Cells were seeded at ~ 60–70% confluence in 6-well plates, transfected for 6 h, then switched to complete DMEM and incubated for another 48 h under normoxic conditions (21% O₂, 5% CO₂, 37 °C). After treatment, total RNA and protein were extracted for the evaluation of HIF1A mRNA and HIF-1α protein levels using qRT-PCR and Western blotting.

Radiation response under hypoxia and normoxia

CAL27 cells were used to evaluate the impact of HIF1A knockdown on radiosensitivity under different oxygen conditions. Cells were first transfected with either left untransfected (Control), sh-NC, or sh-HIF1A. Forty-eight hours after transfection, each group was further divided and cultured under either normoxia (21% O₂) or hypoxia (1% O₂) for 24 h, followed by exposure to a single 2 Gy dose of X-ray radiation using a clinical linear accelerator. Post-irradiation, cells were harvested at specific time points for the following assays: Clonogenic assay, γ-H2AX immunofluorescence, EdU incorporation assay as well as Annexin V/PI staining with flow cytometry.

Rescue by Notch1 overexpression

To evaluate whether Notch1 overexpression can restore Notch pathway activity suppressed by HIF1A knockdown, four groups were defined: sh-NC; sh-HIF1A; sh-HIF1A + OE-NC, HIF1A knockdown plus empty vector (OE-NC); sh-HIF1A + OE-Notch1, HIF1A knockdown plus Notch1 overexpression (OE-Notch1).

Cells were transfected under normoxia and harvested 48 h later. Expression levels of HIF1A, NOTCH1, DLL4, and HES1 were measured via qRT-PCR and Western blotting to assess pathway restoration.

Functional rescue combined with radiation under normoxia

To determine the effect of OE-Notch1 could reverse the enhanced radiosensitivity induced by HIF1A knockdown, four groups were established under normoxic conditions with irradiation: sh-NC + N + IR; sh-HIF1A + N + IR; sh-HIF1A + OE-NC + N + IR; sh-HIF1A + OE-Notch1 + N + IR. After 48 h of transfection, cells were exposed to a single 2 Gy dose of X-ray radiation using a clinical linear accelerator. Following irradiation, a series of functional assays—including Western blotting, clonogenic survival, γ-H2AX immunofluorescence, EdU incorporation, and Annexin V/PI staining—were performed to assess radiosensitization and its potential reversal by Notch1 overexpression.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For reverse transcription, 1 µg of RNA was converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan) in a 20 µL reaction volume. Synthesized cDNA was immediately subjected to real-time PCR or stored at − 20 °C until use.

Quantitative PCR was conducted using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Japan) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each 20 µL reaction contained 10 µL of 2 × TB Green master mix, 0.4 µM of each primer, and 2 µL of diluted cDNA template. The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s.

All reactions were performed in triplicate. The relative mRNA expression levels of HIF1A, NOTCH1, DLL4, and HES1 were calculated using the 2^ − ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene. Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer with protease/phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China), and quantified via BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts (20–30 µg) were separated on 10–12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA).

After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at ambient temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies from Abcam (UK): HIF-1α(1:1000, ab1)、Notch1 (1:1000, ab52627)、DLL4(1:1000, ab176876)、HES1 (1:1000, ab108937)、γ-H2AX (1:1000, ab81299), cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000, ab2302), and β-Actin (1:5000, ab8226), which served as the loading control.

Following three-time TBST washing, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG, ab205718; goat anti-mouse IgG, ab205719; Abcam, 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Thermo Fisher) and imaged with a ChemiDoc XRS + imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ (NIH, USA), and all target signals were normalized to β-actin.

Clonogenic survival assay

At 6 h post-irradiation (2 Gy [12]), cells were seeded at low density (500–1000/well) in 6-well plates. After 10–14 days of incubation under normoxic or hypoxic conditions, colonies (≥ 50 cells) were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde, 15 min), stained (0.1% crystal violet, 20 min). Plates were rinsed gently with distilled water, air-dried, and photographed. Colonies were counted manually using ImageJ (NIH, USA), and plating efficiency (PE) and surviving fraction (SF) were calculated as follows: PE = number of colonies formed/number of cells seeded (non-irradiated control); SF = number of colonies formed after irradiation/(cells seeded × PE). All groups were assessed in biological triplicates.

γ-H2AX immunofluorescence assay

Radiation response under hypoxia and normoxia group was fixed at 2 h and 6 h post-irradiation (2 Gy), while functional rescue combined with radiation under normoxia group was assessed at 6 h only. Fixation was performed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocking in 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature.

Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-γ-H2AX antibody (1:500, ab81299, Abcam), followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, Thermo Fisher, USA) for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL, Beyotime, China) for 5 min. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Axio Observer, Zeiss, Germany), and γ-H2AX foci per nucleus were quantified using ImageJ with manual validation from three independent fields per group.

EdU incorporation assay

At 24 h after 2 Gy irradiation, cells were incubated with 10 μM EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine; BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 488, C0071S, Beyotime, China) for 2 h under normoxic conditions, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized using 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min.

After click reaction with fluorescent azide, DAPI (1 µg/mL) was applied for nuclear staining (5 min), and fluorescent images were acquired using Zeiss Axio Observer microscope (Germany), and the percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated from at least three randomly selected fields per group using ImageJ.

Flow cytometric apoptosis assay

At 24 h post-irradiation (2 Gy), cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in 1 × binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC (5 μL; C1062L, Beyotime, China) and propidium iodide (10 μL) were added to 100 μL of cell suspension and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark.

Samples were immediately analyzed on a flow cytometer (FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences, USA), and apoptotic fractions were quantified using FlowJo software (v10.8), with early (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻) and late (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺) apoptosis reported separately.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses for functional experiments were performed using SPSS software (version 23.0; IBM, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent replicates. Group differences were assessed by one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

HIF1A expression correlates with notch signaling activation in radioresistant HNSCC

To explore transcriptional differences associated with radiotherapy response, differential gene expression analysis was conducted between radiosensitive and radioresistant HNSCC patients. A total of 1,630 DEGs were identified under the threshold of |log₂FC|> 0.585 and p.adj < 0.05. As shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 1A), HIF1A, NOTCH1, and DLL4 were significantly upregulated in the radioresistant group, suggesting potential enrichment of hypoxia and Notch pathway activity. To further characterize the signaling profile associated with HIF1A expression, GSEA was performed. Notably, high HIF1A expression was accompanied by significant enrichment of the Notch signaling pathway, along with apoptosis, DNA repair, and stemness-related gene signatures (Fig. 1C). These results indicate that HIF1A may function as a central regulator linking hypoxia to oncogenic signaling cascades.

Correlation analysis confirmed positive transcriptional associations between HIF1A and multiple Notch pathway components. DLL4 showed a strong correlation with HIF1A (R = 0.36, p = 3.2e–10, Fig. 1D). Similarly, HIF1A expression was significantly correlated with HES1 (R = 0.13, p = 0.025), HEY1 (R = 0.14, p = 0.02), and NOTCH1 (R = 0.19, p = 0.0011), as shown in Fig. 1E–G. Together, these findings suggest the existence of a transcriptional HIF1A–Notch axis in radioresistant HNSCC.

To evaluate the role of HIF1A in radiosensitivity, cells were exposed to 2 Gy X-ray irradiation under normoxia (N + IR) or hypoxia (H + IR). Clonogenic assay revealed that hypoxia markedly increased survival after irradiation, while HIF1A knockdown significantly reduced colony numbers under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (p < 0.001, Fig. 3C). Quantification confirmed that HIF1A silencing reversed hypoxia-induced radioresistance.

γ-H2AX immunofluorescence staining showed marked DNA damage at 2 h post-irradiation (increased γ-H2AX signal). Under hypoxic conditions, γ-H2AX levels were lower than in normoxia, suggesting reduced radiation-induced oxidative damage. Compared to the sh-NC group, HIF1A knockdown significantly increased γ-H2AX signal at both 2 and 6 h post-irradiation (p < 0.001, Fig. 3D), indicating impaired DNA repair and greater damage accumulation.

EdU incorporation assay revealed that irradiation reduced proliferation activity, with hypoxia partially preserving cell proliferation. HIF1A knockdown further suppressed EdU-positive cell rates under both conditions (p < 0.001, Fig. 3E), confirming reduced proliferative capacity.

Consistently, Annexin V/PI staining showed that HIF1A knockdown significantly increased radiation-induced apoptosis under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, with the most substantial effect observed under normoxia (p < 0.001, Fig. 3F).

Notch1 overexpression partially reverses HIF-1α knockdown–induced radiosensitization

To further clarify the mechanistic role of Notch1 in HIF-1α-mediated radiosensitivity, we conducted functional rescue experiments under normoxic conditions. Specifically, Notch1 was overexpressed in HIF-1α-silenced cells to assess whether this could counteract the radiosensitizing phenotype induced by HIF-1α knockdown.

First, clonogenic assays revealed that silencing HIF-1α markedly impaired the ability of cells to form colonies post-irradiation, indicating enhanced radiosensitivity. Next, we examined radiation-induced DNA damage using γ-H2AX immunofluorescence. Cells lacking HIF-1α displayed pronounced accumulation of γ-H2AX foci at 6 h post-irradiation, reflecting elevated DNA double-strand breaks. However, this DNA damage signal was substantially mitigated upon Notch1 overexpression (p < 0.001), implying that Notch1 attenuates irradiation-triggered genomic instability in the absence of HIF-1α (Fig. 5B).

In parallel, EdU incorporation assays revealed a marked decline in proliferation in the sh-HIF1A group, as expected under radiosensitizing conditions. Notably, forced expression of Notch1 substantially rescued this proliferative deficit, restoring EdU positivity close to control levels (p < 0.001), further supporting a protective role for Notch1 in post-radiation recovery (Fig. 5C).

Apoptotic responses were then assessed by Annexin V/PI flow cytometry. HIF-1α knockdown significantly increased the apoptotic fraction following irradiation, consistent with a heightened susceptibility to cell death. This effect was partially reversed in the Notch1 rescue group (p < 0.001), pointing to Notch1-mediated suppression of radiation-induced apoptosis (Fig. 5D).

Finally, immunoblotting confirmed these observations at the molecular level. The expression of γ-H2AX and cleaved caspase-3—markers of DNA damage and apoptosis—was elevated in sh-HIF1A cells but notably reduced upon Notch1 re-expression (p < 0.001), underscoring the modulatory effect of Notch1 on HIF-1α–dependent stress responses (Fig. 5E).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that Notch1 overexpression can partially compensate for the loss of HIF-1α, mitigating DNA damage, enhancing proliferation, and reducing apoptosis under irradiation. This highlights the functional interplay between HIF-1α and Notch1 signaling in shaping the cellular response to radiotherapy.

Disccusion

This study systematically investigated the role of HIF-1α in mediating radiotherapy resistance in HNSCC, with a particular focus on its potential regulation of the Notch1 signaling pathway. By integrating transcriptomic analysis from the TCGA-HNSC cohort and a series of in vitro functional assays, we observed that HIF1A expression was elevated in radioresistant tumors and correlated with increased activity of the Notch signaling axis. Hypoxia exposure upregulated the expression of HIF-1α and its downstream targets (DLL4, Notch1, HES1) at both mRNA and protein levels. Silencing HIF1A sensitized HNSCC cells to ionizing radiation, whereas Notch1 overexpression partially rescued this phenotype. These findings suggest that a hypoxia-induced HIF-1α–Notch1 axis may be involved in the adaptive resistance to radiotherapy in HNSCC.

The activation of HIF-1α under hypoxic stress has been widely documented in solid tumors, where it modulates key pathways related to angiogenesis, metabolism, and cell survival [13]. In this study, transcriptomic data revealed a strong enrichment of Notch signaling and DNA repair pathways in HIF1A-high tumors, which is consistent with previous reports describing HIF-1α’s role in promoting stemness and therapy resistance through transcriptional control of developmental genes [14]. Functional validation in CAL27 and FaDu cells confirmed that hypoxia-induced HIF-1α upregulates key Notch pathway components. These observations are in line with studies suggesting that HIF-1α can directly bind to the promoters of NOTCH1 or DLL4 [15], although this direct interaction was not verified in the present study and requires further mechanistic validation.

Functionally, HIF1A knockdown enhanced cellular radiosensitivity, evidenced by decreased clonogenic survival, increased γ-H2AX foci (indicative of DNA damage), reduced proliferation, and elevated apoptosis. These effects support a model in which HIF-1α promotes radiotherapy resistance by mitigating DNA damage and suppressing apoptotic signaling [16]. Notably, Notch1 overexpression in HIF1A-silenced cells partially reversed these changes, suggesting that Notch1 may mediate, at least in part, the downstream effects of HIF-1α on cell survival under genotoxic stress. Previous studies have also reported that Notch1 contributes to radioresistance by upregulating anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2 and Survivin, while reducing the activation of caspase-dependent pathways [17].

The regulatory interplay between HIF-1α and Notch1 has been demonstrated in other cancers, including glioblastoma and cervical cancer, but its relevance in HNSCC has remained unclear [18]. Our findings provide new evidence that supports the existence of a functional HIF-1α–Notch1 axis in HNSCC, adding to the growing recognition of hypoxia-responsive signaling networks as central modulators of therapy response. However, the incomplete rescue of radiosensitivity by Notch1 overexpression suggests that HIF-1α may act through additional parallel pathways—such as PI3K/AKT or autophagy—highlighting the multifaceted nature of hypoxia-driven resistance mechanisms [7, 19].

In comparison with previous literature, our study aligns with reports showing that hypoxia and Notch1 activation promote therapy resistance in various cancer types [8]. Yet, the current work is, to our knowledge, the first to systematically explore and functionally validate this relationship in HNSCC. The identification of this regulatory axis fills a gap in our understanding of tumor adaptation under radiotherapy and introduces a plausible therapeutic target for radiosensitization.

Despite the strengths of this study—including the integration of transcriptomic data with functional validation—several limitations must be acknowledged. First, all in vitro experiments were conducted in cell lines, which may not fully recapitulate the tumor microenvironment. Second, the direct transcriptional regulation of Notch1 by HIF-1α was not experimentally confirmed, and ChIP or promoter-reporter assays are warranted to clarify this mechanism. Third, the study did not evaluate potential interactions with other signaling pathways or the role of immune and stromal components in radioresistance. These gaps highlight the need for additional studies using animal models, patient-derived xenografts, and multi-omics approaches.

In summary, our findings suggest that HIF-1α contributes to radiotherapy resistance in HNSCC, potentially through activation of the Notch1 pathway. This hypoxia-induced signaling axis may represent a novel therapeutic target to overcome treatment resistance. Future work is needed to confirm the clinical relevance of these observations and to explore the feasibility of targeting HIF-1α or Notch1 to improve radiotherapy outcomes in HNSCC patients.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that HIF-1α may contribute to radiotherapy resistance in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma by activating the Notch1 signaling pathway. Through bioinformatic and functional analyses, we identified a hypoxia-induced HIF-1α–Notch1 axis that promotes DNA repair and cell survival. Silencing HIF1A enhanced radiosensitivity, while Notch1 overexpression partially reversed this effect, suggesting a functional link between the two. These findings highlight a potential therapeutic target to overcome hypoxia-driven treatment resistance in HNSCC.
