Computational study of chalcone-cyanopyrimidine hybrids as LSD1 inhibitors: Assessing the influence of FAD on binding affinity and inhibition
Authors: Md Khalid Saifullah, Amisha Gupta, Darakhshan Parveen, Faizul Azam, M Mumtaz Alam, Mymoona Akhter, Suhel Parvez, Naazneen Maksud, Naila Azeez, M Shaquiquzzaman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe inhibition of lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer treatment due to its critical role in epigenetic regulation. In this study, we conducted an in-silico investigation to explore the potential of chalcone-cyanopyrimidine hybrids as LSD1 inhibitors. Chalcones and cyanopyrimidine derivatives are known for their anticancer properties, making them an ideal combination for targeting LSD1. A library of novel chalcone-cyanopyrimidine hybrids were evaluated using molecular docking, ADMET, MM-GBSA, and molecular dynamics simulations against LSD1, with a particular focus on the role and effect of flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), a cofactor integral to LSD1 activity. The molecular docking results revealed that the chalcone-cyanopyrimidine hybrids demonstrate strong binding affinities with LSD1. Additionally, the results revealed that several compounds within the library in comparison to the reference standards exhibited strong binding affinities towards LSD1. When we evaluated these docking results, it was found that despite the highest binding affinity observed in competitive docking approach, their MD results depicted their instability inside the binding pocket, whereas the MD simulation done in presence of FAD represented a stable RMSF and RMSD profile. It suggests that FAD is an integral part in modulating LSD1 inhibition. This study lays the groundwork for future experimental validation and optimization of chalcone-cyanopyrimidine hybrids as potent LSD1 inhibitors with promising therapeutic implications.Keywords: Cancer, Lysine specific demethylase 1, Pyrimidine, Chalcone, Molecular docking, Molecular dynamics

Introduction

The mortality worldwide due to cancer has been a major concern for decades [1]. The cancer is characterized by the unregulated proliferation of atypical cells, which can invade surrounding tissues and disrupt normal cellular function [2]. In 2022, the global burden [3] of new cancer cases was about 20 million and accounting for approximately 9.7 million fatalities. Lung cancer is the most often occurring cancer accounting for 2.5 million new cases (12.4 %), followed by female breast cancer (11.6 %), colorectal (9.6 %), prostate (7.3 %), and stomach (4.9 %) cancers [3,4].

Cancer therapies involving radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, hormone therapy, targeted medication therapy, and stem cell therapy have always remained challenging treatment options due to their systemic toxicity [5,6]. Despite extensive research efforts and advances in therapies, there remains a considerable distance in the find for a medication with minimal side effects.

A crucial element of effective anticancer drug discovery is focusing on protein targets and mechanisms involved in carcinogenesis, survival, and resistance. There are multiple cancer targets in the body including Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR), Lysine-Specific Demethylase 1 (LSD1), Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), Cyclin-Dependent Kinases (CDKs), Histone Deacetylases (HDACs), etc. [7]. LSD1, is a crucial epigenetic regulator that catalyzes the demethylation of histones [8]. It specifically removes methyl groups from lysine 4 and lysine 9 on histone H3 which alters chromatin structure and regulates gene activity. This epigenetic modification is essential for various cellular processes, including differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis [9,10]. LSD1 dysregulation has been associated with a variety of malignancies like leukaemia, breast cancer, prostate cancer, and several others. Recent studies have demonstrated that the inhibition of LSD1 can lead to a reduction in tumour growth and metastasis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target [11,12].

In response to the growing recognition of LSD1's role in cancer, several inhibitors have been developed to specifically target LSD1 activity. Drugs such as tranylcypromine [13], Pulrodemstat [14], UNC1999 [15], IMG-7289 [16], ORY-1001 [17], ORY-2001 [18], SP-2577 [19], SP-2509 [20]and GSK2879552 [21] etc. have exhibited promising preclinical results, and are currently in clinical trial that demonstrates their ability to inhibit LSD1's function in order to reduce tumour growth. Recent studies have further expanded on the design of more selective inhibitors with improved pharmacokinetic properties, such as the 2-aryl-4-aminoquinazoline based LSD1 inhibitors reported by Wang Bo et al., wherein the most potent LSD1 inhibitor had an IC50 value of 0.108 μM [22]. Another researcher Wei Yu et al. found two most potent compounds with unique scaffolds that effectively inhibited LSD1 with IC50 values of 6.24 and 9.26 μM, [23]. Additionally, recent work by Zhang et al. reported a selective and reversible LSD1 inhibitor with an IC50 value of 0.18 μM [24]. However, more research is needed to fully evaluate their efficacy and clinical applicability in various cancer types.

The literature study suggests that the pyrimidine is indeed a well-explored nucleus in the development of anticancer drugs. This is further exemplified by the multiple pyrimidine nucleus containing FDA approved anticancer drugs available in market like 5-Fluorouracil [25], Brigatinib [26], Capecitabine [27], Gemcitabine [28], Pemetrexed [29], Cytarabine [30], Thioguanine [31], Fludarabine [32] etc. Similarly, the cyano functionality based anticancer drugs available in market include Anastrozole, Bicalutamide and Apalutamide amongst others [33]. Thus, the introduction of a cyano group at various positions on the pyrimidine ring can improve its physicochemical properties and positively influence its biological activity. In addition, the importance of the pyrimidine nucleus and the cyano functionality in the design of LSD1 inhibitors is supported by multiple previously reported literature [34,35]. One such example is of Pulrodemstat (CC-90011), a selective LSD1 inhibitor developed for the treatment of several cancers like glioblastoma, haematological malignancies and various solid tumours, which has shown promising LSD1 inhibitory activity due to its ability to interact with the enzyme's active site [36,37]. The docking interaction of Pulrodemstat reported by Kanouni et al. with LSD1 protein revealed that it formed a salt bridge with ASP555 and a hydrogen bond with LYS661 [14].

Furthermore, the LSD1 inhibitors based on cyanopyrimidine nuclei have been the subject of numerous anticancer studies, with encouraging outcomes. Ding et al. carried out in silico studies of a series of 6-aryl-5-cyanopyrimidine derivatives including molecular docking, MD simulations and 3D QSAR to evaluate the structural specifications necessary for a LSD1 inhibitor [38]. Tasneem et al. [39] synthesized several cyanopyrimidine-hydrazone hybrids, with the most potent one exhibiting an IC50 value of 0.956 μM against LSD1. Sheikh et al. [40] reported cyclopropylamine containing cyanopyrimidine derivatives and the three most potent compounds of the series inhibit LSD1 with IC50 values of 2.25, 1.80, and 6.08 μM, respectively. Ma et al. discovered cyanopyrimidine-thiourea hybrids as LSD1 inhibitors, the most active compound had an IC50 value of 0.65 μM [41]. Ma et al. also designed a series of cyanopyrimidine and 1,2,3-triazole hybrid compounds, with the most potent compound exhibiting an LSD1 inhibition by an IC50 of 183 nmol/L (0.183 μM) [42]. Similarly, the most potent LSD1 inhibitor reported by Wang et al. [43] inhibited LSD1 by IC50 value of 0.89 μM.

Despite the impressive anticancer activity of cyanopyrimidine nucleus, based on the hybrid design approach, fusion of the cyanopyrimidine with another nucleus was hypothesized to improve therapeutic efficacy [44]. Chalcone nucleus, a class of α, β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds [45] that have emerged as potent anticancer agents [46,47] due to their ability to induce apoptosis, arrest the cell cycle, and inhibit metastasis in various cancer types was selected based on an extensive literature survey. Certain chalcone derivatives like Licochalcone a [48], Xanthohumol [49], Isobavachalcone [50], Cardamonin [51], and Butein [52], etc. have demonstrated encouraging anticancer benefits in preclinical testing.

A huge experimental data is also available on chalcones and its derivatives, indicating them as potential drugs in the treatment of cancer. Elkhalifa et al. [53] reported a series of nitrogen-based chalcone analogues, wherein the most potent compound of the series exhibited IC50 in the range of 3.94 and 9.22 μM against MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, and MCF-7 cell line. Chen et al. [54] designed chalcone anologues amongst which the most potent compound had an IC50 value in the range of 0.026 μM–0.035 against HeLa, A549, PC-3, K562, and Bel-7402. Another research by Chinthala et al. [55] reported novel chalcone triazoles as anticancer agents, with the most potent compound depicting an IC50 value of 35.81 μM against the A549 cell line. Li et al. designed a series of orally active drivatives of chalcone with the most potent derivative exhibiting an LSD1 inhibition with an IC50 value of 0.14 μM and IC50 values ranging 0.87 μM–3.85 μM against KE-37, HAL-01, P30–OHK, LC4-1, MOLT-4 and SUP-B15 cells [56].

Pyrimidine and chalcone have also been proposed by numerous studies as powerful anticancer molecules. The imidazo[2,1-b]pyridine/pyrimidine chalcone derivatives reported by Kamal et al. [57] as anticancer agents exhibited GI50 values ranging from 0.28 to 30.0 μM against various cancer cell lines. Mansour et al. [58] developed novel furo[2,3-d]pyrimidine based chalcones. Amongst the series the most potent compound had antiproliferative activity against NCI 59 with GI50 value of 1.23 μM. The most potent 2-thiopyrimidine/chalcone hybrids reported by Lamie et al. [59] showed IC50 = 0.77–1.74 μM, against K-562 cell line; IC50 = 1.37–3.56 μM against MCF-7 cell line, and IC50 = 2.10 and 2.37 μM against HT-29 cell line.

Amalgation of these two bioactive scaffolds presents an exciting strategy in drug design, combining the unique biological activities of both moieties to potentially create more potent and versatile anticancer agents. Based on the above mentioned literature, a novel chalcone-cyanopyrimidine hybrid structure has been proposed. Fig-1 represents the proposed structure and the previously known anticancer drug containing cyano group, pyrimidine and chalcone nucleus. The cyano-pyrimidine and chalcone moieties were linked by an amine group. A primary consideration is the reactive nature of amine groups owed to the lone pair of electrons that makes them groups highly nucleophilic, therefore, allowing them to readily participate in bond formation. Concurrently, the essential role of amine linkages in the formation of peptide bonds for the biological systems played a crucial role in the selection of this particular linkage.

Pyrimidine scaffold from anticancer drugs like Brigatinib and Pulrodemstat, cyano groups from the drugs like Bicalutamide, Apalutamide and the chalcone moiety from Xanthohumol and Licochalcone A. The proposed structure represents cyano-pyrimidine chalcone moiety.

This research aims to investigate the anti-proliferative potential of a library of 2912 novel chalcone-cyanopyrimidine hybrids using in-silico approaches including molecular docking, MM-GBSA, ADME, toxicity, and molecular dynamics simulation. The library of compounds was designed by derivatising the proposed structure given in Fig-1 at three different positions R, R1 and R2. Since, there is no reported general pharmacophore for LSD1 inhibitors, a diverse library of derivatives was designed to ensure broad chemical coverage. The library of compounds was selected based on the reported potent cyano-pyrimidine, chalcone anti-cancer agents and LSD1 inhibitiors available in literature. The substitutions at R include H, Cl, F, NO2, OCH3, iso-propyl at o, p and m position of the ring. The various alkyl chains were anticipated at the R1 position that include n-propyl, iso-propyl, n-butyl, iso-butyl, n-pentyl and benzyl. For the R2 position the substitutions were 4-Acetamido, 4-Trifluoro methyl, 3, 4 -Dihydroxy, 3-OH, 2-OH, 2-NO2 etc were added. The structures of all the 2912 ligands were drawn using ChemDraw and novelty was assured using SciFinder. The novel chalcone-cyanopyrimidine hybrids were screened using molecular docking against the target LSD1 with three different approaches (the competitive docking at the site of FAD, docking at the site of native ligand in the presence of FAD, and at the site of native ligand in the absence of FAD) to see the role, and effect of FAD on binding affinity of our complex to the protein LSD1 [60].

Material and methods

Library creation

The ChemDraw 21.0.0 was used to design the entire set of 2912 novel chalcone-cyanopyrimidine hybrids (Table-S1). The uniqueness of each designed hybrid was confirmed using SciFinder.

Molecular docking

The molecular docking of novel chalcone-cyanopyrimidine hybrids was carried out against LSD1 protein to explore the binding pattern. The three-dimensional crystal structure of LSD1 protein (PDB ID: 2DW4, Resolution: 2.3 Å) was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank [61]. The protein and ligand preparations were conducted using the Protein Preparation Wizard and Ligand Preparation tool of Schrödinger Suite 12.8. The Glide module of Schrödinger Maestro Version 12.8 was employed for molecular docking studies, applying the standard precision (SP) mode.

The molecular docking was performed in three ways which are competitive docking at the site of FAD, docking at the site of native ligand in the presence of FAD and docking at the site of native ligand in the absence of FAD. For these docking studies, the three separate grids were prepared. For Competitive docking at the site of FAD: The X-ray crystal structure of the LSD1 protein (PDB ID: 2DW4) was downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. The protein was prepared using Protein Preparation Wizard, starting with the pre-processing, which involved assignment of bond orders, addition of hydrogen atoms, and deletion of water molecules (beyond 5 Å from het groups), generation of het states and others. After this, the workspace was analyzed upon clicking the ‘review and modify’ tab and all the heteroatoms other than FAD were deleted. This was followed by the ‘refinement’, involving optimization and minimization of protein (involving removal of waters beyond 3 Å hets and using force field OPLS3e). Subsequent to this, the receptor grid was generated at the site of FAD as our focus is on competitive docking with FAD.

The ligands were prepared under default conditions, applying the setting of generating at most of 32 conformers per ligand and OPLS4 force field. Afterwards, the docking of all the novel hybrids was executed by importing both the grid file and the prepared ligands. The findings from the process were then exported and evaluated further. This was the approach adopted for competitive docking and it was performed on entire set of 2912 compounds.

The top 30 test ligands with the highest docking score screened through competitive docking were again docked on 2DW4 protein at the site of native ligand once in the presence of FAD, then at the site of native ligand in the absence of FAD, and finally at the FAD binding site itself.

Docking at the site of native ligand in the presence of FAD: For this, the protein 2DW4 was prepared by the same method as mentioned above but for the grid generation, the coordinates of the native ligand, NY8 was used (taken from PDB: 6TUY) [62]. The binding site residues for NY8 in PDB: 6TUY include: VAL333, THR335, GLN358, PHE382, ASN383, LEU386, ASN535, LEU536, PHE538, ALA539, ASN540, HID551, TRP552, ASP553, GLN554, ASP555, ASP556, HID564, LEU659, TRP695, TYR761, SER762, VAL764, PRO808, ALA809 and THR810. This amino acid chain was selected during the grid generation and the docking was performed at the site of native ligand in the presence of FAD.

Docking at the site of native ligand in the absence of FAD: Here also, the protein preparation is similar as in the above two approaches except that the cofactor FAD was deleted prior to refinement. Grid generation was again based on the NY8 binding site from 6TUY, using the same residues listed above. Docking was then carried out at the native ligand site in the absence of FAD. The docking findings were evaluated and compared on the basis of docking score (in kcal/mol) and various interactions with the amino acid residues of the protein (pi-pi, hydrogen bonding, and salt bridge, etc.).

MM-GBSA analysis

The MM-GBSA (Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area) is a computational method used to estimate the binding free energies of molecular complexes. The MM-GBSA of the top 30 test ligands with the highest docking scores were executed using the Prime tool of Schrödinger suite 12.8 using VSBG solvation model and OPLS4 force field. The test ligands were refined and docking results were validated by the binding free energies (ΔG bind) of the receptor-ligand complex that were acquired from MM-GBSA.

Toxicity profile and ADME prediction

The top 30 test ligands with the highest docking scores were further estimated for toxicity and ADME in order to enhance the drug discovery process as it observes essential pharmacological characteristics. These online tools help us to predict various different molecular descriptors, blood-brain permeability, drug likeliness, total clearance, bioavailability score, AMES toxicity, hepatotoxicity, etc. The pkCSM and SwissADME online softwares were used to analyse toxicity profile, and ADME respectively. The canonical smiles of the test ligands were put into both the softwares to obtain all the attributes [63].

MD simulation

The MD simulation verify and validate the molecular docking results, analyse the stability of complexes, and the effect of solvation, along with identification of the key interactive amino acids, and the dynamic interactions. The MD simulation of top-scoring complex obtained by each type of docking i.e., competitive docking at the site of FAD, at the site of native ligand in the presence of FAD, and at the site of native ligand in the absence of FAD was done using the Desmond tool of Schrödinger suite 12.8.

The simulations were performed for a duration of 250 ns, maintaining a constant temperature of 300 K, and pressure of 1.01325 bar. The Nose-Hoover thermostat method was employed to regulate temperature, while pressure control was achieved using the Martyna-Tobias-Klein method. Before starting the simulations, the system was subjected to minimization and equilibration procedures in accordance with Desmond's default protocols. The complexes were then analyzed using protein-ligand contact plots, simulation quality plots, as well as Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF), Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Radius of Gyration (rGyr), and Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) analyses.

The system was encapsulated in an orthorhombic box of 10 Å with periodic boundary conditions, incorporating explicit water molecules using the Simple Point Charge (SPC) approach. To mimic physiological conditions, a 0.15 M NaCl salt solution was added to neutralize the system. The OPLS3e force field was employed for molecular interactions. Van der Waals and Coulomb interactions were evaluated using a cut-off radius of 9.0 Å, while electrostatic interactions were calculated with the Particle Mesh Ewald method. The analysis of trajectory files was conducted through simulation event, quality assessment, and interaction diagram protocols, and the equations of motion were analyzed using the Reference System Propagator Algorithm (RSPA) [64].

Binding of FAD to the LSD stabilizes the active conformation of the enzyme and shapes a large substrate cavity at the interface between the FAD and substrate-binding subdomains [65]. Electronically, FAD serves as the redox cofactor that accepts electrons during oxidative demethylation, converting the substrate amine to an imine before rapid re-oxidation by molecular oxygen; spectroscopic shifts in the flavin and isotope-labelling studies support cycling between oxidized and reduced states [66]. Through this blend of conformational stabilization and essential redox chemistry, FAD increases binding affinity of LSD1 for substrates and protein partners, while covalent FAD adducts formed by some inhibitors can sterically block partner binding; in the absence of FAD, LSD1 loses proper conformation and catalytic function, driving downstream functional and epigenetic changes [67].

The analysis of docking results revealed that our designed compounds have greater affinity towards LSD1 in all the three different approaches. Our highest-scoring compounds from all the three approaches have similar interactions with the pyrimidine and cyano group containing LSD1 inhibitor Pulrodemstat. The hydrogen bond with LYS661 in Pulrodemstat was also observed in Molecule 1 and Molecule 22, whereas the interacting amino acid ASP555 was similar in Molecule 4 and Pulrodemstat [14].

The affinity of our test ligands is much higher than the reference standards. When the docking was done at the site of FAD, out of the 2912 test ligands, it was decoded that the ligands with 4-acetamido substitution at the R2 position and butyl at substitution R1 position have a better affinity with the LSD1 protein than other ligands. The compounds with 4-trifluoro methyl; 3, 4 -dihydroxy and 3-OH at the R2 position, and benzyl and propyl at the R1 position also showed good binding affinity. The –OCH3, -Cl, –NO2, –F, and isopropyl substitutions at the R position enhance the binding affinity against LSD1. The top 30 ligands were screened from the result of docking at the site of FAD. Among the top 30 ligands when docked at the site of the native ligand in the presence of FAD the 3,4-dihydroxy at R2, benzyl at R1, and 2-Cl at R showed maximum affinity. While as the substitution of 2-OH at R2, benzyl at R1, and 2-OCH3 at R enhanced the binding affinity at the site of native ligand when FAD was absent.

MM-GBSA analysis

The results of the molecular docking studies can be supported using MM-GBSA. This approach allows us to accurately estimate and compare our predicted binding strengths. The top 30 test ligands with best docking scores were selected for the calculation of binding free energy (dG Bind) of the ligand and the receptor complex using MM-GBSA reported using bar graph in Fig-7. The computed binding free energy for all 30 complexes ranged from −50.90 to −99.95 kcal/mol. The MM-GBSA binding energy of Molecule 23 with LSD1 was the highest i.e., −99.95 kcal/mol among all the thirty test ligands with the best docking scores. The Molecule 1 showed the highest docking score but the binding strength was lesser during MM-GBSA analysis, nonetheless, the binding potential was good as compared to most of the other test ligands. The ligands Molecule 4 and Molecule 22 that showed highest docking score when docked at the site of the native ligand in the presence FAD and absence FAD respectively also had lesser binding potential but in contrast to the majority of the other test ligands, the binding potential was still good.

Prediction of ADME properties and toxicity analysis

On the basis of better binding affinity among the entire set of designed test ligands and the reference standards against LSD1, the thirty ligands were screened out for the estimation of ADME and toxicity analysis. Table S2 and Table S3 summarizes various physicochemical properties and the toxicity risks of the thirty screened test ligands using SwissADME and pkCSM online softwares. The use of software-based physicochemical property estimation techniques has made the drug development process simpler, quicker, and less expensive.

For a compound to be considered a potential oral drug, it should ideally meet the set guidelines of Lipinski's Rule of 5. This evaluates the drug-likeness of compounds. All of the tested ligands adhere to the criteria set by Lipinski in Lipinski's Rule of 5 except the molecular weight of the compound. All the test ligands possess fewer than 10 hydrogen bond acceptors and 5 hydrogen bond donors, with an MLogP value below 4.15, indicating suitable lipophilicity. Out of the thirty test ligands, Molecule 11 and Molecule 26 had the least molecular weight of 536.64 g/mol which is not much higher than the specified limit. Also, the molecular weight of all other analogues is less than 593 g/mol except for Molecule 3, Molecule 14 and Molecule 15 viz greater than 600 g/mol. According to the Lipinski's Rule of Five, all of the assessed ligands have a higher chance of having favourable pharmacokinetic properties and being orally bioavailable.

All of the test ligands had a bioavailability score of 0.55, which indicates that they can all enter the systemic circulation. The bioavailability score range is 0–1. The casual or interference molecules that impede assay results are known as pan-assay inference compounds (PAINS); the ADME attributes indicate that tested ligands have zero PAINS except for Molecule 4, 6 and 29, they have 1 similar alert of PAINS i.e. catechol A. Moreover, for synthetic accessibility our compounds demonstrated an average value, meaning they were neither very difficult nor very easy to synthesize. On a scale of 1 (easy to manufacture) to 10 (very difficult to make) they all have values between 4 and 5. The test ligands are poorly soluble when assessed for solubility class, but the solubility of the ligands can be altered and improved by many ways.

The results of absorption, distribution, and toxicity attributes of all the thirty test ligands are reported in Table S3. The main location where a medication is absorbed from an oral solution is the intestinal tract. The human intestinal absorption forecast the percentage of substances that were absorbed through the small intestine of humans. For all the ligands the percentage is greater than 90, suggesting that all the test ligands have excellent intestinal absorption. Another attribute, skin permeability is a key component in verifying the efficacy of drugs, which is especially important for the advancement of transdermal drug delivery. If a compound has logKp more than −2.5, it is regarded as having a moderately low skin permeability. According to Table S3, it is evident that all the thirty test ligands have skin permeability values in the range of −2.7 cm/h, which indicates that the test ligands have good skin permeability, so these molecules can also be delivered transdermally.

In addition to permeability, drug efflux mechanisms also play major role in drug metabolism, particularly the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter is the P-glycoprotein (P-gp), which acts as biological barrier by pumping out poisons and xenobiotics from cells. The results suggest that all the test ligands are P-gp substrates. The brain is shielded from outside chemicals by the blood-brain barrier (BBB). To increase the efficacy of pharmaceuticals with pharmacological action in the brain or to decrease toxicity and side effects, it is crucial to address a medication's ability to reach the brain. The logBB value for our test ligands ranges between −0.2 and −1.68 which is near to −1, suggesting that all the thirty test ligands have poor blood-brain barrier permeability as required. One other possible side effect of dermally administered products is skin sensitization. An essential safety concern is determining whether a substance that may come into touch with the skin can cause allergic contact dermatitis. All the thirty test ligands have no skin sensitization effect.

The test compounds also checked for AMES and hepatotoxicity. All the thirty test ligands are hepatotoxic when checked computationally while as the results for AMES toxicity are mixed. The AMES test is a common technique for determining a compound's mutagenic potential using bacteria. All the thirty test ligands do not have AMES toxicity except Molecules 5, 7, 8, 15, 17, 18, 23 and 25. The ADME and toxicity findings suggest that all the thirty screened ligands have positive results towards the maximum of the ADME and toxicity attributes except for the common violation of Lipinski's rule of 5 and hepatotoxicity.

Molecular dynamics simulation

MD simulation experiments were conducted for the top hits (Molecule 1, 4, and 22) from the molecular docking using three different approaches (1. Competitive docking at the site of FAD; 2. Docking at the site of native ligand in the presence of FAD and 3. Docking at the site of native ligand the absence of FAD.) in order to validate our findings from docking investigations. A 250 ns MD run was used to evaluate the stability, flexibility, and adaptability of protein-ligand complexes in the body micro-environment with respect to solvation, pressure, and temperature. This was accomplished by examining the Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Root Mean Square Fluctuations (RMSF), Radius of Gyration (rGyr), Solvent-Accessible Surface Area (SASA), and hydrogen bonds between the protein residues and the ligands.

The root mean square deviation (RMSD) is widely used to assess equilibration and structural stability of proteins during molecular dynamics simulations, particularly in the context of ligand binding. By comparing the RMSD values of the apoprotein, the free ligand, and the protein–ligand complex, one can evaluate whether the presence of the ligand stabilizes or perturbs the protein conformation over time. A stable RMSD profile for the complex, comparable to or lower than that of the apoprotein, usually indicates favourable conformational stability and proper accommodation of the ligand within the binding pocket. In contrast, significant fluctuations in RMSD may suggest induced structural rearrangements, partial destabilization, or incomplete equilibration of the system. This comparative analysis therefore provides a critical measure of the dynamic stability and reliability of the docked complex throughout the simulation trajectory [68]. Fig-8B represents the RMSD of Molecule 1 (cyan), Molecule 4 (dark blue), and Molecule 22 (purple). It depicted deviations of all three molecules over the simulation timeframe from its initial position at 0ns. The plot shows that the Molecule 1 was more stable than the other two molecules. The Molecule 4 had higher fluctuations initially up to 60 ns but further it got stabilized whereas Molecule 22 was found to be the least stable among all three ligands.

The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) measures the flexibility of individual protein residues during a molecular dynamics trajectory. It indicates how much each residue deviates from its average position over time, highlighting regions of rigidity versus mobility. Stable secondary structures, such as α-helices and β-sheets, usually show low RMSF values, while loop regions or terminal ends often display higher fluctuations. In protein-ligand complexes, residues around the binding pocket tend to exhibit decreased RMSF values compared to the unbound protein, reflecting stabilization caused by ligand interaction. This residue-level insight helps identify structural regions most influenced by binding and supports understanding of how ligands affect local protein dynamics [68].

Additionally, the compactness of the LSD1 protein was examined using the radius of gyration (rGyr) and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA). SASA quantifies the surface area of a protein that is accessible to solvent molecules, typically water. Monitoring SASA over time provides insights into folding and unfolding events, as well as the extent of protein exposure to the solvent. A decrease in SASA generally indicates protein compaction or stabilization, whereas an increase reflects expansion, unfolding, or altered exposure of hydrophobic residues. In protein–ligand complexes, a reduction in SASA around the binding site often suggests ligand-induced shielding of residues from solvent exposure, which is linked to binding stability. However, the rGyr measures the overall compactness of a protein structure by calculating the distribution of its atoms around the center of mass. A stable rGyr value during the simulation indicates a compact and stable protein conformation, while large fluctuations may suggest unfolding, conformational rearrangements, or partial destabilization. When analyzed together with SASA, rGyr provides a reliable view of global stability, with stable values confirming that the protein maintains its structural integrity in the presence of a bound ligand. The rGyr and SASA of Molecule 1, 4, and 22 represented in Fig-11A and 11B respectively. The rGyr and SASA time graph of Molecule 1-LSD1 complex showed consistent stability throughout the simulation time frame. In the case of Molecule 4, the complex attained stability of rGyr after 80 ns, whereas its SASA showed less fluctuations than Molecule 22.

(A) rGyr of the protein in complex with Molecule 1 (cyan), Molecule 4 (dark blue), and Molecule 22 (purple) and (B) SASA of the Protein-Ligand complexes Molecule 1 (cyan), Molecule 4 (dark blue)& Molecule 22 (purple).

The SASA analysis provided additional insights into the relative stability of the complexes. As shown in Fig-11b, the reference compound exhibited consistently lower SASA fluctuations compared to Molecule 22, indicating that binding of the reference compound promoted greater protein compaction and reduced solvent exposure. Such behavior is generally associated with enhanced stability of the protein-ligand complex, as reduced SASA reflects tighter packing and shielding of hydrophobic residues from solvent interactions. In contrast, Molecule 22 displayed higher SASA fluctuations, suggesting greater conformational flexibility and transient solvent accessibility, which may be indicative of weaker stabilization of the protein structure. These findings highlight the stabilizing effect of the reference compound and underscore the comparatively less compact nature of the Molecule 22 complex, thereby contributing to the overall interpretation of binding stability derived from the MD simulations.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive computational investigation (employing molecular docking, MM-GBSA calculations, molecular dynamics simulations, and ADMET profiling) into the potential of chalcone-cyanopyrimidine hybrids as LSD1 inhibitors, with a particular focus on the cofactor FAD's role in modulating binding affinity. The docking and MM-GBSA calculations confirmed the favourable binding affinities and binding energies of the complex, suggesting its potential for high-affinity interactions with LSD1 and the ADMET predictions also provided positive insights into the drug-like properties of the designed analogues. The findings reveal that the Molecule 4-LSD1 complex that has the presence of FAD showed the maximum RMSD stability and the lesser RMSF values than the other two complexes. The molecular docking interactions showed by the complex were also visualized in MD simulation and it also displayed almost all the interactions that were shown by the native ligand of LSD1 in PDB ID 6TUY. These findings also contribute valuable insights into the epigenetic modulation mechanisms relevant to cancer therapy. To translate these computational predictions into therapeutic advances, future work prioritizes the synthesis of top-performing candidates, followed by in-vitro enzymatic inhibition assays, cellular assays to examine epigenetic activity, toxicity profiling and in-vivo evaluations to determine pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and therapeutic efficacy.
