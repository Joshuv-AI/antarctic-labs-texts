Community Assembly Mechanisms Underlying Divergent Responses of Indica and Japonica Rice Rhizosphere Microbiota to Drought Stress
Authors: Sihan Zhou, Junguo Bi, Sheng Zhou, Lijun Luo, Xiaoyuan Yan, Jianwen Zou, Yanhan Ji, Shuai Zhao, Jingrui Qiu, Zhaojing Liu, Jiandong Jiang, Baozhan Wang, Xu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Drought stress markedly reduces rice yield, with notable genotypic variation in drought tolerance. While the rhizosphere microbiome is regarded as the second genome of plants, how the indica and japonica rice rhizosphere microbial communities respond to deficit irrigation and their relationship with yield remain to be elucidated. Here, we conducted field experiments using 12 indica and 12 japonica rice varieties under full and deficit irrigation regimes. Yield-related traits, including filled grain number, seed setting rate, two-plant yield, and thousand grain weight, were measured, and the rhizosphere microbial communities were characterized by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. In line with previous studies, japonica varieties showed superior drought resistance in terms of yield performance. Both rice genotype and irrigation regime significantly influenced the composition and functional potential of the rhizosphere microbiome. Compared to indica rice, the japonica rice rhizosphere was enriched with more beneficial microorganisms. Enrichment of nitrogen‑metabolism‑related groups, such as Microvirga and Nitrososphaeraceae, may contribute to rhizosphere nitrogen cycling and support nitrogen availability for the rice. Similarly, higher abundance of Streptomyces in japonica varieties under drought conditions may be associated with improved drought tolerance. These microbial genera were closely associated with rice yield. Moreover, the japonica rhizosphere microbiome was less disturbed by water limitation, showing higher stability. Overall, the rhizosphere microbiome of japonica rice exhibited functional optimization under drought stress by promoting the enrichment of beneficial and nitrogen-cycling microbes, thereby enhancing drought resistance and yield stability. This study demonstrated a significant correlation between rhizosphere microbial communities and rice yield, providing fundamental insights that may contribute to future strategies for optimizing crop productivity through microbiome management in sustainable agriculture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-025-02647-5.

Introduction

Water availability is one of the most significant environmental factors limiting agricultural productivity [1]. Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a fundamental staple food worldwide, constituting the primary nutritional foundation for nearly half of the global population [2]. Due in part to its semi-aquatic growth habit and its small root system [3], rice is particularly vulnerable to drought stress, which typically results in an average yield reduction of up to 25% [4]. Asian cultivated rice encompasses two distinct subspecies, indica and japonica, which differ in genetics, phenotype, and ecological adaptability [5]. The differences among them are not only reflected in their external morphology and growth habits, but also affect their production performance under different environmental conditions [6, 7]. For instance, japonica cultivars carrying the OsERF103 haplotype (HAP) 2 allele have demonstrated greater drought resilience compared to indica cultivars carrying the HAP1 allele [8]. Plant–microbe interactions have been increasingly recognized as a critical factor in enhancing plant resilience to environmental stresses [9]. Further elucidating how the microbial communities assist in plant stress resistance will pave the way for improving crop growth and sustainable agricultural practices.

There is increasing evidence that the rhizosphere microbiome, recognized as the second genome of plants, plays crucial roles in promoting plant growth and protecting plants against biotic and abiotic stresses [10–12]. In turn, rhizosphere microbiomes are shaped by a complex interplay between environmental factors and host plant influences [13]. Plants actively recruit and regulate specific microbial communities through root exudates to optimize their adaptability under various stress conditions [14–16]. As drought episodes become more frequent and prolonged worldwide [17], it is of paramount importance to gain detailed knowledge of the consequences of water limitation on plant-associated microbiomes, particularly in drought-sensitive crops such as rice. Carbon and nitrogen serve dual roles as vital nutrients for plant development and as fundamental components of greenhouse gases influencing global climate dynamics. Thus, carbon and nitrogen cycling forms the centerpiece of agroecosystems [18]. Microbial communities are central to these biogeochemical processes, catalyzing critical transformations including methanogenesis, methane oxidation, nitrogen fixation, nitrification, and denitrification [19–21]. Studies have shown that indica and japonica rice grown in the field recruit distinct root microbiota, with indica-enriched bacterial taxa contain more genera with nitrogen metabolism functions than japonica-enriched taxa [22]. However, little is known about the variation and functional roles in regulating yield under drought stress in rhizosphere microbiota in rice populations.

In this study, we investigated whether variations in rhizosphere microbiota in response to different irrigation regimes are associated with corresponding differences in rice yield. To this end, 12 indica and 12 japonica rice varieties were cultivated under field conditions with both deficit and full irrigation treatments. The objectives of this study were to: (1) characterize the structural and functional shifts in rhizosphere microbiota associated with indica and japonica rice varieties under full and deficit irrigation conditions; (2) elucidate the community assembly processes and co-occurrence patterns of these microbial communities; and (3) identify key rhizosphere microbial taxa correlated with rice yield. We hypothesized that deficit irrigation will alter the composition, diversity, and functional potential of rhizosphere microbiota in a genotype-dependent manner, leading to distinct community assembly processes between indica and japonica varieties, and that specific microbial taxa will be positively associated with rice yield under water-limited conditions. The present work would provide insight into how the rhizosphere microbiota regulates crop yield in response to water deficit and guidance for water-saving rice cultivation in sustainable agriculture.

Material and Methods

Field Design and Sampling

The field experiment was conducted at the Zhuanghang Comprehensive Experimental Station of the Shanghai Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Shanghai, China (36° 53’N, 121° 23’E). Four treatments were established: (1) indica planted with deficit irrigation management (ID), (2) indica planted with full irrigation management (IF), (3) japonica planted with deficit irrigation management (JD), (4) japonica planted with full irrigation management (JF), representing different rice subspecies and two water conditions (Fig. 1, Fig. S1). The indica and japonica rice varieties were collected from Yangtze River Basin and were representative of the diversity of rice in China. The basic strategy for both irrigation treatments was alternate wetting and drying (AWD). Full irrigation maintained the soil water potential between −30 kPa and 0 kPa throughout the entire rice growth period (Fig. 1a). Each irrigation event accumulated a 3–5 cm water layer on the soil surface. For the deficit irrigation management, drought stress was applied by halting irrigation after the critical stage of productive tillering, with the lowest soil water potential during this stage dropping below −500 kPa (Fig. 1b). Except for this specific period, AWD was used to maintain the soil water potential in the field between −35 kPa and 0 kPa. Each treatment was replicated in three plots (81 rice plants/plot). After maturity, rice yields were calculated. A total of 288 rice soil samples (24 varieties [12 indica + 12 japonica] × 2 irrigation treatments [full and deficit] × 2 soil types [bulk and rhizosphere] × 3 replicate plots) were collected from the four fields, encompassing 12 varieties each of indica and japonica under both full and deficit irrigation treatments. Bulk soil samples were composed of five soil cores at a depth of 0–20 cm randomly collected from each plot. Rhizosphere soil samples were carefully separated from rice root surfaces from three replicate plots. All samples were stored with ice packs, transported to the laboratory within 12 h, and stored at 4 ℃ and −80 ℃ for further processing.

DNA Extraction, PCR Amplification, Sequencing and Bioinformatic Analysis

Total microbial DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of rhizosphere and bulk soils using the FastDNA™ Soil DNA Extraction Kit (MP Biomedicals, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The V4 region of the prokaryotic 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primer pairs 515 F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3’) and 806R (5'-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3'). The PCR reactions contained 4 μL 5 × FastPfu buffer, 2 μL dNTP (2.5 mM), 10 ng DNA, 0.8 μL forward and reverse primers (5 μM), and 0.4 μL FastPfu polymerase. Each sample was amplified in triplicate. After an initial denaturation step at 94 ℃ for 3 min, the targeted region was amplified by 25 cycles of 95 ℃ for 30 s, 55 ℃ for 30 s, 72 ℃ for 60 s, followed by a final elongation step of 5 min at 72 ℃. Then the triplicate PCR products were mixed and purified using an AMPure XP Kit (Beckman Coulter, USA). The purified PCR products were measured by Qubit (Thermofisher, Ireland). Sequencing of purified amplicons was performed on an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) by Majorbio BioPharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Raw 16S rRNA gene sequences were processed using fastp v0.20.0 [23] and FLASH v1.2.7 [24] for demultiplexing, filtering, and merging paired-end reads. The Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2 (DADA2) v1.14 plugin was employed to remove chimeras, and construct amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using QIIME2 v2024.5 [25, 26]. Taxonomic assignment of the representative sequences was performed with a naïve Bayesian classifier using the SILVA v132 database [27]. Finally, to minimize biases associated with sequencing coverage and allow for comparison of community pattern among all samples, the ASV abundance tables were rarefied to 36,689 sequences per sample. Rarefaction analysis was performed using the ‘mecodev’ R package [28].

Microbial Community Composition and Function

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) were used to determine the dissimilarity of samples to each other based on Bray–Curtis distance, conducted with the ‘vegan’ package in R v4.4.0 [29]. The Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was used to detect the major bacteria/archaea groups contributing to the dissimilarities observed in microbial communities between four groups. A significance threshold of alpha parameter of 0.01 for the Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon tests, and a LDA effect size threshold of 3.0 were used to evaluate the biomarkers of all groups. Functional Annotation tool of Prokaryotic Taxa (FAPROTAX) database v1.2.7 [30] was employed for prediction of metabolic functions in rhizosphere prokaryotic communities.

Microbial Co-Occurrence Network Construction and Characterization

Co-occurrence networks of prokaryotic communities in ID, IF, JD, and JF fields were constructed to explore microbial interactions and community structures. These networks (involving taxa with relative abundance exceeding 1% at the ASV level) were constructed based on Spearman’s correlation coefficients (|ρ|> 0.65, P < 0.05) using the ‘igraph’ package in R v4.4.0 [31–33]. A comprehensive characterization of the networks was performed by calculating a variety of topological properties, including the number of nodes, the number of positive and negative links, average degree, average weighted degree, average path length, network diameter, network density, clustering coefficient, betweenness centralization, and modularity.

Keystone taxa, which play a crucial role in the ecological functioning of the networks, were identified using Z- and P-scores. Taxa were categorized into four groups: peripherals (Zi < 2.5, Pi < 0.62), connectors (Zi < 2.5, Pi ≥ 0.62), network hubs (Zi ≥ 2.5, Pi ≥ 0.62), and module hubs (Zi ≥ 2.5, Pi < 0.62) as described by Banerjee et al. [34]. To further clarify microbial interactions, cohesions were assessed as indicators of overall network connectivity and microbial relationships, in alignment with the methods proposed by Herren and McMahon [35].

Microbial Community Assembly

To evaluate the dominance of deterministic versus stochastic processes in community assembly, we employed the neutral community model (NCM) and phylogenetic bin-based null model analysis (iCAMP). These methods were implemented using the ‘Hmisc’, ‘stats4’, ‘iCAMP’ packages in R v4.4.0 to predict the relationship between the detection frequency of ASVs and their relative abundance [36].

In the NCM, the parameter Nm serves as an estimate of dispersal between communities, influencing the correlation between occurrence frequency and regional relative abundance. The parameter R2 represents the overall fit to the neutral model. Calculation of 95% confidence intervals around all fitting statistics was done by bootstrapping with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The ASVs from each group were subsequently categorized into three partitions based on their occurrence relative to the 95% confidence interval of the NCM predictions: above partition (occurring more frequently than predicted), below partition (occurring less frequently than predicted), and neutral partition (falling within the predicted confidence interval).

iCAMP classifies observed taxa into distinct phylogenetic groups (bins) and determines the assembly processes driving turnover within each bin [36]. For each bin, the proportion of pairwise turnovers exhibiting significantly higher (βNRIbin > 1.96) or lower (βNRIbin < −1.96) phylogenetic dissimilarity (measured by β mean pairwise distance) than the null model expectation indicates the impact of heterogeneous or homogeneous selection, respectively. The proportion of turnovers with non-significant phylogenetic dissimilarity (− 2 ≤ βNRIbin ≤ 2) but significantly higher (RCbin > 0.95) or lower (RCbin < − 0.95) taxonomic dissimilarity (Bray–Curtis) than the null model expectation reflects the influence of dispersal limitation or homogenizing dispersal, respectively. The remaining turnovers (− 2 ≤ βNRIbin ≤ 2 and − 0.95 ≤ RCbin ≤ 0.95) are attributed to the influence of drift.

Relationships Between Prokaryotic Taxa and Rice Yields

To identify rhizosphere microorganisms closely associated with rice yields, a Mantel test based on Pearson correlation coefficients was employed to evaluate the relationships between key microbial genera and four yield-related indicators: two-plant yield (TPY), seed setting rate (SSR), filled grain number (FG), and thousand grain weight (TGW). Mantel’s test was performed by ‘linkET’ package in R v4.4.0 [37].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out in R v4.4.0 [38]. The statistical parameter used in this study was mean ± SD (standard deviation). In figures and tables, the asterisks indicate the statistical significance (***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05, and NS, not significant) based on the Kruskal–Wallis test and/or Wilcoxon rank sum test.

Results

Deficit Irrigation Reduced Indica and Japonica Yield

Four rice yield indicators – two-plant yield (TPY), seed setting rate (SSR), filled grain number (FG), and thousand grain weight (TGW) – were statistically analyzed for 12 indica and 12 japonica varieties grown under both full and deficit irrigation conditions (Fig. 1c-f). The results showed that rice yields, with the exception of SSR, were significantly higher under full irrigation compared to deficit irrigation (P < 0.05) for both indica and japonica varieties. Specifically, the SSR of indica was lower under deficit irrigation (P < 0.05), while the SSR of japonica rice showed no significant difference between the two irrigation treatments.

Moreover, with the exception of TGW under full irrigation, there were significant differences in SSR and TGW between the two rice subspecies (P < 0.05; Fig. 1d and f). The SSR of japonica rice was consistently higher than that of indica rice, both under full irrigation (95.33% vs. 86.00%) and deficit irrigation (87.75% vs. 69.58%) (P < 0.05). The TGW of indica and japonica rice was similar under full irrigation, with values of approximately 25.65 g and 27.44 g, respectively. However, under deficit irrigation, japonica exhibited a significantly greater TGW (23.91 g) compared to indica (18.46 g) (P < 0.05; Fig. 1f). No significant differences in TPY and FG were observed between the two subspecies under either irrigation treatment (Fig. 1c and e). These findings underscore the detrimental impact of water limitation on rice yield, and demonstrate that the response to drought stress is largely determined by plant genotype.

Distinct Rhizosphere Prokaryotic Communities Shaped by Water Conditions and Rice Subspecies

Then we investigated the variations in the rhizosphere microbiota of rice under full irrigation and deficit irrigation conditions. A total of 9,886,093 high-quality sequences were obtained from 144 rhizosphere soil samples (average: 68,653; range: 41,128–103,752 reads per sample), resulting in 96,976 ASVs after rarefaction. Rarefaction analysis revealed that our population captured adequate rhizosphere microbiota members from each group (Fig. S2).

The Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) and PERMANOVA analysis based on the Bray–Curtis distance revealed that irrigation treatment had the strongest influence on soil prokaryotic communities (bacteria and archaea) (R2 = 0.360, P < 0.001), followed by rice subspecies (R2 = 0.016, P < 0.001) and soil compartments (R2 = 0.008, P = 0.008; Fig. S3, Table S1). These analyses also revealed four distinct clusters of rhizosphere microbiomes corresponding to the different groups (R2 = 0.417; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2a; Table S1). No significant differences were found in the Observed Species, Shannon and Phylogenetic Diversity (PD) indices between the indica under deficit irrigation (ID) and full irrigation (IF) fields (P > 0.05; Fig. S4). Significant differences in the rhizosphere microbiota between different irrigation levels and rice subspecies were detectable at the phylum level (P < 0.05; Fig. 2d, Table S2). The most abundant bacterial and archaeal phyla were Chloroflexota (19.49%), Acidobacteriota (12.02%), Pseudomonadota (9.05%), Bacillota (6.75%), and Thermoproteota (6.08%). Other notable phyla included Actinomycetota (5.50%), Bacteroidota (5.44%), Thermodesulfobacteriota (3.99%), and Verrucomicrobiota (3.50%) (Fig. 2d). Drought stress increased the relative abundance of Actinomycetota and Bacillota from 4.33% to 6.68% and 5.47% to 8.20%, respectively. Conversely, Chloroflexota exhibited significantly higher relative abundance under full irrigation conditions compared to deficit irrigation (P < 0.05; Table S2). While water management had a greater effect on prokaryotic composition than rice subspecies, the rice variety also significantly affected the relative abundance of certain phyla. For example, Thermoproteota and Bacillota were significantly enriched in the rhizosphere of japonica compared to indica under both irrigation treatments (P < 0.05; Table S2).

To further elucidate the effects of water conditions and rice varieties on the prokaryotic community at the genus level, we performed LEfSe analysis (LDA score > 3.0, P < 0.01), identifying 30 prokaryotic genera with significant differences among four groups (Fig. 2e). A higher number of genera were enriched in JF, with 15 genera, compared to ID (4 genera), IF (3 genera), and JD (8 genera) rhizosphere samples. In deficit irrigation fields, indica rice rhizosphere was specifically enriched with Sphingomonas, MND1, Vicinamibacteraceae, and S0134 terrestrial group, whereas japonica rice rhizosphere showed enrichment of Bacillus, Nitrososphaeraceae, Fictibacillus, Microvirga, Steroidobacter, Pontibacter, Streptomyces, and RB41.

Different Responses of Rhizosphere Prokaryotic Co-Occurrence Networks of Indica and Japonica Under Deficit Irrigation

Co-occurrence networks were conducted to reveal the changes in inter-relationships among rhizosphere prokaryotic community members under different treatments. Compared to full irrigation fields, the positive associations in the prokaryotic taxonomic networks of japonica and indica under deficit irrigation decreased by 54.96% and 93.12%, respectively. Negative associations also decreased by 78.15% in japonica and 98.57% in indica (Fig. 3a, Table S3). Additionally, the size of networks, reflected by the total number of nodes, was larger in full irrigation treatments, as were other network metrics such as network connectivity (total number of links), average degree, average weighted degree and network density. The keystone taxa, identified as module hubs and connectors within the community network, played critical roles in maintaining network linkages. A total of 40, 16, 6, and 4 keystone taxa were identified in JF, IF, JD, ID networks, respectively, based on their links to other microbes within modules (Zi) and their links to other microbes in other modules (Pi) (Fig. 3b). The number of prokaryotic module hubs and connectors was greater in full irrigation networks than in deficit irrigation networks for both rice varieties. In the ID network, all four keystone taxa were classified as Acidobacteriota. Other networks also contained several keystones from this phylum, though with more diverse taxa, especially in the JF network (Fig. 3c). The positive cohesion, absolute value of negative cohesion, and total cohesion (sum of positive cohesion and the absolute value of negative cohesion) showed similar trends. In all networks, the cohesion of microbiomes in well-watered fields was significantly higher than in water-limited fields (P < 0.05; Fig. 3d).

While rhizosphere microbial networks in indica and japonica responded similarly to water reduction, the magnitude of the response differed notably. For instance, the topological parameters of the full irrigation indica microbial network, including the number of nodes and edges, average degree, and network density, were higher than those of the full irrigation japonica network. However, under deficit irrigation, the properties of microbial network exhibited completely opposite patterns, with higher values in japonica rhizosphere prokaryotic co-occurrence networks. Furthermore, the changes in positive and negative cohesion caused by different irrigation treatments were more pronounced in the indica microbial networks than in japonica networks. These results revealed that drought stress significantly disrupted the associations between prokaryotic microorganisms in the rice rhizosphere, with the impact being less severe in japonica than in indica rice.

Diverse Rhizosphere Prokaryotes Drove Differential Elemental Cycling Processes

To further explore microbial activity of elemental cycling related to nutrient absorption in rice, biogeochemical cycles-specially carbon and nitrogen cycling-were functionally annotated and predicted for four sample sets. The functional annotations revealed significant differences in potential carbon and nitrogen metabolism driven by rhizosphere microorganisms in indica and japonica under the two irrigation treatments (P < 0.05; Fig. S5, Table S5).

As water content decreased, there was a marked reduction in the number of ASVs associated with carbon cycling, whereas ASVs related to the nitrogen cycle significantly increased (P < 0.05; Fig. S5a and b). Specifically, the predicted functional profiles suggested that processes such as methanotrophy, methanogenesis, and hydrocarbon degradation might be more active in the rice rhizosphere under full irrigation compared to deficit irrigation. The potential for methane generation and oxidation was predicted to be highest in the JF group.

In contrast, nitrogen metabolism was more active under deficit irrigation than under full irrigation conditions (P < 0.05). Not surprisingly, rice subspecies were also one of the factors affecting the nitrogen cycling function of microbial communities. Nitrogen cycling processes such as aerobic ammonia oxidation, nitrification, and nitrate reduction were predicted to be more pronounced in japonica than in indica under deficit irrigation (P < 0.05). Although prokaryotic microorganisms related to nitrate ammonification, nitrification, and nitrite ammonification were more abundant in the rhizosphere of indica than in japonica under full irrigation management, albeit not significantly (P > 0.05; Fig. S5c). Additionally, functional prediction suggested that japonica may have more active nitrogen fixation in both well-watered and water-limited fields.

To gain deeper insight into the community assembly of rhizosphere prokaryotic microbes, we employed the iCAMP approach to analyze the assembly process based on phylogenetic data. Our analysis revealed that community assembly across all four experimental groups was predominantly governed by stochastic processes (Fig. 4e). Among these processes, drift and others (DR) emerged as the dominant factor, accounting for more than 45% of the relative importance in shaping the overall prokaryotic community. The contributions of the three stochastic components (homogenizing dispersal, dispersal limitation, and drift and others) remained relatively stable between full and deficit irrigation treatments in the japonica rhizosphere. However, these components showed marked changes in response to drought stress in the indica rhizosphere. Moreover, homogeneous selection occupied the largest proportion of deterministic processes. Drought stress significantly increased the importance of homogeneous selection in shaping the japonica rhizosphere community (P < 0.05), whereas the opposite effect was observed in indica. Thus, water-limitation heightened the influence of stochasticity in shaping the rhizosphere prokaryotic communities of indica, while simultaneously amplifying the role of determinism in japonica.

Relationships Between Rice Yields and Prokaryotic Community

Discussion

Effect of Irrigation Volume and Host Genotype on Rice Yield Variability

Our findings indicated that under the AWD model, deficit irrigation led to a significant reduction in rice yield, and this trend was consistent across both tested subspecies (Fig. 1c-f). This is in line with previous reports showing that reduced water availability can markedly constrain crop productivity [39]. Such yield declines were likely related to drought stress induced changes in plant physiological processes, including reduced photosynthetic and respiration rates, as well as increased impaired nutrient uptake [4]. These findings highlighted the potential trade-offs between water savings and yield stability.

Rice genotypes displayed different yield responses to drought stress (Fig. 1c-f). The prevailing consensus is that indica rice, having been long cultivated in rainfed tropical and subtropical regions, has undergone stronger drought-selection pressure and thereby developed greater drought adaptability than japonica rice [40–42]. In contrast, our results showed that under comparable drought conditions, japonica rice exhibited a smaller yield reduction, particularly displaying lower sensitivity in SSR and TGW (Fig. 1d and f). This finding is consistent with Yang et al. [43], who reported yield declines of 13.0% for the japonica cultivar IRAT109 under drought stress, versus much greater reductions of 30.2% and 18.2% for indica cultivars Huanghuazhan and Hanyou73, respectively. The superior drought performance of japonica rice in these specific contexts may be related to its more extensive root system architecture and higher water-use efficiency, suggesting that the conventional paradigm favoring indica drought tolerance may not hold universally. Supporting evidence from earlier studies indicates that japonica rice maintains a mean SSR 4.6% higher than that of indica rice, a difference potentially attributable to distinct genomic architectures and physiological adaptations [44, 45]. These findings point to a remarkable drought tolerance mechanism in japonica varieties, which enables them to better maintain grain size and quality under water-limited conditions. Therefore, japonica cultivation emerges as a promising and economically viable strategy for agricultural regions experiencing water scarcity, offering an optimal balance between crop yield and resource consumption.

Distinct Rhizosphere Microbiome Assembly and Functional Adaptation Shaped by Irrigation Volume and Host Genotype Differentiation

In this study, we provided a detailed characterization of how water scarcity and host genotype influence the rice rhizosphere prokaryotic microbiome. It was showed that the host genotype and water availability significantly affect the direction and magnitude of compositional changes in bacterial and archaeal communities (Fig. 2, Table S1 and S2). Drought is widely recognized as one of the most impactful abiotic stresses on soil biota [46]. Specially, we showed that water limitation led to significant restructuring of the microbiome, with changes being also controlled by the host genotype. Previous studies on rice and other plant species have shown that different plant species or genotypes can select for distinct soil communities [22, 47]. These differential compositions were attributed to increased soil osmotic stress and heterogeneity, limitations in nutrient flow and acquisition, and differences in root exudates [21].

In this study, the decrease in water availability resulted in an increase in the relative abundance of Actinomycetota and Bacillota. According to reports, these bacteria typically exhibit high stress resistance, especially to drought [48]. Evidence suggests that rhizosphere microbiota, including Sphingomonas, Vicinamibacteraceae, Bacillus, Fictibacillus, Microvirga, Steroidobacter, Pontibacter, Streptomyces, have been observed to participate in carbon and nitrogen metabolism in soil, forming close associations with plants to provide host resilience to drought stress and promote growth [49–53]. Streptomyces spp. forms extensive networks covering plant roots and occupying ecological niches, much like the mycelium-like structures of fungi that envelop root systems, thus enhancing plant resistance to various environmental stresses [54, 55]. The enrichment of these genera may be beneficial for stabilizing rice yield under drought stress (Fig. 2e). Therefore, plant roots might selectively recruit microbes with the ability to resist stress and promote nutrient absorption under drought stress, enhancing plant resistance to adversity and compensating for the insufficient efficiency of root nutrient absorption.

Soil functioning depends not only on the composition of diverse microbial community members but also on the associations among co-existing members of microbial communities [54, 55]. Various research findings suggest a strong correlation between the topological properties of ecological networks and the mechanisms by which communities adapt to environmental fluctuations [56–58]. The positive and negative associations reflecting cooperative behaviors (such as cross feeding, symbiosis, mutual benefit), and competitive behaviors (such as limited resource competition) in the prokaryotic taxa networks significantly decreased with the reduction of irrigation water [59]. As ecological modeling predicts that negative associations promote stability [60], the higher percentage of negative associations observed in the rhizosphere prokaryotic network of full irrigated rice implied a more stable community. The comprehensive comparison results based on network topological properties indicated that environmental stress reduced the complexity and stability of microbial networks in both indica and japonica rice [48, 61]. A higher number of keystone taxa generally indicates a more stable network [48]. The fewer keystone taxa and lower cohesion values (positive cohesion, the absolute value of negative cohesion) also suggested that water scarcity could generally reduce the complexity of the rhizosphere microbial network in rice and disrupt its stability [48, 61]. These findings align with research conducted in habitats with four aridity levels, where the complexity and stability of the networks exhibited a significant decline with increasing aridity [59]. Collectively, drought stress reduced the complexity and stability of co‑occurrence networks in rice by weakening associations among rhizosphere prokaryotic microorganisms, which could potentially make the network more vulnerable to subsequent environmental perturbations.

Rice genotype mainly affected the response amplitude of prokaryotic taxa to water limitation. Given the close relationship between microbial community construction and network hubs, module hubs, and connectors [62], these keystones may play disproportionally important roles in the different responses of rhizosphere prokaryotes in indica and japonica to drought stress. Compared to the rhizosphere microbial network of indica rice, more diverse and abundant network keystones in the rhizosphere microbial network of japonica rice under deficit irrigation conditions may explain why the rhizosphere microbial community of japonica rice is less affected by environmental stress than that of indica rice (Fig. 3c). Of these, the majority, including Nitrososphaeraceae, SBR1031, Flavisolibacter, Sphingomonas, RBG-16–58-14, Pontibacter (Table S4), have been proven to participate in carbon and nitrogen metabolism and play key roles in drought stress resistance and plant growth [19, 49, 53, 63]. Therefore, the rhizosphere microorganisms of japonica formed a network containing diverse keystone taxa with high richness, in response to drought stress.

It is widely recognized that microbial community assembly is simultaneously influenced by both deterministic and stochastic factors, with varying degrees of importance in governing community assembly [64]. Our results showed that the stochastic balance between the loss and gain of rice rhizosphere prokaryotic microbes was a key factor in determining their community assembly regardless of water conditions and rice subspecies (Fig. 4a-d), consistent with previous findings [65]. Wahdan et al. [66] reported that microbial communities governed by stochastic processes may exhibit a higher level of network complexity and stability, which strongly aligns with our results for the japonica rhizosphere prokaryotic community. Deficit irrigation led to a significant change in the importance of homogeneous selection [67]. Moreover, the direction of changes in the assembly process of prokaryotic communities mediated by changes in water availability depended on rice subspecies. That is to say, water limitation increased the relative contribution of drift and homogenizing dispersal, thereby intensifying the impact of stochasticity on the assembly of indica rice rhizosphere prokaryotic communities, and increased the relative contribution of homogeneous selection, thereby amplifying the role of determinism in shaping japonica rice rhizosphere prokaryotic communities.

Generally, species diversity supports various ecological functions, and high taxonomic diversity enhances functional diversity, leading to better ecosystem performance [68]. The change in microbial community structure inevitably leads to differential changes in community function. Methane emissions from rice cultivation are estimated to account for 42% of China’s agricultural CH4 emissions, with key influencing factors being cultivation management and soil properties [20]. According to taxonomic-based functional predictions, deficit irrigation was associated with lower relative abundance of taxa linked to methanotrophy- and methanogenesis-related functions in rice rhizosphere soil (Fig. S5, Table S5), which could indicate a lower potential for CH₄ production. Therefore, implementing appropriate deficit irrigation combined with short‑term drought stress at specific growth stages in rice cultivation not only conserves water to address global scarcity but also potentially reduce methane emissions, thereby mitigating environmental degradation. Additionally, previous work has predicted that nitrogen transformation processes, including nitrate ammonification, nitrate denitrification, nitrate reduction, nitrification, and nitrite ammonification, are more prevalent in the root environment of indica than japonica varieties in flooded soils [22]. In contrast, our functional predictions did not fully reproduce these differences between varietal groups. This discrepancy may reflect differences in experimental conditions, such as water management regimes, soil physicochemical properties, or spatial variation, compared with those in previous work. Instead, we observed that predicted nitrogen fixation functions were relatively more enriched in japonica rhizospheres under both full and deficit irrigation (Fig. S5, Table S5). This pattern could be related to the reported lower nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) of japonica and its consequently higher demand for nitrogen sources to support metabolic activities [44]. This contrast between literature-based predictions and our field observations suggested that nitrogen functional dynamics in rice rhizospheres may be influenced not only by variety type, but also by irrigation regime and local soil-microbe interactions. Compared to indica rice, the higher predicted potential for aerobic ammonia oxidation and nitrification in the rhizosphere soil of japonica rice under deficit irrigation may be attributed to the significant enrichment of Nitrososphaeraceae (Fig. 2e, Fig. S5, Table S5), an important diver of nitrification in acidic soils, which can facilitate nitrogen availability and promote plant nutrient uptake [63]. Although deficit irrigation reduced the overall prokaryotic diversity in the japonica rhizosphere, these results suggest that key functional groups were selectively enriched, providing critical ecosystem functions despite lower taxonomic diversity. Such functional redundancy, where specific taxa disproportionately contribute to essential biogeochemical processes, may buffer the impacts of diversity loss on ecosystem performance. These findings indicate that deficit irrigation may lower the potential for CH₄ production in paddy soils while enhancing nitrogen cycling potential, especially in japonica rice. Consequently, the smaller yield reduction observed in japonica compared to indica under deficit irrigation might be related to higher predicted functional potential of microbes such as Microvirga and Nitrososphaeraceae, which could improve nitrogen availability and uptake.

Potential Relationship Between Rhizosphere Prokaryotic Communities and Rice Yields Under the Influence of Irrigation and Host Genotype

Previous studies have shown that certain soil microorganisms can be harnessed to promote plant growth in the rhizosphere and are referred to as plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR). PGPR not only promote plant growth by increasing nutrient availability and production of plant hormones and siderophores but also enhance yield and alleviate abiotic stresses such as water-deficit and salinity in plants [69]. The results of Mantel test indicated strong correlations between beneficial rhizosphere microbiota and rice productivity (Fig. 5). For instance, MND1, belonging to the family Nitrosomonadaceae, is an ammonia-oxidizing bacterium that plays an important role in the nitrogen cycling through nitrification and nitrogen fixation in agricultural soils [70]. There were significant correlations between MND1 and the three yield indicators of FG, TPY, and TGW of indica and FG, TPY of japonica (P < 0.01; Fig. 5). Evidence suggests that Sphingomonas, Microvirga, Streptomyces and Luteimonas are drought-tolerant bacterial genera in soils, which form close associations with plants to provide host resilience to drought stress and possess multiple plant-growth-promoting traits [50, 51, 71]. Streptomyces species have been demonstrated to produce an array of osmolytes, polysaccharides, enzymes and plant hormones that facilitate osmotic adjustment in plant host under drought stress [72]. Moreover, numerous motile Bacillus species exhibit a dual beneficial role: they not only confer direct advantages to plants but also utilize their flagella to transport Streptomyces spores throughout the rhizosphere, thereby enhancing Streptomyces colonization and plant–microbe interactions [73]. The relative abundance of these two genera exhibited significant correlations with FG, TPY, and TGW in indica rice, suggesting that their combination may be more conducive to supporting the growth and productivity of indica rice under water-limited conditions. The beneficial genera Microvirga and Nitrososphaeraceae are nitrogen-fixing bacterium and nitrifying archaea, respectively [63, 71]. They were enriched in the rhizosphere of japonica rice and potentially enhanced nitrogen availability in the soil, exhibited significant correlations with yields. This further revealed the mechanism by which japonica rice recruited beneficial prokaryotic microorganisms in its root to help stabilize yield under environmental stress.

In summary, our data showed that under drought stress, japonica rice roots actively recruit a rich assemblage of microorganisms to aid in drought tolerance and mitigate yield loss. This provides theoretical support for the application of prokaryotic microorganisms in maintaining crop productivity under water-limited conditions. In the rhizosphere soil, prokaryotic microorganisms that interact with rice form a complex network, the assembly and stability of which are strongly influenced by environmental factors and host plant genotype. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the interactions of rhizosphere microbes under different irrigation regimes to assess their impact on rice production and to examine the influence of rice genotypes. It was observed that, compared to indica rice, japonica rice exhibited a smaller reduction in yield under water-limited conditions, which was most likely related to, and potentially influenced by, the distinct compositions and functions of its rhizosphere microbiome. Under drought stress, japonica roots were associated with an increased relative abundance of nitrogen-fixing taxa (e.g., Microvirga) and nitrifying taxa (e.g., Nitrososphaeraceae) in the surrounding soil. This shift was predicted by taxonomic-based functional analysis to potentially influence nitrogen metabolism in the rhizosphere. In addition, PGPR (e.g., Streptomyces, Microvirga) were more prevalent in the japonica rhizosphere under water-limited conditions, which may be related to stable yields.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the yield responses and rhizosphere microbiome variation under drought stress conditions in two rice subspecies, indica and japonica. Water limitation resulted in decreased productivity, with japonica exhibiting superior drought resistance compared to indica, as evidenced by its less pronounced yield reduction under water-limited conditions. The combined effects of irrigation management and rice subspecies induced significant shifts in the rhizosphere prokaryotic communities. The co-occurrence networks and assembly patterns of rhizosphere microorganisms in japonica rice demonstrated lower sensitivity to deficit irrigation compared to those in indica rice. The enrichment of beneficial microorganisms, including MND1, Microvirga, Streptomyces, and Nitrososphaeraceae, in the japonica rhizosphere appeared to potentially accelerate soil nitrogen cycling processes, contributing substantially to rice yield stability under drought stress. These findings underscore the mechanisms by which rhizosphere microbial communities respond to deficit irrigation and contribute to yield stability in rice. This knowledge provides a valuable foundation for developing targeted and sustainable strategies to enhance crop resilience and yield stability in the face of increasing climate variability and water scarcity. Future research should explore the transferability of these findings to other rice varieties and cropping systems, and investigate the potential for microbiome engineering to enhance drought resilience in crops.
