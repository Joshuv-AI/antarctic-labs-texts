Transfer entropy for finite data
Alec Kirkley

Abstract
Transfer entropy is a widely used measure for quantifying directed information flows in complex systems. While the challenges of estimating transfer entropy for continuous data are well known, it has two major shortcomings that persist even for data of finite cardinality: it exhibits a substantial positive bias for sparse bin counts, and it has no clear means to assess statistical significance. By more precisely accounting for information content in finite data streams, we derive a transfer entropy measure which is asymptotically equivalent to the standard plug-in estimator but remedies these issues for time series of small size and/or high cardinality, permitting a fully nonparametric assessment of statistical significance without simulation. We show that this correction for finite data has a substantial impact on results in both real and synthetic time series datasets.

But transfer entropy has two fundamental flaws that persist even in time series of finite length and cardinality and have received less attention. First, it rarely indicates a null effect (transfer entropy of zero) in practice, even for completely uncorrelated time series. Statistical significance is thus typically assessed by permutation testing through simulations
marschinski2002analysing
;
dimpfl2013using
. This approach is not only computationally intensive but requires the choice of an arbitrary significance level, which must be corrected for multiple comparisons when constructing networks from time series
novelli2019large
and arguably should be adjusted based on the length of the time series being studied
lindley1957statistical
. The second fundamental issue is that the standard transfer entropy is constructed using the Shannon conditional entropy, which neglects the information required to specify the joint distribution of symbols among the time series. Ignoring this contribution is known to result in a severe overestimation of mutual information in systems with many clusters
newman2020improved
;
jerdee2023normalized
;
jerdee2024mutual
. Similarly, here we find that this omission results in an extreme overestimation of transfer entropy among uncorrelated time series when counts are sparse—this inevitably occurs for time series with short length or high cardinality, as well as when one wants to forecast with a large lag period. By correcting for finite sample sizes through a more careful treatment of information content, we show that one can derive a nonparametric transfer entropy measure—which we call the
reduced
transfer entropy—that alleviates both of these issues at no additional computational cost.

Microcanonical conditional entropy
—To derive a more appropriate transfer entropy measure for finite time series, we can start by formulating conditional entropy from a combinatorial perspective. Consider a sender, Alice, who wants to transmit a scalar-valued time series
\bm{w}\in\{1,..,C\}^{N}
to a receiver, Bob, with the help of a different (vector-valued) time series
\bm{V}\in\{1,..,C\}^{N\times d}
which is already known by both parties. In order to exploit Bob’s knowledge of
\bm{V}
to transmit
\bm{w}
, Alice must tell him the shared structure among
\bm{V}
and
\bm{w}
, since this shared structure will constrain the possibilities for
\bm{w}
and consequently reduce the number of bits needed for its transmission. The overlap among
\bm{w}
and
\bm{V}
can be summarized with a contingency table
\bm{n}^{(\bm{w},\bm{V})}
as in Eq.
6
.

Since Bob knows
\bm{V}
, Alice only needs to encode contingency tables
\bm{n}^{(\bm{w},\bm{V})}
that satisfy the margin constraint of Eq.
7
for all
\bm{s}
, the sum of the row indexed by
\bm{s}
in the contingency table. There are
{n^{(\bm{V})}_{\bm{s}}+C-1\choose C-1}
ways to assign
C
non-negative integer values to the
\bm{s}
-th row that sum to
n^{(\bm{V})}_{\bm{s}}
. Thus, taking a product over the row indices
\bm{s}
, there are

\displaystyle\Omega(\bm{n}^{(\bm{w},\bm{V})}|\bm{n}^{(\bm{V})})=\prod_{\bm{s}}{n^{(\bm{V})}_{\bm{s}}+C-1\choose C-1}

(8)

total possibilities for the contingency table given the margin constraints Bob already knows. Alice can then construct a fixed length code corresponding to the uniform distribution over these contingency tables with codelength
\log\Omega

. This forms the first contribution to the conditional entropy. It is worth noting that there are other possibilities for encoding contingency tables

;
jerdee2024mutual
, but since they are more complex, require some additional computational demand, and (in the case of
newman2020improved
) a numerical approximation
jerdee2024improved
, we use the one-step encoding scheme described.

Now that Bob knows the series overlap stored in the contingency table
\bm{n}^{(\bm{w},\bm{V})}
, Alice can send him
\bm{w}
at a lower information cost. Since he knows the
n_{\bm{s}}^{(\bm{V})}
locations at which
\bm{V}
takes the value
\bm{s}
, Alice can construct a fixed-length code with a codelength of

\displaystyle H_{M}(\bm{w}|\bm{V})=\frac{1}{N}\log\prod_{\bm{s}}\frac{n_{\bm{s}}^{(\bm{V})}!}{\prod\limits_{r}n_{r\bm{s}}^{(\bm{w},\bm{V})}!}

(9)

bits per sample. (In other words, codelength divided by
N
, for consistency with the Shannon formulation in Eq.
5
.) This is just the logarithm of the number of timestep assignments for
r=1,...,C
in
\bm{w}
consistent with the contingency table. Eq.
9
is a microcanonical variant of the conditional entropy, which is equivalent to the Shannon (canonical) conditional entropy in Eq.
5
when we use the Stirling approximation
\log n!\approx n\log n-n/\ln(2)
(see Supplemental Material).

Putting both contributions together, the coding rate under this transmission scheme is

\displaystyle H_{C}(\bm{w}|\bm{V})

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\Omega(\bm{n}^{(\bm{w},\bm{V})}|\bm{n}^{(\bm{V})})}{N}+H_{M}(\bm{w}|\bm{V})

(10)

bits per timestep for Alice to transmit
\bm{w}
to Bob given their shared knowledge of
\bm{V}
. Eq.
10
is thus a measure of conditional entropy between
\bm{w}
and
\bm{V}
that is corrected to account for the finite nature of the two time series. Applying Stirling’s approximation to Eq.
10
, we obtain

\displaystyle H_{C}(\bm{w}|\bm{V})\approx\frac{\log\Omega}{N}+H_{S}(\bm{w}|\bm{V}),

(11)

where
H_{S}(\bm{w}|\bm{V})
is the standard Shannon conditional entropy of Eq.
5
. For
N\to\infty
and fixed
C
, the correction
\log\Omega/N
vanishes asymptotically (see Supplemental Material) and
H_{C}\approx H_{S}
. However, we will see that in practice the difference between the two expressions can result in quite substantial discrepancies in the transfer entropy.

—Using Eq.
10

\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}

\displaystyle n

To naturally address the issue of statistical significance, the reduced transfer entropy of Eq.
13
allows for negative values. A negative reduced transfer entropy happens precisely when it is not worth using
\bm{X}^{(-k)}
to transmit
\bm{y}^{(+1)}
due to the additional information cost of specifying the more complex contingency table. The finite-size correction is thus effectively performing model selection using the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle
rissanen1978modeling
, which we show in our experiments acts as an alternative to frequentist permutation testing with no need for expensive simulations or the choice of a significance level. The reduced transfer entropy can be calculated with negligible additional computational cost since the correction in Eq.
8
can be computed directly from the contingency table, the construction of which is already necessary to compute the standard transfer entropy.

Additionally, as the microcanonical conditional entropy in Eq.
9
accounts for the information to specify the joint dependencies among two time series, it more heavily penalizes the usage of sparsely sampled symbol combinations for compression. This helps to mitigate the bias of Eq.
3
towards higher values as the time series length
T
decreases, the lags
k,l
increase, or the cardinality
C
increases. Note that, as opposed to the typical statistical estimation bias often noted for entropy estimation (which tends negative
paninski2003estimation
), the bias here refers to Eq.
3
always returning values substantially greater than zero in sparse count settings, even for uncorrelated time series. More details are shown in the Supplemental Material.

Normalization
—In order to have an absolute scale on which to interpret transfer entropies, it is convenient to normalize both the standard transfer entropy
\mathcal{T}
(Eq.
3
) and the reduced transfer entropy
\mathcal{R}
(Eq.
13
) by the maximum value they can attain over all possible
\bm{x}
. Using the bounds obtained in the Supplemental Material, we can construct normalized measures

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{T}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}}{H_{S}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})},

(19)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}}{M^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}},

(20)

where
M^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}={-}\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}
for
\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}\leq 0
and
M^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}=\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}+H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})
for
\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}>0
.

This choice of normalization maps to
\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}\in[0,1]
and
\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}\in[-1,1]
, with both lower bounds saturated if and only if we are completely certain about the value of
\bm{x}_{t}^{(-k)}=\bm{s}
when
\bm{y}_{t}^{(-l)}=\bm{r}
is known, or equivalently when
\bm{x}_{t}^{(-k)}
and
\bm{y}_{t}^{(-l)}
provide redundant information and are identical up to symbol relabelling (i.e. bin permutation). Both upper bounds are saturated when we are completely certain about the value
y_{t+1}=q
when
\bm{x}^{(-k)}_{t}=\bm{s}
and
\bm{y}^{(-l)}_{t}=\bm{r}
are both known, meaning that
\bm{x}^{(-k)}_{t}
maximally reduces the uncertainty about
y_{t}
given the information provided by
\bm{y}^{(-l)}_{t}
. (See Supplemental Material for details.) This allows us to analyze the standard and reduced transfer entropy measures on comparable absolute scales.

Air pollution case study
—As it can capture general nonlinear dependencies in spatiotemporal data, transfer entropy has been employed widely in the climate and atmospheric sciences
runge2012escaping
;
hu2022multi
;
tongal2022transfer
. Here we demonstrate the significant impact the transfer entropy correction can have on scientific findings in real time series data by examining a case study with air pollution data measured across Hong Kong. We collect data on the Air Quality Health Index (AQHI)
wong2013developing
, which categorizes overall health risk due to different air pollutants on a categorical scale
\{\text{low, moderate, high, very high, serious}\}
. Data were collected for the month of April 2025, which had substantial fluctuations in air quality from a set of dust storms. Directed transfer entropy networks were constructed using the reduced transfer entropy—a positive value indicating statistical significance—as well as the standard transfer entropy with a significance threshold set to
p=0.05
, with and without Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, for permutation tests with
1000
trials. The results are shown in Fig.
3
, where we can see major differences in the inferred significant links across the three methods, with the reduced measure being the most conservative in its classification of significance. We find that
18
of
20
edges overlap for the networks constructed with the reduced measure and the network with
p=0.05
, while
15
of
20
edges overlap with the more stringent p-value’s network. When such connections are used as a proxy for causality among the air pollution levels in different areas, one will thus come to qualitatively different conclusions using the finite-size corrected reduced measure. We examine our method on additional real-world data in the Supplemental Material.

Conclusion
—Here we derive a correction to the transfer entropy based on consideration of entropy prefactors relevant for finite data streams, which is essential for unbiased estimation from short and/or sparsely sampled time series. We prove various theoretical properties about our measure and demonstrate it on both real and synthetic data, finding that it judiciously assesses statistical significance and can substantially impact results in practice. It will be important to examine in future research how this measure performs when replacing transfer entropy in various applications inferring information flows in complex systems.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{s}}\log{n^{(\bm{V})}_{\bm{s}}+C-1\choose C-1}

(S8)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{s}}\log\frac{(n^{(\bm{V})}_{\bm{s}}+C-1)^{(C-1)}}{(C-1)!}

(S9)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{s}}(C-1)\log(N+C-1)

(S10)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log(N+C-1)}{N}(C-1)C^{k},

(S11)

where
k
is the dimension (i.e. lag in the transfer entropy calculation) of the time series
\bm{V}
. In the limit
N\to\infty
with
C
fixed, this expression vanishes and
H_{C}\approx H_{M}\approx H_{S}
.

S3
Non-positivity of transfer entropy correction
\Delta

The correction to the transfer entropy in Eq.
Transfer entropy for finite data
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}=\frac{1}{N}\log\frac{\prod\limits_{\bm{r}}\Biggl(\!\begin{array}[]{c}n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)})}_{\bm{r}}+C-1\\
C-1\end{array}\!\Biggr)}{\prod\limits_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}\Biggl(\!\begin{array}[]{c}n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}+C-1\\
C-1\end{array}\!\Biggr)}=\frac{1}{N}\log\prod\limits_{\bm{r}}\frac{\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)})}_{\bm{r}}}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)}{\prod\limits_{\bm{s}}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)},

where

\displaystyle\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)={n+k-1\choose k}

(S16)

is the multiset coefficient counting the number of unique ways to construct a multiset of
k
elements with draws from set of
n
elements with replacement. In
felippe2024network
it was shown that multiset coefficients satisfy

\displaystyle\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{l}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)\geq\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k+l}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right),

(S17)

for any

. This inequality implies that

\displaystyle\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)})}_{\bm{r}}}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)\leq\prod\limits_{\bm{s}}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right),

(S18)

since

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{s}}n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}=n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)})}_{\bm{r}}.

(S19)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{r}}\log\frac{\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)})}_{\bm{r}}}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)}{\prod\limits_{\bm{s}}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{r}}\log\frac{\prod\limits_{\bm{s}}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)}{\prod\limits_{\bm{s}}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{r}}\log(1)=0,

(S20)

and the correction is bounded above by zero. Hence the proposed transfer entropy measure is a “reduced” version of the standard transfer entropy.

S4
Positive bias of the standard transfer entropy

Since it is strictly non-negative, any statistical fluctuations must push the standard transfer entropy of Eq.
3
towards values greater than zero for uncorrelated time series, for which we would ideally return zero to indicate a lack of forecasting capability of
\bm{y}
from
\bm{x}
. We refer to this as the “positive bias” of the transfer entropy, and here try to understand how it behaves analytically.

For completely uncorrelated time series, the true underlying probability of each outcome
(q,\bm{r},\bm{s})
is
1/C^{k+l+1}
. Thus, under a maximum entropy assumption the contingency table
\bm{n}
will be distributed like a multinomial distribution with uniform bin probabilities
1/C^{k+l+1}
. The expected value of the transfer entropy under this null model is

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{T}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle H_{S}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})\rangle-\langle H_{S}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Z}^{(-k,-l)})\rangle

(S21)

\displaystyle=\langle H_{q,\bm{r}}\rangle+\langle H_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}\rangle-\langle H_{\bm{r}}\rangle-\langle H_{q,\bm{r},\bm{s}}\rangle,

(S22)

where

\displaystyle\langle H_{\bm{a}}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\sum_{\bm{a}}\frac{n_{\bm{a}}}{N}\log\frac{n_{\bm{a}}}{N}\right\rangle

(S23)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\sum_{\bm{a}}n_{\bm{a}}\log N-n_{\bm{a}}\log n_{\bm{a}}\right\rangle

(S24)

\displaystyle=\log N-\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\sum_{\bm{a}}n_{\bm{a}}\log n_{\bm{a}}\right\rangle

(S25)

\displaystyle=\log N-\frac{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|}{N}\left\langle n_{\bm{a}}\log n_{\bm{a}}\right\rangle

(S26)

is the expected Shannon entropy of counts indexed by
\bm{a}
when the multi-way contingency table
\bm{n}
of all joint counts is distributed as a uniform multinomial, and
\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|
is the size of
\bm{a}
’s support. This holds since when the full multi-way contingency table entries
\{n_{q,\bm{r},\bm{s}}\}
are uniform-multinomially distributed, all of the table marginals are also uniform-multinomially distributed over the corresponding new smaller dimension. Now, the marginal distribution of
n_{\bm{a}}
is a Binomial with
N
trials and success probability
1/\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|
, so the desired expectation is given by

\displaystyle\left\langle n_{\bm{a}}\log n_{\bm{a}}\right\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}{N\choose k}\left(\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|}\right)^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|}\right)^{N-k}k\log k,

(S27)

which unfortunately is intractable. However, if we approximate the binomial distribution as a Normal distribution in the limit of large
N
and fixed
\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|
, we have that the expectation is approximately

\displaystyle\left\langle n_{\bm{a}}\log n_{\bm{a}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})}[x\log x],

(S28)

where

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|},

(S29)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|}\left(1-\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|}\right).

(S30)

This is also intractable but admits a simple solution if we expand
x\log x
via Taylor expansion about the maximum, thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[x\log x]

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}\left[\mu\log\mu+(1+\log\mu)(x-\mu)+\frac{1}{2\mu}(x-\mu)^{2}\right]

(S31)

\displaystyle=\mu\log\mu+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\mu}

(S32)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|}\log\frac{N}{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|}\right).

(S33)

We thus have

\displaystyle\langle H_{\bm{a}}\rangle=\log\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|-\frac{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|}{2N}\left(1-\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|}\right).

(S34)

Subbing this in to our expression for the expected transfer entropy and replacing the
\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{a})\right|
terms appropriately, we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{T}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle H_{q,\bm{r}}\rangle+\langle H_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}\rangle-\langle H_{\bm{r}}\rangle-\langle H_{q,\bm{r},\bm{s}}\rangle

(S35)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{C^{l}}{2N}\left(1-\frac{1}{C^{l}}\right)+\frac{C^{k+l+1}}{2N}\left(1-\frac{1}{C^{k+l+1}}\right)-\frac{C^{l+1}}{2N}\left(1-\frac{1}{C^{l+1}}\right)-\frac{C^{k+l}}{2N}\left(1-\frac{1}{C^{k+l}}\right)

(S36)

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{l}(C^{k}-1)(C-1)}{2N}

(S37)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{C^{k+l+1}}{N}.

(S38)

Looking at this result, we can see that the positive bias of the standard transfer entropy will become greater as: (1) the cardinality
C
increases; (2) the number of timesteps
T
decreases; or (3) the lags
k,l
increase. This is consistent with the results seen in Fig.
1
and Fig.
2
. All of these factors lead to sparser bin counts, which intuitively should result in less reliable estimation. However, in the same regime of uniformity, the reduced transfer entropy gives a downward adjustment of

\displaystyle\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{r}}\log\frac{\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)})}_{\bm{r}}}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)}{\prod\limits_{\bm{s}}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{n^{(\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)}\sim\frac{C^{l}}{N}\log\frac{\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{N/C^l}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)}{\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{C}{N/C^{k+l}}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)^{C^{k}}}\sim O\left(\frac{C^{k+l+1}\log N}{N}\right).

(S39)

Thus, the reduced transfer entropy helps to correct the positive bias of the standard transfer entropy. This is also consistent with what is observed in our numerical experiments.

S5
Bounds on transfer entropies

From Eq.
3
, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=H_{S}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})-H_{S}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Z}^{(-k,-l)})\leq H_{S}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)}),

(S40)

since the conditional entropies are both non-negative. Thus,
H_{S}(\bm{X}^{(-k)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})
is an upper bound on
\mathcal{T}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}
over all time series
\bm{x}
. Similarly, for the reduced transfer entropy we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}+\frac{1}{N}\log\frac{\prod\limits_{q,\bm{r},\bm{s}}n^{(+1,{-}l,{-}k)}_{q,\bm{r},\bm{s}}!\prod\limits_{\bm{r}}n^{({-}l)}_{\bm{r}}!}{\prod\limits_{q,\bm{r}}n^{({+}1,-l)}_{q,\bm{r}}!\prod\limits_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}n^{({-}l,{-}k)}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}!}

(S41)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}+\frac{1}{N}\log\frac{\prod\limits_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}n^{({-}l,{-}k)}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}!\prod\limits_{\bm{r}}n^{({-}l)}_{\bm{r}}!}{\prod\limits_{q,\bm{r}}n^{({+}1,-l)}_{q,\bm{r}}!\prod\limits_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}n^{({-}l,{-}k)}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}!}

(S42)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}+\frac{1}{N}\log\frac{\prod\limits_{\bm{r}}n^{({-}l)}_{\bm{r}}!}{\prod\limits_{q,\bm{r}}n^{({+}1,-l)}_{q,\bm{r}}!}

(S43)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}+H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)}),

(S44)

giving
\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}+H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})
as an upper bound on
\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}
. Both of these upper bounds are saturated when

\displaystyle n^{({-}l,{-}k)}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}=n^{(+1,{-}l,{-}k)}_{q,\bm{r},\bm{s}}

(S45)

for all
\bm{r},\bm{s}
and some
q
fixed by
\bm{r},\bm{s}
. In other words, this upper bound is saturated when we are completely certain about the value
y_{t+1}=q
when
\bm{x}^{(-k)}_{t}=\bm{s}
and
\bm{y}^{(-l)}_{t}=\bm{r}
are both known, meaning that
\bm{x}^{(-k)}_{t}
maximally reduces the uncertainty about
y_{t+1}
given the information provided by
\bm{y}^{(-l)}_{t}
.

For lower bounds, the standard transfer entropy satisfies
\mathcal{T}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}\geq 0
, as conditioning always reduces entropy
cover1991elements
. Meanwhile, for the reduced transfer entropy we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}+H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})-H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)}).

(S46)

The term
H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})
counts (
1/N
times the logarithm of) the number of valid configurations of
\bm{y}^{(+1)}
given the constraints provided by
\bm{Y}^{(-l)}
and the contingency table
\bm{n}^{(\bm{y}^{(+1)},\bm{Y}^{(-l)})}
. Meanwhile, the term
H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})
counts (
1/N
times the logarithm of) the number of valid configurations of
\bm{y}^{(+1)}
given the constraints provided by
\bm{Y}^{(-l)}
,
\bm{X}^{(-k)}
, and the contingency table
\bm{n}^{(\bm{y}^{(+1)},\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})}
. There must be at least as many valid configurations of
\bm{y}^{(+1)}
under the first set of constraints as the second, since any valid configuration of
\bm{y}^{(+1)}
under the second set of constraints is also valid under the first set of constraints. This is the combinatorial equivalent to conditioning always reducing entropy. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})\geq H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})

(S47)

and so

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}=\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}+H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})-H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})\geq\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}.

(S48)

Thus,
\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}
is a lower bound for
\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}
. Both of these lower bounds are saturated when

\displaystyle n^{({-}l)}_{\bm{r}}=n^{({-}l,{-}k)}_{\bm{r},\bm{s}}

(S49)

for all
\bm{r}
and some
\bm{s}
fixed by
\bm{r}
. In other words, this lower bound is saturated when we are completely certain about the value of
\bm{x}_{t}^{(-k)}=\bm{s}
when
\bm{y}_{t}^{(-l)}=\bm{r}
is known, or equivalently when
\bm{x}_{t}^{(-k)}
and
\bm{y}_{t}^{(-l)}
provide redundant information as they are identical up to relabelings of symbols.

Although it is a valid upper bound,
\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}+H_{M}(\bm{X}^{(-k)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})
may be negative or zero for
\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}\leq 0
, in which case it does not provide a suitable normalization for
\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}
as it does not allow us to uniquely determine the sign of
\mathcal{R}
from its normalized value. Therefore, when
\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}\leq 0
, we can use the alternative upper bound
-\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}\geq 0
, so that the minimum reduced transfer entropy is found at
\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}=\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}
with a normalized value of
\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}=-1
in Eq.
20
.
-\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}
provides a valid normalization for
\mathcal{R}\leq 0
since in this case we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}\right|=-\mathcal{R}^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}=-\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}-[H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})-H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})]\leq-\Delta^{(k,l)}_{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}.

(S50)

The final step follows from
H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)})\geq H_{M}(\bm{y}^{(+1)}|\bm{Y}^{(-l)},\bm{X}^{(-k)})
.

S6
Finite-size deviation between
H_{S}
and
H_{M}

As shown, the Shannon conditional entropy
H_{S}
(Eq.
5
) and the microcanonical conditional entropy
H_{M}
(Eq.
9
) are equivalent when
n_{r,\bm{s}}\gg 1
for all
r,\bm{s}
. However, as discussed, we are often not in this count-rich regime in practice. Therefore,
H_{S}
and
H_{M}
may differ considerably. Outside of the count-rich regime, we must take higher order terms in the Stirling approximation

\displaystyle\log n!\approx n\log n-n/\ln(2)+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi n),

(S51)

giving

\displaystyle N\times(H_{M}-H_{S})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{s}}[\log n_{\bm{s}}!-n_{\bm{s}}\log n_{\bm{s}}]+\sum_{r,\bm{s}}[n_{r,\bm{s}}\log n_{r,\bm{s}}-\log n_{r,\bm{s}}!]

(S52)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{s}}\log(2\pi n_{\bm{s}})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r,\bm{s}}\log(2\pi n_{r,\bm{s}}).

(S53)

This suggests that the discrepancy between
H_{S}
and
H_{M}
vanishes when
n_{\bm{s}}=n_{r,\bm{s}}
for all
\bm{s}
and some single
r
for each
\bm{s}
. But this is simply when
H_{S}=H_{M}=0
are both minimized.

On the other hand, if we are in the regime with

\displaystyle n_{r,\bm{s}}=\frac{n_{\bm{s}}}{\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|},

(S54)

this will be the regime in which the conditional entropies are maximized, since we learn nothing about
r
by knowing
\bm{s}
when
n_{r,\bm{s}}=n_{r^{\prime},\bm{s}}
for all
r,r^{\prime},\bm{s}
. In this case, we have

\displaystyle N\times(H_{M}-H_{S})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{s}}\log(2\pi n_{\bm{s}})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r,\bm{s}}\log(2\pi n_{\bm{s}}/\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|).

(S55)

This can be simplified further when the counts
n_{\bm{s}}=N/\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|
are equal, giving

\displaystyle N\times(H_{M}-H_{S})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{s}}\log(2\pi N/\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r,\bm{s}}\log(2\pi N/\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|/\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|)

(S56)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|}{2}\log\frac{2\pi N}{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|}-\frac{\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|}{2}\log\frac{2\pi N}{\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|}

(S57)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|}{2}\log\frac{2\pi N}{\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|}[1-\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|]+\frac{\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|}{2}\log\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|.

(S58)

This expression suggests that, as
N
grows with
\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|,\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|
fixed, we have multiple distinct regimes:

1.

For
N\lesssim\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|
,
H_{M}
exceeds
H_{S}
, with a considerable discrepancy of at least
\frac{\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|}{2}\log\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|
when
N\lesssim\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|

2.

For
N\gtrsim\left|\mathcal{X}(r)\right|\left|\mathcal{X}(\bm{s})\right|
, we have that
H_{S}
exceeds
H_{M}
.

Thus, the difference between
H_{S}
and
H_{M}
is not consistent for finite data and may impact the transfer entropy differently depending on count sparsity. This is a further reason to use the reduced measure, as it is adapted specifically to the finite-size case as it does not utilize Stirling’s approximation.
