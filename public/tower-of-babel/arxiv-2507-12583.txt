Ranking Vectors Clustering: Theory and Applications
Ali Fattahi, Ali Eshragh, Babak Aslani, Meysam Rabiee

Abstract
We study the problem of clustering ranking vectors, where each vector represents preferences as an ordered list of distinct integers. Specifically, we focus on the k k -centroids ranking vectors clustering problem ( KRC ), which aims to partition a set of ranking vectors into k k clusters and identify the centroid of each cluster. Unlike classical k k -means clustering ( KMC ), KRC constrains both the observations and centroids to be ranking vectors. We establish the NP-hardness of KRC and characterize its feasible set. For the single-cluster case, we derive a closed-form analytical solution for the optimal centroid, which can be computed in linear time. To address the computational challenges of KRC , we develop an efficient approximation algorithm, KRCA , which iteratively refines initial solutions from KMC , referred to as the baseline solution. Additionally, we introduce a branch-and-bound ( BnB ) algorithm for efficient cluster reconstruction within KRCA , leveraging a decision tree framework to reduce computational time while incorporating a controlling parameter to balance solution quality and efficiency. We establish theoretical error bounds for KRCA and BnB . Through extensive numerical experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets, we demonstrate that KRCA consistently outperforms baseline solutions, delivering significant improvements in solution quality with fast computational times. This work highlights the practical significance of KRC for personalization and large-scale decision making, offering methodological advancements and insights that can be built upon in future studies. Key words: ranking vectors clustering, computational complexity, efficient approximation algorithms, personalized recommendations, large-scale group decision making

1
Introduction

We study the problem of clustering ranking vectors for large-scale problems by developing theoretical frameworks and applying them to extensive synthetic and real datasets. A
ranking vector
is an
m
-dimensional vector that represents a permutation of the set
\{1,\dots,m\}
. Each entry in the vector is unique, corresponding to exactly one element from the set. In a ranking vector, the value of each entry indicates the position or rank of the corresponding option in an ordered list
(
Aledo et al. 2018
,
Hou and Triantaphyllou 2019
)
. Note that there are no ties, as each position is assigned to exactly one element. For instance, an individual’s order of preference for movie genres can be represented as a ranking vector, wherein each entry indicates the rank of the corresponding genre. Similarly, in a hiring committee, each member’s preference ranking over applicants captures their individual evaluation of candidates. In a similar vein, students may rank their subject preferences based on interest or perceived difficulty, while in healthcare, patients may rank treatment options according to factors such as effectiveness, side effects, and cost. These examples illustrate how ranking vectors naturally arise in diverse decision making contexts.

The
k
-centroids ranking vectors clustering
problem, denoted by
KRC
, involves partitioning a given set of
n
ranking vectors into
k
clusters and determining the centroid of each cluster by minimizing the sum of the squared Euclidean distances between the
n
ranking vectors and their assigned centroids. While
KRC
may seem similar to the classical
k
-means clustering (
KMC
) problem
(
Lloyd 1982
,
Arthur and Vassilvitskii 2006
)
, where both data points and centroids can take any real values,
KRC
imposes a stricter constraint: both the data points and the centroids must be ranking vectors. This structured discretization of the decision variables introduces new challenges to the problem.

The
KRC
problem enables the personalization of offers, promotions, and recommendations by clustering ranking vectors, making it applicable across various domains such as enhancing user experiences on digital platforms. Beyond personalization, it also plays a crucial role in decision making contexts where diverse perspectives must be aggregated, such as in group decision making. Given its ability to structure ranking data efficiently,
KRC
is particularly valuable for large-scale applications, where
n
can scale to millions, requiring scalable and efficient computational methods.

In Section
1.1
, we propose several applications for
KRC
to emphasize its broad applicability and discuss how our proposed methodology can potentially lead to significant improvements in these applications.
We proceed by presenting our motivating examples in Section
1.1
and outlining our main results and insights in Section
1.2
.

1.1
Motivating Examples

We propose several applications for
KRC
across diverse domains, ranging from online review platforms—illustrated by examples including streaming platforms such as Netflix, Amazon Prime, and HBO Max, hospitality review systems, and dining reservation platforms like OpenTable and Resy—to large-scale group decision making. To the best of our knowledge, the application of ranking vector clustering in these contexts is novel and has not been explored in the existing literature, as detailed in Section
2
.

1.1.1
Online Review Platforms.

On streaming platforms such as Netflix, Amazon Prime, and HBO Max, viewers often rely on the average of individual ratings to decide what to watch. However, differences in genre preferences can result in significant variation in ratings, leading to high volatility. To address this, we propose a personalized approach to presenting average ratings that incorporates viewers’ genre preference rankings. While prior work has explored personalization based on viewer characteristics (Section
2
), to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to personalize ratings using genre preference rankings. This approach is motivated by our hypothesis that differences in genre preferences drive variation in movie ratings.

For instance, consider two types of viewers: A and B. Viewer A prefers comedy over thriller, while viewer B favors thriller over comedy. Suppose type A viewers consistently assign higher ratings to comedies, and type B viewers do the same for thrillers, reflecting the greater enjoyment they derive from their preferred genres. We explore such systematic rating behaviors using the MovieLens dataset (
grouplens.org/datasets/movielens
) in Section
6
. If a type A viewer relies on average ratings to choose between a comedy and a thriller, they might end up selecting a less desirable option. This misalignment is particularly likely when type A viewers are a minority — that is, when the number of type B viewers significantly outweighs that of type A.

Research suggests a positive relationship between customer satisfaction and profitability
(
Pooser and Browne 2018
,
Williams and Naumann 2011
)
. Building on this insight, personalization based on genre preference rankings has the potential to significantly enhance viewer utility, particularly for minority groups. This personalization can be implemented using
KRC
to identify clusters of viewers with similar genre preference rankings. Based on these clusters, we propose that the platform personalize the presentation of average ratings. Specifically, a viewer would see the average rating assigned by others within the same cluster, reflecting shared genre preferences.

Other practical applications of online review platforms include the hospitality industry and dining reservation platforms. In these contexts, a set of
criteria
plays a critical role in shaping customer satisfaction. For instance, in online accommodation booking platforms like Airbnb or TripAdvisor, the criteria might include cost, location, amenities, cleanliness, and proximity to public transportation. A budget-conscious traveler may prioritize cost over location, whereas a wealthier traveler might value location more highly. Similarly, in dining reservation platforms such as OpenTable and Resy, criteria may include cost, menu variety, service quality, location, and reservation availability.

We propose a personalized approach based on criteria preference rankings for these contexts. First, the platform determines each customer’s criteria preference ranking, either directly (e.g., by surveying subscribers) or indirectly (e.g., through contextual data analytics)
(
Panniello et al. 2016
,
Shen et al. 2023
)
. The
KRC
problem is then applied to form clusters of customers with similar preference rankings. Finally, the platform could personalize the presentation of average ratings, as well as tailor offers, promotions, and recommendations for each cluster.

As exemplified in the above applications,
KRC
has significant applications in customer segmentation for personalized and customized products and services, as well as in data summarization and machine learning. It enables targeted marketing, promotions, and tailored recommendations across these domains, leading to better decisions.

1.1.2
Large-scale Group Decision Making.

Group decision making involves a process in which multiple decision makers participate, and a final solution is required that reflects the collective knowledge and preferences of the group
(
Altuzarra et al. 2010
)
. We are particularly focused on large-scale applications, where millions of decision makers could be involved. For example, in urban planning, responding to criticisms regarding the lack of inclusivity and limited public input, involving citizens in the decision making process has become increasingly popular
(
Ertiö 2015
)
.

The application of large-scale group decision making is expanding rapidly due to the growth of social networks, internet-based systems, and the increasing recognition of new paradigms such as e-democracy
(
Kim 2008
)
and international trade law unification efforts, leading to decision making problems involving dozens to thousands of decision makers
(
Tang et al. 2020
)
. Recently, large-scale group decision making has been applied in a variety of fields, including water management
(
Srdjevic 2007
)
, emergency decision making
(
Xu et al. 2015
)
, education management
(
Rodríguez et al. 2018
)
, social credit systems
(
Ma et al. 2019
)
, financial inclusion
(
Chao et al. 2020
)
, hospital ranking
(
Liao et al. 2020
)
, green enterprise ranking problems, and the excavation scheme selection problem for shallow buried tunnels
(
Liao and Wu 2020
)
.

Consider a scenario where the citizens of a metropolitan area have provided their preference rankings for four areas of development: public transportation, affordable housing, recreational facilities, and energy efficiency. These four areas lead to
24
unique preference rankings. Citizens often form diverse clusters, each with significant differences that influence how they evaluate and compare these options. For instance, one cluster may prefer improving public transportation over recreational facilities, while another cluster may favor the opposite. Ignoring these clusters could result in a decision that is not representative of any specific group, nor the overall population.

By applying
KRC
, we identify these clusters and determine a centroid for each one. A centroid can be viewed as the proposal that best represents the preferences of its corresponding cluster. Essentially, a centroid summarizes the collective knowledge and preferences of the group it represents. With the clusters and their centroids identified, a final decision making committee (such as a small group of experts) can make an informed decision, referred to as the consensus reaching process
(
Liang et al. 2022
)
. Identifying clusters also offers additional benefits; for example, mapping them onto geospatial areas can help customize city development plans, providing a more tailored approach rather than a one-size-fits-all solution.

In summary, we illustrated the broad applicability of
KRC
through examples in online platforms and large-scale group decision making. In online platforms, where customers’ criteria preference rankings influence their decisions, identifying clusters with homogeneous rankings enables personalized presentations, promotions, and recommendations, improving user satisfaction and profitability. In large-scale group decision making, clustering ranking vectors helps derive consensus solutions that reflect the preferences of all groups. The
KRC
framework unifies these applications, highlighting the need for effective solution approaches.

1.2
Summary of Main Results

Our contributions are outlined below:

(i)

We present the first comprehensive theoretical study of the ranking vectors clustering problem. We formulate
KRC
mathematically, prove that it is
NP
-hard, and examine its similarities and differences with the
k
-means clustering problem. In addition, we show that if a clustering solution for
KMC
is
\varepsilon
-optimal, it will yield a
(1+2\varepsilon)
-optimal solution for
KRC
. This implies that a solution to
KMC
can be far from the
KRC
solution, as confirmed in our numerical experiments, emphasizing the importance of the theoretical development and applications of
KRC
presented in this paper.

(ii)

We provide a characterization of the feasible set for
KRC
, derive an analytical closed-form solution to determine the optimal centroid for each cluster, and show that it can be computed in linear time relative to the number of observations and options.

(iii)

We develop an approximation algorithm for
KRC
, called
KRCA
. This algorithm starts with an initial solution derived from
KMC
and iteratively refines it through a local search process. During each iteration, the
KRCA
algorithm updates the centroids and reassigns the observation ranking vectors to the nearest centroids. Each iteration leverages the unique structure of ranking vectors to enhance computational efficiency. Notably, if the initial
KMC
solution is
\varepsilon
-optimal,
KRCA
produces a
(1+2\varepsilon)
-optimal solution.

(iv)

We develop a branch-and-bound algorithm, denoted by
BnB
, for reconstructing the clusters at each iteration of the
KRCA
algorithm. The
BnB
algorithm employs a decision tree approach to partition the ranking vectors into subsets, represented as nodes in the tree. Within each node, the
BnB
algorithm updates lower and upper bounds for each centroid, enabling the comparison and elimination of non-promising centroids. Nodes are pruned if they contain no ranking vectors or only one centroid. We show that the
BnB
algorithm offers several advantages over an exhaustive search (
ES
) method, significantly reducing computation time for reconstructing clusters in instances with small
m
and large
n
. Additionally, it incorporates an error factor
\epsilon
, allowing for sub-optimal cluster reconstruction with a worst-case error of
n(k-1)\epsilon
, which balances solution quality and computational efficiency. Furthermore, it leverages the structure of the input ranking vectors, reducing computation time when the input is inherently clustered. We theoretically investigate the
BnB
algorithm’s convergence and establish its worst-case error. Additionally, we provide a bound on the depth of the decision tree as a function of the clusteredness of the observation ranking vectors.

(v)

We conduct a comprehensive numerical analysis to assess the performance of the
KRCA
algorithm, using both simulated synthetic data and real datasets. Our simulations include instances with uniformly generated ranking vectors as well as those where the ranking vectors are inherently clustered. Additionally, we utilize a dataset from MovieLens, develop a procedure for creating benchmark instances from this dataset, and apply the
KRCA
algorithm to these benchmark instances. Our results demonstrate that the
KRCA
algorithm is both computationally efficient and provides substantial improvements compared to benchmark solutions in both simulated and real instances. Furthermore, our numerical results reveal that there are situations in which a solution to the
KMC
problem can be far from the
KRC
problem solution.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews the related literature. Section
3
defines and formulates the
KRC
problem and establishes its
NP
-hardness. Section
4
introduces a closed-form solution for the single-cluster case and further develops the theoretical properties of the
KRC
problem. Section
5
builds on these theoretical foundations to propose the
KRCA
and
BnB
algorithms, along with their analytical properties. Section
6
presents extensive numerical experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets to evaluate the computational performance and solution quality of
KRCA
. Finally, Section
7
summarizes the key findings and outlines potential directions for future research. All proofs are presented in Online Appendix
A
.

2
Literature Review

Clustering is a fundamental problem in data analysis with diverse theoretical and practical applications. Among its many variants,
k
-means clustering has gained considerable attention due to its simplicity and effectiveness in partitioning data
(
MacQueen 1967
,
Ikotun et al. 2023
)
. A discrete variation, known as discrete
k
-means clustering (
DKMC
), introduces additional constraints by restricting both data points and centroids to a finite, predefined set of values. In this setting, inputs and cluster representatives are categorical or ordinal, such as labels or rankings, rather than continuous numerical values. Like
KMC
,
DKMC
also allows for ties, where multiple elements may share the same value
(
Huang 1998
)
.

In addition to classical
k
-means, several other variants have been developed to handle diverse data types and clustering objectives. One such method is
k
-medoids clustering
(
Kaufman and Rousseeuw 1990
,
He and Ni 2024
)
, which replaces centroids with actual data points (medoids). Another variant, known as
k
-medians clustering
(
Jain and Dubes 1988
)
, uses the median and the rectangular distance metric, making it more suitable for data with non-Euclidean geometry or ordinal structure. For purely categorical data, algorithms such as
k
-modes
(
Huang 1998
)
and
k
-histograms
(
He et al. 2005
)
replace the mean with a mode or frequency-based representation, respectively.

The
KRC
problem extends
KMC
and
DKMC
to scenarios where both observation vectors and centroids are ranking vectors. These ranking vectors are permutations of integers where no ties are allowed, meaning every element must hold a unique position. This constraint adds significant complexity to the problem, requiring new theoretical insights and algorithmic innovations to address its unique challenges and applications effectively.

2.1
Theory and Algorithms

The
KRC
problem is closely related to the classical
KMC
problem and its discrete version
DKMC
. The
KMC
problem aims to partition a set of
n
observation points into
k
clusters by minimizing the sum of squared Euclidean distances between each point and the centroid of its assigned cluster
(
Ikotun et al. 2023
)
. Each centroid in
KMC
is computed as the mean of the observation points within the corresponding cluster. By contrast,
DKMC
introduces the constraint that centroids must be chosen from the input data points
(
Aggarwal et al. 2009
)
. Distinguishing itself from both
KMC
and
DKMC
,
KRC
requires that both the observations and centroids are ranking vectors. Both
KMC
and
DKMC
are known to be
NP
-hard
(
Dasgupta 2008
,
Matoušek 2000
)
. In this paper, we establish the
NP
-hardness of
KRC
via a reduction from the hypercube clustering problem, where binary vectors are clustered, and each cluster’s centroid is restricted to be a binary vector
(
Baldi 2012
)
.

Given the challenges of solving
KMC
optimally, many existing studies focus on developing approximation algorithms.
MacQueen 1967
introduced the term “
k
-means” and proposed an iterative method that initializes
k
random points as clusters. At each iteration, points are added to the cluster with the nearest mean (centroid), and the mean is subsequently updated.
Bandyapadhyay and
Varadarajan 2015
developed a bi-criteria polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS) for
KMC
, which identifies
(1+\varepsilon)k
centers with an objective value at most
(1+\varepsilon)
times the optimal solution. However, whether a true PTAS exists for
KMC
in the plane remains an open question.
Kanungo et al. 2002b
presented a polynomial-time approximation algorithm with a worst-case relative error of
(9+\varepsilon)
, which has since been improved to a PTAS for fixed dimensions by
Friggstad et al. 2019a
,
Friggstad et al. 2019b
and
Cohen-Addad et al. 2019
. For arbitrary dimensions,
Ahmadian et al. 2019
proposed a
6.357
-approximation. Our approximation algorithm,
KRCA
, builds upon these advancements by starting from a
KMC
-based initial solution. We prove that if the
KMC
solution is
\varepsilon
-optimal, then
KRCA
achieves
(1+2\varepsilon)
-optimality for the
KRC
problem.

Various adaptations of
DKMC
have been proposed to handle categorical, binary, or ordinal data. Techniques for solving
DKMC
include enumeration, integer programming, linear programming, and dynamic programming
(
Hansen and Jaumard 1997
,
Jensen 1969
,
Vinod 1969
,
Rao 1971
)
. Lagrangian relaxation methods have also been used for datasets with fewer than 200 data points
(
Mulvey and Crowder 1979
)
. However, these methods are unsuitable for
KRC
because it does not restrict centroids to be chosen from observations and is instead designed for large-scale datasets.

Lloyd’s algorithm is a widely used local search method for
KMC
due to its simplicity and effectiveness
(
Lloyd 1982
)
. The algorithm alternates between assigning each observation point to the nearest centroid and updating centroids by calculating the cluster means. The process repeats until a stopping criterion is satisfied. Lloyd’s approach is closely related to the expectation-maximization method
(
Dempster 1977
)
. Several improved versions of Lloyd’s algorithm, such as
k
-means
++

(
Arthur and Vassilvitskii 2006
)
, have been proposed to enhance the initialization process. Our
KRCA
algorithm shares a similar framework but adapts each step to exploit the structure of ranking vectors. We introduce a polynomial-time procedure for calculating the optimal centroid in initialization and update steps and propose a novel
BnB
algorithm for the assignment step. We present theoretical analyses and derive error bounds for both the
KRCA
and
BnB
algorithms.

Similar to
BnB
,
Kanungo et al. 2002a
utilized a decision tree for assigning observations to centroids in
KMC
. However, our
BnB
algorithm introduces a new branching scheme, bounding procedures within tree nodes, and theoretical analyses of error and tree depth. These innovations leverage the unique properties of ranking vectors, making our approach distinct and well-suited to
KRC
.

2.2
Applications

As discussed in Section
1.1
,
KRC
has broad applications in online review platforms and large-scale group decision making. This section reviews the literature on these topics and highlights how
KRC
contributes to these domains.

2.2.1
Online Review Platforms.

The growth of e-commerce has made customer feedback, especially in the form of online reviews, a crucial resource for analyzing market trends and tailoring individual customer experiences
(
Chen and Xie 2008
)
. Online product reviews are the second most trusted source of information for consumers, following referrals from friends and family
(
Nielsen 2012
)
. According to the Pew Research Center’s 2016 Online Shopping and E-Commerce survey
(
Pew Research Center 2016
)
,
82\%
of Americans consult reviews before making a first-time purchase. This highlights the dual importance of reviews for customers making informed decisions and businesses aiming to understand consumer preferences
(
Cui et al. 2012
)
.

A significant body of research focuses on understanding customer preferences through online reviews, particularly by identifying key product features. Early works, such as
Godes and Mayzlin 2004
and
Liu 2006
, utilized human coding to analyze textual data. Recent advancements leverage natural language processing techniques to extract insights methodically
(
Netzer et al. 2012
,
Tirunillai and Tellis 2014
)
. Studies also incorporate demographic and geographic factors to personalize offers and recommendations
(
Raoofpanah et al. 2023
)
. However, to the best of our knowledge, no existing research employs feature rankings as a foundation for personalization. The
KRC
problem addresses this gap by clustering customers based on ranking vectors, offering a novel approach for e-commerce applications.

2.2.2
Large-scale Group Decision Making.

Large-scale group decision making often involves solving vector-clustering problems, which can be classified based on their distance measures, input vector types, and centroid representations. Common distance measures include squared Euclidean distance
(
Tang et al. 2019
)
, Kendall-tau distance
(
Farnoud et al. 2012
)
, and Spearman’s footrule
(
Chen et al. 2011
)
. Input vectors and centroids studied in the literature include ranking vectors
(
Aledo et al. 2018
,
Hou and Triantaphyllou 2019
)
, partial ranking vectors
(
Amodio et al. 2016
,
Dinu and Manea 2006
)
, and score vectors
(
De Leeneer and Pastijn 2002
)
. We focus exclusively on clustering ranking vectors using squared Euclidean distance as the distance measure.

In single-cluster scenarios, this problem is referred to as
rank aggregation
in the group decision making literature
Xiao et al. 2017
. Borda-based heuristics are widely used for determining aggregate rankings
(
Balinski and Laraki 2014
,
Zhou et al. 2024
)
. These methods typically involve converting rankings to scores and aggregating the scores. Recent advances, such as
Zhou et al. 2024
, propose hybrid evolutionary algorithms that effectively handle both complete and partial rankings, using Borda-based heuristics as a starting point.
Brandl and Peters 2019
offers a comprehensive study of the properties of these approaches. For further developments in single-cluster rank aggregation, see
Yoo et al. 2020
,
Aledo et al. 2018
,
Caragiannis et al. 2019
,
Dopazo and
Martínez-Céspedes 2017
,
Chatterjee et al. 2018
,
Chin et al. 2004
, and
Raisali et al. 2013
.

Our contribution to this domain includes developing a polynomial-time procedure for solving
KRC
in the single-cluster case, which determines an aggregate ranking vector that minimizes the squared Euclidean distance. While existing rank aggregation methods, such as those by
Schalekamp and Zuylen 2009
,
Farnoud et al. 2012
,
Dinu and Manea 2006
,
Ding et al. 2018
,
Negahban et al. 2017
, focus on the single-cluster case of
KRC
, they employ different objective functions or input types and are therefore not directly applicable to our setting. For instance,
Schalekamp and Zuylen 2009
minimizes the Kendall-tau distance, while
Farnoud et al. 2012
introduces an approximation method based on Spearman’s footrule distance.
Dinu and Manea 2006
explores rank-distance, a measure for partial rankings, and
Ding et al. 2018
develops a hierarchical heuristic for combining rankings with ambiguous preferences.
Negahban et al. 2017
proposes Rank Centrality, an iterative algorithm that infers scores from pairwise comparisons. In contrast, our approach focuses on full rankings and minimizes the squared Euclidean distance, making it distinct from these prior methods.

For multiple-cluster cases, the literature is relatively sparse. Existing methods are primarily heuristic-based
(
Zhou et al. 2020
,
Wu et al. 2020
,
Tang et al. 2019
)
. The
KRC
problem contributes to this area by introducing an effective approximation algorithm for multi-cluster problems.

We provide a thorough theoretical characterization for the
KRC
problem, prove
NP
-hardness, develop the
KRCA
approximation algorithm, and establish error bounds.

3
KRC
: Mathematical Formulation and Complexity Analysis

In this section, we mathematically formulate the
KRC
problem and rigorously develop its analytical properties. Furthermore, we prove that
KRC
is
NP
-hard and highlight its distinctions from
KMC
. To establish a clear foundation for our analysis, we begin by formally defining the
KMC
problem.

Definition 3.1

(
k
-Means Clustering
(
Lloyd 1982
,
Arthur and Vassilvitskii 2006
)
)

Consider a set of
n
observations
\mathcal{S}=\{{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}\}
, where
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. The
k
-means clustering problem, denoted by
KMC
, is defined as finding a partition of
\mathcal{S}
into
k
mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive subsets
\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{k}
and centroids
{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that the objective function

\displaystyle v_{\mathtt{KMC}}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{k},{\bm{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{y}}_{k})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}_{\ell}\|^{2}

(1)

is minimized, where
\|\cdot\|^{2}
denotes the squared Euclidean distance. Let
v_{\mathtt{KMC}}^{*}
denote the optimal value of the objective function in Equation (
1
) for the
KMC
problem.

It is well established
(
Lloyd 1982
,
Arthur and Vassilvitskii 2006
)
that for any given clusters
\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{k}
, the objective function (
1
) is minimized if the centroids are set to the mean of all observations in the clusters, expressed as:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{\ell}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}}{\bm{x}}}{|\mathcal{S}_{\ell}|}\quad\mbox{for }\ell=1,\ldots,k,

(2)

where
|\mathcal{S}_{\ell}|
is the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
(assuming
|\mathcal{S}_{\ell}|\neq 0
). Consequently, solving the
KMC
problem reduces to finding the optimal clusters. For simplicity, the centroids
{\bm{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{y}}_{k}
are omitted from the notation of the objective function (
1
), and it is denoted as
v_{\mathtt{KMC}}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{k})
, implying that the centroids are optimally determined according to Equation (
2
).

Next, we introduce the
KRC

m
distinct options, such as the criteria or candidates discussed in Section

. Suppose these options are ranked by individuals with no ties, from the least preferred (

m^{\text{th}}

Definition 3.2

(Ranking Vector)

A vector
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called a ranking vector if it is a permutation of the integers
1
to
m
. Additionally, let
\mathcal{X}_{m}
denote the set of all
m!
possible ranking vectors.

We provide an intuitive geometric representation for the space of all
m

\mathcal{X}_{m}
. We demonstrate that ranking vectors lie at the intersection of a specific hypersphere and hyperplane. Let
\mathsf{BHS}({\bm{z}},r^{2})=\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{z}}\|^{2}=r^{2}\}
represent the boundary of a hypersphere centered at

\mathsf{HP}({\bm{y}},c)=\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\langle{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}\rangle=c\}
denote a hyperplane with a normal vector
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Lemma
3.3
characterizes and provides a geometric perspective of
\mathcal{X}_{m}
.

Lemma 3.3

(Characterization of Ranking Vectors)

The set of all
m
-dimensional ranking vectors
\mathcal{X}_{m}
satisfies the following inclusion equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{m}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathsf{BHS}\left(\mathbf{0},\frac{1}{6}m(m+1)(2m+1)\right)\cap\ \mathsf{HP}\left(\mathbf{1},\frac{1}{2}m(m+1)\right),

where
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
are vectors of size
m
with all entries equal to zero and one, respectively.

Lemma
3.3
establishes that ranking vectors reside at the intersection of a specific hypersphere and hyperplane. Figure

\begin{bmatrix}2&1&3\end{bmatrix}
,

With this geometric intuition established, we now formally define the

n

{\bm{x}}_{i}
representing the ranking vector assigned by individual

Definition 3.4

(

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}\}
, where

for

, denote the set of all ranking vectors provided by

\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{k}

{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}
, such that

\displaystyle v_{\mathtt{KRC}}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{k},{\bm{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{y}}_{k})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}_{\ell}\|^{2}

(3)

is minimized.

Let

We now examine the computational challenges of
KRC
. Intuitively, the complexity of
KRC
is influenced by the parameters
n
,
m
, and
k
. As discussed in Section
1.1
, for big-data applications—the primary focus of this paper—
n
can reach the order of millions. In contrast,
m
and
k
may be either small or large, depending on the application. Consequently, developing a solution approach that is both scalable for large
n
and adaptable to varying values of
m
and
k
is essential. While integer programming techniques may be effective for small instances of
KRC
, their applicability diminishes for real, large-scale instances, as outlined in the following remark.

Remark 3.5

(Integer Program for
KRC
)

The
KRC
problem can be formulated as an integer program by (i) enumerating a cost matrix, where rows represent the
n
ranking vectors provided by individuals and columns represent the
m!
possible centroids, and (ii) modeling the problem as selecting
k
centroids and assigning the input vectors to these centroids. This formulation resembles the classical p-median problem
(
Daskin and Maass 2015
)
and is computationally feasible only for small values of
n
,
m
, and
k
.

As highlighted in Remark
3.5
, exact methods such as integer programming are impractical for solving large-scale instances of
KRC
. While the geometric structure of ranking vectors, as characterized in Lemma
3.3
, might indicate potential simplifications, the computational complexity remains substantial. For example, leveraging the insights from Remark
3.5
, the special case of
KRC
with
m=2
is solvable in polynomial time because
\mathcal{X}_{2}
contains only two elements. More generally, if
m
is fixed,
KRC
remains solvable in polynomial time. This follows from the fact that there are
m!
potential centroids, and solving
KRC
requires evaluating subsets of at most
k
centroids. Once these centroids are fixed, the problem reduces to an assignment problem, which is known to be solvable in polynomial time. While this theoretical polynomial-time complexity is reassuring, it remains computationally prohibitive for practical applications, particularly when
m
is large. For general values of
m
,
KRC
is
NP
-hard, as established in the following theorem, using a reduction from Hypercube Clustering Problem
(
Baldi 2012
)
.

Theorem 3.6

(
NP
-hardness of
KRC
)

The
KRC
problem is
NP
-hard.

Theorem
3.6
demonstrates that solving moderate to large instances of
KRC
is likely to be computationally intractable in practice, highlighting the need for effective approximation algorithms. To address this challenge, we first establish the analytical solution for the single-cluster special case of
KRC
in Section
4.1
and derive theoretical error bounds for relaxing
KRC
to
KMC
in Section
4.2
. These results are then leveraged to develop an efficient approximation algorithm for the general case in Section
5
.

4
KRC
Solutions: Exact for Single-Cluster and Theoretical Bounds for Multiple-Cluster

In this section, we establish two key theoretical aspects of the
KRC
problem. In the single-cluster case, discussed in Section
4.1
, we derive a closed-form solution that can be computed in linear time based on the problem parameters. This result not only has standalone significance but also lays the groundwork for designing approximation methods. For the multi-cluster case, studied in Section
4.2
, we establish theoretical error bounds for approximating
KRC
using
KMC
. Specifically, we show that if a solution to
KMC
is used as an approximation for
KRC
, the relative error can exceed 100%. These findings illustrate the fundamental differences between
KMC
and
KRC
, motivating the need for tailored algorithms, which will be presented in Section
5
.

4.1
Single-Cluster
KRC
: Closed-Form Solution

We develop a closed-form solution for the single-cluster special case of
KRC
, which can be computed in linear time with respect to the problem parameters. This special case holds dual significance: it has standalone applications and serves as a critical component of the approximation algorithm introduced in Section
5
. Broadly, a clustering algorithm produces two main outputs: the cluster sets and the centroids of the clusters. As discussed earlier, centroids in
KMC
are determined by computing the mean of the given observation points in each cluster. However, the discrete nature of ranking vectors introduces additional complexity in determining optimal centroids for
KRC
.

Given the independence of centroids across clusters, the problem of finding optimal centroids for a set of clusters naturally decomposes into subproblems, each corresponding to a single cluster. Therefore, without loss of generality, we assume a single cluster containing
n
ranking vectors over
m
options, with
k=1
cluster, for ease of presentation.

One approach to solving the single-cluster problem is to formulate it as an integer program and solve it using commercial solvers. However, such methods are impractical for large-scale instances due to their prohibitive computation times. Alternatively, the problem can be framed as a “Minimum Weighted Bipartite Matching” problem, where the cost of an edge
(j_{1},j_{2})
between two nodes
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
is defined as

\displaystyle c_{j_{1}j_{2}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{ij_{1}}-j_{2})^{2},

(4)

with
x_{ij_{1}}
representing the
j_{1}^{\text{th}}
component of the ranking vector
{\bm{x}}_{i}
. This problem can be solved using the improved Hungarian algorithm by
Edmonds and Karp 1972
and
Tomizawa 1971
, with a worst-case time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(nm^{2}+m^{3})
. The first term corresponds to the computation of edge costs, and the second term pertains to solving the matching problem.

Remarkably, we present a significantly faster procedure for solving the single-cluster case of
KRC
, as provided in Theorem
4.1
. This theorem offers an analytical closed-form solution for determining the optimal centroid, which can be computed in linear time
\mathcal{O}(nm)
with respect to the number of observations
n
and options
m
.

Theorem 4.1

(Optimal Centroid)

Consider a single cluster
\mathcal{S}=\{{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}\}
, where

{\bm{x}}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i1}&\cdots&x_{im}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}

represents the
i^{\text{th}}
ranking vector for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Define

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{.j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}}{n}\quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,m,

and sort these values in non-decreasing order. If
y_{j}^{*}
is defined as the rank of
\bar{x}_{.j}
in this sorted sequence, then

{\bm{y}}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}^{*}&\cdots&y_{m}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}

is an optimal centroid for the cluster. Additionally, if there are ties among
\bar{x}_{.j}
values, any arbitrary ordering of the ties will also be optimal.

Consequently, similar to the
KMC
problem, we simplify the notation of the objective function (
3
) by omitting the explicit representation of centroids
{\bm{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{y}}_{k}
. The objective function is denoted as
v_{\mathtt{KRC}}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{k})
, implying that centroids are set optimally according to Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem
4.1
highlights that the primary computational challenge of the
KRC
problem lies in determining the optimal clusters. Consequently, developing an efficient approximation algorithm for the
KRC
problem reduces to devising a method for approximately clustering the observed ranking vectors. Since
\mathcal{X}_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the
KMC
problem can be regarded as a “relaxation” of the
KRC
problem. Thus, it may be natural to propose using a solution to the
KMC
problem as an approximation for the
KRC
problem. Specifically, one can compute a locally optimal clustering for the
KMC
problem, use it as an initial approximation for the
KRC
problem, and then determine the optimal centroids by applying Theorem
4.1
.

However, as we demonstrate theoretically in Section
4.2
and numerically in Section
6
, such a relaxation can result in significant relative errors when approximating the
KRC
problem. In some cases, these errors may exceed
100\%
, highlighting the limitations of directly applying this approach.

4.2
Relaxing
KRC
via
KMC
: Theoretical Bounds and Errors

As discussed earlier,
\mathcal{X}_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
implies that
KMC
can be viewed as a relaxation of
KRC
, meaning that
v_{\mathtt{KMC}}^{*}\leq v_{\mathtt{KRC}}^{*}
. Consequently, the optimal value of
KMC
serves as a lower bound for the optimal value of
KRC
. Next, we establish an upper bound for
KRC
in Proposition
4.2
and Corollary
4.3
.

Proposition 4.2

(Upper Bound for General Clustering)

Consider the given clusters
\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{k}
for
n
ranking vectors from
\mathcal{X}_{m}
. We have

\displaystyle v_{\mathtt{KRC}}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq 2v_{\mathtt{KMC}}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{k}).

Corollary 4.3

(Upper and Lower Bounds for Optimal Clustering)

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}\}
, where
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Given
\mathcal{S}
, let
v_{\mathtt{KMC}}^{*}
and
v_{\mathtt{KRC}}^{*}
denote the optimal values of
KMC
and
KRC
, respectively. We have

\displaystyle v_{\mathtt{KMC}}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq v_{\mathtt{KRC}}^{*}\leq 2v_{\mathtt{KMC}}^{*}.

Corollary
4.3
states that if we relax
KRC
and instead solve
KMC
as an approximate clustering for the former problem, the relative error for this approximation could be as large as
50\%
, that is,

\displaystyle\frac{v_{\mathtt{KRC}}^{*}-v_{\mathtt{KMC}}^{*}}{v_{\mathtt{KRC}}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Next, we demonstrate the tightness of this bound by constructing an instance of
KRC
that achieves the above worst-case bound.

4.2.1
Tightness of the Error Bound.

We first consider the simplest case of
k=1
with two observations,
{\bm{x}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\end{bmatrix}
and
{\bm{x}}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\end{bmatrix}
. It is readily observed that the optimal centroids of the
KRC
and
KMC
problems are
{\bm{y}}_{\texttt{KRC}}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\end{bmatrix}
and
{\bm{y}}_{\texttt{KMC}}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}1.5&1.5\end{bmatrix}
, respectively (note that
KRC
has an alternative optimal centroid
{\bm{y}}_{\texttt{KRC}}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\end{bmatrix}
). Hence,
v_{\texttt{KRC}}^{*}=2
and
v_{\texttt{KMC}}^{*}=1
, achieving the upper bound in Corollary
4.3
.

Next, we construct an example for
k=2
with four observations
{\bm{x}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3&4&5&6&7\end{bmatrix}
,
{\bm{x}}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&3&4&5&6&7\end{bmatrix}
,
{\bm{x}}_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&5&4&3&6&7\end{bmatrix}
, and
{\bm{x}}_{4}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&5&4&3&7&6\end{bmatrix}
. These four observations are constructed such that the two pairs
({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})
and
({\bm{x}}_{3},{\bm{x}}_{4})
have a squared Euclidean distance of two, and any other pair of observations has a squared Euclidean distance sufficiently greater than two. One can easily verify that
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}=\{{\bm{x}}_{3},{\bm{x}}_{4}\}
are optimal clusters for both the
KRC
and
KMC
problems, with
v_{\texttt{KRC}}^{*}=4
and
v_{\texttt{KMC}}^{*}=2
, respectively, again achieving the given upper bound.

Motivated by these two numerical examples, we generalize this idea to any number of clusters
k
, where
k\in\{1,2,\dots\}
, by constructing instances in which
v_{\mathtt{KRC}}^{*}=2v_{\mathtt{KMC}}^{*}
. For a given value of
k
, let
\bar{m}
denote the smallest
\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
that satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\eta&\eta-1&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}1&2&\cdots&\eta\end{bmatrix}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 4k,

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{(\eta-1)\eta(\eta+1)}{3}

\displaystyle\geq 4k.

Clearly, such an
\bar{m}
exists and
\bar{m}\geq 3
. Now, let
n=2k
,
m=\bar{m}(k-1)+2k
, and define

\displaystyle\theta_{i}=\begin{cases}(\bar{m}+2)(\frac{i-1}{2}-1)+2&\text{for }i=1,3,\ldots,2k-1,\\
(\bar{m}+2)(\frac{i}{2}-1)&\text{for }i=2,4,\ldots,2k,\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}=\begin{cases}\bar{m}&\text{for }i=1,3,\ldots,2k-1,\\
2&\text{for }i=2,4,\ldots,2k.\end{cases}

Construct the set of
n
observations by setting
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdots&m\end{bmatrix}
and forming the observations
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{x}_{i,1}&\cdots&\hat{x}_{i,m}\end{bmatrix}
for
i=2,\ldots,n
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\hat{x}_{i-1,j}&\text{for }j\leq\theta_{i},\\
\lambda_{i}+2\theta_{i}-j+1&\text{for }\theta_{i}+1\leq j\leq\theta_{i}+\lambda_{i},\\
j&\text{for }j\geq\theta_{i}+\lambda_{i}+1.\end{cases}

One can show that for any pair of indices
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n
, if
i_{1}
is an odd number and
i_{2}=i_{1}+1
, we have
\|{\bm{x}}_{i_{2}}-{\bm{x}}_{i_{1}}\|^{2}=2
; otherwise,
\|{\bm{x}}_{i_{2}}-{\bm{x}}_{i_{1}}\|^{2}\geq 4k
.

We next argue that the clusters
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{i}=\{{\bm{x}}_{2i-1},{\bm{x}}_{2i}\}
with centroid
{\bm{x}}_{2i-1}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
, form an optimal solution for
KRC
. Clearly, this is a feasible solution with the objective value of
v_{\mathtt{KRC}}(\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{k})=2k
. It can be readily observed that for any two distinct observations
{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}
, we have
\|{\bm{x}}_{2}-{\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}\geq 2
. Furthermore, for any given
k
clusters with
2k
observations in
\mathcal{X}_{m}
, as the
k
centroids also belong to
\mathcal{X}_{m}
, calculating the objective function
v_{\texttt{KRC}}
requires the summation of at least
k
such difference squared norms, which implies that it is bounded below by
2k
. Thus, this demonstrates the optimality of the clusters
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
for
KRC
, that is,
v_{\texttt{KRC}}^{*}=2k
.

Lastly, consider the
KMC
problem for the constructed instance. The clusters
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
with centroids
\frac{1}{2}({\bm{x}}_{1}+{\bm{x}}_{2})
, …,
\frac{1}{2}({\bm{x}}_{n-1}+{\bm{x}}_{n})
, respectively, form a feasible solution for
KMC
, achieving the objective value of
v_{\texttt{KMC}}(\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{k})=k
. Additionally, Corollary
4.3
states that
v_{\texttt{KMC}}^{*}\geq\frac{1}{2}v_{\texttt{KRC}}^{*}=k
. Therefore, the clusters
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
achieve the lower bound of the corresponding objective value, implying that they are optimal, that is,
v_{\texttt{KMC}}^{*}=k
. Hence,
v_{\texttt{KRC}}^{*}=2v_{\texttt{KMC}}^{*}
.

4.2.2
Theoretical Error Bound.

The relative error provided by Proposition
4.2
applies to the case when
KMC
is solved optimally. However, due to its
NP
-hardness, solving real-size instances of
KMC
optimally is not tractable. Thus, as discussed in Section
2.1
, several approximation algorithms have been proposed in the literature to guarantee finding an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution for
KMC
, for some
\varepsilon>0
, meaning that the objective value of the solution is no larger than
(1+\varepsilon)
times the optimal value.

More precisely, if
\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
is a solution produced by such an approximation algorithm, we have

\displaystyle v_{\mathtt{KMC}}(\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)v_{\mathtt{KMC}}^{*}.

(5)

Such an approximation can also be applied to solve
KRC
. The resulting solution is a feasible clustering for
KRC
, and one needs to apply Theorem
4.1
to compute an optimal centroid for each cluster. Notably, using Inequality (
5
), Proposition
4.2
, and Corollary
4.3
, we establish in Proposition
4.4
that this approach results in a larger relative error of
(1+2\varepsilon)
in the worst case.

Proposition 4.4

(
(1+2\varepsilon)
-Optimal Approximation)

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}\}
, where
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Let
\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
be an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution for
KMC
. This clustering serves as a
(1+2\varepsilon)
-optimal solution for
KRC
, that is,

\displaystyle v_{\mathtt{KRC}}(\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq(2+2\varepsilon)v_{\mathtt{KRC}}^{*},

or equivalently, assuming
v_{\mathtt{KRC}}^{*}\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{v_{\mathtt{KRC}}(\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{k})-v_{\mathtt{KRC}}^{*}}{v_{\mathtt{KRC}}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\varepsilon.

Proposition
4.4
states that while an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution for the
KMC
problem can serve as a reasonable approximation for
KMC
, applying this approximation to the
KRC
problem may result in a significant error, potentially exceeding
100\%
. Through extensive numerical experiments on both real and synthetic data (Section
6
), we demonstrate that this considerable relative error is not merely a by-product of theoretical analysis but can indeed occur in practice.

Furthermore, we intuitively illustrate that an optimal clustering for the
KMC
problem is not necessarily optimal for the
KRC
problem. The intuition behind this observation lies in the distinct nature of centroids in the two problems. While centroids in
KMC
are simply the averages of the points in each cluster, centroids in
KRC
are restricted to be ranking vectors from the set
\mathcal{X}_{m}
. In other words, the centroid of a cluster in
KRC
shifts from the real-valued mean to a nearby discrete point in
\mathcal{X}_{m}
. Consequently, a point near the boundary of a cluster in
KMC
may be better assigned to a different cluster, leading to a different optimal clustering for
KRC
.

These theoretical and numerical findings highlight the critical need to develop an efficient approximation algorithm that captures the specific structure of the
KRC
problem and provides a more suitable approximation. We proceed to develop our new approximation algorithm.

at the cost of generating approximate solutions. Notably, when
\epsilon=0

produces the exact clustering solution obtained by the

enhances
BnB

Generic Framework of the

(i)

(iii)

In this step, the set of updated centroids is provided, and each observation ranking vector is assigned to its closest centroid. An

The

The

The

(a)

Improved computation time for small-

:

(b)

(c)

Exploitation of input structure

5.1.2
Convergence Properties of
BnB
.

Two critical questions concerning the
BnB
algorithm pertain to its convergence and computational efficiency. These are addressed in Theorem
5.1
and Theorem
5.3
below.

Theorem 5.1

(Theoretical Error Bound for the
BnB
Algorithm)

Consider the
KRC
problem with
n
observation ranking vectors and
k
clusters, and given centroids
{\bm{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{y}}_{k}
at a cluster reconstruction step of the
BnB
algorithm. Let

\displaystyle v_{\mathtt{KRC}}(\mathcal{S}_{1}^{*},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{k}^{*},{\bm{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{y}}_{k})

\displaystyle=\min_{\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{k}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}_{\ell}\|^{2},

be the optimal objective value with the optimal clusters
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{*},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{k}^{*}
, and let the clusters
\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
generated by the output of the
BnB
algorithm for
\epsilon\geq 0
have the corresponding objective value

\displaystyle v_{\mathtt{KRC}}(\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{k},{\bm{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{y}}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\ell}}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}_{\ell}\|^{2}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle v_{\mathtt{KRC}}(\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{k},{\bm{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{y}}_{k})-v(\mathcal{S}_{1}^{*},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{k}^{*},{\bm{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{y}}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq n(k-1)\epsilon.

Corollary 5.2

(Convergence Guarantee of the
BnB
Algorithm)

The
BnB
algorithm generates an optimal clustering for
\epsilon=0
.

Theorem
5.1
establishes an error bound for the output of Algorithm
2
in terms of the input hyperparameter
\epsilon
. Corollary
5.2
further demonstrates that when
\epsilon=0
, the algorithm operates without approximation, guaranteeing both the convergence and optimality of
BnB
.

Allowing
\epsilon>0
introduces flexibility to the
BnB
algorithm by enabling early pruning of nodes (Line 19 of Algorithm
2
). This reduces computation time while maintaining control over the trade-off between solution quality and computational efficiency. This adaptability offers a significant advantage over the
ES
method, which lacks such flexibility.

We now discuss the computational advantages of
BnB
. As illustrated in Figure
2
,
BnB
constructs the first layer of the tree (nodes
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
) by assigning each ranking vector to a node based on its first entry and computing the lower and upper bounds. This requires approximately
n+d
operations, where
d
represents the number of operations needed to compute the bounds in
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
. Similarly, constructing the second layer (nodes
\phi_{3}
and
\phi_{4}
) requires another
n+d
operations, leading to a total of approximately
2n+2d
operations.

Now, consider a scaled version of the example in Figure
2
, where
m
,
k
,
{\bm{y}}_{1}
,
{\bm{y}}_{2}
, and
\epsilon
remain unchanged, but the set of observation ranking vectors
\mathcal{S}
is expanded to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. The scaled set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
consists of multiple copies of ranking vectors from
\mathcal{S}
, maintaining the original structure while increasing the dataset size, such that
n^{\prime}=|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|\geq n
. A key advantage of
BnB
is that the number of nodes in the search tree remains unchanged across all such scaled instances, ensuring that the number of operations required to compute bounds remains constant. Consequently,
BnB
solves a scaled instance with
n^{\prime}
observation ranking vectors in approximately
2n^{\prime}+2d
operations, demonstrating its computational advantage over the
ES
method, which has a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(4\times 2\times n)=\mathcal{O}(8n)
. As
m
increases, the number of nodes in the decision tree grows, making
BnB
less competitive than the
ES
method for exact clustering when
\epsilon=0
. However, increasing the controlling parameter
\epsilon
helps enhance computational time of the
BnB
algorithm.

We conclude this section by presenting another computational advantage of
BnB
, establishing a worst-case bound on the
BnB
tree depth. Theorem
5.3
leverages the structure of the input ranking vectors to show that when the observation ranking vectors are inherently clustered around distant centroids, the computational time of
BnB
can significantly decrease due to the reduced tree depth.

Theorem 5.3

(Worst-Case Bound on the
BnB
Tree Depth)

Consider
KRC
with
n
observation ranking vectors
\mathcal{S}
,
m
options, and
2
clusters. In a cluster reconstruction step of
KRCA
, let
{\bm{y}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&\dots&m\end{bmatrix}
and
{\bm{y}}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}m&m{-}1&\dots&1\end{bmatrix}
. Assume all observation ranking vectors are within Euclidean distance
\delta\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
to one of the given centroids; that is, for all
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{S}
, either
\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}_{1}\|^{2}\leq\delta^{2}
or
\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}_{2}\|^{2}\leq\delta^{2}
. Assume

with

is applied to assign the observation ranking vectors to the centroids. Then, the depth of the
BnB

\mu(m,\delta)
, where
\mu(m,\delta)
is the minimum of
m
and

\displaystyle\min\left\{\bar{m}\ \Bigg|\ \underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}_{m},\\
\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{m}}(x_{j}-j)^{2}\leq{\delta}^{2}\end{subarray}}{\max}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(x_{j}-j)^{2}\right)\leq\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}_{m},\\

In summary, we presented our efficient approximation algorithm,
KRCA

In this section, we assess the efficacy of the

The output consists of a partition of the ranking vectors into clusters, where each ranking vector is assigned to a cluster, and each cluster is represented by a centroid computed as the mean of the assigned ranking vectors. algorithm, developed for the cluster reconstruction step of the
KRCA
algorithm (Section

), significantly improves the running time of
KRCA
compared to the
ES
method, depending on the problem size and parameter settings.

The numerical experiments were conducted on a Microsoft Windows 11 Enterprise operating system using
MATLAB
R2024b on a machine equipped with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-9700 CPU (3.00 GHz), featuring 8 cores and 8 logical processors, and 32 GB of RAM.

For the baseline solution, we set the maximum iteration limit of the

6.1.4
Clustered Datasets.

We extend our numerical analysis to scenarios where the input dataset is inherently clustered. Our aim is to investigate the performance of the
BnB
method on clustered datasets, as discussed in Section
5.1.2
and Theorem
5.3
.

We begin by describing a procedure for randomly generating clustered ranking vectors. The process involves generating
k
centroids and creating ranking vectors associated with each centroid, ensuring that different clusters remain sufficiently distinct. We first outline the method for generating a single cluster and then extend this approach to create multiple clusters under the specified assumptions. To generate a cluster, we randomly select a ranking vector
{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}
as the centroid. Clustered ranking vectors are constructed by iteratively swapping consecutive entries of
{\bm{y}}
. Specifically, at each step, a pair of consecutive integers
\ell,\ell+1\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
is randomly chosen, and their positions are swapped. This process is repeated
\omega
times to produce a random ranking vector
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}
. For example, consider the centroid
{\bm{y}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3&4\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{X}_{4}
with
\omega=2
. In the first iteration, if
2
and
3
are selected, swapping them results in
\begin{bmatrix}1&3&2&4\end{bmatrix}
. In the second iteration, if
1
and
2
are selected, the final ranking vector becomes
{\bm{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}2&3&1&4\end{bmatrix}
. The squared Euclidean distance between
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
is given by
\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|^{2}=6
. Proposition
6.1
establishes an upper bound on the squared Euclidean distance between the centroid and any ranking vector generated after
\omega
iterations. This bound ensures that the generated vectors stay within a controlled distance from their centroids, maintaining cluster cohesion. Additionally, it ensures that the randomly generated vectors are approximately uniformly distributed within a controlled distance from the given centroid
(
Diaconis and
Shahshahani 1981
)
.

Proposition 6.1

(Upper Bound on Clustered Vector Distance)

Let
{\bm{y}}
be a given centroid in
\mathcal{X}_{m}
, and let the iterative procedure described above be applied
\omega
times to
{\bm{y}}
to generate a random ranking vector
{\bm{x}}
. For
\omega<m
, it almost surely holds that,

\displaystyle\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\omega^{2}.

To create multiple clusters for a given parameter
\omega
, following the insight from Proposition
6.1
, we generate
k
centroids with distances greater than
2\omega^{2}
. Next, one of the
k
centroids is randomly selected, and the iterative random swapping procedure is applied to generate a ranking vector belonging to the corresponding cluster. This process is repeated
n
times to generate all clustered ranking vectors. This approach helps maintain distinct cluster identities while allowing for partial overlap, reflecting realistic scenarios where input data naturally forms clusters that are not entirely disjoint. Additionally, the expected sizes of the clusters are equal.

Figure
7
(b) displays the computational time of the
BnB
method on clustered datasets, with
n=1{,}000{,}000
,
k=5
, and
m\in\{6,7\}
. The parameter
\omega
controls the concentration of clusters: smaller
\omega

\omega
decreases, the computational time of
BnB
drops sharply. In particular, for highly concentrated datasets (e.g.,
\omega\leq 1
),
BnB
completes the assignment step in just a few seconds. These findings align with the tree-depth characterization in Theorem
5.3
and highlight the algorithm’s ability to exploit data regularity for improved efficiency.

6.2.3

7
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presents a robust framework for clustering ranking vectors, referred to as
KRC
, with broad applicability ranging from personalization to large-scale group decision making.
KRC
enables both personalized recommendations, offers, and promotions, and the summarization of collective preferences, thereby supporting more effective decisions.

We present a comprehensive theoretical study of
KRC
, prove its
NP
-hardness, and derive a closed-form solution for the optimal centroid of each cluster. To address the computational challenges, we introduce the
KRCA
algorithm, which iteratively refines initial solutions derived from
KMC
, along with a novel
BnB
algorithm for efficient cluster reconstruction.

Our extensive numerical experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that
KRCA
significantly improves solution quality while maintaining fast computational times. These results highlight the scalability and efficiency of
KRCA
for large datasets, validating its practical utility in real-world applications. Additionally, the alignment of empirical findings with theoretical results reinforces the reliability of our proposed methodology.

In the future, several directions could be explored to extend this work. First, incorporating dynamic ranking preferences, where user preferences evolve over time, could enhance the applicability of
KRCA
to real-time systems. Second, extending
KRCA
to handle weighted ranking vectors, where some preferences carry more significance than others, could broaden its use in multi-criteria decision making problems. Finally, investigating distributed or parallel implementations of
KRCA
would further improve its scalability for handling massive datasets in real-time applications.

To prove Theorem

Definition A.1

(Hypercube Clustering Problem
(

)
)

Consider a set
{\mathcal{S}}=\{{\bm{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{n}\}
, where each
{\bm{z}}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{\eta}
is an
\eta
-dimensional binary vector for
i=1,\ldots,n
. The “Hypercube Clustering Problem” (

) is defined as finding clusters
{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
and corresponding centroids
{\bm{w}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{w}}_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{\eta}
such that the total Hamming distance,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}}f_{\mathtt{H}}({\bm{z}},{\bm{w}}_{\ell}),

is minimized. Here,
f_{\mathtt{H}}({\bm{z}},{\bm{w}}_{\ell})
denotes the Hamming distance between
{\bm{z}}
and
{\bm{w}}_{\ell}
, defined as the number of positions where the entries of
{\bm{z}}
and
{\bm{w}}_{\ell}
differ.

Theorem A.2

(
NP
-Hardness of
HCP

(
Baldi 2012
)
)

The
HCP
is
NP
-hard.

Definition A.3

(Alternating Pair Transformation)

Let
{\bm{z}}=\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}&\cdots&z_{\eta}\end{bmatrix}\in\{0,1\}^{\eta}
be a binary vector. The alternating pair transformation maps
{\bm{z}}
to an
m

{\bm{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&\cdots&x_{m}\end{bmatrix}
, where

, as follows:

\displaystyle x_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}j+z_{(j+1)/2},&\text{for }j=1,3,\ldots,m-1,\\
j-z_{j/2},&\text{for }j=2,4,\ldots,m.\end{cases}

Lemma A.4

(Alternating Pair Ranking Vector)

The alternating pair transformation maps any binary vector
{\bm{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{\eta}
to a vector
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}
, where
m=2\eta
, called an “alternating pair ranking vector”.

Proof.
From Definition
A.3
, since

satisfies the properties of a ranking vector, and therefore,

Definition A.5

(Inverse Alternating Pair Mapping)

Let
{\bm{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&\cdots&x_{m}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}

\displaystyle z_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }(x_{2j-1},x_{2j})=(2j-1,2j),\\

for

Proof of Theorem

.
To prove the

, we conclude that the

-hard.

Consider the instance
\mathtt{HCP}^{\circ}
, consisting of
n

\eta

{\bm{z}}_{1}^{\circ},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{n}^{\circ}
and a clustering number

. The corresponding instance

n

m

{\bm{x}}_{1}^{\circ},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\circ}

m=2\eta

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

\displaystyle=\min_{y_{k}\in\mathcal{R}_{k}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{ik}-y_{k})^{2}+u_{k-1}(\mathcal{R}_{k}\backslash\{y_{k}\})\right\},

(9)

Lemma A.7

(Maximum Dot Product with a Ranking Vector)

Let
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
be a positive vector. Define the ranking vector
{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}
such that its

component represents the rank of the
i^{\text{th}}
coordinate of
{\bm{x}}
when the elements of
{\bm{x}}
are sorted in ascending order. For example, if a component of
{\bm{y}}
equals one, it corresponds to the smallest element of
{\bm{x}}
. In the case of ties (equal coordinates in
{\bm{x}}
), the ranking is determined based on their order of appearance in
{\bm{x}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{c}}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}}\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{c}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\rangle.

Proof.
Let
{\bm{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&\cdots&x_{m}\end{bmatrix}
and
{\bm{y}}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}&\cdots&y_{m}\end{bmatrix}
. From the definition, we know that for any two indices

\displaystyle(c_{j}x_{i}+c_{i}x_{j})-(c_{i}x_{i}+c_{j}x_{j})

This result implies that by exchanging

and
c_{j}
in the ranking vector
{\bm{c}}
, we can improve the inner product with
{\bm{x}}
. Hence, for any given ranking vector
{\bm{c}}
, by iteratively applying this exchange procedure, we can improve the inner product until we reach
{\bm{y}}
, where no further exchanges are possible. At this point,
{\bm{y}}
represents the ranking vector that maximizes the inner product. This completes the proof.

A.9
Proof of Theorem
5.3

Proof.
Fix any level
\bar{m}\in\{1,\dots,m\}
of the
BnB
decision tree and let
\phi
be an arbitrary node on that level. By construction,
\phi
contains

\displaystyle\bar{m}\text{ cuts of the form }x_{\hat{j}}

\displaystyle=j\quad\text{for }\hat{j}\in\{1,\dots,\bar{m}\},

so all ranking vectors in
\mathcal{S}_{\phi}
share the first
\bar{m}
coordinates
f_{\phi}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{\bar{m}})
. Because
BnB
only creates non-empty nodes,
\mathcal{S}_{\phi}\neq\varnothing
. Moreover, every
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\phi}
is within squared Euclidean distance
\delta^{2}
of at least one of the two apposite centroids

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&\cdots&m\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}m&m-1&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}.

We establish the result in four steps, by analyzing the structure of any node at depth
\bar{m}
and showing under what condition it is guaranteed to be pruned.

Step 1: Bounds with respect to
{\bm{y}}_{1}
.

Assume first that
\mathcal{S}_{\phi}
contains at least one vector whose closest centroid is
{\bm{y}}_{1}
. We have
\mathtt{LB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{1}}\leq\delta^{2}
. To decide whether
\phi
can be pruned, we compare the largest upper bound attainable for
{\bm{y}}_{1}
with the smallest lower bound attainable for
{\bm{y}}_{2}
among all nodes whose first
\bar{m}
coordinates satisfy the above constraint. Define

\displaystyle\mathtt{UUB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{1}}

\displaystyle:=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{m}}(x_{j}-j)^{2}\leq\delta^{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(x_{j}-j)^{2},

\displaystyle\mathtt{LLB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{2}}

\displaystyle:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{m}}(x_{j}-j)^{2}\leq\delta^{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{m}}\bigl(x_{j}-(m-j+1)\bigr)^{2},

where,
{\bm{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&\cdots&x_{m}\end{bmatrix}
.

Step 2: Bounds with respect to
{\bm{y}}_{2}
and symmetry.

Assume instead that
\mathcal{S}_{\phi}
contains a point within
\delta
of
{\bm{y}}_{2}
. Symmetric definitions give

\displaystyle\mathtt{UUB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{2}}

\displaystyle:=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{m}}(x_{j}-(m-j+1))^{2}\leq\delta^{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl(x_{j}-(m-j+1)\bigr)^{2},

\displaystyle\mathtt{LLB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{1}}

\displaystyle:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{m}}(x_{j}-(m-j+1))^{2}\leq\delta^{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{m}}(x_{j}-j)^{2}.

Observe that the transformation
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}:=(m{+}1)\mathbf{1}-{\bm{x}}
is its own inverse, applying it twice returns the original vector. Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathtt{UUB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\mathtt{UUB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{2}},

(13)

\displaystyle\mathtt{LLB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\mathtt{LLB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{2}}.

(14)

Step 3: Pruning condition.

Because
\mathtt{UB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}}\leq\mathtt{UUB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}}
and

\mathtt{LB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}}\geq\mathtt{LLB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}}
by definition, the inequality
\mathtt{UUB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{1}}\leq\mathtt{LLB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{2}}
implies
\mathtt{UB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{1}}\leq\mathtt{LB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{2}}
, so node
\phi
is pruned
(no ranking vector in
\mathcal{S}_{\phi}
can be closer to
{\bm{y}}_{2}
than to
{\bm{y}}_{1}
). By symmetry, that is applying (
13
) and (
14
), the same holds with the roles of the two centroids swapped.

Step 4: Depth bound.

Consequently, the tree can branch beyond level
\bar{m}
only if
\mathtt{UUB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{1}}>\mathtt{LLB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{2}}
. Taking the worst-case node on level
\bar{m}
leads exactly to the optimization problems inside the definition of
\mu(m,\delta)
in the theorem statement. Hence the tree becomes empty and the reconstruction step terminates, once the level index reaches

\displaystyle\bar{m}

\displaystyle=\mu(m,\delta):=\min\Bigl\{\bar{m}\mid\mathtt{UUB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{1}}\leq\mathtt{LLB}_{\phi,{\bm{y}}_{2}}\Bigr\},

so the depth is at most
\mu(m,\delta)
. This completes the proof.
∎
