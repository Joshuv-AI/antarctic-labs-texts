Introduction

Yolk sac tumors (YSTs) are a subtype of germ cell tumors that typically arise in the gonads. Approximately 2%–5% of germ cell tumors occur at extragonadal sites [1]. Although specific epidemiological data on YST are lacking, extragonadal YSTs are extremely rare. The retroperitoneum and mediastinum are the most common extragonadal locations, and only a few cases involving the urothelium have been reported. Herein, we report a case of primary pure YST of the urinary bladder, which to the best of our knowledge is the first such report of a bladder YST without a urothelial component. In this case, the patient received etoposide and cisplatin (EP) chemotherapy after transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TURBT).

Case Presentation

A 67‐year‐old man presented to a local clinic with a chief complaint of gross hematuria. Ultrasonography revealed a 6‐cm mass within the bladder. Cystoscopy revealed a papillary tumor arising from the bladder wall (Figure 1). The patient was then referred to our hospital for surgery. Blood tests showed a mildly elevated C‐reactive protein level (0.64 mg/dL) but were otherwise unremarkable. Urinalysis revealed numerous red and white blood cells, was positive for nitrite, and urine cytology was suggestive of malignancy.

Cystoscopic findings showing a large tumor lesion filling the bladder lumen, observed from the bladder neck.

Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) showed a 3.5 × 6 × 5.5 cm mass protruding from the posterior wall of the bladder with no evidence of metastasis (Figure 2a). Magnetic resonance imaging did not clearly demonstrate invasion of the muscular layer (Figure 2b). TURBT was performed for the suspected bladder cancer.

Histopathological examination revealed tumor cells with clear cytoplasm infiltrating the lamina propria. No invasion of the muscularis propria was observed and no urothelial carcinoma components were identified (Figure 3a). These findings suggested the presence of a germ cell tumor, and additional immunohistochemical staining was performed. The tumor cells were positive for alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP), Glypican‐3, and Sal‐like protein 4 (SALL4) but negative for human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) (Figure 3b–e). Based on these results, the tumor was diagnosed as a YST.

Additional evaluation using testicular ultrasonography revealed no abnormalities in the testes. Serum AFP was within the normal range. Positron emission tomography (PET)‐CT revealed no lesions suggestive of a primary tumor outside the bladder. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with a primary pure YST of the bladder. Postoperatively, the patient received four cycles of EP chemotherapy. On the first cycle, the patient developed febrile neutropenia. Other adverse events were limited to Grade 1 anorexia and Grade 2 alopecia, and the patient completed all four cycles of chemotherapy. The patient was followed up for 2 years after treatment, with no evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

Extragonadal germ cell tumors account for approximately 2%–5% of all germ cell tumors, and YSTs are exceedingly rare [1]. Reports involving the urinary tract are particularly limited, with only five cases of primary bladder YST described to date (Table 1). Serum AFP is elevated in 90%–100% of YSTs and is considered a useful marker for detecting recurrence [2]; however, AFP was not measured preoperatively in the present case and therefore did not contribute to the initial diagnosis. The entire TURBT specimen was histologically assessed, and no urothelial carcinoma components were identified. However, some portions were likely affected by cautery artifacts, which could limit the evaluation of tumor morphology and detection of minor components.

Positive immunohistochemical staining for AFP, glypican‐3, and SALL4 and negative hCG were consistent with previous reports and supported a definitive diagnosis of YST [3]. Postoperative ultrasonography and PET‐CT did not reveal any primary tumors in the testes or other organs, supporting the diagnosis of a primary bladder origin.

Several hypotheses have been proposed regarding the pathogenesis of extragonadal germ cell tumors. One widely discussed theory is that primordial germ cells aberrantly migrate during embryogenesis and become sequestered along midline structures, subsequently giving rise to tumors. Another concept is the “burned‐out tumor,” in which a regressed gonadal primary lesion is no longer detectable and only extragonadal metastatic lesions remain. Previous reports described ultrasonographic findings suggestive of a regressed testicular lesion and retroperitoneal lymph node metastases as common features [4]. Neither was present in our case, making this diagnosis unlikely. Independent of these ideas, some investigations have suggested that germ cell lineage tumors may arise de novo through specific genetic alterations [5, 6, 7]. More recently, Espejo‐Herrera et al. introduced the concept of a somatically derived yolk sac tumor (SD‐YST), in which a pre‐existing somatic carcinoma differentiates into a YST [5]. Among the previously reported bladder YSTs, four of the five cases were accompanied by urothelial carcinoma, and SD‐YST was considered a plausible mechanism in these cases. However, in the present case, no coexisting urothelial carcinoma or other somatic malignancies were identified pathologically, and no findings supported the diagnosis of SD‐YST. Furthermore, because the tumor was confined to the bladder and located along the midline, the aberrant migration of primordial germ cells was considered the most plausible mechanism.

No standard treatment has been established for extragonadal YSTs, and treatment generally follows gonadal YST strategies with cisplatin‐based chemotherapy7. In previously reported bladder YST cases, urothelial carcinoma components and muscular invasion were observed, and the patients subsequently underwent radical cystectomy and chemotherapy. In the present case, no urothelial carcinoma component was identified. The tumor was diagnosed as a pure YST. Therefore, systemic chemotherapy was administered without radical cystectomy.

Cisplatin‐based chemotherapy, particularly bleomycin/etoposide/cisplatin (BEP) chemotherapy, is commonly employed; however, bleomycin is associated with increased pulmonary toxicity in patients over 40 years of age [8]. Although robust evidence regarding the optimal duration of chemotherapy for extragonadal YSTs is lacking, 3–4 cycles of BEP chemotherapy are widely accepted [9, 10, 11]. EP chemotherapy is an effective alternative when bleomycin cannot be used. Despite the absence of clear contraindications to bleomycin, four cycles of EP chemotherapy were selected considering his advanced age and increased risk of bleomycin‐associated pulmonary toxicity.

Regarding prognosis, only one case of cancer‐related death at 9 months was identified among previously reported cases, as described by Espejo‐Herrera et al. [5]. Guo et al. reported that serum AFP levels decrease rapidly after tumor resection but increase during tumor recurrence or metastasis [12]. Although preoperative serum AFP was not measured, AFP was 6.7 ng/mL at 1 month after surgery and before chemotherapy. It was within the normal range and remained normal throughout the 2‐year follow‐up, possibly reflecting complete tumor resection by TURBT.

The favorable clinical course observed after TURBT followed by EP chemotherapy in this extremely rare primary pure bladder YST may provide meaningful insights for future therapeutic decision‐making.

Conclusion

We encountered a rare case of a primary pure YST of the urinary bladder. The patient underwent TURBT followed by four cycles of EP chemotherapy and has remained in remission during 2 years of follow‐up.
