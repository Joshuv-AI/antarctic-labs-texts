Knowledge, attitude and use of menstrual cup among females of Siddharthanagar Municipality, Nepal: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Amisha Bhattarai, Vijaya Laxmi Shrestha, Amrit Bist
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesAlthough menstrual cups have a long history and numerous benefits, many women of reproductive age remain unaware. This study assessed the knowledge, attitude and use regarding menstrual cups and its associated factors among females of reproductive age in Siddharthanagar Municipality, Lumbini Province, Nepal.DesignCross-sectional studySettingSiddharthanagar municipality, Rupandehi was taken as the study setting.ParticipantA total of 250 women of reproductive age group (15–49 years) residing in Siddharthanagar Municipality.Outcome measureKnowledge, attitude and use of menstrual cups were assessed by a pretested structured tool. The knowledge scores for each question were given as one mark for a correct response, and the overall scores were categorised using a modified Bloom’s cut-off as follows: good knowledge≥60% and poor knowledge<60%. Attitudes scores were grouped into three categories: favourable (>80%), neutral (60%–80%) and unfavourable (<60%). Practice was assessed by a single question whether they use a menstrual cup. Pearson’s χ2 test and multivariable logistic regression analysis were performed to determine factors associated with knowledge, attitude and use at the 5% level of significance.ResultsThis study found that the majority (88.04%) had poor knowledge about the menstrual cup, with 31.2% of participants expressing a favourable attitude. Only 10.6% of participants had used a menstrual cup. The odds of adequate knowledge among the participants with bachelor and higher level of education was 3.470 times (adjusted OR (aOR)=3.470, 95% CI 1.08 to 11.10) as compared with participants with secondary or lower level of education, adjusting for other explanatory variables. The odds of having adequate knowledge among participants who were employed was 1.66 times (aOR=1.66, 95% CI 1.24 to 5.83) compared with those who were unemployed, which was 2.487 times (aOR=2.487, 95% CI 1.24 to 7.95) in case of favourable attitude. Similarly, the odds of using a menstrual cup among the participants with an adequate level of knowledge was 7.960 times (aOR=7.960, 95% CI 2.70 to 23.40) as compared with participants who had an inadequate level of knowledge, adjusting for other explanatory variables.ConclusionOur study revealed that, despite their advantages, participants have limited knowledge and usage of menstrual cups. This highlights the need for advocacy and education about menstrual cups to enable informed choices, enhance women’s quality of life and protect the environment by reducing waste from disposable menstrual products.Keywords: Attitude, Nepal, Knowledge

Introduction

Menstrual hygiene management (MHM) is a crucial aspect of women’s health that significantly impacts quality of life, especially in low- and middle-income countries. The WHO and UNICEF in 2012 defined MHM as ‘Women and adolescent girls using a clean menstrual management material to absorb or collect blood that can be changed in privacy as often as necessary for the duration of the menstruation period, using soap and water for washing the body as required and having access to facilities to dispose of used menstrual management materials’.1,3 Globally, women and girls face numerous challenges related to menstruation, including lack of awareness, cultural stigma and limited access to sustainable menstrual products.3 Despite the WHO advocacy to recognise menstrual health as a human right issue, only two out of the five schools worldwide are estimated to provide proper education for menstrual health.4 5

In Nepal, menstrual health practices are heavily influenced by sociocultural beliefs, especially in rural and semiurban areas. For instance, the practice of ‘chhaupadi’, which involves isolating menstruating women and expelling them from home at the time of menstruation, is still prevalent despite government efforts to prevent it by legal measures. The cultural barriers and limited resources have further limited hygiene practices. Limited access to safe and affordable menstrual hygiene products often compels women to resort to alternatives, such as old clothes, which may lead to discomfort and a higher risk of infections.6 These sociocultural barriers and economic constraints place additional burdens on menstruating women in Nepal, impacting their well-being and reinforcing gender inequalities.6

Menstrual cups offer a promising, cost-effective alternative to traditional menstrual products like pads and clothes. Made from medical-grade silicone, they are reusable for up to 5 years, capable of holding 10–38 mL of blood depending on the type and flow, and require monthly sterilisation through boiling.7,9 Due to their durability, comfort, ecofriendliness and low risk of health complications, menstrual cups are a sustainable option that can help address the limitations associated with single-use products.10 Studies indicate that menstrual cups support healthy vaginal microbiota and are generally well accepted by users, leading to fewer reported cases of vaginal health issues compared with other products.3 11 Despite these advantages, the uptake of menstrual cups remains low due to factors like limited awareness, fear of insertion and concerns about leakage, especially in contexts with minimal prior exposure to such products.12,14

Proper MHM is essential to achieving several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), such as gender equality, good health and well-being, water and sanitation and quality education.15 SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) is directly supported by better management of menstrual hygiene since it lowers the risk of infections and enhances the quality of life. Allowing women and girls to fully engage in economic and educational activities also supports SDG 5 (Gender Equality). Additionally, by lowering waste and encouraging ecofriendly behaviours, supporting sustainable menstruation products like menstrual cups helps achieve SDGs 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation) and 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production).16 17 These challenges, when unaddressed, can lead to significant social, educational and health impacts, as women collectively spend an estimated 8 years of their lives menstruating.18 19 In low-resource settings, the lack of access to menstrual products and sanitation facilities can worsen these issues.18 20 21

Despite the potential benefits, we suspect that comprehensive research on the knowledge, attitudes and usage patterns of menstrual cups in Nepal is limited. Understanding these factors contributing to the knowledge and attitude towards menstrual cups is essential for designing effective, region-specific initiatives that encourage sustainable menstrual practices and empower women. This study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes and menstrual cup use among females of reproductive age and its associated factors in Siddharthanagar Municipality, Rupandehi, Nepal. The selected demographic is particularly significant, encompassing women actively menstruating and most likely to benefit from sustainable menstrual products like menstrual cups. By understanding their knowledge and attitudes, the study aims to identify barriers to adoption and provide insights for designing effective, region-specific interventions. Targeting this group addresses immediate health and hygiene needs and fosters long-term empowerment and gender equality by promoting informed choices and sustainable practices. The study seeks to bridge the existing knowledge gaps and promote menstrual cup adoption as a viable, sustainable solution. The findings aim to contribute to ongoing efforts to improve MHM, reduce gender disparities and advance sustainable development in Nepal.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted among women of reproductive age (15–49 years) residing in the Siddharthanagar municipality. Siddharthanagar municipality is located in the southern part of Rupandehi district, Lumbini Province, Nepal, with 15 871 households having a population of approximately 74 436 residents, where women of reproductive age account for 29.67% of the total population.22 This municipality of Rupandehi district was selected for its diverse sociocultural, economic landscape and diverse representation of the nation’s castes and ethnicities and major religions, namely, Hinduism, Islam and Buddhism.23 While access to education and healthcare services is better than in many rural areas, disparities persist due to economic inequality and cultural norms, making it essential to study how these factors influence MHM.24

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula to estimate a proportion (n=z2pq/d2) at a 95% CI and a 5% margin of error. In the absence of prior studies assessing menstrual cup awareness among women of reproductive age in Nepal, we used findings from a study conducted among women of reproductive age in Karnataka, India, which reported an awareness level of 82%. Although this prevalence is relatively higher, it was selected as the closest available reference for a comparable population and construct.25 Considering these past proportions, the initial sample size was estimated to be 227, optimised to 250, adjusting the 10% non-response rate.

We selected all 13 wards within Siddharthanagar Municipality. The information on the total number of women aged 15–49 years residing within the wards was obtained from the health section of the municipality. Based on the estimate provided by the municipality, the total number of samples to be drawn from each of the wards was calculated considering the proportion of the total women aged 15–49 years residing inside the ward. The participants from the wards were selected randomly (online supplemental file 1)

Participants

The women who were in their reproductive age (15–49 years) and permanent residents of the Siddharthanagar municipality were eligible to participate in the study. If there were more than one eligible participant in the selected household, only one was chosen randomly. The exclusion criteria included women who are temporary residents or migrants and women with any medical condition or pregnancy that precludes them from using period products or taking part in menstruation-related surveys.

Data collection

The data were collected through face-to-face interviews, using a structured questionnaire as an interview guideline from February to March 2024. After acquiring their informed consent, the interviews were conducted in the participant’s household. The data collector was well trained in adolescent sexual and reproductive health and is a public health professional. The participants were well oriented about the objective of this study, their rights to withdraw and voluntary participation. For the participants under 18 years of age, written parental consent and participant’s assent were taken. To maintain privacy, interviews were conducted in a private area within the home, away from other household members, and participants were assured that their responses would remain confidential and be used solely for research purposes. The questionnaire consisted of four sections where the first section consisted of questions related to their sociodemographic profile, followed by the second, third and fourth sections, which assessed their knowledge and attitude towards the menstrual cup and its use, respectively. The average time for the interview was about 25–30 min.

The tools were developed through the extensive literature review in English and translated into Nepali before the pretesting. The forward and backward translation was conducted in consultation with subject experts. In order to further validate the reliability of the tool, the questionnaire was reviewed by an expert in reproductive health before the study was conducted. Before real application, the tool was pretested for 10% of a similar population outside the study area to make modifications for increased clarity and question precision. The internal consistency of the tool was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding a value of 0.81, which indicates good reliability (online supplemental file 2)

Study variables

Exploratory variables

The sociodemographic variables included variables, such as age of the participant, ethnicity based on the ethnic codes as defined by the health management information system,26 education referring to the highest level of academic grade completed by the participant, occupation referring to the current economic status of the participant, socioeconomic status based on Kuppuswamy socioeconomic status,27 family type, marital status of the participant and living arrangements referring to either the participant is living alone or with their family or someone else.

Outcome variable

The outcome variables assessed by a pretested tool were the knowledge, attitude regarding menstrual cups and the use of menstrual cups. The knowledge on menstrual cups involved ten questions covering various aspects, such as working mechanism, using technique, material used, cleaning methods, source of information, benefits, side effects, availability and cost. Eight questions were specifically chosen to evaluate the overall knowledge. Each correct response received one score, while incorrect or unknown answers were scored as 0. Knowledge scores were classified using Bloom’s cut-off criteria. Bloom’s criteria are based on the educational framework originally proposed by Benjamin Bloom, which classifies learning into cognitive levels ranging from basic recall of facts to higher order thinking. In public health research, Bloom’s criteria are commonly adapted to evaluate knowledge by converting raw scores into percentage scores that reflect the extent to which respondents have mastered the content being assessed. Knowledge was considered good if the score was≥60% and poor if the score was<60%. Although previous studies used Bloom’s cut-off point as 80%–100% being good scores, 60%–79% being moderate scores and<60% and below for poor scores, our team created two subdivisions instead of three. The ‘moderate’ category was merged with the category of ‘good’ with a score of 60%–100%; the other category, ‘poor’ remains the same as<60%. This was done to better distribute the survey’s scores into more distinct categories, given the number of questions for each knowledge section.28

Attitudes towards menstrual cups were evaluated using 11 statements on a five-point Likert scale. Responses ranged from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’.9 These statements specifically assess the perceptions of menstrual cup convenience compared with other sanitary products, concerns about infection risk with improper use, environmental benefits and societal attitudes, including myths about virginity and cultural acceptance. Scores were then analysed and categorised according to Bloom’s cut-off criteria: a score of 35–44 (>80%) indicated a favourable attitude, a score of 26–34 (60%–80%) indicated a neutral attitude and a score of 0–25 (<60%) indicated an unfavourable attitude.29 The practice section consisted of only one question about whether the participant used menstrual cups.

Statistical analysis

The data were entered using MS Excel using the validation command to minimise the error. The 10% of the entered data were randomly checked manually for review, and no data entry error was observed. The data were imported to Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) V.21.0 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics, such as mean, median, SD, frequency and percentage, were calculated. Pearson’s χ2 test and logistic regression were applied to assess the association between different independent and dependent variables at a 95% CI and 5% significance level. The variables with p value<0.25 in bivariate analysis were included in the final model for multivariable analysis to calculate the adjusted OR (aOR). The variance inflation factor (VIF) test was performed among selected independent variables to be subject to the regression model to manage the issue of multicollinearity. The VIF greater than 2 was taken as an indication of multicollinearity. The variables with p value<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Among the 250 female participants, the mean age was 25.96±6.732 years, with ages ranging from 15 to 48 years. The largest age group was 15–24, accounting for 52% of the sample. The majority were Brahmin/Chhetri (44.8%) and identified as Hindu (93.6%). Almost half (49.2%) had attained a bachelor’s degree or higher. Regarding occupation, 42.4% were employed, and 57.6% were unemployed. More than half (56.4%) lived in joint families, while 43.6% resided in nuclear families. Most participants were classified as upper middle class (44.8%), followed by lower middle (28%). Additionally, 62.4% were unmarried, and 88% lived with their families (table 1).

Discussion

Our study highlights a significant disparity in knowledge about menstrual cups among participants. Only 11.6% of the participants demonstrated good knowledge, while a substantial 88.4% had poor knowledge. This result is consistent with a 2023 study conducted in Bangalore, India, which reported that only 11.9% of participants had an average knowledge of menstrual cups.30 This similarity may be attributed to the comparable age groups of participants in both studies.

In contrast, a study conducted at Sree Uthradom Thirunal Academy of Medical Sciences, Vencode, India, in 2022 reported that 73.7% of participants had good knowledge about menstrual cups.31 This discrepancy might be due to differences in study settings and local advocacy efforts, which can significantly impact awareness and knowledge levels.

Despite the awareness of menstrual cups, only 10.6% of participants reported using them, similar to the 6.6% adoption rate observed among Turkish women,32 but lower than the 15.1% in South Kerala,29 and 12.8% in North Kerala.13 This lower adoption rate could be due to differences in sociodemographic factors and study settings. Variations in cultural attitudes, economic conditions and accessibility may affect menstrual cups’ perceived practicality and acceptance. In contrast, South and North Kerala may have more supportive environments or targeted interventions that facilitate higher adoption rates.

Our study revealed that nearly half (47.1%) of the participants had a neutral attitude towards menstrual cups, while 31.2% had a favourable attitude, and 21.7% had an unfavourable attitude. This distribution mirrors findings from another Indian study, where 64.5% had a neutral attitude, 31% were favourable and 4.5% were unfavourable.14 The attitude variation could be attributed to differences in study settings, participants’ age groups and knowledge levels.

In bivariable analysis, this study found significant associations between knowledge levels and education (p=0.011), occupation (p=0.049) and socioeconomic status (p=0.028). This is consistent with a 2022 study in South Kerala, which also reported associations with these variables but differed regarding marital status.31 The sociocultural and economic circumstances of Nepal and South Kerala may differ, which could explain the observed disparity in marital status. Marital status may be positioned as a crucial predictor of social capital and access to resources in South Kerala due to cultural norms and familial duties, which may impact knowledge acquisition.33 On the other hand, in Nepal, the relative significance of marital status may be lessened due to factors, such as gender inequality, literacy rates and the socioeconomic dynamics of rural and urban areas having a greater impact on knowledge levels.34

This study found an association between menstrual cup use and age (p=0.007) and occupation (p=0.049), but no associations with other sociodemographic reproductive variables. Age may affect the use of menstruation cups in Nepal because of generational disparities in information availability, cultural perspectives and exposure to other menstrual hygiene items. Due to growing knowledge through social media, peer influence and campaigns supporting sustainable menstruation products, younger people, especially those living in urban areas, are more likely to use menstrual cups. On the other hand, because of ingrained cultural customs and restricted access to more modern options, older generations might be less familiar with menstruation cups and more likely to stick with conventional goods like sanitary pads and cloth.35

The use of menstrual cups is also significantly influenced by occupation. There may be greater access to menstrual cups and more opportunities to learn about their advantages for individuals working in urban, health-conscious or professional occupations, such as healthcare or education. These jobs frequently expose workers to workplaces that promote sustainable products or health education initiatives. On the other hand, people who live in rural areas or work in manual, agricultural or low-paying jobs might encounter obstacles to adopting menstrual cups, such as restricted access to these products, a lack of knowledge and possible cultural opposition to implementing new menstrual practices.

The level of knowledge was substantially correlated with participants’ socioeconomic status (aOR=0.05, 95% CI 0.005 to 0.69), education (aOR=4.76, 95% CI 1.36 to 16.7) and usage of menstrual cups (aOR=7.69, 95% CI 2.08 to 28.40) in the multivariable analysis. These results are consistent with the larger picture in Nepal, where socioeconomic level and education play a significant role in determining health literacy and access to contemporary menstruation products. People with more education and a better socioeconomic status are more likely to know about the usage of menstruation cups and to have the money to buy and keep them. These findings were not supported by comparable studies, suggesting a possible field for additional study in Nepal.

This study has some limitations, including urban focus, self-reported data biases and lack of qualitative insights. The finding of this study reveals low menstrual cup knowledge and use among women, influenced by education and socioeconomic factors. Practical education, addressing myths and highlighting benefits like safety and cost-effectiveness, is essential.

Conclusion

This study found that women of reproductive age have limited knowledge and use of menstrual cups despite their benefits. Significant associations were found with education, occupation, socioeconomic status and menstrual cup use. Awareness exists, but sociodemographic factors and perceived challenges influence actual usage. Given the economic and environmental advantages of menstrual cups, increasing their uptake in resource-limited areas should be a priority. We recommend that targeted awareness campaigns, community-based education and integration of menstrual cup information into the existing reproductive health services be implemented. Partnerships with local health workers and female community health volunteers can further support culturally sensitive education and demonstration sessions. Ensuring affordability through subsidies or social marketing initiatives may also help improve acceptability and long-term adoption of menstrual cups among women.
