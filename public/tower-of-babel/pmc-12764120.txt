Shorten swallowing time and its implication in patients with chronic cough: a prospective observational study
Authors: Li Zhang, Jing Pan, Wenbo Shi, Mengru Zhang, Alimire Aierken, Ran Dong, Qiang Chen, Zhongmin Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with chronic cough (CC) frequently exhibit swallowing dysfunction related to laryngeal hypersensitivity. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical utility of swallowing time (ST) measurement in the etiological assessment and management of CC.MethodsThe ST was measured in 176 patients with CC and 82 healthy controls. Follow-up assessments were conducted among 49 patients with gastroesophageal reflux-associated cough (GERC) and 86 with other causes of CC (non-GERC). Associations between ST and clinical features were analyzed, and the predictive performance of ST for guiding treatment intensification was evaluated in GERC patients and compared with the Hull Airway Reflux Questionnaire (HARQ) and Reflux Symptom Index (RSI).ResultsThe ST was significantly shorter in CC than in healthy controls (1.167 ± 0.446 s vs. 1.798 ± 0.721 s, p < 0.001), and varied significantly among etiological subtypes (p = 0.019). ST was notably reduced in GERC compared to non-GERC patients, showing a specificity of 85.7% but sensitivity of 46.5%. In GERC patients, ST correlated negatively with RSI and HARQ scores, and positively with capsaicin cough threshold (C5). Shorter ST (< 1.015 s) was associated with proximal reflux events and greater need for intensified anti-reflux therapy (χ2 = 17.686, p < 0.001). ST also exhibited superior predictive accuracy over HARQ and RSI scores.ConclusionSwallowing reflex hypersensitivity is prevalent in CC, especially in GERC. ST assessment provides valuable information for etiological differentiation and helps guide optimal treatment intensification in GERC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-04015-y.Keywords: Swallowing time, Chronic cough, Gastroesophageal reflux, Swallowing reflex, Irritable larynx

Background

Cough is a vital physiological protective reflex to prevent aspiration into the airways and is interconnected to the swallowing via the laryngeal nerve [1]. Swallowing, on the other hand, is a highly coordinated physiologic process involving three distinct phases: oral, pharyngeal, and esophageal [2]. The onset of the pharyngeal phase is an involuntary response regulated by the responsible brainstem neural networks [2]. This reflexive pharyngeal swallowing may be also a defense mechanism to against the aspiration into the larynx and lower airway [1]. In some individuals, these protective reflexes can become excessive and inappropriately integrated, such as chronic cough (CC), which is problematic and persistent and is characterized by increased cough reflex sensitivity [3].

A large body of evidence have suggested that the sensory neural fibers which are widely distributed in the laryngo-hypopharyngeal mucosa may respond to chemical irritants and trigger cough and swallow reflexes [4, 5]. The irritable larynx or laryngeal hyperresponsiveness also usually produce a swallowing-related cough [6]. In clinical settings, 40–100% of CC patients report experiencing at least one abnormal laryngopharyngeal symptom, such as postnasal drip, frequent throat clearing, hoarseness, and globus hystericus, indicating potential abnormalities in the neural networks of the laryngopharynx [7]. Additionally, there is often a temporal association between cough and swallowing in CC patients [2]. This association may arise from the fact that the same afferent and efferent pathways utilized for coughing are also involved in the swallowing sequence. Consequently, coughing can induce the urge to swallow, or conversely, swallowing can trigger a cough reflex [2]. This suggests an inappropriate swallowing reflex resulting from altered neural or muscular function in the upper airways of CC. However, this hypothesis has not been extensively studied.

Currently, there are no established diagnostic criteria or tools available for swallowing disorders. Instead, the injection of water into the supra-pharynx is a widely used method, since it can effectively elicit water-induced swallowing, primarily in the laryngopharynx [8–12]. The measurement of water swallowing time (ST) provides a quantitative analysis of the swallowing reflex and may indirectly reflect the laryngeal sensitivity [13]. Thus, this prospective clinical study aimed to validate the aforementioned hypothesis and evaluate the utility of ST as a diagnostic and predictive tool for CC.

Methods

Study population

Consecutive patients with CC were recruited from the respiratory clinic of Tongji Hospital between June 2017 and September 2019. Inclusion criteria for participation were as follows: (1) aged between 18 and 80 years old; (2) cough as the only or main symptom persisting for at least eight weeks; (3) absence of other symptoms such as dyspnea, fever, or hemoptysis; (4) forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio ≥ 70% and FEV1 > 80% of predicted; (5) completion of the Hull Airway Reflux Questionnaire (HARQ) and Reflux Symptom Index (RSI) score correctly; (6) cooperating well with ST test. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pregnant or breastfeeding women; (2) individuals with difficulties in reading or writing; (3) individuals whose cough had markedly improved with specific treatment prior to the study; (4) history of pharyngeal surgery, injury, or severe neck disease; (5) swallowing disorders caused by neurological diseases. Eighty-two healthy controls were recruited from medical students, staff, and their relatives. None of these subjects had a history of smoking, chronic diseases, and cough in the past 8 weeks.

Gastroesophageal reflux-related cough (GERC) was diagnosed using multichannel intraluminal impedance and pH monitoring (MII-pH) based on at least one of the following criteria: (I) acid exposure time (AET) > 6%; (II) symptom association probability (SAP) ≥ 95%; or (III) more than 80 reflux episodes within 24 h. GERC was further classified as acid GERC (AET > 6% and/or acid reflux episodes > 80/24 h; SAP ≥ 95% for acid reflux) or non-acid GERC (non-acid reflux episodes > 80/24 h; SAP ≥ 95% for non-acid reflux). All enrolled GERC patients underwent MII-pH monitoring prior to the initiation of any anti-reflux therapy. Those diagnosed with GERC received standard anti-reflux therapy comprising omeprazole (20 mg twice daily) and domperidone (10 mg three times daily) for four weeks. If standard treatment failed, an intensified regimen was required, which involved either double-dose omeprazole or adjunctive neuromodulators therapy for another four weeks (see Supplementary Table 1). Diagnosis of non-GERC was made based on the positive etiological evidence or successful response to targeted therapeutic trials according to the previously described stepwise diagnostic algorithm [14]. Patients who did not respond to disease-specific therapy were excluded from this study.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital (No. LL(H)−2016–354). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [15].

ST measurement

The swallowing reflex was induced by a bolus injection of 1 mL of sterile water into the supra-pharynx using a small nasal catheter (6F, 2 mm internal diameter) with the participant in a seated position, in accordance with established methods [9–12, 16]. The catheter tip was correctly positioned in the supra-pharynx trans-nasal insertion until a sense of resistance was felt, then withdrawn approximately 1 cm, to achieve a final depth of 12–14 cm. Sterile water was rapidly injected within 0.5 s to ensure consistent stimulus delivery between trials and minimize mechanical provocation [10]. The ST was defined as the interval from the initiation of water injection to the onset of swallowing, identified visually by laryngeal elevation (Adam’s apple movement) and timed with a digital stopwatch [9]. Participants were blinded to the exact injection moment, which was consistently delivered near the end of expiration. To ensure reproducibility and minimize inter-observer variability, all ST measurements were performed by a single, trained examiner (author J.P.) adhering strictly to a standardized protocol [9, 11]. Each participant underwent two consecutive measurements, and the average value was used for analysis. Excellent intra-observer reproducibility was confirmed in a pilot study of 30 participants, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.70–0.96, p = 0.003) under a one-way random-effects model.

Study design and procedures

This was a single-center observational study. When patients with CC attended specialist cough clinics, general information was collected and cough condition was assessed by cough symptom score (CSS) [17], RSI [18] and HARQ [19]. All subjects underwent capsaicin cough challenge test [20, 21], ST measurement, and sputum induction. MII-pH was performed [22] when suspicious GERC was considered. Patients in this study underwent follow-up every two weeks, with evaluations of symptom changes and therapeutic responses at each visit. Cough was considered to be resolved when cough disappeared completely, or CSS decreased by ≥ 50% [22].

Statistical analysis

Based on our pilot data (n = 20 per group), the mean ST was 1.302 ± 0.311 s in CC patients and 1.823 ± 0.472 s in healthy controls. We aimed to detect a clinically meaningful difference of 1.0 s using a two-sample t-test with a two-sided α of 0.05 and 90% statistical power. To reflect routine clinical practice, we adopted a 2:1 allocation (CC: controls), yielding 128 CC and 64 control participants by PASS 2022 Power Analysis and Sample Size Software (NCSS, LLC. Kaysville, Utah, USA, ncss.com/software/pass) [23]. Allowing for a 20% dropout rate in the CC group, we enrolled 160 CC patients and retained 64 controls, giving a final sample of 224 subjects for robust group comparisons.

Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas skewed distributions were expressed as median with a 25%−75% interquartile range (IQR). Between-group differences were made assessed using one-way ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis test, unpaired Student’s t-test, Chi-squared test, and Mann–Whitney U-test, as appropriate. Spearman’s rank correlation analyses were conducted to evaluate associations between two continuous variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to assess the predictive value, where applicable. Consistency analysis was performed by Kappa test. Statistical analysis was processed using SPSS 23.0 software (Chicago, IL, USA), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between June 2017 and September 2019, 282 participants were screened, with 266 meeting inclusion criteria (181 CC patients, 85 healthy controls). Five participants with CC and three healthy controls were subsequently excluded due to withdrawal of consent or intolerance of the ST measurement. Ultimately, a total of 258 subjects were included in the baseline analysis.

Among 176 patients with CC, 157 had etiological diagnoses (135 with single-cause and 22 with multi-causes), while 11 were classified as refractory or unexplained. Eight additional patients were excluded owing to lost to follow-up, consent withdrawal, or adverse events. Per-protocol analysis included 49 single-cause GERC patients (19 acid, 30 non-acid) and 86 non-GERC patients. Non-GERC etiologies comprised atopic cough (AC, n = 28), cough variant asthma (CVA, n = 23), eosinophilic bronchitis (EB, n = 14), upper airway cough syndrome (UACS, n = 5), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor-related cough (n = 4), and other conditions (n ≤ 3 cases each). Among GERC patients, 17 responded to standard anti-reflux therapy and 32 required intensified therapy, including increased omeprazole for six patients or neuromodulators (7 with baclofen, 14 with gabapentin, 5 with flupentixol/melitracen) (Supplementary Table 1). Difference in ST value

Compared to the healthy volunteers, the mean STs of CC patients were significantly shorter (1.167 ± 0.446 s vs. 1.798 ± 0.721 s, p < 0.001, Fig. 2A). A significant difference in the ST value was observed (H = 13.49, p = 0.019) among individuals with different single etiology (Fig. 2B). The ST in GERC patients was distinctly shorter than in patients with AC (p = 0.029), CVA (p = 0.006), and other etiologies (p = 0.010), with a moderate specificity but poor sensitivity (Table 2). Significant difference was also observed between GERC and non-GERC (p = 0.001) (Fig. 2C). However, the ST of EB and UACS was comparable with GERC.

Correlation between ST and clinical characteristics in GERC

ST value showed a mild negative correlation with RSI score (r = −0.348, p = 0.014) and HARQ score (r = −0.409, p = 0.003) (Fig. 3A-B). Specifically, an inverse correlation was observed between ST and the item “cleaning throat” in both RSI and HARQ scores, as well as “A tickle in your throat, or a lump in your throat”, “Cough with eating”, and “Cough brought on by singing or speaking” in the HARQ score (Supplementary Table 2). Although no statistical correlation was examined between ST and C2, a low positive correlation was found with C5 (r = 0.322, p = 0.022) (Fig. 3C-D).

Prediction of therapeutic protocols for GERC patients by ST

Patients who required intensified therapy showed a shorter ST (Z = −3.544, p < 0.001), with the area (%) under the ROC curve (AUC) of ST being 82.2 (Fig. 4 and Table 3). Based on a Youden index of 0.649, the optimal cut-off value for ST in determining the need for intensified anti-reflux therapy was identified as 1.015 s.

Then, all GERC patients who received successful anti-reflux therapy were divided into two cohorts (cohort one: ST ≥ 1.015 s; cohort two: ST < 1.015 s). Cohort one presented higher AET (p = 0.032), DeMeester score (p = 0.024), and more acidic reflux events (p = 0.043) while cohort two demonstrated markedly higher RSI scores (p = 0.031), HARQ scores (p = 0.007), non-acid SAP (p = 0.039), more frequent weakly acidic reflux (p = 0.016), gas reflux (p = 0.033) and proximal reflux events (p = 0.009) (Table 4). A shorter ST was more commonly observed in non-acid GERC patients compared to acid GERC patients (76.7% versus 42.1%; χ2 = 5.979, p = 0.014). Additionally, patients in cohort one exhibited a significantly better response to standard anti-reflux therapy and were less likely to require intensified treatment (χ2 = 17.686, p < 0.001). In contrast, those in cohort two showed a superior response to add-on use of Gabapentin (the most commonly prescribed neuromodulator in the intensified regimen) (χ2 = 4.250, p = 0.039) (Table 5).

Comparison in predicting therapeutic intensification for GERC

In comparison, both the ST and HARQ score had a better ability than the RSI score in determining the need for intensified anti-reflux therapy in GERC patients, with an overlap rate of 55.8% between the two measures. However, the HARQ score with a cut-off point of 27 had a lower sensitivity, negative predictive value, Youden index, and κ value than the ST of 1.015 s, while showed a higher specificity and similar positive predictive value (Table 6). The predictive value could be further improved when the combination of ST and HARQ score is considered (Fig. 5A). Additionally, ST and HARQ exclusively predicted 27.9% and 9.3% of GERC cases, respectively. Only 7.0% of GERC cases failed to be predicted by either ST value or the HARQ score (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

The ST value, which captures the latency of the water-induced swallowing reflex, serves as an indirect indicator of laryngopharyngeal function and sensitivity. This study demonstrated that the ST was significantly shorter in patients with CC than in healthy controls, with the most notable reduction observed in those with GERC, suggesting the presence of swallowing reflex hypersensitivity. Among GERC patients, ST correlated inversely with RSI and HARQ scores and positively with capsaicin cough threshold (C5). Those with shorter ST were more likely to exhibit non-acid and proximal reflux episodes and showed the more need to intensified anti-reflux therapy. Additionally, ST measurement exhibited higher predictive accuracy for determining therapeutic intensification compared to HARQ or RSI scores in the management of GERC. These findings support the hypothesis that ST may serve as a clinical marker of laryngeal hypersensitivity-a feature common in GERC and could aid in personalizing treatment intensification.

Swallowing reflex hypersensitivity emerges from integrated peripheral-central neural sensitization and maladaptive neuroplasticity. Irritant exposure (e.g., refluxate) trigger local inflammation that lowers the activation threshold of vagal C-fibers innervating the laryngopharynx. Persistent nociceptive input drives central sensitization within the nucleus tractus solitarius, amplifying synaptic gain and promoting aberrant convergence of pharyngeal sensory signals onto swallowing-pattern generators [2]. Therefore, physiologically innocuous laryngopharyngeal stimuli recruit hyperexcitable neural circuits, precipitating exaggerated or premature swallowing reflexes. Paradoxically, while water-triggered swallowing typically assesses impaired reflexes (delayed > 3 s) [9, 10], we observed a shortened ST in almost CC patients (175 out of 176) versus approximately 10% delayed responses in controls (8 out of 82). The accelerated reflex exposure to water in our CC patients reflects the high prevalence of laryngeal hypersensitivity in CC, consistent with documented laryngeal hyperresponsiveness to stimuli [24]. Water, acting as a chemical stimulus for nerve fibers [8], explains reduced swallowing thresholds in CC [25, 26], aligning with enhanced chemosensitivity. Demographic factors may contribute: our younger, predominantly female cohort contrasts with prior studies [9–12], and both aging (diminished sensitivity) and female sex (enhanced cough reflex) influence sensory thresholds [27, 28]. These findings suggest swallowing reflex hypersensitivity represents a novel mechanistic pathway in cough reflex disorders, with methodological differences in ST assessment potentially explaining conflicting literature. Evaluating the ST value thus offers more accurate reflection of swallowing reflex disorders in patients with CC.

In our study, we found that GERC was the most common cause of CC, followed by AC, CVA and EB. This differs from previous findings regarding the frequency distribution of etiologies in CC [29], which could be attributed to the relatively small sample size of patients in our study. It is important to note that swallowing reflex irritability was present in all the cough cohorts, which resulted in a lack of optimal discriminatory capacity of ST value for any specific cough trigger, with poor sensitivity. This could be due to overlapping characteristics among different cough triggers. Nevertheless, the ST value showed the difference between CC patients and healthy controls, suggesting that the use of the swallowing reflex and ST measurement may be helpful in screening patients with CC.

It has been suggested that the irritable larynx is primarily sustained by LPR [24], which often coexists with GERC [30]. GERC patients with concomitant LPR frequently exhibit a high proportion of proximal reflux, non-acid reflux, and gas reflux [30]. When these types of refluxes occur, the gastric contents can irritate the esophageal mucosa, pharynx, and larynx, potentially leading to laryngopharyngeal paresthesia. Additionally, airway reflux, which consists mainly of a gaseous non-acid mist, can also contribute to laryngeal hypersensitivity and cough when deposited in the upper airways [31]. In line with this, patients with GERC showed a significantly shorter ST value compared to those without reflux, particularly in cases of non-acid GERC. The presence of more non-acid reflux, gas reflux and proximal reflux events, as shown by MII-pH, can explain the increased occurrence of LPR or airway reflux in patients with a shorter ST value. However, the current study was unable to establish a direct causal relationship between swallowing disorders and irritable larynx. It is reasonable to speculate that CC is a primary disorder that results in a reduced threshold of swallowing sensitivity. Prolonged coughing episodes with laryngeal involvement may cause dysfunction of receptors or afferent nerves projecting to higher brain centers, including the coughing and swallowing centers. This speculation is further supported by the observation that the stimulation of the swallowing reflex in humans can occur in response to coughing.

The RSI and HARQ questionnaires are commonly used in clinical practice to assess LPR and cough hypersensitivity [18, 19]. In our study, we observed a mild inverse correlation between the ST value and RSI/HARQ scores in patients with GERC. Although these questionnaires do not directly measure swallowing disorders, certain items strongly indicate laryngeal hypersensitivity. For instance, the item "Clearing throat," which is a manifestation of sensory neuropathic disorder, is involved in the sensitization of the central neural circuit that regulates irritating laryngeal sensations [32]. This item demonstrated a correlation with the ST value in both the RSI and HARQ scores. Therefore, it is not surprising that items such as "A tickle in your throat, or a lump in your throat," "Cough with eating," and "Cough brought on by singing or speaking," which represent laryngeal paresthesia and innocuous stimuli (allotussia), achieved statistical significance when attempting to establish a link with the shortened ST. Furthermore, the cough threshold (C5) in response to inhaled capsaicin, which represents peripheral cough sensitivity, showed a positive association with the ST value. These findings collectively support the notion that swallowing reflex hypersensitivity is closely related to cough hypersensitivity and irritable larynx in patients with GERC.

The findings of this study hold promise for real clinical practice. While acid suppression therapy often proves inadequate for airway reflux or LPR [31, 33], our data showed that 34.7% of PPI-refractory GERC patients achieved symptom improvement with neuromodulators (baclofen, gabapentin, or flupentixol/melitracen). Shorter ST value outperforms RSI and HARQ scores in identifying patients with frequent proximal or non-acid reflux who required neuromodulator therapy. The ST measurement is rapidly obtainable with routine operation. In resource-limited settings lacking MII-pH monitoring, ST can provide an objective, low-cost indicator to guide early neuromodulator initiation for optimal therapeutic efficacy.

There are several limitations that should be addressed in this study. Firstly, sequential measurements of the ST were not performed at every follow-up for all GERC subjects, which may affect the accuracy and generalizability of the research findings. Further validation work is needed to confirm the stability of the results over time. Secondly, sub-second timing of ST and minor intergroup differences among CC etiologies pose technical challenges for precise real-world application. Future studies may benefit from the use of automated, sensor-based timing systems to improve accuracy. Thirdly, the absence of a MII-pH test in the non-GERC group may have had some impact on the reliability of the results. However, it is worth mentioning that the non-GERC group was definitively diagnosed based on specific etiology treatments, and a lower HARQ score indicated a lower likelihood of gastroesophageal reflux.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that shortened ST reflects heightened reflex sensitivity in patients with CC, particularly those with GERC. The evaluation of ST offers valuable clinical insight for delineating etiology in CC and assists in identifying GERC patients who are likely to benefit from intensified anti-reflux treatment. A reduced ST is associated with features of laryngeal hypersensitivity and cough reflex augmentation, supporting its potential role in guiding personalized therapeutic strategies for GERC.
