Who Is Reporting Recovery? Concerns About Guardian-Completed QoR-15 in a Pediatric Anaesthesia Trial [Letter]
Authors: Tianjie Zhu
Published: 2026

Dear editor

Zhang et al should be commended for completing a double-blind randomized comparison of ciprofol and propofol in 160 children undergoing extensive dental treatment under general anaesthesia. Their primary outcome, emergence agitation defined as a PAED score greater than 10 at 5 minutes after awakening, did not differ significantly (3.8% vs 7.5%; absolute difference −3.75%, 95% CI −12.22% to 4.00%). The report therefore relies substantially on secondary outcomes to describe better recovery with ciprofol, including an 8-point difference in QoR-15 score completed by guardians before discharge.1 This particular result requires a measurement-level qualification that is not resolved by randomisation.

The Recovery Instrument Was Validated as an Adult Self-Report

The original QoR-15 was derived from the adult QoR-40 and evaluated in 127 adult surgical patients as a patient-reported measure.2 In the present trial, participants were 3–7 years old, but guardians completed the standard QoR-15.1 Changing both the target population and the respondent changes the measurement: the resulting score is neither the validated adult patient report nor a validated paediatric proxy report. This matters because several QoR-15 items concern internal states—feeling rested, anxiety, sadness, comfort, and a sense of control—that a parent can only infer. A total-score difference can consequently reflect treatment effects on the child, differences in parental observation, or differences in how parents interpret adult-oriented items for a preschool child.

Paediatric Adaptation is More Than Replacing the Respondent

Evidence now available for paediatric recovery measurement illustrates why this distinction is substantive. A pictorial QoR-15 adaptation was psychometrically evaluated using self-report from children aged 7–17 years, not proxy completion of the unmodified adult questionnaire.3 More recently, the Pediatric Scale for Quality of Recovery was developed for children aged 2–17 years using interviews with children, families, and clinicians; crucially, it produced separately analysed self-report and proxy-report forms.4 The lower age boundary of the ciprofol trial makes proxy reporting understandable, but necessity does not establish validity. The manuscript should identify the exact language/version administered, state whether wording was changed for a parent perspective, describe how guardians were instructed to answer unobservable items, and report any evidence supporting the score’s validity and reliability in 3–7-year-old Chinese children.

The reported timing also limits interpretation. Guardians completed QoR-15 before discharge, yet no common post-extubation assessment time is specified.1 Because ciprofol shortened mean emergence time by 1.87 minutes and extubation time by 1.96 minutes, a discharge-linked questionnaire could have been completed at different recovery intervals. Without the time from extubation or awakening to questionnaire completion, the score may partly compare different positions on a rapidly changing recovery trajectory. Randomisation protects against baseline imbalance; it does not make a non-equivalent measurement time or an unvalidated reporter interchangeable.

A Reanalysis Can Preserve the Clinically Useful Findings

The trial contains outcomes that do not depend on the contested proxy composite: emergence time, extubation time, coughing, FLACC pain, rescue sedation, PAED assessment by a blinded nurse, and parental satisfaction. These should be kept conceptually separate. Parental satisfaction is a legitimate parent-reported endpoint; a parent-completed QoR-15 is presented instead as the child’s quality of recovery. The two cannot be combined as convergent evidence without demonstrating that the proxy instrument measures the intended construct.

We suggest reporting item-level response distributions, missingness, internal consistency, and exact assessment timing by group. A sensitivity analysis limited to externally observable items may show whether the between-group difference persists, although such a post hoc subset would remain exploratory and should not be labelled a validated QoR-15 score. If the original forms are available, mapping items to the domains and reporter wording of a paediatric instrument could inform future validation, but it should not retroactively confer validity. Most importantly, the abstract and conclusion should describe the result as a difference in guardian ratings on an adapted or unvalidated QoR-15 administration, rather than established improvement in paediatric recovery quality.

Conclusion

The negative primary outcome already warrants the cautious interpretation acknowledged by the authors. The recovery-quality claim requires an additional, distinct caution: an adult patient-reported instrument completed by proxies for preschool children does not retain its meaning automatically. Clarifying the respondent, adaptation, timing, and psychometric performance would allow readers to distinguish evidence for faster emergence and fewer selected adverse events from evidence for a broader improvement in the child’s recovery experience.
