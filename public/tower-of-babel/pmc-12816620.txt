Characterization and biological health implications of microRNAs from human milk and infant formulas
Authors: Abdelmoneim H Ali, Zain Najjar, Mutamed Ayyash
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroRNAs (miRNAs) in human milk play a crucial role in infant development and health. These molecules are well-known to regulate gene expression and are involved in various biological processes. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the current knowledge on miRNAs in human milk and infant formulas, emphasizing their potential impact on infant health and the future directions for research in this field. Understanding the roles and functions of miRNAs for infants’ health will not only disclose innovative aspects of breastfeeding, but it might pave the way for novel diagnostic and medicinal possibilities as well.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Medical research, Molecular biology

Introduction

MicroRNA (miRNA) was first discovered in 1993 by Victor Ambros and his colleagues while studying the gene Lin-4 in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans1,2. miRNA family involves particularly small non-coding RNA (~ 22 nucleotide in length), which has been extracted from body fluids, tissues, and cells of several mammals3,4. Three basic processes contribute to miRNA biogenesis5; firstly, miRNA is transcribed as primary miRNA (pri-miRNA) from specific genes on DNA by RNA polymerase II, and then processed into a hairpin precursor miRNA (pre-miRNA) by the Drosha–DGCR8 complex. Afterward, mature miRNA is produced from pre-miRNA in the cytoplasm by the Dicer enzyme as shown in Fig. 14,6. In addition, the miRBase database version 22.0 (http://www.mirbase.org) released in 2019 contains 38,589 entries demonstrating hairpin pre-miRNA from 271 organisms, indicating an increase in the sequences with more than a third over the previous version. A total of 48,860 various mature miRNA sequences can be produced by these precursors. Vertebrate genomes comprise thousands of miRNAs, for instance 1917 annotated hairpin precursors and 2654 mature sequences are present in the human genome.

Biosynthesis of miRNAs.

The process begins in the nucleus with the transcription of primary miRNA (pri-miRNA), which is then processed by Drosha and DGCR8 (DiGeorge syndrome critical region 8). Subsequently, exportin-5 (EXP-5) transports the resulting precursor miRNA (pre-miRNA) to the cytoplasm. Once there, Dicer further cleaves the pre-miRNA, producing a double-stranded miRNA duplex. Following strand selection, the mature miRNA joins the RISC. The extent of complementarity between miRNA and its target mRNA determines whether the mRNA undergoes degradation or if the translation process is inhibited280.

In addition to their synthesis and presence among various species, miRNAs have gained significant interest due to their essential roles in controlling gene expression and affecting cellular activities. miRNAs are powerful regulators of gene expression. They are small, non-coding RNA molecules that typically bind to the 3’ untranslated regions of target mRNAs, leading to mRNA degradation or inhibition of translation7. This post-transcriptional regulation can significantly impact protein synthesis, with some studies suggesting that miRNAs can regulate up to 50% of gene expression8. miRNAs act as molecular switches that fine-tune genes activity without changing the original DNA sequence. The effect on protein synthesis is profound. Proteins are important for nearly all cellular functions as they can act as enzymes, structural constituents, signaling fragments, and metabolic pathways regulators. By affecting which proteins are produced and in what amounts, miRNAs help preserve cellular balance and respond to environmental signals9. The disruption in this directing network can lead to irregular levels of protein, causing progression and development of diseases. miRNAs play a significant role in normal physiological processes and various pathologies. Their ability to regulate gene expression makes them key players in maintaining cellular homeostasis. However, when miRNAs expression is dysregulated, they can contribute to the development of numerous diseases10. miRNAs have been described as critical regulators of pluripotency-correlated genes, and they have been employed to transform somatic cells into stem cells with induced pluripotency11,12. As they reduce the requirement of viral vectors, this control could be a potential approach for biomedical studies and regenerative medicine. While viral vectors are employed to convert cells into induced pluripotent stem cells, research has revealed that they can unpredictably affect reprogramming process through arbitrary insertion of foreign genetic sequences into cell’s genome13.

In addition to their therapeutic potential and roles within cells, miRNAs have also been discovered in extracellular settings, revealing attractive possibilities for their functions and transmission in biological fluids. Superior quantities of miRNAs have been identified in several body fluids14. Milk is a noninvasive source of various biological molecules, which may originate locally within the mammary glands or be transported there via the systemic circulation. These molecules have important functions for the newborn and the lactating mother. Noticeably, milk miRNAs appear to be endogenous to the mammary glands15, and accordingly they could be used as a biomarker for the health status and performance of the glands during lactation, and their abnormal growth is correlated with breast cancer16. Foods-derived miRNAs, for example exogenous miRNA-168a17, have been reported to survive in the mammals’ gastrointestinal tract and regulate mammal genes18,19. Human milk is recognized as a rich source of miRNAs, making it critical to investigate their biological roles in newborns during breastfeeding, as well as any long-term effects they may have throughout early development. Curiously, bovine milk miRNAs (miRNA-29b and miRNA-200c), which are also found in the human milk20, have been reported to survive in the gastrointestinal tract of adults and raise in serum post-consumption level21,22. Research has shown that miRNAs encapsulated in bovine milk exosomes can indeed be transferred to human’s colon carcinoma cells and rat’s small intestinal cells through endocytosis23. This finding is significant as it highlights dietary miRNAs potential to impact human health and opens up possibilities for using milk-derived exosomes in therapeutic applications24. Building on these visions into milk miRNAs and their biological significance, the present study aims to further explore their composition and functions in both human milk and infant formulas.

Several studies have previously investigated the occurrence and functions of miRNAs in human milk and infant formulas; however, most have provided a limited scope, either focusing primarily on miRNAs stability and the detection techniques25,26, or highlighting miRNAs-facilitated immune and developmental functions without systematically addressing the compositional dynamics or interspecies analysis27,28. Few studies have assessed how processing and formulation practices in infant formulas impact the preservation and bioactivity of miRNAs. These earlier studies, while foundational, have not widely integrated the profile, biological activity, and health consequences of miRNAs in both human milk and infant formulas. In this context, the main objective of this study is to provide a comprehensive overview of the composition, characteristics, and biological functions of miRNAs in human milk and infant formulas, and to assess their potential implications for infant health and development. This includes highlighting the differences in miRNAs content between human milk and formulas and discussing the prospects to enhance formulas composition to better mimic the benefits of natural breastfeeding.

Methodology

To gather information for this review article, we conducted a comprehensive literature search using electronic databases including Web of Science, PubMed, Google Scholar, and ScienceDirect. The search covered publications from 2000 to 2025, with a focus on studies published in the last 10 years to ensure both relevance and inclusion of foundational research. Search terms included “microRNAs, “human milk,” “infant formula,” “neurogenesis,” “gut health,” and “lipid and glucose metabolism,” used individually and in combination. We included original peer-reviewed research articles encompassing clinical studies, observational studies, animal models, and in vitro experiments. Furthermore, relevant book chapters and systematic reviews were considered. Reference lists of initially identified articles were also reviewed to capture additional relevant literature. Articles were selected based on their relevance to the composition, stability, biological functions, and health implications of miRNAs in human milk and infant formulas. A total of 280 original articles, book chapters, and published theses met the inclusion criteria and were used to develop the content of this review.

Structure and composition of human milk miRNAs

miRNAs in mammal milk

Milk-derived miRNAs are fascinating due to their potential roles in newborn development and health. The key points about milk miRNAs include (i) presence and stability: miRNAs are abundant in milk and are remarkably stable, even under harsh conditions like digestion22. (ii) diverse functions: miRNAs can regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally, influencing various biological processes. (iii) species-specific profiles: milk miRNAs composition can vary significantly between species. Cow, goat, sheep, and donkey milk have shown distinct miRNA profiles, each with unique sets of miRNAs that reflect their specific genetic and physiological characteristics29. (iv) potential health implications: miRNAs transfer from milk to the newborn can have significant health implications. Besides nutrition delivery, human milk has been established to protect newborns from infections and to have fundamental functions to their growth and development. miRNA can be extracted from the major milk fractions, specifically skim milk, lipids, and cells. Among all human body fluids, milk is one of the richest sources of miRNAs, representing about 1400 mature miRNAs30,31. Compared to skim milk fraction, lipids and cellular fractions had the highest miRNAs content32. Large variations in miRNAs expression have been reported among lactating women33, and the factors responsible for these variations having not been investigated so far.

The major source of miRNAs in milk is the mammary glands, with a small proportion from the maternal circulation34. It has been reported that most miRNAs from tammar wallaby milk were differentially expressed between blood serum and skim milk, in spite of that the total numbers of miRNAs were comparable in both serum and milk (82 and 86 miRNAs, respectively)35. In the beginning, it was thought that due to the absence or the low content of ribosomal RNA (18S and 28S) throughout miRNA extraction and identification, miRNAs are less prospective to be produced from milk cells26. The similarities between miRNAs present in milk lipids and milk cells, along with their differences from those found in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells and plasma in both dairy cows36 and human30, indicate a distinct origin. Remarkably, lactocytes, recognized as the prevalent cell type in healthy human milk37, are known to secrete lipids. This confirms the idea that miRNAs found in milk lipids and cells are mainly produced endogenously within the mammary glands30.

Dynamic composition of miRNAs during lactation

The composition of human milk miRNAs has been described in several cross-sectional studies33,38; however, the largest study was reported by Kupsco, et al.,33 assuming that there are only few human longitudinal studies so far39. Hence, to identify a subclass of specific miRNAs with a substantial dynamic expression between the three time points of human milk collection, Raymond, et al.40 applied a model to the whole human milk miRNome dataset. The study revealed that 11 dynamic miRNAs were identified, among which 7 exhibited substantial stage-specific elevation (miR-3184-3p, miR-92b-5p, let-7d-3p, miR-187-5p, miR-196a-5p, miR-516a-5p, and miR-3126-5p), and 4 displayed substantial stage-specific suppression (miR-34a-5p, miR-146a-5p, miR-125b-5p, and miR-1307-3p) from the second week of lactation until the third month. Following the identification of dynamic miRNAs expression profile during lactation, subsequent research has focused on determining the source of these miRNAs in human milk and how they are distributed across different milk components. To investigate the origin of human milk miRNAs, next-generation sequencing technique was applied to identify miRNAs in milk fat and cells as compared to miRNAs from maternal peripheral blood mononuclear cells and plasma30. The study revealed a great correlation in the profiles of miRNAs between the milk fat and cells, while clear differences in miRNAs from peripheral blood mononuclear cells and plasma were observed. This profile of similarity within milk fractions, contrasted with the dissimilar patterns in maternal blood components, supports the suggestion that the mammary epithelium, where epithelial cells are predominant in mature human milk, is the primary source of milk miRNAs, with only a minor contribution from maternal circulation30. The variations in the content and composition of milk miRNAs during lactation stage have been reported39. The total levels of miRNAs remained consistent at 2, 4, and 6 months of lactation. Similarly, the top 20 predominant miRNAs, as well as the overall number and expression patterns of the identified miRNAs across milk fractions, showed minimal variation during this period. Nevertheless, 1/3 of the acknowledged miRNAs had different expression throughout the first 6 months of lactation, with more stated upregulation after 4 months. In another study, the total miRNAs content from USA mothers was compared during lactation41. A little variation was reported, but some of the highest 15 miRNAs showed substantial differences, which might be attributed to the limited number of the analyzed samples/subjects. Remarkably, out of the 15 major milk miRNAs reported in this study of USA mothers, 11 species have also been identified in another study on Australian mothers39. Likewise, all major 10 miRNA species in Chinese mothers’ milk detected by Zhou et al.42 were identified among the predominant 30 miRNAs analyzed in the study of Liao et al.41, suggesting that the ethnicity of mother had a less effect on the major miRNAs in human milk.

While the previous studies underscore the stability and cross-population consistency of the major miRNAs in mature human milk, evolving research has also explored how the profile of miRNAs may vary under diverse physiological conditions, including maternal obesity, gestational diabetes, and type 1 diabetes, in addition to preterm birth. These conditions have been shown to alter the expression of metabolism- and immunity-associated miRNAs in human milk, highlighting their potential roles in shaping infant development and long-term health outcomes. Both preterm and term human milk have different molecular compositions, and it has been hypothesized that gestational age at delivery affects the expression of metabolism-related miRNAs in maternal breast milk43. The study used next-generation sequencing to profile miRNAs in milk fat from mothers of term and preterm infants, revealing 113 miRNAs with significant expression difference. Many of these miRNAs had been previously reported in the literature, reinforcing their biological relevance. From this dataset, 15 miRNAs showing the most substantial variation were selected for functional analysis. The predicted mRNA targets of these miRNAs were enriched in pathways related to catabolism, symbiosis, biosynthesis, viral processing, and cellular nitrogen metabolism. Remarkably, glycosphingolipid biosynthesis emerged as the most notably upregulated pathway in preterm milk43. This is particularly important because glycosphingolipids play a fundamental role in nervous system development, involving neuronal differentiation, synaptic function, and myelination44. The study also revealed that miRNAs profile in term colostrum was like those in term mature milk, while both were substantially different from those in preterm milk. This indicates that premature delivery itself may affect the miRNAs profile of human milk. Particularly, 21 out of 26 miRNAs were significantly related to gestational age, and 6 miRNAs were correlated with the delivery process. These findings highlight the potential of milk miRNAs to expose and probably respond to the developmental needs of preterm infants, especially in immunological and metabolic regulation.

Quantification of miRNAs in milk fractions

Quantifying miRNAs in milk fractions involves several steps and techniques to ensure accurate measurement. Here are some key points: (i) sample preparation: milk samples are typically fractionated to separate the cream, skim milk, and cell pellet; (ii) RNA extraction: total RNA, including miRNAs, is extracted from milk fractions; (iii) quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR): one of the most common methods for quantifying miRNAs is qPCR40,45; (iv) next-generation sequencing: for a more comprehensive analysis, next-generation sequencing can be used to profile miRNAs in milk; (v) normalization and controls: to ensure accurate quantification, internal controls, such as spike-in synthetic miRNAs and normalization strategies are used; (vi) data analysis: the data obtained from qPCR or next-generation sequencing is analyzed to determine the relative or absolute quantities of miRNAs in different milk fractions40.

To better understand the biological implications of quantification techniques, several studies have specifically assessed the abundance and diversity of miRNAs in human milk, mainly in mature milk and colostrum. miRNAs were isolated and identified from 12 numerous fluids in the human body, involving colostrum and mature milk14. Among the analyzed fluids, 386 known mature miRNAs in skimmed colostrum and 429 known mature miRNAs in mature skimmed milk were identified by qPCR. This finding established the richness of milk in miRNA, which is consistent with its high content of total RNA compared to other body fluids (47.24 mg/L against 94 μg/L in urine and 308 μg/L in plasma). In another study, researchers characterized the miRNAs profile present in skimmed human milk46. Numerous analytical techniques, such as qPCR, microarray investigation, and small RNA sequencing, have been used to accomplish the task31,36,47. qPCR is typically preferred for its high specificity and sensitivity, but small RNA sequencing is used when a complete miRNAs profile is desired. As a result, novel miRNAs could be detected and quantified. High-performance next generation sequencing or small-scale next generation sequencing methods are used to analyze the sequence of small RNA molecules48.

While early studies mainly focused on miRNAs profiling in skimmed milk, more recent research has extended to explore the distribution of miRNAs across all milk fractions, comprising the lipid and cellular components, which are now documented as substantial miRNA reservoirs. The profile of miRNAs in human milk was also identified by using microarrays, and 281 various miRNAs in skim milk obtained in the first 6 months of lactation were analyzed46. The early studies focused on the analysis of miRNAs in the skim milk; however, it has been claimed that the other two fractions (cells and lipids) of milk are likely to hold considerable miRNA amounts. Small RNA sequencing was used to analyze the lipids fraction of human milk, and high miRNA quantities were found by stating 308 different miRNAs that were anticipated to target almost 9074 genes31. In another study, the profile of miRNAs in human milk exosomes was studied by employing deep sequencing42. The study revealed that 639 exosomal mature miRNAs initiating from 452 pre-miRNAs were identified42.

To further explore the origin and distribution of human milk-derived miRNAs, a detailed study was conducted using high-throughput screening and comparative analysis across milk fractions and maternal blood components. By using Taqman OpenArray Panel system, 754 human mature miRNAs were screened in lipids and cellular fractions. Additionally, the content of miRNAs was compared with that of maternal peripheral blood mononuclear cells and plasma30. A total of 233 and 293 miRNAs were identified in human milk lipids and cellular fractions, respectively. Maternal plasma contained 169 different miRNAs, whereas 345 miRNAs were found in maternal peripheral blood mononuclear cells. Compared to plasma and lipids, significantly higher contents of miRNAs were observed in human milk cells and peripheral blood mononuclear cells. Milk lipids and cells were highly correlated regarding miRNA expression species and patterns through cluster and correlation analysis; however, no correlation was observed between peripheral blood mononuclear cells and milk miRNAs. In plasma, an obvious inter-individual difference in single miRNA species expression levels was reported. The presence of miRNAs in all three different fractions of milk has been demonstrated, and human milk has been acknowledged to maintain more miRNAs with diverse expression patterns than maternal plasma30, and other human body fluids14. Compared to lipids and cellular fractions, skim milk seemed to be very low in total RNAs and miRNAs32.

Beyond human milk, the presence and diversity of miRNAs in other milk species, specifically livestock, has been explored to better comprehend evolutionary conservation and the potential functional connections. Several studies have analyzed miRNAs in other milk species, in particular the dairy cows29,49,50. Table 2 shows the total number of miRNAs analyzed in different milk species. It has been reported that mature bovine skim milk contained 53 miRNA species by using microarrays26, and 213 using deep sequencing36. Colostrum and mature milk had higher contents of some miRNA species51,52. Compared to mature milk, bovine colostrum displayed higher total miRNAs content and species numbers, since 230 and 100 miRNAs were identified in bovine skim colostrum36. Likewise, microvesicles RNA and total RNA levels in bovine milk were higher in colostrum than mature milk26,53. Furthermore, 53 and 100 mature miRNAs were isolated from mature bovine skim milk and skimmed colostrum, respectively26. Porcine milk exosomes were evaluated by using small RNA sequencing at 6 lactation points within the first month postnatal, and it was shown that 180 pre-miRNAs encoding 237 mature miRNAs47, which were also present in human milk47. Nonetheless, 39 pre-miRNAs of the identified species were not homologues to human milk miRNAs47. The pre-miRNA species common between several mammals possibly have comparable physio-pathological actions and roles in milk47. In all mammals’ milk miRNAs, few miRNA species were found to be highly stated36,47.

Factors affecting the composition of milk-derived miRNAs

The composition of miRNAs in human milk can be affected by numerous factors, such as lactation period, maternal diet and health, gestational age, infant sex, and environmental and genetic factors34,54. Research on lactating rats suggested that a diet promoting obesity, commonly referred to as a cafeteria diet, might influence the levels of specific miRNAs in milk55. The cafeteria diet-fed group displayed higher miR-222 levels and lower miR-200 and miR-26 levels than the control group. Furthermore, cafeteria diet-fed offsprings had a lower Cyclin Dependent Kinase Inhibitor 1 C expression in liver, which is recognized to be implicated in growth and a proved target gene of miR-22, compared to the control. It has been also reported in the same study that the bioactive milk protein content could be altered in this dietary pattern, which might increase adiponectin and leptin levels and decrease irisin level, with a subsequent effect on the metabolism in newborns56. An increase in the expression of new miRNAs, particularly miR-67 and miR-27 was observed in the fat globules of a high-fat diet consuming mothers than those consuming a high-carbohydrates diet31.

Maternal excessive body weight has a potential effect on miRNAs profile. Xi, et al.57 reported that relative to higher body mass index values, adipogenesis-associated miRNAs, such as let-7a, miR-30b, and miR-378, were downregulated. Another study detected a change in human milk miRNAs correlated to adiponectin and leptin functions in mothers with body mass index values more than 25 kg/m2 58. This agrees with the fact that none of these hormones declined during the lactation in mothers with obesity or overweight. In addition, an altered correlation between miRNAs and other attributes, for instance the profile of milk lipids and newborn’s body mass index at two years was observed. miRNA species with a different expression pattern concerning excessive body weight (miR-30, miR-17, miR-103, miR-222, miR-let-7a, and miR-146b) were associated with diverse processes, such as brain and cellular development, as well as miRNA’s transportation and release.

These previous findings highlight the broader associations of maternal body weight on the molecular composition of human milk, mainly miRNAs expression. Building on this indication, further studies have investigated how these miRNAs variations are obvious in milk samples from mothers with varying body mass index profiles. Milk samples were collected from mothers (30 with a normal body mass index [18.5–25 kg/m2] vs 30 with obesity/overweight)59. Lower miRNA-148a and miRNA-30b levels at the first month of lactation in mothers with increased body mass index were observed. Upon controlling for gestational age, birth weight, and sex, substantial correlations were observed between both miRNA-148a and miRNA-30b levels and infant anthropometric measurements during the first three months of breastfeeding. miRNA-148a serves as the precursor to miRNA-148a-3p, which is the most prevalent miRNA found in human milk exosomes and is known to exhibit neuroproliferative and neuroprotective properties. Hence, a decrease in miRNA-148a levels may elevate the likelihood of childhood obesity and negatively impact neurological development in children born to obese mothers. In the study of Shah et al.60, human milk from 32 women diagnosed with gestational diabetes mellitus and 62 women without the condition was examined for levels of metabolism-related miRNAs, including miRNA-148a, miRNA-30b, let-7a, and let-7d. These levels were found to be associated with maternal obesity. The researchers compared these findings with infant growth and body composition during the first six months of life. Human milk from mothers with gestational diabetes mellitus contained lower levels of miRNA-148a, miRNA-30b, let-7a, and let-7d. Furthermore, miRNA-148a demonstrated a negative correlation, while miRNA-30b exhibited a positive correlation with the body weight and fat mass of 1-month-old infants. These findings suggest that mothers with gestational diabetes mellitus produced atypical levels of miRNAs, which were linked to abnormal metabolic outcomes in their breast-fed infants.

Mirza et al.61 investigated the profile of miRNAs in human milk exosomes from mothers with type 1 diabetes mellitus compared to healthy controls. Their findings showed a distinct expression pattern; while miRNAs, such as hsa-miR-29-3p, hsa-miR-200c-5p, and hsa-miR-518e-3p were downregulated, other miRNA species, such as hsa-miR-1246, hsa-miR-4497, hsa-miR-1290, hsa-miR-3178, hsa-miR-320d, and hsa-miR-133a-3p were upregulated. Remarkably, these alterations were not correlated with maternal body mass index or glycemic control. Some of the differentially expressed miRNAs were connected to genes involved in immune responses, indicating a potential immunological impact of maternal diabetes on milk composition. However, the study highlighted that these molecular alterations do not indicate any contraindication to breastfeeding in mothers with type 1 diabetes. In general, these findings support the notion that maternal health conditions, incorporating metabolic syndromes and dietary factors, can affect the composition of human milk-derived miRNAs. This underlines the need for further research to better understand the functional outcomes of such variations in infant development and long-term health consequences.

miRNAs and infant formulas

miRNAs in infant formulas are an emerging area of interest due to their potential health benefits for infants. During the early 1900s, a common misconception in pediatrics held that milk is solely a source of nutrition62. This view overlooked the fact that formula feeding circumvents epigenetic programming facilitated by exosome miRNAs. Research has demonstrated that a gene linked to fat mass and obesity exhibits increased expression in the blood monocytes of infants fed formula compared to those who are breastfed. The fat mass and obesity gene is inhibited by miRNA-30b, which is found in human milk and plays a role in thermogenesis within brown adipose tissue24. Chiba et al.63 compared the expression levels of miR-125b and miR-148a. In all the evaluated formulas, they were respectively lower than 1/100 and 1/500 of the levels in mature human milk. Conversely, many studies have demonstrated that miRNAs were undetectable in infant formulas and their exosome-sized particles, which were found to be casein micelles64. Cheshmeh et al.65 revealed that infants fed with formula exhibited substantially higher current weight, height, and head circumference than those exclusively breastfed. Additionally, formula-fed infants showed notably higher levels of fat mass, obesity, and CPT1A gene expression, while their PPAR-α gene expression was considerably lower than breastfed infants. The study suggests that breastfeeding may have a protective effect against obesity and related non-communicable diseases by influencing the expression of genes associated with obesity predisposition. While research into miRNAs in infant formulas continues to evolve, it is critical to contextualize these findings within the broader framework of human milk’s biological complexity. Beyond its nutritional profile, human milk is considered a dynamic biofluid enriched with regulatory and immunological molecules that play a vital role in shaping infant development.

Human milk offers more than just nutritional benefits for infants. While it contains essential components, such as fat, protein, carbohydrates, minerals, and vitamins, it also provides regulatory and immunoprotective biomolecules. It also includes viable cells that provide essential signals for ideal growth, development, and defense systems in the newborns66,67. Due to the rapid global population evolution over the last century and the elevated claim to offer non-natural milk for newborns, bovine milk-based infant formulas were produced as a substitute or a complementary food for infants originally without access to the human milk68. Despite the widespread belief that infant formulas should be used only when a mother’s milk supply is inadequate, their popularity has recently grown in certain communities for reasons unrelated to medical necessity. This trend has sparked controversy due to concerns about long-term health implications and the sociocultural factors affecting infant feeding options69. One of the arising scientific concerns focuses on the molecular profile of infant formulas, mainly their lack of bioactive components such as miRNAs, which are predominant in human milk and play vital roles in infant development. For instance, miR-159a was the predominant species in both soy-based and bovine milk-based formulas30. miR-159a is a plant-definite species not found in any of the breast samples investigated. The origin of this species might be traced to the diet of the animals whose milk was used in bovine milk-based formulas, or to plant-based oils like soybean oil incorporated during production, as indicated by ingredient lists. While these are plausible sources, the presence of miR-159a in formula products raises questions about the processing and sourcing of ingredients. Nevertheless, there is currently no direct evidence to suggest deliberate addition or contamination during preparation. Further investigation is needed to explain the mechanisms by which plant-derived miRNAs enter formula products and whether they preserve biological activities post-ingestion. This line of inquiry is particularly significant given that other plant-based miRNAs, such as miR-168a, have been reported to affect gene expressions in adult human17, indicating a potential for dietary miRNAs to exert regulatory effects if absorbed.

Given the concerns about the limited presence and potential biological inactivity of miRNAs in infant formulas, it becomes crucial to examine how their molecular profiles compared to those found in both bovine and human milk. This comparison provides vision into the regulatory and nutritional gaps that may exist between synthetic and natural feeding options. Human milk is a complex biological fluid comprising maternal somatic cells, valuable microbiota, and other biomolecules such as miRNAs with beneficial health functions70,71. Most of the commercial infant formulas are made from bovine milk31, and similar to human milk, bovine milk miRNAs are most expected to be greatly preserved in both cells and fat globules. miRNAs may generally survive during the manufacturing preparation processes of milk owing to their stability; however, milk cells and lipids are regularly removed from formulas72, therefore, the presence of miRNAs in formulas is substantially decreased. Several studies have reported that infant formulas displayed much lower miRNAs expression levels compared to those from bovine milk26,73. Seven miRNA species were selected and evaluated as quality control indicators for raw milk and numerous powdered milk products36. Compared to raw milk, the expression levels of these species were extensively lower in the powdered products36. The total RNA level from raw bovine milk was considerably higher than in three infant formula categories (standard, follow-on, and hydrolyzed)26. In addition, miR-148a and miR-200c, two miRNAs abundantly expressed in bovine milk, showed varying levels of expression across the three distinct categories of infant formulas4. The profile and expression levels of human miRNA from two commercial infant formulas, a bovine milk-based and soybean-based formulas were compared30. Among the 754 human mature miRNAs examined by using Taqman Openarray, 45 miRNAs were detected in bovine milk-based formula, while only 22 miRNAs were found in soy-based formula30. Furthermore, the biological activities of the other few miRNAs in the formulas might be affected throughout production stages. It is assumed that some common miRNAs in bovine milk and human milk might have comparable functional roles for mammals’ newborns, highlighting the probable negative effects of the low miRNA content in the synthesized infant formulas on infants. Besides the lack of other immunoprotective aspects of human milk in formula, this could somewhat clarify the decline in diseases protection in formula-fed infants73. The stability and quality of miRNAs in infant formulas could be affected by several technological and environmental factors. Thermal treatments, such as pasteurization and spray-drying are known to break down the exosomal membranes, causing denaturation or fragmentation of encapsulated miRNAs74. Also, lipids removal during the preparation of infant formulas reduces fat globules that naturally protect miRNAs in bovine and human milk. Furthermore, storage conditions, involving repeated freeze–thaw cycles and persistent exposure to oxygen or light, can further speed up the degradation of miRNAs through oxidative stress and RNase activation75. Another evolving factor is packaging-related contamination. Recent studies have reported nanoplastics and microplastics release from the formula packaging materials and feeding bottles into the prepared formulas76,77. These microplastics may absorb or interact with exosomal lipids, possibly changing vesicle stability or bioavailability of miRNAs. Also, the additives and leachates from plastic packaging, such as bisphenols and phthalates, could cause oxidative stress, indirectly affecting the integrity of miRNAs78. While data on the direct effects of microplastics on milk miRNAs are limited, this area demands urgent examination given raising public concern and potential effects for infant health. In summary, preserving miRNAs functionality in the commercial formulas necessitates optimizing the processing parameters, improving the packaging materials, and monitoring the potential microplastic interactions to guarantee the molecular and nutritional integrity of these products.

Biological functional roles of milk miRNAs

Stability and absorption

While comparative studies have emphasized the reduced content of miRNAs in infant formulas relative to human and bovine milk, these findings highlight the importance of employing sensitive and comprehensive analytical practices to precisely characterize the miRNA profile in human milk. Such approaches are needed to identify both common and previously undetected miRNA species that might have significant roles in infant health. The EdgeSeq technique from HTG molecular diagnostics was applied to study miRNAs profile79. A total of 2083 miRNAs were detected, among which 685 mature miRNAs frequently found during lactation period were identified. This collection represents approximately 30% of the total human miRNome of about 2600 miRNAs explained so far80, and in agreement with previous findings in the range between 300 and 1300 human milk miRNAs via similar sequencing methods or another microarray approaches33,81–83. Among the detected 685 miRNAs, a total of 35 miRNAs inherently and highly expressed during lactation stages in all samples were identified. This division of the main human milk miRNome corresponds to miRNAs with an average expression level within the top 20% and within the lowest 10% variability coefficient. Remarkably, among the reported 35 miRNA species, 20 species have been described in cohorts or samples from Europe33,84, China42, Australia30,39, and the United States38,41, while the other 15 species, including miR-2861, miR-4459, miR-4478, miR-4463, miR-4530, miR-4505, miR-6088, miR-6124, miR-6131, miR-320e, miR-149-3p, miR-4763-3p, miR-6727-5p, miR-6741-5p, and miR-6798-5p, were not identified previously, emphasizing the significance of using complementary and delicate analytical approaches.

Researchers have initiated investigations into the fate of miRNAs derived from food, exploring whether they can withstand the gastric system and potentially impact gene expression in mammals, involving human17,18,21. miRNAs specific to certain foods can be detected in the tissues and blood serum of various animals after oral consumption. Notably, miR-168a, a miRNA unique to rice, was identified in the blood serum of individuals from a Chinese population sample17. This miRNA has been shown to bind the low-density lipoproteins receptor adapter protein 1 in mice and human, and could restrain its expression in the liver17, indicating not only survival and absorption of this food-derived miRNAs in human, but affecting tissue functions through epigenetic regulation as well. Nevertheless, consuming food sources containing cross-miRNAs does not always lead to increased levels in mammals. A case in point is broccoli, which has high concentrations of miR-167a and miR-824. When healthy individuals consumed large amounts of broccoli sprouts, researchers observed no changes in the expression patterns of these two miRNAs in their plasma21. In addition, exogenous miRNAs uptake in rice-containing chow-fed mice was studied, and the research did not document the gene-targeting capabilities of the plant-derived miRNA or alterations in expression levels within the animals’ plasma or liver85. The inconsistencies in the literature investigating the transfer of plant-derived miRNAs to mammals might reflect the absence and/or negligible influence of exosomal transfer, as exosome-like assemblies production by plants that could be absorbed by mammal cells needs to be further studied86. Undoubtedly, miRNAs transfer and functions in the recipient could be enhanced by packaging or encapsulating them using a transporting vehicle.

It has been proposed that human milk miRNAs transfer to the bloodstream of the infant is further enabled by the recognized milk miRNAs packaging in “vehicles” structure, for instance exosomes, somatic cells, and other microvesicles that could be vital for miRNAs long-distance transportation, as long as they are enclosed by a bilayer lipid membrane and are fortified with observance fragments, both of which simplify their well-organized endosomal transfer through the intestine epithelial cells42. Milk miRNAs are suggested to be absorbed via these vehicles by the infants and take part in the genetic management of several functions, such as protection and development of the immune system (Fig. 2)19,87. The significance of exosomal transfer of miRNAs derived from milk has been highlighted in numerous studies. Extracellular vesicles involving exosomes can bind to various cell types by endocytosis and to transport miRNAs like miR-21, which downregulated TPM1 and TGFβRII expression in the recipient cells88. These substances were found to carry immunoregulatory miRNAs in bovine milk. Extracellular vesicles can be resistive to severe conditions such as high acidity89. The absorption and functionality, involving medicinal effects of milk miRNAs have been studied both in vivo and in vitro90. Extracellular vesicles derived from bovine milk exhibited higher expression of the immune-associated miRNAs (miR-30a, miR-92a, and miR-223), which were in vitro absorbed by intestinal cells and splenocytes. In mice with experimentally induced rheumatoid arthritis, extracellular vesicles derived from milk were orally administered. These vesicles were absorbed by RAW264.7 macrophages within 1–3 h and by the ileum tissue of the mice after 24 h. Following oral administration daily for 9 weeks, the onset of arthritis was postponed, along with decreased cartilage loss and reduced joint inflammation90.

miRNAs from human milk can be transferred to the infants either as a free molecule in the skim milk or through the absorption of exosomes, cells, and other microvesicles of milk in the gastrointestinal tract. The uptake is expected to take place via the epithelial cells of the intestine, from which milk miRNAs might reach diverse tissues and organs through the bloodstream to possibly perform roles, for instance, regulating immune protection and development. It is worth noting that compared to human milk, infant formulas have an extremely lower content of miRNAs, with potential variations similarly in the biological activities of these substances in formulas that merit more study to be better understood25.

Besides exosomal transfer, human milk cells might substantially contribute to milk-derived miRNAs transfer to the newborn. It has been reported that in mice, immune and stem cells from milk can enter the bloodstream of nursing offspring and afterwards migrate to various tissues91,92. Assuming that milk cells are enriched in miRNAs20, in addition to milk exosomes, this is probably a crucial source of miRNAs for newborns. Infant formulas miRNAs might be transferred to the newborn’s circulation to an insignificant amount. miR-29b and miR-200c derived from bovine milk in adult people were investigated after bovine milk consumption, and it was reported that both miRNAs had increased 2-fold in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells and could potentially change gene expressions21. Both miRNAs displayed high expression in the plasma after few hours of bovine milk consumption and returned to the normal baseline expression level after 24 h of the initial milk consumption21. Commercial bovine milk products exosomes were revealed to be transported into mice primary small intestinal IEC-6 cells and human intestinal colon carcinoma Caco-2 cells via endocytosis in vitro, a procedure that was affected by the host cells surface glycoproteins93. Given the significant disparity in miRNAs content between bovine milk and infant formulas, particularly in colostrum, the biological implications of milk-derived miRNAs warrant closer attention. Nevertheless, infant formulas and bovine milk have different contents of miRNAs, with the former lacking viable cells and exosomes, and accordingly comprising less miRNA levels about 100-fold higher in raw bovine milk and colostrum than formulas made from bovine milk36,94.

Building on the evidence of differential miRNAs profile and delivery mechanisms in bovine milk and infant formulas, it becomes important to evaluate whether these dietary miRNAs can endure the physiological conditions of the gastrointestinal tract and exert functional roles post-ingestion. To confirm food miRNAs functionality in mammals, it is required to validate their persistence in the gastrointestinal tract. Several in vitro studies reported that miRNAs have been shown to have very high stability under several severe environments42,95. For human milk-derived miRNAs, the major concerns are tolerance of the higher acidity and the resistance to the digestion of ribonuclease, and temperatures and freeze/thaw phases in the frozen human milk42 Exosomal miRNAs are thought to be protected from degradation42; however, other microvesicles, such as milk fat globules and apoptotic bodies46, may also contribute to miRNA protection, a possibility that warrants further investigation. The lipid profile of milk-derived vesicles, such as liposomes and exosomes plays crucial roles in protecting and facilitating the functional delivery of miRNAs. The presence of certain phospholipids (phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin) and cholesterol contributes to the rigidity and stability of vesicle membranes under gastrointestinal conditions, thus affecting miRNAs preservation and uptake96. The variations in lipids profile among species, or across lactation periods, may influence vesicle–cell membranes fusion and endocytic efficiency, eventually regulating miRNAs biological activity in targeted cells. Understanding the interactions between vesicle lipids profile and miRNAs functionality remains a promising area for future study. Furthermore, cellular miRNAs might be delivered whole as they are surrounded by cells that have been reported to survive in the gastrointestinal tract of the newborns and to residence in diverse body organs17,35. While in vitro studies and human milk analyses propose that dietary miRNAs may survive in gastrointestinal conditions and potentially exert biological effects, in vivo models are crucial to confirm these findings and evaluate actual absorption and functionality. Another investigation employed a mouse model of miR-375 and miR-200c knockout pups fed by wildtype foster or knockout mothers97. It has been reported that no indication was observed for intestinal absorption in wildtype or knockout pups of miR-375 and miR-200c from foster mother milk. Nevertheless, a slight increase in the plasma content of these miRNA species was noticed in knockout pups after treatment, implying that some copies of miRNA were essentially transported to the bloodstream. Notably, the miRNAs investigated were not abundantly expressed in the wildtype mother’s milk of the mouse model utilized. Additionally, miR-375 and miR-200c are recognized for their roles in regulating endocytosis and/or exocytosis, as well as modifying epithelial function. These characteristics may affect exosomal endocytosis and consequently affect the absorption of the studied miRNAs98,99. To examine the uptake of milk miRNAs by the nursing offspring, this knockout mouse model and the selected miRNAs may therefore not be appropriate. As has been reported previously21,93, not all dietary miRNAs are ideal for exogenous miRNAs absorption analyses100.

Conversely, artificially synthesized miRNAs added to bovine milk were found to be unstable and prone to degradation, much like those in human serum101. In contrast, the naturally occurring, endogenous miRNAs present in bovine milk demonstrated stability26,47. It is acknowledged that human milk comprises high amounts of very stable miRNAs, implying higher stability towards pasteurization and storage processes42,46. miRNAs have been reported to be active and still control their target genes after ultraviolet (UV) radiation treatment102. In C. elegans, various miRNAs, for instance miR-55-3p and miR-57-5p were expressed high stably after treatment with UV-C103. In another study, human primary keratinocytes were subjected to UV-A and UV-B for 6 h, and it was shown that most miRNAs persisted and there was no expression difference, excluding few miRNAs, such as miR-10a (downregulated) and miR-23b (upregulated)104. Nonetheless, it has been reported that skin miRNAs expressed upregulation in response to UV radiation105, which might be included in skin cancer initiation106. To summarize, although most miRNAs in mammalian cells might not be affected by UV radiation, it has been suggested that some miRNA species might be affected, and consequently, this needs further research. The food industry and processors of dairy products, such as milk powder manufacturers, have emphasized that miRNAs found in food and milk are highly stable and can withstand harsh conditions26,36. Consequently, these miRNAs are likely to be absorbed by infants who are fed human milk, particularly when they are encapsulated within protective structures like exosomes and viable milk cells.

Milk-derived miRNAs have emerged as potential bioactive molecules capable of affecting gene expression in recipient cells. Once ingested, certain miRNAs, particularly those encapsulated in exosomes, are protected from degradation in the gastrointestinal tract, facilitating their uptake into circulation and potential delivery to peripheral tissues21. These miRNAs may modulate diverse physiological activities, including immune development, metabolism, and cellular proliferation by targeting specific mRNAs in recipient cells. Functional differences among milk miRNAs can be attributed to numerous factors. First, each miRNA has an exclusive seed sequence that influences its target specificity. Second, the relative abundance of the individual miRNAs in milk differs by species, lactation stage, and maternal diet, indicating functional adaptation. Third, miRNA stability and packaging, whether within protein complexes or exosomes affect bioavailability and biological activity107. Furthermore, only a subset of milk miRNAs appear to be selectively enriched and secreted, indicating active sorting systems for physiologically significant roles. For instance, miR-148a is one of the predominant miRNAs in bovine and human milk and has been shown to target DNA methyltransferase 1, potentially affecting epigenetic programming in infants108. miR-21 and miR-155, commonly found in milk exosomes, are known regulators of immune cell development and may contribute to immune maturation in newborns. These examples emphasize the functional heterogeneity of milk-derived miRNAs and underscore the need for further studies to explain their systemic consequences.

Immune system regulation

The ability of miRNAs to withstand severe conditions and their observed movement into the bloodstream and potentially various organs of infants who are breastfed indicates that they may have significant roles in the epigenetic control of developmental processes. The majority of miRNAs found in human milk are recognized for their contribution to immune system competence26, and they are mainly plentiful31,42. miRNAs are believed to be implicated in various activities of the immune system, for instance B and T cell discrimination and development regulation, and native/adaptive immune reactions109. miRNAs can play a fundamental role in the regulation of autoimmune conditions, for instance inflammatory bowel110. As a result, these could serve as potential biomarkers in milk for identifying immune-related disorders, including allergic reactions111. During the first 6 months of lactation, human milk has been reported to contain high amounts of miRNAs with immune system-associated functions, such as miR-17, miR-155, miR-181a, miR-150, and miR-22346. Specifically, human milk contains high levels of miR-155 and miR-18131 that are proven to control B cell discrimination112, indicating a functional role in infant’s immune system development. Numerous miRNAs target cytokine genes in various immune cells, including monocytes, TH1, and TH2 cells, thereby modulating cytokine expression and circulation levels113. Other miRNAs present in human milk demonstrate diverse immunomodulatory effects on immune cells. For example, miR-10a plays a vital role in regulatory T cell specialization and stability114. Moreover, miRNAs influence B cell development and function, with some, like miR-155, regulating immunoglobulin production by plasma cells. Other miRNAs precisely control receptor editing during B cell maturation (e.g., miR-17∼92 cluster), clonal deletion (e.g., miR-148a), and antibody class switching to IgG and IgE secretion in B cells (e.g., miR-146a)115. Additionally, human milk-derived miRNAs impact non-immune system components involved in innate and adaptive immunity. For instance, miR-27b influences platelet function and reactivity pathways116, affecting their release of inflammatory and bioactive molecules and their immune functions, such as microbial engulfment. In addition, miR-142 may impact the survival and functions of red blood cells117, which act as innate immunity modulators by binding and scavenging specific molecules mediating inflammatory responses, including mitochondrial DNA and chemokines.

Similarly, human milk displayed high levels of miR-17 and miR-92, and given their role in the regulation of monocyte development and maturation and differentiation of B and T cells118,119, they could contribute to the development of infant’s immune system. Human milk also contains high levels of miR-22346, which is anticipated to activate granulocytes proliferation120. B cells-associated miRNAs, such as miR-155 and miR-181, which possibly induce the differentiation of B cells are also found in higher levels121. Conversely, miR-150, which is known to suppress B cells, is found in lower levels in the human milk122. In another study, many miRNAs were identified in human milk exosomes42. Among the predominant 10 miRNAs species, miR-148a-3p, miR-30b-5p, miR-182-5p, and miR-200a-3p were correlated to immune functions42. Particularly, miR-30b-5p is known to decrease immune cells activation and induce immunosuppression123. miR-182-5p encourages T cells-facilitated immune reactions124. Among 87 species detected in human milk exosomes, 59 pre-miRNAs are believed to have immune functions42, which agrees with miRNAs from skim human milk46.

The profile of miRNAs in bovine milk displayed a high similarity to that of human milk regarding immune-associated miRNAs36,53, even if this does not clearly interpret to the human newborns. miR-155 and miR-181a, which have essential functions in inflammation and regulation of the immune system31,125, were identified in colostrum and mature bovine milk, with high levels in the colostrum36,53. Compared to mature bovine milk, immune-associated miRNAs exist at higher amounts in the colostrum36. This seems to be one of the contributing factors in delivering enhanced immune support necessary in early life stages. It aligns with the elevated levels of immune cells and the higher levels of various humoral immune components, such as lactoferrin and secretory IgA, found in colostrum when compared to mature human milk37. Compared to mature bovine milk, colostrum had higher level of development- and immune-related miRNAs species; miR-155, miR-223 miR-34a, miR-130a, miR-15b, miR-27b, and miR-106b26. In addition, colostrum and mature milk bovine miRNA species, including miR-155, miR-223, miR-34a, miR-15b, and miR-106b were analyzed by using qPCR and compared, displaying substantially different amounts26. Compared to bovine milk26,36, porcine milk exosomes47 and rat skim milk126, it has been reported that miRNAs levels and expression in human milk were lower in colostrum than mature milk, since 386 miRNAs were identified in colostrum vs. 429 miRNAs in mature milk14. This warrants validation, along with study of variances in immune-associated miRNAs between mature human milk and colostrum, since these substances could contribute to newborns’ immunoprotection in the first days postnatal when it is most vulnerable, in addition to development of the immune system and long-term protection against infections.

Building on the comparative insights from human and bovine milk, it is also important to consider findings from other mammal milk species, such as porcine and rodent, which further explain the diversity and functional significance of immune-related miRNAs in milk. Skim human milk46 and porcine milk exosomes47 have been reported to contain immune responses-related miRNAs. Furthermore, 58 out of 84 immune-related miRNAs reported in the pathway central database were present in high levels in porcine milk exosomes47, in agreement with another study indicating that human milk exosomes showed higher amounts of immune-related miRNAs42. Among 13 major miRNAs in porcine milk exosomes, 12 species were present in higher amounts in the first 3 days and then decreased after 7, 14, 21, and 28 days postpartum67. These 12 miRNA species were all immune-associated, and have a role in immune response genes and proteins regulation127. Particularly, let-7a-1-5p regulates inflammation-related cytokine IL-6 that induces activators and transducers of transcription 3 signaling128, while miR-30c-2-5p and miR-1-5p regulate immunosuppression123. The intrinsic immune receptors could be regulated and activated by miR-21-5p from porcine milk, and a crucial cytokine receptor through targeting encoded cell death protein 4 and interleukin 12129. In addition, interleukin 12, which is adversely regulated by miR-21-5p can regulate T cells and innate killer cells as well130. The predominant miR-27b from porcine milk has been reported to provoke lipopolysaccharides, which inhibit and impair the peroxisome proliferator-stimulated receptor c, which is influential in inhibiting inflammations through macrophage immune reaction131.

While porcine milk exosomes have also been exhibited to contain a rich repertoire of the immune-correlated miRNAs, it is important to revisit bovine milk to further discover its immunological potential, specifically given its extensive use in infant nutrition. Numerous bovine immune-associated miRNAs109,132 have been extracted from milk microvesicles53, and were reported to be expressed in the mammary glands by using small RNA sequencing133. These species include miR101 and miR150, which are common T cells modulators134, and miR-223 that has been also reported to regulate innate immune cells activation and differentiation120,135. Bovine milk-derived miR-155 and miR-223 are implicated in several immunological functions109, and possibly have anti-inflammatory properties, particularly in colostrum. Furthermore, miR-155 can also regulate the differentiation of B and C cells, in addition to T helper cells modulation136. On the other hand, miR-223 adversely modulates neutrophil activation and proliferation109. miR-25-3p has been identified to target KLF4, which is a powerful inflammation mediator137, and has a functional role in immune development47. miR-30a-5p can foster cellular invasion and immunosuppression through GalNActransferase 7 targeting123. By restraining forkhead box protein O1, miR-21-targeted gene in cancer138,139, miR-182-5p promotes T cells-mediated immune reactions124. miR-200c-3p has been reported to regulate the differentiation of T cells via targeting zinc finger E-box-binding homeobox 1 protein140, in addition to CD4 differentiation regulation141. Furthermore, expression behavior of bovine milk miRNAs has been stated to be varied during serious mammary glands inflammation like mastitis142. Gene target evaluation of upregulated (miR-223) and downregulated (miR-15b) milk miRNAs revealed numerous functions of these species toward mastitis143. During mastitis, most milk miRNAs were downregulated, indicating their active role in regulating the response of mammary immune to Streptococcus uberis, one of the most causes of mastitis in dairy cattle143.

The recent advancement in biotechnology has led to discovery of milk-derived exosomal miRNAs as natural immunomodulatory agents. Numerous studies investigated the use of exosome-encapsulated miRNAs as delivery systems for immunotherapy, vaccines, and autoimmune disease management144,145. Bovine milk exosomes are being explored as scalable, biocompatible carriers for small RNA drugs because of their stability and safety profile146. These developments highlight the potential commercial translation of milk-derived miRNAs into therapeutic and nutraceutical formulations aimed at improving immune functions. The immunomodulatory actions of milk-derived miRNAs are mediated via key signaling pathways that regulate immune cell activation and differentiation. For example, miR-155 regulates both adaptive and innate immune responses by targeting negative regulators of JAK/STAT and NF-κB pathways, in that way supporting T-cell activation and cytokine production147. miR-181a fine-tunes T-cell receptor sensitivity by adjusting phosphatase expression levels involved in antigen recognition148. Likewise, miR-146a functions as a feedback inhibitor of inflammation by targeting TRAF6 and IRAK1, resulting in suppression of excessive NF-κB activation149. These mechanisms ensure immune homeostasis and the maturation of immune competence in newborns.

Gut health

Human milk miRNAs have potential functions in gut maturation. It has been reported that more than 4 miRNA species were identified with high levels and possibly affecting the gut functions and structure27. This effect is exceptionally influential due to gut permeability throughout the first days of life. Epithelial cells of the intestine have an essential role in the selective uptake of nutrients. The interactions between milk extracellular vesicles and intestinal epithelial cells have been demonstrated23, indicating their regulation by the human milk miRNAs. Accordingly, miR-99b, which is a predominant species in milk, may enhance intestinal milk fat globule membrane protein E8 expression and restore enterocyte migration in mice150. The miR-200 group, which is predominant in human milk extracellular vesicles, may also induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition, which regulates epithelia structure. miR-200 species are significant gatekeepers of the epithelial state, restricting promesenchymal genes expression that manage epithelial-mesenchymal transition151. miR-200c has been reported to control various crucial signaling interactions, for instance transforming growth factor β, phosphoinositide 3 kinase/protein kinase, notch, and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells signaling152.

Also, miR-200/375 has been reported to regulate epithelial plasticity-related alternative merging via suppressing RNA-binding protein quaking acknowledged to completely bind to and control alternative merging targets151. miR-200b has been shown to in vitro inhibit strict connection interference of intestinal epithelial cells153. Likewise, miR-21 modifies the functions of intestinal epithelial barrier154. Gut microbiota has several fundamental roles in host metabolism and accordingly, can regulate numerous biological activities. Gut microbiota acts together with the host to retain synergy155. miRNAs have been revealed to be involved in intercommunications between gut microbiota and the host156. This intercommunication embraces the effect of gut microbiota on the host miRNAs expression, but also the effects of host miRNAs on gut microbiota155. miRNAs can regulate the growth of microorganisms via penetrating the microbial cells156. Since gut microbiota could be affected by numerous factors, such as the host diet157, it is assumed that gut microbiota could affect dietary miRNAs. Nevertheless, these potential impacts require further investigation, specifically the roles of milk miRNAs in human newborns and their influences.

In the biomedical sector, miRNAs-based methodologies are being assessed for gastrointestinal health management and microbiome regulation. Milk exosomes have been investigated as oral delivery vehicles for miRNAs targeting intestinal inflammation and epithelial repair, leveraging their natural resistance to the gastric degradation158. These applications propose promising translational pathways for milk-derived miRNAs in the treatment or prevention of gut-associated disorders such as the inflammatory bowel disease159. Several miRNAs in human milk, such as miR-21, miR-200c, and miR-99b, have been reported to regulate intestinal epithelial cell differentiation and barrier integrity through defined molecular pathways160,161. miR-21 regulates PI3K/Akt and TGF-β signaling cascades162, in that way affecting tight-junction assembly and epithelial restitution following injury. Moreover, miR-200c modulates the epithelial–mesenchymal transition by targeting ZEB1 and ZEB2163, maintaining epithelial polarity and preventing permeability loss. In addition, miR-99b enhances enterocyte migration and absorption of lipids through upregulating milk fat globule membranes protein E8 gene150. These processes contribute to gut maturation and establish an efficient intestinal barrier in early life.

Lipids and glucose metabolism regulation

miRNAs have been isolated in large numbers from human milk lipid vesicles involving fat globules31, and from skim milk and cells30. This has established a foundation for utilizing both intra-vesicle and extracellular miRNAs as potential indicators for various diseases in molecular diagnostic applications164. While lipids metabolism is typically controlled by external factors, it has been shown that miRNAs play a role in regulating genes linked to lipids metabolism at the post-transcriptional stages165. These genes are responsible for managing processes, such as cholesterol balance, the breakdown of fatty acids, and the production of lipids. This discovery opens up new avenues for treating various disorders related to abnormal lipid levels in the body166.

There are few miRNAs with lipids regulatory functions, including miR-33, miR-122, miR-335, miR-370, miR-125a-5p, and miR-378-3p166. These species have been detected in a large quantity in the lipids fraction of human milk31, human skim milk14, human milk cells20, human colostrum14, human milk exosomes42, in addition to bovine colostrum and skim milk36, indicating that they have a fundamental role in lipids synthesis and/or metabolism. It has been reported that miR-33 may regulate the homeostasis of cholesterol at cellular levels167,168. ABCA1 is one of the most important expected genes targeted by miR-33. This gene produces cholesterol efflux modulator protein, which is responsible for the regulation of cellular phosphate and cholesterol homeostasis, besides transferring cholesterol outside of the cells169. ABCG1 is also targeted by miR-33, which decreases cholesterol efflux to serum and high-density lipoproteins in macrophages164,170.

Beyond immune regulation, milk-derived miRNAs also play a substantial role in lipid metabolism, influencing the nutritional and functional complexity of milk among species. miR-125a-5p has been also detected in high levels in several mammal milk species, regulating oxysterol binding protein-associated protein 9171, which is included in different lipids metabolism processes172,173 comprising lipids absorption induction by macrophages171. Human milk miR-10314,31 has been shown to regulate the synthesis of dairy lipids, promoting the synthesis of fat globules and agglomeration of unsaturated fatty acids and triacylglycerols174. The elevated expression of miR-103 has been recognized as a key modulator in the production and makeup of milk lipids, as well as the nutritional profile of milk. Notably, reducing miR-103 expression did not appear to influence lipid buildup in goat milk-producing cells, indicating that other mechanisms may be involved in regulating mammary fat metabolism or compensating for this effect174. Moreover, milk-derived miR-193b and miR-365 have been reported to regulate the synthesis of lipids, upregulating brown lipids discrimination through improving the expression of Runt-correlated transcription factor 1 translocated to 1175.

Besides the fat globules-associated miRNAs, numerous species have been shown to be bundled into other carrier lipids, for instance microvesicles, exosomes, and programmed cell death, which are formed by several type of cells, for instance the immune cells176, and several of which are present in human milk42. These are identified as carriers of lipid particles177, packaging both lipoproteins and miRNAs165, and possess a fundamental role in extracellular miRNAs delivery to the recipient cells. For instance, microvesicles transport miR-150 from macrophages-like cells to the microvascular endothelial cells, where it is assumed to control cell migration and target c-Myb178. In addition, miRNA species derived from adipocytes, such as miR-16, miR-27a, and miR-146b transported to other recipients’ cells through microvesicles179. Hence, it is suggested that miRNAs present in milk fat globules and exosomes/microvesicles are relocated to recipient cells of the gastrointestinal system in infant, and a quantity may move to the blood circulation and then transport to other tissues, acting a regulation functionality.

miRNAs play significant roles in regulating glucose metabolism, impacting various aspects of cellular and systemic glucose homeostasis. They can regulate insulin sensitivity by targeting genes involved in insulin signaling pathways. miR-375 has been shown to regulate insulin secretion and β-cell function180. miRNAs have a role in hepatic glucose production since miRNAs such as miR-122 and miR-33a/b are involved in regulating hepatic glucose production. These miRNAs can modulate the expression of genes involved in gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis, thereby affecting blood glucose levels. Furthermore, miRNAs like miR-29 and miR-143 are known to affect glucose uptake and utilization in tissues such as skeletal muscle and adipose tissue. They do this by targeting key proteins in glucose transport and glycolysis pathways181. Abnormal expression of certain miRNAs has been linked to the development of type 2 diabetes. miR-194 has been found to modulate glucose metabolism in skeletal muscle, and its reduced expression is associated with insulin resistance and diabetes180. Given their regulatory roles, miRNAs are being explored as potential therapeutic targets for managing diabetes and other metabolic disorders. Modulating the levels of specific miRNAs could help restore normal glucose metabolism and improve insulin sensitivity.

Besides their activities in lipids metabolism, miR-33 and miR-26a are also shown to regulate the metabolism of glucose. miR-33b is described to restrain expression of glucose 6-phosphatase and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, two main enzymes of hepatic gluconeogenesis causing glucose production regulation182. Hepatic miR-26a participation in the metabolism of lipids and glucose and insulin signaling through regulating key metabolic genes proposes miR-26a is a favorable new target for obesity-related metabolic diseases treatment183. Human milk extracellular vesicles-derived miR-33a and miR-143 have been correlated with energy metabolism and glucose homeostasis in a model of Per-Arnt-Sim kinase–lacking mice fed on a high-fat diet184. miR-143 role in glucose homeostasis has been reported in mice with a high expression of this species185. Furthermore, miR-181b can regulate homeostasis of adipose glucose and sensitivity of insulin186. These findings indicate the potential functions of human milk miRNAs in metabolism, affecting infants’ growth and development, and consequently their instant health besides their future.

Commercial interest in miRNAs-based therapeutics for metabolic syndromes has grown promptly. Synthetic or exosome-derived miRNAs targeting insulin sensitivity and lipids metabolism, such as miR-122 and miR-33 are investigated for dyslipidemia, obesity, and diabetes management187. Exosome-based delivery systems are developed to improve metabolic regulation, emphasizing the translational potential of milk-derived miRNAs as naturally optimized patterns for metabolic health involvements188. The regulatory roles of milk-derived miRNAs in metabolic homeostasis are closely correlated to specific gene targets and signaling cascades. It has been reported that miR-33a/b represses cholesterol efflux by downregulating ABCA1 and ABCG1, accordingly modulating intracellular cholesterol and lipid transport189. In addition, miR-122, a liver-enriched miRNA detected in milk, regulates hepatic lipid and glucose metabolism by modulating SREBP1c, FASN, and AMPK pathways190. Furthermore, miR-103 and miR-107 control insulin sensitivity via the suppression of caveolin-1 and insulin receptor substrate 1, influencing glucose uptake and lipids accumulation191. These mechanisms explain how milk miRNAs can epigenetically impact energy metabolism and lipid synthesis in infants.

Nervous system regulation

Few miRNA species in human milk extracellular vesicles are found to affect neural development in vivo. The predominant species belong to the let-7 group. Neural differentiation of cells is linked to enhanced processing of let-7 precursors, indicating that let-7 plays a role in early developmental control of embryonic stem cell differentiation and neurogenesis, as demonstrated in mice both in vitro and in vivo192. miR-29b has been identified in human milk extracellular vesicles and has been shown to affect neural development. In vivo reduction of miR-29b in mice causes death of nervous cell193. miR-574-5p, which is relatively predominant in human milk extracellular vesicles, also has a role in neural development. The downregulation and overexpression of these species, respectively, restrain and stimulate neural development in mesenchymal stem cells of rats194. Another three species, including miR-15b, miR-132, and miR-210 were identified in low levels in human milk, and were reported as neurogenesis-influencing. Another let-7 group species, miR-15b indorses neuronal discrimination and restrains neural progenitor production in mice195. miR-132 has been reported to affect the central nervous system through memory improvement196. On the other hand, inhibition of miR-210 was stated to improve nerve cells survival in vitro197.

Expanding beyond their developmental functions, various milk-derived miRNAs have also been involved in neurological disorders and show differential expression profiles in preterm milk, signifying potential significance to early neurodevelopment and disease susceptibility. The miR-107 group involves miR-107, miR-16-5p, miR-103a-3p, miR-195-5p and others. The level of these species is greater in moderate/very preterm colostrum compared to moderate/very preterm mature milk. Additionally, miR-16-5p shows reduced expression in moderate/very preterm mature milk when compared to term mature milk. In neuroblastoma cells, increased expression of miR-103 leads to decreased cell division and enhanced differentiation198. miR-103 has a low expression in Alzheimer’s disease patients199 and enhanced in moderate/very preterm contrasted with moderate/very preterm mature milk. In addition, miR-107 is upregulated in the schizophrenia cortex. Possible effects on cells, with notable concentration of miRNA targets in neuronal pathfinding and synaptic plasticity200. miR-107 has been shown to be substantially higher in schizophrenia cerebrospinal fluid201. miR-107 has a lower expression compared to other miRNAs as Alzheimer’s disease increased in brain samples’ progressive cortical grey matter202. The levels of miR-107 significantly decreased even in patients with the earliest stages of Alzheimer’s disease pathology203-downregulation of miR-107 in Alzheimer’s disease grey matter of the middle temporal cerebral cortex204. In Alzheimer’s disease patients, miR-107 is downregulated199. Studies have shown that miR-16 levels are reduced in tissues affected by Alzheimer’s disease and that it helps mitigate the neurotoxic effects of β-amyloid205. Additionally, researchers have observed decreased levels of miR-16-5p in the cerebrospinal fluid of individuals diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease206.

In addition to their altered expression in neurodegenerative conditions, numerous miRNAs found in preterm milk also demonstrate regulatory roles in early neural development, further highlighting their potential implication in neonatal brain maturation. Compared to term mature milk, colostrum has overexpression of miR-141-3p and miR-200c. These species inhibit the neural induction of human embryonic stem cells and show reduced expression in neuroectodermal precursor cells207. Furthermore, miR-200c exhibits elevated levels in moderate/very preterm colostrum compared to moderate/very preterm mature milk. This pattern is like that of miR-30a-5p. An increase in miR-30a-5p leads to a reduction in brain-derived neurotrophic factor protein expression, indicating its role in regulating this neurotrophin during adulthood208.

This early-life regulatory effect of miRNAs naturally extends into broader neurodevelopmental and neurological contexts. Certain miRNAs have fundamental roles in both vertebrates and invertebrates during neurogenesis and brain activities. The flexibility, reversibility, and speed of miRNAs function offer accurate progressive and spatial gene modulatory capabilities that are fundamental for the appropriate performance of the brain209. During pregnancy, the fetus is remarkably susceptible to the influences of diverse environmental conditions, with effects that can endure into childhood, adolescence, and adulthood210. There is growing evidence that neurological disorders are caused by miRNAs malfunction. In addition, autism, schizophrenia, and intellectual disability are examples of neurodevelopmental disorders that can result from pathophysiological conditions that interfere with neural networks211. Gene sets linked to autism, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and depression have highly enriched differentially expressed miRNA targets, but not those linked to epilepsy, neurodegenerative disorders, or other adult-onset mental illnesses212. Different hub miRNA expression patterns or levels can cause and/or worsen several adult-onset nerve-related disorders213. In addition to their gene-regulation capabilities, clusterin gene and calreticulin and kallikrein related peptidase 6 but less intensity, codify chaperone proteins concerned in α-Syn degradation and Aβ clearance214, it associates miRNAs to be the main regulators in the complex genetic network of the central nervous system215.

Given their neurodevelopmental and neuroprotective functions, several milk-derived miRNAs, such as miR-107, miR-132, and miR-29b are being studied as biomarkers or therapeutic candidates for neurodegenerative diseases, such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s216. The biotechnology industry is actively expanding exosome-facilitated delivery approaches for brain-targeted miRNA therapy, developing the ability of lipid vesicles to cross biological barriers217. These insights reinforce the commercial and clinical demand of milk miRNAs for neurotherapeutic revolution. The neuroprotective impact of milk miRNAs is primarily facilitated via pathways that control neurogenesis, neuronal survival, and synaptic plasticity. miR-132 advances neurite outgrowth and synaptic maturation via activating CREB–BDNF axis218. let-7 family members control neural progenitor differentiation through the modulation of the Notch and Lin28 pathways219, ensuring accurate timing of neuronal commitment. miR-107 affects cognitive functions and axonal growth by targeting Dicer1 and BACE1220, in this way linking miRNAs metabolism with neuronal gene expressions. These mechanisms highlight the potential role of milk-derived miRNAs in advancing brain development and neuroplasticity throughout infancy.

Cardiovascular diseases

Ongoing clinical studies on miRNA-based medications reveal that individual patient miRNA levels are now being utilized as a criterion for heart disease prediction. Numerous investigations have observed significant variations in miRNA expression across different conditions, including various heart-related disorders221,222. This has prompted researchers to conclude that miRNAs could serve as effective biomarkers for cardiovascular ailments or as potential targets for therapy. These miRNA alterations were noted in both plasma and heart tissue223. It has become evident that multiple miRNAs can be implicated in a single cardiovascular condition or in various heart-related diseases.

Research has shown that patients with coronary artery disease exhibit elevated levels of miR-1, miR-21, and miR-208, while experiencing reduced expressions of miR-133, miR-126-3p, miR-195, miR-145, miR-17, and miR-155222,224. Dong et al.225 proposed that the high levels of miR-126-3p observed in non-infarcted regions of rat hearts following an infarction might indicate its crucial role in myocardial recovery post-infarction226. Reddy et al.227 and Schulte and Zeller228 revealed a notable connection between increased plasma miR-33 levels and coronary artery diseases. John et al.229 identified miR-93-5p as the most dysregulated miRNA in coronary artery disease patients, suggesting it could be the strongest predictor of the condition in their research. Furthermore, the suppression of ATP-binding cassette A1 by miR-93-5p has been linked to elevated circulating cholesterol levels, potentially contributing to coronary atherosclerosis and coronary artery disease230.

Numerous miRNAs exhibit alterations in heart failure models, including miR-199b, miR-195, miR-100, miR-133, miR-24, and miR-208222. During heart failure, miR-199b levels increase, seemingly targeting the calcineurin/nuclear factor of activated T cells pathway. miR-199b targets the nuclear factor of activated T cells kinase dual-specificity tyrosine-(Y)-phosphorylation regulated kinase 1a, forming a pathogenic feed-forward mechanism that affects calcineurin-responsive gene expression. In mouse models of heart failure, in vivo suppression of miR-199b resulted in normalized Dyrk1a expression, decreased nuclear factor of activated T cells activity, and reduced hypertrophy and fibrosis231. Additional studies on heart failure have demonstrated altered expressions of miRNAs miR-1, miR-214, miR-29b, miR-342, miR-7, miR-107, miR-126, miR-125, miR-122, miR-423-5p, miR-320a, miR-650, miR-1228, miR-662, miR-583, miR-3175, miR-21, miR-22, and miR-92b223,232.

miRNA therapeutics have entered clinical development for cardiovascular conditions, with species such as anti-miR-92a and miR-132 inhibitors undergoing trials for heart failure and ischemia233. Although these are not yet milk-derived, the scalability and stability of milk exosomal miRNAs present an attractive platform for future cardiovascular treatments. The integration of milk miRNA systems into drug delivery pipelines could speed up their commercialization in cardiometabolic medicine. Cardiovascular-related miRNAs found in milk exert their effects through the control of signaling pathways governing cardiac remodeling, angiogenesis, and endothelial integrity. miR-21 promotes cardiac fibroblast activation and fibrosis via TGF-β/Smad and MAPK/ERK pathways234, whereas miR-133 and miR-1 control the cardiomyocyte hypertrophy by targeting calcineurin/NFAT and GATA4 signaling235. In addition, miR-126 supports vascular homeostasis by enhancing endothelial repair through the PI3K/Akt/eNOS axis236. These processes provide a molecular foundation for the cardioprotective and developmental functions of milk-derived miRNAs.

Other benefits of milk miRNAs

There are more opportunities to use miRNAs as biomarkers in both health and disease because of their presence in exosomes and possible role as extracellular regulators237,238, besides modeling for therapy239. Human milk miRNAs may play a number of physiopathological activities, including regulation of cell discrimination and development, besides affecting infant development240,241. miR-148a-3p is one of the predominant species in human milk63, which is also present in other milk species242,243, regulates DNA methyltransferase 3b and inhibits its expression, potentially to accelerate the methylation of DNA during development244. Considering that lactocytes constitute most cells in healthy mature human milk245, miRNA composition in human milk reflects that of the lactating mammary epithelium. This correlation provides a foundation for investigating the potential use of human milk miRNAs as easily accessible, non-invasive biomarkers to assess the functionality of lactating breasts.

In addition to their potential as biomarkers signaling mammary glands function, several miRNAs found in milk seem to originate from maternal tissues, indicating broader physiological roles in supporting infant organ function and development. Human milk also contains some tissues-related miRNAs46, but in lower levels than in organs and tissues42. An example for this, organs such as liver (miR-122), hematogenic system (miR-142-3p and miR-142-5p), and pancreas (miR-216 and miR-217) showed high expression in these species than human milk246, indicating that these miRNA species may come from the maternal bloodstream to particularly target the growth, development, and functions of the corresponding organs in the human milk-fed infants. Simultaneously, they may have specific roles in the breast throughout the lactation period. Likewise, in bovine milk, miRNAs have been detected as tissues-specific miRNAs exist in low amounts and with lower expression in both colostrum and mature milk36. These comprise for instance miR-1 and miR-133 in muscles247, miR-9 and miR-124a in the brain248, miR-216 and miR-217 in the pancreas249, miR-122 in the liver247, miR-451 in blood cells250, and miR-126 in endothelial cells251.

Beyond their tissue-specific origins, milk-derived miRNAs are progressively recognized for their distinct regulatory functions in infant physiology, comprising adipogenesis, immune regulation, neurodevelopment, and appetite modulation. Gene targets of 308 miRNAs in human milk lipids have numerous functions, specifically to regulate immune responses, and gene expression and metabolism31, indicating the prospective significance of these species for human milk-fed infant252. In addition, various human milk miRNAs are expected to contribute to the central nervous system regulation. Human milk miR-118.2 has been reported to target teneurin transmembrane protein 231, implying regulatory functions in neurogenesis and stimulation of connection construction in the nervous system in infants253. Milk-derived miRNAs may also target adipogenesis infants. By targeting the CCAAT-enhancer-binding protein β, excessive expression of miR-155254 has been thought to reduce the mass of brown adipose tissue255. miR-29a derived from milk deactivates insulin-induced gene 1 protein gene256, which is a probable adipogenesis regulator, and this was progressively correlated with body mass index257. Beyond their direct impact on fat accumulation, human milk miRNAs may play a role in regulating infant appetite over short and long periods. This effect, combined with various appetite-controlling hormones found in human milk such as leptin, adiponectin, insulin, ghrelin, and others, contributes to appetite management benefits provided to infants through breastfeeding258.

Beside their engagement in tissue functions and normal metabolism, several miRNA species have been reported to target cancer-related genes259. These molecules could be applied as biomarkers of cancer for both diagnosis and prognosis260, and some of miRNA species exist in milk, and more particularly in human milk. Increased risk of adult cancers associated with bovine milk consumption has been epidemiologically approved261, and this may be due to bovine milk content of oncogenic miRNAs262; however, human milk miRNAs seem to have regular lactation-definite functions for both the infant and mammary glands263. Human milk miRNAs have been suggested to provide protection for the infant through to adulthood from cancer264. miR-21265,266, is similarly identified to have overexpression in human hepatocellular cancer. Accordingly, miR-21 deregulation can be connected with human hepatocellular cancer growth by regulation signaling of mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1, for instance, expression of phosphatase and tensin homolog267.

Challenges, gaps, and limitations

Although significant progress has been made in understanding the functional roles of miRNAs in human milk and infant formulas, several key challenges and critical gaps persist. The variability in sampling, isolation, and identification methods makes cross-studies comparison challenging. Differences in RNA isolation kits, normalization techniques, and sequencing platforms regularly lead to inconsistent miRNA profiles. To enable reproducibility and accelerate meta-analysis across different findings, it is vital to establish standardized methods and confirmed miRNA references. While numerous studies have identified several miRNAs derived from milk, only a small fraction have been functionally validated in vivo. The direct physiological effect of these miRNAs on infant health, particularly regarding their absorption, tissue targeting, and systemic impact, remains insufficiently characterized. Comprehensive animal and clinical models are required to conclude if milk miRNAs can withstand digestion, enter the bloodstream, and impact gene expression in recipient tissues. Most of the existing studies are cross-sectional and involve small sample sizes. There is a pressing need for extensive, longitudinal research to explore how factors such as maternal diet, health status, genetics, and environmental exposures affect milk miRNAs profile over time and how these differences relate to infant growth, immunity, and disease susceptibility. Furthermore, research on miRNAs in infant formula is still limited. The degradation of miRNAs throughout processing, removal of lipids, and storage, along with the bioavailability of the residual miRNAs, need further investigation. Advanced formulation and preservation practices are needed to retain or enhance miRNAs in commercial products to more closely replicate human milk. While milk-derived miRNAs show potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic agents, their application in clinical or nutritional contexts faces regulatory and safety challenges. Systematic pharmacokinetic and toxicological measurements are essential before incorporating miRNAs-based enrichment into infant nutrition strategies.

Conclusion and future prospect

miRNAs in human milk play a crucial role in infant development, particularly in immune system maturation, neural development, and metabolism. These small regulatory molecules are abundant in human milk and are bioavailable to infants, contributing to their overall health and development. In contrast, while infant formulas do contain some miRNAs, their levels are significantly lower compared to human milk. This difference highlights the unique benefits of breastfeeding, as the higher miRNAs content in human milk provides more robust support for the infant’s developmental needs. Overall, the presence of miRNAs in human milk underscores the importance of breastfeeding for optimal infant health, while also pointing to potential areas for improving infant formula to better mimic the beneficial properties of human milk. The extensive research on miRNAs in various biofluids has been driven by their potential applications in preventive medicine and treatment. Notably, the expression of miRNAs in human milk has been linked to certain diseases in infants and mothers, such as breast neoplasms and neonatal jaundice. Although the evidence for this association is uncertain, it represents a potential initial step towards using these molecules for disease monitoring. The methodological techniques used in isolating, characterizing, and functionally analyzing miRNAs in biofluids significantly impact the results obtained. The reviewed studies employed various methods for processing, fractionating, and quantifying miRNAs, indicating potential challenges in standardizing the detection and analysis of these molecules. Future studies on miRNAs in human milk are focusing on several promising areas (i) bioavailability and absorption: researchers are investigating how miRNAs in human milk are absorbed by infants and how they influence gene expression and development, (ii) nutritional therapy: there is growing interest in the potential of miRNAs for preventive strategies in nutritional therapy, particularly in how they might help in programming infant health and development, and (iii) impact on infant health: studies are exploring the role of miRNAs in postnatal gut maturation, nutrient uptake, and overall infant health. This includes understanding how miRNAs vary with factors like gestational age and maternal health. These areas of research could lead to advancements in infant nutrition and health, potentially influencing the development of new infant formulas and therapeutic approaches.
