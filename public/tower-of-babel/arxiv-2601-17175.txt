Beyond Wald’s Equation and the Optional Sampling Theorem
Michael J. Klass, Victor H. de la Peña

Abstract
From the perspective of expectations of randomly stopped sums, Wald’s equation and the Optional Sampling Theorem identify situations in which the stopping time can be decoupled from the stopping place, acting as if the two were independent. Herein we consider arbitrary mean zero Martingales and their extended-valued stopping times, proving two fundamental theorems and a general corollary. Examples, counter-examples and discussion are also included. The first theorem establishes conditions under which a stopped martingale’s expected value can be characterized by a finite real number, also yielding a new expectation limit. Two corollaries and an application provide information on the rate of decay of the tail probability of the stopping time. The second theorem provides sufficient conditions to ensure that an extended-valued stopping time is finite with probability one. We demonstrate these results through various examples and explore their implications for different families of martingales. Our findings extend classical results in martingale theory and provide new insights into the behavior of stopped martingales, especially when the expected values are the stopped Martingale on the set where the extended-valued stopping time T T is finite is different from the expected value of the Martingale at time 1.



1
Introduction

The study of martingales and their stopping times has been fundamental to
probability theory and its applications. In this paper, we examine two key
aspects of this relationship: the expectation of stopped martingales and
conditions needed to ensure that extended-valued stopping times are finite almost surely. Our first result extends the classical
optional stopping theorem by providing conditions under which the expected
value of a stopped martingale can be characterized by a finite real number, even
when the stopping time may be infinite with positive probability.

The second main result addresses a practical question in martingale theory:
When can we guarantee that an extended-valued stopping time is finite? We present sufficient conditions that ensure this property and demonstrate their individual necessity through counter-examples. Our analysis reveals interesting connections between the “wobbling” behavior of martingales and their stopping properties.

We also introduce three families of sums of i.i.d. mean zero random variables with distinct characteristics,
demonstrating how our theoretical results apply in specific contexts. These families provide concrete examples where the expected overshoot remains constant
within each family, leading to explicit calculations of the key quantities in
Theorem 1.
Later this application of constant expected overshoot is expanded to general martingales.

2
Historical Context

The study of stopped martingales has a rich history dating back to Doob’s
fundamental work on martingale theory in the 1940s. One of the earliest
results involving sums of random variables with random stopping times was
introduced by
Wald 1944
in 1944, establishing what came to be known as
Wald’s equation. Soon after,
Blackwell 1946
extended and generalized Wald’s
lemma, providing further foundational tools for analyzing sums at random times.

The optional stopping theorem represents one of the most important results in this area, providing
conditions under which
E(M_{T})\!=\!E(M_{1})
for a martingale
\{M_{n}\}
and a stopping
time
T
.

Significant advancements were made by
Chow, Robbins, and Teicher 1965
in their seminal 1965 paper on moments of randomly stopped sums, which established foundational results for the behavior of stopped sequences. Later,
Klass 1988
,

Klass 1990
provided a best possible improvement of Wald’s equation, extending our understanding of the expectations of randomly stopped sums beyond the traditional i.i.d. setting. In doing so it was shown that the stopping time could be decoupled from a random sum. As noted by
Grimmett and Stirzaker 2001
, “Wald’s equations provide a systematic approach to computing expectations involving random sums, with applications ranging from sequential analysis to financial mathematics” (p.418).

The relationship between martingales and U-statistics was explored by
de la Peña and Lai 1997
, who investigated Wald’s equation and asymptotic bias of randomly stopped U-statistics. Their work demonstrated that “the behavior of randomly stopped U-statistics exhibits systematic biases that can be characterized through martingale methods”
(
de la Peña and Lai 1997
, p.919)
. Further extensions to martingale theory through decoupling techniques were developed by
de la Peña and Zamfirescu 2002
, establishing powerful domination inequalities with applications to Wald’s identity. As they noted, “decoupling inequalities provide a unified framework for establishing sharp bounds on martingale processes”
(
de la Peña and Zamfirescu 2002
, p.158)
.

Contrary to the impression of prior results,
Klass 2009
showed that the stopping times associated with denormalized U-statistics cannot always
be decoupled.

The work presented in this paper builds upon this historical foundation while extending the theory to address cases where traditional assumptions may not hold, particularly when stopping times can be infinite with positive probability. The traditional conclusion that
EM_{T}I(T<\infty)=EM_{1}
need not hold. Our approach integrates techniques from several traditions in probability theory, connecting classical results with modern applications.

Main Results and Examples

3.1
A theorem on expected values of stopped martingales

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\{M_{n}\}
be a mean zero martingale sequence and
T
an extended-valued
stopping time with respect to its filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\}
. Suppose
E|M_{T}|I(T<\infty)

is finite. Then there is a finite real number
L
such that
EM_{T}I(T<\infty)=L

and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}EM_{n}I(T>n)
exists and equals
-L
. (The point is, neither
L
nor

P(T=\infty)
need be 0.)

This theorem extends to random sequences
\{M_{n}\}
taking values in a Banach
space by means of considering
\Lambda(M_{n})
for bounded linear functionals
\Lambda
.

Proof.
By the Dominated Convergence Theorem,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}EM_{T}1(T\leq n)=EM_{T}1(T<\infty)=L.

Since
EM_{T\wedge n}=0
and
M_{T\wedge n}
splits as

\displaystyle M_{T\wedge n}=M_{T}1(T\leq n)+M_{n}1(T>n),

we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}EM_{T}1(T\leq n)+\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}EM_{n}1(T>n)

\displaystyle=L+\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}EM_{n}1(T>n).

Hence the limit of
EM_{n}1(T>n)
always exists and
=-L
, proving the theorem. The same reasoning can be carried out in Banach spaces by applying bounded linear functionals to the martingale.
\square

This result generalizes Theorem 4.1 of Klass (1988) in two ways. In the first place, the stopping time
is allowed to be extended valued, taking value infinity with positive probability. Secondly, the new result
only requires
E|M_{T}|I(T\!<\!\infty)\!<\!\infty
instead of
\lim_{n\to\infty}E|M_{T\wedge n}|<\infty
.
The result also generalizes Theorem 2.3 of Chow, Robbins, and Siegmund (1971) by showing that

L
may exist and be zero (or not) when
\liminf_{n\to\infty}|M_{n}|I(T>n)>0
as the following example reveals.

Example 1.
Let
M_{n}
be a mean zero martingale such that for some
L\!<\!\infty
,
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|M_{n}|=\infty
a.s. and

\liminf_{n\to\infty}|M_{n}|<L
a.s. Let
T_{0}=0
. For all
k\geq 1
, let
T_{2k-1}={\mbox{first }}n>T_{2k-2}
such that
|M_{n}|>k^{3}
and

T_{2k}={\mbox{ first }}n>T_{2k-1}
such that
|M_{n}|<L
. Then for
k\geq 2
, let
T^{*}=T_{2k}
with probability
k^{-2}
and

T^{*}=\infty
otherwise. Then
E|M_{T^{*}}|I(T^{*}<\infty)<\infty
and yet
\lim_{n\to\infty}E|M_{T^{*}\wedge n}|I(T^{*}<\infty)=\infty
.

Take any

(1)

Proof.
Let
U_{n}=M_{n}I(T>n)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}EV_{n}(E|U_{n}|^{p})^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\ .

This corollary is most useful when

. It also applies to Banach space random elements
provided the absolute values are replaced by the norm, or by bounded linear functionals.
The corollary can be further extended, obtaining utmost generality, by means of the Hausdorff-Young inequality.

Applying the essence of the preceding proof to real valued cases in which
EM_{n}^{2}=n

Using
p=2

for any extended-valued stopping time

Three families of martingales

Here are three families of random variables that can be used as summands of
three kinds of martingales which have associated computable quantities.

•

C_{1}
is the family of mean-zero integer-valued random variables whose
maximum value is
+1
.

•

For each
0<p<1
,
C(2,p)
is the family of integer-valued mean-zero

X

P(X=k)=c(1-p)p^{k}

0<c<\frac{1}{p}

•

For each
a>0

Associated with these collections are three families of mean-zero integer-valued martingales

A theorem on almost sure finiteness of the stopping time

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\{M_{n},{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\}
be a mean zero martingale. Suppose:

(i)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}P(\sup_{n\geq k}|M_{n}-M_{k}|>\varepsilon)=1
.

(ii)

\exists B<\infty
such that for every
n\geq 1
,

E\{M_{n}|T\geq n,M_{n}>0\geq M_{n-1}\}\leq\!B
almost surely.

(iii)

Define
T
to be the first
n>0
such that
M_{n}>0
, or set
T=\infty
if no
such
n
exists.

Then
P(T<\infty)=1
and
EM_{T}\leq B
, so Theorem 3.1 applies. Condition
(i) ensures the martingale wobbles arbitrarily far out the sequence.
Condition (ii) bounds the expected overshoot of
M_{T}
beyond zero.

Proof.
For
z>0
let
T_{z}=\begin{cases}\min\{n>0:M_{n}>0\text{ or }M_{n}<-z\}\\
\infty\text{ if no such }n\text{ exists.}\end{cases}

Write
M_{n}=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}
, set
Y_{j,z}=\max\{X_{j},-z\}
and let
M_{n,z}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j,z}
. For each
z>0
,
M_{T_{z}\wedge n,z}
is an
L^{1}
-bounded submartingale,
so it converges almost surely. Hence the wobbles of the submartingale tend to 0.

Fix any
z>0
. We intend to show that
P(T_{z}=\infty)=0
. To obtain a
contradiction, suppose there exists
\delta>0
such that
P(T_{z}=\infty)\!=\!2\delta
. By
condition (i) there exists
0<\varepsilon\leq\min\{\delta,\frac{z}{2}\}
and
k_{0}
such that for infinitely many
k\geq k_{0}
,

P\left(\sup_{n\geq k}|M_{n}-M_{k}|>\varepsilon\right)>1-\varepsilon.

Since
M_{T_{z}\wedge n,z}
converges almost surely there exists
0<\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon
and
k_{1}\geq k_{0}

such that for all
k\geq k_{1}
,

P\left(\sup_{n\geq k}|M_{T_{z}\wedge n,z}-M_{T_{z}\wedge k,z}|>\varepsilon_{1}\right)<\varepsilon.

Hence

\displaystyle 2\delta-\varepsilon

\displaystyle<P\left(\{T_{z}=\infty\}\cap\{\sup_{n\geq k}|M_{T_{z}\wedge n,z}-M_{T_{z}\wedge k,z}|\leq\varepsilon_{1}\}\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(\{T_{z}=\infty\}\cap\{\sup_{n\geq k}|M_{n,z}-M_{k,z}|\leq\varepsilon_{1}\}\right)

\displaystyle<P\left(\sup_{n\geq k}|M_{n,z}-M_{k,z}|\leq\varepsilon_{1}\right)

\displaystyle<\varepsilon,

so
2\delta<\varepsilon+\varepsilon
, a contradiction. Thus
P(T_{z}<\infty)=1
.

Let
P_{n,z}=P(M_{T_{z}\wedge n}>0)
and
Q_{n,z}=P(M_{T_{z}\wedge n}<-z)
.

Since
P(T_{z}<\infty)=1
,
P_{n,z}+Q_{n,z}\rightarrow 1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover,
P_{n,z}
is
non-decreasing in
n
with
P_{\infty,z}=P(M_{T_{z}}>0)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P_{n,z}
.

By condition (i),

BP_{n,z}-zQ_{n,z}>EM_{T_{z}\wedge n}=0.

Sending
n\rightarrow\infty
,

BP_{\infty,z}-z(1-P_{\infty,z})\geq 0.

Hence
P_{\infty,z}\geq\frac{z}{B+z}
. Since
P(T<\infty)\geq P_{n,z}
for all
n\geq 1
,
z>0
,
P(T<\infty)=1
.

5
Two counter-examples of Theorem 4.1

Conditions (i) and (ii) are quite critical. If either condition (or both) fails, then Theorem 4.1 may not hold.

Counter-example 1.
Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
be independent mean zero random variables such that

P(X_{j}=0)=1-j^{-2}
and
P(|X_{j}|=1)=j^{-2}
. Then let

T=\begin{cases}1^{\mbox{st}}\ n\geq 1,\ X_{1}+X_{2}+\dots+X_{n}>0\\
=\infty\ {\mbox{if no such $n$ exists}}.\end{cases}

Then (i) fails and (ii) holds while
P(T=\infty)>0
.

This counterexample illustrates a phenomenon also noted by
Grimmett and Stirzaker 2001

in their discussion of recurrence properties of random walks. The
distinction between recurrence and transience can depend on subtle properties
of the increment distributions, particularly their tail behavior.

Counter-example 2.
Again let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be independent mean zero random variables, but this time such that

P(X_{j}=(2^{j}\!-\!1)/j)=2^{-j}
and
P(X_{j}=-1/j)=1-2^{-j}
. Then define
T
as in Counter-example 1.
Clearly, (ii) fails, (i) holds, and
P(T=\infty)>0
.

6
Polynomial martingales

Thus far we have applied our various theorems to what may be called linear martingales.
But there are also what can be considered square martingales as well as polynomial ones. Our results also
apply to them if they are martingales. As an example, suppose
S_{n}=X_{1}+X_{2}+\dots+X_{n}
is a square integrable
mean zero martingale. Then let
M_{n}=S_{n}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}^{2}
.
M_{n}
is what we mean by
a polynomial martingale of degree 2. A variation on this is
M_{n}^{*}=S_{n}^{2}\!-\!n
, assuming the conditional
variance of every
X_{j}
is 1.

The results presented in this paper extend classical martingale theory in two
important directions:
characterizing the expected value of stopped martingales
when stopping times can be infinite, and providing sufficient conditions
for ensuring that an extended value stopping time is finite almost surely. Our analysis builds upon and complements
previous work by
Wald 1944
,
Blackwell 1946
,
Klass 1988
,
Klass 1990
,
de la Peña and Lai 1997
,
de la Peña and Zamfirescu 2002
, and
Klass 2009

offering a unified perspective on the behavior of stopped martingales.

The three families of martingales we introduced provide concrete examples
where our theoretical results can be applied, demonstrating the practical relevance of our findings. These examples also illustrate how different distributional
properties of the increments can lead to distinct behaviors in the stopped process.

7
Conclusion

The theorems presented here reveal a precise structure underlying the apparent complexity of stopped martingales.

Theorem 3.1 applies to any mean-zero martingale and any extended-valued stopping time
T
, not just first crossing times. It establishes that the condition
\mathbb{E}|M_{T}|\mathbf{1}(T<\infty)<\infty
is necessary and sufficient for the existence of a finite real number
L
characterizing the stopped martingale’s behavior — necessary because
L=\mathbb{E}M_{T}\mathbf{1}(T<\infty)
would otherwise be undefined in the Lebesgue sense, and sufficient as shown by the proof using the Dominated Convergence Theorem. When this condition holds, a fundamental conservation law emerges: the expected value
L
on
\{T<\infty\}
and the limiting expected value on
\{T>n\}
sum to zero. The quantity
L
that characterizes the instances which stop in finite time is exactly balanced by the shortfall, the amount
-L
accumulated by those instances that do not. This duality persists whether
L
is positive, negative, or zero, and whether
\mathbb{P}(T=\infty)
is positive or zero — extending the classical optional stopping theorem into territory where the traditional assumptions fail.

For the important special case of first crossing times, Theorem 4.1 identifies sufficient conditions under which
T=\inf\{n:M_{n}>0\}
is finite almost surely: sufficient wobbling (condition (i)) combined with bounded overshoots (condition (ii)) guarantees that the martingale eventually crosses zero. The counterexamples demonstrate that both conditions are individually necessary in the sense that dropping either one can cause the conclusion to fail, though finding necessary and sufficient conditions for almost sure finiteness of first crossing times remains an open question.
