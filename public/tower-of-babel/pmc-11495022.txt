Study on the balance and gait characteristics of subjects with generalized joint hypermobility residing in high-altitude using wearable devices: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mingwei Liu, Luqi Guo, Jinpeng Lin, Yuepeng Cai, Xiaofan Huang, Yue Wu, Yu Zhang, Shaobai Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the characteristics of balance and gait functions in Generalized Joint Hypermobility (GJH) subjects residing in high-altitude areas.MethodsThis study included 61 university students (28 with GJH and 33 healthy controls) all from the high-altitude region of Linzhi, Tibet Autonomous Region. The Riablo™ wearable intelligent rehabilitation assessment and training system was used to assess static balance (with eyes open and closed) and gait function (during flat walking) in both groups.ResultsCompared to healthy subjects, GJH subjects exhibited significantly impaired balance, indicated by an increased distance of the center of pressure position from the ideal center of gravity(EO: P = 0.007, EC: P = 0.031) and greater amplitude of center of pressure displacements (EO: P = 0.043, EC: P = 0.032). Gait velocity(P = 0.007), stride length(P = 0.012), and swing stance phase of the gait cycle(P = 0.046) were significantly reduced in GJH subjects compared to healthy subjects. A significant increase in the flat-foot phase of the gait cycle(P = 0.022) was observed in GJH subjects compared to healthy subjects.ConclusionThe current study demonstrated that GJH subjects residing in high-altitude areas exhibit impairments in balance and gait, providing a basis for training and prevention strategies tailored for this population. And this study used the wearable intelligent rehabilitation evaluation and training system in high-altitude areas, providing methodological references for scientific research on balance and gait function under non laboratory conditions.Trial registrationControlled Trials No.102772023RT133, Registered 13 October 2023.Keywords: Joint instability, Postural balance, Gait, Altitude sickness

Introduction

Generalized Joint Hypermobility (GJH) is a characteristic of inherited connective tissue disorders, resulting in an excessive range of motion in multiple joints throughout the body [1]. The prevalence of GJH is strongly associated with age, race, and gender [2]. Hypermobility decreases with age and is more common in women than in men, particularly among individuals of Asian and African descent [3, 4]. Joint hypermobility is characterized by an unusually large range of motion in the joints. It is especially common among younger individuals and is generally considered non-pathological [5, 6]. The primary method for diagnosing GJH is currently the Beighton score [3, 7], which assesses hypermobility in the elbows, knees, first and fifth fingers, and forward bending with straight knees. Confirming GJH requires identifying hypermobility in at least four out of the nine movements (score ≥ 4) [7]. Research has shown that GJH subjects exhibit clinical symptoms marked by widespread severe joint hypermobility, instability, and recurrent joint dislocations [8, 9]. Furthermore, individuals with GJH experience impairment in gait and balance functions [10, 11].

The central nervous, proprioceptive, visual, and musculoskeletal systems primarily maintain the crucial functions of balance and gait necessary for daily activities [12–16]. Balance and gait functions, as indicators of overall health [17], are influenced by factors such as joint stability [18], muscle strength [19], and proprioception [20]. Studies have shown that individuals with GJH exhibit heightened anterior tibial translation of the knee, compromised activation of the quadriceps muscles, and diminished proprioception throughout the walking cycle [21–23]. This results in joint instability in the lower limbs and impairs balance and gait function, leading to decreased balance during activities such as walking or climbing stairs.

Previous studies [24–27] have observed differences in balance and gait functions between GJH subjects and healthy populations at lower altitudes. Falkerslev et al. [24] reported decreased trunk stability and inferior balance in both children and adults with GJH compared to healthy individuals. Nikolajsen et al. [25] found a 3.6°increased knee flexion angle during gait in GJH subjects compared to the healthy group. Bates et al. [26] observed that GJH subjects had a 0.05 m decreased stride length compared to the healthy group. Additionally, the GJH group showed lower moments and generated less force at the ankle than the healthy group. Zhong et al. [27] found that participants with GJH exhibited greater anterior translation of the tibia during nearly the entire gait period compared to the healthy group.

GJH subjects residing in high-altitude areas may encounter increased challenges. High altitudes, characterized by low air pressure and reduced oxygen levels, affect the normal functioning of the central nervous and respiratory systems [28]. In hypoxic environments, chemoreceptors in the carotid body detect decreased PaO2, resulting in dizziness, vertigo, and related symptoms [29]. Living at high-altitude areas may induce metabolic abnormalities in humans, leading to muscle atrophy and lower limb strength loss [30]. Consequently, these changes may affect balance and gait. Gait analysis is an important method of assessing an individual’s walking pattern and is essential for understanding gait function. Investigating and assessing gait function can provide an important basis for developing interventions. By identifying and analysing specific problems in gait, intervention strategies to improve gait mechanics can be designed in a more targeted manner. These interventions can not only enhance an individual’s mobility, but also significantly reduce the risk of falls and improve quality of life [31].

The main objective of this study was to observe potential impairments in balance and gait functions using wearable devices among GJH subjects residing at high altitudes. The study hypothesized that GJH subjects residing at high altitudes would exhibit poor static balance and gait function. The secondary aim of this study was to provide guidance and assistance for sports injury prevention tailored to individuals with GJH residing at high altitudes.

Methods

Participants

College students from the Tibet Autonomous Region were enrolled in the study after obtaining informed consent from each participant. The study population comprised individuals living in high-altitude areas (more than 3000 m) who had never lived in the lowland (below 3000 m). The study followed the Helsinki Declaration and obtained approval from the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai University of Sport (Ethics No.102772023RT133). A total of 28 subjects with GJH (male: 15; female:13) and 33 healthy subjects (male: 18; female: 15) were recruited for the study. All subjects had no history of injuries, pain, or sports experience. Table 1 provides the basic information about the subjects. Inclusion criteria for the experimental group included participants aged 18–25 years with a Beighton score of greater than or equal to 4 [20]. The evaluation was conducted by a physician specializing in musculoskeletal rehabilitation. The healthy group consisted of individuals with a Beighton score of less than 4. Exclusion criteria for both the experimental and healthy groups included [1] Marfan syndrome [2], Ehlers-Danlos syndrome [3], severe structural lower extremity trauma, and [4] a history of lower extremity surgery.

Instrumentation

Riablo™ (CoRehab, Trento, Italy) is an adaptive system consisting of five inertial motion unit (IMU) sensors and a static balance plate that are wirelessly connected to a computer. A video interface guides user through prescribed physical activities to enhance the standard rehabilitation program. Weighing 20 g, the device operates based on the wireless Bluetooth communication protocol and functions at a sampling frequency of 50 Hz. Each IMU sensor provides nine degrees of freedom, including a ± 2 g full-scale 3D accelerometer, a ± 2000 dps full-scale 3D gyroscope, and a ± 1000µT full-scale 3D magnetometer. The IMU sensors are factory-calibrated before delivery. The device has been validated and the results are reliable [32–34].

The static balance plate is wirelessly connected to the Riablo™ (CoRehab, Trento, Italy) device. Static balance plates (BPM 120, Physical Support Italia, Italy) with dimensions of 320 cm in length and 75 cm in width were utilized for the analysis of balance parameters. There are several pressure sensors under the balance plate. Data analysis for balance was performed using specialized software (Physical Gait Software Vv.2.66, Physical Support Italia, Italy).

Procedures

Balance Function Test [33]: The entire test was conducted in a quiet room. Before the official test, subjects were instructed to wear standardized socks and uniform shorts. Subjects stood on a stationary balance board to familiarize themselves with the test movements. To assess static stability, participants assumed a standardized, natural stance on the board, positioning their arms at their sides with both heels aligned. A IMU attached to the subject’s sternum via a strap measured SWAY and RMS (SWAY = the distance in centimeters of the center of pressure position from the ideal center of gravity; RMS = the amplitude of center of pressure displacements). Data on static equilibrium were collected under two conditions: eyes opened (EO) and eyes closed (EC). A staff member was present during each test condition to prevent injuries, and three measurements were recorded for each condition. Each measurement lasted 30 s (Fig. 1).

Gait Function Test [34]: Gait performance is evaluated through a walking test. Before the official test, participants were directed to wear standardized socks and place two IMUs in the highest arch position of both feet (Fig. 2). Each participant completed a warm-up program. Initially, participants walked on a flat surface to determine their preferred walking speed. Subsequently, all participants walked in a straight line, covering a distance of 15 m at their most comfortable speed. Considering GJH as a systemic condition with no evidence of preferential gait effects on one side of the body, the mean of the left and right sides was calculated. The gait cycle was divided into the loading response period, flat-foot period, push-off period, and swing period. The acquisition metrics included Speed (m/s), Stride length (m), Loading response (%), flat-foot (%), Push-off (%), and Swing phase (%). Loading response refers to the period from heel-strike to toe-strike. Flat-foot refers to the period from toe-strike to heel-off. Push-off refers to the period from heel-off to toe-off [35]. To ensure the accuracy of the gait analysis, we chose to analyse 10–15 gait cycles. This number provides stable and reliable data. For the 15-metre walk test, we selected only the gait cycles after the subject reached a stable gait pattern, excluding the start (acceleration phase) and stop (deceleration phase). In this way, we ensured that the analyses reflected the subject’s steady gait pattern.

Statistical analysis

SPSS software (version 22, IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) was used for the statistical analysis. Prior to statistical analysis, Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests were conducted to assess normal distribution and variance. Differences in experimental data between groups were compared using the independent sample t-test for data that followed a normal distribution, and the Mann-Whitney U test for data that did not follow a normal distribution. All tests were conducted at a significance level of P < 0.05, indicating statistical significance for observed differences.

Results

The descriptive characteristics of all subjects are provided in Table 1. These groups were homogeneous in age, gender, height, weight, and BMI. As expected, there were significant differences in Beighton scores between the two groups.

The results of the balance test are detailed in Fig. 3. The GJH group exhibited significant balance deficits compared to healthy subjects. In the EO state, the SWAY values of the GJH group were significantly higher than those of the healthy group (8.69 ± 3.86, 6.12 ± 3.27, P = 0.007). Additionally, the RMS values of the GJH group were significantly higher than those of the healthy group in the EO state (1.75 ± 0.91 cm, 1.39 ± 0.42 cm, P = 0.043). In the EC state, the SWAY values of the GJH group were also significantly higher than those of the healthy group (8.92 ± 3.83 cm, 6.96 ± 3.10 cm, P = 0.031). Furthermore, the RMS values of the GJH group were significantly higher than that of the healthy group in the EC state (1.92 ± 1.20 cm, 1.39 ± 0.46 cm, P = 0.032).

The spatio-temporal characteristics of gait are shown in Table 2. The walking speed of the GJH group was significantly lower than the healthy group (P = 0.007). The stride length of the GJH group was also significantly lower than the healthy group (P = 0.012). However, the percentage of the loading response phase of the GJH group and the healthy group was not significantly different (P = 0.155). On the other hand, the percentage of the foot-flat phase was significantly higher in the GJH group than in the healthy group (P = 0.022). The percentage of the push-off phase was not significantly different between the GJH group and the healthy group (P = 0.427). Lastly, the percentage of the swing phase was significantly lower in the GJH group than in the healthy group (P = 0.046).

Discussion

The study utilized an integrated wearable balance and gait analysis device to analyze balance and gait impairments in GJH subjects residing at high altitudes. The results validated objective deficits in balance and gait in subjects with GJH compared to age- and height-matched controls.

In the gait analysis, the GJH group exhibited significantly lower walking speed and stride length compared to the healthy group. Our findings are consistent with a recently published study by Rombout et al., which reported a reduction in velocity of 0.13 m/s and a reduction in stride length of 0.11 m in individuals with joint hypermobility compared to healthy individuals [36]. Alsiri et al. [37] reported a reduction in velocity of 0.19 m/s and a reduction in stride length of 0.16 m in individuals with joint hypermobility compared to healthy individuals. In contrast to most of these studies, we found the GJH group showed a significantly lower percentage in the swing phase. A possible explanation is the large age gap between the subjects. Significant differences exist in the gait of individuals across different age groups [38]. These findings are consistent with the gait changes reported in hypermobility patients by Jeong, which reported a 1.1% increase in the stance phase compared to the healthy group [39]. Prolongation of the support period, reduction in stride speed, and shortening of stride length may be compensatory strategies to maintain stability during walking.

Decreased gait speed, shorter stride length, and longer support period periods can affect overall flexibility and lead to a more effortful walking pattern. These changes may increase the risk of fatigue and reduce walking efficiency [40]. Multiple factors may contribute to gait changes in GJH subjects. Decreased muscle strength in GJH subjects may lead to changes in gait speed and stride length [41]. These adjustments may help maintain balance and reduce the load on the lower limb muscles. Reduced postural stability [42], increased joint laxity [43], and muscle weakness [44] in individuals with GJH may collectively contribute to the observed increase in the stance phase and decrease in the swing phase. This increases the body’s support time against gravity, maintaining stability. Overall, these adjustments may serve as compensatory mechanisms to reduce the load on the lower limb muscles, decrease energy expenditure, and enhance physical stability [45].

In the balance function test, the experimental group exhibited a significant increase in both RMS and SWAY values compared to the healthy group under both EO and EC conditions. This observation suggests that individuals with GJH have poorer balance function compared to healthy individuals. Our findings are consistent with a recently published study by Juul-Kristensen et al. [46], which found that the center of pressure path length increased by 0.27 m/min in GJH subjects compared to healthy individuals. Additionally, Iatridou et al. [47] also demonstrated that GJH group had static balance deficits. This may be due to a lack of proprioceptive ability. Joint proprioception diminishes in GJH subjects [9], affecting sensory input. Individuals with GJH exhibit quadriceps activation disorder [22] and congenital weaknesses in soft tissues, such as laxity in joint capsules and ligaments [48]. These weaknesses can result in joint instability and motor health disorders, further affecting balance function.

The results of this study hold significant clinical implications for understanding and managing subjects with GJH. Clinicians might find it suitable to consider interventions aimed at enhancing balance and gait. Nonetheless, the potential benefits of exercises targeting balance and gait improvement for GJH subjects need exploration in future research.

This study has several limitations. First, the subject selection did not consider gender differences. Additionally, subjects with GJH often exhibit proprioceptive deficits. Dynamic balancing requires heightened joint proprioception, which is particularly relevant to their balance function. Assessments of dynamic balance function were not included in this study. Future research could investigate the effects of gender-specific static and dynamic balances, as well as gait function, in high-altitude areas. Additionally, exploring the effectiveness of lower extremity muscle strength and balance training for the GJH population would be valuable.

Conclusions

This study revealed impairments in balance and gait among GJH subjects residing at high- altitudes. In the balance function, Sway increases (EO: 2.57 cm, EC: 1.96 cm), and RMS increases (EO: 0.36 cm, EC: 0.53 cm). In the gait function, walking speed decreased by 0.14 m/s, stride length by 0.14 m, 1.63% in swing phase, and 3% in flatfoot phase. This provides a theoretical basis for the prevention and exercise interventions for GJH subjects residing at high-altitude areas. Meanwhile, this study utilized a wearable intelligent rehabilitation assessment and training system to evaluate body balance and gait function in high-altitude areas, providing methodological references for scientific research on balance and gait function under non laboratory conditions.
