Large language model derived regular expressions for sleep phenotyping from electronic health record: a feasibility study
Authors: Nathanael Hwang, M Brandon Westover, Diego R Mazzotti, Robert J Thomas, Lynn Marie Trotti, Dennis Hwang, Matt Gratton, Jiaxiao Shi, Rui Yan, Emmanuel Mignot, Gari D Clifford, Umakanth A Katwa, Kendra A Becker, Matthew T Klimper, Jessica K Jara, Haoqi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesLarge language models may efficiently develop Regular Expression (regex)-based natural language processing for electronic health record phenotyping. We present PromptNLP to assess feasibility by evaluating performance and generalizability to clinical notes across multiple institutions.MethodsSleep-related clinical notes from Kaiser Permanente Southern California, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, and Emory University were used to extract apnea-hypopnea index and Epworth Sleepiness Scale. PromptNLP involves two steps: (1) build an apnea-hypopnea index/Epworth Sleepiness Scale phrase library (“Targeted” via manual, “General” via large language models); (2) input the library into an large language model with prompts to generate regex. For model development (Phase 1), we compared PromptNLP regexes with manual regex. For external validation (Phase 2), we adapted the Phase 1 Targeted regex to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and Emory notes. For application (Phase 3), we applied adapted the regex to 207 404 Kaiser Permanente Southern California notes with validation against 8602 structured apnea-hypopnea index.ResultsPhase 1: All Targeted PromptNLP regexes outperformed manual regex for recall (apnea-hypopnea index: 95%–96% vs 80.8%; Epworth Sleepiness Scale: 98%–100% vs 88%; p < .05). General regexes surpassed manual, but with lower recall. Phase 2: Direct Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center application performed poorly (apnea-hypopnea index/Epworth Sleepiness Scale: 30%/12%), but adapting the phrase library improved recall to 96%/84%. For Emory, recall improved from 85%/86% to 94%/89% after adaptation. Across Phases 1 and 2, the PromptNLP regexes achieved 100% precision. Phase 3: 8216 (95.5%) apnea-hypopnea index values were correctly extracted with 99.8% precision.ConclusionsPromptNLP demonstrated feasibility as an efficient, accurate, and generalizable method, outperforming manual approaches and enabling iterative cross-system improvement. Systematic comparisons to direct large language model approaches are needed. Statement of SignificanceTransforming free-text clinical notes into reliable, structured data is essential for large-scale research and innovation in care delivery, yet existing approaches are limited by high manual effort, poor adaptability, or privacy concerns. This study introduces a novel framework, PromptNLP, that combines the strengths of generative artificial intelligence with rule-based extraction to enable accurate and scalable phenotyping from electronic health records. By improving the efficiency and generalizability of extracting key sleep-related measures across health systems, this work supports broader population studies and translational research in sleep medicine. Important next steps include clarifying when different text analysis approaches are most appropriate by comparing natural language processing methods and extending this framework to additional clinical domains.

Introduction

Structured data underpin most epidemiologic and machine learning research. Despite advances in automated data capture into electronic health records (EHRs) through device interoperability, approximately 80% of relevant patient information remains in unstructured formats such as clinical notes [1, 2]. Regular expression (regex) is a commonly applied rule-based natural language processing (NLP) method to convert unstructured information into a structured form based on a flexible and intuitive text processing approach that uses pattern-matching syntax to identify and extract specific text sequences from documents. However, developing regex requires time-consuming manual review and hand-coded pattern recognition, which often yields suboptimal accuracy and is labor-intensive to program [3–5]. Large language models (LLMs) offer powerful capabilities for processing complex language patterns with greater potential for recognizing variations in text patterns [6, 7]. However, an important limitation of LLMs for direct EHR phenotyping is their security concerns over protected health information (PHI) exposure in public domains and the limited access and high costs associated with secure, private LLM environments for large-scale EHR systems [8–10].

To address these challenges, we developed PromptNLP, a hybrid approach that combines the intuitive semantic capabilities of LLMs with the security of regex-based NLP, enabling it to run locally. In this approach, LLMs process samples of unstructured notes containing metrics of interest to generate regex code for large-scale data extraction, reducing manual programming effort while preserving privacy.

Sleep medicine provides an ideal test case to demonstrate proof-of-concept of this novel approach due to the reliance on key discrete metrics amenable to extraction. The apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), derived from polysomnography (PSG) or home sleep apnea testing (HSAT), and the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), obtained from patient questionnaires, are core metrics for diagnosing and assessing the severity of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and evaluating daytime sleepiness [11–13]. These metrics are central to epidemiological research exploring associations between OSA and outcomes such as cardiovascular disease [14–18]. Several clinical trials have not demonstrated a reduction in cardiovascular events with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) but may have been limited by factors such as disease-driver mechanistic heterogeneity, patient selection criteria, and poor therapy adherence [18–21]. To support a planned multi-center target trial emulation (TTE) of the CPAP for Prevention of Cardiovascular Events in Obstructive Sleep Apnea (SAVE) trial, PromptNLP was designed to extract AHI and ESS from EHR notes from multiple institutions [21].

In this study, we evaluated multiple LLMs for extracting AHI and ESS from unstructured EHR notes, compared their performance with a manually developed regex library, and tested generalizability across health systems with varying note formats.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This retrospective, data-only study included three institutions: Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC), Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC), and Emory University. The study was conducted under protocols approved by the BIDMC Institutional Review Board (#2024P000804, #2022P000417), which granted a waiver of informed consent for this retrospective study. Analyses were performed between May and August 2025. KPSC data consisted of diagnostic sleep study interpretations identified by Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes 95810, 95800, 95806, and G0399. Because these interpretations were often generated by sleep study software using templated reports, 15 commonly used templates were compiled into the KPSC training dataset after manual deidentification (removing medical record numbers, names, and dates of birth). BIDMC and Emory data consisted of sleep-related clinic notes retrieved using keyword filters (PSG, HST, HSAT, AHI, Polysomnography, Home Sleep Apnea Test, and Epworth) and deidentified with Philter, an automated tool for removing PHI [22].

Each dataset (KPSC Test Dataset, BIDMC 1 and 2, Emory 1 and 2) included 300 randomly selected notes: 100 notes with AHI values and 50 without plus 100 notes with ESS values and 50 without. Notes without values served as negative controls. This sampling design was applied to both the Development (Phase 1) and the External Validation (Phase 2) study phases described below.

Overall framework of PromptNLP

PromptNLP workflow

PromptNLP is a two-step LLM-assisted process (Figure 1) for generating regexes. Previous attempts at a single-step approach performed poorly (Supplementary Table E5). First, we compiled a “phrase library” capturing variations in AHI and ESS format. Second, we fed the library into an LLM with a structured prompt (Supplementary Table E1A) to generate regexes. The LLMs used were commercial, web-based versions and not private environments using Application Programming Interfaces (APIs). LLM-generated regexes comprised multiple regular expression patterns, each with a capturing group to extract AHI and ESS. Because our planned TTE emulates the SAVE trial, which used the AHI 4 percent hypopnea definition, we extracted “AHI4%” and “AHI” (4 percent definition is standard at KPSC) but excluded AHI 3 percent (a PromptNLP-developed regex to additionally extract "AHI3%" was published on GitHub). The extraction dataset included patient identifier, procedure/note date, CPT code, source narrative segment, and AHI/ESS columns (Figure 2). When a report contained multiple AHI values, these were recorded in separate AHI columns.

PromptNLP process. PromptNLP is a two-step process. Step 1: Identify unique formats for AHI and ESS, then compile the corresponding phrase segments into a phrase library. In the Targeted approach, specific phrases are manually copied and pasted into the library. In the General approach, entire sample reports are provided to the LLM, which is instructed to identify and extract relevant phrases for the library. Step 2: Instruct the LLM to process the phrase library and generate the corresponding regex code (final set of regexes are available in Supplementary Table E4).

Phrase-library strategies

We used both “Targeted” and “General” approaches to create the phrase library. In the Targeted approach, phrases were manually identified and then fed into the LLM to author regex (one manual and one LLM step). The specific segments extracted started with the first letter of the metric of interest and ended with the value of interest. Examples include “AHI: 5,” “Apnea Hypopnea Index is 5,” “ESS = 5,” or “Epworth Sleepiness Scale is 5/24.” In the General approach, the LLM was prompted (Supplementary Table E1B) to identify phrases without manual identification, then prompted to create regexes mirroring the Targeted approach (two LLM steps). PromptNLP encompasses both versions of this two-step process.

Study phases

The study proceeded in three phases. Phase 1 (development) compared the performance of regexes generated by multiple LLMs (ChatGPT-o3, Gemini 2.5-Pro, Claude-Sonnet 4 [all model versions May 2025]) with manual regex using KPSC notes. Phase 2 (external validation) assessed generalizability using BIDMC and Emory notes with variable note formats. Phase 3 (application) applied the Phase 2-adapted regexes to 207 404 KPSC notes to assess extraction yield and validated accuracy in a procedure-date-matched subset of 8602 notes that had existing corresponding structured AHI.

Development and internal validation of PromptNLP (Phase 1—development)

PromptNLP was applied to KPSC sleep study interpretations exported from the EHR (Epic Systems, Verona, WI, USA). As described above, 15 sample reports were deidentified and comprised the Training Dataset (Figure 3). PromptNLP was applied to this dataset and generated Targeted and General regexes. For comparison, a study investigator (M.K.P.G.) with regex expertise manually developed code (with multiple rounds of preliminary testing and improvement) from the same Training Dataset. Overall, seven regex versions were produced: one manual code (R 4.5.1) and six (two per LLM) PromptNLP codes (Python 3.12). Each code extracted both AHI and ESS.

Development (Phase 1) study workflow comparing PromptNLP and manual regular expressions using KPSC clinical notes. In each workflow, target phrases (with unique patterns of AHI and ESS) were identified within the Sample Dataset. In the Manual approach, regex was manually programmed based on the identified patterns. In the PromptNLP approaches, the target phrases were first compiled into a Phrase Library (manually identified in Targeted and LLM identified in General), then input into the LLM to general regex code. Thus, the Targeted and General PromptNLP versions differ in the number of LLM steps (one vs two LLM steps). Each regex was run on a KPSC Test Dataset (300 randomly selected notes—100 with AHI values and 50 without; 100 with ESS values and 50 without) to produce an Extracted Dataset. These workflows were followed for the three LLMs tested (ChatGPT-o3, Gemini-2.5 Pro, Claude-Sonnet 4). Performance of PromptNLP-generated regexes was compared to manually developed regex.

The regex codes were then applied to the Test Dataset (Figure 3), which included 300 KPSC reports. Extracted values were compared against ground truth labels annotated by a sleep physician (D.H.) to determine extraction accuracy. Manual annotation involved reviewing full reports for “AHI” (4 percent definition), confirming that the value was derived from a diagnostic study (not a therapy report), and ensuring it represented the total AHI rather than values specific to body position or sleep stages.

Validating generalizability of PromptNLP (Phase 2—external validation)

Following Phase 1 validation, both Targeted and General approaches were applied to EHR-exported notes (Epic Systems) from two additional health systems (BIDMC and Emory). Their notes comprised non-templated free-text clinic narratives with greater AHI and ESS pattern variability. An iterative process was employed by testing code developed in the prior health system and refining the regexes with data from the subsequent system (Figure 4).

External validation (Phase 2) study workflow for evaluating PromptNLP generalizability. An iterative process applied the top Phase 1 regex (Code 1T) to BIDMC Notes 1, updated it with PromptNLP (Codes 2T, 2G), and tested on new BIDMC Notes 2. The updated regexes were then applied to Emory Notes 1, updated again (Codes 3T, 3G), and tested on new Emory Notes 2. Each set of notes comprised of 300 randomly selected notes (100 with AHI values and 50 without; 100 with ESS values and 50 without). A summary of each code version, the datasets they were trained on, and the datasets they were tested on is summarized in Supplementary Table E6.

KPSC ChatGPT Targeted (Code 1T), the best-performing Phase 1 regex, was applied to BIDMC Notes 1. PromptNLP (Targeted and General) was applied to update the regexes (Code 2T and 2G). Code 2 versions were then applied to a new BIDMC dataset (BIDMC Notes 2), testing performance on notes to which the code was not previously exposed. Code 2 versions were then applied to Emory Notes 1. The PromptNLP process was repeated to update the regex (Code 3), which was again tested on a new dataset (Emory Notes 2). Ground truth labels were annotated by a sleep physician (D.H.). ChatGPT-o3 was the sole LLM used for Phase 2 regex updates.

Large-scale application of PromptNLP-developed regex (Phase 3—application)

Code 3T was applied to 207 404 KPSC diagnostic sleep study reports (CPT 95800, 95806, 95810, G0399) with procedure dates between 2008 and 2018 to evaluate large-scale extraction yield in preparation for the planned TTE. A subset of 8602 reports with corresponding structured AHI obtained separately via automatic data capture (matched by procedure date) enabled a direct comparison. In addition, 500 randomly selected ESS extractions and 100 notes without ESS values were manually validated by chart review.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize correct versus incorrect extractions. Performance was evaluated using accuracy (proportion correctly classified), precision or positive predictive value (proportion of extracted values that were correct), recall or sensitivity (proportion of true values correctly extracted), specificity (proportion of reports without values correctly identified), and the F1 score (harmonic mean of precision and recall). A true positive required both extraction and exact agreement with the ground truth value; incorrect values were counted as false positives. True negatives were reports without values from which no value was extracted. Because some notes (particularly BIDMC) contained multiple AHI values, an extraction was deemed correct only if all instances in the note were accurately captured.

In the Development phase, the performance of each PromptNLP regex was compared with manually coded regex. Because all regex versions rarely produced false positives (and never with PromptNLP regexes), recall was the most informative metric. Differences in performance were tested using Chi-squared and two-proportion z-tests, with two-sided p < .05 considered significant.

In the External Validation phase, the same metrics were used to compare performance before and after the PromptNLP update process to assess iterative improvement. In the Application phase, the same metrics were used to compare extractions from the subset of 8602 reports versus structured AHI values (procedure date-matched); AHI and ESS extraction rates were reported descriptively for the full cohort of 207 404 reports. All analyses used Python 3.12.

Results

Development and external validation (Phases 1 and 2) cohort characteristics

The distributions of ground truth values for Phase 1 and 2 datasets were similar across sites and within expected ranges (e.g. no ESS >24). Mean ± SD (range) values were: KPSC Test Dataset AHI 20.6 ± 19.0 (0.2–81.3) and ESS 8.3 ± 5.3 (1–24); BIDMC Notes 1 AHI 24.9 ± 29.8 (0.1–155.4) and ESS 10.4 ± 6.3 (0–24); BIDMC Notes 2 AHI 27.8 ± 33.5 (0.2–152.4) and ESS 9.3 ± 5.8 (0–24); Emory Notes 1 AHI 17.7 ± 20.5 (0.7–93.1) and ESS 9.4 ± 5.0 (1–24); and Emory Notes 2 AHI 21.1 ± 19.7 (0-85.7) and ESS 9.0 ± 5.8 (0–24). The KPSC Test Dataset notes spanned 2008–2018 and included reports from 31 physicians, while BIDMC and Emory notes spanned 2022–2024. Distribution of ground truth AHI4%/AHI was 35/65 (KPSC), 73/94 (BIDMC 1), 121/58 (BIDMC 2), 0/126 (Emory 1), and 0/149 (Emory 2).

PromptNLP regexes demonstrated significantly higher recall than manual regex; for AHI, recall ranges were 95%–96% with the Targeted approach and 84%–94% with the General approach for the three LLMs used, compared to 80.8% for manual regex. For ESS, recall ranges were 98%–100% with Targeted and 83%–96% with General, compared to 88% for manual regex. Improvements in recall were statistically significant for Targeted regexes versus manual regex (p < .05); among General regexes, only the ChatGPT version significantly outperformed the manual regex.

Comparing performance across different LLMs (Phase 1)

When comparing performance across the three LLMs, the Targeted versions consistently outperformed their corresponding General versions. The ability of the General approach to compile the phrase library was likely restricted by the limited context window of each LLM (ChatGPT-o3: 150 K words [high reasoning], Gemini-2.5 Pro: 750 K words [high reasoning], Claude-Sonnet 4: 150 K words [hybrid reasoning]), which may have caused the LLM to miss some relevant phrases.

Among the Targeted versions, recall was uniformly high (95%–96% for AHI; 98%–100% for ESS); ChatGPT achieved the highest values, though differences versus Gemini and Claude were not statistically significant (p = .93). Among the General versions, recall was slightly lower overall (AHI: 94% ChatGPT, 91% Gemini, 84% Claude; ESS: 96% ChatGPT, 92% Gemini, 83% Claude), with ChatGPT significantly outperforming Claude for both AHI and ESS (p < .05).

Applying and adapting regex on BIDMC notes (Phase 2)

The best-performing Phase 1 regex, ChatGPT Targeted (Code 1T), was applied to BIDMC Notes 1. All regexes in Phase 2 achieved 100% precision; therefore, comparisons again focused on recall (Figure 5). Code 1T recall was poor (AHI/ESS: 30%/12%), likely due to differences in note structure—KPSC comprised largely of templated sleep study interpretations with smaller degrees of free-text narratives, while BIDMC consisted of non-templated clinic notes with greater variability in AHI and ESS patterns.

Despite the low initial performance, PromptNLP Targeted and General approaches generated updated code that substantially improved recall (AHI/ESS: Code 2T 99%/90%; Code 2G 97%/75%). Applying and adapting regex on Emory notes (Phase 2)

When Code 2T and 2G (trained on KPSC and BIDMC) were applied to Emory Notes 1, performance was markedly better than the prior application of Code 1T (trained solely on KPSC) to BIDMC Notes 1, reflecting the iterative improvement in regexes (Figure 5. Recall [AHI/ESS]: Code 2T 85%/86%; Code 2G 74%/86%). Similar to BIDMC, Emory notes were non-templated free-text narratives. PromptNLP then generated updated regexes (Code 3T and 3G), which further improved recall (Code 3T 98%/92%; Code 3G 99%/91%). Validation on an unseen dataset (Emory Notes 2) confirmed sustained performance (Code 3T 94%/89%; Code 3G 90%/86%). The adapted Emory phrase library and regex are shown in Supplementary Table E4.

Large-scale application of regex on KPSC sleep study reports (Phase 3)

Code 3T extracted AHI and ESS values from 207 404 reports. Among 8602 notes from sleep studies with corresponding structured AHI values from automated data capture [mean ± SD 31.4 ± 25.3 (range 0–140.4)], values were extracted from 8231 (95.7%) notes. The remaining 371 notes (4.3%) did not contain extractable AHI values within the narrative (i.e. no AHI text present) and therefore do not reflect regex extraction failure.

Among extracted values, 8216 of 8231 (99.8%) exactly matched the structured data, yielding a precision of 99.8%. The 15 incorrect extractions were due to CPAP trial data mis-entered into a diagnostic sleep study CPT label, and regex incorrectly extracted residual AHI denoted as “residual apnea hypopnea index” and “average AHI” (regex was programmed to ignore “residual AHI” but not “residual apnea hypopnea index” [these issues are corrected in the version published on GitHub]). Supplementary Table E7 provides a tabular representation of these results.

Of the 8602 notes, ESS values were extracted from 6808 (79.1%). Structured ESS data were unavailable for comparison; however, manual chart review of 500 extracted values and 100 notes without extractable values confirmed 100% positive and negative predictive value (although three correct extractions had to be removed due to out-of-range values [e.g. “ESS is 624” instead of “6/24”]). We caution that the performance was based on a limited, manually reviewed sample. Correctly extracted and retained mean ESS values were 9.9 ± 5.7 (range 0-24).

Of the 207 404 reports (176 619 patients), the extraction rates for AHI/ESS were 75.6%/52.0%; 105 823 reports (101 508 patients) had both AHI and ESS values. If applying the SAVE inclusion criteria of moderate–severe OSA (i.e. AHI ≥15) and ESS <16 for the proposed TTE, 41 359 potential participants could be considered for inclusion.

Discussion

We presented PromptNLP, a hybrid approach using LLMs to generate regular expressions for EHR phenotyping, using AHI and ESS extraction in sleep medicine to demonstrate proof-of-concept.

In the Development phase, we demonstrated that PromptNLP (both Targeted and General) regexes performed significantly better than manually developed regex, demonstrating higher recall and perfect precision. The KPSC manual code was programmed to recognize only a few AHI and ESS patterns compared to 36 specific patterns for PromptNLP versions (and over 70 after iterative updates during the External Validation phase, albeit with some redundancy), highlighting the intuitive pattern recognition capabilities of LLMs. Additionally, unlike manual regex, PromptNLP versions included programming to ignore specific AHI variations (e.g. Supine AHI, residual AHI, etc.), and the ease in adding instructions to ignore certain formats is an essential capability. We further emphasize that a key advantage of PromptNLP over manual regex is the lower effort required to program [4, 5]. While a formal time-motion analysis was not assessed in this study, developing the regexes (reviewing sample reports, identifying phrases of interest, and creating regex code manually or via LLM) for KPSC was estimated to take 25 h for manual and less than 20 min using PromptNLP (for both Targeted and General).

We also demonstrated the utility of using PromptNLP to update regex. In the External Validation phase, we validated the process across multiple institutions with variable note formats. Only a few minutes were required using PromptNLP to update the codes when applied to each new institution, resulting in improved performance. Thus, while there was an initial drop in the regex performance from using the KSPC-developed regex applied to BIDMC notes, the subsequent improved and sustained performance validated the generalizability of the PromptNLP process. The sequential, iterative improvement in performance occurred with the efficient accumulation of additional patterns to recognize, which we believe is much less feasible with the manual approach.

We developed PromptNLP to support a multi-center TTE based on SAVE, which requires AHI and ESS values to satisfy inclusion criteria [21]. In the Application phase, we validated the performance of the improved adapted regex (Code 3T) by applying it to a large set of KPSC notes (207 404) and demonstrated near perfect extraction performance of AHI in the subset of notes (8602) that had concurrent values available for comparison acquired by automated data capture. Further, we identified 41 359 potential patients who met the trial’s AHI and ESS inclusion criteria, supporting the applicability of PromptNLP in a real-world use case. We intend to leverage the broad availability of these metrics to support other large-scale epidemiologic and machine learning research [12, 16, 23, 24]. ESS is of particular value since the exclusion of sleepy patients has been proposed to contribute to negative clinical trial results [17–20, 25], but ESS has historically been unavailable as structured data for inclusion as a covariate. To support sleep researchers globally, we published the latest version of the code on an open-source platform (GitHub) along with the prompts to guide the use of PromptNLP for further adaptation to their health system if necessary. Finally, while PromptNLP was developed to extract sleep metrics (additional metrics planned include oxygen desaturation index and other oximetry metrics), we expect that this process should be generalizable to use cases in non-sleep domains [26]. For example, we are exploring its utility in extracting features to calculate social determinants of health.

When applying PromptNLP, LLM model selection mattered primarily in the General approach, in which ChatGPT performed the best. High reasoning models (ChatGPT-o3, Gemini-2.5 Pro) typically outperformed Claude-Sonnet 4 (hybrid reasoning); we anticipate that most state-of-the-art reasoning LLMs are viable options. Additionally, post-extraction evaluation is important—for example, AHI (without the denoted hypopnea definition) will require knowledge of that health system’s standard; or when multiple AHI are extracted from the same report, chart reviews may be required (in our analysis, the highest value was almost always the correct diagnostic AHI). When comparing the Targeted and General approaches, the Targeted approach achieved better performance, whereas the General approach was more efficient. Given that the Targeted approach still only entails minimal time to develop regular expressions, we anticipate that it will typically be the preferred choice. One additional observation concerns the non-deterministic nature of LLMs. When 1T and 1G were developed using identical source notes and prompts, the resulting regexes differed slightly, though performance remained stable (e.g. 1T recall of 94%–95% and 1G recall of 90%–95% across three identical runs); corrections can be addressed with subsequent PromptNLP passes.

Limitations of PromptNLP are primarily related to the limitations of regex. As a pattern-matching method, it can miss phrasings that deviate slightly from the learned patterns (e.g. not recognizing “residaul” due to misspelling) [27]. Regarding the PromptNLP General approach, LLMs may hallucinate when identifying phrases for inclusion in the phrase library. We observed periodic misidentifications when processing large batches of notes, likely due to context-window limits. Mitigations include using higher-reasoning models and splitting phrase discovery across multiple LLM conversations (i.e. start a new conversation for each additional batch of 15 sample notes). Additionally, LLMs may suffer from the “lost in the middle” phenomenon, in which notes positioned in the middle receive disproportionately less attention, potentially increasing the risk that relevant phrases are overlooked. Another potential limitation is when concept extraction requires inference (e.g. nuanced history narratives, family history exclusion, or negation), in which reasoning-capable NLP methods, including direct LLM-based extraction, may be preferable [28]. Finally, all three study sites are located in the United States, utilize Epic Systems, and are based on English-language notes, which could limit generalizability in select use cases.

The primary aim of this study was to demonstrate proof-of-concept for merging LLMs with regex programming, rather than to compare performance with other NLP methods. Therefore, we cannot make claims regarding the superiority of one approach over the other. However, comparing the conceptual advantages of the different approaches can help identify appropriate use cases. Methods that use LLMs as the final extraction step, such as direct LLM extractions or other hybrid approaches (e.g. SPELL, which takes the opposite design approach, using regex to locate relevant snippets that an LLM then interprets), hold great potential, particularly when inference is required [29]. The case for PromptNLP is strongest for numerically explicit metrics, while tasks requiring inference would likely favor the opposite design. Methods that utilize regex as the final extraction step, including PromptNLP, offer distinct advantages. First, the code can be securely executed in a private, local environment, whereas direct LLM-based extractions entail substantial concerns regarding inadvertently exposing PHI unless performed in secure, API-enabled environments, which can be costly [9, 10]. Local open-source models (e.g. small LlaMA variants) may mitigate privacy risks but offer less reasoning power, greater hardware demands, and less scalability [26, 30]. Second, hallucinations have been reported with LLM-based extractions, which can be problematic in clinical settings because we prefer missing values to incorrect ones [8, 26, 31]. Although this limitation can be mitigated through retrieval-augmented generation combined with post-extraction heuristics or hybrid approaches that simplify text prior to LLM extraction (e.g. spaCy) [32], for metrics typically documented in standardized labels, regex extraction may suffice and could be conceptually preferred in select use cases [26, 31, 33]. Taken together, although PromptNLP utilizes LLMs, security concerns may be reduced, albeit not eliminated, because regex serves as the final extraction step; the LLM is applied only to representative samples (thereby limiting the volume of notes containing potential PHI, particularly under the Targeted approach), and its role is restricted to authoring regular expressions [26, 34]. The General approach may be slightly more vulnerable than the Targeted approach because full reports are being inserted. Hallucinations could potentially occur with the LLM step of PromptNLP, although this risk may be mitigated with regex as the final step. By enabling efficient additions of large numbers of phrase variations, PromptNLP overcomes regex’s traditional limitations to promote accuracy and adaptability.

In conclusion, we presented PromptNLP for EHR phenotyping in sleep medicine that resulted in significantly better accuracy while requiring only a fraction of the effort to program compared to manually developed regex. We demonstrated its generalizability across multiple health systems with varying note formats and substantiated its utility for iterative improvement when regex updates were sequentially applied. PromptNLP may serve as a valuable tool for enhancing multi-center EHR phenotyping to support large-scale research and individual-level longitudinal tracking.
