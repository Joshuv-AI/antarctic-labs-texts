Combinatorial Exploration of Multidrug Polyspecificity in Efflux Pumps
Fabio Deelan Cunden, Juan Carlos Jimenez-Castellanos

Abstract
. A major feature of efflux pumps is multidrug polyspecificity, which to date still eludes some of the traditional dogmas of drug binding within protein science. We propose a combinatorial approach to explore, in the vast sequence space of polypeptide chains, the neighbourhood of some superfamilies of efflux pumps. Creating new functional structures as permutations of existing ones has the potential to unveil some of the hidden mechanisms behind the functionality of efflux pumps.



Phenomenology

Efflux pumps (EPs), particularly those belonging to the Resistance-Nodulation-Division (RND) superfamily (e.g., AcrB-TolC), play crucial roles in multidrug resistance (MDR) in clinically relevant Gram-negative bacteria including
Escherichia coli
,
Klebsiella pneumoniae
, and
Acinetobacter baumannii
. A key feature of these pumps is their ability to expel a wide range of chemically unrelated substrates (also known as
polyspecificity
), including antibiotics
[
1
]
. The binding and transport mechanisms of these substrates have been primarily studied in efflux pumps from
E. coli
and
K. pneumoniae
(i.e., AcrB), where both the sequences and structural similarities are high (
>90\%
). Significant questions, however, arise when considering how other pumps in Gram-negative bacteria (e.g., AdeB, AdeH, AdeJ, and MexB) bind and transport a wide range of similar substrates despite their low sequence similarity
[
2
,
3
]
.

Our current understanding of the binding and transport of a wide range efflux pump substrates has primarily been based on traditional methods and using
E. coli
’s AcrB pump as the role model. Substrate polyspecificity has typically been assessed one drug molecule at a time or by individually generating or selecting specific mutants
[
4
]
. This approach has made it challenging to develop new inhibitors targeting key efflux pumps and to rescue or modify existing antibiotics that are susceptible to efflux pumping.

An innovative and systematic approach to generate combinatorial permutations among key RND efflux pumps (hereafter EP-hybrids) from clinically relevant bacteria would be relevant to advance the field of antimicrobial resistance by understanding the overlapping transport mechanisms of different substrates (i.e., polyspecificity) among RND pumps; thus far being essential for creating new efflux pump inhibitors and improving the efficacy of existing antibiotics to combat MDR bacteria.

Protein conformation and protein functionality

Proteins are polymers made of
20
basic amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Natural proteins varies in length from a few dozens to several thousands amino acids. Different side chains of the amino acids have specific mutual interaction which force the linear chain to fold in solution into a particular three-dimensional conformation.

In short, the folding of a polypeptide chain in a given environment is a solution as a large system of (possibly stochastic) dynamical equations, in which the final
conformation
of the protein is the lowest free energy state: this is the ‘uniqueness’ property in Anfinsen’s thermodynamic principle
[
5
]
. A direct solution to the folding problem is computationally unfeasible, and one usually resorts to comparing a given amino acid sequence to similar ones whose structure is already known by using matching and/or perturbation techniques. Nonetheless, we recently witnessed extraordinary advances based on neural network-based algorithms
[
6
,
7
]
.

However, finding the full conformation of a polypeptide chain is only the first step towards our ultimate goal in protein design: identifying the
active sites
within particular geometries and energy profiles responsible for the
functionality
of the protein (i.e., transport or binding activities). We briefly outline a combinatorial approach that explore new chemical spaces in the neighbourhood of native proteins based on several (per)mutations or amino acid combinations. This novel approach should be compared with traditional single and static techniques such as site-directed mutagenesis.

Combinatorial exploration

We denote by
\mathcal{A}
the space of sequences over an alphabet of
20
letters (the amino acids). A polypeptide chain can be thought of as a sequence
w
in
\mathcal{A}
(there are some well-known constraints, e.g., at the beginning and at the end of those sequences, but we will ignore them in the following). For example, there are
\sim 20^{1000}
polypeptide chains of length
n=1000
(a typical length for EPs). But the overwhelming majority of them in such a vast sequence space
corresponds to biologically meaningless polypeptide chains: the subset
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}
of sequences corresponding to native proteins found in living organisms is tiny compared to
\mathcal{A}
.
To every sequence
w\in\mathcal{A}
it is associated a unique
configuration
. In other words, the assumption is that there is a universal function

w\mapsto\operatorname{Conf}(w)
,
associating every polypeptide chain its configuration. (In this simplified description we are neglecting the
environmental parameter space
, since in biology the relevant ranges of values of temperature, pH, etc., are
rather small.)

There are biologically significant substrings within each sequence such as active sites. We think of such features as members of an abstract set of
labels

\mathbb{L}
. They can be positional labels,
functional labels
, etc.
We are interested in families
L\subset\mathcal{A}
of polypeptide chains corresponding to labels
\mathbbm{L}
associated to sequences. Such a set
L
, consisting of the strings that fulfil the function described by
\mathbbm{L}
, is called the
syntactic image
of
\mathbbm{L}
(we translated this neat description from

[
8
]
). Typical examples are sets of polypeptide chains that properly fold under specified conditions, and/or have specific functional domains. As a specific example, the RND superfamily can be thought of as the family of native sequences in
\mathcal{B}
that contain (in the sense of substring inclusion) the syntactic image
L
of the label
\mathbb{L}=\text{`ability to expel the chemical substrate $X$'}
.

We propose to explore the space of amino acids sequences through a combinatorial approach articulated along the following steps:

(1)

Select a family of native proteins sharing the same functional labels
\mathbb{L}_{1},\mathbb{L}_{2},\ldots
. The typical example we have in mind is the RND superfamily (AcrB, AdeB, MexB, etc.);

(2)

Construct a new (non-native) family of polypeptide chains by applying the
same
permutation to the sequences of the family; a permutation is a ‘scrambling’ of the letters forming a sequence;

(3)

Find the sequence and structure similarity of the transformed family by using traditional and new methods of protein conformation field;

(4)

Study (necessarily in laboratory/wet experiments) whether the newly created family have the same and/or new biologically functional labels.

The critical step of such exploration is choosing the permutations in step (2). We start from the following phenomenological observation: although members of a protein family sharing the same functional labels
\mathbb{L}_{1},\mathbb{L}_{2},\ldots
have, in general, high sequence and structure similarity, the syntactic images
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots
(the substrings that fulfil the functionalities) actually differ among members of the family! Hence, we propose to apply permutations that preserve the syntactic images
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots
in each protein, therefore transforming a family of proteins by scrambling everything but the active sites.
Applying several permutations, a pool of new sequences (e.g., EP-hybrids) will be created, which can be studied to determine their
in silico/vitro
protein functionality (e.g., to determine if the syntactic image
L
of the label
\mathbb{L}
can retain the ability to expel the same chemically unrelated substrates of the original sequence).

We conjecture that most permutations will create new families sharing the same functionalities
\mathbb{L}^{\prime}_{1},\mathbb{L}^{\prime}_{2},\ldots
, most likely the same
\mathbb{{L}}_{1},\mathbb{{L}}_{2},\ldots
of the original family. Hence, considerable modication of protein sequence may be made
without loss of function: the functionality is robust against generic perturbations
of the ‘inactive sites’. The conjecture is supported by preliminary analysis of the sequences and structures similarity of permutations in the RND-superfamily.

On the other hand, it is reasonable to expect that some special permutations will split the family i.e., some polypeptide chains will have different functionality than the others (or will be not functional at all in some cases).
Those singular
permutations would signal hidden mechanisms that participate in the functionality of
proteins
without
transforming or mutating the corresponding active sites.

Applications

Protein combinatorics suggests new avenues for drug discovery. By integrating it with methods such as fluorescent assays, phenotypic profiling, Cryo-EM, and medicinal chemistry, it would be feasible to explore previously uncharted chemical spaces within efflux pumps, aiding to advance our structure-activity relationship
knowledge about EPs polyspecificity with the ultimate goal of designing new inhibitors and antibiotics that can evade efflux pumping.
