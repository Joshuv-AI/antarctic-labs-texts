Experimental dataset of sub-critical cylinder wake velocity fields
Authors: Peter I. Renn, Emily H. Palmer, Morteza Gharib
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep learning is of growing interest to the fluids community due its potential applications for real-time prediction and control. Indeed, whereas computational fluid dynamics solvers are prohibitively time-intensive for real-time implementation, deep learning methods can be evaluated in milliseconds once trained. However, the majority of efforts to apply deep learning methods to flows have been performed with computational data rather than experimental data, in part due to the lack of freely available large experimental datasets. In this paper we present a 64.7 GB dataset of two-dimensional velocity fields measured with particle image velocimetry. These fields show the temporal evolution of cylinder wakes in the sub-critical regime for Reynolds numbers ranging from 240 to 2520. The data is broken into discrete time series made up of 100 time-steps each, representing approximately three vortex shedding cycles. The dataset, available for immediate and free use, is poised to facilitate research on deep learning methods in fluids and other endeavors where such a large, detailed experimental dataset is of utility.

Background & Summary/Introduction

Among the most recognizable canonical flows, vortex shedding is famous for its mesmerizing beauty and diverse applications. Indeed, the characteristic pattern of alternating vortices convecting downstream, known as the Kármán vortex street, is observed behind skyscrapers, chimneys, islands, and other bluff bodies submerged in flows. Given the extensiveness of this phenomena, vortex shedding has been studied in detail for decades, giving rise to many comprehensive studies of the features of this phenomenon across Reynolds numbers (Re)1. Briefly, around Re = 50, an instability occurs in the previously steady recirculation region of the wake of a cylinder, which in turn produces a laminar pattern of vortices. The transition to turbulence begins around Re = 190, at which point small-scale three-dimensional instabilities begin to form2. Here, we focus on vortex shedding in the “irregular range” or “sub-critical regime” (Re between 300 and 200,000), which is characterized by increasingly prevalent three-dimensional effects, irregular velocity fluctuations, and the transition of the outer shear layer3 (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Streamwise (top) and spanwise (bottom) velocity fields in a cylinder wake for Re between 240 and 2520. Numerical solvers used for computational fluid dynamics (CFD) are capable of modeling vortical wakes in this regime4. While an excellent tool for many engineering problems, these methods are computationally expensive and prohibitively time intensive for real-time applications. As a potential alternative to CFD, data-driven models have received increased attention for various tasks in fluid mechanics, including for predicting, enhancing, and reconstructing fluid flows through machine learning-based models5–7. Once trained, these methods benefit from fast model evaluation at low cost, potentially enabling practical real-time, real-world applications. However, data-driven methods require copious amounts of data for training, with most data-driven efforts in fluid mechanics, to date, utilizing numerically-simulated dataset. The reliance on simulated data limits the practicality of these approaches for real-world application, as it removes significant challenges related to observability such as limited and imperfect measurements.

To facilitate the inclusion of experimental data in developing methods for predicting fluid flows and for other efforts where such data would be of use, we present a 64.7 GB dataset of velocity fields in the wake of a circular cylinder, recorded at various Reynolds numbers in the sub-critical regime8. We employ digital particle image velocimetry (PIV), a method of quantitative flow visualization which is used here to determine the time-evolving instantaneous flow fields9. The data are organized into 100 time-step sequences, with frame rates chosen such that the nondimensional interframe time is approximately constant regardless of Reynolds number. This dataset is available for immediate use at the CaltechDATA repository8.

Methods

A small free-surface water tunnel was used in experiments to collect the dataset provided. This tunnel has test section dimensions of 0.15 m (W) × 0.15 m (H) with length 0.61 m. An acrylic cylinder was mounted to span the tunnel walls approximately halfway between the free surface and the tunnel floor (Fig. 2). The diameter of the cylinder used was fixed to D = 9.53 × 10−3 m (cylinder aspect ratio L/D = 15.7), and the inlet velocity was varied between U = 0.03 m s−1 to U = 0.26 m s−1 to achieve different Reynolds numbers for the dataset. No free-surface perturbations were observed at the range of Reynolds numbers tested. Flow speeds were determined by taking the mean streamwise flow velocity in a small window in the free-stream. Data Records

The data is available online on the CaltechDATA repository8. Experiments were conducted at six Reynolds numbers (Table 1). Velocity field data from each Reynolds number is stored in a separate Hierarchical Data Format file (h5). Within each file, there are two fields, ‘u_x’ and ‘u_y’, corresponding to the streamwise and spanwise components of the velocity field, respectively. The data have been parsed into n sequences, each 100 timesteps in length, and subsequently organized into an n × 100 × 89 × 133 array, where each i × 100 × 89 × 133 element represents one 100 step time sequence of 89 × 133 pixel (spanwise × streamwise) images. Information on the number of sequences and total amount of data for each Reynolds number is provided in Table 2.

Technical Validation

Identification of recording artefacts

The data were parsed into 100 timestep sequences to standardize the dataset and facilitate the exclusion of recording artefacts. During data collection, individual frames were occasionally corrupted or lost (<0.025% of frames); sequences are chosen so as to not include these erroneous measurements. As a result, while many consecutive sequences were recorded consecutively, there are also cases where consecutive sequences are separated by segments including dropped frames. Thus, the data cannot be restructured as a single, continuous recording of 100 × n frames. Similarly, while it is often true that the ith sequence follows continuously from the i-1th sequence, this cannot be assumed. Three-dimensional effects in wake

While measured data shows only in-plane velocities, vortex shedding is known to be three-dimensional in the subcritical regime1. Indeed, visual inspection of the recirculation region indicates three-dimensional effects (Fig. 4A). If the flow were truly two-dimensional, there should be no points in the wake from which pathlines diverge or to which pathlines converge. Instead, we observe an area with a high density of diverging pathlines in the recirculation region, indicating particles moving into the imaging plane. Indeed, we find that the mean absolute divergence field shows increases in the shear layer and the recirculation region (Fig. 5).Fig. 4Three-dimensional effects in the wake result in a correlation between St and Re. Additionally, an oblique vortex shedding angle was observed when collecting data (Fig. 4B); this is known to have implications on the St-Re relationship11,12. Thus, we cannot guarantee that matching the non-dimensional time (t*) ensures equivalent Strouhal periods (Fig. 4C, Table 1). Given these variations in Strouhal number, each 100-frame sequence represents approximately 2.5 to 3.5 vortex shedding cycles (Table 1).

Since the cylinder was fixed rigidly to the tunnel and not free to vibrate or oscillate, oblique shedding was likely the result of a pressure gradient forming from minor differences in boundary conditions at the spanwise ends of cylinder11. We note that there was a minor asymmetry in cylinder geometries at the two walls, where a screw was used to adjust the exact cylinder length on one side. It is also possible that the difference in boundary conditions was the result of some spanwise deviation in the incoming velocity due to tunnel geometry (e.g., a blockage or crooked flow manipulator) or pump malfunction (e.g., one pump was less efficient than another). Regardless of the exact source of the oblique shedding angle, this effect should not impact the validity of this data for training and predicting cylinder wake velocity fields. In fact, the additional three-dimensional effects may provide more challenging dynamics. We simply note that the deviation in shedding frequency was observed, and that assumptions regarding equivalency between Strouhal period and formation time are not valid across the full range of Reynolds numbers tested.

Usage Notes

A Python script is provided alongside the dataset which loads the dataset, reports the Strouhal and Reynolds numbers of each experimental condition, and generates visualizations of the velocity fields and St-Re relationship.
