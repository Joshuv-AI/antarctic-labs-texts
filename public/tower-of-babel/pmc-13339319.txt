Hands-free control of an assistive robotic arm for high-level paralysis
Authors: Brady A. Hasse, Mario A. Ibarra, Jehad A. Alfaleh, Derek Gin, Drew E. G. Sheets, Aaliyah M. P. A. Thompson-Mazzeo, Andrew J. Fuglevand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent advancements in assistive robotic arms have enabled many people with tetraplegia to perform activities of daily living more independently. Because these systems typically require hand use, they are not a ready option for many individuals with high-level (C4 and above) tetraplegia. Such individuals, however, might be able to use signals that arise from the head and neck to control assistive devices. Therefore, the goal of the study was to evaluate the utility of several signals arising from the head and neck to control a robotic arm during 3D center-out reaching to multiple targets ~ 25–50 cm from the start location.MethodsTen non-disabled human subjects were tested using five non-invasive, hands-free modalities (head position, head velocity, facial electromyography, tongue, and voice) to control a robot arm. For comparison, subjects also used joystick position and joystick velocity methods to control reaching movements of the robotic arm. A one-way repeated measures ANOVA was carried out on key performance indicators including movement time, path efficiency, throughput, and perceived workload.ResultsThe hands-free control modalities of head position, facial EMG, tongue, and voice had average (± SD) movement times (5.8 ± 1.6, 8.2 ± 3.7, 6.3 ± 2.0, and 10.0 ± 3.7 s, respectively). With the exception of voice, none of these times were significantly different than that of the benchmark hand position control of a joystick (6.3 ± 2.3 s). Furthermore, no significant differences were revealed in perceived workload across control modalities.ConclusionsThese results indicate, therefore, that various non-invasive, hands-free methods could be used effectively by people with high-level tetraplegia to operate assistive robotic arms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-02004-8.

Background

Nearly 5.5 million people in the US live with paralysis, many of whom are young [5]. A major cause of paralysis is spinal cord injury (SCI) and ~ 50% of SCIs result in tetraplegia [18, 42]. People with high-level (i.e., injury levels C4 and above) tetraplegia suffer paralysis for both upper and lower limbs, including the hand. Tetraplegia results in profound impairment of autonomous functioning, necessitating assistance for activities of daily living, such as personal hygiene, eating, dressing, and toilet use. The requirement of continuous caregiving significantly elevates healthcare expenses, with costs surpassing one million dollars in the initial years post-injury [42]. Additionally, this near-total dependency leads to increased rates of depression and suicidal ideation in individuals with SCI [16]. Yet even small gains in independence can profoundly and positively impact individuals with SCI [34].

The development of assistive robotic arms attached to wheelchairs has increased the ability of people with tetraplegia to perform activities of daily living [13, 50]. Most of these devices are hand operated, including use of joysticks, keypads, touch screens, computer mice, trackballs, and button switches. For example, an assistive robot arm that used a joystick to control movements of the arm [2] significantly increased independence of its users when performing daily activities [7]. Unfortunately, however, many of those with high-level SCI are unable to take advantage of these devices because they require hand use.

A promising possibility that could enable people with high-level tetraplegia to convey desired actions to a robotic arm would be to decipher them directly from neurons recorded in the cerebral cortex, through brain-machine interfaces (BMIs). Indeed, it has been shown that individuals with long-standing, high-level tetraplegia can use BMIs to perform three-dimensional reach and grasp movements [14, 24]. Nevertheless, BMIs also come with substantial practical limitations. First, the need for brain surgery has inherent risks and high costs. And second, the implanted electrode arrays remain viable only over a limited time due to a host of complex factors [8, 44, 49, 57]. While new developments in electrode technology may partially mitigate some of these factors [25, 62], long-term (i.e., decades) stability needed for BMIs remains a significant challenge.

An alternative and noninvasive approach is to use remaining motor functions of person with high-level tetraplegia to operate a robotic arm [36]. Indeed, control can be established over such a device using a variety of hands-free methods that involve head movements [6, 26, 54, 58], gaze direction [15, 17, 29, 43, 63], surface EEG recordings [40, 53], tongue actions [2–4, 28, 39, 41], facial muscle activity [60] and voice [20, 21, 48, 59]. These simple, low cost, and safe control modalities remain relatively underexplored compared to the burgeoning number of studies involving invasive BMIs. Furthermore, it may be that they could match or exceed performance capabilities of such BMIs.

Most of these studies, however, have focused on a single approach with little systematic comparisons across modalities. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to compare the effectiveness of several hands-free control methods to reach with a robotic arm to targets presented in 3D space to help identify the most efficacious systems for non-invasive control of robotic arms. Furthermore, unlike most other studies, we compared performance of robot-arm control using hands-free methods to that produced using a hand-operated joystick—thereby providing an important benchmark of the relative utility of tested input methods. The average time to reach to various targets in real space using most of the hands-free methods tested here were not significantly different than that using the hand operating a joystick. This finding indicates that multiple control modalities could be used effectively by people with high level tetraplegia, thus enabling some flexibility in choice of control methods depending on specific deficits of individual tetraplegics. Moreover, invasive, costly BMIs may not be necessary to achieve reasonably efficient, intuitive control over an assistive device.

Methods

Subjects

Ten non-disabled subjects participated in this study (5 males and 5 females, ages 21–65 years). Each subject completed eight experimental sessions to control the endpoint of a robotic arm to reach to targets in 3D space. In each session, a different type of control was used involving a variety of input modalities (head movements, facial muscle activity, tongue pressure, and voice) and different command strategies. The command strategies included position control, velocity control, and discrete control. In position control, the input-device signal was mapped to the position of the robot arm, not unlike a computer mouse dictating cursor position on a screen. In velocity control, the device signal controlled the velocity of the robot-arm endpoint. In a coarse way, this is similar to gas pedal position dictating speed in a car. In discrete control, distinct commands instigated continuous movements of the robot endpoint in one direction until another command was given. The University of Arizona Human Investigation Committee approved the procedures and subjects gave informed consent, including reproduction of their image.

Experimental setup and general procedures

For each session, subjects sat centered and behind a six degrees-of-freedom (DOF) ViperX 300 Trossen Robotic Arm that has a total reach of 750 mm (Fig. 1A). Because this experiment only involved reaching, the gripper was replaced with a pointer. As such, the two DOF related to the gripper were not used and only four DOF of the robotic arm were employed. If one regards the robot arm as analogous to a human arm, then those DOF included axial rotation at the shoulder (base), and flexion–extension at the shoulder, elbow, and wrist. The robotic arm was mounted on a table where reach targets were placed. Subjects were directed to move the pointer endpoint to specified targets using one control modality per session. As described below, subjects could move the pointer attached to the robot arm up/down, left/right, and in/out. The desired actions (detected by sensors) along these orthogonal directions were transformed by an inverse kinematics algorithm (see below) into the angular rotations of the robot arm needed to perform these linear actions. Each modality had a distinct hardware setup and calibration routine that needed to be completed before the subject could control the robotic arm. A custom MATLAB script was developed to convert input signals from various devices (described below) into desired 3D robotic arm endpoint locations. The dimensions and characteristics of the ViperX 300 robotic arm were imported into MATLAB, where an inverse kinematics look-up table was generated using the Robotics System Toolbox. Using the look-up table, desired 3D locations of the endpoint were quickly converted into joint-angle commands, which were then delivered through an USB connection to a Robotis Open CM 9.04 microcontroller. This microcontroller was programmed in C + + to directly control the actuators located at different joints on the robotic arm.

For each control method, subjects performed 72 trials, involving reaches to 12 different spherical targets (10 cm diameter) (Fig. 1B). The target order remained the same and was repeated across six sets. A resin ring, mounted on a stand, was placed on the table and used to indicate the location of the virtual spherical target (Fig. 1A). Prior to the onset of each trial, the robotic arm would automatically move the pointer to the upcoming target location. This enabled the experimenter to place a target ring at the desired location.

After the target was placed, the pointer automatically moved to the center of a virtual home position (15 cm sphere, Fig. 1B). Robot arm control was then given over to the subject. Subjects needed to hold the pointer within this sphere for at least one second before a tone signaled the subject to reach with the robotic arm to the placed target. Prior to testing, subjects received the following standard instructions: “Your task in these experiments will be to move the tip of the robot arm, as quickly and efficiently as possible, from a home position to different target locations. The robot endpoint moves in 3-dimensions: right and left, front and back, up and down. Depending on the control method, you may or may not be able to move on the diagonal, so do what's most comfortable and efficient for you. You can stay along the axes of the coordinate system, or try to move directly to the target, whatever you prefer.” A reach was deemed successful when the end point was positioned and held within the spherical target for at least one second. If a successful reach was not accomplished within 60 s, the trial was canceled and scored as an unsuccessful trial. During a trial, four distinct tones notified the subject of: (1) trial start, (2) when the endpoint entered the target, (3) when the endpoint exited the target, and (4) a successful trial.

Immediately after completing each testing session, subjects completed the NASA Task-Load Index (TLX) survey [23]. This widely used and validated instrument [22] evaluates the perceived workload associated with a task based on six weighted subscales (temporal, physical, mental demands, frustration, effort, and performance). Questions associated with each subscale were presented to the subject on a computer screen (e.g., “how hard did you have to work (physically or mentally) to accomplish your level of performance?”). The subject then indicated on a 1–20-point scale (either by moving a slider or typing in a number) their response to a question. The averaged weighted response value across all subscales was calculated. The larger the value, the greater the perceived difficulty or workload associated with the task.

With the exception of sessions 1 and 8, the order of sessions using different input methods was randomized across subjects. The first session always involved movements of the subject’s own hand on a 3D joystick to control the robot. Data from this session served as a benchmark comparison. Because this type of control was the most intuitive, we also used the first session to familiarize subjects to the task and robot characteristics. The last session (session 8) repeated that of the first. We did this to determine if performance in the joystick task might had been better (because of greater overall experience with the contingencies of the task) had it not always been presented as the first session. The other six sessions included: joystick velocity control, head position control, head velocity control, velocity control using tongue pressure, velocity control using facial electromyographic (EMG) signals, and discrete control using voice commands. Velocity control only was used for face EMG and tongue as position control was not a viable option for those modalities. This is because in position mode, three signals were needed to simultaneously indicate the x, y, z position of the endpoint. For EMG control, for example, that would require simultaneous and independent activation of three different face muscles—something beyond the capability of most human subjects. Experimental sessions were mostly held on separate days, although in a few cases, subjects participated in two sessions in one day. In the following sections, the apparatus and methods used for each control modality are described.

Joystick control

Subjects controlled the robotic arm end point in the horizontal (x-y) plane by displacing a 30-cm long joystick (Fig. 2A). The joystick (P3 America, JB40-YO-20GP) was securely fixed to the test table behind the robot arm and with the knob of the joystick at a comfortable height for hand placement. The joystick did not have an automatic return to the vertical orientation. A force transducer (DYMH-103 Mini Tension/Compression Force Sensor) was mounted near the bottom of the joystick shaft and was used to measure compressive and tensile forces applied by the hand to the knob of the joystick. Compressive forces controlled downward while tensile loads actuated upward movements of the robotic arm. The joystick was calibrated prior to each use. This involved recording joystick voltage signals while moving the joystick to its maximal extents in the forward-back (x axis) and left–right (y axis) directions. For the vertical (z axis) directions, subjects were asked to push down and pull up on the joystick handle with intensities that were considered to be at comfortable levels for use over an hour duration of testing. For joystick position control, these measures served as linear calibration values that were mapped to the maximal displacement of robot arm motion in the positive and negative directions along each axis. For velocity control, like that done by Rudigkeit and Gebhard [54], the sensor signal was mapped to a sigmoid velocity profile, with the maximum input signal (measured during the calibration) associated with the maximum speed of displacement (20 cm/s) along one of the six directions. We selected 20 cm/s as the maximum speed, based on preliminary testing, because it seemed reasonably manageable for users to control and was safe. A small buffer region (~ 5% of the maximum input signal) was implemented such that small changes in the input signal around zero did not trigger movements of the robot arm. This helped subjects to hold the endpoint of the robot arm steady when needed.

Head control

A six DOF movement sensor (Polhemus) was placed on the head of subjects to provide input to the robotic arm for head control. The sensor was fixed to a plastic hair band on top of the head (Figs. 1A and 2B). A second Polhemus sensor was taped over the 1st thoracic vertebra. Linear and angular measures of head displacement were made based on output from the first sensor relative to output from the second sensor. In this way, movements of the trunk did not markedly alter head motion signals needed to control the robot arm. Rotation of the head about a vertically oriented yaw axis was used to control left and right motions of the robot arm (Fig. 2B). Likewise, rotation of the head about a horizontally oriented pitch axis was used to control up and down motion of the robot. Finally, linear displacement along a protraction-retraction axis was used to control forward and back motions of the robot (Fig. 2B).

Head motion was calibrated for each subject by having them rotate their head to comfortable positions left and right and up and down. For forward-back motions, subjects were first asked to hold their head in a comfortable, neutral position. Then, linear displacement of the head in absolute units (mm) from this neutral position was used as the input to control forward-back motions of the robot. In position control, relative head rotation and forward-back movements of the head were linearly mapped to endpoint positions in 3D space. Velocity control mode for head motion was handled in a similar way as for joystick velocity control.

EMG control

For this method, participants activated facial/neck muscles to drive movement of the robotic arm. Six pairs of bipolar surface electrodes (Ag–AgCl, 4 mm diameter) were secured to the skin overlying the target muscles (Fig. 2C). The main muscles targeted, and their associated movement direction, included: right platysma (right), left platysma (left), right zygomatic (forward), left zygomatic, (back), frontalis (up), and mentalis (down). However, some subjects had difficulty selectively activating the platysma (see also [60]). In those cases, we typically then used the right and left orbicularis oculi to control forward and back motions, while using the zygomatic muscles to control right and left motions of the robot (Fig. 2C). A ground electrode was placed over the clavicle. EMG signals were amplified (× 1000) and bandpass filtered (30–100 Hz Grass Model 12 EuroData Acquisition System). This somewhat narrow filter range for surface EMG was needed to prevent aliasing with the low sampling-rate analog–digital (A/D) converters that we used. The conditioned EMG signals were digitally sampled at 208 Hz (National Instruments 6001 A/D converter) and routed to a MATLAB script that rectified and low-pass filtered (~ 2 Hz) the signals that were then used to control movements of the robot arm.

During calibration, resting baseline signals were recorded, from which mean and standard deviation (SD) values were determined. Subjects then performed a maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) lasting about 2 s for each muscle. A threshold was set for engaging robot movement at 10 SD above the mean baseline. EMG amplitude was mapped to robot arm movement speed using a sigmoid profile with maximum speed (20 cm/s) corresponding to an EMG level of 60% MVC.

Tongue control

For the tongue modality, participants controlled the robotic arm by pressing with their tongue on individual sensors in an array located on the upper palate (Fig. 2D). The sensors were imbedded in a 3D-printed, ovoid-shaped, 2 mm thick shell with six circular openings to accommodate compressive force sensors (ATO Micro Load Cell). The sensors were fixed to the shell with biocompatible epoxy and with their cables directed to exit from the front of the mouth. The shell was surrounded by a large flange that served as a bite piece to hold the device in place. The flange was secured to a 3D-printed U-shaped frame made of stainless steel. This served to stiffen the device under the otherwise deforming strain applied by the teeth that could be transmitted to the force sensors. Small rubber or plastic domes were fixed over the surface of the transducers to enhance tactile sensing of them by the tongue.

For each subject, a custom mold of their front and bottom teeth was made out of dental impression material (Mark3 3005 VPS Putty Fast Set) that was fixed to the flange. This helped to comfortably secure the system in the mouth. Sensor signals were amplified (× 1000, World Precision Instruments, Transbridge DC amplifiers, powered by an isolated transformer) and routed to an A/D converter (National Instruments 6001) and sampled at 208 Hz. The sampled signals were read directly into a MATLAB script and digitally filtered (low-pass filter, 4 Hz cut-off).

The tongue system exclusively used velocity control to regulate movements of the robotic arm. The force detected on each sensor was mapped to a sigmoidal speed profile in each of six directions. The maximum speed was set to 20 cm/s. To identify the force associated with the maximum speed, during calibration, subjects pressed on each sensor at a level deemed of moderate intensity and comfortable to exert. To minimize the effects of inadvertent application of force across more than one sensor, only the sensor detecting the highest force was used to control the robot arm at any moment. In addition, a threshold level of 10 SD above the baseline mean needed to be exceeded in order to engage robot movement. Such a threshold was used to prevent inadvertent triggering of robot arm motion due to weak tongue contact with the sensors, such as might occur during talking or swallowing. Subjects were provided with an image (like in Fig. 2D) indicating which sensor actuated each movement direction.

Voice control

Subjects controlled robotic arm position through short (single syllable) and discrete commands. A clip-on microphone captured the subject's voice which was delivered to Vosk (pypi.org/project/vosk/), an open-source speech recognition tool kit. A custom Python script loaded and ran a pre-trained voice-to-text model (Vosk model small en us-0.15) simultaneously on a PC’s GPU (using Tensorflow—a machine learning framework). In near real time, words identified were compared to the list of seven robot commands (“right”, “left “up”, “down”, “out”, “in”, “pause”). If a robot command was recognized, the command was delivered to the MATLAB interface. Movements continued along a trajectory at a constant speed (10 cm/s) until a new command was given or the subject said “pause” which halted movement until another command was issued. A lower robot speed was used for voice control compared to other modalities because the moderate delays in voice-command recognition could lead to marked overshoots with higher speeds.

Data analysis

The robot endpoint position, calculated by inverse kinematics, was sampled at 120 Hz and smoothed by an 11- point moving average. Performance metrics used by others [60] were adopted as the main outcome measures for each trial. The three main measures made from each trial included: movement time, path efficiency, and throughput. Movement time was defined as the time elapsed from the exit of the robot endpoint from the home-base sphere until it first entered the target sphere (and when the endpoint stayed within the target for at least 1 s).

Path efficiency was calculated as the minimum path distance (i.e., a straight line) between the home-base exit point and the target entry point divided by the actual path distance between those two points. Theoretically, values can range between 0 and 1 with numbers closer to 1 being desirable. Throughput was calculated as the index of difficulty divided by the movement time. The index of difficulty (ID) is based on Fitts law and was calculated using: ID = log2 (D/W + 1) where D is the straight-line distance between the start location and the target, and W is the width of the target [56]. ID values for our targets ranged from 1.8 to 2.6 bits (see Suppl. Table S1, also includes 3D locations of targets). Because the unit of ID is in bits—the unit of throughput is in bits/s. Throughput provides a standardized way to compare movement performances across studies.

To minimize variabilities associated with initial learning in these novel tasks, only the last 36 of 72 trials in a session were used for analysis. Subjects were not informed that the first 36 trials were not to be used. In this way, subjects were well motivated and focused during these initial trials. For each subject and test condition, average values of the last 36 trials were calculated for each of the three metrics. A repeated measures 1-way ANOVA with modality (joystick position, joystick velocity, head position, head velocity tongue, face EMG, and voice) as the factor. For cases in which data were not normally distributed, data were log transformed prior running the ANOVA. For a given measure, session-type dependence was considered significant for p < 0.05. When warranted, post hoc testing was performed using Tukey tests. Similar statistical analyses were performed for the NASA TLX survey scores.

Two participants completed seven sessions but were unable to carry out the tongue control session because the tongue platform did not fit properly in their mouths. While joystick and head controls permitted simultaneous multi-axis movements, subjects nevertheless tended to control the robot arm using one dimension at a time (Fig. 3B, D). For tongue control (as well as for face EMG, and voice control), subjects were limited to sequential one-dimensional movements (Fig. 3E). In principle, simultaneous control of more than one direction for tongue and EMG inputs could have been enabled. However, preliminary testing revealed that concurrent signaling on more than one channel was usually the consequence of inadvertent (rather than strategic) activation of more than one sensor. For example, as seen in Fig. 3E, the subject unintentionally applied force to the down sensor while attempting to press the right. Therefore, a ‘winner-take-all’ scheme was enacted whereby at any moment, only the channel with the largest signal was used to drive the robot arm.

It is of note that the robot arm could move at much higher speeds in position compared to velocity control (typically limited to 20 cm/s). In position control, the robot arm was displaced almost instantaneously by the extent commanded by the sensor input. As such, maximum possible speeds in position mode could be in excess of 200 cm/s. However, subjects did not make use of high speeds in position control. Instead, they drove the arm relatively slowly (peak speeds of 15–20 cm/s) during position control (e.g. black traces in Fig. 2A, C), to securely regulate displacements of the arm.

Comparison of movement parameters for joystick position-control in session 1 to that in session 8 using t-tests revealed no significant differences (movement time p = 0.59; path efficiency p = 0.51; throughput p = 0.38). Therefore, data for joystick position-control across those sessions were combined (session 1 and session 8 values averaged for each subject).

Average and individual path efficiencies across the seven modalities are shown in Fig. 4B. Path efficiency is the ratio of the ideal to the actual path, with values approaching one representing greater efficiency. There was a significant (p < 0.0001) effect of modality on path efficiency. Interestingly, path efficiency was significantly lower for all sessions involving position control (either joystick or head) compared to other sessions (p < 0.02, Tukey), except for head position compared to head velocity control (p = 0.07). We believe such lower path efficiencies for position control was due to higher degree vacillations (and increase in path length) as the endpoint approached the target. To some degree, this can be seen in the example trajectories of joystick and head position control in Fig. 3B, D. Due to the nature of position control in our experiments, even small changes in the absolute positions of the joystick or head (for example, when attempting to home in on the target) were translated in relatively large actual distances by the robot arm. Such high sensitivity meant subjects tended to overshoot, then overcorrect, when making final adjustments to hit the target.

Figure 4C shows the mean (SD) throughput and individual averages across the seven modalities. Throughput is the ratio of target's index of difficulty and movement time, with higher values indicating greater performance. There was a significant effect (p = 0.003) of modality on throughput. In post hoc testing, head velocity control was shown to have lower throughput values than joystick position (p = 0.003, Cohen’s d = 1.61), joystick velocity (p = 0.0006, Cohen’s d = 1.91), head position (p = 0.0005, Cohen’s d = 1.97), tongue (p = 0.005, Cohen’s d = 2.19), and face EMG (p = 0.0026, Cohen’s d = 1.34) control modalities. The lower throughput of head velocity is consistent with its longer movement time (Fig. 4A). With the exception of head velocity, average throughput values were ~ 0.35 bits/s.

After each session, subjects completed the NASA TLX survey to evaluate perceived workload of the task just performed. Figure 4D shows the mean (SD) and individual NASA TLX scores across modalities. A higher NASA TLX score indicates higher perceived workload. Overall, there was no significant effect of session on perceived workload (p = 0.25).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated different hands-free control methods to operate a robotic arm for a reaching task. Hands-free control modalities (head position, facial EMG, and tongue) demonstrated comparable movement time and control accuracy as the benchmark hand control using a joystick. These results suggest that such hands-free control methods could be readily used by people with high-level tetraplegia to operate assistive robotic arms.

Before discussing the results, it is worth pointing out that there has been extensive use of EEG-based systems as non-invasive BMIs (e.g., [9, 32, 35, 37, 40, 45, 64]). Yet inherent limitations have precluded substantial advancements in the use of EEG signals to control robotic limbs [55]. Furthermore, the use of EEG signals for binary classification or to control simulated movements on a computer may take months of training [37, 45, 64]. Therefore, we decided to forgo testing of EEG as a control modality in the present study.

Comparison of control modalities

Reaching to targets with the robotic arm was readily performed using head position control, tongue, and facial EMG. Based on the relatively low NASA TLX score (Fig. 4D), subjects found these method reasonably simple to use. Average movement times for these control methods were not significanlty different than our benchmark–joystick position control. This implies that there would be no particular disadvantage for a person with high-level tetraplegia to use these hands-free methods to control reaches with a robotic arm compared to a non-disabled person using a joystick with the hand.

For two of the modalities tested (joystick and head), it was possible to directly compare position and velocity control methods for robot arm reaching. Interestingly, while joystick velocity control was just as effective as position control, head velocity control was substantially worse than head position control (Fig. 5). Indeed, head velocity control was associated with the slowest movement times across all methods tested (Fig. 4A), lowest throughput (Fig. 4C), and highest perceived workload (Fig. 4D).

We believe that part of the difficulty with head velocity control was the frequent necessity to turn the head away from the fixated target toward the neutral (zero velocity) position in order to hold the arm at a particular location. This action conflicts with natural behavior to orient the head in the direction of reaching. Furthermore, because there was no distinct clue (other than proprioceptive information) as to what orientation of the head represented the neutral position, robot arm position sometimes drifted away from the desire location in head velocity control mode. While this was also true of joystick velocity control, subjects seemed better able to identify the neutral (vertical) orientation of the joystick than that for the head. Indeed, in some instances, subjects briefly looked at the hand/joystick while locating the neutral position (e.g. Supplemental Video).

Obstacles to practical implementation of control methods

One concern with head motion control is the degree to which individuals with high level lesions (C4 and above) might be impaired in making the needed head movements. Important neck muscles are supplied by motor neurons located at cervical levels C1–C4 [19, 27, 52]. Therefore, motor neurons supplying neck muscles and that control head movements are susceptible to damage in high-level spinal cord injuries. Nevertheless, despite some neck-movement impairments, individuals with injuries at C0–C4 [26, 54, 61] were all able to use head movements to control robotic arms or computer cursors practically as well as able-bodied subjects using the same interfaces. Therefore, using head motion to control assistive devices would seem to be a reasonable option for many high-level tetraplegics.

For a tongue-based system, one significant challenge is to minimize interference with other oral functions, such as speaking and eating. The system we developed for proof-of-principle testing (Fig. 2D) was somewhat bulky. For simplicity, we fabricated only one device and then used dental impression material to customize it (as much as possible) for different subjects. Others, however, have developed wireless, lower-profile interfaces built into custom-fabricated dental retainers [3, 39] (Mouthpad www.augmental.tech). Those devices are minimally obtrusive and would seem to offer little hinderance to eating, drinking, and speaking. We did find, however, that having reasonably sized domes fixed to the sensors greatly aided subjects in identifying and distinguishing different sensors with their tongue.

There are at least a couple practical challenges in using EMG signals to control a robotic arm. One is that there was variance across subjects in the degree to which they could independently control activation of six face/neck muscles needed to control the robot arm. As such, the specific muscles targeted for EMG recording would need to be identified for each subject. Second, surface EMG electrodes, as used here, are not only cosmetically unsuitable but would also require daily application and removal of electrodes by a caregiver (this is also true of EEG systems). On possible solution to this problem would be to use stretchable, thin-film, wireless systems that provide high-fidelity EMG recordings with inconspicuous on-the-skin, durable electrodes [38, 51].

Voice control required significantly more time to acquire reach targets than our benchmark method, joystick position control (Fig. 4A). The slower times for voice control were likely due, in part, to the pretrained voice recognition model not being trained on each subject’s voice. This led to some misinterpretations (particularly for subjects with non-American accents), resulting in incorrect commands and target overshooting. Additionally, there was a not insignificant delay (~ 0.5–1 s) between the command being stated and enacted. Thus, subjects had to carefully gauge the timing of voice commands to avoid overshoot (e.g., see video in Supplemental materials). To partially mitigate this problem, we used slower robotic arm velocities for voice control compared to other modalities, thereby reducing overshoot, but increasing the time to reach targets.

Nevertheless, voice would still seem an acceptable choice to control movements of a robot arm for a variety of reasons. First, voice signals are easy to obtain and require no specialized equipment other than a microphone. Second, voice commands are designed to be immediately understandable. Third, the rapidly expanding use of voice-activated devices makes it likely that processing times for speech recognition will continue to decrease. And forth, an increase in the vocabulary of voice commands needed to enact a larger repertoire of robot-arm actions can be readily implemented. Given these benefits, a number of investigators have already implemented voice commands to control robot arms [20, 30, 47, 48].

It is important to highlight that while muscles of the larynx are usually unaffected by high-level spinal cord injuries, respiratory muscles that drive vocalizations are. For example, a person with a C6 lesion will be able to breath without respiratory assistance but because of impaired activation of expiratory muscles, voicing will tend to be weak and phrasing somewhat brief [10]. Higher level injuries (C3 and above) often require ventilatory support because of significant paralysis of the diaphragm. There are, however, a number of methods that enable speech even with a tracheostomy [11]. In these systems, voicing tends to be restricted to short phrases of varying loudness with relatively long pauses between phrases [10]. Because most commands for controlling a robotic arm would likely entail a single word or brief phrase, it seems probable that even a person with a tracheostomy would be able to effectively convey desired actions verbally, particularly when voice recognition is trained on the user’s own voice.

Limitations

This investigation only evaluated the ability to reach to targets in 3D space using hands-free methods. The utility of different methods for controlling an assistive robotic arm, however, would obviously need to include grasping and transport of objects, as required in activities of daily living. Therefore, future work will need to integrate reaching and grasping in the testing of hands-free methods to control a robot arm to perform tasks similar to those encountered in daily life. This will necessitate control over additional DOFs (e.g., grip aperture, grip longitudinal rotation, and grip pitch). One way that might be accomplished would be to control reaching with one of the methods tested here (e.g., head motion) and gripper actions with another modality, such as voice commands.

Another limitation of the present work was that the targets used for testing were unique to this study. This limits comparisons with other studies. To partly aid future replications of this study, specific locations of the targets are included in the supplemental materials. Also, the use of normalized measures, such as throughput, facilitate across-study comparison. The most important way to enhance comparisons across studies, however, will be to use standardized, validated, reliable, and widely accepted tasks, such as the action research arm task and box-and-block task [12, 33, 46].

All the subjects tested in this project were non-disabled. Future work, therefore, needs to include people with high level tetraplegia in the evaluation of control methods for assistive robotic arms. Such testing will provide insight into physical limitations of people with tetraplegia to use various control methods. Furthermore, it may highlight that different control methods may be needed for different individuals depending on their specific limitations. It should also be mentioned that this study only involved one testing session for each modality. In the future, it will be important to determine the extent to which robot arm performance can improve with practice over several days.

It is of interest to point out that there are rapid advances in the development of autonomous robotic systems that could be used by people with high-level paralysis. These artificial intelligence-based systems use embodied reasoning and computer vision to carry out complex, multistep actions in response to natural language commands [1]. While the performance of these systems is remarkable, a balance will likely need to be struck between robot autonomy, on one hand, and involvement of a person with paralysis, on the other. Indeed, people with paralysis prefer self-control over a robotic arm with slower performance than a highly automatic but faster one, due to the lack of agency associated with the automatic system [31].

Conclusion

Here we demonstrated that various non-invasive, hands-free methods, including those that sense head motion, facial EMG, and tongue pressure can be used to control a robotic arm as effectively as that achieved using a hand-operated joystick. These results suggest that people with high-level tetraplegia have a variety of options that could be deployed to effectively control assistive robotic devices. Furthermore, these findings suggest that such hands-free methods could provide less expensive and less risky alternatives to brain machine interfaces needed to control robotic arms by individuals with high-level paralysis.
