A surgical calm to the storm: managing refractory electrical storms with bilateral thoracoscopic sympathectomy
Authors: Badreddine Belayachi, Gil Frey, Emilie Gagnepain, Peggy Jacon, Sandrine Venier, Pascal Defaye, Pierre-Yves Brichon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesRefractory electrical storms (ES) present a significant challenge in patients with structural heart disease, often persisting despite optimal medical therapy, catheter ablation, and ICD therapy. This study evaluates the efficacy and safety of bilateral thoracoscopic sympathectomy (BCSD) in reducing arrhythmic burden and improving clinical outcomes in this high-risk population.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted at CHU Grenoble-Alpes, including 11 patients with ES secondary to cardiomyopathies treated with BCSD between 2019 and 2024. Baseline characteristics, surgical outcomes, and arrhythmic recurrence were analysed. The procedure involved bilateral video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) targeting the stellate ganglion and T1–T4 sympathetic chain. Patients were followed up for arrhythmic recurrence, complications, and survival.ResultsThe median age of the cohort was 71 years, and 73% had ischaemic cardiomyopathy with a median LVEF of 30%. Immediate postoperative outcomes were favourable, with sinus rhythm restored in all patients. Complications occurred in 18%, including one case each of respiratory distress and vasomotor disturbance. ES recurrence was observed in 18% within 30 days and VT recurrence in 55% at a median of 6.9 months Survival analysis indicated a median survival time of 10 months.ConclusionsBCSD is a viable option for managing refractory ES, offering significant arrhythmic control and symptom relief in the short term. The findings support the integration of BCSD into multidisciplinary care pathways for high-risk patients. Future studies are required to validate long-term efficacy and optimise patient selection.Keywords: Thoracoscopic sympathectomy, Electrical storms, Ventricular arrhythmias, Ischemic cardiomyopathy, Cardiac sympathetic denervation, VATS

Introduction

Refractory ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) and electrical storms (ES) remain a significant challenge in the management of patients with structural heart disease, with up to 20% of patients with ICDs experiencing these life-threatening events despite optimal medical therapy [1]. The pathophysiology of these arrhythmias is strongly linked to autonomic dysfunction, particularly excessive sympathetic nervous system activity, which has been implicated in the initiation and maintenance of arrhythmias [2]. Current treatment strategies include pharmacological agents, catheter ablation, and ICD therapy, but these remain insufficient for a subset of patients who continue to experience recurrent arrhythmias and frequent ICD shocks [3, 4].

In this context, cardiac sympathetic denervation (CSD) has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach, particularly for patients with refractory arrhythmias that are resistant to conventional treatments. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for sympathetic denervation has demonstrated success in a variety of arrhythmic conditions, including ventricular tachycardia (VT) associated with ischemic cardiomyopathy and long-QT syndrome. Studies have reported success rates of 70–80% for VATS in treating refractory VAs, with bilateral thoracoscopic sympathectomy (BCSD) being shown to offer superior results in some cases compared to unilateral approaches [5]​.

This paper presents a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent CSD for ES, aiming to assess the procedure’s efficacy in reducing arrhythmic events and improving clinical outcomes in a cohort of patients with various underlying cardiomyopathies. By evaluating patient characteristics, surgical outcomes, and arrhythmic recurrence, we aim to contribute further evidence to support CSD as a valuable therapeutic option for patients with refractory electrical storms​.

Patients and methods

This retrospective study was conducted at CHU Grenoble-Alpes between 2019 and 2024. We included patients diagnosed with cardiomyopathies who experienced refractory ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) and had failed conventional treatments, including antiarrhythmic drugs, catheter ablation, and implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) therapy. Patients were considered for inclusion if they had recurrent arrhythmic events, evolving to electrical storms, despite optimal pharmacological management and prior interventions.

Patient characteristics were collected from electronic medical records, including demographic data (age, sex), comorbidities, and the type of cardiomyopathy (ischaemic, dilated, or arrhythmogenic). The baseline arrhythmic burden was assessed by reviewing the number of ICD shocks and the occurrence of electrical storms in the month leading up to surgery. Electrical storms were defined as more than three ICD shocks or episodes of antitachycardia pacing within a 24-hour period.

All patients included in the study had undergone a comprehensive preoperative evaluation, which involved consultation with a multidisciplinary team of cardiologists, electrophysiologists, and thoracic surgeons. The decision to proceed with thoracoscopic sympathectomy (CSD) was made based on the persistence of arrhythmic symptoms despite aggressive medical and interventional therapy.

The surgical procedure involved bilateral video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). All patients were anaesthetised under general anaesthesia with single-lung ventilation. The sympathetic chain was accessed through two thoracoscopic ports, one axillary and one more anterior and the procedure involved resection of the stellate ganglion and sympathetic fibres from T1 to T4, depending on the patient’s individual anatomy and response to intraoperative findings. No intraoperative electrophysiological testing, nerve integrity monitoring, or physiological markers (e.g., heart rate or skin temperature changes) were used to confirm the adequacy of denervation. The completeness of the resection was assessed visually and confirmed histologically postoperatively.In cases where the sympathetic chain or stellate ganglion could not be directly visualised due to dense adhesions or anatomical variation, dissection was performed along the expected anatomical pathway of the sympathetic chain, adjacent to the vertebral bodies, from T1 to T4. Peri-vertebral tissue in the presumed trajectory was resected en bloc to maximise the likelihood of effective denervation despite limited visibility.

Postoperative management included close monitoring in an intensive care unit, with particular attention to respiratory function and potential complications such as pneumothorax, Horner syndrome, and compensatory sweating. Patients were discharged once stable and followed up at regular intervals to assess arrhythmic recurrence, including electrical storms.

Ethical considerations

This study complies with the French Jardé Law (Law No. 2012 − 300). It is classified as Category 3 non-interventional research, involving retrospective analysis of anonymised data collected during routine clinical care. As per Article L1121-1 of the French Public Health Code, ethical approval was not required for this type of study. All patients provided verbal consent for the use of their data for research purposes, in accordance with Article L1122-1-1, which allows verbal consent for non-interventional studies involving minimal risk. Patient agreement was documented in their medical records.To ensure compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), all patient data were anonymised prior to analysis, ensuring confidentiality and preventing re-identification.

Results

Initial characteristics

A total of 11 patients were included, with a mean age of 68.2 ± 9.6 years. All patients were male, with 73% classified as ASA 4, reflecting significant comorbidities (Charlson Comorbidity Index mean : 4.6 ± 1.2.). All patients were smokers or ex-smokers. The myopathy was ischemic in 73% of cases, with a mean left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 30.2% ± 9.8%,. All patients were on beta-blockers, diuretics, and anticoagulants, with 73% also receiving antiplatelet therapy (Table 1). Two patients (18%) had a history of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, managed medically prior to sympathectomy. At the time of sympathectomy, 5 patients (45%) had dual-chamber ICDs, 4 patients (36%) had cardiac resynchronisation therapy with defibrillator (CRT-D), and 2 patients (18%) had single-chamber ICDs (Tables 2 and 3).

Baseline characteristics, surgical treatment and outcomes in terms of overall survival

Seven patients underwent ablation for VT, averaging two ablations per patient. Notably, all patients had at least one episode of electrical storm in the month preceding surgery.

Surgical treatment

The mean time from the first electrical storm to surgery was 85.4 ± 35.2 days. Preoperative stellate ganglion blockade was performed in 45% of cases. The left side was approached first in nine patients, with one patient receiving only left-sided sympathectomy. The sympathetic chain was visualized in 83% of patients on the left and 60% on the right, while the stellate ganglion was visible in 45% of patients on the left and 30% on the right. The average operative time was 49 min.

Postoperative outcomes

Immediate postoperative outcomes were favourable, with 82% of patients experiencing an uncomplicated recovery. Sinus rhythm was achieved or maintained in all cases. Complications occurred in two patients: one developed respiratory distress, and another experienced contralateral vasomotor disturbances. During follow-up, VT recurred in six patients (55%), with a mean time to recurrence of 6.9 months. Two patients (18%) underwent repeat catheter ablation due to recurrent arrhythmias despite BCSD. A significant reduction in ICD therapies was observed during the first three months postoperatively: the mean number of ICD shocks per month decreased from 4.2 ± 1.8 to 1.1 ± 0.9 (p = 0.01), and the mean number of ATP episodes per month decreased from 7.6 ± 3.2 to 2.4 ± 1.5 (p = 0.02). Notably, electrical storm recurred in only two patients, and this appeared to correlate with the absence of stellate ganglion resection.

Mortality and survival

The mean follow-up duration was 22.3 ± 9.6 months. During this period, six deaths occurred among the cohort, representing a mortality ratio of 6/11 (54,5%). The causes of death included colonic mass (n = 1), arrhythmia (n = 1), pulseless electrical activity (n = 1), paroxysmal ventricular tachycardia (n = 1), heart failure (n = 1), and postoperative complications following hip arthroplasty (n = 1). Survival analysis indicated a mean survival time of 10.5 ± 4.2 months. The survival curve (Fig. 1) provides a detailed visualization of survival trends over the follow-up period.

Discussion

This retrospective study evaluated the outcomes of bilateral thoracoscopic sympathectomy (BCSD) in patients with electrical storms (ES) who had failed conventional therapies, including antiarrhythmic drugs, catheter ablation, and ICD therapy. The findings underscore BCSD as an effective adjunctive treatment, achieving reductions in arrhythmic burden and suggesting a valuable option for this high-risk patient population.

Our study suggests that BCSD can serve as an effective treatment for patients with refractory electrical storms, particularly those with ischemic cardiomyopathy. The immediate postoperative outcomes were promising, sinus rhythm was maintained or restored postoperatively in all patients. This aligns with previous studies suggesting that sympathetic denervation can be an effective strategy for stabilising cardiac electrical activity [6–8]. For instance, Pêgo-Fernandes et al. discussed the potential utility of surgical sympathectomy in managing severe cardiac arrhythmias, indicating that the mechanism of action may involve sympathetic denervation, which stabilises arrhythmic electrical activity​ [9].

The reduction in arrhythmic burden, particularly in terms of ICD shocks and episodes of electrical storms, underscores the efficacy of the procedure [10]. Our findings are consistent with other reports demonstrating that BCSD significantly reduces the number of ICD shocks and mitigates the occurrence of arrhythmic events [6, 11, 12]. Notably, the majority of patients experienced an extended period without arrhythmias after surgery, indicating that BCSD can provide substantial symptom relief. This is further supported by Vaseghi et al. (2020), who observed similar outcomes in patients with structural heart disease undergoing BCSD​ [6].

Despite the promising short-term results, we observed that 55% of patients in our cohort experienced recurrence of VT, which raises important questions about the long-term efficacy of BCSD. While the short-term benefits are well-documented, the mechanisms behind these recurrences remain unclear. The recurrence of arrhythmias in a subset of patients suggests that the autonomic nervous system’s role in arrhythmogenesis may not be fully addressed by sympathetic denervation alone [13]. Factors such as incomplete sympathetic resection or anatomical variability in sympathetic pathways could contribute to the recurrence of arrhythmic events [14, 15]. Further studies are needed to explore the underlying mechanisms of recurrence and the role of postoperative management strategies in improving long-term outcomes. The impact of early intervention and tailored surgical approaches based on patient-specific characteristics also warrants further investigation.

The correlation between stellate ganglion resection and the recurrence of electrical storms in some patients highlights the complexity of cardiac innervation and its impact on arrhythmogenesis. The presence of ganglion tissue confirmed on pathology was observed in 8 patients. Among these, 4/8 (50%) experienced VT recurrence, whereas all patients without confirmed ganglion resection (n = 3) experienced recurrence. Although the association was not statistically significant (p = 0.20), the data suggest that ganglion resection may correlate with lower recurrence and could represent a surgical endpoint. These findings are in line with studies that have shown the variability of sympathetic innervation in the heart and the challenges of achieving complete denervation through surgical means alone [16]. The variability of the sympathetic nervous system’s contribution to arrhythmia in patients with different underlying heart conditions, such as ischemic cardiomyopathy, further complicates the procedure’s success [17, 18]. Our data also indicate that pathological confirmation of ganglion resection was associated with lower recurrence, supporting its potential as an intraoperative endpoint to assess adequacy of denervation. Although further evidence is needed, this observation aligns with the concept that more complete resection improves outcomes.

Several studies have reported on the anatomical complexity of the cardiac sympathetic system, with some authors suggesting that the sympathetic influence extends beyond the stellate ganglion to involve cervical and lower thoracic ganglia​ [19, 20]. Our study adds to this body of work by suggesting that more extensive resection, may offer better results. However, the risk of complications such as Horner syndrome, compensatory sweating, and brachial plexus injury remains a concern. Additionally, ongoing advancements in intraoperative monitoring, such as real-time imaging of sympathetic pathways, may help refine the technique and improve its success rate [21].

While left-sided thoracoscopic sympathectomy (LCSD) is also an established procedure for arrhythmias, our study suggests that BCSD may offer superior results, particularly in patients with structural heart disease and recurrent VAs. By including right-sided sympathectomy [21–23], BCSD provides a more comprehensive approach to addressing the residual autonomic pathways that may contribute to arrhythmogenesis. This is consistent with findings from other studies showing that right-sided sympathectomy can offer additional benefits in patients with refractory arrhythmias, particularly those with complex arrhythmic substrates or structural heart disease [6]. As such, future research should focus on identifying the factors that predict better outcomes with each approach. Comparative studies between LCSD and BCSD will be essential in refining treatment protocols and ensuring the most effective therapy.

The integration of BCSD into multidisciplinary care pathways is crucial for optimising outcomes [16, 23]. Close collaboration between cardiologists, electrophysiologists, and thoracic surgeons ensures that patients are appropriately selected for surgery and that the procedure is performed at the optimal time. The role of BCSD in a broader therapeutic context, particularly in conjunction with other interventions like catheter ablation and device therapy, should be explored further. Prospective studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up, and advanced intraoperative monitoring techniques will be essential to fully establish the long-term efficacy and safety of BCSD.

In addition, further studies are needed to investigate the impact of postoperative management strategies on the prevention of arrhythmic recurrence. Exploring adjunctive therapies such as pharmacological treatments, device adjustments, or lifestyle interventions may improve long-term outcomes for patients undergoing BCSD.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design and small sample size limit the generalizability of the findings and reduce the statistical power to detect differences between subgroups. The absence of a control group precludes direct comparisons with other therapies, such as LCSD or repeat catheter ablation. Additionally, follow-up duration was limited in some cases, which may have affected the assessment of long-term outcomes. Anatomical variability in sympathetic innervation may have contributed to recurrence in some patients, underscoring the need for intraoperative methods to confirm adequate denervation.

Conclusion

Bilateral thoracoscopic sympathectomy appears to be a viable therapeutic option for patients suffering from refractory electrical storms, particularly when conventional treatments fail. The technique not only seems to extend the period of arrhythmia control but may also lead to symptomatic improvements and enhanced survival. Given the complex nature of cardiac arrhythmias, a multidisciplinary approach involving cardiologists, surgeons, and electrophysiologists is crucial for optimizing patient outcomes. Further research is warranted to establish the long-term efficacy and safety of this intervention in broader patient populations.
