Examining care aides’ perspectives and attitudes towards the adoption and sustainment of technology in care delivery: a scoping review
Authors: Muyibat Omotunde, Kathleen F Hunter, Adrian Wagg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCare aides deliver the majority of hands-on care in long-term care facilities. Despite this involvement in care, a paucity of research explores their perspectives and attitudes regarding the introduction and implementation of healthcare technology.ObjectiveThis scoping review examined and synthesised available evidence regarding care aides’ perspectives and attitudes towards healthcare technology and factors that may influence its adoption and sustainment.Eligibility criteriaLiterature published within the past 22 years that examines care aides’ perspectives and attitudes towards the use of technology in care delivery across any setting.Sources of evidenceCINAHL, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, MEDLINE, SCOPUS, PROSPERO, Web of Science, which includes ProQuest Dissertation & Theses and JBI Evidence-Based Practice databases were searched using keywords (Care, Care Aides, Caregiver, Carer, Innovation, Nursing Assistant, Support Worker, Technology) for publications in any language. Grey literature, review articles and websites were also searched.Charting methodsThe Joanna Briggs Institute method guided this scoping review. The search results were exported into a literature management tool for screening and analysis. Two authors undertook article screening. A framework for evaluating barriers and facilitators to innovation implementation and an innovation theory outlining the steps involved in innovation uptake guided the data analysis.ResultsAfter exclusions, 17 sources from 5 countries were included. One source was written in French, and the other 16 were in English. Settings ranged from home care, an academic medical centre, residential care facilities and a hospital. Findings suggest that care aides are open to technology when it supports workflow, improves care delivery, adequate training and support are provided, and they are involved in implementation. Adequate training can provide information about opportunities and challenges, facilitate clarity, a positive attitude, address misconceptions and negative attitudes care aides may have toward technology.ConclusionsThe limited available evidence suggests modifiable factors that might influence the uptake and implementation of healthcare technology from care aides’ perspectives. Further research exploring care aides’ attitudes towards health technology and their role in implementation is needed.Keywords: Caregivers, Digital Technology, Health Workforce

Background

Health technologies include devices, techniques and measures developed and used to address health challenges and improve quality of life and patient care.1 According to the WHO and the United Nations Children’s Fund, with the increasing ageing population and prevalence of chronic diseases worldwide, the number of people who may benefit from assistive technologies will likely rise beyond 3.5 billion by the year 2050.2 Technology can be used to address health inequities, facilitate health and social care access for rural populations and potentially contribute to enhanced quality care delivery.3–5 However, to be successful, implementation and sustainment require consideration of the perspectives and attitudes of potential users, involvement of appropriate stakeholders in design and provision of comprehensive education on the technology and the importance of intended use.4 6

Care aides (also known as nursing assistants, carers, personal support workers or caregivers) are essential workers who are required to have some high school education and/or complete a healthcare certificate college programme, and provide direct care to patients/residents/service users across the healthcare continuum.7 8 Their extensive contact with residents in continuing care facilities (long-term care/nursing homes) tremendously affects residents’ quality of life.9 Despite their hands-on involvement in care delivery, care aides are often excluded from the care planning process due to the hierarchical nature of teams in these facilities.9 However, empowering care aides can play a critical role in organisational effectiveness, improving service quality, work performance and increasing competence in care delivery, resulting in positive outcomes for residents.9–12

Care aides’ general perceptions and attitudes towards technology may facilitate or hinder their willingness to adopt or resist its introduction.13 Undoubtedly, in continuing care facilities, the uptake and sustainment of technologies requires the involvement of care aides.13 14 Little research provides information on care aides’ perspectives on and attitudes toward healthcare technology, which could guide decision-makers when introducing or implementing such technology. The aim of this scoping review was to examine and synthesise available evidence regarding care aides’ perspectives and attitudes toward healthcare technology uptake and factors that may influence the sustainment of technology in care delivery

Method

Patient and public involvement

None.

The scoping review was conducted using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) scoping review guidance.15 The scoping review title and questions included the population (care aides), concept (technology/innovation) and context (healthcare delivery across healthcare settings) as recommended by the JBI guidance.15 The evidence searches, screening, selection and data extraction were informed by JBI guidance and aligned with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).15 16 The protocol for this scoping review is provided (online supplemental material) and is also available online via https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/bmjopen/14/9/e079475.full.pdf

The PRISMA-ScR checklist is also provided (online supplemental material).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Sources of evidence eligible for inclusion were research studies of any method, government reports, policy documents and review articles that identified a method for the review (meta-synthesis, systematic, scoping and narrative reviews) published within 22 years that included care aides’ (also identified as formal caregivers or carers/nursing assistants, personal/support workers) perspectives and attitudes to the use of innovation/technology in the delivery of care in any setting, without language restrictions. Literature that examined care aides, patients, family caregivers and other healthcare workers’ perspectives and attitudes towards technology was included if data specific to care aides/formal caregivers were reported separately and allowed extraction of care aide specific data.

Exclusion criteria

Literature that did not include care aides’ perspectives and attitudes toward technology was excluded. Papers that included care aides, patients, family caregivers and other health workers were excluded if data specific to care aides were not reported.

Sources of evidence

A librarian scientist guided the search for evidence to be included in the scoping review. A preliminary search of CINAHL Plus (EBSCOhost) on 11 June 2023 using the keywords care aides* and care* and technology* between 2008 and 2023, yielded 388 records. The preliminary search was conducted to estimate the quantity and type of available evidence on the topic being studied. A full search involved searching CINAHL, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, MEDLINE, SCOPUS, PROSPERO, Web of Science, ProQuest Dissertation & Theses, and JBI Evidence Based Practice databases using additional keywords including attitude, caregiver, carer, innovation, nursing assistant, perspective, and support worker for articles between January 2004 and December 2024. An additional search was conducted on 20 February 2026 for articles between January 2025 and February 2026. A grey literature search was conducted by searching the websites of the Alberta Health Services, the Public Health Agency of Canada, the WHO and the Alzheimer’s Society for evidence within the last 22 years. The Aged Care Research & Industry Innovation Australia website was also searched.

Evidence screening and selection

Evidence from the database search was exported to the Covidence literature management software17; this automatically removed duplicates. Two authors (AW and MO) then screened titles, abstracts and full-text articles according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria (figure 1). Two validated translators, DeepL Translator and Google Translate, were used to translate an article written in French into English, and both translations were compared.18 19

Data extracted from the retrieved results included the author, year of publication, country, objectives, study design, participants, settings, intervention (if any), findings, study strengths and limitations (online supplemental table 1).

Findings

Available evidence is summarised in online supplemental table 1. 10 474 articles were retrieved from the database search, and 7 from citation searching. From these, 10 464 were excluded, and 17 articles were included in the review. Studies came from Canada (7), the USA (7) and one each from England, France and Sweden (figure 2). One article was written in French.20 Designs included qualitative,20–27 quasi-experimental28 and mixed-methods studies.29 30 One study included a scoping review, interviews and meetings with stakeholders,31 while another included participatory action research and a qualitative study.32 There were three dissertations33–35 and one presentation.36 Only two included evidence focused solely on care aide participants, all others reported on mixed samples, such as healthcare professionals/support staff, leadership/clients/families.30 36

Evidence by country.46

Studies occurred in home care,25 26 29 31 32 34–36 long-term care/nursing/care/residential homes20 23 27 28 30 33 and hospital/medical centres.21 24 A wide variety of technologies was studied; a sleep tracking device,21 an interactive voice assistant,22 mobile applications,30 32 36 digital tools—computers, smartphones & tablets,20 a bladder ultrasound scanner,28 an automated pain detection system,33 a smartphone with augmented and virtual reality training,23 a mechanical lift,24 an electronic medication administration record,25 information communication technology,29 35 a tablet-based app26 and virtual reality technology.27

The CFIR domains, innovation, inner setting and individuals were covered in all studies. One source36 did not include the implementation process. One study23 specifically addressed a construct (policies) within the outer setting. Rogers’ innovation characteristics identified were compatibility,21 22 26 31 35 36 complexity,20 24 25 29 observability,20 23–25 27 28 32 relative advantage20–23 25–36 and trialability.30 32 Innovation and social systems elements were identified across all included evidence, and two studies identified time, but relating to implementation, rather than the rate of innovation adoption, as described by Rogers.24 25 37 One study identified the communication channel.22

Care aides’ perspectives and attitudes towards technology uptake and sustainment

Given that nearly all included studies were qualitative, and the analysis was largely qualitative, the review question was answered qualitatively rather than quantitatively. Findings varied with the study’s focus and the type of technology. A strong positive correlation between perceived usefulness and the intention to use a technology app was reported in one study, and high acceptance of the technology among care aides was observed.30 In one study, attitudes to technology, for example, sceptical and negative attitudes towards tablet devices, changed to be more positive and receptive after receiving training and simulations.29 This study reported initially negative attitudes, which later changed to positive.29 Attitudinal change was helped by hands-on experience, education, peer coaching, access to resources, as well as explanation of the benefit and intended clinical goals of the technology.13 23 28 31

Factors influencing uptake and sustainment of technology

Technology such as interactive voice assistants was perceived as helpful for care coordination and provision.22 In one study, each technology was also perceived as having the potential to facilitate accountability, enhance communication, make home care work easier and potentially improve clients’ quality of life,31 teamwork, access to educational resources34 and reduce travel time.35 In several studies, technology was perceived as making work easier, helping achieve work goals, providing care-related information, coordinating care, calming residents with dementia and contributing to helping relationships.20 22 27 30 31 However, in one study technology’s ability to enhance communication was hindered by network concerns, particularly in rural areas.34 A lack of budget, inadequate training and privacy concerns were perceived as hindering technology uptake and sustainment in three studies.21 22 24 In another study, nurse assistants perceived electronic medication administration records as highly demanding because they had to perform tasks within time frames, contributing to work-related stress.25 In contrast, another study found that a web-based app allowing access to an electronic medical record system was perceived as helpful in addressing workflow.30

The CFIR and Rogers’ theory facilitated the identification of factors influencing technology uptake and sustainment, which are organised into the following subheadings.37 38

Training

Benefits of training were highlighted, such as facilitating interactive learning and contributing to positive and receptive attitudinal change.23 29 Additionally, a study highlighted learning needs satisfaction and work improvement through access to educational content on technological devices.26 Studies highlighted the need for adequate training for technology implementation.20 21 29 31

Staff involvement

Studies emphasised the importance of active staff involvement in the planning and implementation of technology by seeking their insights and incorporating their perspectives and feedback, thereby fostering a sense of value and commitment to successful implementation.21 In addition, staff perspectives and preferences need to be incorporated into technology design and development to support the workforce in care delivery.31 Furthermore, little or no staff involvement in technology implementation was associated with challenges, fears and resistance, and compromise of technology acceptance.20 35 Therefore, a thoughtful and deliberate approach to involve end-users such as care aides in technology development and implementation is recommended.35

Compatibility with workflow

Technology compatibility with existing workflows was identified as a key factor/characteristic essential for successful diffusion of innovation.21 37 Technologies that integrate into routine practices such as monitoring sleep patterns without disturbing patients were viewed more favourably because they support non-intrusive care, enhance workflow and productivity.21 Furthermore, a technology (prototype’s chat feature) was found to be compatible with communication needs.26

Addressing concerns

It is vital that concerns or ethical considerations raised are addressed, such as privacy and security of patients’ information, to facilitate adoption and sustainment.21 33 Additionally, when technologies address concerns raised by end-users such as care aides, this increases the likelihood of acceptance.35

Consideration of context

A study highlighted that attention must be paid to the context in which technologies are introduced.20 Studies highlighted the importance of considering contextual factors in facilitating technology uptake and sustainment, including policies, physical space in which the technology would be used, its impact on care aide–client relationships, development of more privacy-enabling, user-friendly and less intrusive devices.21–24 29 Furthermore, one study reported that care aides would discontinue use of a technological device if they perceived it negatively affected care or their connection with clients,29 while another highlighted the importance of considering the aide–client relationship when developing technology.22

Perception

This scoping review revealed various perspectives among care aides regarding technology. Technology was perceived as making home care work easier, facilitating communication, improving transparency and facilitating interactive learning.22 23 31 Lost or stolen devices were perceived as potentially threatening the privacy of clients’ data.34 A lack of stable internet connections, perceptions that technology may replace care aides, discomfort and unfamiliarity with technology were perceived as barriers negatively influencing the uptake and sustainment of technology.27 33

key points

Understanding care aides’ perspectives and attitudes is essential for technology uptake in care delivery.

Successful technology implementation requires end-user involvement in designing the technology, understanding its benefits, addressing its challenges and providing training and support for care aides who use it.

Ensuring that technological solutions fit into existing work routines is essential.

Discussion

Findings from this scoping review highlighted care aides’ perspectives and attitudes toward technology, as well as factors influencing technology adoption and sustainment. Understanding care aides’ attitudes and perspectives toward technology, addressing the technology’s relevance to their work in the specific setting, and providing educational support before and during implementation are all important. The potential to engage care aides, as end users in the development of technology, remains challenging and requires further investigation.

Attitude to technology adoption is clearly important in the exploration of caregivers’ attitudes toward robot technology.39 Trust, as a positive attitude, influences the intention to work with technology, highlighting the importance of training end-users before its introduction.39 Moreover, job experience, educational status, knowledge of health technology and computer training were associated with positive attitudes towards health technology.40 Efforts to build a strong, positive attitude through educating users on the relevance of the technology to their work are required, all of which require time, planning and adequate budget.41

The findings highlight factors influencing the uptake and sustainment of technology such training, staff involvement, workflow compatibility, addressing concerns, contextual considerations and perceptions. Training is crucial in the implementation and adoption processes, and in mitigating resistance to technology.3 4 Staff involvement enables the collection of meaningful information to develop suitable technology.42 Given the importance of staff involvement, it is important to recognise that care aides who have the most direct and frequent contact with residents in long-term care, a healthcare setting, are uniquely positioned to influence and monitor care processes; hence, they should be actively involved in quality improvement initiatives and innovative approaches.43 However, such engagement is time-consuming and can put pressure on the budget.42 Likewise, staff involvement is essential for understanding how technology influences workflows and for identifying contextual considerations such as ethical and/or environmental considerations that should inform technology design and support its successful integration.42 For workflow compatibility, it is crucial that technological innovation is compatible with existing tasks and similar innovations to facilitate adoption and sustainment.6 Considering contextual factors facilitates the evaluation of the technology’s suitability for the intended environment and context, and allows for the identification of difficulties and the addressing of concerns users may experience.42 Furthermore, technology adoption is a complex and evolving process shaped by individuals’ perceptions of technology, which play a key role in informing implementation strategies and adoption outcomes.2 6

In a review and analysis of factors influencing the adoption of rehabilitation technologies, key themes were identified: knowledge, design, characteristics and circumstances, a person-centred approach, and the healthcare ecosystem.44 The knowledge theme includes understanding available technology and how to address technological problems, which can be likened to the training and addressing concerns factor identified in the findings section of this scoping review, which is crucial for adoption and sustainment.21 33 35 44 The design theme encompassed features that address specific user needs, workflow support and integration into everyday routine, all of which are crucial for technology sustainment and closely align with contextual considerations and workflow compatibility factors.44 The characteristics and circumstances theme is described as factors influencing technology, while a person-centred approach theme encompasses technology adaptation and preferences of end-users, which can be described as similar to the staff involvement factor that encourages exploration of the perspectives and preferences of staff in technology design and support for the workforce in care delivery.31 44 The healthcare ecosystem theme encompasses organisational operational support, strategic direction and policies, and regulatory & funding structures.44 This theme also incorporates the perspectives of stakeholders that shape technology adoption and sustainment, paralleling the perception factor identified in the findings, which plays a critical role in technology uptake and continued use27 33 44

There was a wide variation in the degree to which potentially influential domains were addressed in the body of literature. One of the strengths of this review is the use of the framework and theory to guide analysis. The CFIR and Rogers’ diffusion of innovation theory also facilitated the identification of factors influencing innovation uptake and sustainment, which are organised into subheadings in the findings section.37 38 For example, one of the factors identified, compatibility with workflow closely aligns with the compatibility construct in the inner setting domain of the CFIR, which is also a characteristic of innovation in Rogers’ theory.21 37 38 In addition, the individual CFIR domain and social system element of Rogers’ theory facilitated the identification of staff involvement as a factor influencing uptake and sustainment of technology.20 21 31 35 37 38

This review identified only two articles that focused solely on care aides, highlighting a paucity of research on their perspectives and attitudes. In settings such as continuing care facilities, the successful implementation of health technology is likely dependent on care aides, and their perspectives should be actively considered.42 43 45 Hence, there remains a need for further research examining their perspectives and attitudes towards health technologies for care delivery.

Future direction

Most of the data came from a few English-speaking countries. Future research should include a wider geographic and cultural range to enhance the generalisability of findings. Expanding the scope to incorporate evidence from underrepresented regions will help identify cultural, organisational and systemic factors influencing technology adoption and sustainment. Such efforts are essential for developing globally relevant strategies that support care aides in delivering high-quality care.

Conclusions

Although care aides use health technologies in care delivery, studies focusing exclusively on their attitudes and perspectives toward technology uptake remain limited. This gap is notable given their direct involvement in everyday technology use and their central role in supporting clients across diverse care settings. Findings from this review indicate that care aides’ perspectives and attitudes may significantly influence the success of technology implementation. Understanding these viewpoints is essential, as they can affect everyday use and ultimately determine whether technologies become integrated and sustained within routine care practices. Taken as a whole, this review synthesises key areas relevant to care aides in the development, introduction and implementation of healthcare technology.
