Engineering cardiac regeneration using stem cells: Cellular sources, differentiation signatures, targeted delivery, and functional recovery
Authors: Sonia Gupta, Md Faiyazuddin, Vrinda Gupta, Rajni Tanwar, Pooja Rani, Wesam Taher Almagharbeh, Neeraj Choudhary, Dinesh Kumar, Thomas J. Webster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, and the limited regenerative capacity of the adult heart continues to delay functional recovery, resulting in myocardial injury. Stem cell–based therapies offer a promising avenue for cardiac repair through mechanisms that include cardiomyogenic differentiation, paracrine signaling, angiogenic stimulation, and immunomodulation. Although pluripotent stem cells, mesenchymal stem cells, and cardiac progenitor cells have demonstrated significant benefits in preclinical models, clinical translation has yielded modest functional improvements, mainly due to poor cell retention, electrical and structural immaturity of transplanted cells, immune-mediated clearance, and concerns regarding tumorigenicity and arrhythmogenicity. Therapeutic efficacy largely derives from extracellular vesicles, secreted microRNAs, pro-survival cytokines, and other bioactive substances released by cells. Direct replacement of cardiomyocytes is challenging because the host tissue environment limits the acceptance and stable integration of exogenous cells. Novel engineered cardiac patches, biomaterial-assisted cell delivery, gene-edited progenitor transplantation, exosome therapeutics, and cell-free therapy are improving safety, the plausibility of cell transplantation, and the precision of tissue targeting. Despite those biotech improvements, there are still significant obstacles to restoring permanent, clinically meaningful cardiac function. Regenerative stem cell and cell-free technologies will have the greatest potential for myocardial repair if integrative approaches to regenerative biology, biomaterial engineering, and immunomodulation are used.

Introduction

The adult mammalian heart has extremely limited intrinsic regenerative capacity. Earlier hypotheses proposed the presence of resident “cardiac stem cells” capable of regenerating the myocardium; however, contemporary evidence indicates that such populations contribute minimally to cardiomyocyte replacement [1]. Current regenerative strategies therefore focus on pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes, cardiac progenitors, engineered tissues, and paracrine mechanisms rather than endogenous cardiac stem cell–mediated regeneration [1]. Early preclinical evidence showed that transplanted cells may differentiate into cardiomyocytes and restructure the myocardium. However, despite years of strong experimental findings, clinical translation has similarly led to only significant and usually temporary benefits in cardiac performance, and the majority of trials have reported the improvement to be less than 25-50% of left ventricular ejection fraction [2]. These meager findings have shown a desperate discrepancy between biological possibility and clinical practice and have challenged the original paradigm of a direct cardiomyocyte replacement [3].

It is currently becoming clear that the therapeutic response to many stem cell therapies does not occur by long-term engraftment or by regeneration of the myocardium, but by an indirect, paracrine and immunomodulatory action [4]. The unsteady survival of the transplanted cells, electrical and structural immaturity of the transplanted cardiomyocytes, immune-mediated clearance and arrhythmogenic risk and tumorigenicity restrict the functioning integration in the long term [5]. Additionally, these differences have been caused by the differing sourcing of cells, standards of manufacturing, and provision, which have resulted in different and non-reproducible clinical outcomes [6]. Thus, the field has become less naive in terms of cell replacement therapy, and more technologically robust, including engineered tissue constructs, biomaterial-assisted delivery, gene-edited progenitors, and cell-free extracellular vesicle-based therapies which are likely to be safer, more specific, and scalable [7].

The presence of these historical problems in the translation of stem cell-based cardiac regeneration is enough to warrant critical re-thinking. The present review does not provide yet another descriptive overview, but rather it is a controlled evaluation of the comparative merits and demerits of the major stem cell populations, quantitative analysis of the findings of the important clinical trials, and discussions of the mechanistic factors involved in the failure of therapeutic endeavors. This article integrates biology knowledge, clinical facts, and innovative bioengineering interventions to discuss the most probable approaches to achieve substantial myocardial repair, and also gives a roadmap as to how regenerative cardiology should advance over the next generation.

Types of stem cells used in cardiac regeneration

The repairing and replacing of damaged heart tissue has been a significant focus of research. An even greater focus of research has been gaining attention with the development of new practices in heart tissue repair and the development of new therapeutic practices as the heart is one of the body's most central organs and has a massive impact on the body's overall health. Using a variety of methods to assist in cell transplantation and tissue recovery, cells can repair heart tissue. Each has great potential to assist in the recovery of heart repair and tissue recovery [8]. Heart tissue and stem cell therapy have little to no research concerning therapeutic potential. Earlier cell populations (such as c-kit+ and Sca-1+ cells) were proposed as cardiac stem cells; however, lineage-tracing studies have shown that these cells contribute negligibly to new cardiomyocyte formation. Their therapeutic relevance is now understood to arise primarily from paracrine signaling and vascular support rather than direct myocardial regeneration as shown in Table 1. Cardiac regeneration is complex and highly diverse, as it relies on the ability to die and regenerate cardiac tissue and to repair damage and regenerate cells within cardiac tissue. Cellular regeneration also occurs in cardiac tissue itself. Other stem cells can die while others can regenerate cardiac tissues to repair damage and construct cells within the cardiac tissues. The therapy has been highly focused, rehabilitating the cardiovascular diseases stemming from cell damage and degeneration of the heart. Embryonic stem cells

Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) are the beginning of any form of life in the human embryo as they are specialized cells that are formed by the inner cell mass of a blastocyst, having the capabilities of developing into any cells in the human body, including cells in the human heart as seen in Fig. 2 and in the case of repairing damaged heart tissues, as in the case of a heart attack (myocardial infarction), they can be transformed into cardiac cells such as cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and smooth muscle cells that are crucial in fully functional heart tissue [17]. Through the use of specific groups of growth-stimulating factors and signaling they can direct the differentiation into specific cells. These factors recapitulate embryonic cardiac stem cell development. The stem cells derived in this instance can contract rhythmically and integrate with other cells within the heart that are in the native developmental stage [18]. However, in clinical practice, the use of embryonic stem cells is subject to strict guidelines due to ethical concerns about using embryos in their formation. There is also ethical case scenarios associated with the death of stem cells if they are not placed inside a body. Because these types of stem cells are not neutral to the body, they may die or face immune rejection [19]. The use of these types of ESCs is to advance stem cell techniques, immune matching, and gene-editing techniques. In other cases, the inner stem cells are valuable in creating a model that can support such tissues. Adult stem cells

Somatic stem cells (SSCs) used in cardiac repair gradually regenerate damaged heart muscle stem cells [21]. Adult stem cells repair and regenerate tissues and organs through differentiation and paracrine mechanisms. SSCs in tissues (such as the heart, bones, and fat) or stem cells in the heart and blood system, include c-kit+ and Sca-1+ stem cells [22]. They assist cardiac repair primarily through paracrine secretion of angiogenic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-fibrotic factors that modulate the local microenvironment, rather than through direct cardiomyocyte differentiation [23]. While it is true that adult stem cells have a more limited differentiation ability compared to embryonic stem cells, adult stem cells have more advantages regarding ethical dilemmas and have a lower chance of tumor formation, allowing them to be more clinically applicable [24]. In addition, obtaining adult stem cells from patients undergoing the procedure during a trial reduces the risk of immune rejection. Among several adult stem cells used in clinical trials, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and cardiac progenitor cells have been able to improve cardiac function and reduce heart scarring in patients after myocardial infarction, though the clinical outcomes have not been consistent [25]. Current efforts aim to combine regenerative medicine, genetic engineering, and other therapeutic interventions to improve adult stem cell function in patients, achieving more consistent and effective results in myocardial tissue repair.

Induced pluripotent stem cells

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) have emerged as game-changing cells for cardiac regeneration as they have the potential to behave similarly to embryonic stem cells, but without the ethical challenges posed by embryonic stem cells themselves. iPSCs are formed by reprogramming adult somatic cells, such as skin fibroblasts or blood cells, into a pluripotent state by introducing specific transcriptional factors, including Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc (Fig. 4). Consequently, these cells have the potential to repair the heart by differentiating into cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and other cardiovascular cells. In cardiac regeneration, iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes have been shown to contract rhythmically, integrate into host tissue, and restore cardiac function in numerous preclinical models of myocardial infarction. A significant benefit of iPSCs is the possibility of autologous cell therapy, in which cells for treatment are taken from the patient, thereby avoiding immune rejection. Furthermore, iPSCs have great potential as tools for disease modeling, drug testing, and the analysis of the genetic underpinnings of cardiac diseases. The cell reprogramming process is genetically unstable, and cells may not fully differentiate, leading to uncontrolled proliferation and tumor formation. For instance, improvements in the reprogramming process and in the safety and efficacy of cardiomyocyte differentiation, cell purity, cardiomyocyte survival, and engraftment have been pursued. Patients with cardiovascular diseases can safely undergo cell treatments and use iPSCs as therapy [26].

Mesenchymal stem cells

Due to the ability to differentiate into numerous cell types and their significantly strong paracrine effects, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have therapeutic prospects. The most potent adult stem cells have been especially used for the regeneration of the heart [27]. The tissues from which MSCs are most frequently obtained include bone marrow, adipose tissues, umbilical cords, and other mesenchymal tissues. MSCs have demonstrated a potential to enhance cardiac function after myocardial injury [28]. MSCs have limited cardiomyocyte differentiation, but their support of heart repair is due to the secretion of bioactive molecules that aid in the angiogenic process, decrease inflammation, and prevent fibrosis in addition to sustaining the survival of indigenous cardiac cells. These paracrine mechanisms evoke the spatial microenvironment that enhances tissue repair and sustains functional recovery [29]. Because MSCs can modulate immune responses, they can be used in allogeneic transplantation with a low risk of rejection. The application of MSCs in clinical trials has resulted in improved left ventricular functionality, decreased cardiac tissue scarring, and improved the quality of life in ischemic heart disease and heart failure patients. MSCs have a low immunogenic profile, easy recovery, and safety, which is the reason they are one of the most researched areas in regenerative cardiology [30]. Cardiac progenitor cells

Cardiac progenitor cells (CPCs) identified by markers such as c-kit and Sca-1 were historically believed to regenerate the myocardium. However, current evidence indicates that their contribution to cardiomyocyte formation is minimal, and the observed benefits in clinical studies are largely attributed to paracrine and remodeling effects. CPCs are found in small specialized areas of the adult heart muscle (myocardium) and are defined by surface markers c-kit+, Sca-1+, and Isl 1 [31,32]. CPCs assist with heart repair both through their differentiation as well as through the delivery of paracrine signals that promote neovascularization, apoptosis inhibition, and tissue remodeling after injury of the heart [27]. CPCs, unlike embryonic or induced pluripotent stem cells, are already somewhat differentiated down a cardiac lineage, which decreases the chances of tumor formation and improves the chances that the cells will functionally integrate into the remaining heart tissue. The SCIPIO and CADUCEUS clinical trials have evaluated the potential of CPCs and have demonstrated, in heart disease patients, a reduction in infarct size as well as an enhancement of cardiac function [33,34]. There are a number of continuing challenges, such as the limited number of CPCs, advanced age, or disease-associated reduced regenerative capabilities, and problems with post-transplant delivery and cell survival, but these challenges are still in the realm of cardiac stem cells in cardiac regeneration as shown in Table 2. The evaluation of different stem cell populations for cardiac regeneration has been studied, but there is still a disparity in their therapeutic performance [42]. Cardiomyocytes from ESCs and iPSCs have the most extraordinary capacity for cardiac myocyte differentiation, but also have the most significant challenges in regard to tumorigenicity, poor electrical maturity, and arrhythmias [43]. Adult stem cells, like MSCs, have a track record of providing paracrine cell impact and unique benefits, but also suffer a lack of differentiation into cardiac myocytes and a slight, transient, and modest increase in left ventricle ejection fraction of 2 to 5% in multiple clinical trial settings [44]. CPC-based therapies in SCIPIO and CADUCEUS had a good start, but have controversies in cell type and the type of clinical benefits from cardiomyocyte regeneration. These factors for the opposed findings is a case for clinically useful isolation techniques, concrete lineage tracking, and consistent outcomes to reproduce clinical endpoints [42].

Mechanism-driven view of stem cell–mediated cardiac repair

Cardiac research focuses on the mechanisms underlying cardiac repair. We must first clarify the functional requirements of cardiomyocytes to assess whether the absence of these characteristics precludes the use of such cells as a direct cardiac source for repair. Cardiomyocytes derived from iPSCs or embryonic stem cells possess no electrical coupling when analyzed in vitro. Electrophysiological parameters indicated immature cells with immature action potential features. Mechanical stimulation could improve these parameters. Resident cardiac progenitor cells were isolated from human heart specimens obtained during transplantation, thereby confirming their presence in adult hearts [45]. Other cells present in the heart with similar functions described currently are epicardium-derived cells. Isolated specimens exhibited differentiation into cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and cardiac fibroblasts. The presence of such progenitor cells suggests that microtears in the heart are a persistent defect that requires replacement by new cardiomyocytes throughout adult life [46,47].

Resident cardiac progenitor cells exhibit paracrine signaling. They release microvesicles containing fibroblast growth factor, Wnt, and microRNA. These vesicles can influence neighboring non-proliferative cardiomyocytes via Wnt signaling, promoting cardiomyocyte proliferation, or influence structural remodeling by participating in extracellular matrix remodeling. They have also been shown to promote angiogenesis and inhibit cardiomyocyte death. The ability to modulate deficient cardiomyocyte plasticity has led to investigations into factors that regulate cardiomyocyte proliferation. The attenuated response to cellular damage appears to be due to multiple factors, including insufficient mitogens and the presence of cell-cycle inhibitors. Specimens isolated from a 94-year-old patient exhibited increased proliferation upon exposure to these factors. Recent studies indicate that the primary benefit of most clinically tested stem cell types is mediated by paracrine signaling rather than direct cardiomyocyte replacement [50]. Secreted EVs, miRNAs (miR-21, miR-146a), and pro-angiogenic cytokines (VEGF, HGF) modulate inflammation, enhance fibroblast remodeling, and promote neovascularization. This mechanistic shift underscores that stem cell therapy may act as an immune-modulatory and microenvironment-modifying intervention rather than a proper regenerative replacement strategy [51].

Cell replacement therapy

This section focuses specifically on direct cell replacement concepts, distinct from paracrine and immunomodulatory mechanisms discussed below. For decades, advancements in medical technology have dramatically improved the management of heart diseases. However, when surgical revascularization or medical therapy fails, heart transplantation remains the only choice. Cardiac transplantation is limited mainly by donor availability [52]. Therefore, various alternative therapeutic strategies like induced pluripotency have been developed to treat and repair heart injury. Guided by a desire to mimic the natural process of development, in which stem cells give rise to various cell lineages, cell replacement therapy has been proposed [53]. Healthcare scientists hypothesize that transplanting non-embryonic stem cells into infarcted or diseased hearts could rescue cardiomyopathic hearts by replacing lost myocytes and restoring function. Embryonic stem cells have pluripotency and can differentiate into any cell lineage. As such, these pluripotent cells may be ideal candidates for cardiac repair therapy; however, profound ethical, immunological, and tumorigenic implications would hinder their human application. Adult stem cells derived from non-embryonic tissues like umbilical cord blood or dental pulp, as well as germline stem cells isolated from bone marrow, could circumvent these limitations. However, these stem cells are often restricted in their ability to regenerate [54,55].

The introduction of mice with the knockdown Oct4 gene, cells derived from the inner cell mass of pre-implantation blastocysts, and the subsequent development of techniques to induce pluripotency by transient expression of a few transcription factors in somatic tissue represented the discovery of induced pluripotent stem cells. Together with potential unlimited growth in vitro, the ability to generate patient-specific stem cell lines including myocytes, the potential to rescue myocyte loss after heart failure, and the ability to modify the electrophysiological properties of diseased hearts through transplantation of genetically altered iPS cell-derived cardiomyocytes with altered sodium or potassium channels, iPS cells represent an excellent candidate resource for the treatment of cardiovascular diseases. Hence, owing to their regenerative capacity, stem cells are unique tools that could be harnessed for cell-based therapeutic strategies in the future [56,57].

Paracrine signaling

In contrast to replacement therapy, this section discusses paracrine signaling as the dominant therapeutic mechanism. As initially proposed, the potential beneficial effects of stem cells in the cardiac microenvironment are mediated by paracrine signaling, in which stem cell secretomes influence surrounding cells and promote their reparative activities. Putative stem cell factors that alleviate cardiac damage, promote angiogenesis and arteriogenesis, prevent apoptosis, and enhance cardiomyocyte or vascular repair and remodeling have been widely studied and include cytokines, growth factors, chemokines, and miRNAs. However, their exact composition and spatiotemporal release profiles remain unresolved and warrant more detailed characterization of the paracrine-specific secretome [58]. In addition to the cardioprotective secretomes, the adverse effects induced by stem cells, also known as “side effects”, have been overlooked. Indeed, this detrimental process is intended to cause the synthesis of myofibroblast drivers, ECM components, and ECM-remodeling factors, thereby initiating and orchestrating subsequent remodeling events, including fibrosis, chamber dilation, and adverse electrical activity. This may contribute to the loss of contractile forces in the late stages of repair. Moreover, only a few works have demonstrated the existence of factors that could better modulate paracrine signaling to achieve reduced undesirable activities or boost favorable effects, such as the use of chemical compounds or compounds that modulate this pathway in mature cardiomyocytes or fibroblasts [59]. It should be noted that, depending on the local microenvironment, stem cells can adapt and switch between “therapeutic” and “adverse” phenotypes, which may also involve sequential phases during cardiac repair. Growing experimental evidence indicates that activating the paracrine activity of stem cells is an alternative, and potentially superior, approach to cell therapeutics. However, the manipulation of stem cell-derived factors that promote secretion is still limited, and bioengineering biomimetic delivery systems for their release would seem an attractive solution [60].

Tissue regeneration and remodeling

Stem cells and their derivatives have been shown to ingraft in donor tissues and induce tissue remodeling through the development of functional tissue structures aligned with the host. It was demonstrated that ES-derived CMs implanted as cell clusters into cryoinjured rat hearts survived for the long term, electrically coupled to recipient cardiomyocytes, and formed tissue structures expressing cardiac proteins that were structurally and functionally more normal than hearts implanted with cell clusters that did not ingraft. Similar structural and functional improvements were observed when hESC-CM cell patches were used to cover a myocardial infarction in rats, even when the hESC-CM patches were electrically dissociated, indicating that direct electrical coupling with host cardiomyocytes is not necessary for successful remodeling. hESC-CM patches have also been shown to survive longer and to remodel more successfully than either skeletal muscle or fibroblast patches [61,62].

In rodent models of myocardial infarction, EPC-, iPSC-, or MSC-derived CMs have shown greater short-term remodeling effects than support cells, at least in part due to their paracrine activity, but without the in vivo coupling and tissue maturation that would support longer-term remodeling. In a pig model of myocardial infarction, CMs derived from either ESCs or iPSCs showed a greater long-term benefit on remodeling and cardiac function. Although the differences in remodeling have been attributed to the greater numbers of implanted cells derived from human pluripotent stem cell lines, the physiological and biochemical improvements were also correlated with the expression of cardiac-specific proteins, as well as with evidence of CMs coupling with recipient cardiomyocytes for both ESCs and iPSCs [53,63].

Immune modulation

The immune response plays a critical role in tissue repair and restoration of homeostasis after injury, including in the heart. The immune system can act both protectively and detrimentally during this process, depending on the duration and type of the induced immune response. In the acute phase, pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines recruit neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocyte cells to the site of lesion [64]. This acute inflammatory response creates a hostile microenvironment for resident and infiltrating cell types, mainly through inducing oxidative stress and releasing cytotoxic cytokines. After phagocytosis of necrotic tissue, macrophages undergo a phenotypic switch from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype to secrete cytokines, release repair proteolytic enzymes, and secrete angiogenic factors that favor neovascularization and scar formation by epithelial and fibroblast cells. Together with transient infiltrating lymphocytes, macrophages confer a protective and pro-healing response during the subacute and chronic phases of repair processes [65]. Currently, the presence of activated macrophages can be interpreted differently because, during a persistent pathological immune response, alternatively activated M2 macrophages can secrete high levels of matrix metalloproteinases, leading to enhanced cardiac necrotic lesions and increased fibrosis.

Due to the dual role of the immune response in cardiac repair, there is ongoing research to explore the potential of different therapeutic approaches, such as immunomodulation using monoclonal antibodies, inflammation-targeting nanocarriers, and pro-regenerative therapies that aim to promote an anti-inflammatory phenotype and maintain its activity to facilitate cardiac repair. The potential of stem cell therapy as a new approach for cardiac repair goes well beyond its first angiogenic/destructive effects due to paracrine factor release as cardiac repair is a complex process that also benefits from modulation of the host's immune response [66]. From the perspective of post-infarct immunomodulation, the central stem cell-based therapies' immunosuppressive effects, both from the donor stem cells and paracrine factors, are also be beneficial. These cells and factors promote a local immune-blunted microenvironment capable of down-regulating the inflammatory response, thereby avoiding chronic inflammatory phase and improving cardiac repair [67].

Delivery methods for stem cells

Several modes of delivery have been evaluated for stem cell injection into the myocardium. These delivery methods are intrinsically linked to the cellular characteristics of the specific stem cell population and to the desired clinical application. Various techniques may confer advantages or pose risks that affect the therapeutic potential of stem cells and, ultimately, the successful recovery of cardiac function.

Intravenous administration

As the simplest and most commonly used mode of delivery, SDF-1 can quickly home to ischemic areas of the myocardium via a honed gradient following injection. This homing effect and function, driven by both intrinsic and extrinsic mechanisms, may be limited by the physical characteristics of the stem cells themselves and by the underlying pathology of CVD. There is also a possibility of embolization into the microvasculature of the lungs; conversely, a non-invasive modality is advantageous, as it can be optimized for heart function and homeostasis. When given multiple times (with restrictions), there are also reduced risks of infection, and costs are also lower [68,69].

Intracoronary injection

Intracoronary delivery of stem cells directly into ischemic regions of the heart enables a more concentrated delivery of stem cells compared to conventional intravenous injection, and can test the stem cells’ properties ex vivo before injection. Also considering ethics and the location of previous samples, this mode of injection would generally require a closed-chest surgical approach. Only months later, clinicians were able to administer stem cells intracoronarily to patients with CAD, with improvements in LVEF. The risk of achieving an arrhythmogenic effect may be exacerbated by the actual method and technical difficulty of preventing retrograde flow into the aorta [70].

Intracoronary injection

In addition to the systemic approach, a transcoronary selective approach has also been developed. Cell delivery via a catheter directly into the area of infarction via intracoronary injection is the most straightforward and direct delivery method [71]. It can be performed quickly during or after coronary angiography, and its main advantage is the ability to impact not only infarct-related arteries but also to viable collateral supply channels, which may ensure adequate blood supply to the area of necrosis. The main disadvantage of this technique is that it is difficult to achieve selective blood flow within the area supplied by the coronary branch to which the cells are injected. This concerns both the injection performed in the immediate postinfarction period and up to several days to weeks after the event [72]. The use of pre- or postconditioning techniques in animals and humans has enabled the creation of reproducible conditions that prevent or minimize cell washout from the target zone. Materials that will allow for selective accumulation include adenosine and its derivatives, hypoxia-sensitive agents administered at moderate doses, cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitors, and d-α-tocopherol polyethylene glycol 1000 succinate. Using these compounds alone or in combination increased the effectiveness of stem cell therapy in animal studies. Nevertheless, cell retention averaged merely about 30–50%, with no difference between various cell types [73].

Human studies showed that intracoronary injection was able to restore myocardial structure and function, but only in the group of patients treated soon after the event, and repeat angiography showed the absence of patency in reperfused arteries after months. Factors influencing the effect of intracoronary injection included delay from the onset of ischemia to treatment, comorbidities, including diabetes, renal disease, and impaired left ventricular function. Despite these unfavorable factors, there were some positive effects on myocardial function. Clearly, the treatment is effective because infarct healing is observed in patients’ months after cell treatment if cells are injected into the arteries regularly [74].

Direct myocardial injection

Direct myocardial injection is among the earliest delivery modalities explored in preclinical studies and clinical trials of stem cells for cardiomyocyte regeneration. Its earliest application was in the injecting of skeletal muscle satellite cells as a potential cellular therapy for dystrophin-deficient skeletal muscle and cardiomyopathy [70]. In a cardiac model, the direct injection of bone marrow-derived stem cells into infarcted sites in vivo was first reported. Although this study was not performed with the heart in mind, subsequent feedback that direct myocardial injection would be needed to treat, has been validated in numerous studies utilizing a variety of adult, embryonic, and pluripotent stem cell types and animal models [75].

Although this method has intrinsic limitations, particularly in its relative efficacy compared with other methods, direct myocardial injection is likely to retain an essential role in bridging early proof-of-principle experiments with pluripotent-derived cardiac cell types to the clinical use of cardiac regeneration therapies. Additionally, with respect to the use of cardiac cell types derived from pluripotent stem cells, direct myocardial injection is likely to remain a central strategy for the earliest clinical trials since, when capitalizing on the natural developmental progression of the cardiac and vascular tissue, these cells are pre-loaded into biodegradable hydrogels before implantation into infarcted heart tissue [76,77]. The deploy-and-forget feature of this approach arguably brings the least risk of complications and follows a well-established early clinical precedent with respect to surgical coronary artery bypass grafting.

Scaffold-based delivery

Biodegradable scaffolds providing quick and stable stem cell retention at the healing site, favorable microenvironments at the implant site and sustained release of bioactive molecules over a long period of time are viewed as an alternative to needle injections of stem cells. Scaffolds serve as a platform to support 3D cell delivery, instead of the 2D cell spreading achieved with syringe injection techniques [78]. Using cell- or protein-seeded porous scaffolds with suitable mechanical properties at either the epicardial surface or within the injury site can combine the advantages of 3D cell loading and minimize material effects for needle-injection cell delivery. It is thought that reducing scaffold-column weight and ensuring adequate mechanical properties for the scaffold-column implant are essential to achieve optimal scaffold implantation without significant biomechanical consequences. Tissue swelling during the post-implantation phase of a biodegradable material within the heart cavity can cause side effects related to local mechanical unloading in the acutely-loaded soft tissue structures over the longer term [79].

Furthermore, cardiac cell sheets can be incorporated into biodegradable conduits or other biodegradable natural structures to enhance myocardial healing within the conduit without long- or mid-term side effects from scaffold presence in the heart after vessel healing. However, biodegradable scaffolds that enable high stem cell retention and long-term structural support have not yet been clinically validated. Such stem cell-collagen scaffold constructs may show significant improvement in ejection fraction with a proper study design. However, the findings must be validated in a larger cohort and in the long term. Given that one of the challenges in current cardiac regenerative medicine is developing evidence-based treatment standards, we believe that cell-based constructs with natural scaffolds loaded with matrix-derived signals may represent a promising alternative strategy in this field [80].

Heart failure therapy

In patients with chronic heart failure, cardiac stem cells have been shown to improve left ventricular ejection fraction by regenerating heart tissue and modulating the local environment to reduce inflammation and fibrosis. This offers a long-term alternative to symptomatic management using drugs or mechanical devices [82].

Personalized drug testing and disease modeling

Researchers are able to generate patient-specific cardiac cells to model heart diseases in vitro and complete individualize drug screening for heart disease by reprogramming patient-derived cells into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). Pediatric cardiac regeneration

Regenerative stem/progenitor cell therapy could improve survival and the quality-of-life outcomes of surgical repairs and the management of chronic heart disease in children with congenital heart defects and children with heart disease related to cardiomyopathy. This therapy could be especially beneficial to children as their growing hearts are more likely to respond to the regenerative signals of the therapy [85,86].

Minimally invasive regenerative therapy

Stem cells can be delivered through an injection via a catheter and are able to go straight to the area of damage. This is a quicker, less risky alternative to open-heart surgeries which is very useful for the older generation or more at-risk patients [87].

Immune and paracrine effects

Along with replacing cells, stem/progenitor cells also have an effect in sending growth factors and cytokines to the surrounding cells which boosts the heart's repair processes through immune system alteration and oxidative stress reduction [88].

Challenges in stem cell therapy for cardiac regeneration

Conventional therapeutic approaches for damaged cardiac tissue, such as implantable devices, surgical revascularization procedures, and heart transplantation, offer survival benefits but do not restore function fully [95]. Although stem cell therapy has great potential in cardiac regeneration and repair, several challenges, including cell survival and engraftment, tumorigenicity concerns, immune rejection, ethical issues associated with certain stem cell types, and long-term efficacy and safety concerns, may hinder the translation of stem cell therapy from bench to bedside [96].

Following cardiac injury, the sustained loss of cardiomyocytes triggers a cascade of pathological events, including apoptosis and necrosis, inflammation, and fibroblast and myofibroblast activation, leading to scar formation and adverse left ventricular remodeling [97]. The microenvironment may play a critical role in the survival and functional efficacy of transplanted stem cells. Poor cell retention and engraftment have been demonstrated in preclinical models and clinical studies [98]. Intra-myocardially delivered stem cells may become trapped within the interstitium, and only a small fraction of the transplanted cells will survive. The addition of supraphysiological doses of cytokines or growth factors into the local microenvironment may help to improve cell retention and repair [99].

Tumorigenicity remains a challenge for stem cell therapy. Pluripotent stem cells have the potential to develop into all three germ layers in the body, including ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm derivatives, particularly after being differentiated, or have the capacity for unlimited in vitro self-renewal and differentiation [100]. These cells may create teratomas after being injected into the host tissues. The mutations or abnormalities during in vitro reprogramming and differentiation make embryonic stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cell–derived somatic-like cells not ideal sources of stem cells for cell therapy [101,102].

Cell survival and engraftment

Despite the promise of stem cells as cellular therapy for cardiac injury and disease, one major limitation is poor cell survival and retention in vivo following transplantation into the host heart. A substantial majority of stem cells engrafted into the myocardium undergo early apoptosis and/or are cleared by the immune system within the first few days after infusion, resulting in limited functional recovery. Thus, we must address the necessity of optimizing both the cellular micro-environment and the clinical conditions to maximize stem cell retention to improve cardiac function and regeneration [103].

Multiple preconditioning strategies and modifications of stem cells, including genetic modification, hypoxia, diazoxide-mediated mitochondrial protection, ischemic or “toxic” preconditioning, and pharmacological and scaffold-based approaches, have been proposed to enhance stem cell engraftment and reduce cell death post-transplantation. There is, however, no “gold-standard” method to optimize stem cell engraftment in the ischaemic heart. This remains an attractive area for targeted research to reduce the overall death rate of transplanted so that their pro-survival and cytoprotective factors can be optimally realized in vivo [104]. Transplantation via intra-coronary infusion or catheter delivery may optimize cell engraftment as compared with direct injection. Similarly, the timing of stem cell delivery post-myocardial infarction may play an essential role in determining cell survival and engraftment. Patients with STEMI have been reported to benefit from peripheral blood mononuclear cell infusion within 24 h of symptom onset and before coronary reperfusion. These results, although small in number, point towards the requirement of optimizing both pre- and post-transplantation conditions to enhance donor cell retention, and thus, maximize the potential benefits of stem cell therapy [70,105].

Tumorigenicity concerns

Tumorigenicity of cells is a critical issue in cardiac regeneration associated with stem cell use. Many previously used embryonic stem cells or pluripotent cells in animal models and clinical trials for heart regeneration have shown tumor formation after transplantation, limiting their practical applicability in regenerative therapy. To mitigate the tumorigenic risk associated with embryonic stem cells or induced pluripotent stem cells, many efforts focus on deriving somatic lineage-restricted progenitor cells. Lineage-restricted progenitor cells, including cardiac progenitor cells, cardiac mesodermal progenitors, and cardiac committed progenitors, exhibit developmental limitations that limit their capacity to generate cardiac cells with regenerative capacity. To date, several types of mature cardiac cells, such as cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and cardiac fibroblasts, have been examined for grafting into heart tissue for repair in preclinical studies. Recently, a cell-free approach using exosomes derived from CPCs has shown promising cardioprotective efficacy in acute myocardial ischemia [100,106].

Tumor formation and other post-transplantation complications following stem cell therapy are attributable to multiple factors. Tissue-contextual factors, such as the ischemic microenvironment, immune cells in the damaged tissue, and fibroblasts, may influence stem cell migration, growth factor production, and paracrine activity. In addition to local tissue conditions, stem cell generation conditions may also affect therapeutic potency, including tumor formation risk. The source of stem cells (including fetal, adult, and induced pluripotent cells), the physical microenvironment of cells (including hypoxia and stiffness), and microRNA modulation during generation may affect the regenerative potency of stem cells. Moreover, additional chemical or genetic modification could improve the therapeutic efficacy and safety of stem cell therapy. As such, further progress in stem cell-based approaches should be made to expedite the translation of those strategies into the clinic [107,108].

Immune rejection

The immune response is critical to tissue regeneration for any non-self-substituted material. This principle holds for all stem cells as well, in fact, the lack of tissue compatibility between implanted stem cells and the host may lead to immune-mediated rejection, which is one of the most relevant issues to address for the safety and efficacy of stem cell therapy. Immune rejection leads to death and loss of function of implanted stem cells, resulting in treatment failure and preventing the potential benefits in regeneration mediated by trophic factors released from stem cells during the healing process. This unsolved problem includes even tissue-specific stem cells from immunologically mismatched donors. It must be adequately considered not only for allogeneic stem cells but also after autologous stem cell transplantation, especially after in vitro expansion and genetic manipulation. The use of fetal or neonatal stem cells has been presented to solve these problems. Owing to their early developmental stage, fetal or neonatal stem cells have a unique, extremely silent immunological profile that protects them from rejection by the host immune system [109].

There is a general belief that critical myocyte loss is followed by nonspecific fibrosis due to matrix expansion and proinflammatory cytokine secretion, which, in turn, promotes T-lymphocyte stimulation; however, innate immune cells can also directly target stem cells in injured heart tissue in response to danger signals. In addition, several studies demonstrate a role for the innate immune system at every step of the stem cell life cycle (migration, engraftment, and differentiation), providing evidence about the effect that protective or injurious innate immune cells exert on stem cell fate and function and the possibility of utilizing transplant-associated prospective innate immune modulation strategies to improve cardiac stem cell-based therapies. Thus, for an adequate stem cell-mediated therapy, a better characterization of the cellular microenvironment, including innate immune cells, is required, and careful modulation of the post-injury immune response may improve the efficacy of cell therapy for regeneration [110].

Regulatory and ethical issues

Stem cell therapy for cardiac regeneration offers the potential for unprecedented medical breakthroughs but simultaneously attracts heightened ethical and regulatory scrutiny. Since the establishment of the first human embryonic stem cell lines, several ethical concerns have emerged, including the destruction of embryos, the use of fetal tissues, the introduction of exogenous genes, and the theoretical risk of creating chimeric or hybrid organisms [111]. Such concerns, coupled with cultural and societal differences, have resulted in regulatory guidelines that often vary across jurisdictions, with many ethical decisions delegated to local or national authorities rather than governed by universal standards [112]. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulates most stem cell–based therapies under the Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue-Based Products (HCT/Ps) framework, requiring Investigational New Drug (IND) approval unless strict minimal manipulation and homologous-use criteria are met. Scientific debate also persists over federal and state funding for embryonic stem cell research, reflecting broader disagreements regarding the moral status of embryos [113]. In Europe, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) classifies stem cell therapies as Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs), mandating rigorous demonstration of identity, purity, potency, genomic stability, and long-term safety before clinical authorization [114]. A further challenge arises from the requirement for Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant production, as clinical-grade stem cells must be expanded in tightly controlled, xeno-free environments using validated reagents and robust sterility assurance systems [115]. However, studies show that large-scale GMP expansion can alter cell phenotype, potency, or differentiation behavior, potentially compromising therapeutic consistency [116]. Therefore, despite growing interest in regenerative therapies due to limited organ availability and unmet clinical needs, stringent oversight, continuous evaluation during cell culture and differentiation, and compliance with FDA/EMA regulatory pathways remain essential before stem cell-based interventions can be safely translated into routine cardiac clinical practice [117].

Long-term efficacy and safety

Long-term studies in large, relevant animal model populations or human clinical trial populations, especially those with more than 3 years of follow-up, remain scarce. The limited preclinical reports could not reliably predict the clinical outcome. Uncertain information and sources regarding stem cells, as well as their application methods, could affect safety and therapeutic benefit. Factors related to stem cell source and quality, preparation, transportation, and storage can alter the metabolic response [27]. Different delivery methods could lead to different outcomes. Preferential cell-lineage commitment, differentiation, or transdifferentiation of implanted stem cells into unwanted immature cardiac phenotypes could, in the long term, lead to other problems, including electrical malfunctions that present as arrhythmias [118]. Mechanical toxicity to the cardiac structure after cell therapy could cause additional myocardial damage. Toxic factors secreted by stem cells could negatively affect long-term efficacy. Previous autologous cell transfer applications have focused on mobilizing hematopoietic stem cells for acute myocardial infarction. Instead of enhancing repair after myocardial infarction, naive stem cells could release detrimental immune-modulatory factors and cytokines during the systemic inflammatory response, which could worsen long-term myocardial function [119]. Future preclinical studies and subsequent human clinical trials will be required to determine the therapeutic efficacy and potential complications of autologous stem cell therapy for cardiac regeneration. The need to balance effectiveness and safety will be pivotal in strategy design. In the future, transplanting non-autologous allogenic stem cells may significantly shorten the therapeutic time if donor compatibility issues and immune rejection responses are solved. Discovering novel biomarkers for detecting cardiac injury and for tracking stem cell fate after implantation would inform future decision-making [120].

In addition to general concerns about tumorigenicity and immune rejection, the safety profile of pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes, particularly iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes, is complicated by their arrhythmogenic potential [121]. These cells frequently exhibit an immature electrophysiological phenotype, including spontaneous automaticity, prolonged action-potential duration, and heterogeneous ion-channel expression, which can promote triggered activity and re-entry circuits after transplantation. Preclinical large-animal studies have reported ventricular tachyarrhythmias following engraftment of human iPSC-derived cardiomyocyte grafts, indicating that even structurally beneficial constructs may destabilize electrical conduction if not adequately matured and integrated [122]. Moreover, variability in differentiation protocols and batch-to-batch differences in ion-channel expression further increase the unpredictability of their electrical behavior [123]. These findings underscore the need for rigorous preclinical electrophysiological characterization, strategies to enhance electrical maturation (e.g., long-term pacing, 3D engineered tissues, co-culture with non-myocytes), and careful rhythm monitoring in early clinical trials. Without systematic mitigation of arrhythmia risk, the widespread clinical use of iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes for cardiac regeneration remains premature [124].

Limitations and failures in clinical translation of cardiac stem cell therapy

Despite promising preclinical findings, the clinical translation of stem cell–based cardiac therapies has been repeatedly constrained by several fundamental limitations that help explain the modest outcomes reported in human studies [125]. One of the most significant barriers is extremely low cell retention and engraftment, with less than 5–10% of transplanted cells remaining in the myocardium within the first 24–48 h, as demonstrated in cell-tracking studies [126]. In addition, pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes frequently display electrical immaturity, which predisposes to ventricular arrhythmias in both large-animal models and early translational work [127]. Tumorigenicity remains a primary translational concern, as undifferentiated ESCs and iPSCs can form teratomas, underscoring the need for rigorous purification protocols before clinical use [128]. Even mesenchymal stem cells and cardiac progenitor cells, often considered safer, can contribute to pro-fibrotic remodeling or display altered phenotypes in inflammatory microenvironments. Furthermore, immune rejection continues to limit the use of allogeneic strategies [129]. However, MSCs exhibit partial immunomodulatory activity; they are not completely immune-privileged, and host immune activation can progressively eliminate donor cells, thereby reducing long-term efficacy [130]. Collectively, these translational failures demonstrate the substantial gap between experimental promise and clinical reality, underscoring the need for improved cell engineering, immune-matched or hypo-immunogenic cell sources, and delivery platforms that enhance engraftment and long-term safety [131]. Overall, inconsistent outcomes across trials primarily arise from low cell retention (<10%), heterogeneous cell phenotypes, patient-dependent variability, and inadequate long-term safety profiling [132]. Standardization of GMP-grade cell production, real-time in vivo cell tracking, arrhythmia monitoring, and integration of cell-free approaches, such as EV-based therapies, are essential to overcome current translational barriers [133].

Future perspectives in cardiac stem cell therapy

Despite considerable research efforts, therapeutic efficacy has not yet reached a satisfactory level in the clinical applications of cardiac stem cell therapy. Improvements in both translational research and clinical practice are necessary [134]. Various advances in tissue engineering and basic science are leading us into novel directions. In this regard, it seems timely to discuss future perspectives, including new delivery systems, combination therapies, and personalized medicine approaches [135].

Innovative delivery techniques

Cardiac progenitor cell delivery to damaged hearts can be achieved using various methods. The primary goal is to optimize the number of cells to increase retention and improve eventual host responses [136]. The need to overcome the limitations of syringe-based cell delivery has led to the development of several modalities to achieve a more uniform distribution of stem cells throughout large areas or volumes of myocardial tissue [137]. Novel injection systems and advanced percutaneous approaches for cell delivery may increase cell retention during injection and enhance homing to target areas. Specifically, intracoronary injections of suspended cells during ischemia-reperfusion and direct intramyocardial insertion of cells via percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty guide wires, are possible options. Moreover, the use of biodegradable scaffolds as cell carriers for stem cell delivery is another innovative option [138].

Combination therapies

To enhance the cardioprotective effects induced by cardiac progenitor cells, so-called combination therapies are now being proposed. This therapeutic approach includes a variety of molecules and substances capable of stimulating tissue regeneration and repair, including biomolecules, small molecules, and physical stimuli [139]. Progenitor cells can also be combined with other cell types that promote cardiac repair to create a microenvironment favorable to neovascularization, ultimately accelerating vascular development and improving cardiac function. Among the possible treatment strategies combining cell therapy and physical stimuli, we can mention the exposure of the ischemic heart to cyclic mechanical stress or electric currents, and ischemic post-conditioning [140].

Emerging therapies in cardiac regeneration

In addition to conventional cell-based approaches, several emerging therapies are reshaping the future landscape of cardiac regeneration by addressing key translational limitations such as low engraftment, tumorigenicity, and immune rejection. Among these, extracellular vesicles (EVs) and exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells and cardiac progenitor cells have gained significant attention as cell-free therapeutic agents [141]. These nano-sized vesicles carry bioactive microRNAs, proteins, and cytokines that promote angiogenesis, reduce apoptosis, and modulate post-injury inflammation without the risks associated with live cell transplantation [142]. Preclinical studies show that exosome-based therapy can improve left ventricular function and attenuate fibrosis, positioning EVs as promising, safer, and more scalable alternatives to whole-cell therapy. Advances in engineered cardiac patches and biomaterial scaffolds are also transforming myocardial repair strategies [143]. Tissue-engineered constructs combining human pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes with biodegradable hydrogels or decellularized matrices have demonstrated improved cell retention, synchronous contraction, and enhanced electrical integration in large-animal models. These patches provide a platform for delivering high-density, pre-aligned, and partially matured cardiomyocytes directly to injured myocardium, overcoming the limitations of cell-injection therapies [144].

Another rapidly advancing area involves gene-edited progenitor cells, including CRISPR-Cas9–modified cardiac progenitors and hypo-immunogenic iPSC lines engineered to evade immune system rejection [146]. Gene editing enables enhancement of cell survival, resistance to apoptosis, improved metabolic fitness, and precise control of differentiation pathways. These engineered cells have shown superior engraftment and reduced arrhythmogenic potential in early preclinical models [145]. Collectively, these emerging strategies, cell-free exosome therapies, advanced cardiac patches, and gene-edited progenitor cells, represent the next generation of regenerative approaches designed to overcome the significant barriers encountered in traditional stem cell transplantation. Their continued development is expected to accelerate translational progress and expand the therapeutic toolkit for effective cardiac repair.

Cell-free therapeutic strategies

Recent advances in regenerative cardiology have increasingly shifted attention toward cell-free therapies, which address many of the limitations associated with traditional stem cell transplantation, including low engraftment, arrhythmogenicity, tumorigenicity, and immune rejection [146]. Among these, extracellular vesicles (EVs) and exosomes released by MSCs, cardiac progenitor cells, and iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes have emerged as potent mediators of cardiac repair. These nanoscale vesicles carry cardioprotective microRNAs (e.g., miR-21, miR-146a, miR-210), growth factors, and anti-inflammatory molecules that reduce apoptosis, promote angiogenesis, and modulate post-infarction immune responses, without requiring viable cell transplantation [147]. Recent studies demonstrate that exosomes not only promote functional recovery but also attenuate ventricular remodeling and fibrosis in preclinical large-animal MI models. Their stability, small size, and capacity for systemic delivery make them an attractive alternative to cell-based approaches, and ongoing efforts aim to engineer designer exosomes enriched with pro-repair cargo or surface ligands to enhance myocardial targeting [148]. In parallel, secretome-based therapies including conditioned media, growth factor cocktails, and purified anti-fibrotic/angiogenic proteins are being explored as scalable, off-the-shelf regenerative products. These formulations circumvent safety concerns associated with live cells while allowing precise control of composition and dosing [149]. Furthermore, exosome-loaded hydrogels and injectable biomaterials developed between 2023 and 2024 have shown extended retention and sustained release in myocardial tissue, significantly enhancing therapeutic impact [150].

Collectively, these developments indicate that cell-free therapies are not merely adjunctive options but are rapidly becoming a central direction in cardiac regenerative medicine [151]. Their superior safety profile, reduced complexity, and translational readiness place them at the forefront of next-generation strategies to overcome the persistent barriers of conventional stem cell therapy [152].

Conclusion

Stem and progenitor cell–based strategies for cardiac repair have shifted from the early expectation of direct cardiomyocyte replacement toward a paradigm centered on paracrine signaling, immunomodulation, and microenvironmental support. Clinical studies have consistently demonstrated safety but only modest functional benefit, largely due to poor cell retention, electrical immaturity of transplanted cells, hostile post-infarction microenvironments, and variability in cell preparation and delivery. Future progress is likely to depend on lineage-restricted or gene-edited progenitors, engineered tissue constructs, biomaterial-assisted delivery systems, and scalable cell-free approaches such as extracellular vesicles. Coupled with standardized manufacturing, optimized delivery routes, and rigorous long-term evaluation, these advances may help bridge the gap between experimental promise and reliable clinical applications in cardiac regeneration.
