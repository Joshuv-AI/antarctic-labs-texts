Visual Cues Support Robust Turn-taking Prediction in Noise
Sam, Naomi

Abstract
Accurate predictive turn-taking models (PTTMs) are essential for naturalistic human-robot interaction. However, little is known about their performance in noise. This study therefore explores PTTM performance in types of noise likely to be encountered once deployed. Our analyses reveal PTTMs are highly sensitive to noise. Hold/shift accuracy drops from 84% in clean speech to just 52% in 10 dB music noise. Training with noisy data enables a multimodal PTTM, which includes visual features to better exploit visual cues, with 72% accuracy in 10 dB music noise. The multimodal PTTM outperforms the audio-only PTTM across all noise types and SNRs, highlighting its ability to exploit visual cues; however, this does not always generalise to new types of noise. Analysis also reveals that successful training relies on accurate transcription, limiting the use of ASR-derived transcriptions to clean conditions. We make code publicly available for future research.

Introduction

Human turn-taking is a remarkable process. There is only 200 milliseconds of silence on average between speaking turns
[
1
]
, yet language production takes over 600 milliseconds
[
2
]
. Listeners must therefore plan what they are about to say whilst their conversational partner is still speaking
[
3
]
. Although effortless for humans, turn-taking is highly challenging for robots. Most turn-taking algorithms in use today are based on a robot reacting to silence after a turn
[
4
]
, resulting in interactions that are less fluid than those between humans
[
5
,
6
]
. Predictive turn-taking models have been proposed to overcome these limitations
[
7
,
4
]
. Predictive turn-taking models learn human-like turn-taking from large corpora of human interaction, e.g. continuously predicting whether a shift between speakers will occur
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
5
]
, akin to human turn-taking
[
3
]
.

Most predictive turn-taking models use speech features
[
4
]
. Yet in human-human interaction, listeners make faster and more accurate turn-taking decisions when they can see and hear a speaker
[
10
]
. Recent work shows multimodal predictive turn-taking models, which also use gaze, head pose and facial expression, outperform audio-only predictive turn-taking models
[
11
,
12
]
, illustrating the known role of visual cues in turn-taking
[
13
,
10
]
. This reflects the many communicative advantages of multimodality
[
14
]
, which include boosting the intelligibility of speech in noise
[
15
]
. MacLeod and Summerfield found that the intelligibility gain from being able to see a speaker was equivalent to an 11 dB increase in the signal-to-noise ratio
[
15
]
. This motivates audio-visual speech recognition, which exploits visual cues to overcome the performance degradation of automatic speech recognition in noise
[
16
]
.

This paper asks if multimodality confers similar performance benefits to predictive turn-taking models in noisy environments. Surprisingly, predictive turn-taking models have to date only been tested on interactions free from background noise interference
[
4
,
11
]
. The impact of noise on predictive turn-taking model performance is therefore unknown and unquantified. This presents a major oversight as predictive turn-taking models will be deployed
in-the-wild
, where they will inevitably encounter diverse sources and levels of noise. We therefore present the first exploration of PTTM performance in background noise, asking:

1.

How is the performance of predictive turn-taking models affected by background noise?
and

2.

Does the inclusion of visual features make multimodal predictive turn-taking models robust to background noise?

This paper explores the crucial capability of a predictive turn-taking model to distinguish holds (no speaker change) from shifts (changes between speakers). In noise-free conditions, a multimodal predictive turn-taking model which incorporates visual features outperforms an audio-only model (
84\%
and
80\%
accuracy, Table
1
). When we add artificial music, babble, and speech noise, the accuracy of both predictive turn-taking models is poor (
51
% and
52\%
in music). Including noise during training increases accuracy (
61
% and
72\%
in music). It also ‘primes’ the multimodal predictive turn-taking model to better exploit visual information (10 dB effective SNR gain over the audio-only predictive turn-taking model). We find this effect generalises to types of noises unseen by the model during training. The inclusion of visual cues therefore makes PTTM performance more robust to noise. We also investigate the reliance of predictive turn-taking models training on accurate transcriptions. When noisy data is re-transcribed with ASR PTTM performance collapses (
50
-
54\%
). predictive turn-taking models training therefore relies heavily on accurately aligned transcripts collected from noise-free recordings, complicating the use of ASR in noisy data.

PTTMs are essential to facilitating interaction between humans and robots, but there is little understanding of how well they could work in-the-wild. Part of the puzzle is understanding how they handle noise and our work therefore represents a significant step forward in predictive turn-taking models development.

2
Predictive turn-taking models

Predictive turn-taking models continually predict future speaking activity, from which turn-taking predictions can be made
[
7
,
8
,
17
]
. For example, if speaker 0 is currently talking, but the model prediction favours speaker 1, a
shift
is predicted. Predictive turn-taking models are developed on corpora of human-human interaction with the long-term eventual goal of deployment to human-robot interaction. Our work considers two-party interaction, where most work has been conducted
[
4
]
.

We evaluate two predictive turn-taking models based on the state-of-the-art
voice-activity projection (VAP)
approach introduced by Ekstedt et al.
[
17
]
as it has been thoroughly explored
[
18
,
9
,
11
]
. Voice-activity projection models predict future speaking activity in 8 bins (4 per speaker). At time
t=0
, bins span
[0,0.2],[0.2,0.6],[0.6,1.2]
and
[1.2,2.0]
seconds into the future. Each bin has a binary label (1=speech if
>50\%
frames contain speech), giving 256 possible outputs.

Voice-activity projection models are neural networks consisting of stacked transformer decoder layers
[
17
,
19
]
. The first model is an
audio-only VAP model
which we re-implement from
[
19
]
. The initial voice-activity projection model used mono audio and ground-truth knowledge of the current speaker
[
17
]
; i.e. it knew when each turn ended. The updated model used here takes stereo audio with 1 channel per speaker and removes the dependency on ground-truth knowledge, reflecting a real-world deployment. We also use a
multimodal VAP (MM-VAP)
model that incorporates facial action unit, facial landmark, gaze and head pose features from OpenFace
[
20
,
11
,
12
]
. As the initial multimodal VAP model also depended on the ground-truth knowledge of the current speaker
[
11
]
, we similarly extended the model to support stereo audio and remove this dependency
[
12
]
. For comparison, we also train a
video-only voice-activity projection
model, identical to voice-activity projection with the audio encoder removed
[
19
]
. Our implementations, along with full architectural details are publicly available in our repository
1
1

1

https://github.com/russelsa/mm-vap
.

Predictive turn-taking models require labels of the start and end of each utterance for training (the
alignment
). Manual transcriptions are typically used e.g. the Switchboard telephone corpus
[
21
,
17
,
22
,
5
]
. Automatic speech recognition more closely represents real-world deployments and has been shown to work well in predictive turn-taking model training
[
23
,
24
]
.

3
Data preparation

This work uses the Candor corpus
[
25
]
, an 850 hr corpus of 1657 unscripted two-party videoconferencing interactions between speakers in US English. The average session duration is 34 min. Recordings are 60 fps video, 32 kHz stereo audio with one channel per speaker. There is no controlled background noise. Recordings take place at a location of the participant’s choosing e.g. home offices. We transcribe with Amazon Transcribe (EN-US model) which outputs word-level timings and preserves most disfluencies
[
23
]
. We extract gaze, head pose, facial action units, and facial landmarks with OpenFace
[
20
]
.

We follow the audio-visual speech recognition literature by artificially simulating noisy conditions, adding noise separately to each channel at controlled signal-to-noise ratios
[
16
]
.
Babble noise
consists of 65,000 unique 30 speaker
babbles
which is synthesised from LRS-3
[
26
,
16
]
(46 hr train, 14 hr test).
Music noise
is sourced from MUSAN
[
27
]
(15 hr train from the
Jamendo
genre, 70 hr test). Interference from other speakers (
speech noise
) is simulated by overlaying another speaker from Candor, ensuring train/test separation.

In audio-visual speech recognition research, noise is typically added to isolated speech segments. However, predictive turn-taking models are trained using conversational speech which includes long pauses. A standard method of adding controlled noise to conversational speech has not been reported. We propose to add noise at a given SNR by considering signal energy during speaking times and then adding scaled noise to the complete recording. Natural variation in amplitude means this yields an average SNR over all utterances.

The
clean test audio
comprises 70 sessions withheld for evaluation. The test audio is also corrupted with -10 to +10 dB signal-to-noise ratios noise in 2.5 dB increments (
noisy test audio
). Shi et al. found that adding noise in training increased audio-visual speech recognition performance. Following their method, in each training session, with probability 0.25 we add either babble, music or speech noise at 0 dB
[
16
]
, yielding the
augmented training audio
. Note that clean and augmented training sets are the same length. We obtain both
clean
and
augmented alignments
for both training sets with Amazon ASR.

4
Model training and evaluation

First, we train with the clean audio + clean alignment, as is standard in the turn-taking literature
[
17
,
24
]
. Then we train on the augmented audio + clean alignment, where noise is added but the transcription remains unchanged
[
16
]
. To test the reliance on accurate ASR, we train using the augmented audio + augmented alignment. This represents a situation where there is noisy data transcribed with ASR. Training batches consist of 20-second windows with 2 second overlap randomly sampled from training sessions. The training procedure is a 5-fold cross-validation with 10 epochs per fold on an NVidia RTX 6000 GPU. A grid search sets the batch size to 16 and the learning rate to 1e-4. The loss function is the sum of cross-entropy (future speaking prediction) and binary cross-entropy (current speaker prediction) losses minimised with AdamW
[
28
]
.

We report the hold/shift prediction accuracy: the ability of the model to distinguish holds (no speaker change) from shifts (speaker change), a common metric in the turn-taking literature
[
17
,
19
]
. First, all silences
>250
ms are identified. Those with speaker changes are shifts (83,158) and those without are holds (206,830)
[
17
]
. Then, the probability of speech in the latter half of the voice-activity projection bins is computed
[
17
]
. If speaker 0 has finished speaking and the probability of speaker 1 exceeds a binary threshold, a shift is predicted. We set the threshold to maximise balanced accuracy on validation sessions and report a 5-fold average on test sessions.

We report effective SNR gain i.e. the SNR at which multimodal VAP achieves the same accuracy as voice-activity projection at 0 dB, following the audio-visual speech recognition literature
[
29
]
. We compare voice-activity projection and multimodal VAP model performance with two-tailed independent t-tests
[
30
]
.

5.1
Baseline performance on clean speech

We begin our analysis by training voice-activity projection and multimodal VAP models using the clean audio and clean alignment, establishing a baseline hold/shift prediction accuracy without added noise (clean test audio, Table
1
, first two rows). The 5-fold average hold/shift prediction accuracy of multimodal VAP model is 84%, greater than the audio-only voice-activity projection at 80% (
p<0.001
). The accuracy and the superior performance of multimodal VAP are in agreement with the predictive turn-taking model literature
[
19
,
24
]
.

The impact of noise on turn-taking models

Training with noisy examples

We re-train both models on the augmented training set and show hold/shift prediction accuracy by noise type in blue in Figure

61\%
for voice-activity projection and
72\%

52\%

The blue lines in Figure

). The effective SNR gain of multimodal VAP has increased to +10 dB and the extreme sensitivity to speech and music noise is reduced. At +10 dB SNR of speech noise, the average accuracy is

52

On the other hand, multimodal VAP backs off to the accuracy of a predictive turn-taking model model trained exclusively on video features (67% vs 66% in -10 dB music noise). The only difference in input is multimodal VAP receives visual features. However, it achieves superior performance in all types of noise and across the full range of signal-to-noise ratios, and performance is similar to a video-only model in extreme (-10 dB) noise. This demonstrates that it exploits visual information to improve accuracy in acoustic interference.

5.5
Generalisation to noise unseen during training

We investigate if multimodal VAP performance extends to types of noise unseen during training. We retrain three times with either 0 dB SNR speech, music or babble noise in 25% of training sessions. The balanced accuracy of hold/shift prediction in Table
2
shows that when the training set includes speech or babble noise, accuracy is poor in other types of noise: e.g.
71\%

in music. However, when trained on speech noise, similar accuracy is obtained in all types of noise
74-75\%
(Table

The critical role of the alignment in training

Finally, we re-train both models using the augmented alignment obtained from Amazon ASR. The orange bars in Figure

50-58\%
and Figure

6
Discussion

Our results show predictive turn-taking models do not maintain high accuracy in types of noises they are likely to encounter in-the-wild (Section
5.2
). We found slight (10 dB SNR) interference from music and other speakers caused accuracy to plummet to 50%. Including noise during training dramatically increased performance (Section
5.3
). This is a common technique in audio-visual speech recognition
[
16
]
that has not previously been considered in the turn-taking literature. Returning to our first research question, our findings therefore show that the predictive turn-taking models in this work are not robust to noise without the prior inclusion of noise in training.

The initial poor performance shows multimodal VAP does not exploit visual cues to their fullest extent (Section
5.2
) without appropriate training. Training on augmented data led to considerably more improvement in the accuracy of multimodal VAP than voice-activity projection across the SNR range. At extreme levels of noise interference (e.g. -10 dB music noise) voice-activity projection fails (50%, i.e. chance) but multimodal VAP backs off to the accuracy of the video-only model (67% vs 66%, Section
5.3
). This highlights the role visual cues play in achieving robust H/S accuracy in noise.

If multimodal VAP is truly able to utilise visual cues to overcome noise, this should generalise to new sources of noise. However, we found that this was only true for the multimodal VAP model trained on speech-noise (Section
5.5
). Our working hypothesis is that mixing in random speakers over the speech signal training process ‘forces’ multimodal VAP to use visual information, rather than relying on the acoustic properties of the noise. Thus, returning to our second research question, predictive turn-taking models can achieve robust performance in noise, but this is intimately related to the training process and does not inherently generalise to new noise types.

It is difficult to interpret hold/shift prediction accuracy numbers as no studies have determined a minimum accuracy needed to support fluid interaction in a human-robot deployment. Taking our 80-84% accuracy on clean speech as a baseline and ignoring the many complexities of a real-world deployment including hardware, real-time performance and human-machine dialogue, a robot would make a correct hold/shift decision 8/10 times. Both multimodal VAP and voice-activity projection would only be correct 5/10 times in 10 dB SNR music interference when trained using conventional methods, significantly complicating interaction. This rises to 6.5/10 for voice-activity projection and 7.5/10 for multimodal VAP when noise is included in training; close to baseline performance, illustrating the power of visual cues in achieving robust performance in noise.

A limitation of our work is we artificially add noise to speech recorded in noise-free settings. Although this is commonplace
[
31
,
16
,
29
]
, it means we do not capture changes to verbal and non-verbal
[
32
]
communication and turn-taking
[
33
]
in noise. Another limitation is our use of videoconferencing. However, no in-person corpora match the size and quality of Candor. Finally, our mixing of noise at a constant energy level means the audio-only voice-activity projection model may be partly capitalising on the natural variations in SNR in long recordings of speech.

Training techniques for multimodal predictive turn-taking models should be further explored to ensure their ability to exploit visual cues generalises to new noise types. As AVSR can exploit visual information in datasets unseen in training
[
34
]
, AVSR forms a natural starting point for future multimodal predictive turn-taking models architectures
[
16
]
. Turn-taking in noise is quite similar to the channel separation problem of speaker diarisation
[
35
]
and our findings support further exploration and the extension to the multimodal setting. There are many other turn-taking events (e.g. backchannels) which require consideration in future work.

7
Conclusion

Accurate turn-taking models are an important milestone on the path to viable human-robot interaction. We have conducted the first exploration of predictive turn-taking model performance in noise, showing that predictive turn-taking models are not robust to typical noises a predictive turn-taking model may encounter once deployed. Training with noise improves overall accuracy and enables multimodal predictive turn-taking model to leverage visual features to overcome acoustic interference. However, this capability does not necessarily generalise to new types of noise. This issue must be solved prior to their deployment in real-world settings. Our work therefore constitutes a significant step forward in our understanding of predictive turn-taking models. Future work in predictive turn-taking model architectures, training techniques and in-the-wild deployment are therefore imperative before deployment to human-robot interaction.
