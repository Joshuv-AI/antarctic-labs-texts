Community awareness of climate change and its public health impacts in the North Bank Region, The Gambia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Bakary Kinteh, Lamin Darboe, Mansour Badjie, Modou S Gaye, Sheriff Ceesay, Amadou Kongira, Sheikh Omar Sillah, Amadou Barrow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesClimate change poses significant public health challenges globally, particularly in vulnerable regions such as The Gambia. Despite growing recognition of environmental impacts, limited research has examined community-level awareness of climate change health effects in West African settings. This study aimed to assess household awareness and perceptions of climate change impacts on public health among vulnerable communities in the North Bank Region, The Gambia and identify socio-demographic determinants of climate change awareness.Design and settingIn this study, we conducted a community-based cross-sectional study. Using multistage sampling, we selected 35 communities across seven districts. Data were collected via structured questionnaires administered in local languages (Mandinka, Wolof, Fula) using KoboToolbox.ParticipantsThis study was conducted among 868 residents aged ≥18 years in the North Bank Region between January and February 2024.ResultsOverall, 85.7% (n=744) of respondents had heard about climate change, with radio (53.6%) being the primary information source. Participants demonstrated high awareness of certain climate hazards, such as excessive heat (76.4%) and altered rainfall patterns (55.2%), but less so for other hazards, such as flooding (30.3%). Respondents correctly identified multiple health impacts to health and livelihoods, including heat stress (65.8%), dehydration (57.3%), respiratory diseases (73.6%), waterborne diseases (59.0%) and crop failure (86.4%). Multivariable analysis revealed that older age (>39 years: adjusted OR (aOR)=2.50, 95% CI 1.49 to 4.21) and tertiary education (aOR=3.93, 95% CI 1.50 to 10.30) were independent predictors of climate change awareness. Approximately 77% of participants reported experiencing climate change effects in their communities within the past 5 years.ConclusionThis first comprehensive assessment of climate-health awareness in the North Bank Region of The Gambia reveals substantial community recognition of climate change and its health consequences. Significant disparities in awareness by age and educational attainment indicate that targeted educational interventions focused on younger populations and those with limited formal education are warranted.

Introduction

Climate change refers to the long-term alteration of the average weather patterns that characterise earth’s local, regional and global climates.1 Changes observed in earth’s climate since the early 20th century are primarily driven by greenhouse gas emissions, causing warmer temperatures, changes in precipitation and sea-level rise.2 The health implications include heat-related disorders, respiratory disorders, vector-borne and waterborne infectious diseases, malnutrition, violence, injuries from extreme weather events and mental health problems.3 4 The impacts of climate change extend beyond geopolitical concerns and affect all aspects of life across continents. Moreover, climate change poses a global public health challenge, prompting various efforts to address and mitigate its effects. The countries that have generated the smallest amounts of greenhouse gas emissions, mainly low- and middle-income countries in the Global South, have suffered the highest rates of adverse health effects due to climate change.

Climate change is projected to significantly increase the global disease mortality burden, with an estimated 250 000 additional deaths annually between 2030 and 2050 from malnutrition, malaria, diarrhoeal diseases and heat stress.4 The economic impact on healthcare systems is anticipated to range between US$2 and US$4 billion per year by 2030, with regions having weaker health infrastructures bearing disproportionate burden.4 This inequitable distribution of climate health impacts underscores the urgent need for targeted interventions in vulnerable populations.

In the African region, climate change exacerbates existing health vulnerabilities by causing rapid changes in ecosystems such as damaging farmland, which forces people to migrate.5 6 The Gambia, situated in West Africa, is classified as highly vulnerable to climate change hazards, including flooding, storms, droughts and coastal erosion.5 6 These climate-related phenomena are directly linked to food insecurity and substantial economic losses.6 The Gambia experiences a Sahelian climate characterised by a lengthy dry season from November to May and a short rainy season from June to October. During the dry season, average temperatures range from 18°C to 30°C, while the rainy season sees temperatures between 23°C and 30°C.7 Climate projections forecast that the average temperature may rise by 3°C to 4.5°C by the year 2075, leading to increased evapotranspiration and further environmental stress.7

Historical climate data reveal that flooding occurs biennially in The Gambia and has accounted for over 50% of climate-related natural disasters in the past three decades. The 2012 drought affected more than 400 000 individuals, representing 20% of the population at that time. In 2021, storms impacted nearly 17 000 people, and flooding in 2022 affected 17 201 individuals.8 These climate-related disasters have resulted in economic losses approaching 4% of gross domestic product, and agricultural production has been adversely affected due to altered rainfall patterns, increased temperatures and rising sea levels, making The Gambia increasingly reliant on imports of various agricultural products.9

Although several climate mitigation strategies have been developed in The Gambia, most plans primarily focus on adapting to climate change, such as by enhancing environmental resilience, while health implications often receive inadequate prioritisation. The country’s health policy has acknowledged the necessity of integrating climate change considerations into health service delivery10; however, limited empirical research has been conducted on this topic. Notable studies include investigations of climate change seasonality and household water security in rural Gambia,11 vulnerability strategies6 and a climate change vulnerability and risk management matrix.12 However, these studies have generally not focused comprehensively on the impact of climate change on public health, with the notable exception of research by Bonell13 examining climate change, maternal health and birth outcomes.13

Understanding community awareness and perceptions of climate-health linkages is critical for designing effective public health interventions and climate adaptation strategies. Community awareness serves as a foundation for behaviour change, policy support and adaptive capacity building. Given this context, and the identified research gap, our study aimed to assess household awareness and perceptions of climate change on public health in vulnerable communities of The Gambia. The findings from this study will help inform evidence-based policy decisions aimed at mitigating and addressing the effects of climate change on population health.

Material and methods

Study design and setting

A community-based cross-sectional study design was conducted to assess the insights on the impact of climate change on public health among households resident in the North Bank Region of The Gambia. The River Gambia divides the country into two halves (North and South), and the Northern Strip of the River stretches from the North Bank Region through the Central and Upper River Regions. The North Bank Region is further divided into two distinctive areas, such as Nuimi and Baddibu. The administrative headquarters is located at Kerewan, a population of 225 516 inhabitants, and farming and business are the main preoccupations of the residents.14

Study population

We recruited study participants aged ≥18 years from selected communities in the North Bank Region to assess their insights on the impact of climate change hazards on public health beyond the environmental impacts. Participants who are residents in the communities for more than a period of 12 months were included in this study. Guests and non-residents were excluded from this study to ensure conformity and neutrality.

Sample size determination

Sample size calculation was performed using Epi Info V.7.0. We used the total population of the North Bank Region (N=225 516) as the base population. With a 99% CI, 50% expected proportion (to maximise sample size in the absence of prior prevalence data) and 1% margin of error, the calculated minimum sample size was 634 participants. To account for potential non-response and enhance statistical power, we added a 10% non-response rate and applied a design effect of 1.3 (to account for clustering in multistage sampling), yielding a final target sample size of 896 respondents.

Sampling strategy

A four-stage probability sampling technique was employed to ensure representativeness across the region. Stage 1 – Regional selection: The North Bank Region was purposively selected based on documented climate vulnerability characteristics, including deforestation, desertification encroachment, flooding frequency and disaster occurrence. Stage 2 – Community selection: Using simple random sampling (lottery method), we selected five communities from each of the seven districts in the region, yielding a total of 35 study communities. This approach ensured geographical diversity and representation across different ecological zones within the region. Stage 3 – Household selection: A proportionate-to-size sampling technique was applied to determine the number of households to be sampled from each community based on community population size. This approach ensured that larger communities contributed proportionally more participants to maintain statistical efficiency. Stage 4 – Individual selection: Within each selected household, simple random sampling (using random number generation) was employed to recruit individual participants, with an average of 25 households sampled per community. In households with multiple eligible respondents, one participant was randomly selected using a random number table. If no eligible respondent was present after two visit attempts, data collectors proceeded to the next household in a clockwise direction to maintain spatial distribution.

Study instrument and variables

The study adapted and contextualised a closed-ended structured questionnaire from validated instruments used in previous climate-health awareness studies.1518 Items were reviewed and modified by the research team to reflect The Gambia’s specific climate hazard profile, including flooding, harmattan winds and drought and local health concerns. Content validity was assessed iteratively through three rounds of team review until consensus was reached on item relevance and clarity.

The questionnaire was pre-tested with 20 participants drawn from a community outside the study region (Brikama Area Council, West Coast Region) to assess item clarity, internal consistency and average completion time. Feedback from pre-testing informed minor revisions, primarily simplified wording of climate terminology and restructured response options for hazard-type items.

The English questionnaire was translated into three local languages (Mandinka, Wolof and Fula) by bilingual members of the research team with training in public health. A back-translation procedure was conducted: each translated version was independently translated back into English by a different bilingual team member not involved in the initial translation. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus. All four language versions were programmed into KoboToolbox, allowing data collectors to switch based on respondent preference. The questionnaire was standardised to reflect community-level realities within The Gambia’s national and local context (see online supplemental file 1 for the full questionnaire).

The instrument comprised five main sections: Socio-demographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, religion, nationality, education level, occupation, family structure and monthly income). Household water and energy sources (drinking water source and location, cooking fuel, lighting source). Climate change awareness (familiarity with the term, information sources, types of climate hazards recognised, perceived causes). Perceived public health impacts of specific climate hazards (extreme heat, extreme cold, flooding, altered rainfall patterns, bushfires). Personal experience with climate change manifestations in the community over the preceding 5 years.

The primary outcome variable was climate change awareness, operationalised as a binary variable (aware/not aware) based on participants’ affirmative response to having heard about climate change. Secondary outcomes included awareness of specific health impacts associated with distinct climate hazards.

Data collection procedures

Data collection was conducted using KoboToolbox, a mobile data collection platform that provides both online and offline functionality with a user-friendly interface for field-based data collection. The digital questionnaire was installed on data collectors’ mobile devices, enabling real-time data capture and quality monitoring.

Data collectors were recruited from the School of Public Health, Gambia College, and received comprehensive 1 day training covering: (1) KoboToolbox platform navigation and functionality, (2) standardised interview techniques to minimise interviewer bias, (3) ethical research conduct principles and (4) translation protocols for administering the English-version questionnaire in three local languages (Mandinka, Wolof and Fula) to accommodate linguistic diversity in the study region.

Field supervision was conducted by team supervisors who monitored data collection activities to ensure: adherence to sampling protocols, appropriate community entry procedures through local leaders, maintenance of data quality and consistency and timely resolution of field challenges. At the conclusion of each data collection day, collected data were synchronised to a central server, where additional quality checks were performed to identify and correct errors or inconsistencies. The final dataset was exported from KoboToolbox to Microsoft Excel for preliminary cleaning and preparation for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

The cleaned data were finally imported to the IBM Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) V.28 for analysis. The continuous variables (age, income) were summarised using means and SDs. Categorical variables (sex, education, occupation, awareness indicators) were summarised as frequencies and percentages. For multiple-response questions (eg, information sources, types of climate hazards), percentages were calculated based on the total number of respondents, recognising that totals may exceed 100%. The Pearson χ2 test was employed to examine associations between socio-demographic characteristics and climate change awareness. Statistical significance was set at α=0.05. Variables demonstrating associations with p<0.25 in bivariate analysis were considered for inclusion in multivariable models. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of climate change awareness. Variables with p<0.25 in bivariate analysis were entered into the multivariable model using the enter method. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and classification accuracy. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), with VIF <5 considered acceptable. Results are presented as adjusted ORs (aOR) with 95% CIs. Statistical significance in the final model was set at p<0.05. As a sensitivity analysis, design-adjusted estimates were calculated using the Complex Samples module in SPSS (V.28), incorporating inverse-probability sampling weights derived from stage-specific sampling fractions. Results were consistent with the primary unweighted analysis (see online supplemental table S1).

Ethical considerations

Permission to conduct the study in respective communities was secured from community entry points including village heads (Alkalos) and Village Development Committees. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to interview commencement, with the consent process conducted in participants’ preferred language. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. To protect participant privacy and confidentiality, no personal identifiers (names, telephone numbers or household addresses) were collected. All data were stored securely with password protection and accessible only to the research team.

Patient and public involvement

The village entry points, such as the Alkalos and Village Development Committee, were used in the identification of households during the data collection. Beyond the household identification, members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of our research work.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 868 respondents from households in 35 communities across the North Bank Region participated in this study, representing a 96.9% response rate from the target sample of 896. Female participants comprised the majority of the sample (55.2%). The largest age group was 39 years and above (37.0%), while respondents aged 18–24 years accounted for approximately one-quarter of the sample. Wolof was the most frequently reported ethnicity, followed by Mandinka and Fula. Nearly all respondents identified as Muslim (99.1%) and as Gambian nationals (97.0%).

Almost half of respondents (46.9%) had no formal education, and 10.1% had attained tertiary education. Farmers were the largest occupational group, followed by business owners/traders and housewives. Extended family arrangements were the most common household type (60.8%), and the majority of respondents (67.6%) reported average monthly household incomes below GMD5000 (approximately US$75). Bivariate analysis revealed statistically significant associations between climate change awareness and ethnicity (p=0.004) and education level (p<0.001). Awareness was highest among respondents with tertiary education (94.3%) and lowest among those with no formal education (82.3%). Age was not significantly associated with awareness (p=0.225). Household water and energy sources

Household water and energy utilisation patterns are presented in table 2. Tap water supplied by the National Water and Electricity Company (NAWEC) and boreholes were the two primary sources of drinking water, accounting for 50.7% and 47.8% of households, respectively. Just over half of respondents (52.8%) had a water source located within their premises, while the remainder accessed water from street or communal points. Firewood was the predominant cooking fuel, used by 89.3% of households, followed by charcoal (29.6%). Gas and electricity were used by 5.1% and 0.6% of households, respectively. For household lighting, the majority of respondents (63.5%) relied on the NAWEC electricity grid, and 27.8% used solar energy systems. The remaining respondents used rechargeable lights, battery-powered lights or candles (figure 1).

Climate change awareness and information sources

Overall, 85.7% of respondents reported prior familiarity with the term ‘climate change’ before this study (table 3). Among aware respondents, radio was the predominant information source (53.6%), followed by television (43.1%) and family or relatives (21.5%). Health workers and teachers each accounted for 13.8% of information sources, while extension workers represented 5.9%. Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive assessment of community-level awareness regarding climate change and its public health impacts in The Gambia. Our findings establish a foundational understanding of household knowledge about climate-health linkages across diverse cohorts in vulnerable communities, revealing high overall awareness but important knowledge gaps that require targeted interventions.

The 85.7% climate change awareness rate observed in this study substantially exceeds rates reported in similar low-income country contexts, including Bangladesh (59.6%)16 and select African nations. This elevated awareness likely reflects multiple factors: The Gambia’s small geographical size facilitating information dissemination, recent high-profile flooding events increasing salience and active community radio networks reaching rural populations. However, our finding aligns with studies from other West African contexts, including Burkina Faso, Mali and Senegal,35 11 13 16 1921 suggesting regional patterns of climate change awareness in the Sahel.

The predominance of radio (53.6%) as the primary information source underscores the critical role of community radio stations in disseminating climate information to rural populations with limited literacy and internet access. This finding diverges from studies in more developed contexts where formal education and healthcare professionals serve as primary information sources.18 The minimal role of agricultural extension workers (5.9%) in climate change communication represents a missed opportunity, as these trusted intermediaries could effectively link climate information to agricultural adaptation strategies in this farming-dependent population.

Climate change hazards and health impact awareness

Respondents demonstrated substantial awareness of specific climate-health linkages. Recognition of excessive heat (76.4%), excessive cold (63.1%) and altered rainfall patterns (55.2%) as primary climate hazards aligns with lived experiences in this Sahelian climate zone. The strong association between extreme heat and both heat stress (65.8%) and dehydration (57.3%) reflects community experience with increasingly frequent heat events. Studies from neighbouring Senegal have documented the public health burden of heat waves in the region,19 20 supporting the epidemiological reality underlying community perceptions. The high recognition of respiratory diseases (73.6%) as impacts of extreme cold likely reflects seasonal exposure to harmattan conditions, when cold, dry, dust-laden winds from the Sahara bring respiratory irritants and temperature stress. This awareness is epidemiologically supported by documented seasonal patterns of respiratory illness in West Africa.3

Flooding awareness patterns reveal a multidimensional understanding of flood-related health risks. Recognition of waterborne diseases (59.0%), injuries (47.7%) and environmental degradation (39.7%) demonstrates comprehensive understanding of flooding’s immediate and intermediate health consequences. This awareness is particularly relevant given that flooding constitutes over 50% of climate-related natural disasters in The Gambia over the past three decades.8 The association between flooding and waterborne disease outbreaks, including cholera and diarrhoeal diseases, has been well-documented in West African contexts.22 The overwhelming recognition that altered rainfall patterns cause crop failure (86.4%) reflects the agricultural community’s direct exposure to climate variability impacts on food production and livelihoods. This finding resonates with research documenting relationships between rainfall variability, crop yields and childhood undernutrition in Burkina Faso23 and broader food security analyses across the Sahel.24 The climate-agriculture-nutrition pathway represents a critical mechanism through which climate change affects population health in subsistence farming communities.

Environmental context and health implications

The near-universal reliance on firewood (89.3%) and charcoal (29.6%) for cooking has profound implications for both climate change and public health. Extensive biomass fuel consumption drives deforestation, which respondents correctly identified as a primary climate change cause (67.9%). However, the health consequences of indoor air pollution from biomass burning may not be fully recognised. Household air pollution from solid fuel combustion is a leading cause of respiratory disease mortality globally, with particular impacts on women and children who experience greatest exposure.3 This creates a vicious cycle: biomass fuel dependence contributes to climate change through deforestation and black carbon emissions, while simultaneously causing direct respiratory health impacts. The energy sector findings also reveal vulnerability to climate-health impacts. The national electricity grid’s dependence on heavy fuel oil25 contributes to greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution, with respiratory health consequences for urban populations. The growing adoption of solar energy for household lighting (27.8%) represents a positive trend toward renewable energy, though accessibility barriers prevent universal adoption in low-income households.

Determinants of climate change awareness

The multivariable analysis revealed age and education as independent predictors of climate change awareness, while income showed no significant association. The positive association between age and awareness (aOR=2.50 for ≥39 years) may reflect several mechanisms: accumulated life experience observing environmental changes, greater exposure to diverse information sources over time and potentially greater concern about environmental legacy for future generations. This finding contrasts with some studies from developed countries where younger populations demonstrate higher climate awareness, possibly reflecting different educational and media landscapes. The strong education gradient in awareness with tertiary-educated respondents showing nearly fourfold higher odds (aOR=3.93) compared with those with no formal education has critical policy implications. Education likely operates through multiple pathways: enhanced scientific literacy enabling understanding of complex climate processes, increased media literacy facilitating critical evaluation of climate information and expanded social networks providing diverse information sources. Notably, primary education alone showed no significant benefit (aOR=0.86), suggesting that interventions must target educational quality and climate curriculum integration rather than merely expanding basic literacy. The absence of a significant income effect, despite a trend toward higher awareness among higher-income respondents, may reflect the overwhelmingly low-income nature of this rural population (67.6% earning <GMD5000 monthly), limiting power to detect income effects. Alternatively, climate change awareness may be less influenced by economic status than by education and age in contexts where environmental changes are directly observable and affect all socioeconomic strata.

Knowledge gaps and misconceptions

Despite high overall awareness, important knowledge gaps emerged. The limited recognition of industrial emissions (24.7%), urbanisation (12.9%) and population growth (20.5%) as climate change drivers reflects The Gambia’s minimal industrial development and rural settlement patterns. However, this localised understanding may limit comprehension of global climate dynamics and support for international climate agreements. Education campaigns should balance local environmental management (reducing deforestation, bushfires) with understanding of global climate mechanisms. The minimal recognition of earthquakes (6.9%), mudslides (3.6%) and tsunamis (3.3%) as climate hazards reflects appropriate geographical context, these events are extremely rare or absent in The Gambia. This specificity suggests community knowledge derives from lived experience rather than generic climate change messaging, which could be leveraged for more effective risk communication.

Study strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several notable strengths. First, it represents the first comprehensive, population-based assessment of climate-health awareness in The Gambia, filling a critical evidence gap. Second, the robust multistage sampling design and substantial sample size (n=868, 96.9% response rate) enhance representativeness and generalisability to similar rural West African contexts. Third, data collection in multiple local languages (Mandinka, Wolof and Fula) minimised language barriers and promoted inclusive participation. Fourth, the use of validated instruments adapted from international studies enables cross-national comparisons while maintaining cultural relevance. Fifth, rigorous statistical approaches including multivariable regression with appropriate model diagnostics strengthen causal inference regarding awareness determinants.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding awareness determinants and cannot assess temporal trends in awareness or behavioural change resulting from knowledge. Longitudinal research would better elucidate awareness trajectories and intervention impacts. Second, the study was conducted exclusively in North Bank Region, potentially limiting generalisability to urban areas, coastal zones or other regions with different climate vulnerability profiles. Third, awareness was assessed through self-report, which may be subject to social desirability bias, with respondents potentially over-reporting knowledge or under-reporting knowledge gaps. Fourth, the study assessed awareness but not comprehension depth, respondents may recognise terms without fully understanding mechanisms. Fifth, no objective assessment of actual climate-health literacy or behavioural practices was conducted; awareness may not translate to protective behaviours. Sixth, the categorisation of awareness as a binary variable (aware/not aware) may oversimplify a continuous construct and reduce analytical nuance. Seventh, design-based weighting using sampling probability weights was not applied in the primary analysis, which may modestly affect SEs and population-level inference; however, a weighted sensitivity analysis yielded consistent findings (see online supplemental table S1).

Public health and policy implications

These findings have several critical implications for public health policy and climate adaptation planning in The Gambia:

Health system integration: The Ministry of Health’s commitment to integrating climate considerations into health service delivery10 Ministry of Health, 2021 should be operationalised through climate-sensitive health programming, including heat-health warning systems, flood preparedness protocols and climate-sensitive disease surveillance for vector-borne and waterborne diseases.

Targeted education interventions: The strong education gradient in awareness indicates that climate-health education should be integrated into primary and secondary school curricula. Additionally, community-based education programmes should target populations with limited formal education, particularly using radio and community health workers as delivery channels.

Youth engagement: The lower awareness among younger age groups represents both a challenge and opportunity. Youth-focused climate education initiatives, potentially through schools and youth organisations, could build long-term adaptive capacity.

Community-based adaptation: The 76.7% of respondents reporting direct experience with climate change manifestations provides a foundation for community-based adaptation interventions. Participatory vulnerability assessments, building on community knowledge, could identify locally appropriate adaptation measures.

Early warning systems: High awareness of flooding and heat risks should be complemented by functional early warning systems, evacuation protocols and emergency response capabilities tailored to recognised hazards.

Sustainable energy transition: The heavy reliance on biomass fuels requires policy interventions promoting clean cooking solutions (improved cookstoves, liquefied petroleum gas subsidies, biogas systems) and expanded renewable electricity access. Such interventions simultaneously address deforestation, indoor air pollution and climate change mitigation.

Climate-resilient agriculture: The strong recognition of rainfall variability impacts on agriculture should catalyse investment in climate-smart agriculture, drought-resistant crop varieties, improved irrigation and crop diversification to enhance food security under changing climate conditions.

Conclusion

This study establishes that communities in North Bank Region, The Gambia, demonstrate substantial awareness of climate change and its multifaceted public health impacts. With 85.7% overall awareness and strong recognition of specific climate-health linkages, these findings indicate a solid foundation for community-based climate adaptation interventions. However, significant disparities exist across educational and age groups, requiring targeted interventions to ensure equitable climate-health literacy. The study reveals that community knowledge derives primarily from direct experience and radio-based information dissemination, with less influence from formal health and agricultural extension systems. This presents both opportunities leveraging trusted radio channels and experiential learning and challenges ensuring scientific accuracy and a comprehensive understanding of climate mechanisms beyond immediate observations. Key determinants of awareness namely age and education, point to specific populations requiring enhanced outreach: younger individuals who will experience long-term climate impacts but currently demonstrate lower awareness, and populations with limited formal education who may lack scientific frameworks for understanding climate processes. Educational interventions should integrate climate-health topics into school curricula while simultaneously implementing community-based education programmes using radio, community health workers and participatory approaches for populations with limited formal education. The overwhelming dependence on biomass fuels (firewood and charcoal) creates interlinked challenges of deforestation, indoor air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions. Addressing this requires integrated policy responses promoting clean cooking solutions, expanding renewable energy access and supporting sustainable land management interventions that simultaneously advance climate mitigation, adaptation and immediate public health goals.
