From intelligent models to clinical tools: the evolving landscape of AI in medical imaging
Authors: Dmitrii Kaplun, Markus Graf, Haifeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntegration of Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, into medical imaging represents a profound shift in diagnostic medicine, moving from purely descriptive analysis to advanced predictive and prescriptive analytics. This Collection explores the rapid advancement of AI-driven tools in their specific fields such as oncology, cardiology, ophthalmology and so on, highlighting their potential to improve diagnostic accuracy, workflow efficiency, and personalized treatment planning. However, significant challenges remain, including the heterogeneity of medical image data, the “black box” nature of some intelligent models, and the critical hurdles of clinical integration and validation. The research presented here addresses these frontiers, showcasing innovations in algorithm development, explainable AI, and translational application. This Editorial synthesizes the contributions and outlines the essential collaborative pathway—uniting computer scientists, clinicians, and regulatory bodies—required to translate algorithmic promise into robust, trustworthy, and equitable clinical tools that genuinely improve patient care.Subject terms: Medical imaging, Imaging

Introduction

The advent of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in medical imaging is not just an incremental improvement but a foundational transformation. Using deep learning, these systems promise to move beyond traditional manual interpretation of images—a task subject to human variability and fatigue—towards a future of automated, quantitative, and predictive analytics. The impetus for this shift is clear: an ever-growing volume of imaging data, increasing computational power, and the urgent need for tools that can support clinical decision-making in the face of complex disease patterns. This Collection, "Artificial intelligence and medical imaging” (https://www.nature.com/collections/bjeiihhgfa), captures a snapshot of this dynamic field at a pivotal moment, presenting research that both demonstrates the remarkable potential of these technologies and thoughtfully engages with the substantial obstacles that lie between a well-trained intelligent algorithm and its effective use at the patient’s bedside.

The promise: enhancing the diagnostic pipeline

The core promise of AI in medical imaging lies in its ability to enhance human expertise. The papers in this Collection exemplify this in various anatomical domains and imaging modalities. For example, the work on intelligent retinal disease detection using deep learning illustrates how algorithms can detect pathologies with high sensitivity, offering a scalable solution for early intervention in populations where access to specialist ophthalmologists is limited 1–3. Similarly, research into a deep learning framework for automated frame selection in kidney ultrasound tackles a fundamental, yet time-consuming, pre-analytic step 4. By ensuring that only diagnostically optimal images are forwarded for interpretation, such tools can streamline workflow, reduce sonographer variability, and allow clinicians to focus their cognitive efforts on diagnosis rather than image curation.

Perhaps most significantly, AI enables a shift from detection to characterization and prediction. The contribution to the diagnosis of NextGen lung disease with explainable artificial intelligence points toward this future 5,6. Here, the goal extends beyond simply identifying a nodule; it involves analyzing its texture, shape, and growth patterns to predict malignancy risk or therapeutic response. This predictive profiling, powered by AI’s capacity to find subtle, multi-dimensional patterns invisible to the human eye, is the cornerstone of moving toward more personalized medicine. It transforms the image from a static picture into a dynamic data source rich with prognostic information. This principle is further exemplified by the work on multi-modal data integration for complex disease diagnosis 7.

The hurdles: robustness, interpretation, and integration

Despite this promise, the path to clinical adoption is fraught with well-documented challenges, a reality this Collection does not shy away from. The introductory note rightly identifies the heterogeneity and complexity of medical images as a primary barrier. For instance, an algorithm trained on curated data from a single manufacturer’s CT scanner can fail spectacularly when applied to images from a different institution with alternative protocols, patient populations, or hardware 8. This lack of robustness and generalizability remains a critical failure point for many intelligent models that perform excellently in controlled retrospective studies but falter in real-world settings, underscoring the need for advanced architectural solutions 9,10.

Closely linked is the challenge of interpretability. Superior performance of a deep neural network often comes at the cost of transparency. This so called “black box” problem—where even its developers cannot fully explain why a model arrived at a specific output—is antithetical to the principles of clinical medicine, where understanding the “why” is essential for trust and informed decision-making. This is why the emphasis on explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), as demonstrated in the lung disease diagnosis paper and others focusing on model transparency and trustworthiness, is so vital 3,11–13. Techniques that generate visual heatmaps or provide logical rationales for a model’s findings are not academic luxuries; they are prerequisites for building clinician confidence and facilitating a productive human-AI partnership.

Finally, and most dauntingly, is the challenge of intelligent model integration into the clinical workflow. A technically brilliant algorithm is useless if it requires 15 additional steps, disrupts a radiologist’s workflow, or outputs information that does not align with clinical decision pathways. Successful integration requires co-design with end-users—like radiologists, technologists, and referring physicians—from the earliest stages of developments. It demands that AI tools can be evaluated not just by metrics such as area-under-the-curve (AUC), but also by their impact on reporting turnaround time, diagnostic confidence, and, ultimately, patient outcomes, as explored in studies on automated analysis for surgical planning and staging 14,15.

The foundations: a framework for responsible translation

To effectively address these challenges, the field must adopt a coordinated, multi-stakeholder framework for the responsible translation of AI technologies into clinical practice. The research presented in this Collection, which is primarily oriented toward intelligent model development and technical validation, constitutes the first essential foundation of this framework 1–3,9,16.

The second foundation is rigorous, prospective clinical validation. This requires moving beyond internal or retrospective test sets to well-designed, multi-centre clinical trials that evaluate an AI system’s performance, clinical utility, and impact on workflow in real-world healthcare settings, with explicit comparison to the current standard of care.

The third foundation is the development and implementation of robust regulatory and ethical governance mechanisms. Regulatory agencies globally are progressively refining their frameworks for software as a medical device (SaMD). Clearly articulated pathways are required to assess AI systems with adaptive or continuously learning behaviour, in a manner that preserves patient safety and clinical effectiveness while not unduly constraining innovation.

From an ethical perspective, it is critical to mitigate algorithmic bias by ensuring that training datasets are representative of heterogeneous and historically underrepresented populations, thereby reducing the risk of systematically poorer performance in these groups. Furthermore, transparent delineation of responsibility and accountability in human–AI collaborative decision-making must be maintained. These concerns are directly engaged by emerging research that characterizes and seeks to remediate bias in medical AI systems 11,17,18.

The road ahead: collaborative intelligence

The future illuminated by this Collection is not one of AI replacing radiologists, but of augmented intelligence. The most powerful diagnostic entity will be the synergy between human clinical judgment, with its nuanced understanding of context and patient narrative, and AI’s computational prowess in pattern recognition and quantitative analysis. The goal is a collaborative loop: AI handles high-volume screening and quantitative measurements, flagging complex cases and presenting predictive analytics; the clinician provides oversight, integrates multimodal data, and makes the final diagnostic and therapeutic judgment.

Future research must therefore focus on designing these collaborative interfaces and studying their effects. We need to understand how AI output should be presented, how it influences decision-making (for better or worse), and how to train the next generation of clinicians to be savvy consumers and interpreters of AI-generated insights. The focus will expand from disease detection or classification to holistic patient profiling, integrating imaging data with genomics, electronic health records, and real-world monitoring data to build comprehensive models for personalized health management, as seen in applications beyond traditional radiology 19–21.

Conclusion

All of the papers gathered in this Collection provide compelling evidence that AI is reshaping the very fabric of medical imaging 1–3,22,23. From automating tedious tasks to unlocking novel biomarkers within familiar images, the potential to improve diagnostic accuracy, efficiency, and personalization is immense. However, as the research also implicitly argues, realizing this potential requires more than sophisticated codes. It demands a fundamental commitment to addressing the "last-mile" problems of clinical integration: building robust, interpretable, and equitable systems through deep collaboration across disciplines. The journey from a promising intelligent model to a trusted clinical tool is complex, but by integrating innovation into clinical need and ethical rigour 11,13,17, we can steer this powerful technology toward a future where it truly improves the care of every patient.
