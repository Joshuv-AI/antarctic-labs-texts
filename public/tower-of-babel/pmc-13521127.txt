Effects of mineral-modified and diluted deep-sea water on laying performance, nutrient utilization, egg quality, yolk lipid composition, oxidative stability, immunoglobulin Y, and mineral composition in laying hens
Authors: Bounmy Keohavong, Sang Jip Ohh, Sung Ki Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effects of filtered deep-sea water (DSW) on laying performance, nutrient digestibility, egg yolk lipid composition, oxidative stability, and immunoglobulin Y (IgY) content in Lohmann Brown Lite hens. A total of 480 hens (25 weeks old) were assigned to four treatments: tap water (control), untreated DSW, reverse osmosis–treated DSW, and nanofiltration–treated DSW, with all DSW diluted 1:20. Over four weeks, production performance and water intake were recorded, and eggs were analyzed at the end of the trial. Filtered DSW, particularly RO- and NF-treated water, improved selected parameters of nutrient digestibility, including crude protein, energy, and ether extract utilization, although responses varied among treatments without affecting egg production, egg weight, or feed efficiency. Yolk fatty acid composition was not significantly altered, and unsaturated fatty acid concentrations did not increase significantly with DSW supplementation, while cholesterol, lipid oxidation (MDA), and IgY concentration were unchanged. These results indicate that filtered DSW may enhance specific aspects of nutrient utilization and modify egg mineral characteristics without compromising productivity or egg immunological properties.

Introduction

Optimizing nutrient utilization, egg quality, and immune status in laying hens remains a major objective in poultry science because these factors directly determine production efficiency, profitability, and the nutritional value of eggs for consumers (Leeson & Summers, 2001). Egg quality is a multifactorial trait influenced by genetic background, laying age, nutrition, environmental conditions, and management practices. Among nutritional characteristics, egg yolk lipid composition, including fatty acid profile and cholesterol content, represents an important determinant of egg nutritional quality and reflects metabolic responses to dietary and physiological conditions (Elkin & Harvatine, 2023). In addition, oxidative stability of yolk lipids is a critical determinant of egg quality and storage stability because lipid peroxidation results in the formation of secondary oxidation products, including malondialdehyde (MDA), which contribute to deterioration of nutritional value and shelf life. Recent studies have demonstrated that dietary strategies involving functional ingredients and bioactive compounds can enhance antioxidant capacity and reduce yolk MDA accumulation during storage (Attia et al., 2024; Deniz et al., 2025). Furthermore, egg yolk immunoglobulin Y (IgY), a major maternal antibody transferred from hens to eggs, plays an important role in passive immunity and has increasingly been considered a functional indicator of hen health and immune competence (Schade et al., 2005). Recent studies have emphasized that egg production performance, egg quality characteristics, and yolk antioxidant capacity are regulated by complex interactions between nutritional strategies and environmental factors. Housing systems, for example, can influence laying persistence, feed efficiency, welfare status, and egg characteristics throughout the production cycle. Enriched cage systems have been reported to improve several production and welfare-related parameters, while also affecting egg quality traits during extended laying periods (Onbaşılar et al., 2020). Similarly, cage type, genotype, and laying age were shown to influence egg weight, shell characteristics, albumen and yolk indices, and Haugh unit values, highlighting the importance of considering environmental conditions when evaluating nutritional interventions in laying hens (Onbaşılar et al., 2018).

Water quality is an increasingly recognized component of poultry nutrition because drinking water represents the most frequently consumed nutrient source and directly influences nutrient utilization, physiological regulation, and animal performance. Mineral composition, total dissolved solids (TDS), and specific ionic profiles of drinking water can affect digestive processes and metabolic responses in poultry. Elevated mineral concentrations in drinking water have been shown to influence amino acid digestibility and growth performance, emphasizing the importance of water composition in precision poultry nutrition (Raut et al., 2025). Deep-sea water (DSW), a naturally mineral-rich resource containing magnesium, calcium, potassium, and various trace elements, has attracted interest because of its potential biological activities. Unlike conventional mineral mixtures that are generally formulated to provide specific minerals at predetermined concentrations, DSW contains a naturally occurring combination of dissolved minerals and trace elements that may provide a broader ionic profile and potential synergistic biological effects. Therefore, DSW supplementation represents a functional water-based nutritional approach rather than merely a source of mineral replacement. Previous research demonstrated that DSW supplementation can influence mineral metabolism and physiological responses in animals, while poultry studies have suggested potential benefits associated with production performance and metabolic regulation (Keohavong et al., 2010). Beyond mineral supply, DSW-derived components have demonstrated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties in other animal models, including reductions in oxidative stress and improvements in lipid metabolism under metabolic challenge conditions (Wu et al., 2022). Moreover, human intervention studies using DSW-based beverages have reported beneficial effects on gut-associated biomarkers and immunological parameters, suggesting that mineral-rich water sources may exert systemic physiological effects beyond simple electrolyte supplementation (Takeuchi et al., 2022).

Nutritional modulation of egg composition and antioxidant capacity has been extensively investigated through dietary approaches involving functional feed ingredients, omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), trace minerals, and phytogenic compounds. These strategies have demonstrated the ability to modify yolk fatty acid profiles, regulate lipid metabolism, enhance antioxidant defense systems, and improve the functional value of eggs (Attia et al., 2024; Elkin & Harvatine, 2023). For example, dietary enrichment with flaxseed-derived products has been shown to influence yolk lipid composition and antioxidant status, providing opportunities to produce value-added eggs with improved nutritional characteristics (Attia et al., 2024). Similarly, phytogenic feed additives have gained attention as natural alternatives to synthetic compounds because of their antioxidant, antimicrobial, and metabolic regulatory properties. Recent evidence demonstrated that dietary fennel volatile oil supplementation improved laying performance, eggshell quality, and yolk oxidative stability by reducing MDA accumulation during storage, indicating the potential of plant-derived bioactive compounds to extend egg shelf life and enhance egg quality (Deniz et al., 2025). However, although considerable progress has been made regarding dietary manipulation of egg quality, the potential application of functional water sources as nutritional interventions remains insufficiently explored, particularly regarding their effects on nutrient digestibility, yolk lipid metabolism, oxidative stability, and immune-related egg components such as IgY. Recent findings from water-based supplementation strategies, including bioactive compounds such as Spirulina-derived nutrients, indicate that drinking water interventions can influence immune responses and egg nutritional characteristics, supporting the concept that water composition may represent an additional pathway for precision nutrition in poultry production (Vui et al., 2025).

Therefore, considering the unique mineral profile of DSW and its reported biological activities across animal and human studies, we hypothesized that filtered DSW, including reverse osmosis (RO) and nanofiltration (NF) treatments, could beneficially regulate nutrient utilization, egg production performance, yolk lipid composition, oxidative stability, and IgY deposition in laying hens. The present study was designed to evaluate the effects of DSW supplementation as a functional water source in Lohmann Brown Lite hens by assessing laying performance, nutrient digestibility, internal and external egg quality traits, egg yolk fatty acid composition, oxidative stability, and immune-related egg characteristics. The findings may provide new insights into the application of mineral-rich water resources as a complementary nutritional strategy for improving egg quality, sustainability, and functional value in modern poultry production.

Materials and methods

Animal care and ethical statement

This experiment was conducted at the facility of Kangwon National University after receiving approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee with ethical code b: KW-220413-1.

Experimental design and animal management

The study was conducted at the animal house facility of the College of Animal Life Sciences, Kangwon National University, Republic of Korea, during the winter season, using 480 Lohmann Brown Lite laying hens (25 weeks of age; mean body weight 1962 ± 190 g; hen-day egg production 92%). Deep seawater (DSW) was collected from a depth of 300 m and processed using either reverse osmosis (RO) or nanofiltration (NF), with both untreated and treated samples provided by the Korea Institute of Water and Environment. Because the salinity of DSW samples was substantially higher than that of conventional drinking water, all DSW sources used for animal supplementation were diluted at a ratio of 1:20 (DSW:deionized water, v/v) before administration to hens. This 1:20 dilution represented the final drinking-water concentration supplied throughout the experiment. Given that DSW salinity ranged from 3.34% to 5.56% (Table 1), all DSW samples were diluted before administration. Table 1 presents both the mineral composition of the original DSW samples and the corresponding mineral concentrations of the final 1:20 diluted drinking-water solutions supplied to the hens. Four experimental treatments were established: T1, control (tap water); T2, untreated DSW; T3, RO-treated DSW; and T4, NF-treated DSW, with all DSW treatments diluted 1:20. Hens were randomly assigned to triple-deck battery cages (35 × 35 × 40 cm) equipped with linear feeders and trough drinkers, housing two hens per adjacent cage and providing 612.5 cm² of floor space per hen, with each replicate comprising 20 hens and six replicates per treatment. Environmental conditions were maintained at 21 ± 2.26 °C with a 16 h light:8 h dark schedule and 70 ± 10.1% relative humidity. Feed and water were provided daily to allow voluntary intake. All DSW-treated groups received the same basal diet (Table 2), while the control group received the basal diet supplemented with 0.2% salt to meet sodium requirements. Feed and water were supplied and checked twice daily at 09:00 and 17:00, during which egg collection and routine husbandry observations were also performed. Egg collection was facilitated by linear plastic collectors, while excreta were removed daily via a semi-automatic belt conveyor system. Water troughs were cleaned daily, and both water provided and residual volumes were recorded to calculate salt intake. Body weight was monitored by randomly selecting and weighing five hens per replicate on days 1, 14, and 28. The four-week experimental period included daily measurements of hen-day egg production, egg weight, feed intake, feed conversion ratio (g feed/g egg), water intake, and mortality (Table 3). Chemical analyses and nutrient assessment

Untreated deep seawater (DSW) and reverse osmosis-treated DSW were obtained as previously described by Keohavong et al. (2010). The mineral composition of untreated DSW (U-DSW) and reverse osmosis-treated DSW (RO-DSW) presented in Table 1 was obtained from Keohavong et al. (2010), whereas the mineral composition of nanofiltration-treated DSW (NF-DSW) was determined in the present study by the Central Laboratory of Kangwon National University using standard analytical procedures. Table 1 has been revised to clearly distinguish literature-derived data from experimentally determined values. To facilitate interpretation of the mineral intake by the experimental birds, the mineral concentrations of the final 1:20 diluted DSW solutions administered to the hens were calculated from the original concentrations and are also presented in Table 1. These values represent the actual mineral concentrations supplied in the drinking water throughout the feeding trial.

Because NF-treated DSW contained relatively high mineral concentrations, NF-DSW was further pre-diluted 1:1000 with deionized water only for laboratory mineral analysis to ensure accurate quantification within the analytical detection range. This analytical dilution was performed exclusively for instrumental analysis and was independent of the animal supplementation protocol. For the feeding experiment, NF-treated DSW was directly diluted 1:20 with deionized water and provided to the hens as the final treatment solution (T4). Mineral analysis of NF-treated DSW was performed by the Central Laboratory of Kangwon National University. The mineral profiles of untreated DSW and RO-treated DSW are cited from the previous characterization reported by Keohavong et al. (2010), as these water sources originated from the same production batches used in the present experiment.

At the end of the feeding trial, chromic oxide (Cr₂O₃) was incorporated into the diets at 3.5 g/kg as an indigestible marker. Colored excreta were collected daily at 09:00 h over three consecutive days (72 h), with approximately 200 g collected per replicate. Excreta collected within each replicate were pooled, dried in a forced-air oven at 65 °C to constant weight, ground, and used as one composite sample for laboratory analysis. The chemical composition of diets and excreta, including dry matter, crude protein, gross energy, ash, and ether extract, was determined using official methods (AOAC International, 2005). Chromic oxide concentrations in both diets and excreta were measured following Fenton and Fenton (1979), and nutrient utilization was calculated according to Tarachai and Yamauchi (2001) (Table 4). At the end of the experimental period, thirty-six eggs per treatment (six eggs per replicate) were collected to evaluate both external and internal egg quality parameters (Table 5). Analysis of immunoglobulin Y

At the end of the experimental period, three eggs per replicate (18 eggs per treatment) of similar weight were collected for egg yolk immunoglobulin Y (IgY) purification and quantification. IgY concentrations were determined for each egg sample, and the average value of the three eggs within each replicate was used for statistical analysis.

Isolation of the water-soluble fraction

Egg yolks were separated from the egg white using an egg yolk separator, rinsed with deionized water, and drained (10 g per sample, equivalent to 10 mL) into 250 mL beakers (SCHOTT DURAN, Germany). Each sample was mixed with 50 mL of cold acidified water (pH 4.0, adjusted with 0.1 N HCl), followed by 40 mL of cold acidified water (pH 2.0, adjusted with 0.1 N HCl) to reach a total volume of 100 mL while stirring gently to minimize foaming. The resulting mixture (pH ∼5.0) was incubated at 4 °C for 24 h, then transferred to graduated cylinders and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant, representing the water-soluble fraction (WSF), was collected for IgY quantification. This procedure was adapted from the lithium sulfate precipitation method of Bizhanov et al. (2004) with minor modifications.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The IgY concentration was determined using an indirect ELISA kit (E30-104) following the procedure described by Keohavong et al. (2010) with slight modifications. Coating solution was prepared by adding 10 µL of goat anti-chicken IgG (A30-104A-11) to 1 mL of 0.05 M carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6), and 100 µL was added to each ELISA well (Apogent Co., Denmark) and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h. Wells were washed five times with PBS-T (50 mM Tris, 0.14 M NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 8.0) and blocked with 200 µL of 1% BSA in PBS-T at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing, 100 µL of diluted WSF samples (1:100,000 in blocking solution) and standards (Chicken Reference Serum, RS10-102-3) were added and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min, followed by five washes with PBS-T. HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (A30-104P, 1:40,000 in PBS-T, 100 µL) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Wells were washed five times, and 100 µL of TMB substrate solution (2 µL 30% H₂O₂ + 1 tablet TMB + 1 mL DMSO + 9 mL phosphate-citrate buffer) was added. After 30 min at room temperature, the reaction was stopped with 100 µL of 2 M H₂SO₄, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader (BioTek, USA). Each sample was assayed in duplicate with three independent repetitions. IgY concentration was expressed as mg/mL of egg yolk.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure of SAS software (SAS Institute, 2004). The experiment was conducted as a completely randomized design, with deep-sea water source included as the fixed effect. Each treatment consisted of six replicates (20 hens per replicate), and the replicate cage was considered the experimental unit for all statistical analyses. Individual hens were not considered independent experimental units. Data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the overall effects of treatments. When a significant treatment effect was detected, differences among treatment means were examined using Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Results are presented as treatment means with the corresponding standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05, while values of 0.05 ≤ p < 0.10 were considered indicative of a statistical trend (Steel & Torrie, 1980). Exact p-values from the ANOVA are reported in the tables.

Results

Laying performance and nutrient digestibility

The effects of filtered deep-sea water (DSW) supplementation on laying performance of hens during 25–29 weeks of age are summarized in Table 3. Overall, DSW treatments did not significantly affect hen-day egg production, egg weight, feed conversion ratio (FCR), or average daily feed intake (ADFI) throughout the experimental period (p > 0.05). Hen-day egg production ranged from 91.04% to 93.61% across treatments, with the highest numerical value observed in the RO-treated DSW group (T3). Similarly, egg weight was consistent across treatments, averaging approximately 60 g, and FCR remained unaffected, indicating that DSW supplementation at a 1:20 dilution did not compromise feed efficiency. Average daily water intake tended to be slightly higher in DSW-treated groups, particularly in the NF-treated DSW group (T4), although differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.164). Mortality was minimal during the experimental period, with no mortality observed during Phase I and only a small numerical incidence recorded in the NF-treated DSW group (T4) during Phase II (0.06%), with no significant differences among treatments.

Nutrient digestibility was significantly influenced by DSW treatments (Table 4). Dry matter digestibility was enhanced in NF-treated DSW hens (T4) compared with the RO-treated DSW group (p = 0.023). Crude protein digestibility was highest in untreated DSW (T2) and NF-treated DSW groups (T4), demonstrating a significant improvement relative to control (p = 0.001). Energy digestibility also increased in NF-treated DSW hens (p = 0.045), while ether extract digestibility was significantly higher in T4 compared with T1–T3 (p = 0.037). Conversely, ash digestibility was reduced in untreated (T2) and RO-treated DSW (T3) groups (p = 0.002).

Egg quality, yolk lipid composition, and oxidative stability

Filtered deep-sea water (DSW) supplementation did not significantly alter external or internal egg quality characteristics in laying hens during the 25–29-week experimental period (p > 0.05; Table 5). Parameters such as egg shape index, shell thickness, shell percentage, shell color, and eggshell strength were comparable across all treatment groups. Likewise, albumen quality, assessed via Haugh unit and albumen height, and yolk quality, including yolk index and color, showed no significant differences between DSW-treated and control hens. These findings indicate that DSW administration, whether untreated or treated by reverse osmosis (RO) or nanofiltration (NF), maintains standard egg quality attributes under standard management conditions.

Egg yolk lipid composition, including total cholesterol, saturated fatty acids (SFA), unsaturated fatty acids (UFA), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), and fatty acid ratios (UFA/SFA, MUFA/SFA, PUFA/SFA, and n6/n3), was not significantly affected by DSW treatments (p > 0.05; Table 6). Numerical trends such as slightly higher PUFA content in the untreated DSW group were observed but did not reach statistical significance, indicating that DSW does not adversely influence yolk lipid profiles. Cholesterol content remained stable across all treatments, suggesting that DSW supplementation does not increase yolk cholesterol levels, a relevant factor for consumer health.

The oxidative stability of yolk lipids, assessed using the 2-thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay, was unaffected by DSW supplementation at all measured storage intervals (1, 4, and 8 days; p = 0.632–0.941; Table 7). Malondialdehyde (MDA) formation was consistent among treatments, demonstrating that DSW does not promote lipid peroxidation during refrigerated storage. Egg yolk immunoglobulin Y (IgY) and mineral composition

The concentration of egg yolk immunoglobulin Y (IgY) was not significantly influenced by DSW supplementation in laying hens (p = 0.621; Table 7). Hens receiving untreated, RO-treated, or NF-treated DSW produced eggs with IgY levels comparable to those of the control group, indicating that filtered DSW does not adversely affect the maternal transfer of IgY to the yolk. This suggests that DSW supplementation maintains the immunological quality of eggs, which is critical for passive immunity in progeny and overall egg functionality.

Egg yolk and albumen mineral composition were significantly affected by DSW treatments, with specific trends depending on the mineral type (Table 8). Significant differences in yolk Mg and Na concentrations were observed among treatments (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01, respectively). Likewise, Ca, K, Cu, Zn, and Fe concentrations in the yolk varied according to the DSW treatment (p < 0.001 to p < 0.01). In the albumen, significant treatment effects were also detected for Na, Mg, and Ca (p < 0.001), whereas K and the trace minerals Cu, Zn, and Fe exhibited treatment-dependent differences (p < 0.05 to p < 0.001). Manganese (Mn) levels in both yolk and albumen remained unchanged across treatments (p > 0.05). These findings indicate that DSW supplementation influenced mineral composition in egg yolk and albumen. However, because DSW is a naturally mineral-rich water source and the treatments were not formulated on an equal sodium basis, the observed differences should be interpreted as reflecting the combined effects of the mineral composition of the drinking water rather than the effect of any individual mineral. Discussion

Laying performance and nutrient digestibility

Supplementation with filtered deep-sea water (DSW) at a 1:20 dilution did not adversely affect productive performance, indicating that the mineral concentrations provided through drinking water were within a physiological tolerance range for laying hens. Although the original DSW contained high salinity (3.34–5.56%), the final supplementation solution after 1:20 dilution provided a much lower mineral concentration, which was comparable to a mild mineral supplementation strategy rather than a high-salt challenge. This concentration was selected to avoid excessive sodium intake while allowing the potential physiological effects of naturally occurring minerals and trace elements to be evaluated. Previous studies have suggested that appropriate mineral supplementation through drinking water can support electrolyte balance, physiological homeostasis, and production stability in poultry (Raut et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2022). The improvement in nutrient utilization observed with NF-treated DSW may be related to the specific mineral profile and ionic composition retained after nanofiltration. Unlike conventional mineral mixtures that provide selected minerals at fixed concentrations, DSW contains a naturally occurring combination of dissolved minerals and trace elements that may influence digestive processes through multiple pathways. Minerals such as magnesium and calcium are involved in enzymatic activation, intestinal function, and nutrient transport, which may contribute to improved nutrient availability when supplied at appropriate levels (Keohavong et al., 2010; Takeuchi et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022). However, the response among DSW treatments was not uniform, suggesting that mineral concentration alone does not fully explain the effects on nutrient utilization. Differences in mineral composition after RO and NF processing may have altered the balance and bioavailability of individual minerals, resulting in treatment-dependent effects on nutrient utilization. In particular, excessive or imbalanced mineral intake may reduce the efficiency of mineral absorption, which could explain the lower ash utilization observed in some DSW treatments. Overall, these findings suggest that appropriately processed and diluted DSW may serve as a complementary water-based nutritional strategy to support nutrient utilization in laying hens. Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying the interaction between DSW-derived minerals and intestinal nutrient transport require further investigation, including studies evaluating mineral absorption pathways, gut microbiota responses, and long-term physiological adaptation.

Egg quality, yolk lipid composition, and oxidative stability

Supplementation of drinking water with filtered deep-sea water (DSW) appeared to maintain normal egg formation processes under the experimental conditions, suggesting that mineral enrichment through drinking water did not disturb physiological mechanisms involved in eggshell formation, albumen deposition, or yolk development. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that bioactive water sources or functional feed additives can support hen physiological status without negatively affecting egg quality characteristics (Vui et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). The absence of major changes in egg quality traits may be attributed to the moderate mineral exposure provided by the diluted DSW, which supplied additional minerals without causing excessive electrolyte imbalance or metabolic stress. The stability of yolk lipid composition across DSW treatments suggests that short-term mineral supplementation through drinking water has limited influence on lipid synthesis and fatty acid deposition pathways in laying hens. Unlike dietary supplementation with lipid-based ingredients, such as omega-3 fatty acids or plant oils, mineral-rich water primarily affects electrolyte balance and mineral availability rather than directly altering fatty acid metabolism. Similar observations have been reported in functional feed studies where improvements in antioxidant status or egg quality were achieved without substantial modification of total yolk lipid profiles (Vui et al., 2024).

Maintenance of yolk oxidative stability during storage indicates that DSW supplementation did not increase susceptibility of yolk lipids to oxidation. This effect may be associated with the contribution of mineral elements present in DSW, including magnesium, calcium, zinc, and other trace minerals, which participate in antioxidant defense systems through enzyme activation and regulation of cellular oxidative balance (Arbabi-Motlagh et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022). Similar mechanisms have been suggested for mineral-based nutritional interventions and bioactive water supplementation strategies that support oxidative stability and product quality (Takeuchi et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025). The relationship between mineral nutrition and yolk lipid oxidation has also been demonstrated in conventional dietary supplementation studies. For example, Dotas et al. (2023) showed that plant-derived bioactive compounds could modify yolk fatty acid composition and improve oxidative stability, whereas Arbabi-Motlagh et al. (2022) demonstrated that zinc and selenium supplementation enhanced antioxidant protection and reduced lipid oxidation under oxidative stress conditions. These findings highlight that antioxidant protection of egg lipids can be achieved through different nutritional pathways, including mineral-mediated and phytogenic mechanisms. Although DSW alone produced limited changes in yolk fatty acid composition, its ability to provide a naturally balanced mineral profile may complement other functional nutritional strategies. Combining mineral-rich water supplementation with targeted bioactive compounds, such as omega-3 sources or natural antioxidants, may represent a potential approach for further improving egg nutritional value and storage stability. Future studies should investigate whether longer-term DSW administration or combination strategies can produce measurable improvements in yolk functional properties.

Egg yolk immunoglobulin Y and mineral enrichment

The preservation of egg yolk immunoglobulin Y (IgY) concentration following DSW supplementation indicates that mineral enrichment through drinking water did not disrupt maternal immune function or the biological processes responsible for antibody transfer into developing yolks. IgY is the primary avian immunoglobulin transferred from hens to eggs and plays an essential role in passive immunity of offspring, making its stability an important consideration when developing functional egg production strategies (Capotă et al., 2025; Schade et al., 2005). The unchanged IgY response may be explained by the highly regulated nature of antibody synthesis and deposition in laying hens. Unlike mineral deposition, which is directly influenced by mineral availability and transport mechanisms, IgY transfer involves specific immunological regulation and receptor-mediated transport pathways that may remain stable under moderate nutritional variation. The structural stability of avian IgY may also contribute to this resilience, as previous studies have demonstrated that IgY maintains functional integrity under various environmental and physicochemical conditions due to unique structural characteristics of the avian immunoglobulin molecule (Gandhi & Alshehri, 2020). Therefore, the current findings suggest that DSW supplementation can modify mineral availability without negatively affecting the functional immune components of eggs. The ability of DSW to influence mineral deposition in egg components reflects the importance of mineral bioavailability and ionic composition in determining nutrient transfer from maternal circulation to developing eggs. Minerals supplied through drinking water may undergo absorption, systemic circulation, and subsequent incorporation into yolk and albumen, similar to minerals provided through conventional dietary supplementation. Previous studies have demonstrated that improved mineral availability can enhance mineral transfer into eggs and influence egg nutritional characteristics (Dong et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024).

The differences among DSW treatments further indicate that water-processing methods may alter mineral profiles and their subsequent biological availability. Reverse osmosis and nanofiltration modify the concentration and balance of dissolved minerals; therefore, the nutritional effects of processed DSW likely depend not only on total mineral concentration but also on the relative proportion and interaction among individual minerals. This highlights the importance of evaluating DSW as a complex mineral matrix rather than as a single-mineral supplementation source. Although mineral enrichment through drinking water may provide opportunities for producing value-added eggs, maintaining an appropriate mineral balance remains essential. Excessive or disproportionate mineral intake can negatively influence physiological regulation, water consumption, and nutrient utilization, as reported for poultry exposed to highly mineralized water sources (Raut et al., 2025). In the present study, the use of diluted and processed DSW provided mineral supplementation within a manageable range, supporting its potential application as a complementary nutritional approach. Overall, the findings demonstrate that DSW supplementation represents an alternative pathway for enhancing egg mineral characteristics while maintaining the stability of immune-related egg components. Further research involving longer supplementation periods, mineral bioavailability assessments, and evaluation of functional properties of mineral-enriched eggs will help clarify the practical application of DSW in sustainable poultry production systems.

Conclusions

Supplementation of filtered deep-sea water (DSW), particularly reverse osmosis- and nanofiltration-treated water, did not adversely affect laying performance, feed intake, or feed conversion ratio, while showing potential to improve specific aspects of nutrient utilization. DSW modulated nutrient digestibility, with significant responses observed only for selected nutrients and specific treatment groups, suggesting that the mineral profile of processed DSW may influence nutrient utilization efficiency. However, these effects were not consistent across all DSW treatments and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Although yolk lipid composition was not markedly altered, DSW supplementation did not significantly increase yolk unsaturated fatty acid concentrations but maintained yolk oxidative stability during storage, indicating that mineral-rich water may support the preservation of egg quality. Egg yolk immunoglobulin Y (IgY) levels remained stable, indicating that maternal antibody transfer and immunological integrity were preserved. Notably, DSW influenced mineral deposition patterns in yolk and albumen, including changes in magnesium, calcium, sodium, and trace mineral concentrations, thereby contributing to the potential nutritional enrichment of eggs. Overall, these findings suggest that diluted and processed DSW may serve as a complementary water-based nutritional approach for laying hens by influencing nutrient utilization and egg mineral composition without negatively affecting production performance. Further studies with longer supplementation periods and dose optimization are required to confirm its practical application in commercial layer production systems.
