LACONIC: Length-Aware Constrained Reinforcement Learning for LLM
Chang Liu, Yiran Zhao, Lawrence Liu, Yaoqi Ye, Csaba Szepesvári, Lin F. Yang

Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) has enhanced the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) through reward-driven training. Nevertheless, this process can introduce excessively long responses, inflating inference latency and computational overhead. Prior length-control approaches typically rely on fixed heuristic reward shaping, which can misalign with the task objective and require brittle tuning. In this work, we propose LACONIC , a reinforcement learning method that enforces a target token budget during training. Specifically, we update policy models using an augmented objective that combines the task reward with a length-based cost. To balance brevity and task performance, the cost scale is adaptively adjusted throughout training. This yields robust length control while preserving task reward. We provide a theoretical guarantee that support the method. Across mathematical reasoning models and datasets, LACONIC preserves or improves pass@1 while reducing output length by over 50%. It maintains out-of-domain performance on general knowledge and multilingual benchmarks with 44% fewer tokens. Moreover, LACONIC integrates into standard RL-tuning with no inference changes and minimal deployment overhead.

Methodology

2.1
Preliminary Background

RL fine-tuning casts text generation as a sequential decision process, where the prompt
q
together with the partial output sequence constitutes the state, selecting the next token is the action, and the language model parameterized by
\theta
serves as the policy
\pi_{\theta}
that maps states to action probabilities. After generating the response, the model receives task rewards
r(q,o)
assigned by a reward model. Policy gradient algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
29
]
and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
[
30
]
then translate such rewards into token-level gradients, so that the model can be updated to increase the corpus-level expected rewards, i.e,

\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)}[r(q,o)].

(1)

In practice, GRPO updates the policy model’s parameters
\theta
by optimizing the following surrogate objective

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q,\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}}\!\Bigl[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|o_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\min(\rho_{i,t}A_{i,t},\,\!

(2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,t},1\!-\!\varepsilon,1\!+\!\varepsilon)A_{i,t})-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}]\Bigr].

where
\rho_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
is the likelihood ratio, and
A_{i,t}
is the group-relative advantage. The likelihood ratio clipping and an extra KL-divergence penalty are adopted to stabilize policy updates.

2.2
Formulation

To explicitly control response lengths, we extend standard RL-tuning to a constrained setting that maximizes task reward under an average token constraint
B
, a pre-specified budget reflecting deployment targets such as latency and computational resources in practice. Formally, letting
L(o)
denote the length of response
o
, we address

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)}[r(q,o)],

(3)

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t. }\;\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)}[L(o)]\leq B.

In
eq.

3
, we enforce a corpus-level average token budget rather than a strict per-sequence length constraint, as response lengths naturally vary across prompts (e.g., math olympiad problems typically require more tokens than simple arithmetic). This allows allocating more tokens to hard instances while still meeting the overall budget constraint.

A standard approach to
eq.

3
is Lagrangian primal-dual optimization. Introduce a dual variable
\lambda\geq 0
and Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(\theta,\lambda):=\mathbb{E}[r(q,o)]-\lambda\left(\mathbb{E}[L(o)]/B-1\right),

then the constrained problem
eq.

3
can be addressed by optimizing the Lagrangian over policy models
\theta
while adjusting
\lambda\geq 0
, i.e.,
\max_{\theta}\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\mathcal{L}(\theta,\lambda)
. Deferring the derivation to
appendix

A
, we obtain the following primal and dual updates:

\theta_{t+1}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}\left[r(q,o)-\lambda_{t}\,\frac{L(o)-B}{B}\right],

(4)

\lambda_{t+1}=\max\{\lambda_{t}+\eta\left(\mathbb{E}[L(o)]/B-1\right),0\}.

(5)

The primal update
eq.

4
is analogous to standard RL-tuning in
eq.

1
. It maximizes expected reward augmented by a linear length-aware cost
\widetilde{c}\coloneqq(L(o)-B)/B
weighted by
\lambda
. However, this linear cost
\widetilde{c}
can be problematic in practice: when
\lambda>0
, the linear cost
\widetilde{c}
consistently incentivizes shorter outputs, and drives the policy towards extremely short responses. This can cause unstable policy updates in training. We observe this behavior in practice and report the empirical results in
appendix

A
.

2.3
LACONIC
: Length-Aware Constrained Policy Optimization

We propose
LACONIC
, a primal-dual method for length-aware RL tuning that preserves the principled constrained-optimization structure, while resolving the collapse to overly short outputs induced by the linear length cost
\widetilde{c}
.

Clipped cost.
We introduce a clipped cost that is zero up to the budget. Specifically, for a prompt
q
and a response
o
generated by the policy model
\pi_{\theta}
, we define

c(q,o)=\max\left\{\frac{L(o)-B}{B},0\right\}.

(6)

This clipped cost assigns equal cost to within-budget responses (
L(o)\leq B
). Therefore, it removes artificial pressure to shorten responses that already meet the token budget.

Algorithm 1

LACONIC
(Length-Aware Constrained Policy Optimization)

Primal updates.
In the primal update, we update the policy model
\theta
by holding
\lambda_{t}
fixed and solving
eq.

4
. This objective has the same form as standard RL-tuning in
eq.

1
with a shaped reward. Therefore, we instantiate the primal update using the RL-tuning procedure (e.g., GRPO) by substituting the task reward
r
with the Lagrangian reward:

\ell_{\lambda}(q,o)=r(q,o)-\lambda\cdot c(q,o),

(7)

where we replace the linear cost with our clipped cost
c
to stabilize policy updates.

Specifically, for each prompt
q
, we sample a group of candidate outputs
\mathbf{o}=\{o_{1},o_{2},\dots,o_{G}\}
from the current policy model
\pi_{\theta}
, and compute their task rewards
\mathbf{r}=\{r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{G}\}
and costs
\mathbf{c}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{G}\}
by
eq.

6
. We then compute the Lagrangian rewards
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\lambda}=\{\ell_{\lambda,1},\ell_{\lambda,2},\dots,\ell_{\lambda,G}\}
by
eq.

7
. For each token
o_{i,t}
, we construct the GRPO-style advantage by normalizing Lagrangian rewards
\ell_{\lambda,i}
within the group, i.e.,

A_{i,t}=\widetilde{\ell}_{\lambda,i}=\frac{\ell_{\lambda,i}-\mathrm{mean}(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\lambda})}{\mathrm{std}(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\lambda})}.

(8)

The policy model is optimized by maximizing the GRPO objective in
eq.

2
where advantages are calculated by
eq.

8
.

Dual updates.
In the dual update, we adjust the dual variable
\lambda
by estimating the expectation in
eq.

5
with the empirical mean response length
\bar{L}
of the current minibatch. This yields the practical dual update

\lambda\leftarrow\textrm{clip}\left(\lambda+\eta\left(\frac{\bar{L}}{B}-1\right),0,\Lambda\right),

(9)

with the step size
\eta
and a
\lambda
-ceiling
\Lambda
. When the batch violates the token budget on average (
\bar{L}>B
), the update increases
\lambda
, raising the effective price of tokens in
\ell_{\lambda}
. Longer responses then receive lower (often negative) advantages than shorter responses with similar task rewards, so the next primal update shifts the policy toward shorter outputs. When the batch falls within the budget,
\lambda
relaxes towards 0. Notably, when
\lambda=0
,
\ell_{\lambda}
reduces to the task reward
r
, and the next primal update is exactly a GRPO step. This feedback adapts
\lambda
to track the budget constraint throughout training as the policy and length distribution evolve.

We cap
\lambda
by a ceiling
\Lambda
to avoid over-penalizing length. For an indicator reward
r(q,o)\in\{0,1\}
, an excessively large
\lambda
can make a within-budget incorrect response (with
\ell_{\lambda}=0
) score above a correct but long response. A sufficient safeguard is to require
\ell_{\lambda}(q,o_{c})>0
for any correct response
o_{c}
, which requires
\lambda<\frac{B}{L(o_{c})-B}
for
L(o_{c})>B
. Using the worst case
L(o_{c})\leq L_{\max}
, where
L_{\max}
is the maximum response length cap, it is sufficient to set
\Lambda=\frac{B}{L_{\max}-B}
.

LACONIC

and illustrate the workflow with two sample steps in

3
Theoretical Results

As discussed in
section

2.3
,
LACONIC
performs policy (primal) updates with a clipped cost
\max\{\frac{L}{B}-1,0\}
, while updating the dual variable
\lambda
using the average linear cost
\frac{\bar{L}}{B}-1
in
eq.

9
. We refer to this combination as the clipped-cost primal-dual update, and we provide the theoretical guarantee in this section.

Notation.
Let
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),\,o\sim\pi(\cdot|q)}[\cdot]
, and
(x)_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
. Define the shorthands
R(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[r(q,o)]
,
\widetilde{C}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\tfrac{L(o)-B}{B}\right]
, and
C(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\tfrac{(L(o)-B)_{+}}{B}\right]
. Then
eq.

3
can be written as
\max_{\pi}R(\pi)
s.t.
\widetilde{C}(\pi)\leq 0
, and let
\pi^{\star}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\pi:\widetilde{C}(\pi)\leq 0}R(\pi)
be an optimal feasible policy.

To isolate the effect of clipping, consider an idealized setting where expectations are exact and each primal step finds an exact maximizer.
The idealized clipped-cost primal-dual updates are

\pi_{t}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\pi}\left\{R(\pi)-\lambda C(\pi)\right\},

(10)

\lambda_{t+1}=\textrm{clip}\left(\lambda_{t}+\eta\,\widetilde{C}(\pi),0,\Lambda\right).

(11)

Appendix

C
shows that
eqs.

10
and
11
converge to a feasible limit pair
(\pi^{\sharp},\lambda^{\sharp})
under mild regularity conditions.
Then
theorem

3.1
quantifies the reward gap between
\pi^{\sharp}
and the optimal policy
\pi^{\star}
, with the proof deferred to
appendix

C
.

Theorem 3.1

(Price of clipped cost)
.

Let
\pi^{\star}\in\max_{\pi:\widetilde{C}(\pi)\leq 0}R(\pi)
be an optimal feasible policy of the length-constrained problem in
eq.

3
. Let
(\pi^{\sharp},\lambda^{\sharp})
be the feasible limit of the idealized clipped-cost primal-dual updates in
eqs.

10
and
11
. Then

0\leq R(\pi^{\star})-R(\pi^{\sharp})\leq\lambda^{\sharp}C(\pi^{*}).

(12)

Moreover, for indicator rewards with the
\lambda
-ceiling
\Lambda=\frac{B}{L_{\max}-B}
and a maximum length cap
L(o)\leq L_{\max}
, we have

0\leq R(\pi^{\star})-R(\pi^{\sharp})\leq\frac{B}{L_{\max}}.

(13)

The bound indicates that the suboptimality induced by clipping is governed by two factors: the limiting multiplier

\lambda^{\sharp}
, and the extent to which the optimal feasible policy
\pi^{\star}
places probability mass above the budget, as captured by
C(\pi^{\star})
.
In the indicator-reward regime, combining the
\lambda
-ceiling with a worst-case tail bound of
\widetilde{C}(\pi^{*})
yields the ceiling-based guarantee
B/L_{\max}
.
This bound is conservative and often loose in practice, but it already implies near-optimality in the worst-case sense whenever
L_{\max}\gg B
.

LACONIC
outperforms existing length-control methods and matches vanilla RL-tuning while significantly reducing response lengths.
On DeepScaleR-1.5B, after fine-tuning,

Related Work

RL fine-tuning.

Reinforcement learning has become a crucial component of LLM post-training, particularly for enhancing large-scale reasoning and aligning model behavior with human preferences or task-specific objectives. Starting from the policy gradient algorithm
[
35
]
,following works
[
40
;
29
]
addressed the instability of early methods. Several GRPO-based extensions have been introduced to address specific challenges in LLM training. SRPO
[
47
]
addresses the problem of ineffective samples through history resampling. DAPO
[
43
]
introduces dynamic sampling to better handle complex reasoning tasks such as chain-of-thought (CoT) generation. Other variants include VAPO
[
45
]
, which adapts advantage estimation to better capture variance across different reasoning depths; GSPO
[
49
]
, which emphasizes group-level structure in sampling; GFPO
[
32
]
, which focuses on sample efficiency in long-horizon settings; GMPO
[
48
]
, which explores geometric averaging of policy gradients for improved robustness; and Dr.GRPO
[
22
]
, which proposes updated formulae for computing advantages and the objective for GRPO. Collectively, these methods demonstrate the growing sophistication of RL fine-tuning techniques and the community’s effort to make them more scalable, stable, and effective for large-scale LLM alignment.

Length-Aware LLMs.
Recent work has investigated various approaches for making large language models (LLMs) aware of output length, including modifications to positional encoding, prompt engineering techniques, and post-hoc truncation methods
[
20
;
41
;
21
]
. A common strategy involves incorporating length preferences into reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning through manually designed reward functions that penalize or incentivize certain output lengths
[
2
;
7
;
17
;
44
;
4
;
15
]
. These methods typically rely on fixed heuristics or penalty terms that remain constant throughout training, and thus optimize a surrogate objective that may be misaligned with the true downstream task reward. This misalignment can lead to suboptimal performance and often requires extensive hyperparameter tuning to balance length control with task-specific quality. Our work differs in that it aims to align length control with task reward in a more adaptive and data-driven manner, avoiding the limitations of fixed shaping objectives.

Constrained RL.
Constrained RL is commonly used to formulate and enforce constraints in the environment. Many previous studies
[
1
;
37
;
33
]
have proposed policy gradient methods for constrained RL. Recent work including this paper has been introducing the constrained RL framework into RL fine-tuning for LLMs.
[
38
]
propose a curriculum strategy to compress inference time of LLMs.
[
46
]
deploy the primal-dual approach to control the divergence of policy updates.

7
Conclusion

We present
LACONIC
, a primal-dual method for length-aware RL fine-tuning that integrates into standard GRPO with minimal changes. It enforces a user-specified token budget via a clipped cost and an adaptive dual variable for excess length, yielding concise generations while preserving accuracy. We also provide a theoretical analysis with convergence and near-optimality guarantees. Across math and out-of-domain benchmarks,
LACONIC
consistently reduces output length while preserving pass@1. Budget-only ablations show precise, stable controllability that lengths track the target budget without retuning hyperparameters. Overall,
LACONIC
makes length control a simple, reliable component of RL-based LLM fine-tuning.

8
Future Work

While
LACONIC
is effective and lightweight, it has several limitations. It currently enforces a global average token budget, which may not capture prompt-specific or context-dependent needs. Our experiments are limited to math reasoning, and future work could validate generality on dialogue, summarization, or code. Finally,
LACONIC
handles a single constraint, but the framework naturally extends to multi-constraint settings such as latency or safety.

Appendix A
Standard Primal-Dual Method and Linear Cost Functions

A.1
Standard Primal-Dual Updates

In this section, we derive the standard primal-dual updates.

Recall the constrained optimization problem of length-aware LLMs formulated in
eq.

3
:

\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)}[r(q,o)],\;\textrm{s.t. }\;\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)}[L(o)]\leq B.

The corresponding Lagrangian is

\mathcal{L}(\theta,\lambda)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)}[r(q,o)]-\lambda\,(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)}[L(o)]}{B}-1),\quad\lambda\geq 0,

(14)

and the problem is commonly approached by solving the saddle point problem:

\max_{\theta}\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\mathcal{L}(\theta,\lambda),

(15)

where
\theta
is the primal variable (in our case, the policy model) and
\lambda
is the dual variable. We assume that
eq.

15
is feasible, and denote the optimal feasible solution by
(\pi^{*},\lambda^{*})
. The standard primal-dual approach solves
\pi^{*}
and
\lambda^{*}
iteratively with partial derivatives. In the primal step, the dual variable
\lambda_{t}
is fixed, and we optimize
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\lambda_{t})
over
\theta
:

\theta_{t+1}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)}[r(q,o)]-\lambda_{t}\,(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)}[L(o)]}{B}-1).

By linearity of expectations, we have

\theta_{t+1}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)}[r(q,o)-\lambda_{t}\frac{L(o)-B}{B}].

Define the linear cost function

\widetilde{c}(q,o)=\frac{L(o)-B}{B},

(16)

and thus the primal update takes the form

\theta_{t+1}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)}[r(q,o)-\lambda_{t}\widetilde{c}(q,o)].

(17)

In the dual update, we decrease
\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t+1},\lambda)
by taking a gradient-descent step for
\lambda
:

\lambda_{t+1}\leftarrow\max\{\lambda_{t}-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t+1},\lambda)}{\partial\lambda},0\}=\max\{\lambda+\eta(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta_{t+1}}(\cdot|q)}[L(o)]}{B}-1),0\}.

(18)

Thus far, we have derived the idealized standard primal dual updates in
eqs.

4
and
5
. Empirically we estimate the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|q)}[L(o)]
with the minibatch mean
\bar{L}
generated by the policy model
\pi_{\theta}
, and with an extra
\lambda
-ceiling
\Lambda
, we derive exactly
eq.

9
:

\lambda\leftarrow\textrm{clip}(\lambda+\eta(\frac{\bar{L}}{B}-1),0,\Lambda).

For the primal update in
eq.

17
, we treat it as a RL-tuning step where the task reward is substituted with a Langrangian reward
\widehat{\ell}_{\lambda_{t}}(q,o)\coloneqq r(q,o)-\lambda_{t}\widetilde{c}(q,o)
.
We refer to the aforementioned method as the standard primal-dual updates (with linear costs). Note that
LACONIC
shares the same dual updates as the standard primal-dual method, while changing the cost function used in primal updates.

Appendix C
Analysis of clipped-cost primal-dual

We first restate some definitions and notations. Let
q\sim P(Q)
denote a prompt and
o\sim\pi(\cdot\mid q)
be a response.
Let
L(o)
be the length of response
o
and
B>0
be the token budget.
Define

R(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi(\cdot|q)}[r(q,o)],\quad\widetilde{C}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi(\cdot|q)}\!\left[\frac{L(o)-B}{B}\right],\quad C(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi(\cdot|q)}\!\left[\frac{(L(o)-B)_{+}}{B}\right],

where
(x)_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
.
The target constrained problem is

\pi^{\star}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\pi}R(\pi)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\widetilde{C}(\pi)\leq 0.

C.1
Idealized clipped-cost dynamics

For any
\lambda\geq 0
, the optimal policy model under clipped-cost corresponding to
\lambda
can be determined and defined as

\pi(\lambda)\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\pi}R(\pi)-\lambda\,C(\pi),

(19)

and then we define the induced true normalized length of the resulting

\mu(\lambda):=\widetilde{C}(\pi(\lambda)).

(20)

We study the deterministic projected update

\pi_{t}=\pi(\lambda_{t}),\quad\lambda_{t+1}=\textrm{clip}(\lambda_{t}+\eta\,\mu(\lambda_{t}),0,\Lambda),

(21)

and assume it converges to a limit
(\pi^{\sharp},\lambda^{\sharp})
with
\pi^{\sharp}=\pi(\lambda^{\sharp})
.

C.2
Bounding Reward Suboptimality of Clipped-Cost

Lemma C.1
.

Denote the response length cap by
L_{\max}
, and
0\leq L(o)\leq L_{\max}
for any response
o
. We also require
L_{\max}>B
, otherwise the problem is trivial. Let
\pi
be any feasible policy for the linear constraint
\widetilde{C}(\pi)\leq 0
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi(\cdot|q)}[L(o)]\leq B
. Then

C(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),o\sim\pi(\cdot|q)}[\frac{(L(o)-B)_{+}}{B}]\leq\frac{L_{\max}-B}{L_{\max}}.

(22)

Proof.

We first notice that for any response
o
,

(L(o)-B)_{+}\leq\frac{L_{\max}-B}{L_{\max}}L(o).

(23)

When
L(o)<B
, then
\textrm{LHS}<0<\textrm{RHS}
. When
B\leq L(o)\leq L_{\max}
, then

(L(o)-B)_{+}=L(o)-B\leq\frac{L_{\max}-B}{L_{\max}}L(o),

where the inequality can be verified by arithmetic rearranging.

Then by taking expecations on both sides of
eq.

23
, we have
