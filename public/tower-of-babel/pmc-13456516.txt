Mechanistic pathways linking gut microbial metabolites, microbial structural products, and host–microbe co-metabolites to mitochondrial function
Authors: Richard E. Frye, Daniel A. Rossignol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGut-derived microbial metabolites modulate host mitochondrial function. To our knowledge, no prior systematic review has synthesized evidence linking these metabolites to direct mitochondrial endpoints across all major metabolite classes. PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science were systematically searched through March 2026 for studies examining direct mitochondrial endpoints in relation to ten classes of gut-derived metabolites. Gut-derived microbial metabolites exert diverse effects on mitochondrial function, ranging from bioenergetic enhancement to direct toxicity. After contextual evidence was excluded from the certainty assessment, four metabolite–mitochondrial outcome groupings had moderate certainty: butyrate, propionate, indoxyl sulfate, and lipopolysaccharide. Evidence for p-cresyl sulfate, H₂S, and urolithin A was rated low; evidence for the remaining metabolite classes was low or very low. Butyrate generally enhances mitochondrial biogenesis, ATP generation, and electron transport chain activity across multiple experimental models. Indoxyl sulfate generally impaired mitochondrial function, whereas p-cresyl sulfate produced concentration- and model-dependent effects. PGC-1α emerges as a central regulatory node, integrating microbial metabolic signals to control mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism. Many microbial metabolites demonstrate concentration-dependent biphasic effects. These findings support a mechanistic microbiome-mitochondrial framework in which diverse gut-derived molecules converge on a limited set of mitochondrial control nodes: electron transport chain activity, reactive oxygen species handling, biogenesis, and mitophagy. The evidence base is predominantly preclinical; these insights should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Introduction

The human gut microbiome produces a diverse array of bioactive metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), tryptophan derivatives, secondary bile acids, and microbial uremic toxins, that enter host circulation through portal and systemic pathways and influence host physiology. Emerging experimental evidence suggests that many of these metabolites directly regulate mitochondrial function, including electron transport chain (ETC) activity, oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial membrane potential, and mitochondrial biogenesis.

These molecules arise through several biological pathways, including bacterially synthesized metabolites that are produced directly by microbial metabolism (e.g., butyrate (BTY) and propionate (PPA)), microbial structural components released during bacterial growth or lysis (e.g., lipopolysaccharide), and host–microbe co-metabolites generated through sequential microbial and host enzymatic transformations (e.g., indoxyl sulfate, p-cresol sulfate). These observations support the concept of a microbiome–mitochondria axis, in which microbial metabolic activity influences host cellular energetics through direct modulation of mitochondrial function. Viewed across classes, these effects repeatedly converge on a small set of mitochondrial control nodes, particularly ETC activity, oxidative stress, biogenesis, and mitophagy, which provides the organizing logic for this review.

Despite growing interest in this biological interface, to our knowledge, no systematic review has comprehensively synthesized the effects of gut-derived metabolites on direct mitochondrial endpoints across major metabolite classes. Individual metabolite classes have been reviewed in disease-specific contexts. For example, uremic toxins have been reviewed in chronic kidney disease, bile acids in liver disease, and SCFAs in colorectal physiology. However, an integrated synthesis spanning all ten classes with standardized quality assessment has been lacking. Because mitochondria integrate metabolic, inflammatory, and signaling inputs, these pathways have implications for metabolic syndrome, chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and brain disorders.

To address this gap, the present systematic review evaluates 91 studies across ten classes of gut-derived metabolites, integrating predominantly preclinical evidence and limited human data regarding their effects on mitochondrial function. Substantial heterogeneity in experimental models, metabolite concentrations, and mitochondrial outcome measures precluded quantitative meta-analysis; therefore, findings are synthesized narratively. Because biological relevance depends not only on metabolite identity but also on whether concentrations reflect colonic, portal, systemic, or tissue compartments, translational interpretation requires careful attention to exposure context.

Accordingly, this is a systematic review with narrative synthesis that applies transparent, reproducible search and quality-assessment procedures while synthesizing results narratively rather than statistically. This approach was chosen for several reasons beyond the heterogeneity noted above. First, the ten metabolite classes act through fundamentally different biochemical mechanisms (e.g., HDAC inhibition, aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR)-mediated transcription, and direct ETC electron donation), making a single pooled effect size biologically uninformative. Second, outcome measures span at least seven distinct mitochondrial endpoints assessed by non-interchangeable assay platforms (e.g., Seahorse respirometry versus spectrophotometric complex assays versus fluorescence-based membrane potential), precluding meaningful statistical aggregation. Third, the evidence base mixes in vitro, ex vivo, animal, and human studies at widely varying metabolite concentrations, violating the assumption of a common underlying effect that meta-analysis requires. A systematic narrative synthesis preserves the rigor of systematic searching, screening, and risk-of-bias assessment while allowing qualitative integration of mechanistically diverse findings.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines for systematic reviews. 1 The review protocol was preregistered at the Center for Open Science. Eligibility criteria, search strategy, risk-of-bias assessment, and adapted GRADE-style certainty procedures were specified a priori and applied consistently across all metabolite classes. A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science through March 2026 using metabolite-specific and mitochondrial function search terms across ten metabolite categories (See Supplementary Methods). The two authors independently screened titles/abstracts of all potentially relevant studies to identify potentially eligible studies. Both authors independently examined each identified study in-depth. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion between the authors.

The database search identified 1,847 records plus 89 records from citations of these records. Of these, 524 duplicates were removed, leaving 1,412 studies for title and abstract screening (see Figure 1 for the PRISMA flow diagram). Following screening, 318 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, resulting in 91 studies included in the final qualitative synthesis. Publications reporting direct functional mitochondrial endpoints or prespecified mitochondrial regulatory endpoints with a gut-derived metabolite as the primary exposure constituted the evidence base for certainty assessment. Reviews, modeling studies, safety-only trials, biomarker studies without the claimed mitochondrial endpoint, and studies without a mitochondrial endpoint could be retained as contextual evidence but did not contribute to certainty ratings. Reviews without relevant mechanistic outcome data, conference abstracts, and publications unrelated to a gut-derived microbial exposure were excluded.Reference lists were hand-searched. English language studies and non-English language studies with translations available were included.

Certainty of evidence and summary-of-findings approach

Randomized and non-randomized human studies were assessed with RoB 2 and ROBINS‑E, respectively, whereas preclinical work was evaluated using a Cochrane-adapted five-domain framework; ratings (low, moderate, or high) were integrated as contextual qualifiers. Certainty was rated using adapted GRADE-style assessments at the metabolite–outcome level, with preclinical evidence starting at low certainty. Ratings were upgraded for large consistent effects, dose-response relationships, and mechanistic coherence, or downgraded for bias, inconsistency, indirectness, or imprecision. Full domain definitions and scoring rubrics for each tool are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Of the 91 included studies, the majority were preclinical (57 in vitro/cell-culture, 28 in vivo animal, 7 ex vivo, and 7 human; totals exceed 91 because some studies used multiple model systems). Among the 91 Appendix A entries, 85 received formal risk-of-bias ratings: 42 were rated low risk, 39 moderate risk, and 4 high risk; 6 entries received N/A ratings. (see Supplementary Methods for full domain-level results). These adapted certainty ratings summarize our confidence that each metabolite exerts a directional effect on host mitochondrial function, rather than the certainty of any downstream clinical outcome. Accordingly, moderate certainty in this review indicates confidence in the direction of a mechanistic metabolite–mitochondrial relationship across models, not confidence that modifying the metabolite will improve patient outcomes. Full methods details are in the Supplementary Methods.

For certainty purposes, a metabolite–outcome grouping was defined as the directional effect of a given metabolite or closely related metabolite subtype on a specific mitochondrial endpoint cluster, not clinical efficacy or therapeutic relevance. Direct functional endpoints included respiration or oxygen-consumption rate, respiratory-chain complex activity, ATP production, and mitochondrial membrane potential. Prespecified mitochondrial regulatory endpoints included mtDNA abundance, PGC-1α-related regulation, and directly assessed mitophagy markers. Reviews, modeling studies, safety-only trials, biomarker studies without the claimed mitochondrial endpoint, and studies without a mitochondrial endpoint were treated as contextual evidence and excluded from certainty ratings. Of the 91 included studies, only seven involved human participants; most provided observational, transcriptomic, or biomarker-level data rather than direct functional mitochondrial measurements.

Microbiome metabolites

We organize the evidence by ten metabolite classes and seven mitochondrial endpoints (ETC function, oxidative phosphorylation, membrane potential, reactive oxygen species, biogenesis, mitophagy/dynamics, and CoA/TCA metabolism). Of these, ETC complex activity (spectrophotometric assays), oxygen consumption rate (Seahorse/Oroboros respirometry), membrane potential (fluorescence-based), and ATP quantification represent direct functional measures of mitochondrial bioenergetics. Biogenesis markers (PGC-1α protein/mRNA, TFAM, NRF1/2, mtDNA copy number) and mitophagy markers (PINK1/Parkin expression, LC3-II/mitochondrial colocalization) are mechanistically upstream indicators that reflect mitochondrial regulatory signaling rather than immediate bioenergetic output; we retain them because they are well-validated proxies for mitochondrial mass and quality control, respectively. ROS measurements occupy an intermediate position: mitochondrial-targeted probes (MitoSOX) directly measure organelle-level oxidative stress, whereas total cellular ROS assays (DCFH-DA) provide indirect evidence. We conclude each metabolite section with a one-sentence GRADE-style certainty statement at the metabolite–outcome level. The ten metabolite classes reviewed here fall into three biosynthetic categories. Microbial metabolites are produced entirely by bacterial metabolism without requiring host enzymatic processing; these include SCFAs (BTY, PPA, acetate), bacterial vitamins (B2, B3, B9, B12), polyamines (spermidine, putrescine), and D-lactate. Microbial structural products are components released during bacterial growth, division, or lysis rather than active metabolic end-products; lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is the principal example. Host–microbe co-metabolites require sequential processing by both bacterial and host enzymes: bacteria generate precursors that are absorbed and further modified by host phase I/II metabolism. This category includes uremic toxins (p-cresol sulfate (pCS) and indoxyl sulfate (IS); bacterial tyrosine/tryptophan metabolism yields p-cresol and indole, subsequently sulfated by host hepatic sulfotransferases), secondary bile acids (produced by bacterial 7α-dehydroxylation of host-synthesized primary bile acids), tryptophan metabolites (e.g., indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), and kynurenine pathway metabolites involving both microbial and host enzymes), and polyphenol metabolites (e.g., urolithin A, equol). This classification is important because it determines which metabolites are amenable to modulation by probiotic or dietary interventions versus those requiring host metabolic capacity.

The 91 included studies spanned a wide spectrum of experimental systems. In vitro work (n = 57) drew on 15 distinct immortalized cell lines, with the most frequently used being SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma (n = 3), C2C12 mouse myoblasts (n = 3), Caco-2 human colon epithelial cells (n = 2), and lymphoblastoid cell lines (n = 2); single uses of HCT116, HCT-8, IPEC-J2, HepG2-like, H9C2, HL-1, IM-BAT, MIN6, GLUTag, THP-1, and prostate cancer lines were also reported. Primary or freshly isolated cells were used in 17 studies and included human fibroblasts (n = 2), human hepatocytes, calf hepatocytes, primary rat colonocytes, primary human pancreatic islets, primary CD4 + T cells, primary mouse enteroendocrine L cells, primary cortical neurons, renal tubular epithelial cells, mesenchymal stem cells, late endothelial progenitor cells from human donors, HUVECs, porcine oocytes, human muscle biopsies, and isolated EDL muscle fibers. Animal in vivo work (n = 28) drew on at least eight species/model categories: C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice; Sprague-Dawley and Wistar rats; Landrace × Yorkshire pigs; C. elegans; calves; and yeast (1 lipidome study). Disease- and genotype-targeted rodent models included germ-free/SPF/gnotobiotic mice (n = 3), 5xFAD Alzheimer mice, TGR5 KO/WT mice (n = 2), propionic-acidemia (PA) mice, KAT CRISPR knockouts, ACSS1 knockouts, Pgam5 knockouts, CKD models including 5/6-nephrectomy and adenine-diet mice (n = 4), doxorubicin-cardiotoxicity mice (n = 2), MCT-induced pulmonary hypertension rats, biliary cirrhosis rats, cholestasis models, diabetic kidney/neuropathy models, cardiac pressure-overload models, atrial-fibrillation models, sepsis/endotoxemia models, and aged-mouse cohorts. Ex vivo preparations (n = 7) included isolated perfused rat livers, Langendorff isolated rat/mouse hearts, isolated rat liver mitochondria, and isolated skeletal-muscle mitochondria. Human-participant studies (n = 7) comprised healthy-volunteer enema, RCT, and Phase 1 trials together with observational cohorts of CKD/ESRD, peritoneal-dialysis, chronic-liver-disease, sarcopenia/elderly, autism, propionic-acidemia, and twin populations. By tissue/organ system, the most represented were intestinal/colonic (n = 8), skeletal muscle (n = 8), cardiac (n = 8), hepatic (n = 8), neuronal/CNS (n = 7), renal (n = 9), and immune (n = 4), with smaller bodies of work on endothelial/vascular, adipose, pancreatic, reproductive, pulmonary, and stem-cell systems.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)

SCFAs are produced by anaerobic bacterial fermentation of dietary fibers. The primary SCFAs, acetate (C2), PPA (C3), and BTY (C4) are present in the colonic lumen at ~50–150 mM in a ~60:20:20 ratio. 2 Each has distinct mitochondrial interactions: BTY is the preferential colonocyte fuel, PPA serves as an anaplerotic TCA substrate, and acetate is converted to acetyl-CoA for mitochondrial oxidation (Figure 2A).

Butyrate

ETC activity, OXPHOS, and ATP production

BTY fuels the ETC through beta-oxidation to acetyl-CoA, driving NADH/FADH2 production. 3 In patient-derived lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs), BTY increased ATP-linked respiration in a dose- and time-dependent manner. 4 In a landmark study (low RoB), germ-free colonocytes showed 16-fold lower NADH/NAD + and 56% lower ATP versus conventional mice; exogenous BTY (4 mM) restored OXPHOS to ~70% of conventional levels. 3 High-resolution respirometry confirmed BTY improves respiratory efficiency in brain synaptosomes while reducing oxidative stress. 5 In primary rat colonocytes, BTY (1 mM) increased OXPHOS and partially rescued TNF-α-induced dysfunction. 6 Studies in Caco-2 cells, 7 finishing pigs, 8 and high-fat diet rodent models 9 , 10 confirm BTY increases oxidative capacity across tissues. One study found BTY increased O2 consumption but decreased ATP/O ratio, indicating uncoupling. 11 The only human study applied BTY enemas (100 mM) to healthy volunteers for 2 weeks with oxidative phosphorylation among the most upregulated transcriptomic pathways. 12

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)

BTY preserves ΔΨm under baseline and inflammatory conditions. In rat colonocytes, BTY (1 mM) preserved ΔΨm and partially rescued TNF-α-induced depolarization. 6

Mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α, mtDNA)

BTY activates PGC-1α-mediated biogenesis across multiple tissues, increasing mtDNA copy number in pigs and rodent models, 8–10 upregulating PGC-1α in patient-derived LCLs, 4 and inducing fatty acid oxidation gene expression in a human BTY enema study. 12

Mitophagy (PINK1/Parkin)

BTY induces AMPK-mediated mitophagy in porcine intestinal epithelial cells and ameliorates oxidative-stress-induced mitochondrial damage in intestinal epithelium via the AMPK-mitophagy pathway 13 and upregulates gene expression PINK1 and FIS1 in patient-derived LCLs, 4 activating canonical mitophagy.

ROS and oxidative stress

BTY enhances mitochondrial antioxidant capacity across tissues. Rodent models confirm BTY increases antioxidant capacity, 9 , 10 and brain synaptosome studies showed reduced oxidative stress alongside improved ETC activity. 5 A human enema study also showed upregulation of oxidative stress response genes. 12

Overall certainty

Across 11 studies, 10 showed enhancement of mitochondrial function, whereas one ex vivo study reported mitochondrial uncoupling (Figure 2A-D). Overall certainty is moderate that BTY enhances mitochondrial biogenesis, ETC activity, and ATP production in colonocyte, neuronal, and hepatic models, supported by large effect sizes and dose–response evidence, but limited human evidence from one small study. 12

Propionate

CoA metabolism and TCA cycle

PPA enters the TCA cycle at succinyl-CoA and supports hepatic gluconeogenesis at physiologic concentrations. However, propionyl-CoA accumulates at elevated concentrations, causing CoA trapping and mitochondrial dysfunction through free CoASH sequestration. 14 A low RoB study using [U-13C3]PPA in perfused rat hearts 15 showed ~101-fold propionyl-CoA accumulation, inhibiting fatty acid oxidation with L-carnitine not relieving this trapping. A pantothenate kinase activator prevented CoA sequestration in a PPA mouse model, 16 normalizing the C3:C2-CoA ratio and confirming therapeutic reversibility.

ETC complex activity and OXPHOS

Propionyl-CoA uncompetitively inhibits PDHc and KGDHc, with mild Complex III inhibition and 50% mtDNA reduction in PPA patient biopsies. 14 In LCLs, low PPA concentrations (0.1–1 mM, below colonic luminal levels but above typical systemic plasma concentrations in humans) increased ATP-linked respiration, while higher concentrations (5 mM, approximating colonic luminal levels) increased proton leak, demonstrating dichotomous dose-dependent effects across low-to-high experimental exposures. 17 High PPA in primary human hepatocytes caused propionyl-CoA accumulation and reduced energy production. 18

Mitochondrial dynamics (fission/fusion)

Elevated PPA induces mitochondrial fragmentation across diverse models, including SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma, 19 C. elegans PCC deletion models, 20 and brain microvascular endothelial cells. 21

Mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α)

At physiologic concentrations, PPA activates PGC-1α-mediated biogenesis in hepatocytes, contrasting with its toxicity at elevated doses. 22

PPA shows the most context-dependent mitochondrial profile among SCFAs, transitioning from beneficial (anaplerotic TCA substrate, PGC-1α activation) to deleterious (CoA trapping, PDHc/Complex III inhibition) as propionyl-CoA accumulates relative to free CoASH. 14 , 22 As a practical threshold, toxicity generally emerges when plasma PPA exceeds ~100 µM or when propionyl-CoA:free CoASH ratios rise above ~1:1, as observed in PPA models. 14 , 15

Overall certainty

Overall certainty is moderate that PPA impairs mitochondrial function at supraphysiologic concentrations through CoA trapping and ETC inhibition in hepatocyte, neuronal, and cardiac models. The certainty assessment was based on eight propionate-specific studies; one SCFA-mixture study was treated as contextual evidence.

Acetate

ETC activity, OXPHOS, and TCA cycle

Acetate is converted to acetyl-CoA by AceCS2/ACSS1 in the mitochondrial matrix or by ACSS2 in the cytosol. AceCS2 knockout mice showed fasting-induced hypothermia, establishing that this enzyme is indispensable for mitochondrial energy production. 23 In germ-free mice, acetate restored membrane potential, Complex II function, and normalized ROS in microglia, with 13C-tracing confirming brain metabolism of gut-derived acetate. 24 Acetate also preserves beta-cell mitochondrial function under oxidative stress 25 and acutely increases brain phosphocreatine. 26

Mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α)

Acetate promotes biogenesis in brown adipose tissue. A moderate RoB study showed that acetate increased PGC-1α (1.9-fold), UCP1 (2.3-fold), and spare respiratory capacity via GPR43 in brown adipocytes and C57BL/6J mice. 27

Acetate serves as a critical mitochondrial fuel via AceCS2-mediated conversion to acetyl-CoA in heart, muscle, and brown adipose tissue. 23 In the brain, microbiota-derived acetate maintains microglial Complex II/SDH activity 24 and promotes biogenesis via GPR43/PGC-1α/UCP1. 27 Notably, human data for acetate are limited to indirect endpoints 26 rather than direct mitochondrial assessments.

Overall certainty

Overall certainty is low that acetate enhances mitochondrial biogenesis and ETC activity in microglial, adipocyte, and cardiac models, due to only 5 consistent preclinical studies and no direct human mitochondrial data.

Secondary bile acids

Primary bile acids are synthesized in the liver and transformed by gut bacteria (e.g., C. scindens, Lachnospiraceae) into secondary bile acids including deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA). Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) is produced by bacterial epimerization.

ETC complex activity and OXPHOS

Secondary bile acids exert opposing ETC effects depending on species. TGR5-knockout muscle fibers abolished DCA-induced Complex I/II impairment, confirming TGR5-dependent toxicity. 28 UDCA also restores mitochondrial function in Alzheimer’s and Parkinson disease patient fibroblasts, 29 , 30 and FXR activation enhances hepatic OXPHOS in humans. 31 Proteomic analysis has identified additional FXR targets involved in mitochondrial metabolism. 32

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)

DCA causes mitochondrial membrane depolarization through direct membrane incorporation triggering mitochondrial permeability transition. UDCA uniformly opposes this effect through membrane stabilization, 29 acting as a mitochondrial membrane protectant across liver, brain, and cirrhosis models. DCA further modulates cell death signaling through alterations in mitochondrial membrane properties. 33 UDCA also prevents mitochondrial oxidative stress in secondary biliary cirrhosis. 34 In isolated rat liver mitochondria, DCA caused 25-fold increased swelling and depolarization, while UDCA blocked all effects. 35

Mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α)

TGR5 activation promotes biogenesis via cAMP/CREB/PGC-1α, increasing spare respiratory capacity and UCP1 thermogenesis across three genetic mouse models and human cells. 36 LCA also shows beneficial muscle effects through TGR5-IGF1 signaling. 37 In a yeast model, LCA altered the mitochondrial membrane lipidome, suggesting that bile acid–mitochondria interactions extend to fundamental membrane-level remodeling. 38

Mitochondrial dynamics (fission/fusion: Drp1)

TGR5 also promotes fission via ERK/DRP1-Ser616 phosphorylation, 36 mediating dual mitochondrial pathways, both cAMP/biogenesis and ERK/fission, with tissue, bile acid species, and concentration determining the outcome. UDCA results in Drp1 redistribution. 29

ROS and oxidative stress

DCA amplifies mitochondrial ROS production through ETC complex impairment and membrane perturbation, with this ROS increase completely abolished in TGR5-KO fibers. 28 UDCA opposes DCA-induced ROS generation, 35 consistent with its membrane-stabilizing protective mechanism. LCA also induces mitochondrial dysfunction through endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and autophagy activation in human prostate cancer cells. 39

Secondary bile acids show strongly divergent mitochondrial effects. DCA acts as a TGR5-dependent mitochondrial toxin. 28 , 35 , 40 UDCA is uniformly protective through membrane stabilization. 29 LCA shows context-dependent effects. TGR5 mediates dual pathways (cAMP/biogenesis and ERK/fission), with outcome depending on tissue, species, and concentration. 36 DCA toxicity and UDCA protection are individually consistent.

Overall certainty

Overall certainty is low that secondary bile acids modulate mitochondrial membrane potential and ETC activity in hepatocyte, muscle, and neuronal models, due to heterogeneity across bile acid species. Pathological bile acid concentrations in chronic cholestasis cause adipose tissue mitochondrial defects, 41 broadening the tissue scope of bile acid–mitochondrial toxicity.

Tryptophan metabolites

Gut bacteria catabolize dietary tryptophan through three major routes: the indole pathway (producing IPA, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), indole), the kynurenine pathway (producing kynurenine, kynurenic acid (KYNA), quinolinic acid), and the serotonin pathway.

ETC complex activity and OXPHOS

CRISPR deletion of KAT1, KAT2, or KAT3 was associated with reduced cerebellar Complex I/II-supported OXPHOS and Complex IV activity, supporting a relationship between KAT-dependent kynurenine metabolism and mitochondrial respiration; the study did not establish KYNA as an essential ETC cofactor. 42 Indole directly inhibits Complex I through an AhR-independent mechanism. 43 , 44 IPA rescues mitochondrial function via PPAR-β/δ in germ-free mice 45 but shows biphasic effects: acute exposure at ~10 µM increases maximal respiration, whereas chronic exposure at ≥ 1 mM inhibits Complex I, II and IV and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) by 30–50%. 46 IPA also attenuates high glucose-induced ER stress and augments mitochondrial function via PERK-mediated signaling. 47

Mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α)

Tryptophan metabolites show opposing mitochondrial effects depending on identity and concentration. IPA is protective at physiological concentrations but impairing at supraphysiologic doses. 46 Indole directly inhibits Complex I. 43 AhR ligands can activate or degrade PGC-1α. 48 KAT-dependent kynurenine metabolism was associated with brain ETC activity, but the study did not establish KYNA as an essential ETC cofactor. 42 Serotonin promotes neuronal biogenesis via 5-HT2A/SIRT1/PGC-1α. 49

Overall certainty

Overall certainty is low that tryptophan metabolites modulate mitochondrial ETC activity and biogenesis in neuronal and cardiac models, due to heterogeneity and concentration-dependence across metabolite subtypes. In a diabetic kidney disease model, IPA exhibited mitochondrial protective effects via SIRT1-mediated signaling. 50 Gut microbiota-derived kynurenine has also been identified as a predictor and modulator of cardiovascular outcomes. 51

Uremic toxins and TMAO

Gut-derived uremic toxins are host–microbe co-metabolites produced by bacterial precursor generation followed by hepatic phase II conjugation. These include the protein-bound toxins pCS and IS, as well as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO). Although grouped together as uremic toxins, IS, PCS, and TMAO have different mitochondrial profiles: IS is generally damaging, PCS produces concentration- and model-dependent effects, and TMAO shows bidirectional effects. Studies using unconjugated p-cresol are discussed only as mechanistic context for p-cresol sulfate biology and not as equivalent analytes.

Indoxyl Sulfate, p-Cresol Sulfate, and Phenylacetylglutamine

ETC complex activity and OXPHOS

Isolated skeletal muscle mitochondria exposed to IS showed inhibition of Complex III and Complex IV. 52 IS additionally inhibited malate dehydrogenase and OXPHOS conductance and increased H2O2 emission. A moderate RoB study of 14 peritoneal dialysis patients and 16 controls 53 showed plasma IS ~21-fold higher in patients, inversely correlated with muscle mass; in vitro IS decreased OXPHOS and ATP. CKD mice confirmed organ-specific OXPHOS impairment. 54

Mitochondrial dynamics (fission/fusion: Fis1/Drp1)

IS induces mitochondrial fragmentation through a ROS-dependent signaling cascade. In vitro studies confirm IS-induced fission via ROS/Fis1/Drp1. 55 A moderate risk of bias study using the Seahorse platform documented that IS decreased OXPHOS and induced mitochondrial fragmentation in a cell model of peritoneal dialysis. 53

Mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α)

IS is an endogenous ligand of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and the AhR acts as a critical PGC-1α regulatory hub. In one context, AhR promotes proteasomal degradation of PGC-1α in tubular epithelial cells. 48 IS suppresses PGC-1α/FNDC5, predicting muscle weakness in ESRD patients, 56 establishing a direct translational link between uremic toxin burden, biogenesis suppression, and functional muscle decline. Potential therapeutic interventions targeting IS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction have been proposed for CKD-associated sarcopenia. 57 IS-induced oxidative stress and impaired biogenesis are partly attenuated by antioxidant supplementation with vitamin C and N-acetylcysteine. 58

ROS and oxidative stress

IS increased H2O2 emission from isolated muscle mitochondria, amplifying ETC complex damage. 52 TMAO suppresses MnSOD in endothelial progenitors, 59 impairing mitochondrial antioxidant defense. Phenylacetylglutamine was associated with heart failure, but the study did not measure a direct mitochondrial endpoint. 60 In a separate atrial-myocyte study, phenylacetylglutamine altered cell viability, ROS production, apoptosis, contractility, and CaMKII/RyR2 signaling, but did not directly measure mitochondrial function. 61 In a separate study, pioglitazone protected mesenchymal stem cells against mitochondrial dysfunction induced by unconjugated p-cresol—not PCS—through activation of NF-κB–PINK1 signaling. 62

Trimethylamine N-Oxide (TMAO)

TMAO shows a bidirectional mitochondrial profile distinct from IS/pCS. At physiologic concentrations (~1–5 µM plasma), TMAO normalizes fatty acid oxidation, OXPHOS and preserves cardiac function. 63 However, at elevated levels (>50 µM, as in CKD), TMAO causes outer membrane disruption in perfused hearts 64 and suppresses MnSOD in endothelial progenitors. 59 This bidirectional profile, together with evidence that TMAO disrupts spermatogenesis through mitochondrial dysfunction, 65 underscores that TMAO biology cannot be reduced to a simple toxin narrative. PAGln was associated with adverse cardiac phenotypes, but neither included PAGln study measured a direct mitochondrial endpoint 60 , 61

Overall certainty

IS generally impaired mitochondrial function through ETC dysfunction, oxidative stress, and suppression of PGC-1α-related regulation. 52,53,55–58 PCS showed concentration- and model-dependent effects: in one renal model, IS and PCS induced ROS-associated fission and reduced mitochondrial mass, 55 whereas low-dose PCS produced reversible AMPK-dependent adaptive hyperfusion with increased mitochondrial mass and bioenergetic capacity. 66 The included evidence did not establish PCS as a preferential Complex II inhibitor. TMAO showed concentration- and model-dependent bidirectional effects. 59,63–65 Certainty was moderate for IS and low for PCS and TMAO. PAGln was treated as contextual evidence because neither included PAGln study measured a direct mitochondrial endpoint. 60 , 61

Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)

H₂S is produced by sulfate-reducing bacteria (Desulfovibrio spp., Bilophila wadsworthia) at colonic concentrations ranging from approximately 30 µM to greater than 1 mM. Its relationship with mitochondria is biphasic: at low concentrations (less than 20 µM), H₂S stimulates electron transport via sulfide:quinone oxidoreductase (SQOR) coupled to Complex III. At high concentrations (greater than 100 µM), it reversibly inhibits cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV). 67

ETC complex activity and OXPHOS

SQOR expression determines whether H₂S acts as an ETC electron donor or Complex IV inhibitor. In CRISPR SQOR-knockout cells, 68 H₂S up to 20 µM stimulated OCR by electron donation to ubiquinol with normal SQOR, while SQOR ablation caused toxicity at 5 µM. H₂S also inhibited BTY oxidation via CoA persulfide inactivation of ACADS. Two additional studies addressed H2S at supraphysiologic concentrations, demonstrating reversible Complex IV inhibition and metabolic reprogramming toward aerobic glycolysis. The paucity of human studies and the dual nature of H₂S effects across concentrations represent significant interpretive challenges.

Supercomplex and cristae integrity

Chronic H₂S exposure impairs respiratory chain architecture, not merely enzymatic activity (Figure 2D). In Caco-2 cells at colonic concentrations (50–200 µM; low RoB), chronic sulfide destabilized respiratory supercomplexes and caused Mic60 loss. 69 Depolarization correlated with loss of cristae architecture at physiologically relevant concentrations.

In summary, H₂S exhibits the biphasic profile described above: protective SQOR-mediated electron donation at low concentrations but Complex IV inhibition and supercomplex destabilization at high concentrations. 69 , 70 H₂S is a canonical example of a metabolite with opposing effects on mitochondria depending on concentration and SQOR expression.

Overall certainty

Overall certainty is low that H₂S produces biphasic effects on mitochondrial ETC activity and membrane potential. Two primary studies supported concentration-dependent SQOR-mediated electron donation at lower concentrations and respiratory inhibition at higher concentrations; two reviews were treated as contextual evidence and excluded from the certainty assessment.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)

LPS is a microbial structural product, a component of gram-negative bacterial outer membranes released during growth, division, or lysis (see Section 3 classification). Under conditions of increased intestinal permeability, LPS translocates into the systemic circulation causing endotoxemia.

ETC complex activity and OXPHOS

LPS impairs ETC activity in neurons but triggers compensatory bioenergetic responses in immune cells (Figures 2A and 2B). In SH-SY5Y cells, LPS increased ROS, reduced ΔΨm, and shifted metabolism to glycolysis, 71 whereas human THP-1 monocytes ultimately increased respiratory activity through endotoxin tolerance and compensatory biogenesis. 72

Mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α, NRF1, mtDNA)

LPS produces temporally distinct, tissue-dependent effects on biogenesis: in rat liver, mtDNA damage triggers compensatory NRF-1-mediated biogenesis, 73 and THP-1 monocytes show coordinated upregulation, 72 whereas neuronal cells show PGC-1α repression. 71

Mitophagy (PINK1/Parkin, PHB2/PGAM5)

LPS activates PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy as an adaptive response. 72 In a mouse LPS sepsis model, 74 PGAM5-mediated dephosphorylation of PHB2 inhibited PHB2 mitochondrial import, mitophagy, and UPRmt, whereas cardiomyocyte-specific Pgam5 deletion preserved mitochondrial function and attenuated LPS-induced myocardial dysfunction.

ROS and oxidative stress

LPS-amplified mitochondrial ROS serves dual roles: a damaging effector in neurons 71 and a required signal for compensatory NRF-1-mediated biogenesis in hepatocytes. 73

In summary, LPS produces the acute damage/compensatory recovery pattern detailed above. Chronic low-grade endotoxemia induces fission via Drp1 and epigenetic reprogramming; failure of compensatory biogenesis in chronic endotoxemia would produce progressive mitochondrial dysfunction. 71 , 72 LPS illustrates a temporal dualism, with acute mitochondrial damage followed by delayed compensatory biogenesis in some tissues.

Overall certainty

Overall certainty is moderate that LPS produces predominantly damaging mitochondrial effects, with compensatory responses varying by exposure duration, model, and endpoint, based on four primary studies.

Bacterial vitamins (B2, B3, B9, B12)

Gut bacteria synthesize essential vitamins that serve as critical cofactors for mitochondrial OXPHOS (Figures 2A and 2B). Riboflavin (B2) provides FAD/FMN for Complexes I and II; niacin (B3) provides NAD + for Complex I and sirtuins; folate (B9) supports mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism and mtDNA replication; and B12 participates in mitochondrial methylation. Dysbiosis-related depletion of vitamin-producing bacteria may contribute to secondary mitochondrial dysfunction.

ETC complex activity and OXPHOS (Riboflavin/B2)

Riboflavin-derived FAD and FMN serve as cofactors for Complexes I and II and for flavoproteins involved in fatty-acid beta-oxidation. 75 Dysbiosis-related riboflavin depletion directly threatens respiratory chain integrity.

OXPHOS and ATP production (Niacin/B3, NAD+)

Bacterially-derived niacin restores NAD + and activates SIRT1/PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis in muscle. Using human metagenomics/metabolomics, aged mice, and C2C myotubes with siRNA, 76 B. adolescentis-derived nicotinic acid restored NAD + in muscle, activating SIRT1/PGC-1α to stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis.

Mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α, mtDNA) (Niacin/B3, NAD+)

A predominantly open-label 5-month intervention enrolled 24 monozygotic twin pairs and analyzed 20 pairs: 16 BMI-discordant and four BMI-concordant. Both cotwins in the BMI-discordant pairs received NR; only the four concordant pairs contributed a blinded randomized NR-versus-placebo comparison 77 NR improved NAD+ metabolism and muscle mtDNA copy number, and NR-conditioned gut microbiota reproduced some effects, supporting a bidirectional microbiome–NAD+ relationship. Folate supports mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism including mtDNA replication and NADPH production. 78

CoA metabolism and TCA cycle (Folate/B9)

Folate supports mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism required for mtDNA replication, NADPH production, and formylmethionyl-tRNA synthesis, 78 linking bacterial folate production to mtDNA maintenance and complex assembly.

ROS and oxidative stress (B12)

In 94 elderly volunteers, B12 levels correlated with mtDNA D-loop methylation. 79 However, the modest correlation, cross-sectional design, and confounding potential substantially limit inference.

Bacterially-derived vitamins provide a nutritional link between gut microbiota and host mitochondrial OXPHOS. Riboflavin (FAD/FMN) is essential for Complexes I/II and fatty acid beta-oxidation. 75 Bacterially-derived niacin restores NAD + and activates SIRT1/PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis in muscle. 76 Folate supports mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism including mtDNA replication and NADPH production. 78 B12 may modulate mtDNA epigenetic regulation. 79

Overall certainty

Overall certainty is low that bacterially derived vitamins enhance mitochondrial OXPHOS and biogenesis in muscle and hepatic models, because studies use heterogeneous vitamin types and are mostly observational or provide indirect evidence. The nicotinamide/NAD+ pathway has the strongest vitamin-related evidence, including a predominantly open-label human twin intervention with a blinded randomized comparison limited to four concordant pairs. 75

Polyphenol metabolites (Urolithin A, Equol)

Urolithin A (UA) is produced exclusively by gut bacterial transformation of ellagitannins (from pomegranate, berries, and walnuts) by Enterocloster, Gordonibacter, and Ellagibacter species. UA is a potent inducer of PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy. 80 Equol is produced from soy isoflavone daidzein by Coriobacteriaceae; only 30–50% of humans produce equol.

Mitophagy (PINK1/Parkin)

UA is among the most potent known inducers of PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy (Figure 2D). In C. elegans, aged mice, and muscle cells, 80 UA activated PINK1/Parkin (deletion of either gene abolished lifespan extension) and improved muscle function via mitophagy-mediated clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria. A Phase I RCT in healthy older adults established UA safety and showed changes in mitochondrial gene-expression and plasma acylcarnitine biomarkers after 28 days; mitophagy was not directly measured in humans 81

ROS and oxidative stress (mitohormesis)

Equol acts through mitohormesis, where a controlled mitochondrial ROS pulse drives beneficial signaling. In primary HUVECs at 100 nmol/L, 82 equol stimulated mitochondrial superoxide required for eNOS activation via Akt and ERK1/2 signaling. Equol also promotes oocyte maturation through activation of the NRF2/KEAP1 signaling pathway, 83 extending equol’s mitochondrial effects to reproductive cell biology.

UA induced PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy and improved muscle function in preclinical models. 80 A review summarized the broader preclinical and translational evidence for UA but did not contribute to the certainty rating. 84 The human trial established safety and mitochondrial biomarker effects without directly measuring mitophagy. 81 Equol activates eNOS and Nrf2 via mitochondrial ROS-mediated hormesis. 82 Only 30–40% of individuals produce UA and 30–50% produce equol, necessitating supplementation or microbiome engineering for non-producers.

Overall certainty

Overall certainty is low for direct evidence that urolithin A induces PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy. One primary preclinical publication directly assessed mitophagy; the Phase I human trial established safety and mitochondrial biomarker effects but did not measure mitophagy, and the remaining Appendix A entry is a review. Overall certainty is low that equol enhances mitochondrial function in endothelial models, based on limited preclinical and observational evidence.

D-Lactate

D-lactate is the stereoisomer of L-lactate, produced almost exclusively by intestinal bacteria (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium) during carbohydrate fermentation. Mammalian cells lack efficient D-lactate dehydrogenase, making D-lactate a metabolite that interacts with mitochondria in distinct ways depending on concentration.

ETC activity and OXPHOS

At low concentrations, D-lactate activates mitochondrial OXPHOS through a direct non-metabolic mechanism. In cancer cells, primary T cells, and SLC16A knockouts, 85 unmetabolized D-lactate suppressed aerobic glycolysis and enhanced T cell proliferation. Isotope tracing confirmed a direct non-metabolic ETC stimulation mechanism. At elevated concentrations (>3 mM, as in short bowel syndrome), D-lactate may impair mitochondrial function, particularly in neurons lacking efficient D-lactate dehydrogenase. Physiological modeling 86 identified brain mitochondria as vulnerable, with proposed disruption via Complex I inhibition and pyruvate competition collectively reducing neuronal OXPHOS capacity; these mechanisms await experimental validation. As a practical threshold, toxicity appears to emerge above ~3 mM plasma D-lactate, a level reached in short bowel syndrome but not under normal physiological conditions.

D-lactate has dual effects: at low concentrations it directly activates ETC independently of metabolism, 85 while at > 3 mM it may impair neuronal mitochondria. 86 These opposing effects reflect genuine concentration-dependence.

Overall certainty

Overall certainty is very low that D-lactate modulates mitochondrial ETC activity in immune and neuronal models, because of two studies with opposing directions of effect.

Polyamines (Spermidine, Putrescine)

Polyamines (putrescine, spermidine, spermine) are produced by gut bacteria including E. coli, Bifidobacterium, and Lactobacillus. Spermidine is the most studied for mitochondrial effects and is a known inducer of autophagy/mitophagy. Spermidine regulates translation of mitochondrial proteins through hypusination of eIF5A.

Mitophagy (PINK1/Parkin, ATM kinase)

Spermidine induces PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy via a distinct upstream kinase. In primary human fibroblasts, 87 ATM inhibition blocked spermidine-induced mitophagy, establishing a novel ATM/PINK1/Parkin axis. In SH-SY5Y cells expressing P301L mutant tau, 88 spermidine improved respiration, membrane potential, ATP production, and decreased ROS, and restored LC3–mitochondrial colocalization and mitophagy in this disease-relevant tauopathy model.

OXPHOS and ATP production

Beyond mitophagy, spermidine restored mitochondrial respiration and ATP production in tauopathy cells, 88 and a human putrescine-production trial improved reactive hyperemia index but did not measure a direct mitochondrial endpoint. 89

CoA metabolism/mitochondrial protein translation (eIF5A hypusination)

Aberrant bacterial polyamine metabolism can generate toxic intermediates disrupting mitochondrial protein synthesis. A 12-week RCT randomized 44 healthy adults to B. animalis plus arginine or placebo; 34 participants remained in the principal analysis. The intervention increased intestinal putrescine production and improved reactive hyperemia index but did not identify N¹-APA or measure a direct mitochondrial endpoint. 89

Spermidine promotes mitochondrial quality control through ATM/PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy 87 and regulates mitochondrial protein translation via eIF5A hypusination. Clinical evidence suggests gut microbial polyamine production can improve vascular health. 89 However, a separate mechanistic study showed that aberrant bacterial polyamine metabolism can produce toxic intermediates (N1-APA) that antagonize mitochondrial function by inhibiting eIF5A hypusination. 90

Overall certainty

Overall certainty is low for spermidine-associated mitophagy and bioenergetic effects, based on two preclinical studies. The human putrescine-production RCT measured reactive hyperemia index rather than mitochondrial function, and the N¹-APA finding came from a separate mechanistic study showing damaging effects on mitochondrial protein translation.

Physiologically relevant concentrations: study doses versus in vivo levels

A key translational question is whether the metabolite concentrations used in the studies reviewed reflect physiologically attainable levels in human tissues. Because mitochondrial responses may differ across colonic luminal, portal, systemic, and tissue compartments, dose relevance must be interpreted in relation to biologic compartment as well as absolute concentration. The following discussion compares typical physiological concentration ranges with the experimental levels reported, as summarized in Table 3.

In most in vitro studies, SCFAs are applied at 1–10 mM, which approximates colonic luminal concentrations but exceeds portal and systemic levels by 10–100-fold. The human enema study by Vanhoutvin et al. 12 used 100 mM butyrate, a concentration within the colonic physiological range. Similarly, studies of secondary bile acids generally employed concentrations within or slightly above normal biliary or intestinal levels.

For tryptophan metabolites, protective effects are typically observed at acute doses of IPA around 10 µM, approximately 10–100 times higher than baseline plasma concentrations, whereas chronic exposure studies often use ≥ 1 mM, exceeding physiological levels by more than 1000-fold. Notably, colonic indole concentrations may reach the lower end of this experimental range.

Among uremic toxins, plasma concentrations in individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD) range widely: IS at 211–940 µM, pCS at 5–22 mg/L, and TMAO at 10–100 µM. Most experimental IS concentrations (100–1000 µM) mirror plasma levels observed in CKD patients, underscoring their clinical relevance. Experimental TMAO exposures ranged from 1 to 300 μM; respirometry in the isolated-heart study was performed at 10 and 100 μM, and the direction of effect varied by concentration and model.

For H2S, large gradients exist between colonic and systemic concentrations due to mucosal detoxification. The SQOR-dependent protective threshold (~10–20 µM) likely reflects portal exposure, whereas chronic exposure studies using 50–200 µM represent colonic, not systemic, levels.

LPS concentrations increase transiently after a high-fat meal (~0.6 EU/mL) and markedly during sepsis. In vitro studies often use 10–1000 ng/mL. Because conversion between ng/mL and EU/mL depends on the lot-specific endotoxin potency, these mass- and activity-based concentrations should not be directly equated; the experimental exposures nevertheless substantially exceed typical healthy circulating levels.

The NR twin intervention employed pharmacological doses to restore NAD+ pools. The contribution of bacterially synthesized vitamins depends heavily on the degree of dysbiosis.

For polyphenol metabolites, in vitro concentrations of UA often exceed plasma levels achievable through diet by several orders of magnitude, raising questions about translational relevance. However, tissue accumulation may partially close this gap. The doses used in equol studies generally fall within the upper physiological range observed in equol producers.

D-lactate levels rise to > 3 mM in short bowel syndrome and can reach 5–7 mM during acidosis episodes. The low-dose ETC activating concentration used by Cai et al. 85 corresponds closely to the upper normal range, whereas the toxic threshold (~3 mM) aligns with levels found in short bowel syndrome.

Polyamine studies frequently use spermidine at 10–100 µM, roughly 100–1000 times higher than circulating plasma levels, though these concentrations may approximate relevant intracellular levels where mitophagy occurs.

Overall, most in vitro studies reviewed here investigate metabolite concentrations that reflect colonic luminal or disease-state plasma levels but substantially exceed those found in healthy systemic circulation. This discrepancy represents a major barrier to clinical translation. The uremic toxin literature provides an exception, as IS and pCS concentrations used in studies correspond closely to CKD plasma values, enhancing clinical relevance. For metabolites with biphasic dose–response relationships (e.g., H₂S, PPA, IPA, TMAO, D-lactate), defining the precise concentrations at which effects shift from protective to harmful remains crucial. Future research should emphasize dose–response designs that encompass physiologically relevant ranges across portal, systemic, and tissue compartments.

Discussion

The metabolite-specific evidence above (summarized in Tables 1 and 2) identifies plausible intervention points rather than established therapeutic targets. Three questions organize the discussion that follows: which metabolite classes have the strongest direct mitochondrial evidence, which mitochondrial control nodes recur across otherwise distinct metabolites, and which features most constrain clinical translation. It should be emphasized that certainty ratings refer to the directional effect of a given metabolite or closely related metabolite subtype on a specific mitochondrial endpoint cluster, not clinical efficacy or therapeutic relevance.

Throughout the Discussion, direct mitochondrial evidence is distinguished from broader mechanistic context and downstream disease implications; the latter are presented as hypothesis-generating extensions rather than as evidence of established clinical efficacy.

Reducing toxic metabolite burden

Dietary approaches that reduce fermentable substrate loads could, in principle, limit excess PPA and D-lactate production. Targeted probiotics (e.g., BTY-producing Faecalibacterium and Roseburia) may competitively suppress Clostridia-derived pCS and PPA, although direct mitochondrial endpoint data remain limited. High-fat diet-disturbed gut microbiota-colonocyte interactions have been shown to dysregulate peripheral tryptophan-kynurenine metabolism, 91 further linking microbial community disruption to mitochondrial pathway dysregulation.

Enhancing protective metabolites

Prebiotic fibers can increase colonic BTY and acetate. Bacterially derived niacin (NAD+) and folate (one-carbon metabolism) may be augmented through targeted supplementation or enrichment of vitamin-producing taxa. 76 , 78 NR improved muscle mitochondrial-biogenesis markers in a predominantly open-label twin intervention; only four concordant pairs contributed a blinded randomized comparison. 77

Modulating biogenesis and mitophagy

.Preclinical studies identify UA as an inducer of PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy. 80 The Phase I human trial established safety and mitochondrial biomarker effects but did not directly measure mitophagy.⁷⁹ Because only 30–40% of individuals harbor UA-producing bacteria, direct supplementation may be required for non-producers. Spermidine and UDCA represent additional candidates for mitophagy induction and membrane stabilization, respectively.

Several translatability caveats warrant emphasis. Most evidence derives from preclinical models using supraphysiologic metabolite concentrations; whether portal and systemic levels in humans reach bioactive thresholds in target tissues remains uncertain. Tissue-specific responses (e.g., neurons versus immune cells for LPS, colonocytes versus hepatocytes for BTY) preclude simple extrapolation across organ systems. The biphasic dose–response profiles documented for H₂S, PPA, IPA, and TMAO mean that therapeutic windows may be narrow and require careful dose optimization in clinical settings.

Drug-repurposing opportunities

Several compounds reviewed here are already approved or in clinical development for other indications, suggesting repurposing hypotheses rather than clinically established mitochondrial therapies. UDCA, licensed for primary biliary cholangitis, restores ΔΨm and Drp1/MFN2 dynamics in fibroblasts from patients with Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease, 29 , 30 and a Phase II neurodegeneration trial is underway. Spermidine, available as a dietary supplement, induces ATM/PINK1/Parkin mitophagy and rescues tauopathy-model bioenergetics. 87 , 88 Preclinical evidence identifies UA as a potent inducer of PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy.⁷⁸ In the Phase I human trial, 28 days of UA supplementation established safety and produced changes in plasma acylcarnitines and skeletal-muscle mitochondrial gene expression, but mitophagy was not directly measured, 81 . NR, an NAD + precursor, improved muscle mitochondrial biogenesis and mtDNA copy number in a predominantly open-label monozygotic-twin intervention; only four concordant pairs contributed a blinded randomized comparison. 77 Because each of these agents targets a distinct node of mitochondrial quality control—membrane integrity (UDCA), mitophagy (spermidine, UA), and biogenesis (NR)—combinatorial strategies that address multiple nodes simultaneously warrant investigation. Fecal microbiota transplantation has demonstrated protective effects against doxorubicin-induced cardiac mitochondrial toxicity through Nrf2-mediated regulation of fission and fusion. 92 Akkermansia muciniphila has been shown to ameliorate doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity through mitochondrial protection. 93

Implications for neurological and psychiatric conditions

Mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a transdiagnostic feature of neurological and psychiatric illness, and the metabolite–mitochondria pathways reviewed here may contribute to disease pathophysiology across multiple conditions. In neurodegenerative diseases, IS suppresses PGC-1α and impairs ETC function in renal and vascular models, whereas PCS has shown mixed, model-dependent effects; whether analogous mechanisms operate in brain cells exposed to circulating toxin levels in chronic kidney disease–associated cognitive decline remains uncertain. BTY’s HDAC inhibitory and PGC-1α–activating properties have shown neuroprotective effects in obesity-induced neuroinflammation models, 5 and UA’s mitophagy induction has direct relevance to Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease where mitophagic failure is well documented.

In neurodevelopmental disorders, PPA-driven CoA trapping and mitochondrial fragmentation 19 may link gut dysbiosis to neuronal energy failure. For mood disorders, serotonin-mediated 5-HT2A/SIRT1/PGC-1α biogenesis 49 and kynurenine pathway modulation of cardiac and potentially cerebral mitochondrial remodeling 51 suggest that gut-derived tryptophan metabolites may influence mitochondrial function in affective circuits. These cross-condition parallels reinforce the value of targeting the microbiome–mitochondria axis as a shared therapeutic strategy. Given that mitochondrial dysfunction has been documented in autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, bipolar disorder, and major depressive disorder, the metabolite–mitochondria pathways identified here may represent shared upstream mechanisms warranting investigation in these populations. Although direct evidence linking gut-derived metabolite concentrations to mitochondrial endpoints in psychiatric cohorts remains sparse, the convergence of PGC-1α dysregulation, oxidative stress, and impaired mitophagy across these conditions suggests that the microbiome–mitochondria axis could serve as a transdiagnostic framework for hypothesis generation and future interventional studies.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. The evidence base is predominantly preclinical; human studies were few and often indirect; assays and exposure paradigms were heterogeneous; and physiologic concentration matching across colonic, portal, systemic, and tissue compartments remains incomplete. These features limit quantitative synthesis and constrain causal clinical inference. In addition, evidence for modulation of mitochondrial function by gut metabolites is uneven; bacterial vitamins, D-lactate, and polyamines remain supported by limited evidence.

The reviewed evidence base is skewed in the cellular and tissue systems on which it was built. Although 91 studies were included, the underlying experimental substrate is concentrated in a small number of cell lines—SH-SY5Y, C2C12, Caco-2, lymphoblastoid lines, H9C2, HL-1, THP-1, IPEC-J2, each used in only a few studies, and several organ systems (pancreatic, reproductive, pulmonary, stem-cell, endothelial) are represented by just one or two studies. Human in vivo evidence remains particularly limited: only seven of 91 studies involved human participants, and most provided observational, transcriptomic, or biomarker-level data rather than direct functional mitochondrial measurements. Animal evidence is dominated by C57BL/6 mice and a small set of disease-targeted rodent models, with limited representation of larger animals (3 pig studies) or non-mammalian systems (3 C. elegans studies, 1 yeast). Cell-type coverage within each tissue is also narrow: cardiac mitochondrial effects, for example, rely heavily on H9C2 and HL-1 cell lines and isolated rodent hearts rather than primary human cardiomyocytes, and intestinal mitochondrial effects are derived predominantly from Caco-2 and HCT lines rather than primary intestinal epithelium. This narrow substrate limits extrapolation to other cell types, tissues, and species, and means that several metabolite–mitochondria conclusions rest on a small and overlapping set of model systems. Future work should prioritize primary human cells, induced pluripotent stem cell–derived tissue models, and underrepresented organ systems.

Conclusions

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 91 studies across ten classes of gut-derived metabolites, revealing a complex network of metabolite–mitochondria interactions. Below, we distinguish conclusions by their level of evidentiary support.

Directly supported by the reviewed evidence

The clearest overall conclusion of this review is that chemically diverse gut-derived metabolites converge on a limited set of mitochondrial control nodes. After contextual sources were separated from eligible primary evidence, four metabolite–mitochondrial outcome groupings had moderate certainty: butyrate, propionate, indoxyl sulfate, and LPS. Butyrate generally enhanced mitochondrial endpoints, although one ex vivo study reported uncoupling; propionate showed dose-dependent effects mediated in part by CoA trapping; and IS was generally damaging. LPS produced predominantly damaging effects, with compensatory responses varying by exposure duration, model, and endpoint. PCS showed concentration- and model-dependent effects and was rated low certainty. H₂S showed a biphasic concentration response but was rated low certainty because only two primary studies contributed to the assessment. Preclinical evidence supports urolithin A-induced mitophagy, whereas the human trial established safety and mitochondrial biomarker effects rather than direct confirmation of mitophagy; certainty for UA-induced mitophagy was therefore low. PGC-1α emerged as a recurrent regulatory node, but human confirmation remains limited.

PGC-1α warrants particular emphasis as an organizing hub rather than a recurrent isolated finding. Across metabolite classes, protective signals tend to activate PGC-1α-linked programs of biogenesis and oxidative metabolism, whereas harmful metabolites often suppress the same axis through AhR signaling, oxidative stress, or related mechanisms. This convergence helps explain why otherwise diverse microbial products can yield partially overlapping mitochondrial phenotypes and identifies a testable framework for future mechanistic and translational studies.

Biologically plausible but unproven

Several mechanistic pathways are supported by preclinical evidence but lack the replication, human data, or consistency required for moderate certainty. These include acetate’s role in microglial metabolic fitness (5 preclinical studies, no direct human mitochondrial data), secondary bile acid modulation of mitochondrial dynamics through TGR5 signaling (heterogeneous effects across bile acid species), tryptophan metabolite regulation of ETC activity (concentration-dependent with opposing effects among metabolite subtypes), bacterial vitamin contributions to mitochondrial cofactor supply (largely observational and indirect), and polyamine-mediated mitophagy induction (limited to in vitro and animal models). The concept of a bidirectional microbiome–mitochondria axis, in which host mitochondrial status may reciprocally shape microbial community composition, is mechanistically coherent but currently lacks direct experimental demonstration.

Speculative. The translation of these preclinical findings into clinical therapeutics remains speculative. Whether portal and systemic metabolite concentrations in humans reach the bioactive thresholds used in cell-culture and animal models is unknown. The proposed drug-repurposing opportunities (UDCA for neurodegeneration, UA for mitophagy-related disease, NR for biogenesis) are extrapolated from mechanistic data and early-phase trials and require confirmation in disease-specific randomized controlled trials. The hypothesis that combinatorial strategies targeting multiple nodes of mitochondrial quality control simultaneously would yield additive or synergistic benefits is theoretically attractive but untested. Similarly, the clinical relevance of metabolite–mitochondria interactions across neurological, psychiatric, and cardiometabolic conditions is inferred from shared mechanistic pathways rather than demonstrated in patient populations.
