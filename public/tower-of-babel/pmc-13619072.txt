Persistent Sleep Disturbance Following Pandemic Lockdowns: Longitudinal Findings From the UK COVID‐19 Symptom Study Biobank
Authors: Barbara Toson, Nathan J. Cheetham, Julia R. B. Brown, Carole H. Sudre, Simon Proctor, Vicky Bowyer, Nicholas R. Harvey, Desaline Joseph, COVID Symptom Study Biobank Consortium, Adam Birdseye, Claire J. Steves, Sutapa Mukherjee, Emma L. Duncan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSleep disturbance and fatigue were highly prevalent during the COVID‐19 pandemic, in both infected and non‐infected individuals, though mostly assessed cross‐sectionally. To assess these symptoms after lockdown cessation, we conducted a prospective longitudinal study in a large UK cohort. Validated questionnaires assessing sleep disturbances, fatigue, depression, and anxiety were administered to COVID Symptom Study Biobank participants at two time periods—Autumn 2021 and Spring 2022 equinoxes—corresponding to lockdown and lifting of restrictions, respectively. After adjusting for differential participation using inverse probability weighting, data from 2390 participants were analysed. Generalised linear models examined SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, post‐COVID‐19 syndrome [PCS], and longitudinal changes of sleep disturbances and fatigue, adjusting for potential confounders. Despite lifting of lockdown, high levels of sleep disturbance and fatigue persisted, with no significant improvement over time, independently of baseline or new SARS‐CoV‐2 infection or PCS. De novo SARS‐CoV‐2 marginally worsened STOP‐Bang scores although below clinical significance. There was a trend for interaction between baseline SARS‐CoV‐2 status and Chalder Fatigue scores on follow‐up fatigue (B = 0.19, 95% CI = −0.01 to 0.39, p = 0.057). Receipt of booster vaccination lowered Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores (B = −0.53, 95% CI = −0.96 to −0.10, p = 0.016) but doubled likelihood of restless legs syndrome symptoms (OR = 2.45, 95% CI = 1.38 to 4.37, p = 0.002). Overall, the high rates of sleep disturbances and fatigue observed during lockdown persisted after restrictions were lifted, independent of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. Sleep disruption reported during lockdown may endure unless actively addressed. Given the high prevalence of both sleep disturbance and fatigue, routine assessment of sleep is recommended for individuals presenting with persistent post‐pandemic fatigue, including PCS.

Introduction

The impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic continues, with ongoing SARS‐CoV‐2 infections, hospitalisations, and deaths. Many individuals report long symptom duration after COVID‐19, with fatigue the commonest symptom. The most recent UK figures (March 2023) showed 1.9 million people (2.9% of UK population) self‐reported ongoing post‐infection symptoms (‘Long COVID’) (Office of National Statistics UK 2023), though these data lack control population prevalences for meaningful comparisons (Amin‐Chowdhury and Ladhani 2021).

Widespread pandemic‐related sleep disturbance was evident from early 2020, in both infected and uninfected individuals (Jahrami et al. 2021). Sleep disturbance commonly causes fatigue; thus, a key question is whether sleep disturbance contributes to fatigue in individuals with Post‐COVID Syndrome (PCS) (World Health Organisation 2023). Our previous cross‐sectional study, in individuals with and without previous SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, found fatigue was common but not necessarily due to SARS‐CoV‐2 per se: rather, fatigue was significantly associated with validated measures of sleep disturbance, and with anxiety and depression (Proctor et al. 2025). This raised the therapeutic possibility of using proven sleep interventions for individuals with PCS, fatigue, and sleep disturbance. Relevantly, to date no effective treatment for PCS has been identified. Our findings concorded with a recent paper exploring sleep quality and fatigue in PCS, showing that many individuals with PCS experience insomnia and/or disrupted sleep patterns, exacerbating tiredness and fatigue, and hindering recovery (Jauregui‐Renaud et al. 2024). These authors also advocated a comprehensive approach to PCS that included addressing sleep issues as a critical component of managing fatigue.

Our cross‐sectional study was conducted at the 2021 autumn equinox, when the UK was experiencing ongoing regional and national lockdowns. After 27 January 2022, all UK lockdown measures ceased. We hypothesised that sleep behaviours might improve after lockdown cessation, with a return towards pre‐pandemic patterns. We therefore resurveyed our cohort at the 2022 spring equinox, assessing for longitudinal changes in validated sleep measures as lockdown measures resolved, in individuals with and without SARS‐CoV‐2 infection.

Methods

COVID Symptom Study Biobank

This prospective longitudinal observational cohort was volunteers from the COVID Symptom Study Biobank (CSSB) (Yorkshire and Humber NHS Research Ethics Committee Approval Ref: 20/YH/0298), itself recruited from the King's College London [KCL]‐COVID Symptom Study (KCL‐CSS)/ZOE study (Drew et al. 2020) (cohort details: Supporting Information). CSSB participants were selected from the KCL‐CSS/ZOE study according to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection status and symptom duration, emphasising recruitment of individuals with long symptom duration (Osterdahl et al. 2023).

Data Collection

CSSB volunteers were invited to participate in the current study (‘Sleeping Through The Pandemic’), comprising online validated sleep, physical health, and mental health questionnaires: Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI) (Buysse et al. 1989), sleep condition indicator (SCI) (Espie et al. 2018), STOP‐Bang (Chung et al. 2016), Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) (Johns 1991), single question screening for Restless Legs Syndrome Symptoms (RLSS) (Ferri et al. 2007), the Chalder Fatigue Scale (CFS) (Chalder et al. 1993; Cella and Chalder 2010), shortened versions of Generalised Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD‐2) (Kroenke et al. 2007) and Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐2) (Gilbody et al. 2007) (questionnaire details: Table S2). Where required, formal permission to use questionnaires was sought and granted (personal correspondence, SM and ELD). The cohort was surveyed twice, before and after cessation of UK lockdowns, as close to autumn and spring equinoxes as feasible to ensure equal sunlight exposure: October 2021 (baseline) and March 2022 (follow‐up). Of note, this cohort was recruited during the pandemic; thus pre‐pandemic data were not available.

Inclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria were: key demographic data (age, sex, self‐defined ethnicity, area of residence) and sufficient self‐reported SARS‐CoV‐2 test results and symptom assessments (self‐reported through the KCL‐CSS ZOE app) for disease grouping assignment (see below). Completion of both baseline and follow‐up questionnaires was required for determination of longitudinal outcomes.

Generation of Inverse Participation Weights

Weights representing the inverse probability of completing both baseline and follow‐up sleep questionnaires were generated from multivariable logistic regression models. Inverse probability weights (IPW) were then used to reduce potential selection bias from differential response rates, as per approaches used in previous CSSB studies (Proctor et al. 2025; Cheetham et al. 2023) (see Supporting Information). As missing data imputation techniques affect IPW robustness, these were not performed.

Demographic characteristics were summarised using mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical data. Between‐group differences based on baseline SARS‐CoV‐2 results (negative/positive) were examined using independent samples T‐tests or chi‐square tests, as appropriate. All subsequent analyses were performed using IPW adjustments.

Sleep scores and mental health measures, both overall and stratified by SARS‐CoV‐2 infection status at baseline, were summarised at baseline and follow‐up, as were differences between these timepoints, using descriptive statistics as above. A McNemar's test was used to examine changes in RLSS between rounds.

A ‘follow‐up adjusted for baseline’ approach was used to estimate longitudinal change over time in outcomes between baseline and follow‐up (Tennant et al. 2022). Generalised linear models (GLMs) were employed to examine the influence of each measure at baseline on the same measure at follow‐up. For continuous sleep and mental health measures, we specified a GLM with a normal distribution and identity link. For categorical sleep measures (specifically, RLSS), we specified a GLM with a binomial distribution and log link. Results are presented before and after adjustment for sex, age, and body mass index [BMI] (at time of joining CSSB), and presence of chronic health problems (at time of app registration) (reduced model). Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) was considered as a proxy for socioeconomic status; however, inclusion in the multivariable models resulted in sparse cells and unstable estimates. IMD was therefore excluded to preserve model stability.

To investigate potential influence of baseline SARS‐CoV‐2 infection status on relationships between each sleep measure over time, the same GLMs as above were applied. The reduced model analyses included an interaction between SARS‐CoV‐2 infection status at baseline (positive vs. negative) and corresponding sleep measure at baseline. The full model analyses included anxiety and depression scores at baseline as well. The same approach was used to assess the potential effect of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection status and symptom duration at baseline.

To detect any effect from contracting SARS‐CoV‐2 between baseline and follow‐up on any difference(s) in sleep measures between the two questionnaires (Delta Δ = Follow‐up [Round 2] − Baseline [Round 1]), we applied GLMs with both reduced and full adjustments for potential confounders, considering SARS‐CoV‐2 status change between timepoints as a factor with four categories: No evidence of infection (only negative tests recorded at baseline and follow‐up) [reference]Initial infection only (positive at baseline only, no subsequent positive test),Reinfection (positive at baseline, at least one additional positive test (≥ 90 days after initial positive test) by follow‐up), and De novo infection (negative at baseline, with at least one positive test by follow‐up).

The ‘follow‐up adjusted for baseline’ approach was also used to determine the effect of receiving booster vaccination between baseline and follow‐up questionnaires, noting that in November 2021 (i.e., between questionnaires) the UK Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunisation recommended booster vaccination for all adults aged ≥ 50 years.

Importantly, although the longitudinal structure allows examination of temporal relationships, this observational study cannot determine causal effect; and all results should be interpreted as associations only. Lastly, as pre‐pandemic data were not available, pre‐pandemic comparisons were not possible.

Results

Cohort

As shown in Figure 1, all CSSB volunteers were invited to participate; 3766 (45.11%) of 8349 completed/partially completed baseline questionnaires and subsequently 3902 (46.74%) completed follow‐up questionnaires with 2778 (73.76% of baseline participants) completing both. Of these 2778 individuals, 2390 (86.03%) had complete information on SARS‐CoV‐2 status, symptom duration, and IPWs, and comprise the current study cohort. The majority of the study cohort (1854 of 2390, 77.6%) continued to log symptoms, SARS‐CoV‐2 test results, and vaccination information regularly (at least fortnightly) between baseline and follow‐up.

Systematic participation bias was evident (some regional differences; some demographic differences [less likely to participate: younger ages, higher BMI; more likely to participate: May 2021 recruitment cohort]; some health differences [more likely to participate: COVID‐positive individuals with longer (vs shorter) duration of symptoms]) (Figure S1). Adjusted demographic summaries accounting for participation bias are provided in Table S3. As previously (Proctor et al. 2025), IPW‐adjusted results were used in subsequent analyses.

Table 2 shows sleep questionnaire scores and mental health measures at baseline and follow‐up, for the cohort overall and by baseline SARS‐CoV‐2 status; and differences over time. At both timepoints, significant sleep disturbance was evident, with mean PSQI scores exceeding a clinically‐significant cutoff (PSQI ≥ 5). RLSS prevalence was considerably higher (approximately three to fivefold) than typical for the general population (5%–10%), at both timepoints. Other scores remained below thresholds of clinical concern (Table S2).

For all scores, differences over time were negligible (statistically and clinically), for the cohort overall and by baseline SARS‐CoV‐2 status. While the marginal proportions of RLSS appeared similar to one another (lowest section of Table 2), considering the 2071 paired weighted counts: 208 weighted pairs reported no RLSS at baseline but had RLSS at follow‐up, while 148 weighted pairs had RLSS at baseline but not at follow‐up, indicating a significant shift in RLSS status over time, with more new cases emerging than resolving (McNemar's chi2 (1) = 10.11, p = 0.002).

Baseline sleep scores, fatigue scores, and mental health measures all had strongly positive associations with corresponding follow‐up results (Table S4). For the unadjusted model: B coefficients were 0.73–0.84 for sleep scores, 0.73 for Chalder Fatigue Scale, and 0.55 and 0.60 for GAD‐2 and PHQ‐2 scores respectively. Similar results were seen after adjustment for sex, age, BMI, and number of comorbidities per baseline data. This was particularly notable for RLSS (odds ratio [OR] = 19.2, standard error [SE] = 4.4, p < 0.001).

Importantly, baseline SARS‐CoV‐2 infection status did not affect the relationship between baseline sleep scores on follow‐up results (Table S5). Assessing for interaction between baseline SARS‐CoV‐2 infection status and baseline Chalder Fatigue Scale scores upon follow‐up results showed a trend towards significance both in the reduced model (B = 0.19, SE = 0.10, p = 0.057) and after the inclusion of baseline anxiety and depression measures in the model (B = 0.19, SE = 0.10, p = 0.062).

Similarly, baseline COVID‐19 symptom duration (negative, short, or long COVID‐19 status) did not significantly affect the relationship between baseline and follow‐up sleep measures (Table S6).

Changing SARS‐CoV‐2 Infection Status and Differences Between Baseline and Follow‐Up Scores

Table 3 shows the data according to SARS‐CoV‐2 status at baseline and follow‐up, including new SARS‐CoV‐2 infection (whether repeat or de novo). There was no impact on differences between rounds in sleep measures (delta Δ), with the exception of SARS‐CoV‐2 status on STOP‐Bang scores (F(3, 1267) = 3.69, p = 0.0117), which were significantly higher for individuals with de novo infection compared to individuals who remained negative (B = 0.24, 95% CI = 0.04–0.45, p = 0.019) or who experienced re‐infection (B = 0.30, 95% CI = 0.08–0.52, p = 0.007). However, these differences were small. Given that a clinically meaningful STOP‐Bang change is typically ≥ 3 points, the small longitudinal change in our cohort—although statistically significant—does not support meaningful clinical change.

Discussion

Here we assessed sleep scores and mental health measures longitudinally, using validated questionnaires, in a large cohort of predominantly middle‐aged females, at two sequential equinoxes (periods with identical light exposure) but with very different social circumstances (during and after lockdown restrictions). Our cohort showed clinically significant sleep disturbance and poor mental health, with little improvement over time despite lockdown release. Neither SARS‐CoV‐2 infection status, at baseline or follow‐up, nor the presence or absence of PCS, had independent effects, suggesting that ongoing poor sleep, fatigue, and mental health of our cohort were not from SARS‐CoV‐infection per se. Our results suggest that once poor sleep was present, it was unlikely to improve without intervention.

Individuals with de novo SARS‐CoV‐2 infection between questionnaires had slight increases in STOP‐Bang scores. This questionnaire assesses for obstructive sleep apnoea and includes snoring and tiredness/fatigue components, which symptoms might reasonably be expected after any viral illness including COVID‐19. However, the average time between de novo infection and follow‐up questionnaire was 70 days.

Receiving booster vaccination slightly reduced subjective sleepiness (ESS) across all participants. Symptom improvement post‐vaccination has been reported in individuals with PCS previously (Rjoob et al. 2025). However, RLSS increased over time, and after vaccination. To contextualise our findings: large international studies showed almost doubling of RLS symptom prevalence during the pandemic (vs. pre‐pandemic rates), associated with social restrictions, insomnia, and depression (Partinen et al. 2024). We previously noted high prevalence of RLSS in our cohort at baseline (Proctor et al. 2025); the odds ratio of RLSS at follow‐up was a log‐order higher for baseline RLSS than for vaccination. RLSS are also associated with anxiety and depression (Miyaguchi et al. 2024), both of which were also common in our cohort at both time points.

Multiple studies have shown deleterious effects of the pandemic on sleep, in both infected and uninfected individuals, and across population strata internationally. Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses highlight disruption of sleep patterns globally, with widespread deterioration in sleep quality, increased insomnia symptoms, and greater use of sleep medication, especially during stringent lockdown periods (Limongi et al. 2023; Jahrami et al. 2022; Ceolin et al. 2024; Ong et al. 2021). One comprehensive review, including meta‐analysis of 250 papers with data from half a million participants and 49 countries, suggested prevalence of sleep disturbance of ~40% globally, particularly during lockdown periods, affecting 52% in individuals with COVID‐19, 46% in children and adolescents, 42% of healthcare workers, 41% in populations with special healthcare needs, 41% of university students, and 37% in general populations (noting these prevalences cannot be directly compared (Jahrami et al. 2022)).

Available data suggest that pandemic‐induced sleep disturbance generally persisted; however, most longitudinal inferences have been drawn from comparisons of cross‐sectional data: few studies have assessed sleep longitudinally and fewer still after lockdown relaxation. A large UK study assessing sleep longitudinally (8163 adults assessed at multiple time points between April 2020 and Jan 2021) and a bespoke survey (self‐report of increased sleep loss) showed marked increases in perceived sleep loss during the pandemic compared with pre‐pandemic sleep. Although prevalences did lessen over time, perceived sleep loss tracked closely with lockdown stringency; the study did not continue after complete lockdown release (Falkingham et al. 2022). A smaller study from Hong Kong assessing sleep longitudinally (339 participants at last assessment in 2021) showed significant and worsening pandemic‐related increases in insomnia symptoms and depressed mood over time; again, this study did not continue after lockdown release (Lam et al. 2021). A large early pandemic study from 2020, using data from approximately 113,000 individuals using wearable devices from 20 countries, showed poorer sleep compared with pre‐pandemic data longitudinally; and, cross‐sectionally, that stricter lockdowns were associated with poorer sleep (Ong et al. 2021); again, data were not tracked after lockdown cessation. Lastly, a longitudinal study in 216 Spanish healthcare workers showed high levels of pandemic‐induced insomnia that persisted after lockdown release; however, individuals remained vulnerable to long‐term sleep disruption from pandemic‐related stress, social isolation, and anxiety (Gil‐Almagro et al. 2024).

Thus, there is a critical data gap regarding the progression of pandemic‐induced sleep disruption, particularly post‐lockdown. Disordered sleep has a myriad of health consequences, affecting adaptive and innate immunity, inflammation, neuroendocrinology, and neuropsychiatric diseases (Medic et al. 2017; Irwin 2015). Our results concur with the broad pre‐pandemic literature: without specific intervention, sleep disturbance triggered by a precipitating factor usually persists beyond the immediate crisis; and once symptoms are habituated, spontaneous resolution is unlikely (Morin et al. 2009, 2020; Saltychev et al. 2021). Our findings reinforce and highlight the importance of monitoring for sleep disruption reported in the context of the pandemic, and, if appropriate, intervening to mitigate the known long‐term health consequences (Altena et al. 2020).

Strengths and Weaknesses

This study represents one of the largest longitudinal sleep cohorts to date, with strong participant retention and detailed self‐reported data on symptoms, testing, and vaccination. While we acknowledge differential participation by demographic characteristics, we employed IPW to address sampling bias due to cohort attrition. However, IPW cannot adjust for unmeasured confounding—including socioeconomic status, changes in employment, and shifts in lifestyle—cohort self‐selection, or the lack of true pre‐pandemic baseline data. Consequently, the cohort may not reflect the wider population, and generalisability is limited. Further, our cohort was predominantly white, middle‐aged, and female—consistent with CSSB and KCL‐CSS/ZOE demographics—limiting our study's generalisability to men, younger populations (including adolescents), and ethnic minorities. We also do not have employment data, including whether employment changed in the pandemic (including furlough) and/or included shift work. We acknowledge that data were entirely self‐reported and not linked to health records. Further, SARS‐CoV‐2 testing was voluntary and individualised, noting, though, that free lateral flow kits were available in the UK until 1 April 2022, covering our study period, and testing was often required by workplaces. Lastly, CSSB recruitment strategy targeted individuals with long symptom duration, restricting some quantitative approaches, although in general our large sample allowed use of parametric methods.

While sleep disturbance was common during and after lockdown, it was not associated with SARS‐CoV‐2 infection per se, or with PCS. However, without pre‐pandemic data, we cannot determine whether the high prevalence of sleep disturbance in our cohort reflects pre‐existing vulnerabilities or enduring consequences of the pandemic. Given the cohort's demographic composition—predominantly older, white women, who are reported to have higher baseline rates of insomnia and sleep disturbance compared to the general population—it is also possible that participants entered the study with higher rates of sleep‐related symptoms. Further, although we assessed participants during contrasting phases of the pandemic—from lockdown to release—the short timeframe may have been insufficient to detect sleep recovery. Nonetheless, pre‐pandemic data suggest induced sleep disorders usually persist; moreover, for many individuals lockdown was traumatic and post‐traumatic stress may have contributed to ongoing sleep disturbance (O'Regan et al. 2021). Finally, as fatigue is the commonest symptom in PCS, and poor sleep quality contributes to fatigue, our data support the potential for proven sleep therapies to help fatigue in individuals with PCS and sleep disturbance—and, soberly, that poor sleep will not improve unless this problem is actively addressed.

Conclusions

Sleep disturbance and fatigue were widespread during the pandemic lockdown and persisted after lockdown ended. Reassuringly, these were not directly related to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. However, clinical intervention is likely to be needed to improve sleep quality in affected individuals—which may also alleviate fatigue in individuals with ongoing pandemic‐related symptoms.
