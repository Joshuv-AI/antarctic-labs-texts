Age‑stratified median effective dose (ED₅₀) of remimazolam besylate for successful gastroscope insertion without body movement in geriatric patients
Authors: Si-Qi Hao, Zhe Ma, Fang-Yu Zhang, Yi-Xin Hu, Li-Xin An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRemimazolam besylate is increasingly used for procedure sedation anesthesia (PSA) in gastrointestinal endoscopy, but age‑specific dosing for elderly patients is unclear. This study determined the median effective dose (ED₅₀) for successful gastroscope insertion without patient movement in two geriatric age groups.MethodsIn a prospective single‑center trial, 51 patients were assigned by age to Group L (65–70 years, n = 27) or Group H (> 70 years, n = 24). All received 0.1 µg/kg sufentanil, followed by an initial remimazolam bolus of 0.15 mg/kg (Group L) or 0.10 mg/kg (Group H). Doses were adjusted in 0.02 mg/kg steps using a modified Dixon up‑and‑down method based on the previous patient’s response (movement vs. no movement). Primary outcome was ED₅₀ for successful insertion. Secondary outcomes were the induction time, hemodynamic changes, hypoxemia, and other adverse events.ResultsED50 was 0.171 mg/kg in Group L, significantly higher than 0.098 mg/kg in Group H (P < 0.001); probit analysis gave similar values (0.175 mg/kg vs. 0.106 mg/kg). ED₉₅ values were 0.235 mg/kg (Group L) and 0.131 mg/kg (Group H). Induction times were comparable (27 s vs. 25 s, P = 0.394). Both groups achieved rapid induction (< 1 min) and recovery time. Transient SpO₂ drops (< 95%) occurred in 1 patient (3.7%) in Group L and 2 patients (8.3%) in Group H; no hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 90%) was observed. Hypotension occurred in 7.4% (Group L) and 20.8% (Group H, P = 0.238). Movement incidence and VAS scores were similar.ConclusionsFor elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy, the ED₅₀ of remimazolam is 0.171 mg/kg for ages 65–70 and 0.098 mg/kg for > 70 years combined with 0.1 µg/kg sufentanil. These ED50 values are specific to this sufentanil co-administration regimen; the ED50 of remimazolam alone would be higher. This regimen provides rapid onset and recovery with low respiratory and circulatory risk in this cohort. However, the higher incidence of hypotension (20.8%) in patients > 70 years warrants increased vigilance for circulatory depression in this age group.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, No:ChiCTinR2500110081. Background

With the growing number of elderly people, there is a real uptick in the need for gastrointestinal endoscopy. However, providing procedural sedation and analgesia (PSA) to older adults faces unique challenges. Physical changes in aging affect drug pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD) and elders may experience adverse events (AEs) like hypotension, respiratory depression, or slow recovery [1]. Moderate sedation helps patients tolerate the procedure better and boosts success, but common meds like propofol and midazolam have major downsides here. We really need safer, more reliable alternatives [2].

Remimazolam besylate, a new ultrashort-acting benzodiazepine, shows promise for procedural sedation. It takes effect quickly, doesn’t depend on specific organs to break down, and can be reversed with flumazenil [3]. Midazolam, by contrast, is metabolized through the liver’s CYP3A4 enzyme. Remimazolam instead uses tissue esterases for breakdown, so its effects are more consistent—even in patients with kidney or liver problems [4]. What’s more, it’s easier on the circulatory system—way less likely to cause hypotension than propofol—making it a great fit for older adults [5]. One study found that in adults getting endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), hypotension rates dropped by half: from 67.9% with propofol to 32.1% with remimazolam [6]. Another study showed that remimazolam tosylate led to higher average mean arterial pressure (MAP), better MoCA scores, less anesthetic used, and faster extubation compared to propofol—based PSA [7]. And for gastrointestinal endoscopy patients, remimazolam cut hypoxemia risk from 17.4% (with propofol) down to just 2.8% [8].

Remimazolam has its perks, but the best dose for elderly gastroscopy patients still isn’t clear. Gastroscope insertion is the most irritating part of the procedure—it often triggers gag reflexes and involuntary movements that mess up the process [9]. Right now, sedation plans usually use fixed doses or borrow dosing from colonoscopy studies. They ignore the big differences in physiology and procedure between these two endoscopies [10]. What’s more, aging varies a lot from person to person. We need to split patients into subgroups (like 65–70 vs. > 70 years) to account for differences in drug sensitivity, body makeup, and frailty [11]. The median effective dose (ED50) is key for setting evidence-based dosing. Past studies mostly focused on colonoscopy or general sedation goals — gastroscopy-specific data is lacking [12]. The up—and—down sequential allocation method (Dixon’s method) works great for finding ED50 in fragile populations. It limits patients’ exposure to too little or too much drug, while still keeping results statistically reliable [13]. This study aims to find the ED50 of remimazolam besylate for smooth gastroscope insertion (no body movement) in elderly patients. We’ll split them by age: 65–70 and > 70 years. Filling this knowledge gap will let us create safer, more personalized sedation plans — ones that boost procedure success and cut risks for this high-risk group.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This prospective clinical trial was conducted at Beijing Friendship Hospital Capital Medical University and approved by the Ethics Committee of the hospital (Approval No: 2025-P2-261–01). This trial was registered at Chictr.org.cn on September 30, 2025, by the principal investigator, Lixin An (ChiCTR2500110081), prior to the first participant enrolled. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participating in the trial.

Study population

Elderly patients aged ≥ 65 years scheduled to undergo gastroscopy procedure under PSA were enrolled from October 9, 2025 to November 15, 2025. They were allocated into two groups naturally according to age: Group L (65 −70 years) and Group H (age > 70 years). The inclusion criteria: (1) ≥ 65 years, regardless of sex; (2) Body mass index (BMI) 18∼28 kg/cm2; (3) American Society Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I/II; (4) STOP-Bang score < 5; (5) Scheduled for gastroscopy under PSA; (6) Understanding clearly, voluntarily participating, and signing the informed consent form themselves. The exclusion criteria: (1) Chronic pain patients with long-term use of analgesics, psychoactive medications or alcohol abuse; (2) Body weight < 15% or > 15% of ideal body weight; (3) Preoperative abnormal elevation of liver/renal function tests; (4) Pre-existing severe central nervous system disorders or major psychiatric diseases; (5) Opioid addiction or dependence; (6) Participation in other clinical trials within the past 4 weeks. Discharge criteria: (1) Withdrawing without providing justification; (2) Failing inclusion criteria or meeting any exclusion criteria, as well as those with no test records; (3) Case report forms (CRFs) with insufficient documentation for efficacy evaluation; (4) Endoscopic procedures exceeding 1 h.

Interventions

All patients underwent gastroscopy without premedication after ≥ 8 h of routine fasting. Upon entering the operating room, the routine vital signs (electrocardiogram (ECG), heart rate (HR), noninvasive blood pressure (NBP) and pulse oximetry (SpO2) were monitored and recorded. Then, intravenous catheterization, left lateral position, and 6 L/min oxygen continuous inhalation via a facemask for 3 min were supplied.

According to the modified Dixon’s up-and-down method (MDUDM) [14], 0.1 μg/kg sufentanil (AB41102211, Yichang Renfu, Hubei,China) was administered intravenously within 60 s. 3 min later, 0.15 mg/kg of remimazolam besylate (AC4100101, Yichang Renfu, Hubei,China) was administered intravenously in Group L and 0.10 mg/kg of remimazolam besylate in Group H within 30 s for anesthesia induction.

We used the modified observer’s assessment of alertness/sedation (MOAA/S) score to check sedation depth [15]. After the patients’ MOAA/S score ≤ 1, the gastroscope insertion was performed by an experienced endoscopist with more than 1000 procedures. The patient’s responses to the gastroscope insertion were classified as either ‘movement’ or ‘no-movement’. The ‘movement’ response was defined as the occurrence of any one of the following objective criteria: (1) Coughing or gagging that interfered with the procedure; (2) Gross purposeful movement of limbs or head; (3) Grimacing or other facial expressions indicating distress; (4) A sudden increase in HR by more than 20 beats/min from the baseline; (5) A sudden increase in systolic blood pressure (SBP) by more than 20 mmHg from the baseline. All assessments were strictly based on these predefined criteria. The hemodynamic thresholds (≥ 20 bpm increase in HR or ≥ 20 mmHg increase in SBP) were used as objective markers of inadequate sedation, as they represent clinically significant autonomic responses to gastroscope insertion that may occur even in the absence of overt body movement. The conditions of endoscope insertion were only evaluated at the first attempt and a bolus of 0.02 mg/kg remimazolam besylate, as a rescue medication, was administered immediately once movement responses or condition of MOAA/S score ≥ 2 occurred during the procedure.

We defined hypotension as mean arterial pressure (MAP) below 65 mmHg or a drop of more than 30% from the baseline value. When MAP starts to drop, intravenous 100 ml crystalloid fluid should be administered quickly. If the hypotension occurs, 6 mg ephedrine or 1 mg methoxamine will be given. Bradycardia was when HR fell below 45 beats/min, and intravenous 0.25–0.5 mg atropine was administered. Hypoxemia was when SpO2 dropped below 90%, which required facemask-assisted breathing or mechanical ventilation if severe. All relevant therapy interventions were documented. Other maintenance-phase management decisions were made by the anesthesiologists and all kinds of adverse events occurred during procedure were treated according to relevant clinical guidelines.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the ED50 of remimazolam besylate for successful gastroscope insertion in elderly patients. ED50 of remimazolam besylate was evaluated according to the MDUDM as above. We stopped enrolling patients until at least seven movement/no-movement pairs occur.

The secondary endpoints included induction time, hypoxemia incidence, hypotension and all other AEs from induction until the end of gastroscopy procedure. Visual analog scale (VAS) scores, the incidence of post operative nausea and vomiting (PONV), satisfaction score of patients and endoscopist were recorded at PACU. Induction time was defined from the beginning given of remimazolam to the time of MOAA/S ≤ 1. Hypoxemia was defined as SpO2 < 90%. Subclinical respiratory depression was defined as 90% ≤ SpO2 < 95% [16]. Satisfaction score was set from 0 to 10, 0 was dissatisfied, 10 was the most satisfied.

Sample size and statistical analysis

According to the the up-and-down method’s stop rule, 20–40 patients each group would yield consistent estimates of ED50/ED95 [14, 17]. Therefore given a drop-out rate of 10%, a sample size was set as 25 patients per group and 51 patients was actual enrolled finally. SPSS27.0.0 software (IBM SPSS Statistics Inc., New York, USA) was used for statistical analysis, and GraphPad Prism 9.3.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was performed for drawing figures.

The MDUDM plots for the success and failure of gastroscopic sedation in the two groups were shown in Figs. 2 and 3, and seven crossovers were reached by each group. The dose–response curve from the probit analysis of the remimazolam besylate dose and the probability of successful sedation in each group were shown in Fig. 4. The mean (95% CI) ED50 of remimazolam besylate combined with 0.1 µg/kg sufentanil for inhibiting the gastroscopy reaction calculated by the MDUDM in Group L (0.171 (95% CI, 0.144–0.198) mg/kg) was significantly higher than Group H (0.098 (95% CI, 0.062–0.142) mg/kg) (P < 0.001). These values are specific to the combination with sufentanil and do not reflect the ED₅₀ of remimazolam as a single agent. It suggested that older elderly patients require less remimazolam besylate to achieve target sedation. To further verify the reliability of this result, we also used probit regression analysis, the mean (95% CI) ED50 and ED95 values estimated by the probit regression analysis were 0.175 (95% CI, 0.131–0.208) mg/kg and 0.235 (95% CI, 0.204–0.470) mg/kg in Group L, and 0.106 (95% CI, 0.098–0.118) mg/kg and 0.131 (95% CI, 0.114–0.148) mg/kg in Group H, respectively (Table 2). Only 1 (3.7%) patient in Group L and 2 (8.3%) patients in Group H experienced a decrease in SpO2 during the procedure, which was below 95%. No patient in either group experienced hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90%), P = 0.607. The incidence of hypotension in Group L was 7.4%, whereas 20.8% in Group H, P = 0.238. No patient happened bradycardia, arrhythmia, or any discomfort reactions such as pain and PONV. All endoscopists and patients were satisfied with anesthesia effect, with a score of 10.

Discussion

The present up-and-down sequential allocation trial provides the first age-stratified determination of the ED50 of remimazolam besylate for successful gastroscopy insertion without body movement in geriatric patients. The ED50 was 0.171 (95% CI 0.144–0.198) mg/kg in the 65–70 years cohort and 0.098 (95% CI 0.062–0.142) mg/kg in patients > 70 years, demonstrating a clear age-related reduction in the required dose. The ED95 of remimazolam besylate in 65–70 years old patients was 0.235 (0.204–0.470) mg/kg and 0.131 (0.114–0.148) mg/kg in patients > 70 years. No hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90%) occurred, though subclinical respiratory depression (90% ≤ SpO2 ≤ 95%) happened in 3.7% of Group L and 8.3% of Group H. When remimazolam besylate is used in patients aged > 70 years, circulation inhibition should be concerned which incidence was more common (20.8% vs. 7.4% in Group L), a difference that, while not reaching statistical significance in this modestly sized study, suggests a clinically important trend that warrants vigilance (P = 0.238). Remimazolam besylate achieved satisfactory anesthetic effects in all elderly patients, with high satisfaction among both endoscopists and patients.

We employed a composite endpoint of the ‘movement’ response to determine the effect dose of remimazolam. This endpoint was chosen because patient movement is a primary cause of failure procedures and complications. To maximize objectivity, the endpoint was predefined to include not only body languages and facial expressions but also quantifiable hemodynamic changes including increase in HR or SBP > 20 mmHg. While neurophysiological monitors like BIS provide objective data on cortical activity, our endpoint directly measures the abolition of the somatic and autonomic responses to pharyngeal stimulus, which is consistent with the direct goal of procedural sedation for this intervention.

Sincerely, Studies employing objective, electronically recorded parameters such as BIS provide a high degree of precision in quantifying the cortical PD effects of sedatives. One population PopPk-PD analysis elegantly demonstrates how BIS can objectively capture the profound influence of aging on the brain's sensitivity to propofol [18]. While BIS excellently measures cortical electroencephalographic suppression, movement and autonomic responses to noxious stimuli are mediated through brainstem and spinal pathways, and their correlation with BIS is variable [19]. Our study, by contrast, mainly focused on elderly patients who require painless gastroscopy. When anesthetizing this large group of patients, the dosage issue needs to be studied. Therefore, we selected a direct clinical endpoint as the ‘movement’ response in the context of PSA to solve this clinical problem, and the obtained drug dosage was intended to provide a recommendation and reference for clinical anesthesiologists facing painless gastroscopy in elderly patients. This choice represents that clinically-grounded approach determines the practical dosage requirement to achieve a specific procedural objective in geriatric subgroups to bridge the gap between physiological measurement and tangible clinical outcome.

The main finding of this study was that there was a significant difference in the recommended dosage of remimazolam when applied to elderly patients of different age groups who require PSA for gastroscopy. The ED50 of 0.171 mg/kg remimazolam besylate was recommended in patients 65 ≤ aged ≤ 70, whereas only 0.098 mg/kg in patients aged > 70 years. Group H (> 70 years) showed a steeper dose–response curve, which aligns with the well-recognized physiological changes of aging, including reduced metabolic capacity and less adaptable drug receptor function. Remimazolam is metabolized through esterase-mediated hydrolysis, a metabolic pathway clinically considered to have the potential to mitigate interindividual variability in drug effects [20]. Our probit regression analyses uncovered a notable contrast: the 95% CI for ED50 in Group H was substantially narrower (0.098–0.118 mg/kg) versus Group L (0.131–0.208 mg/kg), suggesting a more predictable drug response in older patients.

Gastroscopy insertion involves a brief but intense pharyngeal stimulus that triggers a strong gag reflex—an observation consistently noted in clinical practice when advancing transoral endoscopes, a finding that directly reflects the potent sensory input from the oropharynx [21]. The gag reflex is regulated by afferent fibers of the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves, which terminate in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS); this brainstem nucleus then activates autonomic and somatic pathways, resulting in increased sympathetic tone and involuntary movements that can interfere with procedural progress [22]. In contrast, colonoscopy-induced discomfort is more prolonged, primarily arising from colonoscope looping, mesenteric stretching, and distension related to insufflation of air or carbon dioxide. Randomized trials evaluating responsive insertion technology (RIT) have shown that the sustained mechanical stress of colonoscope advancement leads to higher VAS pain scores over several minutes. By comparison, the discomfort from gastroscope insertion peaks within seconds and rapidly diminishes [23]. This fundamental difference explains the paradox: the brief yet high-intensity pharyngeal stimulus of gastroscopy requires a deeper level of sedation to adequately suppress the gag reflex, while the longer, lower-intensity mechanical stress of colonoscopy can be managed with lighter sedation regimens. This highlights the clinical significance of investigating the optimal dosage of remimazolam for gastroscopy in elderly patients.

Indeed, the division of patients into those aged 65–70 and those > 70 years was based on a key clinical observation: the progression of aging is not linear. While all older adults experience declines in metabolic function and drug response, the period after age 70 typically marks a shift into a distinct physiological category. This shift involves a more pronounced loss of hepatic and renal reserve, a greater degree of frailty, and a higher burden of comorbidities like hypertension and coronary heart disease [11, 20]. Collectively, these factors significantly alter how patients process and respond to sedatives. It’s this acceleration in vulnerability that we captured in our data. The jump in hypotension incidence—from 7.4% in the younger group to 20.8% in the older group—clinically validates the need for this distinction. Applying a single ED₅₀ value for a 66-year-old and an 75-year-old would ignore this physiological distinction. The result would be a problematic trade-off: a dose sufficient for a safe procedure in the older cohort might leave a younger patient inadequately sedated, while a dose effective for the younger group could dangerously depress the older patient's circulation. Therefore, this stratification is not merely a statistical choice, but a practical framework for precision. It allows dosing recommendations to be specifically calibrated to the differing safety and efficacy thresholds that exist within the geriatric population undergoing gastroscopy.

All ED₅₀ values in this study are determined under the condition of pre-administration with 0.1 µg/kg sufentanil. which is a long-acting µ-opioid that eases pain and blunts the pharyngeal reflex. Over the past few years, several studies have pointed to a synergistic effect when sufentanil is paired with remimazolam. Lyu et al. found that adding sufentanil allows the remimazolam dose to be cut to 0.075 mg/kg without losing sedation depth, which highlights a clear dose-sparing advantage [24]. A thorough review also noted that using 0.239 mg/kg remimazolam alongside 0.1 mg/kg sufentanil delivers enough pain relief and sedation for painful upper gastrointestinal procedures — confirming this synergy matters in real clinical practice [25]. For patients with liver cirrhosis, Cao et al. reported that the ED50 of remimazolam (when boosted with sufentanil as an adjuvant) fell to 0.097 mg/kg — much lower than the dose required without any opioid support [25]. Similar dose cuts have been seen in colonoscopy: starting with 0.20–0.25 mg/kg remimazolam plus 5 µg/kg sufentanil led to higher induction success rates and fewer hemodynamic upsets compared to propofol-based treatment plans [26]. Putting all these results together, it’s clear that sufentanil boosts remimazolam’s hypnotic effects. This lets clinicians use less of the benzodiazepine while still keeping patients immobile and comfortable during procedures. Therefore, the ED50 values reported in this study apply exclusively to remimazolam besylate when administered in combination with 0.1 µg/kg sufentanil; use of remimazolam without opioid adjuvants would require a substantially higher effective dose.

Midazolam, the classic benzodiazepine, is metabolized through the liver’s CYP3A4 enzyme. For patients with impaired liver functions, its clearance slows down significantly—leading to inconsistent sedation depth and longer recovery times [4]. In contrast, remimazolam is quickly broken down via ester hydrolysis, giving it a context-sensitive half-life of around 6 to 7 min. That means even with repeated doses, patients wake up fast [4]. Our study backs up these pharmacological perks: despite the strong pharyngeal stimulation from endoscopy, the median induction time was under 1 min, and wake-up times were similar across both age groups. What’s more, we saw no cases of severe hypotension, bradycardia, or hypoxemia. Every endoscopist and patient was happy with remimazolam-based procedural sedation and analgesia (PSA)—proving this anesthesia approach works reliably.

Propofol is still the most commonly used sedative for gastrointestinal endoscopy, but its tendency to trigger hypotension and respiratory depression gets worse in older adults. In a multicenter randomized trial, 36.5% of patients had propofol-induced hypotension, compared to just 6.8% with remimazolam—this really drives home remimazolam’s hemodynamic edge [27]. In our current study, only 1 patient in Group L and 2 in Group H had subclinical respiratory depression (90% ≤ SpO2 ≤ 95%), and no one developed hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90%). These findings highlight remimazolam’s strong safety profile: even when paired with sufentanil, serious respiratory depression didn’t crop up in elderly patients, though direct comparisons to propofol or midazolam within our cohort are limited.

The overall incidence of hypotension in this experiment was 13.7% (7/51), and the majority of hypotension occurred in patients aged > 70 years (5/24, 20.8%). Compared with traditional PSA anesthesia with propofol and midazolam, such as a systematic review which produced a hypoxemia incidence of 42% in propofol group, while adding midazolam reduced it to 37%, though the difference was not statistically significant [28], this incidence has significantly decreased. However, it still reminds us that for elderly patients > 70 years and undergoing PSA gastroscopy, when using remimazolam, close attention should still be paid to circulation and active management should be carried out.

There were several limitations in present study. First, the assessor of the primary endpoint was not blinded to the dosing sequence. Furthermore, the hemodynamic criteria used in this study are not entirely specific to gastroscopic stimulation, as they may also be influenced by emotional stress, baseline autonomic tone, or pharmacological effects. To minimize subjective evaluation bias, we applied strictly predefined quantitative thresholds for hemodynamic changes to standardize endpoint judgment and reduce arbitrary interpretation. Secondly, there was a numeric imbalance in sex distribution between groups (44.4% male in Group L vs 29.2% in Group H). Although ASD indicated no significant imbalance, sex-related differences in remimazolam pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics cannot be excluded and may represent a potential limitation. Thirdly, regarding the effective dose reporting, this sequential method study was primarily designed to determine and compare the ED50 across age strata. Although ED95 estimates are provided via probit regression for clinical reference, the 95% CI particularly in Group L are wider. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to precisely determine the ED95. Fourthly, regarding the age group division, we only divided it into two groups: 65–70 years old group and > 70 years old group, without further categorizing elderly people over 70 years old, such as 70–75 years old, 75–80 years old, etc. The specific reasons were as follows: the patients we included were all elderly patients who had undergone anesthesia evaluation and needed to undergo gastroscopy under PSA. When patients are over 75 years old, many are not suitable for general anesthesia due to complications. Most elderly people over 75 years old are more suitable for endoscopic examination under conscious sedation anesthesia, which can achieve good anesthesia effects. Fifthly, we restricted the study population to ASA I/II patients with STOP-Bang score < 5, so we cannot discuss our results for high-risk elderly patients (ASA III/IV, obese or with obstructive sleep apnea syndrome) who may have different pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic reactions to remimazolam.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides the first age-stratified ED50 estimates of remimazolam besylate for successful gastroscope insertion in geriatric patients. The significantly lower ED50 in patients over 70 years supports the need for age-adjusted dosing to achieve optimal sedation while minimizing risks. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the use of remimazolam as a safe and effective sedative in the elderly and add to the existing evidence on remimazolam’s utility in geriatric procedural sedation, though generalizability to high-risk patients (e.g., ASA III/IV) requires further validation.
