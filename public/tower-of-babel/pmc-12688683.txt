Study design and protocol for a randomized clinical trial investigating a brief fear of uncertainty-focused intervention as an adjunct to project safe guard for military personnel
Authors: Samantha E Daruwala, Kayley Stratton, Grace Y Cho, Michael D Anestis, Craig J Bryan, Nicholas P Allan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the United States (US), the proportion of firearm suicide deaths are higher among military personnel compared to the general population. Secure firearm storage practices (e.g., gun safe, unloaded) can reduce the risk of firearm suicide. A randomized controlled trial, called Project Safe Guard (PSG) found that providing lethal means counseling (LMC) to firearm-owning service members increased secure firearm storage practices; however, some still stored at least one firearm unsecurely. Intolerance of uncertainty (IU) may be a barrier to secure firearm storage that decreases the effectiveness of LMC. This study will examine the impact of a brief intervention that targets IU (i.e., Cognitive behavioral therapy for Uncertainty Enhanced; CUE) on improving the effectiveness of PSG-LMC on secure firearm storage practices among firearm-owning service members. In this protocol paper, we describe the rationale and methodology for this study.MethodsThis study uses a counterbalanced design with two conditions to test the acceptability, feasibility, and potential efficacy of both interventions with 100 current or recently discharged service members who report elevated IU and storing at least one firearm unsecurely. Participants are randomized to either receive CUE or PSG-LMC first, and then receive the second intervention approximately two weeks later. Participants will complete a baseline assessment, ecological momentary assessment (EMA) via their mobile devices, and follow-ups at 1-, 3-, 6-, 9-, and 12-months post-baseline. All interventions will be delivered remotely.ConclusionsThis study aims to inform how to improve firearm safety in military personnel with elevated IU.Keywords: Firearms, Suicide prevention, Brief interventions, Military

Introduction

In the United States (U.S.), suicide is a significant public health concern among military personnel. Firearms are the most common method for suicide death among service members, and the proportion of firearm suicide deaths is higher compared to the U.S. general population [1]. Firearm ownership and access are associated with an increased risk for suicide death [2], and this risk extends beyond the individual firearm owner to the entire household [3]. Research suggests that secure firearm storage (e.g., locking device, safe) can reduce suicide risk [2,4]. Troublingly, secure firearm storage practices are not readily used by service members [5,6], and promoting such practices is a top priority for suicide prevention efforts within the Department of Defense (DoD). Lethal means counseling (LMC) is a recommended best practice for suicide prevention that can promote secure firearm storage practices [7]. A DoD-funded randomized controlled trial (RCT), called Project Safe Guard (PSG), examined the efficacy of an LMC protocol using a motivational interviewing (MI) framework and the provision of cable locks in promoting secure firearm storage practices among firearm-owning National Guard members [8]. Findings showed that having a brief, one-time LMC session promoted the use of secure firearm storage practices (e.g., locking device, safe). While LMC was superior to control, 45 % of service members who received LMC still maintained at least one firearm that was stored non-securely [8], suggesting that they experienced barriers to behavior change.

An important barrier to secure firearm storage may be fear of the unknown [9], which is a form of anxiety that is commonly experienced among individuals with low tolerance for uncertainty [10] and may manifest as concerns over personal safety or unpredictability in emergency situations, leading to a preference to keeping firearms easily accessible. Intolerance of uncertainty (IU) involves an aversion to ambiguity and increased distress when faced with uncertainty, which contributes to heightened anxiety and arousal [11]. As a result, individuals may be motivated to engage in maladaptive cognitive and behavioral strategies to reduce the distress associated with uncertainty. In the context of firearm availability, firearm owners with heightened IU may use easy firearm access to decrease their uncertainty-related distress and therefore be less open to adopting secure firearm storage practices. Indeed, research has found higher IU is associated with firearm purchasing among first-time and established firearm owners [12] and greater risk for using unsecure storage practices [9].

Anxiety of the unknown may serve as an important barrier for secure firearm storage practice and decrease the effectiveness of LMC. If IU is reduced, firearm owners receiving LMC may be more amenable to using secure storage practices. The current study will use a counterbalanced design to examine the impact of a brief intervention that has been shown to reduce IU on improving the effectiveness of LMC on secure firearm storage practices among firearm-owning service members. We will examine IU and implicit bias towards uncertainty and firearms as potential mediators. The first aim is to assess the preliminary efficacy of a LMC intervention, augmented with an IU intervention, on firearm storage practices. We hypothesize that, compared to participants who first receive LMC, those who receive the IU intervention first will be more likely to use secure firearm storage practices after each intervention visit. Additionally, we hypothesize that individuals who receive the IU intervention first will report significantly higher willingness to use secure firearm storage practices after both intervention visits. The second aim is to examine the mechanisms through which the IU intervention and LMC increase secure firearm storage practices. We hypothesize that post-baseline changes in IU, willingness to use secure firearm storage practices, and implicit biases about firearm storage will predict the use of firearm storage practices. Finally, our third aim is to assess treatment credibility and acceptability of LMC. We hypothesize that participants who receive the IU intervention first will report high satisfaction and acceptability of LMC. Results could inform community-based suicide prevention efforts in the DoD and may also improve the effectiveness of LMC delivered within healthcare settings.

Methods

Study design and procedures

This study is one of five DoD-funded projects within the Augmenting Suicide Prevention Interventions for Service Members (ASPIS) consortium (W81XWH2220072). The current study uses a completely counterbalanced design with two conditions to test the acceptability, feasibility, and potential efficacy of brief treatment of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Uncertainty Enhanced (CUE) and the PSG-LMC protocol with 100 current or recently discharged service members recruited from across the nation. A counterbalanced design is a within-subjects design in which participants are exposed to each condition in different orders. This approach maximizes internal validity by evenly distributing the influence of ordering and sequencing effects [13], allowing us to determine if the order of the interventions differentially impacts our outcomes. All study procedures are conducted remotely via teleconference software through The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center (OSUWMC). Metricwire, a HIPAA-compliant mobile app, is used to capture ecological momentary assessments (EMA) and store information from each intervention (described in further detail below).

Participants are recruited through online social media advertisements, word of mouth, advertisements at Fort Campbell research site, and through community events. Participants are also recruited via BuildClinical, an encrypted and secure data-driven platform that helps academic researchers recruit participants for research studies more efficiently using social media, software, and machine learning. Eligible participants are current or recently discharged (i.e., within the last 3 months) U.S. military service members with elevated IU and at least one firearm stored unsecurely. Interested individuals first complete an online screener to determine eligibility. Interested and eligible individuals are then contacted by a study team member to review the purpose of the study, confirm eligibility, and provide informed consent. To confirm eligibility, a trained team member contacts the potential participant to confirm military status, firearm access, and willingness to download a survey app. Then, before beginning the baseline assessment, the baseline assessor asks to view their military ID to ensure military status. After consenting, participants complete the remote baseline assessment visit and are randomized to receive one of the two interventions (PSG-LMC or CUE). Participants are scheduled for their first intervention session within seven days of the baseline assessment. At the end of the first intervention session, participants download the MetricWire mobile app to their smartphone and receive a brief tutorial about how the application works. The second intervention is scheduled approximately two weeks after the first intervention, with 14 days of EMA and use of the mobile app to reinforce gains made during the intervention session after each of the intervention sessions. Approximately 3 days before the second intervention session, participants are sent a mid-point assessment via email. After the second intervention session, participants complete follow-up assessments via email (1 month post-baseline), and 3, 6, 9, and 12 months post-baseline. The total estimated completion time is 13 h over the course of one year. This study is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT05818696).

To reduce barriers to follow-up, all study procedures are conducted remotely using HIPAA-compliant platforms (REDCap, MetricWire) with participant contact information tracked through Ripple Science. These systems allow for flexible scheduling, automated reminders, and multiple verified contact methods to accommodate relocation, deployment, or separation from service. Participants can complete assessments from any device and location, and technical support is available as needed. Compensation is provided immediately after each completed follow-up to reinforce engagement. Together, these procedures minimize logistical and technological barriers to follow-up at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants are eligible for enrollment if they (1) are currently serving in any branch or component of the U.S. military, or were discharged from the U.S. military within the past 3 months; (2) are at least 18 years of age; (3) endorse elevated IU on the Intolerance of Uncertainty-12 Scale, Short Version (IUS-12) (i.e., a total score above 30, which is approximately half a SD above the community mean) [14]; (4) own or have access to at least one firearm located in their home or vehicle that is stored unlocked and loaded (i.e., not secure storage); and (5) are able to speak and understand the English language.

Participants will be excluded if they (1) do not pass the Informed Decision Making Capacity (IDMC) screener, indicating they are not able to provide informed consent; or (2) have a psychiatric or medical condition that would prevent them from providing informed consent or from participating in the treatments (e.g., psychosis, mania, acute intoxication); or (3) no or limited access to an Android or Apple smartphone that is compatible with the EMA application.

Treatment conditions

Participants are randomized to either CUE or PSG-LMC first using a counterbalanced design to reduce order effects. Both interventions are delivered remotely and video recorded for fidelity purposes. After the intervention is delivered, participants download the MetricWire mobile app where key lessons from the interventions are uploaded. All participants receive one daily survey sent at a fixed time every morning and five surveys that are quasi-randomly delivered throughout the remainder of the day.

CBT for Uncertainty Enhanced (CUE)

CUE is a 1-h manualized intervention that focuses on reducing IU, or fear of the unknown. For the current study, clinicians focus on IU, in general, and how it relates to home safety, or the state of feeling physically, emotionally, and psychologically protected from harm and danger. The virtual session is divided into three components: 1) psychoeducation about IU and home safety and IU's role in exacerbating anxiety in general and as it relates to home safety, 2) introduction and practice of challenging unhelpful thoughts or myths about uncertainty, and 3) identification of behavioral experiments or activities to challenge myths about uncertainty. The psychoeducation component provides a brief overview of CBT principles and three common uncertainty myths: 1) uncertainty is bad, 2) I can avoid uncertainty when I plan enough, and 3) I won't be able to cope with the negative outcome. Participants then learn to challenge these myths and become more comfortable with the unknown by examining their own personal myths about uncertainty. Lastly, participants identify in vivo situations where they can test out and disconfirm their negative predictions about uncertainty. Interventionists then troubleshoot any barriers to practicing these behavioral activities or experiments and conclude the session by reviewing key takeaways and guiding the participant with the MetricWire set-up. Participants are asked to complete and track their experiences completing one behavioral activity per day for two weeks through MetricWire, including subjective levels of distress before and after completing the behavioral activity.

Post-CUE digital intervention

Following the CUE session, participants track their experiences completing their daily behavioral activities through the MetricWire mobile app. To help participants challenge their anxiety related to uncertainty, ecological momentary intervention is used to remind participants of their key takeaways when they report elevated levels of anxiety. If participants endorse high scores (“somewhat” to “very much”) on indicators of anxiety, they are asked how much of their anxiety is influenced by fear of the unknown. If they endorse “somewhat” to “very much,” they are likely to receive reminders of their key takeaways created during the CUE session. The reminders of the key takeaways will only occur 75 % of the time (randomly determined). If participants do not report elevated anxiety scores, they will still receive reminders of the key takeaways 10 % of the time (randomly determined). Key takeaways are deployed in this matter to prevent habituation or fatigue to the prompts.

Project safe guard lethal means counseling (PSG-LMC)

The LMC condition, previously developed by Bryan and colleagues [[15], [16], [17]], has demonstrated efficacy in promoting more secure firearm storage practices [8]. The 15–20 min intervention uses MI principles to identify methods and reasons for secure firearm storage. The use of MI skills such as open-ended questions, affirmations, reflective listening, and summary statements allows the participant to consider implementing safer practices without judgment or resistance. For the current study, interventionists guide participants through an informal conversation designed to engage the participant in a discussion about firearms focusing on issues of home safety and storage. After identifying potential methods for more secure storage and discussing the pros and cons of changing behavior to increase firearm safety practices, the interventionist and the participant collaboratively develop a plan to implement more secure practices. The plan is then uploaded to the MetricWire mobile app.

Post-LMC digital intervention

Similar to the CUE intervention, event-contingent sampling and EMA intervention allows the participant to record implementation of their storage plans and review content learned during the PSG-LMC session.

Randomization and stratification

Participants are randomized to either the initial CUE or PSG-LMC session by a stratified randomization algorithm designed to balance participants across both conditions based on biological sex (male vs. female), race (White vs. person of color), and endorsement of suicidal ideation within the past week (present vs. absent). The use of a computerized randomization technique prevents any potential influence of human bias on treatment assignment.

Training, supervision, and monitoring of study personnel

All study personnel participate in training sessions focused on study procedures for the assessment and intervention sessions. Research associates involved with screening and baseline assessments either hold or are in the process of earning a bachelor's degree in psychology or a related field. Interventionists are either trained pre-doctoral interns or hold at least a master's degree in a licensable mental health discipline. Assessors and interventionists practice delivering content and must test out to be able to deliver the assessments and/or sessions to participants. For assessment sessions, fidelity sheets are used to periodically evaluate that research assistants are adhering to the protocol 100 %. Project personnel falling below the 100 % threshold receive retraining as needed. Training consists of lectures about the protocol and role plays that are coded for fidelity. Interventionists must achieve 80 % fidelity to test-out of practice and administer the interventions to participants. All sessions are coded for fidelity with approximately 20 % of the sessions double-coded to avoid bias. Retraining occurs when the interventionist fails to meet the fidelity threshold. Interventionists also participate in weekly supervision sessions to review cases and discuss feedback with study personnel who are licensed clinical psychologists (N.A., S.D.).

Assessments

Participants complete an EMA battery for approximately 28 days. Prompts are sent six times per day with one static morning survey and five administered at quasi-random times during the remainder of the day. Given the importance of continuous quality monitoring of EMA, the study team conducts daily quality checks regarding data collected via MetricWire. The EMA battery assesses active duty status, social context, mood state, stressful experiences, uncertainty, suicidal ideation, brief suicidal cognitions scale, and firearm storage.

All projects being conducted through the ASPIS consortium collect Common Data Elements (CDEs) from participants at specific time points (i.e., pre-treatment, and 3-, 9-, and 12-months post-treatment). Project-specific variables and assessments are summarized in Table 1. All self-reported assessments are completed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure, electronic survey platform [18,19]. Participants are e-mailed via REDCap with a link to complete each assessment.

For the current study, key variables (firearm storage practices and intolerance of uncertainty) are assessed at baseline, midpoint, post-intervention, and at all follow-ups. Participants complete measures of IU and firearm storage practices after the first intervention session via e-mail (Midpoint assessment). After each intervention session, participants complete daily surveys on the mobile app and log their homework on the mobile app for two weeks.

The baseline assessment takes approximately 1 h to complete while the follow-up surveys take approximately 30–60 min to complete. Participants are compensated $25 for baseline and follow-up visits. Participants are not compensated for the two intervention sessions. Participants are compensated $1 per EMA survey completed. An incentive is offered to increase EMA compliance: $20 for completing >85 % of surveys. Compensation is provided by the study team after the completion of each study visit, while compensation for the EMA portion of the study is provided after completion of the 1-month follow-up.

Primary outcome: firearm storage practices

The primary outcome of this study is firearm storage practices. Firearm storage practices are assessed using researcher-developed items that have been used in our previous studies to measure firearm ownership status, frequency of storing firearms loaded, frequency of storing firearms separate from ammunition, frequency of storing firearms using a locking device (e.g. cable lock, trigger lock), and frequency of storing firearms in a gun safe or lock box [20].

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes for this study are willingness to establish a firearm storage plan under a variety of circumstances (e.g., when children are visiting, during emotional crises), treatment credibility, and treatment acceptability. Willingness to establish a firearm safety plan was derived from researcher-developed items used in our previous studies [21,22]. Treatment credibility is assessed using the 6-item Credibility/Expectancy Questionnaire (CEQ) [23]. Each item is measured on a 9-point scale from 1 to 9 with higher scores indicating a more positive rating. Treatment acceptability is assessed using an adaptation of the 11-item Program Satisfaction Questionnaire (PSQ) [24]. Each item is measured on a 5-point scale from (1) Strongly Disagree to (5) Strongly Agree; higher scores indicate greater satisfaction with the intervention components, with scores ranging from 11 to 55.

Mediators and covariates

Mediators include IU and implicit bias towards uncertainty and firearms. Intolerance of uncertainty is measured using the self-report IUS-12 [14], which assesses one's ability to tolerate uncertainty in ambiguous situations, and the IU Implicit Association Test (IU-IAT), which was developed by PI Allan to provide an objective, noninvasive measure of IU and is currently undergoing psychometric validation (RMH116311A). Implicit beliefs about firearms are assessed using the Firearm IAT developed by Co-I Anestis. The firearm IAT task examines reaction time as an indicator of implicit biases toward firearms. Both IAT tasks are administered during the baseline, post-intervention, and all follow-ups.

Covariates include the ASPIS CDEs and firearm acquisition (i.e., purchasing/obtaining additional firearms). Two suicide-related CDEs are collected: suicide attempts and other suicidal behaviors (e.g., interrupted attempts, preparatory behaviors) are assessed using the Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviors Interview-Revised (SITBI-R) [25]; suicidal ideation is assessed using the Scale for Suicide Ideation (SSI) [26]. Seven suicide theory-specific CDEs are collected: Perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness are assessed using a shortened Interpersonal Needs Questionnaire (INQ) [27,28]; Hopelessness is assessed using an abbreviated version of the Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS) [28,29]; Suicidal beliefs are assessed using the Brief Suicide Cognitions Scale (B-SCS) [30]; Emotion regulation is assessed using the Difficulties with Emotion Regulation Scale-Short Form (DERS-SF) [31]; Impulsivity is assessed using the Monetary Choice Questionnaire (MCQ) [32]; Reinforcement sensitivity is assessed using the behavioral inhibition system and behavioral activation system (BIS/BAS) scales [33]. A standardized demographics form is used to collect demographics (e.g., sex, gender, age, race) and military-specific variables (e.g., rank, service branch). Functional impairment is measured by the Work and Social Adjustment Scale (WSAS) [34]. Mental health treatment utilization is measured using the Cornell Services Index Short Form (CSI-SF) [35]; and the Post-Treatment Health Interview (PTHI), a researcher-developed instrument we have previously used in DoD-funded studies. Mental health symptoms (e.g., depression, anxiety, insomnia) and quality of life will be assessed using PROMIS Computer Adaptive Tests (CATs) [36].

Planned statistical analyses

For all variables, we will first examine the distribution for potential nonnormality and outliers. If necessary, appropriate data transformations or alternative data analysis procedures (e.g., nonparametric, bootstrapping) will be used. To account for the hierarchical structure of EMA data (i.e., multiple measurements within each participant), we will use multilevel modeling (MLM) to test our hypotheses. MLM is a robust approach that allows for unbalanced and/or missing data, two data features that are common in EMA studies. An intent-to-treat (ITT) approach will be used, including all participants randomized to a treatment condition with sensitivity analyses examining only those who attend both intervention sessions.

To assess the preliminary efficacy of the combined CUE/PSG-LMC on firearm storage practices, we will use mixed regression modeling (MRM) within MLM, as this approach increases power when data are being collected across multiple time points and can easily address nonlinear and categorical data. We will focus on within-person change in firearm storage practices across three time points: baseline, 2 weeks, and 1 month. Treatment condition will be included as a within-subject factor to account for intervention order. Firearm storage practices will then be compared at 2 weeks and 1 month. We will assess within-person changes from baseline to follow-up to provide preliminary support for the combined intervention. In all analyses, sensitivity analyses will be conducted including covariates that significantly differed between conditions at baseline as auxiliary variables [37].

To assess the preliminary efficacy of the combined CUE/PSG-LMC on willingness to establish a firearm safety plan, we will use the same analytic approach described for firearm storage practices. To test mediation effects, we will conduct mediation analysis within an MLM framework [38]. The primary mediator will be change in IUS-12 scores 1-month post-baseline. Changes in willingness to use safe firearm storage practices and changes in IU-IAT scores 1-month post-baseline will be considered as secondary mediators, and a final full model will be analyzed including all significant mediators to examine unique and shared variance in firearm safety practices captured by the mediators. We will also conduct three-level MLMs to examine changes in anxiety captured by the EMA as a mediator of firearm storage practices [39]. The primary analysis of firearm storage practices will be evaluated without multiplicity adjustment, whereas secondary and mechanistic analyses will be controlled within prespecified families using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (q = .05).

Treatment credibility and acceptability of the combined CUE/PSG-LMC will be assessed using several metrics. First, we will track the number of participants who are recruited per month to calculate the monthly rate of recruitment. We will calculate the percentage of participants who complete the protocol and follow-ups as a metric of acceptability. Second, we will use an 11-item adaptation of the PSQ, that assesses both general program satisfaction (usefulness, acceptability) and perceived utility of each treatment. An average PSQ general satisfaction score of ≥3 (on a 5-point scale) and an average PSQ component rating (i.e., intervention session, ecological momentary intervention of ≥3 (on a 5-point scale) will provide support for the acceptability of the combined CUE/PSG-LMC. Third, we will use CEQ scores to assess credibility and expectancy of the combined CUE/PSG-LMC.

Handling missing data

Missing data in the full analysis set will occur if participants discontinue prior to the end of the study or do not complete an outcome measure. Participants will not be discontinued from the study because of non-adherence, and all will remain in the study unless consent is withdrawn or if there are concerns regarding participant safety. Missing data for the ITT analysis set will be addressed using full information maximum likelihood (FIML), which is superior to most alternative missing data approaches even when data is missing completely at random or missing at random.

Power analysis

We conducted power analyses to determine the sample size needed to detect significant changes in firearm storage practices from baseline to post-intervention. We were interested in the power to detect changes in firearm storage practices across both conditions. Based on the prior RCT (PSG) [8], we expect a clinically meaningful odds ratio of 2.0 from baseline to 1-month follow-up firearm storage practices (Aim 1). Based on base rates of safe firearm storage practices, we expect 25–50 % of the sample to report using each particular firearm storage practice. We chose a conservative estimate of 30 % for power analysis. The McNemar Test was conducted in the PASS program; Type I error rate was set at α = .05 and Type II error rate was set at .20, indicating power (1 - .20) = .80. The power analysis revealed that we would need 77 participants to detect a significant change in safe firearm storage practices. The sample was increased by 23 participants (30 %) to account for missing data and attrition (N = 100). Pilot data will be used to examine between-group differences based on order of the intervention components. Aim 2 focused on examining treatment mechanisms. Based on mediation power tables developed by Fritz and Mackinnon [40], we would be powered to detect significant mediation effects using percentile-based bootstrapping if the a and b paths are medium in size or larger (n = 78). All power analyses are conservative given that MLM is being used to conduct our analyses.

Conclusions

This protocol demonstrates how to efficiently deliver a brief intervention that could enhance the effectiveness of the PSG LMC protocol for firearm-owning service members who do not use secure firearm practices and experience elevated IU. The data from this study will provide acceptability, feasibility, and preliminary efficacy of a two-session telehealth intervention to improve firearm safety in military personnel with elevated IU. It is anticipated that the IU intervention could enhance the effectiveness of LMC for a subset of service members who otherwise would not benefit from or respond to LMC. With evidence suggesting the availability of lethal means is one of the strongest predictors of suicide [41] and the majority of service members do not safely store their firearm(s) [42], this study's intervention protocol is expected to result in downstream effects on suicide as well.
