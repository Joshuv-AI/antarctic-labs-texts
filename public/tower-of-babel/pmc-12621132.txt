A practical approach to handling missing sputum samples in airway disease research
Authors: Laurits Frøssing, Susanne Hansen, Morten Hvidtfeldt, Asger Sverrild, Celeste Porsbjerg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMissing sputum samples are common in research, hampering the use of paired sputum-based outcome measures. This study aimed to illustrate the effects of different methods for handling missing data.MethodsData from three intervention studies were pooled. Eosinophil and neutrophil counts were imputed using unconditional median imputation (UCM) and multiple imputation (MI). First, the impact of imputation on the prediction of improvement in lung function and airway hyperreactivity was compared using linear regression. Second, missing data was simulated at different frequencies (20%, 40% and 60%) and the imputed values were compared with the true values.ResultsWe included 115 patients, of whom 103 had at least one available sputum sample. No significant difference was found between the imputation methods and they had similar effects on the prediction model for improvement in clinical outcomes (n=115) in terms of β, se and significance level with the estimates of effect slightly more conservative using MI. Through simulation, we found an increasing bias with increasing mean for both UCM and MI. UCM consistently provided narrower limits of agreement, but importantly also introduced a systematic proportional bias. The width of the limits of agreement was drastically narrower when only 20% of samples were missing.ConclusionImputation increases statistical power and addresses rather than ignores missing data. We suggest that sputum-based outcome measures should be reported using both complete case analyses and with missing data imputed with the choice of imputation method decided based on study design, consequences of over- and under-estimation and ability to address bias.

Introduction

Asthma is recognised as a heterogenous disease consisting of several endotypes [1]. Overall, type-2 (T2) inflammation is perceived as the main pathogenetic mechanism in the majority of asthma cases and monoclonal antibodies targeting the T2 pathway have revolutionised the treatment of asthma.

With the increasing number of biologics available, biomarkers directly assessing the level and type of airway inflammation are becoming increasingly important, both for targeting treatment to the individual patient and for assessing the effect [1–3].

The introduction of real-life prospective severe asthma registries has created a unique opportunity for large-scale longitudinal data collection on patients on biologic treatment. Coupled with biosampling, it will advance our understanding of asthma immunology and help refine targeting of the different asthma phenotypes in the coming years.

Sputum cell differential count (CDC) is the gold standard for airway inflammometry in asthma, but utilisation of paired sputum-based outcome measures is rare, including in new prospective registries. Historically, the logistics of both sputum sampling and handling have been considered the limiting factors; however, recent advances simplifying both sample handling and analyses will surely allow for a more widespread use of sputum sampling in the coming years. Despite these logistic advances, the high prevalence of missing samples (20–56%) remains a key barrier for the use of sputum-based outcome measures. A high proportion of missing samples not only increases the number of participants necessary to obtain the required power, but may also skew the eventual findings due to selection bias, as samples are not missing at random [3–9].

To date, most studies utilising sputum-based outcome measures have used baseline values to predict outcomes or utilised complete-case (CC) analysis without addressing the potential bias conferred by missing samples [5, 10–13] despite it being well known that missing data can potentially lead to substantial bias when inadequately handled [14]. In fact, reflections on the missingness of sputum samples and how to handle them have, until recently, been absent [6, 7].

In this study, we aim to illustrate the effects of different methods for handling missing data, namely unconditional median imputation (UCM) and multiple imputation (MI), and ultimately identify the least comprehensive method necessary to adequately account for missing values.

Although MI is statistically regarded as superior to single imputation, we hypothesised that UCM and MI would perform equally well with regard to the prediction of clinical outcomes, based on the findings of van der Heijden et al. [15], who found that the two methods performed equally well in a study evaluating the diagnosis of pulmonary embolism.

Methods

Study population

Data from three intervention studies were pooled for this study.

The RECONSTRUCT study was a single-arm intervention study (1600 µg inhaled budesonide once daily for 16 weeks) of steroid-free asthma patients with airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) to mannitol [16]. The UPSTREAM study was a placebo-controlled intervention study of predominantly moderate-to-severe asthma patients with AHR to mannitol, evaluating the addition of anti-thymic stromal lymphopoietin (tezepelumab) for 12 weeks [17]. Only the patients in the active arm were included from the UPSTREAM study. The SIGNATURE study was a single-arm intervention study of patients with severe asthma, evaluating the addition of 37.5 mg oral prednisolone for 2 weeks [12].

Successful sputum induction was not required for inclusion in any of the studies and maintenance treatment prior to enrolment remained unchanged throughout the study period.

Patient examinations

All patients underwent spirometry and a bronchial challenge test with inhaled mannitol (patients with forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) >70% predicted), assessment of fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO) and blood and induced sputum CDCs. All patients completed a self-administered Danish version of the Asthma Control Questionnaire-5. Detailed descriptions are available in previous publications [12, 16, 17].

Atopy was defined as atopic sensitisation with elevated specific IgE or a positive skin-prick test, to at least one aeroallergen in a standard panel of 10 aeroallergens (birch (Betula), grass (Phleum pratense), mugwort, horse, dog, cat (Fel d), house dust mite (Der p 1 and Der f 2) and fungi (Alternaria and Cladosporium).

Sputum induction and analyses

In all three studies, sputum was collected following a mannitol challenge test in a specimen jar (Petri dish) with the sample quality continuously evaluated by a trained laboratory technician. The sputum CDC was performed on a light microscope by an experienced lab technician at Bispebjerg University Hospital, Copenhagen, Denmark. Cell viability was evaluated using the Trypan blue exclusion method. Samples were processed using the plug selection method and cut-off values for eosinophilia and neutrophilia were ≥3% and ≥61%, respectively.

Efforts to handle missing data

We examined two methods for handling missing data. First, we used single UCM, in which missing data are replaced by the median derived from the observed values in the study population for the given variable. Second, we used MI, which estimates a distribution of the missing variable, taking multiple aspects of uncertainty in imputations into account. From this distribution, values are generated and filled in for the missing test result. Imputation was repeated 100 times. Choice of variables for the model was based on our previously published findings [6]. The model included sputum eosinophil count (S-eos) and neutrophil count (S-neu) at both baseline and follow-up in addition to age, biological sex, smoking pack-years, baseline blood eosinophil count (B-eos), baseline FENO and cumulative inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) dose [6, 7].

Statistical analyses

SAS Enterprise guide version 7.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) was used for the data analyses. Means (sds) were presented for normally distributed data and medians (quartile 1–quartile 3) are presented for nonparametric data.

Nonparametric variables were converted to logarithmic form (log10) before imputation and were subsequently reversed.

Illustration of the statistical methodology.

Prediction of clinical outcomes

First, we created a multiple regression model (specified in “Efforts to handle missing data”, above) for the prediction of improvement in lung function and airway hyperreactivity. Regression coefficients and standard errors were calculated and compared across methods with CC analysis as reference.

For these analyses, patients with at least one sample were included.

Lung function and airway hyperreactivity were evaluated as dichotomous variables with an improvement in FEV1 of 200 mL and 10% and change from a positive to a negative mannitol challenge test as primary outcomes.

Simulation

Using this approach, we selected the subset of patients with a complete dataset at each visit (i.e., all patients with a baseline sample and all patients with a follow-up sample, respectively) and randomly introduced missing values using the Bernoulli distribution. The imputed values were compared to the true values and the accuracy of the imputation methods were evaluated using the Bland–Altman approach at both baseline and follow-up [18]. Raw-bias (true value – imputed value), percent-bias (truevalue−imputedvaluetruevalue) and limits of agreement ((true value – imputed value)±2sd) was calculated and compared across imputation methods.

These analyses were performed at different frequencies of missing data (20%, 40% and 60%, respectively) representing the range of rate of missing samples reported in the literature [3–7].

Ethics

The RECONSTRUCT, UPSTREAM and SIGNATURE studies were all approved by the local scientific ethics committee (H-16043663, H-16002008 and H-17004938, respectively). All patients gave written and oral informed consent prior to participating in the studies.

A total of 115 patients were included, 20 from UPSTREAM, 49 from SIGNATURE and 46 from RECONSTRUCT. Half (n=60, 52%) of the patients had both samples collected whereas 90% (103/115) had at least one sample collected. A missing sample at follow-up was more prevalent than at baseline (24% versus 34%, respectively).

Being female was the only significant variable associated with missing samples at baseline (p=0.05, β −0.89, se 0.5) in a logistic regression model containing age, biological sex, body mass index, number of exacerbations in the previous year, smoking pack-years, FEV1 (% pred) at baseline, ICS dose (µg budesonide equivalent), baseline FENO and baseline B-eos. At follow-up, only ICS dose was significantly associated with a missing sample (β −0.0008, se 0.0003, p=0.01).

Patients with at least one sample collected were included (n=103) in the prediction of improvement in lung function and airway hyperreactivity.

For eosinophils, raw bias, i.e. the difference in eosinophil counts expressed in percentages between imputed and true values, ranged numerically from 0.1 to 3.4 corresponding to percent bias ranging from 4 to 209%. Confidence intervals for estimates of raw and percent bias were consistently narrower for follow-up compared with baseline for both UCM and MI, independent of cell type and proportion of missing samples, which naturally was reflected in narrower limits of agreement.

The numeric size of raw bias, percent bias and the width of the limits of agreement increased with an increasing proportion of missing samples. UCM and MI both performed the best when 20% of samples were missing with very accurate estimates, particularly for samples at baseline (raw bias: UCM −0.1 (−3.0–4.9); MI: −0.7 (−2.9–5.5)); however, the confidence intervals for the estimates were still rather wide compared to the clinically relevant cut-point (3%).

As illustrated in figures 3 and 4, imputation using UCM exhibited a positive linear relationship for eosinophils and neutrophils at both baseline and follow-up independent of the proportion of missing samples, translating into a systematic bias causing an increasing underestimation of imputed values with increasing mean.

An increasing mean was also associated with increasing differences between the imputed and true values for MI. Importantly, these observations were scattered randomly for eosinophils, independent of missing frequency, whereas a degree of proportional bias was observed for neutrophil count at both baseline and follow-up at higher frequencies of missing samples.

Discussion

We identified no striking differences in the direction, magnitude and precision of the regression coefficients for the prediction models using UMC and MI and, like van der Heijden et al. [15], we found single imputation to perform equally well in the prediction of clinical outcomes with estimates of effect being slightly more conservative using MI.

By simulating missing samples, we found UCM to consistently provide narrower limits of agreement but importantly also to introduce a positive systematic proportional bias. In contrast, we observed an increasing error with increasing mean using MI, but notably no systematic bias was observed for eosinophils, the key cell of interest, whereas a potential proportional bias was observed for neutrophil counts at high frequencies of missing samples.

In the research setting, imputation provides a marked increase in statistical power and, in contrast to CC analysis, attempts to accommodate for the established significant association between induction success and both response to treatment and asthma pathology and severity [6, 7]. This marked strength must be weighed against the introduction of both statistical error and bias through imputation. Collectively, we believe that imputation has a place in the exploratory research setting, particularly in the field of omics, as the increase in depth of analyses provided by the increase in power outweighs the limitations.

We suggest that researchers utilising paired sputum-based outcome measures report analyses using both complete case data and imputation of missing samples to adequately address the missing sputum samples.

In the clinical setting, the presence of airway eosinophilia is the key treatable trait in asthma, allowing for phenotype-guided treatment and treatment with monoclonal antibodies in patients with severe asthma. It is therefore of paramount importance to accurately identify airway eosinophilia as treatment with monoclonal antibodies, when targeted correctly, provides a reduction in exacerbations and improvement in asthma control, but also holds huge medical costs for healthcare providers [19].

Therefore, it is important to assess the performance of imputation in the clinically relevant ranges and in relation to the treatment guiding cut-off (3%). While the estimates of bias for eosinophils at baseline were all promising, particularly when 20% of the samples were missing, the corresponding limits of agreement were markedly wider than the clinically relevant range. Consequently, we consider the statistical accuracy to be too low for imputation to play a role in clinical decision-making. In hindsight, too wide limits of agreement for clinical use were to be expected, given the large relative difference between the numerical range of the variables (0–100%) and the clinically relevant cut-off (3%).

Although larger studies are warranted to both confirm our findings and to further our understanding of how to best handle missing sputum samples, it is apparent that weighing accuracy, bias and the consequences of over- and underestimation are crucial when choosing to perform imputation and particularly when choosing which imputation method to employ.

UCM may be preferable in trials focusing on clinical outcomes, despite conferring bias, as the accuracy in estimating eosinophils is high in the clinically relevant range and the bias (a systematic underestimation) is more intuitively understood and subsequently addressed as the statistical methodology is more transparent. In contrast, MI is likely to be the superior method in larger, more exploratory analyses where bias is not as intuitively identified.

We observed a marked widening of the limits of agreement with increasing prevalence of missing samples, almost following a logarithmic curve, translating to the limits of agreement being drastically narrower when 20% of the samples were missing compared to 40% and 60%, respectively. Based on our findings, we believe that imputation should be performed with caution when >20% of the samples are missing, but larger studies addressing this issue are warranted, as the sample size in this study did not allow us to meaningfully perform a threshold analysis.

In this study, we assessed the impact of imputation through two complementary approaches, each with its own strengths and weaknesses, which we consider a marked strength. In contrast, the sample size, despite being similar to most studies utilising sputum-based outcome measures, remains a key weakness as it raises the question of whether the wide limits of agreement are truly reflective of a large variation of the differences or a result of a small sample size.

Another potential limitation is the use of mannitol-induced sputum samples, which, while comparable with samples induced with hypertonic saline with regards to the successful induction and analyses of inflammatory cells and soluble markers [20, 21], are associated with significantly lower total cell count (3.8 versus 2.1×106, p=0.003) compared with hypertonic saline [23]. Further, significant differences in inflammatory phenotype classification using mannitol and hypertonic saline induction have been reported which may skew our findings slightly, as the paucigranulocytic phenotype has been associated with a lower sputum success rate at follow-up [6, 20, 21]. While we believe our findings translate to the more widely used hypertonic saline induced samples, we acknowledge that this need to be addressed in future studies.

Missing data pose a challenge in the design and analyses of not only sputum-based outcome measures, but also in clinical trials in general and are often not handled adequately.

Based on theoretical reasoning and findings from simulation studies, imputation of missing data is widely regarded as a better option than ignoring missing data, where MI is highlighted as statistically superior to single imputation [15, 22–24]. We have in this study illustrated the practical consequences of different methods of handling these missing data and believe our findings warrant a more conscious approach to missing data, not only using sputum-based outcome measures.

In conclusion, missing samples are common and markedly limit the utility of paired sputum-based outcome measures. Imputation provides a marked increase in statistical power and, in contrast to CC analysis, attempts to accommodate for the fact that the samples are not missing at random.

We suggest that researchers utilising paired sputum-based outcome measures report analyses using both CC analysis and analyses with missing data imputed but suggest imputation to be used with caution when >20% of samples are missing. The choice of imputation method will be a balancing act, weighing the outcome measure, the consequences of over- and underestimation, and the ability to address bias.
