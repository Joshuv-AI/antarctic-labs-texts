Evolution of localized pulses in the defocusing modified Korteweg–de Vries equation theory
L. F. Calazans de Brito, A. Gammal, A. M. Kamchatnov

Abstract
In this work, we develop, in the Gurevich-Pitaevskii framework, an analytic theory for the evolution of localized pulses in the defocusing modified Korteweg–de Vries equation theory for situations when a dispersive shock does not eventually transform into a sequence of well-separated solitons. We found solutions to the Whitham modulation equations for the corresponding so-called “quasi-simple” dispersive shock waves and illustrated this solution with concrete examples of an initial pulse. Comparison of the analytical solution with direct numerical simulations showed that the modulation theory provides a very accurate description of the wave pattern even at one wavelength scale.

I
Introduction

The study of nonlinear phenomena has reached an advanced theoretical stage that explains a wide range of real-world physical phenomena on the basis of nonlinear wave models. Such advances can be seen, for example, in the development of the theory of
dispersive shock waves
(DSWs) (see, e.g., review articles
7
;
19
), which consist of coherent nonlinear wave structures whose amplitudes change slowly from their small amplitude edges to soliton edges. These wave patterns form after the pulses’ breaking in various media with competing dispersion and nonlinearity effects, so they were observed in such systems as plasma
28
;
29
, electron beams
24
, optics
26
;
10
,
Bose-Einstein condensates
27
;
4
, and others.

The theory of dispersive shocks was first developed by Gurevich-Pitaevskii
13
. In their work, they observed that the different scales between fast oscillations and slowly changing parameters along the DSW zone make the Whitham modulation theory
32
;
33
an appropriate mathematical tool for the analytical description of DSWs. As a result, one can find the slowly changing parameters as solutions of
first-order partial differential equations of hydrodynamic type (i.e., of the Whitham modulation equations). If the nonlinear wave equation for the model
under consideration is completely integrable, as it occurs in the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation case, then the Whitham equations can be cast into a diagonal form for the so-called Riemann invariants. In the simplest scenario with only one Riemann invariant changing along a DSW, we obtain a “simple wave” solution of the Whitham equations. This kind of structures can be observed, for example, in the so-called Riemann problem, where one studies the evolution of an initial step-like discontinuity.
This problem was considered for many completely integrable equations, in particular, for the KdV equation
13
, the modified Korteweg-de Vries (mKdV) equation
6
, the Gardner equation
18
, and the nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation
5
. It should be
noticed that even in this case of simple-wave solutions, the modulation theory for the Gardner and mKdV equations with cubic nonlinearity turns out to be quite complicated in comparison with the KdV equation case.
Since these equations are not genuinely nonlinear
22
, it turns out that one set of Riemann invariants, obtained as solutions of the Whitham equations, corresponds to two different wave patterns.

Although the simple-wave problems have many applications to situations that include the evolution of step-like discontinuities, this particular case is not applicable to a localized initial pulse when several Riemann invariants are changing along a DSW. Just this situation is considered, for instance,
in typical experiments on shallow water DSWs (see, e.g., Refs.
14
;
30
and references therein). The corresponding theory was developed in Refs.
12
;
9
;
8
;
16
with two
Riemann invariants changing along DSWs for the KdV equation case, and such waves were called quasi-simple DSWs.
In this work, we develop the analytical theory of quasi-simple DSWs for the defocusing mKdV equation,

u_{t}-6u^{2}u_{x}+u_{xxx}=0,

(1)

in case of non-solitonic initial pulses and compare our analytical solution with a numerical experiment.

This article is organized as follows. Section II is devoted to the calculation of the pulse evolution before the wave-breaking moment, i.e., the so-called gradient catastrophe. In section III, we develop the Whitham theory and show how the evolution of Riemann invariants reproduces the shape of DSWs in the mKdV equation case. In section IV, we study the motion of the DSW edges by the classical hodograph transform method. In section V, we obtain parameters inside the DSW zone by the generalized Tsarev’s hodograph method, and, finally, we solve a specific problem in section VI in order to compare our analytical results with the direct numerical simulation.

II
Non-dispersive limit for smooth pulse evolution

To solve the initial problem for the defocusing mKdV equation (
1
), we consider an arbitrary initial smooth pulse
u_{0}(x)
with a single extremum value
u_{m}
at the coordinate position
x_{m}
(as shown in Fig.
1
(a)).
At the stage of evolution before the wave-breaking moment, the pulse deforms without loss of smoothness. In this regime, if the pulse with the amplitude
u_{m}
has a long enough characteristic length
{l}\gg 1
, the third-order derivative term in Eq. (
1
),
u_{xxx}\sim u_{m}/{l}^{3}
, is small compared with the nonlinear term
u^{2}u_{x}\sim u_{m}^{3}/{l}
, and we can neglect it.
This means that the dispersive effects are much smaller than the nonlinear ones, and as a result, at this stage of evolution, the pulse evolves according to the Hopf equation

r_{t}-6rr_{x}=0,

(2)

where we defined the variable
r=u^{2}
for convenience of comparison with limiting values of the Riemann invariants in the theory of DSWs developed below.

Eq. (
2
) can be solved by the standard method of characteristics, and the solution in implicit form reads

x+6rt=\bar{x}_{1}(r)\quad\text{and}\quad x+6rt=\bar{x}_{2}(r)

(3)

where
\bar{x}_{1}(r)
and
\bar{x}_{2}(r)
represent the two branches of the inverse function of the initial distribution
r_{0}(x)=u_{0}^{2}(x)
, as depicted in Fig.
1
(b). These two branches evolve independently as long as the solution remains single-valued. However, the characteristics start to intersect each other at the instant of wave breaking,

t_{\text{WB}}=-\frac{1}{6\max\left(dr/dx\right)},

(4)

so the solution becomes multi-valued. Close to this moment, the dependence
u(x,t)
on
x
becomes very steep near the wave-breaking location, so the derivative becomes very large and the dispersive term of Eq. (
1
)
cannot be neglected anymore. As a result, the interplay of dispersion and nonlinearity effects leads to the formation of a DSW. In the Gurevich-Pitaevskii approximation, the dispersive shock occupies a finite interval between the small-amplitude edge (
x_{l}
) and the soliton edge (
x_{r}
). As will be shown, this region expands
with time, and it matches with the dispersionless solution (
3
) at its edges. Within the DSW region,
x\in[x_{l},x_{r}]
, the solution is obtained using the Whitham modulation theory, and outside those boundaries the shape of the pulse still evolves according to the solution (
3
) of Eq. (
2
).

u_{0}(x)
. (b) The inverse function of this initial
condition defines two branches
\bar{x}_{1}(r)
and
\bar{x}_{2}(r)
,

III
Whitham modulation theory and DSW structure in mKdV equation

The Whitham equations for Eq. (
1
) were first found in Ref.
3
(see also Ref.
1
for details) by averaging the conservation laws of Eq. (
1
) over fast oscillations, followed by diagonalization of the averaged system. As a result, the modulation equations for the smooth functions
r_{i}(x,t)
for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
are obtained, which change slowly along a DSW region. These variables
r_{i}(x,t)
are called Riemann invariants of the Whitham system of modulation equations. In the case of quasi-simple solutions one of them is constant. To be definite, we assume that the Riemann invariants are ordered according to the inequalities

0=r_{1}<r_{2}\leq r_{3}.

(5)

By taking
r_{1}
equal to zero, we do not change the main characteristic features of DSWs, but simplify notation and calculations. Depending on the initial conditions, we obtain different wave patterns (see Refs.
1
;
18
;
6
), and we are interested in situations with the formation of non-solitonic pulses similar to what was found in Refs.
12
;
9
;
8
;
16
for the KdV equation case. When the solution of the Whitham equations is found, the physical variables can be expressed in terms of them by the formulas

u(x,t)=\pm\frac{\sqrt{r_{3}}(\sqrt{r_{3}}-\sqrt{r_{2}})-(r_{3}-r_{2})\mathrm{sn}^{2}(\theta/2,m)}{\sqrt{r_{3}}-(\sqrt{r_{3}}-\sqrt{r_{2}})\mathrm{sn}^{2}(\theta/2,m)},

(6)

where the modulus
m
of elliptic functions and the wave phase
\theta
are also functions of the Riemann invariants.
The parameter
m
satisfies the condition
0\leq m\leq 1
, and it is defined by the expression

m=1-\frac{r_{2}}{r_{3}},

(7)

while the phase has the usual form

\theta(x,t)=kx-\omega t+\theta_{0},

(8)

where the local wave number and wave frequency are given, respectively, by the expressions

k=2\sqrt{r_{3}}\quad\text{and}\quad\omega=kV,

(9)

with the phase velocity

V=-2(r_{2}+r_{3})

(10)

of the nonlinear periodic wave.

Since we take
r_{1}=0
and only
r_{2}
and
r_{3}
change along a DSW, the Whitham system reduces to two equations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{2}}{\partial t}+v_{2}(r_{2},r_{3})\,\frac{\partial r_{2}}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=0,

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{3}}{\partial t}+v_{3}(r_{2},r_{3})\,\frac{\partial r_{3}}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=0,

where the Whitham velocities are defined as

v_{i}(r_{2},r_{3})=\left(1-\frac{L}{\partial L/\partial r_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}\right)V.

(12)

Here,
L
is the local wavelength of the nonlinear periodic solution (
6
),

L=\frac{2}{\sqrt{r_{3}}}K(m),

(13)

where
K(m)
is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind.
Substituting Eq. (
13
) into Eq. (
12
), we can write
v_{i}
as

\displaystyle v_{2}(r_{2},r_{3})=-2(r_{2}+r_{3})-\frac{4(1-m)(r_{3}-r_{2})K(m)}{E(m)-(1-m)K(m)},

(14)

\displaystyle v_{3}(r_{2},r_{3})=-2(r_{2}+r_{3})+\frac{4mr_{3}K(m)}{E(m)-K(m)},

where
E(m)
denotes the complete elliptic integral of the second kind.
In contrast to the KdV equation case, where the evolutions of positive and negative pulses are qualitatively different, in the mKdV equation case the positive and negative localized pulses evolve symmetrically with respect to the
x
-axis. By definition, we have
r=u^{2}>0
, so the distributions of the same set of Riemann invariants provide solutions for both positive and negative initial pulse.

IV
Hodograph transform and expansion of DSW region

As mentioned above, the DSW region expands during its evolution. In this section, we will develop the theory of the DSW expansion by means of the hodograph transform.

IV.1
Hodograph transform

The main idea behind the hodograph technique consists of “swapping” the independent and dependent variables in the system (
11
), that is

(r_{2}(x,t),r_{3}(x,t))\rightarrow(x(r_{2},r_{3}),t(r_{2},r_{3})).

(15)

We assume that the Jacobian

J=\frac{\partial x}{\partial r_{2}}\frac{\partial t}{\partial r_{3}}-\frac{\partial x}{\partial r_{3}}\frac{\partial t}{\partial r_{2}}

(16)

does not vanish,
J\neq 0
. Then, the transformation to new variables is performed by means of the expressions

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{2}}{\partial x}=J^{-1}\frac{\partial t}{\partial r_{3}},\quad\frac{\partial r_{2}}{\partial t}=-J^{-1}\frac{\partial x}{\partial r_{3}},

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{3}}{\partial x}=-J^{-1}\frac{\partial t}{\partial r_{2}},\quad\frac{\partial r_{3}}{\partial t}=J^{-1}\frac{\partial x}{\partial r_{2}},

and after substitution of Eqs. (
17
) into Eq. (
11
), we obtain the system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x}{\partial r_{2}}-v_{3}(r_{2},r_{3})\,\frac{\partial t}{\partial r_{2}}

\displaystyle=0,

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x}{\partial r_{3}}-v_{2}(r_{2},r_{3})\,\frac{\partial t}{\partial r_{3}}

\displaystyle=0.

It must be fulfilled along the whole dispersive shock, but at first, we will study it at the shock’s edges in order to find their paths.

IV.2
Path of the soliton edge

and the shock lies entirely within region

. Panels (b) and (c) represent a more general situation where
a<u_{m}^{2}
at different stages: (b) before the soliton edge reaches the second branch
\bar{x}_{2}(r)
, the DSW is located in region
A
to the left of
x_{m}
; (c) after it enters this branch, the shock requires two distinct analytical descriptions, one for region
A
(
[x_{l},x_{m}]
) and another for region
B
(
[x_{m},x_{r}]
).

At the soliton edge,
x_{r}
, we have
r_{2}\rightarrow r_{1}=0
and
r_{3}\rightarrow r=u_{r}^{2}
(see Fig.
2
). Substituting these values in Eqs. (
14
), we find that at this boundary
v_{2}(0,r)=-2r
and
v_{3}(0,r)=-6r
. So, the first Eq. (
18
) reduces to

\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{2}}(x+6rt)=0,

(19)

from which we reproduce the background solution for the smooth limit shown in Eq. (
3
),

x+6rt=\bar{x}_{i}(r).

(20)

At this edge, the second equation depends only on
r
, which is the value of the Riemann invariant at the location of the DSW’s edge, so along the path of the edge we have
\partial/\partial r_{3}\rightarrow d/dr
, and

\frac{dx}{dr}+2r\frac{dt}{dr}=0.

(21)

Substituting the value of
x(r)
from Eq. (
20
) in Eq. (
21
), we obtain the differential equation

\frac{dt}{dr}+\frac{3t}{2r}=\frac{1}{4r}\frac{d}{dr}\bar{x}_{i}(r),

(22)

which can be readily solved to give

t(r_{r})=\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r_{r}}\right)^{3/2}t_{\text{WB}}+\frac{1}{4r_{r}^{3/2}}\int_{r_{0}}^{r_{r}}\sqrt{r}\frac{d}{dr}\bar{x}_{i}(r)dr,

(23)

where
r_{0}
represents the wave amplitude at the breaking point
t_{\text{WB}}
, and
r_{r}
is the background value at the position of the soliton edge. The coordinate of the soliton edge is obtained after the substitution of Eq. (
23
) into Eq. (
20
),

x(r_{r})=-6r_{r}t(r_{r})+\bar{x}_{i}(r_{r}).

(24)

IV.3
Path of the small-amplitude edge

At the small amplitude edge,
x_{l}
,
r_{1}\rightarrow u^{2}=0
,
u
being here the vanishing amplitude of the background wave. At this edge, in the Gurevich-Pitaevskii approximation, the Riemann invariants
r_{2}
and
r_{3}
take the same value which we denote as
a
,
r_{2}=r_{3}=a
. Then we get from Eq. (
14
), that
v_{2}=v_{3}=-12a
, so both hodograph equations (
18
) reduce to

\frac{dx}{da}+12a\frac{dt}{da}=0,

(25)

that is, the small-amplitude edge propagates along the characteristic line defined by the equation

\frac{dx_{l}}{dt}=-12a.

(26)

Actually, this is the group velocity of the packet associated with the small-amplitude edge. Indeed, the linearized Eq. (
1
) gives the dispersion relation

\omega=-6u^{2}k-k^{3},

(27)

so, at this edge, with zero background amplitude
u\rightarrow 0
, the group velocity is equal to

v_{g}=\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial k}=-3k^{2}.

(28)

From Eq. (
9
) we find that here we have
k=2\sqrt{a}
, so we get again Eq. (
26
).

Depending on the initial profile of the pulse, we get two different situations. If at the time of wave breaking
we have
a=u_{m}^{2}
(see Fig.
2
(a)), then the value of
a
remains constant during the
evolution. Consequently, Eq. (
26
) yields the motion of the small-amplitude edge with constant velocity,

x_{l}(t)=-12u_{m}^{2}(t-t_{\text{WB}}).

(29)

The second more general possibility corresponds to
a<u_{m}^{2}
(see Fig.
2
(b)-(c)), so
a
changes during the evolution of the pulse. In this case, as shown in Fig.
2
(b) the DSW is first in the region on the left from the maximum
u_{m}
, that is, the soliton edge propagates along the first branch
\overline{x}_{1}(r)
of the inverse function of the initial distribution; we say that this edge is located inside the region
A
. At some instant of time, the soliton edge reaches the maximum of the distribution
u=u_{m}
, and after that it propagates along the second branch
\overline{x}_{2}(r)
of the inverse function of the initial distribution. Hence, the DSW consists of two parts: region
A
on the left from the maximal point
u_{m}^{2}
of two matching Riemann invariants
r_{2}
and
r_{3}
, and region
B
on the right from this maximal point of two matching Riemann invariants. This case is depicted in Fig.
2
(c). Actually, the first case (a) corresponds to situations when the region
A
shrinks to a point, so there remains only the region
B
.
In case (b), we cannot find the function
a(t)
just from the limiting
equation (
25
), so we have to solve the hodograph equations along the whole DSW, and solution of this problem is necessary for the analytical description of the DSW’s profile.

V
General quasi-simple solution of Whitham Equations

In this section, we use the method of Refs.
11
;
16
to explicitly solve the Whitham equations (
11
) in the region
\left(x_{l},x_{r}\right)
(see Fig.
2
). As a result, we find
the distributions of
r_{2}(x,t)
and
r_{3}(x,t)
, which determine the modulated wave function (
6
).

V.1
Generalized hodograph method and Euler-Poison-Darboux equation

The generalized hodograph method, proposed by Tsarev in Ref.
31
, is based on the assumption that the solution of Eqs. (
11
) can be found implicitly in the form

\displaystyle x-v_{2}(r_{2},r_{3})t=w_{2}(r_{2},r_{3}),

(30)

\displaystyle x-v_{3}(r_{2},r_{3})t=w_{3}(r_{2},r_{3}).

These equations combined with Eqs. (
18
) yield an important relationship

\frac{1}{w_{i}-w_{j}}\frac{\partial w_{i}}{\partial r_{j}}=\frac{1}{v_{i}-v_{j}}\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial r_{j}}.

(31)

The symmetry of this relationship with respect to the transposition
v_{i}\leftrightarrow w_{i}
suggests that we can find the solution of
w_{i}(r_{2},r_{3})
in the form similar to
v_{i}(r_{2},r_{3})
in Eq. (
12
), that is

w_{i}(r_{2},r_{3})=\left(1-\frac{L}{\partial L/\partial r_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}\right)W(r_{2},r_{3}).

(32)

Then, following the methods of Refs.
11
;
19
;
20
, we find from Eqs. (
31
),
(
32
), and (
12
), that the function
W(r_{2},r_{3})
must satisfy the Euler-Poisson-Darboux equation

\frac{\partial^{2}W}{\partial r_{2}\partial r_{3}}=\frac{1}{2(r_{3}-r_{2})}\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial r_{3}}-\frac{\partial W}{\partial r_{2}}\right).

(33)

Consequently (see Refs.
11
;
19
;
20
), the general solution of Eq. (
33
) can be
written in the form

\displaystyle W(r_{2},r_{3})=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r_{2}}\frac{\Phi(\mu)d\mu}{\sqrt{(r_{3}-\mu)(r_{2}-\mu)}}

(34)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{r_{3}}\frac{\Psi(\mu)d\mu}{\sqrt{(r_{3}-\mu)|r_{2}-\mu|}},

where
\Phi(\mu)
and
\Psi(\mu)
are arbitrary functions to be found from the appropriate boundary conditions.
Therefore, the solution depends on the position of the soliton edge. If the soliton edge is located in the branch
A
, we denote it as
W^{A}(r_{2},r_{3})
, otherwise, if the soliton edge is moving through the second branch,
the solution is denoted as
W^{B}(r_{2},r_{3})
.

Immediately after the wave-breaking moment, the soliton edge starts its motion along the branch
A
.
At this boundary, we have
r_{2}\rightarrow 0
, so Eq. (
34
) becomes

W^{A}(0,r_{3})=\int_{0}^{r_{3}}\frac{\Psi^{A}(\mu)d\mu}{\sqrt{\mu(r_{3}-\mu)}}.

(35)

This is well-known Abel equation, so its solution is given by the formula

\frac{\Psi^{A}(\mu)}{\sqrt{\mu}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{d}{d\mu}\int_{0}^{\mu}\frac{W^{A}(0,r)}{\sqrt{\mu-r}}dr.

(36)

The function
W^{A}(0,r)
can be found from the second equation of the system (
30
).
In the soliton limit this equation reduces to

x+6rt=w^{A}_{3}(0,r),

(37)

so, while the soliton edge is located in the region
A
, that is
x=\bar{x}_{1}(r)
, this equation coincides with Eq. (
20
). Consequently, we get

w^{A}_{3}(0,r)=\bar{x}_{1}(r),

(38)

and then Eq. (
32
) converts in this limit into the differential equation

\bar{x}_{1}(r)=\left(1+2r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)W^{A}(0,r).

(39)

This equation can be readily solved,

W^{A}(0,r)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\int_{0}^{r}\frac{\bar{x}_{1}(\rho)}{\sqrt{\rho}}d\rho.

(40)

so we can rewrite Eq. (
36
) as

\frac{\Psi^{A}(\mu)}{\sqrt{\mu}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d}{d\mu}\int_{0}^{\mu}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{r(\mu-r)}}\int_{0}^{r}\frac{\bar{x}_{1}(\rho)}{\sqrt{\rho}}d\rho\right)dr.

(41)

We change the order of integration,

\frac{\Psi^{A}(\mu)}{\sqrt{\mu}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d}{d\mu}\int_{0}^{\mu}\left(\frac{\bar{x}_{1}(\rho)}{\sqrt{\rho}}\int_{\rho}^{\mu}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{r(\mu-r)}}\right)d\rho,

(42)

where the inner integral is elementary,

\int_{\rho}^{\mu}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{r(\mu-r)}}=\arccos\left(\frac{2\rho}{\mu}-1\right).

(43)

Thus, we arrive at the expression

\frac{\Psi^{A}(\mu)}{\sqrt{\mu}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d}{d\mu}\int_{0}^{\mu}\frac{\bar{x}_{1}(\rho)}{\sqrt{\rho}}\arccos\left(\frac{2\rho}{\mu}-1\right)d\rho,

(44)

or

\Psi^{A}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\mu}}\int_{0}^{\mu}\frac{\bar{x}_{1}(\rho)}{\sqrt{\mu-\rho}}d\rho.

(45)

It is easy to show that if
\Psi(r)+\Phi(r)\neq 0
, then Eq. (
34
) with
r_{3}=r_{2}+\varepsilon
diverges in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, so we must have
\Psi(r)=-\Phi(r)
, and we arrive at the final expression for the solution of the Euler-Poisson-Darboux equation which satisfies the boundary conditions corresponding to the DSW located in the region
A
:

W^{A}(r_{2},r_{3})=\int_{r_{2}}^{r_{3}}\frac{\Psi^{A}(\mu)d\mu}{\sqrt{(r_{3}-\mu)(\mu-r_{2})}}.

(46)

The soliton edge enters the region
B
at the point with
r_{3}=u_{m}^{2}
, and after that moment the DSW consists of two parts. In the region
A
the solution is still given by the formulas (
30
), (
32
) with
W=W^{A}(r_{2},r_{3})
defined by Eq. (
46
). The solution in the region
B
corresponds to another function
W=W^{B}(r_{2},r_{3})
, which must satisfy the boundary condition
W^{A}(r_{2},u_{m}^{2})=W^{B}(r_{2},u_{m}^{2})
. Therefore, we look for the solution of the Euler-Poisson-Darboux equation in the form

W^{B}(r_{2},r_{3})=W^{A}(r_{2},r_{3})+\int_{r_{3}}^{u_{m}^{2}}\frac{\Psi^{B}(\mu)d\mu}{\sqrt{(\mu-r_{2})(\mu-r_{3})}}.

(47)

Then at the soliton edge (
r_{2}\rightarrow 0
) we get

W^{B}(0,r_{3})-W^{A}(0,r_{3})=\int_{r_{3}}^{u_{m}^{2}}\frac{\Psi^{B}(\mu)d\mu}{\sqrt{\mu(\mu-r_{3})}}.

(48)

This is the Abel equation again, so the inverse Abel’s transform yields

\frac{\Psi^{B}(\mu)}{\sqrt{\mu}}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{d}{d\mu}\int_{\mu}^{u_{m}^{2}}\frac{W^{B}(0,r)-W^{A}(0,r)}{\sqrt{r-\mu}}dr.

(49)

Then, for the propagation of the soliton edge along the second branch, instead of Eq. (
39
), we get

\bar{x}_{2}(r)=\left(1+2r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)W^{B}(0,r)

(50)

which must be solved with the boundary condition
W^{B}(0,u_{m}^{2})=W^{A}(0,u_{m}^{2})
. Thus, taking into account Eq. (
40
), we obtain

W^{B}(0,r)=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\int_{r}^{u_{m}^{2}}\frac{\bar{x}_{2}(\rho)}{\sqrt{\rho}}d\rho+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\int_{0}^{u_{m}^{2}}\frac{\bar{x}_{1}(\rho)}{\sqrt{\rho}}d\rho.

(51)

We subtract Eq. (
40
) from this equation and arrive at

W^{B}(0,r)-W^{A}(0,r)=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\int_{r}^{u_{m}^{2}}\frac{\bar{x}_{2}(\rho)-\bar{x}_{1}(\rho)}{\sqrt{\rho}}d\rho.

(52)

By substituting this expression into Eq. (
49
), changing the order of integrations, and simplifying, we arrive at the final expression for the function
\Psi^{B}(\mu)
:

\Psi^{B}(\mu)=-\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\mu}}\int_{\mu}^{u_{m}^{2}}\frac{\bar{x}_{2}(\rho)-\bar{x}_{1}(\rho)}{\sqrt{\rho-\mu}}d\rho.

(53)

Subsequently, substituting Eq. (
53
) into Eq. (
47
) yields the solution to the Euler-Poisson-Darboux
equation for region
B
. The formulas derived in this section provide the complete solution for the Whitham equations,
expressed in terms of the initial distribution
r=u_{0}^{2}(x)
and the two branches of its inverse functions
\bar{x}_{1}(r)
and
\bar{x}_{2}(r)
.

V.2
Phase shift correction

When the Riemann invariants
r_{2}=r_{2}(x,t),r_{3}=r_{3}(x,t)
, as slow functions of
x
and
t
, are found, we can easily
calculate the other envelope functions of the DSW, for example its amplitude. In particular, the wave number
k
and
the frequency
\omega=kV
in the phase (
8
) are given by Eqs. (
9
), (
10
). However, the phase
shift
\theta_{0}
in Eq. (
8
) also becomes a slow function of
x
and
t
in a modulated DSW. It turns out
that this function can also be expressed in terms of the same Riemann invariants
r_{2}
and
r_{3}
(see, e.g., Ref.
7
;
21
;
15
).
To do this, we differentiate Eq. (
8
) with respect to
x
to obtain

\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}=k+\sum_{i=2}^{3}\left[\frac{\partial k}{\partial r_{i}}x-\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial r_{i}}t+\frac{\partial\theta_{0}}{\partial r_{i}}\right]\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}

(54)

By definition, we have
\partial\theta/\partial x=k
, and since the Riemann invariants
r_{2},r_{3}
are independent of each other, we get

\frac{\partial k}{\partial r_{i}}x-\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial r_{i}}t+\frac{\partial\theta_{0}}{\partial r_{i}}=0.

(55)

Using Eq. (
12
) we find

\frac{\partial\omega/\partial r_{i}}{\partial k/\partial r_{i}}=\frac{\partial(kV)/\partial r_{i}}{\partial k/\partial r_{i}}=\left(1+\frac{k}{\partial k/\partial r_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}\right)V=v_{i}.

(56)

Now we divide Eq. (
55
) by
\partial k/\partial r_{i}
, and substitute Eq. (
56
) in it to obtain

x-v_{i}t=-\frac{\partial\theta_{0}/\partial r_{i}}{\partial k/\partial r_{i}}.

(57)

Comparison with the hodograph solution of the Whitham equations (see Eqs. (
30
), (
32
))
gives

-\frac{\partial\theta_{0}}{\partial r_{i}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}\left(kW\right),

(58)

and, after evident integration, we find the explicit expression for the phase correction

\theta_{0}=-kW+C.

(59)

So, we can rewrite the expression (
8
) for the phase as

\theta(x,t)=kx-\omega t-kW+C,

(60)

where
C
is an arbitrary constant and
W
is
W^{A}
or
W^{B}
, depending on the branch of location of the soliton
edge.

VI
Analytical and Numerical Solutions: Comparison and Discussion

In this section, we compare our analytical theory with a direct numerical experiment. In our numerical simulations, we use the fourth-order split-step Fourier method for the mKdV equation, similar to the approach of Refs.
25
;
2
. To avoid artifacts caused by periodic boundary conditions, we employ a large computational domain,
x\in[-1500,1500]
, with
N=2^{16}
equally spaced points; the time step used is
\Delta t=0.001
. This method is applied to study the evolution of a quasi-simple DSW evolved from the initial condition

u(x,0)=\begin{cases}\pm\sqrt{\frac{x}{60}},\qquad 0\leq x\leq 60,\\
\pm\sqrt{\frac{120-x}{60}},\qquad 60\leq x\leq 120,\\
0,\qquad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(61)

where the sign represents a “positive” and “negative” initial pulse, respectively. This choice of the initial distribution is convenient for comparing with the analytical theory since, in this case, the wave breaking leads to the quasi-simple DSWs with the pattern of the Riemann invariants shown in Fig.
2
(a).
Actually, the transformation
u^{2}(x,t)\rightarrow r(x,t)
shows that both positive and negative initial pulses are described by the same solution in terms of the Riemann invariants.

Non-dispersive regime and wave breaking

VI.2
Solution of the dispersive shock problem

After the wave-breaking moment, all parameters of the DSW can be found from the calculation of the Riemann invariants as solutions of the Whitham modulation equations. Here we find these Riemann invariants for the DSW evolving from the initial condition (
61
). As mentioned above, the dynamics of the DSW is treated differently depending on the region where the soliton edge of the DSW is located, namely, in the region
A
or
B
. In the case of the region
A
, we find the potential function
W^{A}(r_{2},r_{3})
from Eq. (
46

45
) gives the expression

\Psi^{A}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\mu}}\int_{0}^{\mu}\frac{60\rho}{\sqrt{\mu-\rho}}d\rho=\frac{40\mu}{\pi}.

(65)

Consequently, an easy calculation gives

\begin{split}W^{A}(r_{2},r_{3})&=\frac{40}{\pi}\int_{r_{2}}^{r_{3}}\frac{\mu d\mu}{\sqrt{(r_{3}-\mu)(\mu-r_{2})}}\\
&=20(r_{2}+r_{3}),\end{split}

(66)

so we get from Eq. (
32

w_{i}^{A}(r_{2},r_{3})=20(r_{2}+r_{3})-20\left(\frac{v_{i}}{2}+r_{2}+r_{3}\right).

(67)

This solution exists as long as the leading soliton does not reach the branch
B
, and this moment corresponds to the limit

r_{2}\rightarrow 0,\quad r_{3}\rightarrow r,\quad v_{2}\rightarrow-2r,\quad v_{3}\rightarrow-6r.

(68)

Hence, we get

w^{A}_{2}(0,r)=20r\quad\text{and}\quad w^{A}_{3}(0,r)=60r,

(69)

so formally the soliton reaches the branch
B
at the moment
t
and at the point
x_{r}
which satisfies the system

x_{r}+2rt=20r,\qquad x_{r}+6rt=60r.

(70)

Its solution obviously gives
t=10=t_{\text{WB}}
, that is, the soliton edge reaches the branch
\bar{x}_{2}(r)
exactly at the moment of the wave breaking; see Fig.
3
(c). So, in our particular case with
the initial distribution (
61
), the evolution of the DSW is described only by the parameters corresponding to the region
B
. In this region, we find from Eq.(
53
)

\begin{split}\Psi^{B}(\mu)&=-\frac{60}{\pi\sqrt{\mu}}\int_{\mu}^{1}\frac{1-\rho}{\sqrt{\rho-\mu}}d\rho\\
&=-\frac{80}{\pi\sqrt{\mu}}(1-\mu)^{\frac{3}{2}}\end{split}

(71)

Consequently, Eq.(
47

\begin{split}W^{B}(r_{2},r_{3})&=20(r_{2}+r_{3})\\
&-\frac{80}{\pi}\int_{r_{3}}^{1}\sqrt{\frac{(1-\mu)^{3}}{\mu(\mu-r_{2})(\mu-r_{3})}}d\mu,\end{split}

(72)

so, according to Eq. (

r_{2}
and
r_{3}
must satisfy the system

w_{2}^{B}(r_{2},r_{3})-w_{3}^{B}(r_{2},r_{3})+(v_{3}(r_{2},r_{3})-v_{2}(r_{2},r_{3}))t=0,

(73)

where the values of
w^{B}_{2}(r_{2},r_{3})
and
w^{B}_{3}(r_{2},r_{3})
can be calculated from Eq. (
32

W^{B}(r_{2},r_{3})
defined by Eq. (

r_{2}
for any given
r_{3}

\theta_{0}(r_{2},r_{3})=-kW^{B}(r_{2},r_{3})+C,

(74)

where
k=2\sqrt{r_{3}}
and in our model
C=0
.

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we have shown that the Gurevich-Pitaevskii approach based on Whitham modulation theory provides a very accurate description of the evolution of an initial pulse in the case of mKdV dynamics.
We considered situations where the initial pulses do not eventually evolve into trains of well-separated solitons, so our theory extends the results obtained earlier in Refs.
9
;
8
;
16
for the
KdV case to the mKdV situation. We found that the Gurevich-Pitaevskii analytical approach agrees very well with the direct numerical simulations even within one wavelength scale after taking into account the phase shift correction. Thus, the Whitham modulation theory turns out to be very accurate almost everywhere along a DSW for a not-too-small time of evolution after the wave-breaking moment, so it must provide a quite reliable description of DSWs observed in real experiments. This theory can find applications to observations of dispersive shocks in magneto-hydrodynamic nonlinear Alfvén waves and in two-component Bose-Einstein condensates, where the wave dynamics can be reduced to the mKdV equation (see, e.g.,
20
;
23
;
17
).
