Ethnic and social diversity among hospital pharmacy residents in the Netherlands and its importance in resident selection
Authors: Rianne J. Zaal, Floor van Rosse, Nicole Vink-van Kimmenade, Nicole Veldhorst-Janssen, Marleen Kemper, Paul D. van der Linden, Sumeyye Kabadayi, Karen M. Stegers-Jager, Ingeborg Wilting
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEthnic and social diversity in contemporary societies is not adequately reflected among medical residents and medical specialists, and this disparity may also exist within hospital pharmacy. Although at least 35% of pharmacy students have a Turkish, African, Latin American or Asian (TALA) background, it remains unclear whether this extends to hospital pharmacy residents.This study examined the diversity among hospital pharmacy residents in the Netherlands, in terms of gender, age, ethnic background and social background. In addition, the extent to which diversity is considered in selection procedures was explored.MethodsA quantitative cross-sectional national study was conducted using questionnaires delivered to hospital pharmacy residents, who started their residency between 2018 and 2023, and their supervisors in the Netherlands. Residents reported their gender, age, ethnic and social background. Ethnic background of residents was compared with that of young adults (aged 20-30 years) in the general population and pharmacy students. Supervisors indicated whether diversity, considering gender, ethnic background and their combination with previous experience and interests, was considered during selection and rated importance hereof (scale 1 “unimportant” to 5 “very important”). ResultsIn total, 131 of 169 (78%) invited residents and 32 of 37 (87%) supervisors were included. Among residents 30% were male and 18% had a Turkish, African, Latin American or Asian (TALA) background, compared to 20% of young adults in the general population and 35% of students. Compared to other residents, TALA residents were less likely to have a parent with at least a bachelor's degree (44% versus 76%, p=0.004) or a parent registered as healthcare professional (4% versus 26%, p=0.043).Most supervisors reported considering gender balance (73%), ethnic diversity (69%), and the combined diversity factors (90%) during selection. Gender and ethnic diversity were rated fairly important (median 3); while the combined diversity factors were rated as important (median 4).Conclusions The ethnic diversity of hospital pharmacy residents in the Netherlands is comparable to that of young adults in the general population but appears to decline in the transition from student to resident. Future research should focus on this transition, including career choices and the residency selection procedures.

Background

In multicultural societies, a diverse healthcare workforce is essential for providing culturally sensitive care. Ethnic diversity among health care professionals enhances physicians’ cultural sensitivity, incorporates the knowledge and skills of professionals with a migration background, and improves quality of patient care [1–3]. From a social justice perspective, equal career opportunities for medical specialists, including hospital pharmacists, are also essential for inclusive selection procedures [4]. Despite this need, the ethnic diversity of healthcare professionals, particularly medical residents and specialists, does not reflect the diversity of contemporary societies [5–8]. In the Netherlands, 20% of the young adults has a Turkish, African, Latin American or Asian (TALA) background, whereas only 5 to 7% of the medical residents and 2 to 4% of medical specialists share this background, compared to about 30% of the medical students in 2021/2022 [5, 9, 10]. This decline of ethnic diversity along the career path to medical specialist is referred to as “the leaky pipeline phenomenon”, referring to the loss of diversity at various stages, such as the transition from secondary school to medical school and from physician to medical specialist [5, 7, 11]. Besides, physicians whose parents have lower incomes or who don’t have a parent registered as healthcare professional have lower odds to become a medical specialist [7]. In addition, inclusion of first-generation students, i.e. students whose parents or guardians have not completed a bachelor’s degree, may require special attention, since these students experience difficulties, such as limited guidance and the need to make decisions independently [7, 12]. Socioeconomic status, gender and social network connections, have also been identified as determinants for admission to health professions education or residency programs [7, 13–15].

Pharmacy students in the Netherlands include individuals with diverse backgrounds; although exact numbers on TALA background are unknown, 35% have a Turkish, African, Latin American or Asian (excluding Japanese) background [9]. However, data on diversity along the career path of hospital pharmacists are lacking.

Adequate selection of residents is essential for building a diverse workforce. Selection into hospital pharmacy residency programs is highly competitive and can be implicitly influenced by perceived similarity between applicants and selection committee members, as well as by cultural matching. Therefore, insight in these procedures is important.

This study aimed to describe the diversity among hospital pharmacy residents in the Netherlands, in terms of gender, age, and social background, and to explore the consideration of diversity during residency selection procedures.

Methods

Design

A quantitative cross-sectional national study was conducted using electronic questionnaires administered to hospital pharmacy residents and their supervisors in the Netherlands.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Netherlands Association of Medical Education Ethical Review Board (NVMO-ERB; reference number 2023.2.55). This study fell outside the scope of the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act. All procedures were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Setting and participants

The hospital pharmacy residency program in the Netherlands is a postgraduate educational program with nationally defined final attainment levels and learning objectives focused on entrustable professional activities, similar to medical residency programs [16].

Hospital pharmacy residency programs are offered in 37 hospital pharmacies in approved training institutions across 7 regions each linked to one of the seven academic medical centers in the Netherlands. The program lasts four years: one year of orientation on professional activities, 21 months of broadening and deepening pharmaceutical knowledge and skills, and 15 months of research and specialization. In each approved hospital pharmacy, one hospital pharmacist is responsible for the residency program. This pharmacist, referred to as supervisor in this study, is also responsible for the selection procedure of residents. In recent years, an increasing number of supervisors have chosen to organize their selection procedures jointly with some or all other teaching hospitals in their region. Recruitment and selection procedures differ across regions but generally start with a publicity advertised vacancy. In most regions selection follows a two-step process: candidates are first screened by a selection committee based on their motivation letter and curriculum vitae, after which selected candidates are invited to one or more interviews with the committee, during which the final selection is made. The committee usually consists of supervisors from the region and may include one or more senior residents or an HR officer.

In this study, all residents who started their residency between January 2018 and September 2023 were invited to complete the resident questionnaire. All supervisors were invited to complete another questionnaire. Members of our study team who were supervisors in their hospital (PvdL, NVJ, IW, RZ) were replaced by their formal substitutes to avoid potential bias.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were the proportion of residents with a Turkish, African, Latin American or Asian (TALA) background and gender, age and social background of residents, stratified by ethnic background. Secondary outcomes were the proportion of supervisors who considered gender and ethnic diversity, as well as the combination of diversity aspects, in their selection procedure and the median importance assigned to these criteria.

Data collection

Residents and supervisors were invited for participation by email by the Dutch Society of Hospital Pharmacy. The email contained a link to the detailed information about the study and the questionnaire in Castor Electronic Data Capture, a secure, web-based application for managing clinical research databases that complies with Good Clinical Practice [17]. After providing digital informed consent, participants could open and complete the questionnaire. To optimize the response rate, three email reminders were sent.

The questionnaire for residents was available from mid-October 2023 until mid-December 2023. Residents were asked about their gender (female, male, X, prefer not to say), age, year of starting their residency and the region of their residency. Age at the start of the residency was calculated by subtracting the difference between 2023 and the year of start from their current age. To define their ethnic background, residents were asked whether at least one of their parents was born in Turkey or in a country in Africa, Latin America or Asia. Residents who answered “yes” were classified as having a TALA background; those who answered “no”, were classified as having “other background”, which includes residents whose parents were born in the Netherlands. This classification, based on continents, aligns with the definitions introduced by Statistics Netherlands (CBS) in 2022 [18]. To prevent identification of individual residents, we did not ask for the specific country of birth of residents or their parents and did not distinguish between different countries of origin.

The proportion of young adults (aged 20 to 30 years) in the general population with a TALA background (non-western migration background plus Japanese and Indonesian background), estimated at 20% based on CBS data, was used as a descriptive benchmark for diversity among residents, given the importance of adequate societal representation [10]. This age group was chosen because most residents fall within this range.

Data on students with a Japanese background are lacking, due to the former CBS definition of non-Western migration background [19]. Therefore, the proportion of students with a Turkish/African/Latin American or Asian (excluding Japanese) background − 35%- was used to illustrate the transition from student to resident [9].

As indicator of social background, residents were asked whether at least one of their parents obtained a bachelor’s degree or higher and whether at least one of their parents was registered as healthcare professional (e.g., pharmacist or physician).

The supervisor questionnaire was open from early November 2023 to early January 2024, a period chosen because most regions finalize their selection procedure by November. The questionnaire included general questions about the residency program and whether selection was organized locally or regionally. Supervisors were asked whether diversity within their team of residents and hospital pharmacists, regarding gender and ethnic background, was taken into account during the selection procedure and how important these criteria were considered (1 = unimportant, 2 = somewhat important, 3 = fairly important, 4 = important, 5 = very important). In addition, they were asked whether diversity in previous experience and interests of candidates, combined with gender and ethnicity, was incorporated as a selection criterion.

None of the questions were mandatory. At the end of the questionnaire both residents and supervisors were asked to answer the open question “What do you want to share with us on the topic of diversity?”.

To guarantee pseudonymity of residents and supervisors regions were coded in the results.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 28.0). Descriptive statistics were used to examine associations between resident characteristics, using χ2 tests with a significance level of p < 0.05. When the expected count for a cell was below 5, the Fisher-Freeman-Halton Exact Test was applied. For age at the start of training, treated as a continuous variable, the Mann-Whitney U test was used.

For statistically significant associations (p < 0.05), effect sizes (φ) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. An absolute value of φ of 0.10–0.29 was considered a small effect size, 0.30–0.49 a medium effect size and equal to or greater than 0.5 a large effect size.

Results

Demographics, ethnic and social background of residents

A total of 131 of the 169 invited residents (78%) were included in the analysis. This number reflects the exclusion of one resident who did not provide informed consent; one had started the program before 2018 and therefore did not meet the inclusion criteria, and two residents consented but did not complete any of the questions. Thirty-four residents (20%) did not respond to the questionnaire.

Age at start of the residency ranged from 24 to 58 years with a median of 28 years (Fig. 1). Distribution of gender, age at start of residency and social background, stratified by ethnic background, is presented in Table 1. Of the respondents 18% had a TALA background; which is comparable to the benchmark of 20% among young adults in the general population [10]. Although a direct comparison with pharmacy students cannot be made, the proportion of residents with a TALA background is lower than the proportion of students with a TALA (excluding Japanese) background (35%) [9].

Response of supervisors and general characteristics of the selection procedure

All 37 supervisors were invited; 32 supervisors responded, gave informed consent and were included, resulting in a response rate of 87%. Most supervisors (87%) selected their residents through a joint regional selection procedure. A median of six hospitals (range 2–7) participated in these joint procedures, which had been in place for several years (median 6 years, range 2–25 years, n = 27). Active involvement of a human resources department was mentioned by 13% of the supervisors.

Diversity criteria used during selection of residents

During the selection process, 73% of supervisors reported taking gender diversity within their team into account. Ethnic diversity was considered by 69% of supervisors. Both criteria were rated as fairly important (median 3, range 1–5). Diversity in the combination of previous experience, interests, gender and ethnicity was mentioned as a selection criterion by 90% of supervisors and was regarded as important (median 4, range 1–5).

Comments of residents and supervisors on diversity

In their answer to the open question 10% of residents and 50% of supervisors shared a comment on the topic of diversity, as presented in Tables 3 and 4. Both residents and supervisors acknowledge the importance of diversity (for example R5, S1, S8, S14, S15), whereas others indicated that they found the topic difficult or associated it with potential challenges (R1, R10, S13). One supervisor expressed the view that the attention given to diversity in selection procedures is exaggerated (S16). Both residents and supervisors commented on the selection procedure and selection criteria (R3, R6-R10, S4-S15). Selecting the best candidate and ensuring suitability for the role were mentioned by both groups, suggesting that candidate qualities should remain decisive in the selection process, regardless of other determinants (R7, S7, S9).

Discussion

We found that 18% of hospital pharmacy residents had a TALA background. This proportion aligns with the benchmark we defined, namely the proportion of younger adults with a TALA background in the Netherlands (20%) [10]. Our findings are also comparable with those of a recent cohort study in an academic medical center in the Netherlands, which reported that 19% of residents across all specialties had a migration background (Western or non-Western) [6].

Although exact numbers of students with a TALA background could not be retrieved, it is known that 35% of pharmacy students have a Turkish, African, Latin American or Asian (excluding Japanese) background [9]. Nevertheless, there appears to be a loss of diversity in the transition from graduated pharmacist to resident. Including students with a Japanese background -likely a small group- would slightly increase the proportion of students with a TALA background slightly and thereby further increase the gap between ethnic diversity of students and residents.

Because we used descriptive controls, statistical testing of differences between the population, students, and residents was not possible. Nevertheless, these findings provide direction and a starting point for further research.

The observed decline in diversity from pharmacy student to resident aligns with findings showing that medical students with a migration background have lower odds of becoming a medical specialist [7]. Given the overrepresentation of students with a TALA background compared with the general population, regression to the mean may play a role. However, because detailed data on career preferences of pharmacists, selection procedures, and applicant pools are lacking, further explanations for this decline remain speculative. Career preferences could contribute, but self-selection could also be relevant. Applicants may decide not to apply based on their perceived likelihood of success, which can be influenced by actual and perceived barriers, such as fear of not fitting in, discouragement from teachers, or lacking a professional network. Such mechanisms have been described in admission to higher educational programs using selection procedures, including pharmacy [15, 20]. This is supported by a small survey among members of the junior department of Dutch Association of Hospital Pharmacists (JongNVZA), residents and pharmacists working in a hospital, in which 13% of the respondents reported that their ethnic background (as perceived by others) might have negatively influenced their chance for admission to their recent job [21]. Future research should examine what drives junior pharmacists’ career choices and how these are associated with demographic and socioeconomic factors.

Unintentional barriers in the selection procedure may also contribute to the loss of diversity. Members of selection committees, may unintenionally create barriers for inclusive resident selection, even when they are supportive to diversity initiatives [22]. For example, the tendency to search for “the best” candidate -and to define merit without considering historical context or strucutral advantages-, may disproportionately favor already privileged applicants [22]. Because selecting the best candidate was also mentioned in the open comments by supervisors and a resident in our study, this mechanism may play a role in hospital pharmacy. In addition, prioritizing candidates who are perceived as a good “fit” with the resident group or hospital pharmacy team can hinder diversity by favoring similarity [22]. As we did not assess the ethnic diversity of the supervisors or hospital pharmacy teams, future studies should address this. To draw conclusions about equity in the selection process, studying the applicant pool and the selection process in detail is essential [15, 20, 21]. In our study, ethnic diversity of residents did not differ significantly between regions. However, ethnic diversity in Dutch society varies considerably by region, ranging from 7% of inhabitants with a non-Western migration background in the more rural North of the Netherlands to 29% in the urban Western part [23]. Possibly, our small sample size or regional differences in response rates may have limited our ability to detect regional differences.

In this study, age and gender of residents were not associated with ethnic origin. However, significantly fewer residents with a TALA background have a higher-educated parent or a parent registered as a healthcare professional. This aligns with findings that residents with a migration background are less likely to have a parent working as a physician [6]. This is an important observation, as lacking a higher-educated parent or a parent registered as a healthcare professional can lower the odds of becoming a specialist [7]. Besides, earlier in the pathway toward specialization, having a parent registered as a healthcare professional is also associated with selection into health professions education programs, including pharmacy [20]. Therefore, students without higher-educated parents or without parents working in healthcare may benefit from additional support -such as job application and interview training, career coaching or mentoring- during the transition from student to pharmacist. Although causality between selection procedures and residents’ social background cannot be inferred from our results, these findings highlight the importance of considering candidate’s historic context, social background and structural advantages during selection [13].

In our study, male residents were underrepresented. This is not surprising, as only about 40% of bachelor and master pharmacy students are male [9]. We found no clear loss of gender diversity in the transition from pharmacy student to resident.

Not all supervisors take diversity in gender and ethnic background within their team into account when selecting residents. However, most supervisors did consider diversity regarding previous experience, interests, gender and ethnicity as an important selection criterion. Open comments from both residents and supervisors also indicate that not everyone is engaged with the topic of diversity, and others are struggling with it, particularly in the context of selection procedures.

Because perceiving diversity as important does not necessarily mean that measures to improve it are implemented during selection, this should be addressed in future research. Moreover, current selection practices vary, and the implications of our findings for diversity need to be studied in more detail. The composition and diversity of selection committees should also be considered, as more diverse committees may contribute to more inclusive selection processes. Nevertheless, the perceived importance expressed by supervisors and the open comments suggest that awareness of the importance of a diverse workforce could be strengthened.

Limitations

Clearly, response bias cannot be excluded, despite the relatively high response rate among residents and supervisors. It is conceivable that residents with a TALA background or those without higher educated parents were more engaged with this topic and therefore more willing to participate than residents who are not personally affected. As a result, the proportions of residents with a TALA background or without a higher-educated parent may be overestimated and could be lower than reported.

Additionally, we acknowledge that defining ethnicity is a sensitive issue that depends on context and definitions vary between studies and settings, and over time. We chose to use a broad and inclusive classification based on the regional origin of residents’ parents, distinguishing only between residents with a TALA-background and all other backgrounds. For example, in the former CBS definition of a non-Western migration background, Japan and Indonesia were considered as Western countries, whereas we made no distinction between Japanese, Indonesian and other Asian backgrounds [19]. Therefore, comparisons with other studies should be interpreted with caution. In particular, CBS data on students are not directly comparable with our data. Moreover, diversity encompasses more than the distribution of age, gender, work experience, and specific interests; it also includes, for example, religion, sexual orientation, language, physical and mental abilities, personality traits and personal competencies. We focused on gender, age, ethnic and social background, because these dimensions are most described for other medical specialties.

The relatively small sample size could possibly lead to identification of individual residents (for example through the combination of region and ethnic background). Therefore, we chose not to examine residents’ own country of birth or the specific country of origin of their parents. Studying ethnic origin in more detail could be valuable because the odds of being selected for a residency may differ between specific different countries of origin [7].

Our results reflect the situation in the Netherlands. As residence selection procedures vary between medical specialties and across countries, caution is warranted when generalizing our findings to other settings. However, it is notable that across different medical specialties the ethnic background of residents differs from that of the general population. Hospital pharmacy in the Netherlands seems an exception, but the decline in diversity during the transition from student to resident needs attention [24–27]. Our findings may serve as a starting point for investigating and improving diversity among hospital pharmacists internationally.

Strengths

To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study specifically examining diversity among hospital pharmacy residents. Our response rate was relatively high, and including supervisors’ perspectives on selection further strengthened our study.

Conclusions

The current ethnic diversity of hospital pharmacy residents reflects Dutch society, but there appears to be a loss of ethnic diversity in the transition from student to resident. Causes for this decline need to be addressed in future research, including career choices and details of selection procedures.
