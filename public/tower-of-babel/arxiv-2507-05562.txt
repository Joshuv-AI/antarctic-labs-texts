Exact and efficient basis pursuit denoising via differential inclusions and a selection principle
Gabriel P. Langlois, Jérôme Darbon

Abstract
Basis pursuit denoising (BPDN) is a cornerstone of compressive sensing, statistics and machine learning. While various algorithms for BPDN have been proposed, they invariably suffer from drawbacks and must either favor efficiency at the expense of accuracy or vice versa. As such, state-of-the-art algorithms remain ineffective for high-dimensional applications that require accurate solutions within a reasonable amount of computational time. In this work, we address this issue and propose an exact and efficient algorithm for BPDN using differential inclusions. Specifically, we prove that a selection principle from the theory of differential inclusions turns the dual problem of BPDN into calculating the trajectory of an integrable projected dynamical system, that is, whose trajectory and asymptotic limit can be computed exactly. Our analysis naturally yields an exact algorithm, numerically up to machine precision, that is amenable to computing regularization paths and very fast. Numerical experiments confirm that our algorithm outperforms the state-of-the-art algorithms in both accuracy and efficiency. Moreover, we show that the global continuation of solutions (in terms of the hyperparameter and data) of the projected dynamical system yields a rigorous homotopy algorithm for BPDN, as well as a novel greedy algorithm for computing feasible solutions to basis pursuit in strongly polynomial time. Beyond this work, we expect that our results and analysis can be adapted to compute exact or approximate solutions to a broader class of polyhedral-constrained optimization problems.

Contributions of this paper:

(i) In this work, we prove that a selection principle from the theory of differential inclusions turns the dual problem of BPDN into calculating the trajectory of an
integrable
projected dynamical system, that is, whose trajectory and asymptotic limit can be computed exactly. Our results and extensive analysis culminate in Algorithm 1, an active set algorithm that computes the optimal dual solution to BPDN and recovers an optimal primal solution exactly. As a by-product, our results provide a novel solution method for solving a broad class of projected dynamical systems, which should find relevance to applications outside BPDN.

(ii) We present a detailed perturbation analysis of solutions to BPDN (in terms of the hyperparameter and data) via a global continuation argument of solutions of the projected dynamical system characterizing BPDN. (iii) Our numerical experiments show that Algorithm 1 vastly outperforms the state of the arts in accuracy while also achieving the best overall performance, highlighting a key feature of Algorithm 1: it neither compromises accuracy nor computational efficiency.

\frac{d\boldsymbol{p}}{d\tau}(\tau)\in-\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau);t,\boldsymbol{b}),\qquad\boldsymbol{p}(0)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b}).

3
The minimal selection principle

The minimal selection principle stipulates that solutions to gradient inclusions exist, are unique, and evolve in the steepest descent direction
[
3
, Chapter 3]
. As the minimal selection principle is central to this work, we state it below in full:

Theorem 3.1

(Existence and uniqueness of solutions to differential inclusions with maximal monotone mappings)
.

Let
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, lower semicontinuous, and convex function and let
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial g
. Consider the differential inclusions

\frac{d\boldsymbol{p}}{d\tau}(\tau)\in-\partial g(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)),\qquad\boldsymbol{p}(0)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}.

(10)

Then, there exists a unique solution
\boldsymbol{p}\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathrm{dom~}\partial g
satisfying (
10
). Moreover:

(i)

For every
\tau\geqslant 0
, we have
\frac{d\boldsymbol{p}}{d\tau}(\tau)=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\to 0\\
h>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\boldsymbol{p}(\tau+h)-\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)}{h}
. Moreover,
\tau\mapsto\frac{d\boldsymbol{p}}{d\tau}(\tau)
is right-continuous and
\tau\mapsto\left\|{\frac{d\boldsymbol{p}}{d\tau}(\tau)}\right\|_{2}
is nonincreasing.

(ii)

(The minimal selection principle.) The function
\boldsymbol{p}(\cdot)
satisfies the initial value problem

\frac{d\boldsymbol{p}}{d\tau}(\tau)=-\mathrm{proj}_{\partial g(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau))}(\boldsymbol{0}),\qquad\boldsymbol{p}(0)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}

(11)

at
\tau=0
and almost everywhere on
(0,+\infty)
.

(iii)

(Asymptotic limit.) If
g
achieves its minimum at some point, then
\boldsymbol{p}(\cdot)
converges to such a point:

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}g(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau))=\min_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}g(\boldsymbol{p})\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}g(\boldsymbol{p}).

Proof.

See
[

\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
indexed by
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p})
. With the notation set, we have the following:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
t\geqslant 0
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
. The direction of change
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
of the slow system (
12
) is the unique solution to the cone projection problem

\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}+(\boldsymbol{b}+t\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}\right\|_{2}^{2}\ \text{subject to}\ \bm{D}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}\bm{A}^{\top}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}\boldsymbol{d}\geqslant\boldsymbol{0}.

(14)

It admits the characterization

\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\bm{A}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})-(\boldsymbol{b}+t\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),

(15)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
is a solution to the NNLS problem

\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\|{\bm{A}\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\boldsymbol{u}-(\boldsymbol{b}+t\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}\right\|_{2}^{2}\ \text{subject to}\ \begin{cases}&\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}\geqslant\boldsymbol{0},\\
&\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}=\boldsymbol{0}.\end{cases}

(16)

Moreover:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})[\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})]_{j}=0\ \text{for every }j\in\{1,\dots,n\},

(17a)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\langle\boldsymbol{b}+t\boldsymbol{p}_{0},\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle=0.

(17b)

Proof.

For every
t\geqslant 0
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
, the subdifferential
\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
is non-empty, closed and convex. Hence the projection of
\boldsymbol{0}
onto
\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
exists and is unique (see Definition
A.17
). The projection is given precisely the NNLS problem (
16
) or equivalently by its dual problem, the cone projection problem (
14
).

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{A}\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{A}\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})-\boldsymbol{b}-t\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\boldsymbol{b}+t\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\langle\boldsymbol{b}+t\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle,

where the last line follows by (
15
). Finally, combine (
15
) with (
17a
) to deduce (
17b
).
∎

[

]
(see also
[

,

]

3.3
The minimal selection yields a descent direction

Lemma
3.1
shows that the minimal selection
-\mathrm{proj}_{\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{0})
, equivalently the instantaneous direction
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau);t,\boldsymbol{b})
of the slow system (
12
), is calculated from a cone projection problem. The next proposition shows that this characterization implies
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau);t,\boldsymbol{b})
is a
descent direction
for
V
and, crucially, obeys an
evolution rule
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
t\geqslant 0
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
. Then:

(i)

There exists
\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})>0
such that
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\in\mathrm{dom~}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
for every
\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})]
, where

\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\min_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{sgn}{\left\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle}-\left\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{0},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle)}{\left\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle}\right\}.

(18)

Moreover,
\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=+\infty\iff\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{0}\iff\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{0}
.

(ii)

(Descent direction) For every
\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})]
, we have

V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b});t,\boldsymbol{b})-V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\Delta\left(t\Delta/2-1\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(19)

In particular, if
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, then it is a descent direction of
V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
.

(iii)

For every
\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})]
, we have the inclusion

-(1-t\Delta)\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\in\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b});t,\boldsymbol{b}).

(iv)

For every
\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))
, we have the inclusions

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))\subset\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})

and

\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b});t,\boldsymbol{b})\subset\{t\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\}+\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}).

(v)

(Evolution rule) For every
\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))
, the following evolution rule holds:

\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b});t,\boldsymbol{b})=(1-t\Delta)\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}).

(20)

Proof.

See Appendix
B.1
for the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3

(Drift to infinity)
.

Without the full row rank assumption
\mathrm{rank}({\bm{A}})=m
, there may be some
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
for which
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\neq\boldsymbol{0}
with
\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{0}
. In this case,
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
for every
\Delta\in[0,+\infty)
, hence
\lim_{\Delta\to+\infty}V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))=+\infty
by (
18
). This will have implications for the calculation of the trajectory of the slow system (
12
) when
\mathrm{rank}({\bm{A}})\neq m
.

4
Dynamics of the slow system

4.1
Explicit local solution

The evolution rule (
20
) describes how the descent direction
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
evolves
locally
in
\Delta>0
along the line
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
. This evolution is local because it holds on some possibly finite interval
\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))
, with
\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
given by (
18
). This behavior, a consequence of the minimal selection principle, fundamentally characterizes the dynamics of the slow system (
12
). It also appears as the main obstacle for computing the slow solution.

Here, we show that this behavior is no obstacle as it appears: Locally, the slow system evolves as that of its non-projected counterpart, in the sense that

\frac{d\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)}{d\tau}=-\mathrm{proj}_{\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau);t,\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{0})=-t(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)-\boldsymbol{p}_{0})+\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\ \text{ for small enough times $\tau>0$.}

Its local solution can therefore be computed explicitly, as the following Theorem makes precise.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
t\geqslant 0
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
. The slow system (
12
) with initial value
\boldsymbol{p}(0)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}
coincides with the initial value problem

\frac{d\boldsymbol{p}}{d\tau}(\tau)=e^{-t\tau}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\quad\boldsymbol{p}(0)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}

(21)

on
\tau\in[0,\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))
, where

\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\begin{cases}\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};0,\boldsymbol{b})&\text{if}\ t=0,\\
-\ln\left(1-t\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\right)/t&\text{if}\ t>0\ \text{and}\ 1-t\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})>0,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(22)

In particular, the slow solution is given explicitly on
[0,\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))
by

\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+f(\tau,t)\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),

(23)

where

f(\tau,t)=\begin{cases}\tau&\text{if}\ t=0,\\
\left(1-e^{-t\tau}\right)/t&\text{if}\ t>0.\end{cases}

(24)

Proof.

We will use the evolution rule (
20
) to show that the affine system

\frac{d\boldsymbol{p}_{a}}{d\tau}(\tau)=-t(\boldsymbol{p}_{a}(\tau)-\boldsymbol{p}_{0})+\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\qquad\boldsymbol{p}_{a}(0)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}.

(25)

satisfies the slow system (
12
) on
[0,\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))
and conclude using uniqueness.

First, consider the affine system (
25
). A short calculation shows that its unique, global solution is given by

\boldsymbol{p}_{a}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+f(\tau,t)\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}).

Substitute the solution in (
25
) above to find

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{p}_{a}}{d\tau}(\tau)

\displaystyle=(1-tf(\tau,t))\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})

(26)

\displaystyle=e^{-t\tau}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}).

Next, we invoke the evolution rule (
20
) in Proposition
3.1
(v) with
\Delta=f(\tau,t)
to find

-\mathrm{proj}_{\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+f(\tau,t)\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),t)}(\boldsymbol{0})=(1-tf(\tau,t))\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})

(27)

whenever
0\leqslant f(\tau,t)<\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
. Notice
\tau\mapsto f(\tau,t)
increases monotonically from
0
to
1/t
(0 to
+\infty
) when
t>0
(
t=0
). Hence if
t=0
, the largest value of
\tau
for which (
27
) holds is
\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};0,\boldsymbol{b})
. If
t>0
and
1-t\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})>0
, then
(1-e^{-t\tau})/t
is equal to
\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
at
-\ln\left(1-t\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\right)/t
. If
t>0
and
1-t\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))\leqslant 0
, then (
27
) holds for every
\tau\geqslant 0
. Taken together, we find

\frac{d\boldsymbol{p}_{a}}{d\tau}(\tau)=-\mathrm{proj}_{\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+f(\tau,t)\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),t)}(\boldsymbol{0})\ \text{for every $\tau\in[0,\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))$}.

Uniqueness follows from Theorem
3.1
, and hence (
21
) follows from (
26
).
∎

4.2
Continuation of the local solution

Theorem
4.1
provides the explicit local solution to the slow system (
12
) on the interval
[0,\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))
. When
\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})<+\infty
, what can we say about the slow solution and its time derivative at
\tau=\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
? The next result shows that the slow solution is continuous at
\tau=\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
but its time derivative is not.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
t\geqslant 0
,
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
, and suppose
\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})<+\infty
. Then (
23
) holds at
\tau=\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
but (
21
) does not:

\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}));t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}<e^{-t\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}.

(28)

Proof.

See Appendix
B.2
for the proof. ∎

4.3
Explicit global solution

So far, we have computed the explicit local solution of the slow system (
12
) on an interval
[0,\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))
which, when
\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
is finite, can be extended to
\tau=\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
. We can apply Theorem
4.1
and Lemma
4.1
again with the initial condition
\boldsymbol{p}(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))
to extend the local solution to
[0,\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}));t,\boldsymbol{b})]
. This is because
\boldsymbol{p}(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
and all assumptions of Theorem
4.1
hold.

We now apply this argument repeatedly to compute explicitly the global solution of the slow system. Let
\boldsymbol{p}^{(0)}\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
,
\tau^{(0)}=0
, and starting from
k=1
let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}^{(k-1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)};t,\boldsymbol{b}),

(29)

\displaystyle\Delta^{(k-1)}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)};t,\boldsymbol{b}),

\displaystyle\tau^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)};t,\boldsymbol{b}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)}+\begin{cases}\min\left(\Delta^{(k-1)},1/t\right)\boldsymbol{d}^{(k-1)}&\text{if}\ t>0\\
\Delta^{(k-1)}\boldsymbol{d}^{(k-1)}&\text{if $t=0$ and $\Delta^{(k-1)}<+\infty$},\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The slow solution is given piecewise on the intervals
[\tau^{(k-1)},\tau^{(k)})
:

\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)}+f(\tau-\tau^{(k-1)},t)\boldsymbol{d}^{(k-1)}\ \text{over}\ \tau\in[\tau^{(k-1)},\tau^{(k)}).

(30)

This gives the explicit solution of the slow system over
[0,\tau^{(k)})
up to any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that if
t>0
, then the minimum in (
29
) is attained at
1/t\iff\tau^{(K)}=+\infty
for some
K\in\mathbb{N}
.

What can be said about the asymptotic limit
k\to+\infty
? The following Theorem asserts the limit converges in
finite time
, in the sense that there exists some nonnegative integer
K
such that
\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{p}^{(K)}
over the interval
[\tau^{(K)},+\infty)
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
t\geqslant 0
and
\boldsymbol{p}^{(0)}\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
. Consider the slow system (
12
) with initial condition
\boldsymbol{p}(0)=\boldsymbol{p}^{(0)}
, whose solution is given by (
30
). Then there exists a nonnegative integer
K
such that
\boldsymbol{d}^{(K)}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{p}^{(K)}
on
\tau\in[\tau^{(K)},+\infty)
.

Proof.

We will show there exists a nonnegative integer
K
for which
\boldsymbol{d}^{(K)}=\boldsymbol{0}
. This suffices because the projection onto the closed, convex set
\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
is unique (see
A.17
), and so the subgradients are different if and only if their norms are different.

Let
k
be a positive integer and suppose
\tau^{(j)}<+\infty
for every
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
in (
29
). On the one hand, Lemma
4.1
implies
\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}^{(j)}}\right\|_{2}<\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}^{(j-1)}}\right\|_{2}
. Hence the descent directions
\{\boldsymbol{d}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{d}^{(k)}\}
are obtained from projections landing all on different faces of the convex cone
\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
. On the other hand, since
\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
has at most
4^{n}
faces and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathrm{Im}({\bm{A}})
, there is at least one face on which the projection is zero. Let
K
denote the smallest such integer in the construction (
30
). Then
\boldsymbol{d}^{(K)}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{p}^{(K)}
on
[\tau^{(K)},+\infty)
.
∎

Remark 4.1

(Drift to infinity, again)
.

Without the full row rank assumption
\mathrm{rank}({\bm{A}})=m
(more generally,
\boldsymbol{b}\in\text{Im}(\bm{A})
), there may be some
K\in\mathbb{N}
for which
\boldsymbol{d}^{(K-1)}\neq 0
with
\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}^{(K-1)}=\boldsymbol{0}
, so that
\Delta^{(K-1)}=+\infty
and
\tau^{(K)}=+\infty
. This does not cause an issue when
t>0
since
\boldsymbol{p}^{(K)}
remains finite. However, when
t=0
, we have
\lim_{\tau\to+\infty}\left\|{\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)}\right\|_{2}=+\infty
, which means the trajectory of the slow system diverges and there are no feasible solutions to the corresponding (
BP
) and (
dBP
) problems.

4.4
Recovery of solutions to basis pursuit denoising

The analysis in Sections
4.1
–
4.3
yields the global solution to the slow system (
12
). Crucially, its asymptotic limit can be computed explicitly from (
30
) because it converges in finite time, meaning
\lim_{\tau\to+\infty}\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{p}^{(K)}
for some nonnegative integer
K
. This recovers a pair of primal and dual solutions
(\boldsymbol{x}^{s}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t,\boldsymbol{b}))
to BPDN. Indeed, the minimal selection princple implies
\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{p}^{(K)}
, while a primal solution follows from the optimality conditions (
6
) and Lemma
3.1
: Letting
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(K)}
denote an optimal solution to the NNLS problem (
16
), then

-t\boldsymbol{p}^{(K)}=\boldsymbol{b}-\bm{A}\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(K)})\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(K)}\implies\boldsymbol{x}^{s}(t,\boldsymbol{b})=\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(K)})\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(K)}.

4.5
An active set algorithm for basis pursuit denoising

Taken together, our results yield Algorithm 1, presented in the introduction of this work. It computes the trajectory of slow system (
12
) until the asymptotic limit is reached in finite time. Thanks to (
29
) and (
30
), the trajectory is integrated
exactly
because the descent direction and timestep can be computed using, e.g., active set NNLS or cone projection algorithms as per Remark
3.2
in Section
3.2
.

Algorithm 1 requires some input
\boldsymbol{p}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\left\|{\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}^{(0)}}\right\|_{\infty}=1
to serve as the initial condition of the slow system (
12
). For this, one can take
\boldsymbol{p}^{(0)}=-\boldsymbol{b}/\left\|{\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}}\right\|_{\infty}
. In addition, Algorithm 1 is naturally suited for computing regularization paths. To do so, one selects a decreasing sequence of nonnegative hyperparameters
\{t^{(l)}\}_{l=0}^{L}
and sequentially computes the dual solutions
\{\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t^{(l)},\boldsymbol{b})\}_{l=0}^{L}
, using
\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t^{(l)},\boldsymbol{b})
as the input in Algorithm 1 for computing
\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t^{(l+1)},\boldsymbol{b})
. Again, one can start with
t^{(0)}=\left\|{\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}}\right\|_{\infty}
and
\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t^{(0)},\boldsymbol{b})=-\boldsymbol{b}/t^{(0)}
.

5
Perturbation analysis of the slow system

Sections
3
–
4
describes how BPDN is characterized from the dynamics of the slow system (
12
) and its slow solution (
30
). In particular, the analysis yields an exact algorithm that computes a pair of primal and dual solutions to BPDN for
fixed
hyperparameter
t
and data
\boldsymbol{b}
. What happens, however, to the dynamics of the slow system (
12
) when the hyperparameter or data or both are perturbed? This matter in practice if, for example, one seeks to assess robustness of solutions with respect to the hyperparameter and data
[
21
,
46
,
28
]
. Another example is if the desired hyperparameter is unknown. In this case, one typically compute a regularization path or the entire solution path
t\mapsto(\boldsymbol{x}^{s}(t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t,\boldsymbol{b}))
and then select the desired hyperparameter, e.g., based on the sparsity of the solution or data-driven methods
[
30
,
50
,
25
,
22
,
11
]
. In any case, the perturbation analysis of BPDN is crucial to tackle these questions. In the context of minimal selection principle, this amounts to analyzing the dependence on parameters of the slow system and its slow solution.

Motivated by these matters, we characterize here how the dynamics of the slow system and its slow solution behave under perturbations on the hyperparameter
t
and data
\boldsymbol{b}
. First, we present in Section
5.1
how the descent direction of the slow system behaves under perturbations. We then use this in conjunction with the minimal selection principle in Section
5.2
to describe the local dependence on the parameters
t
and
\boldsymbol{b}
of the slow solution and, therefore, the primal and dual solutions to BPDN. As we argue in Section
5.3
, this local dependence leads to a possibly non-unique local continuation of the primal solution to BPDN. This non-uniqueness issue, a well-known problem in the literature
[
63
]
, arises from the non-uniqueness of solutions to an NNLS problem, as also previously reported by
[
11
]
. Finally, we present in Section
5.4
the global dependence on parameters of the slow solution, naturally yielding a rigorous homotopy algorithm, similar to the one proposed in
[
11
]
but based firmly on the minimal selection principle, for computing the solution path to BPDN.

5.1
Perturbations of the descent direction

We first describe how the descent direction
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t_{0},\boldsymbol{b})
changes under perturbations in the hyperparameter and data. This will be used in Section
5.2
to characterize the local dependence on parameters of the slow solution (
30
).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
t_{0}\geqslant 0
, let

6
Greedy approach for feasibility of basis pursuit

Sections

t
and data
\boldsymbol{b}

to provide a characterization of the global dependence of the asymptotic limit of the slow system on the hyperparameter

\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{w}^{\text{min}}_{\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b}))}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b})&=\min(\boldsymbol{0},-\bm{D}_{\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b}))}\boldsymbol{x}^{s}_{\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b}))}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b})-\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\text{LSQ}}_{\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b}))}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b})),\\
\boldsymbol{w}^{\text{min}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b})}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b})&=-\bm{D}_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b}))}\boldsymbol{x}^{s}_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b}))}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{b}).\end{cases}

In this case, the solution

6.3
A greedy algorithm for computing feasible solutions

We now combine Propositions
5.1
and
6.1
to obtain Algorithm 3. This greedy algorithm computes a pair of feasible primal and dual solutions to (
BP
) and (
dBP
) in strongly polynomial time, as Theorem
6.1
below demonstrates.

Input :
A matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and a vector
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathrm{Im}({\bm{A}})\setminus{\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}
.

Output :
A pair of vectors
(\boldsymbol{x}_{f},\boldsymbol{p}_{f})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
where
\boldsymbol{x}_{f}
has at most
m
nonzero components,
\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{f}=\boldsymbol{b}
, and
\left\|{-\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{f}}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
.

Set
\boldsymbol{b}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{b}
,
t^{(0)}=\left\|{\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}^{(0)}}\right\|_{\infty}
,
\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
\boldsymbol{p}^{(0)}=-\boldsymbol{b}^{(0)}/t^{(0)}
, and
\mathcal{M}^{(-1)}=\{1,\dots,n\}
;

1

for

k=1
until convergence

do

2

Compute
\mathcal{E}^{(k-1)}=\{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}:|\langle-\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle|=1\}
;

3

Compute
\mathcal{M}^{(k-1)}\in\sup_{|\mathcal{M}|}\left\{\text{$\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})\cap\mathcal{M}^{(k-2)}$ : s.t. $\{\bm{A}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{M}}$ are linearly independent}\right\}
;

4

Compute
\bm{D}^{(k-1)}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathrm{sgn}{(-\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)}})\right)
;

5

Compute
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\text{LSQ}}(t^{(k-1)},\boldsymbol{b}^{(k-1)})
using Equation (
39
);

6

Compute
\boldsymbol{w}^{(k-1)}=\boldsymbol{w}^{\text{min}}(t^{(k-1)},\boldsymbol{b}^{(k-1)})
using Equation (
46
);

7

Compute
\boldsymbol{q}^{(k-1)}=-\bm{A}\bm{D}^{(k-1)}\boldsymbol{w}^{(k-1)}/t^{(k-1)}
;

8

Compute
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{(k-1)}=\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\text{LSQ}}(t^{(k-1)},\boldsymbol{b}^{(k-1)})+\boldsymbol{w}^{(k-1)}
;

9

Compute
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k-1)}=t^{(k-1)}\left(\bm{A}_{\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}\bm{D}^{(k-1)}_{\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\text{LSQ}}(t^{(k-1)},\boldsymbol{b}^{(k-1)})+\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)}\right)
;

10

Compute
C^{(k-1)}=\min_{j\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}{(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{sgn}\left(\langle\bm{D}^{(k-1)}\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k-1)},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle\right)-\langle\bm{D}^{(k-1)}\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle)}{\langle\bm{D}^{(k-1)}\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k-1)},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle}\right\}
;

11

Update
t^{(k)}=t^{(k-1)}/(1+t^{(k-1)}C^{(k-1)})
;

12

Update
\boldsymbol{b}^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{b}^{(k-1)}+\left(1-\frac{t^{(k)}}{t^{(k-1)}}\right)\bm{A}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}\bm{D}^{(k-1)}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}\boldsymbol{w}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}^{(k-1)}
;

13

Update
\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{x}^{(k-1)}+\left(1-\frac{t^{(k)}}{t^{(k-1)}}\right)\bm{D}^{(k-1)}\boldsymbol{v}^{(k-1)}
;

14

if

t^{(k)}=0

then

15

Compute
\boldsymbol{x}_{f}=\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}-\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\left(1-t^{(k)}/t^{(k-1)}\right)\bm{D}^{(k-1)}\boldsymbol{w}^{(k-1)}
;

16

Compute
\boldsymbol{p}_{f}=\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)}
;

17

break
//
The algorithm has converged
;

18

else

19

Update
\boldsymbol{p}^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)}+(1/t^{(k)}-1/t^{(k-1)})\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k-1)}
;

20

end for

21

Algorithm 3

Greedy algorithm for computing a pair of feasible primal/dual solutions to the basis pursuit problem.

Theorem 6.1
.

After initialization, Algorithm 3 converges in at most
m
iterations. That is, there exists an integer
K\leqslant m
such that
t^{(K)}=0
,
\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{(K)}=\boldsymbol{b}^{(K)}
and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{(K)}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{1}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}^{(K)},

(48)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}^{(K)}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\,\langle\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{b}^{(K)}\rangle\quad\text{subject to}\quad\left\|{-\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1,

where

\boldsymbol{b}^{(K)}=\boldsymbol{b}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(1-t^{(k)}/t^{(k-1)}\right)\bm{A}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}\bm{D}^{(k-1)}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}\boldsymbol{w}^{(k-1)}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}.

(49)

Moreover, the vectors
(\boldsymbol{x}_{f},\boldsymbol{p}_{f})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
computed on lines 16-17, namely

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{f}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{(K)}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(1-t^{(k)}/t^{(k-1)}\right)\bm{D}^{(k-1)}\boldsymbol{w}^{(k-1)}\ \text{and}\ \boldsymbol{p}_{f}=\boldsymbol{p}^{(K-1)},

(50)

are a pair of feasible primal and dual solutions to (
BP
) and (
dBP
).

Proof.

Line 1 initializes the primal and dual solutions at hyperparameter
t^{(0)}=||\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}^{(0)}||_{\infty}
, the smallest hyperparameter for which
(\boldsymbol{x}^{s}(t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{p}^{s}(t,\boldsymbol{b}^{(0)})=(\boldsymbol{0},-\boldsymbol{b}^{(0)}/t)
on
t\in[t_{0},+\infty)
. The
\emph{for}
loop on lines 2-20 then sequentially applies Propositions
5.1
and
6.1
repeatedly and correctly. We show below that the algorithm converges in at most
m
iterations by positing the following loop invariant: at iteration
k
, the set
\mathcal{M}^{(k-1)}
contains at least
k
indices.

At initialization
k=1
, the equicorrelation set
\mathcal{E}^{(0)}
is non-empty because
\text{rank}(\bm{A})=m
by assumption, and so the set
\mathcal{M}^{(0)}
is non-empty as well, with
|\mathcal{M}^{(0)}|\geqslant 1
. Suppose now that the loop invariant holds at
k\geqslant 1
. We may assume
\boldsymbol{p}^{(k)}\neq\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)}
, for otherwise
t^{(k)}=0
and we are done. Thus at the next iteration
k+1
we compute the equicorrelation set
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}
and find, through Proposition
6.1
(ii) and (iii), that
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}
adds at least one index corresponding to a column vector of
\bm{A}
linearly independent from
\{\bm{A}\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}
. Hence the subset
\mathcal{M}^{(k)}
contains one new index, and so
|\mathcal{M}^{(k)}|\geqslant|\mathcal{M}^{(k-1)}|+1\geqslant k+1
.

We claim there is
K\leqslant m
such that
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{(K-1)}
solves the NNLS problem (
35
) exactly, yielding
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(K-1)}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
C^{(K-1)}=+\infty
. Indeed, the loop invariant guarantees
|\mathcal{M}^{(k)}|\geqslant k+1
and
\mathrm{rank}({\bm{A}})=m
; if
K=m
, then
|\mathcal{M}^{(K-1)}|=m
and the matrix
\bm{A}_{\mathcal{M}^{(K-1)}}
is invertible, yielding

\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\text{LSQ}}_{\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(K-1)})}(t^{(K-1)},\boldsymbol{b}^{(K-1)})=-t^{(K-1)}\bm{D}^{(K-1)}_{\mathcal{M}^{(K-1)}}\bm{A}^{-1}_{\mathcal{M}^{(K-1)}}\boldsymbol{p}^{(K-1)}

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(K-1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}\bm{D}^{(K-1)}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\text{LSQ}}_{\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(K-1)})}(t^{(K-1)},\boldsymbol{b}^{(K-1)})+t^{(K-1)}\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}_{\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(K-1)})}\bm{D}^{(K-1)}_{\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(K-1)})}\left(-t^{(K-1)}\bm{D}^{(K-1)}_{\mathcal{M}^{(K-1)}}\bm{A}^{-1}_{\mathcal{M}^{(K-1)}}\boldsymbol{p}^{(K-1)}\right)+t^{(K-1)}\boldsymbol{p}^{(K-1)}

\displaystyle=-t^{(K-1)}\boldsymbol{p}^{(K-1)}+t^{(K-1)}\boldsymbol{p}^{(K-1)}=\boldsymbol{0}.

The subsequent iterations terminate because
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(K-1)}=\boldsymbol{0}\implies C^{(K-1)}=+\infty\implies t^{(K)}=0
.

After termination, the vectors
(\boldsymbol{x}^{(K)},\boldsymbol{p}^{(K)})
constitutes a pair of primal and dual solutions to the (modified) basis pursuit problem (
48
). Formula (
49
) follows from line 13 and carrying out the sum until
\boldsymbol{b}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{b}
. Combining (
49
) with
\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{(K)}=\boldsymbol{b}^{(K)}
gives

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{(K)}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(1-t^{(k)}/t^{(k-1)}\right)\bm{A}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}\bm{D}^{(k-1)}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}\boldsymbol{w}^{(k-1)}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k-1)})}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(K)}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(1-t^{(k)}/t^{(k-1)}\right)\bm{D}^{(k-1)}\boldsymbol{w}^{(k-1)}\right)=\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{f}.

It follows that
(\boldsymbol{x}_{f},\boldsymbol{p}_{f})
is a pair of feasible primal and dual solutions to (
BP
) and (
dBP
).
∎

Remark 6.1
.

A similar reasoning shows Algorithm 3 works when relaxing the assumption to
\boldsymbol{b}\in\text{Im}(\bm{A})
. In this case, the algorithm terminates in at most
\mathrm{rank}({\bm{A}})
iterations.

Remark 6.2

(Complexity of the algorithm)
.

Each line in Algorithm 3 can be computed in strongly polynomial time, including, crucially, the calculation of
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\text{LSQ}}(t^{(k-1)},\boldsymbol{b}^{(k-1)})
and
\boldsymbol{w}^{(k-1)}
. Since the iterations converge in at most
m
steps, Algorithm 3 produces a pair of feasible primal and dual solutions to (
BP
) and (
dBP
) in strongly polynomial time.

Conclusion and future work

In this work, we proved that a minimal selection principle from the theory of differential inclusions (Theorem
3.1
) enables one to compute an optimal solution of (
dBPDN
) from the asymptotic limit of the slow system (
12
). As the results in Section
3
and
4
show, the slow system can be integrated, yielding the slow solution (
30
). The slow solution converges in finite time and, at convergence, yields the optimal solution to (
dBPDN
). From it, one can recover an optimal solution to (
BPDN
). Taken together, these results yielded Algorithm 1. We also presented, in Section
5
, a detailed perturbation analysis of the slow system, including its local and global dependence on the hyperparameter and data. The global continuation of the slow solution provided a rigorous, generalized homotopy algorithm for BPDN, as well as Algorithm 3, a greedy algorithm for computing feasible solutions to basis pursuit in strongly polynomial time. Our numerical experiments showed that Algorithm 1 vastly outperforms the state of the arts in accuracy while also achieving the best overall performance, highlighting its key feature that it neither compromises accuracy nor computational efficiency.

While this work focused largely on BPDN, our results provided a novel solution method for solving a broad class of projected dynamical systems. We therefore expect that this method will be relevant to applications involving variational inequalities and projected dynamical systems. In addition, we expect that our results can be adapted to compute exact or approximate solutions to a broader class of convex polyhedral-constrained optimization problems. This includes: (i) variations of BPDN where the
\ell_{1}
norm is replaced by a polyhedral norm or the function
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\left\|{\bm{M}\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{1}
for some appropriate real matrix
\bm{M}
, (ii) other
\ell_{1}
-regularized problems such as logistic regression, Poisson regression, support vector machines and boosting problems, and (iii) extensions to inequality constraints. In particular, as basis pursuit and linear programming are theoretically equivalent problems
[
64
]
, the results of this work suggest it may be possible to devise algorithms for linear programming based on differential inclusions. These problems will be investigated in future work.

Definitions

Definition A.1

(Convex sets)
.

A subset
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex if for every pair
(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\in C\times C
and every scalar
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the point
\lambda\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
is contained in
C
.

Definition A.2

(Closed convex polyhedra)
.

A nonempty set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed convex polyhedron if it can be expressed as

C\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{j},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\leqslant r_{j}\ \text{for every $j\in\{1,\dots,l\}$}\right\},

where
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{l}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\{r_{1},\dots,r_{l}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
.

Definition A.3

(Convex cones)
.

A nonempty set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a cone if
\boldsymbol{0}\in K
and
\lambda\boldsymbol{x}\in K
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
and
\lambda>0
. A cone
K
is convex if it contains the point
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
whenever
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\in K
and
\eta_{j}\geqslant 0
for
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
.

Definition A.4

(Conical hulls)
.

The conical hull of
k
vectors
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as the closed convex cone

\mathrm{cone}\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}:\eta_{j}\geqslant 0\,\text{for every}\ j\in\{1,\dots,k\}\right\}.

Definition A.5

(Polyhedral cones)
.

A nonempty cone
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is polyhedral if it can be expressed as
K=\mathrm{cone}\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
for some finite collection of vectors
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition A.6

(Proper functions)
.

A function
f
defined over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is proper if its domain

\mathrm{dom~}f\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(\boldsymbol{x})<+\infty\right\}

is nonempty and
f(\boldsymbol{x})>-\infty
for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
.

Definition A.7

(Lower semicontinuity)
.

A proper function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is lower semicontinuous at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
if for every sequence
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
converging to
\boldsymbol{x}
,

\liminf_{k\to+\infty}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\geqslant f(\boldsymbol{x}).

We say that
f
is lower semicontinuous if it is lower semicontinuous at every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
.

Definition A.8

(Convex functions)
.

A proper function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is convex if its domain
\mathrm{dom~}f
is convex and if for every pair
(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\in\mathrm{dom~}f\times\mathrm{dom~}f
and every
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

f(\lambda\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\leqslant\lambda f(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})+(1-\lambda)f(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}).

It is strictly convex if the inequality above is strict whenever
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\neq\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and it is
t
-strongly convex with
t>0
if for every pair
(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\in\mathrm{dom~}f\times\mathrm{dom~}f
and every
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

f(\lambda\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\leqslant\lambda f(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})+(1-\lambda)f(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})-\frac{t}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Definition A.9

(The set
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
)
.

The space of proper, lower semicontinuous and convex functions over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Definition A.10

(Differentiability)
.

Let
f
be a proper function with
\mathrm{int}{~\mathrm{dom~}f}\neq\emptyset
. The function
f
is differentiable at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{int}{~\mathrm{dom~}f}
if there is
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for every
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{d})=\left\langle\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{y}\right\rangle.

If
\boldsymbol{s}
exists, then it is unique, it is called the gradient of
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}
, and it is written as
\boldsymbol{s}\equiv\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})
.

Definition A.11

(Subdifferentiability and subgradients)
.

A function
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is subdifferentiable at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if there exists
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for every
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
,

f(\boldsymbol{y})\geqslant f(\boldsymbol{x})+\left\langle\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle.

(51)

In this case,
\boldsymbol{s}
is called a subgradient of the function
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}
. The set of subgradients at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called the subdifferential of
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}
and is denoted by
\partial f(\boldsymbol{x})
. Moreover:

(i)

When nonempty, the subdifferential of
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}
is a closed convex set. The set of points
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
for which
\partial f(\boldsymbol{x})
is nonempty is denoted by
\mathrm{dom~}\partial f
.

(ii)

The function
f
has a subgradient
\boldsymbol{s}
at a point
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f\iff f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{d})\geqslant\langle\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{d}\rangle
for every
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
54
, Theorem 23.2, page 216]
.

(iii)

The function
f
has a unique subgradient at
\boldsymbol{x}
if and only if
f
is differentiable at
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\partial f(\boldsymbol{x})=\{\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})\}

[
26
, Proposition 5.3, page 23]
.

(iv)

If
f
is strictly convex, then the inequality in (
51

\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{y}
. If
f
is
t
-strongly convex with
t>0
, then
f
is subdifferentiable at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if there exists
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for every
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{dom~}f

f(\boldsymbol{y})\geqslant f(\boldsymbol{x})+\left\langle\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle+\frac{t}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(52)

Definition A.12

(Monotone and maximal monotone mappings)
.

Let
F
denote a set-valued mapping from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with graph
\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\boldsymbol{v}\in F(\boldsymbol{x})\}
. The set-valued mapping
F
is monotone if for every
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\in F(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in F(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
,

\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rangle\geqslant 0.

(53)

It is maximal if no other set-valued mapping
\tilde{F}
contains strictly the graph of
F
.

If
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then its subdifferential is a maximal monotone mapping
[
55
, Theorem 12.17]
. If also
f
is
t
-strongly convex with

, then for every
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\in\partial f(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in\partial f(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
we have the stronger monotone inequality
[
54
, Corollary 31.5.2]
,

\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rangle\geqslant t\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(54)

Definition A.13

(Descent directions)
.

A vector
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a descent direction for a proper function
f

\tau>0

\boldsymbol{x}+\tau\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathrm{dom~}f

.

Definition A.14

(Convex conjugates)
.

Let
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The convex conjugate
f^{*}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
of
f
is defined by

f^{*}(\boldsymbol{s})\coloneqq\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\left\langle\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle-f(\boldsymbol{x})\right\}.

By definition, the function

is in
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

, Definition 4.1, page 17]
.

Definition A.15

(Coercive functions)
.

A proper function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is coercive if for every sequence
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}}\right\|_{2}=+\infty

\lim_{k\to+\infty}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})=+\infty.

\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathrm{ri}\,\mathrm{dom~}f^{*}

Definition A.16

(Characteristic functions)
.

The characteristic function of a nonempty subset

is defined as

\chi_{C}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}0,\;&\text{if}\;x\in C\\
+\infty\;&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

We have
\chi_{C}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if
C
is nonempty, closed and convex, and it is coercive if and only if
C
is bounded.

Definition A.17

(Euclidean projection)
.

Let
C
be a nonempty, closed and convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We denote by
\mathrm{proj}_{C}(\boldsymbol{x})
the projection of
\boldsymbol{x}
on

\mathrm{proj}_{C}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\argmin_{\boldsymbol{y}\in C}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}}\right\|_{2}.

The projection of
\boldsymbol{x}
on
C
exists, is unique, and satisfies the characterization

\langle\boldsymbol{x}-\mathrm{proj}_{C}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{y}-\mathrm{proj}_{C}(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle\leqslant 0\;\text{for every }\boldsymbol{y}\in C.

(55)

See
[
38
, Section III, 3.1]
for details.

Definition A.18

(Normal cones)
.

Let
C
be a nonempty closed convex set. The subdifferential of the characteristic function
\chi_{C}

\boldsymbol{x}\in C

\partial\chi_{C}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq N_{C}(\boldsymbol{x})=\left\{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\rangle\geqslant 0\ \text{for every $\boldsymbol{y}\in C$}\right\}.

The set

Theorem A.3

(The primal problem and the dual problem)
.

Let
f_{1}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
f_{2}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
and let
\bm{A}
denote a real
m\times n
matrix. Consider the “primal” minimization problem

\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})+f_{2}(\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x})\right\}.

(57)

Assume

\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathrm{ri}(\bm{A}~\mathrm{dom~}f_{1}-\mathrm{dom~}f_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathrm{ri}(\mathrm{dom~}f_{1}^{*}+\bm{A}^{\top}\mathrm{dom~}f_{2}^{*}).

(58)

Then:

(i)

The primal problem (
57
) has at least one solution. If
\boldsymbol{x}^{s}
denote such a solution, then it satisfies the first-order optimality condition

\boldsymbol{0}\in\partial(f_{1}+f_{2}\circ\bm{A})(\boldsymbol{x}^{s})\quad\iff\quad\boldsymbol{0}\in\partial f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{s})+\bm{A}^{\top}\partial f_{2}(\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{s}).

(59)

(ii)

The “dual” maximization problem

\sup_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{-f_{1}^{*}(-\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{p})-f_{2}^{*}(\boldsymbol{p})\right\}

(60)

has at least one solution and is equal in value to the primal problem (
57
). If
\boldsymbol{p}^{s}
denote such a solution, then it satisfies the first-order optimality condition

\boldsymbol{0}\in\partial(f_{1}^{*}\circ(-\bm{A}^{\top})+f_{2}^{*})(\boldsymbol{p}^{s})\quad\iff\quad\boldsymbol{0}\in-\bm{A}\partial f_{1}^{*}(-\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}^{s})+\partial f_{2}^{*}(\boldsymbol{p}^{s}).

(61)

(iii)

The optimality conditions (
59
) and (
61
) are equivalent and can be written as

\boldsymbol{p}^{s}\in\partial f_{2}(\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{s})\ \text{and}\ -\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}^{s}\in\partial f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{s}).

(62)

Proof.

See
[
54
, Theorem 31.2 and Corollary 31.2.1]
and
[
3
, Proposition 1, Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, pages 163–167]
for a proof. The equivalences in (
59
) and (
61
) follow from (
58
) and the rules in Proposition
A.2
. The equivalence in (
62
) follows from Fenchel’s inequality (
56
).
∎

Remark A.1
.

The assumptions in (
58
) read: there exists
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f_{1}
such that
\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{ri}\ \mathrm{dom~}f_{2}
and there exists

A.15

Proof of Proposition
3.1

Part 1.
First, we show there is
\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})>0
such that
\left\|{-\bm{A}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
for every
\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})]
. Multiply the vector
-\bm{A}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))
by the matrix of signs
\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
and take the inner product with respect to the unit vector
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
with
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
:

\langle-\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle=\langle-\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{0},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle-\Delta\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle.

(63)

By definition of the equicorrelation set (
4
) and the matrix of signs
\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
, we have

0\leqslant\langle-\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{0},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle\leqslant 1\ \text{for every $j\in\{1,\dots,n\}$.}

We now proceed according to whether
j\in\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
or
j\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
.

First, suppose
j\in\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
. Then
\langle-\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{0},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle=1
and
\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle\geqslant 0
by the KKT condition (
17a
). If the latter is strictly positive, then we can increase
\Delta
until the left-hand-side of (
63
) is equal to
-1
. The smallest such number is

\Delta_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}=\min_{j\in\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}2/\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle,

which may be the extended value
\{+\infty\}
if
\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle=0
for every
j\in\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
.

Next, suppose
j\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
and
\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle\geqslant 0
. The same reasoning as above shows

\Delta_{\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),\geqslant}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\\
\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle\geqslant 0\end{subarray}}\frac{1-\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{0},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle}{\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle}

is the smallest number for which
\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle\geqslant 0
among
j\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
, which may be the extended value
\{+\infty\}
if
\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle=0
for every
j\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
.

Finally, suppose
j\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
and
\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle<0
. Then we can increase
\Delta
until the left-hand-side of (
63
) is equal to
1
. The smallest such number is

\Delta_{\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),<}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\\
\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle<0\end{subarray}}\frac{1+\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{0},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle}{|\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\rangle|}.

Hence we find
\left\|{-\bm{A}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
for all
\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})]
, where

\displaystyle\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min{(\Delta_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})},\Delta_{\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),\geqslant},\Delta_{\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{C}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),<})}

\displaystyle\equiv\min_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{sgn}{\left\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle}-\left\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{0},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle)}{\left\langle\bm{D}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle}\right\}.

Now, for the equivalence, note the assumption
\mathrm{rank}({\bm{A}})=m
implies
\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{0}\iff\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{0}
. Clearly
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{0}
implies
\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=+\infty
. Finally,
\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=+\infty
only if
\bm{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{0}
, which is equivalent to
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{0}
.

Part 2.
Using Part 1, we find
V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b});t,\boldsymbol{b})<+\infty
for every
\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})]

\displaystyle V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b});t,\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\langle\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle

\displaystyle=t\Delta\langle\boldsymbol{p}_{0},\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle+\Delta^{2}t\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}/2

\displaystyle+\Delta\langle\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle+\frac{t}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\langle\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=t\Delta\langle\boldsymbol{p}_{0},\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle+\Delta^{2}t\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}/2

\displaystyle+\Delta\langle\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle+V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}).

Substituting (
17b
) in the above and rearranging yields

V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b});t,\boldsymbol{b})-V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\Delta(t\Delta/2-1)\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

This proves Equation (
19
). Finally, suppose
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, meaning
0<\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})<+\infty
. Taking
\Delta\in(0,\min(\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),2/t))
in (
19

V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b});t,\boldsymbol{b})-V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})<0
. Hence
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
is a descent direction.

Part 3.
Let
\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})]
and
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Since
-\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\in\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
and
V(\cdot;t,\boldsymbol{b})
is
t
-strongly convex, the subdifferentiability property implies

\displaystyle V(\boldsymbol{q};t,\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle\geqslant V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})+\langle-\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\rangle+\frac{t}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})+\langle-\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{q}-(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))\rangle+\langle-\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{t}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{q}-(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})+\langle-\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{q}-(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))\rangle-\Delta\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{t}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{q}-(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\Delta^{2}t}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+t\Delta\langle\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{q}-(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))\rangle.

Use Equation (
19
) in the inequality above to simplify the right hand side:

\displaystyle V(\boldsymbol{q};t,\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle\geqslant V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b});t,\boldsymbol{b})+\langle-(1-t\Delta)\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{q}-(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{t}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{q}-(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

By definition of subdifferentiability, this means

-(1-t\Delta)\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\in\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b});t,\boldsymbol{b})\ \text{for every $\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})]$}.

Part 4.

Now, let

In particular,

\boldsymbol{s}-t\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{b}+t\boldsymbol{p}_{0}-\bm{A}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t))}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t))}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t))}.

Part 5.
Next, we prove identity (
20

\displaystyle+\frac{t}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})-\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})+\langle\boldsymbol{s}-t\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{\Delta^{2}t}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})+\Delta\langle\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})\rangle-\frac{\Delta^{2}t}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(64)

Next, we use the inclusion
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))\subset\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})

\displaystyle V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle\geqslant V(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b});t,\boldsymbol{b})+\langle\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}-(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))\rangle

(65)

65

\langle\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),(1-t\Delta)\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})+\boldsymbol{s}\rangle=0.

Proof of Lemma
4.1

Suppose
\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})<+\infty
. By Proposition
3.1
(iii) and Theorem
4.1
, we have

As a consequence, we can extend the analysis done in Theorem

\boldsymbol{p}(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))=\boldsymbol{p}_{a}(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}))
, and so (
23
) holds at
\tau=\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
.

Next, we prove inequality (
28
). It suffices to show

\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}));t,\boldsymbol{b})\neq e^{-t\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}).

Indeed, the projection onto the closed and convex set
\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}V(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}));t,\boldsymbol{b})
is unique (see

), so the subgradients are different if and only if their norms are different. First, we apply Theorem
4.1
with the initial value

(68)

in the time interval

\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}));t,\boldsymbol{b})=e^{-t\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})

) can be developed further into

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\left(\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})+f(\tau-\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),t)e^{-t\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\right)\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}).

However, this would imply

\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})+f(\tau-\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),t)e^{-t\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}>\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})

\tau\in(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}),\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}));t,\boldsymbol{b})]
, contradicting the definition of the number
\Delta_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
in (
18
). We conclude
\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}(\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b}));t,\boldsymbol{b})\neq e^{-t\tau_{*}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0};t,\boldsymbol{b})
.
