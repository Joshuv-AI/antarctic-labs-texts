The effect of Covid-19 vaccines on semen parameters of patients undergoing infertility investigations: a self-controlled before-and-after study
Authors: Sana Ben Hadj Said, Amira Sallem, Manel Boussabbeh, Amira Dhaoui, Ilhem Ghodhbane, Faouez Boujelbene, Ranin Selmi, Hana Messadi, Meriem Mehdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCOVID-19 vaccination faced significant hesitancy among adolescents and men of reproductive age due to concerns regarding its potential impact on male fertility. This study aimed to compare semen parameters before and after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination. We conducted a self-controlled before-and-after study that included patients referred to the Reproductive Biology Department of Monastir for semen analysis within a 3-month interval before (T1) and after (T2) COVID-19 vaccination. Patients who underwent medical or surgical treatment that could influence sperm parameters between the two compared semen analyses were excluded. Sperm parameters were analyzed by the same trained technician and interpreted according to the 2021 WHO guidelines. The study included 48 patients with a mean age of 40.4 ± 7.7 years. A total of 22 patients received an mRNA vaccine, 15 patients received a viral vector vaccine, and 6 patients received an inactivated vaccine. Viability (p = 1), progressive motility (p = 0.240), total motility (p = 0.762), and sperm morphology (p = 1) were similar before and after vaccination. We observed a decrease in semen pH (p = 0.012), and an increase in sperm concentration (p = 0.026) after vaccination. Analysis of the different vaccine subgroups showed no alteration in sperm parameters. According to WHO criteria, qualitative analysis revealed no clinically meaningful alterations in all semen parameters (p > 0.05). The results of this study support semen parameters stability after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination and provide a starting point for reassuring adolescents and men seeking fertility regarding their reproductive potential after vaccination.Keywords: Male infertility, Semen analysis, SARS-CoV-2, Covid-19 vaccinesSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Urology

Introduction

Global reproductive health is significantly affected by male factor infertility, which is an etiologic contributor in approximately 40% to 50% of infertile couples1. The cornerstone of male infertility investigation remains the semen analysis, a standardized laboratory assessment of ejaculate volume, pH, viscosity, sperm concentration, motility, morphological integrity, and cellular vitality2. These parameters are highly sensitive to physiological disruptions and may be altered by systemic pathologies, localized infections, inflammatory states, and various environmental or lifestyle factors3.

The emergence of the severe respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 pandemic caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus has necessitated intensive investigation into its potential impact on several organs among which the reproductive tract. Pathophysiological data suggest that the virus may compromise spermatogenesis through multiple direct as well indirect pathways. It has been shown that angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor implicated in the pathogenesis of COVID-19, is widely distributed in the testis including Leydig and Sertoli cells4. Furthermore, indirect testicular damage may be related to indirect mechanisms including sustained hyperthermia (fever), systemic cytokine-mediated inflammation, intensified oxidative stress, and structural disruption of the blood-testis barrier5,6.

To mitigate the pandemic’s impact, a global immunization effort was launched utilizing various pharmacological platforms, most notably messenger RNA (mRNA) and adenoviral vector-based vaccines7. Despite their documented efficacy, concerns regarding their secondary effects on male reproductive potential have persisted.

Such anxiety was especially prevalent among adolescents and men of reproductive age, often acting as a significant driver of vaccine reluctance8.

Current clinical evidence indicates that COVID-19 immunization does not lead to a deleterious shift in semen quality9. However, the existing literature is characterized by small sample sizes, heterogeneous methodologies, and brief follow-up intervals10.

Furthermore, longitudinal studies comparing intra-individual semen parameters before and after vaccination across different vaccine types remain limited.

The present study seeks to address these gaps by evaluating the influence of COVID-19 vaccination on semen parameters of patients addressed to the Department of Reproductive Biology of the Maternity and Neonatology Center of Monastir for semen quality investigation in a context of couple infertility.

Patients and methods

Study design

This self-controlled before-and-after study took place at the department of Reproductive Biology located in the Maternity and Neonatology Center in Monastir.

Study setting

The department of Reproductive Biology is a department located in the Maternity and Neonatology Center of the University Teaching Hospital of Fattouma Bourguiba in Monastir, Tunisia. It specialized in investigating and managing couple infertility.

Participants

Inclusion criteria

Patients referred to the department of reproductive biology for semen analysis between March 2021 and November 2022 were included if they had undergone a semen analysis within 90 days (corresponding to a spermatogenesis cycle) prior to COVID-19 vaccination and another one within 90 days following vaccination. For each participant, two time points were analyzed: T1, corresponding to the pre-vaccination semen analysis, and T2, corresponding to the post-vaccination analysis. According to national recommendations issued by the Tunisian Ministry of Health via the EVAX platform in 202111, individuals with a documented history of SARS-CoV-2 infection received vaccination only after a minimum interval of three months.

Non-inclusion criteria

We did not include in our cohort patients reporting changes in lifestyle habits known to affect semen quality including stress, smoking, alcohol consumption, body mass index and sleeping hours. Patients who received medication (such as antibiotics or antioxidants) between the two sperm analysis timepoints, those who underwent chemo/radiotherapy as well as urogenital surgery (for conditions like varicocele or inguinal hernia) during that time were also not included. Furthermore, sperm analysis conducted more than 90 days after receiving the SARS-CoV-2 vaccine were not included in the current study, also who did not comply with national vaccination timing recommendations after SARS-CoV-2 infection were also discarded.

Semen parameters assessment

Semen samples were collected via masturbation in the laboratory after a period of 2–5 days of sexual abstinence, which complies with the WHO-standardized criteria (2–7 days). The same trained technician performed the semen analysis for each patient at the two compared time points.

Results were interpreted in accordance with the 2021 WHO guidelines (World Health Organization)2 with thresholds detailed in Appendix I. Sperm morphological abnormalities are assessed according to the modified DAVID classification12.

Data collection and operational definition of variables

Variables including sociodemographic and medical data, lifestyle factors, infertility history, and semen parameters at two different analysis time points, were gathered from medical records at the department of Reproductive Biology in Monastir. Any changes in these parameters (medical and lifestyle data) between the two time points were noted, and patients were excluded if the observed changes were known to affect semen parameters.

In this study, various lifestyle factors that may affect fertility were examined, particularly tobacco use, alcohol consumption and having an occupation that could compromise fertility potential13,14. Among lifestyle factors, occupations that were considered to compromise fertility in the current study were those exposing to X-rays, high temperature, pesticides (farmers and gardeners), metals (metal workers and welders), chemical products (painters, varnishers, ceramics and paper industry workers)10.

When establishing patients’ subgroups according to the age, the threshold of 45 years old were considered according to literature data stipulating an impairment of semen parameters beyond this limit15. When it comes to infertility duration the threshold of 12 months was taken into consideration as recommended by the WHO16.

Patients provided their weight and height, which allowed for the calculation of body mass index (BMI) using the standard formula of kg/m². According to WHO guidelines, a BMI between 25 and 30 is classified as overweight, while a BMI of 30 or higher defines obesity17.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23.0. After testing distribution normality using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, quantitative variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as median and quartiles, while qualitative variables were expressed as frequency (n) and percentage (%). To compare semen parameters before and after the SARS-CoV-2 vaccination in both the overall cohort and within the specific subgroup analyses, the matched samples Student’s t-test was used for variables with a symmetric distribution and the Wilcoxon test with Bonferroni correction for those with an asymmetric distribution. The McNemar test was applied for matched qualitative variables.

A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of Monastir granted approval for our study, with reference number IORG 0009738 N˚115/OMB 0990–0279.

The study was performed in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. We protected the privacy of the included patients and the confidentiality of their personal data during the study period. Only the investigator knew who the patients were. Every patient received a code number, and the list that linked their names to attributed codes was kept apart in a secure area with limited access. The investigator was the only person able to link trial data to particular patients.

Transparency statement

Artificial intelligence–based language tools were used to assist in improving English language clarity, academic style, and reference management. These tools were not used for data analysis, interpretation, or scientific content generation. All intellectual content, methodological design, and interpretation of results were developed and validated by the authors.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the study population

Sociodemographic and lifestyle habits of the study population

Our study included a total of 48 patients. The mean age was of 40.4 ± 7.75 years old. Participants under 45 years old constituted the largest demographic segment, representing 77.1% (n = 37) of the whole study population.

All patients were from urban areas and were married. More than one-quarter of them 27.1% (n = 13) had occupations that could compromise reproductive potential.

Regarding lifestyle habits, smoking was reported by more than half (66.7%) of the patients (n = 32), and alcohol consumption was noted in 20.8% of cases (n = 10).

The body mass index was found to be normal in 31.1% of the study population, whereas obesity was observed in 12.5% of the patients.

Semen volume

The median ejaculate volume was of 3 ml at the two compared timepoints. The interquartile rage changed from [1.85–4.15] ml at baseline to [1.5–4.0] ml following vaccination (p = 0.080).

Semen pH

The semen pH exhibited a statistically significant decline following vaccination (p = 0.012). The median pH decreased from 7.815 [7.695–7.945] before vaccination to 7.69 [7.0–7.82] afterward.

Sperm vitality

Sperm vitality showed no significant difference following COVID-19 vaccination as the median value of dead spermatozoa was of 77 [56.0–89.0] before vs. 76.0 [69.0–86.0] after vaccination (p = 1).

Sperm concentration

Sperm concentration showed a statistically significant increase after vaccination (p = 0.026). The median sperm concentration rose from 43.0 million/ml [4.65–118.5] before vaccination to 66.5 million/ml: [15.75–129.0] afterward.

Sperm motility

When focusing on initial progressive motility, we noticed similar median values before and after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination 15.0% [0–23.75] and 15.0% [5.0–20.0] respectively; p = 0.240). These results were also confirmed when considering initial total motility (30.0 [0–40.0]vs median 30.0 [10.0–40.0] with p = 1).

Sperm morphology

The median percentage of morphologically abnormal spermatozoa was of 93.0% [90.0–97.0] before vaccine administration and showed no significant change following it (94.0% [90.0–97.0]; p = 1). Similarly, the Multiple Abnormalities Index (MAI) showed a baseline median of 1.75% [1.59–2.1] and a post-vaccination median of 1.81% [1.645–2.02]. This minor fluctuation did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.182).

Leukocytospermia

Median seminal leukocyte concentration showed a similar neutral trend, declining from 0.34 [0.2–1.29] × 10⁶/mL pre-vaccination to 0.2 [0.1–0.645] × 10⁶/mL post-vaccination (p = 0.306).

Qualitative semen parameters

A normal sperm viscosity was seen in 98% patients before vaccination and in the whole study population after receiving the vaccine.

Discussion

The SARS-CoV-2 virus, has had a major global impact, leading to millions of deaths and long-term health complications18,19. Beyond its effects on the respiratory system, the virus can also affect other organs including the urinary and reproductive systems.

Research has shown that the virus may be found in the urine and testicular tissue, suggesting that it could directly affect these organs20.

Several studies have also reported changes in sperm quality in men who have recovered from COVID-19, including lower sperm count, reduced motility, and abnormal morphology21,22. These potential effects on male reproductive health have heightened interest in both preventive strategies and therapeutic solutions. In response to the global burden of COVID-19, numerous clinical trials have been launched to evaluate the safety and efficacy of various vaccines. Scientists have leveraged prior knowledge from research on related viruses, such as SARS-CoV and Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (MERS-CoV), to accelerate the development of vaccines and other medical interventions. Alongside vaccination efforts, researchers continue to explore the virus’s long-term impacts, diagnostic tools, and treatment options23,24.

Given the reported impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection on male reproductive parameters, there is a growing need to investigate not only the consequences of the virus itself but also the potential reproductive effects of COVID-19 vaccination. While vaccines have proven effective in reducing the severity and spread of the disease, questions remain regarding their influence on male fertility and were the main cause of vaccine reluctance among adolescents and men of reproductive age. Building on these concerns, our study set out to determine whether SARS-CoV-2 vaccination has any measurable impact on male reproductive health.

We evaluated, using pre-experimental study (before and after), sperm parameters of 48 men addressed for sperm analysis in a context of couple hypofertility and for whom two spermograms were performed during a maximal period of 3 months before and after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination.

The mean age of the patients was 40.4 ± 7.75 years and the most represented age group was under 45 years old (n = 37; 77.1%). More than one-quarter (27.1%) of the patients (n = 13) had occupations that could compromise reproductive potential and nearly half of the participants (n = 32) reported tobacco use.

Although the primary aim of this study was not to investigate sociodemographic or lifestyle influences, it is important to note that many participants presented with known risk factors for impaired semen quality, such as a high prevalence of tobacco consumption and exposure to occupational reprotoxic agents14.

When focusing on semen parameters of the study population before as well as after vaccination, all spermograms were pathological and most altered features were sperm motility (81.3%) and morphology (83.3%) which are crucial for both natural and assisted reproduction. Beyond playing an essential role in the functionality of male gametes, lifestyle factors could compromise the ability of spermatozoa to fertilize the oocyte and could impair the subsequent embryo development when leading to an altered sperm DNA quality25,26. In the current study, male-factor infertility was identified in 75% of cases, which underscores its significant role in couple infertility.

In light of these data, and considering that no participants included in the analysis experienced changes in these risk factors between the two time points (T1 and T2), the alterations observed in semen quality cannot be linked to variations in lifestyle or sociodemographic conditions.

Considering the type of administered vaccine, the majority of the included patients (45.8%; n = 22) received an mRNA vaccine. This category was further split between BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech), administered to 25.0% (n = 12), and mRNA-1273 (Moderna), administered to 20.8% (n = 10) of the patients.

The second most common vaccine type was the recombinant viral-vectored one (31.3%; n = 15), with the Ad26.COV2. S (Johnson & Johnson/Janssen) single-dose regimen representing the majority of this subgroup (27.1%; n = 13).

In contrast, inactivated vaccines accounted for the smallest proportion (12.5%; n = 6) and only 5 patients were unable to recall the vaccine they had received.

Analysis of the vaccination regimen in the study cohort revealed that the majority of participants had completed the recommended primary vaccination scheme : 54.2% (n = 26) received two doses, while 43.8% (n = 21) received a single dose (predominantly the Ad26.COV2.S Johnson & Johnson vaccine, which is licensed as a one-dose vaccine27). Only 2.1% (n = 1) had received a third booster dose at the time of the second semen analysis. This distribution reflects the real-world vaccination policy in Tunisia during the study period (2021–2023), where the single-dose viral-vector vaccine was widely used, and boosters were not yet universally recommended or available for younger men28,29.

These distributions are in line with international vaccination trends during the early phases of the COVID-19 immunization campaign, when mRNA and viral vectored vaccines represented the majority of administered vaccines, while inactivated vaccines were less frequently used depending on regional supply and national guidelines29,30.

Understanding this heterogeneity in vaccine types and regimens is essential, as potential differences in immunological response or systemic side effects could, in theory, influence male reproductive health. Consequently, documenting the type and number of doses received provides a necessary framework for interpreting potential variations in sperm parameters before and after vaccination in the study population.

When comparing semen quality before and after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination, our results have shown stable sperm parameters except for pH and sperm concentration.

These findings are in agreement with previously published clinical data indicating that mRNA COVID-19 vaccines do not exert harmful effects on male reproductive function. Indeed, Gonzalez and colleagues7 reported stability in key semen parameters following vaccination.

Contrasting with these data, Massaretti and collaborators31 who reported a volume decrease from 3.0 ml to 2.6 ml after vaccination, despite noting a statistical difference, conclude that such small changes are within the expected biological variability of semen parameters. Possible explanations for such transient, minor volume decreases often include shortened abstinence periods before the post-vaccination sample collection, rather than a direct pathological effect of the vaccine31. The comparison of abstinence period in our study population revealed the same median of 3 days but a shift in the distribution of this variable among the included patients. However, it bears mentioning that we only included patients with an abstinence period (between 2 and 5 days) which strictly aligns with internationally recommended guidelines. When considering the impact of each vaccine type, the semen volume was similar in all the studied SARS-CoV-2 vaccines.

Furthermore, we have shown that semen pH significantly decreased after vaccination from 7.81 [7.69–7.94] to reach 7.69 [7.0–7.82]. This could reflect changes in accessory gland fluid secretion (prostatic or seminal vesicle fluid) which heavily influence pH. Despite the statistical evidence for a change, the observed shift is not considered clinically meaningful (p = 0.32). The post-vaccination mean pH remained comfortably within the WHO reference range of 7.2 to 8.02, indicating a physiologically normal environment for sperm function.

The majority of literature data have shown stable semen pH before and after receiving SARS-CoV-2 vaccines34–36. Dil.E found an unchanged pH when considering all vaccine types, but a statistically significant decrease in semen pH after vaccination with both Sinovac/CoronaVac (p = 0.004) and Pfizer/BioNTech (p < 0.001) in a population of patients with idiopathic infertility37. When performing a subgroup analysis based on the type of administered vaccine in our cohort, pH was similar at the two compared timepoints.

The third sperm parameter for which we noted a significant variation was sperm concentration which surprisingly increased after COVID-19 vaccination (from 43.0 [4.65–118.5] to 66.5 [15.75–129.0] (million/ml); p = 0.026). When compared to the WHO threshold, the observed difference was not clinically relevant (p = 0.43). These data suggest that vaccination does not exert a detrimental effect on the quantitative aspect of spermatogenesis.

In a recently published study (2025), including 31 patients who received COVID-19 vaccination, authors showed that the median sperm concentration increased from 25 to 43 million/ml after vaccination in a mixed mRNA and viral-vector cohort. They noted that individual variation and shorter abstinence before the post-vaccine sample could partly explain observed increases in concentration during short-term follow-up32.

When performing a subgroup analysis, similar medians of sperm concentration before and after vaccination were spotted. In line with these results, and in a cohort of 101 patients who received 2 doses of inactivated vaccines, authors showed no variation in not only all semen parameters but also IVF outcomes (number and quality of obtained blastocysts, biochemical and clinical pregnancy rates)4.

In this study, sperm morphology remained stable following vaccination (93.0% [90.0–97.0] before vaccine vs. 94.0% [90.0–97.0] afterward) with p-value = 1. The proportion of abnormal sperm forms showed no statistically significant difference between baseline and post-vaccination assessments, and a similar pattern was observed for the multiple abnormalities index (MAI) (p = 0.182). These findings indicate that vaccination does not negatively influence sperm morphological characteristics.

The MAI provides additional information by reflecting the number of defects per abnormal spermatozoon. The lack of a significant post-vaccination change in MAI suggests that vaccination does not increase the severity or complexity of sperm defects. These results are consistent with recent studies demonstrating that vaccination does not adversely affect male reproductive parameters9. In contrast, febrile illnesses and systemic viral infections such as COVID-19 have been shown to temporarily impair spermatogenesis and worsen sperm morphology33,34. From this perspective, vaccination may play a protective role by reducing the risk of infection-related testicular damage.

Following SARS-CoV-2 immunization, sperm motility in this study stayed constant. Sperm movement and functional capacity have not been affected by vaccination, as neither progressive motility nor total motility shown statistically significant variations between pre- and post-vaccination tests.

As one of the main determinants of male fertility, sperm motility is particularly sensitive to testicular dysfunction and systemic stresses. Our findings suggest that SARS-CoV-2 vaccines have no detrimental effect on both progressive (p = 0.24) and total motility (p = 1).

Available research corroborates this observation as studies on the effects of vaccinations, including the mRNA vaccines, have frequently reported no statistically significant adverse effects on sperm motility35. The immune response induced by vaccination is not associated with direct alterations in spermatogenesis or sperm motility. The mRNA vaccines, which work by eliciting an immune response without the use of live virus, do not interfere with sperm production or function.

The physiological processes governing sperm motility include various factors, such as mitochondrial activity, seminal fluid composition, temperature, and hormonal levels, that are shown to be unaffected by vaccination35.

In studies assessing the influence of vaccines on male reproductive health, particularly concerning other types of vaccines, there were also limited reports of significant adverse effects on sperm motility. The meta-analysis of Huang and colleagues published in 202336 which included twelve cohort studies involving a total of 914 participants, found that semen parameters were similar before and after vaccination when pooling all vaccine types. The obtained results remained similar across messenger ribonucleic acid, viral-vector, and inactivated COVID-19 vaccines.

Massarotti and al31., noted an increase in progressive motility from 50% to 56% after taking the vaccine. This measurable difference could be attributed to intraindividual variations in sperm parameters. Furthermore, minor fluctuations in sperm motility can occur due to external factors such as lifestyle, stress, and environmental exposures. COVID-19 vaccination does not appear to be a contributing factor24. These potential confounding factors were accounted for in the current study.

It’s important to mention that semen parameters are known to fluctuate within the same individual, even under standardized collection conditions and identical periods of abstinence. In andrological literature, this is quantified by the within-subject coefficient of variation37,38. In the current study, no significant variations in semen quality were observed in relation to COVID-19 vaccination status. Interestingly, this lack of fluctuation following vaccination aligned with our broader findings, which demonstrated an absence of seasonal variability within our cohort.

The Tunisian Minister of Health launched a vaccination campaign in March 2021 targeting individuals over 60 years old, healthcare workers, and patients with chronic diseases. The campaign expanded in August 2021 to include all individuals over 18 years old11.

The vaccines deployed in the campaign include mRNA vaccines, non-replicating viral vector vaccines, and inactivated virus vaccines. Based on pharmacovigilance data, certain vaccines are recommended for specific age groups. Booster doses are administered exclusively with mRNA vaccines, regardless of the vaccine used in the primary vaccination schedule.

Our study was conducted on different vaccines where one quarter of patients took an mRNA vaccine BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech).

When focusing on this type of vaccine, we noticed a single significant parameter change which concerns MAI which surprisingly decreases after vaccination. The remaining semen parameters were similar at the two compared timepoints.

Comparing sperm analysis before and after taking mRNA vaccine BNT162b2 in 41 volunteer men, Gonzalez and al., confirm that there is no significant decrease in any of the sperm parameters post vaccination. As the vaccine contains mRNA and not alive virus, it is unlikely to induce semen quality impairment7.

In another study with a larger sample size (n = 216), Gat and al39 have shown a temporary impairment in sperm concentration and total motile count three months after Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine. However, by five months post-vaccination, these parameters had fully recovered to baseline levels. One can also conclude that while mild, transient changes in semen quality may occur after vaccination, there is no evidence of lasting harm to male fertility after receiving BNT162b2 vaccine.

In the same context, Olana and al40 conducted a prospective study involving 47 healthy men aged 18–45 years who received the full BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech) mRNA COVID-19 vaccine cycle. Semen samples were collected before the first dose and three months later. The researchers evaluated standard sperm parameters as well as seminal plasma markers, including reactive oxygen metabolites, electrolyte levels, and Interleukin-6 (IL-6) concentration.

No statistically significant differences were observed between pre- and post-vaccination values for any sperm parameter. Furthermore, the activity of the cell membrane, seminal plasma oxidative stress markers and IL6 as a marker of inflammation were unchanged. Although we observed a significant decrease in the MAI value, contrary to the findings of Olana et al., this parameter should not be interpreted in isolation; it must be evaluated alongside the percentage of abnormal spermatozoa, which remained unchanged in the BNT162b2 vaccine subgroup in the current study.

About 21% of our study population received the mRNA-1273 Moderna vaccine. We have demonstrated that all semen parameters remained stable and unaffected following immunization in this small size subgroup. These preliminary observations are consistent with the broader trend and support the overall stability of semen parameters.

Among our cohort of patients, 27.1% received the Ad26. COV2. S (Johnson & Johnson/Janssen) vaccine. None of the analyzed semen parameters was impaired after vaccination in this subgroup. Only two patients took ChAdOx1 (Oxford Astra Zeneca) vaccine. The vaccine utilizes a non-replicating chimpanzee adenovirus vector. Because this vector does not possess the same tissue tropism for the testes as the live SARS-CoV-2 virus, the blood-testis barrier remains intact. The immune response is systemic and localized to the injection site, avoiding the cytokine storm or direct testicular inflammation (orchitis) associated with the disease41. These data simply reflect the general trend observed in the main cohort.”

Indeed, although subgroups analysis demonstrated stable semen parameters, these findings should be considered as preliminary and cannot be extrapolated to a broader population given the small samples’ size.

There were six patients among our study population who received inactivated vaccines. In a cohort of 271 men who received CoronaVac vaccine, no adverse effects on motile sperm count as well as ART outcomes were noticed when comparing these parameters to a group of non-vaccinated men42.

This is to the best of our knowledge, the first Tunisian and North African study to evaluate the impact of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines on sperm parameters. It has another strength that could increase the robustness of the obtained results as each patient was serving as his own control. This could exclude any interindividual variation in semen parameters. Furthermore, sperm analysis was performed by the same trained technician at the two compared timepoints. Confirming the stability of semen parameters after Covid-19 vaccination, our findings could be a starting point for health authorities to convince adolescents and young men to adhere to SARS-CoV-2 vaccination.

It bears mentioning that several limitations warrant consideration. Primarily, the modest cohort size and the observational, single-center nature of our study preclude definitive conclusions regarding causality or absolute safety. Furthermore, without a parallel control group, the observed stability in semen parameters should be viewed as preliminary evidence rather than a final determination of vaccine safety. Although we minimized intra-individual variability by applying strict non-inclusion criteria, a potential confounding effect of temporal and seasonal variations cannot be entirely ruled out when comparing semen parameters before and after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination. Additionally, an inherent selection bias must be acknowledged, as our study population consisted exclusively of men undergoing infertility evaluation. Since many participants presented with suboptimal baseline semen parameters, these findings may not be fully generalizable to the broader, normozoospermic male population. It’s also crucial to mention that some important clinical and biological markers, such as the total motile sperm count (TMSC), the DNA fragmentation index (DFI), hormonal profiles (including testosterone and FSH), and testicular volume, were not evaluated. Even if we have shown stable semen parameters after vaccination, the absence of these parameters limits our ability to assess the potential impact of the vaccine on deeper mechanisms of spermatogenesis or sperm chromatin integrity. Future research incorporating these advanced diagnostic tools is necessary to provide a more holistic view of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines on reproductive safety. Furthermore, and in line with literature data, we defined the maximal interval between vaccination and post-vaccination semen analysis as a three-month period which corresponds to the duration of a spermatogenesis cycle. However, a long-term follow-up could be worth implementing as a perspective of the current study.

Conclusion

Although the development of SARS-CoV_2 vaccines was considered a notable scientific achievement during the pandemic period, concerns have been raised regarding their possible detrimental impact on male fertility. Owing to concerns about the potential analogous effects of the coronavirus vaccine to the impact of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) on male fertility, we conducted the current exploratory study to address vaccine hesitancy among Tunisian adolescents and young men. Our findings demonstrate that COVID-19 vaccination does not result in clinically significant deterioration of semen quality. No clinically meaningful changes were observed in semen parameters, including semen volume, sperm concentration, motility, morphology, vitality, leukocyte count, or multiple anomaly index following vaccination.

Our results indicate that no clinically meaningful deterioration in semen parameters was observed within this specific cohort. However, the study design precludes drawing inferences regarding the absolute safety of the vaccine on male reproductive health.

Furthermore, our study did not evaluate advanced reproductive parameters such as the sperm DNA Fragmentation Index (DFI) and serum hormone levels (testosterone, FSH) as these are not recommended as first-line investigations by the WHO guidelines.

Consequently, while conventional semen parameters remained stable, we cannot exclude subtle damage to sperm genetic material or subclinical changes in the hormonal regulation of spermatogenesis. Future mechanistic studies incorporating these advanced parameters are warranted to confirm our findings at a molecular level.

The findings of the current exploratory Tunisian study are of particular importance for adolescents and reproductive-age individuals and contribute to reducing unwarranted concerns that may influence vaccine acceptance.

Large-scale, longitudinal researches are essential to definitively characterize long-term reproductive outcomes after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination.
