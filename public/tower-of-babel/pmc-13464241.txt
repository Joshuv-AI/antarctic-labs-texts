Catheter Impaction After Pericardial Drainage Due to Inadvertent Entry Into the Transverse Pericardial Sinus
Authors: Tetsuro Nagao, Riku Arai, Shu Hirata, Koichiro Hori, Suguru Migita, Ryuta Watanabe, Keisuke Kojima, Koichi Nagashima, Yasuo Okumura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPericardial drainage is a lifesaving intervention for cardiac tamponade. Difficulty in catheter removal is rare and is typically attributed to thrombus formation or pericardial adhesions.Case SummaryA 66-year-old woman developed cardiac tamponade after cryoablation for atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia. Subxiphoid pericardiocentesis was performed without resistance, and a 6-F pigtail catheter was placed under fluoroscopy. Two days later, removal was hindered by marked resistance. Computed tomography revealed inadvertent catheter passage through the transverse pericardial sinus, posterior to the ascending aorta, with lateral reentry into the pericardial cavity, creating a sharply angulated, mechanically anchored course. Saline-induced re-expansion combined with gentle diastolic-phase traction allowed safe removal.DiscussionThe transverse pericardial sinus, a well-known surgical landmark, is a previously under-recognized site of pericardial-drainage catheter impaction.Take-Home MessageSmooth catheter insertion does not guarantee correct placement; transverse-sinus malposition should be considered when removal resistance occurs.Key words: ablation, complication, computed tomography, pericardial drainage, pericardial effusion, tamponade, transverse pericardial sinus

Timeline and Mechanism of Catheter Impaction via the Transverse Pericardial Sinus

(Left) Three-dimensional (3D) volume-rendered computed tomography showing the drainage catheter coursing through the transverse pericardial sinus between the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk. (Middle) Fluoroscopy during drainage illustrating the anatomical corridor (dotted cylinder) through which the guidewire traversed the sinus. (Right) The 3-stage pathophysiology of this complication: 1) uneventful insertion through a distended sinus, 2) delayed impaction caused by collapse of the sinus around the angulated catheter after drainage, and 3) safe retrieval by saline-induced re-expansion of the pericardial cavity combined with gentle diastolic-phase traction. The take-home message emphasizes that smooth catheter insertion does not guarantee correct placement; if unexpected resistance occurs during removal, transverse sinus malposition should be suspected, forceful traction avoided, and saline re-expansion with diastole-synchronized gentle retrieval considered.

History of Presentation

A 66-year-old woman had been experiencing intermittent palpitations for approximately 5 years, which had previously been infrequent and managed conservatively. Over the preceding several months, the frequency of palpitations increased, prompting referral to our institution. At initial presentation, narrow QRS tachycardia was documented and was successfully terminated with intravenous adenosine triphosphate, and she was admitted for an electrophysiology study and catheter ablation. The electrophysiology study performed on hospital day 2 confirmed the diagnosis of atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia, and cryoablation of the slow pathway was performed. Approximately 2 hours after returning to the ward, the patient developed hypotension (blood pressure: 65/47 mm Hg), jugular venous distension, and vomiting. Bedside transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated a large pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology, prompting emergent pericardial drainage.

Past Medical History

She had a history of paroxysmal atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia for approximately 5 years, without other significant comorbidities. She was not on antithrombotic therapy and had no prior history of cardiac surgery, pericarditis, or pericardial effusion.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for catheter retention included pericardial adhesions or encasement in fibrinous inflammatory tissue,1 pericatheter thrombus formation, catheter knotting or looping around the heart,2 catheter kinking or fracture,3 and entrapment within an anatomical recess such as the transverse pericardial sinus.

Investigations

Initial presentation

TTE demonstrated a large pericardial effusion with predominant inferior distribution and tamponade physiology (Figure 1). Fluoroscopy during the subsequent pericardial drainage showed the guidewire advancing smoothly along the cardiac silhouette without resistance or abnormal trajectory (Figure 2A, Video 1). Blood gas analysis of the drained pericardial fluid revealed a venous-type profile (pH: 7.329, Po2: 45.8 mm Hg, Pco2: 43.5 mm Hg, So2: 77%), consistent with a low-pressure venous bleeding source.

Removal failure

Two days after drainage, marked resistance was encountered during attempted catheter removal. Computed tomography demonstrated that the catheter had advanced from the anterosuperior aspect of the ascending aorta into the transverse pericardial sinus, coursed posterior to the ascending aorta within the sinus, and exited laterally into the main pericardial space, creating a sharply angulated configuration consistent with mechanical impaction (Figure 3, Video 2).

Management

Initial pericardial drainage

Emergent pericardial drainage was performed via a subxiphoid approach under fluoroscopic guidance.4 A 6-F pigtail drainage catheter was inserted without resistance, and approximately 250 mL of bloody pericardial fluid was drained, restoring hemodynamic stability.

Catheter retrieval

When marked resistance was encountered during attempted catheter removal on day 2, forceful traction was avoided pending further imaging. After computed tomography confirmed transverse pericardial sinus malposition, retrieval was performed under fluoroscopic guidance. A total of 100 mL of normal saline was instilled into the pericardial space to re-expand the collapsed cavity and widen the transverse pericardial sinus. Sustained gentle traction was then applied; the catheter remained stationary during systole but advanced incrementally during diastole, suggesting that reduced aortic distension during diastole may transiently widen the sinus and facilitate catheter passage. Marked resistance and chest and back pain occurred at the onset of traction; once the catheter passed the lateral exit of the transverse pericardial sinus, smooth removal was achieved without complications.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient had no procedural complications after catheter removal. Follow-up TTE showed no recurrent effusion. She was discharged on hospital day 11 and remained asymptomatic at the 4-month follow-up.

Discussion

Although the exact mechanism of perforation could not be definitively identified, the venous-type blood gas profile of the drained pericardial fluid, together with the inferior distribution of the effusion and the delayed onset of tamponade approximately 2 hours after the procedure, is most consistent with a slow venous bleed. Given that cryoablation itself carries minimal perforation risk,5 inadvertent injury of the coronary sinus or one of its tributaries during mapping catheter placement is considered the most likely etiology.6

The transverse pericardial sinus is a well-known surgical landmark but is rarely considered a pathway for pericardial drainage hardware.7,8 Anatomically, the sinus is a narrow tunnel bounded anteriorly by the posterior surfaces of the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk and posteriorly by the roof of the left atrium. Inadvertent guidewire entry into the sinus, with the wire passing posterior to the ascending aorta, followed by lateral re-entry into the main pericardial space, can create a sharply angulated catheter course that becomes mechanically anchored, particularly after pericardial decompression when the sinus narrows around the catheter.

Importantly, the impaction does not manifest during initial catheter placement, when the sinus is distended by the effusion and accommodates the catheter without resistance; only after drainage does the sinus collapse around the sharply angulated catheter, producing mechanical entrapment. This delayed impaction—smooth insertion followed by unexpected resistance during removal—is the characteristic feature of this complication and explains why the malposition is not recognized at the time of drainage.

Careful guidewire manipulation and confirmation in multiple fluoroscopic projections are essential to avoid this pitfall. If resistance is encountered during catheter removal, forceful traction should be avoided, and transverse sinus malposition should be considered as a cause of catheter impaction.

Conclusions

Inadvertent passage of a pericardial drainage catheter through the transverse pericardial sinus is a previously under-recognized mechanism of catheter impaction. Saline re-expansion of the pericardial cavity combined with gentle diastolic-phase traction enables safe removal without surgical intervention.
