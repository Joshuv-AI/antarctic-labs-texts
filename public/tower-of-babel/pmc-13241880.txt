Synergistic Effect of Linebacker-1 With Radiation Therapy in a Mouse Lung Cancer Model
Authors: Sayeda Yasmin-Karim, Geraud Richards, Gerassimos Mike Makrigiorgos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMyricetin is a small-molecular-weight U.S. Food and Drug Administration-recognized dietary supplement showing efficient anticancer effects. The recent development of myricetin derivative Linebacker-1 (LB1), which changes a hydroxyl group on the B ring to a chlorine atom, exhibits DNA-binding ability by interacting with the minor grooves of DNA and may also act as an antitumor drug. This study demonstrates the anticancer effect and radiosensitization of LB1 in a lung cancer model.Methods and MaterialsIn vitro studies were performed in human A549 (American Type Culture Collection [ATCC]) and HT29 (ATCC), and in murine Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC1) (ATCC) cancer cell lines. A syngeneic murine model of lung adenocarcinoma was generated subcutaneously in 1 flank of wild (+/+) C57/BL6 background mice using the LLC1 cell line. Image guided radiation therapy (IGRT) targeting the tumor was administered with a small animal radiation research platform.ResultsOur in vitro study observations demonstrate that LB1 induces an antineoplastic effect in human lung A549 (P < .001) and colon HT29 (P < .001) adenocarcinoma cell lines. In the murine lung cancer model, we further demonstrate that a single dose of 8 Gy IGRT substantially enhances treatment response by reducing tumor volume (P < .01) and increasing survival percent and duration (P < .001). In addition, adding LB1 with a checkpoint inhibitor (antibody to programmed death-1 [anti-PD1]) or with IGRT+ anti-PD1 further increases the survival percentage (P < .05, P < .05, respectively), with no significant change in mouse body weight or body score.ConclusionsLB1 demonstrates enhancement of the antitumor effect of radiation in a lung cancer model, whereas anti-PD1 treatment further enhances the effect. The results provide impetus for further studies, including in vivo studies in orthotopic lung cancer models, to provide robust preclinical data for potential future application of LB1 as a radiosensitizer in the clinic.

Introduction

Exposure to environmental challenges directly affects barrier organs1 such as the lungs.1,2 The cross-sectional epidemiological analysis shows that lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in men and women in the United States and worldwide,3,4 where non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) represents approximately 84% of all lung cancers.5 Radiation and chemotherapy are the most common modalities of cancer treatment worldwide.6 Radiation induces apoptosis, causing cancer cells to be fragmented and exposing the cancer-associated antigens to the tumor microenvironment.7 These can be recognized by antigen-presenting cells to induce the antineoplastic effect by activating cytotoxic T cells.8

Flavonoids are natural compounds widely found in fruits and vegetables and display efficient anticancer properties.9 For example, they can induce growth inhibition and apoptosis in human lung cancer cells involved in the SIRT1/AMPK (Silent Information Regulator 1/Adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase) signaling pathway.10 Myricetin is a small-molecular-weight U.S. Food and Drug Administration-recognized dietary supplement showing an efficient anticancer effect11,12 that exhibits DNA-binding ability by interacting with minor DNA grooves.13 Flavonoids like myricetin induce apoptosis in diverse cancer cells, including lung cancer.10,12,14 The recent development of the myricetin derivative Linebacker-1 (LB1) has the hydroxyl group at 5’ position of β-ring replaced by a chlorine group, which enhances DNA binding.13 The potential of LB1 as an antineoplastic agent has not been previously tested. This study investigates the anticancer effect of LB1 in a lung cancer model using immunocompetent mice. In view of the structural similarity between LB1 and myricetin, we hypothesized that LB1 might also inhibit the progression of lung tumor growth and amplify damage to tumors treated with radiation therapy (RT).

A combination of RT with immunotherapy is now routinely used for different stages of lung cancer care.14,15 Immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as antibody to programmed death-1 (anti-PD1) immunotherapy with radiation, have emerged as a standard of care for late-stage NSCLC over the past decade.16,17 Because LB1’s “mother compound,” Myricetin, induces apoptosis in cancer cells (like radiation), we hypothesized that LB1 may act in a similar manner and that adding LB1 to radiation treatment may further enhance cancer apoptotic cell death. Moreover, apoptosis exposes cancer-related antigens to the tumor microenvironment, thereby triggering T cell-mediated tumoricidal effects.8,18 Therefore, adding LB1 to radiation and an immune checkpoint inhibitor, anti-PD-1, may further enhance T cell-mediated cytotoxicity and can result in better survival compared with radiation and anti-PD1 alone. Under this hypothesis, we also investigated the combination of LB1 with radiation and the immunotherapeutic programmed death-1 (PD-1) with radiation treatment.

Methods and Materials

Cell lines, cell culture

Adenocarcinoma human alveolar basal epithelial cells line: A549 (CRM-CCL-185), human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line (HTB-38), and C57/BL6 background murine Lewis lung carcinoma cell line LL/2 (LLC1) (CRL-1642) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). A549 cells were cultured in RPMI cell culture media (Gibco), the HT29 cell line was cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium (ATCC), and the LLC1 cell was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Media (Gibco). All media used were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen). All cell lines were grown within an incubator under the following environmental conditions: 5% carbon dioxide atmosphere, 37°C, and 90% relative humidity. Cells used for experiments had passage numbers less than 30. For high-grade/aggressive LLC1 cancer cell generation, a cell line with >80 passages was used. All cell culture plastics were obtained from Corning Life Sciences. For all experiments, cell viability % and number of live cells were measured via LUNA II automated cell counter (New England BioGroup), and cells with viability of >90% were used in all in vitro and in vivo experiments.

LB1, and anti-PD1

LB1 was obtained as a powder from Prophase Diagnostics Labs. This is a small molecule compound (MW 336.68) synthesized by Impact BioMedical and licensed to ProPhase BioPharma for potential use in treating cancer, inflammatory diseases, and neurological disorders. It is a modified polyphenol, derived from the plant flavonoid myricetin. The radiation dose for animal studies, 8 Gy, was selected from previous studies where radiation treatment was used along with myricetin doses.19 Working stock solutions of 100 mM of LB1 were prepared in DMSO obtained from Sigma Aldrich.6,20,21 Anti-PD1 was obtained from BIOXCELL and injected (100 µg/mouse) via intraperitoneal (IP) injection.

Methyl dimethylthiazol diphenyltetrazolium bromide growth assays

For methyl dimethylthiazol diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assays, HT-29 and A549 cells were seeded in 3000 cells/well, and LLC1 cells were seeded in 2000 cells/well in 96-well plates (Corning Inc). Cells were treated with either LB1 or vehicle dissolved in the corresponding cell culture media, and RT was given in the corresponding plates 24 hours after treatment.

After a 96-hour incubation period ended, 5 mg/mL Thiazolyl Blue Tetrazolium Bromide solution in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to each well for a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL in each well. Plates were incubated for 3 hours at 37 °C. Following the 3-hour incubation period, all media were gently removed without disturbing the attached MTT crystals. DMSO, 100 µL, was then added to each well to dissolve the crystals, and each plate was placed on a shaker for 20 minutes to ensure that all crystals dissolved. Once dissolved, the absorbance was determined via the ClarioStar Plus Microplate Reader by BMG biotech at a wavelength of 570 nm spectrophotometrically. The raw data obtained was transferred to Excel for further analysis.

Clonogenic survival assay

As previously mentioned, HT-29 cells and A549 cells were cultured for clonogenic assays. Once cells reach confluency, trypsin-EDTA was used to remove cells, which were then added to fresh media and spun down into a cell pellet, and the supernatant is discarded. Cells were then resuspended in fresh media and seeded in 12-well plates at 200 cells/mL and 300 cells/mL concentrations for A549 and HT-29, respectively. LLC-1 cells were also processed the same way to seed 200 cells/well. After 48 hours, seeding cells were treated with LB1 or vehicle, as previously mentioned. Radiation treatment was added 24 hours after the drug treatment in the respective plates. The media were changed 24 hours after the radiation treatment. After the designated treatment(s) were finished, plates were washed with 1% PBS, and fresh medium was added to each well by cell type 24 hours after the radiation treatment (96 hours after the treatment started with LB1/vehicle). Plates were then incubated for 2 weeks, and the medium was changed for all plates every 48 hours. Then, the medium was removed from all plates, and cells were fixed with 75% ethanol in PBS and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Plates were washed gently using water to remove the extra crystal violet stain, imaged via a flatbed scanner, and transferred to Image J for colony counting and analysis.

In vivo mice studies

All C57BL/6NTac, stock B6, male and female mice were obtained from Taconic Biosciences. Background, 10- to 12-week-old, wild-type (W+/+) mice were purchased from Taconic Bioscience. A subcutaneous (SQ) tumor was generated on the flank of each mouse with LLC-1 cells (50,000 cells/tumor) suspended in 100 µL of PBS as described.8,18 Cells were injected via a BD 1 mL syringe with an 18-gauge hypodermic needle. LLC-1 cells injected into mice were tested to be mycobacterium-free to avoid any potential adjuvant effect. Pulpable size (2-3 mm in diameter) tumor bearings were randomized to different cohorts for treatment. All mice were maintained in the Institutional Animal Core Facility following the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approved protocol (Protocols IACUC-15-040 and 17-007).

Mice treatments, preparations, and schedule

Before treatments began, mice with tumors were randomized into cohorts. Mice under the RT or RT combination cohorts received a single dose of 8 Gy computed tomography (CT) image guided radiation therapy (IGRT) on day 0 of the study.15 Mice receiving RT alone and the control mice received 6 intertumoral and 6 IP injections of the same volume of the vehicle (50 µL of PBS+) each. (PBS+ was designated by adding 0.2% of DMSO (LB1 solvent) to PBS.) Mice under LB1 or LB1 combination cohorts received a total of 6 doses of approximately 1.7 mg/kg each of LB1 every 3 days via 50 µL intratumoral injections. In all cases, working solutions were diluted with PBS. Anti-PD1 was prepared by diluting stock solutions from BIOXCELL with PBS. Anti-PD1 injection was administered to mice via IP injection for 6 doses at 200 µg/50 µL of PBS.18 All experiments were performed following the Institutional Animal Protocol.

Tumor volume assay, mice survival assay, toxicity monitoring

The efficacy of treatments used in the in vivo mice studies was determined primarily by tumor volume and mice survival assays. The toxicity of the treatments toward mice was monitored via body weight and body score.

To observe the tumor growth, a Vernier caliper was used to measure the length and width of the SQ tumors. The dimension of imaginary longitude to the leg was designated as length, and the perpendicular was designated as width. The tumors were measured between the skin surface layers. Tumor volumes were calculated as (length × width2)/2. The tumor volume was plotted against time. Mouse body weight and body score were taken regularly. Data for tumor volume and mice bodyweight/score were transferred to Excel to be further analyzed.

Mice's survival was monitored by recording the number of days each mouse stayed alive throughout their respective treatments in this study. Day 0 was considered the first day, and the amount of time spent alive was recorded in Excel until a humane endpoint was reached. Mice were euthanized for 3 reasons: ulcerated tumors (>5 mm of ulcer diameter or deep ulcer), the SQ tumor had become large, and the mice exhibited a debilitating state (increased lethargy) from health complications. Mice survival data were transferred to GraphPad Prism for further analysis and data plotting. All animals were housed in the Institutional Animal Care facility, and the experiments complied with the IACUC guidelines and regulations.

Cell irradiation, CT imaging, and IGRT to mice

A small animal radiation research platform (SARRP, Xtrahl, Inc) at the institutional animal facility was used for x-ray radiation of the cells (2-6 Gy) using 220 kVp energy. CT imaging was performed in mice using 65 kVp of energy and 0.8 mA. CT IGRT was performed under 220 kVp of energy, 13 mA, with a 0.15 mm copper (Cu) filter. A 10 × 10 mm2 collimator was used to deliver 1 × 8 Gy of x-ray irradiation to the palpable SQ tumors, at a rate of 360 cGy/min.22 Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane for both CT images and IGRT delivery. Studies support the hypothesis that high doses (8 Gy) of gamma-irradiation can trigger a p53-independent apoptotic mechanism, possibly through mitotic catastrophe, in epithelial cells in vivo.23 Guided by previous studies using myricetin, a single dose of 8 Gy of IGRT was delivered to the mice.19 All experiments were performed in accordance with the institutional IACUC guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Survival data were plotted, and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v7.0. The Kaplan-Meier statistics (Madsen 1986, Statistical concepts, Prentice Hall) were used. A log-rank test was employed to determine the P value for the Kaplan-Meier curves. Statistical analyses for tumor volume were achieved using analysis of variance (2 factors with replication) and a standard 2-tailed Student’s t-test. *P< .05, **P < .01, ***P< .001, and ****P < .0001 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The synthetic LB1 was generated by substituting a hydroxyl ion group in the B ring of the myricetin with a chloride ion group (Fig. 1A). Treatment with LB1 (Fig. 1B) resulted in a dose-dependent inhibitory effect on the growth of the human colon cancer cell line HT-29, starting at 75 µM (P < .001 compared with 50 µM) (Fig. 1C), and on the human lung cancer cell line A549, beginning at 50 µM (P < .001 compared with 25 µM) (Fig. 1D), with concentrations measured using the MTT growth assay. This was further confirmed by a clonogenic survival study, which showed that the median inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of LB1 were 75 μM and 50 μM for the HT-29 and A549 cell lines, respectively. Moreover, LB1 demonstrates a significantly higher growth inhibitory effect on lung cancer cells (P < .001) than on colon cancer cells, with an effective treatment concentration starting at 30 µM (Fig. 1E and Fig. E1A, B). Based on these data, we continued our investigation into lung cancer. To explore further, we also added radiation to the LB1 treatment, because radiation plays a significant role in lung and other cancer treatments.24 Myricetin, the parent compound of LB1, also demonstrates potential as a radiosensitizer.25 We used lung cancer cell lines for treatment with LB1 and radiation (Fig. 1F). For further in vivo confirmation, we intend to use a mouse lung cancer model with the LLC1 cell line. To examine the antineoplastic effect of LB1 in the mouse lung cancer cell line (LLC1), we performed a clonogenic survival assay of LLC1 alongside the A549 cell line and confirmed the antineoplastic effect of LB1 on both human and mouse lung adenocarcinoma cancer cell lines under 25 and 75 µM doses of LB1 (Fig. 1H and Fig. E2C, D). Next, for in vivo studies, we analyzed the effect of LB1 on tumor regression with or without CT IGRT using an SARRP irradiation facility (Fig. 2A, B). Here, we used a single fraction of 8 Gy of radiation19,23 (Fig. 2C). Starting from the same day, the LB1 and RT+LB1 cohorts were given the LB1 treatment with radiation to the palpable-sized tumors, intratumorally, with 1.77 mg/kg body weight per dose for 6 doses, following the safest dose of myricetin in an animal model19 (Fig. 3A, B and Fig. E3). Tumor volume kinetics were measured along with survival percentages, overall body scores, and body weights. Studies have shown that flavonoids, including myricetin, induce apoptosis, leading to immunogenic cancer cell death and exposing cancer-specific antigens to the immune system.20 This initiates tumor-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell activation, causing direct cancer cell death.8,20 Checkpoint inhibitors, such as anti-PD1 antibodies, further enhance the T cell-mediated anticancer effects of the immune system.18 To further explore the effectiveness of LB1 in combination with immunotherapy, such as anti-PD1, we performed another in vivo experiment (since in vitro testing is not feasible for this) using C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 4A, B and Fig. E4). Our study on day 12 posttreatment shows that adding anti-PD1 with LB1 (LB1+anti-PD1) further reduces the tumor volume progression, anti-PD1: 36.5 ± 42.88-fold and LB1+anti-PD1 15.11 ± 8.62-fold increase from the “day 0” of the treatment started with statistical significance of P < .05. Adding a single dose of radiation further reduces tumor growth progression (LB1+anti-PD1+RT: 2.47 ± 1.26; P < .0001). Accordingly, we further inferred that a combination of LB1+anti-PD1 reduces tumor growth progression (15.11 ± 8.62) compared with treatment with RT (35.57 ± 22.10, P < .001) or LB1 (21.95 ± 14.65, P < .01 (Fig. 4C), and improves the survival percentage significantly compared with the single-treated group with anti-PD1 (P < .05). However, lifespan was further prolonged by adding a low-dose radiation treatment (P < .001 for LB1, P < .001 for RT, and P < .01 for anti-PD1) (Fig. 4D), with no significant differences in mouse body weight or body score (Fig. 4E, F). Taken together, these data indicate that LB1, when combined with a single dose of radiation, significantly improves survival rate and duration in mice. Discussion

We observed that adding LB1 to the radiation treatment significantly radiosensitized. A single radiation dose was used in this pilot study for simplicity. Detailed studies comparing various radiation doses, fractionated RT regimens, FLASH RT, or proton RT can provide additional insights into the potential radiosensitizing effects of LB1. The future combination of LB1 with internally administered radiopharma-ceuticals,21,26 brachytherapy,27 and theranostic agents, including 225Ac, 90Y, or 131/125/123I,28, 29, 30 which create inhomogeneous radiation distributions26,31 targeted to metastatic tumors, is another interesting possibility to explore. The current data support the notion that combining LB1 with radiation could allow lower radiation doses for lung cancer treatment to reduce radiation-related toxicity. Preliminary data also show that adding LB1 to the current treatment regimen, in combination with immunotherapy (radiation and checkpoint inhibitor, PD1), may further reduce tumor progression and increase overall survival. Further investigation is needed to confirm this latter point.

Furthermore, direct intratumoral administration of LB1 is not very pragmatic for human NSCLC. To deliver LB1 locally to lung cancer tissue, alternative approaches can be contemplated. For example, we recently demonstrated an aerosolized nanoparticle formulation for the flavonoid hesperetin. Aerosol delivery of hesperetin in a nanoparticulate format, in combination with immunotherapy, resulted in preferential uptake by lung tumor tissue and greater overall efficacy.32 Such approaches may also be considered for nanoparticle-LB1 formulations for aerosolized delivery.

In terms of LB1 toxicity, like previous investigations with myricetin and other flavonoids, we did not observe any significant differences in body weight or body score between the treatment groups and the controls. Although detailed pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and toxicity studies were not conducted here, in our experience with other flavonoids, we did not observe any renal or hepatic functional abnormalities.32 In conclusion, in this preliminary study, we showed that the novel myricetin derivative LB1 has potential as an anticancer agent and synergizes with RT. Adding a low dose of a chemotherapeutic or immunotherapeutic agent further enhances the LB1 antitumor activity.
