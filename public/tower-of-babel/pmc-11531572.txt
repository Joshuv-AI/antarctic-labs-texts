Suppression of magnetic vortex losses in submicron NbN coplanar waveguide resonators
Authors: Ariel Roitman, Leonid Burlachkov, Amos Sharoni, Avner Shaulov, Yosef Yeshurun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a method for improving the performance of microwave coplanar resonators in magnetic fields, by using narrow superconducting strips of width close to the London penetration depth. In a range of low fields, the narrow strips inhibit the presence of magnetic vortices, thus preventing the generation of losses caused by their motion, leading to enhanced resistance to magnetic fields. Our method provides a more straightforward solution compared to previously proposed techniques designed to restrict vortex motion, holding potential for the development of improved devices based on microwave resonators. Introduction

Superconducting microwave resonators are employed in a broad range of applications, including microwave kinetic inductance detectors (MKIDs), circuit quantum electrodynamics, and quantum bits (qubits)1–9. The resilience of these resonators to magnetic fields is pivotal for stable and consistent performance in environments where magnetic fields are present. For example, the internal quality factor, , of MKIDs is significantly deteriorated by magnetic fields10,11. This has been attributed to losses caused by motion of magnetic vortices. Various strategies have been explored in earlier studies to minimize these losses by restraining vortex motion12–22. This was achieved, e.g., by incorporating artificial pinning centers13–19. However, these methods fall short of preventing vortex entry into the resonators. While pinning centers significantly reduce losses due to vortex motion, the presence of vortices leads to an increase in the density of quasi-particles, giving rise to losses and alterations in the resonance frequency.

In this paper, we propose a method to eliminate vortices altogether by using thin superconducting strips of width smaller than the London penetration depth, . In this geometry, the Pearl penetration depth23, , (d is the film thickness) is significantly larger than the strip width, w, preventing the presence of any vortices within the resonator below a certain critical field24. As demonstrated later in this paper, the resonance frequency and the quality factor of such a resonator exhibit significantly higher resilience to magnetic fields compared to resonators with strips of width larger than This approach not only inhibits vortex formation but also enables a more compact, high-impedance resonator.

Experimental

Here we compare two types of resonators, made of NbN strips of widths 10 μm and 200 nm. Half-wavelength spiral coplanar waveguide resonators coupled to a 50 Ω transmission line were designed using the SONNET electromagnetic simulation software, see Fig. 1. In zero magnetic field and they exhibited resonance frequencies of 3.120 and 3.631 GHz, respectively. The NbN strips were fabricated from a 50 nm thick NbN layer with that was deposited onto a sapphire substrate using DC reactive magnetron sputtering (for further details, see supplementary). The lithography was conducted using the Elionix ELS 100 keV e-beam lithography machine. Both resonators were fabricated simultaneously on the same NbN film to ensure consistency and eliminate material and fabrication differences. A resist mask, 495PMMA A6, was employed, and Reactive Ion Etching (RIE) with Cl2/BCl3 gas was used to pattern the resonators. Subsequently, the resist was removed using acetone to eliminate any potential impact on the resonators. After fabrication, the transition temperatures of the narrow and wide resonators reduced to 9.3 and 11.5 K, respectively.

A clear difference between the behavior of the narrow and wide resonators is also demonstrated when comparing the increasing and decreasing of the external field, (see Fig. 4). In contrast to the wide resonator, the narrow one shows no hysteresis in both and upon increasing and decreasing the field. The hysteresis in the wide resonator is due to pinning of vortices causing different distribution of vortices on increasing and decreasing the field, as previously discussed by Bothner et al.11,13. The absence of vortices in the narrow resonator gives rise to reversible behavior without hysteresis.

Finally, the narrow resonator exhibits no distinction between FC and ZFC data up to ~ 1100 Oe (see inset to Fig. 4a), after which they diverge. As previously discussed, the Bean-Livingstone barrier is absent in FC measurements, and, therefore, according to Eq. 2 the initial vortex penetration in FC is theoretically expected at ≅850 Oe, in a reasonable agreement with our experimental results. In contrast, the wide resonator displays a notable difference between ZFC and FC measurements, since in both cases vortex penetration occurs at fields close to 0. Such a difference between the ZFC and FC measurements in a wide resonator was observed previously and attributed to the combined effect of vortex pinning and screening currents resulting from field gradients in the ZFC, whereas in FC such currents are absent11.

Conclusion

We have successfully fabricated a microwave coplanar resonator, employing narrow superconducting with width close to the London penetration depth. The magnetic field dependence of both the resonance frequency and the Q-factor in this resonator differ markedly from that observed in the resonator with wide strips, exhibiting a considerably enhanced resistance to magnetic field. This improvement is ascribed to the presence of a uniform field and the absence of vortices within the narrow strip. The results of this research may significantly contribute to the development of improved devices that exploit microwave resonators, such as MKIDs and qubits.
