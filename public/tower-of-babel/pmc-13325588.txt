The association between plasma IgG N-glycosylation and viral encephalitis in children: a hospital-based case-control study
Authors: Yan Zhang, Zi Lin, Hui Niu, Yinghao Wang, Xiangqian Jin, Lihua Tang, Haifeng Hou, Cancan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundViral encephalitis (VE) is an acute inflammatory disease caused by viral infection. Children are at a significantly higher risk of developing VE than adults. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) N-glycosylation plays a key role in regulating the balance between anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory responses. This study aimed to investigate the profile of IgG N-glycosylation among children with VE.MethodsThis case-control study included 117 children with VE and 117 healthy controls. Plasma IgG N-glycans were detected using hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography with the ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (HILIC-UPLC). An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit was applied to detect inflammatory cytokines. Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) was performed to investigate the correlation between IgG N-glycans and inflammatory cytokines. The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) and machine learning algorithms were used to identify the significant glycans.ResultsAmong 24 initially detected glycans, 14 were significantly higher and 3 lower in children with VE compared to the healthy controls (P < 0.05). Moreover, children with VE showed lower levels of fucosylation (P < 0.001) and agalactosylation (G0) (P < 0.001), whereas higher levels of bisecting N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) (P = 0.026), sialylation (P < 0.001), and monogalactosylation (G1) (P < 0.001). Notably, the level of bisecting GlcNAc was significantly higher in VE children who had a shorter hospital stay compared to those with a longer hospital stay (P = 0.034). The levels of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin-2 (IL-2), IL-6, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were significantly higher in children with VE than in the healthy controls (P < 0.001). IgG N-glycans composition was strongly correlated with inflammatory cytokines (r = 0.849). GP1, GP3, GP6, GP7, GP9, GP16, GP21, GP22, GP23, and GP24 were identified by Boruta algorithms to develop a glycan-based diagnostic model for VE. The areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUCs) were 0.987 for the training set and 0.998 for the validation set.ConclusionIgG N-glycosylation may contribute to the pathogenesis of VE. IgG N-glycans may act as potential biomarkers for monitoring VE in high-risk children.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-026-02273-y.

Background

Viral encephalitis (VE) is an acute infectious inflammatory disease induced by DNA or RNA viruses. It progresses rapidly and poses a particularly high risk to children, often leading to adverse physical and neurological outcomes [1]. In children, the annual incidence of VE is around 16/100,000 person-years at 2 years of age, stays high until 10 years of age, and drops to approximately 1/100,000 person-years by 15 years of age [2]. VE typically occurs after the virus spreads hematogenously to the central nervous system [3]. The severity of the disease varies between individuals, and patients with severe infection may develop progressive neurological damage or even die [4].

The early diagnosis of VE is difficult due to lack of clinically specific manifestations. At present, the main diagnostic methods for VE include cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) etiological examination, electroencephalogram (EEG), head computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [5, 6]. However, due to low specificity and high cost, EEG, CT, and MRI are generally not employed as routine examination methods for VE [7]. CSF examination can reveal pathological changes associated with nervous system infections; however, it clinical utility may be limited by invasiveness and the timing of sampling [8]. Thus, there is an urgent need to discover biomarkers that can provide new approaches for the early screening and targeted prevention of VE.

Glycosylation, a crucial post-translational modification [9], involves the transfer of oligosaccharides to specific sites on proteins [10]. There are several different isoforms of glycosylation, among which N-glycosylation is the most common form [11]. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) is the core component of the human immune system, playing a key role in immune defense [12, 13]. IgG comprises a variable region [F(ab)₂] and a Fragment crystallizable (Fc) region [14]. Each IgG molecule contains two N-linked glycosylation sites located at asparagine 297 (Asn-297) in the Fc region of the heavy chains [15]. These sites allow for the attachment of various sugar residues, including core fucose, bisecting N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc), sialic acid, and galactose [16]. The glycan residues can regulate complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) and antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), thus influencing the effector functions of IgG [17–19]. Studies have indicated that IgG N-glycosylation maintains a relatively stable level in healthy individuals but may be altered under both physiological disorders and disease conditions [20, 21]. N-glycosylation of IgG has been identified as a possible biomarker of multiple diseases, such as inflammatory, chronic, and neurodegenerative diseases, demonstrating considerable diagnostic potential [22–24]. Thus, we conducted this study to investigate the profile of IgG N-glycosylation in children with VE and healthy children, and to examine its association with VE.

Methods

Study participants

This case-control study enrolled 117 children with VE and 117 healthy controls from Taian Maternal and Child Health Hospital, Taian Central Hospital, and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University between October 2023 and December 2024. The inclusion criteria for VE were as follows: (1) under the age of 16; (2) blood samples available; (3) meeting the clinical diagnostic criteria for VE; and (4) clinical information and follow-up data available. The exclusion criteria were (1) patients with autoimmune encephalopathy; (2) those with leukodystrophy or metabolic encephalopathy; (3) individuals with mental disorders; and (4) patients who had received antiviral treatment before or shortly after admission.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University (No. 2023-074). Informed consent was obtained from legal guardians.

Diagnosis of VE

The diagnosis of viral encephalitis was established based on clinical manifestations, CSF examination, and auxiliary investigations. Clinically, patients presented with fever (≥ 38 °C) accompanied by headache, vomiting, and/or nuchal rigidity, together with altered mental status lasting no less than 24 h, including decreased consciousness, altered awareness, lethargy, or personality changes, not attributable to other causes. CSF analysis obtained via lumbar puncture (LP) revealed at least one of the following: elevated intracranial pressure (> 200 mmH₂O), pleocytosis (white blood cell count ≥ 5/µL), elevated protein (> 0.4 g/L), normal or mildly decreased chloride, or clear to slightly turbid appearance. Neuroimaging or electroencephalographic findings were also required, with MRI showing T2/FLAIR hyperintensities, diffusion restriction on DWI, brain edema, hemorrhagic changes, or asymmetric involvement of the temporal lobes, frontal lobes, insula, thalamus, or brainstem, or EEG demonstrating diffuse or focal slowing, periodic lateralized epileptiform discharges (PLEDs), or epileptiform activity [25]. Patients fulfilling all three criteria were classified as having clinically diagnosed viral encephalitis.

Collection and storage of blood samples

Blood samples were collected from children with VE within 72 h of disease onset. After an overnight fast, 2 mL of venous blood was collected and divided into two tubes. Plasma was separated from blood collected in EDTA-containing vacuum tubes for IgG N-glycans analysis, whereas serum separation was conducted on samples in non-EDTA tubes to determine inflammatory cytokines and blood biochemical parameters.

Assessment of demographic characteristics and biochemical indicators

Demographic data were collected through interviews with participants and their guardians. Biochemical indicators were measured using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The indicators included white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), absolute neutrophil count (NEUT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), hemoglobin (Hb), and platelet (PLT).

Analysis of inflammatory cytokines

The levels of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin-2 (IL-2), IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) were quantified using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, China) [26].

Analysis of IgG glycans

IgG N-glycosylation was measured as previously reported [16, 27]. Briefly, a 100 µL plasma sample was added to a 96-well protein G monolithic plate (BIA Separations, Slovenia) for the separation of IgG. The isolated IgG samples were denatured with 1 mL of 0.1 mol/L formic acid and immediately neutralized with 1 mol/L ammonium bicarbonate. To release N-glycans, 4 µL of PNGase F enzyme was added to the IgG samples, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 18 h. The liberated N-glycans were subsequently labeled with 2-aminobenzamide and placed in an oven at 65 °C for 3 h. Finally, IgG N-glycans were analyzed by hydrophilic interaction ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (HILIC-UPLC), resulting in 24 IgG glycan peaks (GPs). Reference datasets established using mass spectrometry were used to assign glycan structures [28]. Furthermore, based on the initial glycan measurements, 54 derived glycan traits were calculated [29].

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was conducted to assess the normality of quantitative data. Normally distributed data were presented in the form of mean ± standard deviation (SD) and assessed by an independent samples t-test. Conversely, non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (P₂₅-P₇₅) and evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test. Qualitative data were presented as n (%) and compared by the chi-square test. Pearson correlation analysis was applied to normally distributed variables, whereas Spearman correlation analysis was applied to data that did not conform to a normal distribution. Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) was utilized to investigate the overall association between IgG N-glycans and inflammatory cytokines.

Participants were divided into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio [30]. Within the training set, we applied four machine learning algorithms for glycan selection: LASSO with 5-fold cross-validation, Boruta and XGBoost (top 10 features by importance), and SVM-RFE with 5-fold repeated cross-validation. Stepwise logistic regression was applied to adjust for age and other clinical covariates. To prevent information leakage, all feature selection was performed exclusively on the training set, with final model performance assessed on the independent validation set. All random seeds and algorithm parameters were documented to ensure reproducibility. Finally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the performance of the glycan-based diagnostic models.

Statistical analyses were carried out with SPSS software (version 27.0, IBM, USA) and R software (version 4.5.0, R Core Team, New Zealand). A P value < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.

In addition, we calculated 54 derived glycan traits based on the initial glycan measurements [31]. As indicated in Supplementary Table 2, 38 of the 54 derived glycan traits exhibited differences between the children with VE and the healthy controls.

Correlation of IgG N-glycans with inflammatory cytokines

IgG N-glycan composition was significantly correlated with inflammatory cytokines (canonical r = 0.849, P < 0.001). As shown in Fig. 3, nine glycans (GP1, GP3, GP7, GP9, GP16, GP19, GP21, GP23, GP24) were associated with IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, hs-CRP, and IFN-γ. GP24 showed the strongest loading among canonical variables (loading = -0.634), with IL-6 demonstrating the highest canonical loading (-0.931). As shown in the results of the Spearman correlation analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2), IL-10 had a negative correlation with GP1, GP3, GP7, GP9, GP16, GP19, GP21, and GP24, while the other inflammatory cytokines showed positive correlations with these glycans.

Diagnostic model of VE using IgG glycans

To develop a diagnostic model for VE children, the LASSO algorithm and logistic regression analysis were performed (Fig. 4A and B), identifying seven significant glycans (GP1, GP4, GP5, GP9, GP17, GP22, and GP24). Furthermore, three machine learning algorithms (Boruta, XGBoost, and SVM-RFE) were applied to develop glycan-based diagnostic models, respectively (Fig. 4C for Boruta, 4D for XGBoost, and 4E–F for SVM-RFE). As shown in Fig. 5, all four models showed satisfactory performance for the diagnosis of VE, and Model 2 exhibited the best performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.987 and a 95% confidence interval (95%CI) of 0.975 to 0.999. In the validation dataset, the AUC values of the four glycan-based models were 0.994 (95%CI: 0.985, 1.000), 0.998 (95%CI: 0.992, 1.000), 1.000 (95%CI: 1.000, 1.000), and 0.991 (95%CI: 0.979, 1.000), respectively.

In addition, to address the potential confounding effect of age, we employed four machine learning algorithms (LASSO, Boruta, XGBoost, SVM-RFE) to identify discriminative GPs between VE and control groups. The selected GPs from each method were subsequently entered into stepwise logistic regression models, with forced inclusion of age and key clinical covariates (NEUT, HB, PLT, ALT, and AST) to control for potential confounding effects. Notably, despite different GPs being selected by the four machine learning approaches, consistent independent predictors emerged across all final models: GP1, GP9, GP21, GP22, GP24, and age. This convergence across multiple analytical strategies strengthens the reliability of our findings. ROC curve analysis demonstrated excellent discriminative ability for the multivariate model incorporating glycan markers (GP1, GP9, GP21, GP22, GP24) and age, with AUC = 0.990 (95% CI: 0.980, 1.000) in the training set and AUC = 0.991 (95% CI: 0.979, 1.000) in the validation set (Fig. S3).

Discussion

VE is a common infectious disease of the central nervous system in children [33]. Due to immature immune systems in children, viruses can readily penetrate the blood-brain barrier [34]. The diagnosis of VE is based primarily on identifying viruses or viral antibodies in CSF obtained by LP. However, up to 60% of cases remain etiologically unexplained [35–37]. Studies have shown that alterations in IgG N-glycosylation are linked to a variety of viral diseases, including Haemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS), Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19), dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF), and West Nile fever (WNF) [38–40]. Therefore, identifying specific features of IgG N-glycosylation may facilitate auxiliary diagnosis and enable early screening of high-risk children, allowing timely and targeted clinical intervention.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize IgG N-glycosylation profiles in children with VE. Figure 6 illustrates the proposed biological mechanism linking altered IgG N-glycosylation to VE pathogenesis. We observed that compared with the healthy controls, children with VE had decreased fucosylation, along with increased sialylation and bisected GlcNAc. Levels of hs-CRP, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, and IL-6 were elevated, while IL-10 was reduced in VE children than in controls. These findings suggest that abnormal IgG N-glycosylation of may contribute to VE development by modulating cytokine networks and immune cell activation [41, 42]. Furthermore, we developed a glycan-based diagnostic model for screening VE, that demonstrated robust diagnostic efficacy.

During the pathogenesis of VE, the neuroinflammatory response plays a key role [43]. Owing to viral stimulation and neuronal injury, marked inflammatory responses occur within the brain tissue [34]. Studies have found that the expression of TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-6 is increased in virus-infected diseases [44, 45], consistent with our results. TNF-α is widely regarded as a major pro-inflammatory cytokine, which causes damage to the respiratory epithelium and stimulates collagen synthesis and fibrosis in the respiratory system [46]. Elevated TNF-α promotes the progression of viral infection and development of organ damage [47]. IL-6 is a prototypical cytokine with functional pleiotropy, which acts as a pro-inflammatory cytokine to up-regulate inflammatory response during infection or tissue injury [48]. In contrast, IL-10 is an important anti-inflammatory cytokine that serves to restrict immune cell activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production [49]. This study showed that IL-10 was reduced in VE children compared with the controls.

Core fucosylated N-glycans are widely distributed in a variety of proteins and are altered under certain pathological conditions [50]. Fucosylation of IgG inhibits the activation of ADCC by reducing binding to FcγRIIIa receptors on natural killer (NK) cells. This mechanism decreases pro-inflammatory cytokine release and immune cell activation, thus downregulating IgG-mediated pro-inflammatory activity [51, 52]. Our results showed a reduction in fucosylation of IgG Fc in children with VE. Similarly, another study also showed that infection by enveloped viruses decreases the Fc core fucosylation of IgGs induced by membrane-associated antigens [53]. Moreover, IgG with lower core fucosylation stimulates monocytes to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, exacerbating the inflammatory response [54]. This study showed that levels of IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-6, hs-CRP, and TNF-α are significantly elevated in VE patients relative to the control group, which further supports the above conclusion [55–57].

Bisected GlcNAc glycosylation enhances the binding affinity between the Fc region of IgG and Fcγ activating receptors, strengthening the biological activity of IgG and further promoting the activation of intracellular signaling pathways in immune cells, ultimately inducing pro-inflammatory responses [58]. In this study, we found that the level of bisecting GlcNAc glycosylation was higher in children with VE than in the healthy controls, which is consistent with the results of bisecting GlcNAc glycosylation in patients with HFRS [38]. Furthermore, a higher level of bisecting GlcNAc was observed in VE children with shorter hospital stays compared to those with longer stays. This finding suggested that bisecting GlcNAc may be associated with short-term prognosis of children with VE. A clinical glycoproteomics study indicated that bisecting GlcNAc glycosylation is associated with therapeutic efficacy in patients with HBeAg-positive chronic hepatitis B (CHB) [59], and this correlation may influence the length of hospital stay.

Sialylation of IgG also acts as a modulator of anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory responses [60]. The spatial structure of sialylation modifications influences the immune activity of IgG, potentially due to the larger space occupied by sialic acid, reducing hinge region elasticity, consequently diminishing receptor binding capacity [61–63]. Sialic acid deficiency significantly affects IgG function, shifting it from anti-inflammatory to pro-inflammatory [29]. Studies have shown that the IgG sialylation levels are significantly reduced in patients with colorectal, ovarian, and gastric cancers [64–66]. However, our study found elevated IgG sialylation in VE patients, indicating distinct roles in viral infection compared with chronic systemic inflammation. It has been demonstrated that the level of sialylation is elevated during influenza virus or Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infections [67, 68], which is potentially linked to the expansion of antibody-secreting memory (ASM) B cell and plasmablast (PB) subsets [69].

Galactosylation can enhance IgG binding to the inhibitory FcγRIIb, boosting its anti-inflammatory functions [70]. Conversely, decreased galactosylation exposes GlcNAc residues originally covered by galactose groups, increasing IgG binding to mannose-binding lectin, upregulating CDC activity and initiating inflammation [71–73]. Previous studies have shown that galactosylation levels are closely associated with the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and CRP [74, 75]. In this study, we observed elevated IgG galactosylation and TNF-α expression in children with VE, demonstrating that IgG galactosylation is involved in the up-regulation of inflammation and the development of VE. These results are consistent with the findings reported in SARS-CoV-2 infection [31], but differ from those in chronic diseases and cancers [76]. This may be attributed to the increase in galactosylation levels, causing an increased distance between the CH2 domains and exposing more amino acid sequences that can bind to FcγRIIIa, thereby enhancing complement-dependent cytotoxicity [77].

The VE manifestations are notably diverse and nonspecific, frequently resulting in misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis in clinical practice [34]. Currently, the non-invasive IgG N-glycan assay achieves a high degree of precision in monitoring inflammatory diseases [78]. In this study, we developed a glycan-based diagnostic model to explore a pathway to improve non-invasive VE diagnosis. The findings support IgG N-glycans as a novel biomarker for monitoring VE.

This study has several limitations. First, the study utilized a case-control design, which cannot determine whether glycosylation changes are disease-specific, infection-related, or reflect systemic inflammation, making it challenging to infer the chronological sequence and causal association. Second, there was a difference in age between the VE and the control groups, which may limit the robustness of the results. Third, although we described the profiles of IgG N-glycans of VE, we did not classify the patients in accordance with the species and origins of the virus, nor did we perform comparison with other CNS infections (e.g., bacterial meningitis, autoimmune encephalitis). Fourth, participants in this study were recruited from a single center in Taian City, Northern China. This geographic restriction may limit the generalizability of our findings to populations with different genetic backgrounds, environmental exposures, and viral epidemiology. Additionally, the sample size was relatively small. Future multi-center studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these findings and enhance their clinical applicability.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that IgG N-glycosylation plays an important role in VE pathogenesis. Altered fucosylation, bisecting GlcNAc, sialylation, and galactosylation are significantly associated with VE, suggesting that IgG N-glycans may serve as noninvasive biomarkers for auxiliary diagnosis.
