Deep learning based heat transfer simulation of the casting process
Authors: Jinwu Kang, Jiwu Wang, Xiao Han, Qichao Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
To avoid the necessity of constitutional models, computational intensity, and the time-consuming nature inherent in numerical simulations, a pioneering approach utilizing deep learning techniques has been adopted to swiftly predict temperature fields during the solidification phase of casting processes. This methodology involves the development of rapid prediction models based on modified U-net network architectures, augmented by the integration of Inception and CBAM (Convolutional Block Attention Module) modules. The construction of the training set involved utilizing 200 diverse geometric models with each containing three kinds of components (casting, mold, and chill), where the temperature fields at a specific time, ti, were input data, while that of the subsequent time point, ti+1, served as the corresponding labels. The geometric models were generated by the erosion of 2D arbitrary shapes through an erosion method, and then their associated temperature fields were obtained via FDM-based numerical simulation. The trained deep learning models exhibit proficiency in promptly forecasting temperature fields during the solidification process for arbitrarily shaped castings at different times. The average accuracy of the predicted outcomes reaches 94.5% as the absolute temperature error set as 7 ℃ and the prediction just takes one second for a time step. Notably, these models are adept at handling multi-component with multi-materials within a geometry model, such as casting, chill, and mold corresponding to the intricate casting process.

Introduction

Numerical simulation, a computer-based research methodology, employs specific mathematical models to replicate real-world physical processes. It stands as a robust analytical tool for intricate physical and engineering challenges, necessitating interdisciplinary integration spanning mechanics, materials, computing, and physics. The fundamental step in numerical simulation involves formulating partial differential equations (PDEs) and subsequently solving them using numerical methods. Common methods include the finite element method (FEM), finite difference method (FDM), and finite volume method (FVM). In their work, Liu et al.1 integrated FDM for heat transfer simulations and FEM for stress analysis in castings by converting temperature results into thermal loads within FEM for stress evolution. Schneider et al.2 combined a staggered-grid FVM for discretizing the Navier-Stokes equations in the free-flow subdomain with a vertex-centered FVM in the porous medium. Addressing complex problems typically involves adopting multiple methods to solve multi-physical equations across various scales. Tong et al.3 introduced a diverse array of numerical simulation methods for heat transfer and fluid flow issues, encompassing macroscopic continuum methods, mesoscopic particle-based lattice Boltzmann methods, direct simulation Monte Carlo, dissipative particle dynamics, and microscopic molecular dynamics. Computational tasks involve hardware components such as hard drives, memory units, CPUs, graphics cards, and more. Initially, discretizing the Computer-Aided Design (CAD) model is essential, which can be challenging for intricate objects. Calculations with numerous meshes can be time-consuming, and achieving convergence may pose challenges for some problems. Notably, altering even one parameter for optimization often necessitates restarting the entire process from scratch.

Artificial intelligence (AI) stands in stark contrast to numerical simulation, as it emulates the intricate structure of the human brain, specifically its layers of neurons, to drive decision-making processes. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have found widespread application in image processing tasks such as image segmentation and recognition4–6.

In recent years, a growing number of researchers have endeavored to integrate deep learning methodologies into predictive tasks as a substitute for traditional numerical simulations. These investigations span across disciplines such as mechanics, thermodynamics, fluid flow, and other scientific domains. Raissi et al.7 pioneered a concealed fluid mechanics framework. This innovative approach leverages a physics-informed deep learning architecture capable of embedding the Navier-Stokes equations into neural networks, operating independently of specific geometries or initial and boundary conditions. This advancement enables the direct extraction of velocity and pressure fields from images. Hemmasian et al.8 delved into simulating the temperature dynamics within a molten pool’s cross-section under varying pressure and velocity scenarios. Their methodology involved the fusion of two convolutional neural networks through transfer learning, yielding relative root mean squared errors ranging from 2 to 3%. Kim et al.9, on the other hand, employed a convolutional neural network to forecast temperature distribution and heat flux based on the thermal attributes and geometric configuration of a slab-wall building structure. Additionally, they utilized a multi-layer perceptron to estimate heat loss and thermal bridge coefficients. Remarkably, the network exhibited the ability to predict heat transfer processes within a second, showcasing average error rates of 0.09% and 0.18% for predicted heat loss and thermal bridge coefficients, respectively. Nonetheless, these studies have primarily focused on individual materials or overlooked geometric considerations. The utilization of deep learning models as surrogates for addressing numerical simulation challenges remains in its nascent stages. Several critical issues, including complexities arising from three-dimensional spaces, intricate geometries, diverse materials, multiple scales, and evolving boundary conditions, necessitate further exploration and resolution.

Casting stands as a pivotal manufacturing method for producing parts, with the defects, microstructures, and properties of castings intricately linked to their solidification processes. Analyzing the temperature distributions during solidification can aid in predicting defects like shrinkage porosity, cavities, and hot cracks, along with determining grain orientations and mechanical characteristics of castings. Chen et al.10 provided a comprehensive overview of the historical evolution and current state of numerical simulations in the casting process, encompassing aspects from solidification to stress fields. Furthermore, Fix et al.11 ventured into predicting solidification zones within a circular slab caster using a modified SolSlab deep learning model, yielding a relative deviation of approximately 14%.

Consequently, this paper proposes the utilization of modified U-net neural networks integrated with attention and Inception modules as surrogate models for simulating a real manufacturing process, casting, accounting for diverse shapes with multiple material types, and solidification with latent heat release and varying thermal properties with temperature, and multi-boundary conditions.

Scheme of AI-based modeling and simulation of the casting process

The casting process is a profoundly intricate procedure, encompassing the infusion of molten metal into a mold followed by its subsequent solidification. Throughout solidification, a myriad of phenomena occur, including heat transfer, solute migration, microstructure evolution, as well as stress and strain alterations, rendering the process exceptionally complex. While various commercial software packages specialize in casting processes, and some general finite element analysis tools offer capabilities for numerical simulations of casting, the process itself demands constitutive equations, discretization methods, and considerable computational time. At the core of the casting process lies heat transfer, governing solidification patterns, stress development, microstructural transformations, and the emergence of defects like shrinkage porosity, cracks, and deformations. Consequently, delving into heat transfer dynamics during casting serves as a focal point for deep learning investigations, encompassing the preparation of training datasets, network model construction, and methods for assessing accuracy and practical applications.

The schematic representation of AI-driven modeling and simulation of the casting process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Initially, foundational casting shapes are established, which are then sculpted to attain intricate forms, with discernment and removal of isolated regions. Subsequently, risers are introduced atop the castings and chills at their base, culminating in the creation of comprehensive sets of casting and ancillary models. These models undergo analysis using the finite difference method (FDM) to derive a sequence of temperature distributions at distinct time intervals. The geometric models are then paired with their respective temperature fields to forge the training dataset. Simultaneously, an AI model is devised by modifying the U-net model, followed by training and validation using the prepared dataset. Ultimately, the trained AI model proves instrumental in predicting temperature fields for diverse casting shapes with accuracy and versatility.

CBAM-U-net and I-U-net models

For the 2D simulation of heat transfer in casting processes, the network model necessitates inputting images of geometric models, initial conditions, and boundary conditions, with the output comprising images depicting the fields of physical variables. Neural networks (NN) typically handle a limited number of input and output variables, posing challenges for numerical simulations involving 2D and 3D field complexities. To address this, the U-net model, originally designed for image segmentation—accepting images as input and producing images as output—stands out as the foundational model for this simulation endeavor.

Enhancements to the U-net model have led to the development of the CBAM-U-net and I-U-net models, illustrated in Fig. 2, where CBAM denotes the Convolutional Block Attention Module, and I signifies the Inception module. The initial module is bifurcated into two distinct modules, each tasked with receiving input regarding the geometric shape of the casting model and the temperature field at a specific instant. These inputs are amalgamated in the depth dimension before proceeding to the subsequent down-sampling module. Both down-sampling and up-sampling sequences consist of three layers. Each down-sampling layer comprises a 2 × 2 pooling step and two 3 × 3 convolution operations. In the I-U-net, the first convolution operation of each down-sampling layer became the Inception module12. In the CBAM-U-net, the CBAM module13 was added after the second convolution operation of the last two down-sampling layers. The I-U-net and the CBAM-U-net had the same up-sampling stages, each involving a 3 × 3 deconvolution operation and two 3 × 3 convolution operations. At last, a 1 × 1 convolution step was added. The final output mirrors the input image size, facilitating a comprehensive depiction of the simulated physical variables.

Preparation of training and validation sets

The cornerstone of the neural network lies in its training phase. Hence, meticulous preparation of a comprehensive training dataset becomes imperative. To uphold the precision and efficacy of the I-U-net model, a diverse array of casting geometries must be encompassed within the training set, capturing a broad spectrum of casting features. Simultaneously, temperature field data at various time instants should be meticulously curated for each distinct geometry, a task executed through numerical simulation methodologies. Post-training, the AI model seamlessly generates predictions devoid of the constraints associated with traditional numerical simulations, embodying a swift and direct predictive capability.

Preparation of casting models

Generation of basic geometrical models

Constructing CAD models for a multitude of geometries directly through CAD software poses a significant challenge. To circumvent this obstacle, a novel approach has been introduced for the creation of diverse 2D geometries. Leveraging the expansive character databases available in document editing platforms like Microsoft Word or WPS, which encompass characters from various languages and a plethora of symbols, each characterized by distinct shapes, serves as the foundation for this innovative method. In pursuit of a comprehensive casting process, the mold dimensions are predefined, establishing a framework within which the castings are contained. Characters and symbols, meticulously typed into a document file with uniform size, character spacing, and line spacing, are utilized as the primary shapes for castings. Subsequently, these character images are captured, with each character or symbol meticulously extracted from the image based on a consistent outline size. The document background is rendered black, with the characters displayed in white, ensuring clear visibility and demarcation. This method offers a streamlined approach to generate a vast array of 2D basic casting shapes. By inputting hundreds or even thousands of characters—ranging from English and Greek characters to Chinese characters and fundamental mathematical symbols—a diverse repertoire of shapes is easily attainable, as depicted in Fig. S1.

Erosion of basic models

To enhance the diversity of casting geometries, a novel Random Erosion Method (REM) was introduced. This method employs a circle as an erosion source, traversing the image field to gradually erode the casting geometries upon encountering casting pixels. The circle’s placement within the image is randomized, and multiple erosion iterations are conducted to generate a multitude of new casting shapes, as illustrated in Fig. S2. The resulting eroded shapes may not always constitute a single contiguous piece. In such instances, it becomes imperative to assess the topological aspects of the shapes. Any isolated pieces identified are subjected to scrutiny, with the largest segment retained while the rest are eliminated. To facilitate this process, a two-pass method is employed to distinguish and retain the predominant casting shapes. Standardizing the image size to 128 × 128 pixels, the casting dimensions are constrained within a range of approximately spanning 0.3 to 0.8 of the image size. This controlled framework ensures the generation of diverse and appropriately scaled casting shapes, enriching the variability and complexity of the casting geometries under consideration.

Extension of mold and chill models

Drawing upon the generated casting models, the inclusion of risers and chills is imperative to accurately mirror real-world casting dynamics. By assessing the upper and lower boundaries of these models, a systematic approach was adopted: a riser was generated by extending the top section of the casting model to the image’s uppermost border at a taper angle such as 2°, rendered in white to align visually with the casting itself. Conversely, the lower segment of the casting was elongated by 20 pixels, symbolizing a chill and distinguished by a grey hue. This strategic design choice imbues the casting with a top riser and bottom chill configuration, optimizing the sequence of solidification and material feeding throughout this critical phase, as elucidated in Fig. S3. The immediate vicinity surrounding the casting and chill components is representative of the sand mold setting, encapsulating the holistic casting environment.

Preparation of simulated results

The casting models incorporating chills and molds were preserved in image format. In preparation for numerical simulation, each pixel was considered as an individual mesh in the finite difference model. Consequently, a grid of 128 × 128 meshes was derived for each scenario, as depicted in Fig. S4. With the assumption that each pixel represents a mesh, the dimensions of each pixel were standardized to 0.005 m×0.005 m to approximate common casting sizes ranging from 200 mm to 500 mm. This alignment ensures that the mesh size corresponds directly to that of a pixel. The materials assigned to the casting, chill, and mold mirror typical selections in casting production, namely aluminum alloy, steel, and sand mold, respectively. Boundary conditions were established for the casting’s top surface and the mold interfacing with the environment, configured as convection and radiation boundaries. The initial temperatures of the castings were designated as the liquid melt temperature, while those of the chill and mold were specified as the ambient temperature. Tables S1 and S2 outline the material properties, initial temperatures, and boundary conditions employed in the simulation. For this study, the casting under investigation is an aluminum-silicon alloy A356, characterized by liquidus and solidus temperatures of 616 ℃ and 556 ℃, respectively, with a crystallization latent heat of 425,000 J/kg. The thermal properties of materials, were temperature-dependent variables, with specific parameter values tabulated at certain temperatures, and the intermediate values are obtained by interpolation. Training and validation

The program, coded in Python v3.8, relies on TensorFlow v2.1 with CPU backend support, operating within the Windows 10 environment. Hardware and software specifications are detailed in Table S3, encapsulating the computing infrastructure utilized for this endeavor. Key hyperparameters employed during training are listed in Table S4.

Three distinct U-net models—namely, U-net-2, U-net-3, and U-net-4—each representing 2, 3, and 4 layers of convolutions in the down-sampling segment, respectively, were scrutinized to evaluate the impact of layer depth. Furthermore, a comparative analysis involving three models—U-net-3, I-U-net-3, and CBAM-U-net-3—was conducted to forecast sequential temperature fields during the solidification process. Each of the six models was trained by pairs of geometry models and concurrent temperature fields. The training dataset encompasses 800 instances, featuring 200 distinct geometry models and capturing temperature data at four time intervals (5 min, 10 min, 15 min, and 20 min) for each geometry model. The training process, outlined in detail in Table S5, involves splitting the dataset—80% earmarked for training and 20% for validation—to optimize model performance and generalization capabilities.

Discussion

The impact of varying the number of layers within the U-net model on the training and validation losses is consolidated in Table S6. With an increase in the number of layers, a decrease in loss was observed; however, this trend was accompanied by heightened training durations and resource utilization. Consequently, the U-net-3 model was selected to strike a balance between precision and training efficiency.

A detailed comparison involving the U-net-3, I-U-net-3, and CBAM-U-net-3 models is furnished in Table S7. Intriguingly, the inclusion of the CBAM module does not substantially improve accuracy levels. This marginal impact may be attributed to the model’s pre-existing adeptness in spatial and channel fitting, potentially leading to some overfitting upon module integration. Consequently, the CBAM module’s inclusion in the network architecture may not be warranted. Notably, the I-U-net-3 model outperformed the others, attaining a remarkable accuracy of 94.5% and exhibiting the best loss function values. Moreover, the average Pearson coefficient soared to 0.99985, underscoring its superior predictive capabilities. An illustrative comparison of prediction outcomes at 10 min, employing the U-net-3 and I-U-net-3 models, is depicted in Fig. 8. Evidently, the I-U-net-3 model excels with heightened accuracy levels, substantiating its superior performance in temperature field prediction.

In scenarios involving singular or dual material types, Peng et al.14 leveraged a signed distance function (SDF) in lieu of pure colors, a strategy proven to enhance prediction accuracy. However, the realm of casting processes introduces a multitude of materials—insulation layers, surface sand, backbone sand, core sand, molds, and chills—each potentially exhibiting distinct characteristics or non-uniform initial temperatures, as delineated in Fig. S6. These complexities pose formidable challenges for SDF application, particularly in scenarios encompassing multiple materials or non-homogeneous temperature distributions. Notably, the SDF method remains restricted to handling only two material types effectively.

It just took the trained deep learning models only 1 s to predict and visualize test dataset outcomes, contrasting the 917 s necessitated by traditional numerical simulation programs for 5 min long solidification of one casting, signifying a 900-fold surge in computational efficiency. This is due to the unique AI model structure and the separation of the training process from the prediction application.

The fundamental tenets of convolutional neural networks share certain core principles, with the encoder and decoder structures serving as foundational architectural components. In convolutional neural networks, two- or three-dimensional temperature fields can be effectively visualized in image format, allowing for comprehensive analysis. The image illustration is necessary to deal with the geometric intricacies of casting, mold, core, and chill, and multi-boundary conditions. Consequently, the selection of the U-net neural network in this study aligns with the imperative to account for these complex geometrical considerations. While Gupta et al.15,16 introduced a multiwavelet-based operator in solving coupled partial differential equations. However, this model does neither address real industrial problems such as geometries with components of different materials nor involve the parameters varying with temperature, time, and the results.

The successful implementation of AI models for predicting temperature fields in casting processes not only underscores their efficacy but also hints at their potential utility in forecasting other variable distributions such as stress, strain, and concentration profiles. This paradigm shift opens up avenues for deploying trained models in equipment for real-time simulations and decision-making, leveraging their rapid response times and accurate predictions. Such advancements are poised to streamline the development of intelligent equipment and foster the evolution of intelligent manufacturing processes, ushering in a new era of efficiency and innovation in industrial settings.

Conclusions

To address the limitations of numerical simulations, a novel approach leveraging deep learning technology was implemented to establish a surrogate model based on the U-net network architecture, aimed at predicting temperature fields in the solidification phase of casting processes. This innovative model demonstrates the capability to swiftly forecast temperature distributions during the solidification of castings across varied shapes and time intervals. An extensive array of casting shapes, alongside their corresponding numerical simulation temperature data, constituted the training and validation datasets for the U-net model. The creation process involved generating numerous 2D casting models using symbolic representations, subsequently subjecting them to a randomized perturbation technique. Additional elements such as risers, chills, and sand were incorporated into these models. The resultant models, along with simulated temperature fields derived through the finite difference method, were directly converted into images to form a comprehensive training and validation dataset.
