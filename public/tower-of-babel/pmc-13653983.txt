Effects of dietary Purple Perilla seed oil on performance, egg quality, and yolk deposition of n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids and functional metabolites in laying ducks
Authors: Yulong Feng, Meijuan Li, Yumeng Li, Yuxi Lu, Guotao Dai, Chengcheng Tian, Yu Zhao, Jianwei Li, Yongbao Wu, Zhiguo Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effects of perilla seed oil (PSO) supplementation at graded levels (0%, 1%, 2%, and 4%) on production performance, egg quality, fatty acid composition, cholesterol levels, oxidative status, and metabolic profiles in laying ducks. A total of 256 Sansui ducks were randomly allotted to four treatment groups and fed the experimental diets for 8 weeks, including a 1-week adaptation period followed by 7 weeks of data collection. Results showed that supplementation with 2% and 4% PSO significantly increased egg production and egg weight (P < 0.05) without negatively affecting feed conversion efficiency or egg quality parameters (P > 0.05). Yolk total cholesterol was significantly reduced in ducks fed PSO (P < 0.05), and yolk vitamin A content exhibited a dose-dependent increase with increasing dietary PSO levels (P < 0.05). Furthermore, increasing dietary PSO levels led to a progressive elevation in yolk n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), particularly docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), accompanied by a marked reduction in the n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratio (P < 0.05). Metabolomic analysis identified 105 differential metabolites in the 4% PSO group, which were mainly associated with unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis. In conclusion, dietary PSO supplementation improves laying performance and enhances the nutritional quality of duck eggs by increasing n-3 PUFAs content and reducing cholesterol. However, the potential oxidative stress associated with PUFAs enrichment should be considered, and antioxidant co-supplementation may be warranted in future applications.

Introduction

With the improvement of living standards, consumers are increasingly prioritizing the quality of their daily diets over quantity. Foods enriched with functional ingredients, such as n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 PUFAs), carotenoids, have gained popularity among consumers. The established benefits of n‑3 PUFAs, such as docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, C22:6 n-3), docosapentaenoic acid (DPA, C22:5 n-3), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, C20:5 n-3), and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA, C18:3 n-3), against hypertension, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular diseases and diabetes are accompanied by enhanced antioxidant and anti‑inflammatory capacities (Macho-Gonzalez et al., 2021). Among these, long-chain n-3 PUFAs, particularly DHA, are crucial for human health (Trautwein, 2001). Although endogenous synthesis of DHA from ALA and EPA occurs in humans, the process is severely constrained by low conversion efficiency (Fraeye et al., 2012), making dietary intake the primary source of DHA. Unfortunately, the n-3 PUFAs intake from regular foods does not meet the recommended daily intake for humans (Shahidi and Ambigaipalan, 2018). Research over the past few decades indicates that animal products enriched with n-3 PUFA can be significantly modified by supplementing different n-3 PUFAs sources into their diets (Long et al., 2018; Kralik et al., 2021). Consequently, n-3 PUFAs-enriched foods are likely to become a key method for humans to obtain these essential fatty acids. Traditional sources of n-3 PUFAs, such as linseed and marine products (including fish oil and microalgae), have certain drawbacks, like the low conversion rate of ALA to DHA in flaxseed (Fraeye et al., 2012), and the fishy odor in animal products derived from marine sources (Gonzalez-Esquerra and Leeson, 2000). These limitations have restricted their use in producing n-3 PUFAs-enriched foods. Therefore, it's crucial and unavoidable to explore new sources or methods of obtaining n-3 PUFAs that can address the shortcomings of traditional sources and meet the daily intake requirements for humans.

Eggs are crucial component of daily diets and serves as a significant protein source for consumers. Duck eggs are widely consumed due to its favorable nutritional properties, such as high levels of fatty acids and amino acids (Onk et al., 2019). In particular, duck egg production in China has been rising each year to meet the demand for preserved egg and salted duck egg, a traditional Chinese dish. Additionally, research has demonstrated that the n-3 PUFAs composition and levels in egg yolk can be altered by supplementing different dietary n-3 PUFAs sources, such as flaxseed oil, fish oil, and microalgae (Bruneel et al., 2013; Lawlor et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2019). However, the n-3 PUFAs contents generally have a negative correlation with antioxidant capacity (Shahid et al., 2019). Reduced antioxidant capacity leads to meat that is difficult to store and prone to deterioration (Ahmad et al., 2015). Therefore, it is crucial to develop new strategies to enhance the antioxidant capacity of n-3 PUFAs-enriched meat in the future.

Perilla seeds, a high-quality natural oil source, presents significant development potential and application prospects. Perilla seed oil (PSO) contains up to 65% ALA, also contain β-carotene, flavonoids, polyphenols, and other substances. β-carotene is a potent antioxidant known for its role in scavenging free radicals and maintaining cellular health. Research has demonstrated that β-carotene and flavonoids, naturally occurring bioactive compounds widely distributed in plants, exhibit diverse biological and pharmacological activities and may contribute to the prevention or mitigation of cancer, cardiovascular disease, and ocular disorders (Shashirekha et al., 2015; Saini et al., 2022). It suggested that PSO has the potential to simultaneously serve as antioxidant and n-3 PUFAs source for generating functional eggs. Therefore, the effects of dietary PSO supplementation on productive performance, yolk chemical composition, fatty acid profile, carotenoids content, antioxidant capacity and metabolome were investigated in the present study to comprehensively evaluate the functions of dietary PSO supplementation in Sansui laying ducks.

Materials and Methods

Ethics statement

The animal study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Welfare Committee of the Institute of Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Medicine, Guizhou Academy of Agricultural Sciences (approval number: AWE-GZSXMSY-2024-31).

Birds, diets, and experimental design

A total of 256 ducks were randomly assigned to four dietary treatments (64 ducks per treatment). Each treatment consisted of 8 replicates, and each replicate comprised 8 birds housed in four vertically stacked cages (two birds per cage, 60 cm × 60cm × 40 cm) within a four-level battery cage system. Each dietary treatment with 8 replicates included 0, 1%, 2%, or 4% of PSO. Ducks were raised in a controlled lighting and ventilation poultry house, providing 16 h of light duration. The temperature was retained at 22 to 26°C during the experiment. All ducks were provided with pelleted diets and water ad libitum for 8 weeks, following a 1-week adaptation period (wk1).

All experimental diets were formulated in accordance with the nutrient demands of laying sheldrake ducks (GB/T 41189-2021, Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of the People’s Republic of China, 2021; Wu et al., 2026). Experimental diets were corn-soybean meal basal diets with 0% (control), 1%, 2%, or 4% PSO, which was added by replacing an equivalent amount of corn (Table 1). All diets were formulated to be isocaloric and isonitrogenous to isolate the effects of PSO inclusion. PSO used in this study was prepared by Guizhou Youyan Chunxiang Ecological Grain and Oil Technology Co.,Ltd. Productive performance

Following a 1‑week adaptation period (wk1), egg production performance was recorded daily over the subsequent 7 weeks (wk 2 to 8), the following laying performance parameters were recorded daily for each replicate cage: egg number, egg weight, the incidence of shell-less and cracked eggs, and feed intake. In retrospect, total egg mass, egg production, average egg weight, daily egg mass, feed conversion ratio, daily feed intake, and uncracked shell egg rate were calculated for the entire experiment (wk 2 to 8).

Egg quality parameters and sampling

At the end of the 7‑week feeding period, a total of 16 eggs (2 randomly selected eggs per replicate) were harvested from each group for egg quality analysis. Egg shape index was expressed as the ratio of vertical length to width, which were measured by vernier caliper. Egg weights were measured individually using an electronic analytical balance. Eggshell thickness and Egg Force (ORKA Food Technology Ltd., Ramat HaSharon, Israel) was used to measure the parameters of eggshell strength and eggshell thickness. Measurements of yolk color, Haugh units (HU), and albumen height were performed in Egg Analyzer (ORKA Food Technology Ltd., Ramat HaSharon, Israel). Following the measurement of egg quality traits, intact yolks were carefully extracted from albumen using a yolk separator, weighed individually, frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen in an individual container, and then divided equally into two parts with a blade. One batch of egg yolk samples were lyophilized and then stored at -80°C for analyses of chemical composition, carotenoids, and fatty acid profile. Meanwhile, dry matter content of the yolk was assessed by measuring fresh and dried yolk weight. Another batch of egg yolk samples were stored at -80 °C for metabolomics analysis. The yolk percentage was calculated using the weights of the fresh yolk and the whole egg (Feng et al., 2023).

Chemical and antioxidant analysis

Crude protein content was measured in egg yolk according to the methods provided by AOAC (2006). Yolk lipids was extracted with a chloroform and methanol mixture (2:1, v/v) and quantified following the method of Folch et al. (1957). Total cholesterol and triglyceride contents in egg yolk were analyzed via an ultraviolet spectrophotometer with commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), following the available protocols in the kit pack.

Yolk antioxidant status, including total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), malondialdehyde (MDA), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), was assessed using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Carotenoids and vitamin A contents in egg yolk

The carotenoids contents and vitamin A of samples were analyzed by UHPLC-APCI-MS/MS. In brief, 50 mg aliquot of freeze-dried egg yolk powder was weighed and homogenized with 0.5 mL of a mixed solvent system (n-hexane/acetone/ethanol, 1:1:1, v/v/v). The sample extracts were analyzed using an UPLC-APCI-MS/MS system. The analytical parameters were set as follows. The YMC C30 column (particle size 3 μm, dimension 100 mm × 2.0 mm) was used for the HPLC separation. The mobile phase consisted of two solvent systems: phase A was a mixture of methanol and acetonitrile at a volume ratio of 1:3, supplemented with 0.01% butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) and 0.1% formic acid; phase B consisted of methyl tert-butyl ether containing 0.01% BHT. A gradient elution program was adopted: the proportion of phase B was maintained at 0% for the initial 3 min, elevated to 70% within 3-5 min, raised to 95% during 5-9 min, and finally reverted to 0% in the period of 10-11 min. Other operational conditions were as follows: flow rate, 0.8 mL/min; column temperature, 28°C; injection volume, 2L.

A QTRAP 6500+ LC-MS/MS System (Sciex) was employed for all analyses; the system was conFig.d with an APCI heated nebulizer, operated in positive ion mode, and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software. The working parameters of the APCI ion source were defined as follows: ionization mode was set to APCI+, source temperature was maintained at 350°C, and curtain gas (CUR) pressure was adjusted to 25.0 psi. Carotenoids were quantified by scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (sMRM), with data acquisition and preliminary processing completed. Subsequent quantitative analysis of all metabolites was conducted with Multiquant 3.0.3 software (Sciex). For each individual MRM transition, key mass spectrometric parameters including declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) were subjected to further optimization. Moreover, a distinct set of MRM transitions was assigned for each time segment, corresponding to the metabolites eluted during that interval. Carotenoids contents were quantified by MetWare using an AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS platform.

Egg yolk fatty acid composition

Analysis of yolk fatty acid profiles was performed with gas chromatograph Agilent 7890B, equipped with a DB-FastFAME capillary, coupled to an Agilent 5977B mass spectrometer, according to the procedure described in our previous report (Feng et al., 2023). An internal standard (Anpel Laboratory Technologies Inc., Shanghai, China) was used to quantify the fatty acid concentration of the egg yolk. In brief, 1 μL of the analyte sample was introduced in split injection mode with a split ratio of 5:1. Helium served as the carrier gas, with a front inlet purge flow rate set at 3 mL/min and the column gas flow maintained at a constant pressure of 46 psi. The column temperature program was programmed as follows: the initial temperature was held at 50°C for 1 min, ramped up to 200°C at a rate of 50°C/min and held for 15 min, then increased to 210°C at 2°C/min with a 1-min hold, and finally elevated to 230°C at 10°C/min and held for 15 min. The injection port and transfer line were maintained at 240°C, the quadrupole at 230°C, and the ion source at 150°C. Electron impact ionization was adopted with an ionization energy of -70 eV. After a 7-min solvent delay, mass spectrometry data were collected in combined Scan/SIM mode, with the detection range set at an m/z of 33-400.

Metabolomic analysis of egg yolk

At the end of the experiment (wk 8), two eggs were collected from each replicate. For each replicate, the yolks from the two eggs were pooled to form a single sample, yielding a total of eight samples for subsequent metabolomic analysis. Aliquots of yolk samples (25 mg ± 1 mg) were weighed accurately, mixed thoroughly with grinding beads and 500 μL of extraction solvent (methanol/acetonitrile/water, 2:2:1, v/v) spiked with deuterated internal standards. The resultant mixture was vortexed vigorously for 30 s, followed by homogenization at 34 Hz for 4 min and ultrasonication in an ice-water bath (4°C) for 5 min; this homogenization-ultrasonication cycle was repeated three times. To precipitate protein, the treated samples were incubated at -40°C for 1 h, then subjected to centrifugation 15 min at 13,800 × g under 4°C. The resulting supernatant was collected and transferred to a clean glass vial for subsequent instrumental analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were formulated by pooling equivalent volumes of the supernatant obtained from each individual sample.

Polar metabolites were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) and coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo). The mobile phase consisted of (A) 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and ammonia hydroxide in water (pH 9.75) and (B) acetonitrile. Samples were maintained at 4°C in the auto-sampler, with an injection volume of 2 μL. Xcalibur software controlled the mass spectrometer in IDA mode, continuously evaluating full-scan MS spectra to trigger MS/MS scans. ESI source parameters were: The mass spectrometry was operated with the following key parameters: sheath gas (50 arb), auxiliary gas (15 arb), capillary temperature (320 °C) For mass resolution, the full MS mode was adjusted to 60,000, while the MS/MS mode was set to 15,000. Stepped normalized collision energy (NCE) was conFig.d as 20/30/40, and the spray voltage was set at 3.8 kV in positive ionization mode or -3.4 kV in negative ionization mode. Raw mass spectrometric data were first transfered into mzXML format via ProteoWizard software, then processed using a self-developed in-house program built on the R platform and integrated with the XCMS toolbox. This customized program was employed to conduct peak detection, extraction, alignment, and integration. Additionally, metabolite identification was accomplished with the aid of relevant R packages.

Multivariate analyses and statistical analyses

Results were reported as mean values with the pooled standard errors. The homogeneity and normality of variances were assessed using Levene and Shapiro-Wilk tests, respectively. Subsequently, the data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with SAS software. If the dietary treatment effect was significant (P < 0.05), means were compared using Duncan’s multiple range test with the same software. P-value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant for all the analyses performed. Intergroup metabolic discrimination was performed via OPLS‑DA, with model validity assessed by R² and Q². Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores and unpaired t‑tests served as complementary criteria for metabolite selection, requiring VIP > 1.0 and P < 0.05 for significance.

Discussion

PSO is rich in alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), accounting for about 46% of the total fatty acids, which is close to the linolenic acid content in flaxseed oil (Pi et al., 2016), and is a typical n-3 PUFAs-enriched vegetable oil. In the present study, high PSO-supplemented groups exhibited significantly greater total egg mass, egg production, average egg weight, and daily egg mass than the control, with the 2% PSO group showing the greatest response. Daily feed intake also increased with PSO supplementation and was highest in the 4% PSO group, whereas FCR remained unaffected throughout the experimental period. The increases in feed intake and egg production suggest that the improved laying performance may be partly associated with greater feed consumption. This response occurred despite similar dietary energy levels among treatments, potentially reflecting the bioactive components of PSO (Zhong et al., 2024). However, the effects of n-3 PUFAs-rich dietary sources on poultry performance remain inconsistent. Previous studies have reported decreased (Gonzalez-Esquerra and Leeson, 2000), increased (Petrovic et al., 2012), or unchanged (Ceylan et al., 2011) production performance following n-3 PUFAs supplementation, and similar discrepancies have been observed with n-3 PUFAs source inclusion (Ehr et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2019). Such variation may be related to differences in dietary composition, nutrient density, fatty acid profiles, bird age, and genotype (Wang et al., 2026).

Egg quality is an important indicator of the nutritional value of the diet and egg freshness. In the present study, PSO supplementation did not significantly affect most egg quality traits, including eggshell strength, eggshell thickness, yolk weight, yolk color, and albumen height. However, HU, a widely used indicator of internal egg quality and freshness, showed a numerical decline in the PSO-supplemented groups, with a tendency toward a more rapid decrease during storage at room temperature. This finding suggests that PSO supplementation may have some influence on egg freshness, although the difference in HU was not statistically significant under the present experimental conditions. A similar phenomenon has been reported in laying hens (Wen et al., 2019) and meat ducks (Zhuang et al., 2026) fed rubber seed oil, another plant oil rich in n-3 PUFAs. The potential reduction in HU may be related to the increased PUFAs deposition in egg yolks of PSO-fed ducks. The high degree of unsaturation of PUFAs makes them particularly susceptible to lipid oxidation (Ide et al., 2000), and the higher MDA concentrations observed in the PSO groups further suggest an increased degree of lipid peroxidation. Oxidative deterioration of yolk lipids may subsequently impair albumen quality and contribute to reductions in HU during storage (Fraeye et al., 2012). In addition, dietary fat source may influence egg moisture loss during storage. Eggs from laying hens fed soybean oil have been reported to exhibit greater water loss than those from hens fed lard (Fernandes Pereira et al., 2011), indicating that diets rich in unsaturated fatty acids may promote greater moisture loss. This effect may be associated with alterations in the eggshell cuticle, which is largely composed of lipids and may contribute to water retention depending on the dietary fat source (Peebles et al., 1998). Although HU values showed a numerical decrease in the PSO-supplemented group compared with the control (ranging from 69.83 to 73.64), this difference was not statistically significant. The elevated MDA levels observed in the PSO group suggest increased lipid peroxidation, which could potentially affect egg white quality over extended storage; however, under the present experimental conditions, the impact on HU did not reach statistical significance. This trend may warrant further investigation, particularly regarding storage stability and the potential cumulative effects of dietary PUFAs on albumen quality over time.

Eggs are an important source of high-quality nutrients, as egg yolk provides essential amino acids, vitamins, fatty acids, and minerals. Increasing attention has been paid to functional eggs enriched with bioactive compounds or reduced cholesterol because of their potential nutritional benefits. In the present study, PSO supplementation did not affect yolk dry matter, total lipid, or protein contents, consistent with previous findings that the major chemical composition of yolk remains relatively stable following supplementation with PUFAs-rich vegetable oils (Faitarone et al., 2013). In contrast, yolk cholesterol was significantly reduced by PSO supplementation. Similar reductions have been reported following dietary supplementation with rubber seed oil (Wen et al., 2019; Zhuang et al., 2026), linseed oil (Petrovic et al., 2012), and pomegranate seed oil (Gharagozloo et al., 2023). However, responses to n-3 PUFAs enrichment have been inconsistent, with both increased (Hoan and Khoa, 2016) and unchanged (Milinsk et al., 2003) yolk cholesterol also reported. Although large-scale meta-analyses of clinical trials have generally indicated no significant association between egg consumption and mean serum total cholesterol levels at the population level, substantial inter-individual variability exists (Djousse and Gaziano, 2008). This heterogeneity is largely attributed to genetic polymorphisms and background dietary composition, particularly the intake of saturated fatty acids, which may modulate individual cholesterol responsiveness to dietary cholesterol.

Cold-pressed plant oils contain various bioactive compounds, including polyunsaturated fatty acids, sterols, tocopherols, carotenoids, and polyphenols (Grajzer et al., 2020). PSO is particularly rich in ALA and bioactive compounds such as polyphenols, flavonoids, and carotenoids, supporting its potential application in the production of functional animal products (Hou et al., 2022). Previous studies have shown that dietary PSO can improve meat nutritional quality and color without affecting growth performance (Cui et al., 2019). In the present study, PSO supplementation significantly increased yolk lutein caprate and lutein stearate contents, indicating enhanced deposition of lutein-derived compounds. However, yolk color did not differ among treatments, consistent with findings in laying hens fed perilla seed meal (Zhang et al., 2023). This may be related to the reduced dietary corn inclusion and the consequent decreases in yolk β-cryptoxanthin, α-cryptoxanthin, and violaxanthin dilaurate, which may have offset the pigmentation effect of PSO.

Perilla and its by-products have been reported to possess antioxidant properties (Zhang et al., 2023; Phan and Tran, 2024), although the antioxidant activity of perilla seed oil varies considerably among sources and may be negligible in some cases (Tian et al., 2014). Such discrepancies may be related to differences in oil source, cultivar, processing, and inclusion level. In the present study, PSO supplementation did not significantly affect T-SOD, GSH-PX, or T-AOC activities, whereas MDA content was significantly higher in the PSO groups than in the control. This result is consistent with the findings of Cui et al. (2019), who reported that PSO supplementation did not improve the antioxidant capacity of liver or breast muscle in yellow-feathered chickens. However, partial replacement of PSO with anise or ginger oil increased GST and GSH activities and reduced MDA concentrations in breast muscle, indicating that the antioxidant response may depend on the composition of the dietary oil mixture (Cui et al., 2019). The lack of an increase in antioxidant enzyme activities in the present study may also be associated with the high PUFAs content of PSO. ALA-rich diets have been shown to enhance hepatic mitochondrial and peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation, while PSO can upregulate genes involved in peroxisomal fatty acid metabolism (Ide et al., 2000). Thus, although the higher MDA concentration indicates increased lipid peroxidation, PSO supplementation did not change the measured endogenous antioxidant defenses. Given the high susceptibility of PUFAs-rich products to oxidation, the combined use of appropriate antioxidants may be beneficial for maintaining product quality and storage stability.

A major nutritional benefit of PSO supplementation was the production of n-3 PUFAs-enriched eggs. Dietary n-3 PUFAs, including ALA, EPA, and DHA, are associated with cardiovascular health, neurodevelopment, and regulation of inflammatory responses (Pottel et al., 2014). In the present study, yolk total PUFA and n-3 PUFAs concentrations increased, whereas n-6 PUFAs concentrations and the n-6 PUFAs ratio decreased with increasing dietary PSO. Yolk ALA, EPA, and DHA concentrations also increased, consistent with previous studies (Ehr et al., 2017; Wen et al., 2019). These changes indicate that dietary ALA from PSO was efficiently incorporated into yolk lipids and, to some extent, converted to long-chain n-3 PUFAs. ALA is metabolized to EPA and subsequently to DPA and DHA through sequential desaturation and elongation reactions, in which Δ6-desaturase is an important rate-limiting enzyme (Petrovic et al., 2012; Fraeye et al., 2012). The increase in DHA from 194.8 to 356.9 mg/egg, together with the increase in ALA from 0.12 to 1.16 mg/egg, suggests active ALA metabolism; however, these data alone cannot be used to quantify conversion efficiency. Reduced yolk C18:2 n-6 may have further alleviated competition for shared desaturation pathways, potentially favoring ALA metabolism (Burdge, 2004). Conversely, increased availability of EPA and DHA may exert feedback regulation on desaturase activity (Gatrell et al., 2015), suggesting that substrate competition and feedback control may jointly shape the yolk fatty acid profile. The linear decrease in C18:1 n-9 accompanied by an increase in C18:3 n-3 likely reflects greater incorporation of dietary ALA and dilution of the endogenous oleic acid pool rather than direct conversion of n-9 fatty acids to n-3 PUFAs, as vertebrates lack Δ12- and Δ15-desaturases. Overall, the progressive reduction in the yolk n-6 PUFA ratio demonstrates the effectiveness of PSO in modifying egg fatty acid composition and supports its potential application in producing n-3 PUFAs-enriched eggs.

To further characterize the metabolic responses to dietary PSO, untargeted metabolomics was performed to profile small-molecule metabolites in egg yolk. A total of 989 metabolites were identified, of which 179 DAMs were detected among the four dietary treatments. Notably, the number of DAMs increased progressively with increasing PSO supplementation, indicating a dose-dependent metabolic response. Lipids and lipid-like molecules represented the predominant DAM category, and pathway enrichment highlighted unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and sphingolipid metabolism as major affected pathways. These findings extend the results of the fatty acid analysis and indicate that PSO supplementation alters not only the abundance of individual fatty acids but also the broader lipid metabolic network in egg yolk. Such metabolic remodeling is physiologically relevant because yolk lipids undergo continuous changes during formation to provide energy substrates and membrane components for embryonic development(Wang et al., 2023a; Wang et al., 2023b). Compared with conventional analyses of total lipids or free fatty acids, metabolomics provides a more comprehensive view of these dietary responses by capturing changes in diverse low-molecular-weight metabolites (Ogura et al., 2020). Previous lipidomic studies have similarly shown that dietary n-3 PUFAs sources are differentially incorporated into yolk lipid classes. ALA from flaxseed was preferentially incorporated into triacylglycerols (TAGs), whereas DHA supplied by Schizochytrium sp. residue was more extensively incorporated into glycerophospholipids, particularly phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). Lipid remodeling is evident during prolonged egg storage and embryogenesis, suggesting that dynamic regulation of yolk lipid composition is important for maintaining oxidative stability and meeting developmental energy and membrane requirements (Meng et al., 2021, 2022; Liu et al., 2021).

Consistent with the results of fatty acid composition, PSO supplementation increased the abundance of metabolites containing EPA and DHA, supporting the enhanced deposition of long-chain n-3 PUFAs in the yolk. In contrast, several DPA-containing lysophospholipids, including LysoPC (22:5) and LysoPE (22:5/0:0), were decreased. Similar patterns have been reported in laying hens, in which DHA-rich Schizochytrium sp. residue resulted in greater deposition of DHA-containing lipids than DPA-containing lipids, whereas flaxseed supplementation altered the distribution of DPA among glycerophospholipids and TAGs (Wu et al., 2020). These observations suggest that the availability of dietary precursors may influence the partitioning of n-3 PUFAs among different lipid classes, with EPA and DHA potentially competing with DPA for incorporation into specific lipid species or DPA being preferentially redistributed into TAGs or other phospholipids. The altered abundance of acylcarnitines further suggests changes in fatty acid transport and oxidation. The concurrent increases in several PUFA-related acylcarnitines, including tetradecadienedioylcarnitine and octadecatetraenoylcarnitine, indicate enhanced trafficking and metabolic utilization of unsaturated fatty acids. Changes in oxodecenoylcarnitine and oxooctanoylcarnitine may additionally reflect adjustments in fatty acid oxidation associated with energy homeostasis. Taken together, these changes suggest that yolk lipid metabolism responds to increased dietary PUFA supply through coordinated remodeling of fatty acid incorporation, transport, and utilization, thereby accommodating the altered lipid composition required for embryonic development.

Beyond fatty acids, PSO supplementation increased several phenolic metabolites in yolk. Perilla frutescens contains abundant phenolic acids, phenols, flavonoids, and related polyphenolic compounds (Ahmed, 2019), and the deposition of dietary bioactive compounds in animal-derived products is influenced by their dietary availability (Giannenas et al., 2021). Accordingly, apigenin, 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde, 3-hydroxybenzaldehyde, resorcinol, gardenin B, and benzoic acid were all more abundant in yolk from PSO-supplemented ducks than from control ducks. These findings indicate that PSO supplementation not only modifies yolk lipid metabolism but also promotes the deposition of dietary-derived bioactive metabolites, further supporting its potential for producing eggs with an altered functional metabolite profile.

Conclusion

In summary, dietary PSO supplementation improved laying performance, enriched yolk with n-3 PUFAs (especially DHA) and the deposition of functional metabolites, while simultaneously decreasing yolk cholesterol levels. This suggests PSO's potential as a feed ingredient to develop functional eggs for laying ducks. However, the potential oxidative stress associated with PUFAs enrichment should be considered, and antioxidant co-supplementation may be warranted in future applications.
