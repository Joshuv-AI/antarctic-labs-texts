Sequential use of continuous glucose monitoring, with or without exercise trackers, significantly improves glycemic control in patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Anushka Lahiri, Suan Tee Lim, Htike Kyu, Yock Young Dan, Chin Meng Khoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsContinuous glucose monitoring systems (CGM) and exercise trackers offer real-time feedback on the effects of lifestyle intervention on blood glucose. This randomized controlled trial aimed to examine whether an additional exercise tracker (Fitbit) would improve glycemic control, in combination with sequential CGM (FreeStyle Libre), in middle-aged patients with Type 2 Diabetes.MethodsThis 6-month prospective study included 158 patients, of whom 138 participants completed the protocol, with a median age of 58 (54, 62) years and HbA1c of 9.7% (8.8, 10.4). There were 69.6% participants on insulin therapy. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups: a group wearing a CGM alone (CGM, n = 73) or a group wearing both a CGM and a Fitbit INSPIRE HR (CGM-Fit, n = 65). All participants wore three sequential CGM (2 weekly for 6 weeks), coupled with diabetes education and diet counselling.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated sufficient CGM data capture at each two weeks (89.8%, 90.8%, 92%), with an average wear time of 14 days and 6–7 scans per day. Overall, time-in-range increased (61%, 67%, 71%; p < 0.01) and time-above-range decreased (35%, 27%, 25%; p < 0.01). HbA1c reduced significantly (p < 0.0001) between baseline and 3 months by 1.5% in both groups, independent of weight changes. However, between 3 and 6 months, the CGM group experienced a rise in HbA1c of 0.7% (p < 0.0001) compared (p < 0.05) to the 0.4% rise (p < 0.0001) in the CGM-fit group. The CGM-Fit group maintained their physical activity levels, whereas the CGM group experienced a significant reduction (p < 0.01) in physical activity.ConclusionsThe use of sequential CGM, guided by Diabetes counselling and dietary advice, significantly improved HbA1c. Using an exercise tracker may help sustain physical activity and glycemic control.

Introduction

Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) affects over 500 million people worldwide [1], with healthcare costs approaching one trillion dollars, primarily due to treating complications [2]. Chronic hyperglycemia increases the risk of serious comorbidities, however, even 1% reductions in glycated hemoglobin can significantly lower the risk of morbidity and mortality [3]. This highlights the need for effective strategies to improve glycemic control. Optimizing lifestyle interventions, including diet and exercise, are essential for managing T2D due to their cost-effectiveness and health benefits [4].

A crucial element in fostering effective health behavior change is the provision of ‘just-in-time’ feedback [5]. Advancements in technology have enabled wearable devices to interact almost instantly with users, enhancing behavioral interventions. Continuous glucose monitoring systems (CGM) offer real-time insights into glucose levels which empower patients to make informed dietary choices and modify physical activity based on their glucose patterns. Unlike traditional self-monitoring methods, CGM provides continuous data, highlights trends in glycemic excursions and detects impending hypoglycemia [6].

While CGM has been extensively utilized in patients with Type 1 Diabetes [7–9], emerging evidence supports its potential benefits in managing T2D. A recent systematic review by Kong et al. reported an absolute change in HbA1c of up to −0.5% among T2D patients using real-time CGM [10]. However, while many studies indicate positive short-term outcomes with CGM usage, there remains a notable gap in understanding the long-term benefits of these technologies beyond controlled experimental settings.

Given the potential of CGM to facilitate personalized interventions and improve overall patient-centered care, this study aimed to examine the use of sequential CGM in middle-aged patients with suboptimally controlled T2D. We hypothesized that sequential CGM, combined with education, would lead to a 0.5% reduction in HbA1c levels. Furthermore, we postulated that the simultaneous use of an exercise tracker would contribute to an additional 0.5% decrease in HbA1c.

Research design and methods

The study was a six-month prospective study conducted within the Diabetes Clinic at National University Hospital, Singapore. The research received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the National Health Group in Singapore, and participants provided informed consent before the study. Participants included patients with T2D, aged 50 to 65 years, with HbA1c levels of ≥ 8%, with a mobile phone. Exclusion criteria included: significant weakness after a stroke, severe cardiac, kidney (stage 4) or respiratory disease, malignancy, dementia, degenerative motor diseases, musculoskeletal conditions, bedbound patients, inability to commit to follow-up.

All study participants underwent three consecutive 2-weekly sequential CGM (Freestyle Libre 2.0), for a total of six weeks, paired with personalized diabetes education and dietary advice (Fig. 1). Study-dedicated diabetes nurse educators (DNEs) were responsible for the application and removal of the CGM devices for all participants. During the first study visit (enrolment visit), the study DNE helped set up the LibreLink app and provide education on the CGM logistics. Two weeks after this, during study visit 2, the DNE removed the first CGM and applied the second CGM, this was repeated for visit 3. During study visit 4, the DNE removed the third CGM. The study DNE reviewed the CGM glucose data, explained the glycemic trends, and educated patients on how to improve glycemic variability during study visits 2 to 4. Participants were provided diet counselling by a dietitian during study visits 2 and 4 (Fig. 1).

A total of 122 participants completed two dietitian counselling sessions, and nine participants completed one dietitian counselling session. The dietitian reviewed the participants’ dietary habits and advised on portion control and the quality of carbohydrates. In four participants, the CGM had to be replaced in line with standard operating procedures.

There was a randomization arm to examine if the combination of CGM and an exercise tracker would further improve glycemic control. For this purpose, participants underwent stratified randomization by the trial administrator based on sex and age into two groups: sequential CGM alone (CGM) or sequential CGM with a Fitbit (FITBIT INSPIRE HR), a wearable exercise tracker (CGM-Fit). Randomization was done using a coin toss method before patient recruitment. In the CGM-Fit group, the participants wore the exercise tracker for the full six-month study period. Advice regarding the Fitbit was limited to wearing the monitor throughout the day and syncing the data regularly.

The primary outcome was the change in HbA1c from baseline at third month (visit 5), and sixth month (visit 6). Changes in body weight and blood pressure measurements were recorded. Exercise levels were quantified at each visit using the World Health Organization Global Physical Activity Questionnaire [11].

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables that were normally distributed were analyzed using unpaired Student’s t-tests and reported as mean (SD). Non-normally distributed continuous data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test and reported as median (IQR).

For repeated measures of non-parametric data, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. For normally distributed repeated measures, a mixed-effects one-way ANOVA was performed to account for both fixed effects (e.g. group assignment) and random effects (individual participant variability). For example, a mixed-effects model was used to examine changes in exercise levels over time, considering both group and individual variation.

Time-in-range was defined as the percentage of time a person’s glucose level is within a target range, typically 4.0 to 10.0 mmol/L. Time-above-range is defined as the percentage of time glucose levels are greater than 10.0 mmol/L.

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Version 10.2.0, with a statistical significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Of 165 patients recruited, 138 completed the study. Seven patients withdrew before CGM initiation and 20 withdrew during the study period, resulting in 73 participants in the CGM group and 65 in CGM-Fit group who completed the study (Fig. 2). There were predominantly males (55%) and Chinese ethnicity (43%). Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of study participants. Study participants had a median age of 58 (54, 62) and duration of diabetes of 15 years (9, 21). The median HbA1c was 9.7% (8.8, 10.4), and BMI 28.2 kg/m2 (25.1, 31.6).

HbA1c

HbA1c significantly decreased absolutely by 1.5% from baseline at the third month of follow-up but rebounded between the third and sixth months of follow-up. The CGM group rebounded 0.7% compared to 0.4% in the CGM-fit group (Fig. 3). At the end of the study (6 months follow-up), 73% of participants experienced a decreased HbA1c from baseline, less than 1% remained unchanged, and 26.4% experienced an increase.

CGM parameters

Overall, time-above-range reduced from an average 35% to 25% (p < 0.01). Time-in-range increased from an average 61% to 71% (p < 0.01), with no changes to time spent in hypoglycemia or time spent below range. The average blood glucose level decreased by 1.2 mmol/l (p < 0.0001). Glucose Management Indicator, a metric that estimates a person’s average A1C level based on data from a CGM, reduced from 7.3% to 6.9% (p < 0.05). Glycemic variability was reduced by approximately 5%.

Physical activity

Participants in both groups started at the same baseline levels of physical exercise. The CGM-Fit group maintained their physical activity levels between baseline and six months. In contrast, those in the CGM group showed a reduction in their physical activity (−540 MET minutes/week, p < 0.01).

DNEs were trained to titrate the glucose-lowering medications according to the glycemic trend from CGM data. Regarding medication, 23.4% of participants experienced an increase, 35.0% a decrease, and 41.6% who maintained their medication dose. There were no statistical differences between medication changes prior to CGM enrollment and at the first routine Doctor visit (Study visit 5), and between groups.

Discussion

This study found that sequential CGM significantly improved glycemic control, comparable to available glucose-lowering agents. We used three consecutive CGM: the first demonstrated how lifestyle habits can influence blood glucose levels (self-awareness); the second engaged patients in changing their lifestyle (assisted adjustment); and the third consolidated these efforts (self-adjustment). The study design was based on the premise that immediate feedback and positive outcomes would reinforce healthy behaviors [12]. Overall, we found that sequential CGM improved HbA1c by a mean of 1.5% three months after the third CGM, demonstrating that real-time glucose readings can help willing patients make and sustain informed treatment decisions.

Peng et al. [13] synthesized 25 studies examining the use of fitness trackers in managing patients with T2D. However, these studies focused on achieving weight loss, which led to consequent improvement of HbA1c. To our knowledge, this is the first study combining sequential CGM and wearable fitness trackers with the primary aim of reducing HbA1c in patients with T2D. Furthermore, participants with suboptimal management of T2D (> 8%) are thought to be less likely to benefit from lifestyle interventions [14]; however, our study’s results suggest otherwise.

CGM provides feedback on the relationship between nutritional choices and glucose levels. This enables users to take proactive steps to manage glycemia levels [7]. The three interactions between patients and DNEs were crucial in enhancing self-efficacy. We are aware that the results of this study could be attributed to the Hawthorne effect [15], in which participants may have altered their behavior knowing they were part of the study.

There was a rebound in HbA1c levels six months after the final CGM, but the improvement in HbA1c remained significant, with a mean decrease of 0.8%. This rebound could be attributed to regression to previous habits due to a prolonged absence of tangible reinforcement. Patients may benefit from monthly CGM to keep them on track, which can be explored in future trials. Similarly, previous studies examining the use of CGM in patients with T2D over a 6-month period, without midpoint CGM measurements, found only a modest reduction in HbA1c [10, 16].

Notably the CGM group experienced a significantly greater increase in HbA1c (+ 0.7%) than the CGM-Fit group (+ 0.4%). This difference can be attributed to the decrease in exercise levels in the CGM group, compared to the maintenance of exercise levels in the CGM-Fit group. These changes were independent of body weight, which remained stable over the study duration. Our findings align with the literature, which demonstrates that maintaining physical activity can aid in glycemic control, while a decrease in physical activity can contribute to a deterioration in glycemic control [13, 17].

There are some limitations, notably, this was a single-center study conducted in Singapore. Although our population was ethnically diverse, the cultural and infrastructural facets of local healthcare may affect the generalizability of these results. We cannot report whether there is any change in motivation or patient activation with the use of a CGM or fitness tracker. However, these trackers help to highlight patients’ cognitive dissonance, between entrenched health beliefs or perceptions and objective information from the wearables. We did not have a control group that monitored capillary blood glucose (SMBG), periodic CGM or a single ad hoc CGM. In a recent meta-analysis of 11 high-quality studies involving 1425 individuals with T2D, Lu et al. demonstrated that CGM is superior to SMBG in glycemic management, particularly in patients using real-time CGM [18]. Furthermore, Kim et al. [16] demonstrated the superiority of CGM with or without structured education in reducing HbA1c in patients with T2D. We also did not test a group without diabetes education or dietary advice. However, we believe that diabetes and dietary education should be bundled as part of the CGM service to increase its efficacy.

The potential of CGM lies in its ability to provide real-time feedback and facilitate a deeper understanding of the interplay between lifestyle choices and treatment effects. Although the short-term economic investment in providing these wearable monitors to patients with T2D would be high, health policy must capitalize on these available and well-tested medical wearables to optimize treatment adherence [19].

In conclusion, we showed that the sequential use of CGM with support from a DNE and dietitian significantly improves diabetes control. The concurrent use of an exercise tracker helps maintain physical activity and sustain glycemic improvement. Future research should focus on long-term outcomes, exploring how policymakers can effectively help support the implementation of these technologies to optimize diabetes management in the population setting.
