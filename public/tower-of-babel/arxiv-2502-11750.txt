Rheological response of soft Solid/Liquid Composites
Elina Gilbert, Anniina Salonen, Christophe Poulard

Abstract
Understanding a material’s dissipative response is important for their use in many applications, such as adhesion. In dispersions the interplay between matrix and inclusions complicates any description. Fractional rheology is conveniently used to fit the storage and loss moduli of complex materials, although the physical picture is often elusive. We study the rheology of soft solid/liquid composites of liquid poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) droplets in a soft poly(dimethylsiloxane) (PDMS) matrix. We analyze the influence of the droplets through fractional rheology and a time-concentration superposition. Viscous dissipation increases proportionally with volume fraction, and we show that a simple, well-fitting model can induce misinterpretation of the physics in a complex material.

Introduction

Before even the foundation of the Society of Rheology in 1929, one of the main concerns of soon-to-be rheologists was to describe the flow behavior of complex materials using the simplest possible models, and to link those models to physical-chemical parameters of the studied systems. In 1921, Nutting proposed a logarithmic model to improve previous descriptions by Michelson on viscoelastic materials (then called "elastico-viscous")
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, leading to a power-law deformation curve:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma}{\gamma}\propto n\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}+m\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\tau}

(1)

where
\gamma
is the shear strain,
t
the experimental time and
\tau
the shear stress. The experimental observations behind this empirical law also led Nutting to suggest a relationship between solicitation time and temperature of the material, thirty years before the well known Williams-Landel-Ferry equation
[
4
]
. About twenty years later, Blair and his collaborators came back to these equations while trying to describe the firmness and springiness of cheese, as the elastic modulus and the viscosity proved insufficient
[
5
]
. Blair proposed a "principle of intermediacy" for complex materials with behaviors in-between Newtonian liquids and Hookean solids, with a non-unit derivative, such that:

\tau(t)=\mathds{V}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{\alpha}\gamma}{\mathrm{d}t^{\alpha}}

(2)

where
\alpha\in[0;1]
and
\mathds{V}
in
.^{\alpha}
is a "quasi-property" of the material
[
6
,
7
]
. When
\alpha=0
, the material is fully elastic and
\mathds{V}
is the shear modulus
G
. When
\alpha=1
, it is a viscous liquid, and
\mathds{V}
is the viscosity
\eta
. Between those extreme behaviors,
\alpha
represents a relative importance of the dissipative character of the material, and
\mathds{V}
characterizes an integrated view of dynamic relaxations in the sample relating to its structure.

It took another twenty years until Caputo formally defined the fractional derivative
[
8
]
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{\alpha}f}{\mathrm{d}t^{\alpha}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(1-\alpha\right)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{f(x)}{(t-x^{\alpha})}\mathrm{d}x

(3)

with the gamma function
\Gamma(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{x-1}e^{-z}\mathrm{d}z
.

The introduction of the Fourier transform of the fractional model yet another twenty years later, allowed for its use in oscillatory models.

\tau^{*}(\omega)=\mathds{V}(\mathrm{i}\omega)^{\alpha}

(4)

This allowed for physical analysis of the fractional model which has been convenient to compare engineered materials and to implement in finite element models, but with little physical basis. Bagley and Torvik established an analogy between a fractional model and a Rouse polymer represented by an extended Maxwell model: integrating over all the branches of the model led to a frequency dependency to the power
1/2
in the complex modulus
[
9
,
10
]
. Gloeckle and Nonnenmacher used a fractional Zener model with a fractional integration method to fit experimental data on polymer melts
[
11
]
. This model showed fractal time processes in the relaxation spectrum
[
12
,
13
]
whose study inspired another model, similar to an extended Maxwell model but with a recursive definition of each branch in a ladder structure
[
14
,
15
]
.

Due to the complexity of the processes at play no unified theory linking fractional models to the structure of complex systems has yet emerged, however a recent series of papers use the fractal ladder model and its fractional approximation to study the behavior of colloidal gels. The model shows a link between the fractal dimension in the structural heterogeneities and the rheological behavior of the theoretical gels
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.

In the last decades, solid-liquid composites (SLC), particularly those consisting of elastomer matrices with liquid droplet inclusions, have emerged as a promising class of materials that combine the structural integrity of solids with the properties of liquids
[
19
]
. These innovative systems offer opportunities for developing multi-functional materials with tailored characteristics, making them increasingly important in various fields of materials science and engineering
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. However, the rheological response of such systems has not been studied extensively.
In the present study, we focus on soft SLC, composed of encapsulated liquid droplets in a soft solid continuous phase, which we also call "solid emulsions".
We fit these SLC them using fractional rheology, and use superposition methods to link the model to the response of the system, focusing on the influence of the liquid droplets on viscous dissipation.

Materials and methods

Generation of the solid emulsions

3.2
Fractional Kelvin-Voigt model

Elastomers can usually be represented by a fractional Kelvin-Voigt model in the frequency interval of our study. The storage modulus is on the elastomeric plateau while the loss modulus is increasing following a power-law with a non-integer exponent
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
. Thus, we can already fit the silicone continuous phase with a fractional Kelvin-Voigt model, with one purely elastic branch for the storage modulus, and a fractional branch for the loss modulus. This gives us a first model of the form

G^{*}_{0}=\mathds{V}_{0}(\mathrm{i}\omega)^{\alpha_{0}}+G_{0}

We extend this model to our solid emulsion samples, by changing the branch at play for the low-frequency behavior into a second fractional branch, in order to have two power-law behaviors, as follows:

G^{*}=\mathds{V}_{0}(\mathrm{i}\omega)^{\alpha_{0}}+\mathds{V}_{1}(\mathrm{i}\omega)^{\alpha_{1}}

(5)

We can interpret

In this case, it would seem that the inclusion of droplets mainly impacts system elasticity, however their slight dissipative behavior is extremely important to describe the full rheological behavior of the solid emulsions. Indeed, if the inclusions were only elastic, there would be no impact on the loss modulus of the emulsions, instead of the asymptote observed low frequencies. The variation in loss moduli are interesting, as bulk dissipation in elastomeric materials drives their adhesive behavior

We can thus draw a link between volume fraction and time-dependency in our solid emulsions. In fractional material, the exponent characterizes microscopic dynamic relaxation events. This linear increase in
\alpha_{1}
suggests either an increase in the number of events or an increased impact of these events. It is difficult to choose between either mechanism without a clear theoretical framework.

3.3
Time-volume fraction superposition

The simple fractional model we proposed does not allow a simple interpretation of the rheological behavior of our solid emulsions. Thus, we need other methods to create a physical picture from this peculiar rheological behavior. Based on the similar asymptotic behavior between all samples and the continuous phase at high frequencies, we propose a time-volume fraction superposition.
To obtain a time-volume fraction superimposed master-curve, we need to find rescaling factors in frequency,

Because of experimental variables such as bubbles trapped in the sample during generation or the transition between the two asymptotic behaviors not being fully captures in the interval of frequencies we studied, some room was allowed in the estimation of
a
. Minimal and maximal possible values were estimated so that the high-frequency behavior of the loss factors could be within
20\%

4
Conclusion

We have studied the rheological response of soft solid emulsions formed of drops of PEG in PDMS. Our materials show a weak increase in storage modulus close to the elastomeric plateau of the pure continuous phase, and two asymptotic behaviors in loss modulus. At high frequency, the emulsions loss modulus increases like the loss modulus of the continuous phase, whereas it flattens at low frequencies, showing the influence of the encapsulated droplets. We employed two different analytical methods commonly used in the field of rheology of dispersed systems to better understand this dissipative behavior.
Since the rheological response of the silicone continuous phase follows a fractional rheological model, which is commonly used to describe the behavior of elastomers, we first fit our experimental data using an extended fractional model based on a Kelvin-Voigt model. The resulting fit parameters may not necessarily reflect the underlying physical principles but effectively adjust all our data.
Based on this fractional Kelvin-Voigt model, we establish a "closest fit" curve to compare the rheological behavior of solid emulsions at varying frequencies, in order to apply recent approaches that establish time/volume fraction equivalences.
Thus, we transition from purely mathematical adjustments to physically-based modeling. We show that at high rescaled frequencies, the moduli collapse on a curve corresponding to the evolution of the pure continuous phase. Increasing the solicitation frequency thus "hides" the droplets’ dissipative behavior in the solid emulsions. On the other hand, at low rescaled frequencies, the solid emulsions’ moduli diverge from the master-curve, emphasizing the influence of the dispersed liquid.
At low frequencies, the liquid inclusions in the elastomer have sufficient time to respond to the imposed deformations, resulting in a rheological behavior dominated by the presence of the droplets and their interactions with the solid matrix. The linear relationship of the loss modulus with volume fraction indicates that the dissipation is indeed proportional to the number of droplets within the material. Conversely, at high frequencies, the droplets do not have enough time to deform, causing the material to behave more elastically, as its properties are dominated by the solid matrix.
This allows us to eliminate the frequency dependence of the viscous dissipation in the range where droplets dominate the dissipation, as shown in Figure
5
, where
G^{\prime\prime}
increases linearly with volume fraction before reaching a plateau independent of frequency. Similarly, the very small value of
\alpha_{1}
in the fractional Kelvin-Voigt model indicates that the behavior of
\mathds{V}_{1}
closely resembles that of a pure elastic material. This is also confirmed by the evolution of
G^{\prime}
which remains close to the pure continuous phase value, even as volume fraction increases.
These findings deepen our understanding of the intricate interactions in soft solid emulsions materials and provide essential guidance for developing new materials. Future investigations could examine the impact of various types of liquid inclusions and their interactions with the elastomer matrix, further advancing our knowledge and optimizing material performance. We believe that our experimental results create an opportunity to improve and expand existing models, moving past purely mathematical methods.
