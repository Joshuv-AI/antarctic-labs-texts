Smartphone gaze-tracking for accessible psychiatric assessment
Authors: Gancheng Zhu, Hanyu Shao, Hongyan Liu, Peng Zhang, Xiaoting Duan, Zehao Huang, Rong Wang, Zhiguo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEye movements are promising biomarkers for psychiatric and neurological disorders, yet conventional recording methods rely on bulky, expensive, and laboratory-bound devices. Deep-learning-based gaze-tracking technology now provides a practical means to capture these biomarkers outside the lab, with consumer-grade smartphones offering a particularly scalable platform. To evaluate the feasibility of this approach for psychiatric assessment, we conducted two complementary studies: a clinical investigation of hospitalized patients with schizophrenia and a normative study of healthy college students. In Study 1, we collected gaze data from individuals with clinically diagnosed schizophrenia (N = 134) and matched healthy controls (N = 130) using both an iPhone and a research-grade EyeLink eye-tracker. In Study 2, we used smartphone gaze-tracking data from a university cohort (N = 631) to classify depressive symptoms. Results demonstrated that smartphone-derived gaze metrics can distinguish psychiatric conditions. For schizophrenia detection, the smartphone model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 87.00% and an accuracy of 83.33%, comparable to the EyeLink benchmark (AUC = 87.12%; accuracy = 86.67%). For classifying depressive symptoms, a free-viewing task on Android smartphones yielded an AUC of 75.54% and an accuracy of 75.79%. These findings highlight the potential of smartphone gaze-tracking as an accessible, privacy-preserving tool for real-world psychiatric assessment and treatment monitoring.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Over half the global population, approximately 4.3 billion people, now use smartphones1. The rapid rise in smartphone ownership over the past decade has created an unprecedented platform for behavioral research2–5. These devices, which accompany users throughout their daily lives, have become powerful tools for digital phenotyping, i.e., the passive, continuous measurement of individual behaviors via personal digital devices. Compared with traditional research methods, smartphones capture data that provide ecologically valid and fine-grained insights into real-world human behavior.

Digital phenotyping has brought transformative applications to mental health and psychiatric research6–10, with its clinical potential demonstrated in the early detection of autism11, remote assessment of brain health, and diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment12. Among the various modalities in digital phenotyping, gaze-tracking has emerged as a valuable window into human attention, cognition, and social functioning. Human gaze provides measurable indicators of higher-order processes—such as attention control, working memory, and social responsiveness—that are often disrupted in psychiatric conditions. For instance, atypical gaze patterns during reading tasks have been linked to dyslexia13, deficits in attention and inhibitory control are often reflected in the gaze behavior of individuals with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder14, and reduced visual engagement is a well-established early marker in autism spectrum disorders15–17.

Despite its demonstrated potential in mental health research, gaze-tracking remains underutilized in clinical practice. Traditional gaze-tracking systems rely on specialized infrared imaging hardware and controlled laboratory environments, making them costly and difficult to scale. However, recent advances in computer vision and deep learning have enabled gaze-tracking using standard front-facing smartphone cameras18–20. This breakthrough lowered the financial and technical barriers, enabling smartphone-based gaze-tracking in naturalistic settings without bulky hardware or expert operators.

Despite its transformative potential21, the use of smartphone gaze-tracking in severe psychiatric disorders, particularly schizophrenia spectrum disorders and major depressive disorders, remains limited. Both conditions are highly prevalent and burdensome, and they share significant genetic determinants22. Recent surveys show that depressive symptoms affect roughly 33.6% of university students23, with a rising trend documented in China over recent years24. Schizophrenia, though less common (affecting ~1% of the population25), imposes a tremendous socioeconomic burden, with global social costs varying markedly from USD 94 million in Puerto Rico to USD 102 billion in the US26. Depression and anxiety alone are projected to cost the global economy USD 16 trillion by 2030, through productivity losses27. These realities underscore the urgent need for accessible, scalable screening and diagnostic tools to facilitate early intervention and treatment monitoring28,29.

In schizophrenia, abnormalities in smooth pursuit, attentional allocation, and inhibition, long-documented in oculomotor studies, have been proposed as robust endophenotypes linked to neural circuitry dysfunction30. In depression, altered visual attention toward emotional stimuli reflects key cognitive and affective impairments20,31,32. These subtle yet clinically informative gaze patterns have been difficult to examine outside laboratory settings due to equipment and privacy constraints. In response to these challenges, this study presents a smartphone-based gaze-tracking system designed to evaluate schizophrenia and depression. A key feature of this system is that gaze is estimated on user devices, thereby preserving privacy by preventing the transmission of sensitive facial or ocular data.

Integrating smartphone-based gaze-tracking into psychiatric assessment holds significant promise, yet real-world validation, particularly in subclinical and clinical psychiatric populations, remains limited. Addressing this gap could enable non-invasive mental health assessment and expand access to timely, personalized care worldwide.

Methods

All participants provided informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received monetary compensation for their participation.

Participants

The schizophrenia study (Study 1) was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Department of Psychology at Zhejiang Sci-Tech University (IRB No. 202312E001). A total of 134 individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia were recruited from a local hospital. Clinical and psychiatric histories were obtained through a review of medical records and semi-structured interviews. Diagnostic confirmation and symptom severity assessments were conducted by trained clinical psychologists using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5), alongside standardized rating instruments, including the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS), the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS), the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS), and the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). A demographically matched control group of 130 healthy individuals was recruited from the local community. Control participants reported no personal or familial history of psychiatric disorders and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. To assess subclinical traits within the general population, control participants completed the 74-item Schizotypal Personality Questionnaire (SPQ-74)33,34.

Two independent cohorts were recruited at two time points, separated by a 10-month interval. The first cohort included both individuals with schizophrenia and demographically matched healthy controls. The second cohort comprised a mix of newly recruited patients and controls, along with a subset of the schizophrenia patients from the first cohort (63 in total) who were assessed for a second time.

In the depression study (Study 2), a total of 631 undergraduate students were recruited from three local universities. All participants self-reported normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity and no history of visual impairment. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Department of Psychology at Zhejiang Sci-Tech University (IRB No. 202312E002).

Participants completed a battery of standardized self-report measures, including the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7), and the Social Anxiety Scale (SAS-20), before the experimental tasks. Participants then completed a free viewing task designed to assess depressive symptomatology, followed by a face matrix task and a dot-probe task targeting anxiety-related attentional biases. However, due to the low prevalence of anxiety symptoms in this cohort, the distribution of anxiety scores was highly imbalanced, limiting the feasibility of robust binary classification analyses. Consequently, only data related to depressive symptoms, including questionnaire scores, task performance, and gaze-tracking measures, were retained for final analysis and form the basis of the present report.

Smartphone gaze-tracking system

A customized, appearance-based gaze-tracking system was deployed on a smartphone to estimate gaze positions during the visual tasks, following the methodology established in our previous work20. The system utilizes the front-facing camera to capture real-time facial images. Initial face detection is performed using BlazeFace to verify the presence of a valid facial region in the captured frame. A face mesh model is further employed to extract a comprehensive set of 478 facial landmarks, from which localized image ROIs (regions of interest) related to the gaze estimation, including face patches, eye patches, and delineated boundaries of the eyes and face, are subsequently derived. These patches serve as input to a convolutional neural network (CNN) for gaze estimation, which concurrently predicts uncalibrated gaze coordinates and a compact intermediate gaze representation (i.e., a feature embedding), consistent with our previous framework19,20.

In alignment with standard practices in gaze-tracking research, and to ensure individualized mapping between gaze representation features and real-world gaze coordinates, the system requires a calibration before task initiation. This process entails using canonical calibration methods (e.g., five-point, nine-point, or pursuit), during which the system acquires a series of face images while the gaze is directed to predetermined spatial locations. For each image, the extracted gaze feature is paired with its corresponding ground-truth gaze location, forming a feature-label dataset that is subsequently utilized to train a subject-specific calibration model based on Support Vector Regression (SVR).

During the visual tasks, each frame captured by the front camera is processed through the pipeline briefly outlined above: facial landmark detection, ROI extraction, gaze feature encoding via the CNN model, and application of a pre-trained SVR model to yield the final, calibrated gaze position. This hybrid architecture, which combines appearance-based gaze encoding with model-based personalized calibration, facilitates high-precision gaze estimation across a broad range of task conditions. Further details on the gaze-tracking pipeline can be found in previous works19,20.

Stimuli and tasks

In Study 1, to assess whether smartphone gaze-tracking can achieve a classification accuracy comparable to commercial-grade systems for schizophrenia detection. In this study, we collected gaze data using both a smartphone gaze-tracking system and an EyeLink 1000 Plus system while the participants completed three visual tasks: free viewing, smooth pursuit, and fixation stability (see Fig. 1a). Device and task order were counterbalanced across the participants. The three tasks are briefly described below.

In the free-viewing task, stimuli were drawn from the Open Affective Standardized Image Set (OASIS)35, which comprises 900 color photographs depicting people, animals, objects, and scenes. Twenty image pairs were selected for this task. Each trial began with a centrally presented white fixation cross (“+”) for 1 s, followed by the simultaneous display of one image above and one below the center for 5 s. Each image subtended approximately 8.64° × 6.42° of visual angle. Participants passively viewed 20 trials in total (duration: 120 s; see Fig. 1b).

In the fixation stability task, participants fixated a central circular target for 90 s. During fixation, a red distractor flashed eight times at random intervals ranging from 0.5 to 1.5 s. Distractors appeared on an imaginary circle of 2° diameter centered on the fixation target. Participants were instructed to ignore all peripheral flashes and maintain central fixation throughout (see Fig. 1c).

In the smooth pursuit task, participants followed a white dot moving along a Lissajous trajectory with their gaze (see Fig. 1d). The x- and y-axis frequencies of the Lissajous curve were set to 1/8 and 1/12 Hz, respectively, yielding a total stimulus duration of 24 s.

In Study 2, participants completed a free-viewing task designed to assess depressive symptomatology, followed by a face matrix task and a dot-probe task targeting anxiety-related attentional biases. However, due to the low prevalence of anxiety symptoms in this cohort, the distribution of anxiety scores was highly imbalanced, limiting the feasibility of robust binary classification. The present study analyzed only the free-viewing task.

All image stimuli used in the free-viewing task were also selected from OASIS, as in Study 1. OASIS images are sourced from publicly available online databases and are normatively rated for affective valence and arousal via large-scale online surveys (N = 822). Prior studies have demonstrated high reliability and consistency of these normative ratings across gender groups35. Diverging from our earlier work, the present study selected 24 high-arousal and 24 low-arousal images from the OASIS database to facilitate rapid screening for depressive symptoms. Each high-arousal image was paired with a low-arousal image, and during each trial, one image was presented in the upper half of the screen and the other in the lower half. Each image pair was displayed for 4 s, followed by a fixation cross for 1 s. The visual angle of each image was approximately 10.31° (width) × 8.25° (height). The total duration of the task was 120 s.

Apparatus and tools

In Study 1, visual tasks and the smartphone gaze-tracking system were implemented as an iOS application developed using Swift and Objective-C (++). The app was deployed on an iPhone 14 Plus, serving as both the stimulus presentation and gaze-tracking device. During the tests, the iPhone was mounted in a holder, and the participant adjusted their head position to ensure the front-facing camera was directly in front of them. Participants were seated approximately 35–45 cm from the phone screen. A standard 5-point calibration and a 9-point validation procedure were performed before each task. During the task, stimuli were presented on the smartphone, and gaze estimation ran unobtrusively in the background, continuously recording gaze coordinates and iris diameters in real-time. Simultaneously, eye movements were recorded using the SR Research EyeLink 1000 Plus system. Stimuli were presented using PsychoPy36, while Pylink was used to control the EyeLink device, e.g., to establish a connection, log events, and record gaze data. Stimuli were presented on an ASUS VG248 60 Hz monitor (1920 × 1080 pixels, visible screen dimensions: 53.5 cm × 30.2 cm) connected to a Lenovo ThinkPad. The participants rested their heads on a chin-and-forehead rest, with an approximate eye-to-screen distance of 75 cm. A 9-point calibration and a 9-point validation procedure were conducted prior to collecting eye-movement data.

In Study 2, the smartphone gaze-tracking system and the free-viewing task were implemented in an Android application running on a Samsung Galaxy Note 20 smartphone. Except for the use of a pursuit calibration and a 24-point validation procedure in the smartphone gaze-tracking system, the data collection protocol otherwise matched that described in Study 1.

Data analysis protocol

For data analysis, we used a multistage protocol that encompassed data quality assessment, feature extraction, machine-learning classification, and feature-importance interpretation. First, the quality of gaze-tracking data was assessed using standard metrics, including spatial accuracy and spatial precision. Subsequently, a comprehensive set of gaze features was extracted from fixation and saccade events, taking into account task parameters. These features were then used to train a suite of supervised machine learning models for the detection of schizophrenia and depression symptoms. To enhance model interpretability, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to quantify the contribution of individual features to classification outcomes, offering insight into the decision-making process of the models. Collectively, this analysis pipeline provides a robust and interpretable framework for computational psychiatry, grounded in gaze-tracking data.

Data quality

Data quality is a critical metric for evaluating the performance of an eye tracker. The core of data quality lies in its ability to accurately and reliably capture gaze. Common metrics include spatial accuracy and spatial precision. Spatial accuracy refers to the average angular distance between the true gaze position and the measured gaze point. In contrast, spatial precision reflects the consistency or variability of the measured gaze points over time when maintaining gaze. In the smartphone gaze-tracking system, we employed the D3 algorithm37 during the validation phase to select representative samples for computing gaze accuracy and precision. In contrast, for the EyeLink system, accuracy data were directly extracted from its raw EDF files, and spatial precision was estimated with data from the fixation stability task.

Event detection

Eye movement data are inherently high-dimensional time series that pose challenges for direct application to conventional machine learning models. To address this, hand-crafted feature extraction is typically employed—both to reduce dimensionality and to enhance model interpretability, particularly in psychiatric applications. A foundational step in this process is event detection, which identifies distinct eye movement events, such as fixations and saccades, from continuous gaze data. These events serve as the basis for most feature-extraction strategies38. While the EyeLink system can automatically label such events in its native EDF files, the smartphone gaze-tracking system lacks a built-in event classification module. In this study, we used the I-DT (Dispersion Threshold-based Identification) algorithm39 to detect fixations and saccades in data collected using the smartphone system, with a minimum fixation duration of 120 ms and a dispersion threshold of 1.5°.

Feature extraction

In the schizophrenia study, a total of 84 task-level eye-movement features were extracted from fixation, saccade, and smooth-pursuit movements. Fixation-related features included fixation count, total dwell time, fixation duration (mean, SD, median, and 1st and 3rd quantiles), and spatial dispersion, resulting in 24 features in total. Saccade-related features included saccade duration, amplitude, peak velocity (each summarized by five statistical descriptors), and scan path length, contributing 48 features. For smooth pursuit, we computed metrics reflecting gaze-target coordination, including positional offset (mean and SD of x and y distances), correlation between gaze and target trajectories, velocity gain, root-mean-square error (RMSE), and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), contributing 12 features in total. In the depression study, 139 eye-movement features were extracted from a free-viewing task consisting of 24 image-pair trials. Features were hierarchically defined at the task, trial, ROI, cross-trial, and subject levels. Task-level features characterized global fixation and saccade behavior, including fixation duration, spatial distribution, information entropy, transition dynamics, and saccade amplitude, duration, velocity, and main-sequence properties. Trial-level features summarized fixation statistics and temporal patterns by their mean and trial-level variability. ROI-level features captured fixation behavior within high- and low-arousal regions, as well as trial-level attentional preference indices. A cross-trial ROI consistency measure and basic demographic variables (age and gender) were additionally included. Training used AdamW with cosine annealing and gradient clipping. For depression classification, we trained for 40 epochs; for schizophrenia detection, we used 30 epochs due to smaller sample sizes. Model training and evaluation followed a rigorous protocol. To ensure a fair comparison and to assess the temporal stability of smartphone-based diagnosis, we randomly selected 30 patients with schizophrenia from a subset of 63 patients who were assessed twice at a 10-month interval, along with 30 healthy controls randomly selected from the first cohort. The patients and controls from the initial recruitment constituted the test dataset, while the same 30 patients reassessed 10 months later, together with controls, constituted the retest dataset. The ratio of the train and test/retest datasets was approximately 3:1. In contrast, for depression symptom classification, an 85-15 split was adopted due to the substantially larger sample size; in this context, allocating 15% to the test set provides sufficient statistical power for performance estimation while retaining ample data for model training and validation. Given that the depression symptom dataset contained more features than the schizophrenia dataset, we applied SelectKBest feature selection (using the F-test) to reduce dimensionality by retaining the 120 highest-scoring features.

Performance was assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Statistical significance was determined through 1000 bootstrap resamples to compute 95% confidence intervals. We compared our approach against eight established machine learning algorithms (Support Vector Machine (SVM), logistic regression (LR), KNN, Naïve Bayes (NB), decision trees (DT), random forests (RF), bagging (BAG), and CatBoost (CB)) as baselines using identical feature sets and evaluation protocols. Hyperparameter optimization of the baselines was conducted within the training set using an exhaustive grid search strategy combined with cross-validation.

To quantitatively validate the contribution of the interpretable PLL to model performance, we conducted a systematic ablation study. The core objective was to isolate the effect of the prototype-based reasoning mechanism by comparing the full EMProtoNet architecture with a variant in which the PLL was removed.

Feature importance

To better understand the contribution of individual features to model predictions and to examine whether the feature importance patterns in smartphone-based schizophrenia detection align with those derived from EyeLink-based detection, we employed SHAP values for feature importance analysis. SHAP is a game-theoretic approach that assigns each feature an importance value based on its marginal contribution across all possible feature subsets. This method provides both global interpretability (i.e., identifying features that are generally important across the dataset) and local interpretability (i.e., explaining the prediction for a specific instance).

In this study, SHAP was applied to the trained machine learning models, particularly the SVM, to quantify and visualize feature contributions. SHAP summary plots revealed the features with the greatest impact on model outputs and indicated whether their influence on the prediction of a given class was positive or negative.

If the feature importance profiles are consistent across the two modalities, this would provide supporting evidence for the potential of smartphone-based tools to serve as a practical alternative to laboratory-based EyeLink systems in detecting schizophrenia-related indicators.

Results

We evaluated the diagnostic potential of a smartphone-based gaze-tracking system in two independent studies. Study 1 focused on schizophrenia spectrum disorders (134 patients and 130 controls), and Study 2 focused on large-scale screening for subclinical depression symptoms among college students (631 students from three local universities). In both studies, participants completed visual tasks on a smartphone while a CNN-based gaze-tracking model estimated their gaze in real time. The recorded gaze data were analyzed offline to extract interpretable eye movement features, which were then used to classify psychiatric conditions with machine learning and deep neural network models (Fig. 2a). The schizophrenia study (Study 1) evaluated three visual tasks (fixation stability, smooth pursuit, free-viewing; Fig. 2b–d) and compared smartphone gaze-tracking with an industrial gold-standard EyeLink 1000 Plus eye-tracker. The depression study (Study 2), which focused on psychiatric screening using smartphone-based gaze-tracking, evaluated an adapted version of the same free-viewing task (Fig. 2e).

Smartphone gaze data quality

Accuracy reflects the spatial closeness between estimated gaze points and the actual target, whereas precision quantifies the consistency of gaze estimates across repeated measurements. Together, these metrics provide crucial insights into the reliability and stability of gaze data.

The distributions of smartphone-based tracking accuracy and precision in Studies 1 and 2 are shown in Fig. 3a, b, respectively. In Study 1 (schizophrenia), the SZ group showed lower overall gaze accuracy, with a mean angular error of 2.37° (SD = 1.83°), compared with 1.64° (SD = 1.54°) in the HC group (t(733) = −5.84, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.43, 95% CI = [−0.62, −0.27]). Precision was also significantly lower in the SZ group (mean = 0.18°, SD = 0.07°) than in the HC group (mean = 0.15°, SD = 0.06°) (t(733) = −6.72, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.50, 95% CI = [−0.64, −0.35]), indicating greater instability in gaze behavior. Gaze data collected with the high-speed EyeLink 1000 Plus system also revealed a group difference in tracking accuracy (see Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). The SZ group showed lower gaze accuracy, with a mean angular error of 0.69° (SD = 0.75°), compared with 0.44° (SD = 0.24°) in the HC group (t(459) = −5.35, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.53, 95% CI = [−0.72, −0.40]). In addition, a two-sample t-test revealed a significant group difference in precision (estimated on the fixation stability task) between individuals with schizophrenia and healthy controls (t(243) = −2.95, p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = −0.38, 95% CI = [−0.839, −0.089]). The mean precision for the SZ group was 0.01° (SD = 0.00°), compared with 0.01° (SD = 0.01°) for the HC group.

Eye event identification and feature extraction

The raw gaze data, i.e., gaze position time series, were analyzed with a dispersion-based algorithm to identify fixations and saccades39, chosen for its robustness at low sampling rates. Because the sampling rate of the smartphone gaze-tracking system (~25 Hz on Android, ~55 Hz on iOS) was much lower than that of the EyeLink tracker (1000 Hz), we assessed whether essential spatiotemporal properties of saccades were preserved. To this end, we examined the main sequence of saccades, the canonical relationship between saccade amplitude and both peak velocity and duration45,46. As shown in Fig. 3c, d, both healthy controls (HC) and schizophrenia patients (SZ) exhibited the expected pattern: saccade duration increased linearly with amplitude, while peak velocity scaled systematically with amplitude. Smartphone gaze recordings yielded a slightly more linear amplitude–velocity relationship than EyeLink recordings, consistent with previous observations that lower sampling rates underestimate peak velocity and smooth saccadic profiles47. Nevertheless, the smartphone gaze-tracking system well preserved the fundamental main sequence characteristics, as captured by the high-speed EyeLink tracker (see Fig. 3c, d), underscoring its potential in clinical assessments.

Features for psychiatric condition classification were derived from raw gaze data, task properties (target movement trace), visual stimuli (region of interest, ROI), and eye events (fixations and saccades). For Study 1 (schizophrenia), 84 features were derived from fixation stability, smooth pursuit, and free-viewing tasks, capturing the spatiotemporal properties of gaze traces and the distribution of eye events. For Study 2 (depression), 139 features were extracted from the free-viewing paradigm, incorporating task-, trial-, ROI-, cross-trial-, and subject-level metrics of fixations and saccades. A summary of the extracted features is provided in Table 1, and a complete list of all features and their descriptions is presented in Supplementary Table 1. Furthermore, Supplementary Table 2 presents a between-group comparison of features in the schizophrenia study. At the same time, Supplementary Table 3 summarizes the feature differences between symptomatic and non-symptomatic participants in the depression study.

To evaluate feature consistency across devices, we computed cross-device correlations for each feature. As shown in Fig. 3e, fixation stability features showed the strongest agreement (mean r = 0.28 ± 0.13), followed by smooth pursuit (mean r = 0.23 ± 0.19) and free viewing (mean r = 0.19 ± 0.13). A full correlation heatmap is provided in Supplementary Fig. 2. Across all tasks, the mean cross-device feature correlation was 0.23 ± 0.16, indicating a modest level of alignment between the smartphone-based gaze features and those obtained from the gold-standard EyeLink system. Although the two systems are not interchangeable at the level of individual feature measurements, the moderate correlation suggests that the smartphone platform captures sufficient gaze signals to retain clinical utility in downstream classification tasks.

Model evaluation

To achieve high diagnostic performance while maintaining interpretability, we developed an Eye Movement Prototypical Network (EMProtoNet). This framework integrates hierarchical feature extraction with a prototype-based learning architecture, producing both a detection classification and a confidence estimate. A comprehensive comparative analysis shows that EMProtoNet consistently outperforms most traditional supervised learning algorithms: SVM, logistic regression (LR), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), naïve Bayes (NB), decision trees (DT), random forests (RF), bagging (BAG), and CatBoost (CB), as shown in Fig. 4a–d.

For schizophrenia classification (Study 1), EMProtoNet achieved superior performance on the EyeLink dataset, with an accuracy of 86.67%, an F1-score of 86.20%, a precision of 89.28%, and a recall of 83.33%. This exceeded the best traditional classifier on the same dataset, SVM, which had an accuracy of 81.67%. On the smartphone dataset, EMProtoNet achieved an accuracy of 83.33%, matching the top-performing SVM classifier (83.33%) and exceeding CatBoost (CB, 80.00%). On the smartphone dataset, EMProtoNet exhibited perfectly balanced performance, with precision, recall, and F1-score all equal to 83.33%, effectively mitigating the precision–recall trade-off observed in other models.

Regarding AUC, a critical metric of diagnostic performance, the results were nuanced (Fig. 4c). On the EyeLink dataset, LR again achieved the highest AUC of 91.38% (95% CI: 82.94–97.13%), while EMProtoNet achieved 87.12% (95% CI: 74.53–96.04%). On the smartphone dataset, the LR classifier achieved the highest AUC of 90.59% (95% CI: 81.48–97.00%), followed by RF (89.27%; 95% CI: 79.64–96.30%), BAG (88.76%; 95% CI: 78.93–95.60%), SVM (88.66%; 95% CI: 78.45–96.00%), and EMProtoNet (87.00%; 95% CI: 75.80–95.29%). These findings indicate that while EMProtoNet may not consistently outperform traditional models in AUC, it offers competitive, well-calibrated classification performance, particularly in maintaining balanced precision and recall. Its primary advantage lies not in ROC-AUC superiority, but in robust overall performance (as highlighted by its F1-score) across datasets, making it a reliable choice for tasks where class imbalance and precision–recall trade-offs are of concern.

For depressive symptom classification using smartphone gaze-tracking (Study 2), EMProtoNet achieved the highest accuracy (75.79%) and precision (74.29%), with recall (65.00%) and AUC (75.54%; 95% CI: 64.57-86.19%), outperforming all conventional classifiers on primary metrics (see Fig. 3b, d). Given that the model incorporated demographic features such as gender and age, we further evaluated two baseline models to assess the contribution of gaze features: one using only demographic features and the other using only gaze features. The results showed that the model relying solely on gaze features achieved an AUC of 71.99% (95% CI: 61.04–82.14%), whereas the model using only demographic features attained an AUC of 57.18% (95% CI: 46.68–68.85%). These findings clearly demonstrate that gaze features contribute substantially to the detection of depressive symptoms, with predictive power that significantly exceeds that of demographic characteristics.

Feature importance

To examine the contribution of individual gaze-tracking features and enhance model interpretability, we used SHAP to quantify the impact of each feature on model predictions. The top 15 most influential features for the EyeLink- and smartphone-based models are shown in Fig. 4e. Features derived from all three experimental tasks contributed to classification performance, though their relative importance differed between devices.

Across both datasets, several features consistently showed high importance, including fixation dispersion, vertical (y-axis) smooth-pursuit gain, horizontal (x-axis) gaze offset (reflecting gaze–target distance), fixation count and dwell time, saccade amplitude metrics, and smooth-pursuit SNR. Notably, fixation dispersion was the most influential feature in both models, underscoring its robust contribution to distinguishing schizophrenia from healthy controls. Saccade amplitude and variability-related measures during free-viewing and fixation-stability tasks, as well as pursuit-related features (e.g., gain, RMSE, and offset variability), were also prominent contributors.

The SHAP results were highly consistent with the ANOVA findings (Supplementary Table 2). For instance, fixation dispersion was significantly elevated in individuals with schizophrenia across both recording systems, and the SHAP analysis further showed that lower dispersion values strongly pushed model predictions toward the healthy control class. Together, these findings indicate that both EyeLink and smartphone-based models rely on convergent, physiologically meaningful eye-movement features despite differences in acquisition hardware.

Test–retest reliability

To assess the longitudinal reliability of the diagnostic models, a subset of patients with schizophrenia completed a follow-up assessment 10 months after the initial evaluation. The test–retest performance of the classification models is summarized in Fig. 5.

Test–retest performance of classification models.

Classification accuracy, precision, and recall are shown for models trained on the smartphone (a–c) and EyeLink (d–f) datasets. Models were evaluated on independent test and retest datasets. The test dataset comprised schizophrenia patients and matched controls from the initial cohort; the retest dataset included the same patients reassessed 10 months later, alongside their original controls. Bars represent mean performance across models. Comparable results between test and retest datasets indicate strong temporal stability, particularly for smartphone-based gaze classification.

On the smartphone dataset, EMProtoNet achieved a retest accuracy of 81.67%, a minimal decrease of only 1.66% from its initial accuracy of 83.33%. Retest precision and recall were 81.70 and 81.67%, respectively, indicating well-preserved diagnostic consistency. This decrease in performance was substantially smaller than that of other machine learning models on the same dataset; for instance, Naïve Bayes and Logistic Regression each declined by 10.00%, and Decision Tree by 5.00%.

On the EyeLink dataset, EMProtoNet showed a more pronounced decline: retest accuracy was 80.00%, down 6.67% from the initial 86.67%. Nonetheless, this decrease remained among the lowest across all models evaluated on the laboratory-grade data, with methods such as k-NN and Logistic Regression showing comparable or greater declines (e.g., 6.66%).

These findings suggest that the smartphone-based eye-tracking system, coupled with the EMProtoNet model, provides superior temporal stability for repeated psychiatric assessments compared with both traditional machine-learning methods and the same model trained on high-precision EyeLink data.

Ablation study

To evaluate the contribution of the PLL in the EMProtoNet architecture, we conducted an ablation study. In the ablated model, this layer was removed, reducing the model to a standard embedding network with a fully connected classifier. The ablated model was then trained and evaluated under the same conditions as the full EMProtoNet.

The results demonstrate a consistent and significant decline in performance across both psychiatric assessment tasks. For schizophrenia classification (Study 1), on the EyeLink dataset, the accuracy of the ablated model fell to 75.00%. On the smartphone dataset, accuracy decreased from 83.33 to 78.33%. For depressive symptom detection (Study 2), the ablated model achieved an accuracy of 71.58%, underperforming the full model.

The performance decline in the ablated models confirms the critical role of the PLL. It likely enables the model to learn more robust and generalizable representations by summarizing class-specific patterns, which is particularly beneficial for psychiatric assessment, where intra-class variability is high.

Discussion

The present study aims to establish a smartphone gaze-tracking approach for clinical diagnosis of schizophrenia and subclinical assessment of depressive symptoms, demonstrating the feasibility of psychiatric assessments using consumer-grade smartphones. Here, we performed three levels of validation, i.e., (a) gaze accuracy and precision in the raw gaze signal, (b) eye event identification and eye movement feature extraction, and (c) model classification performance, to support the conclusion that smartphone gaze-tracking holds significant potential for scaling future gaze-tracking research and enabling remote psychiatric assessments.

We systematically evaluated the accuracy and precision of the raw gaze data recorded with smartphones in both schizophrenia and depression studies. The average gaze accuracy and precision were 1.40° and 0.14° in the depression study. The accuracy and precision among the healthy controls in the schizophrenia study were 1.64° and 0.15°, respectively. These results were consistent with our prior findings (accuracy = 1.32°, precision = 0.18°)20. As expected, patients with schizophrenia showed poorer accuracy (2.37°) and precision (0.18°). The discrepancies in tracking quality between the schizophrenia and depression cohorts may be explained by three factors. First, the gaze estimation model was primarily trained on data from university students, lacking representation from broader age ranges. This domain mismatch likely reduced accuracy when applied to clinical populations such as individuals with schizophrenia. Second, calibration quality in the schizophrenia study may have been affected by poor compliance or limited cooperation, introducing noise into the SVR-based calibration process and degrading performance. Third, different calibration procedures were used: the schizophrenia study employed a five-point calibration to accommodate participant compliance, whereas the depression study used a pursuit calibration. Our prior work has shown that pursuit calibration yields higher accuracy and robustness than sparse point calibration, which may partly explain the higher data quality in the depression study.

Eye events and eye-movement features can be successfully extracted despite the smartphone gaze-tracking system operating at a lower sampling rate (~25 Hz on Android and ~55 Hz on iOS) and achieving lower accuracy and precision than research-grade eye-trackers. To assess whether smartphone-acquired gaze data are sufficient to reliably identify critical eye movement events (i.e., fixations and saccades), we examined the relationship among saccade amplitude, duration, and peak velocity (the saccade main sequence). The observed patterns closely matched established findings in the literature and were consistent with the EyeLink system, indicating that saccade detection remained robust despite variability in tracking quality (Fig. 3c, d). In the schizophrenia study, all participants also completed the same task on an EyeLink tracker, enabling a direct comparison of key eye movement features, such as fixation count, average fixation duration, saccade amplitude, and saccade velocity, derived from smartphone gaze data with those from the EyeLink tracker. Although correlations between smartphone and EyeLink features spanned the small-to-large range by Cohen’s standards (r = 0.10–0.50; Fig. 3e), smartphone-derived measures retained sufficient discriminative information to yield comparable classification accuracy, supporting their utility in clinical populations.

Our comprehensive evaluation shows that the EMProtoNet framework offers a robust and balanced approach to eye movement-based psychiatric assessment. EMProtoNet achieves strong performance in schizophrenia classification, particularly on the precise EyeLink dataset, indicating that its prototype-based learning effectively captures discriminative, class-typical gaze patterns. Importantly, on noisier smartphone gaze data, EMProtoNet matches the accuracy of top-performing machine learning models, such as SVM, underscoring its practical utility for accessible digital phenotyping. In summary, EMProtoNet is comparable to, and often superior to, established supervised learning algorithms. Notably, while traditional models such as Logistic Regression achieved the highest AUC, EMProtoNet excels in balanced accuracy and F1-score. This suggests that EMProtoNet provides more clinically actionable and well-calibrated classifications, with a better balance between precision and recall. For screening tools, where both false positives and false negatives carry significant consequences, this balance is critical.

EMProtoNet also demonstrates high reliability, a finding especially significant for the smartphone dataset. In this dataset, the model showed minimal performance decay over a 10-month interval. This temporal stability suggests that its learned prototype representations capture stable, trait-like oculomotor signatures that are more resistant to the measurement variability typical of consumer-grade hardware. Consequently, the smartphone-based EMProtoNet is a reliable tool for longitudinal monitoring of disease progression or treatment response. Furthermore, an ablation study confirmed the critical role of the PLL. Its removal caused a significant performance drop, demonstrating that this mechanism is essential for learning robust, generalizable feature embeddings that capture the defining characteristics of each class.

For schizophrenia classification (Study 1), the smartphone model achieved an AUC of 87.00% and an accuracy of 83.33%, comparable to the EyeLink benchmark (AUC = 87.12%; accuracy = 86.67%). This level of model performance aligns with recent studies on gaze-based classification of clinical psychiatric conditions using research-grade eye-trackers48–53. For depressive symptom detection (Study 2), the best smartphone-based model achieved an accuracy of 75.79%, consistent with prior work using research-grade gaze-tracking data32,54–57. For example, a recent study using a 60-Hz budget eye-tracker (Gazepoint GP3) and lengthy cognitive tasks (~40 min) reported a classification accuracy of 64.58% with gaze data alone31, about 10% lower than that achieved by the 25-Hz smartphone gaze-tracking system in the present study.

The SHAP interpretability analysis reinforces the neurophysiological validity of our approach. Of the top 15 features, 13 are consistent across the smartphone and EyeLink systems, including fixation dispersion, saccade amplitude, and smooth pursuit gain. This high degree of consistency in feature importance, extending beyond mere trends to specific identities, strongly indicates that the models capture biologically plausible oculomotor endophenotypes rather than device-specific artifacts. Such convergence at the feature level bridges the gap between high-precision laboratory research and scalable digital assessment, confirming that smartphone sensors can probe the same underlying physiological constructs as research-grade equipment. This provides empirical support for the potential of smartphone platforms to partially replace professional eye trackers in such assessments.

Firstly, the use of different smartphone platforms, i.e., iPhone for the schizophrenia dataset and Android devices for the depression dataset, may have introduced variability in gaze data due to differences in camera hardware and system-level image-processing pipelines. For example, in the present study, the gaze sampling rate was approximately 55 Hz on the iPhone and 25 Hz on the Samsung Android devices. To mitigate the potential impact of hardware differences on downstream analysis and classification, gaze data from both platforms were processed using the same gaze estimation framework and a standardized preprocessing pipeline, which included calibration, coordinate normalization, and feature extraction. Moreover, the extracted eye-movement features (e.g., fixation duration, saccade amplitude, and scan path metrics) are higher-level behavioral descriptors rather than raw pixel-level signals. These aggregated spatiotemporal features are generally less sensitive to minor hardware-related variations. Importantly, model training and evaluation were conducted independently within each dataset; thus, the reported classification performance reflects discrimination within a single device ecosystem rather than cross-device generalization. Nevertheless, explicit cross-device validation and systematic testing for device invariance would further strengthen the present findings. For real-world applications where hardware owned by users may vary, cross-device generalizability will be critical to success. This remains an important direction for future work.

A further limitation of the present study was that detailed medication dosage information was not available in the current datasets. Participants in the schizophrenia group were receiving antipsychotic treatment at the time of testing; we cannot entirely disentangle whether the eye-movement patterns identified by the machine learning model reflect core pathophysiological features of the disorder or are partially influenced by medication status. Although previous research has suggested that certain oculomotor abnormalities may represent stable trait markers that are not strongly associated with current neuroleptic dosage or polypharmacy30, the absence of detailed medication data (e.g., chlorpromazine equivalents) precluded us from conducting a formal exploratory analysis, such as the correlation between critical features identified in the SHAP analysis and medication exposure. Future studies incorporating comprehensive medication records are warranted to validate the specificity of these biomarkers and to further clarify the extent to which pharmacological treatment modulates eye-movement dynamics in schizophrenia.

The present findings demonstrate that the smartphone gaze-tracking system provides measurements with sufficient precision to detect critical eye events, ensuring that downstream analyses of eye movement features remain valid for clinical applications. Building on this foundation, the EMProtoNet framework achieved balanced and stable classification performance across psychiatric symptoms, translating established laboratory biomarkers into a scalable digital tool that bridges precise neurophysiology and accessible phenotyping. By leveraging ubiquitous devices, this approach provides a pragmatic path toward decentralizing mental health assessment and potentially addressing a critical gap in global access to care. To realize its full potential, future work should validate the framework in larger, more diverse populations, extend its application to other psychiatric conditions, and integrate multimodal data streams such as voice and motor activity.
