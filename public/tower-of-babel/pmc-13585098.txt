Evaluation of Clinical Performance of Stainless Steel Crowns and 3D-printed Resin Crowns in Primary Molars of Children: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Bollepalli Bhavana, Gadisetti Lakshmi Surekha, Guduri Vineeth, Peddi Ravigna, Penmatsa Chaitanya, Spandana Cheeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and backgroundDigital dentistry has paved the way for the use of permanent resins, which are biocompatible and esthetic, as an alternative to stainless steel crowns (SSCs). So, this study evaluated and compared the clinical performance of SSCs and 3D-printed resin crowns (3D-PRCs) in pulpectomized primary molars in children.Materials and methodsThis split-mouth randomized clinical trial was registered under the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI) number: CTRI/2024/02/062466. Thirty-two children aged 6–9 years with 64 pulpectomized primary second molars requiring bilateral full-coronal restorations were randomly assigned to group I (SSCs) and group II (3D-PRCs). Survival rate, crown retention, and gingival health were assessed at 6 and 12 months. Data were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier (KM), Mann–Whitney U, and Friedman tests.ResultsA statistically significant difference was observed for survival rate and crown retention (p = 0.001) between the SSCs (100%) and 3D-PRCs (43.8%) at both intervals, whereas both groups experienced a slight deterioration of gingival health over the 12-month follow-up period, with no statistically significant difference.ConclusionSSCs demonstrated superior performance in terms of crown retention, longevity, and gingival health. 3D-PRCs offered a distinct advantage in esthetic appeal.Clinical significanceThe esthetic limitations and potential hypersensitivity reactions associated with SSCs underscore the need for economical and durable alternatives in pediatric restorative dentistry. Integrating these 3D-PRCs with refined fabrication techniques, enhanced retention strategies into clinical practice could significantly improve treatment acceptance and long-term oral health outcomes in children.How to cite this articleBhavana B, Surekha GL, Vineeth G, et al. Introduction

Deciduous teeth aid in mastication and language development, and they also promote the growth of the stomatognathic system. Dental caries is one of the most frequent chronic conditions in childhood. The prevalence of dental caries in primary dentition among children in the world was reported to be 46.2%.1 Caries prevalence in children aged 6–11 years in India was reported to be 78.9%.2

Untreated early childhood caries (ECC) can result in pain, infection, premature tooth loss, impaired chewing and nutrition deficiency, esthetic considerations that might contribute to stunted growth, sleep disruption, and poor academic performance. Additionally, it can lead to malocclusion, speech difficulties, altered facial development, and behavioral challenges due to oral dysfunction.

Children commonly visit dentists due to pain and extensive tooth decay, often necessitating full-coverage restorations following pulpectomy. Pediatric dental crowns such as stainless steel crowns (SSCs), resin-filled celluloid types, and other crowns are vital for restoring function, alleviating sensitivity, supporting oral health, and enhancing the psychological well-being of children.

Stainless steel crowns (SSCs), introduced in 1947, remain the gold standard in pediatric dentistry due to their durability, cost-effectiveness, and efficacy in restoring pulp-treated teeth by reducing fracture risk. However, concerns such as nickel-related hypersensitivity and poor esthetics leading to lower parental acceptance have driven current research towards more esthetic and biocompatible alternatives.3–5

Advancements in pediatric dentistry have introduced esthetic alternatives to SSCs such as composite, polycarbonate, resin, and zirconia crowns. With the advent of digital dentistry, 3D printing technology enables the design of customized, esthetic crowns using resin-based materials that mimic dentin, offering advantages like efficient production, minimal material wastage, and precise fit over traditional methods.6,7

There were limited in vivo studies published in the literature comparing SSCs and 3D-printed resin crowns (3D-PRCs) for restoration of primary molars in 6–9-year-old children. Hence, the present study aimed to compare the clinical performance of SSCs and 3D-PRCs in primary molars of children aged 6–9 years.

The objectives were to assess the survival rate, crown retention, and gingival health at 6 and 12 months of follow-up. The null hypothesis states that there will be no significant difference in the survival rate, crown retention, and gingival health of SSCs and 3D-PRCs for primary molars at 12 months' follow-up.

Materials and Methods

This randomized clinical trial (CONSORT guidelines) was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee (IECVDC/23/PG01/PPD/IVV/25) and registered under the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI) registration no.: CTRI/2024/02/062466.

The sample size was calculated based on the study results of Al-Halabi et al. and Harshitha et al., using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software (Franz Faul, Universitat Kiel, Germany). The calculated sample size with 80% power and 95% confidence interval was 32 in each group.

This study employed a convenience sampling method to recruit 32 children aged 6–9 years visiting the outpatient department (OPD), Department of Pedodontics and Preventive Dentistry. After obtaining written informed consent from their parents/guardians, they were enrolled in the study based on predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Children requiring bilateral crowns without having any systemic diseases, exhibiting Frankl's positive and Frankl's definitely positive behaviors, were included in the study. Children exhibiting parafunctional habits and teeth with decay extending beneath the free gingival margin were excluded.

Based on computer-generated block randomization, using opaque sealed envelopes with sequential numbers, allocation concealment was accomplished. These envelopes were then used to randomly assign each tooth to one of the two groups.

Sixty-four pulpectomized primary second molars were divided into two groups. Group I (SSCs) (n = 32) and group II (3D-PRCs) (n = 32). For SSCs, occlusal reduction of 1 mm using a no. 330 bur and interproximal slicing with a no. 169L bur were done, and appropriately sized preformed SSCs (3M, India) were selected and tried for a trial fit. Trimming, festooning, contouring (no. 137), and crimping (no. 114) of crowns were done for proper fit. Polishing of the crowns was done using a green stone bur. After radiographic confirmation, the crowns were cemented using type I glass ionomer cement (GIC) (Hy-bond Glasionomer CX-Smart, Japan). For 3D-PRCs, occlusal reduction of 1.5 mm and proximal, buccal, and lingual reduction of 0.8–1.0 mm using a TR-11 bur was done with a chamfer finish line, and a vertical groove (TF-S21) was incorporated for additional retention. Scanning was done with the 3Shape Trios intraoral scanner, and the obtained stereolithography (STL) files were subjected to designing and printing. Printing was done with Chitu Box software using Phrozen Mini 4K. The obtained 3D-PRCs were tried for trial fit and were cemented using type I GIC (Hy-bond Glasionomer CX-Smart, Japan) (Fig. 1).

CONSORT flowchart

An experienced pediatric dentist assessed the survival rate, crown retention, and gingival health at 6- and 12-month intervals. The parents were advised to report to the clinician in case of any dislodgement or fracture of the crowns. Completely dislodged crowns were replaced with SSCs during the study period.

The primary outcomes assessed were survival rate and crown retention using U.S. Public Health Service (USPHS) criteria. The secondary outcome assessed was gingival status using the Loe and Silness index. The obtained data were subjected to statistical analysis using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (Armonk, NY, IBM Corp.).

The statistical analyses done for intergroup and intragroup regarding crown retention and gingival health were the Mann–Whitney U test and Friedman test, respectively. Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis was done for assessing the survival rate.

Results

The children in this study were aged between 6 and 9 years, with a mean age of 6.4 + 0.8 years. Out of 32 children who participated in the study, 14 (43.75%) were males and 18 (56.25%) were females.

On intragroup comparison at 6 months' follow-up, crown retention for 3D-PRCs was observed in 53.1% (17 out of 32) with intact crown structure (score 0), and it further declined to 43.8% (14 out of 32) at 12 months' follow-up. These findings indicate decreased crown retention of 3D-PRCs over time. In the 3D-PRC group, a statistically significant difference in crown retention was observed at 6 and 12-month intervals, whereas SSCs exhibited superior crown retention with 32 crowns intact and normal (score 0), showing a 100% success rate at 6- and 12-month follow-ups. The SSCs group showed no statistically significant difference in crown retention across the two intervals.

On intergroup comparison at the end of 6 months' follow-up, retention of 3D-PRCs was seen in 53.1% with normal and fully intact crown structure (score 0), while complete loss of crown (score 3) was seen in 37.5%, and 9.4% of crowns exhibited major fracture or crack (score 2), whereas SSCs showed 100% retention with fully intact and normal crowns (score 0). The difference was statistically significant, demonstrating superior performance of SSCs (p = 0.001). At the end of 12 months of follow-up, the cumulative failure rate of 3D-PRCs was 56.2% (score 3) with only 43.8% remaining fully intact and normal (score 0), whereas SSCs exhibited a 100% success rate with normal and intact crowns (score 0). This difference remained statistically significant (p = 0.001), reinforcing the superior long-term retention of SSCs (Fig. 2).

Intergroup comparison of crown retention at baseline, 6 months and 12 months

On intragroup comparison, at baseline all 3D-PRCs and SSCs exhibited normal gingival status (100%). At 6 months' follow-up among 20 retained 3D-PRCs, 40% showed mild inflammation, whereas at 12 months' follow-up, 42.9% showed mild gingival inflammation among 14 retained 3D-PRCs. These findings indicate deterioration in gingival health over time. In the 3D-PRCs group, a statistically significant difference in gingival health was observed across the two time intervals (p = 0.011). Mild gingival inflammation was present in 21.9% of 32 retained SSCs at 6 months and increased to 40.6% at 12 months, with one crown (3.1%) developing moderate gingival inflammation. The SSCs group demonstrated a statistically significant change in gingival health over time (p = 0.001). These results suggest that both groups experienced a significant deterioration in gingival health over the 12-month follow-up period.

On intergroup comparison, at the 6-month follow-up, a decline in gingival health was observed. Out of 20 retained 3D-PRCs, 40% exhibited mild gingival inflammation compared to 21.9% out of 32 retained SSCs. The difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.165). At the 12-month evaluation, further decline in gingival health was observed. In the 3D-PRCs group, among 14 retained crowns, 42.9% showed mild gingival inflammation, whereas in the SSCs group, 40.6% among 32 retained crowns showed mild inflammation, and one crown (3.1%) exhibited moderate inflammation. Overall, a slight increase in gingival inflammation was observed by the end of the 12-month period in both groups; the differences were not statistically significant at any time point (Fig. 3).

Intergroup comparison of gingival health at baseline, 6 months and 12 months

A KM survival analysis was conducted to compare the clinical performance of 3D-PRCs and SSCs over a 12-month period. The survival curve revealed a clear difference between the two groups. The 3D-PRCs experienced a continuous decline in survival, whereas the SSCs maintained a 100% survival rate throughout the observation period. Statistical comparison using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test showed a highly significant difference between the two survival distributions (χ² = 25.247, df = 1, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that SSCs showed a significantly superior survival rate compared to 3D-PRCs within the study period (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Early childhood caries (ECC) is a widespread concern among preschoolers, leading to pain, growth issues, and reduced quality of life if left untreated. Full-coronal restorations, particularly SSCs, are widely used for treating ECC. Esthetics and nickel-related hypersensitivity remain as drawbacks for SSCs. With increasing demand for esthetics, zirconia crowns have gained popularity for their strength and clinical performance in regard to crown retention and better gingival health, despite cost and wear concerns.8–10 Advances in digital dentistry and the development of resin-based materials are paving the way for innovative, biocompatible, and esthetically pleasing solutions in the manufacture of durable pediatric full-coverage restorations.

Digital dentistry employs two key fabrication methods: computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) (subtractive) and 3D printing (additive). While CAM uses milling tools to shape resin blocks, 3D printing builds objects layer-by-layer with minimal material waste and greater design flexibility. 3D printing offers precision, biological compatibility, and operational efficiency, making it a credible and effective alternative to conventional methods of restoring the tooth.11,12 This study sought to explore the potential of 3D-PRCs and compared these with SSCs, the conventional method of restoring pulpectomized primary molars.

This study employed a split-mouth design involving 32 children, with a mean age of 6.4 ± 0.8 years and a total of 64 teeth were randomly assigned into two groups. Group I (SSCs) and group II (3D-PRCs). Follow-ups were conducted at 6- and 12-month intervals. The evaluated parameters include survival rate, crown retention, and gingival health. Crown retention was assessed using USPHS criteria, and gingival health was assessed using the Loe and Silness index.13

Tooth preparation for 3D-PRCs in this study involved 1.5 mm occlusal reduction and 0.8–1.0 mm axial reduction. A chamfer finish line was used to enhance marginal fit, as supported by Mugri et al.14 A standardized taper of 6° was employed in this current study to aid in retention of the crown. Numerous studies have evaluated how varying crown taper angles ranging from 6° to 20° could affect the retention of crowns and reported varying success rates.15,16

The occluso-cervical height is critical for crown retention; for posterior teeth a minimum of 4 mm is recommended. However, achieving this can be challenging in primary dentition with severe caries, fractures, occlusal disharmony, or developmental defects. In such situations, incorporating auxiliary features like grooves or proximal boxes enhances the retention by increasing the surface area.17 In this study, a vertical groove was incorporated as an auxiliary retentive aid because of its ease of placement and conservative tooth preservation.

Resin-based materials are favored for full-coverage restorations due to their esthetics, customizability, and dentin-like elasticity, making them highly suitable for dental applications.6,7 However, factors such as material characteristics, occlusal stress, and thickness might contribute to the occurrence of cracks or perforations in prosthetic crowns. The thickness of the restoration also affects its mechanical properties, including fracture resistance, flexural strength, and wear behaviors. Two distinct wear behaviors were identified: two-body wear and three-body wear. In three-body wear, particles serve as an intermediate layer between two surfaces, leading to abrasion of both the surfaces and the particles themselves. Such wear patterns are more commonly observed in 3D-printed resins and may contribute to structural failure.18 In this study, 9.4% of 3D-PRCs exhibited major cracks/perforations at 6 months' follow-up (Figs 5A and B). This might be potentially due to the wear mechanism and the resin's ductile nature, which allows plastic deformation of resin before fracture.19 In an in vitro study, Giridharan et al. observed that the fracture resistance was higher in preformed SSCs when compared to 3D-printed SSCs.20

Perforated and dislodged 3D-PRCs

In vitro studies have shown that 3D-PRCs can withstand forces far exceeding average pediatric bite forces.21,22 However, at the 6-months follow-up in this present study, crown fractures were observed. The disparity in the results between the above-mentioned in vitro studies and this present study might be attributed to the lack of aging simulations and use of static load testing in vitro studies that failed to reflect the complex, dynamic forces present in the mixed dentition.

Digital impressions were made using the 3Shape Trios scanner to generate STL files for 3D-PRCs fabrication. Digital impressions offer comparable accuracy to conventional methods while minimizing material wastage and enhancing patient comfort by reducing the gag reflex and also reducing chairside time.23

In this study, 3D-PRCs were designed using computer-aided designing (CAD) software (3Shape), which has shown reliable performance in achieving optimal crown fit. An in vitro study by Al-Hendi et al. reported that crowns designed with 3Shape and Exocad software provided similar marginal and internal fits, while crowns designed using 3Shape yielded superior compressive strength.24

This present study employed isopropanol as the cleaning agent. Postprocessing of 3D-PRCs is crucial for removing uncured resin, and this ensures optimal bond strength. It includes solvent-based cleaning with isopropanol or butyldiglycol-based solutions and also a centrifugation technique.25

Employing GIC for cementation of SSCs may help to mitigate stress levels, offering better durability in restoring primary molars when compared to self-cure adhesive cement.26 In a finite element analysis study, even distribution of stresses offered better bond strength between GIC and 3D-acrylic resins than that of other cementing agents.27 Hence, SSCs and 3D-PRCs were cemented using type I GIC (HY-bond Glasionomer CX-Smart).

Retention of 3D-PRCs demonstrated a decline over time. In contrast, SSCs exhibited complete retention, with 100% of crowns remaining intact at both intervals. Complete dislodgement of 3D-PRCs might result from adhesive or cohesive failures of the luting cement. One of the potential factors for adhesive failure is its sensitivity to moisture during the initial setting phase. It can leach out essential cement-forming cations and promote absorption, leading to surface erosion, especially during early setting. Conversely, excessive drying can cause the material to shrink, which might result in cracking.28 Occlusal perforations observed at the end of 6 months' follow-up in this study were found dislodged by the end of 12 months; one of the reasons for this failure might be attributed to leaching out of luting cement. An in vitro study done by Sayed et al. observed that resin-based cements enhanced failure stress more effectively than the conventional cement in both milled and printed crowns.16

Restorative materials exhibit varying tendencies for plaque accumulation due to factors such as differences in surface texture, overall surface area, degree of smoothness, and marginal adaptation.29 In this study, significant deterioration in gingival health was observed over time in both groups.

In this study, 3D-PRCs showed higher gingival index values at 12 months. In accordance with these study results, Al-Halabi et al. and Lee et al. observed poor gingival health in 3D-PRCs.21,30 This might be attributed to the inherent rougher surface texture of resins and also a lack of standardized polishing protocols for 3D-PRCs, which might promote biofilm formation and increase the risk of gingival inflammation when compared to SSCs.

Stainless steel crowns also demonstrated elevated gingival index scores at the 12-month follow-up in this study, aligning with the findings reported by Rubino et al. and Biradar et al.29,31 Notable morphological and volumetric changes, such as increased surface roughness and larger marginal gaps, might be the reasons for increased gingival scores at the end of the 12-month follow-up in this present study. 32

In this study from KM curve analysis, 3D-PRCs showed a continuous decline in survival rate. SSCs showed a 100% survival rate, whereas 3D-PRCs showed 43.8% survival rate at the end of 12 months. These findings indicate that SSCs have a significantly superior survival rate compared to 3D-PRCs within the study period.

A similar study done by Lee et al. observed a 100% success rate for SSCs, which is in accordance with our study results, whereas a slight decline in survival rate of 3D-PRCs was reported in our study. The possible reason for variation in survival rates of 3D-PRCs in both these studies might be attributed to differences in the type of 3D-printed resin materials and luting cements employed.30

Although literature from in vitro studies supports good marginal integrity and compressive strength for 3D-PRCs, the present study reported the instances of dislodgement, crown perforation, and fractures (Fig. 5). The reasons for 3D-PRCs failures might be attributed to both mechanical and biological factors. Mechanical aspects encompass tooth preparation parameters, suboptimal internal fit, and inappropriate cementation methods, whereas biological considerations include plaque accumulation and gingival inflammation.

Proper surface treatment of the tooth prior to cementation might minimize the adhesive failure by eliminating the smear layer, thereby increasing the surface area and promoting bonding. Pretreating the intaglio crown surface both chemically and mechanically before final cementation can also improve the adhesive bond strength, which might positively influence the overall durability of the restoration.

As there is a paucity of literature regarding in vivo studies on the application of 3D printing with the use of permanent resins for restoring primary molars, further research involving a larger sample size is warranted. Future investigations should consider occlusal dynamics and should include long-term assessments of wear behavior and color stability of the 3D-PRCs.

Conclusion

Stainless steel crowns offered excellent retention, survival rate, and superior gingival health. 3D-PRCs were esthetically pleasing but were suitable for short-term use. By refining fabrication techniques and enhancing retention strategies, PRCs could potentially bridge the gap between esthetics and longevity, offering a more viable alternative to SSCs.

Clinical Significance

The esthetic limitations and potential hypersensitivity reactions associated with SSCs underscore the need for economical and durable alternatives in pediatric restorative dentistry. Integrating resin materials with 3D printing into clinical practice could significantly improve treatment acceptance and long-term oral health outcomes in children.
