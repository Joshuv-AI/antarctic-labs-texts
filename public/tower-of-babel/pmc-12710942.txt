Tetanus toxoid immunization coverage and its determinants among pregnant women in Somalia: A cross-sectional analysis of the 2020 Demographic and Health Survey
Authors: Hamze G. Dahir, Abdisalam Hassan Muse, Abdirashid M. Yousuf, Abdisalam Mahdi Hassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMaternal and neonatal tetanus (MNT) remains a significant, yet preventable, cause of death. Given that Somalia faces critically low immunization coverage, identifying the coverage and associated factors is essential for developing targeted public health strategies. This cross-sectional study analyzed data from 6411 pregnant women (aged 15–49 years) extracted from the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey (SDHS). Multivariable logistic regression was employed to determine the sociodemographic, geographic, and healthcare-related factors influencing satisfactory Tetanus Toxoid-Containing Vaccine (TTCV) immunization (≥2 doses). Satisfactory TTCV coverage was critically low at 20.20%. Significant socioeconomic disparities were observed; women in the highest wealth quintile had nearly three times higher odds of immunization (AOR: 2.76; 95% CI: 2.10–3.62) compared to those in the lowest quintile. Antenatal care (ANC) access was crucial, with 6–10 ANC visits increasing immunization odds by 2.68 times (AOR: 2.68; 95% CI: 1.81–3.96). Notably, nomadic women were 1.90 times more likely to be immunized than settled rural women (AOR: 1.90; 95% CI: 1.59–2.26). Facility-based delivery increased vaccination odds by 50% (AOR: 1.50; 95% CI: 1.28–1.76). Tetanus immunization coverage in Somalia is extremely insufficient and driven by deep-seated inequities. While nomadic populations show relative success due to outreach efforts, the health system fails socioeconomically disadvantaged and settled rural women. An urgent dual strategy is needed: strengthening routine ANC services and expanding tailored outreach to prevent tetanus deaths.

Introduction

Tetanus is a life-threatening, non-communicable infectious disease caused by the neurotoxin of the anaerobic bacterium Clostridium tetani, whose spores are ubiquitous in the environment.1 Although it can affect anyone, the disease is particularly devastating for pregnant women and their newborns, a form known as Maternal and Neonatal Tetanus (MNT). Vaccination with the Tetanus Toxoid-Containing Vaccine (TTCV) during pregnancy is a highly effective and critical public health strategy to prevent this fatal disease by conferring passive immunity to the infant.2 The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that pregnant women receive at least two doses of TTCV to ensure protection at birth, with a full course of five doses providing lifelong immunity.3

The global effort to combat tetanus has achieved both remarkable success and faced persistent challenges. Through systematic immunization programs, the global incidence of neonatal tetanus has been reduced by an astounding approximately 96% from its peak levels.4 Despite its proven preventability, MNT remains a significant contributor to mortality, accounting for approximately 14% of all neonatal deaths and 5% of maternal deaths globally.5,6 This paradox a preventable disease continuing to claimthousands of lives – highlights the deep-seated health inequities that leave the most vulnerable populations behind.

The burden of MNT is not distributed equally across the globe; it is intensely concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, with Sub-Saharan Africa bearing nearly half of the world’s neonatal tetanus deaths.7,8 Countries like Ethiopia report that only 48% of women receive adequate TTCV protection, contributing to persistently high mortality rates.9 Somalia, alongside neighbors like South Sudan and other nations such as Afghanistan, is among the few remaining countries where neonatal tetanus remains a severe public health crisis, with mortality rates exceeding 1000 deaths per 100,000 people.10 This regional context underscores the critical need for focused research and intervention in these high-burden settings.

The situation in Somalia is particularly alarming. Data from the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey (SDHS) reveals that only 20.20% of pregnant women received the recommended two or more doses of TTCV (TTCV2+). This extremely low coverage rate signifies a profound public health failure, leaving the vast majority of mothers and newborns unprotected from a disease that has a case-fatality rate approaching 100% in settings without access to intensive medical care. This gap between the known efficacy of the vaccine – which can reduce neonatal tetanus mortality by 94% – and its actual uptake in Somalia represents a critical area for investigation.11,12

Furthermore, while previous research in other sub-Saharan African nations has identified key determinants of TTCV uptake, including educational attainment, household wealth, access to antenatal care (ANC), and urban-rural residence,9,13 there is a scarcity of recent, comprehensive evidence from the unique context of Somalia. Understanding the specific barriers and facilitators within Somalia – a country marked by protracted conflict, a large nomadic population, and a fragmented health system – is essential for designing effective, context-specific public health strategies. Therefore, this study aims to determine the proportion of and identify the individual- and community-level determinants associated with receiving two or more doses of TTCV among pregnant women in Somalia, using the recent 2020 SDHS dataset. The findings are intended to provide actionable evidence for policymakers, health program managers, and international partners to strengthen immunization programs and accelerate progress toward the elimination of maternal and neonatal tetanus in Somalia.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design utilizing secondary data from the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey (SDHS). This design allows for the examination of coverage and the statistical association between various sociodemographic and healthcare-related factors and the uptake of tetanus toxoid immunization among pregnant women at a single point in time.

Study setting

The study covered the entire nation of Somalia, including all administrative regions surveyed in the 2020 SDHS. The survey was designed to be nationally representative, capturing the diverse contexts within the country, including urban, rural, and nomadic populations. This comprehensive scope ensures that the findings reflect the varied challenges and opportunities for maternal healthcare delivery across Somalia.

SDHS data collection

Data for the 2020 SDHS were collected between January 2018 and February 2019 through nationally representative household surveys. Standardized questionnaires, administered by trained field enumerators, were used to gather information on a wide range of health and demographic indicators. The Woman’s Questionnaire, administered to women of reproductive age (15–49), was the primary source of data for this study, containing detailed sections on reproductive history, antenatal care, and vaccination status.

Sample and sampling procedure

It is important to note that the authors did not collect primary data for this study; rather, this analysis utilized the preexisting, nationally representative dataset from the 2020 SDHS. The original survey employed a complex, multi-stage stratified cluster sampling methodology designed to cover all administrative regions and residence types (urban, rural, and nomadic) across Somalia.

For the current study, a secondary analysis was performed on a specific subset of the SDHS dataset. The selection process involved filtering the original data to identify women of reproductive age who were pregnant at the time of the survey. From the total survey population, a final analytical sample of 6411 pregnant women aged 15–49 years was extracted, retaining only those with complete information for all study variables. To ensure the findings accurately reflect the national population distribution and to correct for the complex survey design, all analyses were conducted using the individual sample weights provided in the SDHS dataset.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The primary outcome variable for this study was “Satisfactory Tetanus Toxoid Immunization.” This was a binary variable defined based on the WHO recommendation for protective Tetanus Toxoid-Containing Vaccine (TTCV) protection at birth, which advises at least two doses of a Tetanus Toxoid-Containing Vaccine (TTCV) during pregnancy.11 A woman was categorized as having satisfactory immunization if she had received two or more doses of TTCV. Independent variables

The selection of independent variables was guided by a comprehensive review of literature identifying key determinants of maternal immunization and healthcare utilization. Sociodemographic factors included the woman’s age, her educational attainment (No Education, Primary, Secondary, Higher) and that of her partner, household wealth index, and type of residence (urban, rural, or nomadic), all of which are consistently shown to influence health-seeking behaviors across sub-Saharan Africa.8,11,14 The model also incorporated the respondent’s region of residence and employment status for both the woman and her partner to account for geographic and economic contexts.15 Reproductive and healthcare-related variables, known to represent critical opportunities for health intervention, included birth order, place of the last delivery, and the number of antenatal care (ANC) visits, with ANC attendance being a particularly strong predictor of TTCV uptake in numerous studies.13,16 Finally, factors representing access to information and autonomy, such as exposure to media via television and perceived barriers to care like needing permission to seek medical help and the distance to a health facility, were included to capture broader contextual influences on a woman’s ability to access services.14,17 All variables were categorized and operationalized according to the standard definitions used in the 2020 SDHS questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

All data processing and statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 16.0. The analysis proceeded in three stages. First, descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were calculated to summarize the characteristics of the study sample and determine the overall proportion of satisfactory TTCV immunization. Second, bivariate analysis using the Chi-square (χ2) test was conducted to assess the unadjusted association between each independent variable and the outcome variable. Finally, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify the key determinants of satisfactory TTCV immunization. This model included variables that showed significance in the bivariate analysis or were considered important based on existing literature. Adjusted Odds Ratios (AORs) with their corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) and p-values were reported. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Ethical considerations

This study is based on a secondary analysis of publicly available, anonymized data from the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey (SDHS). The original survey protocol, including informed consent procedures, was reviewed and approved by the relevant institutional review boards in Somalia and the ICF International Institutional Review Board. As all personal identifiers were removed from the dataset before public release, no further ethical approval was required for this specific study.

Results

The study included a total of 6411 women of reproductive age. The overall proportion of satisfactory tetanus toxoid immunization was critically low, with only 20.20% of participants having received the recommended two or more TTCV doses, while the majority (79.80%) remained inadequately protected (Table 1). The demographic profile of the sample revealed that the largest proportion of women were in their peak childbearing years (25–34 years). A significant portion of the population was non-urban, with 34.21% identified as nomadic and 27.58% as rural. Educational attainment was extremely low, with a striking 84.29% of women reporting no formal education. Socioeconomic disparities were also pronounced, with 24.19% of women falling into the lowest wealth quintile. Access to health information and services was limited, as indicated by low television viewership (89.56% not at all) and the perception of distance to a health facility as a major problem for 63.77% of women. After controlling for potential confounding variables in the multivariable logistic regression model, several factors remained significant independent predictors of receiving two or more doses of TTCV Table 3. Regional disparities persisted as one of the strongest predictors; for instance, women residing in Woqooyi-galbeed had 51% lower odds of being adequately immunized compared to those in the reference region of Awdal (AOR: 0.487; 95% CI: 0.342–0.693). Residence type remained a powerful factor, specifically regarding nomadic populations. Nomadic women were nearly twice as likely to have satisfactory immunization as their rural counterparts (AOR: 1.897; 95% CI: 1.591–2.262). However, the difference between urban and rural women was not statistically significant (AOR: 1.178; p = .093). Socioeconomic status was a critical determinant, as the odds of immunization increased substantially with household wealth; women in the highest wealth quintile had 2.76 times higher odds of receiving adequate TTCV compared to those in the lowest quintile (AOR: 2.756; 95% CI: 2.097–3.62). The use of formal healthcare services was also strongly associated with immunization uptake. Women who had 6–10 antenatal visits had over 2.6 times the odds of being adequately immunized (AOR: 2.675; 95% CI: 1.808–3.958) compared to those with fewer visits. Similarly, delivering in a health facility significantly increased the odds of satisfactory immunization by 50% (AOR: 1.504; 95% CI: 1.283–1.762).Table 3.Multivariable logistic regression of Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive national analysis of Tetanus Toxoid-Containing Vaccine (TTCV) coverage among pregnant women in Somalia, revealing a critically low coverage of satisfactory immunization and identifying key individual, household, and healthcare-related determinants. The findings underscore that Maternal and Neonatal Tetanus (MNT) in Somalia is not merely a clinical issue but a profound challenge of health equity, shaped by geography, socioeconomic status, and access to the formal health system.

The most striking finding is the alarmingly low TTCV2+ coverage rate of just 20.20%. This figure is substantially lower than the sub-Saharan African average of approximately 49.8% and rates reported in regional neighbors like Ethiopia (41.6%) and Benin (69%), highlighting the severe vulnerability of mothers and newborns in Somalia.11,13,15 While some nations like Sierra Leone have achieved higher coverage (82.1%), the rate in Somalia reflects a critical public health failure, likely exacerbated by the country’s protracted conflict, a fragmented and under-resourced health system, and substantial barriers to care.16 This places Somalia among the countries with the most significant gaps in protecting women and children from this entirely preventable disease.

One of the most notable findings is the paradoxical role of residence type. Contrary to a large body of literature where rural and remote populations consistently face the greatest immunization disadvantages,8,18 this study found that nomadic women had 1.90 times higher odds (AOR: 1.897; 95% CI: 1.591–2.262) of being adequately immunized compared to their settled rural counterparts. This unexpected result may reflect the relative success of targeted public health interventions, such as mobile health clinics and national immunization campaigns, which are specifically designed to reach highly mobile and hard-to-reach populations. In contrast, settled rural communities may be systematically underserved by a weak static health system characterized by inconsistent service availability, a challenge documented in other low-income settings.

The pervasive impact of socioeconomic status was a strong and consistent finding. The clear, positive gradient in immunization coverage across both wealth quintiles and education levels aligns with extensive research from other low- and middle-income countries. The finding that women in the highest wealth quintile had 2.76 times higher odds (AOR: 2.756; 95% CI: 2.097–3.62) of being immunized than those in the lowest quintile is consistent with studies in Ethiopia and Nigeria.9,19 Higher education enhances health literacy, empowering women to understand the benefits of preventive care and navigate the health system more effectively.12 Similarly, greater household wealth mitigates financial barriers, such as costs associated with transportation and lost time from work, which can be prohibitive even when the vaccine is provided free of charge.15 This reinforces that poverty and lack of education are fundamental drivers of health inequity in Somalia, limiting women’s capacity to access essential maternal health services.

The critical role of the formal healthcare system was unequivocally demonstrated. Antenatal care (ANC) attendance and facility-based delivery were among the most powerful predictors of receiving TTCV2+. The finding that women who attended 6–10 ANC visits were 2.68 times more likely (AOR: 2.675; 95% CI: 1.808–3.958) to be adequately immunized is a testament to the fact that ANC appointments are the primary delivery platform for maternal immunization. This is consistent with multi-country analyses across Sub-Saharan Africa which show that each additional ANC visit increases the likelihood of TTCV uptake.20 The high rate of home births in our sample (76.84%) directly correlates with missed opportunities for vaccination and reflects a deep disconnect between many communities and the formal health sector. Furthermore, the finding that women whose partners were educated were more likely to be immunized highlights the importance of male involvement and family support in women’s health-seeking behaviors, a critical factor in patriarchal societies that has been noted elsewhere.21

Conclusion

In conclusion, this national analysis, reveals that the coverage of satisfactory Tetanus Toxoid-Containing Vaccine (TTCV) among pregnant women in Somalia is critically insufficient, with only one in five women receiving adequate protection. This public health failure is driven by a complex web of inequities, where socioeconomic status, education, and geographic location create formidable barriers to immunization, particularly for women who are rural, less educated, and living in poverty. This leaves the vast majority of mothers and newborns in the country dangerously vulnerable to a fatal but entirely preventable disease.

A central insight from this study is the critical programmatic paradox observed: while targeted mobile health strategies appear to be reaching nomadic populations with relative success, settled rural communities remain profoundly underserved by the static health system. This finding underscores that a one-size-fits-all approach is inadequate for the Somali context. Therefore, achieving the goal of Maternal and Neonatal Tetanus elimination will require a strategically designed dual approach that not only scales and sustains effective outreach models but also urgently works to rebuild and strengthen the primary healthcare system, ensuring it can deliver equitable, reliable, and accessible care to all.

Policy implications

The findings of this study have significant and actionable implications for public health policy and programming in Somalia, demanding a multi-pronged and equity-focused approach. The critically low immunization coverage necessitates a dual service delivery strategy that both strengthens the static health system for urban and settled rural populations and simultaneously scales up the mobile outreach models that have shown relative success in reaching nomadic communities. Crucially, a “no missed opportunities” policy must be systematically implemented, ensuring that every contact a pregnant woman has with the health system – particularly during antenatal and postnatal care visits – is used to screen for and administer TTCV. This requires reinforcing the entire health system infrastructure, from ensuring reliable vaccine supply chains to training and supporting healthcare workers on current immunization protocols. Furthermore, to address the profound socioeconomic and regional disparities identified, interventions must be explicitly designed to reach the most vulnerable. This includes developing targeted programs for women in the lowest wealth quintiles and those with no formal education, potentially incorporating demand-side financing to mitigate transportation costs and deploying community health workers for tailored health education. Finally, these supply-side efforts must be complemented by culturally sensitive health communication campaigns, utilizing accessible channels like radio to enhance health literacy and foster community demand, with a specific focus on engaging male partners to build the family support crucial for improving women’s health-seeking behaviors.

Limitations and future research directions

The primary strength of this study is its use of a large, nationally representative dataset, which enhances the generalizability of the findings. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference of causality. Second, the reliance on self-reported immunization status is subject to recall bias. Third, a significant limitation is the lack of supply-side health system variables, such as the density of healthcare personnel and the distribution of primary healthcare centers, which were not available in the household-level dataset. Consequently, we could not account for facility-level factors such as vaccine stock-outs, provider knowledge, or the quality of ANC counseling, which remain important unmeasured confounders.

Future research should aim to address these gaps. Qualitative studies are needed to explore the sociocultural barriers and facilitators to TTCV uptake from the perspectives of women, their families, and community leaders. Additionally, health facility assessments should be conducted to evaluate vaccine availability, cold chain integrity, and the knowledge and practices of healthcare providers. Finally, implementation research is crucial to rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of different service delivery models (e.g., mobile outreach vs. enhanced static services) in improving TTCV coverage in the complex Somali context.
