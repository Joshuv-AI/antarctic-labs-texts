Assessing vascular inflammation during continuous TSH suppression in differentiated thyroid cancer: a longitudinal [18F]FDG PET/CT analysis
Authors: Holger Einspieler, Bengt Hennig, Hannah Klimpfinger, Song Xue, Aleksandar Debeljkovic, Bettina Reiterits, Marcus Hacker, Georgios Karanikas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThyroid hormone imbalance has been linked to cardiovascular effects, including atrial fibrillation and cardiac hypertrophy. More recently, studies also suggest higher arterial inflammation during TSH-suppressive therapy. We therefore examined whether continuous TSH suppression is associated with longitudinal increases in arterial inflammation and progression of vascular calcification.ResultsWe retrospectively analyzed 51 patients with differentiated thyroid cancer who underwent two [18F]FDG PET/CT scans, both performed under continuous TSH suppression, after initial treatment with thyroidectomy and radioiodine ablation. The interval between scans was 4.6 ± 4.2 years. A region of interest (ROI) was placed in the carotid artery, aortic arch, ascending-, descending-, and abdominal aorta to extract standard uptake values (SUVs). Blood pool activity of the superior cava vein was used for target-to-background ratio (TBR). We found that FDG uptake (TBRmax) was higher on the follow-up PET/CT in the carotid arteries and aortic arch (mean TBRmax ± SD: 1.55 ± 0.28, 1.78 ± 0.31 vs. 1.39 ± 0.29, 1.65 ± 0.28, respectively; both p < 0.05). No significant differences were seen in the ascending-, descending-, or abdominal aorta, nor when all arterial segments were combined (all p > 0.05). No relevant correlation was observed between interscan interval and TBRmax. Additionally, calcified plaque (CP) scores were derived from the CT component for each arterial site. CP score was markedly higher at follow-up scan in all arteries and correlated moderately with the interscan interval (p < 0.05). A weak, non-significant inverse trend was found between FDG uptake and CP score (p = 0.09).ConclusionsContinuous TSH suppression was associated with modest increases in arterial FDG uptake at certain sites. The lack of a significant correlation between interscan interval and FDG uptake suggests non-linear remodeling rather than steadily increasing inflammation. These findings highlight the need for prospective studies and suggest that individualized TSH suppression strategies, along with cardiovascular risk assessment, may be warranted.Keywords: Arterial inflammation, TSH suppression, Thyroid cancer, FDG, PET/CT, Atherosclerosis, Calcification

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of death worldwide, and early detection and treatment are critical to improving outcomes. Lifestyle factors, such as smoking, obesity, and psychosocial stress, as well as physiological factors, e.g. diabetes, high blood pressure and cholesterol are well-established contributors [1, 2].

Beyond these well-known risks, iatrogenic endocrine states may also affect vascular health and CVD. One such state is thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) suppression, which is routinely induced in patients with differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) after primary therapy, consisting of thyroidectomy and radioiodine treatment. DTC represents the most common subtype of thyroid cancer and is therefore one of the most frequent malignancies worldwide. In patients with DTC, TSH suppression is indicated to reduce the risk of recurrence by preventing stimulation of residual thyroid tissue and malignant cells [3]. However, TSH suppression has been linked to increased arterial inflammation recently [4, 5], which is a major risk factor for developing atherosclerosis. Atherosclerosis is characterized by the accumulation of lipid-rich plaques within the arterial wall, causing progressive thickening [6]. As plaques enlarge, they narrow the lumen and may become unstable. Progressive stenosis or plaque rupture with superimposed thrombosis can cause chronic or acute occlusion of arteries supplying vital organs such as the heart and brain [1].

Arterial inflammation can be quantified using 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-D-glucose ([18F]FDG) positron emission tomography (PET/CT). FDG PET/CT is a highly effective imaging technique for visualising vascular inflammation and, consequently, for the early detection of atherosclerosis. While [18F]FDG is a glucose analogue that accumulates in metabolically active atherosclerotic plaques, its uptake - quantified on PET/CT - reflects macrophage burden and vessel-wall inflammation [7].

This study builds on our previous work [5] by performing a longitudinal analysis of arterial inflammation and vascular calcification in a substantially larger cohort, with longer interscan interval. Our aim was to determine whether arterial inflammation continues to progress during prolonged TSH suppression or reaches a plateau, and to assess concurrent changes in calcification.

Methods

Patients

In this retrospective study, we identified patients with DTC following thyroidectomy and radioiodine therapy, who received at least one FDG PET/CT scan in the Division of Nuclear Medicine at the Vienna General Hospital from 01.01.2016 to 01.05.2025 (index scan). For the longitudinal analysis, we included only patients with a second FDG PET/CT and selected, for each patient, the scan with the longest time interval from the index scan (up to 15 years earlier) as the paired examination. All patients included were followed at our outpatient clinic and required to maintain continuous TSH suppression according to our institutional target (TSH < 0.3 µU/mL). TSH was assessed at each scan timepoint.

Patients with a known diagnosis of diabetes, in an euthyroid state, or undergoing systemic therapy, including chemotherapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, regular anti-inflammatory or cortisone intake right before and between both scans, which could potentially influence vascular inflammation, were not eligible.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna (EK: 1640/2023). Written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study.

PET/CT, Image analysis and data acquisition

Methods, including used PET/CT scanners, image and data acquisition as well as image analysis, were identical to our previous report [5]. Briefly, whole-body [18F]FDG PET/CT scans were performed 60 min after the tracer injection of approximately 3 MBq/kg body weight of [18F]FDG. Two experienced nuclear medicine physicians used a Hybrid 3D software (version 4.17, Hermes Medical Solutions, Stockholm, Sweden) to extract maximum standardized uptake values (SUVmax) from different arterial sites and organs.

While regions of interest (ROIs) were placed in the carotid artery, thoracic ascending aorta, aortic arch, thoracic descending aorta, and in the abdominal aorta, volume of interests (VOI) were placed in the superior cava vein (SCV), spleen and bone marrow.

Additionally, the extent of calcification in the different arterial segments was determined using the calcified plaque (CP) score in axial CT sequences. For this purpose, a semi-quantitative scale that had already been published [8] was used: 0 = no sign of calcified plaque within the vessel; 1 = plaques covering < 10% of the artery circumference; 2 = plaques covering 10%-25% of the artery circumference; 3 = plaques covering 25%-50%; 4 = plaques covering > 50% of the artery circumference.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in IBM SPSS (Mac) Version 30. Continuous data are reported as mean ± SD or median (min–max); categorical data as n (%). The SUVmean of the SCV was used as background correction, creating a target-to-background ratio (TBR). Change in TBRmax was defined as ΔTBRmax = TBRmax 2. PET/CT minus TBRmax 1. PET/CT (positive values indicate an increase). Normality of the paired differences was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Longitudinal change was tested with a paired t-test and reported as mean TBRmax with 95% CI and p-value. Associations between FDG uptake, CP score and/or time of TSH suppression were examined with Spearman’s ρ. Two-sided α = 0.05. Visualization included boxplots of TBRmax at both time points and scatterplots of the scan interval, ΔTBRmax, and Δ summed CP scores. A large-language-model tool was used only for proofreading/readability, not for analysis or interpretation.

Patients had been suppressed for 3.8 (± 3.9) years before the baseline scan, and the baseline to follow-up interval (time between 1. and 2. PET/CT) was 4.6 (± 4.2) years. FDG uptake of multiple arterial sites at both timepoints

Compared with baseline, follow-up FDG PET/CT demonstrated higher FDG uptake in the carotid arteries (ΔTBRmax = 0.16, 95% CI 0.05–0.27) and in the aortic arch (ΔTBRmax = 0.13, 95% CI 0.01–0.26; both p < 0.05), as can be seen in Table 2; Fig. 1. No significant differences were observed in the ascending aorta (ΔTBRmax = 0.07, 95% CI -0.04–0.19), descending aorta (ΔTBRmax = 0.01, 95% CI -0.11–0.12), or abdominal aorta (ΔTBRmax = -0.01, 95% CI -0.14–0.10), nor in the combined arterial measure (ΔTBRmax = 0.07, % CI -0.03–0.17; all p > 0.05). The spleen (ΔSUVmean = 0.04, 95% CI -0.06–0.14) and bone marrow (ΔSUVmean = -0.10, 95% CI -0.23–0.03; both p > 0.05) showed no significant change in FDG uptake between both time points.

Correlation between FDG uptake, CP score and scan interval

We did not observe a significant correlation between the interscan interval and mean TBRmax of all arterial sites combined (r=-0.223, p = 0.117). In contrast, a statistically significant moderate correlation was observed between the interscan interval and the extent of calcification (r = 0.546, p < 0.001). A weak, non-significant trend was determined between FDG uptake and CP score (r=-0.240, p = 0.09) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we observed statistically significant increases in FDG uptake at two arterial sites after a mean follow-up of more than four years under continuous TSH suppression. Furthermore, calcification increased markedly and consistently across all arterial segments at follow-up.

Thyroid hormone imbalance and its vascular effects are increasingly being studied. One study linked higher free T4 levels with greater arterial stiffness [9], while another observed a higher prevalence of subclinical coronary artery disease in patients with low-normal TSH levels [10]. Furthermore, a large-scale study in Korea revealed an elevated risk of coronary heart disease and ischemic stroke in thyroid cancer patients [11].

More recently, Boswijk et al. were the first authors reporting higher arterial FDG uptake in patients with suppressed TSH [4]. Consistent with this observation, our earlier case-control study found higher TBR values in TSH-suppressed versus euthyroid patients, whereas its small longitudinal subgroup (~ 2-year scan interval) showed no significant change [5]. This present, larger cohort with a longer interval between both scans still suggests small inflammatory increases at select sites alongside clear progression of calcification. Notably, patients who were included in this study had already undergone TSH suppression for several years, suggesting that arterial inflammation in TSH-suppressed patients may still increase modestly after prolonged exposure.

However, the absence of a meaningful correlation between interscan interval of TSH suppression and ΔTBR, despite small increases at select sites on the second scan, suggests that vascular inflammation under prolonged TSH suppression does not progress linearly. Rather, it appears to fluctuate and then plateau as plaques calcify. The observed trend toward lower FDG uptake with higher CP scores supports this remodeling pattern [12, 13].

Overall, the clinical relevance of our findings remains uncertain, particularly in the absence of long-term outcome data. It is also important to point out that other determinants of vascular inflammation and calcification may have influenced our findings but could not be comprehensively accounted for given the retrospective design and limited availability of longitudinal risk-factor data. Several studies have shown that carotid FDG uptake is significantly associated with cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and age [14, 15]. In particular, elevated blood pressure is an important contributor to aortic atherosclerosis [16]. Moreover, the observed increase in calcification should be interpreted cautiously, as longitudinal follow-up inherently involves aging over time, and age is a well-established determinant of vascular calcification [17].

TSH suppression is an essential component of follow-up after primary treatment for differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC), aimed at significantly reducing the risk of recurrence. Nevertheless, given potential systemic side effects, the intensity and duration of suppression should be individualized to each patient’s risk and regularly re-evaluated, especially considering our finding that arterial inflammation did not decrease, rather tended to remain stable, and in some arterial sites slightly increased. Multiple studies have already shown that TSH suppression is associated with cardiovascular risk - including atrial fibrillation and cardiac hypertrophy – underscoring the need for careful, individualized management and regular reassessment in these patients [18–21].

There are several limitations in this study. First, the retrospective design and modest sample size limit generalizability and statistical power. Second, lifestyle- and disease-related confounders (e.g., smoking) were not excluded; although the within-subject design reduces between-person confounding, changes in these factors between scans could not be fully accounted for. Third, active oncologic disease may influence arterial FDG uptake, as malignancy-related systemic inflammation can increase vascular signal [22]; the within-subject design partly mitigates this by using each patient as their own control, but cannot eliminate it. Fourth, baseline and follow-up PET/CT scans were not always acquired on the same PET scanner, which may have introduced slight technical variability in quantitative measurements and could have reduced sensitivity to detect small longitudinal changes. Fifth, because multiple arterial segments were analysed separately, the risk of chance findings due to multiple comparisons is increased; therefore, results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Sixth, although calcification increased significantly over follow-up in this cohort under continuous TSH suppression, age-related progression may also have contributed to the observed increase in calcification, as already mentioned above.

Taken together, these factors underscore that our findings are observational and should be interpreted cautiously; prospective studies with longitudinal follow-up are needed to better define the impact of long-term TSH suppression.

Conclusion

In this longitudinal cohort of patients with differentiated thyroid cancer under continuous TSH suppression, we observed small but detectable increases in arterial FDG signal after a mean interscan interval of more than four years, while calcification progressed markedly. However, the absence of a significant correlation between the interscan interval time and FDG uptake, together with a trend toward lower FDG signal at higher calcification scores, is compatible with non-linear plaque behavior - episodic inflammatory activity followed by calcific remodeling and relative stabilization rather than a uniform, time-dependent rise.

Accordingly, we suggest that the intensity and duration of suppression should be individualized and periodically re-evaluated alongside standard cardiovascular risk management. Larger prospective studies with clinical outcomes are needed to determine whether these observations contribute to increased cardiovascular risk and to clarify their clinical significance.
