Integrated chromatin accessibility and transcriptomic profiling provide insights into inflammation-associated endothelial remodeling in capillary malformations
Authors: Irving Mao, Vi Nguyen, Fenrong Li, Camilla F. Wenceslau, Wenbin Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Capillary malformation (CM) is a congenital vascular anomaly yet the regulatory mechanisms driving endothelial remodeling remain incompletely defined. Our previous data have shown that CM lesion-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), their induced ECs (iECs) and vascular organoids preserve vascular phenotypes. These models do not harbor detectable GNAQ p.R183Q and other common low varaint allele frequency mutations after reprogramming. To further explore the pathological signaling underlying endothelial phenotypes in these models, we profiled bulk chromatin accessibility from ATAC-seq and gene expression from RNA-seq in CM and control iECs. A panel of integrated genes was revealed by intersecting differentially accessible regions, differentially expressed genes, and peak-to-gene candidate links. The signatures of this panel highlighted the enriched pathways involving stem cell development and differentiation, inflammatory signaling, extracellular matrix reorganization, and endothelial function. Locus-level of KLF6 and TGF-beta-induced loci illustrated concordant accessibility and expression changes in CM iECs. These results are consistent with a model of inflammation-associated endothelial remodeling in CM. Furthermore, several transcription factors and transcriptional co-regulators associated with endothelial differentiation displayed increased distal chromatin accessibility accompanied by reduced transcript abundance. This data suggests that distal regulatory elements may participate in active transcriptional repression and contribute to impaired endothelial differentiation in CM iECs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s44505-026-00007-9.

Introduction

Cutaneous capillary malformations (CMs, a.k.a., Port Wine Stains) are congenital vascular anomalies affecting 0.3–0.9% of newborns and are characterized by progressive capillary dilation and vascular remodeling in the skin [1]. Histologically, CM lesions exhibit of endothelial cell (EC) and smooth muscle cell (SMC) proliferation, basement membrane replication, vascular barrier disruption, and progressive vessel dilatation [2, 3]. CM ECs also display differentiation-impaired, stem-cell-like features [2, 4, 5]. Several somatic mutations have been associated with CMs [6, 7], among which GNAQ p.R183Q is the most frequently identified mutation. It is typically present at low variant allele fractions (VAF) (about 4.25% on average in skin lesions) and distributed among multiple cell types within lesions [6, 8, 9]. Endothelial force-expression of GNAQ p.R183Q promotes vascular enlargement [10]. Recently, we generated CM specific induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), induced ECs (iECs) and vascular organoids (VOs) that recapitulate multiple disease-relevant phenotypes despite lacking detectable GNAQ p.R183Q and other common low-VAF mutations [5, 11–14].

Emerging evidence reveals inflammation in CM pathology [15]. Spatial transcriptomic analysis of CM vasculature revealed signatures of endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), adherens junction disruption, and endothelial differential abnormalities, and dysregulation of nuclear receptor subfamily 2 group F member 2 (NR2F2/COUP-TFII) [5]. However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying endothelial remodeling remain incompletely defined.

In this study, we performed integrative bulk ATAC-seq and bulk RNA-seq analysis in CM and control iECs. Multi-omics integration highlighted inflammation-associated endothelial remodeling in CM iECs. This study nominates a set of regulatory elements and implies potential therapeutic targets for CM treatment.

Materials and methods

CM iPSC culture and iEC differentiation

The generation and maintenance of control (52521_9 and 52521_4) and CM iPSC lines (4221_3, 3921_9, and 3921_16) were reported previously [5, 11–14]. An iEC differentiation protocol was tailored [16–19]. Briefly, on day 1 of iPSC differentiation, the medium was replaced with mesodermal induction medium (DMEM/F12 with 50 ng/mL Activin A, 1X B27 without insulin, 1X N2 supplement, 1X Glutamax, 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.1X geltrex). On day 2, the medium was replaced with mesodermal induction plus medium (DMEM/F12 with 15 ng/mL BMP4, 10 ng/mL bFGF, 50 ng/mL VEGF, 1X B27 without insulin, 1X N2 supplement, 1X Glutamax, 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.1X geltrex). On day 4, the medium was replaced with a fresh medium (DMEM/F12, 15 ng/mL BMP4, 10 ng/mL bFGF, 100 ng/mL VEGF, 1X N2 supplement, 1X Glutamax, 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.1X Geltrex). On days 5 and 7, the medium was replaced with iEC medium (StemPro complete medium containing 1X Glutamax, 200 ng/mL VEGF, 200 µM 8-Br-cAMP, 2 μM Forskolin, and 10 µM SB431542). On day 9, the cells were digested using 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA and passed into a 6-well plate. The cells were incubated with StemPro complete medium containing 200 ng/mL VEGF, 200 µM 8-Br-cAMP, and 10 ng/mL bFGF. On day 10, the iECs were maintained in regular EC medium and collected on day 14 for analysis. These models do not harbor detectable GNAQ p.R183Q and other common low-VAF mutations after reprogramming from lesional dermal fibroblasts [11]. The molecular phenotype characterization of monolayer iEC was reported previously with confirmation of endothelial biomarkers (CD31 and CDH5) and formations of capillary-like structures in vitro and in vivo [12, 14].

Bulk RNA-seq

The total RNAs from iECs were extracted using a Qiagen RNA isolation kit. The ribosomal RNAs were depleted using a Qiagen rRNA HMR kit. RNA-seq libraries were constructed using a Qiagen Standard RNA Library kit followed by the manufacture manual. The insertion size of the libraries was determined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Santa Clara, CA, USA). Library quantification was carried out using a NGS library quantification kit from Takara Bio. Paired-end sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) for 25–50 M reads per library. The raw reads were aligned and mapped to human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR [20]. Validation and raw counts of RNA transcripts were generated using FeatureCounts [21]. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using pyDESeq2 (v0.5.2) [22]. The filtered count matrix was normalized using DESeq2's median-of-ratios method to account for differences in library size and RNA composition between samples. False discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 plus an absolute log2FoldChange threshold of 0.583 was considered significant.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEApy package (prerank module, v1.1.9) to identify biological pathways and processes enriched among differentially expressed genes. Genes were ranked by their log₂ fold change values, with positive values indicating upregulation in CM samples relative to controls. Enrichment analysis was performed against curated gene sets including KEGG pathways, MSigDB Hallmark gene sets, and Human Phenotype Ontology terms. Enrichment significance was assessed using 1,000 permutations of the ranked gene list, and pathways with an FDR q-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Bulk ATAC-seq

Bulk ATAC-seq was performed on iECs using a kit from Active Motif (Carlsbad, CA, USA) or Diagenode (Denville, NJ, USA). The cells were harvested and treated using ATAC lysis buffer, followed by transposome-mediated tagmentation with barcode insertion into genome. Genomic DNA was extracted and ATAC-seq library was amplified. Library purification and size selection were performed using AmpureXP beads (Beckman, Brea, CA, USA). The insertion sizes of libraries were determined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Santa Clara, CA, USA). Library quantification was performed using a NGS library quantification kit from Takara Bio (Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). Paired-end sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) for 25–50 M reads per library. Tn5 transposase adapter sequence was removed from all paired-end reads using Cutadapt. Trimmed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using Bowtie2 [23]. Aligned reads were used for BAM conversion, followed by sorting and indexing by samtools. Duplicate reads were identified and marked using Picard MarkDuplicates which were excluded for subsequent analysis by MACS2 [24]. Open chromatin regions were identified for each sample individually using MACS2 for peak calling with a q-value cutoff of 0.05. Differential chromatin accessibility between CM and control samples was assessed using the DiffBind R package, followed by quantification of read counts within consensus peaks. DESeq2 was used for peak normalization and differential analysis. Differentially accessible regions (DARs) were defined by an FDR < 0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold-change > 0.583.

HOMER [25] was used to assign each DAR to a genomic context (promoter, exon, intron, intergenic region, etc.) and identify the nearest gene based on the GRCh38 reference annotation. Transcription factor (TF) binding motif enrichment within DARs in CM relative to control was assessed using HOMER's curated motif database. Complementarily, Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool (GREAT) [26] was applied to associate DARs with putative regulatory target genes based on genomic domain logic. GREAT was used to identify over-represented biological processes, molecular functions, and disease ontology terms associated with genomic regions of DARs, for gene-level biological interpretation of chromatin accessibility changes.

TF activity inference, footprinting analysis, and peak-to-gene linking analysis

TF activity was inferred from ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data using chromVAR and the DoRothEA/VIPER framework [27, 28], respectively. chromVAR deviation scores were calculated from a DiffBind-derived consensus peak set using JASPAR TF motifs, while VIPER estimated TF activity from variance-stabilized RNA-seq data and high-confidence DoRothEA regulons. TFs with concordant activity changes across chromatin accessibility and gene expression were integrated. To assess TF occupancy from ATAC-seq data, we performed footprinting analysis using TOBIAS (v0.17.3). Condition-merged BAM files (CM and control) were bias-corrected for Tn5 insertion preferences and analyzed within a DiffBind-derived consensus peak set. Footprint scores were calculated to estimate TF binding, and differential occupancy between conditions was determined using BINDetect with JASPAR vertebrate motifs. Aggregate footprint plots were generated for selected TFs to visualize condition-specific binding patterns across genome-wide motif sites.

To identify functional regulatory relationships between chromatin accessibility and gene expression, we performed correlation-based peak-to-gene linking analysis. Using GENCODE v44 annotations, gene regulatory windows (±100 kb from transcription start sites, TSS) were used, and overlapping consensus ATAC-seq peaks were identified to generate candidate peak-to-gene pairs. Sample-matched ATAC-seq accessibility (log2-transformed fragment counts) and variance-stabilized RNA-seq expression values were used to preserve sample-level variation for correlation analysis. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for each peak-to-gene pair across matched samples. Links were classified as promoter-proximal or distal based on distance to the TSS. Candidate regulatory interactions were defined by |r|> 0.8 and nominal p < 0.05, and differential accessibility and expression were assessed using log2 fold changes between CM and control samples.

Results

ATAC-seq identifies DARs and enhancer-dominated remodeling in CM iECs

To determine whether CM iECs exhibit global alterations in chromatin accessibility that may underlie disease-associated transcriptional regulations, we profiled chromatin accessibility by bulk ATAC-seq. ATAC-seq data metrices, including high mapping rates and expected fragment size, are shown in Suppl. Figure 1. Genome-wide peak distributions were largely comparable between CM and control iECs, indicating preserved global chromatin accessibility (Fig. 1A; Suppl. Figure 2A). Most accessible regions localized to intronic and intergenic regions, whereas promoter-associated peaks represented a smaller fraction (Fig. 1B; Suppl. Figure 2C). Strong enrichment of accessibility around TSSs was observed (Fig. 1C; Suppl. Figure 2B). Differential accessibility analysis identified 877 upregulated DARs and 224 downregulated DARs in CM iECs compared with controls (Fig. 1F). These results indicate that CM iECs retain overall chromatin organization but exhibit selective remodeling of regulatory elements, predominantly within distal genomic regions. DAR-associated motifs and pathways enriched by inflammation-related signaling

To identify the regulatory elements related to altered chromatin accessibility, we analyzed transcription factor motif enrichment and biological pathways associated with DAR-linked genes. CM up-DARs were enriched for motifs corresponding to TFs of endothelial identity and remodeling, including FOX-family, NKX2.5, and FLI1 motifs (Fig. 1D), whereas CM down-DARs were enriched for RUNX, RFX, and FOS motifs (Fig. 1E). Functional enrichment of genes associated with CM up-DARs highlighted TGF-β/SMAD, Wnt signaling, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), leukocyte aggregation and chemotaxis (Fig. 1G). In addition, CM up-DARs were enriched for negative regulation of biosynthetic, differentiation- and developmental-related processes (Fig. 1H). Histone H3 demethylation was the only significantly enriched term among CM down-DARs (Fig. 1H). Together, these findings suggest that chromatin remodeling in CM iECs preferentially activates inflammatory and developmental regulatory programs.

RNA-seq reveals inflammatory activation and altered differential processes

To determine how chromatin remodeling is reflected at the transcriptional level, we performed bulk RNA-seq profiling of CM and control iECs. Bulk RNA-seq identified 1142 upregulated and 1149 downregulated DEGs in CM iECs (Fig. 2A). Enrichment analyses revealed dysregulation of stem cell differentiation and chromatin remodeling, inflammatory pathways, apoptosis, cell–cell adhesion, phagocytosis, extracellular matrix disassembly, and metabolic signaling pathways (Fig. 2B, C, D). Particularly, enriched inflammatory pathways in CM iECs included TNF/NF-κB signaling, IL-1, 2, 6, 10 pathways, TOLL-like cascades, and leukocyte and lymphocyte activation (Fig. 2C). Representative genes involved in endothelial differentiation, remodeling, adhesion, and inflammatory pathways showed coordinated expression changes across CM and control groups (Fig. 2E-G). These transcriptional changes indicate that CM iECs adopt an inflammatory and differentiation-impaired state consistent with endothelial remodeling. TF activity inference and footprinting support altered regulatory networks

To nominate upstream regulators that may contribute to the observed transcriptional changes, we inferred TF activity and examined TF footprinting patterns from ATAC-seq data. TF activity inference showed concordant shifts of regulators linked to endothelial identity and inflammatory signaling, including NR2F2, GRHL2, RELA/REL, and the Notch effector RBPJ (Suppl. Figure 3A). TF footprinting demonstrated altered accessibility at motif sites corresponding to RUNX, FOX, GRHL2, and NR2F2 family members (Suppl. Figure 3B). These results suggest coordinated rewiring of endothelial developmental and inflammatory transcriptional networks in CM iECs.

Peak-to-gene linkage analysis nominates candidate regulatory elements

To connect chromatin accessibility changes with transcriptional regulation, we performed peak-to-gene linkage analysis using matched ATAC-seq and RNA-seq datasets. Candidate regulatory relationships spanned both promoter-proximal and distal regulatory elements (Suppl. Figure 4A-B), including canonical endothelial genes (e.g., TEK, CDH5, FGD5, NOTCH1) and other loci prioritized by correlation strength and genomic context (Suppl. Figure 4C). Both concordant and discordant accessibility-expression relationships were observed (Suppl. Figure 4D). Multiple endothelial and developmental genes were linked to candidate regulatory peaks across broad TSS distances (Suppl. Figure 4E). These analyses nominate candidate cis-regulatory elements that may contribute to endothelial remodeling in CM.

Integrative analysis reveals a high-confidence CM remodeling signature

To identify the highest-confidence regulatory events underlying CM endothelial remodeling, we integrated DAR-associated genes, DEGs, and high-confidence peak-to-gene candidate links. This analysis identified 156 overlapping genes supported by 232 linked peaks (Fig. 3A). These loci exhibited both concordant and discordant chromatin-transcription relationships and varying peak-to-gene correlation strengths (Fig. 3B). GREAT analysis of the integrated peak set showed enrichment of stem cell development and differentiation, inflammatory pathways, extracellular matrix reorganization, and lipid related metabolisms (Fig. 3C-E). Particularly, Inflammatory enrichment prominently included TNF/NF-κB signaling, TOLL-like pathways, and leukocyte and lymphocyte activation and aggregation (Fig. 3D). Although many loci exhibit coordinated changes in chromatin accessibility and expression, others show more complex patterns that may reflect context-specific or multilayered regulation (Fig. 3F). At locus-level, a CM-associated accessible element (peak #8184) at the KLF6 locus exhibited increased accessibility and expression in CM iECs (Fig. 3G), whereas a regulatory element at the transforming growth factor beta-induced (TGFBI) locus (peak #65,114) showed decreased accessibility accompanied by reduced transcript abundance (Suppl. Figure 5F).

Overall, these integrative analyses nominate a regulatory network linking chromatin remodeling to inflammatory activation and impaired endothelial differentiation in CM iECs.

Distal regulatory elements with increased accessibility but reduced transcription are enriched for developmental regulators

To determine whether increased chromatin accessibility can coexist with reduced transcriptional output and identify candidate mechanisms of transcriptional repression, we analyzed loci exhibiting increased ATAC accessibility but decreased RNA expression that were supported by high-confidence peak-to-gene candidate links. This analysis identified 40 genes associated with 54 candidate regulatory peaks (Fig. 4A). Nearly all discordant chromatin-transcription events were associated with distal regulatory elements (53 out of 54 peaks) while only one promoter-proximal peak was identified (Fig. 4A). These findings suggest that discordant chromatin-transcription relationships in CM iECs are predominantly associated with distal cis-regulatory elements rather than promoter accessibility. To characterize the biological functions of these discordant loci, we classified the encoded proteins and performed pathway enrichment analysis. Functional categorization demonstrated enrichment of TFs and transcriptional regulator (32.5%, 13/40 genes), signaling transduction molecules (22.5%, 9/40 genes), and structural/matrix proteins (20%, 8/40 genes) (Fig. 4B). The pathways related to these distal peak-associated genes were enriched for development and morphogenesis, including heart development, embryonic morphogenesis, endodermal cell fate specification, angiogenesis involved in coronary vascular morphogenesis, and regulation of basement membrane in embryonic body (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest that increased distal chromatin accessibility is preferentially associated with suppressive developmental regulatory mechanisms.

To identify candidate regulators underlying these discordant chromatin-transcription patterns, we examined representative transcriptional regulators within this gene set. The heatmap showed major TFs (RARB, NANOG, SOX2, TFAP2A, GLIS3, BNC2, and ZIC4), transcriptional co-regulators (IRF2BPL and NCOR2), and chromatin remodeling factors (ARID5B, DRAXIN, DPF1, DPF3), and other transduction receptors (APLNR, TGFBR1, and TLR4) (Fig. 4D). Majority of them have roles in endothelial differentiation and functions (Fig. 4D). The coexistence of open chromatin and transcriptional repression at these loci suggests that these regions may represent actively repressed regulatory elements, potentially mediated by distal transcriptional repressors or chromatin remodeling complexes rather than promoter closure. Collectively, these discordant chromatin-transcription signatures nominate distal regulatory elements as candidate cis-regulatory regions that may contribute to transcriptional repression of endothelial differentiation in CM iECs.

Discussion

This integrative chromatin accessibility and transcriptomic analysis showed a dysregulated endothelial state characterized by inflammatory signaling, endothelial dysfunction, and ECM remodeling in CM iECs.

TNF/NF-κB and Notch signaling emerged as prominent pathways from transcriptomic profiles in CM iECs. NF-κB activation has shown to drive broad transcriptional reprogramming affecting endothelial barrier function and remodeling [29–31], which aligns with the integrative signatures observed in this study. Notch signaling regulates endothelial fate, vascular patterning, and barrier properties, and intersects with inflammatory control [32, 33]. In our data, Notch-relevant genes, including NOTCH1 and DLL4, were represented among the peak-to-gene candidate linkage and an essential Notch transcriptional effector RBPJ shows differential TF activity patterns, suggesting a role of Notch pathway in rewiring endothelial remodeling in CM.

Importantly, Notch and NF-κB can engage in bidirectional crosstalk in ECs [32, 33], providing a plausible route of coupling inflammatory cues and endothelial fate at the chromatin level [29, 33]. Inflammatory NF-κB activation may modify the expression or activity of components of the Notch pathway, whereas Notch signaling can modulate NF-κB-dependent inflammatory responses and endothelial activation. Such interactions provide a potential mechanism through which inflammatory cues could be coupled to endothelial fate and differentiation programs. In our ATAC-seq findings, altered accessibility at regulatory elements associated with endothelial and developmental genes further raises the possibility that this signaling crosstalk is accompanied by remodeling of the cis-regulatory landscape. This interpretation is particularly relevant to the differentiation-impaired phenotype previously observed in CM-derived endothelial models [5], because persistent inflammatory signaling coupled with altered Notch-dependent fate regulation could interfere with acquisition or maintenance of a mature endothelial state. However, their causal roles will require direct testing through NF-κB and Notch perturbation, followed by assessment of endothelial differentiation, barrier function, inflammatory activation, and associated chromatin accessibility changes.

There are several limitations of this study. First, peak-to-gene candidate links are correlation-based, therefore representing candidate regulatory relationships rather than causal interactions. Further experiments will be required to validate the actual contribution of these links. Second, bulk profiling masks potential heterogeneity within iEC populations. Third, the limited sample size necessitates cautious interpretation. The identified regulatory elements and locus-specific examples should be considered hypothesis-generating. Fourth, this descriptive study lacks validation experiments. Inflammation-associated endothelial remodeling could be a cause or a response to disrupted vascular integrity in CM. Future studies using CRISPR, pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB, or Notch signaling, and enhancer perturbation will be necessary to determine whether the candidate regulatory elements revealed here are necessary or sufficient to drive disease-associated endothelial phenotypes. Fifth, the observed chromatin accessibility patterns are consistent with retained disease-associated regulatory states. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that these changes may be partially related to donor-specific epigenetic variation, reprogramming-associated remodeling, and batch-to-batch differentiation-related effects. Sixth, our data has shown enriched histone modification terms in pathway analysis. These findings raise the possibility that disease-associated chromatin states may be incompletely reset during reprogramming or re-established during endothelial differentiation; however, the molecular basis of this phenomenon remains unknown and needs further investigation.

Overall, this study provides an insight into candidate regulatory elements that modulate inflammation-related transcriptional activation underlying endothelial remodeling and ECM reprogramming in CM. In addition, a subset of transcription factors and transcriptional co-regulators involved in endothelial differentiation exhibited increased distal chromatin accessibility despite decreased RNA expression. These discordant chromatin-transcription pattern suggests that distal regulatory elements may mediate active repression of endothelial differentiation in CM.
