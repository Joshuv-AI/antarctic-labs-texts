Identified VCAM1 as prognostic gene in gastric cancer by co-expression network analysis
Authors: Wenjuan Li, Hong Gao, Jianjun Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe diffuse gastric cancer (DGC) is a malignant tumor distinct from intestinal gastric cancer (IGC). This study aims to identify genetic variances and potential diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for diverse types of gastric cancer utilizing an extensive dataset. Data from RNA sequencing and clinical pathological details were acquired from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset. Co‐expression gene modules were constructed via Weighted Gene Co‐Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA), followed by deciphering gene functions and protein–protein interaction networks within significantly associated modules. In total, analysis was conducted on 56,753 genes from 247 individuals with gastric cancer. Particularly, 621 genes from the green module exhibited strong associations with the Lauren type of gastric cancer. The prominent genes in the green module showed enrichment in processes such as signal transduction, immune response, and the positive regulation of GTPase activity. Noteworthy among these, VCAM1 was identified as the central gene linked to patients’ prognosis. Moreover, 72 gastric cancer specimens were collected from The First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated a significantly higher expression of VCAM1 in DGC compared to IGC (p = 0.019). Furthermore, it was confirmed that VCAM1 expression serves as a prognostic indicator for patients with DGC (p = 0.002), a correlation not observed in IGC (p = 0.760). In conclusion, this study identifies VCAM1 as a promising diagnostic and prognostic factor, suggesting novel avenues for diagnostic and therapeutic approaches in gastric cancer.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Lauren classification, WGCNA, VCAM 1

Introduction

Gastric cancer ranks as the fifth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related death globally. In 2023, there were an estimated over 26,500 new cases and 11,130 deaths in the United States [1]. Adenocarcinoma is the primary histological type of gastric cancer, characterized by notable heterogeneity in cell differentiation, architecture, and molecular pathogenesis. The Lauren classification for gastric cancer patients was established by Lauren and colleagues in 1965 [2]. Under this classification system, gastric adenocarcinomas are classified into two main histological types: DGC and IGC. DGC is defined by poor differentiation and the existence of individual or loosely connected tumor cells lacking gland formation, whereas IGC exhibits well differentiation and glandular structures. Remarkably, DGC patients are generally younger, exhibit increased lymph node metastasis rates, and typically have a worse prognosis compared to IGC patients [3, 4]. It is acknowledged that DGC and IGC are distinct diseases characterized by varying gene expression profiles, implying the feasibility of subtype-specific targeted therapies [5–7]. Although there is existing knowledge on the subject, only a few pilot studies have investigated the transcriptome-level differences between these subtypes of gastric cancer. The distinct transcriptomic variances between DGC and IGC are still not well-understood.

The WGCNA is extensively employed in tumor research due to its capacity to identify co-expressed genes, which sets it apart from other bioinformatics methodologies [8–11]. WGCNA not only assesses gene expression disparities across samples but also explores the intrinsic connections among genes [12]. Within the framework of WGCNA, genes displaying comparable expression patterns are clustered into modules through specific calculation methods. Following this, the correlation coefficients between these modules and clinical traits are computed, with modules showing the strongest correlations suggesting notable links to clinical disease phenotypes. This study aims to bridge this gap through an in-depth analysis of a large dataset to reveal genetic variations and investigate potential diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for different forms of gastric cancer.

Materials and methods

Data download and study design

The raw three-level RNA sequencing data and clinical-pathological information were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/). Only gastric cancer patients with confirmed Lauren classification status were included. The association between gene expression levels of gastric cancer patients and survival was analyzed using the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset (GSE 14210, GSE 15459, GSE 22377, GSE 29272, and GSE 51105), accessible through the KM plotter tool (http://kmplot.com).

The WGCNA analysis in RNA-seq data of gastric cancer

The RNA-seq data were first quantile-normalized and log2-transformed before analysis. In cases where genes had multiple probes, the gene expression values were averaged. Given that only highly expressed genes are likely to be functionally relevant in tumor cells, we selected the top 25% highest expressed genes for further analysis. The selected genes underwent evaluation using the WGCNA algorithm, with cluster analysis performed using R software. Initially, a sample cluster was created using Pearson’s correlation analysis, and samples showing outliers were removed. Subsequently, the appropriate power value for module scale independence and average connectivity was determined through a gradient test ranging from 1 to 20. Following this, the optimal power value, based on the results of Pearson’s correlation analysis, was selected for module construction. To ensure robust analysis, modules containing more than 50 genes were retained. Finally, using the WGCNA algorithm, the relationship between co-expression modules and clinical phenotypes was analyzed with R software, and the results were visualized using the Heatmap toolkit.

Function annotation of clinically significant modules

For exploring the biological functions of the most clinically relevant module, the genes contained in this module underwent analysis using The Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) for Gene Ontology (GO) function annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. The significance thresholds applied were p < 0.01 and FDR < 0.05.

Hub genes identification

The identification of hub genes was conducted in three steps. Initially, the most clinically significant module showing correlation with the cancer phenotype was selected. Subsequently, gene significance (reflecting the correlation between genes and specific clinical phenotypes) and module membership (indicating the correlation between genes and the module’s expression profile) were calculated, each with its predefined threshold. A correlation value greater than 0.9 for cor-gene-Module-Membership and above 0.2 for cor-gene-Trait-Significance were set as criteria for hub gene screening within each module. Additionally, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network for the hub module was constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/), where genes with a minimum required interaction score exceeding 0.9 were identified as potential hub genes within the PPI network. Lastly, the key genes were determined by extracting genes that were common between the module hub genes and the PPI network hub genes.

Hub genes validation

The expression levels of key genes across different Lauren types were illustrated in boxplots using the ggplot2 package. Statistical significance was determined through analysis of variance (ANOVA). Subsequently, only genes showing significant differential expression between tumor and non-tumor tissues underwent further analysis. The prognostic potential of each key gene was assessed using Kaplan–Meier (K–M) survival analysis, with validation of results using the KM plotter tool (http://kmplot.com). Optimal cutoff points were automatically chosen, and log-rank P-values were calculated independently.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue samples obtained from gastric cancer patients, cut into 4 μm-thick sections. The tissues were deparaffinized in xylene and then rehydrated accordingly. Subsequently, the tissues underwent overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary anti-VCAM1 antibody (HPA034796, Sigma-Aldrich; 1:100). Protein expression levels were assessed using standard scoring criteria, where scores up to 50% indicated low expression and scores above 50% denoted high expression. Two pathologists independently evaluated both the staining process and the results.

The average age of the participants was 64.9 years, with a median follow-up period of 10.1 months.

Construction of gene co-expression modules for gastric cancer

Following the previously described method, this study analyzed 14,189 genes from gastric cancer tissues. The mean connectivity of these genes was computed to set the soft thresholding power at 9 (Fig. 2B, C). The co-expression modules were then examined using this soft thresholding power, leading to the identification of 12 modules through the WGCNA package. Figure 3 shows that the green module, which consisted of 621 genes, displayed the highest correlation with the Lauren subtypes of gastric cancer.

Gene function and annotation enrichment analysis of green module

The functions of genes within the green module were examined. Figure 4A displays the GO and KEGG enrichments specific to this module. With 621 genes in the green module, key genes were linked to signal transduction (GO: 0007165), immune response (GO: 0006955), and positive regulation of GTPase activity (GO: 0043547). Further analysis involved exploring KEGG pathways, indicating enrichment in cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (hsa04060) and the chemokine signaling pathway (hsa04062) (Fig. 4B).

Identification of hub genes for Lauren type in the green module

A total of 130 hub genes were identified in the green module (Fig. 5A). Additionally, the top 50 hub genes from the PPI network analysis were recognized and visualized using Cytoscape. By integrating the potential hub genes identified through these two methods, 13 genes (BTK, CCR4, RHOH, PTPRC, IRF4, ITGAL, CD3G, CD247, VCAM1, CD28, CD3D, JAK3, GRAP2) were identified as significant in both approaches (Fig. 5B). Comparative analysis of gene expression levels between DGC and IGC patients revealed significantly higher expression levels in all 13 genes in DGC (Fig. 5C). However, only 8 genes (CCR4, RHOH, PTPRC, ITGAL, CD3G, VCAM1, CD28, JAK3) displayed notably elevated expression levels in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 5D). This suggests that these 8 genes may play a pivotal role in tumor initiation and differentiation.

Survival analysis

GC patients were stratified into high and low expression groups based on the mRNA levels of genes in tumor tissue. Survival analysis was then performed using follow-up data from the TCGA database, employing log-rank tests to ascertain statistical significance. The results revealed a potential correlation between elevated VCAM1 expression and poorer prognosis among patients (Fig. 6A). This observation stimulates the hypothesis that VCAM1 could be a key player in tumor progression.

A Kaplan–Meier Analysis of VCAM 1 in the TCGA data set. B Kaplan–Meier Analysis of VCAM 1 in the GEO data set. The DGC patients’ survival was affected by the expression of VCAM 1 (C), whereas, the IGC patients were not (D)

Hub genes validation

The relationship between VCAM1 expression and survival outcomes in gastric cancer patients was validated using the KM plotter tool (http://kmplot.com). The optimal cutoff value was determined automatically for this analysis. The study included 593 patients from five Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets (GSE 14210, GSE 15459, GSE 22377, GSE 29272, and GSE 51105). Patients were classified into high and low VCAM1 expression groups based on VCAM1 levels and the automatically determined optimal cutoff. Notably, as shown in Fig. 6B, patients in the high VCAM1 expression group exhibited inferior overall survival rates (HR = 0.79, 95% CIs 0.65–0.96, p = 0.018). Subgroup analysis indicated a significant impact of VCAM1 expression on the survival of DGC patients, whereas no significant effect was observed in IGC patients (Fig. 6C, D).

Tissue validation was conducted to further affirm the association of VCAM1 with the Lauren classification of gastric cancer. Immunohistochemical analysis was performed on 72 gastric cancer tissues from The First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China. Representative staining images are depicted in Fig. 7A, and detailed clinical and pathological characteristics are provided in Table 2. Among the analyzed tissues, 33 were categorized as DGC and 39 as IGC. The analysis revealed significantly higher VCAM1 expression in DGC compared to IGC (p = 0.019). Moreover, Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis demonstrated a correlation between elevated VCAM1 expression in DGC patients and poorer prognosis (Fig. 7B), while no significant association was observed between VCAM1 expression and overall survival (OS) in IGC patients (Fig. 7C).

Discussion

In this study, utilizing data from the TCGA and GEO datasets, we investigated the gene expression differences between DGC and IGC using the WGCNA method. Through this analysis, we identified 12 gene modules that were associated with various patient phenotypes. The significant variations observed in these modules are likely attributed to differences in tumor phenotypes. Particularly, the green module showed the strongest correlation with patients’ Lauren phenotypes. The GO and KEGG analyses revealed that the green module was predominantly enriched in immune response, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, cell adhesion molecules, and positive regulation of GTPase activity. The distinct gene functions observed between DGC and IGC strongly suggest that these subgroups represent not only distinct cancer types at the morphological level but also at the molecular level. Notably, within the green module, only VCAM1 showed an association with survival outcomes.

DGC and IGC exhibit significant differences not only in histological morphology but also in biological behavior. It is well-established that patients with DGC, as opposed to those with IGC, are generally younger and often manifest advanced lymph node metastasis, leading to a poorer prognosis [3, 4]. Despite the clinical distinctions, the molecular differences that delineate DGC from IGC remain ambiguous. In 2014, based the data from 32 gastric cancer samples, Tanabe et al. found that the mRNA ratio of CDH2 to CDH1 could discriminate between the Lauren subtypes [13]. Nevertheless, this marker did not outperform the diagnostic accuracy of histological examination. During the progression of stomach cancer through multistep carcinogenesis, a variety of genetic and epigenetic changes accumulate. Some of these alterations are common between intestinal and diffuse gastric cancer subtypes, while others are unique to specific histological types. Tumor initiation and advancement involve the coordinated influence of multiple genes, contributing to the diverse nature of tumor growth. DGC exhibits a more aggressive biological behavior compared to IGC, suggesting the presence of unique gene expression profiles associated with DGC progression. The identification of these genetic markers is crucial for precise diagnosis and effective treatment of gastric cancer patients. With advancements in sequencing technology, a growing number of oncogenes have been discovered. A comprehensive analysis by The Cancer Genome Atlas research network, based on genomic profiles of 295 primary gastric cancer samples, classified gastric cancer into four molecular subgroups. The study showed that DGC predominantly clustered within the group displaying genomic stability (73%), while IGC was more prevalent in cases with chromosomally unstable tumors. Clearly, at the molecular level, DGC and IGC represent distinct disease entities [14].

Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) play a pivotal role as essential cell surface proteins in cellular signal transduction. Intercellular adhesion, facilitated by CAMs, plays a significant role in cell differentiation, with disruptions in regular cell–cell contacts being evident during neoplastic transformation and metastasis [15–20]. CAMs expressed on lymphocytes and vascular endothelial cells are thought to have a substantial impact on the immune response to tumors and tumor metastasis [21]. Numerous studies have indicated a correlation between cell adhesion and various Lauren types of gastric cancer; however, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this relationship remain elusive [22–24]. Dysfunctions in tumor cell adhesion play a critical role in tumor metastasis [25, 26]. The loss of tumor cell adhesion can detach individual tumor cells from the primary tumor, allowing these cells to migrate freely. Subsequently, these detached tumor cells can spread to distant organs through blood vessels and lymphatic channels [15, 27]. Although the mechanisms behind the loss of tumor cell adhesive molecules are not yet fully understood, VCAM1, a widely distributed protein constitutively expressed on immune cells, epithelial cells, and endothelial cells, has been implicated in tumor metastasis [28, 29]. Numerous studies indicate that VCAM1 plays a crucial role in tumor metastasis. For example, Li Yang et al. identified VCAM1 expression in gastric cancer cells, noting significantly higher levels in gastric cancer compared to adjacent normal gastric tissue (75.6% versus 12.2%, p < 0.05). Remarkably, they further revealed that elevated VCAM1 expression correlated with an increased rate of lymph node metastasis [30]. In 2006, Heonyong Park and colleagues illustrated the co-localization of VCAM1 with Caveolin-1, suggesting the participation of the VCAM1-Caveolin-1 complex in controlling the metastatic capabilities of gastric tumor cells. These discoveries provide evidence for the hypothesis that VCAM1 potentially has a pivotal role in gastric cancer differentiation [31]. In the current study, through WGCNA analysis, we have pinpointed VCAM1 as an oncogene that drives differentiation in GC histology. Furthermore, elevated expression of VCAM1 has been associated with adverse outcomes in patients diagnosed with DGC. Given the unfavorable prognosis linked with DGC, directing therapies towards VCAM-1 may offer a promising novel treatment avenue for DGC patients in the upcoming years.

In conclusion, our study utilized a co-expression network to underline unique molecular features across diverse subgroups of gastric cancer. Furthermore, we have elucidated a significant correlation between VCAM1 expression levels and the prognosis of DGC. The identification of potential target genes for different gastric cancer subgroups in our research offers a promising direction for future therapeutic interventions in gastric cancer treatment.
