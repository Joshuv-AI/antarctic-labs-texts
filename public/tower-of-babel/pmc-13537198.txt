Validation of a Novel Dietary Knowledge and Needs Questionnaire: The Postoperative Colorectal Cancer TCM Diet Questionnaire (PCRC-TDQ)
Authors: Lanxia Pan, Ankang Liu, Jie Zhang, Xia Li, Xiaodong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative colorectal cancer (CRC) patients may benefit from both conventional nutritional support and culturally specific Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) dietary guidance, yet no validated instrument specifically assesses their TCM dietary knowledge and educational needs. This study aimed to develop and preliminarily validate the Postoperative Colorectal Cancer TCM Diet Questionnaire (PCRC-TDQ) for assessing postoperative CRC patients’ TCM dietary knowledge and educational needs.MethodsAn initial 40-item pool was generated through literature review, multidisciplinary discussion, qualitative interviews, two rounds of Delphi expert consultation with 20 experts, and pilot testing, resulting in a 30-item preliminary questionnaire. The questionnaire was administered to 252 postoperative CRC patients, and 250 valid questionnaires were included in the psychometric analysis. Item analysis, content-validity assessment, exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and reliability testing were conducted.ResultsThree items (K6, N7, and N9) were removed after exploratory factor analysis (EFA), resulting in the final 27-item PCRC-TDQ comprising 19 knowledge items and eight needs items. The PCRC-TDQ contained two conceptual domains (TCM dietary knowledge and dietary needs), with four underlying factors identified within these domains, explaining 51.43% of the total variance. Item-level content validity indices ranged from 0.88 to 1.00, and the scale-level content validity index/average was 0.990. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.899 for the total instrument, 0.913 for the knowledge domain, and 0.849 for the needs domain.ConclusionThe 27-item PCRC-TDQ demonstrated satisfactory preliminary content validity, structural validity, internal consistency, and test–retest reliability in the study sample. It may serve as a preliminary assessment tool during initial postoperative dietary consultations to identify TCM dietary knowledge gaps and educational needs. Independent structural validation, including confirmatory factor analysis in separate cohorts, and multicenter validation are required before routine clinical implementation.Keywords: colorectal cancer, traditional Chinese medicine, dietary knowledge, educational needs, questionnaire development, psychometric validation, postoperative care

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies and a leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide.1,2 Its development is influenced by multiple environmental factors, lifestyle behaviors, and alterations in the gut microbiota.3 In China, the burden of CRC continues to increase, with dietary and lifestyle factors contributing to disease risk.4,5 Although surgical resection remains the primary curative treatment, postoperative patients frequently experience gastrointestinal dysfunction, altered bowel habits, malnutrition, and reduced quality of life.6 Therefore, dietary management and education represent important components of postoperative recovery and survivorship care.

Postoperative dietary management for CRC patients traditionally focuses on nutritional adequacy, symptom management, and prevention of malnutrition. Existing nutritional assessment tools, such as the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002)7 and the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST),8 are widely used to identify nutritional risk but primarily focus on nutritional status rather than patients’ understanding of dietary principles or educational needs. Consequently, these instruments cannot fully capture culturally specific dietary beliefs and information needs among patients receiving Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)-based dietary guidance.

TCM dietary guidance provides a culturally embedded approach that emphasizes individualized dietary regulation based on concepts including food–medicine homology, the four qi, five flavors, syndrome differentiation, and dietary contraindications.9,10 Unlike standardized nutritional recommendations, TCM dietary practice considers individual constitution, disease characteristics, and treatment-related conditions when providing dietary advice. For postoperative CRC patients, concepts such as regulating the spleen and stomach, avoiding inappropriate dietary factors, and selecting foods according to individual conditions constitute important components of TCM dietary education.11–15

Recent studies have explored the potential biological effects of TCM dietary components in cancer care. For example, fermented Coix seed polysaccharides and other plant-derived polysaccharides have shown potential immunomodulatory and gut microbiota-related effects in preclinical studies.16–18 However, these findings remain preliminary and mainly provide biological context rather than direct evidence for clinical dietary intervention. Clinical studies have suggested that TCM-based dietary interventions may alleviate treatment-related symptoms and improve quality of life among cancer patients.19,20

Despite increasing interest in TCM-based supportive care, validated instruments specifically assessing TCM dietary knowledge and educational needs among postoperative CRC patients remain unavailable. Existing dietary assessment tools developed for oncology populations mainly evaluate nutritional risk, dietary intake, or malnutrition rather than culturally specific dietary knowledge and information needs.7,8,21 In China, previous studies have explored dietary factors associated with CRC outcomes and patient dietary experiences; however, many self-developed questionnaires lack systematic psychometric validation.22 Furthermore, quantitative evidence regarding the prevalence of TCM dietary practices specifically among postoperative CRC patients remains limited. Therefore, a scientifically developed instrument is needed to assess patients’ TCM dietary knowledge and educational needs.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and preliminarily validate the Postoperative Colorectal Cancer TCM Diet Questionnaire (PCRC-TDQ), an instrument designed to assess TCM dietary knowledge and educational needs among postoperative CRC patients. The PCRC-TDQ was developed to identify patients’ knowledge gaps and information needs, thereby supporting more targeted and culturally appropriate dietary education during survivorship care.

Theoretical Framework

The PCRC-TDQ was developed based on fundamental concepts of TCM dietary theory, particularly the principle of “food and medicine sharing the same origin”, which describes the relationship between dietary materials and therapeutic practices.15,23 The theoretical framework incorporated several key TCM dietary principles, including the four qi (Cold, Hot, Warm, and cool), five flavors (Sour, Bitter, Sweet, Pungent, and Salty), syndrome differentiation, meridian-related dietary considerations, and dietary taboos. These concepts are particularly relevant to postoperative CRC dietary management because they emphasize individualized dietary regulation according to patients’ physical conditions, disease characteristics, and treatment-related needs. Based on this theoretical framework, relevant concepts and keywords were extracted to guide item generation and questionnaire development.

Methods

Aims and Study Design

The primary aim of this study was to develop the Postoperative Colorectal Cancer TCM Diet Questionnaire (PCRC-TDQ) and evaluate its preliminary psychometric properties for assessing TCM dietary knowledge and educational needs among postoperative colorectal cancer (CRC) patients. The study consisted of two phases: questionnaire development and psychometric validation. The questionnaire development phase included literature review, multidisciplinary discussion, qualitative interviews, expert consultation, and pilot testing. The psychometric validation phase evaluated item performance, content validity, construct validity, and reliability of the preliminary questionnaire.

Questionnaire Development

Item Generation

A literature review was conducted to identify relevant concepts related to postoperative CRC dietary management, TCM dietary principles, and existing dietary assessment approaches. Literature published from database inception to March 2019 was screened according to predefined criteria. After screening and selection, 411 relevant documents were retained (Figure 1). In addition, relevant books, clinical guidelines, and expert consensus documents related to cancer nutrition and TCM dietary management were reviewed.

A total of 43 candidate content elements were extracted from the literature and supporting materials. After discussion and consolidation by the research team, an initial item pool consisting of 40 items was developed, including 30 items assessing TCM dietary knowledge and 10 items assessing TCM dietary needs.

Multidisciplinary Discussion and Qualitative Interviews

A multidisciplinary discussion group was established to incorporate perspectives from healthcare professionals and postoperative CRC patients. The group consisted of 26 participants, including two physicians, 20 nurses, and four postoperative CRC patients. Through structured discussions and brainstorming sessions, 10 themes related to TCM dietary management were identified, including TCM dietary theory, food classification and functions, cooking methods, dietary principles, food therapy, dietary contraindications, and differences between professional and patient understanding.

To further explore practical challenges and information needs related to TCM dietary guidance, semi-structured qualitative interviews were conducted with 23 participants, including two physicians, nine nurses, and 12 postoperative CRC patients. Each interview lasted approximately 30–45 minutes. Interview data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method.24,25 Healthcare professionals mainly emphasized the complexity of TCM dietary concepts, the need for standardized dietary guidance, and uncertainty regarding professional responsibilities. Patients mainly reported insufficient understanding of basic TCM dietary concepts, strong interest in food categories and functions, misconceptions regarding dietary contraindications, and unrealistic expectations regarding food therapy.

Four cross-cutting themes were identified: limited understanding of basic TCM dietary theory, high demand for information regarding food categories and functions, misunderstanding of dietary contraindications, and idealized expectations regarding dietary therapy. These findings informed subsequent item refinement and questionnaire development.

Expert Consultation and Pilot Testing

Two rounds of Delphi expert consultation were conducted to evaluate item relevance, clarity, and applicability. Experts were eligible if they had at least 10 years of clinical or research experience in relevant fields and agreed to participate in the study. The final expert panel consisted of 20 experts, including five experts in TCM oncology, ten experts in integrated Chinese and Western medicine nursing, three faculty members in TCM nursing, and two registered dietitians. The characteristics of the expert panel are presented in Table 1. Experts evaluated each item regarding importance, rationality, and feasibility and provided written recommendations. Expert consensus was assessed using item mean scores, coefficients of variation, full-score rates, and Kendall’s coefficient of concordance. Based on expert feedback and research team discussion, the questionnaire was refined into a 30-item preliminary version consisting of 20 knowledge items and 10 needs items.

A pilot study was subsequently conducted among 40 postoperative CRC patients to evaluate item comprehension, wording clarity, response options, item sequence, and completion time. Feedback from participants was reviewed by the research team, and the 30-item questionnaire was retained for large-scale psychometric validation.

Psychometric Validation

Participants and Study Setting

Patients were recruited by convenience sampling from the outpatient clinic and oncology department of a tertiary Grade A TCM hospital in Shanghai, China, between August 2019 and January 2020. The inclusion criteria were: (a) diagnosis of colorectal cancer (CRC) based on the 2018 national guidelines;26 (b) voluntary participation after understanding the study purpose and procedures; (c) postoperative recovery stage; and (d) ability to communicate clearly and respond independently. Exclusion criteria were other serious gastrointestinal diseases, a stoma or non-sphincter-preserving surgery, and severe comorbidities preventing questionnaire completion. Based on the recommended ratio of 5–10 participants per item and allowance for approximately 10% invalid or missing questionnaires, the target sample for the 30-item preliminary questionnaire was 165–330 participants.27

Questionnaire Structure and Scoring

The final PCRC-TDQ consisted of 27 items organized into two conceptual domains: TCM dietary knowledge and TCM dietary needs (Supplementary Material). Following exploratory factor analysis, four underlying factors were identified within these two domains. Knowledge items assessed patients’ understanding of TCM dietary concepts and were rated using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“very unclear”) to 5 (“very clear”). The possible knowledge-domain score ranged from 19 to 95, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. Needs items assessed patients’ requirements for TCM dietary information and guidance and were rated from 1 (“not needed at all”) to 5 (“very much needed”). The possible needs-domain score ranged from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating stronger educational needs. Because knowledge and needs represent related but distinct constructs, the two domain scores were interpreted independently rather than combined into a single total score.

Data Collection and Quality Control

Data collection was performed by a trained survey team using both online and paper-based questionnaires. Before data collection, investigators received standardized training regarding study objectives, questionnaire administration procedures, and ethical requirements. Only investigators who completed the training assessment were permitted to collect data. During questionnaire completion, researchers provided standardized instructions and assistance, when necessary, particularly for elderly participants or those with visual difficulties. Completed questionnaires were reviewed immediately after submission to identify missing or inconsistent responses. Participant confidentiality was maintained throughout the data collection process.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for descriptive and psychometric analyses. For item discrimination, preliminary questionnaire total scores were ranked and the upper and lower 27% groups were selected according to the classical extreme-group method.28 The resulting groups contained 63 and 70 participants, respectively. The critical ratio was defined as the independent-samples t statistic comparing item scores between the two groups. The conventional t test was used when Levene’s test supported homogeneity of variance, and Welch’s t test was used when variances were unequal. Items with |t| < 3.00 or P > 0.05 were considered insufficiently discriminative. Corrected item–domain correlations were assessed using Pearson coefficients; coefficients of at least 0.40 with P < 0.05 were considered acceptable.

For content validity, eight representative experts were selected from the Delphi panel: three TCM CRC specialists, three oncology nursing experts, one nutritionist, and one statistician. Each expert rated item relevance on a five-point scale. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) was calculated as the proportion rating an item 4 or 5, and the scale-level CVI/average (S-CVI/Ave) was the mean of all I-CVIs.29

Construct validity was examined using EFA with principal component extraction and Varimax rotation. Sampling adequacy was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin statistic and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Factor retention was guided by eigenvalues greater than 1, the scree plot, interpretability, and the prespecified two-domain framework. Items were considered acceptable when communalities were at least 0.20, primary factor loadings were at least 0.40, and the difference between primary and secondary loadings was at least 0.15. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and split-half reliability. Test–retest reliability was evaluated in 30 participants who repeated the questionnaire after two weeks. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Municipal Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine in 2018 (Approval No. 2018SHL-KY-17).

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 252 questionnaires were distributed, and 250 valid questionnaires were included in the analysis, yielding a valid response rate of 99.21%. The sample included 153 men and 97 women, with a mean age of 66.36 ± 10.06 years. Among the participants, 148 had colon cancer and 102 had rectal cancer. In addition, 217 participants (86.80%) reported previous exposure to TCM dietary information. Treatment-related characteristics, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy exposure, were summarized descriptively and were not included in item analysis or psychometric validation models.

Questionnaire Development

The literature review identified 411 eligible documents and 43 candidate content elements, which were consolidated into an initial 40-item pool comprising 30 TCM dietary knowledge items and 10 TCM dietary needs items. The multidisciplinary discussion generated ten themes related to TCM dietary management, and qualitative interviews identified four cross-cutting themes: limited understanding of basic TCM dietary theory, high demand for information regarding food categories and functions, misunderstanding of dietary contraindications, and idealized expectations regarding dietary therapy. Two rounds of Delphi expert consultation were conducted with 20 experts. In the first round, 26 questionnaires were distributed and 20 valid responses were received (response rate: 76.92%); the second-round response rate was 100%. The mean expert authority coefficient was 0.90, and Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (Kendall’s W) values were 0.737 and 0.766 for the first and second rounds, respectively. Based on expert recommendations and research team discussion, the questionnaire was refined into a 30-item preliminary version consisting of 20 knowledge items and 10 needs items. The 30-item questionnaire was subsequently pilot-tested in 40 postoperative CRC patients to evaluate item comprehension, wording clarity, and completion time.

Item Analysis

All 30 preliminary items demonstrated satisfactory discrimination between the upper- and lower-score groups, with critical ratio values greater than 3.000 and P values ≤0.001. For items showing unequal variances, variance-adjusted comparisons were performed using Welch’s t test, and the conclusions were consistent with those obtained from the conventional independent-samples t test. Corrected item-domain correlation coefficients ranged from 0.433 to 0.715 for the TCM dietary knowledge domain and from 0.450 to 0.817 for the TCM dietary needs domain. All correlations were statistically significant. No item was removed solely based on critical-ratio analysis or corrected item-domain correlation analysis.

Content Validity

Eight representative experts selected from the Delphi panel independently assessed the content validity of the questionnaire. The expert panel included three TCM CRC specialists, three oncology nursing experts, one nutritionist, and one statistician. All items demonstrated acceptable content relevance. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) ranged from 0.88 to 1.00, and the scale-level content validity index/average (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.990, indicating excellent agreement regarding item relevance and overall content coverage.

Exploratory Factor Analysis and Factor Structure

For the 30-item preliminary questionnaire, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin value was 0.866 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ2 = 3667.709, df = 435, P < 0.001). Initial extraction produced eight eigenvalues greater than 1, but the scree plot and factor interpretability supported a four-factor solution. After removal of K6, N7, and N9 because they did not meet the prespecified loading and cross-loading criteria, the final 27-item analysis yielded a Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin value of 0.861 and a significant Bartlett’s test (χ2 = 3149.169, df = 351, P < 0.001). Four factors explained 51.43% of the total variance, and primary loadings ranged from 0.405 to 0.894. Based on item content, the factors were named Practical TCM Dietary Guidance and Food Therapy, Fundamental TCM Dietary Concepts, Needs for Fundamental TCM Dietary Knowledge, and Needs for Practical Postoperative Dietary Guidance. The first two factors represented the TCM dietary knowledge domain, whereas the latter two represented the TCM dietary needs domain. Therefore, the final PCRC-TDQ consisted of two conceptual domains and four underlying factors.

Reliability

The final 27-item PCRC-TDQ demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.899 for the total instrument, 0.913 for the TCM dietary knowledge domain, and 0.849 for the TCM dietary needs domain. Split-half reliability coefficients were 0.919 for the overall questionnaire, 0.928 for the knowledge domain, and 0.853 for the needs domain. Test–retest reliability was evaluated in 30 participants who completed the questionnaire again after two weeks. Intraclass correlation coefficients were 0.990 for the total instrument, 0.990 for the knowledge domain, and 0.988 for the needs domain (all P < 0.001), indicating excellent temporal stability.

Discussion

Questionnaire Development and Content

This study developed a culturally specific instrument for assessing TCM dietary knowledge and educational needs among postoperative CRC patients. The development process integrated literature review, multidisciplinary discussion, patient and professional interviews, expert consultation, pilot testing, and large-sample psychometric evaluation, consistent with established principles for developing health measurement instruments.30 The progression from 40 initial items to a 30-item preliminary questionnaire and finally to the 27-item PCRC-TDQ reflects sequential content refinement and empirical evaluation.

To our knowledge, the PCRC-TDQ represents one of the first systematically developed instruments specifically designed to assess both TCM dietary knowledge and educational needs among postoperative CRC patients. In the validation sample, 86.80% of participants reported previous exposure to TCM dietary information; however, exposure does not necessarily indicate accurate understanding or sufficient mastery of TCM dietary concepts. The present study focused on instrument development and psychometric evaluation rather than estimating population-level knowledge or educational needs.

Construct and Content Validity

EFA is commonly used during the preliminary structural evaluation of newly developed instruments, whereas independent CFA is recommended for confirmation of the proposed factor structure.31 EFA supported a four-factor structure corresponding to two conceptual domains: TCM dietary knowledge and TCM dietary needs. The four factors reflected distinctions between fundamental TCM dietary concepts and practical dietary guidance within both domains. The lowest retained loading was 0.405, which exceeded the prespecified threshold of 0.40 and was considered conceptually interpretable.

The I-CVI range of 0.88–1.00 and S-CVI/Ave of 0.99 indicated strong expert agreement regarding item relevance and content coverage. Content validity assessment considers item relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility during instrument development.32 However, EFA provides exploratory evidence of construct validity, and independent confirmatory factor analysis using a separate sample is required to further verify the stability of the factor structure.

The knowledge and needs domains represent related but distinct constructs. Therefore, domain scores should be reported and interpreted independently in clinical or research applications. A higher knowledge score should not be interpreted as indicating lower or higher educational needs, and combining the two domains into a single total score is not recommended.

Reliability

Reliability is an important measurement property reflecting the consistency and reproducibility of an instrument.33 The total instrument and both domains demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, split-half reliability, and two-week test–retest reliability. These findings support the reproducibility of the PCRC-TDQ within the study sample. Nevertheless, the very high test–retest coefficients may partly reflect the relatively short retest interval, and future studies with larger samples and longer follow-up intervals are needed to further confirm temporal stability.

Potential Clinical Application

The PCRC-TDQ may serve as a preliminary assessment tool during initial postoperative dietary consultations to identify TCM dietary topics that patients do not understand and areas where additional educational support is needed. The instrument is intended to complement, rather than replace, conventional nutritional-risk screening tools such as NRS-20027 and MUST.8 The results may help healthcare providers prioritize educational content and develop more individualized dietary counseling strategies. However, the PCRC-TDQ does not provide treatment recommendations and has not been demonstrated to improve recurrence, survival, nutritional status, or quality of life. Future prospective studies are required to determine whether its application can improve patient-centered outcomes.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include the use of multiple content-generation approaches, participation of both patients and multidisciplinary professionals, two rounds of Delphi expert consultation, pilot testing, and comprehensive psychometric evaluation. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, participants were recruited using convenience sampling from a single tertiary TCM hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions and healthcare settings. Second, variation in postoperative duration among participants may have introduced heterogeneity in dietary experiences and educational needs. Third, questionnaire responses were self-reported and may have been influenced by recall bias or response bias. Fourth, the factor structure was explored in a single sample, and independent confirmatory factor analysis in a separate cohort is required to further establish structural validity. Fifth, Criterion validity is challenging when no appropriate gold standard exists for the target construct;30 therefore, comparison with related nutritional, educational, or survivorship measures was not performed and should be considered in future validation studies.

Conclusion

The final 27-item PCRC-TDQ comprises 19 TCM dietary knowledge items and eight TCM dietary needs items organized into two conceptual domains and four underlying factors. The instrument demonstrated satisfactory preliminary content validity, EFA-based construct validity, internal consistency, and test–retest reliability in the study sample. The PCRC-TDQ may serve as a preliminary assessment tool during postoperative dietary consultations to identify TCM dietary knowledge gaps and educational needs. Independent confirmatory factor analysis in a separate cohort, multicenter validation, comparison with relevant external measures, and prospective evaluation of clinical utility are required before routine clinical implementation.
