Morphological characteristics of corneal stromal stem cells and their age-related decline
Authors: Mitsuyo Maeda, Asami Eguchi, Yosky Kataoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe corneal limbus contains limbal epithelial stem cells, stromal stem cells (SSCs), and stromal cells (SCs), which collectively contribute to the regeneration and maintenance of corneal epithelial integrity and transparency. However, the functional relationship among these three cell types remains unclear. The aim of this study was to investigate their morphological characteristics, potential inter-cell-type transformation mechanisms, and age-related alterations in these processes in the mouse corneal limbus. Keratocan-positive stromal cells (keratocytes) were predominantly localized in the posterior cornea and exhibited telocyte features, including expression of CD34, PDGFRα, and NG2. In contrast, SCA-1- and CD90-positive stromal stem cells were primarily distributed in the anterior stroma, where they maintained contact with stromal processes and extended toward the epithelial basal layer. A subset displayed epithelial stem cell-like features, consistent with a mesenchymal–epithelial transition-like process within the limbal niche. Aging (to 2 years) in mice was associated with reduced SSC numbers and morphological alterations, suggesting a potential decline in niche-associated regenerative capacity. Together, these findings clarify the spatial organization and cellular interactions within the limbal microenvironment and provide a foundation for developing SSC-based strategies for ex vivo corneal epithelial regeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50744-3.Keywords: Stromal stem cells, Stromal cells, Limbal epithelial stem cells, Aging, Mesenchymal-epithelial transition, TelocytesSubject terms: Cell biology, Stem cells

Introduction

The visual function and transparency of the cornea depend on the integrity of the continuously renewing corneal outer epithelial layer, which is maintained by a population of stem cells located in the corneal limbus, the transitional zone between the cornea and sclera1. The corneal limbus comprises corneal epithelium, stroma, and endothelium, and the limbal cells exist within a unique local microenvironmental niche surrounded by the extracellular matrix containing stromal cells (SCs)2,3, where they are maintained as limbal epithelial stem cells (LESCs). However, the relationships among stromal stem cells (SSCs), SCs, and corneal LESCs within this niche remain poorly understood.

The limbal niche harbors LESCs, characterized by markers such as ABCG2, which differentiate into corneal epithelial cells. It also contains SSCs that produce interstitial cells. The corneal epithelium forms the outermost layer of the cornea, separated from the stroma by the Bowman layer. This stratified structure relies on continuous renewal from basal LESCs and provides protection, light refraction, and clear vision4. In addition to epithelial stem cells, the limbal stroma contains SSCs, a population of mesenchymal cells with stem cell characteristics, which together with the SCs (keratocytes), support corneal LESCs viability5. The corneal stroma accounts for approximately 80% of total corneal thickness and consists of a highly organized collagen-rich matrix with keratocytes (produced by SSCs) and proteoglycans6,7. SSCs are neural crest-derived cells exhibiting self-renewal and multipotent differentiation capabilities. They maintain the transparency and stability of the matrix and regulate the homeostasis of the corneal epithelium in collaboration with SCs, corneal epithelial cells, and endothelial cells5. Additionally, their ability to differentiate into multiple cell types, including SCs, positions them as a potential source for these rare cells5,8. Their anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and angiogenic properties enable them to suppress inflammation in diseased stroma, reconstruct damaged tissue, and restore transparency9.

SCs, or keratocytes, are spindle-shaped derivatives of SSCs. They interact with various nearby cells via dendritic processes. Recent morphological and immunolabeling studies in various organs have identified them as telocytes10,11. These SCs are characterized by a spindle- or triangular-shaped cytoplasm, few intracellular organelles, and multiple elongated telopods that enable communication between telocytes or adjacent cells, forming complex three-dimensional (3D) networks. Telocytes have been implicated in heterocellular signaling via direct contacts and extracellular vesicles, including exosomes7,10–14.

In other tissues, including the brain, skin, and gastrointestinal tract, telocyte-like cells express nerve–glial antigen 2 (NG2), a chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan associated with cell migration and survival14–17. However, NG2 expression in corneal limbal SCs remains unexplored. Generally, telocytes coexist with tissue-resident stem/progenitor cells, forming part of stem cell niche microenvironments across organs. They engage in crosstalk with stem and mesenchymal cells through direct cell-to-cell contacts or via the release of extracellular vesicles such as exosomes13,18–21.

Recent evidence suggests that SSCs can transdifferentiate into epithelium22. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to investigate the spatial organization, morphological characteristics, and cellular interactions of SSCs, SCs, and LESCs in the mouse corneal limbus using array tomography, immunoelectron microscopy, and immunohistochemistry. Additionally, we compared 8- and 2-year-old mice to investigate age-related alterations in these cellular populations. Elucidating these interactions may provide insights into limbal niche dynamics and inform future approaches to SC-based strategies for corneal epithelial regeneration.

Results

Corneal limbal cell architecture

The corneal limbus contains scleral connective tissue and vascular nerves, resulting in greater thickness than that of the central cornea. For observation, the region anterior to the vascular nerve layer was designated as the epithelial side, and the region posterior to this layer was designated as the endothelial side. On the endothelial side, spindle-shaped cells aligned parallel to epithelial cells between dense collagen fibers. The vascular-nerve layer contained mildly rounded LESCs and leukocytes around blood vessels. In contrast, the epithelial side featured oval-to-round cells with lower cell density and random arrangement (Fig. 1a–d).

Cluster of differentiation (CD) 90-positive SSCs were present on the epithelial side and around blood vessels

Immunostaining using a spinocerebellar ataxia type 1 (SCA-1) antibody, a selective marker commonly associated with mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), revealed co-expression with CD90; therefore, CD90 was used as an MSC marker. SCA-1/CD90-positive cells localized mainly to the epithelial side (Fig. 2a). Immunoelectron microscopy showed that CD90-positive cells on the epithelial side were primarily oval in shape, whereas those located in perivascular regions were more frequently round. Some CD90-positive cells were observed adjacent to or in direct contact with the epithelial basement membrane. Ultrastructurally, these cells exhibited centrally positioned nuclei with dense nuclear membranes and abundant heterochromatin immediately beneath the nuclear membrane. Their cytoplasm was slightly more abundant than that of SCs and contained identifiable organelles, including mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum. A few round cells with similar nuclear features were observed surrounding blood vessels (Fig. 2b). Overall, SCA-1/CD90–positive stromal cells were primarily distributed on the epithelial side of the limbus, with a smaller population in perivascular regions, and displayed morphological features distinct from those of resident stromal cells.

Stromal cells (keratocytes) express CD34, platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFRα), and NG2 and exhibit telocyte-like morphology

The SCs of the corneal limbus, commonly referred to as keratocytes, are spindle-shaped. These cells were densely packed and arranged nearly parallel to the corneal epithelium on the endothelial side. In contrast, they were fewer in number, irregularly shaped, and randomly oriented on the epithelial side. These cells co-expressed the keratocyte marker keratocan and PDGFRα (Fig. 3a). Therefore, PDGFRα was used as an SC marker. Furthermore, PDGFRα co-localized with CD34 and NG2, markers commonly associated with telocyte-like populations (Fig. 3b).

Immunoelectron microscopy revealed that PDGFRα-positive cells were spindle-shaped, with both nuclear and cytoplasmic components, and showed positive reactions along the cell membrane (Fig. 3c). Nuclei were elliptical-to-spindle-shaped, centrally located, and featured densely stained heterochromatin beneath the nuclear membrane. Cytoplasm was sparse across all layers and contained few organelles, including small Golgi bodies, mitochondria, and rough ER. Additionally, three to five long, thin processes extended from the cytoplasm on both sides of the cells.

These cells were identified as telocytes based on immunohistochemical and morphological characteristics. Their cytoplasmic processes surrounded blood vessels, adhered to each other between collagen fibers to form a dense stromal network, and were accompanied by a few extracellular vesicles (Fig. 3d–g). Based on these staining results and ultrastructural findings, the SCs of the corneal limbus, previously termed keratocytes, were reclassified as telocytes expressing CD34, PDGFRα, and NG2.

SC processes contact and sometimes surrounds SSCs

Because SSCs predominantly reside on the epithelial side, we investigated the relationship between SSCs and SCs in the anterior corneal epithelium using electron microscopy. On the epithelial side, one side of SSCs was in contact with long, thin SC processes (Fig. 4a, b). Notably, 3D electron microscopy using serial sections revealed that theSCs processes formed a dish-like depression around the contact site, cradling SSCs. At the contact site, SSCs extended numerous fold-like processes that contacted SCs processes (Fig. 4cI–IX, d). Age-related changes in SCs and SSCs

To assess age-related changes, the numbers of SCs and SSCs were measured using electron microscopy in five young (8-week-old) and five old (2-year-old) mice. Ten fields of view per eyeball were analyzed at 3000× magnification. No significant differences were observed in the numbers of SSCs or SCs on the epithelial side between young and aged mice. However, a significant reduction in SCs was observed on the endothelial-side in aged mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7a–d). In addition, electron-dense, lipofuscin-like round structures were sporadically observed in the cytoplasm of SCs and SSCs in aged mice, with more pronounced accumulation in SSCs. These degenerative features were not detected in young mice (Fig. 7e).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the relationships between SCs and NG2-positive telocytes, including cytoplasmic contact between SCs and SSCs, the transformation of SSCs into LESCs, and age-related changes in the morphology and abundance of SCs and SSCs.

Telocytes have recently been described in multiple organs in both animals and humans12. In ocular tissues, telocyte-like cells have been reported in ciliary body, sclera, and uvea of mice, and in the ciliary body stroma of humans10,11. In the human corneal stroma, both C-kit–positive and C-kit–negative telocyte populations have been identified, suggesting heterogeneity within this cell type. In the present study, stromal cells in the limbal stroma co-expressed CD34, PDGFRα, and NG2 and exhibited elongated cytoplasmic processes forming an interconnected network. These features are consistent with characteristics described for telocyte-like stromal populations in other tissues10,12–14,23. The observed NG2 immunoreactivity further aligns with reports of NG2-positive telocytes in the skin and gastrointestinal tract14–17. Given the known role of proteoglycans in regulating extracellular matrix organization and collagen spacing, NG2 expression in limbal stromal cells may contribute to stromal structural integrity.

A key finding of this study was that SSCs were found to be partially surrounded by SC processes while approaching the epithelial basement membrane (Bowman’s membrane) and invading the epithelium, as seen in serial sections. These cells were smaller than the surrounding basal cell layer epithelium, exhibiting swollen nuclei, sparse cytoplasm, and absent intercellular bridges, and morphologically resembling LESCs in the epithelial basal layer24. These findings suggest a morphological relationship between SSCs and SCs (telocytes).

Recent studies have described MSC-epithelial differentiation and showed that SSCs (including MSCs) and adipose-derived stem cells can differentiate into corneal epithelial cells both in vitro and in vivo, particularly after injury25–29. Corneal SCs also exhibit epithelial differentiation-inducing ability22,30. In this context, the findings of the present study suggest that SSCs may invade the epithelium and convert into LESC-like cells, with SC required for this process.

Aged corneas exhibit increased epithelial permeability due to disrupted epithelial barrier function, prolonged tear contact time, and increased infection risk31. Additionally, age-related declines in the phagocytic capacity of polymorphonuclear leukocytes and reduced ability to increase adhesion molecules contribute to reduced bacterial infection clearance32,33. Furthermore, with aging, the corneal epithelium undergoes morphological changes, such as a decrease in the density of keratinized cells in the corneal stroma, destruction and reduction of collagen fibers, and the appearance of collagen-free spaces. Moreover, an increase in lipofuscin granules in the stroma has been reported34. In the present study, we observed a decrease in the abundance of PDGFRα-positive SCs in the corneal limbal endothelial side with aging, along with numerous deposits of lipofuscin granules with high electron density in the cytoplasm of SSCs and SCs. The deposition of lipofuscin granules was more pronounced in SSCs than that in SCs. These findings represented morphological changes associated with aging35.

Generally, telocytes play a crucial role in promoting organ development and in maintaining local tissue homeostasis21. Consequently, the age-related degeneration and reduction of SCs (telocytes) may downregulate proteoglycan expression in the mesenchyme, potentially leading to impaired cell survival and regenerative changes in stem cells of aged mice. In animal models of corneal injury, cell therapy using SSCs has shown improvements in stromal thickness, normalization of collagen arrangement, restoration of stromal structure, improved corneal transparency, and inhibition of corneal scarring36–40. Additionally, micro RNAs (miR-29a and 381-5p) in stem cells have been reported to suppress inflammation and fibrosis in the stroma following corneal injury in mice and promote scar-free wound healing41,42. Furthermore, SSCs reportedly have immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and angiogenic effects, and clinical applications of SSCs in the lamina cribrosa are expected in the near future6,43. SSCs contribute to the regeneration of both the corneal epithelium and the stroma after injury. These tissues can be obtained through biopsy and used as clinical-grade cultures from human cadaveric corneas, rendering them a scalable and transplantable source and a promising therapeutic option6.

In the present study, the close association between SCs and SSCs, as well as between SSCs and corneal LESCs, was confirmed, suggesting the potential for SSCs to induce epithelial differentiation. Additionally, the findings reveal that the presence of SCs is necessary for maintaining their survival function.

Nevertheless, some limitations need to be acknowledged. This study primarily relied on morphological and immunohistochemical analyses, and the underlying molecular mechanisms were not investigated. Future studies should incorporate molecular profiling and lineage-tracing approaches to determine whether MSCs invade the epithelium and acquire LESC-like properties. Furthermore, in vitro studies are warranted to quantitatively assess the co-expression of epithelial and mesenchymal markers in these cells. Further research is also required to elucidate the differences between MSC-derived LESCs that invade the epithelium and native LESCs, along with elucidating the behavior of MSC-derived LESCs after intraepithelial invasion. A deeper mechanistic understanding of stromal–epithelial interactions within the limbal niche may contribute to improving the effectiveness of stem cell-based regenerative strategies.

Methods

Animal experiments and ethical approval

The experimental protocol used in this study was approved by the Ethical Committee on Animal Care and Use of RIKEN Centre for Biosystems Dynamics Research, and all experiments were performed in accordance with Principles of Laboratory Animal Care (NIH Publication No. 85 − 23, revised 1985). During the experiment, mice were freely fed food and water and maintained in a controlled environment (temperature 22–23 °C, relative humidity 50%–60%) with a 12 h light/dark cycle. Fifteen young (8-week-old, sexually mature) and 15 old (2-year-old, mouse lifespan) male C57BL/6 mice were used in this study. Mice were anesthetized deeply with isoflurane and perfused transcardially with saline and then with a 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and 0.1% picric acid (PA) solution (0.1 M PB) for immunohistochemical examination, a mixture of 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 4% PFA (0.1 M PB) for electron microscopy, and 0.1% glutaraldehyde and 2% PFA (0.1 M PB) for immunoelectron microscopy.

Tissue preparation and immunohistochemistry

After perfusion with the fixing solution, eyes were removed and post-fixed overnight at 4 °C with 2% PFA and 1.2% PA, then immersed in 30% sucrose solution and frozen using liquid nitrogen. Frozen thin eye Sect. (20 μm thick) were cut on a cryostat (Microm HM 560, Microedge Instrument, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and placed on a slide coated with 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane. The sections were incubated with polyclonal rabbit anti-NG2 IgG (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), rat anti-CD34 IgG (1:100; Novus Biological, Centennial, USA), goat anti-PDGFRα IgG (1:100; Santa Cruz, CA, USA), guinea pig anti-keratin 3 IgG (1:100; Progen, Heidelberg, Germany), rabbit anti-keratocan IgG (1:100; Novus Biological, Centennial, USA), and mouse anti-SCA-1 (1:200; Abcam). After washing with PB, the sections were incubated with Cy2, Cy3, and Cy5 (1:500; Jackson, PA, US), mounted with Hoechst dye 33,258 (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), and examined under a confocal laser microscope (Digital Eclipse CI, Tokyo, Japan).

Electron microscopy of SCs and SSCs and acquisition of 3D images via serial sectioning

For electron microscopy, perfused eyeballs were removed and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2% PFA and 0.1% PA solution. Eyeballs were cut in half along the visual axis, fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h post-fixation, dehydrated in alcohol, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were sliced with an ultramicrotome and mounted on silicon wafers, and sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Samples were viewed with a JSM-IT800 scanning electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Scanning electron microscopy images of the samples were acquired using a reflection electron detector (BED-C, voltage 7 kV, PC current 1.8 nA, work distance 4). By inverting the color tone of the acquired electron micrographs, high-quality images such as those obtained using transmission electron microscopy could be obtained.

To determine the number of SSCs and SCs in the limb, 10 images were taken at a magnification of 3000×; the number of each cell was measured on the images, and a histogram was produced. SSCs were present in the anterior cornea and were recognized by their morphological characteristics of an oval nucleus and processes extending in two directions. SCs were distinguished by morphological characteristics, such as a near-circular to oval nucleus and the presence of processes extending in 3–5 directions. In addition, to observe the adhesive state of stromal stem and SCs, approximately 1,000 ultrathin serial sections were manually cut to a thickness of 70 nm, and serial images were acquired. For wide-field electron microscopy, some of these sections were joined in Fiji to form a single image. Next, 3D images were obtained from serial array sections while adjusting the axis of each image using the ImageJ software Fiji. The reconstructions were verified by an independent observer.

Immunoelectron microscopy

The avidin–biotin complex method (ABC method) was used to observe CD90-positive and PDGFRα-positive SC populations. Perfused eyes were removed and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2% PFA and 0.1% PA solution. They were immersed in 30% sucrose in 0.1 M PB for 24 h and flash-frozen under liquid nitrogen. Sections were pre-treated with 10% normal goat serum for 30 min at room temperature (20–23 °C). These sections were incubated with rat anti-CD90 IgG (1:100; Abcam) and rabbit anti- PDGFRα IgG (1:100; Novus Biological), respectively, overnight at room temperature. After 30 min of washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with goat biotinylated anti-rabbit IgG (1:500; Vector Labs, Burlingame, CA, USA) or goat biotinylated anti-rat IgG (1:500; Vector Labs) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by avidin–biotin western horseradish peroxidase complex (Vector Labs) and incubated at room temperature for 60 min. After 30 min of washing with PBS, the sections were stained with Tris-HCl containing 3,3-diaminobenzidine (0.2 mg/mL) and 0.003% H2O2. After the diaminobenzidine reaction for 5 min, the sections were post-fixed in 1% OsO4 for 1 h, dehydrated in alcohol, and embedded in epoxy resin. Serial ultrathin sections were cut, placed on silicon wafers, electron-stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and electron micrographs were obtained using a JSM-IT800 scanning electron microscope (JEOL).

Statistical analysis

The numbers of SSCs and SCs were compared between young and aged mice. Both left and right eyeballs from young and aged mice (n = 5 mice per group) were analyzed. For each eye, 10 fields of view from both epithelial and endothelial sides of the corneal limbus were photographed at 3000× magnification. The number of SSCs and SCs was quantified from the acquired images using ImageJ software. Group comparisons (young vs. aged) were performed using the Student’s t-test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, with n = 5 per group. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05.
