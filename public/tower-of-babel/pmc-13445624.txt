A Multistate Analysis of Longitudinal Care Outcomes Among People Newly Linked to Human Immunodeficiency Virus Care in Missouri
Authors: Aditi Ramakrishnan, Daniel Vo, Ernie-Paul Barrette, Rachel M Presti, William G Powderly, Anne Trolard, Corey Grejtak-Heaps, Elvin H Geng, Aaloke Mody, Lindsey M Filiatreau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvancing the HIV response in Missouri, an Ending the HIV Epidemic priority state, requires stronger characterization of treatment gaps. Routine cross-sectional reporting may fail to capture important gaps in care that longitudinal methods can reveal.MethodsWe characterized clinic engagement and viral suppression among people with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) aged ≥18 years who were newly linked to clinic in St Louis, Missouri, between 1 January 2015 and 15 March 2020. Using multistate analytic methods that capture change over time, we estimated the prevalence of individuals in 9 mutually exclusive and exhaustive states over 3 years following linkage to clinic. Care states were based on engagement (disengagement ≥90 days late for a scheduled appointment) and viral load status (suppression defined as viral load <200 copies/mL ≤200 days).ResultsOverall 1110 patients were newly linked to clinic during the observation period, contributing 2255.4 person-years of follow-up. One year following linkage, 54.8% (95% CI, 51.6%–57.9%) were continuously retained, 15.2% (95% CI, 13.0%–17.7%) were reengaged, and 29.3% (95% CI, 26.6%–32.2%) were disengaged. In addition, 93.0% (95% CI, 90.6%–95.1%) of continuously retained individuals were virally suppressed at 1 year as compared with 81.6% (95% CI, 75.2%–87.7%) of reengaged individuals. Younger individuals and those from historically marginalized communities were more likely to disengage, reengage, and become viremic.ConclusionsMultistate methods revealed a pattern of disengagement from clinic and reengagement, accompanied by elevated risk of viremia during disengagement. Shifting retention efforts from preventing isolated missed visits toward supporting sustained, long-term engagement in care is a key priority.Keywords: EHE, ending the epidemic, HIV care cascade, longitudinal epidemiology, multistate models

Despite the Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) goal to have increased viral suppression among people with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV-1, PWH) to 95% by 2025, national retention and viral suppression estimates remain well below this target [1, 2]. Moreover, traditional cross-sectional analyses, which are commonly used to report these metrics, provide a limited snapshot of the true HIV care continuum journey, failing to comprehensively quantify when and how PWH transition into and out of care over time [3]. Thus, the dynamic state of the care continuum is poorly understood, creating a critical barrier to strengthening retention and reengagement services. This is particularly true of EHE priority states such as Missouri, which ranks the lowest in the nation in terms of public health spending per capita [4]. Nuanced data regarding the care continuum in these settings are urgently needed to inform a more robust but cost-effective HIV response to end the epidemic.

Multistate methods are a novel longitudinal analytic approach that are being increasingly used to reveal more detailed and dynamic care trajectories for PWH, thus providing stronger evidence to improve service delivery. This method facilitates descriptions of how individuals transition into and out of distinct clinical care states over time and identifies specific gaps in—and opportunities to improve—HIV care engagement along the care continuum [5, 6]. For example, multistate models can quantify the frequency, prevalence, and duration of retention, reengagement, and disengagement, including their relation to other care outcomes (eg, viral suppression) and across subpopulations (eg, age categories) [5, 7, 8]. Therefore, multistate techniques can guide resource allocation through identifying gaps in the care continuum and the subpopulations at risk during these time points. Despite the promise of multistate methods, these approaches have not been applied to explore care continuum outcomes in EHE priority states, perpetuating a significant challenge in mounting a robust data-driven response to ending the epidemic in these settings [7, 9].

Missouri, an EHE state with urban and rural epidemics, is home to an estimated 13 271 PWH, of whom 47% reside in St Louis [10, 11]. In 2022, 65.1% of PWH linked to care were virally suppressed, and 67.7% of Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program clients in the state were retained, estimates well below EHE goals [11, 12]. While these data, derived from cross-sectional analyses, provide a basic picture of the current state of HIV care in Missouri, they do not facilitate the necessary planning to develop effective interventions to improve outcomes. Such a response requires an understanding of the magnitude, frequency, and duration of disengagement and reengagement over time, as well as how these outcomes differ across distinct subpopulations. (1) To address these gaps in understanding care continuum outcomes in the state, (2) to strengthen local retention and reengagement services, and (3) to improve HIV care outcomes across the continuum, we conducted a multistate analysis to characterize transitions into and out of distinct HIV care states over time among PWH in St Louis, the region with the highest incidence of HIV in Missouri [10, 11]. Through this analysis, we depict more nuanced patient care journeys and highlight potential gaps that clinics, programs, and policies should target to improve service delivery and outcomes.

METHODS

Patient Population and Setting

We analyzed electronic health record (EHR) data from a cohort of PWH aged ≥18 years who were newly linked to care at a Ryan White–funded academic HIV primary care clinic (Washington University in St Louis Infectious Diseases Clinic, St Louis, Missouri) between 1 January 2015 and 15 March 2020. This latter date was selected as the end of the observation period, as clinic-level policies and processes subsequently shifted due to the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic and patient care outcomes have been shown to differ across these temporal periods [13–16]. This clinic is the largest provider of HIV primary care services in the St Louis region and offers integrated health care services, including case management and mental health support.

Data Source and Measurements

We extracted demographic, visit, and laboratory data from the EHR system. Demographic characteristics of interest included age at the time of linkage to the HIV clinic, gender, race and ethnicity, and primary mode of HIV acquisition. Individuals were considered newly linked to clinic based on the date of the first visit. We also undertook targeted chart review to distinguish if patients had transferred to the clinic or were newly diagnosed with HIV. If they did not have previous clinic visits as far back as 2008, they were classified as newly linked.

Analyses

Using EHR visit data and viral load–testing data, we specified 9 mutually exclusive and exhaustive clinical care states based on retention and viral load status. Clinical states were as follows: (1) linked to clinic, (2) retained at clinic and virally suppressed, (3) retained at clinic and viremic, (4) retained at clinic and unknown or late viral load, (5) disengaged from clinic, (6) reengaged at clinic and virally suppressed, (7) reengaged at clinic and viremic, (8) reengaged at clinic and unknown or late viral load, and (9) death (Figure 1).

State transitions framework for multistate analysis. Individuals could be in 1 of 9 mutually exclusive and exhaustive states at each time point. The states included the following: (1) linked to clinic, (2) retained at clinic and virally suppressed, (3) retained at clinic and viremic, (4) retained at clinic and unknown or late viral load, (5) disengaged from clinic, (6) reengaged at clinic and virally suppressed, (7) reengaged at clinic and viremic, (8) reengaged at clinic and unknown or late viral load, and (9) death.

Individuals were considered disengaged from clinic if they were >90 days late for a scheduled appointment; for example, if a patient was seen 1 January 2018 and scheduled for a follow-up clinic visit 1 April 2018, that person would be considered disengaged on 1 July 2018 (ie, >90 days late for a scheduled appointment). If one did not have a scheduled follow-up appointment, that person would be considered disengaged >90 days after a no-show appointment. Individuals were considered virally suppressed if they had a viral load <200 copies/mL ≤200 days from their most recent viral load test; viremic if they had a viral load >200 copies/mL ≤200 days from their most recent viral load test; and unknown or late viral load if they had a gap in viral load measurement >200 days.

We explored 5 composite outcomes based on the clinical states previously described:

In care at clinic: any state describing linkage to clinic (state 1), retention (states 2–4), and reengagement (states 6–8)

Retained at clinic: any state describing retention (states 2–4)

Reengaged at clinic: any state describing reengagement (states 6–8)

Viremia: any state describing an elevated viral load (states 3 and 7)

Viral suppression: any state describing viral suppression (states 2 and 6)

We used descriptive statistics to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort: count and proportion for categorical variables and median and IQR for continuous, nonnormally distributed variables. To characterize longitudinal care outcomes, we categorized participants into 1 of the 9 specified care states at every time point in the first 3 years following linkage to clinic. As states are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, patients can only be in a single care state but must be in at least 1 care state at any given point in time. We then used nonparametric multistate analytic techniques—employing the Aalen-Johansen estimator to account for competing events and transitions between states—to quantify the prevalence of patients in each care state across time and the estimated length of stay (ie, the restricted mean time spent) in each care state during the first 3 years following linkage to the clinic [17, 18]. For these analyses, time zero was defined as the first attended clinic visit date, and patients were censored at the time of death, end of 3 years of follow-up, or end of the observation period (15 March 2020). As data regarding whether patients presented with new diagnoses were not available, we excluded individuals with a viral load <1000 copies/mL at entry as they were hypothesized to have previously been in care.

We performed subsequent analyses to examine outcomes among (1) those who had disengaged from clinic and (2) those who experienced viremia at least once during the observation period. For these analyses, time zero was respectively defined as the first date of disengagement from clinic and viremia, and persons were censored at the time of death or the end of the observation period (15 March 2020).

We performed analyses in the overall population as well as across sociodemographic groups. In all multistate models, we used nonparametric bootstrapping with 1000 resamples of the data to obtain 95% CIs.

Finally, we used Cox proportional hazards models to examine associations between baseline sociodemographic characteristics and (1) time to the first episode of disengagement and (2) time to the first episode of reengagement. We did not develop a multivariable model as there was not an established causal relationship between the available sociodemographic characteristics and outcomes of interest. All variables were missing <10% data unless otherwise indicated. As such, subgroup analyses excluded observations with any relevant missingness, as the low proportion of missingness was considered unlikely to bias estimates of association [19].

Analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute), Stata/MP 16.1 (StataCorp), and the mstate package in R 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) [20, 21].

This study was approved by the Washington University in St Louis Institutional Review Board.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

During the observation period, 1110 patients were newly linked to the clinic, contributing 2255.4 person-years of follow-up (Table 1). The median time of observation was 849 days (IQR, 423–1095). The median age was 34 years (IQR, 25–47), 865 (77.9%) were cisgender men, 754 (67.9%) identified as Black, and 705 (63.5%) identified as men who have sex with men. Regarding age distribution, 241 (21.7%) were 18 to 24 years old at entry.

Longitudinal Care Cascade Outcomes Following Initial Linkage to Clinic

Following initial linkage to clinic, at 1 year 70.0% (95% CI, 67.1%–72.8%) of individuals were in care at clinic (linked to clinic, continuously retained, or reengaged; Figure 2, Supplementary Table 1). This consisted of 54.8% (95% CI, 51.6%–57.9%) who were continuously retained and 15.2% (95% CI, 13.0%–17.7%) who were reengaged. At 2 years, the proportion in care at clinic decreased to 59.5% (95% CI, 56.2%–63.0%), which included 33.0% (95% CI, 30.0%–36.0%) who were continuously retained and 26.5% (95% CI, 23.7%–29.9%) who were reengaged.

Longitudinal care outcomes among people with HIV newly linked to clinic at the clinic of interest in St Louis, Missouri, between 2015 and 2020 (n = 1110). This figure illustrates the proportion of patients estimated to be in each care state at any given time point during the observation period, representing the overall multistate analysis.

At 2 years from linkage to clinic, the prevalence of those currently disengaged from clinic was 39.7% (95% CI, 36.4%–43.0%), and the cumulative incidence of ever being disengaged from clinic was 66.2% (95% CI, 63.1%–69.3%; Supplementary Table 1).

Among the overall cohort at 1 year, 59.4% (95% CI, 56.3%–62.4%) were in care at clinic with documented viral suppression, 6.2% (95% CI, 4.8%–7.8%) were in care at clinic with documented viremia, 4.4% (95% CI, 3.2%–5.9%) were in care at clinic with unknown/late viral load measurements, and 29.3% (95% CI, 26.6%–32.2%) were disengaged from clinic. Differences in documented viral suppression between those continuously retained at clinic and reengaged at clinic were observed at 1 year: 93.0% (95% CI, 90.6%–95.1%) of those continuously retained were virally suppressed as compared with 81.6% (95% CI, 75.2%–87.7%) of those who were reengaged. With increasing follow-up, the prevalence of documented suppression decreased while disengagement from clinic increased, but the proportion suppressed among those with viral load measurements remained stable (Supplementary Table 1).

Average Length of Stay in Care States

In the 2 years from being linked to clinic, individuals were continuously retained and virally suppressed for approximately a year (estimated length of stay or restricted mean time, 355.4 days; 95% CI, 338.1–371.9) and reengaged and virally suppressed for approximately 2.5 months (restricted mean time, 73.8 days; 95% CI, 64.5–82.2). Regardless of viral suppression status, patients were continuously retained for approximately 15 months (restricted mean time, 449.7 days; 95% CI, 434.1–465.7) and reengaged for approximately 3 months (restricted mean time, 96.0 days; 95% CI, 85.4–105.9).

Longitudinal Care Cascade Outcomes Following Disengagement From Clinic

Among those who had ever disengaged from clinic, 54.5% (95% CI, 50.8%–58.5%) had reengaged at clinic at least once, but only 41.3% (95% CI, 37.1%–45.3%) remained reengaged at clinic 1 year after initial disengagement (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 2). Additionally, at 1 year, 45.5% (95% CI, 41.5%–49.2%) remained disengaged without ever reengaging at clinic, while an additional 13.1% (95% CI, 10.1%–16.4%) had reengaged but subsequently disengaged from clinic again.

Longitudinal Care Cascade Outcomes Following Viremia

Among those with documented viremia, 55.0% (95% CI, 47.4%–62.9%) overall were in care at clinic and virally suppressed 1 year after the first elevated viral load, while 16.6% (95% CI, 10.6%–22.8%) still had documented viremia (Figure 4, Supplementary Table 3). Additionally, 25.6% (95% CI, 18.9%–32.6%) were disengaged from clinic, another 31.2% (95% CI, 23.8%–38.2%) were reengaged, while only 43.1% (95% CI, 35.9%–51.2%) remained continuously retained at clinic. Among those who had a viral load rechecked, the proportion suppressed was 76.8% (95% CI, 68.9%–84.8%) overall but ranged from 81.5% (95% CI, 71.3%–90.4%) for those who remained continuously retained at clinic to 70.0% (95% CI, 54.9%–82.4%) for those who reengaged at clinic. Similar differences in viral suppression between populations continuously retained and reengaged at clinic were observed at 2 years.

Stratified Analyses

Patterns in viremia, disengagement, and reengagement varied by age and race. Younger persons and Black individuals (vs non-Hispanic White) were more likely to experience viremia within 2 years from linkage to clinic (Figure 5A). These populations were also more likely to experience disengagement from clinic at least once within 2 years of being linked to clinic (Figure 5B) and reengagement at clinic 1 year after the first episode of disengagement (Figure 5C). However, these trends were not observed in the composite in care at clinic group at 2 years following linkage to clinic (Figure 5D).

Predictors of Disengagement and Reengagement

Bivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models demonstrated that younger age and Black race were significantly associated with the first episode of disengagement following linkage to clinic. As compared with younger people (18–24 and 25–35 years), older ones (≥55 years) were more likely to disengage from clinic (crude hazard ratio [cHR], 1.41 [95% CI, 1.06–1.87]; cHR, 1.36 [95% CI, 1.03–1.79], respectively; Table 2). Black persons as compared with non-Hispanic White ones were more likely to experience disengagement (cHR, 1.34 [95% CI, 1.12–1.60]).

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to characterize nuanced metrics of engagement and reengagement in HIV care in Missouri to better inform the design and implementation of effective retention and reengagement services in EHE priority states. Our findings demonstrate that disengagement, reengagement, and viremia over time among PWH are far more common than previously reported in the state. While we observed that retention at clinic was initially strong at 79.9% (95% CI, 77.6%–82.3%) 6 months following linkage to clinic, subsequent disengagement from clinic was high, as 66.2% (95% CI, 63.1%–69.3%) had ever disengaged from clinic at 2 years following linkage. Furthermore, our results demonstrated that 2 years following the first episode of disengagement, 60.7% (95% CI, 56.5%–64.6%) of those who had disengaged at least once were reengaged at clinic, illustrating the prominent pattern of reengagement in the continuum. Contrary to how the HIV care continuum is often presented in a linear fashion, these results challenge this simpler depiction by demonstrating the cyclical nature of disengagement and reengagement in HIV care over time.

Other US-based cohort analyses, applying variable analytic methods and definitions of retention and reengagement, have identified repeat disengagement and reengagement rates ranging from 42.1% to 59% [7, 22, 23]. While traditional retention strategies have focused on clinic populations prior to the initial episode of disengagement, we found that repeat disengagement from clinic was high in the 3-year follow-up period; as such, these results highlight that clinics must also focus on supporting populations before and after reengagement. As viremia was higher among those who had reengaged at clinic as compared with those who were continuously retained at clinic, with unknown outcomes among those who had disengaged from clinic, it is also integral to develop public health and clinic-based strategies to support those who have reengaged and repeatedly disengaged to prevent transmission and morbidity.

Our findings illustrate that the traditional cross-sectional retention metrics developed by the Health Resources and Services Administration, which must be reported by Ryan White–funded programs, provide a limited understanding of the process of disengagement, reengagement, and viremia over time [24, 25]. For example, these required metrics indicate that 12-month retention in Missouri among PWH attending clinics receiving Ryan White funding has recently ranged from 67.7% to 76.8% [11, 12]. However, our analysis indicates that at 12 months from linkage to clinic, 54.8% (95% CI, 51.6%–57.9%) were continuously retained at clinic and 15.2% (95% CI, 13.0%–17.7%) were reengaged at clinic, highlighting that continuous retention may be lower than what is being commonly reported and that important nuances regarding care continuity are not captured by current required metrics. Because a substantial proportion of PWH experience this type of cyclical engagement, multistate techniques are needed to strengthen routine monitoring systems and, thereby, our understanding of critical time points in the care continuum to appropriately target improved retention and reengagement services to end the epidemic.

Consistent with national data and other studies, the majority of those who were continuously retained at clinic were virally suppressed at all time points in this analysis [26–29]. Our results add to the literature through revealing that the majority of those who reengaged at clinic were virally suppressed as well. This may reflect continued refills of antiretroviral therapy despite missed appointments and blood tests, as well as the effectiveness of integrase inhibitors, which are the first-line class of antiretroviral therapy recommended in treatment guidelines [30, 31]. However, viral suppression was significantly lower among those who were reengaged at clinic as compared with individuals continuously retained over time, signaling the need for strategies to support access and adherence to treatment among those who experience disengagement and reengagement.

Our findings demonstrated that younger and Black populations were more likely to experience disengagement, reengagement, and viremia as compared with other groups in this cohort. Other studies of disparities in retention among PWH in the United States during the past decade have highlighted similar patterns. Certain analyses have identified that younger age [7, 32, 33], Black race [34, 35], and minoritized race [29] are associated with poor retention, as well as younger age [35] and Black race [7] with reengagement in care. Younger, Black [34], and minoritized groups [29] have also been associated with viremia. While some studies have found that Hispanic ethnicity was associated with improved retention [32], reengagement, and viral suppression [35], we did not identify this pattern possibly due to the low representation in our sample. Few studies have begun to develop retention interventions focused on these populations [13, 36–39]. Given these results, it is crucial to integrate perspectives from younger and historically marginalized communities with designing and implementing effective retention and reengagement services.

Our results suggest specific recommendations to accelerate ending the epidemic, particularly in EHE priority states. Real-time data and comprehensive metrics, beyond what are required for Ryan White–funded program reporting, are essential to develop truly effective data-driven implementation strategies to strengthen the HIV continuum of care. EHE priority states, including Missouri, need additional resources to provide such robust data and program monitoring to comprehensively support PWH and efficiently counter the epidemic. Furthermore, trials that have improved retention and reengagement need to be adapted and brought to scale in clinical and public health settings. For example, the Cooperative Re-Engagement Controlled Trial demonstrated that a collaborative data-to-care strategy employed by US health departments and clinics resulted in significantly improved retention, reengagement, and time to viral suppression as compared with the standard-of-care arm [23]. In a time of public health and HIV funding upheaval, this study, among others, demonstrates that increased resources are needed to successfully shift research findings to clinical and public health implementation to end the epidemic.

This study is limited in that we did not include antiretroviral medication refill or adherence patterns, as these data were not available. However, as viral load measurements were central to this analysis, these can be viewed as a proxy for adherence, particularly in the era of integrase inhibitors. This analysis included only persons newly linked to clinic, some of whom may have been subject to misclassification bias. Yet, we reviewed the EHRs of patients for whom this status was unclear to mitigate this limitation. While we present data collected prior to Medicaid expansion in Missouri and long-acting injectable (LAI) ART implementation, our findings are timely and relevant given that the effect of Medicaid expansion on retention trends nationally are unclear [40], and proposed policies to decrease Ryan White and AIDS Drug Assistance Program funding may influence retention as well [41, 42]. LAI ART still requires in-person visits, and an understanding of retention in the landscape of oral ART is necessary to improve retention for those receiving LAI ART. A final limitation is that data regarding whether patients presented with new diagnoses were not available. We addressed this limitation through excluding those with a viral load <1000 copies/mL at entry, as they were hypothesized to have previously been in care. Given this limitation and lack of data regarding persons transferring care elsewhere, our analysis focused on patterns of retention at clinic instead of retention in care.

Disengagement, reengagement, and viremia over time are far more common than previously reported in Missouri. While engagement was initially strong, subsequent disengagement and reengagement were high, with viremia being proportionally higher among those who reengaged. Additionally, patterns of disengagement, reengagement, and viremia varied by age and race. Multistate analyses can strengthen our comprehensive understanding of such tenuous time points in the care continuum and thus guide data-driven implementation strategies to optimize retention and reengagement services to end the epidemic in EHE priority jurisdictions.
