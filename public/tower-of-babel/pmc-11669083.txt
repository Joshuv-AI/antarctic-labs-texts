n-Type Conjugated Polymers Based on Double B←N Bridged Bipyridine Unit
Authors: Bin Meng, Jun Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ConspectusBoth p-type conjugated polymers and n-type conjugated polymers are required for organic optoelectronic devices, such as organic solar cells (OSCs), organic field-effect transistors (OFETs), organic thermoelectrics (OTEs), etc. The development of n-type conjugated polymers lags far behind that of the p-type counterparts in view of material diversity and optoelectronic device performance. This is mainly due to the lack of strong electron-withdrawing building blocks, which are always based on the imide unit. Double boron–nitrogen coordination bond (B←N) bridged bipyridine (BNBP), which was first developed in 2016, is an alternative kind of electron-withdrawing building block based on a B←N unit. BNBP itself possesses a planar and fixed configuration, low-lying electronic energy levels, strong fluorescence, and facile functionalization. A family of BNBP-based conjugated polymers has been developed. They show excellent and tunable optoelectronic properties, such as high electron mobility, low-lying and tunable lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level (ELUMO), medium bandgap, narrow absorption spectra in the visible range, high fluorescence quantum efficiency, etc. With rational molecular design of BNBP-based conjugated polymers, they have been widely used in organic optoelectronic devices with high performance, including OSCs, OFETs, and OTEs, indoor photovoltaics (IPVs), organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), organic photodetectors (OPDs), etc. Therefore, BNBP-based conjugated polymers have become an important class of optoelectronic materials. In this Account, we summarize the research progress on BNBP-based conjugated polymers.At first, we discuss BNBP itself, including its molecular design, synthesis, chemistry, and optoelectronic properties. Then we introduce the optoelectronic properties of BNBP-based conjugated polymers, including their light absorption property, fluorescence, electron mobility, and frontier electronic energy levels. We have systematically elucidated the relationship between the chemical structures, optoelectronic properties, and optoelectronic device performance of BNBP-based n-type conjugated polymers. The unique property of BNBP-based conjugated polymers is the high electron mobility in the amorphous state. Other noteworthy properties of these polymers are the medium bandgap and absorption spectra in the visible range. Next, we discuss the applications of BNBP-based conjugated polymers in OSCs, IPVs, OFETs, OTEs, OPDs, and OLEDs. The excellent optoelectronic device performance is noteworthy, such as power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 10% in OSCs, PCE of 26% in IPVs, electron mobility of 0.3 cm2 V–1 s–1 in OFETs, power factor of 25 μW m–1 K–2 in OTEs, specific detectivity of 1.79 × 1013 cm Hz1/2 W–1 in OPDs. Finally, we propose that great attention should be paid to the deep understanding of the electronic structures of BNBP itself and BNBP-based conjugated polymers as well as the new applications of BNBP-based conjugated polymers.

Introduction

π-Conjugated polymers have received great attention because of their wide applications in organic optoelectronic devices with the great advantages of flexibility, solution processing, and low cost.5,6 Both p-type conjugated polymers and n-type conjugated polymers are required for organic solar cells (OSCs), organic field-effect transistors (OFETs), organic thermoelectrics (OTEs), etc. p-Type conjugated polymers are hole-transporting and should have a high lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level (ELUMO) and highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy level (EHOMO). n-Type conjugated polymers are electron-transporting and should have low-lying ELUMO and EHOMO.7,8 The development of n-type conjugated polymers lags far behind that of the p-type counterparts in view of material diversity and optoelectronic device performance. This is mainly due to the lack of strong electron-withdrawing building blocks, which are required for low-lying ELUMO and EHOMO of conjugated polymers.7,8

The majority of strong electron-withdrawing building blocks in n-type conjugated polymers are based on the imide unit, such as bithiophene imide (BTI) and its derivatives, naphthalene diimide (NDI), and perylene diimide (PDI).7,8 Recently, some strong electron-withdrawing building blocks based on the cyano moiety or amide unit have also been reported.7 In 2015, the authors discovered that the boron–nitrogen coordination bond (B←N) could greatly downshift ELUMO and EHOMO of conjugated polymers.9 This discovery indicates a new principle for the molecular design of n-type conjugated polymers. In 2016, the authors developed a brand-new electron-withdrawing building block based on B←N, i.e., double B←N bridged bipyridine (BNBP).1 BNBP could be used to design n-type conjugated polymers with excellent and tunable optoelectronic properties, such as high electron mobility,4 low-lying and tunable ELUMO,10 narrow absorption and fluorescence spectra,11 high fluorescence quantum efficiency,12 etc. Therefore, rationally designed BNBP-based conjugated polymers have been widely used in organic optoelectronic devices with high performance, including OSCs, OFETs, OTEs, indoor photovoltaics (IPVs), organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), organic photodetectors (OPDs), etc.13−19 BNBP-based conjugated polymers have become an important class of n-type conjugated polymers.7

In this Account, we summarize the research progress on BNBP-based conjugated polymers since the invention of BNBP in 2016. At first, we discuss the chemistry, electronic structure, and optoelectronic properties of BNBP itself. Then we talk about the molecular design and optoelectronic properties of the BNBP-based conjugated polymers. Finally, we introduce the applications of BNBP-based conjugated polymers in various kinds of organic optoelectronic devices.

The BNBP Building Block

Synthesis and Structural Features

The chemical structure of BNBP is shown in Figure 1a. According to the single-crystal structure of a model compound, the B–N covalent bond length in BNBP is 1.49 Å, and the B←N coordination bond length is 1.58 Å.1 BNBP exhibits a planar configuration and adopts slipped face-to-face stacking with a mean π-stacking distance of 3.57 Å (Figure 1b). This planar configuration and small π-stacking distance are favorable for charge transporting properties of BNBP-based conjugated polymers.4

BNBP is stable under ambient conditions and in acidic or basic solution.1 The excellent chemical stability of BNBP enables its applications in many chemical reactions, such as nucleophilic substitution, Stille coupling, Suzuki coupling, and direct C–H arylation.13,14 BNBP can be facilely functionalized at various positions. For example, the N atom at the 3-position of BNBP can be functionalized with alkyl chains, which are necessary for the solubility of BNBP-based conjugated polymers. The N atom at the 3-position can also be functionalized with aromatic moieties for optimized optoelectronic properties.20 The boron atoms of BNBP are always modified with fluorine atoms with a small steric hindrance effect. Indeed, the boron atoms can also be functionalized with bulky moieties, such as a diphenyl unit or borafluorene unit, for a non-coplanar configuration of BNBP-based conjugated polymers.21,22 The 5- and 5′-positions of BNBP can be functionalized with bromo or tributyltin groups to give functionalized BNBP monomers for polymerizations.3 The resulting BNBP-based polymers are highly stable under ambient conditions.13,14

Figure 2a shows the synthetic routes for the BNBP dibromo monomers and the ditributyltin monomers. Dimerization of commercially available 2,5-dibromo-3-nitropyridine affords 1, which is then reduced by tin(II) dichloride dihydrate to give 2. The nucleophilic reaction of 2 and alkyl iodide and subsequent treatment with BF3·ether readily afford the dibromo BNBP monomers. It is noteworthy that this synthetic route of the BNBP dibromo monomer is different from that reported in our original research paper.1 All four steps in this synthetic route can be performed in >65% yield without purification by column chromatography. Using this synthetic route, dibromo BNBP monomers can be facilely synthesized in large quantity, high yield, and low cost. The detailed synthetic procedures can be found in the Supporting Information. The ditributyltin BNBP monomers can be prepared by Stille coupling of the dibromo BNBP monomers and excess hexabutylditin.3

Optoelectronic Properties

Because of the two B←N units, BNBP exhibits low-lying ELUMO and EHOMO of −3.19 and −5.63 eV, respectively.1 This indicates that the n-type semiconducting property can be expected with BNBP-based conjugated polymers. The introduction of B←N units into BNBP results in a red shift of the absorption spectrum by ca. 80 nm (Figure 1c, black line).1 This red-shifted absorption spectrum is beneficial for sunlight harvesting capability of BNBP-based conjugated polymers. BNBP in solution at room temperature emits green light with peak emission wavelength of 513 nm and fluorescence quantum efficiency (ΦF) of near unity (Figure 1c, red line).23 Such an excellent fluorescence property is rarely reported for building blocks of conjugated polymers. At 77 K, BNBP in solution shows phosphorescence with the peak emission wavelength of 685 nm (Figure 1c, blue line).23

Polymerization of BNBP Monomers

A large variety of BNBP-based conjugated polymers has been synthesized. The homopolymers of BNBP can be synthesized by Yamamoto polymerization of the dibromo BNBP monomers (Figure 2b).22 The donor–acceptor (D–A)-type alternating copolymers are always synthesized by Stille polymerization of the dibromo BNBP monomers and the ditrimethyltin monomer of electron-donating building blocks (Figure 2c).1 The acceptor–acceptor (A–A)-type alternating copolymers are always prepared by Stille polymerization of the ditributyltin BNBP monomers and other dibromo monomers of electron-accepting building blocks (Figure 2d).3 The resulting D–A-type BNBP-based conjugated polymers always exhibit ELUMO of −3.5 to −3.8 eV, and the A–A-type BNBP-based conjugated polymers always show ELUMO of −4.0 to −4.4 eV.3,13 The chemical structures of the BNBP-based conjugated polymers discussed in this Account are shown in Figure 3.

Properties of BNBP-Based Conjugated Polymers

Light Absorption

The majority of BNBP-based conjugated polymers (e.g., P1, P2) exhibit medium bandgaps of 1.7–1.9 eV with maximum absorption wavelengths of 600–700 nm (Figure 4a).13,24 The absorption spectra of BNBP-based polymers overlap well with the emission spectra of artificial lighting sources, such as light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and fluorescent lamps. This makes BNBP-based conjugated polymers suitable for IPV applications.14 By copolymerization of BNBP with some low-bandgap building blocks, the onset absorption wavelength of BNBP-based conjugated polymers (e.g., P3) can be red-shifted to ca. 800 nm.25 It is noteworthy that BNBP-based conjugated polymers always exhibit narrow absorption spectra with full width at half-maximum (fwhm) of 50–100 nm.13,14 In particular, the absorption spectrum fwhm of only 16 nm can be obtained with the BNBP homopolymer, P1.24 These narrow absorption spectra indicate that BNBP-based conjugated polymers can be used to develop color-selective OPDs.

Fluorescence

Despite the strong fluorescence of BNBP itself, most BNBP-based conjugated polymers do not efficiently emit light in thin film because of the aggregation of polymer chains.13,14 Excellent light-emitting properties can be obtained by introducing steric hindrance into the BNBP-based conjugated polymers to prevent the aggregation of the polymer chains (Figure 4b). For example, the BNBP alternating copolymer incorporated with dichlorobenzene units in the backbone (P4) emits red light with ΦF = 0.64 in solution and 0.11 in thin film.12 The BNBP homopolymer with diphenyloxy moieties on the boron atoms (P5) in solution emits near-infrared (NIR) light with the maximum wavelength of 749 nm and ΦF = 0.68 in solution.11 It is noteworthy that while NIR-emissive conjugated polymers are always D–A-type copolymers, P5 is a homopolymer.

Energy Levels

The striking feature of BNBP-based conjugated polymers is the well-delocalized LUMO and the tunable ELUMO.10 The benchmark n-type conjugated polymers are based on the NDI unit. The LUMOs of these NDI-based conjugated polymers are localized on the NDI unit (Figure 5a).16 Therefore, their ELUMO values are determined by the NDI unit and are difficult to tune. In contrast, the LUMOs of BNBP-based conjugated polymers are well-delocalized on the polymer backbone (Figure 5a).16 Their ELUMO values can be effectively tuned by modifying the chemical structures of either the BNBP unit or the copolymerizing unit (Figure 5b).10 Indeed, the ELUMO of BNBP-based conjugated polymers can be tuned in the wide range of −3.3 to −4.4 eV.3,10

In OSC applications, the BNBP-based polymers with relatively high-lying ELUMO of ca. −3.3 eV can work as electron donors and the BNBP-based polymers with low-lying ELUMO of −3.4 to −3.8 eV can work as electron acceptors.13,14 With the optimized ELUMO of −3.5 eV, the resulting OSC devices of BNBP-based polymers can give remarkably high open-circuit voltage (VOC) of 1.3 V.26 Some A–A-type BNBP-based conjugated polymers exhibit ELUMO of lower than −4.0 eV, which enables their n-doping for n-type OTE applications.3,27

Electron Mobility

Because of the fused-ring structure, planar configuration, and central symmetry of BNBP, the BNBP-based conjugated polymers exhibit close π stacking in the solid state and show excellent electron-transporting properties. Their electron mobility (μe) estimated by the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) method (perpendicular to the substrate) is about 10–5 to 10–3 cm2 V–1 s–1, which is adequately high for OSC applications.13,28 For example, the μe of P6 estimated by the SCLC method is as high as 1.68 × 10–3 cm2 V–1 s–1.2 The μe estimated by the OFET method (parallel to the substrate) is about 10–1 cm2 V–1 s–1.29,30 It is worth noting that carefully designed BNBP-based conjugated polymers can exhibit high electron mobility in the amorphous state, which is unprecedented for n-type conjugated polymers.4,31 For example, OFET devices with an amorphous thin film of P7 as the active layer exhibit decent μe of 0.34 cm2 V–1 s–1.4 This unique electron-transporting property is probably attributed to the ultrarigid polymer backbone and small π-stacking distance of P7.

Crystallinity in Thin Film

The active layer morphology plays an important role in organic optoelectronic device performance. We have studied the relationship between the chemical structures and crystallinity in solid-state BNBP-based conjugated polymers.32 For polymers, the flexibility of polymer chains plays an important role in high crystallinity. BNBP-based conjugated polymers can change from amorphous to semicrystalline as the length of the copolymerizing oligothiophene unit is extended.32 The alternating copolymers of BNBP with thiophene or bithiophene show poor crystallinity in the solid state. In comparison, the alternating copolymers of BNBP with terthiophene, tetrathiophene, or pentathiophene exhibit semicrystallinity in thin film, which is supported by the strong diffraction bands in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern and the melting peaks in differential scanning calometry (DSC) measurements. Since BNBP itself is a rigid fused-ring building block, alternating copolymers of BNBP with other fused-ring building blocks have rigid conjugated polymer backbones and are always amorphous in thin film,4 whereas alternating copolymers of BNBP with some long nonfused building blocks, e.g., 4,7-dithienyl-2,1,3-benzothiadiazole, can exhibit high crystallinity in thin film.2

Optoelectronic Device Applications of BNBP-Based Conjugated Polymers

Organic Solar Cells

OSCs use blends of organic and polymer electron donors and electron acceptors as active layers to convert solar light energy to electricity. Figure 6 lists the chemical structures of organic and polymer electron donors discussed in this Account. Figure 7a and Figure 7b display the device structure and working principle of OSCs, respectively. To achieve high power conversion efficiency (PCE) of OSCs, electron acceptors of OSCs should have wide absorption spectra, low-lying and optimal ELUMO/EHOMO, high electron mobility, and excellent donor/acceptor phase separation morphology. BNBP-based conjugated polymers can fulfill all these requirements and have become an important class of electron acceptors for OSCs.13,14 In the first report of BNBP in 2016, the alternating copolymer of BNBP and thiophene (P8) was used as the electron acceptor to blend with a polymer donor, PTB7, to construct all-polymer solar cells (all-PSCs).1 P8 shows an ELUMO/EHOMO of −3.50 eV/–5.77 eV, an electron mobility of 6.9 × 10–5 cm2 V–1 s–1, and an absorption peak at 622 nm. The all-PSC device with PTB7:P8 as the active layer exhibits a short-circuit current density (JSC) of 7.09 mA cm–2, a VOC of 1.09 V, and a fill factor (FF) of 0.44, corresponding to a PCE of 3.38%. Soon after, we developed the alternating copolymer P9 based on BNBP and 2,2-difluorobithiophene (fBT).33 The fBT unit has a planar conformation, which can be “locked” by the F···S interaction. Moreover, the fBT unit can produce the pseudostraight polymer backbone configuration of P9. As a result, compared to P8, P9 shows slightly downshifted ELUMO of −3.62 eV and much enhanced electron mobility of 2.4 × 10–4 cm2 V–1 s–1. The all-PSCs with P9 as the electron acceptor and PTB7-Th as the electron donor exhibit a PCE of 6.2% with a photon energy loss as low as 0.51 eV. The best-performing BNBP-based polymer acceptor is P6, which is the alternating copolymer of BNBP and 4,7-dithienyl-2,1,3-benzothiadiazole.2 Its all-PSC device exhibited a VOC of 1.17 V, Jsc of 13.39 mA cm–2, and FF of 0.64, corresponding to a PCE of 10.07% (Figure 7c,d). Its excellent device performance is attributed to the optimized ELUMO/EHOMO, red-shifted absorption spectrum, and improved donor:acceptor blend morphology. This remarkably high PCE indicates that BNBP-based polymers are a class of high-efficiency polymer acceptors for OSCs. A noteworthy feature of all-PSCs based on BNBP-based polymer electron acceptors is the high VOC of up to 1.3 V, which is attributed to the optimized ELUMO.26

High-efficiency OSCs always use blends of polymer donors and small molecular acceptors as the active layer.34,35 Polymer acceptor/small molecular donor (PA/SD)-type OSCs always suffer from low PCE (PCE < 3%) because of large-size donor:acceptor phase separation in the active layer. The emergence of BNBP-based polymer acceptors provides an opportunity to develop high-efficiency PA/SD-type OSCs.36 In 2017, we attempted to fabricate PA/SD-type OSCs using P10 as the polymer acceptor and (p-DTS(FBTTh2)2) as the small molecular donor.37 The OSC device exhibits a VOC of 1.08 V and a PCE of 3.50%. The phase separation morphology of PA/SD blends is improved by suppressing the overcrystallization of the small molecular donor in the phase separation, which is realized by increasing the molecular weight of the BNBP-based polymer acceptor,38 improved aggregation of the polymer acceptor in solution,39 introducing steric hindrance to the core of the small molecular donor,40 ternary blends,41 etc. As a result, a small-size phase separation morphology is obtained, and high efficiency PA/SD-type OSCs are demonstrated. The OSCs based on the blends of P11 and BD3T exhibit a JSC of 13.82 mA cm–2, VOC of 1.04 V, and FF of 0.66, corresponding to a PCE of 9.51% (Figure 7e,f).39 This PCE is the highest value for PA/SD-type OSCs reported so far. A striking property of this OSC device is its superior thermal stability. After thermal annealing of the active layer at 180 °C for 7 days, the PA/SD-type OSC based on P12 and DR3TBDTC maintains 89% of the initial PCE, while the typical PD/SA-type OSCs can only maintain 55% of the initial PCE.28 Based on the excellent thermal stability, we are able to construct OSCs operating at high temperature, i.e., 150 °C, which cannot be realized with inorganic solar cells and organic/inorganic hybrid solar cells. After thermal treatment of the OSC device based on the BD3T:P11 blend at 150 °C for 72 h, the device can retain 84% of its initial PCE value, while the typical PD/SA-type OSCs can only maintain 62% of the initial PCE (Figure 8a,b).39 The excellent thermal stability is attributed to the morphological stability of the PA/SD-type active layers. As shown in Figure 8c–f, the polarized optical microscopy (POM) images of BD3T:P11 remain almost unchanged after the thermal treatment. In contrast, the POM images of the typical PD/SA blend (Figure 8g–j) show the emergence of clusters during the thermal treatment, which is due to the aggregation of the small molecular donors and indicates inadequate morphological stability. These results indicate a new advantage of the high temperature tolerance of OSCs.

Indoor Photovoltaics

IPVs have recently received increasing attention because of their promising applications as power sources for devices of the Internet of Things ecosystem.42 In comparison to OSCs, IPVs work under indoor artificial light illumination with narrow emission spectra and a low light intensity. Since BNBP-based polymers exhibit light absorption in the visible range and tunable ELUMO, they are suitable to be used in IPVs with high VOC. For example, P13 has a medium bandgap of 1.95 eV and an optimal ELUMO of −3.42 eV (Figure 9a). In 2019, we selected P13 as the polymer acceptor and chose CD1 as the polymer donor to fabricate the first all-polymer IPV device.43 The all-polymer IPV cell shows the photoresponse wavelength range of from 390 to 630 nm, and its external quantum efficiency (EQE) matches well with the emission spectrum of a fluorescent lamp (FL). Under 1000 lx FL illumination, the all-polymer IPV cell shows a remarkably high VOC of 1.16 V and a high PCE of 26.2% (Figure 9b,c). This PCE is fairly comparable to those of other types of IPVs, such as microcrystalline silicon (mc-Si), amorphous silicon (a-Si), perovskite, and gallium arsenide (GaAs) (Figure 9d).43 We have also used random copolymers based on BNBP (P14) to fabricate an all-polymer IPV modulus. The IPV modulus has an active area of 10 cm2 and exhibits a PCE of 12.04%.44

Organic Thermoelectrics

OTEs are a clean energy technology capable of directly converting thermal energy to electrical energy via the Seebeck effect.15,45 A–A-type conjugated polymers based on alternating BNBP and other electron-accepting units show very low-lying ELUMO and can be readily n-doped for high electrical conductivity (σ) and consequently high power factor (PF).3,27,46 For example, the A–A-type alternating copolymer of BNBP and thieno[3,4-c]pyrrole-4,6-dione (TPD) dimer (P2) exhibits an ELUMO of −4.02 eV and can be n-doped by tetrakis(dimethylamino)ethylene (TDAE) vapor (Figure 10a).3 The n-doped P2 shows a σ of 7.8 S cm–1 and a Seebeck coefficient (S) of 178.8 μV K–1, corresponding to a PF of 24.8 μW m–1 K–2 (Figure 10b). This PF value is fairly comparable to those of other high-performance n-type OTE materials.47,48 We also systematically investigated the effect of the number of TPD units on the thermoelectric performance of the BNBP-based conjugated polymers.27 We found that the TPD dimer leads to a pseudolinear configuration of the conjugated polymer backbone, which can significantly enhance polymer crystallinity and improve thermoelectric performance.

Organic Field-Effect Transistors

The aforementioned excellent electron-transport property of BNBP-based conjugated polymers indicates that they can be used as active layers of n-type OFETs (Figure 11a). In 2017, we attempted the application of BNBP-based conjugated polymers in OFETs. The OFETs based on P15 exhibit electron-dominated ambipolar charge-transporting characteristics with an electron mobility of 0.32 cm2 V–1 s–1 and hole mobility of 0.015 cm2 V–1 s–1.30 The OFET device of P16 shows unipolar n-type behavior with an electron mobility of 0.19 cm2 V–1 s–1.49 The ELUMO/EHOMO of these polymers plays an important role in the ambipolar/unipolar device behaviors, and the crystallinity of these polymers contributes to the high electron mobility.

Generally, n-type polymer semiconductors with a high electron mobility are semicrystalline. However, the copolymer of BNBP and benzobisthiazole (BBTz) (P7) can give high electron mobility of 0.34 cm2 V–1 s–1 in a near-amorphous state,4 which is unprecedented for n-type conjugated polymers. Both BNBP and BBTz are centrosymmetric, planar, electron-accepting, and fused-ring building blocks, so P7 has ultrahigh backbone stiffness and consequently is near-amorphous in the thin film. The small π–π stacking distance of 3.47 Å should play an important role in the high electron mobility of P7 (Figure 11). Besides, the π–π stacking distances of the alternating copolymer of BNBP and BBTz can be further minimized to 3.41 Å by moving out the branching point of the alkyl side chains.31

Organic Photodetectors

The narrow absorption spectra and excellent electron transport properties indicate that BNBP-based conjugated polymers can be used in organic phototransistors (OPTs) with color selectivity (Figure 12a).50 P17 exhibits low-lying ELUMO/EHOMO of −4.02 eV/–6.07 eV and decent electron mobility of 0.018 cm2 V–1 s–1 in OFETs. Its absorption spectrum has a maximum absorption wavelength of 608 nm and fwhm of only 36 nm (Figure 12b). The OPTs with P17 as the active layer exhibit excellent red-light photodetection performance with a responsivity (R) of 1743 A W–1 and a specific detectivity of 1.3 × 1015 Jones.50 Moreover, due to the narrow absorption spectrum of P17, the photosensitivity of the OPT devices for red light is 2 orders of magnitude higher than those for green and blue light (Figure 12c,d).

In addition, BNBP-based conjugated polymers can be used as electron acceptors to construct photovoltaic-type OPDs. We have reported OPDs using CD1:P18 as the active layer and dilute hydrochloric acid-modified indium tin oxide (ITO) as the anode.51 The device exhibits photoresponse in the visible range with a high specific detectivity of 1.79 × 1013 cm Hz1/2 W–1 at −1 V bias and a maximum R of 0.30 A W–1.

Organic Light-Emitting Diodes

BNBP-based conjugated polymers with steric hindrance and high fluorescence quantum efficiency can be used as an active layer to fabricate OLEDs. P4, which shows red fluorescence with a ΦF of 11% in thin film, can be used not only as an acceptor in OSCs but also as an active layer in OLEDs.12 Its solution-processed OLED devices emit pure red light with a luminance efficiency of 0.99 cd A–1, a turn-on voltage of 5.4 V, a maximum brightness of 721 cd m–2, and CIE coordinates of (0.67, 0.32).

Generally, conjugated homopolymers based on six-membered rings, e.g., polyfluorene, always exhibit blue emission and high ΦF, whereas conjugated homopolymers based on five-membered rings, e.g., polythiophene, always exhibit red emission and low ΦF. Despite the six-membered-ring structure of BNBP, the homopolymer of BNBP bearing large steric hindrance on the boron atoms emits deep-red light or near-infrared light with high ΦF of ca. 0.6 in solution.11,22 OLEDs with P19 as the active layer emit pure red light with a maximum current efficiency of 1.00 cd A–1 and a maximum luminance of 169 cd m–2.22 Near infrared (NIR)-emissive conjugated polymers are always D–A-type copolymers. The homopolymer P5 emits high-quality NIR light with a peak wavelength of 749 nm, fwhm of 48 nm, and ΦF in solution of 0.68. The film with 0.5 wt % P5 dispersed in poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) can convert the red light from red OLED devices into invisible NIR light.11

Summary and Outlook

This Account summarizes the research progress on BNBP-based conjugated polymers. BNBP itself possesses a planar and fixed configuration, low-lying ELUMO/EHOMO, strong fluorescence, and facile functionalization. Conjugated polymers based on BNBP exhibit excellent optoelectronic properties, including low and tunable ELUMO, high electron mobility, narrow absorption and fluorescence spectra, and high fluorescence quantum efficiency. Rationally designed BNBP-based conjugated polymers can be used as key materials for various organic optoelectronic devices with excellent device performance. Therefore, BNBP-based conjugated polymers have emerged as an important class of optoelectronic materials.

BNBP is a B←N-based electron-accepting building block, which is different from typical electron-accepting building blocks based on amide, imide, or cyano.7 Excellent or unique optoelectronic properties of the BNBP-based conjugated polymers should originate from the electronic structure of BNBP itself, which is far from well understood.23 Therefore, great attention should be paid to the electronic structures of BNBP itself and the BNBP-based conjugated polymers. The deep understanding of electronic structures is expected to lead to new optoelectronic properties and excellent optoelectronic device performance of BNBP-based conjugated polymers.

Particular attention should be paid to the high electron mobility in the amorphous state of BNBP-based conjugated polymers.4 This phenomenon possibly indicates disorder-free electron transport in conjugated polymers, which is unprecedented. Moreover, the amorphous property can be used in developing large-area OFET circuits and arrays with excellent uniformity, which is important for practical applications of OFETs. Another noteworthy property of BNBP-based conjugated polymers is their medium optical bandgap and light absorption spectra in the visible range. Their absorption spectra overlap well with the emission spectra of indoor light sources, making BNBP-based conjugated polymers suitable for IPV applications.43

New applications of BNBP-based conjugated polymers must be explored. Recently, Long et al. used the combination of BNBP-based conjugated polymers and reduced graphene oxide (rGO) as metal-free organic electrocatalysts for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), which is important for fuel cells and metal–air batteries. Excellent electrocatalytic activity has been demonstrated.52 This work opens up new applications of BNBP-based conjugated polymers in electrocatalysis.53−55
