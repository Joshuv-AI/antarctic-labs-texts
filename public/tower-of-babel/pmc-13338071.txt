Quantum-resistant hybrid encryption framework for secure and intelligent Vehicle-to-Vehicle communication using deep representation learning models
Authors: Tawfiq Hasanin, Zahyah H Alharbi, Majdy M Eltahir, Radwa Marzouk, Khalid F Alsirhani, Safa Alsafari, Turke Althobaiti, Sultan Almutairi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVehicular communications are applied in different security and business applications in the digital world for the user’s advantage. During this, vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication allows clients to exchange necessary information with neighbouring vehicles directly. Generally, vehicles move quickly on the highway instead of the crossing road environment, and therefore, a strong method is needed to communicate securely and efficiently. Vehicular Ad Hoc Networks (VANETs) have several distinct features, comprising fast movable nodes, scattered networks, often changing topologies and self-organisation. A malicious attack changes the packet information in the fabrication attack, causing jamming and higher delays in the vehicular network. In this study, a Quantum-Resistant Hybrid Encryption for Secure Vehicle-to-Vehicle Communication Using Deep Representation Learning (QRHEV2V-DRL) method is proposed. The primary aim of the QRHEV2V-DRL method is to demonstrate significant potential in the development of V2V communication networks to ensure long-term data security in the face of evolving cyber threats. In this V2V communication stage, the QRHEV2V-DRL method performs cluster formation using the Kernel Fuzzy C-Means (KFCM) model to partition the communication network into distinct clusters based on similarities in in-vehicle communication patterns. In this attack detection stage, the QRHEV2V-DRL approach utilises min–max normalisation to scale the features within a particular range. Afterwards, the stacked sparse autoencoder (SSAE) model is used for attack classification. In the encryption stage, the quantum-resistant hybrid encryption (QRHE) model is employed to transmit the data in the CH securely to the cloud. The comparison analysis of the QRHEV2V-DRL approach portrayed a superior accuracy value of 99.62% over existing models under the ToN-IoT dataset.Keywords: Vehicle-to-Vehicle, Quantum-resistant hybrid encryption, Kernel Fuzzy C-Means, VANET, Clustering, Deep learningSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

As the Internet of Vehicles (IoV) has become more dynamic and intelligent, vehicles can communicate with more dynamic applications, which face security threats. IoV is presently attracting significant attention from scholars1. Securing data in IoV communication is vital currently. Cybercriminals persistently attempt to steal information and gain IoV communication. To solve this, various tools effectively provide security as well as safety. Security denotes authenticity, integrity, and privacy2. If an unauthorised individual, by some means, accesses information, they will not scan it jointly. Data security plays a significant part in today’s rapidly evolving world. IoV is an enormously connected system that integrates crucial information, like pathways, traffic conditions, position, and vehicle details3. Encryption techniques are prevalently employed methods to attain privacy, and there are diverse approaches for their execution. This encryption technique is classified into two types: symmetric and asymmetric key encryption. The initial encryption provides low computational overhead and latency in VANET, making it appropriate for time-critical safety messages. The asymmetric key encryption is contrary to the initial, thus affecting real-time performance. The encryption employs the same key in symmetric encryption, whereas asymmetric encryption utilises different keys4. Currently, VANET has increased in popularity and appeal by presenting user services, such as traffic mobility efficiency, safety, navigation, and entertainment. This detail inspired public transport agencies and several car manufacturers to get involved in VANET applications5. Even though VANETs are currently growing, there are still unsolved research concerns.

The primary issues are integrity, security, and privacy. Those issues are complex to solve due to the familiar evolving nature and infrastructure independence properties of VANETs6. VANETs assist dual communication methods: V2V as well as vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I). Within V2V, communicating vehicle data exchange regarding their location, speed, traffic jams, etc., with each other to prevent accidents, develop the driving environment, and reduce traffic congestion7. V2I produces a communication network among infrastructure and vehicles to enhance comfort, safety, and efficacy. But regardless of the assistance they present, VANETs are still experiencing difficulties regarding privacy and authentication. In VANETs, vehicles communicate with each other via a wireless medium. An invader can use that when initiating attacks8. Diverse security measures are presented, including anonymity, key management systems, cryptographic algorithms, trust management models, traceability techniques and more. Currently, numerous investigators have shown that incorporating artificial intelligence (AI) in intrusion detection systems (IDS) boosts their efficiency in identifying threats. IDS is a broadly utilised methodology that examines the traffic for security breach signs and generates an alarm for detected security anomalies9. Furthermore, deep learning (DL) can understand anomaly-based detection systems that can detect zero-day and unknown attacks, learning and training on their own through examining system behaviour and strengthening their recognition precision eventually. Using DL models for ID in VANET is especially important because of their large volume of transmitted data and the variety of possible attacks10.

Challenges and motivation

Secure key management is attained through dispersed trust models in decentralised and infrastructure-less VANETs. It is challenging to deploy PKI in highly dynamic vehicular networks, and privacy-preserving authentication is significant in VANETs, as legitimacy must be verified, unlike conventional authentication, which assumes stable identities and persistent connections. IoV operates in a highly dynamic and open environment, unlike traditional networks, and the involvement of heterogeneous entities and continuous wireless broadcasting of location-sensitive data make authenticity, integrity, and user privacy significantly more challenging. These limitations motivate the requirement for specialised security and privacy-preserving mechanisms for IoV. Furthermore, it becomes difficult to maintain stable routes and persistent trust relationships for secure data dissemination due to the highly dynamic topology of IoV/VANETs caused by rapid vehicle mobility and frequent network disconnections. Conventional symmetric encryption techniques are insufficient in large-scale IoV networks because they depend on pre-shared keys and centralised key management, which do not scale well and introduce high overhead under frequent node join/leave events. Moreover, a BC network using the proof of authority and association (PoA2) Roadside Unit (RSU), consensus mechanism is proposed, for safe classification of vehicle Electronic Control Unit (ECU) data. Li et al.12 presented FEXGBIDS, a privacy-preserving IDS depending on federated XGBoost. Incorporating Federated Learning (FL) model alongside cryptographic optimiser methods, FEXGBIDS tackles information storage tower as well as security problems in vehicular networks. The limitations of the existing studies include high computational overhead in BC and FL-based IDS models, which may also affect real-time V2V operations. Stage I: V2V communication in VANET

Cluster formation using the Kernel FCM

The QRHEV2V-DRL method performs cluster formation using the KFCM model to partition the communication network into distinct clusters based on similarities in in-vehicle communication patterns42. This model is chosen for its capability in managing nonlinear, dynamic, and uncertain V2V environments. The cluster separability is improved by the kernel function by mapping data into a higher-dimensional space. Its fuzzy membership allows vehicles to belong to various clusters, highlighting overlapping communication regions. KFCM also averts rigid clustering and enhances accuracy compared to k-means. Unlike DBSCAN, it is less sensitive to density discrepancies, making KFCM more reliable for vehicular networks. This model assists in improving communication efficiency by creating stable clusters with low overhead and enhances attack detection accuracy by effectively handling nonlinear and uncertain vehicular data. This also improves network scalability and stability in both highway and intersection scenarios.

Stage II: attack detection framework in VANET

Feature scaling via min–max technique

In this attack detection stage, the QRHEV2V-DRL method utilises min–max normalisation for scaling the features inside a particular range44. This method is chosen for scaling features to a fixed range (usually 0–1), ensuring uniform contribution of all attributes during model training. The original data dispersion and relative relationships are conserved, which is considered crucial for learning-based models. It is also computationally simpler and avoids negative values compared to z-score normalisation. Unlike robust scaling, it does not distort bounded vehicular data. This makes min–max normalisation efficient and well-suited for real-time V2V communication systems.

The maximal‐minimum normalisation technique presented in Eq. (22) is applied to standardise the feature values in the dataset to make it simpler to compare the results. The value is scaled into the numerical range [0, 1] utilising the min‐max normalisation approach.

SSAE-driven classification module

Afterwards, the SSAE technique is employed for the attack classification process45. This model exhibits robust ability in automatic feature extraction from high-dimensional vehicular network data. The attack detection accuracy is improved, and the sparsity constraint assists in learning discriminative and compact representations. SSAE performs well with restricted labelled data, commonly seen in real-world V2V scenarios. Furthermore, SSAE needs fewer computational resources and does not rely on a spatial data structure compared to CNN. Unlike RNNs, it avoids sequential dependency issues, making SSAE more efficient and stable for real-time attack detection. The model assists in attaining higher classification accuracy while maintaining low false alarm rates. This method improves robustness by learning invariant and high-level features that adapt to varying traffic conditions and growing attack patterns, thereby maintaining reliable detection performance. Stage III: secure data encryption using QRHE

In the encryption stage, the QRHE is employed to transmit the CH data securely to the cloud46. This model is chosen for its ability to securely transmit cluster head (CH) data to the cloud, ensuring protection against both classical and quantum attacks. This also attains robust security with mitigated computational overhead by incorporating post-quantum cryptography with symmetric encryption. Unlike conventional RSA or ECC, QRHE remains secure in the presence of quantum adversaries. The hybrid approach enhances efficiency and scalability, compared to standalone post-quantum methods. This makes QRHE highly appropriate for latency-sensitive V2V cloud communication. This ensures data confidentiality and integrity during V2V and cluster-head-to-cloud communication by integrating robust post-quantum cryptography with effectual symmetric encryption to resist both classical and quantum attacks. The QRHE mechanism incorporates quantum computing concepts and cryptographic principles to accomplish strong security. By combining asymmetric and symmetric encryption, QRHE obtains the authenticity, confidentiality and integrity of data, whereas the quantum‐resistant model deals with developing quantum computing threats. The QR symmetrical encryption in QRHE utilises the theory of superposition in quantum technology, allowing composite encryption keys as opposed to quantum decryption. The QR key exchange model uses difficulty, while dual entangled particles keep connected conditions through distances, permitting secure and interception-proof key exchanges. In this model, selected Learning with Error (LWE) and its version, Ring‐LWE, are QR models owing to some major causes. Initially, Ring‐LWE and LWE depend on lattice‐based cryptographic issues that are extensively measured to be resilient to either quantum or classical attacks. This is due to solving lattice issues, like the LWE’s issues, which are highly demanding regardless of quantum computers, and present stronger security against upcoming quantum threats. They permit QRHE to attain stronger security assurances without imposing extreme computational cost, thus allowing effective and scalable processes. This incorporation of practical efficiency and theoretical security brings LWE and Ring‐LWE the desired selection for this presented model.

Conclusion

In this study, a QRHEV2V-DRL methodology is presented. The primary aim of the QRHEV2V-DRL methodology is to demonstrate significant potential in the development of V2V communication networks to ensure long-term data security in the face of evolving cyber threats. In this V2V communication stage, the QRHEV2V-DRL method performs cluster formation using the KFCM model to partition the communication network into distinct clusters based on similarities in in-vehicle communication patterns. In this attack detection stage, the QRHEV2V-DRL method undergoes min–max normalisation for scaling the feature in a specific range. Afterwards, the SSAE technique is employed for the attack classification process. In the encryption stage, the QRHE is used to transmit CH data securely to the cloud. The comparison analysis of the QRHEV2V-DRL approach portrayed a superior accuracy value of 99.62% over existing models under the ToN-IoT dataset. The model introduces minimal overhead, maintaining real-time V2V communication efficiently. The model also highlights high consistency across diverse attack types and varying network densities, maintaining robust accuracy, low latency, and robust privacy protection under all tested conditions. The model is highly scalable and adaptable, efficiently handling large-scale VANET deployments and rapidly changing topologies without compromising performance or reliability. The limitations include restricted scalability in extremely dense vehicular networks and reliance on ideal communication conditions, which may not always hold in urban environments. Latency issues may also be encountered during high traffic loads, affecting real-time performance. Furthermore, integration with heterogeneous IoT devices is not fully explored, restricting broader applicability. Future work may concentrate on adaptive network protocols, edge-computing integration for mitigating latency, and energy-efficient security mechanisms. Expanding the dataset with more diverse and large-scale V2V scenarios can further improve model generalisation and robustness.
