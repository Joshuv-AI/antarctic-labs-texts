Morphological and functional characterization of respiratory muscles in male runners across different race distances: an ultrasound and physiological assessment
Authors: María García-Arrabé, Rebeca Del Prado-Álvarez, Fabien Guérineau, Lucas Le Roux, Marta de la Plaza, Patricia Rico-Mena, Ángel González de la Flor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRunning performance depends not only on aerobic capacity but also on the functional integration of the respiratory system and core musculature. However, morphological and functional adaptations of the diaphragm and abdominal muscles across different running disciplines remain insufficiently characterized. The aim of this study is to analyze diaphragm and abdominal muscle morphology using ultrasound, as well as respiratory muscle strength and pulmonary function, in amateur runners classified according to their primary competition distance.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted including recreational and competitive male runners (18–60 years) with at least two years of training experience. Participants were divided into 3 groups: 5–10 km, half-marathon, and marathon. Diaphragm thickness at end-inspiration (Tdi,ei) and end-expiration (Tdi,ee), and diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF) were assessed by ultrasound. Abdominal muscle thickness (RA, EO, IO, and TrA) was also measured. Respiratory muscle strength was assessed using maximal inspiratory (MIP) and expiratory pressures (MEP), and pulmonary function by spirometry. Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA.ResultsRunners specializing in marathon showed significantly greater diaphragm thickness (Tdi,ei and Tdi,ee) and higher MIP compared with runners specializing in 5–10 km (p < 0.05). Additionally, IO thickness was significantly greater in runners specializing in half-marathon. No significant differences were observed in FEF25–75%, FEV1 or FVC between groups. These findings indicate distance-dependent adaptations in both inspiratory and expiratory musculature.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that running specialization is associated with distance-dependent morphological and functional adaptations of the respiratory system. Marathon runners show greater diaphragm thickness and inspiratory muscle strength, while half-marathon runners exhibit increased IO thickness. No differences in standard spirometric parameters were observed between groups, suggesting that these adaptations are primarily reflected in respiratory muscle structure and strength rather than conventional pulmonary function measures in amateur runners.

From a practical perspective, these findings have several implications:

Endurance training appears to induce measurable structural adaptations in respiratory muscles, which may contribute to improved performance.Respiratory muscle assessment via ultrasound could be a useful, non-invasive tool for monitoring training adaptations.Targeted inspiratory and expiratory muscle training may be particularly beneficial for middle-distance runners aiming to enhance performance toward long-distance.

Introduction

Running is one of the most widely practiced sports worldwide, both at recreational and competitive levels. Performance across its different disciplines depends not only on aerobic capacity but also on biomechanical efficiency and the functional integrity of the structures responsible for trunk stability [1]. In this context, the respiratory muscles and the abdominal wall play critical and synergistic roles [2].

The concept of the “core” can be functionally defined as an integrated system in which the abdominal musculature forms the anterior wall and the diaphragm acts as the superior boundary [3]. This unit is essential for postural stability, balance, and the efficient transfer of forces between the trunk and the extremities during locomotion [4, 5]. The diaphragm, as the primary inspiratory muscle, contributes to the generation of the negative intrathoracic pressure required for ventilation and, additionally, plays a role in trunk stabilization through its interaction with the abdominal and paravertebral muscles [6].

The abdominal wall—comprising the rectus abdominis (RA), external oblique (EO), internal oblique (IO), and transversus abdominis (TrA)—acts in a coordinated manner in trunk stabilization, forced expiration, and modulation of intra-abdominal pressure [7]. During running, particularly due to its repetitive and high-impact nature, cyclic increases in intra-abdominal pressure and high activation of the core musculature occur, which may induce specific functional adaptations or contribute to mechanical overload in these structures [8, 9]. In this regard, core strength has been shown to influence the ability to generate and transfer force to the extremities [10, 11].

In recent years, musculoskeletal ultrasound has emerged as a non-invasive, valid, and reliable tool for assessing the morphology and dynamic behavior of the diaphragm [12] and abdominal muscles [13]. This technique enables the quantification of variables such as muscle thickness and diaphragmatic thickening fraction, which have demonstrated a strong correlation with muscle cross-sectional area measured by magnetic resonance imaging in individuals with different training levels [14, 15].

In addition, the assessment of respiratory muscle strength through maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) and maximal expiratory pressure (MEP), together with pulmonary function testing using spirometry, provides a comprehensive evaluation of the functional status of the respiratory system [16].

Although respiratory efficiency has been shown to optimize oxygen transport and acid–base balance, and respiratory muscle training has been proposed as an effective strategy to induce physiological adaptations and improve performance [17], the available evidence in runners from different disciplines remains limited. Most studies have focused on ventilatory and metabolic variables [18, 19], whereas the combined characterization of diaphragm and abdominal muscle morphology and function using ultrasound has been scarcely investigated in this population.

This gap in knowledge is particularly relevant given that physiological demands vary according to race distance. Athletes specializing in 5–10 km races, half-marathon, and marathon runners differ substantially in training volume, relative intensity, and exercise duration, factors that may lead to specific adaptations in the respiratory system.

Understanding the potential morphological and functional adaptations of the respiratory system according to specialization in different race distances may provide valuable insights for optimizing training strategies, preventing respiratory muscle fatigue, and enhancing athletic performance. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to analyze the ultrasound characteristics of the diaphragm and abdominal musculature, as well as respiratory muscle strength and pulmonary function, in recreational and competitive male runners classified according to their usual race distance.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional observational descriptive study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Universidad Europea (approval code: 2025 − 700).

The study was carried out at the Physiotherapy Laboratory of Universidad Europea and adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects. All participants received detailed information regarding the study objectives and procedures and provided written informed consent prior to inclusion. Data confidentiality and anonymization were strictly ensured.

Participants

The sample consisted of recreational and competitive male runners aged 18–60 years, with at least two years of regular training experience. Participants were recruited through open calls via posters and flyers displayed at the European University of Madrid, as well as through social media posts (Instagram). Interested runners voluntarily contacted the research team after viewing these advertisements.

Eligibility criteria included regular running training, defined as a minimum of three sessions per week for at least two years, provision of written informed consent, and absence of diagnosed cardiorespiratory or neuromuscular diseases. Exclusion criteria included a history of chronic respiratory disease, neurological disorders, previous thoracic surgery, current musculoskeletal injury at the time of assessment, use of doping substances or medications affecting respiratory function, and inability to adequately perform the functional tests.

Participants were classified into three groups according to their usual competition distance: 5–10 km runners, half-marathon runners (21 km), and marathon runners.

To minimise potential confounding effects related to biological sex on respiratory muscle function, and in line with previous evidence reporting sex-related differences in respiratory physiology, the present study included only participants of a single sex [20]. This approach was adopted to ensure a more homogeneous sample and to specifically isolate the effect of running distance on inspiratory and expiratory muscle characteristics and respiratory function [21].

Sample size was estimated using a priori power analysis with G*Power software. Assuming a significance level of α = 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and a moderate effect size (f = 0.25) for detecting differences among the three groups using one-way ANOVA, a minimum sample size of 45 participants was required.

Variables and assessment procedures.

In this study, both sociodemographic and sport-related variables were collected. Specifically, age (years), height (cm), and body mass (kg) were recorded as basic anthropometric variables. In addition, sports experience (years) was assessed, along with training load indicators, including the number of training days per week (days/week) and weekly training volume expressed as mileage (km/week).

Respiratory muscle function assessment

Respiratory muscle strength was assessed by measuring maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) and maximal expiratory pressure (MEP) using a calibrated handheld mouth pressure device (Micro RPM®, MicroMedical-Carefusion, Rochester, UK) following standardized recommendations [22].

For MIP measurement, participants performed a maximal expiration to residual volume followed by a maximal inspiratory effort against an occluded airway sustained for at least one second, and the highest negative pressure was recorded. For MEP measurement, participants performed a maximal inspiration to total lung capacity followed by a maximal expiratory effort against an occluded airway sustained for at least one second, and the highest positive pressure was recorded [23].

Each measurement was repeated at least three times with a minimum rest interval of one minute between attempts, and the highest reproducible value was retained for analysis.

Pulmonary function assessment

Pulmonary function was assessed using forced spirometry with a calibrated spirometer (EasyOne Connect®, ndd Medical Technologies, Switzerland) in accordance with the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society guidelines [24].

Participants performed a maximal inspiration to total lung capacity followed by a forced and sustained expiration to residual volume. The following variables were recorded: forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1), and the FEV1/FVC ratio. Variables such as peak expiratory Flow (PEF) and Forced Expiratory Flow between 25% and 75% (FEF 25%–75%) were also measured.

At least three acceptable maneuvers were performed, and the two best values were considered valid if reproducible. The highest values were used for analysis [25].

Ultrasound assessment of the diaphragm and abdominal wall muscles

A state-of-the-art ultrasound system (LOGIQ S7 Expert, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA), equipped with a linear transducer operating at 4–15 MHz (broad-spectrum linear matrix array probe ML6-15 H40452LY, with a 50 mm field of view), was used to acquire ultrasound images for the evaluation of the diaphragm and abdominal wall muscles.

Diaphragm ultrasound assessment was performed with participants in the supine position using a high-frequency linear transducer (5–12 MHz). The probe was placed at the zone of apposition of the diaphragm, between the eighth and ninth intercostal spaces along the anterior axillary line, oriented perpendicular to the thoracic wall to optimize muscle visualization [26, 27]. Diaphragm thickness was measured at end-expiration and end-inspiration (Fig. 1). Measurements were obtained over three consecutive respiratory cycles, and the mean value of the three measurements was used for analysis [27].

Ultrasound assessment of the abdominal wall was also performed in the supine position using a high-frequency linear transducer. For the lateral abdominal muscles, the probe was placed transversely along the lateral abdominal wall at the level of the umbilicus along the mid-axillary line, adjusting its position to clearly identify the three muscle layers: EO, IO and TrA [13] (Fig. 1).

The RA muscle was assessed with the probe placed transversely, approximately 2–3 cm lateral to the linea alba [28]. In every case, muscle thickness was measured at rest during quiet breathing on three separate occasions, and the average of these three measurements was used for analysis. Measurements were performed at the end of expiration (end-expiratory phase) [29] (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 29 for Windows. Participants were classified into three discipline-based groups: 5–10 K, half-marathon, and marathon. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for raw values. Between-group differences in spirometric and ultrasound variables were first explored using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. To account for potential confounding, the main adjusted analyses were performed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with discipline as the fixed factor and age and weekly mileage as covariates. Adjusted values are reported as estimated marginal means with 95% confidence intervals. When the adjusted group effect was significant, pairwise comparisons between groups were performed using estimated marginal means with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Effect size was reported as partial eta squared (partial η²) for ANCOVA, with values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively [30]. In addition, diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF) was calculated as (diaphragm thickness at inspiration − diaphragm thickness at expiration) / diaphragm thickness at expiration × 100. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated in the total sample to explore associations between anthropometric, spirometric, and ultrasound variables. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as trivial (< 0.10), small (0.10–0.29), moderate (0.30–0.49), strong (0.50–0.69), and very strong (≥ 0.70) [31]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 53 runners were included in the baseline analysis after excluding the two sprint athletes. Participants were classified into three groups according to their usual competition distance: 5–10 K (n = 15), half-marathon (n = 22), and marathon (n = 16). In the overall sample, mean age was 35.25 ± 10.82 years, mean height was 175.26 ± 5.82 cm, mean body mass was 71.08 ± 7.49 kg, and mean sports experience was 8.74 ± 8.83 years. Participants trained a mean of 3.89 ± 1.01 days/week and covered 49.54 ± 25.57 km/week (Table 1).

Baseline characteristics of the sample

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise indicated. p values correspond to one-way ANOVA for continuous variables

Regarding group-specific characteristics, 5–10 K runners were younger (30.53 ± 13.77 years) and had lower weekly mileage (41.87 ± 18.43 km/week) than marathon runners. Half-marathon runners showed intermediate age values (34.68 ± 8.60 years) and similar weekly mileage to the 5–10 K group (41.02 ± 9.28 km/week). Marathon runners were older (40.44 ± 8.60 years) and accumulated the highest weekly mileage (68.44 ± 35.81 km/week). One-way ANOVA revealed significant between-group differences for age (p = 0.034) and weekly mileage (p = 0.001). No significant differences were observed for height, body mass, sports experience, or training days per week (all p > 0.05). In all three groups, the dominant leg was predominantly the right leg (Table 1).

Respiratory muscle and pulmonary function

After adjustment for age and weekly mileage, a significant between-group effect was observed for MIP (F = 4.696, p = 0.014, partial η² = 0.167). Marathon runners showed the highest adjusted MIP values, followed by half-marathon and 5–10 K runners. Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons indicated that marathon runners had significantly higher MIP than 5–10 K runners (adjusted mean difference = 34.18 cmH₂O, 95% CI 6.33 to 62.03, p = 0.011). No other pairwise differences were observed for MIP.

No significant adjusted between-group differences were found for MEP, FVC, FEV₁, PEF, FEF25–75%, FEV₁/FVC or DTF. (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Ultrasound measurement

After adjustment for age and weekly mileage, significant between-group effects were observed for IO thickness, diaphragm thickness at expiration, and diaphragm thickness at inspiration.

For IO thickness, ANCOVA showed a significant group effect (F = 3.716, p = 0.032, partial η² = 0.137). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons showed that half-marathon runners had greater adjusted IO thickness than 5–10 K runners (adjusted mean difference = 0.178 cm, 95% CI 0.005 to 0.352, p = 0.042). No other pairwise differences were significant.

For diaphragm thickness at expiration, ANCOVA showed a significant adjusted group effect (F = 4.020, p = 0.024, partial η² = 0.146). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons indicated that marathon runners had greater diaphragm thickness at expiration than 5–10 K runners (adjusted mean difference = 0.057 cm, 95% CI 0.004 to 0.110, p = 0.030). The comparison between half-marathon and marathon runners approached significance but did not reach the adjusted threshold (adjusted mean difference = 0.046 cm, 95% CI -0.001 to 0.093, p = 0.060).

For diaphragm thickness at inspiration, ANCOVA also showed a significant group effect (F = 3.465, p = 0.039, partial η² = 0.128). Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons showed that marathon runners had greater diaphragm thickness at inspiration than 5–10 K runners (adjusted mean difference = 0.085 cm, 95% CI 0.003 to 0.166, p = 0.039). No other pairwise comparisons were significant.

No significant adjusted differences were observed for EO, TrA, or RA. (Table 3; Fig. 2). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons for the significant ANCOVA outcomes are presented in Table 4.

Correlations in the total sample

In the total sample, Pearson correlations were calculated between spirometric and ultrasound variables. Only one significant association was found: FEF 25%-75% was positively correlated with diaphragm thickness at expiration (r = 0.313, p = 0.022), indicating a small-to-moderate relationship. No other spirometry-ultrasound correlations reached statistical significance.

Discussion

The sample of runners in this study was generally homogeneous in terms of basic anthropometric parameters and training frequency, providing a valid basis for comparing physiological adaptations across different distance specializations. Notably, younger athletes were more prevalent in the 5–10 km group, a pattern previously observed in running populations, where shorter-distance events tend to attract comparatively younger participants.

Differences in accumulated weekly mileage across distance specializations are consistent with the progressively greater endurance demands of longer events. Marathon training typically involves higher training volumes to sustain prolonged energy expenditure and support physiological adaptation, whereas 5–10 km training may prioritize relative speed and running economy over high mileage.

Taken together, these sociodemographic patterns highlight how age and training load are interrelated with running specialization. This contextualization is crucial when interpreting subsequent physiological findings, as age-related and load-dependent adaptations may influence respiratory muscle structure, pulmonary function, and other performance-related traits relevant to endurance performance [32].

The present cross-sectional study aimed to compare diaphragm morphology and abdominal wall muscle thickness, respiratory muscle strength, pulmonary function, across runners specializing in different competition distances. The main findings indicate that long-distance runners (marathon group) exhibit significantly greater diaphragm thickness (both at end-inspiration and end-expiration), higher maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), and increased diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF surrogate via Tdi changes) compared with runners covering 5–10 km. Additionally, IO thickness was significantly greater in half-marathon runners compared with 5–10 km runners, suggesting distance-dependent adaptations in expiratory musculature. No statistically significant differences were found for FEF25–75% or other spirometric variables across groups.

Diaphragm adaptations to endurance load: morphology and function

A key finding of this study is the progressive increase in diaphragm thickness across distance specializations, particularly at inspiration, together with higher inspiratory muscle strength in long-distance runners. This combined pattern supports a structure–function relationship consistent with chronic inspiratory muscle loading in endurance athletes [33]. However, the interpretation of diaphragm thickness differences should be made with caution, as the marathon group was older and diaphragm thickness has been reported to increase with age independently of training in some studies [34] although this finding is not consistently supported across the literature [35].

Marathon runners are exposed to sustained ventilatory demands over prolonged periods which may be associated with greater force-generating capacity and fatigue resistance of the diaphragm [36].

In this context, the greater maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) observed in long-distance runners reinforces the functional relevance of the morphological findings. Increased inspiratory strength may reflect neuromuscular adaptations, including improved motor unit recruitment and coordination, which together with structural changes could enhance ventilatory efficiency during prolonged exercise [37].

These findings are in line with previous evidence showing that endurance training is associated with differences in inspiratory muscle characteristics, including greater strength and resistance to fatigue [17]. The gradient observed across distance groups further suggests a potential dose–response relationship, whereby cumulative ventilatory load contributes to the magnitude of both structural and functional adaptations [38]. However, this interpretation should be approached with caution, as direct mechanisms underlying diaphragm thickening were not specifically assessed in the present study.

Abdominal wall adaptations

The results of the present study highlight the potential functional relevance of expiratory muscles, particularly the IO. Half-marathon runners showed significantly greater IO thickness, while no differences were observed in the EO, TrA and RA. These findings should be interpreted within an integrated locomotor–respiratory framework, in which respiratory muscle function, trunk stabilization, and lower limb mechanics interact to optimize force transmission [39] and movement efficiency during endurance running [40].

The IO contributes to active expiration through its role in increasing intra-abdominal pressure and assisting rib cage depression during forced or exercise-induced expiration [41]. In prolonged endurance exercise, where ventilatory demand remains elevated for sustained periods, repeated recruitment of the IO may contribute to respiratory efficiency and trunk stabilization. Therefore, the greater IO thickness observed in half-marathon runners may reflect a chronic adaptation associated with the ventilatory and postural demands of endurance running [42].

In contrast, no between-group differences were found in the TrA or EO. This finding may suggest a more specific involvement of the IO in the combined respiratory and lumbopelvic control requirements associated with endurance running. Previous biomechanical studies have proposed that the IO contributes not only to expiratory function but also to rotational trunk control and force transmission during gait, particularly under repetitive cyclic loading conditions [43]. However, these mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

From a broader integrative perspective, these findings may also reflect discipline-specific adaptations in musculotendinous and neuromuscular systems driven by the mechanical and ventilatory demands of different sports modalities. Endurance running requires continuous trunk–limb coupling and repetitive force transmission across the lumbopelvic region during cyclic locomotion, which may preferentially increase the functional involvement of the IO. In support of this interpretation, previous studies have demonstrated that different sports induce distinct adaptations in both respiratory and peripheral musculotendinous structures according to their specific movement patterns and loading characteristics [44].

Our findings are nevertheless consistent with previous ultrasound studies in runners reporting greater IO thickness and adaptations of the lateral abdominal wall associated with training status [45]. Additional evidence from athletes exposed to high rotational and trunk stabilization demands has also shown preferential IO development compared with other abdominal muscles [46], supporting the possibility that this muscle may be particularly responsive to repeated endurance-related loading.

Taken together, the greater IO thickness observed in half-marathon runners suggests that abdominal musculature plays a relevant role in trunk stabilization and expiratory support during endurance running. Specific assessment and training of the IO and associated expiratory muscles could complement traditional programmes for strengthening the inspiratory muscles, potentially helping to optimise ventilatory mechanics and trunk stability in endurance athletes.

Pulmonary function

Interestingly, traditional spirometric indices (FEV₁ and FVC) did not show marked differences between groups, which is consistent with previous literature indicating that spirometry is relatively insensitive to training status in healthy individuals. However, although FEF25–75% showed numerical differences between the groups—particularly with higher adjusted values among marathon runners compared to half-marathon runners—the overall ANCOVA was not statistically significant after adjusting for age and weekly mileage (F = 2.389, p = 0.103, partial η² = 0.092).

These findings suggest that baseline lung volumes remain unchanged across groups, while functional respiratory parameters may show subtle differences potentially related to neuromuscular and mechanical adaptations. Although marathon runners showed numerically higher FEF25–75% compared with 5–10 km runners, this difference did not reach statistical significance after ANCOVA adjustment and should not be interpreted as a group-level effect. FEV1 and FVC did not differ between groups.

FEF 25–75% reflects expiratory flow during the mid-phase of forced expiration and may be influenced by both airway-related structural factors and expiratory muscle performance. Therefore, although the higher values observed in marathon runners may suggest improved expiratory flow dynamics, the present study does not allow a direct determination of whether these differences are primarily attributable to structural airway adaptations, neuromuscular contributions of expiratory musculature, or a combination of both. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Alternatively, these higher FEF 25–75% values in marathon runners may also be partly explained by intrinsic anatomical variability of the airways, such as dysanapsis (i.e., a disproportion between airway calibre and lung size), which can lead to higher mid-expiratory flows independently of training status or global lung function [47].

Further research is needed to better distinguish these mechanisms and clarify their implications for respiratory efficiency and endurance performance, particularly in populations with altered expiratory flow such as individuals with asthma [48, 49].

Clinical and performance implications

From an applied physiology and endurance performance perspective, the present findings suggest that respiratory musculature may be relevant within the endurance training continuum, rather than representing a purely passive or supportive system. The greater diaphragm thickness and inspiratory strength observed in runners specializing in marathon are consistent with greater MIP, the only respiratory outcome to reach statistical significance between groups (Table 4). This finding may reflect a greater force-generating capacity of the inspiratory musculature, which could be associated with a more efficient breathing strategy and a potentially lower relative work of breathing at submaximal intensities — a key determinant of endurance performance. Notably, no significant between-group differences were observed for spirometric volumes or flows (FVC, FEV1, FEF25–75%), indicating that the functional advantage appears to reside primarily in inspiratory muscle strength rather than in static lung mechanics.

Such respiratory characteristics may be associated with a reduced reliance on accessory respiratory muscle recruitment and a lower ventilatory demand for a given workload, which could theoretically influence the distribution of blood flow during prolonged exercise [50–52]. However, these interpretations should be considered cautiously given the cross-sectional nature of the data and the absence of direct intervention evidence. Notably, the significant positive correlation observed between FEF25–75% and diaphragm thickness at expiration (r = 0.313, p = 0.022) in the total sample provides some empirical support for a structural–functional link between diaphragm morphology and mid-expiratory flow dy-namics, warranting further investigation in future studies.

The use of ultrasound imaging provides a sensitive and non-invasive approach to quantify structural adaptations in both inspiratory and expiratory muscles, offering potential utility for monitoring training-induced changes that are not detectable through conventional spirometry.

Finally, while the present results may support the consideration of respiratory muscle conditioning strategies within endurance training programs, particularly in athletes transitioning to longer distances, such recommendations remain hypothesis-generating and should be confirmed through future training intervention studies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; therefore, it cannot be determined whether the observed differences are attributable to training-related adaptations or to pre-existing individual characteristics. Second, participants were classified according to competition distance; however, no additional stratification was performed based on physical fitness or performance level, which may have introduced heterogeneity within groups and potentially influenced the observed differences. Third, training load was self-reported, which may introduce recall bias.

In addition, the study included only male participants, which limits the generalisability of the findings to female populations. Given the well-documented influence of biological sex on respiratory muscle structure and function, future studies should include both sexes to determine whether the observed associations across running distance groups are consistent in women.

Importantly, another relevant limitation is the absence of dynamic assessments of respiratory muscle function. The present study relied on static measurements, which do not fully capture the functional behaviour of the respiratory muscles during exercise conditions. This restricts the interpretation of the functional relevance of the structural and static functional findings, particularly in athletic populations where respiratory muscles operate under dynamic loading conditions. Current evidence supports the inclusion of dynamic evaluation approaches to better characterise respiratory muscle function in athletes [53] and future studies should incorporate these methodologies to improve ecological validity and translational relevance.

Finally, objective performance indicators such as personal best times were not available, which limits the detailed characterization of athletes’ performance level.

Future directions

Future longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm the causal relationship between training volume and respiratory muscle adaptations. A future research should prioritize randomized clinical trials to determine whether targeted respiratory muscle training (inspiratory and expiratory) and interventions aimed at improving thoracopulmonary compliance can enhance both respiratory function and running performance.

These studies should incorporate multimodal outcomes, including ultrasound-derived muscle morphology, respiratory pressures (MIP/MEP), ventilatory efficiency, and performance metrics (e.g., running economy, time-to-exhaustion).

Additionally, stratifying participants by training level and incorporating objective performance indicators, such as personal best race times alongside competition distance, may help to better identify which subgroups benefit most, thereby facilitating more individualized respiratory training strategies in endurance athletes.

Conclusion

These findings indicate that running specialization is associated with distance-dependent morphological and functional adaptations of the respiratory system. Marathon runners show greater diaphragm thickness and inspiratory muscle strength, while half-marathon runners exhibit increased IO thickness. No differences were observed in standard spirometric parameters between groups, suggesting that these changes are primarily reflected in respiratory muscle structure and strength rather than conventional pulmonary function measures in amateur runners.
