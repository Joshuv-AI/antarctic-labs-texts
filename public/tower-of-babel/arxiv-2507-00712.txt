Optimising finite-time quantum information engines using Pareto bounds Preprint: APS/123-QED
Rasmus Hagman, Jonas Berx, Janine Splettstoesser, Henning Kirchberg

Abstract
Information engines harness measurement and feedback to convert energy into useful work. In this study, we investigate the fundamental trade-offs between ergotropic output power, thermodynamic efficiency and information-to-work conversion efficiency in such engines, explicitly accounting for the finite time required for measurement. As a model engine, we consider a two-level quantum system from which work is extracted via a temporarily coupled quantum harmonic oscillator that serves as the measurement device. This quantum device is subsequently read out by a classical apparatus. We compute trade-offs for the performance of the information engine using Pareto optimisation, which has recently been successfully used to optimise performance in engineering and biological physics. Our results offer design principles for future experimental implementations of information engines, such as in nano-mechanical systems and circuit QED platforms.



I
Introduction

Traditional heat engines convert thermal energy into mechanical work by operating between two heat reservoirs at different temperatures. In recent years, heat engines have been analysed in quantum and nanoscale systems, both theoretically and experimentally
Benenti et al. 2017
;
Binder et al. 2018
;
Kirchberg and Nitzan 2022
;
Kirchberg and Nitzan 2023
;
Campbell et al. 2025
. Information engines produce work not only by using heat as a resource, but also by exploiting information and feedback
Maruyama et al. 2009
;
Sagawa and Ueda 2010
;
Mandal et al. 2013
. In contrast to standard heat engines, they can even extract energy from a single heat reservoir by processing information. It is the fundamental link between information and thermodynamics, exemplified by Maxwell’s demon, that makes information engines possible
Szilard 1929
;
Bennett 1982
;
de Oliveira Junior et al. 2025
. The possibility to exploit information to convert energy to work has been studied theoretically
Bresque et al. 2021
;
Fadler et al. 2023
;
Sánchez et al. 2019
and it has been successfully demonstrated in experiments in the classical and quantum regime
Raizen 2009
;
Toyabe et al. 2010
;
Koski et al. 2014
;
Pruchyathamkorn et al. 2024
. To analyse the performance of such an information engine, information has to be quantified as a resource, which is crucial to determine the efficiency of the engine
Paneru et al. 2018
;
Monsel et al. 2025
. Even fluctuation relations including information
Sagawa and Ueda 2010
;
Sagawa and Ueda 2012
;
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
and the precision of information engines
Paneru et al. 2020
;
Monsel et al. 2025
have been analysed.

A fundamental question arises regarding the duration of a measurement and, consequently, how quickly information can be acquired to perform feedback on the system to extract work
Bußhardt and Freyberger 2010
;
Bresque et al. 2021
;
Kirchberg and Nitzan 2025
.
This timescale may be particularly important in quantum information engines. Due to their small size, the
operation
time can be very short, making the measurement duration comparable to the engine’s cycle time.

Consequently, the duration of the measurement becomes a key factor in the overall performance of the engine. In particular, the energy cost arising from the system-meter coupling but also the potential extracted work through measurement depend on the measurement time, which thus influences,
inter alia
, the efficiency of converting information into work and the operational power (i.e., the work extracted per measurement time).

This raises an important question when operating an information engine: What are the constraints on measurement time and how does it impact performance?

In this paper, we address this challenge and treat the measurement time as a crucial parameter, as it intrinsically links information acquisition, energy cost and extractable work. We thereby extend a previous study
Kirchberg and Nitzan 2025
and complement earlier work on finite-time cyclic information engines that consider bounds on their performance metrics with respect to the dissipation time into the heat reservoir
Fadler et al. 2023
.
Specifically, we present a comprehensive thermodynamic analysis of a quantum information engine, such as
illustrated in Fig.
1
, where we examine the time-dependent coupling between the quantum system and the quantum meter, focusing on their joint evolution. By placing the Heisenberg cut between the quantum meter and the classical apparatus, the study preserves quantum coherent evolution during measurement and enables analysis of measurement time and its associated energy costs. This is in line with von Neumann’s framework
Von Neumann 1955
, according to which a quantum measurement begins with a ”premeasurement,” which involves an interaction between the target system and a secondary system known as the “quantum meter.” The quantum meter is then collapsed by a classical measurement apparatus, rendering the measurement outcome objective
Korbicz et al. 2017
.

For this analysis, we here introduce the experimentally relevant system-meter configuration shown in Fig.
1
: a two-level system measured by a quantum harmonic oscillator. This setup is directly relevant to potential experimental platforms for quantum information engines such as
nitrogen-vacancy centres, where spin degrees of freedom can be coupled and decoupled to a nano-mechanical oscillator by switching an external magnetic field on (coupling) and off (decoupling)
Arcizet et al. 2011
. Another example arises in quantum computing, where the state of a qubit is read out by coupling it to a microwave resonator
Blais et al. 2004
;
Cottet and Huard 2018
.

A key feature of our study is the identification of distinct thermodynamic regimes of operation. Depending on the measurement duration, the engine can function either as a heat engine—producing net work—or as a heat valve—transferring heat between reservoirs through work input.

To determine optimal engine configurations, a set of selected performance metrics must be optimised simultaneously, i.e., achieving both high power and high efficiency in converting information and energy into work via measurement.
This is a challenging task, as all performance metrics are interdependent through the underlying engine parameters; achieving maximum power and maximum efficiency simultaneously is not possible. This interdependence gives rise to fundamental trade-offs—known as Pareto fronts—between the metrics
Emmerich and Deutz 2018
.
Pareto optimality is well-established in economics
Fudenberg and Tirole 1991
and engineering
Tomoiagă et al. 2013
, and has recently been applied in fields such as stochastic thermodynamics
Berx and Proesmans 2024a
;
Berx and Proesmans 2024b
;
Forão et al. 2025
and biological physics
Sheftel et al. 2013
;
Berx et al. 2025
. However, to our knowledge, it has not yet been employed in the optimisation of quantum information engines.
In this paper, we apply multiobjective optimisation to explore these Pareto trade-offs in the context of information engines for the first time. We anticipate that this approach will provide practical guidance for jointly tuning experimental parameters to realise information engines operating in desired regimes.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we introduce the general model for the information engine and describe its operational cycle. In Sec.
III
, we illustrate the implementation of the engine using a two-level system coupled to a harmonic oscillator, where time-dependent information acquired through measurement is subsequently converted into work. Sec.
IV
presents a detailed performance analysis, including the efficiencies of information-to-work and energy-to-work conversion, as well as the power output, all as functions of the measurement time. In
Section

V
we analyse the Pareto-optimal trade-offs between the performance metrics—such as achieving maximum power at maximum efficiency—and determine optimal combinations of system-meter parameters and measurement duration. Finally, Sec.
VI
summarizes our main results, outlines guidelines for experimental setups and lists potential research avenues for extending our Pareto optimisation in information-driven processes.

II
Information-engine principles

II.1
General model

The core of the quantum information engine consists of a system, from which work is extracted, and a meter obtaining information about the system; see the sketch in Fig.
1
for the concrete implementation introduced in Sec
III
. The system and the meter are coupled for a certain time period — the measurement time
t_{\mathrm{m}}

Kirchberg and Nitzan 2025
.
Due to this coupling, the meter obtains information about the system, which is subsequently used to extract work.
This system-meter setup is captured by the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\text{S}}+\hat{H}_{\text{M}}+\hat{V}_{\text{I}}(t)

(1)

where
\hat{H}_{\text{S}}
is the Hamiltonian of the system,
\hat{H}_{\text{M}}
is the Hamiltonian of the meter, and
\hat{V}_{\text{I}}(t)
describes their time-dependent interaction. This interaction part of the Hamiltonian is finite in a time interval
t\in(0,t_{\mathrm{m}})
and zero otherwise.

In time intervals in which system and meter are uncoupled from each other, the system interacts with a heat bath at temperature
T_{\text{S}}
while the meter interacts with a heat bath at temperature
T_{\text{M}}
. We refer to these two baths as the system bath and the meter bath.

II.2
Engine cycle

The engine cycle consists of the following steps, see also
Kirchberg and Nitzan 2025
where an analogous cycle was analysed.

Initialization:

The system (S) and the meter (M) are initially uncoupled and in thermal states

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}(0)=\frac{\exp(-\hat{H}_{\text{S}}/k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{S}})}{\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\exp(-\hat{H}_{\text{S}}/k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{S}})\right\}}\ ,

(2)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{M}}(0)=\frac{\exp(-\hat{H}_{\text{M}}/k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{M}})}{\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\exp(-\hat{H}_{\text{M}}/k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{M}})\right\}}\ ,

(3)

specified by the temperatures
T_{\text{S}}
and
T_{\text{M}}
of the respective baths they are initially coupled to. Here,
k_{\text{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant. The total initial state is hence captured by
\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(0)=\hat{\rho}_{\text{S}}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{\text{M}}(0)
.

Unitary evolution:

Next, the system and meter are decoupled from their respective baths. Then, for the period of time
0<t<t_{\text{m}}
, which we refer to as the
measurement time
, the system and meter are coupled to each other. This coupling is reflected by
\hat{V}_{\text{I}}(t)
being non-zero for times
0<t<t_{\text{m}}
. Importantly, since
[\hat{V}_{\text{I}}(t),\hat{H}_{\text{M}}]\neq 0
, the states of the system and of the meter become correlated during the time period
t_{\text{m}}
.
Assuming instant switching of the coupling Hamiltonian at times
t=0
(“on”) and at
t=t_{\text{m}}
(“off”), the measurement work, namely the work spent to correlate the system and meter states, is

W_{\text{meas}}(t_{\text{m}})\equiv\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{[\hat{H}_{\text{S}}+\hat{H}_{\text{M}}]\big(\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})-\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(0)\big)\right\},

(4)

where
\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(0)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
with
\hat{U}(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp{\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\hat{H}(t^{\prime})\right]}
, while
\mathcal{T}
being the time-ordering operator and
\hbar
being the reduced Planck constant. Note that the Hamiltonian entering the expression in Eq. (
4
) is the
total
Hamiltonian at times
t=0
and
t=t_{\mathrm{m}}
, where the system-meter coupling is switched off,
\hat{V}_{\rm I}=0
.

Projective measurement & information acquisition:

At time
t_{\text{m}}
the coupling between system and meter is turned off.
The state of the meter is determined by a projective measurement on the eigenstates
\ket{n}
of
\hat{H}_{\text{M}}
by a classical meter
Taranto et al. 2023
. This quantum-to-classical transition is the Heisenberg cut
Heisenberg 1949
;
Von Neumann 1955
;
Atmanspacher 1997
. After the projective measurement we can formulate the conditional density matrix

\hat{\rho}_{\rm S}(t_{\text{m}}|n)=\frac{\braket{n|\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})|n}}{\operatorname{tr}_{\text{S}}\left\{\braket{n|\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})|n}\right\}}\equiv\frac{\braket{n|\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})|n}}{P(n,t_{\text{m}})},

(5)

which depends on the measurement outcome
n
.
The conditional probability of finding the system in state
\ket{i}
, given the measurement outcome
n
, is given by the diagonal elements of
\hat{\rho}(t_{\text{m}}|n)
:

P(i|n,t_{\text{m}})=\braket{i|\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}}|n)|i}.

(6)

Similarly, the joint probability of the meter being in state
\ket{n}
and the system being in state
\ket{i}
at time
t_{\text{m}}
is given by

P(i,n,t_{\text{m}})=\braket{i|\braket{n|\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})|n}|i}.

(7)

Starting from Eq. (
5
), the conditional entropy
S_{n}(t)
given the measurement outcome
n
reads

S_{n}(t)=-k_{\text{B}}\sum_{n}\hat{\rho}(t_{\text{m}}|n)\ln\hat{\rho}(t_{\text{m}}|n).

(8)

Averaging over engine cycles, the entropy
S
is

S(t)=\sum_{n}P(n,t)S_{n}(t),

(9)

with
P(n,t)
as defined in
Eq.

5
.

Thus, we quantify the information gain,
I(t_{\text{m}})
, during the measurement process by the entropy difference
Kirchberg and Nitzan 2025
, see discussion in
Appendix

A
,

I(t_{\text{m}})\equiv S(0)-S(t_{\text{m}}).

(10)

Work extraction:

Given the measurement outcome in step
c
, useful work can be extracted from the system, specifically when the system is more likely to be found in the excited state. Conversely, no work is extracted if the system is more likely to be found in its ground state, as attempting to do so would lead to a net energy cost rather than a gain, see later discussion in
Section

III
(d).
We determine the extractable work by ergotropy, the maximal work that can be extracted under unitary transformation
Allahverdyan et al. 2004
;
Francica et al. 2017

W_{\text{ext}}(t_{\text{m}}|n)=\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{S}}(t_{\text{m}}|n)\hat{H}_{\text{S}}\right\}\\
-\min_{\hat{U}_{n}}\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{U}_{n}\hat{\rho}_{\text{S}}(t_{\text{m}}|n)\hat{U}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{H}_{\text{S}}\right\}.

(11)

The first term on the right-hand side of
Eq.

11
is the energy of the system after measuring the outcome
n
, whereas the second term is the energy of the system after applying a unitary transformation
\hat{U}_{n}
. Preferably the unitary transformation,
\hat{U}_{n}
, makes the second term vanishingly small to yield a maximal
W_{\text{ext}}
. In the example case of a two-level system, as discussed later in Sec.
III
, this unitary work extraction can be realized by stimulated emission by a
\pi
-pulse

.
Calculating the ensemble average over many engine cycles yields for the maximally extractable work

W_{\text{ext}}(t_{\text{m}})=\sum_{n}P(n,t_{\text{m}})\big[\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{S}}(t_{\text{m}}|n)\hat{H}_{\text{S}}\right\}\\
-\min_{\hat{U}_{n}}\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{U}_{n}\hat{\rho}_{\text{S}}(t_{\text{m}}|n)\hat{U}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{H}_{\text{S}}\right\}\big].

(12)

Resetting:

The system and the meter are coupled to their respective baths, allowing both to rethermalise, thus closing the information-engine cycle.

W_{\text{ext}}\equiv\mathcal{Q}_{\rm S}
. The energy invested for the measurement
W_{\text{meas}}
, Eq. (
4

.

W_{\rm reset}=T_{\rm reset}\mathcal{S}

Next, we consider the
cycle time
, which is the sum of times associated with measurement, projection, work extraction, and resetting. In this study, as alluded to in the introduction, we identify the measurement time
t_{\text{m}}
under step
b
, the time period for system-meter coupling, as the largest and therefore dominant time scale for the cycle. In order to motivate this, we examine other timescales related to different steps discussed above.

First, we estimate the time for a projective measurement as described in step
c
. In principle, the quantum meter needs to be coupled and eventually decoupled from another quantum meter and so on, forming an infinite chain of meters, a well-known issue in measurement theory
Mensky 2000
.
In our model, we use a classical meter to read out the quantum meter where the time can be estimated based on a decoherence mechanism
Zeh 1970
. This related decoherence time can be significantly shorter than
t_{\text{m}}
due to, for example, strong coupling or high complexity of the classical meter subspace as discussed in
Taranto et al. 2023
;
Shettell et al. 2023
.

Secondly, the time required for work extraction in step
e
can be estimated, e.g., for a two-level system with energy splitting
\Delta E
by the duration of stimulated emission under the influence of a
\pi
-pulse, which is on the order of the period time of the system and could be as short as
\hbar/\Delta E
(ranging from
10^{-12}
to
10^{-15}
seconds in molecular systems
Nitzan 2024
and
10^{-9}
to
10^{-10}
seconds for transmon qubits
Blais et al. 2004
). This process can be considered faster than
t_{\text{m}}
. In particular, in the operation of the information engine to produce net useful work, we find for the prototype engine studied in
Section

III
that the system energy is larger than the energy,
\hbar\omega
, associated with the quantum meter. Therefore, we have
(\Delta E/\hbar)t_{\text{m}}>\omega t_{\text{m}}
which motivates the discussions of the measurement time in terms of
\omega t_{\text{m}}
in
Sections

IV
and
V
.

Thirdly, considering the finite sizes of the system and meter, the reset time in step
f
(thermal relaxation) can be considered rapid when connected to heat reservoirs of infinite size
Taranto et al. 2023
(approximately
10^{-12}
seconds in condensed molecular systems
Nitzan 2024
).

After outlining the detailed operational cycle of the quantum information engine, we now discuss possible performance metrics of the engine, with particular attention to the previously mentioned measurement time which dominates the cycle time.

II.3
Performance metrics

As measurement time, information acquisition, energy cost for measurement and the extracted work are interrelated, we consider the following three performance metrics: (A) the ratio of extracted work to information acquired from measurement, which we henceforth name information efficiency, (B) the power, and (C) the efficiency of converting the input energy to net extracted work, i.e., the thermodynamic efficiency.

(A) Measurement yields information, which in turn bounds the amount of work that can be extracted from the system.
We define the information efficiency, i.e., the conversion of information to extracted work by the ratio

\eta_{\rm info}(t_{\text{m}})\equiv\frac{W_{\text{ext}}(t_{\text{m}})}{T_{\text{S}}I(t_{\text{m}})},

(13)

where
W_{\text{ext}}
and
I
are defined by Eqs. (
12
) and (
10
), respectively.

Since the maximal extractable work is given by the free energy difference after the projective measurement, as shown in
Appendix

B
and Ref.
Kirchberg and Nitzan 2025
,

\displaystyle\Delta F(t_{\text{m}})=\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{H}_{\text{S}}\big(\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})-\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(0)\big)\right\}+T_{\rm S}I(t_{m}),

(14)

we have
W_{\text{ext}}(t_{\text{m}})\leq\Delta F(t_{\text{m}})
. However, for a non-demolishing measurement, i.e.,
[\hat{H}_{\text{S}},\hat{V}_{\text{I}}(t)]=0
, we find that the first term in Eq. (
14
) vanishes, so that
W_{\text{ext}}(t_{\text{m}})\leq T_{\text{S}}I(t_{\text{m}})
(see
Appendices

B
and
C
) and the information efficiency, Eq. (
13
), is bounded by
0\leq\eta_{\text{info}}\leq 1
.

(B) The maximum power of the engine is given by

\Pi(t_{\text{m}})\equiv\frac{W_{\text{ext}}(t_{\text{m}})-W_{\text{meas}}(t_{\text{m}})}{t_{\text{m}}}=\frac{W_{\text{net}}(t_{\text{m}})}{t_{\text{m}}}

(15)

with the net extracted work defined by the difference between the extracted work, Eq. (
12
), and the measurement cost, Eq. (
4
). The measurement time
t_{\text{m}}
is considered the cycle time of the information engine as discussed in the previous
Section

II.2
and, thus, enters in the denominator of Eq. (
15
).
We note that equation (
15
) represents an upper bound on the power output. First, the ergotropy which is used to quantify the extracted work
W_{\text{ext}}
is a bound as it describes the maximum work extracted under unitary transformation, see Eq. (
12
). Second, the measurement time
t_{\text{m}}
sets a lower bound to the total duration of the engine cycle.

(C) Finally, we consider the efficiency of converting the input energy
\mathcal{Q}_{\rm S}
to net extracted work
W_{\text{net}}=W_{\text{ext}}-W_{\text{meas}}
. We recognize that the engine can operate in various regimes, serving different thermodynamic purposes, namely as a heat engine if
W_{\text{net}}>0
or as a heat valve/pump if
W_{\text{net}}<0
. For the thermodynamic efficiency, we limit our consideration to the heat engine regime, where
W_{\text{net}}\geq 0
. In this regime, heat flows from the bath at temperature
T_{\text{S}}
into the bath at temperature
T_{\text{M}}
, while the engine delivers a net positive amount of work. That is, heat flows from hot to cold, and work is extracted.
As such we define the thermodynamic efficiency as

\eta_{\rm HE}(t_{\text{m}})\equiv\frac{W_{\text{ext}}(t_{\text{m}})-W_{\text{meas}}(t_{\text{m}})}{\mathcal{Q}_{\rm S}(t_{\text{m}})}=1-\frac{W_{\text{meas}}(t_{\text{m}})}{W_{\text{ext}}(t_{\text{m}})}.

(16)

Note that we take
\mathcal{Q}_{\rm S}\equiv W_{\text{ext}}
in Eq. (
16
), that is, all energy taken from the system heat bath can be extracted as work, as discussed under step
e
in Sec.
II.2
.
Since
W_{\text{net}}\geq 0
the efficiency, Eq. (
16
), is bounded by
0\leq\eta_{\rm HE}(t_{\text{m}})\leq 1
.

III
Two-level system measured by a harmonic oscillator

To illustrate the general model introduced in Sec.
II.2
, we consider a quantum two-level system (
TLS
) with energy-level spacing
\Delta E
as the system S and a quantum harmonic oscillator (
QHO
) of frequency
\omega
and mass
\mathcal{M}
as the meter, as shown in
Fig.

1
. The Hamiltonian of the system and the meter are given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{S}}=\Delta E\ket{1}\bra{1},

(17a)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{M}}=\frac{\mathcal{M}\omega^{2}}{2}\hat{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mathcal{M}}\hat{p}^{2},

(17b)

where the energy of the
\ket{0}
state is set to zero. The interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{V}_{\text{I}}(t)
is switched on for the measurement time only, i.e.,

\hat{V}_{\text{I}}(t)=\begin{cases}g\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\hat{p},\quad t\in(0,t_{\text{m}})\\
0,\quad t\notin(0,t_{\text{m}}).\end{cases}

(17c)

For this specific example system, the generic information-engine cycle described in
Section

II.1
takes the following concrete form:

Initialization:

The initial state of the combined system and meter is

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(0)

\displaystyle=\hat{\rho}_{\text{S}}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{\text{M}}(0)

(18)

\displaystyle=\big(a\ket{0}\bra{0}+b\ket{1}\bra{1}\big)\otimes\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{Z_{M}}e^{-\beta_{M}\hat{H}_{\text{M}}}\ket{n}\bra{n},

where
a=(1+e^{-\beta_{\text{S}}\Delta E})^{-1}
and
b=(1+e^{+\beta_{\text{S}}\Delta E})^{-1}
are the initial populations of the ground and excited states of the
TLS
and
\beta_{\text{S}}=(k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{S}})^{-1}
is the inverse temperature of the system bath.
The initial meter state in Eq. (
18
) is thermal, defined by
\beta_{\text{M}}=(k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{M}})^{-1}
and the partition function
Z_{\text{M}}=\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hat{H}_{\text{M}}}\right\}
.

Unitary evolution:

During the time interval
t\in(0,\,t_{\text{m}})
, the system and the meter evolve unitarily under the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{\text{S}}+\hat{H}_{\text{M}}+\hat{V}_{\text{I}}(t)
, resulting in an entangled state.
The state of the full system at time
t_{\text{m}}
is thus given by

For our proposed quantum information engine, it is desirable to achieve both a high rate of work extraction—characterised by a large positive power
\Pi
,
Eq.

15
—and high efficiency in converting both input energy and information into useful work, respectively given by the thermodynamic efficiency
\eta_{\mathrm{HE}}
,
Eq.

\eta_{\rm info}
,
Eq.

13
.
However, these performance metrics depend on a shared set of system and meter parameters: the system and meter temperatures
T_{\text{S}}
and
T_{\text{M}}
, system and meter energy level spacings
\Delta E
and
\hbar\omega
, their coupling strength
g_{\text{eff}}^{2}
and, of particular interest here, the measurement time
t_{\text{m}}
. Changing the engine’s parameters thus influences all performance metrics concurrently and leads to the emergence of trade-offs between them, which need to be optimised in particular for experimental realizations of the proposed engine.

In this section, we compute a simultaneous optimisation of our metrics by determining Pareto-optimal trade-offs, also called Pareto fronts: a set of engine configurations where improving one metric necessarily compromises at least one other. Standard optimization methods generally either compute only a single optimum e.g., high power, or involve scalarisation of different objectives into a single weighted “cost function” which is subsequently minimised
Erdman et al. 2023
. Pareto fronts, however, represent the best possible balance between all metrics simultaneously. While originally developed in the context of economics and engineering, Pareto optimisation has been increasingly used in fields such as biology
Sheftel et al. 2013
;
Berx and Proesmans 2024b
and stochastic thermodynamics
Forão et al. 2025
.
However, to the best of our knowledge, Pareto optimisation has not previously been applied in the context of quantum information engines.

A more technical description of Pareto-optimal fronts is given in Appendix
I
.
We compute the fronts numerically using a variant of the elitist genetic algorithm NSGA-II
Deb 2008
, which is able to accurately determine non-convex parts of the fronts, in contrast to standard optimization methods. In particular, we will focus on pairwise trade-offs between power, thermodynamic efficiency and information efficiency.

VI
Conclusions

We studied a cyclic information engine in which measurement and subsequent feedback are used to convert energy into useful work. Information is considered as a crucial
resource
that must be exchanged between the system and the meter over a finite time. This
measurement time
can be comparable to the engine’s operational time, especially in the nanoscale regime. Importantly, each measurement also incurs an intrinsic cost that depends on the measurement time and must be properly accounted for. In this work, we investigated the interrelation between information acquisition, extractable work, and the energetic cost of measurement — all as functions of the measurement time.

As a prototype, we formulated and analysed a quantum information engine cycle involving a two-level system measured by a quantum harmonic oscillator—a setup inspired by experimental platforms such as nitrogen-vacancy centers or superconducting qubits coupled to resonators. Within this framework, we found that a finite measurement time is essential to transfer sufficient information about the system to the meter to achieve positive power output and high efficiency in the conversion of energy and information into work. We found that lower meter temperatures and stronger system–meter coupling enhance the meter’s sensitivity, thereby improving information transfer. While no information can be transferred in the absence of system–meter interaction, excessively strong coupling can become detrimental due to increased measurement costs, ultimately leading—under certain conditions—to zero and even negative net work. This highlights the fundamental fact that information, as the critical resource of information engines, cannot be acquired instantaneously and without cost. Information acquisition through measurement, the related energetic cost and the subsequent work extraction are highly interrelated by the measurement time.

Our results further show that achieving positive power output and high efficiency in the conversion of information and energy into useful work requires careful tuning of the measurement time and of other system and meter parameters. This reveals intrinsic trade-offs between these performance metrics, which we analysed using Pareto fronts—a global optimization framework. Notably, we identified a global maximum in power output at a finite efficiency, occurring at a specific, finite measurement time for the prototype information engine.

Since the Pareto fronts naturally bound the trade-offs between the engine’s performance metrics, they identify the optimal combinations of system and meter parameters for maximizing information engine performance. This offers practical guidance for experimentally selecting optimal engine configurations.

Exploiting measurement and information as a
resource
for controlling and optimising
outputs
can be vital not only in contexts such as energy transduction in nanoscale devices, but also in other research fields such as chemistry to improve reaction yields
Pruchyathamkorn et al. 2024
.
In particular, the consideration of measurement time is crucial to control and optimise time-dependent processes.
Moreover, at the nanoscale of information driven processes, additional complexity may arise due to fluctuations in quantities such as the power output. This might lead to future investigations of,
inter alia
, the relation between the signal-to-noise ratio, the acquired information and the measurement time.

\pi-

T_{\text{p}}(n,t_{\text{m}})=\\
\frac{\Delta E}{k_{\text{B}}}\Bigg[\ln{\Bigg(\frac{P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})}{P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})}\Bigg)^{-1}}\Theta\Big(P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})-P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})\Big)\\
+\ln{\left(\frac{P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})}{P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})}\right)^{-1}\Theta\Big(P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})-P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})\Big)}\Bigg].

(41)

The Carnot efficiency gives the maximum amount of work that can be extracted through heat transfer as

W=Q\left(1-\frac{T_{\text{C}}}{T_{\text{H}}}\right)\Rightarrow dW=dQ\left(1-\frac{T_{\text{C}}}{T_{\text{H}}}\right),

(42)

where
T_{\text{C}}
and
T_{\text{H}}
are the temperatures of the cold and hot reservoirs, respectively.
There are two scenarios; (a) the passive state is hotter than the thermal state, that is,
T_{\text{p}}>T_{\text{S}}
, or (b) the passive state is colder than the thermal state, that is,
T_{\text{p}}<T_{\text{S}}
.

First investigate scenario (a),
T_{\text{p}}>T_{\text{S}}
.
In this case
T_{\text{C}}=T_{\text{S}}
, and
T_{\text{H}}=T_{\text{}}
varying from
T_{\text{p}}
to
T_{\text{S}}
yielding the thermal work

W_{\text{th}}^{\text{(a)}}=-\int_{T_{\text{p}}}^{T_{\text{S}}}dT\,C(T)\left(1-\frac{T_{\text{S}}}{T}\right).

(43)

Next, investigate scenario (b),
T_{\text{p}}<T_{\text{S}}

In this case we have
T_{\text{H}}=T_{\text{S}}
, and
T_{\text{C}}=T
varying from
T_{\text{p}}
to
T_{\text{S}}
.
The difference between scenarios (a) and (b) is that in scenario (b), part of the heat must be expended to heat up the
TLS
:

dQ_{\text{sys}}=dQ\frac{T}{T_{\text{S}}}

(44)

which raises the temperature by

dT=\frac{dQ_{\text{sys}}}{C(T)}=dQ\frac{T}{T_{\text{S}}\,C(T)}.

(45)

Hence, the work that can be extracted is

dW=dQ\left(1-\frac{T}{T_{\text{S}}}\right),

(46)

and

\begin{split}W_{\text{th}}^{\text{(b)}}&=\int_{T_{\text{P}}}^{T_{\text{S}}}dTC(T)\left(1-\frac{T}{T_{\text{S}}}\right)\\
&=\int_{T_{\text{p}}}^{T_{\text{S}}}dTC(T)\left(\frac{T_{\text{S}}}{T}-1\right)\\
&=-\int_{T_{\text{p}}}^{T_{\text{S}}}dTC(T)\left(1-\frac{T_{\text{S}}}{T}\right)\\
&=W_{\text{th}}^{\text{(a)}},\end{split}

(47)

meaning that in either case the extracted work is the same.
On average then, the extracted work is

W_{\text{th}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n,t_{\text{m}})\int_{T_{\text{p}}}^{T_{\text{S}}}dTC(T)\left(\frac{T_{\text{S}}}{T}-1\right).

(48)

Evaluating the integral

W_{\text{th}}=\int_{T_{\text{p}}}^{T_{\text{S}}}dTC(T)\left(\frac{T_{\text{S}}}{T}-1\right)

(49)

by making the variable substitution
y=\Delta E/k_{\text{B}}T
yields

W_{\text{th}}=\frac{\Delta E\left(1-\frac{T_{\text{S}}}{T_{\text{p}}}\right)}{1+e^{\Delta E/k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{p}}}}-k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{S}}\ln\left(\frac{1+e^{-\Delta E/k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{p}}}}{1+e^{-\Delta E/k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{S}}}}\right).

(50)

Inserting the expression for
T_{\text{p}}
into
Eq.

50
yields

W_{\text{th}}=k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{S}}\Big[P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})\big(\ln P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})-\ln b\big)\\
+P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})\big(\ln P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})-\ln a\big)\Big]\\
\times\Theta\big[P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})-P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})\big]\\
+k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{S}}\Big[P(0|m,t_{\text{m}})\big(\ln P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})-\ln a\big)\\
+P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})\big(\ln P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})-\ln a\big)\Big]\\
\times\Theta\big[P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})-P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})\big].

(51)

Adding and subtracting the term

k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{S}}\Big[P(0|m,t_{\text{m}})\big(\ln P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})-\ln a\big)+\\
P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})\big(\ln P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})-\ln a\big)\Big]\\
\Theta\big[P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})-P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})\big],

(52)

simplifying, and inserting the resulting expression into the average work defined in
Eq.

48
yields

W_{\text{th}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{S}}\Big\{\Big[P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})-P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})\Big]\ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)\\
\times\Theta\big[P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})-P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})\big]\\
+P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})\big(\ln P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})-\ln a\big)\\
+P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})\big(\ln P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})-\ln b\big)\Big\}.

(53)

Noting that
\ln{(b/a)}=-\Delta E/k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{S}}
and using the ergotropy as defined in
Eq.

25
we see that the first term is the average ergotropy,
-W_{\text{erg}}
. Further, noting that the two last terms make up the mutual information defined in
Eq.

10
we can write the average thermal work as

W_{\text{th}}(t_{\text{m}})=-W_{\text{erg}}(t_{\text{m}})+T_{\text{S}}I(t_{\text{m}}).

(54)

Appendix D
Evaluation of the Measurement Work

We assume a Hamiltoninan of the form

\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{\text{S}}+\hat{H}_{\text{M}}+\hat{V}_{\text{I}}(t)

(55)

as per the main text where
\hat{H}_{\text{S}}
is the system from which work is to be extracted,
\hat{H}_{\text{M}}
is the meter used to probe the state of the system, and
\hat{V}_{\text{I}}
is the interaction Hamiltonian which in this case has the form

\hat{V}_{\text{I}}(t)=\begin{cases}g\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\hat{p},\quad t\in(0,t_{\text{m}})\\
0,\quad t\notin(0,t_{\text{m}})\end{cases}.

(56)

The time-evolution of the system is described by the density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)=\exp\big(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\hat{H}(t^{\prime})\big)\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(0)\exp\big(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\hat{H}(t^{\prime})\big)
and we note that
\dot{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}(t),\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)]
.
The system energy is given by
E(t)=\langle\hat{H}(t)\rangle=\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)\hat{H}(t)\right\}
and the time derivative is

\displaystyle\dot{E}(t)=\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\dot{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}}(t)\hat{H}(t)\right\}+\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)\dot{\hat{H}}(t)\right\}=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\big[\hat{H}(t),\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)\big]\hat{H}(t)\right\}+\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)\dot{\hat{H}}(t)\right\}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)\dot{\hat{H}}(t)\right\}

(57)

where we used that
[\hat{H}(t),\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)]=i\dot{\hat{\rho}}/\hbar
.
Thus the energy difference between time
t=0
and time
t=t_{\text{m}}
is

E(t_{\text{m}})-E(0)=\int_{0}^{t_{\text{m}}}dt\dot{E}(t)=\int_{0}^{t_{\text{m}}}dt\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)\dot{\hat{H}}(t)\right\}.

(58)

Here we may note that
\hat{H}_{\text{S}},\,\hat{H}_{\text{M}}
are both time-independent and thus
\dot{\hat{H}}(t)=\dot{\hat{V}}_{\text{I}}(t)
.
We write the interaction Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\text{I}}(t)=g\Theta(t_{\text{m}}-t)\Theta(t)\big(\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\hat{p}\big)=g\Theta(t_{\text{m}}-t)\Theta(t)\hat{V}_{\text{I}}

(59)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\dot{\hat{V}}_{\text{I}}(t)=g\Big(-\delta(t_{\text{m}}-t)\Theta(t)+\Theta(t_{\text{m}}-t)\delta(t)\Big)\hat{V}_{\text{I}}.

(60)

Inserting the time-derivative from
Eq.

59
into
Eq.

58
yields

\displaystyle E(t_{\text{m}})-E(0)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{\text{m}}}dt\,\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)g\Big(-\delta(t_{\text{m}}-t)\Theta(t)+\Theta(t_{\text{m}}-t)\delta(t)\Big)\hat{V}_{\text{I}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(0)\hat{V}_{\text{I}}\right\}-\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})\hat{V}_{\text{I}}\right\}

(61)

which is the result used in
Eq.

20
.
Hence, the energy difference created simply by coupling and decoupling the system and meter is exactly what we defined as
W_{\text{meas}}
.

We can further expand this by evaluating each of the traces on their own.
Expressing the initial density matrix as
\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(0)=\big[a\ket{0}\bra{0}+b\ket{1}\bra{1}\big]\otimes\sum_{m}P_{m}\ket{m}\bra{m}
where
\sum_{m}P_{m}=1
are the probabilities associated with the eigenstates of the meter.
Thus the first of the traces can be evaluated as

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(0)\hat{V}_{\text{I}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{1}\bra{n}\bra{i}\Big(a\ket{0}\bra{0}+b\ket{1}\bra{1}\Big)

\displaystyle\otimes\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}P_{m}\ket{m}\bra{m}g\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\hat{p}\ket{i}\ket{n}

\displaystyle=bg\sum_{n}\sum_{m}P_{m}\braket{n|m}\braket{n|\hat{p}|m}

\displaystyle=bg\sum_{m}P_{m}\braket{m|\hat{p}|m}=0

(62)

where we have used the completeness relation to eliminate the sum over
n
.
The conclusion is that, for this particular model, turning the coupling on has no cost.

The second trace can similarly be evaluated, however this time the density matrix is no longer the initial density matrix but is rather time-evolved.
Performing the same type of polaron transform used in
Appendix

F
, we may insert shift operators
\hat{D}^{\dagger}\hat{D}
in strategic places, use the completeness relation, and observe that just as for the first trace only the
i=1
term contributes to yield

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)\hat{V}_{\text{I}}\right\}=bg\sum_{m}P_{m}\braket{m|e^{it\hat{H}^{\prime}/\hbar}\hat{p}e^{-it\hat{H}^{\prime}/\hbar}|m}

\displaystyle=bg\sum_{m}P_{m}\braket{m|\hat{D}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{D}\hat{p}\hat{D}^{\dagger}\hat{D}(t)|m}.

(63)

Here we use commutators and the commutation relation
[f(\hat{x}),\hat{p}]=i\hbar\frac{df(x)}{dx}
to migrate the momentum operator from the centre to the left hand side, and the shift operators should cancel due to being Hermitian.
In practice this becomes

\displaystyle bg\sum_{m}P_{m}\braket{m|\hat{D}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{D}\hat{p}\hat{D}^{\dagger}\hat{D}(t)|m}

\displaystyle=bg\sum_{m}P_{m}\braket{m|\hat{D}^{\dagger}(t)(-Mg+\hat{p})\hat{D}\hat{D}^{\dagger}\hat{D}(t)|m}

\displaystyle=-bg^{2}M+bg\sum_{m}P_{m}\braket{m|\hat{D}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{p}\hat{D}(t)|m}

\displaystyle=-bg^{2}M+\sum_{m}P_{m}\braket{m|\big(Mg\cos(\omega t)+\hat{p}\big)\hat{D}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{D}(t)|m}

\displaystyle=-bg_{\text{eff}}^{2}\big(1-\cos(\omega t)\big).

(64)

Here
g_{\text{eff}}^{2}=g^{2}M
and
M
is the mass of the harmonic oscillator.
All in all the measurement work is then

W_{\text{meas}}=\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(0)\hat{V}_{\text{I}}\right\}-\operatorname{tr}_{\text{}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})\hat{V}_{\text{I}}\right\}=bg_{\text{eff}}^{2}\big(1-\cos(\omega t_{\text{m}})\big).

(65)

Appendix E
Explicit Forms of the Joint Probabilities

The joint probabilities of finding the two-level system in state
\ket{i}
and the meter in state
\ket{n}
at time
t_{\text{m}}
is given by

P(i,n,t_{\text{m}})=\braket{i|\braket{n|\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})|n}|i}

(66)

according to
Eq.

7
.
Here we present the calculations that result in explicit forms of these probabilities for our given system in
Section

III
.

E.1
Joint Probability in the Absence of Interaction

The coupling (
Eq.

17c
) is active only when the
TLS
is in the excited state.
We begin with the easier case of examining the joint probability
P(i=0,n,t_{\text{m}})
where the coupling is inactive.
With
\hat{H}_{0}=\Delta E\ket{1}\bra{1}+\frac{\mathcal{M}\omega^{2}}{2}\hat{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mathcal{M}}\hat{p}^{2}
the joint probability is

\begin{split}P(0,n,t_{\text{m}})&=\bra{0}\bra{n}\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})\ket{n}\ket{0}\\
&=\bra{0}\bra{n}e^{-it_{\text{m}}\hat{H}_{0}/\hbar}(a\ket{0}\bra{0}+b\ket{1}\bra{1})\\
&\otimes\sum_{m}P_{m}\ket{m}\bra{m}e^{it_{\text{m}}\hat{H}_{0}/\hbar}\ket{n}\ket{0}\\
&=a\sum_{m}P_{m}\bra{n}m\rangle\bra{m}n\rangle=aP_{n}\\
&=a\frac{1}{Z_{0}}e^{-\beta_{M}\hbar\omega(n+1/2)}\\
&=a\left(1-e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega}\right)e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega n}\end{split}

(67)

with the thermal distribution
P_{n}=e^{-\beta_{M}\hbar\omega(n+1/2)}/\sum_{n}e^{-\beta_{M}\hbar\omega(n+1/2)}=e^{-\beta_{M}\hbar\omega(n+1/2)}/Z_{0}
and
\beta_{\text{M}}
being the inverse temperature of the meter.

E.2
Joint Probability in the Presence of Interaction

In this case the coupling is active and evaluation is less straightforward.
We again make use of the polaron transformation described in
Appendix

F
and write

\begin{split}P(1,n,t)&=\bra{1}\bra{n}e^{-it\hat{H}_{0}/\hbar}\hat{D}(\alpha)\hat{\rho}_{\text{0}}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)e^{it\hat{H}_{0}/\hbar}\ket{n}\ket{1}\\
&=b\sum_{m}P_{m}\bra{n}\hat{D}(\alpha)\ket{m}\bra{m}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\ket{n}\\
&=b\sum_{m}P_{m}\left|\bra{n}\hat{D}(\alpha)\ket{m}\right|^{2}.\end{split}.

(68)

The displacement operator has here been rewritten in the form

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\alpha)=e^{\alpha(t_{\text{m}})\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}(t_{\text{m}})\hat{a}}

(69)

\displaystyle\alpha\equiv\alpha(t_{\text{m}})=g\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{M}}{2\hbar\omega}}\Bigg(\sin(\omega t_{\text{m}})-i\Big(\cos(\omega t_{\text{m}})-1\Big)\Bigg).

(70)

The identity from Ref.
Cahill and Glauber 1969

\braket{m|\hat{D}(\alpha)|n}=\left(\frac{n!}{m!}\right)^{1/2}\alpha^{m-n}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}L_{n}^{(m-n)}(|\alpha|^{2})

(71)

yields

P(1,n,t_{\text{m}})=\\
b\sum_{m}P_{m}\left|\left(\frac{m!}{n!}\right)^{1/2}\alpha^{n-m}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}L_{m}^{(n-m)}\left(|\alpha|^{2}\right)\right|^{2}

(72)

where
L_{m}^{(n-m)}(x)
are the generalised Laguerre polynomials and the thermal occupation
P_{m}=e^{-\beta_{M}\hbar\omega(m+1/2)}/\sum_{n}e^{-\beta_{M}\hbar\omega(m+1/2)}=e^{-\beta_{M}\hbar\omega(m+1/2)}/Z_{0}
with
\beta_{\text{M}}
being the inverse temperature of the meter.

In the limit
\hbar\omega\gg k_{B}T_{\text{M}}
, we can assume that only the ground state of the oscillator is occupied, that is,
m=0
and
P_{m=0}=1
.
Eq.

72
can than be written as

\displaystyle P(1,n,t_{\text{m}})=b\left(\frac{1}{n!}\right)|\alpha|^{2n}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}

(73)

\displaystyle=b\left(\frac{1}{n!}\right)\bigg[\frac{g_{\rm eff}^{2}}{\hbar\omega}\big(1-\cos(\omega t_{\text{m}})\big)\bigg]^{n}e^{-\frac{g_{\rm eff}^{2}}{\hbar\omega}\big(1-\cos(\omega t_{\text{m}})\big)}.

Appendix F
Work Extraction in the Zeno Limit

The quantum Zeno effect is essentially a freezing of the system dynamics through fast, repeated measurement
Misra and Sudarshan 1977
;
Facchi and Pascazio 2008
.
Referring to the engine in the main text, we may then ask how it behaves in the Zeno limit and if net positive work extraction is possible.

The net work output of the engine is
W_{\text{net}}=W_{\text{ext}}-W_{\text{meas}}
, hence the condition to fulfill is
W_{\text{ext}}>W_{\text{meas}}
.
Using
Eqs.

25
and
4
, the condition is

\Delta E\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n,t_{\text{m}})\Big[P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})-P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})\Big]\\
\Theta\Big(P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})-P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})\Big)>bg_{\text{eff}}^{2}(1-\cos(\omega t_{\text{m}})\big)

(74)

and we remind ourselves that
a,\,b
are the initial ground and excited state populations of the
TLS

\displaystyle a=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta_{S}\Delta E}}

(75)

\displaystyle b=\frac{e^{-\beta_{S}\Delta E}}{1+e^{-\beta_{S}\Delta E}}

(76)

where
\beta_{S}
is the inverse temperature.

When the system evolves unitarily there are essentially two timescales; the time scale of the harmonic oscillator
\omega t_{\text{m}}
and the time scale of information transfer
g_{\text{eff}}t_{\text{m}}
.
We would argue that in the Zeno limit both of these timescales must be small.
Expanding the measurement work,
W_{\text{meas}}
,
Eq.

20
, around
\omega t_{\text{m}}=0
yields

W_{\text{meas}}\approx bg_{\text{eff}}^{2}\frac{\omega^{2}t_{\text{m}}^{2}}{2}.

(77)

We rewrite the extracted work in the following way: let
n^{\prime}=\min_{n}\big(P(1|nt_{\text{m}})>P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})\big)

and find

W_{\text{ext}}=\Delta E\sum_{n=n^{\prime}}^{\infty}\Big[P(1,n,t_{\text{m}})-P(0,n,t_{\text{m}})\Big].

(78)

where Bayes’ rule was used.
The joint probabilities of
TLS
state
i
and
QHO
state
n
at time
t_{\text{m}}
are given by
\braket{i|\braket{n|\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})|n}|i}
.
The density matrix is
\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})=e^{-it_{\text{m}}\hat{H}/\hbar}\hat{\rho}_{\text{S}}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{\text{M}}(0)e^{-it_{\text{m}}\hat{H}/\hbar}
with

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\text{S}}(0)=a\ket{0}\bra{0}+b\ket{1}\bra{1}

(79)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\text{M}}(0)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{Z_{\text{M}}}e^{-\beta_{M}\hbar\omega(n+1/2)}\ket{n}\bra{n}

(80)

and
Z_{\text{M}}
is the partition function.
We can also diagonalise the Hamiltonian by way of the displacement operator
\hat{D}=e^{iMg\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\hat{x}/\hbar}
to get

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t_{\text{m}})=e^{-it\hat{H}^{\prime}/\hbar}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(t_{\text{m}})\hat{D}\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(0)\hat{D}^{\dagger}\hat{D}(t_{\text{m}})e^{it\hat{H}^{\prime}/\hbar}

(81)

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\prime}=\left(\Delta E-\frac{g_{\text{eff}}^{2}}{2}\right)\ket{1}\bra{1}+\frac{1}{2\mathcal{M}}\hat{p}^{2}+\frac{\mathcal{M}\omega^{2}}{2}\hat{x}.

(82)

Here we also remind ourselves that
g_{\text{eff}}^{2}=g^{2}\mathcal{M}
where
\mathcal{M}
is the mass of the harmonic oscillator.

With these preliminaries out of the way we may now write the average work extracted as

W_{\text{ext}}=\Delta E\sum_{n=n^{\prime}}^{\infty}\Bigg[b\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{Z_{\text{M}}}e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega(m+1/2)}\\
\braket{n|\hat{D}^{\dagger}(t_{\text{m}})\hat{D}|m}\braket{m|\hat{D}^{\dagger}\hat{D(t_{\text{m}})}|n}\Big)\\
-a\frac{1}{Z_{\text{M}}}e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega(n+1/2)}\Bigg].

(83)

Using the Zassenhaus formula
Magnus 1954
, evaluating the sum of the second term in
W_{\text{ext}}
,
Eq.

83
, and simplifying now yields the result for the condition
W_{\text{ext}}>W_{\text{meas}}

\sum_{n=n^{\prime}}^{\infty}b\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega m}\braket{n|e^{-igt_{\text{m}}\hat{p}/\hbar}|m}\braket{m|e^{igt_{\text{m}}\hat{p}/\hbar}|n}\\
>\frac{ae^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega n^{\prime}}}{1-e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega}}+\frac{bg_{\text{eff}}^{2}\omega^{2}t_{\text{m}}^{2}}{2\Delta E(1-e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega})}.

(84)

At this time we make use of the second condition,
gt\ll 1
, expand to order 2, and simplify further to get

\sum_{n=n^{\prime}}^{\infty}e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega n}\Bigg[-\left(\frac{gt_{\text{m}}}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\braket{n|\hat{p}^{2}(0)|n}\\
+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega m}\left(\frac{gt_{\text{m}}}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\braket{n|\hat{p}(0)|m}\braket{m|\hat{p}(0)|n}\Bigg]\\
>\frac{(g_{\text{eff}}\omega t_{\text{m}})^{2}}{2\Delta E(1-e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega})}+\frac{(a-b)e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega n^{\prime}}}{b(1-e^{-\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega})}

(85)

Here, a useful trick is to rewrite the momentum operator in terms of the creation and annihilation operators.
Doing this and simplifying results in the final condition of
Eq.

85

4n^{\prime}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega}{2}\right)>\\
\frac{\hbar\omega}{\Delta E}e^{\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega n^{\prime}}+\frac{2\hbar(a-b)}{g_{\text{eff}}^{2}\omega t_{\text{m}}^{2}b}.

(86)

Now,
n^{\prime}
which we may call an activation threshold for when we choose to extract work will be a function of
t_{\text{m}}
since it depends on where
P(1|n,t_{\text{m}})>P(0|n,t_{\text{m}})
.
Nevertheless, for our model
Eq.

86
provides a condition for when positive work can be extracted from the engine.

We may also use
Eq.

86
to investigate the behaviour of
W_{\text{ext}}
as a function of
t_{\text{m}}
.
First we identify that the first term on the right hand side comes from the measurement work,
W_{\text{meas}}
, and ignore this term to yield to condition
W_{\text{ext}}>0
:

4n^{\prime}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega}{2}\right)>\frac{2\hbar(a-b)}{g_{\text{eff}}^{2}\omega t_{\text{m}}^{2}b}.

(87)

Solving for
\omega t_{\text{m}}
gives

\omega t_{\text{m}}>\sqrt{\frac{2\hbar\omega(a-b)}{2n^{\prime}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\beta_{\text{M}}\hbar\omega}{2}\right)g_{\text{eff}}^{2}b}},

(88)

where we see that a higher activation threshold
n^{\prime}
allows for shorter measurement times.
Or, conversely, going to shorter measurement times pushes the activation threshold higher, meaning that lower meter states will not contribute to the extracted work

Appendix I
Pareto optimization and genetic algorithms

Assume we have a system with a compact set of
m
governing parameters
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
that fully describes the system at hand, and a set of
n
feasible objectives

\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where each objective is a function
f_{i}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the governing parameters, i.e.,
y_{i}=f_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m})
.
To compute mutually optimal relations between the different objectives we make use of multi-function optimisation to find the parameter values
\mathcal{X}
that correspond to Pareto-optimal configurations. A configuration is said to be Pareto-optimal if the improvement of any objective is necessarily detrimental to at least one of the others, where we always assume that improving an objective means minimisation; other types of optimisation can always be written in this form. Mathematically, this condition can be encoded through the concept of Pareto dominance
Emmerich and Deutz 2018
. A vector
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
is said to
dominate
another vector
\mathbf{y^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{Y}
, expressed by
\mathbf{y}\succ\mathbf{y^{\prime}}
, iff

\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}:y_{i}\leq y^{\prime}_{i},\,\text{and }\,\exists j\in\{1,\dots,n\}:y_{j}<y_{j}^{\prime}\,.

(98)

The minimal elements of the Pareto dominance partial ordering are called the Pareto-optimal objective vectors, and the subset of all such non-dominated objective vectors in
\mathcal{Y}
then constitutes the Pareto front, denoted by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\subset\mathcal{Y}
.

Figure 15:
Mappings (dashed lines) from the decision space
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
(panel a, blue region) to the objective spaces (panels b and c). A point
p\in\mathcal{X}
is mapped to a point
\mathcal{P}_{3}(p)
on the two-dimensional Pareto surface
\mathcal{P}_{3}
(gray surface in (b)) only if it lies within the gray subset of
\mathcal{X}
in (a). When projected onto a two-dimensional subspace, e.g., the
f_{2}-f_{3}
plane in panel c, it can become suboptimal with respect to points on the pairwise Pareto front
\mathcal{P}_{2}
; only points in the red region in (a) are Pareto-optimal in this case.

A simple representation is shown in Fig.
15
, where the full set of feasible, i.e., physically allowed governing parameters (blue region) is mapped onto a three-dimensional objective space
(m=2,\,n=3)
. Only a particular subset of these parameters (gray region) is associated with the Pareto hypersurface (gray surface in (b)). Note that projecting the Pareto hypersurface down results in the majority of the previously Pareto-optimal points not being optimal any more. However, the pairwise Pareto front can then either be computed through marginalisation or through a new Pareto optimisation directly onto that lower-dimensional objective space.

To compute numerically the Pareto fronts, we use the controlled elitist NSGA-II genetic algorithm
Deb 2008
to perform the optimisation. Unlike, e.g., reinforcement learning approaches for determining Pareto fronts
Erdman et al. 2023
, which often require extensive model tuning and hyperparameter optimization, our genetic algorithms heuristically explore the parameter space without the need for such adjustments.
