Prevalence and distribution of dental anomalies in permanent teeth among children and adolescents attending the University Dental Hospital in Sivas, Türkiye: a radiographic cross-sectional study
Authors: Arzu Kockanat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental anomalies (DA) in pediatric patients can have wide-ranging implications, including impaired aesthetics, compromised oral function and adverse effects on psychological well-being. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence and distribution of DA according to gender and jaw in permanent teeth of children and adolescents attending the University Dental Hospital in Sivas, Türkiye and to compare the findings with reports from other regions of Türkiye and worldwide populations.MethodsThis cross-sectional study evaluated digital panoramic radiographs and clinical records of 1030 patients aged 8 to 15 years who attended for routine treatment at Sivas Cumhuriyet University Dental Hospital between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023. A calibrated examiner conducted the evaluation to identify dental anomalies, which were categorized into four subgroups: number, size, shape and position anomalies. The significance of associations between categorical variables was assessed using the Chi-square test and Fisher’s Exact test, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsOf the patients included in the study, 53.6% were female and 46.4% were male. The prevalence of dental anomalies was 20.7% and the mean age of children with dental anomalies was 10.88 ± 2.32 years. The most prevalent dental anomaly was tooth agenesis (9.7%), followed by ectopic eruption (3.3%), root dilaceration (2.8%), impacted teeth (1.8%) and taurodontism (1.2%) respectively. Taurodontism was significantly more prevalent in males than in females (p = 0.043). A significantly higher number of dental anomalies were observed in the maxilla compared to the mandible (p = 0.004). Hyperdontia, macrodontia, microdontia, talon cusp and ectopic eruption were significantly more prevalent in the maxilla, while hypodontia was more prevalent in the mandible (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe prevalence of dental anomalies in permanent teeth among children and adolescents in Sivas was relatively high. The most common dental anomaly was tooth agenesis. The results of this hospital-based study provide baseline information for the region.Keywords: Child, Adolescent, Tooth abnormalities, Hypodontia, Taurodontism, Impacted teeth

Background

Dental anomalies (DA) are defined as abnormal variations in the size, number, shape, position, eruption and structure of teeth resulting from disturbances during tooth development [1, 2]. These anomalies can be congenital, developmental or acquired and may be associated with various syndromes or systemic diseases or they may present as isolated defects independent of these conditions [3, 4].

DA can significantly impact the aesthetic appearance of teeth, occlusion, patients’ psychological well-being, quality of life, and self-esteem [5–7]. Children with DA may experience malocclusion, difficulties with chewing and speech, periodontal problems, and temporomandibular joint pain. Additionally, anomalous teeth can obstruct the eruption of normal teeth and lead to serious complications such as cyst formation or root resorption in adjacent teeth. These complications can complicate extractions, as well as restorative, endodontic, or prosthetic procedures [8, 9]. Furthermore, these anomalies are often asymptomatic and may present as incidental clinical or radiographic findings. Therefore, enabling dentists to detect DA early through comprehensive radiographic evaluations is crucial for simplifying complex interventions and ensuring optimal patient management [10].

The prevalence and distribution of DA vary among different populations and ethnic groups. Studies conducted across various populations provide valuable insights for genetic research and enhance our understanding of the environmental and genetic factors influencing these anomalies both within and between populations [11]. Furthermore, understanding the frequency of anomalies in a specific population enables clinicians to shape treatment approaches and develop effective preventive strategies. Although numerous studies have assessed the prevalence and distribution of DA in different countries and regions, considerable variations have been reported across populations, likely reflecting differences in genetic, environmental, and ethnic factors [1, 2, 10–14]. Regional differences have also been reported within Türkiye, highlighting the need for region-specific data. Although a previous study in Sivas investigated DA in primary teeth, information regarding permanent teeth among children and adolescents in this region is still lacking [15]. Such data may contribute to the existing epidemiological literature by providing region-specific evidence and allowing comparisons with other populations in Türkiye and worldwide and supporting regional oral health planning and integration of the findings into broader epidemiological evidence. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the prevalence and distribution of DA according to gender and jaw in the permanent teeth among children and adolescents attending the University Dental Hospital in Sivas, Türkiye, using digital panoramic radiographs and to compare these findings with those reported from other regions of Türkiye and worldwide populations.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was designed as a cross-sectional study and conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) protocol [16]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Sivas Cumhuriyet University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee with permission number 2024/03–17. Written informed consent and age-appropriate assent were obtained from parents/legal guardians and children, respectively, in accordance with local institutional ethical requirements. The ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed.

Sample size and selection

This study was based on the radiographic and clinical records of pediatric patients aged 8 to 15 years who attended Sivas Cumhuriyet University Faculty of Dentistry between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023 (a three-year period). The source population comprised 28,883 patients who attended the Department of Pediatric Dentistry during this period. To estimate the prevalence of DA, the minimum sample size was calculated assuming a 3% margin of error (e = 0.03), a 95% confidence level (z = 1.96), and maximum variability (p = 0.5), yielding a required sample of 1,030 radiographic images [17].

For the current study, the inclusion criteria were as follows: patients in the mixed or permanent dentition stages, aged between 8 and 15 years. The age range of 8 to 15 years was chosen because, by the age of 8, the crown calcification of all permanent teeth except the third molars can be reliably identified on panoramic radiographs, thereby minimizing false-positive diagnoses of tooth agenesis [18]. The upper age limit of 15 years was selected because this age represents the upper limit of the pediatric patient population routinely treated in our Department of Pediatric Dentistry. Radiographic quality was assessed prior to inclusion in the study. A radiograph was considered acceptable when it demonstrated adequate density and contrast, full coverage of both dental arches, and correct patient positioning without significant rotation, tilt, or magnification errors. Images exhibiting motion artefacts, overlapping structures, or any form of distortion that could obscure the visualization of tooth crowns or roots were excluded from the analysis. For patients with multiple panoramic radiographs, the first radiograph was selected [10]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: all patients with incomplete records (including radiographs and clinical notes), patients with a history of previous trauma and tooth loss due to trauma, patients undergoing orthodontic treatment, patients with tooth loss resulting from caries or periodontal disease, and patients with craniofacial malformations (e.g., cleft lip/palate) or syndromic conditions. To reduce potential confounding related to ethnic differences in the prevalence of dental anomalies, non-Turkish patients were excluded. Third molars were also excluded.

The IT department of Sivas Cumhuriyet University Faculty of Dentistry generated a spreadsheet (Microsoft 365 Excel) containing the case numbers of patients within the predefined age range. Simple random sampling was then performed to select potential study participants. Subsequently, the selected clinical records and digital panoramic radiographs were reviewed by a single calibrated examiner to determine eligibility according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria and to assess radiographic quality. Records meeting any exclusion criterion or presenting inadequate radiographic quality were excluded and replaced by newly randomly selected cases from the remaining eligible pool until the target sample size of 1,030 panoramic radiographs was reached.

Calibration

The assessment of DA was conducted by a pediatric dentist with ten years of clinical experience. Prior to the main evaluation, a pilot study was performed using 100 panoramic radiographs that were not included in the final dataset. For calibration purposes, the radiographs were independently assessed by both a dentomaxillofacial radiologist and the pediatric dentist. To determine intra- and inter-observer reliability, the observers repeated the evaluation two weeks later under identical viewing conditions. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to assess reliability. The intra-observer and inter-observer kappa coefficients were 0.91 and 0.87, respectively. Any discrepancies identified during the calibration phase were resolved by consensus before the commencement of the main study.

Digital panoramic radiography assessment

All radiographs were obtained using the same X-ray machine (Instrumentarium OP 200D, Instrumentarium Dental Tuusula, Finland) and following standard protocols. All digital panoramic radiographs were evaluated on a Dell OptiPlex (Dell Technologies) desktop computer equipped with a 64-bit Windows 7 (Microsoft Corporation) operating system, an Intel i5 3 GHz processor, 8 GB of RAM, an Intel HD Graphics 4600 graphics card, and a monitor with a resolution of 1600 × 900 (32-bit) using Cliniview software (version 10.12.1).

Simple image processing techniques, including adjustments to brightness, contrast and sharpness were utilized to enhance the accuracy of image interpretation. All assessments were conducted under standardized lighting conditions, consistent screen brightness, and uniform resolution. Data, encompassing age, gender and medical history were recorded on a customized data collection sheet. DA was evaluated by categorizing the images into four distinct groups: number, size, shape, and position, as defined in previous studies [11, 12, 19, 20].

Number

Tooth agenesis was defined as the absence of evidence of crown calcification on radiographs, considering the chronological age of tooth eruption. Hypodontia was defined as the absence of one to six teeth, while the absence of six or more teeth was classified as oligodontia. Teeth that exceed the normal dentition, regardless of whether they were impacted or erupted are classified as supernumerary teeth, and supernumerary teeth located between the central incisors referred to as mesiodens [11, 18, 19].

Size

Microdontia and macrodontia were determined by comparing the mesiodistal crown dimensions of the tooth in question with those of the contralateral and adjacent teeth, as well as with established normal tooth size references. Teeth were classified as microdontic when they were distinctly smaller and as macrodontic when they were distinctly larger than the reference standards [11].

Shape

The division of a tooth germ into two, resulting in a normal number of teeth in the arch, was considered gemination. The fusion of two tooth germs to form a single tooth, resulting in a decrease in the number of teeth in the arch, was considered fusion. Abnormal angulation or sharp curvature of the tooth root was considered root dilaceration; vertical extension of the pulp chamber was considered taurodontism; an accessory cusp-like structure originating from the lingual surface of anterior teeth was considered talon cusp [11, 21].

Position

The presence of displacement of two adjacent teeth was considered as transposition, the eruption of a tooth in an abnormal position was considered as ectopic eruption, a tooth whose eruption in normal functional occlusion was prevented by bone, soft tissues or other teeth as impacted tooth, additionally the developmentally reversed position of the tooth germ as inversion [4, 10, 19].

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Science version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for data analysis. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations, while categorical variables are presented as percentages and frequencies. The chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were employed to assess the differences in the prevalence of each anomaly concerning gender and jaw localization. The significance level was set at 0.05.

Results

A total of 1,030 panoramic radiographs that met the inclusion criteria were included in the study, while 72 records were excluded based on the predefined exclusion criteria. Among the subjects, 53.6% (n = 552) were female, and 46.4% (n = 478) were male. The mean age of the participants was 10.6 ± 2.2 years.

In the present study, the prevalence of DA, was found to be 20.7% (n = 213). Among the 213 patients diagnosed with DA, 93% exhibited a single anomaly, while 7% had two or more anomalies. In total, 230 DA were reported. The mean age of children with DA was 10.88 ± 2.32 years, compared to 10.57 ± 2.28 years for children without DA.

The prevalence of various types of DA is presented in Fig. 1. The most common DA was tooth agenesis (including hypodontia and oligodontia), followed by ectopic eruption, root dilaceration, impacted teeth, taurodontism, microdontia and supernumerary teeth. The least common DA were inversion, gemination, macrodontia and talon cusp. Transposition and fusion anomalies were not observed in the current study.

Discussion

Studies on the prevalence of DA in various populations offer valuable insights for genetic research and enhance our understanding of the environmental and genetic factors that influence these anomalies both within and across populations. Early diagnosis of DA is essential for preventing potential complications and facilitating timely treatment. Panoramic radiographic examinations provide a simple method for detecting DA, particularly those that may be missed during routine oral examinations [11]. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence and distribution of DA according to gender and jaw in permanent teeth of children and adolescents attending the University Dental Hospital in Sivas, Türkiye and to compare the findings with reports from other regions of Türkiye and worldwide populations.

The prevalence of DA in the study sample was 20.7%. Lagana et al. reported a similar DA prevalence of 20.9% in their study conducted in Italy involving patients aged 8 to 12 years [12]. Alanzi et al. found a DA prevalence of 20.1% in their study conducted in Kuwait on children aged 8 to 12 years [10]. Slightly higher DA prevalence was reported in studies involving Nigerian (26.6%) and Indian (25.8%) children [8, 22]. Conversely, Dang et al. reported a lower DA prevalence in the Australian population [19]. The differing results obtained in studies examining DA in the literature may be attributed to genetic disorders, environmental factors and ethnic backgrounds. Additionally, study design, sample size, age group and selection criteria may also influence the results.

The most common DA was tooth agenesis, occurring in 9.7% of cases. Tooth agenesis, a numerical anomaly, is also the most frequently reported DA in the literature [14, 23]. Mahjoub et al. reported a higher prevalence of hypodontia in their study conducted in Saudi Arabia compared to our study, which may be attributed to their inclusion of a broader age range and third molars [24]. Alanzi et al. found a similar prevalence of hypodontia (9.3%) to our study, while Bilge et al. reported a lower prevalence (5.5%) [1, 10]. Tooth agenesis has been shown to vary across populations and ethnic groups, and its distribution pattern differs geographically. Mandibular second premolars and maxillary lateral incisors are consistently reported as the most frequently missing permanent teeth in different populations [25]. In the present study, the mandibular second premolars were the most commonly missing teeth. Similarly, studies conducted in the Turkish population have also reported that mandibular second premolars exhibit the highest prevalence of agenesis [26–28]. No significant gender difference was observed in the prevalence of hypodontia in the present study. Although some studies have reported a higher prevalence in females [29, 30], the literature suggests that gender distribution may vary depending on the severity of hypodontia. Supporting this, Kanchanasevee et al. reported a higher female predominance with increasing number of missing teeth [31]. Consistently, in our study, 75% of oligodontia cases were observed in females.

Esenlik et al. reported a prevalence of supernumerary teeth of 2.7% in their study of the Turkish population [32]. The prevalence of supernumerary teeth has been shown to vary between 0.2% and 4.25% in the literature [1, 12, 14, 23]. The present study found a prevalence of 1.1%, which is consistent with these reported values. Furthermore, the maxillary arch (81.8%) exhibited a higher frequency of supernumerary teeth compared to the mandibular arch (18.2%). This finding is also consistent with previous studies [12, 33, 34].

The second most common DA identified in this study was ectopic eruption, a positional anomaly, with a prevalence of 3.3%. Although there was no significant difference in the prevalence of ectopic eruption between genders, it was found to be significantly more common in the maxillary arch than in the mandibular arch. Alanzi et al. reported a prevalence of ectopic eruption similar to our findings (2%) [10], while Bodrumlu et al. found a higher prevalence (9.2%) [14]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in study populations, age distributions, sampling methods, and diagnostic criteria. The prevalence of impacted teeth, another positional anomaly, was found to be 1.8%. Saberi and Ebrahimipour reported a slightly higher prevalence of impacted teeth in their study of the Iranian population (3.4%) [4]. Among other positional anomalies in the current study, transposition was not observed, while inversion was reported in one individual. Variations in prevalence rates reported in the literature may be attributed to ethnic differences, as well as differences in study design and diagnostic methods.

The third most common dental anomaly was root dilaceration, a shape anomaly, with a prevalence of 2.8%. Although it was more common in females and in the maxilla, the difference was not statistically significant. Aldowsari et al., in their study of pediatric patients in Saudi Arabia, reported a slightly lower prevalence of root dilaceration (0.75%) compared to our findings [35]. In contrast, Wagner et al. found a considerably higher prevalence of root dilaceration (38%) [11]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in diagnostic thresholds, classification criteria, or the characteristics of the sampled population.

The prevalence of taurodontism, another shape anomaly, was 1.2% and was significantly more common in males. Alanzi et al. reported a higher prevalence of taurodontism (6.6%) [10], while Lagana et al. reported a lower prevalence (0.04%) [12]. Neither study found a significant difference in taurodontism prevalence between genders. Aldowsari et al. reported a prevalence of taurodontism similar to our findings (1.66%), which was more common in males, although the difference was not statistically significant [35]. Kathariya et al. also reported that the prevalence of taurodontism was significantly higher in males [22].

Among other shape anomalies, the prevalence of gemination and talon cusp, was 0.2% and 0.7%, respectively, with no significant differences between genders. Fusion anomaly was not observed. Jain et al., in their study of shape anomalies in the Indian population, reported prevalences of 0.03% for gemination and 2.23% for talon cusp, also noting no significant gender differences [36]. These findings suggest that the prevalence of shape anomalies varies across populations and sample sizes. Moreover, these anomalies are clinically important and should be considered in endodontic and orthodontic treatment planning.

In this study, the prevalence of macrodontia and microdontia was found to be 0.3% and 1.2%, respectively. These size anomalies were significantly more common in the maxilla. Previous studies in the literature, consistent with our findings, have reported that these anomalies are less common compared to other DA and primarily affect the maxillary incisors [10, 11, 24, 35, 37].

The literature reports varying results regarding the prevalence of DA in the pediatric populations. Differences in study design, inclusion criteria, and ethnicity may contribute to this variation. Studies focusing specifically on orthodontic patients or individuals with particular syndromes may produce more conflicting findings due to an increased risk of selection bias. This study investigated non-syndromic DA in the pediatric population using digital panoramic radiographs to minimize selection bias. Furthermore, our study included randomly selected pediatric patients presenting for routine examinations, thereby providing more reliable results that better represent the study population.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As the study was conducted in a single university dental hospital, the results may not be fully generalizable to the general pediatric population and should therefore be interpreted within the context of a hospital-based sample. The combined use of clinical examination and radiographic evaluation allows for a more precise and reliable diagnosis of many DA. However, due to the design of the present study, patients did not undergo intraoral examinations. To avoid compromising data quality, anomalies such as amelogenesis imperfecta, dentinogenesis imperfecta, dens invaginatus, and dens evaginatus—which may be overlooked in radiographic assessments—were not evaluated. This limitation may have led to an underrepresentation of certain DA in the sample. Future studies should integrate radiographic screening with direct clinical examinations to enhance diagnostic accuracy and enable a more comprehensive evaluation of DA.

Another limitation of this study is the use of panoramic radiographs, which provide two-dimensional images of three-dimensional structures, increasing the risk of misinterpretation or overdiagnosis. Advanced imaging techniques, such as intraoral radiographs or cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), offer greater accuracy, particularly for detailed morphological assessments. However, due to radiation exposure concerns in the pediatric patient group and the fact that CBCT was performed only, when necessary, the opportunity to include a large population is limited. Therefore, this study utilized digital panoramic radiographs, which can be applied to large samples without introducing selection bias when determining the prevalence of DA.

In addition, some studies suggest that anomalies of third molars should be regarded as natural variations reflecting the evolutionary adaptation process rather than DA [11, 38]. Therefore, third molars that could significantly influence the prevalence of DA were excluded from the current study. In future studies, inclusion and exclusion criteria should be determined meticulously, and standardized criteria should be adopted for DA radiographic diagnosis to minimize the diversity caused by the diagnostic criteria to be used.

Conclusion

Despite its limitations, this study demonstrated a relatively high prevalence of DA among the study population of Sivas, Türkiye. The most frequently observed anomaly was tooth agenesis, followed by ectopic eruption, root dilaceration, impacted teeth, and taurodontism. Although the overall prevalence of DA did not differ significantly between genders, taurodontism was found to be more common in males. These findings provide descriptive information on the distribution of dental anomalies in a hospital-based pediatric population and may serve as a baseline information for future studies in similar settings.
