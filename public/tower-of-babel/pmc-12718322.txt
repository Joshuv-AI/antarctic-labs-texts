Can speed cameras make streets safer? Quasi-experimental evidence from New York City
Authors: Aaron Stagoff-Belfort, Jonathan Ben-Menachem, Brenden Beck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMany US cities are turning to automated speed cameras as a promising alternative to traditional traffic enforcement by police, aiming to reduce collisions without the risks of racial bias or harmful police encounters. But how effective are they? This study evaluates the largest automated speed enforcement program in the United States—2,000 cameras deployed across New York City from 2014 to 2023. Leveraging data on 700,000 collisions and 18 million speeding tickets, the analysis finds that cameras lead to persistent and sustained reductions in crashes. These results demonstrate that automated enforcement can meaningfully improve road safety while avoiding the harms associated with conventional traffic safety approaches.

Automobile crashes cause approximately 40,000 deaths and two million injuries each year in the United States, generating an estimated $340 billion in economic costs (1, 2). Motor vehicle injuries rank third among the leading causes of preventable injury related-mortality; by contrast, homicides account for roughly half as many deaths each year (3, 4). The US motor vehicle crash death rate is also the highest among 29 high-income countries (5). Black pedestrians and cyclists are, respectively, two and four times more likely than their white counterparts to be killed in traffic crashes (6, 7). These disparities highlight the unequal burden of motor vehicle crashes. Taken together with the scale and persistence of traffic deaths overall, these patterns underscore the urgency of effective traffic safety policies as a core public health priority.

Traffic enforcement has historically been the responsibility of local law enforcement agencies. After motor vehicle crash rates ballooned in the early 20th century, the scope and reach of police surveillance in everyday life expanded dramatically (8). Traffic stops are now the most common form of contact between police and the public, and these encounters have expanded the scope of racially biased policing, as officers conduct so-called pretextual stops for minor violations that are often unrelated to road safety or crash prevention (9–11). Police traffic enforcement carries a host of collateral consequences, and research finds that routine police stops can negatively affect mental health, political participation, and civic engagement (12–16). Having armed officers conduct routine traffic enforcement means that these interactions can sometimes turn fatal. For example, the police killings of Philando Castile and Daunte Wright occurred following traffic stops (17). Police traffic enforcement may also foster perverse incentives, sometimes known as “policing for profit,” where police issue more tickets irrespective of motorist behavior to generate municipal revenue for cash-strapped local governments. This practice subverts the mission of traffic safety and functions as a regressive tax that can trap drivers in cycles of debt, disproportionately impacting low-income drivers who are more likely to operate older cars with minor vehicle issues (18–21).

Automated speed cameras have become an increasingly popular alternative to police enforcement. They impose uniform penalties for dangerous driving while potentially avoiding many of the collateral consequences of policing. A National Academies report recommended automated enforcement as a promising safety intervention, finding that speed cameras reduce crashes across numerous local case studies, while also emphasizing gaps in knowledge and the need for more rigorous evaluation (22). Indeed, few studies to date have employed credible causal inference methods to assess the effect of traffic cameras on road safety.

Prior evaluations largely find that automated traffic enforcement reduces speeding and collisions (23, 24). However, existing research is hampered by key methodological limitations. Many studies employ naive before–after comparisons, empirical Bayes, or interrupted time series designs (25, 26). These approaches may impose very strong, arguably implausible assumptions to identify causal effects (27). Some recent studies advance improved research designs that account for endogenous treatment timing and formally test causal identification assumptions through more explicit counterfactual modeling. This body of work produces mixed results (28–31). Further research is needed in order to estimate the causal effect of automated traffic enforcement on road safety.

This study presents a quasi-experimental evaluation of automated speed camera implementation in New York City between 2014 and 2023. We leverage the staggered rollout of 1,868 cameras in school speed zones across New York City to estimate the causal effect of automated enforcement on traffic collisions and injuries.

Using a difference-in-differences design, we compare outcomes at treated intersections (those with cameras) to those at intersections slated for treatment at a later date (not-yet-treated units). Because the city did not install all the cameras at once, some intersections received cameras earlier while others were scheduled for later installation. This staggered rollout allows for a plausible counterfactual comparison: At a given point in time, we compare changes in crashes at intersections with newly activated cameras to changes at similar intersections that had not yet received them. This approach isolates the effect of speed cameras by ensuring that we are comparing structurally similar locations and helps us separate the effect of cameras from broader citywide trends such as seasonal traffic patterns, concurrent traffic safety interventions and campaigns, and disruptions like the COVID-19 pandemic or extreme weather. For further discussion of this methodology, see our Supplementary Materials.

We find that cameras reduce total collisions by approximately 5% per month in 900-foot circular buffer zones around cameras, compounding to a 30% reduction during the seven months following their activation. Injuries fall by 2.5% per month in these buffer zones, totaling a 16% reduction over seven months. The largest effects were observed in Manhattan and Brooklyn and in buffer zones with higher baseline traffic volume and greater enforcement intensity. Speeding violations dropped sharply beginning in month two, suggesting rapid behavioral adaptation to automated enforcement.

Results

New York City’s Automated Speed Enforcement Program.

New York City operates the most expansive network of automated speed enforcement cameras in the United States (32). The program was initially authorized in August 2013 as a 5-y pilot program in 20 school speed zones (33). By law, the city can only install fixed cameras within designated school speed zones: defined as streets abutting a school or within a quarter-mile of school property. The city must also post notices indicating a camera is in use. As of 2023, there were about 2,300 school speed zones in New York City and approximately two-thirds of New York City’s streets were eligible for speed camera placement—including 87% of streets in Manhattan, 82% in Brooklyn, and 74% in the Bronx (34). Speed limit signs are posted ahead of all camera sites. Speed camera locations are selected by the NYC Department of Transportation based on an analysis of vehicle speeds, road design, and crash history in a given school speed zone.

Cameras issue speeding violations when a radar unit detects that a driver is traveling more than 10 mph above the posted speed limit. The camera captures an image of the vehicle and its license plate, but not a photograph that can identify the driver. DOT staff technicians then review and verify the image, and if the violation is confirmed, a Notice of Liability is mailed to the vehicle’s registered owner. Violations carry a flat $50 fine with no points or administrative fees, regardless of how far above the speed limit a driver travels. These fines are significantly lower than those issued by police officers, do not escalate with repeated offenses, and are not factored into insurance premiums or license suspension decisions. By contrast, police-issued speeding summonses can range from $90 to $600 depending on the driver’s speed, plus an $88 state surcharge and a penalty of 3 to 11 points on the driver’s license (35). However, failure to pay speeding tickets from automated traffic cameras can lead to late fees. If the violation enters judgment, motorists are subject to further penalties such as booting, towing, or license suspension. The first camera-based violation was issued in January 2014. In March 2019, the New York State Assembly reauthorized and expanded the program to 750 new school zones (36). Fig. 1 illustrates the staggered implementation of the automated enforcement program. By the end of 2023, our sample included 1,868 automated speed enforcement cameras installed across New York City. Parallel Trends Analysis.

First, we formally assess the plausibility of the parallel trends assumption, that absent treatment (camera activation), crash trends would be the same across treatment and control groups. In our context, we examine whether buffer zones treated earlier resemble those treated later. We estimate an event study model in Fig. 3 that generates monthly estimates of the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) by event time in the 12 mo before and 7 mo after a camera was installed within each buffer zone. These dynamic difference-in-difference (DiD) estimates enable visual inspection of both pretrends and posttreatment effects. The ATT estimates hover near zero in the pretreatment period, while after activation, we observe consistent declines in collisions and injuries over the 7-mo posttreatment window. These results are consistent with the assumptions of the DiD framework and support a causal interpretation of the reduced-form estimates from Eq. 1 (Materials and Methods).

Camera Enforcement and Traffic Safety Outcomes.

We present estimated average treatment effects for treated buffer zones (ATT) from our quasi-experimental specifications (i.e., Eq. 1) for both collisions and injuries, using log-transformed outcomes and reporting the corresponding 95% CI. Our models estimate that cameras reduced collisions by an average of 5% per month during the first seven months after installation (95% CI: −6.8 to −3.2%), amounting to a 30% cumulative reduction over this posttreatment window. Injuries declined by an average of 2.5% per month over the same period (95% CI: −4.3 to −0.6%), yielding a 16% cumulative reduction. For both traffic safety outcomes, the largest effects were observed between three and six months posttreatment.*

In our Supplementary Materials, we present additional analyses that explore the sensitivity and robustness of our findings: the potential confounding impact of the COVID-19 pandemic (SI Appendix, Table S5 and Fig. S1), robustness to buffer zones of different sizes (SI Appendix, Table S6), alternative posttreatment window lengths (SI Appendix, Table S7), models with covariates (SI Appendix, Table S3), a placebo test (SI Appendix, Table S8 and Fig. S2), and tests for spillover and interference (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Notably, effects stabilize between months 6 and 10, suggesting a plateau within our observation window; we refrain from extrapolating beyond this time horizon, and future work could analyze how these effects persist over longer periods (SI Appendix, Table S7).

Camera Enforcement and Effect Heterogeneity.

To investigate treatment effect heterogeneity, we disaggregated the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) estimates across three dimensions: borough, enforcement intensity, and baseline pretreatment traffic volume (SI Appendix, Tables S9–S12). Brooklyn and Manhattan exhibited the largest reductions in collisions and injuries, while Staten Island and the Bronx showed the smallest effects, likely reflecting differences in population density and traffic volume. While we cannot measure a precise dose–response relationship in our DiD model, we ran specifications that were stratified by enforcement intensity, measured using the number of camera-issued speeding violations. Treatment effects tended to be larger in areas with higher enforcement intensity. Areas with more violations—whether total, within the first three months, or within the first six months—saw greater reductions in collisions and injuries. Buffers with higher average traffic volume in the six months prior to camera installation also experienced larger safety benefits.

Camera Enforcement and Driver Behavior.

We also examined trends in the number of camera-issued speeding violations following treatment to explore whether changes in driver behavior may underlie the observed reductions in collisions. Violations spiked immediately following activation, then declined rapidly: a 26.5% drop from the first to second month and a 47.7% drop from baseline by the fifth month (SI Appendix, Table S13 and Figs. S4 and S5). These sharp reductions point to rapid behavioral adaptation to camera enforcement (less speeding) even with a modest, flat $50 fine. This finding suggests that effective deterrence does not require harsh or escalating penalties. In fact, when a small penalty is applied consistently, it may reduce both the need for high fines and ease the disproportionate financial burden of enforcement on low-income drivers (i.e., “swift, certain, and fair” deterrence).

Camera Enforcement, Race, and Income.

Prior investigations have found that traffic cameras were disproportionately located in Black and Hispanic neighborhoods in Washington D.C. but evenly distributed in Chicago and New York City (37–39). We performed an analysis for the year 2022, finding that buffer zones with cameras had a slightly higher proportion of Black residents compared to the city overall (25 vs. 21%, P < 0.001 in a one-sample t test) and a lower proportion of Hispanic residents (20 vs. 29%, P < 0.001). Additionally, buffer zones had a lower poverty rate than the citywide average (12 vs. 14%, P < 0.001) (SI Appendix, Table S14). Since cameras are sited near schools and major arterial roads, and these are unevenly distributed across the city, the observed demographic differences may reflect underlying variation in the distribution of schools and street infrastructure. However, recent literature has found that the racial demographics of drivers and the residential population in a neighborhood are highly correlated (40).

Camera Enforcement and Spillovers.

Our design, like other implementations of the Callaway and Sant’Anna estimator, assumes no spillovers of camera effects or driving behavior across buffer zones that could contaminate untreated units. Conceptually, spillovers could cut in two directions: Drivers might shift speeding to untreated corridors to avoid cameras, reducing program benefits outside treated zones, or cameras might deter speeding more broadly by promoting generalized deterrence, which would make our estimates conservative. While current software does not allow us to test for spillover in our staggered difference-in-difference framework, we estimate alternative correlation (i.e., noncausal) models that incorporate spatial weights measuring associations between cameras and outcomes in nearby buffers. Our main findings are robust to the inclusion of spatial weights and suggest cameras promote generalized caution rather than displaced speeding. Greater camera activity in one buffer is associated with fewer collisions in nearby buffers and shows no relationship with injuries. (See SI Appendix, Fig. S3 and the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials for further discussion).

Discussion

This study finds that the largest automated traffic enforcement program in the United States led to substantial improvements in road safety. In the seven months following the implementation of speed cameras, treated buffer zones experienced an average 5% monthly reduction in collisions (a 30% cumulative decline) and a 2.5% average monthly reduction in injuries (a 16% cumulative decline). Notably, speeding violations dropped in buffers by nearly 50% within five months, even as the greatest safety gains occurred between the third and sixth months, suggesting that cameras effectively reduce dangerous driving and prevent crashes. Vehicle speed is perhaps the critical factor in determining whether a collision leads to injury or death. The risk that a pedestrian will be killed doubles when struck by a car traveling 30 miles-per-hour compared to 25 miles-per-hour (41). Our findings are consistent with the idea that automated enforcement improves road safety by targeting a key mechanism, reducing vehicle speeds, and turning potentially fatal or serious crashes into survivable or noninjury collisions. These results are robust across multiple model specifications, estimation strategies, and sensitivity checks.

Our findings challenge the claim from prior research that appropriately identified causal inference research designs tend to eliminate the observed safety benefits of automated enforcement (28). To the contrary, we observe treatment effects that are large, durable over the course of half a year, and robust to various model specifications.

Our study is not without limitations. While we leverage a large dataset spanning nearly a decade, our analysis is confined to a single city, limiting the generalizability of our findings to jurisdictions with different camera implementation strategies, siting practices, traffic volume, enforcement policies, or street infrastructure. Additionally, our data on collisions are based on incidents reported to the police, which might undercount the true number of crashes. However, because insurance claims typically require police documentation, this reporting bias is likely smaller than in other types of police-generated data. As such, the collision counts we analyze should be interpreted as conservative estimates. Because New York City’s camera program is limited to school zones, it is possible that these intersections are systematically different from other road segments. Nonetheless, the vast majority of New York City streets fall within the legal boundaries of the program, suggesting broader generalizability within the city context. Another potential concern is that camera sites might have had rising traffic volumes relative to comparison sites. In the Supplementary Materials, we show baseline balance on average traffic levels and find program effects across all traffic strata, with larger effects at higher-volume sites. Results are also unchanged when average traffic is included as a covariate in our DiD models. Taken together with parallel pretrends in crashes and injuries and time fixed effects, these checks suggest that traffic growth is unlikely to bias our findings; if anything, it would make them more conservative.

A practical constraint of automated enforcement is that license plate–based systems can sometimes be evaded through false, obscured, or stolen plates. A 2024 audit by the New York City Comptroller’s Office characterized this type of evasion as a “worsening problem,” estimating $108 million in lost revenue in the prior year (42). This does not bias our crash- and injury-based estimates, but it does highlight that our results capture the effectiveness of New York City’s program under real-world operating conditions, rather than a theoretical best-case setting where every violation is detected. Ongoing policy efforts such as curbing the black market for fraudulent temporary license plates, enacting stricter plate visibility requirements, improving temporary tag systems, and contracting for better plate readability could increase speed camera efficacy.

Further research should continue to investigate how automated traffic enforcement performs compared to human police officers across a broad set of outcomes, including its effectiveness in improving road safety, whether it reduces racial and economic disparities, and whether it exacerbates concerns related to civil liberties and surveillance. Automated traffic enforcement has tremendous potential to reduce racial disparities in traffic stops and fines by removing police discretion and human bias (9, 43, 44). By design, automated cameras enforce speed limits uniformly, without regard for a driver’s racial identity, gender, or socioeconomic status.

At the same time, the equity implications of camera siting are more complex. Even if this layer of human-generated disparities is removed, critics have raised concerns that automated enforcement may still produce racially inequitable outcomes if cameras are disproportionately sited in communities of color, increasing financial burdens on minority drivers. In our case, we find suggestive evidence that New York City’s automated speed enforcement program is disproportionately concentrated in Black (although not Hispanic or poor) neighborhoods. It is difficult to draw firm conclusions from this finding, as it may reflect the geography of traffic crashes or school speed zones across the city. Prior research suggests that Black neighborhoods experience disproportionately high crash rates due to faulty road construction and historical disinvestment in transportation infrastructure (45–47). Therefore, automated enforcement may mitigate existing racial inequities by reducing police involvement in routine traffic enforcement and by lowering crash risks that disproportionately affect Black residents (48). At the same time, if cameras are concentrated in communities of color, they may produce unequal financial burdens or traffic safety provision across neighborhoods.

Furthermore, our finding that there was a sharp decline in violations issued beginning in the second month postinstallation suggests that the cameras effectively deter speeding even as citation volume falls. This indicates that the intervention deters speeding by changing underlying driver behavior rather than relying on sustained revenue extraction. Given that nearly one-third of traffic deaths involve speeding as a contributing factor, the general deterrence effect of automated enforcement offers a promising avenue to improve traffic safety while minimizing the collateral social costs of monetary sanctions (49).

Future research should investigate the mechanisms through which automated enforcement improves traffic safety. While reduced vehicle speeds are likely the primary channel, automated enforcement likely works through multiple pathways. Citations can induce individual learning, and awareness of camera locations—through public postings, peer communication, and navigation apps such as Waze—may also generate social-norm and deterrence effects that shape broader driving culture. At the same time, the persistent plateau of violations after several months suggests heterogeneity in responsiveness. It may be the case that most drivers’ behavior was changed, but a smaller subset of speeders, such as those unaware of camera placement or those most prone to high-risk driving, remain undeterred. Future studies should examine this possibility through mediation analyses incorporating driver characteristics.

Our results provide compelling evidence for an intervention to equitably address one of the nation’s leading public safety challenges. Automated speeding enforcement can serve as a central pillar of effective traffic safety regimes: reducing vehicle crashes, promoting changes to driver behavior, and mitigating the negative consequences of relying solely on police to manage road safety.

Materials and Methods

Data.

We assess New York City’s automated enforcement program by geocoding and combining publicly available administrative data from four sources: data on Parking Violations Issued from the New York City Department of Finance and data on Motor Vehicle Collisions from the NYPD, both accessed via the City’s OpenData Portal; average annual daily traffic data from the New York State Department of Transportation; and the American Community Survey five-year census tract estimates. The data from New York City contain records of every motor vehicle collision reported to the police in between January 2012 and December 2024, and every parking and traffic violation issued by the city between January 2014 and September 2023, the last month for which violation data were publicly available at the time of our analysis. We use the terms “tickets” and “violations” synonymously. We constructed circular buffer zones using 900-foot diameters around each camera. The final analytic sample includes 234,495 buffer-month observations between January 2012 and September 2023, covering 1,868 speed enforcement cameras and encompassing 18,633,432 traffic violations. During this period, the dataset includes 783,874 vehicle collisions and 239,219 injuries.

Automated Ticket Data.

New York City does not share the locations of their automated speed enforcement cameras. We determined camera locations from the intersections cited in speeding tickets. We merged yearly files for all parking violations issued in New York City between January 1, 2014, and September 1, 2023, focusing specifically on the violation code associated with speed camera tickets. This allowed us to isolate and identify the universe of automated speed enforcement incidents. To identify unique camera locations, we created a cleaned intersection field by standardizing and formatting the address data based on unique combinations of the two intersecting street names and the borough associated with each violation. This approach allowed us to consolidate violations issued by the same camera across time and eliminate duplicate or inconsistently formatted entries.

Most camera address records between 2014 and 2016 were missing county (borough) information, approximately 21% of all observations. We addressed this through a three-step imputation process. First, we used cross-year matching: If a 2014–2016 address matched a later-year violation that included county data, we transferred the known borough back to the earlier observation. For the remaining cases, we conducted multiborough geocoding, assigning each ambiguous intersection to five hypothetical boroughs (The Bronx, Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, and Staten Island). We constructed full address strings (intersection + borough + “New York, NY”) and geocoded them. For each camera ID, we calculated the number of distinct latitude–longitude pairs returned. If a particular borough assignment consistently yielded a unique set of coordinates, we accepted that match; if multiple boroughs returned coordinates or none did, the record was flagged for manual review, and we hand-coded county information for problematic rows where automated methods failed.

Constructing Camera Buffer Zones.

We then conducted a series of quality assurance steps, including dropping any addresses with fewer than 100 total violations, a threshold designed to ensure robust representation at each camera site. We retained 99.3% of the total speeding violations from the original dataset. About 75% of the violations we dropped were excluded due to missing or incomplete address information, with the remainder filtered out for issuing fewer than 100 violations. Our final count of unique camera sites closely matches official aggregate tallies reported by the City of New York. Finally, we geocoded all camera street addresses. All automated speed cameras in our dataset are located within school speed zones, as mandated by state law.

Using ArcGIS, we placed each camera’s location on a map of New York City streets using the Digital City Map Shapefile from the OpenData portal. We drew circular buffers with 900-foot diameters around each camera. We chose 900 feet because that is the approximate length of New York City’s “long” blocks, those that stretch between avenues. Because some buffers overlapped, we also drew Voronoi (i.e., nonoverlapping) polygons around each camera and clipped the buffers along the Voronoi polygon boundaries. Voronoi polygons draw lines between two or more adjacent buffers that maximize the area of each buffer. Studies with researcher-devised geographies like this one can be subject to the modifiable areal unit problem whereby results vary depending on the size and shape of the areas drawn by the researchers (50). To guard against this, we also drew 600-foot buffers and calculated results with that smaller geography. See Fig. 4 for an illustration of the buffers, clipped to avoid overlap.

Collision Data.

The collision data we used included all reported motor vehicle crashes in New York City from January 1, 2012, to September 1, 2023. These data capture a wide range of incidents involving collisions between motor vehicles, as well as crashes involving vehicles and pedestrians and vehicles and cyclists. Each record contains detailed information on the date, time, and location of the crash, along with counts of persons injured or killed, disaggregated by role (e.g., pedestrian, cyclist, motorist). We then merged collision and injury records with the appropriate camera buffer zone based on matching geographic coordinates. Further, 4.4% of the observations in the collisions dataset lacked latitude and longitude coordinates and could therefore not be geocoded using full street intersection information. To evaluate whether this introduced bias, we conducted balance checks and assessed whether excluded observations were systematically different from those in our analytic sample. We computed standardized mean differences (SMDs) for the number of collisions and injuries and found no meaningful differences.

Methods

Measuring Traffic Camera Effects on Collisions and Injuries.

We estimate the effect of traffic cameras on vehicle collisions and injuries using a staggered difference-in-differences (DiD) design and the doubly robust estimator developed by Callaway and Sant’Anna (51), available using the did package in R. This estimator addresses key limitations of traditional two-way fixed effects models as identified by recent difference-in-differences literature: specifically, bias arising from treatment effect heterogeneity and staggered adoption (52, 53). We leverage the staggered rollout of speeding cameras, comparing treated units to plausible counterfactuals. We compare treated buffer zones to those zones that will be treated at a later point in time (not-yet-treated), an approach that has become common in contemporary causal inference research (15, 28, 54). Even if treated and never-treated units systematically differ in some unobservable respect, our analysis reduces selection bias and ensures that treatment and control groups are more structurally similar, and therefore more plausible counterfactuals. As shown in SI Appendix, Table S16, each treatment cohort G = g consists of units first treated in calendar month g, resulting in 40 distinct cohorts containing between 10 and 90 treated buffers. At each event time, the control group comprises all units that have not yet been treated. This setup follows the standard implementation of the Callaway & Sant’Anna (51) estimator.

We measure outcomes using monthly panel data and 900- and 600- foot circular “buffer zones” surrounding each speeding camera. The unit of analysis is the buffer-month, i.e., the number of collisions or injuries observed in each buffer in a given month. We transform both of our outcomes prior to estimation using their natural logarithm. To assess the cumulative impact over the seven month study period, we compounded the average monthly treatment effect estimate to reflect how proportional reductions accumulate over time and to avoid overstating effects through simple linear extrapolation (55).

Typically, the estimand in DiD analyses is the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT); in this specific context, it is the group-time average treatment effect, which is the ATT indexed by group and time. The outcome variable is the log-transformed count, log1+Yit, where Yit is the observed count (e.g., collisions or injuries).

We visualize dynamic treatment effects in our event study figures, ATTe by event time: e=t-Gi, for every time e covering the window e ∈ [−12, 6]. We report the pooled ATT, a single aggregated estimate across the entire posttreatment window (e = 0 to e = 6).
