A just-infinite iterated monodromy group without the congruence subgroup property Thanks: The author is supported by Grigorchuk’s Simons Foundation Grant MP-TSM-00002045 and the department of Mathematics of Texas A&M University.
Santiago Radi

Abstract
. We prove that the iterated monodromy group of the polynomial z 2 + i z^{2}+i is just-infinite, regular branch and does not have the congruence subgroup property. This yields the first example of an iterated monodromy group of a polynomial with these properties. Additional information is provided about the congruence kernel, rigid kernel and branch kernel of this group.



1.
Introduction

Given a residually finite group
G
, one can fix a sequence of epimorphisms
{\left\{\varphi_{n}\right\}}_{n\geq 0}
from
G
onto finite groups such that the sequence of normal subgroups
{\left\{\ker(\varphi_{n})\right\}}_{n\geq 0}
is partially ordered with respect to the inclusion and we also have
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}\ker(\varphi_{n})=1
. Each subgroup in this sequence is called a principal congruence subgroup, and a subgroup of
G
is called a congruence subgroup if it contains a principal congruence subgroup.

The congruence subgroup problem asks whether every finite-index subgroup in
G
is a congruence subgroup. If this happens, we say that
G
has the congruence subgroup property with respect to a given sequence. For example, it is proved by Bass, Milnor and Serre that if
K
is a number field and
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is its ring of integers, then
\SL_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
has the congruence subgroup property whenever
n\geq 3
and it does not have the congruence subgroup property if
n=2
(see
[
5
]
).

As observed in
[
11
]
, every countable residually finite group can be embedded in the group of automorphisms of a spherically homogeneous infinite rooted tree
T
, which is denoted
\Aut(T)
. The group
\Aut(T)
is residually finite and the principal congruence subgroups used are the stabilizers of level
n
for each
n\geq 0
. The stabilizer of level
n
, denoted
\St(n)
, consists of all the elements that fix all the vertices at the level
n
of the tree. If
G
is a subgroup of
\Aut(T)
, we can consider
{\left\{\St(n)\cap G\right\}}_{n\geq 0}
as the principal congruence subgroups of
G
.

The study of the congruence subgroup property in groups acting on rooted trees (more specifically for branch groups) was initiated by Grigorchuk, Herfort and Zalesskii in
[
13
]
and since then, few examples of groups with or without the congruence subgroup property are known in the literature. In
[
12
]
, Grigorchuk proved that the group nowadays known as the first Grigorchuk group (see
[
10
]
) has the congruence subgroup property (see
[
12
, Proposition 10]
). In
[
7
]
, Fernández-Alcober, Garrido and Uria-Albizuri proved that the class of Grigorchuk-Gupta-Sidki (GGS) groups (see
[
17
]
) with non-constant defining vector also have the congruence subgroup property. On the other hand, Pervova constructed the first family of groups without the congruence subgroup property in
[
22
]
, by defining the groups EGS, a slightly different version of the GGS groups. Generalizing the result of Pervova, Thillaisundaram and Uria-Albizuri in
[
27
]
studied the multi-EGS groups (defined in
[
19
]
), classifying the cases where they do not have the congruence subgroup property. In
[
4
]
, Bartholdi, Siegenthaler and Zalesskii proved that the twisted twin of the Grigorchuk group and the Hanoi tower group (defined in
[
15
]
) do not have the congruence subgroup property. Finally, in
[
9
]
, Garrido proved that in the case of branch groups, the congruence subgroup property is independent of the branch action of the group.

Within the groups acting on trees, one important family is the family of iterated monodromy groups, used by Bartholdi and Nekrashevych to solve the Twisted Rabbit Problem in
[
3
]
. One celebrated example, and the main object of this article, is the iterated monodromy group of the polynomial
z^{2}+i
. This 3-generated group that acts on the binary tree was initially considered in
[
2
]
and it was proved in
[
6
]
that the group has intermediate growth. In
[
16
]
, authors proved that this group is regular branch (a particular case of branch group) over a maximal branching subgroup which we will denote
K
in this article, and found an
L
-presentation, i.e. a presentation of a group by generators and relations that involves a finite set of relators and their iterations by a substitution (see
[
21
]
). In
[
16
]
, it was also shown that the group exhibits interesting spectral properties. Recently, the authors in
[
18
]
study applications of this group (and other contracting groups) as a group-based cryptosystem.

Our main result is the following:

Theorem A
.

The group
\IMG(z^{2}+i)
is just-infinite and does not have the congruence subgroup property.

Recall that a group is just-infinite if every non-trivial quotient is finite. This constitutes the first example answering the congruence subgroup property of a just-infinite iterated monodromy group of a quadratic complex polynomial.

The properties just-infinite and congruence subgroup property are related in the case of finitely generated groups acting on trees. It was proved by Francoeur in
[
8
, Theorem 4.4.4]
that groups with the congruence subgroup property are just-infinite. In virtue of our result in
Theorem

A
, the group
\IMG(z^{2}+i)
constitutes a counter example to the converse of
[
8
, Theorem 4.4.4]
. This group is not the first counterexample of this kind, as the majority of the branch groups without the congruence subgroup property previously mentioned are also just-infinite.

The strategy in this article will be based on the maximal regular branching subgroup
K
and the
L
-presentation of
\IMG(z^{2}+i)
found in
[
16
]
. If we let
C_{m}
denote the cyclic group with order
m
, the steps are the following:

(1)

We find
S_{K}={\left\{x,y,z,w,t\right\}}
, a set of five generators of
K
where all the elements have order
4
. Therefore, we have
K/K^{\prime}\leq C_{4}^{5}
. As
\IMG(z^{2}+i)
is regular branch over
K
, this in particular implies that
\IMG(z^{2}+i)
is just-infinite by
[
12
, Theorem 4]
. In order to prove that
\IMG(z^{2}+i)
does not have the congruence subgroup property, the goal will be to prove that
K/K^{\prime}\simeq C_{4}^{5}
, namely, the quotient has no other relations.

(2)

We consider
\bigoplus_{\mathbb{N}}S_{K}
, the free monoid over the alphabet
S_{K}
and we define the morphism of monoids
e:\bigoplus_{\mathbb{N}}S_{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, that returns the parity of the length of the word. Using the
L
-presentation of
\IMG(z^{2}+i)
and the Reidemeister algorithm, we find an
L
-presentation for
K
. With this presentation, we prove that the function
e
descends to a function on
K
.

(3)

Using the action of
K
on the first
n
levels of the tree, we deduce that the only possible relations are
ty^{-2}\in K^{\prime}
and
wz^{-2}\in K^{\prime}
.

(4)

Using the function
e
and a subgroup
\mathcal{K}
, which depends on
e
and satisfies
K^{\prime}\leq\mathcal{K}\leq K
, we deduce that
ty^{-2}
and
wz^{-2}
are not in
\mathcal{K}
.

In conclusion:

Theorem B
.

Let
K
be the maximal branching subgroup of
\IMG(z^{2}+i)
. Then
K/K^{\prime}
is isomorphic to
C_{4}^{5}
.

Although it is not necessary for the proof of
Theorem

B
, we will prove that
K/\mathcal{K}\simeq C_{4}\times C_{2}\times C_{2}
. As a consequence, the quotient
\mathcal{K}/K^{\prime}
is an abelian group of order
64
.

Using the algorithm presented in
[
4
, Remark 2.8]
, we also obtain:

Theorem C
.

The rigid kernel of
\IMG(z^{2}+i)
is trivial whereas the congruence kernel and the branch kernel are isomorphic to
C_{4}[[\partial T]]
, namely, the abelian group of formal sums of elements in
\partial T
with coefficients in
C_{4}
, where
\partial T
represents the boundary of the binary tree
T
.

For definitions of rigid kernel and congruence kernel, see
Section

2.5
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A/B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi(A)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi(A)/\pi(B)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{2}}

2.3.
Groups acting on rooted trees

A
d-regular rooted tree

T
is a tree with infinitely many vertices and a root
\emptyset
, where all the vertices have exactly
d
descendants. In this article we will in general work with the binary tree, so
d=2
. The binary tree may also be identified with the finite words over the set
X={\left\{1,2\right\}}
. The set of vertices at distance exactly
n\geq 0
from the root forms the
n
-th level of
T
, denoted
\mathcal{L}_{n}
. The set of vertices whose distance is at most
n
from the root forms the
n
-th truncated tree of
T
, denoted by
T^{n}
. The set of infinite paths starting from the root, or equivalently, the infinite words in
X
, is called the boundary of the tree and is denoted by
\partial T
.

The group of automorphisms of
T
, denoted
\Aut(T)
, is the group of bijective functions from
T
to
T
that preserve adjacency between vertices. This in particular implies that
\Aut(T)\curvearrowright\mathcal{L}_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For any vertex
v\in T
the subtree rooted at
v
, which is again a binary tree, is denoted
T_{v}
. The action of
\Aut(T)
on
T
will be on the left, meaning that if
g,h\in\Aut(T)
and
v\in T
, then
(gh)(v)=g(h(v))
.

Given a vertex
v\in T
, we denote by
\st(v)
the
stabilizer of the vertex
v
, namely, the subgroup of the elements
g\in\Aut(T)
such that
g(v)=v
. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
\St(n)=\bigcap_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}}\st(v)
for the
stabilizer of level
n
. It is not hard to see that
\St(n)
is a normal subgroup of finite index. We endow
\Aut(T)
with a topological group structure by declaring
{\left\{\St(n)\right\}}_{n\geq 0}
to be a base of neighborhoods of the identity. This makes
\Aut(T)
a profinite group, homeomorphic and isomorphic to the inverse limit

\varprojlim\Aut(T)/\St(n).

The transition maps are given by
\pi_{n}:\Aut(T)\rightarrow\Aut(T^{n})
, where
\pi_{n}
restricts the action of an element of
\Aut(T)
to the first
n
levels of the tree.

Let
v\in T
and
g\in\Aut(T)
. By preservation of adjacency, there exists a unique map
g|_{v}\in\Aut(T_{v})
such that for all
w\in T_{v}
, we have

g(vw)=g(v)g|_{v}(w).

This map is called the
section
of
g
at the vertex
v
. The sections satisfy the following two fundamental properties:

(1)

\displaystyle(gh)|_{v}=g|_{h(v)}h|_{v}\text{ and }(g^{-1})|_{v}=(g|_{g^{-1}(v)})^{-1}.

for all
g,h\in\Aut(T)
and
v\in T
.

The properties in
Equation

1
yield the following isomorphism for any natural number
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{n}:\Aut(T)\to(\Aut(T_{v_{1}})\times\dotsb\times\Aut(T_{v_{N_{n}}})\rtimes\Aut(T^{n})\\
\text{ via }g\mapsto(g|_{v_{1}},\dotsc,g|_{v_{N_{n}}})\pi_{n}(g)\end{split}

where
v_{1},\dotsc,v_{N_{n}}
are all the distinct vertices in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
.

To describe the elements of
\Aut(T)
, we will use the isomorphism from
Equation

2
with
n=1
. In some cases, we will also use the notation
(g_{0},\dots,g_{N})_{n}
that corresponds to the image of an element in
\St(n)
via the map
\psi_{n}
.

Now, let us fix a subgroup
G\leq\Aut(T)
. The group
G
inherits natural actions on
T
and each set
\mathcal{L}_{n}
. We define the vertex stabilizers and level stabilizers of
G
by restricting the corresponding stabilizers of
\Aut(T)
to
G
, i.e,

\st_{G}(v):=\st(v)\cap G\text{ and }\St_{G}(n):=\St(n)\cap G

for
v\in T
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

A group
G\leq\Aut(T)
is
level-transitive
if the actions
G\curvearrowright\mathcal{L}_{n}
are transitive for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The group
G
is said to be
self-similar
if
g|_{v}\in G
for all
v\in T
and
g\in G
.

Given
G\leq\Aut(T)
, a vertex
v\in T
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
rigid vertex stabilizer
of
v
, denoted by
\rist_{G}(v)
, as the subgroup of
G
consisting of automorphisms that fix every vertex outside the subtree
T_{v}
. The
n
-th rigid level stabilizer
, denoted
\RiSt_{G}(n)
, is the subgroup generated by the subgroups
\rist_{G}(v)
with
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
. It is not hard to see that
\RiSt_{G}(n)\lhd G
and that
\RiSt_{G}(n)\leq\St_{G}(n)
.

We say that a group
G\leq\Aut(T)
is a
branch group
if
\RiSt_{G}(n)\leq_{f}G
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

2.4.
Regular branch groups

Within the class of branch groups, we have the class of regular branch groups.

If
K
is a subgroup of
\Aut(T)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
geometric product
of
K
on level

as

K_{n}:={\left\{g\in\St(n):g|_{v}\in K\text{ for all $v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}$}\right\}}.

Using the isomorphism in
Equation

2
, if
g\in K_{n}
, we can represent it as

g=(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})_{n},

where
N=\#\mathcal{L}_{n}=2^{n}
and

for all

is a subgroup, then each element
g_{i}:=(1,\dots,1,k_{i},1,\dots,1)_{n}\in K_{n}
and so

can be split as the product of the elements

. We conclude that if

(3)

{\left\{(1,\dots,1,s,1,\dots,1)_{n}:s\in S,i=1,\dots,2^{n}\right\}}

.

A subgroup

Notice that by iterating the inclusion

(

If

The following lemma will be useful in the proof of

Lemma 2.4

Let

. We take

and apply

\varphi:\pi_{n+1}(K)/\pi_{n+1}(K^{\prime})\rightarrow\pi_{n}(K)/\pi_{n}(K^{\prime})

Thus, our goal is to prove this inclusion for all

First of all, we have

. Indeed,

2.5.
Congruence subgroup property

Let
T
be a
d
-regular rooted tree and let
G
be a subgroup of
\Aut(T)
. Since
\Aut(T)
is a topological group with
{\left\{\St(n):n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}
as a base of neighborhoods of the identity, then the closure of
G
in
\Aut(T)
is isomorphic to

\overline{G}=\varprojlim G/\St_{G}(n)

by
[
24
, Corollary 1.1.8]
. In the case that
G
is a branch group, then

\widetilde{G}:=\varprojlim G/\RiSt_{G}(n)

is also a profinite group and since
\RiSt_{G}(n)\leq\St_{G}(n)
we have a well-defined surjective homomorphism

\psi_{1}:\widetilde{G}\rightarrow\overline{G}.

The kernel of
\psi_{1}
is called the
rigid kernel
.

Another standard construction in group theory is the
profinite completion
of a group, defined as the inverse limit

\widehat{G}:=\varprojlim G/N,

where the limit runs over all normal subgroups
N
of finite index in
G
. Since
G
is branch, the family of rigid stabilizers is a subset of the family of normal subgroups of finite index of
G
, and therefore we have a well-defined surjective homomorphism

\psi_{2}:\widehat{G}\rightarrow\widetilde{G}.

The kernel of
\psi_{2}
is called the
branch kernel
. Composing the last two maps, we obtain an epimorphism

\psi_{3}=\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}:\widehat{G}\rightarrow\overline{G}.

The kernel of
\psi_{3}
is called the

5.
The exponent function in
K

Define
S_{K}={\left\{x,y,z,t,w\right\}}
and the function
e:\bigoplus S_{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
where given a word with letters in
S_{K}
, it returns the parity of the length of the word. Clearly, the function
e
is a morphism from the free monoid
\bigoplus S_{K}
to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. In this section, we aim to prove that
e
descends to a well-defined function
e:K\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. To achieve this, we must show that for every relation
\mathfrak{R}
in
S_{K}
, we have
e(\mathfrak{R})=0
.

The simplest approach would be to imitate the proof for the Basilica group presented in
[
14
, Lemma 1 page 7]
. However, there is a big difference between that case and ours. In the Basilica group, if
g=(g_{0},g_{1})
, then the lengths of
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
in terms of the generators are strictly smaller than the length of
g
. In our case, this property does not hold, as for example
xy^{-1}x^{-1}=(txy,1)
, where the length remains the same or
xy^{-2}x^{-1}=(txytxy,1)
, where the length is actually bigger. For this reason, we adopt this more sophisticated strategy, which involves finding all relations
\mathfrak{R}
in
K
and verifying that
e(\mathfrak{R})=0
. Thus, we need to show that

e(\tau(s_{t,\alpha})s_{t,\alpha}^{-1})=e(\tau(tRt^{-1}))=0\pmod{2}

for all
\alpha\in S_{G}
,
t\in\mathcal{T}
and
R
a relation in
G
.

Define also the function
\ell:\bigoplus S_{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
where given a word in
S_{G}
, it returns the parity of the number of letters
a
and
c
in the word and
\beta:\bigoplus S_{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, that returns the parity of the number of letters
b
in the word. For example, we have
\ell(abacac)=5\equiv 1\pmod{2}
and
\beta(abacac)\equiv 1\pmod{2}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The functions
\ell
and
\beta
descend to well-defined functions from the quotient
G/K
to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, if
\omega
is a word in
S_{G}
, then we have
\ell(\omega^{n})=n\ell(\omega)
and
\beta(\omega^{n})=n\beta(\omega)
.

Proof.

The first claim follows from the fact that
K={\left\langle{[a,b],[b,c]}\right\rangle}^{G}
and the generators satisfy
\beta([a,b])=\beta([b,c])=\ell([a,b])=\ell([b,c])=0
. The second part is straightforward.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

For all

, we have

.

Proof.

Consider first the case when

with

. In this case, the result follows directly from the explicit expressions for the elements

given in

Now consider the case when

. Using the property

, we have

completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.3

Let

and

Write

and

\displaystyle e(\tau(tRt^{-1}))=\sum_{i=1}^{m}e(s_{\mathscr{R}(\beta_{1}\dots\beta_{i-1}),\beta_{i}})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}e(s_{\mathscr{R}(t\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{j-1}),\alpha_{j}})

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{m}e(s_{\mathscr{R}(tR\beta_{m}\dots\beta_{k+1}),\beta_{k}}).

By the choice of the elements in
\mathcal{T}
, only
\beta_{1}
can be

, and by

. This simplifies the previous formula to

\displaystyle e(\tau(tRt^{-1}))=e(s_{1,\beta_{1}})+\sum_{j:\alpha_{j}=b}e(s_{\mathscr{R}(t\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{j-1}),b})+e(s_{\mathscr{R}(tR\beta_{m}\dots\beta_{2}),\beta_{1}}).

We have

for all
\alpha\in S_{G}
,

by

\displaystyle e(\tau(tRt^{-1}))=\sum_{j:\alpha_{j}=b}\ell(t\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{j-1})+e(s_{\mathscr{R}(tR\beta_{m}\dots\beta_{2}),\beta_{1}}).

\displaystyle e(\tau(tRt^{-1}))=\sum_{j:\alpha_{j}=b}\left(\ell(t)+\ell(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{j-1})\right)+e(s_{\mathscr{R}(tR\beta_{m}\dots\beta_{2}),\beta_{1}})

On the other hand,

\displaystyle e(\tau(R))=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e(s_{\mathscr{R}(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{j-1}),\alpha_{j}})=\sum_{j:\alpha_{j}=b}\ell(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{j-1}),

\displaystyle e(\tau(tRt^{-1}))=\ell(t)\beta(R)+e(\tau(R))+e(s_{\mathscr{R}(tR\beta_{m}\dots\beta_{2}),\beta_{1}}).

Therefore, combining both cases, we conclude

Lemma 6.1

Lemma 6.2

The following conjugation relations hold:

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}axa=x^{-1}&bxb=x^{-1}&cxc=txy\\

\end{matrix}

In particular, for any

and

, we have

Proof.

We label the relations as in a

Theorem 7.1
